The Influenza‐Specific Immunoglobulin Repertoire – Nimble Yet Steeped in Tradition
Authors: Sarah F. Andrews, Masaru Kanekiyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza virus has circulated in the human population and co‐evolved with us for centuries or longer, shaping our immune repertoires through a continuous host–virus arms race. Across individuals, antibodies repeatedly converge on shared solutions for capturing this shape‐shifting virus by precisely targeting conserved sites of vulnerability—sites the virus cannot alter without functional cost—through structural features encoded directly by germline immunoglobulin genes. These recurring public antibodies, termed multi‐donor class antibodies, have been pivotal for understanding human anti‐influenza immunity and in guiding vaccine strategies that may shift the balance of the host–virus arms race. As single‐cell biology, structural biology, and computational approaches mature, previously uncharted areas of our immune repertoires are coming into view. Here, we review the current understanding of the influenza‐specific immune repertoire, focusing on antibodies and multi‐donor antibody classes directed toward antigenic supersites on viral hemagglutinin and neuraminidase—the two major targets of protective antibodies—and discuss how these insights may advance the development of game‐changing influenza vaccines.

Introduction

Influenza viruses have a negative‐sense single‐stranded RNA genome composed of 8 different genomic segments encoding 10 major proteins. Two of these are glycoproteins on the viral surface, hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), the primary targets of protective B cell responses and the main focus of this review. Influenza infection is mediated through engagement of sialylated host cell receptors via the receptor‐binding site (RBS) on the HA head and endocytosis. Endosomes are acidified and HA undergoes a conformational change, mediating fusion of the viral and endosomal membranes and releasing the viral genome. After synthesis of viral proteins in the host cell, newly formed virions are exported to the cell surface. Detachment of HA from the cell surface and virion egress is mediated by the viral sialidase, NA. Antibodies that block HA and NA therefore independently inhibit the virus lifecycle. The HA molecule is a homotrimer composed of two major functional regions, the globular head which contains the RBS, and the stem or stalk which houses the viral fusion machinery. Influenza HA can be phylogenetically divided into type A and type B. Within type A, multiple HAs are further divided into antigenically distinct group 1 and group 2. One subtype from each group currently co‐circulates in the human population, H1 (group 1) and H3 (group 2). Similarly, NA are subdivided into group 1 and group 2 within type A, with only N1 (group 1) and N2 (group 2) present in currently circulating human viruses. Within each subtype, there is continual antigenic drift leading to emergence of new strains. Most antigenic changes occur in the HA head region, particularly surrounding the RBS mediated by antibody‐driven escape mutations. In contrast, the HA stem remains relatively conserved within subtypes and across subtypes and is the primary target of broadly neutralizing antibody responses.

Convergence of the HA‐Specific Repertoire

The B cell repertoire is the total, diverse collection of unique antigen receptors within an individual. On a population level, studies of the HA‐specific repertoire have revealed that despite the incredible human immunoglobulin (Ig) repertoire diversity, epitope‐specific responses exhibit immunogenetic convergence across individuals. As seen in the response to other viruses, enrichment for Ig heavy‐ and light‐chain genes and complementarity‐determine region (CDR) sequences is observed for antibodies targeting the same influenza HA epitope. This molecular convergence can take the form of specific variable heavy and light (VH and VL) gene pairs plus CDRH3 sequences with specific amino acid (aa) motifs, or be more loosely defined based on preferential usage of particular VH or VL genes or particular residues in a CDR critical for binding a certain epitope. In this review, we will be referring to these convergent modes of engagement of an epitope with shared molecular characteristics as multi‐donor classes. It is also important to note that shared variable gene usage does not necessarily equate to engaging the epitope the same way, as described in more detail below, so the identification of particular classes can be difficult. Within the same individual, multiple clonal lineages belonging to the same multi‐donor class binding the same epitope are often present, indicating multiple instances of molecular convergence from the naïve B cell repertoire within the same individual [1, 2, 3]. Both genetics and particular influenza exposures likely play a role in shaping convergent responses. As single‐cell sequencing methodologies continue to improve and antigen‐specific B cell repertoire databases grow, it is likely that more examples of molecular convergence will become apparent within individuals and across the population.

Convergence in the HA Head‐Specific Repertoire

Multiple instances of multi‐donor classes binding HA head epitopes have been described over the years that are both broadly cross‐reactive and narrowly specific. Interestingly, the majority have been identified after first exposure to a novel HA. For example, first exposure to the pandemic 2009 H1N1 virus enriched for rare classes of antibodies. Three groups independently identified BCR lineages expressing VH3‐7 paired with JH6 with an 18‐aa‐long CDRH3 with a common motif binding the Sa antigenic site on the HA head [2, 3, 4]. This class was expanded in individuals responding for the first time to the pandemic 2009 H1N1 strain either by infection or vaccination, but was not observed in subsequent responses to pandemic H1N1, suggesting it may be a subdominant response in the presence of newly generated pandemic 2009 H1N1 specific B cells [2, 3, 4]. Antibodies binding the lateral patch on the HA head were also identified in multiple individuals after 2009 H1N1 vaccination with a restricted usage of VH and VK genes, often VH3‐23 and VK1‐33 with a YXR (where X can be any aa) motif in the CDRH3 [5]. Another class identified in individuals vaccinated against 2009 H1N1 is characterized by VH2‐70 and DH4‐17 paired most often with either VL3‐21 or VL3‐9. The CDRH3 in this class extends into the RBS and engages the pocket primarily with a YGD motif [6, 7, 8, 9]. Other RBS‐specific antibodies with diverse variable gene usage have also been shown to engage the pocket with a central Asp in the CDRH3 [10] suggesting a common molecular strategy to engage the RBS. The CDRH3 Asp mimics the carboxylate interactions of sialic acid with HA and a Val residue often immediately preceding the Asp mimics the sialic acid acetamide interactions, though this feature is inconsistent across antibodies [10]. The HA trimer interface has been identified as a target of broadly binding antibodies with canonical features as well [11, 12]. Antibodies binding to this epitope use diverse VH genes but have shared VK1‐39 gene usage. Acidic residues (Asp or Glu) in the CDRH3 along with the canonical CDRL2 of VK1‐39 are critical for engaging this epitope [11].

Multi‐donor classes can also be detected in de novo responses from the naïve B cell repertoire upon first exposure to a novel HA subtype. In response to H2 vaccination in individuals with no prior exposure to H2, three separate multi‐donor classes, each with unique VH and VK gene pairing and canonical CDRH3s, binding three different HA head epitopes were detected in several individuals [13]. In addition, a class of H5‐specific antibodies with broad neutralization capacity across H5 clades was identified in five individuals vaccinated with an H5 immunogen [14]. This class was characterized by a light chain encoded by VK2‐28 containing germline‐encoded residues in the CDRL1 and CDRL2 that served as critical contacts for binding a lateral epitope conserved across H5 subtypes [14]. Thus, multi‐donor classes are present both in the memory and naïve B cell repertoire and can respond both in recall and de novo responses to influenza infection or vaccination.

Convergence in the HA Stem‐Specific Repertoire

Unlike the HA head with many antigenic sites, antibodies binding the HA stem are largely restricted to two main areas in the human repertoire [15, 16]. One surrounds a hydrophobic groove near the center of the stem region, termed the central epitope and the other is toward the base of the HA ectodomain, termed the anchor or lower epitope. The central epitope is highly conserved and the target of broadly neutralizing antibodies able to bind and neutralize subtypes within group 1 or group 2 or subtypes in both groups. Antibodies binding the lower region of the HA stem are often subtype‐specific but still have broader breadth within subtypes than most head‐targeting antibodies. A striking feature of antibodies binding the HA stem is the restricted repertoire both within individuals and across the population. In response to vaccination with group 1 immunogens, multi‐donor classes were identified in 40/40 individuals ranging from 60% to 98% of their stem‐specific repertoire across two vaccine studies [1, 15]. The response to group 2 immunogens is more diverse, but multi‐donor classes were still identified in 33/41 individuals in two vaccine studies, making up 1%–60% of their repertoire [1, 16].

Five major multi‐donor classes have been described binding the central epitope (Figure 1). The largest and most prevalent is the VH1‐69 class, identified in all individuals we have immunized with group 1 immunogens including an H1‐stabilized stem, H5, or H2 HA [1, 15, 17]. In response to group 2 immunogens, a class defined as VH1‐18 with a QXXV CDRH3 motif was the most prevalent, found in ~60% of vaccinated individuals [1, 16]. Additional classes encoded by a VH1‐18 with an ILTG CDRH3 motif or VH6‐1 with a DH3‐3‐encoded IFGV/I CDRH3 motif have also been identified but are less prevalent [18]. Structurally, this latter class uses the DH3‐3‐encoded IFGV/I motif to contact the Trp21HA2 hydrophobic groove, with the VH6‐1‐encoded CDRH2 providing supplementary interactions [18, 19]. In addition to these classes, DH3‐3 with an IFGV/I motif or DH3‐9 as either an ILTG or LXYFXWL, depending on the reading frame, is a recurring strategy to engage the stem hydrophobic groove through the CDRH3, independently of VH gene usage (Figure 1) [7, 20]. Certain VH genes, such as VH3‐30 is also enriched in stem‐binding antibodies, but structural analysis of antibodies encoded by this VH gene demonstrates various modes of binding and it is unclear what drives the preference for this VH gene [17, 21].

Different multi‐donor classes target the HA stem. For each multi‐donor class, the core motif engaging the HA stem, the primary influenza specificity of each class, and level of breadth typically achieved through somatic hypermutation are indicated.

Though highly conserved, the central stem regions of group 1 and group 2 HA subtypes differ in both glycosylation patterns and orientation relative to the HA head. Thus, each multi‐donor class differs in the level of cross‐reactivity it normally achieves based on its mode of binding. The VH1‐69 class engages the central stem hydrophobic groove surrounding Trp21HA2 primarily through its germline‐encoded CDRH2 dipeptide Ile‐Phe (IF) motif and requires a limited number of somatic hypermutations to achieve high‐affinity binding [22]. However, with a couple of notable exceptions [23, 24], VH1‐69 class antibodies only bind and neutralize group 1 subtypes primarily due to the inability to accommodate a glycan at position 38HA2 present on most group 2 subtypes [23]. Interestingly, when a full set of endogenous murine DH and JH genes are replaced by human DH and JH genes in the context of a human VH1‐69 gene transgenic mouse, [25] their B cell responses to the HA stem can shift from VH1‐69‐mediated recognition to DH3‐9‐dependent binding, depending on the immunizing HA stem characteristics [26]. Upon immunizing with a group 1 stem that supports canonical CDRH2‐mediated VH1‐69 recognition, they mount human‐like VH1‐69‐dependent B cell responses [25, 26, 27]. However, upon immunizing with a group 2 stem that is incompatible with canonical VH1‐69 recognition, the same mice shift to CDRH3‐mediated LXYFXWL motif‐dependent recognition [20]. This switch is primarily determined by the altered glycosylation pattern of group 1 and group 2 HA stems [26]. Though these findings have limited translational or clinical relevance, they suggest strategies for redirecting aberrant immune responses through selective antigen design.

VH1‐18 ILTG and VH6‐1 + DH3‐3 classes also exhibit broad breadth and potent neutralization across group 1 subtypes and are likely derived from naïve precursors in response to H1 HAs. However, there are multiple examples of these two classes acquiring breadth to group 2 subtypes through somatic hypermutations [1, 28]. Analysis of the maturation process of these classes from being group 1‐specific to cross‐group specific indicates that it is multi‐faceted involving mutations on both the heavy and light chains [29]. The VH6‐1 + DH3‐3 class, including MEDI8852 and 56.a.09 [18, 19], represents one of the broadest and most potent stem‐directed multi‐donor classes, though it is only identified in a subset of individuals [1, 15, 18, 19].

In contrast to the other three classes discussed, the VH1‐18 QXXV class is first elicited by group 2 subtypes, and then gains cross‐reactivity to group 1 subtypes. An N55T mutation in the CDRH2 was found to be sufficient to gain binding to group 1 subtypes, though additional mutations are needed to gain the ability to neutralize group 1 subtypes [30]. Many VH1‐18 QXXV lineages found in the human population can only neutralize group 2 subtypes [1, 28, 31].

Antibodies binding the anchor stem epitope on group 1 subtypes are also extremely canonically defined by primarily VH3‐23/VH3‐30/VH3‐48 paired with VK3‐15/VK3‐11 with a germline‐encoded NWP motif in the CDRL3 (Figure 1). The CDRL3 serves as the primary contact with the HA fusion peptide along with CDRH2 Tyr58 [15, 32, 33]. In a survey of the antibody repertoire binding the anchor stem epitope, this class was identified in 19/20 individuals representing the majority of antibodies binding this antigenic site. For reasons that are not completely understood, this class of antibodies is primarily restricted in binding to H1 HAs with only occasional instances of binding other group 1 subtypes, H5 and H2 [15, 32]. In contrast to antibodies binding the group 1 anchor epitope, the antibody repertoire binding a lower stem antigenic site on group 2 subtypes is quite diverse with no clear multi‐donor classes [16]. Antibodies targeting this group 2 epitope are also largely restricted to binding only H3 and H10 strains within group 2 [16]. Despite considerable conservation in this region, residue differences between subtypes and across H3 strains contribute to varying antibody cross‐reactivity and potency [16, 34].

Altogether, the strong molecular convergence of the antibody repertoire targeting the HA stem provides unique opportunities to design immunogens that would specifically engage precursors of these broadly neutralizing and prevalent antibody classes.

Interspecies Conservation of HA‐Specific Repertoire

Nonhuman primates, particularly macaques, have been central to biomedical research for decades because of their evolutionary proximity to humans. Humans and chimpanzees shared a common ancestor 6–9 million years ago, while macaques diverged from humans over 25 million years ago [35]. Thus, their immune repertoires, including Ig germline genes, have independently evolved over millions of years to combat infectious and non‐infectious threats, driven solely by survival pressure. Although influenza virus or other human pathogens likely had none or minimal impact on the selective pressure to shape immune repertoires of these species or other experimental animal models such as rodents, their immune systems exhibit striking convergence in responding to certain structural features on viral proteins.

In 1993, a murine monoclonal antibody, C179, was reported as the first broadly neutralizing HA stem‐directed antibody across H1 and H2 subtypes [36]. Two decades later, the structure of C179 was determined, [37] unveiling surprising similarities to human antibodies to the HA stem epitope with the aforementioned DH3‐9‐encoded LXYFXWL motif [20]. The core YFXW motif of human antibodies in this class, such as FI6v3, [38] makes critical contacts nearly perfectly reproduced by the YFXY motif within the CDRH3 of murine C179 [37]. Though the LXYFXWL motif is encoded by the germline DH3‐9 gene in humans, mice lack the same motif in their germline DH genes, suggesting the stochasticity of the YFXY motif within the VDJ junction and may explain why such broadly neutralizing antibodies are rare in this species.

Macaque Ig repertoires show both differences and similarities to humans [39, 40]. Many human VH gene orthologs are present in macaques with varying sequence homology. For example, the macaque VH1‐69 ortholog, VH1‐138, shares high sequence homology and the characteristic hydrophobic CDRH2 tip, yet lacks the critical IF dipeptide critical to canonical HA stem recognition in humans [22]. For this reason, it was long assumed that macaques could not mediate HA stem recognition in a human‐like manner via VH1‐138. Recent data, however, show that macaques do preferentially use VH1‐138 for HA stem‐specific B cells [41]. Like human VH1‐69, macaque VH1‐138 recognition of the stem relies almost exclusively on antibody heavy chain contacts. Although the specific CDRH2 interactions differ, both use their hydrophobic CDRH2 tips to access the same hydrophobic groove surrounding Trp21HA2 on the HA stem. VH1‐138 recapitulates not only hydrophobic contacts by CDRH2 but also interactions made by CDRH1 and a centrally positioned CDRH3 Tyr as in human counterparts.

Other human multi‐donor classes are also recapitulated by macaques including the human VH6‐1 + DH3‐3 class. Notably, while macaques lack the VH6‐1 gene, they possess a macaque DH3‐41 gene nearly identical to the human DH3‐3 gene [39]. Consequently, macaques recurringly generate IFGV/I‐harboring antibodies recognizing the HA stem, representing a macaque multi‐donor class, albeit the specificity is often limited to group 1 HAs [42]. Furthermore, a broadly neutralizing antibody, 789‐203‐3C12, was later isolated from an immunized macaque, featuring broader cross‐group breadth and potency approaching the human VH6‐1 + DH3‐3 class antibodies [43]. This antibody utilizes the DH3‐41‐encoded IFGV motif yet is derived from a different VH gene family from the multi‐donor class. The IFGV motif of 789‐203‐3C12 aligns precisely with that of human 56.a.09, illustrating another example of interspecies convergence at viral sites of vulnerability [43]. Although it is not definitive, this mode of recognition may be more prevalent, as EMPEM analysis of macaque sera detects such antibody responses post‐immunization [41, 44].

The human multi‐donor class targeting the HA stem anchor epitope with the VH3‐23/VH3‐30/VH3‐48 gene and CDRL3 NWP motif is also recapitulated in macaques. These stem anchor‐directed B cells are elicited by seasonal influenza vaccines, chimeric HA vaccines, and HA stem nanoparticle in humans [15, 32]. Macaques immunized with the same HA stem nanoparticle faithfully recapitulate the stem anchor epitope‐specific B cell responses, including the immunogenetic and structural signatures of the human multi‐donor class [41]. Macaques use either the human VH3‐23 or VH3‐48 ortholog paired with the human VK3‐15 ortholog, precisely harboring the CDRL3 NWP motif [41]. The structure of a macaque anchor antibody, T009‐3E04, bound to HA is indistinguishable from its human homologs. This demonstrates that macaques (and potentially other nonhuman primate species) are capable of eliciting “public” B cell responses to influenza HA that are convergent with humans at atomic resolution. This atomic‐level congruence in epitope recognition far exceeds prior understanding of HA recognition across species, whether between humans and mice [45] or the evolutionarily distant lampreys [46]. While there are more examples of antibody convergence between humans and macaques toward other viral targets, including human hepatitis C virus [47] and HIV‐1, [48, 49] they are outside the scope of this review and not discussed in here.

Non‐HA Targeting Repertoire

Neuraminidase

The crystal structure of NA was resolved in 1983, revealing the homotetrameric assembly and catalytic sites on each protomer that hydrolyze sialosides [50, 51]. Human monoclonal antibodies target three principal antigenic surfaces: the catalytic center and its vicinity, the lateral surface perpendicular to the tetramer axis, and the underside or dark side facing the viral membrane [52]. Additional potential antigenic sites include the four‐fold symmetry axis pore, the tetramer interface, and the stalk, which connects the globular head domain and viral membrane. The most broadly cross‐reactive antibodies target the catalytic site, competing directly with the substrate and inhibiting sialidase activity [53, 54, 55]. Lateral surface and underside antibodies typically show narrower cross‐reactivity, restricted to within‐subtype breadth, and inhibit sialidase activity through indirect steric hindrance [56, 57, 58]. Antibodies targeting the four‐fold axis pore, tetramer interface, or stalk may also inhibit sialidase activity and show cross‐reactivity, but more examples are needed to map the complete antigenic landscape of NA. Gaps remain in our understanding of the NA antigenic landscape and there has been relatively little characterization of the B cell repertoire recognizing NA. Responses to NA are minimal following vaccination with currently licensed influenza vaccines likely due to low NA content and/or quality, [59] making it difficult to study human B cell responses. However, influenza virus infection triggers robust NA‐directed plasmablast responses in humans, [59] exposing a key gap in current vaccine design. New vaccine platforms like mRNA‐LNP may circumvent immunogenicity challenges of NA. Results from early clinical trials show promising NA‐directed serum antibody responses, suggesting that NA‐based vaccines, when delivered and presented properly, can engage the B cell repertoire established by past influenza exposures [60].

Though NA‐specific BCR sequences have expanded exponentially in recent years, they remain far fewer than HA‐specific sequences. Consequently, there is little characterization of multi‐donor classes or longitudinal lineage tracing for NA‐directed antibodies. Despite these constraints, broadly cross‐reactive antibodies to the catalytic site from unrelated individuals show striking structural convergence in how they mimic the atomic interaction of sialic acid with the catalytic pocket [54, 61, 62, 63, 64]. This mimicry occurs through a dipeptide motif—Asp‐Arg or Arg‐Asp (DR or RD)—in the CDR H3 loop. The carboxyl group of Asp mimics the carboxylate of sialic acid, while the guanidino group of the Arg replaces the acetamido interactions [62]. In rare cases, Glu substitutes for Asp to achieve the carboxylate mimicry, exemplified by the human antibody NA‐45 [65]. The notable exception is 1G01, [53] which achieves broad breadth and potency across NA subtypes including influenza B viruses without precise sialic acid mimicry. Nonetheless, a conserved CDR H3 structural motif mediating carboxylate and acetamido mimicry remains a hallmark of most human antibodies to the NA catalytic site. The DR or RD motif is often centrally positioned and emerges from the non‐templated DH–DJ junction, rather than germline DH or JH sequences [62]. These antibodies typically have extended CDR H3 loops (18+ residues) that project the DR/RD motif into the catalytic pocket. Remarkably, the selection criteria parallel those governing antibodies targeting the HA RBS pocket as described above. Analogous to NA catalytic site antibodies, mutagenesis experiments show that Glu can substitute for Asp to mimic carboxylate interactions, demonstrating motif flexibility [10].

The theoretical precursor frequency for HA RBS antibodies is estimated as 1/30,000 without accounting for central B cell tolerance and compatible light chain pairing, if only considering a CDRH3 18–20 residues long with a hydrophobic residue followed by Asp at the tip [10]. More recently, Jo and colleagues empirically calculated the precursor frequency for NA catalytic site antibodies [62] using existing ultradeep sequencing data from multiple individuals [66, 67]. Using CDRH3 criteria alone (19–30 residues long with DR or RD motif at the tip), they calculated a frequency of ~1/500 though the authors acknowledged this is likely a massive overestimation [62]. Applying stricter criteria, including DH gene usage and exact DR/RD positioning, reduced the frequency to 0–14 per 1,000,000, depending on donor and reference antibody [62]. Although most HA RBS and NA catalytic site antibodies use the JH6 gene, this preferential gene usage correlates with CDRH3 length rather than functional selection [67, 68, 69]. Importantly, both HA RBS‐ and NA catalytic site‐directed B cells recur in multiple individuals with nearly identical CDRH3 structural motifs. This convergence, despite the motif not being germline‐encoded, suggests that our immune repertoire iteratively discovers the same solution to target viral vulnerabilities. This brings up the possibility that a single antibody could recognize both the HA RBS and NA catalytic site through sialic acid mimicry. While differences in chemical and structural milieu between HA and NA sialic acid binding pockets make this unlikely, such antibodies would theoretically inhibit both viral attachment and egress, two spatially and temporally separate steps in virus lifecycle.

Other Viral Proteins

Immune repertoires targeting non‐HA/NA proteins remain poorly characterized. A study investigating plasmablast responses found that plasmablasts elicited by vaccination and infection target starkly different viral targets [70]. Intramuscular vaccines skewed the plasmablast response toward HA (~90%), whereas influenza infection, regardless of the infecting virus subtype, elicited more diffuse responses with 40%–50% targeting non‐HA/NA proteins, including ~20% toward the viral nucleoprotein (NP) [70]. However, this study did not analyze Ig gene usage or identify public clonotypes in these plasmablasts. A complementary study characterized NP‐specific memory B cells from healthy human subjects using fluorescently labeled recombinant NP probes [71]. Somewhat surprisingly, among 20 healthy individuals, NP‐specific IgG+ memory B cells were equally prevalent as HA‐specific B cells at steady state, [71] suggesting that these NP‐specific B cells are elicited and maintained by recurrent infections. BCR sequencing of NP‐specific B cells from three individuals (~150 sequences) revealed no biased gene usage or recurrent sequence motifs. NP‐specific antibodies lack virus‐neutralizing activity in vitro and little protective efficacy in mice, despite representing a large proportion of infection‐induced B cell responses [70, 71]. They could function as immunological decoys or distracting epitopes (“distractopes”) that compete with productive and protective antibody targets.

The M2 ectodomain (M2e), a small transmembrane ion channel, is another target of protective antibodies [72, 73, 74, 75]. Although B cell responses to M2e are rare following infection or vaccination, efforts to isolate human monoclonal antibodies to this domain have yielded several antibodies, including the clinically evaluated antibody TCN‐032 [74]. In this study, M2e‐specific antibodies were generated from IgG+ memory B cells from five of 23 M2e‐seropositive individuals (out of 140 subjects they screened). Interestingly, all 15 recovered antibodies used a single VK gene, VK1‐39, though this observation is limited by the small sample size and lacks structural or sequence evidence supporting molecular convergence. The lead human antibody TCN‐032 was tested for efficacy against influenza in Phase 2 clinical trials, but failed to meet its primary endpoint despite showing weak signals in reducing symptoms after experimental infection [76].

Longitudinal Analysis of the Influenza‐Specific Repertoire

Most individuals are first exposed to influenza and establish an influenza‐specific immune repertoire within the first 2–3 years of life which then evolves over a person's lifetime with subsequent influenza exposures through infection or vaccination. The extent to which the repertoire changes over time is unclear as comprehensive longitudinal studies of the influenza specific repertoire over many years are lacking, but studies looking at influenza repertoires in different age groups suggest that early responses are more diverse and in later years become more oligoclonal likely from continual influenza exposure and exacerbated by immune senescence [77]. Consistent with this, one study also found less mutational diversity within influenza‐specific clones in the elderly [78]. H2N2 only circulated in the human population between 1957 and 1968 providing an opportunity to look at the H2‐specific repertoire generated in childhood without subsequent H2N2 exposure. A study looking at the response to H2 vaccination in 2018 using a cohort with or without prior exposure to H2 based on year of birth demonstrated that memory B cells from childhood infection were maintained in the memory B cell repertoire throughout life and responded to the H2 vaccine 50 years later largely to the exclusion of a de novo B cell response [13]. Further, these long‐lived memory B cells were more oligoclonal, less diverse than newly generated memory B cells generated in individuals born after 1968 in response to the vaccine [13]. A similar phenomenon was observed when looking at the long‐lived smallpox B cell repertoire generated over 40 years ago after smallpox vaccination [79]. This suggests that over time certain HA‐specific clonotypes have a competitive advantage for long term survival, even in the absence of antigen exposure, and the antigen‐specific repertoire within an individual becomes less diverse. Another study analyzed two broadly neutralizing lineages in an adult over a 10‐year period that recognized a conserved HA stem epitope [80]. These lineages were detected throughout that period and responded to multiple influenza vaccines containing different HA subtypes over the years. However, there was little evidence of further maturation or evolution of these lineages over that time period [80]. Vaccination with different HAs expanded different branches of the lineage phylogeny, but did not drive detectable progressive maturation. All together, these studies paint a picture whereby the B cell repertoire established early in life is maintained and continues to respond to antigen exposure but undergoes little change over time after initial maturation.

Determinants of Repertoire

Genetics

While multi‐donor classes are frequently detected in individuals, molecular diversity within these classes and outside of them varies substantially from person to person [1, 15]. V gene allelic polymorphisms are abundant in the repertoire and play an important role in determining the HA‐specific repertoire. One study looking at over 1000 published antigen–antibody structures determined that V gene allelic polymorphisms could be found in 52% of the antibody paratopes and in many cases an alternative allele would have disrupted the interaction [81]. Indeed, one study identified the VH2‐70/DH4‐17 head binding class in an individual heterozygous for VH2‐70*01 and VH2‐70*15 [7]. HA‐binding antibodies were enriched for VH2‐70*01 and it was found that the VH2‐70*15 allele contains an Arg50 just outside the paratope that was incompatible with HA binding [7].

VH1‐69 is one of the classic examples of allelic polymorphism. It was recognized many years ago that certain alleles encode a Phe54 in CDRH2, part of the canonical IF motif, while roughly 13% of the population is homozygous for a Leu54 instead [27, 82, 83]. However, individuals homozygous for alleles encoding Leu54 can still mount robust VH1‐69 class HA stem responses using alternative modes of engaging the HA stem [17, 27, 84]. Using a humanized mouse model, Sangseland et al. found that VH1‐69 antibodies from both mice and humans encoded by a Leu54 were capable of binding HA but were more polyreactive and underwent central tolerance in mice [27]. Nevertheless, it is not uncommon to find VH1‐69 class antibodies with a Leu54 in the human population, so it is unclear if similar mechanisms are at play [17]. Overall, this is an example where allelic polymorphisms do not abolish a particular type of response but change the paratope interactions with consequences that are not entirely understood.

Beyond Genetics

It has become increasingly clear, however, that in conjunction with genetic polymorphisms, the particular subtype and/or strain of influenza exposure can also affect the B cell repertoire. Immunizing with H1 expands a less cross‐reactive stem‐binding repertoire than H5 or H2 HA vaccination [15]. This was found to be due to both differences in the ratio of antibodies binding the more conserved central epitope and the less conserved anchor epitope as well as differences in the breadth of antibodies binding each of these epitopes (Figure 2). Immunizing with H1 increases the ratio of the response targeting the pan‐H1 anchor epitope whereas immunizing with H2 or H5 shifts the response to target more the central epitope [15]. In addition, the few antibodies elicited by H2 vaccination binding the anchor epitope are enriched for the infrequent antibodies with increased cross‐reactivity capable of binding this subtype. Even within VH1‐69 class antibodies binding the central epitope, there are shifts in breadth depending on the vaccinating immunogen [15, 17]. Within the HA stem hydrophobic pocket H2 HA contains a Phe54HA2 while H5 HA contains an Ile54HA2. The aromatic ring in Phe54HA2 is predicted to clash with many central stem‐binding antibodies including VH1‐69 class antibodies. We found that immunizing with H2 or H5 did not change the magnitude of the VH1‐69 class response, but lineages of this class expanded by H2 vaccination were selected for those able to accommodate Phe54HA2 and were more cross‐reactive than VH1‐69 class antibodies induced by H1 or H5 vaccination. Different immunogens can also selectively expand different arms of a particular lineage within an individual. This was illustrated in a study where members of a VH6‐1 class broadly neutralizing lineage were isolated at multiple timepoints across a 10‐year span. The broadest clonal members were detected after H7 vaccination, as gaining breadth to H7 required more somatic mutations and maturation than other subtypes. Phylogenetic analysis indicated clonal members capable of binding H7 were maintained in the repertoire throughout this time period but were selectively expanded upon H7 immunization [80]. Together, these data highlight the variability within multi‐donor classes and how one residue change in an immunogen can alter the B cell repertoire elicited upon vaccination both within and across clonotypes.

Exposure to different subtypes changes the breadth of the HA‐specific response. Upon vaccination with H1 or H2, the ratio of the antibody response targeting the central or anchor epitope differs and the cross‐reactivity of antibodies targeting each of these epitopes also varies. H2 vaccination elicits a more cross‐reactive response overall.

These changes in expansion of pre‐existing memory B cells with different immunogens, however, are usually temporary and unlikely to change the long‐term pre‐existing memory B cell repertoire. It's long been known that the influenza strains first encountered in life fundamentally shape influenza immunity and protection [85, 86] but the links between these changes in immune protection and the B cell repertoire have only begun to emerge. A recent study looking at the repertoire of infants first infected with H1N1 or H3N2 found that the subtype of first infection changed the repertoire of cross‐group antibodies binding the central epitope [87]. First infection with H1 drove a largely H1‐neutralizing repertoire of both pre‐ and post‐pandemic H1N1 strains, whereas H3 infection enriched for cross‐group‐binding antibodies able to neutralize both H3 and post‐pandemic H1N1 strains [87]. These differences in reactivity were linked to a single residue change in HA at position 46HA2. These changes in the cross‐group repertoire established upon first infection may carry through life and affect protection to subsequent influenza exposures. In a separate study, we looked at the central epitope response to H5 vaccination in individuals first exposed to H1 (born after 1977) or H2 (born between 1957 and 1968 when H2 circulated) [17]. We investigated whether first exposure to an HA subtype with an Ile54HA2 vs. a Phe54HA2 changed the memory B cell repertoire responding to H5 vaccination decades later. The magnitude of the VH1‐69 class response did not differ between the two cohorts. However, VH1‐69 class antibodies responding to H5 vaccination in individuals first exposed to H2 were more cross‐reactive with H2 than antibodies from individuals first exposed to H1 influenza strains [17]. The imprinting strain fundamentally altered the stem‐specific repertoire for life.

Strategies to Engage a Specific B Cell Repertoire

It has been estimated over 1500 antibodies with distinct paratopes bind HA in mice as a primary response [88]. Progressively fewer of those will survive and be maintained in the memory repertoire after recurring exposures of antigenic variants over time. Targeting certain antibody lineages or multi‐donor classes is an attractive strategy to enrich for highly protective responses while minimizing unproductive responses (Figure 3). There are multiple factors that influence elicitation, maintenance, specificity, and evolution of antibodies. As elaborated above, subtle differences like Phe45HA2 instead of Ile at the central stem of H2 subtype make a sizable impact on the specificity of VH1‐69 lineage antibodies [17]. Altering glycan positioning on the stem has been shown to switch the antibody specificities from group 1 to group 2 in mice, [89] although this does not result in cross‐group reactivity. Immunizing with an antigen that is incompatible with the predominant repertoire may shift the repertoire, even temporarily, to a subdominant repertoire that can tolerate the change [26]. At a more global scale, conformational states and/or dynamics of viral proteins can have a huge influence on the antibody repertoires they engage. Chimeric HA immunogens consist of heterologous head and stem domains that were cleverly designed to preferentially elicit antibodies targeting the conserved stem domain [90]. However, this design introduces instability in the HA trimer, resulting in partial unraveling of the trimers, exposing cryptic epitopes occluded under a physiological state [91]. Consequently, when chimeric HAs were tested in humans, they inadvertently elicited antibodies targeting trimer interface epitopes [92]. While trimer interface and other cryptic site‐specific B cells do naturally exist in humans, [93] their role in conferring protection against influenza infection outside mouse models remains unclear.

Changing the pre‐existing repertoire through vaccine design strategies. To design vaccines that improve immunity to influenza, it is important to understand the pre‐existing repertoire and leverage this knowledge to selectively enrich and expand protective components of the repertoire across the human population.

Modifications that change the chemical properties of substrate binding may also impact antibody repertoire engagement. Removing a hydroxyl group on Tyr98HA1 (also known as Y98F) is a commonly used method to diminish the sialic acid‐binding property of HA [94]. This substitution has been shown to alter the reactivity of certain HA RBS antibodies as well as polyclonal sera, suggesting that it may not engage the repertoire specific to this site when used in immunogens [95]. However, in humans immunized with H2 HA nanoparticle vaccine containing the Y98F substitution, HA RBS antibodies, including cross‐reactive heterosubtypic antibodies, were elicited [96]. Thus, receptor‐binding‐site antibodies can be elicited with an immunogen containing this substitution. There are also a few naturally occurring NA substitutions that change the sialic acid‐binding affinity and/or the catalytic activity of NA. D151G occurs naturally in H3N2 viruses and has been shown to increase sialic acid‐binding while diminishing the sialidase activity [97]. A similar substitution was also found in H1N1 (G147R) which increases sialic acid‐binding but reduces sialidase activity [98]. Sialidase activity was reported to associate with local reactogenicity in mice when NA was intradermally delivered as an mRNA [99]. This local reactogenicity was reduced by the introduction of the D151G substitution, [99] suggesting the potential utility of this and similar substitutions in vaccine design.

To engage specific B cells without distraction or competition, more reductionist approaches have also been explored. One of the more interesting ideas empirically tested is based on the “inner image” of anti‐idiotype antibodies [100]. Anti‐idiotype antibodies recognize specific paratopes on the target antibody, creating an inner image of structural and chemical features. Hypothetically, this inner image recapitulates the epitope which the target antibody recognizes; therefore, engaging B cells that recognize the same epitope. This hypothesis has been tested in the context of influenza as well as many other viruses. In a study in 1989, at least two such anti‐idiotype antibodies were shown to elicit antibodies similar to the template antibodies [101]. More recently, anti‐idiotype antibodies have also been used as a probe to isolate B cells expressing VH6‐1 antibodies ex vivo, [102] suggesting the potential to specifically engage multi‐donor class B cells in this way. Computational approaches have also been employeed to engraft minimal structural features of an epitope onto an irrelevant protein scaffold, a technique pioneered in HIV‐1 research [103]. The same principle was used to design immunogens recapitulating features of the stem epitope, though the resultant immunogen had limited success [104].

In addition, recent advances in HIV‐1 vaccine research in macaques highlight the use of the nonhuman primate model to study human‐like broadly neutralizing antibodies and B cell development. In particular, two groups showed successful elicitation of specific broadly neutralizing B cell lineages targeting two separate epitopes on HIV‐1 Env with distinct DH‐encoded structural features in macaques [105, 106]. In one case, human DH3‐15*01‐encoded YYEDDY*G (in which Y* is sulfated) motif was faithfully reproduced in macaques immunized with HIV‐1 Env specifically designed to engage human B cell precursors recognizing the V2 apex epitope [105]. The other study designed Env to specifically target human B cell precursors recognizing the V3‐glycan supersite and used it to immunize macaques [106]. This class of human antibodies uses the DH3‐3‐encoded motif—the same motif used by the HA stem‐directed VH6‐1 + DH3‐3 multi‐donor class—reproduced by the macaque ortholog DH3‐41‐encoded motif as seen in the stem‐directed macaque multi‐donor class. These independent demonstrations of human antibody class mimicry by macaques through germline‐targeted immunizations represent a remarkable milestone and show promise for using such approaches to study other targets such as influenza.

As the single‐cell technologies and analytical tools mature, ex vivo experimental approaches such as organoids may play important roles to filling gaps in our knowledge and capabilities. Human tonsil organoids has been developed as a powerful tool to study human adaptive immune responses in a dish [107]. It has been used to study B cell response against different influenza vaccine modalities [108] as well as systems immunology dissecting correlates of protection by different influenza vaccines [109]. This technology creates opportunities to test multiple vaccines in parallel using organoids derived from a single individual. While immune repertoires represented by these organoids are inherently limited, repeatability and translational potential would be attractive for evaluating candidate vaccines as well as testing novel vaccine concepts.

Concluding Remarks

There are many questions yet to be answered about the B cell repertoire to influenza. The majority of studies have analyzed the repertoire present in peripheral blood yet natural infection occurs through the upper respiratory tract. Whether the repertoire of influenza‐specific B cells present in the mucosal tissues is comparable to what has been described in peripheral blood has not been fully explored. As antibodies and tissue‐resident B cells in the upper respiratory mucosa represent the first line of defense to influenza, this is an important question. Likewise, most studies focus on the B cell repertoire after vaccination, not infection. While many of the B cells responding to vaccination represent pre‐existing memory B cells likely first established through infection, whether this is representative of the entire repertoire is unknown. As discussed above, several influenza proteins are targeted upon infection, [70] while current vaccines focus on HA responses, but within the HA‐specific repertoire there could also be differences between vaccination and infection. A recent study investigating the B cell response to SARS‐CoV‐2 in pharyngeal lymphoid tissue and peripheral blood after either infection or intramuscular vaccination found differences in the phenotype of the B cells but did not investigate fine antigen specificity or functional characteristics of the antibodies expressed by those B cells [110]. A large percentage of the population has been exposed to influenza by both infection and vaccination and the impact of vaccination on the pre‐existing repertoire established by infection or vice versa is also unknown. As discussed above, once established, the repertoire in circulation appears to be quite stable. Subsequent infections and vaccinations with influenza variants can add to the repertoire, but there is little evidence they can lead to a substantial shift in the repertoire [13, 17, 85, 111]. This means that once an individual is exposed to a particular viral strain or vaccine, protective immune cells are maintained for life. However, this long‐term immunity could also lead to reduced protection if it distorts the response toward non‐protective epitopes. Understanding the adaptability and malleability of the B cell repertoire is critical as we seek to develop immunogens capable of durably reshaping the influenza‐specific repertoire toward broader and more potent protection.
