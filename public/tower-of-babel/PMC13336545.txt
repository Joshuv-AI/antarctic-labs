Case History

A 1-year-old male child, born at full term by vaginal delivery to non-consanguineous parents, presented to the outpatient department with multiple constriction bands on fingers, amputated fingers, and toes, and congenital talipes equinovarus (CTEV) of the right foot. The child presented with upper limb deformities classified as Category 1 and Category 4 under Patterson’s classification, featuring constriction bands and amputated fingers [Figures 1 and 2 respectively]. The lower limbs exhibited Category 2 deformities, including absent toes and CTEV [Figure 3], as well as Category 3 changes characterized by acro syndactyly [Figure 4]. No craniofacial anomalies, lymphedema, clefting of face, or neurological deficits were observed.

Introduction

Amniotic Deformity, Adhesions, and Mutilations Complex (ADAM Complex), also known as Amniotic Band Syndrome (ABS) or Streeter’s Syndrome, is a non-genetic congenital anomaly. Its severity ranges from superficial constriction rings on limbs to severe deformities like amputations, growth retardation, craniofacial anomalies, and neurological deficits.[1] The incidence varies from 1:1200 to 1:15,000 live births and 178:10,000 spontaneous abortions, without any gender predilection or genetic recurrence.

Pathogenesis

The most widely accepted theory is Torpin’s amniotic disruption hypothesis,[2] which posits that an in-utero rupture of the amniotic sac leads to exposure of foetal parts to the chorionic cavity. The foetal parts are subsequently compressed by remnants of the amniotic membrane, causing constriction and deformities. The timing of the rupture is critical[3]:

Neurological Sequelae of Amniotic Band Syndrome[1]

Central nervous system (CNS) anomalies, including cleft lip and palate, anencephaly, acrania, and hydrocephalus, may develop if the amniotic bands affect the head or brain during early gestation (before 45 days). These can result in speech and language delays and cognitive impairment.Peripheral neurological deficits are more common and can result in motor or sensory impairments due to nerve compression, reduced blood supply, or ischemic injury. These deficits may lead to chronic pain, muscle weakness, or paralysis in affected areas.Functional impairments such as delayed motor development and fine motor skill deficits often stem from physical deformities, including amputations or joint abnormalities.

Diagnosis

Prenatal ultrasonography plays a critical role in identifying ABS.[5] Key ultrasound findings include constriction bands, limb deformities, and amputations. Postnatal clinical examination confirms the diagnosis, aided by imaging for anatomical details.

Treatment

Management is tailored to the severity of the condition:

Surgical Intervention: Reconstructive surgery for releasing constriction bands and correcting deformities.Rehabilitation: Physical and occupational therapy to maximize functional outcomes.Prosthetics: Used for severe limb amputations to aid mobility and functionality.
