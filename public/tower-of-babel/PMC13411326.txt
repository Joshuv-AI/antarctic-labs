Rethinking cancer control in low- and middle-income countries: from workforce expansion to systems transformation
Authors: D Cristina Stefan, Edward Christopher Dee, Frederic Ivan Leong Ting, Geoffrey C. Buckle
Published: 2026

Cancer is now a defining health challenge for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Population growth, ageing, changes in lifestyle and environmental exposures, persistent infection-related cancers, and unequal access to proven tools for prevention and treatment are all shaping a growing burden of disease [1, 2]. For many countries, cancer is no longer a future epidemic. It has become a constant presence throughout society—from rural households to community clinics, and national hospitals, while placing increasing pressure on health budgets that are ill-equipped to manage chronic, complex and resource-intensive care.

At the same time, the global cancer community has never had more knowledge about ways to reduce cancer morbidity and mortality. Vaccination against oncogenic infections, tobacco control, screening for selected cancers, early diagnosis, pathology, imaging, surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, palliative care and survivorship support are all essential components of the cancer-control continuum. Some LMICs have shown that progress is possible. High coverage of HPV vaccination in Rwanda, expanded screening and decentralization efforts in India, and referral innovations such as Mexico’s Código Cancer illustrate that cancer control can move forward when evidence is linked to delivery [3–5].

Yet this progress remains uneven. In many settings, the problem goes beyond simple resource constraints. Cancer services are all too often delivered through health systems that are fragmented, underfinanced, weakly coordinated and often unable to sustain continuity of care. National cancer control plans are essential, but they are not sufficient. Recent analyses show that many countries have developed cancer policies and plans; yet, many lack realistic budgets and many omit important details on implementation, equity, financing, workforce planning, research capacity and accountability [6]. The central question is therefore not only what interventions are known to work, but how they can be delivered reliably and fairly in real health systems.

This is where further research is urgently needed. Too much of the cancer-control literature still documents burden, describes gaps, or reports isolated interventions. These contributions are important, but they do not address the harder implementation questions. Which models of early diagnosis are feasible in rural or under-resourced settings? How can referral pathways be made faster and more accountable? What workforce configurations are safe, affordable and sustainable? How can oncology nurses, pharmacists, pathologists, community health workers and patient navigators be better integrated into cancer care? How can cancer registries and outcome data become part of routine care rather than parallel projects?

Implementation science offers an important lens because it asks not only whether an intervention works, but whether it can be delivered, adapted, scaled and sustained under real-world conditions [7]. In LMIC cancer control, this means studying the systems around the intervention: referral mechanisms, diagnostic turnaround times, supply chains, treatment completion, multidisciplinary decision-making, patient navigation, palliative care access, data flows and financial protection. Without these elements, even effective interventions may fail to reach the patients who need them most.

Workforce is another critical area where more evidence is needed. The global shortage of oncology professionals is well recognized, but simply calling for more specialists is not enough. The WHO Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health emphasizes the need for health workforces that are fit for purpose, equitably distributed and aligned with universal health coverage [8]. For cancer care, this requires research on team-based models, task sharing, regional training hubs, digital mentorship, retention, career pathways and leadership development. It also requires greater attention to the often-invisible workforce that makes cancer care possible: nurses, pharmacists, laboratory scientists, social workers, psychologists, data managers and palliative care teams.

Early detection is equally complex. Raising awareness or introducing screening without the capacity to confirm diagnosis and provide treatment may increase anxiety and inequity rather than improve outcomes. A phased approach to breast cancer early detection has shown the importance of aligning early detection with diagnostic and treatment capacity [9]. Similar work is needed across cancer types and health-system contexts. Research should help determine which early detection strategies are effective, context-appropriate, affordable, acceptable and equitable, and how they can be connected to timely care.

Cancer control in LMICs should also be understood as a field of knowledge generation, not only a field of need. Research embedded in care can identify causes of diagnostic delay, evaluate models of decentralization, study financial toxicity, test resource-adapted pathways and generate locally relevant evidence. Education embedded in care can strengthen teams and improve quality. Local registries, clinical audit, outcome monitoring and multidisciplinary review should be seen as core components of cancer systems, not optional additions.

Partnerships remain essential, but they too require scrutiny. Global collaborations have contributed to training, infrastructure, medicines access and research. However, partnerships can also reproduce unequal power if priority-setting, data ownership, authorship and leadership remain externally driven. The critique of the “foreign gaze” in global health remains relevant [10]. Future research should examine how partnerships can move from capacity building to co-creation, with shared governance, mutual accountability, fair authorship, equitable data use and long-term institutional strengthening.

Digital health and artificial intelligence may create new possibilities for risk stratification, telepathology, referral support, decision support, patient navigation and outcome tracking. However, technology is not a substitute for health-system readiness. Research is needed on digital equity, data governance, interoperability, workforce capacity, ethics, patient trust and implementation in settings where infrastructure may be fragile.

This Collection invites contributions that move beyond describing the cancer burden in LMICs toward understanding how cancer control can be implemented, sustained and improved. We welcome studies on health-system strengthening, workforce models, early diagnosis, screening implementation, access to treatment, palliative care, survivorship, cancer registries,, digital health, patient navigation, implementation science, education, policy translation and equitable partnerships.

The next era of cancer control in LMICs should be judged less by the number of projects launched and more by the systems changed. The challenge is no longer only to know what should be done. It is to understand how governments, institutions, funders, communities and partners can build delivery systems that allow what is already known to reach every patient who needs it everywhere.
