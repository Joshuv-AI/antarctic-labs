Optimal Online Equitable Allocation with Indivisible Resources
Ramiro N. Deo-Campo Vuong

Abstract
Equitable allocation of indivisible goods to agents in online settings is an algorithmic primitive with applications for load balancing, network routing, online marketplaces, and multi-agent systems. We consider a general setting in which allocations are constrained to be bases of discrete polymatroids that arrive online. Our work demonstrates that a simple, myopic algorithm called Brick-Laying , which greedily minimizes the sum of squared loads on agents, achieves a universal and objective-free notion of optimality called majorization minimax-optimality 2 for this setting. As a consequence, Brick-Laying simultaneously guarantees minimax optimal competitive ratios and regret for all Schur-concave and Schur-convex objectives, and for any number of agents and resources (despite being agnostic to problem scale). Departing from popular primal-dual analysis, we employ majorization to compare allocations. We leverage the conjugates of integer partitions – which act as a discrete dual to majorization – to characterize worst-case instances for the Brick-Laying algorithm. Our approach reveals a novel structural connection between the geometry of partitions and online equitable allocation.

Introduction

Equitable allocation of indivisible resources (goods/chores) to agents in online settings is a fundamental algorithmic primitive.
In such problems, resources arrive over time, and must be irrevocably assigned according to some specified combinatorial constraints.
Equity is measured in terms of some function of the allocations, and optimizing it is challenging because assignments are made without knowledge of future constraints.

In this work, we consider the following general variant of this problem: feasible allocations are described by a sequence of discrete polymatroid bases that arrive online.
We model worst-case settings by assuming these allocation constraints are designed by an adaptive adversary who has access to past resource assignments.

1.2
Majorization Minimax-Optimality

In this section, we introduce majorization minimax-optimality (
MMO
) as a natural objective-free notion of optimality and illuminate how it implies optimal
\alpha
-regret for all comparison factors
\alpha
.
To show that deterministic allocation strategy
\mathcal{A}
minimizes regret under equity measure
f
and comparison factor
\alpha
, it suffices to show
\textsc{Reg}_{f,\alpha}^{n,m}(\mathcal{A})\leq\textsc{Reg}_{f,\alpha}^{n,m}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
for all alternate policies
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. Since regret considers worst-case adversarial play, this is equivalent to the following: for all deviation policies
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and adversarial strategies
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
, there exists an adversarial strategy
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
such that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is no better against
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
than
\mathcal{A}
against
\mathcal{P}
:

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\,\,\forall\mathcal{P}\,\,\exists\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\quad c_{f,\alpha}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\leq c_{f,\alpha}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}).

This condition still depends on an objective
f
.
To obtain an objective-free criterion, we shift to pairwise comparison of load vectors.
Let
\textsc{OPT}_{f}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\in\arg\max_{\bm{\ell}^{*}\in H(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})}f(\bm{\ell}^{*})
denote an arbitrary vector that is most equitable in hindsight.
A sufficient condition for the inequality above is that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
simultaneously achieves weakly higher allocation equity and weakly lower hindsight equity than
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, i.e.,
f(\bm{\ell}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}))\geq\mathbb{E}f(\bm{\ell}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}))
and
f(\textsc{OPT}_{f}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}))\leq\mathbb{E}f(\textsc{OPT}_{f}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}))
.
This reduces optimality to pairwise comparisons of load vector equity, as measured by
f
.
To remove dependence on
f
, we can ask: is there an objective-free notion of vector equity that recovers the above as a consequence for all reasonable equity-promoting objectives?

Majorization theory answers this question in the positive.
Majorization, a preorder relation on vectors, is founded on the Dalton-Pigou transfer or Robin principle that specifies that transferring resources from the wealthy to the poor yields more equitable outcomes
1
;
30
.

Definition 1

(Majorization)
.

For
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with
\mathbf{x}([n])=\mathbf{y}([n])
, vector
\mathbf{x}
majorizes
\mathbf{y}
(written as
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
) when
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}([k])\leq\mathbf{y}^{\uparrow}([k]),\forall k\in[n]
, where the
i^{\text{th}}
element of
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
smallest element of
\mathbf{x}
.

We equivalently write
\mathbf{y}\preceq\mathbf{x}
when
\mathbf{x}
majorizes
\mathbf{y}
.
If vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are equivalent up to permutation (i.e.,
\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
), we write
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{y}
.
Notably, vectors lower in the majorization order are more equitable.
The majorization order is aligned with Schur-monotone functions, which include most equity-promoting objectives.

Definition 2

(Schur Monotone Functions)
.

Function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is Schur-concave when
\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{y}
implies
f(\mathbf{x})\geq f(\mathbf{y})
.
In contrast,
g:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is Schur-convex when
\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{y}
implies
g(\mathbf{x})\leq g(\mathbf{y})
.

A vector
\mathbf{x}
deemed to be most equitable under majorization maximizes Schur-concave functions, such as the matching objective
\sum_{i\in[n]}\min(b,\mathbf{x}(i))
for any
b>0
, Nash Social Welfare (NSW), and egalitarian welfare.
Such an
\mathbf{x}
simultaneously minimizes Schur-convex objectives, which include popular inequity measures such as
p
-norms and makespan.
See
Appendix

A
for more details.

Equipped with majorization, we now present majorization minimax-optimality.
We alter the previous sufficient condition for optimal regret in two ways.
First, we apply
1
below, which establishes the existence of a majorization-minimal hindsight load vector.
This implies that there is a randomized load vector
\bm{\ell}^{*}
, depending only on the realization of the set
H(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
, such that
\bm{\ell}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\bm{\ell}\in H(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})}f(\bm{\ell})
for all Schur-concave
f
.
We call this vector
\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\coloneqq\bm{\ell}^{*}
.
Second, we use majorization to perform pairwise comparisons:
f(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{y})
is replaced by
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
.
We require majorization to hold with probability
1
to preserve the consequence of optimal
\alpha
-regret.

Definition 3

(Majorization Minimax-Optimality)
.

For
n
agents and
m
resources, a deterministic allocation strategy
\mathcal{A}
is majorization minimax-optimal (
MMO
) if for all alternative strategies
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and adversarial strategies
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
, there exists an adversarial strategy
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
satisfying the following with probability
1
:

\displaystyle\bm{\ell}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\preceq\bm{\ell}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\succeq\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})

Like an equilibrium strategy in the online allocation game, an
MMO
strategy implies that deviation from the strategy leaves the algorithm designer susceptible to worse game outcomes.
The notable difference is that
MMO
uses majorization to compare outcomes, rather than regret (or competitive ratios).
Thus, an
MMO
strategy is more robust than a regular equilibrium strategy because it implies minimax optimal regret under any reasonable equity-promoting objectives.

1.3
Brick-Laying

At this point, it is unclear whether any strategy satisfies the stringent
MMO

A myopic allocation strategy, which we call
brick-laying

Each round, this algorithm selects a majorization-minimal basis (i.e., a basis that minimizes the sum of squared loads on agents).
We call the algorithm

Proof Overview and an Illustrative Example

Since
MMO
resembles an equilibrium condition, our analysis parallels equilibrium analysis in two-player zero-sum games.
In particular, the crux of our proof is showing that the adversary’s best response to the algorithm designer using
brick-laying
is to employ a
nested strategy
.

Definition 5

(Nested Strategy)
.

An adversarial strategy
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
is nested if, for any strategy
\mathcal{A}
, the profile
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
induces a polymatroid sequence
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
whose rank functions
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
take the form
r_{t}(A)=\mathds{1}\left\{A\cap N_{t}\neq\emptyset\right\}
for
N_{1}\supseteq\dots\supseteq N_{m}
with probability
1
.

Nested strategies pose a challenge because the algorithm designer would prefer to assign resources to agents who are absent from future neighborhoods, but she cannot identify such agents from past information.
Notably, nested strategies are semi-matching instances in which online nodes arrive one at a time: online node
t
has neighborhood
N_{t}

In

1

brick-laying
.
We do so by treating the load vector produced by

as an integer partition of

Definition 6

The conjugate load vector of

\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)=|\{i\in[n]\mid\mathbf{x}(i)\geq j\}|

j

2.1
Nestedness is Worst-Case: a Semi-Matching Example

We present an online semi-matching example to give intuition for our most technical contribution:
Lemma

1
.
To show nested strategies are the adversary’s best response to
brick-laying
, we analyze
Algorithm

2
, which takes as input an adversarial sequence
\mathcal{P}
and produces a nested strategy
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
on which
brick-laying
yields weakly worse outcomes.
To simplify our analysis, we assume

tie-breaks in favor of agents with a lower index when presented with a rank
1

This is without loss of generality because different tie-breaking yields the same intermediate load vector up to permutation; the adversary can adjust to these different permutations using their adaptive power.
Again, the

\chi_{i}(j)=\mathds{1}\left\{i=j\right\}
.

Input:

nested under

\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})\succeq\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
.

Define the empty sequence

Compute

We note that each epoch

(Nested is Worst-Case)
.

Given
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,m}
,
Algorithm

2

to get an instance
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
on which the

The most technical hurdle of
Lemma

1
is proving that the hindsight optimal
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})
remains feasible (up to permutation) when the adversary plays strategy
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.
In our analysis, we leverage the brick representation of allocations.

Definition 7

(Brick Representation)
.

The brick representation of
\mathbf{x}\in P
on
\bm{\ell}
is
M_{\mathbf{x}}^{\bm{\ell}}=\{(i,l)\in[n]\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\bm{\ell}(i)+1\leq l\leq\bm{\ell}(i)+\mathbf{x}(i)\}
.
An element
(i,l)\in M_{\mathbf{x}}^{\bm{\ell}}
is called a brick of
\mathbf{x}
.

Since the
brick-laying
allocations on
\mathcal{P}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
are equivalent up to permutation, the union of their brick representations over all rounds is equivalent up to a relabeling of the agents.
This yields a natural bijection between
brick-laying
bricks on strategy
\mathcal{P}
with those bricks on strategy
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.
Our approach is to compose this natural bijection with a bijection from
brick-laying
bricks on
\mathcal{P}
with bricks in the optimal hindsight solution on
\mathcal{P}
to reconstruct the optimal hindsight allocation on
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
up to permutation.
This second bijection, described in
Lemma

2
, is carefully selected so that the reconstructed allocation remains feasible.
At a high level, if we map a hindsight brick
(i^{*},l^{*})
to a later-arriving epoch
j
, we have to ensure it will have sufficiently high
brick-laying
load
\bm{\ell}_{h}(i^{*})
to ensure feasibility; we require
i^{*}
to have load at least
j
so the mapped brick is feasible in
[\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)]
.

Lemma

2
guarantees that each brick
(i^{*},l^{*})
has sufficiently large
brick-laying
load.

Lemma 2

(Majorization Minimality Bijection)
.

Fix load vector
\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, polymatroid
P
, and arbitrary basis
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\textsc{Bases}(P)
.
A basis
\mathbf{x}\in\textsc{Bases}(P)
with
\mathbf{x}+\bm{\ell}\preceq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\bm{\ell}
for all bases
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\textsc{Bases}(P)
admits a bijection
\phi:M_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}^{\bm{\ell}}\to M_{\mathbf{x}}^{\bm{\ell}}
where
(i,l)=\phi(i^{*},l^{*})
implies
\bm{\ell}(i^{*})+\mathbf{x}(i^{*})\geq l-1
for all bricks
(i^{*},l^{*})\in M_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}^{\bm{\ell}}
.

Lemma

2
is easier to argue about in the special case when polymatroid
P
is rank
1
.
In this case, every basis has a single brick in its brick representation.
A majorization minimal basis will allocate a brick
(i,\bm{\ell}(i)+1)
to the agent
i
with the lowest load.
Since
\bm{\ell}(i)
had minimal load, every other
i^{*}
satisfies
\mathbf{x}(i^{*})+\bm{\ell}(i^{*})\geq(\bm{\ell}(i)+1)-1
; any other singleton brick will admit the desired bijection.
The proof of
Lemma

2
in its full generality necessitates polymatroid machinery, so we defer it to
Appendix

D
.
These ideas may be of independent interest.

With the intuition of

1
.
In our proofs, the
i^{\text{th}}
component of the vectors
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}

i^{\text{th}}

Proof.

Define
(\mathbf{x}_{t})_{t\in[h]}
to be the allocations produced by
brick-laying

\mathcal{P}
, which admit load vectors
\bm{\ell}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{s\in[t]}\mathbf{x}_{s}
and
\bm{\ell}_{0}=\vec{0}

We also take
\bm{\ell}\coloneqq\bm{\ell}_{h}=\bm{\ell}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})
for convenience.
The conjugate
\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)
is non-increasing in

, thus the strategy
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
is nested.

We establish some notation.
Let
\widehat{P}_{1},\dots,\widehat{P}_{m}
be the polymatroids that are deterministically played by
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.
Notice the sequence has length
m
, because each polymatroid is associated with one resource.
Define
\tau_{j}=\sum_{k\in[j]}\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(k)
to be the last round in epoch
j
.
Take
(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau})_{\tau\in[m]}
and
(\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{\tau})_{\tau\in[m]\cup\{0\}}
to be the allocations and load vectors generated by
brick-laying
on
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.
Let
(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})_{t\in[h]}
be a feasible allocation for the polymatroids realized by
(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})
with
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})=\sum_{t\in[h]}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}
;
this hindsight solution is deterministic since

and
\mathcal{P}
are deterministic.
Finally,
M_{t}\coloneqq M_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\bm{\ell}_{t-1}}
and
M_{t}^{*}\coloneqq M_{\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}}^{\bm{\ell}_{t-1}}
are the brick representations of
(\mathbf{x}_{t})_{t\in[h]}

We show that
\bm{\ell}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})\sim\bm{\ell}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
.
For induction on epochs
j
, assume that
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{\tau_{j}}(i)=j
for all

.
The claim holds for

since
\tau_{0}=0
and
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{0}=\vec{0}
.
Consider epoch
j+1
.
In this epoch, the adversary presents
\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j+1)
copies of polymatroid
\widehat{P}
with rank function
\widehat{r}(A)=\mathds{1}\left\{A\cap[\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)]\neq\emptyset\right\}

\{\chi_{i}\mid i\in[\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)]\}=\textsc{Bases}(\widehat{P})
.
Since all agents in
[\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)]
have the same level at the beginning of the epoch and

allocates to the lowest load agent, breaking ties in favor of lower indices,

will select bases
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j+1)}
in epoch

.
For

\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{\tau_{j+1}}(i)=\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{\tau_{j}}(i)+1=j+1

The inductive claim implies that
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{m}(i)\geq\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{\tau_{j}}(i)\geq j
where

is the latest epoch with
i\in[\bm{\ell}^{\circ}(j)]
.
This inequality, in addition to conjugation giving
j=\bm{\ell}^{\downarrow}(i)
and yields
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{m}(i)\geq\bm{\ell}^{\downarrow}(i)
.
By definition
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{m}([n])=\bm{\ell}([n])=m

\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{m}(i)=\bm{\ell}^{\downarrow}(i)

.

We now show
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})\succeq\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})

to a new allocation
(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}^{*})_{\tau\in[m]}

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
and yields a load vector

sharing an equivalence class with that of

.
This implies the claim, as
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\preceq\hat{\bm{\ell}}^{*}\sim\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\mathcal{P})
by the majorization-minimality of optimal hindsight solutions.
Each hindsight allocation brick
(i^{*},l^{*})
has an associated agent
i^{*}
and level
l^{*}
, which we map independently.
First, we map agents according to the bijection
\kappa:[n]\to[n]
for which
\bm{\ell}(i)<\bm{\ell}(i^{\prime})
implies
\kappa(i)>\kappa(i^{\prime})
for all
i,i^{\prime}
.
To map levels to a round, we compose several functions.
First, for each
t\in[h]

\phi_{t}:M_{t}^{*}\to M_{t}

\bm{\ell}_{t-1}(i^{*})+\mathbf{x}_{t}(i^{*})\geq l-1

.
The last function is a bijection

.

We now present the second half of our supporting claims to prove
Theorem

1
.
The goal is to show that
brick-laying
performs weakly better than any alternative allocation strategy against nested strategies.
This involves relabeling of agents so that alternative strategies that do not maintain relatively equal loads on agents cannot allocate to low load agents in the future.
We call the adversarial strategy that performs this careful relabeling a nested response strategy.

Definition 8

(Nested Response Strategy)
.

Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be a non-increasing sequence of integers.
Given a history of play prior to round
t
, the nested response strategy
\mathcal{P}
for allocation strategy
\mathcal{A}
seeded by
(z_{t})_{t\in[m]}
presents the polymatroid
P_{t}
on round
t
with rank
r_{t}(A)=\mathds{1}\left\{A\cap N_{t}\neq\emptyset\right\}
, where
N_{t}=\arg\max\{\bm{\ell}_{t-1}(A)\mid|A|=z_{t}\}
for intermediate loads
\bm{\ell}_{t-1}
produced by
\mathcal{A}
.

Observation 1

(Nested Response Strategy)
.

The nested response strategy
\mathcal{P}
for any allocation strategy
\mathcal{A}
seeded by non-increasing
(z_{t})_{t}
is a nested strategy.

Proof.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
be the polymatroids realized by
\mathcal{P}
against any allocation strategy.
Loads produced by the allocation strategy are non-decreasing each round and
(z_{t})_{t}
is non-increasing, so by construction
i\in N_{t}
implies
i\in N_{t-1}
.
The strategy
\mathcal{P}
is nested.
∎

We now show that the
brick-laying
algorithm is the most resistant algorithm to nested response strategies for any seed sequence.

Lemma 3

(
brick-laying
vs. Alternatives)
.

Fix a non-increasing integer sequence
(z_{t})_{t}
and strategy
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be the nested response strategies seeded with
(z_{t})_{t}
on
BL
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, respectively.
These satisfy
\bm{\ell}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\preceq\bm{\ell}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
and
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\sim\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
with probability
1
.

Proof.

We first focus on the property
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\sim\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.
Let
P_{1}^{\prime},\dots,P_{m}^{\prime}
be the polymatroids realized by
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.
All realizations of the sequence
(P_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in[m]}

In addition, these realizations further share allocations, up to permutation, with the sequence
(\widehat{P}_{t})_{t}
.
This implies that the majorization minimal element of every realization
(\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\prime})_{t}
is equivalent up to permutation to the majorization minimal element of
(\widehat{P}_{t})_{t}
.
This immediate yields
\textsc{OPT}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\sim\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
with probability
1
.

We now show
\bm{\ell}(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})\preceq\bm{\ell}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
with probability
1

(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t})_{t}

(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{t})_{t}
, and loads
(\widehat{\bm{\ell}}_{t})_{t}
produced by profile strategy
(\textsc{BL},\widehat{\mathcal{P}})
.
Likewise, let
(\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\prime})_{t}
,
(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\prime})_{t}
, and
(\bm{\ell}_{t}^{\prime})_{t}
be an arbitrary realization of the same on profile
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.
Without loss of generality, we relabel agents so that

; this also implies that
\widehat{N}_{t}=N_{t}^{\prime}
for each

.
For induction on
k

The claim holds for

Consider the claim for

Let
\tau=\max\{t\mid k-1\in N_{t}\}
be the latest round on which there is a feasible allocation to

The total number of allocated resources up to including this round is
\tau

Further, the total load on agents in

Fact 3

]

A discrete polymatroid

•

Zero-vector membership:

•

Downward closure: if

B.2
Convex Games

A convex game is a special case of a cooperative game among
n
agents.
These games admit a non-empty core – outcomes that cannot be improved upon by any coalition of players – that can be characterized by a simple greedy algorithm

]
.
Discrete convex games are characterized by a characteristic function

The function must be normalized

The core of the game is the set of payoff vectors
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
that are efficient

and incentivize every coalition to participate in the grand coalition
\mathbf{x}(A)\geq v(A)
for all coalitions

.

Our results extend to the problem of computing equitable cores in convex games, as they are intimately related to bases of polymatroids.
Specifically, a vector is a basis of the polymatroid with rank function

if and only if the same vector is a core of the convex game with characteristic function
