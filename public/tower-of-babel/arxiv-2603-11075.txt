Unifying Logical and Physical Layout Representations via Heterogeneous Graphs for Circuit Congestion Prediction ISBN: 978-1-4503-XXXX-X/2018/06
Runbang Hu, Bo Fang, Bingzhe Li, Yuede Ji

Abstract
. As Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) designs continue to scale in size and complexity, layout verification has become a central challenge in modern Electronic Design Automation (EDA) workflows. In practice, congestion can only be accurately identified after detailed routing, making traditional verification both time-consuming and costly. Learning-based approaches have therefore been explored to enable early-stage congestion prediction and reduce routing iterations. However, although prior methods incorporate both netlist connectivity and layout features, they often model the two in a loosely coupled manner and primarily produce numerical congestion estimates. We propose VeriHGN, a verification framework built on an enhanced heterogeneous graph that unifies circuit components and spatial grids into a single relational representation, enabling more faithful modeling of the interaction between logical intent and physical realization. Experiments on industrial benchmarks, including ISPD2015, CircuitNet-N14, and CircuitNet-N28, demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art methods in prediction accuracy and correlation metrics.

Challenges.

Despite substantial progress, existing methods face two fundamental challenges.
(i) First,
the integration of netlist connectivity and physical layout geometry remains
incomplete or indirect
. Geometry-centric models struggle to reason about long-range routing demand induced by netlist structure, while netlist-centric models often fail to incorporate fine-grained spatial constraints essential for accurate congestion estimation in placed designs.
Even multi-view approaches typically rely on loosely coupled or view-biased fusion
strategies, making it difficult to fully synchronize logical intent with physical realization in dense and irregular layouts.

(ii) Second, most learning-based models rely on loosely coupled or view-biased fusion strategies that process netlist and layout information through separate encoders with late-stage aggregation. This indirect integration constrains cross-modal interaction and may fail to capture subtle correlations between netlist structure and physical layout, especially in locally dense and irregular regions. From a modeling perspective, routing congestion prediction is not merely a regression task over spatial features or netlist statistics. Instead, congestion emerges from the complex interaction between logical connectivity, physical placement, and multi-scale routing resource competition. Local geometric density alone is insufficient to explain congestion hotspots induced by long-span or high-fanout nets, while purely topological representations fail to capture spatial constraints imposed by the routing grid.

Such observations suggest that effective congestion prediction requires a unified representation that can jointly reason over circuit structure and spatial layout, while supporting information exchange across multiple granularities. However, existing approaches typically decompose this problem into loosely coupled views, limiting their ability to capture fine-grained cross-domain interactions.

To address these challenges, we propose VeriHGN, a unified heterogeneous graph–based framework for early-stage routing congestion prediction. VeriHGN represents cells, nets, and spatial regions as distinct node types and integrates them into a single relational graph, enabling direct interaction between netlist connectivity and physical layout geometry. Spatial regions are further organized into a hierarchical grid, allowing congestion information to propagate across both neighboring regions and multiple spatial resolutions.

This unified formulation allows the model to jointly capture local congestion caused by dense placement and regional congestion induced by long-span or high-fanout nets that are difficult to model using single-view or single-resolution approaches. By explicitly coupling netlist topology with multi-scale spatial context, VeriHGN overcomes the indirect fusion and limited cross-domain interaction of prior methods, leading to more faithful congestion prediction.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Learning-Based Congestion Prediction

Recent years have witnessed a growing body of work on applying machine learning techniques to predict routing congestion from pre-routing design representations. These methods can be broadly categorized along two axes:

Geometry-based methods.

Geometry-based approaches rasterize the chip layout into grid-based feature maps and employ convolutional or vision transformer architectures for congestion prediction. Early work RouteNet
(
25
)
proposed encoding cell density, pin density, and RUDY (Rectangular Uniform wire DensitY)
(
21
)
features as multi-channel images and training a U-Net
(
16
)
to predict congestion maps in a pixel-wise fashion. Subsequent methods have introduced more sophisticated architectures: ST-FPN
(
2
)
combines Swin Transformer blocks with a feature pyramid network to capture multi-scale spatial patterns, while Lay-Net
(
33
)
employs a pure vision transformer backbone with layout-aware positional encodings. These methods benefit from the mature ecosystem of image-based deep learning, but inherently lose the topological structure of the circuit netlist during rasterization. As a result, they struggle to capture long-range routing demand induced by nets spanning distant regions of the chip, and their predictions tend to be biased toward local density patterns rather than true routing congestion.

Topology-based methods.

Topology-based methods model circuit netlists as graphs or hypergraphs and exploit graph learning techniques to capture signal connectivity and information flow. Early representative work, such as CongestionNet
(
11
)
, formulates a cell-level graph where edges encode shared nets and employs message-passing neural networks to estimate each cell’s congestion. Subsequent approaches further refine this representation by explicitly modeling the heterogeneous nature of netlists. For example, CircuitGNN
(
28
)
introduces a bipartite graph with distinct cell and net nodes, enabling a more faithful representation of connectivity and improved reasoning over multi-fanout structures. Recent studies have explored multi-view formulations that jointly consider topological and geometric cues. MIHC
(
32
)
, for instance, constructs parallel hypergraph views to model netlist topology, physical layout geometry, and spatial proximity. While such designs move toward more comprehensive congestion modeling, their reliance on separate encoders with late-stage fusion constrains cross-modal interaction. As a result, subtle correlations between netlist structure and physical layout—especially in locally dense and irregular regions may not be fully captured.

Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks

Graph neural networks have become a dominant paradigm for learning on structured data
(
10
;
22
;
26
)
. Standard homogeneous GNNs assume a single node type and edge type, applying uniform transformation and aggregation across the entire graph. While effective for many tasks, this formulation is insufficient for domains where relational heterogeneity carries important semantic meaning. In EDA, circuit netlists naturally give rise to heterogeneous graphs with multiple node types (e.g., cells, nets, pins, I/O ports) and edge types (e.g., cell-to-net connectivity, spatial adjacency, timing arcs)
(
17
)
. Heterogeneous GNNs address this by maintaining type-specific transformation and aggregation functions, enabling the model to learn distinct relational semantics for different edge types.
Several architectures have been proposed for such graphs. R-GCN
(
19
)
introduces relation-specific weight matrices and aggregates messages from each edge type independently before combining them, effectively decomposing the heterogeneous neighborhood into relation-specific subspaces. HGAN
(
24
)
adopts a hierarchical attention mechanism that first computes node-level attention within each meta-path-based subgraph and then applies semantic-level attention to fuse information across different meta-paths. HGT
(
8
)
further advances this line by parameterizing attention heads with node-type and edge-type dependent projections, learning fine-grained type-aware interactions without manually defined meta-paths. More recently, Simple-HGN
(
29
)
demonstrates that incorporating edge-type information into standard GAT-style attention with residual connections can achieve competitive performance at significantly reduced complexity.

Despite their expressiveness, applying heterogeneous GNNs to EDA problems introduces unique challenges.
Particularly, circuit graphs are typically much larger than those in common benchmarks (often containing millions of nodes), exhibit extreme degree heterogeneity (e.g., power nets connecting thousands of cells versus local signal nets with only a few pins), and involve edge types with fundamentally different physical semantics. These characteristics demand careful architectural choices in message function design, aggregation strategy, and scalability optimization considerations.

2.3.
Multi-Scale Spatial Modeling for Routing Congestion

Routing congestion arises from interactions across multiple spatial scales. Local congestion hotspots are often driven by dense placement and pin access contention, while broader regional congestion patterns emerge from long-span nets and routing paths that traverse multiple neighboring areas. Capturing both effects requires models that can reason over fine-grained spatial detail and larger regional context.

Most geometry-based congestion prediction methods
(
6
;
23
)
discretize the layout into a fixed-resolution grid and process it using convolutional or transformer-based architectures
(
28
;
2
)
. While effective for modeling local density patterns, single-resolution grids struggle to represent long-range routing interactions and region-level congestion accumulation. Increasing grid resolution improves localization but significantly increases computational cost, whereas coarser grids sacrifice fine-grained detail.

Several works incorporate multi-scale spatial context through hierarchical feature aggregation or multi-resolution encoder
(
32
)
. However, these approaches typically operate on rasterized representations and treat spatial hierarchy as an implicit architectural property, without explicitly modeling interactions between spatial regions and netlist connectivity.

In contrast, explicitly representing spatial regions at multiple resolutions as nodes enables direct information exchange across scales and between layout and netlist structures. Motivated by this, our approach models hierarchical grids as first-class entities within a unified heterogeneous graph, enabling explicit multi-scale message passing for congestion prediction.

3.
Methodology

3.1.
Congestion Prediction Graph Construction

We construct a heterogeneous congestion prediction graph that explicitly encodes both circuit connectivity and spatial routing demand. Figure
2
illustrates the overall graph construction process.
The key design principle is to expose the primary sources of routing congestion—logical fanout, geometric proximity, and regional resource contention—as first-class entities in the graph structure. Rather than treating netlist and layout information as separate modalities, our formulation integrates them into a single relational graph, allowing congestion to be modeled as an emergent property of their interaction.

Node types.

The graph contains three types of nodes:
(1)
Cell nodes
, corresponding to standard cells and macros in the netlist. Cell nodes represent instance-level routing demand and serve as prediction targets for cell congestion. (2)
Net nodes
, corresponding to electrical nets connecting multiple cells. Net nodes aggregate routing demand induced by their incident cells and propagate it through the graph. (3)
Grid nodes
, corresponding to spatial regions obtained by discretizing the placement area. Grid nodes represent regional layout and are organized in a hierarchical structure. This tripartite node design reflects the multi-origin nature of routing congestion. Cell nodes represent localized routing demand induced by instance geometry and pin access. Net nodes capture global routing pressure arising from multi-terminal connectivity and long-span nets. Grid nodes abstract the spatial routing resources available in different regions of the layout. Modeling these components explicitly enables the learning process to disentangle whether congestion is driven primarily by local density, net-induced routing demand, or regional resource imbalance.

Hierarchical grid construction.

To capture spatial routing patterns at different scales, we construct grid nodes at multiple resolutions. At the finest level (L0), the placement area is divided into an
M\times N

fine grid
. Each fine grid cell corresponds to one grid node and captures local routing density and resource contention.
Coarser grid levels are constructed by spatial aggregation. Specifically, each level-
\ell{+}1
grid node represents a non-overlapping
2\times 2
block of level-
\ell
grid nodes. Higher levels summarize routing demand over progressively larger regions. Grid nodes from all levels are included in the congestion prediction graph. The hierarchical grid construction is motivated by the observation that routing congestion exhibits strong multi-scale characteristics. While fine-grained grids are necessary to localize congestion hotspots, regional congestion often arises from routing paths that traverse multiple neighboring tiles or span large portions of the layout. A single-resolution grid is therefore insufficient to capture such patterns.

Edge types.

Edges in the graph encode logical and spatial relationships: (1)
Pin edges
connect cell nodes and net nodes, representing cell–net incidences derived from the netlist. (2)
Geometric cell edges
connect spatially nearby cell nodes. These edges model local competition for routing resources among neighboring instances. (3)
Grid adjacency edges
connect grid nodes within the same resolution level using 4-connected spatial neighborhoods. (4)
Hierarchical grid edges
connect each coarse grid node to its corresponding fine grid nodes, explicitly encoding parent–child relationships between grid levels. This construction results in a single congestion prediction graph that jointly represents instance-level connectivity, local spatial interactions, and multi-scale regional routing demand.

3.2.
Enriched Feature Engineering

The heterogeneous graph is parameterized by node and edge level attributes that encode geometric, topological, and semantic information available from the placed design. Rather than relying on deep propagation to implicitly recover routing demand signals, we explicitly construct informative features for different graph components. These features provide strong inductive biases and improve sample efficiency on limited benchmark designs.

Cell node feature.
Each cell
v
is represented by a 13-dim vector:

(1)

{x}_{v}^{\text{cell}}=\big[\underbrace{x,y,w,h,a}_{\text{geometry}},\;\underbrace{d_{\text{net}},p,\bar{l},\hat{l},\bar{d}}_{\text{topology}},\;\underbrace{m,r_{\text{in}},f_{t}}_{\text{type}}\big],

where
(x,y)
are placement coordinates,
(w,h)
are cell dimensions, and
a=w\cdot h
is area.
Topological statistics include the incident-net count
d_{\text{net}}
, pin count
p
, the mean and max half-perimeter wire length (HPWL) of incident nets
(\bar{l},\hat{l})
, and the mean incident-net degree
\bar{d}
.
Type-related attributes include a macro indicator
m
, input-pin ratio
r_{\text{in}}
, and normalized type frequency
f_{t}
(fraction of cells sharing the same master). Continuous features are standardized.

Net node features.
Each net
e
is initialized by a 6-dim vector:

(2)

{x}_{e}^{\text{net}}=\big[\text{HPWL}(e),\;\Delta x(e),\;\Delta y(e),\;|e|,\;\log|e|,\;A_{\text{bbox}}(e)\big],

where
\Delta x,\Delta y
denote bounding-box spans,
|e|
is net degree, and
A_{\text{bbox}}=\Delta x\cdot\Delta y
is bounding-box area. The logarithmic term stabilizes heavy-tailed degree distributions.

Grid node features.

Each grid node
g
corresponds to a spatial bin at a given resolution level and aggregates local placement statistics within its spatial extent. We initialize each grid node with a feature vector:

(3)

\boldsymbol{x}_{g}^{\text{grid}}=\big[\rho_{\text{cell}}(g),\;\rho_{\text{pin}}(g),\;\rho_{\text{net}}(g),\;\bar{a}_{\text{cell}}(g),\;\bar{\ell}_{\text{net}}(g)\big],

where
\rho_{\text{cell}}(g)
,
\rho_{\text{pin}}(g)
, and
\rho_{\text{net}}(g)
denote the normalized densities of cells, pins, and nets intersecting grid
g
, respectively.

\bar{a}_{\text{cell}}(g)
is the mean cell area within the grid, and

\bar{\ell}_{\text{net}}(g)
is the mean HPWL of nets overlapping the grid region.
The same feature definition is applied consistently across fine and coarse grid levels, enabling multi-resolution spatial reasoning.

Edge features.

Each edge type is associated with its own feature representation.
Pin edges

(v,e)
encode cell–net incidences and are initialized with a feature vector:

(4)

{x}_{v\to e}^{\text{pin}}=\big[\text{dir}(v,e),\;\delta_{x}(v,e),\;\delta_{y}(v,e)\big],

where
\text{dir}(v,e)\in\{0,1\}
indicates signal direction (input or output), and
(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})
denote the normalized offset of the cell location relative to the centroid of the net bounding box.

Geometric cell edges

(v_{i},v_{j})
encode spatial proximity between neighboring cells and are initialized as:

(5)

{x}_{ij}^{\text{geom}}=\big[\Delta x_{ij},\;\Delta y_{ij},\;d_{\text{manh}}(v_{i},v_{j}),\;d_{\text{eucl}}(v_{i},v_{j})\big],

where
(\Delta x_{ij},\Delta y_{ij})
denote relative displacement, and
d_{\text{manh}}
,
d_{\text{eucl}}
are the Manhattan and Euclidean distances between cells.

Hierarchical grid edges
connect grid nodes across adjacent resolution levels. These edges are unweighted and do not carry explicit features; instead, they serve as structural links that propagate spatial context between fine and coarse grids during message passing.

3.3.
Relation-Specific Message Passing

Figure
3
illustrates the message passing process during training. Specifically, Each edge type induces a distinct message function, enabling the model to propagate routing-demand signals along connectivity and geometry relations.

Cell-net message passing.

For each pin edge
(v,e)
connecting a cell node
v
to a net node
e
, we compute a pin aware message conditioned on both the cell embedding and the pin attributes:

(6)

\boldsymbol{m}_{v\to e}=\text{MLP}_{\text{pin}}\big([\boldsymbol{h}_{v}\;\|\;\boldsymbol{p}_{v\to e}]\big),

where
\boldsymbol{h}_{v}
is the current cell embedding and
\boldsymbol{p}_{v\to e}
encodes signal direction and relative pin offset.
Messages from all incident cells are aggregated to form an intermediate net representation:

(7)

\boldsymbol{h}_{e}^{\text{cell}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(e)|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}(e)}\boldsymbol{m}_{v\to e},

where
\mathcal{N}(e)
denotes the set of cells connected to net
e
.
The final net embedding is obtained by combining the aggregated cell messages, aggregated grid messages, and the transformed raw net features through a learnable projection.

Grid-net message passing.

Each net
e
spans a set of grid tiles
\mathcal{G}(e)
determined by the bounding box of its pins. To incorporate spatial congestion information from the routing grid, we aggregate grid-level embeddings as

(8)

\boldsymbol{h}_{e}^{\text{grid}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}(e)|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{G}(e)}\boldsymbol{W}_{t\to e}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{t},

where
\boldsymbol{h}_{t}
denotes the embedding of grid tile
t
, and
\boldsymbol{W}_{t\to e}
is a learnable projection. This operation propagates localized congestion context from the routing grid to the net representation.

Cell-grid message passing.

Cell-level information is propagated back to the routing grid to ground learned cell embeddings in spatial context. For each grid tile
t
, we aggregate messages from the set of cells
\mathcal{C}(t)
whose placements fall within its spatial extent:

(9)

\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\text{cell}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}(t)|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}(t)}\boldsymbol{W}_{v\to t}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{v},

where
\boldsymbol{h}_{v}
denotes the embedding of cell
v
, and
\boldsymbol{W}_{v\to t}
is a learnable projection matrix. This aggregation transfers localized cell-level information to the corresponding grid tile.

The grid tile embedding is then updated by combining the original grid representation with the aggregated cell messages through a residual transformation:

(10)

\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\prime}=\text{ReLU}\Big(\boldsymbol{W}_{t}\big[\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\;\|\;\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\text{cell}}\big]\Big)+\boldsymbol{h}_{t},

where
\boldsymbol{W}_{t}
is a learnable linear projection.

Net-cell message passing.

Updated net embeddings are propagated back to connected cells through pin edges, allowing each cell to receive routing-demand information accumulated at the net level:

(11)

\boldsymbol{h}_{v}^{\text{net}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}(v)|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}(v)}\boldsymbol{W}_{e\to v}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{e},

where
\mathcal{E}(v)
denotes the set of nets incident to cell
v
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{e\to v}
is a learnable transformation.

Geometric cell–cell message passing.

For each geometric edge
(i,j)
connecting spatially nearby cells, we apply a gated aggregation to modulate neighbor influence based on spatial relevance:

(12)

\alpha_{ij}=\sigma\Big(\boldsymbol{W}_{g}[\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\;\|\;\boldsymbol{h}_{j}\;\|\;\boldsymbol{g}_{ij}]\Big),

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{ij}
encodes relative displacement and distance metrics.
The geometric message aggregated at cell
i
is then computed as:

(13)

\boldsymbol{h}_{i}^{\text{geom}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{g}(i)|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{g}(i)}\alpha_{ij}\,\boldsymbol{W}_{g}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{h}_{j},

where
\mathcal{N}_{g}(i)
denotes the set of geometric neighbors of cell
i
.
This mechanism captures local routing competition while suppressing spatially proximate but congestion-irrelevant neighbors.

Cell state update.

The final cell embedding is obtained by fusing messages from net connections and geometric neighbors through a residual update:

(14)

\boldsymbol{h}_{v}^{\prime}=\text{ReLU}\Big(\boldsymbol{W}_{f}[{h}_{v}\;\|\;\boldsymbol{h}_{v}^{\text{net}}\;\|\;\boldsymbol{h}_{v}^{\text{geom}}]\Big)+{h}_{v},

where the residual connection preserves gradient stability and mitigates oversmoothing during training.

3.4.
Hierarchical Grid Representation Learning

Figure 4.
Hierarchical grid representation learning. Grid features are aggregated from fine to coarse levels and then propagated back from coarse to fine levels, enabling multi-scale spatial information exchange.

Grid node embeddings are learned on the hierarchical grid subgraph to capture spatial routing patterns across multiple resolutions.
As shown in Figure
4
,
grid nodes are organized into fine and coarse levels, where coarse grids summarize larger spatial regions and fine grids preserve local detail. The hierarchy enables efficient propagation of global spatial context while maintaining fine-grained congestion sensitivity.

Fine-to-coarse aggregation.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\text{grid}}^{(\ell)}
denote the set of grid nodes at level
\ell
, with
\ell=0
corresponding to the finest grid.
Each coarse grid node
g^{(\ell+1)}
aggregates information from its associated fine-grid children

\mathcal{C}(g^{(\ell+1)})\subset\mathcal{V}_{\text{grid}}^{(\ell)}
:

(15)

{h}_{g}^{(\ell+1)}=\phi_{\text{fc}}^{(\ell)}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}(g)|}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(g)}{h}_{g^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{h}_{g^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}
denotes the grid embedding at level
\ell
, and

\phi_{\text{fc}}^{(\ell)}(\cdot)
is a learnable transformation (implemented as a linear layer followed by nonlinearity).
This bottom-up aggregation allows each coarse grid node to summarize routing demand over an increasingly large spatial region.

Coarse-to-fine propagation.

After aggregation at coarse levels, spatial context is propagated back to finer grids through the hierarchical parent–child relations.
For each fine grid node
g^{(\ell)}
, we propagate information from its parent node
\pi(g^{(\ell)})\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{grid}}^{(\ell+1)}
:

(16)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{g}^{(\ell)}=\phi_{\text{cf}}^{(\ell)}\big(\boldsymbol{h}_{\pi(g)}^{(\ell+1)}\big),

where
\phi_{\text{cf}}^{(\ell)}(\cdot)
is a learnable projection.
This top-down propagation injects global and regional routing context into fine-grid nodes, enabling local regions to reason about congestion beyond their immediate neighborhood.

Gated integration.

To balance local fidelity and global context, we adaptively combine the propagated hierarchical feature with the original fine-grid embedding using a learnable gate:

(17)

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{\gamma}\big[\boldsymbol{h}_{g}^{(0)}\;\|\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{g}^{(0)}\big]\right),

(18)

\boldsymbol{h}_{g}^{\text{out}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}\odot\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{g}^{(0)}+(1-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g})\odot\boldsymbol{h}_{g}^{(0)},

where
\boldsymbol{h}_{g}^{(0)}
is the original fine-grid embedding and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.
The gate
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}
controls the influence of multi-scale spatial context on each grid node, ensuring that the hierarchical representation enhances congestion reasoning without overwhelming fine-grained local information.

3.5.
Congestion Prediction and Training Objective

Figure
5
illustrates the final prediction stage following the message passing process.
Separate prediction heads are applied to the final cell embeddings to produce instance-level and region-level congestion estimates. This dual supervision allows the model to learn congestion patterns at both fine granularity (cell-level hotspots) and coarse granularity (regional routing pressure).

Weighted regression loss.

Routing congestion exhibits a highly imbalanced distribution in real designs: the majority of cells and grid regions have near-zero congestion, while a small fraction forms critical hotspots that dominate routing difficulty. Under standard mean squared error, the optimization process is dominated by the abundant low-congestion samples, often causing the model to converge to trivial low-variance predictions.

To mitigate this issue, we adopt a weighted mean squared error that assigns higher importance to congested regions:

(19)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{wmse}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}w_{v}\,(\hat{c}_{v}-c_{v})^{2},

where
c_{v}
and
\hat{c}_{v}
denote the ground-truth and predicted congestion values, respectively, and
w_{v}
is a congestion-dependent weight. By upweighting high-congestion samples, the loss explicitly steers the model toward accurately ranking and differentiating congestion hotspots, which are most critical for downstream placement and routing decisions.

Figure 5.
Embedding and readout. Embeddings from different node types (cell, geom, net, and grid) are concatenated and mapped through a readout layer to produce the final congestion heatmap.

Variance regularization.

Although weighted regression mitigates label imbalance, it does not fully prevent prediction collapse. We therefore include a variance regularization term that penalizes insufficient output dispersion, discouraging near-constant predictions and preserving relative differences among congestion levels.

Optimization and early stopping.

The final objective combines cell-level and grid-level weighted regression losses with variance regularization. Since raw loss values can be dominated by low-congestion regions, model selection is based on validation Spearman rank correlation, which better reflects congestion ordering quality. Early stopping under this criterion prioritizes correct hotspot ranking over average numerical error.

4.1.
Experimental Setup

Datasets.
We evaluate on CircuitNet
(
27
)
, an open-source dataset for machine learning applications in EDA, including three public datasets, ISPD-2015, CircuitNet-N14, and CircuitNet-N28.
Table
1
presents an overview of the datasets used in our experiments. The benchmarks span both academic and industrial settings, with circuit sizes ranging from 35K to 1.5M cells and 38K to 1.6M nets, and include multiple placement and routing snapshots for robust evaluation.

Data preprocessing.
For each dataset, we parse placement DEF files to extract cell coordinates, dimensions, and netlist topology. Ground-truth congestion maps at the original global-routing resolution (which varies per design) are area-averaged to the target grid resolution using bilinear downsampling, ensuring that congestion from the full chip area is faithfully represented rather than truncated to a fixed-size window. All congestion targets across the three datasets are normalized via
\log(1+x)/c_{\max}
, where
c_{\max}
is the per-dataset maximum of
\log(1+x)
, compressing the heavy-tailed distribution to approximately
[0,1]
.

Evaluation metrics.

We evaluate congestion prediction quality using a combination of rank-based correlation metrics and error-based metrics, capturing both the correctness of congestion ordering and the accuracy of predicted magnitudes. Specifically, we report three correlation coefficients that measure different aspects of agreement between predicted congestion values
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{n})
and ground-truth values
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, as well as two absolute error metrics.

Pearson correlation coefficient (
r
)
measures the linear relationship between predictions and targets:

(20)

r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-\bar{\hat{y}})(y_{i}-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-\bar{\hat{y}})^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}}}

where
\bar{\hat{y}}
and
\bar{y}
are the respective means. Pearson
r\in[-1,1]
captures whether the model’s predictions scale linearly with the true congestion values. It is sensitive to outliers and requires the predicted values to maintain both correct ranking
and
correct relative magnitudes.

Spearman rank correlation (
\rho_{s}
)
assesses monotonic association by computing the Pearson correlation on the rank-transformed variables:

(21)

\rho_{s}=r\big(\text{rank}(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}),\;\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{y})\big)

where
\text{rank}(\cdot)
converts values to their ordinal ranks. Spearman
\rho_{s}\in[-1,1]
is robust to non-linear monotonic transformations and directly measures whether the model correctly orders cells/grid bins from least to most congested. This is particularly important for practical layout verification, where identifying relative hotspot severity matters more than predicting exact congestion magnitudes.

Kendall rank correlation (
\tau
)
measures pairwise ordering consistency:

(22)

\tau=\frac{n_{c}-n_{d}}{\binom{n}{2}}

where
n_{c}
is the number of concordant pairs (pairs
(i,j)
where
\hat{y}_{i}>\hat{y}_{j}
and
y_{i}>y_{j}
, or vice versa) and
n_{d}
is the number of discordant pairs. Kendall
\tau\in[-1,1]
provides a more conservative and interpretable ranking measure: a value of
\tau
means that the model correctly orders
\frac{1+\tau}{2}
fraction of all cell pairs. It is more robust than Spearman to tied ranks, which frequently occur in congestion data where many cells share near-zero values.

Mean absolute error (MAE)
quantifies the average absolute deviation between predicted and ground-truth congestion values:

(23)

\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}\rvert.

MAE directly reflects the typical magnitude of prediction errors and is less sensitive to outliers than squared-error measures.

Root mean squared error (RMSE)
emphasizes larger deviations by squaring errors before averaging:

(24)

\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}}.

RMSE penalizes large congestion mispredictions more heavily and complements MAE by highlighting severe local errors. Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of congestion prediction performance: Pearson measures value-level linear accuracy, Spearman and Kendall focus on ranking quality and hotspot ordering, while MAE and RMSE quantify absolute prediction errors.
All metrics are computed separately for cell-level and grid-level predictions.

Implementation details.

VeriHGN is implemented in PyTorch 2.1 with PyTorch Geometric 2.4
(
5
)
. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU with CUDA 11.8. We train with AdamW optimizer (learning rate
5\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay
10^{-4}
) and StepLR scheduler (step size 50,
\gamma=0.5
) for up to 200 epochs. Early stopping with patience 20 is based on average validation Spearman correlation
\bar{\rho}_{s}=(\rho_{s}^{\text{cell}}+\rho_{s}^{\text{grid}})/2
. We adopt Spearman rather than validation loss for early stopping, as we empirically observed that low MSE loss does not always indicate good ranking quality—a model that predicts near-constant values achieves low loss on the sparse congestion distribution but zero correlation.

Overall, VeriHGN achieves the strongest or near-strongest performance across both granularities, outperforming GCN, GAT and prior heterogeneous graph baselines on most error-based and correlation-based metrics. Across datasets, VeriHGN demonstrates a balanced improvement in absolute error and ranking quality (Spearman and Kendall), indicating that it not only reduces numerical prediction error but also preserves the relative ordering of congestion severity, which is critical for practical hotspot identification.

And also several important trends can be observed from the table. First, VeriHGN shows robust improvements in rank-based metrics across nearly all datasets and prediction levels, achieving the highest Spearman and Kendall correlations in most cases, which suggests a stronger ability to capture relative congestion patterns compared to existing methods. Second, while prior methods such as MIHC occasionally achieve competitive or slightly better performance on individual metrics (e.g., Pearson correlation or MAE on specific datasets), VeriHGN consistently delivers more uniform gains across multiple metrics, avoiding the trade-off between error minimization and ranking fidelity. Third, the performance gap between cell-level and grid-level prediction highlights the increased difficulty of grid-level modeling; nevertheless, VeriHGN maintains its advantage at the grid level, particularly on the larger CircuitNet-N28 benchmark, demonstrating effective generalization from fine-grained cell representations to coarser spatial aggregations. Collectively, these results indicate that VeriHGN provides a stable and scalable solution for congestion prediction across different design granularities and dataset scales.

4.3.
Ablation Study

To quantify the contribution of each proposed component, we conduct a systematic ablation study on CircuitNet-N28, which is a representative benchmark with varying scale designs. In our evaluation. Starting from the full VeriHGN model, we remove or replace one component at a time and report cell-level and grid-level performance. The results are summarized in Table
3
.

Several key observations emerge from the ablation results. First, Hierarchical grid representation is critical for grid-level prediction.
Removing the multi-resolution grid hierarchy causes the largest drop in grid-level Spearman correlation (
0.401\to 0.342
,
-14.7\%
), confirming that multi-scale spatial aggregation is essential for capturing regional routing pressure that spans beyond fine-grid neighborhoods. Second, Disabling grid-to-net messages leads to substantial degradation in both cell-level Spearman (
0.548\to 0.503
) and grid-level Spearman (
0.401\to 0.356
). This result validates our design choice of propagating localized routing-resource information from grid tiles to net representations, which enables nets to be aware of the spatial congestion context along their routing paths. Furthermore, replacing the hand-crafted features with minimal coordinate and connectivity features yields the largest overall performance drop, with cell-level Spearman falling from
0.548
to
0.478
. This highlights that explicitly encoding routing-demand signals such as HPWL statistics, pin density, and type-related attributes is far more sample-efficient than relying on deep message passing to implicitly recover these signals, particularly given the limited number of training designs in current EDA benchmarks.

As shown in Table

We attribute this improved relative transferability to two design choices. First, the unified heterogeneous graph formulation captures design-agnostic routing-demand patterns (e.g., high-fanout nets competing for local routing resources) that partially transfer across process nodes. Second, the enriched feature engineering normalizes design-dependent quantities (e.g., cell dimensions, HPWL) on a per-design basis via standardization, partially mitigating the distributional gap between source and target domains. In contrast, MIHC’s loosely coupled multi-view architecture may overfit to view-specific distributional characteristics of the training design.

These results highlight that cross-design generalization remains a challenging open problem for learning-based congestion prediction. While VeriHGN’s unified representation provides a better starting point, effective deployment on a new design node will likely require at least a small amount of in-domain fine-tuning data.

5.
Conclusion

We presented VeriHGN, an early-stage congestion prediction framework that unifies circuit components and spatial grids into a single heterogeneous graph, enabling direct modeling of the interaction between netlist topology and physical layout geometry. By combining enriched feature engineering, relation-specific message passing, hierarchical grid representation learning, and a congestion-weighted training strategy, VeriHGN achieves competitive or superior performance compared to existing methods on ISPD2015, CircuitNet-N14, and CircuitNet-N28 benchmarks. Future work includes incorporating geometry-based encoder branches to further strengthen spatial reasoning, extending the framework to timing-aware congestion prediction, and improving cross-technology transferability through domain adaptation techniques.
