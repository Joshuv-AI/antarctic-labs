Leveraging biologgers to better forecast animal movement and range shifts across scales
Authors: Alexa L. Fredston, Roxanne S. Beltran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To predict how movement strategies shape fitness in a changing world, we must build mechanistic models of species’ ranges and range shifts that align environmental data with the scales of individual perception and decision-making and jointly model biological processes from the individual to the population scale.

Main text

Animals are on the move across space and time. As human impacts alter the climate, birds have shifted higher in mountain ranges and fishes have spread hundreds of kilometers beyond their historical ranges. At the same time, the daily, seasonal, and interannual rhythms of animal life—breeding, hatching, foraging, migrating—are also changing, with consequences for individual and population-level persistence. These phenological and distributional changes have become a central focus of ecology under the umbrella of “species on the move” [1].

What drives species on the move? Animals respond to changing physical environments and resource landscapes at different scales—particularly migratory species that undergo large-scale movements while making fine-scale decisions [2, 3]. Fredston, Tingley, et al. [1] recently proposed that species on the move may be best understood by simultaneously considering species’ responses to all available thermal habitats in both space and time and from small to large scales. This “SOTM framework” can be extended to other abiotic and biotic drivers; for animals, incorporating prey, risk, and other species interactions will be critical.

The science of “species on the move” (SOTM) has focused on connecting spatial and temporal shifts to changing climates, and on predicting past and future range or phenological shifts with concurrent environmental data. For range shifts in particular, this is typically accomplished with species distribution modeling (SDM, a form of ecological niche modeling). SDMs infer statistical associations between the environment and species’ occurrences and sometimes abundances. The processes by which individuals perceive, decide, and move are only implicit in these models, and SDMs typically model suitable habitat—not necessarily occupied habitat. The predictive skill of SDMs is limited [4], although models that leverage biologically relevant predictors at appropriate scales (e.g., microclimate) may perform well [5]. Range change in animals is difficult to model for several reasons. Animals often do not colonize or repeatedly use all environmentally suitable areas because movement decisions are constrained by dispersal ability, memory, social structure, predation risk, and imperfect information about the landscape. Further, many animals are unlikely to track climate directly; for example, mobile predators that appear to be shifting in response to climate change may actually be tracking temperature-sensitive prey [6]. Movement-based connectivity approaches (e.g., climate connectivity, functional connectivity) address related questions at the landscape scale, but typically parameterize movement potential from landscape structure and trait-based generalizations rather than from observed individual behavior. A major barrier to operationalizing the SOTM framework is the scale mismatch between existing environmental data (often coarse, static, and regional) and the scales at which animals actually sense and respond to them (typically fine, dynamic, and context-dependent). This mismatch leads to models that describe population-level shifts but miss the mechanisms that individual animals use to navigate changing landscapes and models that describe individual behavior without scaling to populations [7].

The study of how animals respond to a changing environmental and resource landscape has unfolded in two parallel directions. First, SDMs have provided substantial insights into species range dynamics [8] and sometimes predict the occurrence and/or density of animals, even on short time scales [9]. Second, our understanding of how animals make decisions based on internal states, perceptual limits, thermal and energetic constraints, and memory, is growing rapidly with the advent of biologging technologies [10, 11].

Importantly, modern biologging technologies increasingly provide information not only on movement trajectories, but also on physiological state (e.g., heart rate, body temperature, energetic state), environmental conditions experienced by individuals (e.g., temperature, humidity, depth), reproductive behavior, migration phenology, and other processes that influence dispersal, connectivity, habitat selection, survival, recruitment, and reproduction. Together, these behavioral and physiological mechanisms ultimately determine whether populations persist, decline, colonize new areas, or shift their ranges. Linking individual decisions and life-history responses to demographic outcomes therefore provides a pathway from individual behavior to species-level range dynamics. In particular, biologging-informed models may help identify the behavioral and physiological mechanisms that influence dispersal, colonization, and persistence at range edges. Here we argue that integrating biologging with process-based models of species distributions can bridge the gap between individual decision-making and population redistribution, enabling mechanistic prediction of animals on the move at unprecedented resolution and scale (Fig. 1).

The movement ecology paradigm [12] and recent advancements in biologging tools and theory [11], combined with the advent of process-based models for species distributions that can integrate multiple data streams [13], have positioned us to operationalize the SOTM framework. By integrating individual movement trajectories with dynamic environmental and ecological data at biologically relevant spatial (meters to global) and temporal scales (seconds to years), we can model how animals actually experience and respond to changing conditions. This integration will allow us to move beyond static, often two-dimensional representations of habitat utilization toward dynamic, multidimensional, individual-based descriptions of how decisions unfold across temporal and spatial scales, from fine-scale behavioral responses to large-scale redistribution. This approach allows environmental variability to be mapped onto the decision space of the animal, rather than imposed at arbitrary spatial or temporal resolutions. As a result, movement becomes an emergent outcome of interactions between individuals and their environments, rather than a pattern fitted retrospectively.

Mechanistic movement models grounded in behavior, sensory ecology, and energetics are uniquely positioned to forecast animal dynamics in a changing climate [14]. These models explicitly represent how animals make decisions—for example, by linking movement, migration timing, breeding behavior, energetic costs, prey encounters, social dynamics, or perceived risk—and scale those processes to entire populations. Critically, biologging technologies now provide measurements of movement, environment, and physiology at biologically relevant spatiotemporal scales—meaning that models can now be fitted to both individual-level and landscape-scale data. These time-matched tools will thus enable us to link trajectories to the conditions animals encounter and the states they occupy, creating an integrated view of movement as a sequence of decisions shaped by dynamic gradients in space and time.

Unified movement models that connect individual decisions to population- and species-level outcomes could also advance ecological and biogeographical theory. By linking the environments animals experience, the decisions they make, and the fitness consequences of those decisions, these models would allow researchers to test when movement buffers individuals from environmental change, when it exposes them to new risks, and how individual behavioral variation scales up to alter population persistence or range limits. This new direction would move biologging from documenting movement to testing how movement strategies shape fitness under environmental change.

Integration at matched scales is newly-enabled by an evolving data landscape. Computational power and increasingly large datasets are available to scale up methods that were originally developed to model the dynamics of individual animals [14]. Important challenges remain, including taxonomic, geographic, and body size biases in biologging datasets, variation in the ecological information captured by different sensor types, limited understanding of the fitness consequences of many movement strategies, and the need to integrate biologging with complementary data sources to characterize species interactions, resource dynamics, and both occupied and available habitat. Deployments spanning many individuals, populations, and years can help characterize a broader range of environmental conditions and behavioral responses, particularly in systems with substantial individual and temporal variation. Nevertheless, high-resolution biologging and near-real-time remote sensing now exist for thousands of species [15]. Thus, we argue that mechanistic models integrating heterogeneous data streams across scales are poised to become a standard approach for forecasting animals on the move.

Conclusion

This transition will allow us to move beyond merely describing where animals are toward understanding how and why they are there. By aligning environmental data with the scales of individual perception and decision-making, and jointly modeling biological processes from the individual to the population scale, we can generate mechanistic forecasts of redistribution under novel conditions and begin to predict how movement strategies influence fitness, demographic processes, and ultimately species redistribution in a changing world.
