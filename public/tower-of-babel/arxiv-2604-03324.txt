The first fatal axiom for weakened sequential products on finite MV-effect algebras Local obstruction, exact low-rank classification, and the rank-one boundary case
Joaquim Reizi Higuchi

Abstract
We study total binary operations on effect algebras obtained by truncating the Gudder–Greechie axiom package for a sequential product. The point is not to reprove the known nonexistence of non-Boolean full sequential products on finite chains, but to determine, axiom by axiom, where finite MV-effect algebras first fail. We prove two structural facts valid on every effect algebra. First, the operation σ E ​ ( a , b ) = { 0 , a = 0 , b , a ≠ 0 , \sigma_{E}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a=0,\\ b,&a\neq 0,\end{cases} satisfies (S1)–(S3), so (S3) is never fatal by itself. Second, any operation satisfying (S1)–(S4) already has the right-unit property a ∘ 1 = a a\circ 1=a , even without (S5). From this we derive a local obstruction theorem: if an effect algebra contains an atom of finite isotropic index at least 2 2 , then it admits no (S1)–(S4) operation. Consequently, a finite MV-effect algebra admits such an operation if and only if it is Boolean. In this precise sense, (S4) is the first fatal axiom on finite MV-effect algebras. On the constructive side, let E 𝐮 = [ 0 , 𝐮 ] ⊆ ℤ r E_{\mathbf{u}}=[0,\mathbf{u}]\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r} be the simplicial interval representation of a finite MV-effect algebra. We show that additive maps E 𝐮 → E 𝐯 E_{\mathbf{u}}\to E_{\mathbf{v}} are exactly the restrictions of positive group homomorphisms ℤ r → ℤ s \mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}^{s} , equivalently maps x ↦ M ​ x x\mapsto Mx given by nonnegative integer matrices with M ​ 𝐮 ≤ 𝐯 M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v} . This yields a complete classification of (S1)+(S2) operations by row-wise subunital matrices. We then solve the first genuinely higher-rank (S1)–(S3) problem: on the rank-two Boolean algebra B 2 = E ( 1 , 1 ) ≅ C 1 2 B_{2}=E_{(1,1)}\cong C_{1}^{2} , all such operations are classified and there are exactly 34 34 . Thus the finite-chain collapse at (S3) is a rank-one boundary phenomenon, whereas on finite MV-effect algebras the sharp threshold for nonexistence occurs exactly at (S4).

Introduction

Effect algebras, introduced by Foulis and Bennett
[
1
]
, provide an algebraic framework for unsharp quantum events. Gudder and Greechie
[
2
]
defined a
sequential product
on an effect algebra in order to abstract sequential measurement; a standard motivating example is the Lüders product
A\circ B=A^{1/2}BA^{1/2}
on Hilbert-space effects. Since then, most of the literature has continued to work with the full sequential-effect-algebra axiom package, or with stronger settings such as operator, convex, or normal sequential effect algebras; see for instance
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. In the present paper we keep the same five Gudder–Greechie axioms (S1)–(S5), but we study what happens when only the initial segment (S1)–(Sk) is imposed.

Known finite nonexistence results already settle the full-axiom picture in the rank-one direction. In particular, chain-finite sequential effect algebras are Boolean
[
5
]
, so a paper devoted only to finite chains would amount to a clean but essentially known reproof. The novelty of the present work is therefore
not
a new full-sequential-product nonexistence theorem for finite chains or finite MV-effect algebras. Rather, the goal is to determine the
axiom threshold
at which finite MV-effect algebras first collapse, and to identify how the chain phenomenon changes in higher rank.

The first main contribution is structural and negative. We show that the universal operation

\sigma_{E}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a=0,\\
b,&a\neq 0,\end{cases}

satisfies (S1)–(S3) on every effect algebra, so (S3) is never fatal by itself. We then prove that any (S1)–(S4) operation already satisfies the right-unit law
a\circ 1=a
, although that law is usually viewed as a standard consequence of the full axioms. From this we derive a local obstruction theorem: if an effect algebra contains an atom of finite isotropic index at least
2
, then no (S1)–(S4) operation can exist. Specializing to finite MV-effect algebras, we obtain the main threshold statement of the paper: (S1)–(S3) are always realizable, but (S4) already forces Booleanity. In short, (S4) is the first fatal axiom on finite MV-effect algebras.

The second main contribution is constructive. A finite MV-effect algebra is a simplicial interval
E_{\mathbf{u}}=[0,\mathbf{u}]\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and hence a finite product of finite chains
[
4
]
. In this representation we classify all additive maps
E_{\mathbf{u}}\to E_{\mathbf{v}}
by nonnegative integer matrices
M
with
M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
. This should be read as candidate-space infrastructure rather than as the sole novelty claim: it turns the weakened sequential-product problem into an explicit combinatorial classification of left translations. Using that framework, we classify all (S1)+(S2) operations on finite MV-effect algebras and then solve the first genuinely higher-rank case at the (S3) stage. Namely, on the rank-two Boolean algebra
B_{2}=E_{(1,1)}\cong C_{1}^{2}
, we classify all (S1)–(S3) operations and show that there are exactly
34
. This exact low-rank classification makes the rank-one/higher-rank contrast concrete: the chain collapse at (S3) is exceptional, whereas higher rank already admits many additional candidates before (S4) is imposed.

To avoid misunderstanding, we stress once more what is and is not claimed here. We do not claim novelty for the standard simplicial-interval coordinatization of finite MV-effect algebras, and we do not claim a new full-SEA Booleanity theorem. The novelty lies in the weakened-axiom regime: a sharp cutoff at (S4), a local obstruction theorem that applies to arbitrary effect algebras, and an exact higher-rank (S1)–(S3) classification on
B_{2}
.

Main contributions.

1.

A universal (S1)–(S3) model on every effect algebra and a derivation of the right-unit law
a\circ 1=a
from (S1)–(S4) alone.

2.

A finite-isotropic-atom obstruction theorem, yielding the threshold theorem that a finite MV-effect algebra admits an (S1)–(S4) operation if and only if it is Boolean.

3.

A matrix classification of additive maps on finite MV-effect algebras, hence a complete classification of (S1)+(S2) operations.

4.

An exact classification of (S1)–(S3) operations on
B_{2}=E_{(1,1)}
, with the sharp count
34
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
fixes notation and recalls the simplicial representation of finite MV-effect algebras. Section
3
proves the universal (S1)–(S3) model and the right-unit theorem. Section
4
contains the finite-isotropic-atom obstruction and the finite-MV threshold theorem. Section
5
gives the matrix classification of additive maps and of (S1)+(S2) operations, together with explicit higher-rank flexibility results. Section
6

B_{2}
. Section
7
returns to finite chains and shows that the additional collapse at (S3) is a rank-one boundary phenomenon.

2
Preliminaries

An
effect algebra
is a quadruple
(E,0,1,\oplus)
in which
\oplus
is a partially defined binary operation satisfying the standard axioms of commutativity, associativity, orthosupplementation, and the zero–one law
[
1
]
. We write
a\mathrel{\perp}b
when
a\oplus b
is defined, and
a^{\prime}
for the unique orthosupplement determined by
a\oplus a^{\prime}=1
. The induced order is

a\leq b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists c\in E\text{ such that }a\oplus c=b.

We use without further comment the standard cancellation and positivity properties of effect algebras; see
[
5
, Definition 1.1 and the discussion after it]
. In particular, if
x\oplus y=0
, then
x=y=0
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in E
, when the repeated orthogonal sum exists we write

na=\underbrace{a\oplus\cdots\oplus a}_{n\text{ times}}.

The
isotropic index
of
a
is

\operatorname{ord}(a):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:na\text{ exists}\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

An
atom
is a minimal nonzero element.

We fix the Gudder–Greechie axioms for a total binary operation
\circ
on an effect algebra
E
:

(S1)

the map
b\mapsto a\circ b
is additive, i.e.

b\mathrel{\perp}c\Longrightarrow a\circ(b\oplus c)=(a\circ b)\oplus(a\circ c);

(S2)

1\circ a=a
;

(S3)

a\circ b=0\Longrightarrow a\circ b=b\circ a
;

(S4)

if
a\circ b=b\circ a
, then
a\circ b^{\prime}=b^{\prime}\circ a
and

a\circ(b\circ c)=(a\circ b)\circ c;

(S5)

if
c\circ a=a\circ c
and
c\circ b=b\circ c
, then
c\circ(a\circ b)=(a\circ b)\circ c
, and also
c\circ(a\oplus b)=(a\oplus b)\circ c
whenever
a\mathrel{\perp}b
.

If
\circ
satisfies (S1)–(Sk) we call it an
(S1)
–
(Sk)
operation
. As usual, we abbreviate
a\circ b=b\circ a
by
a\mid b
.

We shall use the standard coordinatization of finite MV-effect algebras. Let

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{r},\qquad E_{\mathbf{u}}:=[0,\mathbf{u}]:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}:0\leq x_{i}\leq u_{i}\text{ for all }i\}.

We regard
E_{\mathbf{u}}
as an effect algebra with partial addition

x\oplus y=x+y\qquad\text{whenever }x+y\leq\mathbf{u},

and orthosupplement
x^{\prime}=\mathbf{u}-x
. Its top element is
\mathbf{u}
. By standard structure theory, every finite MV-effect algebra is isomorphic to some
E_{\mathbf{u}}
; equivalently, every finite MV-effect algebra is a finite product of finite chains
[
4
, pp. 211–212]
.

We write
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
for the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

In
E_{\mathbf{u}}
, the atoms are exactly
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
, and
\operatorname{ord}(e_{i})=u_{i}
for every
i
.

Proof.

Each
e_{i}
is nonzero. If
0\leq x\leq e_{i}
, then every coordinate of
x
except possibly the
i
th is zero, and the
i
th coordinate is either
0
or
1
; hence
x\in\{0,e_{i}\}
. So each
e_{i}
is an atom.

Conversely, let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\in E_{\mathbf{u}}
be nonzero. Choose an index
i
with
x_{i}>0
. Then
e_{i}\leq x
. If
x
is an atom, this forces
x=e_{i}
. Thus the atoms are exactly the
e_{i}
.

Finally,
ne_{i}
exists in
E_{\mathbf{u}}
exactly when
n\leq u_{i}
, because
ne_{i}=(0,\dots,0,n,0,\dots,0)
. Hence
\operatorname{ord}(e_{i})=u_{i}
.
∎

3
Universal (S1)–(S3) model and right unit

We begin with a universal model for (S1)–(S3).

Proposition 3.1

(A universal (S1)–(S3) operation)
.

For every effect algebra
E
, the binary operation

\sigma_{E}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a=0,\\
b,&a\neq 0,\end{cases}

(3.1)

satisfies
(S1)
,
(S2)
, and
(S3)
.

Proof.

Fix
a\in E
. If
a=0
, then
b\mapsto\sigma_{E}(a,b)
is the zero map, hence additive. If
a\neq 0
, then
b\mapsto\sigma_{E}(a,b)
is the identity map, hence additive. Therefore (S1) holds.

Since
1\neq 0
, we have
\sigma_{E}(1,b)=b
for every
b\in E
, so (S2) holds.

Finally, suppose
\sigma_{E}(a,b)=0
. If
a=0
, then
\sigma_{E}(b,a)=\sigma_{E}(b,0)=0
. If
a\neq 0
, then the definition of
\sigma_{E}
forces
b=0
, and again
\sigma_{E}(b,a)=\sigma_{E}(0,a)=0
. Thus
\sigma_{E}(a,b)=\sigma_{E}(b,a)
whenever
\sigma_{E}(a,b)=0
, which is exactly (S3).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Proposition
3.1
shows that (S3) alone never yields a nonexistence theorem. Any first-failure result must therefore use at least (S4).

The next theorem is the basic structural step behind the obstruction theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(Right unit from (S1)–(S4))
.

Let
E
be an effect algebra and let
\circ
be an
(S1)
–
(S4)
operation on
E
. Then

a\circ 1=a\qquad(a\in E).

In other words, the right-unit law already follows from
(S1)
–
(S4)
.

Proof.

Fix
a\in E
. Since
0\oplus 0=0
, axiom (S1) gives

a\circ 0=a\circ(0\oplus 0)=(a\circ 0)\oplus(a\circ 0).

By positivity, this implies
a\circ 0=0
.

Applying (S2) with
a=0
gives
1\circ 0=0
. Hence (S3) yields

0\circ 1=1\circ 0=0.

Now let
b\in E
be arbitrary. Since
1=b\oplus b^{\prime}
, axiom (S1) applied to the left translation by
0
gives

0=0\circ 1=(0\circ b)\oplus(0\circ b^{\prime}).

Again by positivity,
0\circ b=0
. Thus
0\circ a=0=a\circ 0
, so
a\mid 0
.

Axiom (S4) with
b=0
now gives
a\mid 0^{\prime}
. Because
0^{\prime}=1
, we obtain
a\mid 1
. Finally,

a\circ 1=1\circ a=a

by commutativity with
1
and axiom (S2).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For full sequential effect algebras, both
a\circ 0=0
and
a\circ 1=a
are standard consequences of the Gudder–Greechie axioms; compare
[
5
, Proposition 3.2]
and
[
7
, Proposition 17(1)]
. Theorem
3.3
shows that (S5) is not needed for the right-unit law.

4
Finite isotropic atom obstruction and the first fatal axiom

We now turn the right-unit law into a general obstruction.

Theorem 4.1

(Finite isotropic atom obstruction)
.

Let
E
be an effect algebra. Assume that
E
contains an atom
p
with finite isotropic index

2\leq\operatorname{ord}(p)<\infty.

Then
E
admits no
(S1)
–
(S4)
operation.

Proof.

Assume toward a contradiction that
\circ
is an (S1)–(S4) operation on
E
. Put

n:=\operatorname{ord}(p)\geq 2,\qquad q:=(np)^{\prime}.

Then
np\oplus q=1
.

By Theorem
3.3
,

p=p\circ 1=p\circ(np\oplus q)=p\circ(np)\oplus(p\circ q).

Repeated use of (S1) gives
p\circ(np)=n(p\circ p)
, so

p=n(p\circ p)\oplus(p\circ q).

(4.1)

Hence
p\circ p\leq p
. Since
p
is an atom, either
p\circ p=0
or
p\circ p=p
.

Suppose
p\circ p=p
. Then (
4.1
) implies
np=n(p\circ p)\leq p
. Because
n\geq 2
, the element
2p
exists and satisfies
0<2p\leq np\leq p
. Since
p
is an atom, this forces
2p=p
. But then

p=p\oplus p,

contradicting cancellation. Therefore

p\circ p=0.

(4.2)

From the trivial equality
p\circ p=p\circ p
we have
p\mid p
, so axiom (S4) gives
p\mid p^{\prime}
. Using (
4.2
) and Theorem
3.3
, we obtain

p=p\circ 1=p\circ(p\oplus p^{\prime})=(p\circ p)\oplus(p\circ p^{\prime})=0\oplus(p\circ p^{\prime})=p\circ p^{\prime}.

Since
p\mid p^{\prime}
, this yields

p^{\prime}\circ p=p.

(4.3)

Now apply Theorem
3.3
to
p^{\prime}
and use
1=np\oplus q
:

p^{\prime}=p^{\prime}\circ 1=p^{\prime}\circ(np\oplus q)=p^{\prime}\circ(np)\oplus(p^{\prime}\circ q)=n(p^{\prime}\circ p)\oplus(p^{\prime}\circ q).

By (
4.3
), this becomes

p^{\prime}=np\oplus(p^{\prime}\circ q).

Hence
np\leq p^{\prime}
, so
(n+1)p=np\oplus p
exists. This contradicts the maximality of
n=\operatorname{ord}(p)
. Therefore no (S1)–(S4) operation exists on
E
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The proof uses neither lattice structure nor the Riesz decomposition property. The hypothesis is purely local: one finite-isotropic atom already prevents the existence of any (S1)–(S4) operation.

The chain case is contained in Theorem
4.1
, but it is convenient to record an intermediate one-atom consequence.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
E
be a finite effect algebra with a unique atom
p
. Then every element of
E
is of the form
kp
for a unique
k\in\{0,1,\dots,\operatorname{ord}(p)\}
, and hence
E
is the finite chain

\{0,p,2p,\dots,\operatorname{ord}(p)p=1\}.

In particular, if
\operatorname{ord}(p)\geq 2
, then
E
admits no
(S1)
–
(S4)
operation.

Proof.

Let
x\in E
be nonzero. Because
E
is finite, the interval
[0,x]
has a minimal nonzero element, hence an atom. By uniqueness of atoms, that atom is
p
, so
p\leq x
. Thus every nonzero
x
can be written as
x=p\oplus x_{1}
. If
x_{1}\neq 0
, the same argument gives
p\leq x_{1}
, so
x_{1}=p\oplus x_{2}
. Iterating and using finiteness, we obtain
x=kp
for some
k\geq 1
. Uniqueness of
k
follows from cancellation.

Taking
x=1
shows that
1=np
for some
n
, necessarily
n=\operatorname{ord}(p)
. Hence
E
is exactly the displayed chain. The final claim follows from Theorem
4.1
when
n\geq 2
.
∎

We now specialize to finite MV-effect algebras.

Theorem 4.4

(Threshold theorem for finite MV-effect algebras)
.

Let
E
be a finite MV-effect algebra. The following are equivalent:

(i)

E
is Boolean;

(ii)

E
admits an
(S1)
–
(S4)
operation;

(iii)

E
admits a sequential product.

In particular, for finite MV-effect algebras the first fatal axiom is
(S4)
.

Proof.

The implication (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is immediate. If
E
is Boolean, then the meet operation
a\circ b:=a\wedge b
is a sequential product
[
2
]
, so (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii).

It remains to prove (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Write
E\cong E_{\mathbf{u}}
for some
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{r}
. By Lemma
2.1
, the atoms are
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
and
\operatorname{ord}(e_{i})=u_{i}
. If some
u_{i}\geq 2
, then Theorem
4.1
forbids the existence of an (S1)–(S4) operation. Therefore every
u_{i}=1
, so
E\cong E_{(1,\dots,1)}
, which is a finite Boolean algebra.

Finally, Proposition
3.1
shows that every effect algebra, hence every finite MV-effect algebra, admits an (S1)–(S3) operation. Therefore (S4) is indeed the first fatal axiom in this class.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Theorem
4.4
is the paper’s main finite-MV cutoff statement. It is strictly finer than the known full-sequential-product Booleanity results: it identifies the exact stage in the weakened axiom hierarchy at which non-Boolean finite MV-effect algebras fail.

5
Candidate-space classification on finite MV-effect algebras

We now describe the constructive side of the picture.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{r}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{s}
. A matrix
M\in M_{s\times r}(\mathbb{N})
is called
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
-subunital
if
M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
coordinatewise. In the special case
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}
, we simply say that
M
is
\mathbf{u}
-subunital
.

Theorem 5.2

(Matrix classification of additive maps)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{r}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{s}
. For a map
L\colon E_{\mathbf{u}}\to E_{\mathbf{v}}
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

L
is additive, i.e.

x\mathrel{\perp}y\Longrightarrow L(x\oplus y)=L(x)\oplus L(y);

(ii)

there exists a unique
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
-subunital matrix
M\in M_{s\times r}(\mathbb{N})
such that

L(x)=Mx\qquad(x\in E_{\mathbf{u}}).

Equivalently, the additive maps
E_{\mathbf{u}}\to E_{\mathbf{v}}
are exactly the restrictions to
E_{\mathbf{u}}
of positive group homomorphisms
\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}^{s}
whose values on
\mathbf{u}
lie below
\mathbf{v}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
L
is additive. For
j=1,\dots,r
, put

c_{j}:=L(e_{j})\in E_{\mathbf{v}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{s},

and let
M
be the
s\times r
matrix with columns
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
. We claim that
L(x)=Mx
for every
x\in E_{\mathbf{u}}
.

Write
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\in E_{\mathbf{u}}
. Since

x=x_{1}e_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{r}e_{r}

in
E_{\mathbf{u}}
, repeated additivity gives

L(x)=L(x_{1}e_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus L(x_{r}e_{r}).

Applying additivity again to each
x_{j}e_{j}=e_{j}\oplus\cdots\oplus e_{j}
(
x_{j}
times), we obtain

L(x_{j}e_{j})=x_{j}L(e_{j})=x_{j}c_{j}.

Therefore

L(x)=x_{1}c_{1}+\cdots+x_{r}c_{r}=Mx.

In particular,

M\mathbf{u}=L(\mathbf{u})\in E_{\mathbf{v}},

so
M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
. Thus
M
is
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
-subunital. Uniqueness is immediate, since the
j
th column of
M
must equal
L(e_{j})
.

Conversely, let
M\in M_{s\times r}(\mathbb{N})
satisfy
M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
, and define
L(x)=Mx
. For
x\in E_{\mathbf{u}}
we have
0\leq x\leq\mathbf{u}
, hence

0\leq Mx\leq M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v},

so
L(x)\in E_{\mathbf{v}}
. If
x\mathrel{\perp}y
in
E_{\mathbf{u}}
, then
x+y\leq\mathbf{u}
, and therefore

L(x\oplus y)=M(x+y)=Mx+My=L(x)\oplus L(y),

because
Mx+My=M(x+y)\leq M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
. Hence
L
is additive.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

When
r=s=1
and
\mathbf{u}=(n)
, Theorem
5.2
says that additive self-maps of the chain
C_{n}\cong E_{(n)}
correspond exactly to
1\times 1
matrices
[m]
with
mn\leq n
, hence to
m\in\{0,1\}
. This is precisely the zero/identity dichotomy from the finite-chain note.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
E\cong E_{\mathbf{u}}
be a finite MV-effect algebra. A binary operation
\circ
on
E
satisfies
(S1)
and
(S2)
if and only if for each
a\in E
there exists a unique
\mathbf{u}
-subunital matrix
M_{a}\in M_{r}(\mathbb{N})
such that

a\circ x=M_{a}x\qquad(x\in E_{\mathbf{u}}),

and the top row is normalized by

M_{\mathbf{u}}=I_{r}.

In particular, the rows
a\mapsto M_{a}
are independent except for the single condition
M_{\mathbf{u}}=I_{r}
.

Proof.

Axiom (S1) says exactly that each left translation

L_{a}\colon E_{\mathbf{u}}\to E_{\mathbf{u}},\qquad L_{a}(x)=a\circ x,

is additive. By Theorem
5.2
, this is equivalent to the existence of a unique
\mathbf{u}
-subunital matrix
M_{a}
with
L_{a}(x)=M_{a}x
. Since the top element of
E_{\mathbf{u}}
is
\mathbf{u}
, axiom (S2) is exactly the requirement

\mathbf{u}\circ x=x,

which means
M_{\mathbf{u}}=I_{r}
.

Conversely, any family
(M_{a})_{a\in E_{\mathbf{u}}}
with
M_{a}\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{u}
for all
a
and
M_{\mathbf{u}}=I_{r}
defines a binary operation by
a\circ x=M_{a}x
, and Theorem
5.2
yields (S1), while
M_{\mathbf{u}}=I_{r}
gives (S2).
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

Let

\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{u}):=\{M\in M_{r}(\mathbb{N}):M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{u}\}

be the set of
\mathbf{u}
-subunital matrices. Then

\#\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{u})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\#\bigl\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}:\alpha\cdot\mathbf{u}\leq u_{i}\bigr\},

and the number of binary operations on
E_{\mathbf{u}}
satisfying
(S1)
and
(S2)
is

\#\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{u})^{\,|E_{\mathbf{u}}|-1}.

Since
|E_{\mathbf{u}}|=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(u_{i}+1)
, this exponent is explicit.

Proof.

A matrix
M\in M_{r}(\mathbb{N})
is
\mathbf{u}
-subunital exactly when each row
\alpha
of
M
satisfies
\alpha\cdot\mathbf{u}\leq u_{i}
in the corresponding row position. Hence the rows are independent, and the displayed product formula follows.

By Corollary
5.4
, every element
a\neq\mathbf{u}
may be assigned an arbitrary matrix from
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{u})
, while the row at
\mathbf{u}
is forced to be
I_{r}
. Thus the number of (S1)+(S2) operations is
\#\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{u})^{|E_{\mathbf{u}}|-1}
.
∎

The homogeneous case has a particularly simple form.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(n,\dots,n)\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{r}
, so that
E_{\mathbf{u}}\cong C_{n}^{r}
. Then the additive self-maps of
E_{\mathbf{u}}
are exactly the coordinate-picker maps

L(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})=\bigl(x_{\varphi(1)},\dots,x_{\varphi(r)}\bigr),

where
\varphi\colon\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{0,1,\dots,r\}
and
x_{0}:=0
. Consequently,

\#\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{u})=(r+1)^{r},

and the number of
(S1)
+
(S2)
operations on
C_{n}^{r}
is

\bigl((r+1)^{r}\bigr)^{(n+1)^{r}-1}.

Proof.

If
M
is
\mathbf{u}
-subunital, then each row
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
satisfies

n(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r})=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{u}\leq n,

so
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}\leq 1
. Hence every row is either the zero row or a standard basis vector. Conversely, any such matrix satisfies
M\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{u}
. Therefore additive self-maps are exactly the coordinate-picker maps described above, one independent choice for each row. There are
r+1
possibilities per row and hence
(r+1)^{r}
such maps in total.

Finally,
|C_{n}^{r}|=(n+1)^{r}
, so Corollary
5.5
gives the displayed number of (S1)+(S2) operations.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

Already for
C_{1}^{2}
, the coordinate swap
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(x_{2},x_{1})
is additive. Thus the chain dichotomy “every left translation is zero or identity” is genuinely rank one.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
\mathbf{u}=(n,\dots,n)\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{r}
, so that
E_{\mathbf{u}}\cong C_{n}^{r}
. Let
P
be a nonidentity permutation matrix. Define

\tau_{P}(a,x)=\begin{cases}0,&a=0,\\
x,&a=\mathbf{u},\\
Px,&a\notin\{0,\mathbf{u}\}.\end{cases}

Then
\tau_{P}
is an
(S1)
–
(S3)
operation on
E_{\mathbf{u}}
.

In particular, for every homogeneous power
C_{n}^{r}
with
r\geq 2
, the rank-one uniqueness phenomenon at
(S3)
fails.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.6
, the zero map, the identity map, and the permutation map
x\mapsto Px
are all additive self-maps of
E_{\mathbf{u}}
. Hence each left translation of
\tau_{P}
is additive, so (S1) holds. The row at
\mathbf{u}
is the identity, so (S2) holds.

To verify (S3), suppose that
\tau_{P}(a,x)=0
. If
a=0
, then
\tau_{P}(x,a)=\tau_{P}(x,0)=0
because every additive map sends
0
to
0
. If
a=\mathbf{u}
, then
\tau_{P}(a,x)=x=0
, so again
\tau_{P}(x,a)=\tau_{P}(0,\mathbf{u})=0
. Finally, if
a\notin\{0,\mathbf{u}\}
, then
Px=0
, and since
P
is invertible we get
x=0
; therefore
\tau_{P}(x,a)=\tau_{P}(0,a)=0
. Thus
\tau_{P}(a,x)=0
always implies
\tau_{P}(x,a)=0
, which is exactly (S3).
∎

6
Exact low-rank (S1)–(S3) classification on
B_{2}

We now bridge the gap between the general (S1)+(S2) matrix classification and the rank-one chain collapse by solving the smallest higher-rank case at the (S3) stage. This is the first place where the paper gives an exact higher-rank (S1)–(S3) theorem rather than a general obstruction or candidate-space description.

Let

B_{2}:=E_{(1,1)}=\{0,p,q,1\},\qquad p=(1,0),\ q=(0,1),\ 1=p\oplus q.

Because
B_{2}
is Boolean, an additive map
L\colon B_{2}\to B_{2}
is determined by the orthogonal pair
\bigl(L(p),L(q)\bigr)
, and conversely every orthogonal pair determines an additive map. Equivalently, by Corollary
5.6
, the additive self-maps of
B_{2}
are exactly the nine coordinate-picker maps.

Theorem 6.1

(Exact (S1)–(S3) classification on
B_{2}
)
.

A binary operation
\circ
on
B_{2}
satisfies
(S1)
,
(S2)
, and
(S3)
if and only if there exist nonzero additive maps

A,B\colon B_{2}\to B_{2}

such that

A(q)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B(p)=0

(6.1)

and

0\circ x=0,\qquad p\circ x=A(x),\qquad q\circ x=B(x),\qquad 1\circ x=x\qquad(x\in B_{2}).

(6.2)

Equivalently, if we write

u:=A(p),\ v:=A(q),\ s:=B(p),\ t:=B(q),

then
u\mathrel{\perp}v
,
s\mathrel{\perp}t
,
(u,v)\neq(0,0)
,
(s,t)\neq(0,0)
, and

v=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s=0.

There are exactly
34
such operations.

Proof.

Assume first that
\circ
satisfies (S1)–(S3). Since
1\circ 0=0
, axiom (S3) gives
0\circ 1=0
. Now

1=x\oplus x^{\prime}

for every
x\in B_{2}
, so additivity of the left translation by
0
yields

0=0\circ 1=(0\circ x)\oplus(0\circ x^{\prime}).

By positivity,
0\circ x=0
for all
x\in B_{2}
. Thus the row at
0
is the zero map.

Let

A(x):=p\circ x,\qquad B(x):=q\circ x.

By (S1), both
A
and
B
are additive. By (S2), the row at
1
is the identity map.

We claim that
A
and
B
are nonzero. If
A=0
, then in particular
p\circ 1=0
, so (S3) gives

1\circ p=p\circ 1=0,

contradicting (S2). Hence
A\neq 0
. The same argument shows
B\neq 0
.

Finally,

A(q)=p\circ q=0

if and only if

q\circ p=0,

and the latter equality is exactly
B(p)=0
. Therefore (
6.1
) holds, and (
6.2
) follows from the definitions.

Conversely, assume that
A
and
B
are nonzero additive maps satisfying (
6.1
), and define
\circ
by (
6.2
). Then (S1) holds because each left translation is additive, and (S2) holds because the row at
1
is the identity.

It remains to prove (S3). Suppose that
a\circ b=0
.

If
a=0
, then
b\circ a=b\circ 0=0
because every additive map sends
0
to
0
. If
a=1
, then
b=1\circ b=a\circ b=0
, so again
b\circ a=0
. Thus only
a\in\{p,q\}
needs attention.

If
b=0
, then
b\circ a=0
as above. If
b=1
, then
a\circ 1
is either
A(1)
or
B(1)
, and this is nonzero because
A
and
B
are nonzero additive maps on
B_{2}
. Hence the case
b=1
cannot occur under the assumption
a\circ b=0
.

If
(a,b)=(p,p)
or
(q,q)
, then
b\circ a=a\circ a=0
trivially. If
(a,b)=(p,q)
, then
A(q)=0
, so (
6.1
) gives
B(p)=0
, hence

b\circ a=q\circ p=B(p)=0.

The case
(a,b)=(q,p)
is symmetric. Therefore
a\circ b=0
always implies
b\circ a=0
, so (S3) holds.

For the counting statement, note that a nonzero additive map
A
is determined by an orthogonal pair
(u,v)=(A(p),A(q))\neq(0,0)
. There are exactly three such pairs with
v=0
, namely

(p,0),\ (q,0),\ (1,0),

and exactly five such pairs with
v\neq 0
, namely

(0,p),\ (0,q),\ (0,1),\ (p,q),\ (q,p).

Likewise, a nonzero additive map
B
is determined by an orthogonal pair
(s,t)=(B(p),B(q))\neq(0,0)
; there are three possibilities with
s=0
and five with
s\neq 0
. Condition (
6.1
) matches the two zero-patterns, so the total number of operations is

3\cdot 3+5\cdot 5=34.

∎

Remark 6.2
.

Theorem
6.1
is the first complete higher-rank (S1)–(S3) classification in the paper. It shows that the rank-one collapse to a unique operation is not merely absent in higher rank; it already fails on the smallest higher-rank finite MV-effect algebra.

7
Finite chains as the rank-one boundary case

We now return to the original chain computation and place it inside the general theory. The point of this section is not to recover a new full-sequential-product nonexistence theorem, but to identify finite chains as the rank-one boundary case of the broader finite-MV picture developed above.

For
n\geq 1
, write

C_{n}=\{0,e,2e,\dots,ne=1\}

with partial addition
(ie)\oplus(je)=(i+j)e
whenever
i+j\leq n
. Identifying
ie\leftrightarrow i
, this is exactly
E_{(n)}
.

By Remark
5.3
, the additive self-maps of
C_{n}
are exactly the zero map and the identity map. Hence Corollary
5.4
immediately recovers the first stage of the chain computation: there are exactly
2^{n}
operations on
C_{n}
satisfying (S1) and (S2).

In contrast with Theorem
6.1
, the next proposition shows that the further collapse at (S3) is special to rank one.

Proposition 7.1

(Rank-one (S1)–(S3) collapse)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, there is exactly one binary operation on
C_{n}
satisfying
(S1)
,
(S2)
, and
(S3)
. It is

\sigma_{n}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a=0,\\
b,&a\neq 0.\end{cases}

(7.1)

Proof.

Proposition
3.1
shows that
\sigma_{n}
satisfies (S1)–(S3).

Conversely, let
\circ
be an (S1)–(S3) operation on
C_{n}
. Since the additive self-maps of
C_{n}
are exactly
0
and
\mathrm{id}
, every left translation
L_{a}(x)=a\circ x
is either the zero map or the identity map.

Fix
a\neq 0
. If
L_{a}=0
, then
a\circ 1=0
. By (S3) we obtain
a\mid 1
, hence

a=1\circ a=a\circ 1=0

by (S2), a contradiction. Therefore
L_{a}=\mathrm{id}
for every nonzero
a
.

Finally, since
1\circ 0=0
, axiom (S3) gives
0\circ 1=0
. The left translation by
0
cannot therefore be the identity map, so it is the zero map. Hence
\circ=\sigma_{n}
.
∎

Combining the rank-one uniqueness with the general obstruction yields the full finite-chain picture.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
. On the finite chain
C_{n}
:

(i)

there are exactly
2^{n}
operations satisfying
(S1)
and
(S2)
;

(ii)

there is exactly one operation satisfying
(S1)
–
(S3)
, namely
\sigma_{n}
;

(iii)

if
n\geq 2
, there is no operation satisfying
(S1)
–
(S4)
;

(iv)

C_{n}
admits a sequential product if and only if
n=1
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from Corollary
5.5
in the case
\mathbf{u}=(n)
, since then
\#\mathcal{M}((n))=2
. Part (ii) is Proposition
7.1
.

If
n\geq 2
, then the atom
e\in C_{n}
has isotropic index
n
, so Theorem
4.1
gives (iii). Finally, (iv) follows from part (iii) for
n\geq 2
, while for
n=1
the Boolean algebra
\{0,1\}
carries the sequential product
a\circ b=a\wedge b
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

Corollary
7.2
recovers the complete axiom-by-axiom collapse of the original finite-chain note. What changes in the present paper is the interpretation. The chain proof is no longer the main theorem; it is the rank-one model case of a broader structure theory in which (S4) is the first fatal axiom on all finite MV-effect algebras, while Theorem
6.1
shows that the collapse at (S3) is exceptional to chains.
