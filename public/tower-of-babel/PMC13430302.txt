Zero Events, Limited Precision, and the Interpretation of Olanzapine Safety in Patients with Diabetes Receiving Cisplatin [Letter]
Authors: Shiying Shen, Zhenrong Wang
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the article by Hayashi et al evaluating short-term low-dose olanzapine in patients with cancer and diabetes mellitus (DM) receiving cisplatin-based chemotherapy.1 The authors should be commended for addressing an important clinical dilemma arising from the discrepancy between international antiemetic guidelines and Japanese regulatory labeling. Their analysis provides preliminary real-world evidence in a population commonly excluded from pivotal trials. Nevertheless, the interpretation of the zero-event outcomes, exposure classification, residual confounding, and implications for clinical practice warrant careful consideration.

No DM-related adverse events, defined as diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state, were detected within 30 days. This was observed both in the matched comparison between patients with and without DM who received cisplatin plus olanzapine (84 per group) and in the comparison between olanzapine-treated and untreated patients with DM receiving cisplatin (89 per group). Although reassuring, zero observed events do not establish safety or equivalence. The exact 95% confidence intervals had upper bounds of approximately 4.1% to 4.3% in the larger cohorts. These bounds describe uncertainty in each cohort’s event proportion and should not be interpreted as estimates of excess risk attributable to olanzapine. Nevertheless, they indicate that clinically meaningful event probabilities remain compatible with the data. As Altman and Bland emphasized, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.2

Because no events occurred in either group in either matched comparison, the data do not permit estimation of a conventional relative risk and yield a highly imprecise risk-difference estimate. The “rule of three” indicates that, after zero events among n individuals, the approximate upper 95% confidence bound for the underlying event probability is 3/n.3 With fewer than 90 patients per group, event probabilities of several percentage points therefore cannot be excluded. Substantially larger cohorts would be required to evaluate small absolute increases in these rare outcomes with adequate precision.4 The findings are thus best interpreted as showing that no severe glycemic safety signal was detected, rather than demonstrating comparative safety or noninferiority.

This distinction is relevant to the article’s conclusions. The statement that olanzapine was “not associated with detectable severe glycemic complications” appropriately reflects the observations. However, assertions that olanzapine “does not substantially exacerbate preexisting DM,” that glycemic fluctuations are “modest and manageable,” or that it does not pose a significant additional clinical concern may exceed what the study design and sample size support. A zero-event analysis cannot establish zero risk or show that risk lies below a clinically acceptable threshold without a prespecified margin and a design capable of evaluating it.

The exposure definition also warrants clarification. Olanzapine administration from day −1 through day +7 relative to cisplatin qualified as exposure, whereas outcome assessment began after the cisplatin index date. Patients who first received olanzapine after cisplatin, possibly as rescue treatment, were therefore classified in the exposed cohort from baseline. Assigning follow-up before olanzapine initiation to the exposed group may result in exposure-time misclassification and raises the possibility of immortal time bias because patients must remain alive and eligible until treatment initiation.5 The direction and magnitude of any resulting bias cannot be determined from the reported information.

The exposure window may also combine prophylactic and rescue administration, which can represent clinically different circumstances. Rescue use may be associated with severe nausea and vomiting, reduced oral intake, dehydration, or additional treatment, all of which may affect glucose and healthcare utilization. The day −1 to day +1 sensitivity analysis is helpful but still permits post-index initiation and includes only 20 patients per group. Reporting the timing of the first dose and, if feasible, restricting baseline exposure to initiation before or on the cisplatin date, separating prophylactic from rescue use, or modeling olanzapine as a time-varying exposure would strengthen the analysis.

Residual confounding is another concern. Before matching, insulin use was more frequent among olanzapine-treated than untreated patients with DM. This may reflect differences in diabetes severity, treatment intensity, or monitoring and does not establish that treated patients had lower metabolic risk. Even after matching, factors that may be incompletely captured in electronic health records, such as glycated hemoglobin, previous metabolic emergencies, glucose variability, and clinician-assessed metabolic stability, could have influenced treatment selection. Because prescribing olanzapine to patients with DM is inconsistent with current Japanese labeling, clinicians may have preferentially selected patients whom they considered metabolically stable or suitable for close monitoring. Such channeling bias may persist after matching on measured covariates.6

Almost all patients received glucocorticoids, but dexamethasone dose and duration were unavailable. Because corticosteroid exposure is an important determinant of chemotherapy-associated hyperglycemia, adjustment for glucocorticoid use as a binary variable may not account for differences in dose and timing.7 Mean glucose concentrations may also obscure extreme values relevant to acute metabolic deterioration. Future studies should assess peak glucose, change from baseline, prespecified hyperglycemic thresholds, and initiation or intensification of glucose-lowering therapy.

The authors appropriately acknowledge that the absence of events in a small cohort does not exclude clinically meaningful differences and that studies with prespecified equivalence or noninferiority margins are needed. However, the plain-language recommendation that physicians should be allowed to offer olanzapine to patients with DM appears to extend beyond what this retrospective zero-event analysis can establish. Monitoring may facilitate early detection and treatment, but it does not by itself establish treatment safety. Conversely, these limitations do not demonstrate that short-course olanzapine is unsafe or that the current Japanese contraindication is necessarily warranted. The findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence that informs, but does not by itself resolve, the clinical and regulatory uncertainty surrounding olanzapine use in patients with DM.

Hayashi et al have highlighted an important evidence gap and provided hypothesis-generating evidence that no severe glycemic signal was detected in their selected cohort. Larger prospective studies or rigorously designed multicenter observational studies are needed to define the balance between antiemetic benefit and metabolic risk in patients with DM. Such studies should verify olanzapine dose and timing, align exposure definitions with the start of follow-up, systematically assess glucose outcomes, prespecify clinically justified safety margins, and account for dexamethasone dose and duration.
