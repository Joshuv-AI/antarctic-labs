Paramagnetic chlorine functionalized nitrogenated multiwall carbon nanotubes (N-MWCNTs: Cl) in biomedical application
Authors: Tapaswini Chhotaray, Dilip Kumar Mishra, Shivani Das, Sekhar Chandra Ray, W F Pong, André M Strydom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChlorine-functionalized nitrogenated multiwall carbon nanotubes (N-MWCNTs: Cl) were synthesized, and their structural, electronic, and paramagnetic behavior was investigated to explore their potential future paramagnetic biomedical applications. The structural and electronic characteristics were analysed using X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy, and a superconducting quantum interference device magnetometer was employed to study the paramagnetic behavior of N-MWCNTs: Cl. Chlorine functionalization modifies the local structural defects and electronic structure of N-MWCNTs via charge transfer from chloride ions (Cl⁻) to the nanotube framework, thereby increasing charge-carrier density. The formation of various bonds, viz. C–Cl, N–Cl, and C–N, along with the induced sp3 hybridized carbon atoms, lead to an elongation of the neighboring C–C bond lengths, which contributes to the enhancement of the paramagnetic nature of N-MWCNTs: Cl. Introduction

In the modern era, researchers are extensively exploring carbon nanotubes (CNTs) to develop next-generation, safe, and effective nanomedical applications across various medical fields.1–5 The CNTs are promising quasi-one-dimensional nanomaterials with well-ordered, flat networks of fused benzene rings or hollow, graphitic, cylindrical tubular structures consisting of a hexagonal arrangement of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms.6–9 CNTs are mainly classified as single-, double-, triple-, and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). One of the most promising derivatives of MWCNTs is surface-engineered MWCNTs, an emerging family of carbon materials that enable numerous biomedical applications.5 Functionalization plays a pivotal role in the design of MWCNT-based nanomaterials by rendering them more biocompatible and less toxic, with increased dispersibility in comparison to pristine MWCNTs. MWCNTs are functionalized through covalent grafting of biochemical moieties onto the surface and non-covalent interactions via adsorption or wrapping of functional molecules.10–12 For example, barium sulfate-based compounds with high X-ray absorption efficiency are commonly utilized in bio-imaging13,14, while magnetic resonance imaging could be performed using paramagnetic materials such as Fe₂O₃/Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, and gadolinium (Gd)-based compounds.15,16 Among the diverse nanostructured materials, carbon-based nanomaterials like MWCNTs have gained significant attention as promising candidates for biomedical applications owing to their distinctive chemical, optical, thermal, and photothermal properties, along with favorable biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxic effects.17.

In this study, we investigated nitrogenated multiwalled carbon nanotubes (N-MWCNTs)18 functionalized with chlorine (N-MWCNTs: Cl), which can serve as paramagnetic nanomaterials for both bioimaging for disease detection (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), biosensors), and various biomedical therapy (e.g., targeted drug delivery, photothermal therapy, gene and DNA/RNA delivery) applications. In N-MWCNTs: Cl, both nitrogen (N) and chlorine (Cl) increase the solubility of the MWCNTs in various biological fluids, increasing their compatibility with the human body. Incorporating N atoms into the MWCNT lattice alters its electronic structure and magnetic properties, enhancing biocompatibility and reducing toxicity. The functionalization process changes the electronic structure within the N-MWCNTs that interacts with external and intrinsic magnetic fields, influencing the behavior of water molecules in their vicinity and altering the relaxation times of water protons in surrounding tissues. The Cl introduced to the N-MWCNT surface serves as a versatile functional group for covalent and noncovalent attachment to target molecules (e.g., peptides and antibodies), often through chemical reactions, and can modify the electronic density of the N-MWCNT surface after creating C-Cl bonds in the electronic structure. This enables N-MWCNTs: Cl to target diseased tissues, improving diagnostic accuracy and making them a promising next-generation diagnostic tool for enhancing imaging sensitivity and specificity in biomedical applications. To evaluate the use of N-MWCNTs: Cl in various biomedical applications, we have investigated the electronic structure and paramagnetic behavior of N-MWCNT and N-MWCNT: Cl materials. The electronic and microstructural properties were examined using Raman spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). The paramagnetic characteristics were analysed using magnetization versus magnetic field (M–H) measurements along with temperature-dependent field-cooled (FC) and zero-field-cooled (ZFC) magnetic studies, which were performed using a SQUID-type magnetometer.19 In addition, the crystal structure was analysed by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), whereas the surface morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images. X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy was also employed to characterize the electronic and bonding structures of the paramagnetic N-MWCNT and N-MWCNT: Cl materials.

Figure 1(f) presents the Raman spectra of synthesized N-MWCNTs and chlorine-functionalized N-MWCNTs. For N-MWCNTs, four peaks are observed at ~ 1343 cm⁻¹, ~ 1574 cm⁻¹, ~ 2665 cm⁻¹, and ~ 2917 cm⁻¹, which correspond to the D-band (disordered graphitic sp2 ring breathing band), G-band (sp2-bonded graphitic stretching band), 2D-band, and very weak (D + G)-band, respectively.22–27 The D-band originates from defects and disorder in the graphitic lattice22–27, whereas the G-band is associated with the doubly degenerate E2g vibrational mode at the Brillouin zone center, arising from the stretching vibration of C–C bonds in graphitic carbon.22 The 2D band represents the overtone of the D-band and results from a second-order two-phonon scattering process, whereas the (D + G) band arises from the combined vibrational modes of the D and G bands. Chlorination alters these peak positions and intensities by introducing sp3 hybridization. The resulting carbon lattice distortion and C–Cl bond formation shift the electronic band structure, ultimately modifying N-MWCNTs behavior. Specifically, the incorporation of a Cl atom into N-MWCNTs induces sp3 hybridization in adjacent carbon atoms. This structural shift alters bond lengths and electronic charge distribution, subsequently modifying the overall band structure and density of states (DOS). Since the Cl atom adsorbs onto the surface of N–MWCNT, a nearby C atom can undergo interaction and change in bonding character. The carbon atoms adjacent to the adsorbed Cl atom change from sp2 to sp3 hybridization, forming a pyramidal structure,26,28,] leading to slight structural changes and the formation of a covalent bond with the Cl atom. The intensity (ID/IG) ratio decreases from 1.19 for N-MWCNTs to 0.96 for N-MWCNTs: Cl, as shown in Fig. 1(d), indicating that structural modification occurs after the formation of C–Cl bonds and sp3-hybridized carbon atoms on the surface of N-MWCNTs: Cl. The decrease in the ID/IG ratio upon Cl inclusion in N-MWCNTs indicates that the Cl-atom creates sp3 after breaking the sp2 network (sp2 →sp3), indicating an increase in structural order or a reduction in the density of effective defects through Cl passivating or binding to the vacancy/nitrogen sites, stabilizing the sp2 network and removing amorphous carbon or residual metal from the surface of N-MWCNTs. Figure 3 displays the C K-edge XANES spectra of N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl (Cl/N = 0.7), with highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG) used as a reference for comparison. The inset above in Fig. 3 shows the corresponding Cl 2p core-level XPS spectra. In the C K-edge XANES spectrum of HOPG, the most intense feature appears at approximately 285.5 eV, which corresponds to the π* antibonding state arising from out-of-plane bonds in the sp2 configuration.30 In contrast, the π* resonance peaks of the N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl composite are located at ~ 286.5 eV and ~ 286.7 eV, respectively, corresponding to shifts of approximately 1.0 eV and 1.2 eV relative to that of HOPG (285.5 eV). These features correspond to the 1s → π* (e2u) transition associated with pyridine-like sp2 C–N structures.31 Pyridine is known to possess two unoccupied π* orbitals similar to benzene, namely the antibonding e2u state (with antisymmetric wave functions) and the bonding b2g state (with symmetric wave functions).31 The peak at approximately 289.4 eV in the π* region for N-MWCNTs: Cl is assigned to the 1s → π* (b2g) transition, similar to the symmetric bonding state in pyridine31 and/or contributions from C–Cl bonds. A comparable feature near 289.0 eV was reported by Unger et al. in XPS studies of Cl-functionalized MWCNTs32 and is consistent with the double-feature characteristics observed in C K-edge XANES spectra in carbon systems.33 For N-MWCNTs, minor features with shoulder peaks at approximately 287.6 eV and 289.1 eV (marked by a vertical line) are attributed to C–H bonds and interlayer/bilayer graphite states, respectively.34 In the σ* region, the main peaks for N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl are centered at 293.5 eV and 293.7 eV, respectively, similar to those reported for pyridine structures.30 Notably, upon chlorination, the intensities of the 1s → π* (e2u) (286.5/286.7 eV) and σ* (293.5/293.7 eV) transitions decrease; whereas the intensity of the 1s → π* (b2g) feature (indicated by the arrow) increases, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3. This enhancement is attributed to C–Cl bond formation, suggesting the development of a more symmetric bonding environment in pyridine-like N-MWCNTs: Cl than in N-MWCNTs. Typically, C–Cl bonding gives rise to a feature in the C 1s XPS spectrum near ~ 286.5 eV, whereas the corresponding chlorine contribution appears in the Cl 2p spectrum as spin–orbit split peaks centered at approximately 200.5–200.6 eV, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3. The exact binding energies may vary slightly depending on the local chemical environment, carbon hybridization, and neighboring functional groups. The main Cl 2p peaks are observed at ~ 198.6 eV and ~ 200.2 eV, corresponding to the spin–orbit components Cl 2p3/2 and Cl 2p1/2, respectively, with a binding energy separation (ΔE)BE of ~ 1.6 eV. The variation in spectral features and intensities with increasing chlorination further confirms that chlorine incorporation significantly modifies the atomic structure and electronic/bonding characteristics, leading to the formation of sp3-rich N-MWCNT: Cl materials. This interpretation is supported by the emergence of C–Cl bonds in the C 1s and Cl 2p XPS spectra, alongside the previous Raman spectroscopy results discussed earlier.

The magnetic field–dependent magnetization (M–H) curves and temperature-dependent field-cooled (FC) and zero-field-cooled (ZFC) magnetization measurements of the synthesized N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl are shown in Fig. 4. The M–H curves were recorded at 300 K, 40 K, and 1.8 K, and exhibited predominantly paramagnetic behavior, as shown in Fig. 4(a–d). The slight dependence of magnetization on the applied field suggests paramagnetic behavior; specifically, the observed increase at low temperatures is attributed to the alignment of atomic spins.35,36 At 10 kOe, the magnetization shows a linear dependence on the applied magnetic field, consistent with paramagnetism. However, saturation effects may appear at very high fields and low temperatures, as predicted by the Brillouin function. The saturation magnetization (MS) of N-MWCNTs: Cl varies with the Cl/N loading ratio. Specifically, increasing Cl content reduces MS, indicating that the paramagnetic response is sensitive to the incorporation of Cl and N. The measured MS values are approximately 0.096 emu/g for pristine N-MWCNTs, 0.055 emu/g for N-MWCNTs: Cl with Cl/N = 0.3, 0.050 emu/g for Cl/N = 0.5, and 0.047 emu/g for Cl/N = 0.7. In contrast, the MS of pristine N-MWCNTs remains largely temperature-independent across the temperature range 1.8–300 K. The decrease in saturation magnetization (Mₛ) due to the disturbance of the local electron spin distribution after the introduction of Cl atoms. Theoretical models suggest that single Cl adatoms can induce specific spin-up configurations that compete with the underlying magnetic moments of the carbon lattice.37 Chlorination increases sp3 hybridization at carbon sites, reducing the sp2 network and generating weakly interacting paramagnetic centers.38 Temperature-dependent ZFC and FC magnetization measurements, including paramagnetic, blocking temperature (𝑇𝐵), and magnetic irreversibility, were employed to investigate the magnetic behaviour, as presented in Fig. 4(e-h). For an ideal paramagnetic material, the ZFC and FC curves overlap completely; however, our measurements show a small bifurcation (< 5 × 10−3 emu/g), which is consistent with near-paramagnetic behavior and in agreement with the M–H data. A cusp at ~ 50 K in the ZFC curves indicates the onset of paramagnetic contributions, and the splitting of the ZFC and FC curves below ~ 50 K defines the blocking/ordering temperatures (TB). For a low Cl content (Cl/N = 0.3), the FC magnetization exceeds the ZFC magnetization at low temperatures. When Cl content is higher (Cl/N = 0.5 and 0.7), then the FC magnetization is lower than the ZFC magnetization, demonstrating the tunable nature of the magnetic response. These findings demonstrate that the paramagnetic properties of N-MWCNTs strongly depend on the formation of C–Cl, N–Cl, and C = C/C–C bonds within the structure as observed in the XPS study. Controlling structural modifications through chlorination, a strategy to tune the paramagnetic properties of N-MWCNTs, enabling their potential for various bioimaging applications. Furthermore, owing to the low toxicity of carbon-based materials, N-MWCNTs: Cl represent a viable candidate for broader biomedical applications, including therapeutic and regenerative uses.39–41 However, the presence of C-Cl and non-bonded ions (Cl−) has been restricted in medicine due to its toxic effects. To overcome these toxic effects, passing water or ethanol vapor over the CNTs after chlorination can effectively remove C-Cl and non-bonded Cl− ion from the surface of N-MWCNTs: Cl, or using H₂ gas at high temperatures can convert C-Cl species into HCl gas, which is then swept away by the gas flow.42.

To obtain deeper insight into the effect of chlorination on N-MWCNTs: Cl, the valence band density of states (DOS) near the Fermi level was investigated via ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) with He-I (hν = 21.22 eV) and He-II (hν = 40.81 eV) excitation sources, as shown in Figs. 5(a-c) and 6 (a-h), respectively. First-principles calculations attribute variations in magnetic properties to defect formation within the graphitic lattice.43 A defect created at a single carbon site in the graphitic structure can induce a shift in quasilocalized electronic states near the Fermi level, and these quasilocalized states are considered the primary contributors to itinerant magnetism in graphitic carbon.43 As observed in Fig. 5, the spectral shift of N-MWCNTs: Cl away from the Fermi level indicates a reduction in the DOS near Ef compared with that of N-MWCNTs,44 which correlates with the observed decrease in magnetization shown in Figs. 4(a–d) and 4(e–h). However, in N-MWCNTs: Cl, the apparent contradiction arises because charge transfer typically increases carrier density, which should theoretically be reflected as an increase in the Density of States (DOS) at the Fermi level. However, spectroscopic observations, as shown in Fig. 5, show a reduced DOS or a pseudogap near the Fermi level. This discrepancy is attributed to the synergy between chemical bonding and structural localization. In N-MWCNTs: Cl, the introduction of N and Cl creates defects in the carbon lattice. These defects act like speed bumps or potholes (trapping electrons in specific spots rather than letting them flow freely, i.e., structural defects trap the carriers) around the defect sites, leading to the observed drop in the DOS at the Fermi level.44,45 If those carriers are trapped in localized Cl-induced potentials, they do not participate in the delocalized π bands that lead to a decrease in the DOS,46 which is responsible for the overall observed decline in magnetization. Furthermore, the energy difference between the valence band maximum (VBM) and the Fermi level (Ef − EVB) was estimated by extrapolating the linear portion of the valence band edge to intersect the background, as illustrated in the inset of Fig. 5(c), allowing determination of the VBM positions for both N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl. The VBM positions change from 0.19 (N-MWCNTs) → 0.49 (N-MWCNTs: Cl, Cl/N = 0.3) → 0.88 (N-MWCNTs: Cl, Cl/N = 0.5) → 1.42 (N-MWCNTs: Cl, Cl/N = 0.7), as shown in Fig. 5(c). The high electronegativity of chlorine facilitates charge transfer from the C and N atoms within the N-MWCNTs to the Cl atoms. This process decreases the density of free charge carriers in the N-CNTs: Cl. From the UPS He-I spectra, we also estimated the work functions of the N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl using the procedure shown in Fig. 5(b). The work function decreases from 4.45 (N-MWCNTs)→ 4.43 (N-MWCNTs: Cl, Cl/N = 0.3) → 4.36 (N-MWCNTs: Cl, Cl/N = 0.5) → 4.38 (N-MWCNTs: Cl, Cl/N = 0.7), as shown in Table 1. Chlorine functionalization generally decreases the work function of N-MWCNTs, enhancing their electron field emission properties by increasing the charge carrier density through charge transfer and altering their electronic structure. This electronic modification leads to an upward shift in the Fermi level, which is directly related to a decrease in the work function. The observed variations in the work function and valence band maximum are closely associated with changes in the dipole moment. For example, the measured difference in the work function of approximately 0.3 eV suggests a reduction in the dipole moment, which directly affects charge redistribution within the material. This redistribution of charge can generate unpaired electrons (vacancy-related states), ultimately influencing the magnetization behavior of N-MWCNTs: Cl, as discussed earlier in Figs. 4(a-d) and 4(e-h).47 For typical pristine semiconducting CNTs, 𝐸𝐹 is near the middle of the band gap. In the case of chlorinated CNTs, the general band structure is similar. In contrast, the Fermi level (𝐸𝐹) is significantly lower in energy because of the introduction of the C–Cl bonds, which are positioned much closer to the valence band maximum (𝐸𝑉). Changes in the band structure may alter the work function, thereby influencing the magnetic properties of the CNTs.

To quantitatively evaluate the density of states (DOS) of valence electrons associated with various bonding and hybridized states, UPS measurements of the valence band (VB) region were performed using He-II radiation (hν ≈ 40.81 eV). The spectra for N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl, recorded over the energy range of 0–16 eV, are presented in Fig. 6(a–d) along with their corresponding Gaussian fitting curves. After background subtraction, each spectrum was deconvoluted into individual component peaks, as shown in Fig. 6(e–h). For N-MWCNTs, the fitted Gaussian components are attributed to C 2p–π states (~ 3.8 eV), C 2p– σ states (~ 5.1 eV), overlapping 2p (π–σ) states (~ 7.3 eV), C 2sp mixed states (~ 9.8 eV), and contributions above 13 eV corresponding to C-2s states.48,49 The peaks of the Cl-functionalized N-MWCNTs shifted toward lower energy levels and their intensities decreased significantly, indicating the insulating nature of the materials. Nevertheless, several reports attribute the peaks near 13.0 eV and 12.0 eV to σ and π bonding states associated with C = O groups and oxygen lone-pair electrons, respectively.48–52 When Cl adatoms interact with the N-MWCNT surface, they can form stable C–Cl covalent bonds or lead to the adsorption of chloride ions (Cl⁻), involving both chemisorption and physisorption processes depending on the local bonding environment and nanotube diameter. We also performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to clarify the bonding characteristics of pristine N-MWCNTs and Cl-functionalized N-MWCNTs (N-MWCNTs: Cl) using the B3LYP functional with the 6-31G(d) basis set as implemented in the Gaussian 09 software program.53 Structural models of N-MWCNTs and their most stable complexes with Cl2 were constructed, as shown in Fig. 7(a, b). In these models, hydrogen atoms were used to passivate the dangling carbon bonds, simulating the nanotube framework in both N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl in a realistic manner. The N-doped configuration yielded a chemically stable singlet ground-state structure, which was subsequently employed to investigate the interaction with Cl2 molecules in their singlet ground state. Since a singlet molecule reacting with a singlet system typically produces a singlet product, the bonding nature of the resulting N-MWCNTs–Cl₂ complexes was analysed in the singlet configuration. The optimized structure (Fig. 7b) indicates that Cl2 dissociates, with individual Cl atoms interacting weakly with the N-MWCNT surface, primarily via physisorption (van der Waals interactions) rather than strong covalent bonding. Notably, the preferred adsorption site for Cl atoms is above carbon atoms rather than directly above nitrogen-doped sites. Although the overall configuration remains a singlet with paired electron spins, the electrons associated with the adsorbed Cl atoms behave as nearly unbound in the presence of an external magnetic field. An effective transition from a singlet state to a triplet state requires a difference in the local magnetic fields experienced by the two electrons. Owing to the inherent curvature of N-MWCNTs, electrons localized on different Cl atoms are likely to experience slightly different local magnetic environments. Consequently, under an applied magnetic field, the spin vectors may precess at different rates, enabling periodic singlet–triplet transitions. This mechanism can contribute to the observed paramagnetic behavior of the N-MWCNT: Cl system. In this context, the Cl-functionalized system exhibits magnetic characteristics that make it a promising candidate for different applications. However, the DFT results suggest physisorption of Cl because Cl2 dissociation on CNT walls has a high barrier (~ 1.5–2.0 eV) and/or the presence of molecular Cl2 or Cl− ions on the surface of CNT walls; while in experimental covalent reality, the atomic Cl* radical (plasma/radical etching) species bypasses dissociation barriers, enabling direct attachment. As discussed above in Figs. 5 and 6, the DOS decreases with chlorination due to the trapping of some structural defects. Therefore, we may consider both effects, the physisorption of Cl and the formation of covalent C-Cl bonds in the N-MWCNTs: Cl lattice structure. However, the induced defect and vacancies by N are also considered for the DOS analysis. Summary and conclusion

The chlorination of N-MWCNTs serves two primary roles: it acts as a purifying agent that removes structural defects and impurities, and Cl as a chemical modifier alters the electronic structure and magnetic properties of the N-MWCNTs, primarily through the formation of C–Cl, N–Cl, and pyridine-like C–N bonds. This mechanism is governed by the strong electronegativity of Cl, which promotes C-atom substitution and reorganizes the N-bonding environments. Such modifications induce charge transfer between the CNTs and Cl atoms, thereby modulating the electronic structure and the resulting magnetic properties. The electronic structure of Cl-doped N-MWCNTs is elucidated by combining vibrational, core-level, and valence-band data to map how heteroatoms modify the carbon lattice. The ID/IG ratio decreases with chlorination, indicating that the Cl atom creates sp3 after breaking the sp2 network (sp2 →sp3), indicating increased structural order, i.e., removing the disordered phases. This confirms that Cl atoms are not adsorbed but are covalently integrated, causing corrugated wall morphologies. The appearance of C–N–Cl features from the XPS study suggests that chlorine preferentially interacts with C-sites and N-sites, forming C-Cl, pyridinic-like defects, and strengthening C–N bonding; while on chlorination, a decrease of work function and DOS with an increase of VBM, due to the trapping of some structural defects, modifies the local electron spin state distribution and competes with the underlying magnetic moments, reducing the saturation of magnetization of paramagnetic N-MWCNTs: Cl lattice. These N-MWCNTs: Cl could be useful in electronic devices, sensors, and various bioimaging applications in biomedical and biomedical engineering, therapeutics, and regenerative capacities.

Synthesis process of N-MWCNTs: Cl and experimental details

Synthesis process. The N-MWCNTs are synthesized via spray pyrolysis of a 0.01 g/ml solution containing ferrocene [Fe(C5H5)2] as an Fe-catalyst precursor, dissolved in a mixture of toluene (C7H8), as a C-source, and acetonitrile (CH3CN) as a C- and N- source.20 The solution is sprayed into a hot furnace under an inert atmosphere, namely Ar, at high temperature. This high temperature (≈ 850 °C) causes ferrocene to decompose, forming Fe-nanoparticles (NPs) that act as catalysts. These Fe-NPs catalyse the decomposition of the hydrocarbon precursors C7H8 and CH3CN into carbon atoms, which then assemble around the Fe-NPs to form concentric layers of N-MWCNTs. Furthermore, N-MWCNTs are treated with pure chlorine gas phase (Cl2) at the same temperature (i.e., 850 °C) for 30 min, 60 min, and 90 min, which can effectively remove metallic catalyst particles if present and introduce chlorine functional groups onto the N-MWCNT surface. The N-MWCNTs were purified by stirring the products in 30% HNO3 at room temperature for 30 min. The synthesized N-MWCNT and N-MWCNT: Cl were washed multiple times with acetone and distilled water. Once the filtrate reached a neutral pH, the samples were dried in an oven at 120 °C overnight.

Measurements. The surface morphology of the N-MWCNT and N-MWCNT: Cl nanocomposites was examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) with a JEOL JEM 2100 instrument and transmission electron microscopy (TWM) using a JEOL (JEM-2100), 200 keV, transmission electron microscope (TEM) with a LaB6 electron source. Structural characterization of the synthesized composites was carried out using an X-ray diffraction with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). Raman spectra were recorded on a HORIBA Scientific XploRA system using a 532 nm (∼2.41 eV) wavelength laser excitation source. The C K-edge XANES spectra of N-MWCNTs and N-MWCNTs: Cl were collected at the high-energy spherical grating monochromator beamline (20 A) at the Taiwan Light Source (TLS), Hsinchu, Taiwan. Core-level XPS measurements of C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, and Cl 2p were performed using a KRATOS-SUPRA spectrometer equipped with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV) under a base pressure of 1.2 × 10⁻⁹ Torr. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UV-PES) measurements were conducted using He-I (hν = 21.22 eV) and He-II (hν = 40.8 eV) radiation to investigate the valence band electronic structure. A high-resolution SQUID-type magnetometer was used for M-H loop measurements at 300 K, 40 K, and 1.8 K, and the temperature-dependent FC/ZFC was measured within the temperature range of 2–300 K. Details of all other measurement techniques are available elsewhere.21.
