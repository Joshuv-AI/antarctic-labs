SurvGME: an R package for survival analysis with graphical and measurement error models
Authors: Li-Pang Chen, Grace Y. Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Analyzing time-to-event data, such as cancer patient survival time, is a central task in survival analysis. Numerous modeling methods and inference strategies have been developed for various application settings, where the primary goal is to assess the relationship between survival time and covariates. However, the applicability of existing approaches is often hindered by two major challenges. First, covariates (e.g., gene expression levels) typically exhibit complex network structures. Second, they are prone to measurement error, which can substantially bias inference if ignored.Results:To address these challenges, we developed the R package SurvGME (Survival analysis with Graphical and Measurement Error models). The package provides a comprehensive framework for survival analysis in the presence of both graphical dependence structures and measurement error.Conclusions:It supports a range of commonly used survival models, and its utility and performance are illustrated using a breast cancer dataset.

Introduction

Analyzing time-to-event data arising from cancer or tumor studies is an important topic in medical research. With the increasing availability of gene expression data, survival models are commonly used to characterize the relationship between time-to-event outcomes and gene expression measurements. A main concern in survival analysis is data incompleteness due to censoring, such as right-censoring (e.g., [1–3]).

In the context of gene expression analysis, additional challenges arise. First, as noted in [4], measurement error is often unavoidable when collecting gene expression data. Second, gene expression datasets are typically high-dimensional, which likely include irrelevant variables with respect to the time-to-event outcome. Moreover, another ubiquitous feature in high-dimensional setting is the presence of sparse regulatory networks among gene expressions (e.g., [5, 6]]). Consequently, to obtain reliable inference results, it is crucial to address these issues.

In the literature, there are some related studies that may partially address the issues. For example, under the additive hazards (AH) model, [7] considered variable selection for precisely measured covariates, while Yan and Yi [8] proposed the corrected estimating equation methods to handle measurement error effects with low-dimensional data. For the Cox proportional hazards (PH) model, [9] applied the adaptive lasso method to handle variable selection. Chen and Yi [10] proposed the focus information criterion with measurement error corrected to select informative variables. Under the accelerated failure time (AFT) model, [11] derived the parametric likelihood function and applied the simulation and extrapolation (SIMEX) method to deal with measurement error. A recent paper by Chen and Qiu [12] extended the SIMEX method to deal with variable selection for error-prone covariate when the survival data suffer from biased sampling and interval-censoring. Li et al. [13] proposed the penalized marginal likelihood function with failure times and the covariates characterized by transformation models. Wang and Wang [14] studied differential measurement error and validation sampling under semiparametric transformation models. With proportional odds models, a special case of transformation models, Lu and Zhang [15] proposed the penalized marginal likelihood method to handle variable selection, and Sinha and Ma [16] explored a measurement error correction strategy.

While many methods have been developed to address variable selection, measurement error, or both, relatively few approaches are available for detecting network structures for gene expression variables. To address this gap in survival analysis, Chen and Yi [17] introduced graphical proportional hazards measurement error models and applied the SIMEX method to a penalized likelihood function, thereby enabling simultaneous mitigation for measurement error effects and detection of network structure.

From the computational perspective, several R packages have been developed to handle survival analysis with different features. For example, [18] developed the R package glmnet to deal with variable selection for the Cox PH model. [19] introduced the package SurvBoost to address high-dimensional data for the Cox model. Gorst-Rasmussen and Scheike [20] developed the R package ahaz to perform variable selection with additive hazards models. The R package aftgee, which was developed by Chiou et al. [21], provides the rank-based estimates and least-square estimates to the accelerated failure time (AFT) model. Zhou et al. [22] developed the R package TransModel for using transformation models to characterize survival data. In the presence of measurement error, He et al. [23] introduced the R package simexaft to address measurement error in covariates for the AFT model. The R package AFFECT developed by Cheng and Huang [24] aims to analyze the AFT model with nonlinear effects in covariates and measurement error in failure time. However, these R packages were mainly developed for individual survival model, and most of them are only applicable to handle measurement error or variable selection but not both. Furthermore, they cannot accommodate network structures of covariates.

To provide a unified package with various commonly used survival models accommodated, we develop a new R package, called SurvGME, to incorporate the detection of network structures and address effects of measurement error in covariates. Our package extends the strategy in [17] to a wider class of regression models, including the additive hazards model, the accelerated failure time model, and the transformation model. Moreover, our package implementation can handle complex scenarios, such as discrete covariates and parts of covariates are error-prone but the others are precisely measured. The functions in this package provide flexible arguments to handle various scenarios and derive the reliable results.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In “Notation and framework” section, we introduce the setup of survival analysis, network structure, and the measurement error model. In “Overview of methodology” section, we extend the estimation strategy, developed by [17], to handle various survival models. In “Syntax of R package” section, we introduce the R package SurvGME, with detailed descriptions of the associated functions in this package. In “Numerical illustrations” section, we demonstrate the R package with two real data applications. We conclude the article with a discussion summarized in “Concluding remarks” section.

Moreover, because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi (X_r)$$\end{document} does not involve the parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _r$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{s\nu }$$\end{document} and will be eliminated in the estimation procedure (see “Construction of estimating functions”), we leave \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi (X_r)$$\end{document} unspecified.

Consequently, to incorporate network structures among covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{X}$$\end{document}, we generalize models (1)-(4) by setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g\left( \mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\alpha }\right) $$\end{document} as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log P(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\beta },\boldsymbol{\Theta })$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\alpha } \triangleq \{\boldsymbol{\beta }, \boldsymbol{\Theta }\}$$\end{document}. This leads to graphical PH, AH, AFT, and transformation models, respectively, with the graphical PO model arising as a special case of the graphical transformation model via (10). For ease of referral, we denote these models by (1)*, (2)*, (3)*, (4)*, and (10)*, respectively.

Construction of estimating functions

Remark 2.1

Although estimating functions (12), (13), (14), and (15) are constructed using standard techniques for the corresponding survival models, our formulation differs from existing approaches in that it explicitly accommodates pairwise dependence among covariates. This added flexibility, however, introduces additional challenges for variable selection of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _r$$\end{document} and network structure identification of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{s\nu }$$\end{document}.

Overview of methodology

Measurement error models

In practice, covariates are often subject to mismeasurement. The transformation model

To implement Algorithm 1 to handle the model (4)*, we apply the function SIMEX_TM, whose syntax is:

SIMEX_TM(surv, status, X, Sigma_e, Psi, K, r, order),

where all the arguments and the output format are the same as those in SIMEX_PH, except that the unique argument r in this function is the positive constant r in (5). Since the Cox model (r=0) can be implemented by the function SIMEX_PH, we focus on r>0 in this function.

Other functions

In addition to functions for the implementation of various survival models in “The cox model”-“The transformation model” sections, here we additionally provide two functions. The first function VS_network is used to summarize the variable selection results and provide the network structure of the covariates for the visualization. The other function bootstrap employs the bootstrap method to compute the variances and p-values for the estimators of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\beta }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Theta }$$\end{document}, respectively.

Specifically, the function VS_network can be implemented as follows:

VS_network(beta,theta,labels,label.sizes,node.size)

The arguments beta and theta are the estimates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\beta }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Theta }$$\end{document} derived by functions in “The cox model”-“The transformation model” section. The argument labels records variables’ names used to present the informative variables and the dependence structure. Finally, the last two arguments label.sizes and node.size are used to display the nodes in the graphical structure, where the default values are set to 6. This function gives informative variables and the associated graphical structure of variables.

The function bootstrap can be implemented by issuing the following command

bootstrap(surv, status, X, Sigma_e, Psi, K, r, dist, shape, scale, order, B, model)

Basically, this function integrates all functions in “The cox model”-“The transformation model” section with some common arguments. The argument B denotes the number used in the bootstrapping procedure and the computation of variances (24), and the argument model represents the survival models in “Survival models with graphical structures in covariates” section, where the choices include model = "PH", "AH", "AFT", and "TM" for (1)*, (2)*, (3)*, and (4)*, respectively. Based on the bootstrapping result, the output of this function includes estimators of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\beta }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Theta }$$\end{document}, and their variances and p-values.

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we introduce a new R package, SurvGM, which enables the analysis of right-censored data with graphical structures and error-prone covariates under a variety of survival models. In addition to the Cox PH model considered in [17], we also explore several other commonly used models, including the additive hazards model, the AFT model, and the transformation model. Our package provides analysts with the flexibility to analyze complex survival data using different survival models, which allows variable selection, parameter estimation, network structure identification, and accommodation of measurement error to be carried out simultaneously. Since the objective functions in (12), (13), and (14) are likelihood- or least-squares-based functions, the coordinate descent algorithm can be used to solve the corresponding penalized problem in (18). In contrast, expression (15) under the transformation model is formulated as an estimating function for which direct application of the penalization approach in (18) is not straightforward. To address this issue, we implement a boosting algorithm that facilitate variable selection and has been shown to yield an approximate regularization path similar to that the lasso method (e.g., [34]).

Our package is developed for right-censored survival data. In future work, it is useful to extend the R package to accommodate survival data with other features, such as interval-censoring and left-truncation. In the current development, we adopt a bootstrap approach to empirically compute the standard errors of the estimators in the R package. Given the estimating functions described in “Construction of estimating functions” section, it is in principle possible to derive the asymptotic variance of the estimators under models (1)*, (2)*, (3)*, and (4)* and to implement the corresponding variance formulas for numerical computation; it is interesting to workout technical details for future work.

Another important issue concerns the specification of the classical measurement error model (17). Although this model is widely used, the true measurement error mechanism is typically unknown in applications, and it cannot be formally tested without access to validation data. As summarized in [32, Section 2.6], a variety of more complex measurement error models have been proposed. Estimation performance may deteriorate when the measurement error model is misspecified.

Similarly, the error variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\epsilon }$$\end{document} in model (17) is usually unknown and can only be estimated from additional data, such as validation data. While our package allows users to flexibly specify \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\epsilon }$$\end{document}, misspecification of this quantity may adversely affect resulting estimators. These considerations motivate future extensions of the package to incorporate the scenario with validation data, thereby enabling simultaneous model checking and parameter estimation for measurement error models. This research warrants careful studies.

Finally, several research directions merit further investigation for extending the currently developed R package to accommodate other model strategies. For example, [36] considered the restricted mean survival time (RMST) as an alternative summary for time-to-event outcomes, due to its interpretability and model-free nature. Building on this idea, it would be natural to extend our framework to incorporate network structures and measurement error in covariates when analyzing RMST, and to develop corresponding estimation procedures within the current package.
