First-principles multiscale modeling of cerebral hemodynamics enables personalized predictions of human brain temperature
Authors: Dongsuk Sung, Peter A. Kottke, Benjamin B. Risk, Jason W. Allen, Fadi Nahab, Andrei G. Fedorov, Candace C. Fleischer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Computational bioheat modeling offers a powerful approach for characterizing brain temperature when direct measurements are limited. We developed a multiscale biophysical model with conservation of mass, momentum, and energy across all spatial scales to predict human brain temperature and compared the temperature maps with a previously developed model without global momentum conservation and in vivo magnetic resonance (MR) thermometry. Subject-specific brain anatomy obtained from MR images was incorporated into bioheat equations, integrating physiological parameters such as metabolic heat and cerebral blood flow. A novel approach using “transitional” microvasculature was introduced to bridge macro- and micro-scale domains, enabling momentum conservation and accurate prediction of cerebral hemodynamics. Across 30 healthy subjects, voxel-wise mean absolute differences of 0.18–0.36 °C between modeled and experimentally measured temperatures were observed. ROI-based analysis showed significant correlations between model predictions and MR measurements of brain temperature when system-wide momentum is properly conserved, but no correlation was observed for model predictions without momentum conservation. A simulated scenario of middle cerebral artery occlusion highlighted the importance of momentum conservation to mimic realistic physiological changes. The agreement of model predicted temperatures with MR measurements support future clinical applications where brain temperature may serve as a biomarker for neurological disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56922-7.

Introduction

Despite the response of brain temperature to injury or ischemia1–3, a lack of standardized and widespread protocols for brain thermometry has limited the use of brain temperature as a predictive biomarker in routine clinical decision making. Non-invasive brain temperature measurements can be facilitated using magnetic resonance (MR)-based techniques4–6, among others,7,8 but these experimental measures alone cannot provide a full picture of the thermal response of the brain to injury due to limitations in acquisition speed, accuracy, and resolution. As a complementary method to experimental measurements of brain temperature, computational models utilizing bioheat transfer equations applied to vascularized tissue provide an opportunity to gain insight into experimentally unobservable behavior and to develop a mechanistic understanding of brain thermal dynamics that could be correlated with physiological changes for diagnosis9–12.

Developing a computational model for a biological system necessitates a meticulous integration of realistic anatomy and physiology, typically extracted from imaging, to accurately determine heat transfer to and from vascularized tissue. The construction of such models involves the formulation and implementation of algorithms derived from the conservation laws of physics and guided by known biological processes. Starting from the simplest bioheat transfer equation suggested by Pennes13, several computational models have been developed and improved upon to capture the bioheat transfer processes in vascularized tissue; however, many of the previously reported approaches modeled non-brain regions such as liver, tongue, or a portion of a vascularized tissue9,10,12,14. A brain-specific model is necessary due to the heterogeneity of the brain, thermal decoupling of the brain from the external environment by the insulating skull, and relatively high heat generation via oxygenated metabolism, all of which contribute to local temperature variations. Models with realistic brain anatomy and vasculature have been developed11,15, but with significant limitations: 1) ad hoc approach to modeling blood flow without conservation of momentum between 1-D blood vessels (i.e., arterial or venous networks modeled in 3-D but flow is restricted to 1-D laminar flow within each blood vessel) and 3-D tissue voxels, 2) approximate treatment of advective heat flow that violates local energy conservation, 3) use of non-subject-specific brain structures that fail to capture local variations in blood delivery and metabolic heat generation, and 4) lack of model assessment with in vivo MR or other experimental measurements. More recent research has addressed some of these limitations by utilizing subject-specific brain structures and comparing modeled temperatures with MR-measured temperatures acquired from healthy individuals16,17. Prediction of spatial gradients in temperature were similar to patterns observed across all subjects as well as local patterns unique to individuals in agreement with experimental thermometry; however, a key limitation is the lack of momentum conservation between 1-D blood vessels and 3-D porous tissue voxels due to the use of an ad hoc approach to prediction of blood flow in different brain domains and hemodynamic coupling across different length scales.11 Momentum conservation is crucial for accurate modeling of blood flow and flow resistance, particularly when modeling injury or ischemia where changes to blood flow resistance due to e.g., stenosis or occlusion, can substantially influence local temperature predictions.

Momentum conservation locally and across the whole brain requires determination of the inter-domain flow conductance values, relating the local and global blood flow rate to the driving pressure gradient between 1-D vascular structures and 3-D porous tissue media. Segmenting microscale vasculature at the capillary level across the whole brain remains arduous and computationally demanding due to the limited experimental data available at the required resolution and the large number of vessels. Previous attempts to consider multiscale effects for predicting blood flow in human brain were focused on hemodynamics only18–21, and these approaches have not been coupled to energy conservation for thermal energy transport and metabolic heat generation to predict brain temperature. Amare et al. introduced a simplified bioheat transfer model which incorporates the interaction between 1-D blood vessels and 3-D porous tissue; however, the target organ was the frog tongue rather than the human brain, and while the model predictions conserved momentum across 1-D and 3-D domains, the values were not validated with in vivo measurements12.

In this study, we present a subject-specific biophysical model of human brain temperature developed based on fundamental principles of heat, mass, and momentum transport along with the multiscale simulation framework to ensure full momentum conservation across brain domains via the inter-domain coupling of blood flow from arteries to tissues to veins. In addition to momentum conservation, we utilized high resolution metabolic input data to model metabolic heat generation due to oxygenated metabolism along with cerebral blood flow (CBF) maps with spatial distribution that more accurately mimics realistic physiology. The model predictions are compared to MR thermometry measurements to demonstrate the predictive power of the proposed comprehensive modeling approach.

Theory

Blood flow calculations with mass and momentum conservation

The underlying first-principles for biophysical modeling include mass, momentum, and energy conservation. For proper blood flow calculations, mass and momentum should be conserved both locally and globally. The principle of mass conservation is straightforward; within a control volume (a unit volume in an intravascular or extravascular region where the temperature is determined), in-flow to and out-flow from the control volume should be the same under steady-state conditions (i.e., hemodynamics reaches the steady-state nearly instantaneously on the time scale of microseconds). In arterial or venous domains, the blood flow vector (direction and magnitude) within a blood vessel segment is determined by the flow conductance of the vessel segment and the pressure gradient between the two nodes on either end of the vessel segment. Likewise, in the porous tissue domain, the blood flow vector is determined by the flow conductance and pressure gradient between neighboring voxels. Brain temperature calculations using bioheat transfer equations

In the steady state, the temperature for a given tissue voxel does not change across time, thus all heat generated by oxygenated metabolism is transferred to neighboring voxels or intersecting blood vessels via advective, conductive, and convective heat flows. First, advective heat flow occurs via bulk motion of fluids within blood vessels or capillary beds. Sensitivity analysis of physical and physiological parameters

Sensitivity of model-predicted brain temperatures to key physical and physiological factors was evaluated, focusing on the microvascular and thermal parameters used in Eqs. (4-6), including capillary diameter, capillary tortuosity, blood viscosity, blood density, tissue density, thermal conductivity of blood, and the specific heat capacities of blood. For each parameter, temperatures predicted using the lower and upper limit of a physiologically plausible range reported from previous literature28–30 were compared with predictions using the current value (Supplementary Table 2). Agreement between temperature predictions was quantified using Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC)31. Analysis was performed on region-averaged temperatures across 26 brain regions (13 per hemisphere), consistent with the regional comparison framework used for evaluating model-predicted and MR-measured temperatures in the previous section. Across all parameters, little variation was observed, and the agreement between original temperature predictions and those with varied input parameters was substantial to almost perfect (CCC ranged from 0.96 to > 0.99; see Methods)32 (Supplementary Table 3). These findings indicate the steady-state temperature predictions are robust to physiologically realistic variations in the examined parameters.

Discussion

Our multiscale modeling approach ensures the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy within and across domains to provide confidence in temperature predictions when provided with accurate model inputs. Momentum conservation was facilitated by a novel approach for calculating inter-domain flow conductance, bridging the macro and micro scales using the concept of “transitional” microvasculature to couple the terminal nodes of 1-D vasculature and intersecting tissue voxels for effective hemodynamic property predictions. Through the integration of individual experimental data encompassing vascular and tissue structures specific to each subject, we illustrate the feasibility of generating whole brain temperature maps that align with measurements obtained using experimental MR thermometry.

In this study, we adopt CBF as the primary subject-specific hemodynamic input, rather than cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), due to current practical limitations in noninvasively measuring CPP in human subjects. While CPP would theoretically enable a more complete pressure-based characterization of vascular function, achieving this would require vascular imaging at spatial resolutions that are not presently feasible in vivo. By fixing inlet and outlet pressure within the model and incorporating experimental CBF, we have been able to incorporate this physiologically reasonable linkage in the model using local linear momentum conservation. Importantly, inter-subject variability is preserved through the introduction of subject-specific mesoscale “transitional” vascular networks, which modulate flow and emulate the effects of underlying pressure heterogeneity. This strategy balances physiological realism with current measurement constraints and provides a scalable framework that can readily incorporate pressure-based information as imaging and measurement technologies advance.

Using full momentum conservation across all domains implemented in this study, we further evaluated our model predictions by simulating a major artery occlusion. Comparing our globally momentum conserving model with the state-of-the-art method16,17 implemented based only on local momentum conservation (only within a domain) shows global momentum conservation across whole domains in the brain is crucial to accurately predict CBF and brain temperature. By linking each terminal node from 1-D macro-scale vasculature to intersected porous tissue media possessing 3-D micro-scale vasculature via “transitional” meso-scale vasculature, only the affected downstream nodes (decreased CBF and blood pressure) and corresponding intersected tissue voxels are locally impacted by the occlusion. Hence, our model established a first-principles and multiscale framework capable of generating physiologically plausible healthy brain temperature maps as well as temperature after a perturbation such as ischemic stroke.

Model predictions showed good agreement of average temperatures in different brain regions with MR thermometry, both qualitatively and quantitatively. Spatial thermal patterns, particularly in posterior brain regions, were largely reproduced in model predictions within the spatial resolution and accuracy limits of experimental chemical shift thermometry (Fig 4, Supplementary Fig. 2, and Supplementary Fig. 3). This suggests the model predictions are robust and in close agreement to brain temperature measurements within the accuracy possible by experimental MR thermometry. In comparison with a previously reported brain thermal model without global momentum conservation16,17, the current model with global momentum conservation better captures the spatial variations in temperature observed in MR-measured temperature maps. Qualitatively, low and high temperature regions which distinguish GM and WM were better captured across multiple subjects (Fig. 4) and across axial slices in the same subject (Supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, quantitative comparisons of regional values showed temperatures resulting from the previous model without global momentum conservation were poorly predicted for those regions with higher MR-measured temperatures (e.g., parietal and temporal lobes) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The degree of agreement between model predictions and MR measurements varied across regions, ranging from good concordance in some to relatively poor concordance in others. The smallest IQR of temperature differences was observed in right cerebral WM (0.12 °C). Two potential explanations for these observations are 1) lower within-region variations of oxygenated metabolism33 and 2) relatively higher quality of MR data acquired in these regions due to minimal artifacts34. Moreover, the largest negative difference from MR measurements was observed in cerebellum (− 0.36 °C), likely due to the placement of the suppression band for echo planar spectroscopic imaging (EPSI) used for MR thermometry16,17. The largest positive difference was observed in putamen (0.44 °C), potentially due to overestimated CBF in putamen. We used publicly available pseudo continuous arterial spin labeling (pcASL) data as an initial CBF estimate for the model, but ASL has known limitations of partial volume effects in deep GM regions such as putamen35. Thus, overestimated CBF may lead to overestimated brain temperatures in the model.

An important advance in multiscale biophysical modeling of thermal behavior in the human brain presented here establishes the fundamental foundation for incorporation of additional phenomena and further assessment and refinement of critical model inputs to facilitate individualized patient-specific cases. Although brain metabolism and perfusion are inherently dynamic, thermal homeostasis in healthy tissue is tightly regulated, such that temperature fluctuations induced by localized perturbations rapidly subside via conductive and convective heat transfer. Under these conditions, a steady-state temperature model provides a physiologically appropriate approximation. A prior theoretical study showed the characteristic timescale for establishing steady-state temperature in brain tissue is on the order of tens of seconds36, suggesting transient effects are unlikely to persist long enough to substantially impact the modeled outcomes given MR thermometry using EPSI takes ~15 min.

The sensitivity analysis demonstrated the current model is robust to physiologically realistic variability in thermophysical parameters. Across all tested parameters, predicted regional brain temperatures showed little change when input values used in the model were varied within reported physiological ranges. Collectively, these results support the use of fixed, literature-based average values for thermal and physical parameters in the model, as variations across bounds from physiologically reasonable ranges do not meaningfully alter predicted temperature distributions.

The present study focuses on steady-state temperature distributions following a single perturbation and does not explicitly model transient thermal dynamics. In acute settings or in disease states associated with rapidly changing metabolic demand or perfusion, a transient formulation may be necessary and will be explored in future work. Furthermore, subject-specific measurements such as scalp thermal imaging, arterial temperature, or blood pressure used as the model boundary conditions may improve physiological accuracy, as well as incorporation of convective heat transfer with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), whose regulatory role is increasingly recognized37–39.

Despite the overall agreement in voxel-wise and regional brain temperatures, a moderate correlation was observed between region-averaged temperatures derived from the model and those measured by MR thermometry. Differences in temperatures between model predictions and MR measurements primarily stem from challenges encountered during MR thermometry acquisition in specific regions such as susceptibility near the sinus cavity and suppression band placement. Susceptibility artifacts have been identified as significant contributors to spectral quality deterioration and reduced accuracy in MR thermometry34,40,41. While manual B0 shimming and suppression band adjustments were implemented to mitigate susceptibility, enhancements in MR spectroscopy acquisition methods may overcome some of these challenges. Although the suppression band resulted in partial signal loss in the frontal lobe as described in previous reports34,42, this suppression is important for reducing spectral contamination from susceptibility artifacts or field inhomogeneity, particularly during echo planar acquisition from sinuses or other cavities. Despite the important role of the suppression band, relatively large within-region thermal variations were observed across cerebellum and temporal lobe (>0.8 °C) due to signals from these regions being partially suppressed during acquisition.

For those regions relatively less affected by the susceptibility artifacts, within-region thermal variation in MR measurements ranged from 0.41 °C (right insula) to 0.66 °C (right frontal lobe) when averaged across all subjects. We also observed the largest between-region thermal variation in MR measurements (0.68 °C) between right putamen and left cerebellum. These within- and between-region thermal variations are consistent with previous results. Zhang et al. reported SD of regional temperature in healthy humans ranges from 0.3 to 0.8 °C when the whole brain temperature was acquired using EPSI43. Furthermore, Maudsley et al. observed the maximum error of ~0.6 °C in EPSI-based MR thermometry after Monte Carlo simulation34. These prior reports strongly suggest the temperature discrepancies we report here between model predictions and MR are reasonable when using EPSI for MR thermometry. Future advances in MR thermometry with improved sensitivity and spatial fidelity will be critical for enabling more rigorous, spatially resolved validation of first-principles brain temperature models.

Lastly, several avenues for future work could further enhance the accuracy of the model. First, direct measurements of both CBF and metabolic heat generation rates at the individual subject level is an aspirational goal to achieve the most accurate predictions. Second, further sensitivity analyses of additional model parameters are needed to fully characterize the influence of physiological parameters on modeled temperatures, including values that vary substantially from average values in the healthy brain to mimic the diverse range of clinically relevant scenarios (e.g., injury, ischemia). Finally, more detailed vascular data enabled by advances in ultra-high-field MRI44, super-resolution methods45, and standardized segmentation metrics will enhance modeling of microvascular geometry and strengthen validation across both healthy and diseased populations. Together, these directions provide a clear path toward refining patient-specific brain thermal modeling for future clinical applications.

In summary, we have presented a comprehensive thermodynamic model for predicting the human brain temperature based on rigorous treatment of mass, momentum, and energy conservation, both locally and across the whole brain. Momentum conservation was facilitated by a novel approach for calculating inter-domain flow conductance bridging the macro and micro scales, using the concept of “transitional” microvasculature to couple the terminal nodes of 1-D vasculature and intersecting tissue voxels for effective hemodynamic property predictions. With full momentum conservation, our model pioneers a viable multiscale method to predict brain temperature in patients, particularly in cases where flow conductance is altered due to geometric changes in vasculature after brain injury or ischemia, using total CBF as the single model calibration parameter for individualized predictions. Exploiting the complementary strengths of model predictions and MR measurements in spatial resolution and accuracy provides a promising approach for developing brain temperature as a biomarker for prognosis and monitoring in clinical practice.

Methods

Study participants and data acquisition

This study was approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board and included 30 healthy volunteers [15 males and 15 females; mean ± SD age = 26 ± 4 years old] who provided written informed consent prior to participation. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines and regulations. We included medically healthy volunteers between 18 and 45 years old. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy, claustrophobia, metal or implants not compatible with 3 T magnetic field strength, and history of brain injury or neurological disease (e.g., ischemia, moderate to severe traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, neurodegenerative disease, central nervous system surgery, etc.). Study participants were scanned on a 3 T MR scanner (Prisma-Fit, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany), and data were acquired using a 32-channel phased array head coil (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Arterial structure was extracted from images acquired with 3-D time-of-flight (TOF) MR angiography [MRA; repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE)/flip angle (FA) = 22 ms/ 3.86 ms/ 15°, spatial resolution = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.6 mm3, generalized autocalibrating partial parallel acquisition (GRAPPA) acceleration factor = 2]. For tissue structure, T1-weighted images were acquired using a magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo sequence [TR/inversion time (TI)/TE = 2300/900/3.39 ms, FA = 9°, spatial resolution = 1.3 × 1.3 × 1.0 mm3, GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2]. Venous structure was acquired from MRV using 2-D TOF sequence (TR/TE/FA = 18 ms/3.79 ms/60°, spatial resolution = 0.9 × 0.9 × 3.0 mm3, GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2). To compare with model-predicted temperatures, whole brain MR thermometry was determined from EPSI (TR1/TR2/TE = 1551/511/17.6 ms, FA = 71°, k-space sample = 500 points with 1250 Hz spectral bandwidth, nominal resolution = 5.6 × 5.6 × 10 mm3, interpolated resolution = 4.4 × 4.4 × 5.6 mm3) with a lipid inversion nulling (TI = 198 ms) and interleaved non-water suppressed and water-suppressed (using the chemical shift selective suppression sequence with suppression bandwidth of 35 Hz). In addition to MR acquisition, axillary temperatures were recorded using fiber optic temperature sensors (OTG-MPK5, Opsens, Quebec, QC, Canada) inserted under the right and left arms at three time points during the EPSI sequence acquisition: at the beginning, 8 minutes (mid-way), and at the end of the scan. The effect of using subject-specific arterial (body) temperatures in the model was estimated using the average axillary temperature for each subject. To approximate inlet arterial temperatures, we used the previously published relationship between left (L) / right (R) axillary (ax) and PA temperatures (TPA,L = TL,ax + 0.47 °C / TPA,R = TR,ax + 0.50 °C)46. After PA temperature of each subject was calculated by averaging left and right temperatures [TPA = (TPA,L+TPA,R)/2], a global inlet arterial temperature (single value) was calculated by averaging all TPA for all subjects.

Biophysical input parameters

To solve the linear system of equations, biophysical input parameters were determined using MR data and prior literature values. From T1-weighted anatomical images, the human head was classified into five tissue domains [gray matter (GM), WM, CSF, scalp, and skull] using SPM 12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/).47 Based on the classification results, a voxel-wise tissue probability map (TPM) with voxel-wise percentages for each tissue domain was acquired. The TPM was used to define voxel-wise thermophysical parameters, including tissue density (ρ), thermal conductivity (K), and specific heat capacity (c) of tissue and blood. The domain-wise values were used as previously reported from in vivo or ex vivo experiments48–50. A publicly available ASL dataset (https://users.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/~chappell/asl_primer /data/index.html) was used to generate CBF maps54, which were then transformed to subject-specific T1-weighted image space for initial estimation of each subject’s CBF map. Specifically, the “Group ASL data” was used which included pcASL data averaged across eight healthy subjects. To provide an initial estimate of CBF for each voxel, the “Group ASL data” were realigned and linearly transformed to each subject’s T1-weighted image space using FMRIB’s Linear Registration Tool (FLIRT, FSL v6.0.3)55–57. The final resolution of the CBF map was the same as T1-weighted images (1.3×1.3×1.0 mm3). The estimated CBF was leveraged to generate mesoscale and microscale synthetic vasculature to augment structure acquired from experimental MRA or MRV.

Lastly, the boundary conditions were obtained from both from previous literature and additional temperature measurement on axillary temperature The brain was modeled in the steady state, with a thermal boundary condition of uniform scalp temperature = 33.5 °C, except at the skull base where an adiabatic boundary condition is applied. The inlet blood temperature at the right and left internal carotid arteries and basilar artery was set to 36.7 °C based on the average PA temperature (TPA) across all subjects calculated from the left and right axillary temperatures (see Methods, section Study participants and data acquisition). This is consistent with previously reported carotid artery temperature (36.6 °C)58. The inlet arterial blood pressure was set to 11510 Pa, which is the mean arterial pressure calculated from systolic (15065 Pa) and diastolic (9733 Pa) blood pressure at the basilar artery, simulated in a hemodynamic model with a detailed arterial network that quantifies regional pressure in cerebral vessels19. Outlet venous blood pressure was set to 687 Pa, which is the median value of the normal mean jugular venous pressure59.

Macro-, meso-, and micro-scale vascular models

Vasculature used in the model is generated at three scales: 1) MR-measured macroscale backbone structure, 2) mesoscale fine vessel structure based using the initial estimate of voxel-wise CBF, and 3) microvasculature connecting mesoscale vasculature and porous tissue media.

Backbone structures of arteries and veins were acquired from MRI. For arterial structures, major arteries (e.g., anterior cerebral arteries, middle cerebral arteries, etc.) and Circle of Willis were segmented from MRA images. For venous structures, major dural venous sinuses, vein of Galen, and additional venous branches were segmented from MRV images. There are five steps in vessel segmentation. First, preprocessing including N4 bias field correction60, skull stripping using AFNI61, image contrast enhancement (Y = 0.01X3), and denoising using an anisotropic diffusion filter62 were performed. Second, arterial and venous vessels were segmented using a NiftyNet63 from the preprocessed MRA and MRV images, respectively64,65. Third, the vessel segmented images were realigned and transformed to T1-weighted image space using FLIRT (FSL v6.0.3)55–57. Fourth, a python-based algorithm originally developed for 3-D neuron morphology tracing, Rivulet, was used to trace vessel nodes within segmented vessel structures66. The tracing result was saved in a swc file format which consists of geometric information of the “current” nodes, including locations in Cartesian coordinates, their diameters, and the node index of “previous” nodes. Vessel segments have two ends, “current” and “previous” nodes. When a new node (or “current” node) is generated, there is a “previous” node connected to the “current” node (except for the first node). Finally, manual removal of outlier vessel segments, assignment of three inlet arterial nodes (inlet blood flow from the heart) and two outlet venous nodes (outlet flow leaving the brain) were performed using the graphical user interface for the neuTube software67.

As MRA, MRV, and T1-weighted images have coarse in-plane spatial resolution of ~1 mm isotropic voxels, further vessel augmentation is necessary to provide fine vascular structure in the brain. Based upon the backbone structure segmented from MRA and MRV, additional vessel nodes were added using an RRT algorithm68. The RRT algorithm generates a node based on the initial CBF estimate with the assumption denser vasculature will be observed where more blood flow is supplied. The newly generated node is connected to the closest vessel node of the existing vasculature. As reported in a previous post-mortem study which investigated vascular geometry of arteries69, the maximum length of a vessel segment was set as 35.68 mm. The branches were added until the vascular density reached an a priori threshold. Vascular density was defined as the ratio between the number of voxels intersecting with vessel segments and the total number of voxels of a specific tissue type (e.g., GM, WM, CSF, etc.). We assumed most mesoscale vessels will present in GM; thus, previously reported arterial and venous densities in GM (3.6% for arteries and 7.0% for veins) were used as a priori thresholds70.

Most of the RRT-generated blood vessels do not reach the arteriolar or venular level even after reaching vascular density thresholds (3.6% for arteries and 7.0% for veins). As porous tissue media includes capillaries within tissue (diameter < 10 μm), additional branching was generated to connect 1-D mesoscale vessel structure with 3-D capillary beds. To connect the two heterogeneous structures while ensuring momentum conservation, “effective” flow conductance from the terminal vessel node to the intersecting tissue voxel should be properly calculated. Thus, we assumed 1) there is an iteratively bifurcating tree structure starting from each terminal node and 2) all terminal nodes after the last bifurcation will have the same pressure. The iterative bifurcations continue until the last bifurcated nodes have diameters less than a cut-off diameter. The cut-off diameters for arteries and veins were set to be the minimum diameter of arterioles (10 μm) and venules (20 μm), as previously reported71. The effective volume of this “transitional” branching structure was defined as all voxels intersecting with the terminal nodes of the imaginary generated “transitional” branches (dashed line in Fig. 7). Numerical implementation for blood flow and temperature calculations

The momentum-conserving flow rates can be obtained numerically by solving a linear system of equations (Ax=b) assembled by applying the law of momentum conservation between 1-D blood vessels based on Eq. (1) and between neighboring tissue voxels (with intersected terminal node) based on Eq. (3) within the discretized brain model. The coefficient matrix, A, consists of flow conductance values between each blood vessel node to a neighboring blood vessel node, each tissue voxel to a neighboring tissue voxel, and each terminal blood vessel to intersecting tissue voxels. The vector, b, is determined from the boundary conditions of the inlet and outlet pressure to the main arteries and veins, respectively. The unknown vector, x, represents pressure (P) at each vessel node or tissue voxel in the brain, which can be used to calculate the flow rates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{F}$$\end{document}) using the local conductance values, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{F}=G\Delta P$$\end{document}.

Similarly, bioheat transfer equations (Eqs. (4–6)) representing the heat transfer in these three different domains (arteries, porous tissue, and veins) can be translated into the Ax=b formulation by numerical discretization of the governing energy conservation equations. A is a matrix composed of coefficients that include the combination of thermophysical and transport properties, such as K, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{c}$$\end{document}, ρ, c and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${U}_{n}$$\end{document}; b is a known vector representing boundary conditions including inlet arterial temperature or scalp temperature; and x is an unknown vector including arterial, tissue, and venous temperatures at each discretization node, which is calculated by solving the system of linear algebraic equations Ax=b. A full derivation and more detailed numerical implementations was previously reported16.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of uncertainty in physical and physiological parameters on model-predicted brain temperature. We selected 6 key physical parameters used in the model (Eqs. (4-6)), including capillary diameter, capillary tortuosity, viscosity of blood, blood density, thermal conductivity of blood, and specific heat capacity of blood and evaluated prior ranges in healthy humans28–30. For each parameter, three simulations were conducted using the current model value used throughout (val0), the lower physiological bound (val1), and the upper physiological bound (val2) of the reported range in healthy volunteers (Supplementary Table 2). Predicted temperatures, including region-averaged temperatures across 26 predefined brain regions (13 per hemisphere) consistent with the regional analysis used for comparison with MR thermometry, were compared pairwise for val0 and val1 as well as val0 and val2. Agreement between temperature predictions was quantified using Lin’s CCC, providing a measure of both correlation and absolute agreement between regional temperature distributions31. The performance was evaluated based on McBride’s classification criteria, categorizing results as poor (CCC < 0.90), moderate (0.90 ≤ CCC ≤ 0.95), substantial (0.95 ≤ CCC ≤ 0.99), and almost perfect (CCC > 0.99)32.
