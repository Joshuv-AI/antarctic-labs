Abstract
. For a divisor representing a function and another divisor representing a differential form on a normal surface singularity, there is a notion of motivic and topological zeta function. In this paper, given a finite morphism between two normal surfaces, we prove that the set of poles of the motivic zeta function associated with the target is contained in the one associated with the source. We illustrate by examples that this inclusion is strict in general, and that on the topological level there are in general no inclusions between the sets of poles on source and target. On the other hand, when the morphism is the quotient map induced by an action of a finite abelian group on ℂ 2 \mathds{C}^{2} , and the divisor associated with the differential form on the source is trivial, we do show equality between the corresponding sets of poles, both on motivic and topological level. In addition, again for the quotient map induced by an action of a finite abelian group on ℂ 2 \mathds{C}^{2} , but now with a general divisor associated with a differential form, we provide a criterion when the topological zeta function on the target is just a multiple of the one on the source. Finally, we compare log canonical models on source and target with a view on zeta functions.

Introduction

Motivic and topological zeta functions are important invariants in singularity theory that have been widely investigated.
Originally defined for functions on smooth varieties, they now admit a more general definition, including a divisor
D

resembling a function and another divisor
W
representing a differential form, on a possibly singular variety
S
,
see e.g.
[
8
,
31
,
6
]
. More recently, we have developed a theory of zeta functions over
\mathds{Q}
-Gorenstein
varieties for
\mathds{Q}
-divisors using the so-called
\mathds{Q}
-Gorenstein measure
[
18
]
.

Topological zeta functions are defined in Section
1.2.1
; in particular one observes that they are rational functions over
\mathds{Q}
in a variable
s
, with possible poles in
\mathds{Q}
.
We will denote the topological zeta function associated to
D
and
W
on a variety (germ)
(V,o)
by
Z_{\topo,(V,o)}(D,W;s)
.

Assume now that
G
is a small finite subgroup of
\GL(n,\mathds{C})
,
and let
\pi:\mathds{C}^{n}\to\mathds{C}^{n}/G
be the quotient morphism. Consider two normal crossing
\mathds{Q}
-divisors
\overline{D}
and
\overline{W}
in
\mathds{C}^{n}/G
, and denote by
D
and
W
their corresponding pullbacks in
\mathds{C}^{n}
. Then

(1)

Z_{\topo,(\mathds{C}^{n}/G,[0])}(\overline{D},\overline{W};s)=|G|\cdot Z_{\topo,(\mathds{C}^{n},0)}(D,W;s),

see
[
18
, Corollary 3.4]
. This equality is a specialization of the motivic relation
found in
[
18
, Theorem 3]
. Motivated by this observation, we study in this work relations between motivic and topological
zeta functions under finite morphisms. In general we cannot expect an equality of type (
1
) to be true.
For a precise description of the main results we present in this paper, some notation needs to be introduced.

Let
(S,o)
be a normal algebraic surface germ. Take a nonzero effective
\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisor
D
and a
\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisor
W
on
(S,o)
.
We say that
W

has no logarithmic poles outside

D
if any component of
\supp(W)\setminus\supp(D)
has associated multiplicity different from
-1
in
W
, see Setting
1.4
. Let us denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\topo}_{(S,o)}(D,W)\subset\mathds{Q}
the set of poles of
Z_{\topo,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
.
In the motivic case the notion of pole is not straightforward, since the Grothendieck ring is not a domain
[
22
,
5
]
.
However, there exists well-defined notions of poles and their multiplicities, see Remark
1.6
for the details.
Analogously, the set of poles of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{\mot}_{(S,o)}(D,W)\subset\mathds{Q}
.
We note that, taking any embedded resolution
\pi
of
\supp(W)\cup\supp(D)
, each irreducible component of
\pi^{-1}(\supp(W)\cup\supp(D))
induces a candidate pole of both the associated topological and motivic zeta function, and that each pole is of this form, see Section
1.2
.
Also, we have that

(2)

\mathcal{P}^{\topo}_{(S,o)}(D,W)\subset\mathcal{P}^{\mot}_{(S,o)}(D,W),

since the topological zeta function is a specialization of the motivic one via the Euler characteristic
[
7
]
.
The other inclusion is not true in general as Example
4.1
shows, see also
[
1
, Example 2.10]
for a higher dimensional example based on
[
14
, Example 4.5]
.

Before discussing the technical content, we already mention two important techniques that have been employed throughout the paper. First, we amply use
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions, which are partial toric resolutions arising naturally from weighted blow-ups, see Section
1.1
. Second, the so-called
\alpha
-values appear naturally in residues of candidate poles; the corresponding
\alpha
-relations and
\alpha
-conditions have been successfully adapted to the setting of
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions, see Sections
1.3
and
4.1
.

The main aim of this manuscript is to elucidate the relations between the sets of poles of zeta functions for divisors on normal surfaces, that are related by a finite morphism. First, we can prove a very general inclusion result about poles on the level of motivic zeta functions.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\rho\colon(S,o)\to(\overline{S},\overline{o})
be a finite
morphism between normal surfaces
S
and
\overline{S}
, with associated ramification divisor
\Ram_{\rho}
on
S
. Fix two
\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisors
\overline{D}
and
\overline{W}
on
(\overline{S},\overline{o})
, with
\overline{D}
effective and nonzero, and
\overline{W}
without logarithmic poles outside
\overline{D}
. Put
D=\rho^{*}\overline{D}
and
W=\rho^{*}\overline{W}+\Ram_{\rho}
on
(S,o)

Then

\mathcal{P}^{\mot}_{(\overline{S},\overline{o})}(\overline{D},\overline{W})\subset\mathcal{P}^{\mot}_{(S,o)}(D,W).

Furthermore,

1.
Zeta functions of surface quotient singularities

This section introduces the framework for the remainder of the paper. First, we collect some basic facts about quotient singularities and
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions. In Section
1.2
, we
give the definitions of the motivic and the topological zeta functions and present some formulas in terms of
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions. After Definition
1.10
we formulate Theorem
1.11
, characterizing the poles of the motivic zeta functions in terms of
such resolutions. Finally, in Section
1.3
we use a generalized adjunction formula for Weil divisors on normal surfaces to present useful arithmetical relations between numerical data of exceptional divisors, adapted to the context of
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions.

1.1.
Preliminaries on quotient singularities and embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions

A
V
-
manifold
(also called an
orbifold
) of dimension
2
is a complex analytic surface
X
,
admitting an open covering
\{U_{i}\}_{i}
, where each
U_{i}
is analytically isomorphic to a quotient singularity,
see e.g.
[
27
]
. In our case we will be dealing with quotients
\mathds{C}^{2}/\mu_{m}
by a finite cyclic subgroup

\mu_{m}
of
\GL_{2}(\mathds{C})
with
m
elements, where
\zeta\in\mu_{m}
acts as

\zeta\cdot(x,y)=(\zeta^{a}x,\zeta^{b}y),

for some
a,b\in\mathds{Z}
. We denote this type of singularity by
X(m;a,b)
. If
\gcd(m,a,b)=1
, then the action is faithful, it is also free if
\gcd(m,a)=\gcd(m,b)=1
. If these last conditions are verified, we say that the action of
\mu_{m}
is
small
. Note that being small is equivalent to the natural covering
\mathds{C}^{2}\to X(m;a,b)
being unramified. In general, the ramification divisor of the covering is
(e_{1}-1)L_{1}+(e_{2}-1)L_{2}
, where
e_{1}=\gcd(m,b)
and
e_{2}=\gcd(m,a)
, and
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
are the divisors defined by
\left\{x=0\right\}
and
\left\{y=0\right\}
, respectively.

Some isomorphisms between quotient singularities are given below. Note, however, that the isomorphisms involved do not necessarily respect the orbifold structures, see e.g.
[
2
, Lemma 1.2]
.

(a)

X(m;a,b)=X(km;ka,kb)
for any positive integer
k
;

(b)

X(m;a,b)=X(m;ka,kb)
for any positive integer
k
satisfying
\gcd(m,k)=1
;

(c)

X(m;a,b)\cong X(m;b,a)
;

(d)

X(m;a,b)\cong X(m/e;a,b/e)
, where
e=\gcd(m,b)
.

The last two isomorphisms are given by
[(x,y)]\mapsto[(y,x)]
and
[(x^{e},y)]\mapsto[(x,y)]
, respectively. By using these isomorphisms and group operations, it is possible to restrict
X(m;a,b)
to a singularity of type
X(m;1,q)
with
1\leq q\leq m-1
and
\gcd(m,q)=1
, also known as an
A_{m,q}
-singularity
or
Hirzebruch–Jung singularity
, see e.g.
[
12
]
.
We say that a point
P
in a
V
-manifold
X

has order
m
if
X
is locally an
A_{m,q}
-singularity at
P
.

Remark 1.1
.

Several characterizations of
A_{m,q}
-singularities can be found in e.g.
[
21
, Section 2.3]
. In particular, they can be obtained as the normalization of the singular surface
X_{m,q}:=\left\{uv^{m-q}=w^{m}\right\}\subset\mathds{C}^{3}
via the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}X(m;1,q)&\longrightarrow&X_{m,q}\\
\penalty\ [(x,y)]&\longmapsto&(x^{m},y^{m},xy^{m-q}).\end{array}

Remark 1.2
.

Any quotient space
\mathds{C}^{2}/G
by a finite abelian group
G\subset\GL_{2}(\mathds{C})
is isomorphic to some
X(d;a,b)
, where
d\mid|G|
, see
[
2
, Lemmas 1.1&1.2]
. Moreover,
d
,
a
and
b
can be chosen such that
\gcd(d,a,b)=1
, and moreover also such that
\gcd(d,a)=\gcd(d,b)=1
if we make use of isomorphisms of type
[(x^{e},y)]\mapsto[(x,y)]
as in (d) above. Note that, if
\gcd(d,a,b)=1
and
e_{1}:=\gcd(d,b)
,
e_{2}:=\gcd(d,a)
, then

\begin{array}[]{ccc}X(d;a,b)&\longrightarrow&X\left(\frac{d}{e_{1}e_{2}};\frac{a}{e_{2}},\frac{b}{e_{2}}\right)\\[5.0pt]
[(x,y)]&\longmapsto&\left[\left(x^{e_{1}},y^{e_{2}}\right)\right]\end{array},

is an isomorphism.

Using quotient singularities, one can generalize the notion of embedded resolution of singularities given by a finite composition of blow-ups. Following
[
28
]
, a divisor
D
on a surface
X
has

\mathds{Q}
-normal crossing
if it is analytically isomorphic to a germ given by

x^{k_{1}}y^{k_{2}}\colon X(m;a,b)\to\mathds{C}.

It should be stressed that the quotient space
X(m;a,b)
carries a particular choice of coordinates coming from a diagonal action on
\mathds{C}^{2}
.

Definition 1.3
.

An
embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution
of a
\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisor
D
on a normal
surface germ
(S,o)
is a proper bimeromorphic map
\pi:X\to(S,o)
such that

(1)

X
is a
V
-manifold with abelian quotient singularities,

(2)

the restriction of
\pi
to
X\setminus\pi^{-1}(D)
is an isomorphism,

(3)

\pi^{-1}(D)
is a
\mathds{Q}
-normal crossing Weil divisor on
X
.

Moreover,
\pi
is called
minimal
if it does not factorize through another embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution of
(S,D)
. Since minimal usual embedded resolutions exist for surfaces, minimal embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions also exist in this context.

Recall that on a
V
-manifold with cyclic quotient singularities,
\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisors and
\mathds{Q}
-Cartier divisors coincide, see e.g.
[
9
,
2
]
.

A useful tool to find an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution is a weighted blow-up. Set
(p,q)\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 1}^{2}
and consider the weighted projective line
\mathds{P}^{1}_{(p,q)}
, i.e., the quotient of
\mathds{C}^{2}\setminus 0
by the
\mathds{C}^{*}
-action
(x,y)\sim(t^{p}x,t^{q}y)
. The
(p,q)
-
blow up
of
X(m;a,b)
at the origin is the proper birational map
\pi_{(p,q)}:X\to X(m;a,b)
, where

X=\left\{\left((x,y),[u:v]\right)\in\mathds{C}^{2}\times\mathds{P}^{1}_{(p,q)}\mid(x,y)=(t^{p}u,t^{q}v),\ t\in\mathds{C}\right\}\Big/\mu_{m},

and
\pi_{(p,q)}
maps
\left((x,y),[u:v]\right)
to
[(x,y)]
. Here the action of
\mu_{m}
is extended to
\mathds{P}^{1}_{(p,q)}
as
\zeta\cdot[u:v]:=[\zeta^{a}u:\zeta^{b}v]
.
The surface
X
is covered by two open sets
X=U\cup V
, where
U=\{u\neq 0\}

and
V=\{v\neq 0\}
. The orbifold charts are given by

(4)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\varphi:X\left(\begin{array}[]{c|cc}p&-1&q\\
pm&a&pb-qa\end{array}\right)=:X_{1}\longrightarrow U,\quad[(x,y)]\mapsto((x^{p},x^{q}y),[1:y]),

\displaystyle\psi:X\left(\begin{array}[]{c|cc}q&p&-1\\
qm&qa-pb&b\end{array}\right)=:X_{2}\longrightarrow V,\quad[(x,y)]\mapsto((xy^{p},y^{q}),[x:1]).

Here
X\left(\begin{array}[]{c|cc}m_{1}&a_{1}&b_{1}\\
m_{2}&a_{2}&b_{2}\end{array}\right)
denotes the linear action of
\mu_{m_{1}}\times\mu_{m_{2}}
on
\mathds{C}^{2}
given by

(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\cdot(x,y):=(\zeta_{1}^{a_{1}}\zeta_{2}^{b_{1}}x,\zeta_{1}^{a_{2}}\zeta_{2}^{b_{2}}y).

Note that the exceptional divisor
E_{(p,q)}=\pi_{(p,q)}^{-1}(0)
is identified with
\mathds{P}_{(p,q)}^{1}/\mu_{m}
. Also,
\mathds{P}_{(p,q)}^{1}\cong\mathds{P}^{1}\cong\mathds{P}^{1}/\mu_{m}
, so all of them are smooth varieties. For more details about this construction, see e.g.
[

]
.

1.2.

Our formulation here is inspired by

Setting 1.4
.

Let
(S,o)

\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisor

(S,o)
, and a
\mathds{Q}
-Weil divisor
W
on
(S,o)
, such that any component of
\supp(W)\setminus\supp(D)
has associated multiplicity different from
-1
in
W
. We say that
W
has no logarithmic poles outside
D
.

We set
M:=\supp(D)\cup\supp(W)
and fix a usual embedded resolution
\pi\colon X\to S
of
M
in
(S,o)
; note that this implies that both
\pi^{*}D
and
\pi^{*}W
are normal crossing
\mathds{Q}
-divisors on
X
.
Let
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be the irreducible components (exceptional components and strict transforms) of
\pi^{-1}(M)
.
It is convenient to use the partition
I=I_{e}\cup I_{s}
to distinguish the components of the exceptional divisor and those of the strict transform of
\pi^{-1}(M)
, respectively. It is also customary to denote by
E_{i}^{\circ}
, for
i\in I_{e}
, the set
E_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{j\in I,j\neq i}E_{j}
.

In the sequel, we denote the canonical divisor (class) of a normal variety by
K_{V}
. The
relative canonical divisor
of
\pi
, denoted
K_{\pi}
, is the unique representative of
K_{X}-\pi^{*}K_{S}
that is supported on its exceptional locus. Writing

(5)

K_{\pi}=\sum_{i\in I_{e}}(b_{i}-1)E_{i},

the
\left\{b_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{e}}
are called
log discrepancies
of
\pi
.
Denote further

N_{i}=\mult_{E_{i}}\pi^{\ast}D\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu_{i}=b_{i}+\mult_{E_{i}}\pi^{\ast}W.

Then we have the following relations:

(6)

\displaystyle\pi^{*}W

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}+\sum_{i\in I_{e}}(\nu_{i}-b_{i})E_{i},

\displaystyle\pi^{*}D

\displaystyle=\widehat{D}+\sum_{i\in I_{e}}N_{i}E_{i},

where
\widehat{D}
and
\widehat{W}
are the strict transform of
D
and
W
. We will call the pair
(N_{i},\nu_{i})
the
numerical data
of
E_{i}
,
i\in I
, associated with
(D,W)
. For the sake of brevity, we will say that
\pi
is a resolution of the pair
(D,W)
. Note that the numbers
\nu_{i}
are in general rational, and can be positive, negative or zero. The numbers
N_{i}
are non negative rational numbers, which are integers if
D
is a usual effective divisor.

1.2.1.
Topological zeta function

We use the symbol
\chi(\cdot)
to denote the topological Euler characteristic. For
(S,o)
and
(D,W)
as in Setting
1.4
, the
local topological zeta function
of
(D,W)
on
(S,o)
is defined by

(7)

Z_{\topo,(S,o)}(D,W;s):=\sum_{i\in I_{e}}\frac{\chi(E_{i}^{\circ})}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq I}\frac{\chi(E_{i}\cap E_{j})}{(\nu_{i}+N_{i}s)(\nu_{j}+N_{j}s)}.

A standard calculation shows that this expression is invariant under a blow-up, and hence it does not depend on the chosen embedded resolution
\pi
.
Notice that the curves
E_{i}
,
i\in I_{e}
, appearing in this definition can have arbitrary genus
g_{i}
, and in particular one has the following identity:

(8)

\chi(E_{i}^{\circ})=2-2g_{i}-\Card\left(E_{i}\cap\left(\cup_{j\neq i}E_{j}\right)\right).

It is also clear that
\chi(E_{i}\cap E_{j})=\Card(E_{i}\cap E_{j}).

1.2.2.
Motivic zeta function

For the definition of the motivic zeta function, we first introduce briefly the Grothendieck ring of varieties over
\mathds{C}
. Denote by
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})
the Grothendieck ring of algebraic varieties over
\mathds{C}
, that is, the free abelian group generated by the symbols
[V]
, where
V
is a variety, subject to the relations
[V]=[V^{\prime}]
if
V\cong V^{\prime}
, and
[V]=[V\setminus V^{\prime}]+[V^{\prime}]
if
V^{\prime}
is Zariski closed in
V
. Its ring structure is given by
[V]\cdot[V^{\prime}]:=[V\times V^{\prime}]
. It is customary to denote
\mathds{L}:=[\mathds{A}^{1}]
.

Let
(S,o)
and
(D,W)
be as in Setting
1.4
. The
local motivic zeta function
of the pair
(D,W)
on
(S,o)
is given
in terms of the resolution
\pi
by the following formula
[
26
,
32
,
20
]
:

(9)

\displaystyle Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)=\mathds{L}^{-2}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{e}}[E_{i}^{\circ}]\frac{\mathds{L}-1}{\mathds{L}^{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}-1}

\displaystyle+\mathds{L}^{-2}\sum_{\left\{i,j\right\}\subset I}[E_{i}\cap E_{j}]\frac{(\mathds{L}-1)^{2}}{(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}-1)(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{j}+N_{j}s}-1)}.

Here
\mathds{L}^{-s}
should be considered as a formal variable
T
. This convention comes from the analogy with the
p
-adic Igusa zeta function, which is a rational function in
p^{-s}

[
13
]
. Then the expression above is the traditional way to write

\mathds{L}^{-2}\sum_{i\in I_{e}}[E_{i}^{\circ}]\frac{(\mathds{L}-1)T^{N_{i}}}{\mathds{L}^{\nu_{i}}-T^{N_{i}}}\\
+\mathds{L}^{-2}\sum_{\left\{i,j\right\}\subset I}[E_{i}\cap E_{j}]\frac{(\mathds{L}-1)^{2}T^{N_{i}+N_{j}}}{(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{i}}-T^{N_{i}})(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{j}}-T^{N_{j}})}.

This last expression lives in the localization
\mathcal{A}_{T}
of a polynomial ring
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})[\mathds{L}^{-1/r}][T^{1/r}]
(for some appropriate divisible enough
r\in\mathds{Z}_{>0}
), with respect to the multiplicative system generated by the elements
\mathds{L}^{a/r}-T^{b/r}
, for
a\in\mathds{Z}
and
b\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}
with
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
; for more precise details see
[
26
, Section 4]
.

Again, classical arguments can be used to verify that the defining expression (
9
) does not depend on the chosen embedded resolution.

Remark 1.5
.

In some cases, these expressions come from an intrinsically defined converging
motivic integral
.
Namely, in the context of motivic integration, one can associate to
(D,W)
an integral over the arc space of
S
(and also more generally within an extension of our setting to arbitrary dimension), see e.g.
[
8
,
18
,
6
]
. In case this integral converges, it is a formal power series in
T
over a certain completion of
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})[\mathds{L}^{-1/r}]
, and one proves an expression (
9
) for it in terms of a resolution. But for arbitrary
W
, that integral often does not converge.

Remark 1.6
.

Since
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})
has zero divisors
[
22
,
5
]
, it is not clear a priori what would be the definition of a pole of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
. By using the localization
\mathcal{A}_{T}
referred above, given
s_{0}\in\mathds{Q}
, it is possible to define when
\mathds{L}^{-s_{0}}
is a pole of such a motivic zeta function, together with its pole order, mimicking as much as possible the obvious notions of pole and pole order for rational functions over a domain.
In particular, if
\mathds{L}^{-s_{0}}
is a candidate pole of order
1
, there is a natural notion of residue, as we will see below, and
\mathds{L}^{-s_{0}}
is a pole if and only if its residue is nonzero.
We will say in short that
s_{0}
is a pole of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
if
\mathds{L}^{-s_{0}}
is. A thorough treatment of those constructions is given in
[
26
, Section 4]
.

In practice, many of the factors
\mathds{L}^{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}-1
in the expression (
9
) cancel in the final sum.
In higher dimension it is a very difficult open problem to determine exactly the set of poles of motivic and topological zeta functions. The general philosophy behind this quest is that the poles of these zeta functions are induced by certain ‘relevant’ components
E_{i}
, appearing in every resolution.
When
W=0
, the poles of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
are completely determined by strict transforms, cycles of rational exceptional components, and exceptional components which are either non rational or of rupture type, see
[
26
, Theorem 3.4]
. A generalization to arbitrary
W
in the context of
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions will be presented in Theorem
1.11
below.

A prominent role in this type of results is played by the following numbers, which are central in the understanding of the poles for both motivic and topological zeta functions, see also
[
19
,
29
,
31
,
26
]
.
Fixing an exceptional component
E_{i}
with numerical data
(N_{i},\nu_{i})
of the embedded resolution
\pi
, the value

\alpha_{j}:=\nu_{j}-(\nu_{i}/N_{i})N_{j},

associated to the intersection with another component
E_{j}
, is called the
\alpha
-value
of
E_{j}
with respect to
E_{i}
.
In fact, suppose that
E_{i}
intersects exactly
k
times other components, say
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
, with numerical data
(N_{j},\nu_{j})
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. If

\nu_{j}/N_{j}\neq\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for each
j
, then the contribution of
{E}_{i}
to the residue of

s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for the topological zeta function (
7
) is given by

\mathcal{R}_{i}^{e}:=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\left(\chi({E}_{i}^{\circ})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{{\alpha}_{j}}\right).

Similarly, from
[
26
, Section 5.4]
, the contribution of
E_{i}
to the residue of
s_{0}
for the motivic zeta function (
9
) is

(10)

\mathcal{R}_{i}^{e}:=\frac{1-\mathds{L}}{N_{i}\mathds{L}^{-\nu_{i}/N_{i}}}\left([E_{i}^{\circ}]+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\mathds{L}-1}{\mathds{L}^{\alpha_{j}}-1}\right),

considered in a ring
\mathcal{A}
, that is the localization of
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})[\mathds{L}^{-1/r}]
with respect to the multiplicative system generated by the elements
1-\mathds{L}^{a/r}
and
b
, for
a,b\in\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

Likewise, when
E_{i}
is a component of the strict transform of
\pi
, intersecting the (unique) exceptional component
E_{j}
, its contribution to the residue of
s_{0}
is

(11)

\mathcal{R}_{i}^{s}:=\frac{1-\mathds{L}}{N_{i}\mathds{L}^{-\nu_{i}/N_{i}}}\cdot\frac{\mathds{L}-1}{\mathds{L}^{\alpha_{j}}-1}\in\mathcal{A}.

In any case, in order to determine which components of
\pi
contribute to a pole, it is useful to study relations between the
\alpha
-values and also their range.
A noteworthy property in this direction, for plane curves and when
W=0
, is the following.

Proposition 1.7

(
[
19
, Proposition II.3.1]
)
.

Assume that
\pi\colon X\to\mathds{C}^{2}
is the minimal embedded resolution of an effective divisor
D
.
Let
E
be an exceptional component of
\pi
, and take another component
E_{j}
, either of the exceptional divisor or of
\widehat{D}
, intersecting
E
. Denote by
(N,\nu)
and
(N_{j},\nu_{j})
the numerical data of
E
and
E_{j}
, respectively. Then
\alpha_{j}:=\nu_{j}-(\nu/N)N_{j}\in\penalty\ [-1,1)
.

With this result at hand, one may characterize the contributing components to the poles of, for instance, the motivic zeta function.

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
s_{0}\in\mathds{Q}
.

(1)

Then
s_{0}
is a pole of order
2
of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
if and only if there exist two intersecting components
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
with
s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}=-\nu_{j}/N_{j}
.

(2)

Let
E_{1},\dots,E_{m}
be precisely the components with
s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
, and assume that
E_{i}\cap E_{i^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for
i\neq i^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{m}
be their residue contributions to
s_{0}
, respectively, where the
\mathcal{R}_{i}
are the expressions in (
10
) or (
11
), depending on
E_{i}
being an exceptional or strict transform component.
Then
s_{0}
is a pole of order
1
of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{R}_{m}\neq 0
(in the ring
\mathcal{A}
).

The original formulation of Proposition
1.8
is given in
[
26
, Propositions 5.3 and 5.4.1]
, when
W=0
. A straightforward computation is enough to extend the original proof to the case
W\neq 0
.

Towards the understanding of the poles by means of embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions, we present in Definition
1.10
an analogue of the
\alpha
-values for singular points on surfaces. Later on, in Section
4.1
, we will see some conditions that allow us to extend Proposition
1.7
to singular quotient spaces, see Proposition
4.2
.

1.2.3.
Zeta functions in terms of
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions

We elaborated in
[
18
]
a theory of zeta functions for
\mathds{Q}
-divisors on
\mathds{Q}
-Gorenstein
varieties, using embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions.
We deduce now some consequences for surface quotient singularities. Suppose that
(S,o)\cong(X(m;a,b),[0])
, where the action is
small, and assume that
M=\supp(D)\cup\supp(W)
has
\mathds{Q}
-normal crossing.
Moreover, assume that locally at
(X(m;a,b),[0])
one can express
D
and
W
as

D\colon x^{N_{1}}y^{N_{2}}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad W\colon x^{\nu_{1}-1}y^{\nu_{2}-1}=0,

where
W
has no logarithmic poles outside
D
.
Using this notation,
[
18
, Theorem 3]
gives the following explicit formula for the motivic zeta function:

Z_{\mot,(X(m;a,b),[0])}(D,W;s)=\mathds{L}^{-2}\mathcal{S}_{m,a,b}(\bm{N},\bm{\nu})\frac{(\mathds{L}-1)^{2}}{(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{1}+N_{1}s}-1)(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{2}+N_{2}s}-1)},

with factor

\mathcal{S}_{m,a,b}(\bm{N},\bm{\nu}):=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mathds{L}^{\frac{1}{m}\big(\nu_{1}[{ia}]_{m}+\nu_{2}[{ib}]_{m}+\left(N_{1}[{ia}]_{m}+N_{2}[{ib}]_{m}\right)s\big)}.

Here
[{k}]_{m}
denotes the class of
k
modulo
m
satisfying
0\leq[{k}]_{m}\leq m-1
. Note that this factor does not induce any candidate pole, and also that the specialization map satisfies
\chi(\mathcal{S}_{m,a,b}(\bm{N},\bm{\nu}))=m
.

For
(S,o)
and
(D,W)
as in Setting
1.4
, take an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution

\pi\colon X\to(S,o)
of
M:=\supp(D)\cup\supp(W)
with associated irreducible components
\left\{E_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
. Consider the finite set of points

(12)

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}:=(\Sing X)\cup\bigcup_{\left\{i,j\right\}\subset I}(E_{i}\cap E_{j}),

and set
E_{i}^{\circ}=E_{i}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
. For each
P_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
, we have that
(X,P_{k})
is analytically isomorphic to some
(X(m_{k};a_{k},b_{k}),[0])
, where the action is small. Denote the local data for
D
and
W
at
P_{k}
by
\bm{N}_{k}=(N_{1,k},N_{2,k})
and
\bm{\nu}_{k}=(\nu_{1,k},\nu_{2,k})
.
Then
[
18
, Theorem 4]
says that
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
equals

\mathds{L}^{-2}\sum_{i\in I_{e}}[E_{i}^{\circ}]\frac{\mathds{L}-1}{\mathds{L}^{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}-1}+\mathds{L}^{-2}\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}\mathcal{S}_{m_{k},a_{k},b_{k}}(\bm{N}_{k},\bm{\nu}_{k})\frac{(\mathds{L}-1)^{2}}{(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{1,k}+N_{1,k}s}-1)(\mathds{L}^{\nu_{2,k}+N_{2,k}s}-1)}.

For the topological zeta function, one gets the simpler formula

(13)

Z_{\topo,(S,o)}(D,W;s)=\sum_{i\in I_{e}}\frac{\chi(E_{i}^{\circ})}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}+\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}}\frac{m_{k}}{(\nu_{1,k}+N_{1,k}s)(\nu_{2,k}+N_{2,k}s)}.

Furthermore, the hypothesis that the action on
P_{k}
is small is not necessary to apply the former formula for the topological zeta function.

Lemma 1.9
.

Let
\overline{D}\colon x^{N_{1}}y^{N_{2}}=0
and
\overline{W}\colon x^{\nu_{1}-1}y^{\nu_{2}-1}=0

\mathds{Q}

Z_{\topo,(\mathds{C}^{2}/G,[0])}(\overline{D},\overline{W};s)=\frac{|G|}{(\nu_{1}+N_{1}s)(\nu_{2}+N_{2}s)}.

Proof.

It is known that
(\mathds{C}^{2}/G,[0])\cong(X(m;a,b),[0])

\displaystyle=\frac{|G|}{(\nu_{1}+N_{1}s)(\nu_{2}+N_{2}s)}.

In the plane curve case, it is customary to call an exceptional component, intersecting at least three times other components of the total transform, a

. These components are very important in singularity theory, in particular they characterize the exceptional components contributing to poles of local zeta functions when
W=0

[

]
. Condition
(iv)
in Theorem

serves to justify the extension of the name to a singular ambient surface and general

.

Definition 1.12
.

Let
E
be an irreducible component of an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution

of

. If

is a rational exceptional curve containing at least three points

with

.

1.3.
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions and relations between
\alpha
-values

Let
(S,o)
and
(D,W)
be as in Setting

. Fix an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution
\pi\colon X\to S
of
M=\supp(D)\cup\supp(W)
, and let
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be the irreducible components of
\pi^{-1}(M)
with numerical data
\left\{(N_{i},\nu_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. Recall that the relative canonical divisor of
\pi
is expressed, in terms of the log discrepancies
\left\{b_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{e}}
, as
K_{\pi}=\sum_{i\in I_{e}}(b_{i}-1)E_{i}
. The next result provides arithmetic relations between the
\alpha
-values
that become crucial when studying poles of local zeta functions; it generalizes the
classical case to
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions. We give the details of the proof for completeness; below we could treat all components
E_{j}
simultaneously, resulting in a more compact proof, but we think it is useful to exhibit the differences between exceptional components and various sorts of strict transforms.

Lemma 1.13
.

Denoting by
g_{i}
the genus of the exceptional component
E_{i}
, we have that

\sum_{P\in E_{i}}(\alpha_{P}-1)=2g_{i}-2.

Alternatively, by using the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}

in (
12
), we may take

P\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
in the previous sum, since

\alpha_{P}=1
outside

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
.

Proof.

We fix an index
i\in I_{e}
. The identities (
5
) and (
6
) lead to

(16)

\displaystyle K_{X}

\displaystyle=\pi^{\ast}K_{S}-\pi^{\ast}W+\widehat{W}-\sum_{j\in I_{e}}(\nu_{j}-1)E_{j}+\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}\left(\pi^{\ast}D-\widehat{D}+\sum_{j\in I_{e}}N_{j}E_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi^{\ast}\left(K_{S}-W+\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}D\right)+\sum_{j\in I_{e}}\left(\nu_{j}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}N_{j}-1\right)E_{j}+\widehat{W}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}\widehat{D}.

and then the intersection of
K_{X}+E_{i}
with
E_{i}
is

(17)

(K_{X}+E_{i})\cdot E_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{e}\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\left(\nu_{j}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}N_{j}-1\right)E_{j}\cdot E_{i}+\widehat{W}\cdot E_{i}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}\widehat{D}\cdot E_{i}.

With the aim of developing (
17
) conveniently, we first introduce some notation.
Assume

intersects

times other components (not necessarily exceptional) of
\pi^{-1}(M)
, say
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}
. In fact, we could have
E_{j}=E_{j^{\prime}}
, for

, when

intersects several times
E_{i}
,
but we keep this convention for convenience. Also
E_{i}
may contain singular points of
X
, say
P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}
,
that do not belong to any other divisor

. This way the set
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
from (

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}(E_{j}\cap E_{i})\cup\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}\},

see Figure

. The corresponding
\alpha
-values are the ones from (

).

In order to compute the left-hand side of equation (
17
), namely the intersection
(K_{X}+E_{i})\cdot E_{i}
,
we use a generalized adjunction formula for Weil divisors on normal surfaces
that requires a correction term, called the

,

\left.(K_{X}+E_{i})\right|_{E_{i}}=K_{E_{i}}+\Diff(E_{i}),

see e.g.
[
16
, Section 2.2]
or
[

, Chapter 16]
. Recall that the degree of the canonical divisor of

is
2g_{i}-2
. Since

is a

-manifold, the pair
(X,E_{i})
is log canonical with only finitely many
cyclic quotient singularities, and the different at

is given by

\Diff(E_{i})=\sum_{P\in E_{i}}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{P}}\right)[P],

see e.g.
[

, Proposition 16.6]
.
Note that, since
m_{P}=1
except for finitely many points in
E_{i}
, the previous sum is finite.
This sum can be decomposed into

\sum_{P\in E_{i}}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{P}}\right)[P]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)[E_{j}\cap E_{i}]+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{P_{j}}}\right)[P_{j}],

where
\frac{1}{m_{P_{j}}}
is precisely the
\alpha
-value at
P_{j}
, see (
14
).
The degree of the left-hand side of (
17
) is thus

(18)

2g_{i}-2+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(1-\alpha_{P_{j}}\right).

Using the
\alpha
-values from (
14
), and the fact that the degree of

is
\frac{1}{m_{j}}
(
[
10
,
3
]
), we can compute the degree of the right-hand side of (
17
) as

(19)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\in I_{e}\end{subarray}}^{k}\frac{\nu_{j}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}N_{j}-1}{m_{j}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
E_{j}\text{ in }\widehat{W}\end{subarray}}^{k}\frac{\nu_{j}-1}{m_{j}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
E_{j}\text{ in }\widehat{D}\end{subarray}}^{k}\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}\frac{N_{j}}{m_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\in I_{e}\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(\alpha_{j}-\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
E_{j}\text{ in }\widehat{D}\cup\widehat{W}\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(\frac{\nu_{j}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}N_{j}}{m_{j}}-\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\in I_{e}\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(\alpha_{j}-\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
E_{j}\text{ in }\widehat{D}\cup\widehat{W}\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(\alpha_{j}-\frac{1}{m_{j}}\right).

Here equality
(*)
deserves some attention. Observe that

\widehat{D}\cup\widehat{W}=(\widehat{D}\setminus\widehat{W})\sqcup(\widehat{W}\setminus\widehat{D})\sqcup(\widehat{D}\cap\widehat{W})
, while
W=(\widehat{W}\setminus\widehat{D})\sqcup(\widehat{D}\cap\widehat{W})

and

D=(\widehat{D}\setminus\widehat{W})\sqcup(\widehat{D}\cap\widehat{W})
.
Then
(*)
follows by computing the contributions of the corresponding terms to each one of the previous sets
\widehat{D}\setminus\widehat{W}
,

\widehat{W}\setminus\widehat{D}
, and
\widehat{D}\cap\widehat{W}
.
Joining (
18
) and (
19
), we thus derived the following identity from for (

):

2g_{i}-2=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\alpha_{j}-1\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\alpha_{P_{j}}-1\right),

which can be more compactly written as

(20)

\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}}(\alpha_{P}-1)=2g_{i}-2.

Since
\alpha_{P}=1
outside
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
, we can rewrite equation (
20
) as
the more conceptual relation

(21)

\sum_{P\in E_{i}}(\alpha_{P}-1)=2g_{i}-2.

∎

2.
Finite morphisms of surfaces and
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions

We briefly recall some notions related to ramification.
Let
\varphi\colon V\to\overline{V}
be a finite morphism of degree
d
between normal varieties; it induces a well-defined pullback
\varphi^{*}
of Weil divisors (see e.g.
[
11
, Section 4.2]
,
[
16
, Section 2.3]
).
So in particular, for any prime divisor
\overline{D}
on
\overline{V}
, we can express
\varphi^{*}{\overline{D}}
as
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}D_{i}
, where
\varphi^{-1}{\overline{D}}=\cup_{i=1}^{m}D_{i}
and all
r_{i}\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 1}
. The number
r_{i}
is the
ramification index of
D_{i}
along
\varphi
. If
r_{i}>1
, we say that
\varphi
is ramified at
D_{i}
and that
\varphi(D_{i})
belongs to the
branch locus
of
\varphi
. If
r_{i}=1
, we say that
\varphi
is unramified at
D_{i}
.
The
ramification divisor of
\varphi
is

\Ram_{\varphi}:=\sum_{i}(r_{i}-1)D_{i},

where
i
runs over the (finitely many) divisors
D_{i}
on
V
along which
\varphi
is ramified. The
branch divisor
B_{\varphi}
of
\varphi
is the
\mathds{Q}
-divisor on
\overline{V}
whose pullback is

\Ram_{\varphi}
, that is,
B_{\varphi}=\sum_{i}(1-\frac{1}{r_{i}})\varphi(D_{i})
. We have Hurwitz’s formula:

(22)

K_{V}\sim_{\mathds{Q}}\varphi^{*}K_{\overline{V}}+\Ram_{\varphi}.

When both
V
and
\overline{V}
are smooth curves,
\Ram_{\varphi}
is supported on a finite set of points
\left\{P_{i}\right\}_{i}
. Taking degrees, and denoting by
g(\cdot)
the genus, one recovers the well-known relation between ramified coverings of curves:

2g(V)-2=d\cdot(2g(\overline{V})-2)+\sum_{i}(r_{i}-1).

2.1.
Numerical relations between compatible
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions

In order to prove relations between poles of zeta functions for finite coverings of normal surfaces, we study common
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions where the finite map restricts to

\mathds{Q}

6.
Comparison of log canonical models

An alternative approach to proving our main theorems could be via
comparing the log canonical models of both source and target. Here we state some results in this direction that are of independent interest, especially in the context of Setting
2.5
.

A log canonical model is typically associated to a pair consisting of a normal variety and a reduced divisor on it, or, more generally, to a
boundary
, which is a
\mathds{Q}
-divisor with coefficients belonging to the interval
[0,1]
. It can be considered as some ‘partial log resolution’ of the pair.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
V
be a normal variety and
B
a boundary on
V
such that the divisor
K_{V}+B
is
\mathds{Q}
-Cartier.
A log canonical model of the pair
(V,B)
is a normal variety
X
, equipped with a projective birational morphism
\pi:X\to V
, such that, denoting by
E
the reduced exceptional divisor of
\pi
,

(1)

the pair
(X,\widehat{B}+E)
is log canonical, and

(2)

the divisor
K_{X}+\widehat{B}+E
is
\pi
-ample.

Such a model exists and is unique, see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 1.32]
. In dimension
2
the condition that
K_{V}+B
is
\mathds{Q}
-Cartier is not necessary.

As in Section
2.1
, we now fix a finite morphism

between normal surfaces

and
\overline{S}

on

on

7.
An extended Residue Theorem

In this section, we present a proof of Theorem
1.11
.
The geometric determination of the poles of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
attempts to characterize the components
E_{i}
, contributing effectively to a given candidate pole; there are several results for the case
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,0;s)
, e.g.
[
29
,
31
,
26
,
24
,
25
]
. When
W\neq 0
, some extra care is required
[
32
,
20
]
. We will follow the approach of
[
26
]
to characterize geometrically the poles of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
for arbitrary
W
.

We prove this geometrical characterization in terms of an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution in two steps. First, we prove the following generalization of
[
26
, Theorem 3.4]
when
W\neq 0
for usual embedded resolutions. In a later section, we extend it for
\mathds{Q}
-resolutions.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(S,o)
and
(D,W)
be as in Setting
1.4
. Fix an embedded resolution
\pi\colon X\to(S,o)
of
M=\supp(D)\cup\supp(W)
and let
s_{0}\in\mathds{Q}
. With the notation introduced before, we have that
s_{0}
is a pole of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
if and only if

(i)

s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for some irreducible component
E_{i}
in the set
\widehat{D}\cap\widehat{W}
or in the set
\widehat{D}\setminus\widehat{W}
(in which case
\nu_{i}=1
), or

(ii)

s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for some non-rational exceptional curve
E_{i}
, or

(iii)

s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for a cycle of rational exceptional curves
E_{i}
, or

(iv)

s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for some rational exceptional curve
E_{i}
, intersecting at least three times other components
E_{j}
with associated value
\alpha_{j}=\nu_{j}-(\nu_{i}/N_{i})N_{j}\neq 1
.

7.1.
Proof of Theorem
7.1

Recall first that the numbers
\alpha_{j}
appearing in (
10
) satisfy (
20
). In particular, when
E_{i}
is rational and
k=2
(resp.
k=1
), we have that
[E_{i}^{\circ}]=\mathds{L}-1
and
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=0
(resp.
[E_{i}^{\circ}]=\mathds{L}
and
\alpha_{1}=-1
), and then clearly
\mathcal{R}^{e}=0
.
Hence, if
s_{0}\in\mathds{Q}
is a pole, it must come from one of the situations described in
(i)
,
(ii)
,
(iii)
or
(iv)
.

Now assume on the other hand that
s_{0}\in\mathds{Q}
is a candidate pole induced by one of these four situations.
Since, by Proposition
1.8
, a candidate pole of order
2
is automatically a pole of order
2
, we can assume that
s_{0}
is a candidate pole of order 1. Furthermore, note that the situation described in
(iii)
induces a candidate pole of order 2, thus we only need to deal with the other cases.

Due, for instance, to the occurrence of zero divisors in
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})
and
\mathcal{A}
, it is often not easy to show that an element in these rings is nonzero. A standard method is to use a specialization morphism to a ring without zero divisors. As in
[
26
]
, we will use Hodge polynomials to proceed this way.

The
Hodge(-Deligne) polynomial
of a quasi-projective variety
V
is

H(V):=\sum_{p,q}e^{p,q}(V)u^{p}v^{q}\in\mathds{Z}[u,v],

where
e^{p,q}(V)=\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{i}h^{p,q}(H_{c}^{i}(V,\mathds{C}))
and
h^{p,q}(H_{c}^{i}(V,\mathds{C}))
denotes the rank of the

(p,q)
-Hodge component of the
i
th cohomology group with compact support of
V
. The following explicit formulas for the Hodge polynomial are well known, see e.g.
[
26
]
. First,
H(E_{i}\cap E_{j})=\chi(E_{i}\cap E_{j})=\Card(E_{i}\cap E_{j})
. Second,

(32)

H(E_{i}^{\circ})=uv-g_{i}u-g_{i}v+1-\Card\left(E_{i}\cap\left(\cup_{j\neq i}E_{j}\right)\right),

where
g_{i}
is the genus of
E_{i}
.

The assignment
V\to H(V)
above induces a ring homomorphism
H:K_{0}(\Var_{\mathds{C}})\to\mathds{Z}[u,v]
, which further induces a ring homomorphism
H
from
\mathcal{A}_{T}
to the field of rational functions in (rational powers of)
u
and
v
over
\mathds{Q}
.
Using (
32
), the images of the expressions (
10
) and (
11
) are

(33)

R^{e}:=H(\mathcal{R}^{e})=\frac{(1-uv)}{N_{i}(uv)^{-\nu_{i}/N_{i}}}\left(uv-g_{i}u-g_{i}v+(1-k)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{uv-1}{(uv)^{\alpha_{j}}-1}\right).

and

(34)

R^{s}:=H(\mathcal{R}^{s})=\frac{(1-uv)}{N_{i}(uv)^{-\nu_{i}/N_{i}}}\cdot\frac{uv-1}{(uv)^{\alpha_{j}}-1},

respectively.

Suppose now that
s_{0}\in\mathds{Q}
is a candidate pole of order 1, and denote by
I(s_{0})
the subset of indices
i\in I
, corresponding to all curves
E_{i}
in
\pi^{-1}(M)
with numerical data
(N_{i},\nu_{i})
such that
s_{0}=-\nu_{i}/N_{i}
. We use the partition
I(s_{0})=I_{e}(s_{0})\cup I_{s}(s_{0})
to denote the components of the exceptional locus and the strict transform, respectively.
Assume that a curve
E_{i}
, for
i\in I(s_{0})
, intersects exactly
k_{i}
times other components, say
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k_{i}}
, with numerical data
\left\{(N_{j},\nu_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{i}}
. Since
s_{0}
is not a double pole,
\nu_{j}/N_{j}\neq\nu_{i}/N_{i}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k_{i}\}
.

We will show in each case that the image by
H
of the total residue of
s_{0}
is nonzero, which then implies that this total residue itself is nonzero in
\mathcal{A}
.

The contribution
H(\mathcal{R}^{e})
or
H(\mathcal{R}^{s})
of
E_{i}
to the residue of
s_{0}
is, according to (
33
) and (
34
), given by

\displaystyle R^{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-uv)}{N_{i}(uv)^{s_{0}}}\left(uv-g_{i}(u+v)+(1-k_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{i}}\frac{uv-1}{(uv)^{\alpha_{j}}-1}\right),\quad\text{if $i\in I_{e}(s_{0})$},

\displaystyle R_{i}^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-uv)}{N_{i}(uv)^{s_{0}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k_{i}}\frac{uv-1}{(uv)^{\alpha_{j}}-1}\right),\quad\text{if $i\in I_{s}(s_{0})$}.

Note that
k_{i}=1
for
R_{i}^{s}
, but we will keep this notation in order to get more compact expressions in what follows.
Now, the whole residue of
s_{0}
is given by

R=\sum_{i\in I_{e}(s_{0})}R^{e}_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{s}(s_{0})}R^{s}_{i}.

We will analyze that residue by studying the expression

\mathcal{T}=\frac{(uv)^{s_{0}}}{1-uv}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{e}(s_{0})}R^{e}_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{s}(s_{0})}R^{s}_{i}\right).

In order to deal with the different contributions, we split the previous expressions as follows. Put
k_{i}=k_{i}^{+}+k_{i}^{-}
, where
k_{i}^{+}
denotes the number of intersections with components
E_{j}
having
\alpha_{j}>0
, and
k_{i}^{-}
the number of intersections with components
E_{j}
having
\alpha_{j}<0
. Furthermore, assume that
\alpha_{j}>0
precisely for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k_{i}^{+}\}
. We also use the convention
\beta_{j}=-\alpha_{j}
when
\alpha_{j}<0
. For instance, using these notations, we may rewrite
R^{e}_{i}
as follows:

\frac{(1-uv)}{N_{i}(uv)^{s_{0}}}\left(uv-g_{i}(u+v)+(1-k_{i}^{-})+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{i}^{+}}\frac{uv-(uv)^{\alpha_{j}}}{(uv)^{\alpha_{j}}-1}+\sum_{j=k_{i}^{+}+1}^{k_{i}}\frac{(uv)^{\beta_{j}}(uv-1)}{1-(uv)^{\beta_{j}}}\right).

A similar formula is obtained for
R^{s}_{i}
by removing the expression
uv-g_{i}(u+v)
. Recall that
(k_{i}^{+},k_{i}^{-})
is either
(1,0)
or
(0,1)
for strict transforms.

We first deal with case
(ii)
. Suppose that at least one of the exceptional curves is non-rational, say the genus of
E_{i_{0}}
is
g_{i_{0}}>0
.
Note then that

\lim\limits_{u\to 0}\mathcal{T}=\sum_{i\in I_{e}(s_{0})}\frac{1-k_{i}^{-}-g_{i}v}{N_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I_{s}(s_{0})}\frac{k_{i}^{+}}{N_{i}}.

Since
g_{i_{0}}>0
and
g_{i}\geq 0
for
i\in I_{e}(s_{0})
with
i\neq i_{0}
, the latter is a linear polynomial with a negative coefficient in front of
v
.
So for this case, the residue of
s_{0}
is not zero and
s_{0}
is thus a pole of order one.

We now treat the remaining cases. If
\lim_{u\to 0}\mathcal{T}\neq 0
we are done, so we assume that
\lim_{u\to 0}\mathcal{T}=0
. In particular this implies that

(35)

0=\sum_{i\in I_{e}(s_{0})}\frac{1-k_{i}^{-}}{N_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I_{s}(s_{0})}\frac{k_{i}^{+}}{N_{i}}.

Finally, computing the following limit together with (
35
), we obtain

\lim\limits_{uv\to\infty}\frac{1}{uv}\mathcal{T}=\sum_{i\in I_{e}(s_{0})}\frac{1-k_{i}^{-}}{N_{i}}-\sum_{i\in I_{s}(s_{0})}\frac{k_{i}^{-}}{N_{i}}=-\sum_{i\in I_{s}(s_{0})}\frac{k_{i}}{N_{i}}.

Note that case
(i)
is equivalent to
\lim\limits_{uv\to\infty}\frac{1}{uv}\mathcal{T}\neq 0
.
Otherwise, we are left with the case where there are at least three exceptional rational curves contributing to
s_{0}
as candidate pole (i.e., case
(iv)
), but no strict transform is contributing.
The proof in this case is already provided in
[
26
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Remark 7.2
.

Let
(S,o)
and
(D,W)
be as in Setting
1.4
. As an ‘intermediate’ between the motivic and topological zeta function, the
Hodge zeta function
associated to the pair
(D,W)
on

is given by

{Z_{\Hodge}}_{,(S,o)}(D,W;s):=\sum_{i\in I_{e}}H(E_{i}^{\circ})\frac{uv-1}{(uv)^{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}-1}\\

The arguments above yield the complete analogue of Theorem
7.1
, generalizing also
[
26
, Theorem 3.4]
to arbitrary
W
.

7.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.11

It suffices to prove that the geometric situation described in case
(iv)
of Theorem
1.11
is equivalent to case
(iv)
of Theorem
7.1
, once we transform an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution into a usual embedded resolution by resolving the remaining quotient singularities.

Assume that
\pi\colon X\to(S,o)
is an embedded
\mathds{Q}
-resolution of
M
. We can produce an actual embedded resolution
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to(S,o)
of
M
by resolving the finite set of quotient singular points by a finite sequence of blow-ups
h\colon X^{\prime}\to\cdots\to X
. For each
P\in\Sing X
,
h^{-1}(P)
is a chain of finitely many rational curves
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}
, where
r>0
depends on
P
.
Thus, cases (i), (ii) and (iii) of Theorem
1.11
for
\pi
lift automatically into cases
(i)
,
(ii)
and
(iii)
of Theorem
7.1
for
\pi^{\prime}
, respectively.

Now let
E_{i}
be a rational exceptional component with numerical data
(N_{i},\nu_{i})
, and fix a point
P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
.
Denote by
\widetilde{E}_{i}
the strict transform of
E_{i}
by
h
.
Recall that
P_{j}
is either a (possibly singular) intersection point with another irreducible component, say
E_{j}
, or it belongs to
\Sing X\cap\left(E_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{\ell\in I,\ell\neq i}E_{\ell}\right)
.
Assume that
P_{j}\in E_{i}\cap\Sing X
with
\alpha
-value
\alpha_{j}=\frac{\nu_{j}-(\nu_{i}/N_{i})N_{j}}{m_{j}}
with respect to
E_{i}
, where
(N_{j},\nu_{j})
are the numerical data of
E_{j}
if
P_{j}
is the intersection point between
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
, and
(N_{j},\nu_{j})=(0,1)
otherwise. Let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}
be the chain of rational exceptional curves in
X^{\prime}
, associated to
P_{j}
, such that
F_{r}
intersects
\widetilde{E}_{i}
transversely.
The numerical data
(N_{r},\nu_{r})
of
F_{r}
can be computed using
[
30
, Lemma 2.4]
, namely

\nu_{r}+sN_{r}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{1,r}}\big((\nu_{j}+sN_{j})+\Delta_{1,r-1}(\nu_{i}+sN_{i})\big),

where
\Delta_{k,\ell}
is the absolute value of the determinant of the intersection matrix
\left(-F_{t}\cdot F_{s}\right)_{t,s=k}^{\ell}
. It is well known known that
\Delta_{1,r}
equals the order
m_{j}
of
P_{j}
in
X
.
Computing the
\alpha
-value of
F_{r}
with respect to
\widetilde{E}_{i}
, we thus have that

\alpha_{r}=\nu_{r}-\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}N_{r}=\frac{\nu_{j}-(\nu_{i}/N_{i})N_{j}}{m_{j}}=\alpha_{j}.

Then the condition
\alpha_{j}\neq 1
for
P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
in
X
is equivalent to
\alpha_{r}\neq 1
for the intersection point
\widetilde{E}_{i}\cap F_{r}
in
X^{\prime}
.
Since points
P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\cap E_{i}
are in bijection with points on
\widetilde{E}_{i}
that intersect other components, this proves the equivalence between Theorem
1.11
(iv)
for
\pi
and Theorem
7.1
(iv)
for
\pi^{\prime}
.

Finally, since the exceptional sets of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
only differ by finite disjoint unions of chains of rational components, then, by Theorem
7.1
applied to
\pi^{\prime}
, we have covered an exhaustive list of cases in Theorem
1.11
where a true pole of
Z_{\mot,(S,o)}(D,W;s)
is provided.
