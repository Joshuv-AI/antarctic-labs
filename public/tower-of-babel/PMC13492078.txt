Health system responses to homelessness in the United States: prevalence and predictors
Authors: Alisa Dewald, Naquia Unwala, Timothy B Creedon, Phillip M Singer, Charley E Willison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction2024 marked the greatest number of Americans experiencing homelessness ever recorded. Health systems play a pivotal role in caring for unhoused populations, and reducing homelessness decreases healthcare costs and improves health outcomes. As homelessness increases, health systems face mounting pressure to address the social determinants of health, yet little is known about their responses to homelessness.MethodsIn 2024, we collected primary data from the 200 largest health systems websites to measure the prevalence of health system homeless mitigation programs and systems-level factors predicting program presence.ResultsOver half (56%) of the 200 largest health systems had a homeless mitigation program. The most common program types included affordable and permanent supportive housing, homeless outreach, and shelter medicine. Health systems located in Medicaid expansion states had more than twice the adjusted odds of having a homelessness mitigation program compared with those in non-expansion states (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.31; 95% CI, 1.11-4.79). Health systems in states with greater shelter capacity were more likely to have a homeless mitigation program, with each additional shelter bed per 10 people experiencing homelessness associated with about 12% higher adjusted odds (aOR: 1.12; 95% CI, 1.01-1.24).ConclusionAs federal spending for safety net programs—including homelessness, housing, and Medicaid—precipitously declines, health systems' homeless mitigation programs may increasingly fill critical policy gaps.

Introduction

In 2024, the United States experienced its highest level of homelessness on record, with an estimated 771 480 people experiencing homelessness on any given night.1 Housing is a crucial social determinant of health (SDOH): unhoused individuals experience higher rates of chronic physical and mental health conditions, with an all-cause mortality rate of up to 10 times that of the general population.2-4 Given their higher risk of complex medical needs and decreased access to services, unhoused patients present more frequently to the emergency department (ED) for both primary care services and temporary shelter.5,6 These patients, termed “frequent utilizers,” often cycle through health systems, straining system resources and perpetuating cycles of homelessness. Medicaid is the primary insurance system for people who are unhoused, as well as for frequent utilizers.7 While frequent utilizers make up only 1%-5% of the total patient population, their visits to the ED constitute 12%-18% of all annual visits, with 3.8 times higher total expenditures compared to other Medicaid recipients.8,9

Health systems play a pivotal role in caring for unhoused populations, as evidence demonstrates that pairing permanent supportive housing (PSH) with wraparound medical and case management services leads to diminished healthcare costs and improved health outcomes for people experiencing homelessness.10 Housing is one of the core SDOHs. The US Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) recognizes SDOHs as the social, environmental, and economic factors that contribute to health inequalities.11 Subsequent federal legislation has targeted the role of health systems in addressing the SDOH. Starting in 2010, the Affordable Care Act (ACA) imposed “community benefit” requirements for tax-exempt nonprofit hospital systems to maintain their 501(c)(3) status.12 This required hospital systems to conduct a community needs assessment and associated implementation strategy every 3 years, create formal policies regarding financial assistance for emergency or medically necessary care, and comply with specific billing and collection policies.12

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid (CMS) also increasingly recognized the need for health system involvement in addressing SDOH. In 2013, CMS released state-level guidance aimed at reducing the disproportionate share spending of frequent utilizers through care coordination models that included housing and social services.13 Subsequently, CMS began to directly encourage states to use Medicaid 1115 Waivers to target the SDOH, including housing-related services for people experiencing chronic homelessness.14,15 By 2022, CMS developed the “health related social needs” framework, explicitly encouraging states to pay for direct housing costs through the 1115 Waiver program.15 CMS also added requirements for hospital systems to screen for the SDOH as a part of the Hospital Inpatient Quality Reporting program.16

Health systems appear to be heeding these requirements. Since 2017, nonprofit hospitals have increased community benefit spending by over 50%, representing on average 15.1% of total expenses.17 However, little is known about how exactly these investments target the SDOH. Only a fraction of this funding is allocated for broader community health improvement projects, while nearly half (with some estimates of up to 85%) goes toward financial assistance for uninsured patients including nonreimbursed costs from Medicaid and other government programs.17,18 To our knowledge, only one prior study has examined health system involvement in addressing the SDOH at the health system level. That study identified 57 health systems (across 917 hospitals) that announced investments in SDOH-focused projects and found that these health systems were more likely to be larger academic centers participating in an accountable care organization.19 Of these programs targeting the SDOH, over 66% focused on housing-related issues.19

Early evidence suggests that health system investment in housing resources contributes to decreased ED visits, inpatient hospital admissions, and an overall reduction in costs for hospitals.20-22 One study found that the Bon Secours hospital earned a social-return-on-investment of $1.30-$1.92 for every dollar spent on supportive housing, contributing to broader positive impact on the community, environment, and economy.23 While these studies support the crucial role of hospitals in addressing housing, the degree of the health systems' involvement in homeless mitigation efforts specifically remains unknown. Given the high risk of adverse health consequences and high costs to health systems, health systems may be more likely to invest in homeless response programs, in particular. In this paper, we evaluate the extent to which health systems are involved in responses to homelessness and the system-level factors that predict their involvement.

Methods

Health systems data and analyses

We constructed a novel dataset on homelessness mitigation programs within the largest health systems in the United States. We selected the top 200 health systems with the highest count of hospital beds from the 2023 Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Compendium of U.S. Health Systems (2023). (The entire AHRQ compendium can be accessed at https://www.ahrq.gov/chsp/data-resources/compendium-2023.html) The primary outcome variable is the presence of a homeless mitigation program at the health system level. As some health systems operate in multiple states, we used the primary site-location listed in the compendium (city/state) to attribute each health system to a single state. We analytically validated this choice, as all homelessness response programs were located in the primary site state, except for 2 cases where there was an additional location in another state. This selection strategy may therefore be an undercount of health system homeless programs in some cases and should be considered a baseline estimate. To collect health system responses to homelessness, we reviewed health system websites for primary documents related to homeless mitigation programming (data collected in 2024 and validated in 2025). For a few cases, news articles linking back to health system sources were included. This methodology is consistent with that used by Horwitz et al.,19 in which data on health systems’ investments in the SDOH was collected using newspaper articles and press releases published via LexisNexis and Google. We defined health system homelessness mitigation programs as a program targeting homelessness (programs referencing other SDOH but not directly referencing homelessness, housing insecurity, or unhoused were excluded) that existed at the time of data collection; designated a clear responsibility for the health system; and was formalized (we excluded voluntary programs that did not have formal institutional commitment). We qualitatively coded and categorized each homelessness mitigation program by the objective of the program (see Table S1 for program examples). For example, “housing” programs included any program with a focus on the provision of housing to address homelessness or housing insecurity. Two coders applied the same search strategies to collect the primary data, with iterative cross-validation.

To evaluate which systems-level factors were predictive of a health system having a homeless mitigation program, we selected measures of health system resources at the health system level (teaching hospital status; primary care physician density [this logged proportion of primary care physicians per bed gives us a measure of primary care physician density controlling for system size; for sensitivity, we ran the model with the raw count of primary care physicians; model results are similar but with different results for health system revenue and primary care physicians; see Table S3]; total revenue [the original model included bed count; however, bed count was highly collinear; given this, we dropped bed count and retained total revenue]; and nonprofit status) and health system need at the health system level (high disproportionate share hospital patient percentage and system-wide uncompensated care burden—the high disproportionate share patient population variable refers to whether the health system includes a hospital with a high percentage of patients covered by Medicaid and Medicare, while the high uncompensated care burden variable refers to whether a health system has a high burden of charity care and bad debt expenses; see Table S2 for complete variable definitions) from the compendium (for complete variable definitions, see Table S2). We include 2 state-level controls (Medicaid expansion, and shelter-bed rate or bed availability per 10 people experiencing homelessness in each system's state [Medicaid expansion status collected from the Kaiser Family Foundation: https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/; shelter-bed rates by state calculated from state-level counts of homelessness divided by state total shelter beds available per 10 people; data collected from HUD Point in Time counts: https://www.hudexchange.info/programs/hdx/pit-hic/#2024-pit-count-and-hic-guidance]). Crucially, shelter-bed rate is only available at the Continuum of Care (CoC) level or the state level. CoCs are the federally mandated unit of observation for measuring and tracking homelessness. Most CoCs do not align with census-tract boundaries, comprising either part of, or many units of local government. As most of the 200 largest health systems span multiple cities or regions and most span multiple states, and since our measure is the health system level and not hospital level, we include state-level shelter-bed rate. Medicaid expansion was included as a control, given that Medicaid is the primary insurer for people experiencing homelessness and may signal additional resources available to health systems to invest in responses to homelessness.6,7 All other explanatory variables exist at the health system level.

To investigate which health system attributes were most predictive of having a homeless mitigation program, we first tabulated the distribution of different kinds of health system programs targeting homelessness. We then made bivariate comparisons of system-level factors to assess which may have been associated with the presence of a health system homeless mitigation program. Finally, we fit a logistic regression model to evaluate, holding all else equal, the independent association of each system- or state-level characteristic with the presence of a homeless mitigation program, presented in terms of adjusted odds ratios. To account for skewed distributions and improve interpretability, we applied natural log transformations to hospital total revenue and primary care physician density. We clustered the standard errors at the state level to account for possible correlation of errors among systems located in the same state. In sensitivity analyses, we ran the model with and without standard errors clustered at the state level, and with the raw count of primary care physicians vs primary care physicians per bed density measure (see Table S3).

Results

Summary of findings

Of the 200 largest health systems across the United States, most health systems(n = 113, 56.5%) had a homelessness mitigation program in 2024. The most common type of program was housing, including affordable housing and PSH programs, with nearly half of the 113 programs including housing. In adjusted models, 2 health system characteristics were associated with higher odds of having a homeless mitigation program: health systems in Medicaid expansion states and those in states with greater shelter-bed availability. Primary care staffing relative to system beds was also positively associated, though the association was not statistically significant. Other characteristics, including system revenue, showed no clear association. We present the full model results below.

Type of homeless mitigation program

As shown in Figure 1, of the 200 largest health systems across the country that had a homeless mitigation program, there were 234 individual programs. From our analysis, we found that systems had 9 main types of programs. Of those health systems with a homeless mitigation program, most (mode, 1) had only one program type in their homeless mitigation program, while the average number of program types per health system was 2.1. The most common type of program, by more than 20% points, was housing. We defined housing programs as those focused on building or funding long-term housing, including affordable, low-income, and/or PSH in the community as a means of addressing homelessness. The next 2 most common program types included outreach (such as street outreach programs or mobile medical vans) and coordination and identification efforts (such as screening for homelessness and coordinating resources for patients). The least common types of programs included Medical Respite, Shelters and Temporary Housing, and Behavioral Health. Overall, we see health systems emphasizing permanent long-term housing solutions and process investments (outreach and coordination) rather than temporary housing or shelter solutions, and acute health and social services. Characteristics of health systems invested in homeless mitigation efforts

Among the 112 health systems that had a homeless mitigation program in 2024, the average health system total revenue was roughly $4 billion greater than health systems that did not have a homeless mitigation program (see Table 1). Health systems with homeless mitigation programs also employed over 330 more primary care physicians, with a teaching hospital present in over 76% of these health systems. Approximately 81% of these health systems were based in a state with Medicaid expansion, yet the percentage of hospitals with a high disproportionate share hospital patient percentage was 12% points lower than health systems without a homeless mitigation program.

Predictors of health system involvement in homeless mitigation programs

In the multivariable logistic regression, 2 state-level characteristics were associated with systems having homeless mitigation programs (Table 2). Health systems in Medicaid expansion states had more than twice the adjusted odds of having a program compared to systems in nonexpansion states (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.31; 95% CI, 1.11-4.79). Greater state shelter-bed availability was also associated with higher odds of having a program, with each additional shelter-bed per 10 people experiencing homelessness corresponding to about 12% higher adjusted odds of having a program (aOR: 1.12; 95% CI, 1.01-1.24).

Differences in system total revenue, by contrast, corresponded to little or no difference in the odds of having a program (aOR: 1.01; 95% CI, 0.75-1.36).

Discussion

Of the largest 200 health systems in the United States, most (56%) had a homeless mitigation program in 2024. Most programs focused on long-term housing provision, rather than short-term medical and social services. Health systems located in Medicaid expansion states and those with a higher rate of shelter-beds available had higher odds of having a homeless mitigation program.

One notable finding is that health systems located in states with greater shelter-bed availability relative to homelessness were more likely to have a homeless mitigation program. Rather than indicating a reduced need for health system involvement, this relationship may reflect the importance of local service infrastructure in facilitating health system engagement. Health systems often rely on partnerships with homeless service providers, housing organizations, and public agencies to develop and sustain homelessness programs. States with more robust homelessness-response systems may therefore provide institutional networks and implementation capacity that make health system investment more feasible. Conversely, health systems operating in areas with limited homelessness infrastructure may face greater barriers to establishing programs despite substantial unmet need. This finding suggests that health system responses to homelessness are shaped not only by internal organizational resources, but also by the broader service environments in which they operate.

Despite these incentives to create homeless mitigation programs, significant federal policy changes since 2025 may alter the environment that helped support health system involvement in homelessness responses. From the Obama administration through 2024, federal policymakers increasingly emphasized the intersection of homelessness, housing, and healthcare, including expanding Medicaid waivers to support housing-related services and encouraging states to address health-related social needs through Medicaid.7,13-15 These developments created new financing mechanisms and policy incentives for health systems to invest in homelessness mitigation efforts. However, recent efforts to retrench or dismantle evidence-based homeless policy and Medicaid24-25 will influence health systems' decisions and ability to establish or continue homeless mitigation programs in the future. Homeless mitigation programs relying on Medicaid financing may be cut due to declining enrollment resulting from work requirements,26 as well as reduced state-level Medicaid financing due to federal cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA).25 By contrast, attempts to dismantle evidence-based “housing first” homeless policy, including PSH, are constraining the capacity of an already fragmented homeless response system. Given this, health systems may continue or expand their programming to mitigate service gaps.27 However, just as rural health systems are more likely to close as a result of federal retrenchment under OBBBA,28 lower-capacity health systems, especially those in states unable to offset Medicaid payment losses, may be less likely to offer or continue homeless programming.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we relied on health system websites to identify homelessness mitigation programs. Although we sought to include only active programs, websites may not fully capture the extent of health system involvement, and some programs may have been missed due to limited public documentation.

Second, our contextual measures, including shelter capacity relative to homelessness and Medicaid expansion status, were observed at the state level. This approach obscures local variation in homelessness and service infrastructure that may influence health system investment decisions. However, a finer geographic approach was difficult because many health systems span multiple metropolitan areas or states and lack a single sub-state catchment area.

Finally, our analysis was cross-sectional and identifies associations rather than causal effects. Establishing causal relationships will require longitudinal data and quasi-experimental designs that leverage changes in health system characteristics, policy environments, or local homelessness conditions.

Conclusion

This study is the first to investigate efforts to address homelessness by US healthcare systems and the factors associated with their involvement. While few studies on individual hospitals suggest that homeless mitigation programs yield both financial and community benefits, significantly more research is needed at the health system level. As healthcare systems continue to adapt to an increasingly fragmented system, further questions remain about the evolving role of health systems as homeless service providers and the efficacy of these programs.
