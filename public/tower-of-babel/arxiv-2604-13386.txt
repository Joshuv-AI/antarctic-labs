Linear Probe Accuracy Scales with Model Size and Benefits from Multi-Layer Ensembling
Erik Nordby, Tasha Pais, Aviel Parrack

Abstract
Linear probes can detect when language models produce outputs they “know” are wrong, a capability relevant to both deception and reward hacking. However, single-layer probes are fragile: the best layer varies across models and tasks, and probes fail entirely on some deception types. We show that combining probes from multiple layers into an ensemble recovers strong performance even where single-layer probes fail, improving AUROC by +29% on Insider Trading and +78% on Harm-Pressure Knowledge. Across 12 models (0.5B–176B parameters), we find probe accuracy improves with scale: ∼ 5 % {\sim}5\% AUROC per 10 × \times parameters ( R = 0.81 R{=}0.81 ). Geometrically, deception directions rotate gradually across layers rather than appearing at one location, explaining both why single-layer probes are brittle and why multi-layer ensembles succeed.

1
Introduction

Several recent papers have already shown that frontier language models will use deception or manipulation when placed in high stakes contexts
(
13
)
. Linear probes have been shown to outperform sparse autoencoders in downstream detection tasks
(
7
;
16
)
. Moreover, there is evidence that probes trained in simple contrastive scenarios generalize to detect strategic deception in complex settings such as insider trading
(
8
)
. Subsequent work has evaluated the cost-effectiveness of these probes and their reliability in production settings
(
11
)
.

However, the robustness of these methods is understudied. The Liars’ Bench
(
12
)
revealed that detector performance fluctuates considerably between different types of lies. Similarly, Smith et al.
(
15
)
identify three core challenges in deception-detection methodology. Given these successes and challenges, it is important to understand whether deception signals are a consistent property of LLM representations across models and deception contexts.

Our main contribution is demonstrating that multi-layer ensembling recovers strong performance where single-layer probes fail, grounded in geometric analysis showing deception is encoded as a gradually-rotating feature across layers. We also provide a systematic evaluation across 12 models and three families, finding that detection scales with model size but varies substantially across deception types. The key challenge we address is that prior work either evaluates probes on single models
(
8
)
or finds inconsistent cross-task results
(
12
)
. We show that neither single-layer probes nor fixed-depth heuristics work universally, but the underlying representational structure enables ensembling as a principled solution.

2
Related Work

2.1
Deception in Large Language Models

Multiple studies document LLMs’ capacity for deception
(
13
;
14
)
. The definitions of deception used by these various papers vary along key axes. Some emphasize intentional manipulation of beliefs for instrumental goals
(
3
)
, while others focus on asserting believed-false statements regardless of intent
(
12
)
. For the purposes of this paper, we adopt
deception
to classify outputs where a model produces statements that contradict information it would produce under normal, non-adversarial conditions. This includes role-played lies, strategic omissions, and responses which achieve instrumental goals through manipulation.

2.2
Linear Probes for Deception Detection

Our work builds on recent advances in activation-based deception detection. Zou et al.
(
17
)
introduced Representation Engineering (REPE), demonstrating that linear probes can extract high-level features like honesty from model activations using contrastive prompt pairs. Goldowsky-Dill et al.
(
8
)
extended this approach to strategic deception, training probes on simple roleplaying scenarios and testing generalization to complex multi-turn settings. Their probes achieved AUROCs between 0.96–0.999 on evaluation datasets. Notably, recent work shows that linear probes outperform sparse autoencoders (SAEs) on downstream detection tasks
(
7
;
16
)
, motivating our focus on linear methods rather than more complex feature extraction approaches.

Subsequent work has revealed important limitations. Kretschmar et al.
(
12
)
introduced Liars’ Bench, a systematic evaluation across five deception types, finding significant performance variation across types of deception. Notably, their mean linear probe achieved only 0.54 balanced accuracy on several datasets (including High Pressure Choice and High Pressure Knowledge which we test), suggesting fundamental limitations in single-layer probing approaches. Similarly dataset leakage issues
(
1
)
have been posed which indicate probes may be finding elicitation artifacts rather than underlying deceptive patterns.

More fundamentally, Smith et al.
(
15
)
identify three core challenges in the evaluation of deception probes: distinguishing strategic deception from lower-level conditioned responses that merely appear deceptive, determining whether models instructed to roleplay as deceptive characters possess genuine deceptive intent, and the possibility that prompts modify model beliefs rather than induce lying, meaning apparent contradictions may reflect honest responses under different beliefs. Our work addresses some of these concerns by evaluating across diverse deception types and focusing on relative improvements rather than absolute claims.

2.3
Linear Representations

The assumption that features are linearly represented in activations is central to mechanistic interpretability. Gorton
(
9
)
argues the transformer’s additive residual stream structure favors linear representations, supported by empirical findings that magnitude-based superposition appears only in small models
(
5
)
. For deception specifically, Bürger et al.
(
2
)
find that larger models exhibit more distinct and linearly separable deception representations, motivating our scaling analysis.

2.4
Multi-Layer Analysis

REPE
(
17
)
addresses multi-layer
steering
but not detection ensembling. Constitutional Classifiers++
(
6
)
ensembles probes with external classifiers for jailbreak detection. Work on hallucination detection
(
4
)
leverages layer selection, but principled multi-layer ensembling for deception detection remains unexplored.

3
Methods

3.1
Models

We evaluate 12 open-weights models across three families: Llama (1B, 3B, 8B, 70B), Qwen (0.5B, 3B, 14B, 72B), and Mistral (7B, NeMo 12B, Small 24B, Mixtral 8x22B). All share core architectural choices (RMSNorm, RoPE, SwiGLU, GQA). Mixtral uses Mixture of Experts but performed comparably to dense models, suggesting residual stream aggregation provides sufficient signal regardless of expert routing.

3.2
Probing Methodology

For each model and layer, we train a standard L2-regularized logistic regression probe using scikit-learn. Training proceeds as follows:

1.

Extract activations
: For each dialogue, we extract the residual stream activations at positions marked by the dataset’s detection mask (the span containing deceptive/honest content).

2.

Standardize
: Activations are standardized to zero mean and unit variance across the training set.

3.

Fit logistic regression
: We train
sklearn.LogisticRegression
with
C=0.1
(equivalently,
\lambda=10
L2 penalty) and no intercept, following prior work
(
10
;
12
)
.

4.

Score
: At inference, we compute the dot product between the standardized activation and the learned weight vector. Higher scores indicate predicted deception.

Probes are trained independently at each layer. We evaluate using AUROC: the probability that a randomly chosen deceptive sample scores higher than a randomly chosen honest sample. Our implementation builds on the codebase from
8
.

3.3
Datasets

Training:
We use the REPE honesty dataset
(
17
)
, consisting of 612 contrastive prompt pairs where instructions (“honest AI” vs. “dishonest AI”) differ while responses remain identical. We use a 80/20 train-validation split.

Evaluation:
We test generalization on Liars’ Bench
(
12
)
, spanning five deception types.
Explicit instruction-following
(Convincing Game, Instructed Deception) where the model is told to lie.
Strategic role-play
(Insider Trading) where the model adopts a persona with incentives to deceive.
Implicit pressure
(Harm-Pressure Choice/Knowledge) with no explicit instruction but pressure to comply with harmful requests. We hypothesize explicit deception is easiest to detect while implicit deception is hardest. Examples in Appendix.

3.4
Ensembling

To improve robustness over single-layer probes, we combine information from multiple layers. The key insight is that probes at different layers make different errors. If we can identify layers with complementary failure modes, their combination should outperform any single layer.

Layer Selection.

We use
double-fault analysis
to identify complementary layers. For each pair of layers, we compute how often both probes misclassify the same sample. Low double-fault indicates complementary errors: when one probe fails, the other often succeeds. We select layers greedily to minimize the maximum pairwise double-fault within the ensemble.

Score Combination.

Given
k
selected layers, each single-layer probe produces a score (mean activation-direction dot product) for each dialogue. For
k=2
, we grid-search over mixing weights
\alpha\in[0,1]
to maximize AUROC on a validation set. For
k\in\{3,5\}
, we collect the
k
per-layer scores into a feature vector and train a second logistic regression (the “stacking” classifier) to combine them. We use an 80/20 train-test split: layer selection and stacking weights are learned on the training portion, and all reported ensemble results are evaluated on the held-out 20%.

3.5
Metrics

We evaluate using AUROC (Area Under the ROC Curve), which represents the probability that a randomly chosen deceptive sample scores higher than a randomly chosen honest one. AUROC is threshold-independent, making it suitable for comparing probes across diverse architectures and tasks where optimal operating points may differ.

4.5
Probe Ensembling Analysis

If ensembling is to succeed, we need probes that fail on different samples. We quantify this using double-fault analysis: for each pair of layers, we measure how often both probes misclassify the same sample. A pair with low double-fault rate makes a good ensemble candidate because their errors are complementary.
Figure

7
reveals clear block structure in the double-fault matrix. Probes within the same network region (e.g., layers 30 to 50) fail together frequently, while probes spanning early and late layers rarely share failures. This structure directly motivates our cross-depth ensembling strategy.

We first test whether simple two-layer combinations can improve detection. For each pair of layers, we combine their probe scores using a weighted average and optimize the mixing weight on validation data.
Figure

8
shows the resulting AUROC for all layer pairs on Llama-70B. The best pairs achieve approximately 4 AUROC points above the best single layer (0.92 to 0.96), with optimal performance when combining layers from different network regions. Balanced weights in the 0.3 to 0.7 range work well, suggesting both probes contribute meaningfully. TPR at 1% FPR shows similar patterns (see
Figure

17
in Appendix).

5
Limitations

Domain Gap and Probe Validity.

We train probes on REPE
(
17
)
, which uses simple contrastive pairs (“honest AI” vs. “dishonest AI” system prompts), and evaluate on Liars’ Bench scenarios involving complex role-playing and strategic contexts. This domain gap raises concerns about what probes actually detect. As Boxo et al.
(
1
)
note, probes may identify elicitation artifacts (e.g., detecting “dishonest AI system prompt” style) rather than underlying deceptive reasoning. More broadly, Smith et al.
(
15
)
identify fundamental challenges: it remains unclear whether labeled deceptive examples reflect genuine strategic reasoning or arise from conditioned behaviors, role-playing, or prompt-induced belief changes. Our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating structure in deception-like behaviors, not as establishing a definitive detector of deceptive intent.

Transferability.

We evaluate within-family scaling but do not systematically test whether probes or ensemble configurations transfer across model families. The family-specific patterns we observe (e.g., Llama’s strong Insider Trading performance) suggest that optimal approaches may not generalize, limiting practical applicability when deploying probes on new model releases.

Evaluation Scope.

Our evaluation uses short-context, static datasets. Kramár et al.
(
11
)
show probes fail on long-context inputs where relevant information is distributed across many tokens. Similarly, Cunningham et al.
(
6
)
demonstrate vulnerability to targeted, multi-turn attacks and output obfuscation. Our methods do not address these adversarial and complex deployment scenarios.

6
Discussion

Two-layer ensembles with grid-searched mixing weights fail to universally improve because optimal weighting varies substantially across tasks—a configuration that helps Insider Trading may hurt Instructed Deception. The double-fault matrix (Figure 7) shows probes within the same network region have high co-failure rates, while probes spanning early and late layers show complementary failures. Five-layer ensembles with learned stacking weights (logistic regression) succeed, but because we used different weight-learning methods for two-layer versus larger ensembles, we cannot isolate whether gains come from additional layers, the switch to logistic regression, or both. Both factors likely contribute: more layers sample the full gradient of the gradually-rotating deception representation, while learned stacking allows task-appropriate combination of these signals.

Probes work well on explicit deception (
>
0.95 AUROC on Convincing Game, Instructed Deception) but struggle on implicit deception (Harm-Pressure tasks). This suggests current methods detect surface-level deceptive framing rather than genuine strategic reasoning. For safety applications, practitioners should interpret high probe scores as evidence of deception-like patterns, not proof of intent.

We considered alternative explanations. Probes might detect prompt artifacts rather than deception, but generalization from REPE to diverse Liars’ Bench scenarios argues against this. The scaling trend might reflect task difficulty rather than representational quality, though the geometric analysis suggests genuine representational structure. Ensemble improvements might reflect overfitting, but we mitigate this through held-out evaluation (80/20 split). Deception detection relates to detecting reward hacking and sycophancy, where models produce outputs they “know” are suboptimal. Both involve mismatches between internal representations and outputs, and our methods could extend to such behaviors.

7
Future Work

Our ensembling analysis was limited to linear probes on a single model (Llama-70B). Combining linear probes with non-linear probes (MLPs or attention-based classifiers) may capture complementary aspects of deception that linear methods miss. Training task-specific probes on individual deception types (e.g., a probe specialized for harm-refusal scenarios) and ensembling them with general-purpose probes could improve performance on challenging tasks.Beyond ensembling, evaluation on long-context and adversarial scenarios where current methods fail
(
11
;
6
)
remains critical, as does understanding whether probe and ensemble configurations transfer across model families.

8
Conclusion

We show that single-layer linear probes for deception detection are brittle: optimal layers vary across models and tasks, and probes fail entirely on some challenging deception types. Our geometric analysis reveals why: deception directions rotate gradually across layers, so no single layer captures the full signal. This theoretical insight motivates multi-layer ensembling, and our empirical results validate the approach. On Llama-70B, a 5-layer ensemble with logistic regression stacking achieves +13% mean AUROC improvement across five Liars’ Bench tasks, with the largest gains precisely where single-layer probes struggle most (Insider Trading +29%, Harm-Pressure Knowledge +78%). Because we used grid search for two-layer ensembles but logistic regression for larger ensembles, we cannot fully disentangle whether gains come from more layers or from the improved weight-learning method—but the geometric analysis suggests both matter: more layers sample the rotating representation more thoroughly, while learned weights combine these complementary signals effectively. We tested only linear probe ensembles on a single model, and gains on already-strong tasks were minimal or negative. Whether these improvements generalize across model families, scale to larger ensembles, or extend to hybrid approaches combining linear and non-linear probes remains open.

Probe accuracy generally scales with model size (
{\sim}5\%
AUROC per 10
\times
parameters across 12 models), so larger models are easier to monitor. Explicit deception (role-playing, instruction-following) is reliably detectable while implicit deception remains challenging across all methods we tested.

The geometric coherence we observe (probes at nearby layers detect similar features, with directions rotating gradually through the network) suggests deception is encoded as a unified representation rather than scattered noise. This provides hope for mechanistic understanding. However, the gap between detecting role-played deception in controlled settings and catching genuine strategic deception in deployment remains substantial.

Impact Statement

This work advances AI safety through improved deception detection. While detection techniques could potentially inform evasion strategies, we believe transparency serves the broader goal of developing trustworthy AI systems.
