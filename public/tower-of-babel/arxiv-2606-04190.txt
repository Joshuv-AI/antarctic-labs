Abstract
We discuss optimal recovery for classes of multivariate convex functions from given point samples, as well as the sampling numbers of these classes, corresponding to optimal sample choices. Upper and lower bounds for either variant are established when the reconstruction error is measured in L p L_{p} for 1 ≤ p ≤ ∞ 1\leq p\leq\infty . These bounds match, sometimes up to logarithmic factors, and therefore characterize the respective optimal rate of decay. For classical smoothness classes such as Sobolev, Hölder or Besov spaces, it is well known that the optimal decay rate of sampling numbers can be achieved by sampling on uniform tensor product grids and using linear methods of reconstruction, such as piecewise polynomial interpolation. One of the main findings in this paper is that for classes of convex functions, these procedures generally produce suboptimal rates, except when p = 1 p=1 and p = ∞ p=\infty , and are outperformed by nonlinear reconstruction methods that do not employ tensor product grids.



1
Introduction

1.1
Recovery from point samples

The problem of recovering a general multivariate function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, defined on a closed set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
from point samples is ubiquitous in a large number of applications. Given a set

\Lambda:=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}
of sample sites
x^{j}=(x^{j}_{1},\dots,x_{d}^{j})\in\Omega
, and the data vector

w=w(f):=(f(x^{j}))_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(1.1)

of a continuous function
f\in C(\Omega)
, we are interested in constructing an approximation
\tilde{f}
to
f
with
recovery error
\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{X}
measured in some Banach space norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. In this paper,
X
will always be an
L_{p}
space with
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and
\Omega
will be the unit cube, that is,

X=L_{p}(\Omega)\quad{\rm and}\quad\Omega=\Omega_{d}:=[0,1]^{d}.

(1.2)

In order to give quantitative bounds on the recovery error in
X
, one needs to make more assumptions about
f
than the fact that
f
is merely continuous. Indeed, otherwise
f
can be anything away from the data sites.
Such an additional assumption is commonly referred to as a
prior
or
model class assumption
and consists of a requirement that
f\in{\cal K}
, where

{\cal K}
is a compact set in
X
. Given the data sites
\Lambda
and such a model class
{\cal K}
, a recovery algorithm is a mapping

A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X,

(1.3)

and the approximation to
f
by this algorithm is

\tilde{f}:=A(w(f)).

(1.4)

We denote by

{\cal A}:=\{A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X\}

(1.5)

the set of all possible mappings.

The performance of an algorithm
A
on an individual
f
is given by

E(f;\Lambda,A)_{X}:=\|f-A(w(f))\|_{X},

(1.6)

whereas the worst case performance on the model class
{\cal K}
is

E({\cal K};\Lambda,A)_{X}:=\sup_{f\in{\cal K}}E(f;\Lambda,A)_{X}.

(1.7)

The optimal recovery of the functions
f
in the model class
{\cal K}
from the data observations
w(f)
is then given by the quantity

\rho({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X}:=\inf_{A\in{\cal A}}E({\cal K};\Lambda,A)_{X},

(1.8)

that describes the performance of a recovery algorithm that is optimal over the class
{\cal K}
for the given data sites
\Lambda
.

There is a simple theoretical description of an optimal recovery algorithm and its performance error.
Given a set
\Lambda
of data sites and a data vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
{\cal K}_{w}
denote the set of all functions
g\in{\cal K}
which satisfy the given data, i.e.,
w(g)=w
. Consider a ball
B_{w}=B(f_{w},r_{w})\subset X

of smallest possible radius
r_{w}
that contains
{\cal K}_{w}
. Then, the center
f_{w}
of
B_{w}
is an optimal recovery from the given data vector

w
, and the radius
r_{w}
is the optimal recovery error for the observed data vector
w
.
Note that for those
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
{\cal K}_{w}
is empty, one has
r_{w}=0
.
Any mapping
A^{*}=A^{*}({\cal K},\Lambda)
such that

A^{*}:w\to f_{w},

(1.9)

when
{\cal K}_{w}
is not empty, is an optimal recovery algorithm for
{\cal K}
and
\Lambda
,
and we can identify

\rho({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X}=\sup_{f\in{\cal K}}\|f-A^{*}(w(f))\|_{X}=\sup_{f\in{\cal K}}r_{w(f)}

(1.10)

as the optimal recovery error.

The optimal recovery mapping
A^{*}
is only of theoretical interest. In applications, one wants a more practical mapping.
Notice that if in place of
A^{*}
one constructs a mapping
\tilde{A}
that, when
{\cal K}_{w}
is not empty, maps each
w
into an element
g_{w}\in{\cal K}_{w}
, i.e., finds an element in the model class which satisfies the measurements,
then the corresponding algorithm is
near optimal
. Namely, we have

\|f-\tilde{A}(w(f))\|_{X}\leq 2\rho({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X},\quad f\in{\cal K}.

(1.11)

The maps
A^{*}
and
\tilde{A}
are actually optimal, respectively near-optimal,
not only over the class
{\cal K}
, but also over the set
{\cal K}_{w}
for each individual possible data instance
w
.
This property is usually referred to as
instance optimality
.

Note that the optimal recovery framework does not impose constraints on the recovery map
A
. Nevertheless, in practical applications, it is often preferable to restrict
A
to be
linear
. This leads to the definition of linear optimal recovery
\rho^{\ell}({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X}
, where in (
1.8
) the infimum is taken over the class
{\cal A}^{\ell}
of all linear maps from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
X
.
Obviously, one has that

\rho({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X}\leq\rho^{\ell}({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X}.

(1.12)

In both cases, a crucial question that arises is the best choice of data sites. For a fair competition, we fix the number
n
of the data sites and define

\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{X}:=\inf_{\#(\Lambda)=n}\rho({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X},

(1.13)

which are called the
sampling numbers
for the model class
{\cal K}
in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. One similarly defines the
linear sampling numbers

\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal K})_{X}:=\inf_{\#(\Lambda)=n}\rho^{\ell}({\cal K};\Lambda)_{X}.

(1.14)

There is a practical interest in evaluating sampling numbers.
Indeed, minimizing the number of point evaluations needed for approximating functions from a given class with some prescribed accuracy is particularly important when each evaluation is costly (it may for example necessitate solving a PDE, or running a physical or numerical experiment).

Sampling numbers can be related to classical quantities that describe the complexity of compact sets. The first classical notion are the
Kolmogorov widths

d_{n}({\cal K})_{X}:=\inf_{\dim(X_{n})\leq n}\max_{f\in{\cal K}}\min_{g\in X_{n}}\|f-g\|_{X},

(1.15)

which describe the best possible approximation of
{\cal K}
by an
n
-dimensional linear space. Obviously, one has the lower bound

d_{n}({\cal K})_{X}\leq\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal K})_{X}.

(1.16)

The second classical notion are the
entropy numbers

\varepsilon_{n}({\cal K})_{X}:=\inf\{\varepsilon\;:\;\exists f_{1},\dots,f_{2^{n}}\in X\;{\rm s.t.}\;\max_{f\in{\cal K}}\min_{i}\|f-f_{i}\|_{X}\leq\varepsilon\},

(1.17)

which, for every fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are the smallest
\varepsilon
such that
{\cal K}
is covered by
2^{n}
balls of radius
\varepsilon
. It has been shown in
[
5
]
that the rate of decay of the worst case error in approximating
{\cal K}
by any stable, linear or non-linear, approximation method using
n
parameters cannot be faster than the rate of decay of the entropy numbers. Therefore, we do not expect
\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{X}
to decay faster than
\varepsilon_{n}({\cal K})_{X}
.

The sampling numbers and linear sampling numbers have been extensively studied in the literature.
In particular, their asymptotic rate of decay have been determined for many classical model classes.
For example, if
{\cal K}
is the unit ball of a Lipschitz, Sobolev, or Besov space which is embedded in
C(\Omega)
, one knows the asymptotics of the sampling numbers
\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{L_{p}}
and linear sampling numbers
\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal K})_{L_{p}}
, and their decay rates agree. Moreover, it is known that, choosing the data sites
on uniform tensor product grids, realizes this optimal rate of decay. We refer to
[
15
]
, see Theorem 23, for results of this type,
or

[
12
]
, see Theorem 11.1, for a complete account on various notions of widths
for Sobolev classes, including their sampling numbers.

1.2
Model classes of convex functions

In some applications, one is interested not only in classical smoothness classes but in
model classes which impose different natural constraints for membership.
The present paper is interested in one such setting where the model classes consist of convex functions, which are ubiquitously encountered in optimization tasks coming from various applications.

We denote by

{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega)

the set of all convex functions on
\Omega
.
Recall that a function
f
is in
{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega)
, if and only if its restriction
f|_{L}
to each line segment
L\subset\Omega
is a convex univariate function. Note that every

f\in{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega)
is
continuous on
{\rm Int}(\Omega):=(0,1)^{d}
, but generally not in

C^{1}
.

We use the standard notation
v\cdot w
for the scalar product of
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
|v|
for the Euclidean norm of
v
.
At each point
x\in{\rm Int}(\Omega)
,
the set

\partial f(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~f(y)\geq f(x)+v\cdot(y-x)~\text{for all $y\in\Omega$}\},

(1.18)

is called the
subgradient
of
f
at
x
.
The set
\partial f(x)

is non-empty, convex, and compact. Moreover,
an important property of the subgradient is that it is
monotone
in the sense that whenever
x,y\in\Omega
,
v\in\partial f(x)
, and
w\in\partial f(y)
, then

(v-w)\cdot(x-y)\geq 0.

(1.19)

For a univariate convex function
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
,
its
subgradient is the interval

\partial g(t)=[g^{\prime}_{-}(t),\,g^{\prime}_{+}(t)],

(1.20)

and each of the functions
g^{\prime}_{-}
and
g^{\prime}_{+}
is monotone nondecreasing. More precisely, we have that

g^{\prime}_{-}(t_{1})\leq g^{\prime}_{+}(t_{1})\leq g^{\prime}_{-}(t_{2})\leq g^{\prime}_{+}(t_{2}),\quad\hbox{for every}\,\,a<t_{1}<t_{2}<b.

(1.21)

Also
g^{\prime}_{-}(t)
has a limit at
t\to a^{+}
. We define
g^{\prime}_{-}(a)
to be this limit (which could be
-\infty
). We define
g^{\prime}_{+}(b)
(which could be
+\infty
) similarly.

We consider two sub-classes of
{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega)
imposing the following restrictions:

•

Uniform bound on the functions:

{\cal B}={\cal B}(\Omega):=\{f\in{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega):\;|f(x)|\leq 1,\;x\in\Omega\}.

(1.22)

•

Uniform bound on the subgradient and the function:

{\cal L}={\cal L}(\Omega):={\cal B}\cap\{f\in{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega):\;\forall v\in\partial f(x),\;x\in{\rm Int}(\Omega),\ {\rm we\ have}\ |v|\leq 1\}.

(1.23)

Obviously, one has

{\cal L}\subsetneq{\cal B}
. It is
easily checked that the class
{\cal L}
can equivalently be defined by the Lipschitz condition:

{\cal L}=\{f\in{\cal C}_{\rm conv}(\Omega):|f(x)|\leq 1,\,\,\;|f(x)-f(y)|\leq|x-y|,\;x,y\in\Omega\}.

(1.24)

1.3
Outline of the paper

The main results in this paper provide matching upper and lower bounds (up to logarithmic factors) for the sampling numbers of the model classes
{\cal B}
and
{\cal L}
. We also determine the rates of the
optimal recovery error when the data sites are restricted to be tensor product grids.
Our main finding is that, for both classes
{\cal B}
and
{\cal L}
, the sampling rates cannot in general be achieved if one imposes either the restriction that the sampling grid is a tensor product grid, or the restriction that the recovery is given by a linear mapping
A
. Specifically, except when
p=1
or
p=\infty
, the decay rates for the sampling numbers cannot be achieved using tensor product grids. Further, when
p=2
, the decay rates of the linear sampling numbers are substantially worse than those of their nonlinear counterparts.

We stress that, while the classes
{\cal B}
and
{\cal L}
are
contained in classical
smoothness classes
{\cal K}
, such as balls of
W^{s}(L_{p})
for certain ranges of
s
and
p
, their
sampling numbers cannot generally be derived from these embeddings.

We begin this paper by studying the setting where the set of sample points is a tensor product grid. For the class
{\cal L}
, we show in §
2
that, when
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
uniform tensor product grids
\Lambda_{n}
with
n=\#(\Lambda_{n})
, give the optimal recovery rate

\rho({\cal L},\Lambda_{n})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-r/d},\quad n\geq 1.

(1.25)

where

r:=r(p)=1+\frac{1}{p}.

(1.26)

This rate is also proved
to be optimal even when the competition is allowed to take place over all possible
tensor product grids (not necessarily uniform) consisting of
\asymp n
data sites and can be achieved by a simple linear recovery method.
Our analysis is based on approximation error estimates for multilinear interpolation
of convex functions, which seem to be new, and could be of interest in numerical analysis.
It follows from these results that one has

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\leq\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{$~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle<}}{{\sim}}~$}n^{-r/d}.

(1.27)

We also establish lower bounds for the sampling numbers of
{\cal L}
. In the univariate case, they
match the upper bounds,
and thus the rate (
1.25
) is optimal

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-r/d},\quad d=1,\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

(1.28)

When
d\geq 2
, the rate in (
1.25
) is also optimal for the sampling numbers when
p=1
or
p=\infty
, namely,

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{1}}\asymp\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{1}}\asymp n^{-2/d}\quad{\rm and}\quad\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp n^{-1/d}.

(1.29)

It is also optimal for the linear sampling numbers when
p=2
, that is,

\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}\asymp n^{-\frac{3}{2d}}.

(1.30)

It turns out, as described below, that for other values of
p
, determining the asymptotic decay of the sampling numbers of
{\cal L}
is quite subtle and cannot be obtained by simple constructions using tensor
product grids or linear recovery.

For the larger class
{\cal B}
, we show in §
3
that, restricting to tensor product grids, the rates (
1.25
) also hold for
1\leq p<\infty
, with the notable difference that the tensor product sampling grid which achieves this rate can no longer be taken as uniform, but must be chosen
with a grading that depends on
p
. Note that the sampling numbers of the class
{\cal B}
do not decay to zero in
the case
p=\infty
.

We turn next in the paper to a more refined study of the optimal sampling rate

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
. The rather surprising result, proved
in §
4
, is that, when
1<p<\infty
,
by using specific sets
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
of data sites that are not of tensor product type,
one achieves substantially better rates
than the optimal rates for tensor product grids. More precisely, up to logarithmic
factors, we obtain the optimal rate for the sampling numbers

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-\tilde{r}/d},

(1.31)

where

\tilde{r}:=\tilde{r}(p,d)=1+\min\Big\{\frac{d}{p},1\Big\}=\begin{cases}2,&\,1\leq p\leq d,\\
1+\frac{d}{p},&\,p\geq d.\end{cases}

(1.32)

For
1<p<\infty
and
d\geq 2
, this optimal rate thus improves on the rate of decay
n^{-r/d}
achievable
by tensor product grids, as well as on the rate of decay of the linear sampling numbers when
p=2
.
We extend these results to the larger class
{\cal B}
in §
5
, reaching the same optimal rate of decay
n^{-\tilde{r}/d}
, except in the case
p=\infty
where, as we have already mentioned, the sampling numbers do not converge to zero.

The proof of the optimal rates
in §
4
and §
5

is not fully constructive. We prove the existence of sampling sets
\Lambda^{*}_{n}
of cardinality
n
and of optimal recovery maps
A
that achieve an error bound (up to logarithmic factors)

E({\cal K},\Lambda^{*}_{n},A)_{L_{p}}\lesssim n^{-\tilde{r}/d},

(1.33)

when
{\cal K}={\cal L}
, or
{\cal K}={\cal B}
.
We present in §
6

computationally feasible near optimal
recovery maps
A_{{\cal L}}
and
A_{{\cal B}}

that are inherently nonlinear and achieve such error bounds. We conclude the paper
with §7, where some remarks
involving widths, entropy numbers, and open questions are discussed.

In what follows, we will use

C
to denote constants in our upper bounds, and
c
to denote constants in our lower bounds. These constants depend only on the dimension
d
(and sometimes also on
p
), and their values can change from formula to formula. We also use the notation

A\asymp B
when
cA\leq B\leq CA
, or
B\lesssim A
, if
B\leq CA
.

2
Sampling on tensor product grids

One of the most natural choices for the set
\Lambda
of sampling sites
is a tensor product grid.
Suppose that
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{d}
are sets with
m_{1},\dots,m_{d}
points in
[0,1]
, respectively. Then, the tensor product grid

\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}:=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}

(2.1)

is a set of
n=m_{1}\cdots m_{d}
sampling sites from
\Omega
. A special case is the
uniform grid

\Lambda_{n}:=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}
with
\Gamma_{j}:=\Gamma:=\{0,1/m,2/m,\dots,1\}
for all
j=1,\dots,d
, and
n=(m+1)^{d}
.

We let
{\cal T}_{n}
denote the class of all tensor product grids consisting of at most
n
sampling sites.
In this section, we analyze the performance of sampling on such tensor product grids for the recovery of functions in the model class
{\cal L}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that when imposing tensor product grids, not all possible values of
n\geq 1
appear in their cardinalities. Since
the quantities
\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{X}
and
\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal K})_{X}
are obviously non-increasing, for establishing an algebraic rate of decay
n^{-\alpha}
on these quantities, it is enough to show this
rate for all
n
in a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that
\frac{n_{k+1}}{n_{k}}
is uniformly bounded.

2.1
Recovery of a function from a tensor product grid

Suppose that
\Lambda
is a set of data sites given by a tensor product grid of the form (
2.1
), and further let
{\cal K}
be a model class. Given data consisting of the values of a function
f\in{\cal K}
at these data sites, we are interested in constructing a
practical
recovery map
A
that achieves near optimal performance for
{\cal K}
given that
\Lambda
has been fixed. A natural choice for
A
is to use the data observations to create a function
g
that interpolates the data. While there are many possibilities for such interpolants, we put forward in this section a form of piecewise multi-linear interpolation, which we go on to show is near optimal for tensor grid sampling whenever
{\cal K}
is one of our two model classes
{\cal L}
or
{\cal B}
.

To precisely describe the multi-linear interpolation map that we will use, let us first consider the case
d=1
. In this case the data sites are a set
\Lambda:=\{0=\xi_{0}<\xi_{1}<\cdots<\xi_{m}=1\}
of
m+1
points from the interval
[0,1]
and our data consists of the values of a function
f
at these data sites. We define

I_{\Lambda}^{1}(f):\ [0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R},

(2.2)

to be the continuous piecewise linear function on
[0,1]
which interpolates the values of
f
at the data sites and has its only breakpoints at the data sites. Note that
I_{\Lambda}^{1}(f)
is equal to

I_{\Lambda}^{1}(f)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}f(\xi_{i})\ell_{\Lambda,i},

(2.3)

where
\ell_{\Lambda,i}
are the unique piecewise-linear (hat) functions, with breakpoints only at the datasites
\Lambda
, such that
\ell_{\Lambda,i}(\xi_{j})=\delta_{ij}
.

When
d>1
and
\Lambda=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}
, where the
\Gamma_{j}:=\{0=\xi_{0}^{i}<\cdots<\xi^{j}_{m_{j}}=1\}
are one-dimensional data sites (potentially different for each
i
), we define the interpolating function
I_{\Lambda}^{d}(f)
via

I_{\Lambda}^{d}(f):=\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{m_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{d}=0}^{m_{d}}f(\xi_{i_{1}},...,\xi_{i_{d}})\ell_{\Lambda,i_{1},...,i_{d}},

(2.4)

where the Lagrange functions are given by the tensor product

\ell_{\Lambda,i_{1},...,i_{d}}(x_{1},...,x_{d}):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\ell_{\Gamma_{j},i_{j}}(x_{j}).

(2.5)

Note that the operator
I_{\Lambda}^{d}
is simply the standard
d
-dimensional multi-linear interpolation operator on a tensor product grid.

We will use that these interpolants can also be characterized inductively as follows:

•

Suppose that
I_{\tilde{\Lambda}}^{d-1}(\tilde{f})
has been defined for every tensor product grid
\tilde{\Lambda}
of
[0,1]^{d-1}
and every function
\tilde{f}
defined on
\Omega_{d-1}=[0,1]^{d-1}
.

•

Let
\tilde{\Lambda}:=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d-1}
, which is a tensor product grid for
[0,1]^{d-1}
. Note that
\Lambda=\tilde{\Lambda}\times\Gamma_{d}
, and

I^{d}_{\Lambda}(f)(x)=I^{1}_{\Gamma_{d}}\Bigl(I^{d-1}_{\tilde{\Lambda}}(f(\cdot,t))(\tilde{x})\Bigr)(x_{d}),\quad x=(\tilde{x},x_{d}),

(2.6)

where the one-dimensional interpolation
I_{\Gamma_{d}}^{1}
is applied to the univariate function

t\rightarrow I^{d-1}_{\tilde{\Lambda}}(f(\cdot,t))(\tilde{x}).

(2.7)

2.2
Main results for sampling on tensor product grids for the model class
{\cal L}

We recall the notation

r=r(p):=1+\frac{1}{p},\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

(2.8)

Our main results in this section are given in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

For the model class
{\cal L}={\cal L}(\Omega_{d})
, we have the following results :

(i)
If
\Lambda_{n}\subset\Omega_{d}
,
d\geq 1
, is a uniform tensor product grid and
n=(m+1)^{d}
,
then for
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have

\rho({\cal L},\Lambda_{n})_{L_{p}}\leq\rho^{\ell}({\cal L},\Lambda_{n})_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d},

(2.9)

with the constant
C
depending only on
d
. It follows that for
1\leq p\leq\infty
and for every
n\geq 1
,

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\leq\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d}.

(2.10)

(ii)
For any tensor product grid
\Lambda\in{\cal T}_{n}\subset\Omega_{d}
,
d\geq 1
, any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and any
n\geq 1
, we have the lower bounds

\rho^{\ell}({\cal L},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}\geq\rho({\cal L},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-r/d},

(2.11)

where
c
depends only on
d
.

(iii)
In the case
d=1
and
1\leq p\leq\infty

we have

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\asymp\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-r/d}.

(2.12)

Also, the estimate (
2.12
) holds if
d>1
and
p=1,\infty
.

(iv)
When
p=2
and all
d\geq 1
, one has

\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}\asymp n^{-r/d}=n^{-\frac{3}{2d}}.

(2.13)

The equivalency constants depend only on
d
.

(v)

When
1<p<\infty
and
d>1
, one has

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-\tilde{r}/d},\quad\tilde{r}:=\tilde{r}(p,d)=\min\{2,1+d/p\}<r,

(2.14)

where
c
depends only on
d
.

In other words, (i) and (ii) of this theorem show that
n^{-r}
is the best recovery rate that one can obtain for the model class
{\cal L}
when one restricts the sampling sites to lie on a tensor product grid. Moreover, this rate can be obtained using uniform sampling and a linear method of recovery.
This obviously implies an upper bound
Cn^{-r}
for the rate of decay of
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
and
\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
that is valid for all
d
and
p
. Item (iii) determines the asymptotic behavior
of
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
,
n\geq 1
, but only in the special cases
d=1
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
and for
d>1
when
p=1
or
p=\infty
. Item (iv) determines the asymptotic behavior of
\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}
. Item (v) gives a general lower bound
cn^{-\tilde{r}}
,
n\geq 1
, for
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Since
\tilde{r}>r
this lower bound does not match the upper bound
Cn^{-r}
. Later in this paper, we shall show that the upper bound for the decay rate can be improved to
n^{-\tilde{r}}
, save for logarithmic factors, which in turn show that the correct decay rate is
n^{-\tilde{r}}
. According to Theorem
2.2
, this improved rate cannot be obtained using tensor product grids and according to (iv) cannot be obtained with a linear method of recovery.

2.3
Upper bounds for uniform tensor product grids

The upper bounds for the recovery error of tensor grid sampling given in Theorem
2.2
are obtained by uniform sampling and recovery using
multilinear interpolation as described in §
2.1
. Therefore, in this section we restrict ourselves to analyzing this case. We will frequently use the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a univariate convex function and
\hat{g}
be its linear interpolant at the endpoints of the interval, that is,
\hat{g}
is the unique affine function such that

\hat{g}(a)=g(a)
and
\hat{g}(b)=g(b)
. Then we have

\|g-\hat{g}\|_{L_{p}([a,b])}\leq(b-a)^{1+1/p}(g^{\prime}_{-}(b)-g^{\prime}_{+}(a)),\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

(2.15)

Proof:.

The function
\tilde{g}:=g-\hat{g}
is
convex, negative and satisfies
\tilde{g}(a)=\tilde{g}(b)=0
.
Since

\delta:=(g^{\prime}_{-}(b)-g^{\prime}_{+}(a))=(\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{-}(b)-\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{+}(a))\geq 0,

(2.16)

one has

0\leq\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{-}(b)\leq\delta\quad{\rm and}\quad-\delta\leq\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{+}(a)\leq 0.

(2.17)

Using the first inequality, the convexity of
\tilde{g}
, and the fact that
\tilde{g}(b)=0
, we find that

0\geq\tilde{g}(x)\geq\delta(x-b)\geq\delta(a-b),\quad x\in[a,b],

(2.18)

which gives the
L_{\infty}
bound. The
L_{p}
bound follows by integration. Note that the bound (
2.15
) could be improved by a multiplicative constant smaller than
1
, where the optimal constant corresponds to the extreme case when
\tilde{g}
is piecewise affine
with slope
-\delta/2
on

[a,(a+b)/2]
and
\delta/2

on
[(a+b)/2,b]
.
∎

We now consider the multidimensional case where
d\in\{2,3,\dots\}
and
\Omega=\Omega_{d}=[0,1]^{d}
. Any point
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\Omega
can be written as
x=(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})
where
\tilde{x}_{d}\in[0,1]^{d-1}
is the vector of its first
d-1
components.
We define the uniform tensor product grid

\Lambda_{n}:=\Lambda_{n}(d):=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}
with
\Gamma_{j}:=\Gamma:=\{0,1/m,2/m,\dots,1\}
,
j=1,\dots,d
, consisting of
n=(m+1)^{d}
points and denote by
T_{k}
the cell

T_{k}:=[k_{1}h,(k_{1}+1)h]\times\cdots\times[k_{d}h,(k_{d}+1)h],\quad k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d},

(2.19)

where

h:=m^{-1}\asymp n^{-1/d},

(2.20)

and where we use the notation

{\cal S}_{m}:=\{0,\dots,m-1\},

(2.21)

and thus

{\cal S}_{m}^{d}:=\{0,\dots,m-1\}^{d}
.

If
k=(\tilde{k}_{d},k_{d})
, where

\tilde{k}_{d}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d-1})\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d-1}
and
k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}
,
we have

\quad T_{k}=T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}\times[k_{d}h,(k_{d}+1)h],\quad k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}.

(2.22)

Given
f\in{\cal L}
and the point values
of
f
at the data sites in
\Lambda_{n}
, we consider the multi-linear interpolant

I_{h}^{d}f:=I^{d}_{\Lambda_{n}}f:\Omega_{d}\to\mathbb{R},

(2.23)

defined in §
2.1
.
Given the relation (
2.20
) between
h
and
n
, the upper bound (
2.9
) will follow directly
by proving the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
d\geq 1
. Then, whenever
f\in{\cal L}(\Omega_{d})
, we have

\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{p}}\leq 2dh^{r}.

(2.24)

Proof:.

We only need to show (
2.24
) for
p=1
and
p=\infty
, since the general case
then follows from the Hölder’s inequality

\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{p}}\leq\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{1}}^{1/p}\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{\infty}}^{1-1/p}.

(2.25)

We first treat the case
d=1
,
and then deal with the
case
d>1
by an induction argument. In
the univariate case, one has

T_{i}=[ih,(i+1)h],\quad i\in{\cal S}_{m}

(2.26)

and
I_{h}^{1}f
is the piecewise linear interpolant, which on each
T_{i}
coincides with the linear interpolant
of
f
at the end points

ih
and
(i+1)h
. Assuming
f\in{\cal L}(\Omega_{1})
, Lemma
2.3

says that on each interval
T_{i}
,

\|f-I_{h}^{1}f\|_{L_{\infty}(T_{i})}\leq h(f^{\prime}_{-}((i+1)h)-f^{\prime}_{+}(ih))\leq 2h.

(2.27)

Maximizing over
i
, results in (
2.24
)
for the value of
p=\infty
.

We next turn to bounding the error in
L_{1}
. Lemma
2.3

also gives

\|f-I_{h}^{1}f\|_{L_{1}(T_{i})}\leq h^{2}(f^{\prime}_{-}((i+1)h)-f^{\prime}_{+}(ih)),

(2.28)

and after summing over
i
and using the convexity of
f
, see (
1.21
), we obtain that

\|f-I_{h}^{1}f\|_{L_{1}(\Omega_{1})}\leq h^{2}(f^{\prime}_{-}(1)-f^{\prime}_{+}(0))\leq 2h^{2},

(2.29)

which is (
2.24
)
in the case
p=1
. Due to (
2.25
), estimate (
2.24
) is proven in the case
d=1
.

We turn now to the general case
d>1
. We first observe that the restriction of a
multivariate convex function
f\in{\cal L}(\Omega_{d})
, obtained by freezing some of its coordinates,
remains a convex function of the rest of the variables. Moreover, at every point, the restricted
f
has all its
subgradients bounded by
1
. In particular, when we fix the first
(d-1)
coordinates, the univariate function
t\mapsto g(t):=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},t)
belongs to
{\cal L}(\Omega_{1})
. Let us assume that (
2.24
) holds for dimension
(d-1)
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
and seek to establish it for dimension
d
.

Case
p=1
:
For
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}
, consider the cell

T_{k}

and let
x=(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})\in T_{k}
. We define

\theta(x_{d}):=h^{-1}(x_{d}-k_{d}h)\in[0,1],\quad{\rm when}\quad x_{d}\in[k_{d}h,(k_{d}+1)h].

(2.30)

Using the inductive definition of
I^{d}_{h}f
, we can bound the interpolation error by

\begin{array}[]{ll}|f(x)-I^{d}_{h}f(x)|&=|f(x)-(1-\theta(x_{d}))[I^{d-1}_{h}f(\cdot,k_{d}h)](\tilde{x}_{d})-\theta(x_{d})[I^{d-1}_{h}f(\cdot,(k_{d}+1)h)](\tilde{x}_{d})|\\
\\
&\leq E_{1}(x)+E_{2}(x)+E_{3}(x).\\
\end{array}

Here, the first error term
E_{1}
, is defined for
x=(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})\in T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}\times[k_{d}h,(k_{d}+1)h]
by

E_{1}(x):=|f(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})-(1-\theta(x_{d}))f(\tilde{x}_{d},k_{d}h)-\theta(x_{d})f(\tilde{x}_{d},(k_{d}+1)h)|.

(2.31)

It is the one dimensional interpolation error in the direction
x_{d}
when using the exact values of
f
on the faces
T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}
. The other two error terms are

E_{2}(x):=(1-\theta(x_{d}))|f(\tilde{x}_{d},k_{d}h)-I^{d-1}_{h}[f(\cdot,k_{d}h)](\tilde{x}_{d})|,

(2.32)

and

E_{3}(x):=\theta(x_{d})|f(\tilde{x}_{d},(k_{d}+1)h)-I^{d-1}_{h}[f(\cdot,(k_{d}+1)h)](\tilde{x}_{d})|.

(2.33)

They account for the error of
(d-1)
dimensional interpolation of the
convex functions
f(\cdot,k_{d}h)
and

f(\cdot,(k_{d}+1)h)
, respectively.
In summary, we have

\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{1}}\leq\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}E_{1}+\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}E_{2}+\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}E_{3}=:\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}+\Sigma_{3}.

(2.34)

We now bound the three sums. For the first sum, we write

\Sigma_{1}:=\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}E_{1}(x)dx=\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}\left[\int_{k_{d}h}^{(k_{d}+1)h}E_{1}(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})dx_{d}\right]d\tilde{x}_{d},

(2.35)

and observe that for each fixed
\tilde{x}_{d}\in T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}
, the univariate integral is the
L_{1}
error of interpolation of the univariate function
f(\tilde{x}_{d},\cdot)\in{\cal L}(\Omega_{1})
on
[k_{d}h,(k_{d}+1)h]
. We first sum over
k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}
for fixed
\tilde{k}_{d}
and then over all
\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d-1}
,
which gives

\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d-1}}\,\,\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}\|f(\tilde{x}_{d},\cdot)-I_{h}^{1}f(\tilde{x}_{d},\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\Omega_{1})}d\tilde{x}_{d}\leq 2h^{2},

(2.36)

where we have used the result
for
d=1
.

Next, consider the second sum
\Sigma_{2}
.
Each summand in
\Sigma_{2}
is

\int_{T_{k}}E_{2}(x)dx=\Bigl(\int_{k_{d}h}^{(k_{d}+1)h}(1-\theta(x_{d}))dx_{d}\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}|f(\tilde{x}_{d},k_{d}h)-[I^{d-1}_{h}f(\cdot,k_{d}h)](\tilde{x}_{d})|d\tilde{x}_{d}\Bigr).

(2.37)

The first factor has value
\frac{h}{2}
and the second factor is the
L_{1}
error of interpolation of the
(d-1)
dimensional function
f(\cdot,k_{d}h)

on
T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}
.
Similarly to what we did in the estimate for
\Sigma_{1}
and using (
2.37
), we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}E_{2}(x)dx=\sum_{k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d-1}}\,\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}|f(\tilde{x}_{d},k_{d}h)-[I^{d-1}_{h}f(\cdot,k_{d}h)](\tilde{x}_{d})|d\tilde{x}_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}}\frac{h}{2}\|f(\cdot,k_{d}h)-I^{d-1}_{h}f(\cdot,k_{d}h)\|_{L_{1}(\Omega_{d-1})}.

Note that
f(\cdot,k_{d}h)

belongs to
{\cal L}(\Omega_{d-1})
. By the induction hypothesis, we have

\Sigma_{2}\leq\sum_{k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{m}}\frac{h}{2}\cdot 2(d-1)h^{2}=(d-1)h^{2}.

(2.38)

The estimate for the third sum
\Sigma_{3}
in (
2.34
) is the same as (
2.38
), this time applying the inductive hypothesis for the
(d-1)
dimensional function

f(\cdot,(k_{d}+1)h)
, namely,

\Sigma_{3}:=\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}E_{3}(x)dx\leq(d-1)h^{2}.

(2.39)

It follows from (
2.34
), (
2.36
), (
2.38
), and (
2.39
) that

\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{1}}\leq 2h^{2}+2(d-1)h^{2}=2dh^{2},

(2.40)

which concludes the proof in the case
p=1
.

Case
p=\infty
:
In this case, we only use the fact that the functions from
{\cal L}
are Lipschitz functions and do not utilize the fact that they are convex. We could therefore invoke classical results on multivariate spline approximation of functions such as
[
6
]
where general estimates are stated in terms of moduli of smoothness, but we present an elementary proof for completeness.

For each
k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}
, let
V_{k}
denote the set of vertices of
T_{k}
. Let us observe that the value
I^{d}_{h}f(x)
,
x\in T_{k}
, is a convex combination of the values of
f
at these vertices, namely

I_{h}^{d}f(x)=\sum_{v\in V_{k}}\alpha_{v}(x)f(v),\quad\sum_{v\in V_{k}}\alpha_{v}(x)=1,\quad\alpha_{v}(x)\geq 0.

(2.41)

Since both
f
and
I_{h}^{d}f
are continuous, there is
\bar{x}\in T_{k}
for some index
k
, such that

\displaystyle\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|f(\bar{x})-I_{h}f(\bar{x})|\leq\sum_{v\in V_{k}}\alpha_{v}(x)|f(\bar{x})-f(v)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{k}}\alpha_{v}(x)|\bar{x}-v|\leq\sqrt{d}h,

where we have used

|\bar{x}-v|\leq\sqrt{d}h
whenever
v,\bar{x}\in T_{k}
.
We can rewrite the estimate above as

\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 2dh,

(2.42)

so that we have the same constant as in the case
p=1
. The result for
1<p<\infty
then follows from (
2.25
). This completes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Note that if we consider only Lipschitz functions with a Lipschitz constant
1
, we can obtain the upper bound
\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq Ch
, with
C
only depending on
d
, and arrive at the known result

\|f-I_{h}^{d}f\|_{L_{p}}\leq Ch.

Observe that the gain of the extra factor
h^{1/p}
in the error bound for functions from the class
{\cal L}
is due to the convexity of
f
, exploited in the
L_{1}
estimate, where we gain an extra factor
h
.

Lemma
2.4
serves to prove the upper bounds on tensor product sampling stated in Theorem
2.2
. Namely, we have proved (
2.9
), from which (
2.10
) follows, see (
1.12
), (
1.13
), and (
1.14
), for all values

n=(m+1)^{d}
, and therefore for all
n\geq 1
, according to Remark
2.1
.

2.4
Lower bounds for tensor product grids: proof of (ii) in Theorem
2.2

Let
\Lambda:=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{d}
be any tensor product grid
in
{\cal T}_{n}
. Since
m_{1}\cdots m_{d}=n
, at least one of the sets
\Gamma_{k}
consists of

m:=m_{k}\leq n^{1/d}
points, and therefore there is an interval
(a,b)\subset[0,1]
of length
at least
h:=(m+1)^{-1}
that does not contain any points from
\Gamma_{k}
. Let
a^{*}:=(a+b)/2
be the midpoint of this interval and define the functions
\pi,\tilde{\pi}:\Omega_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, as follows

\pi(x):=\pi(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=|x_{k}-a^{*}|,\quad{\rm and}\quad\tilde{\pi}(x):=\max\Big\{\frac{h}{2},\pi(x)\Big\},\quad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\Omega.

Both of these functions are in
{\cal L}
and their values agree at all data sites. We also have that the difference
E:=\tilde{\pi}-\pi
satisfies

E(x)\geq\frac{h}{4},\quad x\in\tilde{\Omega}:=\Big\{x\in\Omega:|x_{k}-a^{*}|\leq\frac{h}{4}\Big\}.

(2.43)

Since
|\tilde{\Omega}|=\frac{h}{2}
, this gives that

\rho({\cal L},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\|E\|_{L_{p}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\frac{h}{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}}\frac{h}{4}\geq cm^{-(1+\frac{1}{p})}\geq cn^{-r/d},

(2.44)

where the constant
c
depends only on
d
. This proves the
lower bound (
2.11
) in (ii).

2.5
Lower bounds for sampling numbers of
{\cal L}
: proof of (iii), (iv), and (v) in Theorem
2.2

The upper bounds in (iii), (iv), and (v) of Theorem
2.2
follow from what we have already proved for tensor product grids. To prove the lower bounds, let
\Lambda
be any set of
n
sampling sites (not necessarily on a tensor grid).

We first consider the case
d=1
and observe that there
exists an interval
(a,b)\subset[0,1]
of length at least
h:=(n+1)^{-1}

that does not contain any point from
\Lambda
. We then proceed as
in the proof of (ii), by introducing the functions
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}

(that are now univariate, i.e.
d=1
), and noting that (
2.44
) becomes

\rho({\cal L}(\Omega_{1}),\Lambda)_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-r}.

(2.45)

Therefore, since
\Lambda
is arbitrary,

\rho_{n}({\cal L}(\Omega_{1}))_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-r},

(2.46)

for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
, which is the lower bound in (
2.12
) when
d=1
.

Next, we consider the case
d>1
and
p=\infty
.
By standard packing arguments, there exists a point
x^{*}\in\Omega
and a positive constant
c
such that all the points in
\Lambda
are outside the ball
B

with center
x^{*}
and radius
\delta=cn^{-1/d}
, and this ball is completely contained in
\Omega_{d}
. The constant
c

depends only on
d
. We then define two functions
\phi,\tilde{\phi}:\Omega_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

\phi(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}|x-x^{*}|,\quad{\rm and}\quad\tilde{\phi}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\max\{\delta,\phi(x)\},\quad x\in\Omega_{d}.

(2.47)

Note that
\phi,\tilde{\phi}\in{\cal L}
and their values are in agreement at all data sites. Moreover, their difference satisfies

\|\tilde{\phi}-\phi\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega_{d})}\geq\tilde{\phi}(x^{*})-\phi(x^{*})=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}.

(2.48)

It follows that

\rho({\cal L}(\Omega_{d}),\Lambda)_{L_{\infty}}\geq\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{d}}\geq cn^{-1/d},

(2.49)

where
c
depends only on
d
. Since
\Lambda
is an arbitrary set of data sites, this proves the
lower bound

\rho_{n}({\cal L}(\Omega_{d}))_{L_{\infty}}\geq cn^{-1/d},

(2.50)

which is the lower bound in (
2.12
) when
p=\infty
.

Next, we consider the case
d>1
and
p=1
. In this case, we follow an approach already discussed in
[
9
]
, where we chop off (while preserving the convexity) parts of a particular function that are away from the given data sites. More precisely, we consider the function
\psi:\Omega_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as

\psi(x):=\frac{1}{d+1}|x|^{2}=\frac{1}{d+1}(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}),

(2.51)

which belongs to
{\cal L}
. Let
\Lambda
be any set of
n
sample sites in
\Omega_{d}
.
For an appropriate constant
c
that depends only on
d
, we can always find

2n
points
y^{1},\cdots,y^{2n}
such that the balls
B(y^{i},h)
with center
y^{i}
and radius

h=cn^{-1/d}

are all disjoint and contained in

\Omega_{d}
. It follows that there exist
n
points
y^{i}

such that the balls
B(y^{i},h)
do not contain any points from
\Lambda
.
Up to re-indexing we denote these points by
y^{1},\dots,y^{n}
.

We will construct a function
\tilde{\psi}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\tilde{\psi}\in{\cal L}
, that differs from
\psi
for
x\in B(y^{i},h)
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and agrees with
\psi
in the rest of
\Omega
, and thus at all data sites in
\Lambda
. We notice that when
x
is on the boundary of
B(y^{i},h)
we have

(d+1)\psi(x)=|x|^{2}=|y^{i}+x-y^{i}|^{2}=(|x-y^{i}|^{2}-|y^{i}|^{2}+2y^{i}\cdot x)=(h^{2}-|y^{i}|^{2}+2y^{i}\cdot x),

and therefore
\psi
agrees on this boundary
with the affine function

\psi_{i}(x)=a_{i}+\langle b_{i},x\rangle,\quad a_{i}=\frac{1}{d+1}(h^{2}-|y^{i}|^{2}),\quad b_{i}=\frac{2}{d+1}y^{i}.

We define
\tilde{\psi}
by

\tilde{\psi}(x):=\begin{cases}\psi_{i}(x),&\quad x\in B(y^{i},h),\quad i=1,\dots,n,\\
\\
\psi(x),&\quad x\in\Omega\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{n}B(y^{i},h).\end{cases}

(2.52)

By construction,
\tilde{\psi}
is convex
and belongs to
{\cal L}
. For

x\in B(y^{i},h)
, the difference between
\tilde{\psi}
and
\psi
is

\tilde{\psi}(x)-\psi(x)=\psi_{i}(x)-\psi(x)=\frac{1}{d+1}(h^{2}-|x-y^{i}|^{2})\geq 0.

Thus, this difference is larger than
\frac{3}{4}\frac{h^{2}}{d+1}
on the ball
B(y^{i},h/2)
for each
i=1,\dots,n
. It follows that

\|\tilde{\psi}-\psi\|_{L_{1}(\Omega_{d})}=\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\psi}(x)-\psi(x))dx\geq ch^{2}\geq cn^{-2/d},

(2.53)

where
c
depends only on
d
. It follows that

\rho({\cal L},\Lambda)_{L_{1}}\geq cn^{-2/d}.

(2.54)

Since
\Lambda
is arbitrary, this shows that

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{1}}\geq cn^{-2/d},

(2.55)

which is the lower bound in (
2.12
) when
p=1
.

Next, we prove (v). We use the functions
\phi,\tilde{\phi},\psi,\tilde{\psi}
defined above. For each
1<p<\infty
, we
know that

\|\phi-\tilde{\phi}\|_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{p})},

(2.56)

because
|\phi-\tilde{\phi}|\geq cn^{-1/d}
on a set of measure
cn^{-1}
.
We also know that

\|\psi-\tilde{\psi}\|_{L_{p}}\geq\|\psi-\tilde{\psi}\|_{L_{1}}\geq cn^{-2/d}.

(2.57)

This shows that a lower bound for the sampling numbers
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
is given
by
c\max\{n^{-(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{p})},n^{-2/d}\}
, which is exactly (
2.14
).

Finally, we prove (
2.13
) of (iv).
The upper bound is a consequence of (i). In order to obtain the lower bound, we use the fact that
\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}\geq d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}

and argue that the Kolmogorov widths, see (
1.15
), satisfy

d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}\asymp n^{-\frac{3}{2d}}.

(2.58)

We only need to consider the case
d\geq 2
since the one-dimensional result was proved in
[
10
]
, see (
7.4
). In the case
d\geq 2
, for
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the “hinge” function:

\eta_{\omega,b}(x):=(\omega\cdot x+b)_{+}=\max\{\omega\cdot x+b,0\},\quad x\in\Omega_{d}.

(2.59)

Note that for a dimension dependent constant
c
, we have that whenever
|\omega|,|b|\leq c
, the function
\eta_{\omega,b}\in{\cal L}
. Hence, if we define

{\cal L}_{0}:=\overline{\text{conv}\{\pm\,\eta_{\omega,b},~|\omega|,|b|\leq c\}}

(2.60)

to be the closure of the convex hull of
\pm\,\eta_{\omega,b}
, it follows that
{\cal L}_{0}\subset\text{conv}\{-{\cal L}\cup{\cal L}\}
. On the other hand, by the triangle inequality,
one has

d_{n}(\overline{\text{conv}\{-{\cal L}\cup{\cal L}\}})_{L_{2}}=d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}.

(2.61)

Therefore, we have
d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}\geq d_{n}({\cal L}_{0})_{L_{2}}
. We can now apply Theorem 9 from
[
16
]
, where it was shown that

d_{n}({\cal L}_{0})_{L_{2}}\gtrsim n^{-\frac{3}{2d}}.

(2.62)

This completes the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

3
Optimal tensor product sampling for
{\cal B}

In this section, we prove the following result, again using the notation
r:=1+\frac{1}{p}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For the model class
{\cal B}={\cal B}(\Omega_{d})
, we have the following results:

(i)
For each
1\leq p<\infty
, and
d\geq 1
, there is a non-uniform
tensor product grid
\Lambda_{n}\in{\cal T}_{n}
, depending on
p
, such that,

\rho({\cal B},\Lambda_{n})_{L_{p}}\leq\rho^{\ell}({\cal B},\Lambda_{n})_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d},

(3.1)

with the constant
C
depending only on
d
and
p
. It follows that for
1\leq p<\infty
and every
n\geq 1
,

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\leq\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d}.

(3.2)

(ii)
For any tensor product grid
\Lambda\in{\cal T}_{n}\subset\Omega_{d}
,
d\geq 1
, any
1\leq p<\infty
, and any
n\geq 1
, we have the lower bounds

\rho^{\ell}({\cal B},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}\geq\rho({\cal B},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-r/d},

(3.3)

where
c
depends only on
d
.

(iii)
In the case
d=1
and
1\leq p<\infty

we have

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\asymp\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-r/d},

(3.4)

where the equivalency constants depend only on
d
and
p
.
Also, the estimate (
3.4
) holds if
d>1
and
p=1
.

(iv)

When
p=2
and
d\geq 1
, one has

\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{2}}\asymp n^{-r/d}=n^{-\frac{3}{2d}},

(3.5)

where the equivalency constants depend only on
d
.

(v)

When
1<p<\infty
and
d>1
, one has

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-\tilde{r}/d},\quad\tilde{r}:=\min\{2,1+d/p\}<r,

(3.6)

where
c
depends only on
d
.

One main difference between Theorem
3.1

and Theorem
2.2
is that non uniform tensor product
grids are required. More precisely, we need sampling grids
that get finer near the boundary of
\Omega
. The reason for this is that we no longer have a uniform bound on the subgradient of functions
f\in{\cal B}
. However, because of convexity, we know that, the subgradients can only get large
near the boundary. Another difference is that the non uniform tensor product grid depends on the value
p
of the
L_{p}
norm in which we measure error. Otherwise, the proof given below follows the lines
of the proof for
{\cal L}
.

Note that the value
p=\infty
is excluded
(whereas all rates of decay are the same for both classes

{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
when
p<\infty
). In fact, it is easily seen that
when
p=\infty
, the sampling numbers do not decay towards
0
,
that is,

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp\rho^{\ell}_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp 1.

(3.7)

Consider, for example, the case
d=1
and observe that
for any data sites
0\leq x^{1}<\cdots<x^{n}\leq 1
, we can
create two functions in
{\cal B}
that agree at these points and
differ by
1/2
in the
L_{\infty}
norm. If
x^{1}>0
,
we take the functions
\Bigl(1-\frac{x}{x^{1}}\Bigr)_{+}
and
\Bigl(1-2\frac{x}{x^{1}}\Bigr)_{+}
, and in the case
x^{1}=0
,
we replace
x^{1}
by
x^{2}
in the above definitions. This example can be easily modified to create functions with the same property in any dimension
d>1
.

Observe that since
{\cal L}\subset{\cal B}
, we only need to prove part (i), since the other statements in (ii), (iii) and (iv) will follow from the lower bounds that were proved for
{\cal L}
in the previous section. Therefore, we limit ourselves to the proof of (i) as we proceed forward in this section.

3.1
Upper bounds for tensor product grids: proof of Theorem
3.1
, (i),
d=1

We first prove the upper bound
(
3.1
) when
d=1
.
We begin by noting that
for every univariate function

g\in{\cal B}
, any
t\in(0,1)
, one
has the upper and lower bounds

-\frac{2}{t}\leq g_{-}^{\prime}(t)\leq g_{+}^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{2}{1-t}.

(3.8)

Indeed, since
\|g\|_{L_{\infty}([0,1])}\leq 1
, these bounds follow from the definition (
1.18
) of the subgradient, applied to
y=0
,
x=t
, and

y=1
,
x=t
, respectively.

We fix the value of
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We define the sampling sites
t_{k}
as follows:

t_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{p+1},\quad k=0,\ldots,m,

(3.9)

with
t_{0}=0
,
t_{m}=\frac{1}{2}
,
and

t_{2m-k}:=1-t_{k},\quad k=0,\ldots,m-1,

(3.10)

with
t_{2m}=1
, which are thus symmetric with respect to
\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, the set of data sites is
\Lambda_{n}:=\{t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{2m}\}
and their number is
n=2m+1
.

We consider the interpolation operator
I_{n}:=I_{n}^{1}:=I_{\Lambda_{n}}
, which assigns to any
f\in{\cal B}(\Omega_{1})
the continuous piecewise linear interpolant of

f
at the
n
points
t_{0},\dots,t_{2m}
.
In order to prove (
3.1
), we will show that
for any
1\leq p<\infty
and
f\in{\cal B}(\Omega_{1})
, we have

\|f-I_{n}f\|_{L_{p}(\Omega_{1})}\leq Cn^{-r},

(3.11)

where
C
depends only on
p
.

We begin by noting that for
T_{k}:=[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
,
k=1,\ldots,m-1
, we have

|T_{k}|=t_{k+1}-t_{k}\asymp k^{p}m^{-p-1},

(3.12)

with constants in this equivalence and the inequalities that follow in this proof depending only on
p
.
The local estimate of
Lemma
2.3
applied to
T_{k}
gives for
k=1,\dots,m-1
,

\displaystyle\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{k})}\leq|T_{k}|^{p+1}(f^{\prime}_{-}(t_{k+1})-f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k}))^{p}\lesssim m^{-(p+1)^{2}}k^{p(p+1)}(f^{\prime}_{-}(t_{k+1})-f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k}))^{p},

(3.13)

where the multiplicative constants in
\lesssim
here and later in this section only depend on
p
.
For the extreme left interval
T_{0}=[0,\frac{1}{2}m^{-p-1}]
we have

\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{0})}\leq|T_{0}|\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{\infty}(T_{0})}\leq 2^{p-1}m^{-p-1},

(3.14)

because both
f
and
{\cal I}_{n}f
have
L_{\infty}
norms at most one.

We next focus on
[0,1/2]
and use the above estimates, together with (
3.13
), to obtain

\displaystyle\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}([0,1/2])}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{k})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle m^{-p-1}+m^{-(p+1)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k^{p(p+1)}[f^{\prime}_{-}(t_{k+1})-f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})]^{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{-p-1}+m^{-(p+1)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left[k^{(p+1)}(f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k+1})-f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k}))\right]^{p},

where
we have used that
f^{\prime}_{-}(t_{k+1})\leq f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k+1})
, due to the convexity of
f
.
We bound the sum in the second term by

\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left[k^{p+1}(f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k+1})-f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k}))\right]^{p}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k^{p+1}[f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k+1})-f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})]\right)^{p}=:S^{p},

(3.15)

which gives

\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}([0,1/2])}\lesssim m^{-p-1}+m^{-(p+1)^{2}}S^{p}.

(3.16)

To bound
S
, we perform summation by parts. Let
K<m
be the largest index
k
for which
f_{+}^{\prime}(t_{k})<0
. Summing by parts, we obtain

S\lesssim(m-1)^{p+1}f^{\prime}_{+}\left(t_{m}\right)-f^{\prime}_{+}\left(t_{1}\right)-\sum_{k=2}^{m-1}f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})[k^{p+1}-(k-1)^{p+1}].

(3.17)

We use the estimate (
3.8
) to obtain the bounds

f_{+}^{\prime}(t_{m})=f^{\prime}_{+}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq 4,

(3.18)

and

-f_{+}^{\prime}(t_{1})=-f^{\prime}_{+}\left(\frac{1}{2}m^{-p-1}\right)\leq 4m^{p+1}.

(3.19)

Therefore,

S\leq 8m^{p+1}+\Sigma,

(3.20)

where

\Sigma:=\sum_{k=2}^{K}|f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})|[k^{p+1}-(k-1)^{p+1}].

(3.21)

Since

k^{p+1}-(k-1)^{p+1}\asymp k^{p}
,
we have

\Sigma\lesssim\sum_{k=2}^{K}|f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})|k^{p}.

(3.22)

By convexity, we note that
for
k\in[2,K]
, one has

k^{p}m^{-p-1}|f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})|\asymp|T_{k-1}||f^{\prime}_{+}(t_{k})|\leq f(t_{k-1})-f(t_{k}),

(3.23)

and therefore

\Sigma\lesssim m^{p+1}
because
\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}\leq 1
.

It follows then from (
3.20
) that

S\lesssim m^{p+1},

(3.24)

and substituting this into (
3.16
) gives

\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}([0,1/2])}\lesssim m^{-p-1}.

(3.25)

The same inequality holds for
\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}([1/2,1])}
, and since
n\asymp m
, we reach (
3.11
) when
d=1
.

3.2
Upper bounds for tensor product grids: proof of Theorem
3.1
, (i),
d>1

The proof for
d>1
is similar to the proof
of Theorem
2.2
for the model class
{\cal L}
since it uses induction on the dimension
d
and a multilinear interpolation operator, based on a tensor product grid. Let
\Gamma:=\{t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{2m}\}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, be the set of points
that were introduced in the case
d=1
, see (
3.9
)-(
3.10
). We define our tensor product grid for
[0,1]^{d}
as
\Lambda_{n}:=\Gamma\times\cdots\times\Gamma
. Notice that the spacing of the grid points is the same in each coordinate direction. In other words, the data sites in
\Lambda_{n}
are

x^{k}=(t_{k_{1}},\dots,t_{k_{d}}),\quad k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d},

(3.26)

where
{\cal S}_{2m}=\{0,\dots,2m-1\}
.
The number of data sites is now

n=(2m+1)^{d}.

(3.27)

We denote by
I_{n}^{d}:=I_{\Lambda_{n}}
the interpolation operator given in §
2.1
.
We denote by
T_{k}
the rectangular cell

T_{k}:=[t_{k_{1}},t_{k_{1}+1}]\times\cdots\times[t_{k_{d}},t_{k_{d}+1}],\quad k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d},

(3.28)

and if
k=(\tilde{k}_{d},k_{d})
, where

\tilde{k}_{d}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d-1})\in\{0,\dots,2m-1\}^{d-1}
,
we have

\quad T_{k}=T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}\times[t_{k_{d}},t_{k_{d}+1}].

(3.29)

Given
f\in{\cal B}(\Omega_{d})
,
I_{n}^{d}f
can be
described inductively for
x=(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})\in T_{k}
by

I^{d}_{n}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=(1-\theta(x_{d}))[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})](\tilde{x}_{d})+\theta(x_{d})[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}+1})](\tilde{x}_{d}),

(3.30)

where

n:=(2m+1)^{d},\quad n^{\prime}:=(2m+1)^{d-1},\quad\theta(x_{d}):=\frac{x_{d}-t_{k_{d}}}{t_{k_{d}+1}-t_{k_{d}}}\in[0,1],\quad x_{d}\in[t_{k_{d}},t_{k_{d}+1}].

(3.31)

Before going further, let us observe that in the case of
p=\infty
,
we have

\|f-I^{d}_{n}f\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 2,

(3.32)

since
I^{d}_{n}
does not increase the

L_{\infty}
norm. As already explained, we cannot expect any decay of the error in the
L_{\infty}
norm.

In order to prove (
3.1
), we will show that
for any
1\leq p<\infty
and
f\in{\cal B}(\Omega_{d})
,
d\geq 1
, we have

\|f-I^{d}_{n}f\|_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d},\quad n\geq 1,

(3.33)

where
C
depends only on
d
and
p
.

To prove (
3.33
),
we use induction on
d
. We have already proved the validity of (
3.33
) for
d=1
. Assume
that (
3.33
) holds for dimension
(d-1)
.
We note that for
k=(\tilde{k}_{d},k_{d})\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d}

and
x=(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})\in T_{k}=T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}\times[t_{k_{d}},t_{k_{d}+1}]
, we have that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|f(x)-I^{d}_{n}f(x)\|_{L_{p}(T_{k})}&=\|f(x)-(1-\theta(x_{d}))[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})](\tilde{x}_{d})-\theta(x_{d})[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}+1})](\tilde{x}_{d})\|_{L_{p}(T_{k})}\\
\\
&\leq\|E_{1}(x)\|_{L_{p}(T_{k})}+\|E_{2}(x)\|_{L_{p}(T_{k})}+\|E_{3}(x)\|_{L_{p}(T_{k})},\end{array}

where we have used the notation,

E_{1}(x):=f(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})-(1-\theta(x_{d}))f(\tilde{x}_{d},t_{k_{d}})-\theta(x_{d})f(\tilde{x}_{d},t_{k_{d}+1}),

(3.34)

E_{2}(x):=(1-\theta(x_{d}))(f(\tilde{x}_{d},t_{k_{d}})-[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})](\tilde{x}_{d})),

(3.35)

and

E_{3}(x):=\theta(x_{d})(f(\tilde{x}_{d},t_{k_{d}+1})-[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}+1})](\tilde{x}_{d})).

(3.36)

We thus have

\displaystyle\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(\Omega_{d})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d}}\|f-I_{n}f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{k})}\leq\sum_{k\in{\cal M}_{d}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{3}\|E_{i}(x)\|_{L_{p}(T_{k})}\right]^{p}

(3.37)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4^{p-1}\left(\sum_{k\in{\cal M}_{d}}\|E_{1}(x)\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{k})}+\sum_{k\in{\cal M}_{d}}\|E_{2}(x)\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{k})}+\sum_{k\in{\cal M}_{d}}\|E_{3}(x)\|^{p}_{L_{p}(T_{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle 4^{p-1}(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}+\Sigma_{3}).

.

For the first sum, we write

\Sigma_{1}:=\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}|E_{1}(x)|^{p}\,dx=\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d-1}}\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}\left[\int_{t_{k_{d}}}^{t_{k_{d}+1}}|E_{1}(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})|^{p}\,dx_{d}\right]d\tilde{x}_{d},

(3.38)

and observe that for each fixed
\tilde{x}_{d}\in T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}
, the univariate integral is the
p
-th power of the
L_{p}
error on
[t_{k_{d}},t_{k_{d}+1}]
between the univariate function
g:=f(\tilde{x}_{d},\cdot)
and its interpolant
I_{m}^{1}g
. Notice that
g
is in
{\cal B}(\Omega_{1})
. To evaluate
\Sigma_{1}
, we sum over
k_{d}
for fixed
\tilde{k}_{d}
and then over all
\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal M}_{d-1}
. Using the univariate interpolation error estimate for
g
, see (
3.11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d-1}}\,\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}\sum_{k_{d}=0}^{2m-1}\left[\int_{t_{k_{d}}}^{t_{k_{d}+1}}|E_{1}(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})|^{p}\,dx_{d}\right]d\tilde{x}_{d}=\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d-1}}\,\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}|E_{1}(\tilde{x}_{d},x_{d})|^{p}\,dx_{d}\right]d\tilde{x}_{d}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle m^{-p-1}\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d-1}}\,\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}1\,d\tilde{x}_{d}=m^{-p-1}\lesssim n^{-rp/d},

where we have used that
m\asymp n^{1/d}
.

Each summand in the second sum
\Sigma_{2}
is

\displaystyle\int_{T_{k}}|E_{2}(x)|^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl(\int_{t_{k_{d}}}^{t_{k_{d}+1}}(1-\theta(x_{d}))^{p}dx_{d}\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{T_{\tilde{k}_{d}}}\|f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})-[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})](\tilde{x}_{d})\|^{p}d\tilde{x}_{d}\Bigr)

(3.39)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle m^{-1}\|f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})-[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})]\|_{L_{p}(T_{\tilde{k}_{d}})}^{p},

where we have used that
\int_{t_{k_{d}}}^{t_{k_{d}+1}}(1-\theta(x_{d}))^{p}dx_{d}\lesssim|t_{k_{d}+1}-t_{k_{d}}|\leq m^{-1}
.
Similarly to what we did in the estimate for
\Sigma_{1}
and using (
3.39
), we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d}}\int_{T_{k}}|E_{2}(x)|^{p}dx\lesssim\sum_{k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}}m^{-1}\sum_{\tilde{k}_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}^{d-1}}\|f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})-[I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})]\|_{L_{p}(T_{\tilde{k}_{d}})}^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}}m^{-1}\|f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})-I^{d-1}_{n^{\prime}}(f\cdot,t_{k_{d}})\|^{p}_{L_{p}(\Omega_{d-1})}\lesssim\sum_{k_{d}\in{\cal S}_{2m}}m^{-1}[n^{\prime}]^{-\frac{rp}{d-1}}\lesssim n^{-rp/d},

where we have used the induction hypothesis for the
(d-1)
dimensional convex function
f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}})
.

The estimate for the third sum
\Sigma_{3}
in (
3.37
) is handled the same way as the estimate for
\Sigma_{2}
, this time applying the inductive hypothesis for the
(d-1)
dimensional function

f(\cdot,t_{k_{d}+1})
, and obtaining that

\Sigma_{3}\lesssim n^{-rp/d}.

(3.40)

Using the estimates for
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}
in (
3.37
) gives (
3.33
).

We have therefore proved (
3.1
).
The estimate (
3.2
) follows for all values

n=(2m+1)^{d}
, and, on account of Remark
2.1
, therefore for all
n\geq 1
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

4
Sharper upper bounds for the sampling numbers of
{\cal L}

It turns out that sampling on tensor product grids, as analyzed in the previous two sections, does not give the optimal sampling rates for the model classes
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
. In this section, we prove the following new upper bounds for the sampling rates
of
{\cal L}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
1<p<\infty
. Then, for any
n\geq 2
, we have

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\leq C\kappa(n,p,d):=C\begin{cases}n^{-(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d})}(\log n)^{\frac{2}{p}},&p>d,\\
n^{-\frac{2}{d}}(\log{n})^{\frac{3}{d}},&p=d,\\
n^{-\frac{2}{d}}(\log{n})^{\frac{2}{d}},&p<d,\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
C
depends only on
p
and
d
2
2

2

Throughout this paper
\log
indicates the logarithm to the base
2
.
.

The bounds (
4.1
) are significantly better than those obtained using tensor product grids.
Moreover, these new bounds are optimal up to the logarithmic factors, since we have
proved in (iv) of Theorem
2.2
that there is a
c>0
depending only on
d
and
p
such that for
\tilde{r}=\min\{2,1+d/p\}
we have

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\geq cn^{-\tilde{r}/d},\quad n\geq 1.

(4.2)

Recall that the upper bound
Cn^{-\tilde{r}/d}
for this sampling rate is attained without a logarithmic factor when
p=1
and
p=\infty
, see (iii) of Theorem
2.2
.

We fix
d\in\{1,2,\dots\}
for the remainder of this section. All constants that appear below in this section will depend only on
d
. In order to establish Theorem
4.1
, we will use particular sets of
n

non tensor product data sites, described in the next subsection.

4.1
Data sites used for sharper upper bounds for the sampling numbers of
{\cal L}

In this subsection, we prove for every
n\geq 2
the existence of special data sites
\Lambda^{*}_{n}:=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}\subset\Omega
,
having the following two properties:

Property 1:
Any closed convex set
S\subset\Omega
with no data site
x^{i}
in its interior
{\rm int}(S)
satisfies the following estimate for its
volume
|S|
,

|S|\leq C\frac{\log n}{n},\quad n\geq 2,

(4.3)

for some constant
C
.

Property 2:
For all
x\in\Omega
, one has

\min_{i=1,\dots,n}|x-x^{i}|\leq Cn^{-1/d},\quad n\geq 2.

(4.4)

Both constants
C
depend only on
d
.

We shall use probabilistic methods to prove the existence of sets
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
that satisfy these two properties. Consider the tensor product grid
\Lambda_{m}
with spacing
1/m
, where
m
is the largest integer such that
m^{d}\leq n
. We continue to use our previous notation for such tensor product grids, in particular the notation
T_{k}
for the cells,
k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}
, produced by the grid (see (
2.19
)). For each
k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}
we choose a point
x_{k}
uniformly at random from the cell
T_{k}
. The number of these randomly chosen points is

c^{\prime}n<m^{d}\leq n,\quad c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(d).

(4.5)

We additionally choose
n-m^{d}
points chosen from
\Omega_{d}
at random and let
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
denote the corresponding (random) set of points. It is clear that
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
automatically satisfies Property 2, since for each
k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}
the cell
T_{k}
contains a point from
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
. The following result shows that with positive probability it also satisfies Property 1.

Proposition 4.2
.

For each
d=1,2,\dots
, there are constants
L,A,\alpha>0
, depending only upon
d
, such that for every
n\geq 2
and every
C\geq 1
, we have

\pi(C;n,d):=\ \mathbb{P}\left(\exists S\subset\Omega:~\text{$S$ is convex, }|S|\geq C\frac{\log n}{n},~\text{and $S\cap\Lambda_{n}^{*}=\emptyset$}\right)<Ln^{A-\alpha C}.

(4.6)

In particular, the probability that
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
doesn’t satisfy Property 1 is less than
1
if
C
is sufficiently large.

The first step in the proof of Proposition
4.2
is to reduce the set of convex sets
S\subset\Omega
to a finite set of simplices. To do this, let
r\geq 1
be an integer (which will be determined later) and consider the uniform grid
\Lambda_{r}
with spacing
1/r
. We have the following simple geometric result, which essentially bounds the measure of the boundary of a convex set.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and let
S\subset\Omega_{d}
be closed convex set. Then, we have

|S\setminus\text{conv}(S\cap\Lambda_{r})|\leq 2d^{2}r^{-1}.

(4.7)

Proof:.

Note that the difference
S\setminus\text{conv}(S\cap\Lambda_{r})
is covered by the set of cubes
T_{k}
for
k\in{\cal S}_{r}^{d}
which intersect both
S
and
S^{c}
. It is known that the number of such cubes is bounded by
Cr^{d-1}
(see for instance
[
13
]
), which completes the proof since the measure of each such cube is
r^{-d}
. However, for the reader’s convenience, we give a simple proof of this bound.

Given any cube
T_{k}
,
k\in{\cal S}_{r}^{d}
, with
T_{k}\cap S\neq\emptyset
, and a coordinate direction
v\in\{\pm e_{i}:~i=1,...,d\}
, we define the height
h(T_{k},v)
of
T_{k}
in the direction
v
as follows:

h(T_{k},v):=\max\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:~T_{k+tv}\cap S\neq\emptyset\}.

(4.8)

In words, the height
h(T_{k},v)
is the maximum number of cubes one can move in the
v
-direction which still intersect
S
.

We claim that if
T_{k}
intersects both
S
and
S^{c}
, then there exists a direction
v
such that

h(T_{k},v)\leq d-1.

(4.9)

Indeed, suppose that
T_{k}
intersects both
S
and
S^{c}
. Let
y\in T_{k}\cap S^{c}
and take a hyperplane
H
separating
y
from
S
, and let
w
be the unit normal to
H
such that
w\cdot x<w\cdot y
for all
x\in S
. Let
v^{*}
be a coordinate direction such that

w\cdot v^{*}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}.

(4.10)

Such a direction can always be found by taking the coordinate of
w
with the largest absolute value.

Now, suppose that
z\in T_{k+tv^{*}}
. Observe that
z-y=tv^{*}+\rho
where
|\rho|\leq\sqrt{d}r^{-1}
. This implies that

w\cdot z=w\cdot y+tw\cdot v^{*}+w\cdot\rho\geq w\cdot y+\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}-\sqrt{d}.

(4.11)

Now, if
t\geq d
, this implies that
w\cdot z\geq w\cdot y
, and thus
z\notin S
. Hence
T_{k+tv^{*}}\cap S=\emptyset
for all
t\geq d
, which gives that
h(T_{k},v^{*})\leq d-1
as desired. This means that

\{T_{k}:~T_{k}\cap S\neq\emptyset~\text{and}~T_{k}\cap S^{c}\neq\emptyset\}\subset\bigcup_{v}\{T_{k}:~h(T_{k},v)\leq d-1\}.

(4.12)

We then observe that for each direction
v
we have

\#\{k\in{\cal S}_{r}^{d}:~h(T_{k},v)\leq d-1\}\leq dr^{d-1},

(4.13)

which in view of (
4.12
) completes the proof.

∎

We will also need the following straightforward geometric result, which will allow us to reduce the proof of Proposition
4.2
to the case that
S
is a simplex.

Lemma 4.4
.

Every closed convex set
S\subset\Omega_{d}
contains a closed simplex
\Delta
such that

|\Delta|\geq c|S|,\quad c=c(d)=\frac{2^{1-d}}{d!}.

Moreover, the vertices of
\Delta
can be taken as extreme points of
S
.

Proof:.

The lemma follows from a closely related result of Hadwiger
[
8
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we give the following proof which constructs the vertices of
\Delta
using a greedy-type procedure.
We start with the following two points from
S
.
Let
z^{0},z^{1}\in S
be points of maximal distance, i.e., such that

|z^{1}-z^{0}|=\max_{x,y\in S}|x-y|.

(4.14)

Observe that the function
f(z^{0},z^{1})=|z^{1}-z^{0}|
is a convex function on the set
S\times S
. Hence, it achieves its maximum at an extreme point of
S\times S
. Thus, we may take
z^{0}
and
z^{1}
to be extreme points of
S
.

Let
v_{1}
be the unit vector
v_{1}:=\frac{1}{|z^{1}-z^{0}|}(z^{1}-z^{0})
. Note that
S
is contained in the strip

L_{1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:z^{0}\cdot v_{1}\leq x\cdot v_{1}\leq z^{1}\cdot v_{1}\}

(4.15)

of width
|z^{1}-z^{0}|
. Indeed, if
x\in S
and
x\cdot v_{1}>z^{1}\cdot v_{1}
, then

|x-z^{0}|\geq(x-z^{0})\cdot v_{1}>(z^{1}-z^{0})\cdot v_{1}=|z^{1}-z^{0}|,

(4.16)

which contradicts the maximality of
|z^{1}-z^{0}|
. A similar argument holds if we assume that
x\in S
and
x\cdot v_{1}<z^{0}\cdot v_{1}
.

Next, we inductively construct points
z^{k}\in S
for
k=2,...,d
, as follows. Suppose that
z^{0},...,z^{k-1}
, have
been chosen. We denote by
F_{k}
the
(k-1)
-dimensional affine space spanned by
these points, i.e.,

F_{k}:=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{i}z^{i}\;:\;\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{i}=1\right\}.

(4.17)

We find a point
z^{k}\in S
at maximal distance to
F_{k}
, i.e.,

\mathop{\rm dist}(z^{k},F_{k})=\max_{x\in S}\mathop{\rm dist}(x,F_{k}).

(4.18)

Note that the distance to
F_{k}
is a convex function, and thus
z^{k}
may be taken to be an extreme point of
S
.

Denote by
P_{k}z^{k}:=\arg\min_{y\in F_{k}}|y-z^{k}|
its projection onto
F_{k}
, and let
v_{k}
be the unit vector defined as

v_{k}:=\frac{1}{\mathop{\rm dist}(z^{k},F_{k})}(z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k})=\frac{1}{|z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k}|}(z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k}).

Note that
v_{k}
is orthogonal to the affine space
F_{k}
, and thus, by construction,
all vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
form an orthonormal system. We observe that
S
is contained in the strip

L_{k}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(2P_{k}z^{k}-z^{k})\cdot v_{k}\leq x\cdot v_{k}\leq z^{k}\cdot v_{k}\}

(4.19)

of width
2|z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k}|
. This holds since if
x\in S
but
x\notin L_{k}
, then

|x-P_{k}z^{k}|\geq|(x-P_{k}z^{k})\cdot v_{k}|>(z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k})\cdot v_{k}=|z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k}|,

which implies
\mathop{\rm dist}(x,F_{k})>\mathop{\rm dist}(z^{k},F_{k})
, and thus contradicts the choice of
z^{k}
. Similarly, one shows that

x\in S
and
x\cdot v_{k}>z^{k}\cdot v_{k}
is not possible.

Once we have constructed the points
z^{0},...,z^{d}
, we consider the simplex

\Delta:=\text{conv}(z^{0},...,z^{d}).

Clearly
\Delta\subset S
since
S
is convex and each
z^{i}\in S
. Recall from the construction that each
z^{i}
is also an extreme point of
S
.

Using that the directions
v_{k}
are orthogonal,
we calculate that the volume
|\Delta|
is

|\Delta|=\frac{1}{d!}|z^{1}-z^{0}|\prod_{k=2}^{d}|z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k}|.

(4.20)

Since
S
is contained in the rectangle
R=\bigcap_{k=1}^{d}L_{k}
, its Lebesgue measure satisfies

|S|\leq|R|=|z^{1}-z^{0}|\prod_{k=2}^{d}(2|z^{k}-P_{k}z^{k}|)=2^{d-1}d!|\Delta|,

(4.21)

which is the bound we wanted to show.
∎

We will now give the proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 1
be arbitrary but fixed. Given any
C\geq 1
, we consider the collection

\Gamma:=\Gamma(C;n,d):=\left\{S:\ S\ {\rm is\ closed,\ convex\ and}\ |S|>C\frac{\log{n}}{n}\right\}.

(4.22)

Let us choose
r=4d^{2}n
, which will make the right hand side of (
4.7
) less than
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log{n}}{n}
. Then for
S\in\Gamma
, using Lemma
4.3
, we will have that

|\text{conv}(S\cap\Lambda_{r})|\geq\frac{C}{2}\frac{\log{n}}{n}.

(4.23)

Further, by applying Lemma
4.4
to the set
\text{conv}(S\cap\Lambda_{r})
(whose extreme points clearly lie in
\Lambda_{r})
, we obtain a simplex
\Delta
with vertices lying on
\Lambda_{r}
such that
\Delta\subset S\cap\Lambda_{r}\subset S
, and

|\Delta|\geq c\frac{C}{2}\frac{\log{n}}{n},

(4.24)

where
c=c(d)
is the constant in Lemma
4.4
. In going further, we define
\tilde{\Gamma}:=\tilde{\Gamma}(C;n,d)
to be the set of all such simplices.

We know that for any
S\in\Gamma
, there is a
\Delta\in\tilde{\Gamma}
such that
\Delta\subset S
. Therefore, we have the estimate

\pi(C;n,d)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\ \Delta\in\tilde{\Gamma}\ \text{with $\Delta\cap\Lambda_{n}^{*}=\emptyset$}\right)=:\tilde{\pi}(C;n,d).

(4.25)

To bound
\tilde{\pi}
, we consider any simplex
\Delta\in\tilde{\Gamma}
and the probability

\mathbb{P}(\Delta\cap\Lambda_{n}^{*}=\emptyset).

(4.26)

Recall that for the regular grid
\Lambda_{m}
,
m
is the largest integer such that
m^{d}\leq n
. We fix a cell
T_{k}
with
k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}
. The point in
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
which is chosen randomly from
T_{k}
does not lie in
\Delta
with probability

\mathbb{P}(T_{k}\cap\Lambda_{n}^{*}\cap\Delta=\emptyset)=1-\frac{|\Delta\cap T_{k}|}{|T_{k}|}.

(4.27)

Hence, we have

\mathbb{P}(\Delta\cap\Lambda_{n}^{*}=\emptyset)=\prod_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\left(1-\frac{|\Delta\cap T_{k}|}{|T_{k}|}\right)\leq\mathop{\rm exp}\left(-\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\frac{|\Delta\cap T_{k}|}{|T_{k}|}\right),

(4.28)

where we have used the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for
x\geq 0
. Now we simply calculate

\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}\frac{|\Delta\cap T_{k}|}{|T_{k}|}=m^{d}\sum_{k\in{\cal S}_{m}^{d}}|\Delta\cap T_{k}|=m^{d}|\Delta|>c^{\prime}n|\Delta|,

(4.29)

where we have used (
4.5
).
Hence, for each fixed simplex
\Delta\in\tilde{\Gamma}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\Delta\cap\Lambda_{n}^{*}=\emptyset)<\mathop{\rm exp}(-c^{\prime}n|\Delta|).

(4.30)

Finally, we apply a union bound over all simplices
\Delta\in\tilde{\Gamma}
. The cardinality
\#(\tilde{\Gamma})
is bounded by
(4d^{2}n+1)^{d(d+1)}
, since this is the number of
(d+1)
tuples of points in
\Lambda_{r}
. This shows that

\pi(C;n,d)\leq\tilde{\pi}(C;n,d)<[4d^{2}n+1]^{d(d+1)}\mathop{\rm exp}\left(-c^{\prime}c\frac{C}{2}\log(n)\right)<Ln^{A-\alpha C},

(4.31)

where
L,A
and
\alpha
depend only on
d
. This completes the proof.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

We note that point sets satisfying Property 1, which are sometimes called Danzer sets in the literature, have also been constructed and analyzed in
[
2
,
17
,
18
]
. However, the constructions in these papers are much more complicated than our construction in this section. A precise estimate of the constant has also been obtained in
[
1
]
, however only for
d=2
. Let us also note that it appears to be an open problem whether the logarithmic factor in (
4.3
) is required (see
[
2
,
17
]
). If this factor could be removed, it would remove the logarithmic factors in Theorem
4.1
, removing the gap with the lower bounds.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We fix
d
and
\Omega=\Omega_{d}
, fix
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and fix
n\geq 2
. Let
\Lambda^{*}:=\Lambda_{n}^{*}
be any set of
n
points that satisfy Proposition
4.2
. We shall show that sampling at the data sites
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
satisfies

\rho({\cal L}(\Omega),\Lambda_{n}^{*})_{L_{p}}\leq C\kappa(n,p,d),

(4.32)

for all
n
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
In order to prove (
4.32
),
it suffices to consider any two functions

f,g\in{\cal L}:={\cal L}(\Omega)
that agree at the data sites in
\Lambda^{*}

and show that

\|f-g\|_{L_{p}}\leq C\kappa(n,p,d).

(4.33)

Indeed, this shows that
any map that produces from the data vector
w=w(f)=(f(x^{1}),\ldots,f(x^{n}))
a function

g\in{\cal L}
that agrees with the data
on
\Lambda^{*}
will satisfy
(
4.33
)
and therefore prove the corresponding bound on
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
. A constructive way to produce
such a recovery map is proposed in Section §
6
.

Next, note that whenever
f,g\in{\cal L}
, the function
\max\{f,g\}
is also in
{\cal L}
, and we have

\|f-g\|_{L_{p}}\leq\|f-\max\{f,g\}\|_{L_{p}}+\|g-\max\{f,g\}\|_{L_{p}}.

(4.34)

Therefore, we assume without loss of generality that

g(x)\geq f(x),\quad x\in\Omega.

(4.35)

For the remainder of this section, we fix any
f,g\in{\cal L}
that satisfy (
4.35
) and agree on the data sites
\Lambda^{*}
and show that (
4.33
) is valid. For this purpose, we introduce the distribution function

\mu(t):=|A_{t}|,\quad A_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega\;:\;g(x)-f(x)>t\},\quad t\geq 0.

(4.36)

The following lemma gives the bound we shall prove for
\mu
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f,g\in{\cal L}
satisfy
(
4.35
)
. Then, the following bounds hold for
\mu
:

\mu(t)\lesssim\begin{cases}0,&t\geq Cn^{-1/d},\\
t^{-d}\left(\frac{\log{n}}{n}\right)^{2},&cn^{-2/d}(\log{n})^{2/d}<t<Cn^{-1/d},\\
1,&t\leq cn^{-2/d}(\log{n})^{2/d},\end{cases}

(4.37)

where the constants
C
and
c
depend only on
d
.

Before proceeding to the proof of this lemma, let us show how this lemma proves (
4.33
) and therefore proves Theorem
4.1
. We have

\|g-f\|^{p}_{L_{p}(\Omega)}=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\mu(t)dt\lesssim\left(\delta^{2p/d}+\delta^{2}\int_{c\delta^{2/d}}^{Cn^{-1/d}}t^{-d+p-1}dt\right),\quad\delta:=\frac{\log{n}}{n},

(4.38)

where the constant in
\lesssim
depends on
d
and
p
.
Evaluating the latter integral gives

\int_{c\delta^{2/d}}^{Cn^{-1/d}}t^{-d+p-1}dt\lesssim\begin{cases}n^{-(\frac{p}{d}-1)},&p>d,\\
|\log\delta|,&p=d,\\
\delta^{-2+\frac{2p}{d}},&p<d,\end{cases}

(4.39)

where again, the constant in
\lesssim
depends only on
d
and
p
.
Estimates (
4.38
) and (
4.39
) give
(
4.33
).

4.3
Proof of Lemma
4.6

The last statement in (
4.37
) is trivial since
|\Omega|\leq 1
. To prove the first statement of the lemma, we note that since the set
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
satisfies Property 2, and
f,g\in{\cal L}
, we have

t\geq 2Cn^{-1/d}\implies\mu(t)=0,

(4.40)

where
C
is the constant in (
4.4
). Indeed, due to Property 2,
for every
x\in\Omega
, we can find
x^{i}\in\Lambda^{*}
, such that

|x-x^{i}|\leq Cn^{-1/d}
. Since
f(x^{i})=g(x^{i})
and
f,g\in{\cal L}
, we have

g(x)-f(x)=(g(x)-g(x^{i}))-(f(x)-f(x^{i}))\leq 2|x-x^{i}|\leq 2Cn^{-1/d},\quad x\in\Omega,

and thus, if
x\in A_{t}\neq\emptyset
, then
t<2Cn^{-1/d}
.
This observation also shows that

\|f-g\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 2Cn^{-1/d},

(4.41)

which is the optimal decay rate for
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{\infty}}
that was already established in Theorem
2.2
.

In going further, we therefore focus on values of
t
with

cn^{-2/d}(\log{n})^{2/d}<t<Cn^{-1/d}.

(4.42)

We fix such a value of
t
and bound
\mu(t)=|A_{t}|
by covering
A_{t}
by a finite collection of sets

S(x)
defined as follows. Given any
x\in\Omega
, we pick and fix a
u\in\partial g(x)
and introduce the set

S(x):=\{y\in\Omega:f(y)\leq g(x)+u\cdot(y-x)\}.

(4.43)

Here, we have suppressed the dependence of
S(x)
on
u
and all subsequent arguments do not depend on the particular choice of
u
. The mapping

y\mapsto g(x)+u\cdot(y-x)
is any affine function lying below
g
and interpolating
g
at
x
.

We begin by observing some important properties of the sets
S(x)
,
x\in\Omega
. For any
x\in\Omega
, we have
x\in S(x)
,
S(x)
is closed, and
S(x)
is a convex set since the mapping
y\mapsto f(y)-u\cdot(y-x)
is a convex function.
Second, whenever
g(x)>f(x)
, we have

f(y)<g(x)+u\cdot(y-x)\leq g(y),\quad y\in\text{int}(S(x)),

(4.44)

because
g
is convex.
Hence,
\text{int}(S(x))
doesn’t contain any of the data sites
x^{i}
. Therefore, from Property 1, we have (see (
4.3
)),

|S(x)|\leq C\frac{\log{n}}{n},\quad x\in A_{t}.

(4.45)

Our third observation is that if
x\in A_{t}
and
y\in B(x,t/2)
, one has

f(y)\leq f(x)+\frac{t}{2}\leq g(x)-\frac{t}{2}\leq g(x)+u\cdot(y-x),

(4.46)

where we have used the fact that
f
and
g
are both Lipschitz with constant
1
, and in particular that
|u|\leq 1
. Thus, we have

B(x,t/2)\cap\Omega\subset S(x),\quad\hbox{when}\quad x\in A_{t}.

(4.47)

In particular,
x\in\text{int}(S(x))
.

Our strategy to estimate the size of
A_{t}
is to cover it with sets
S(z^{i})
for properly chosen points
z^{i}\in A_{t}
. We proceed
in an inductive manner. Beginning with
A_{t}^{0}:=A_{t}
, we construct points
z^{i}
as follows:

(i)

If
A_{t}^{i}=\emptyset
the construction ends;

(ii)

Otherwise, choose any element
z^{i+1}\in A_{t}^{i}
;

(iii)

Set
A_{t}^{i+1}=A_{t}^{i}\setminus S(z^{i+1})=A_{t}\setminus(S(z^{1})\cup\cdots\cup S(z^{i+1}))
.

Construction Claim:

We claim that the above construction ends after
k
steps with

k\leq Ct^{-d}\frac{\log{n}}{n},

(4.48)

where
C
depends only on
d
.

Assuming for the moment that the above claim is true, we have

A_{t}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}S(z^{i}),

(4.49)

and thus by (
4.45
), since
z^{i}\in A_{t}
, it follows that

\mu(t)=|A_{t}|\leq Ck\frac{\log n}{n},

(4.50)

which finishes the proof of Lemma
4.6
and therefore, as we observed above it also finishes the proof of Theorem
4.1
. Accordingly, the remainder of this section is devoted to proving the above construction claim.

For this purpose, we will use the concept of the polar
S^{\circ}
of a closed convex set

S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
0\in\text{int}(S)
,
S^{\circ}
is
defined as the convex set

S^{\circ}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:z\cdot y\leq 1~\text{for all $y\in S$}\}.

(4.51)

A fundamental fact, first proved by Mahler
[
14
]
, see also Remark
4.8
below, is that

|S||S^{\circ}|\geq c,

(4.52)

where
c
depends only on
d
. For any
x\in A_{t}
, we consider the set

S^{*}(x):=u+(t/3)(S(x)-x)^{\circ}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~(v-u)\cdot(y-x)\leq t/3~\text{for all $y\in S(x)$}\},

(4.53)

where
u\in\partial g(x)
is the subgradient, used in the definition of
S(x)
, see (
4.43
). It follows from (
4.52
)
that

|S^{*}(x)|=(t/3)^{d}|(S(x)-x)^{\circ}|\geq c(t/3)^{d}|S(x)|^{-1}.

(4.54)

Combining this estimate with (
4.45
), gives that for
x\in A_{t}
, we have

|S^{*}(x)|\geq ct^{d}\frac{n}{\log n},

(4.55)

where
c
depends only on
d
.
The following technical lemma forms the crux of the proof of the construction claim. It will be used to bound
k
in (
4.50
) and explains the choice of
t/3
in (
4.53
).

Lemma 4.7
.

If
x,x^{\prime}\in A_{t}
, and
x^{\prime}\notin S(x)
, then
S^{*}(x)\cap S^{*}(x^{\prime})=\emptyset
.

Proof:.

Let
u\in\partial g(x)
and
u^{\prime}\in\partial g(x^{\prime})
denote the subgradients which define
S(x)
and
S(x^{\prime})
, respectively. We will assume that there exists
v\in S^{*}(x)\cap S^{*}(x^{\prime})
and reach a contradiction.

First, we observe that it suffices to work in the univariate case. Indeed, for the general case
d\geq 1
, let us restrict our attention to the line
L
determined by
x
and
x^{\prime}
(which are distinct since
x^{\prime}\notin S(x)
). We pick a point
x_{0}\in L\cap\Omega
,
and set
e:=|x^{\prime}-x|^{-1}(x^{\prime}-x)
, so that
L=\{y:\,y=x_{0}+se,\,s\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
Then, the convex univariate functions

s\mapsto f(x_{0}+se)=:f_{0}(s),\quad s\mapsto g(x_{0}+se)=:g_{0}(s),

(4.56)

which are defined on a bounded interval, satisfy the exact same assumptions
as
f
and
g
, when
x
and
x^{\prime}
are replaced by
the points
s_{0}:=(x-x_{0})\cdot e
and
s_{0}^{\prime}:=(x^{\prime}-x_{0})\cdot e
, noting that
u\cdot e
and
u^{\prime}\cdot e
are subgradients of
f_{0}
and
g_{0}
at these points, respectively. Note that
x=x_{0}+s_{0}e
,
x^{\prime}=x_{0}+s^{\prime}_{0}e
,
g(x)=g_{0}(s_{0})
, and
g(x^{\prime})=g_{0}(s^{\prime}_{0})
.

If
v
belongs to
S^{*}(x)\cap S^{*}(x^{\prime})
, then it is easily seen that
v\cdot e
belongs to its univariate counterpart
S^{*}(s_{0})\cap S^{*}(s_{0}^{\prime})
. Indeed, if
v\in S^{*}(x)
, then for all
y\in S(x)
, we have

(v-u)(y-x)\leq t/3
.
Note that

\displaystyle S(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{y\in\Omega:\,f(y)\leq g(x)+u\cdot(y-x)\}=\{y\in\Omega:\,f(y)\leq g_{0}(s_{0})+u\cdot(y-x_{0}-s_{0}e)\}

We can then choose
y=x_{0}+se\in S(x)
, to obtain,

\{x_{0}+se\in\Omega:\,f_{0}(s):=f(x_{0}+se)\leq g_{0}(s_{0})+u\cdot e(s-s_{0})\}\subset S(x).

Moreover, since
s_{0}e=x-x_{0}
, we see that

(v\cdot e-u\cdot e)(s-s_{0})=(v-u)(x_{0}+se-x)=(v-u)(y-x)\leq t/3,

holds for all
s\in\{s:x_{0}+se\in\Omega,\,\,f_{0}(s)\leq g_{0}(s_{0})+u\cdot e(s-s_{0})\}=S(s_{0})
. Thus,
v\cdot e\in S^{*}(s_{0})
. Similarly, one can show that if
v\in S^{*}(x^{\prime})
, then
v\cdot e\in S^{*}(s^{\prime})
.

In view of the above considerations, we proceed by working in the case
d=1
, with

\Omega
being a bounded closed interval, assuming that
x<x^{\prime}
. The proof when
x^{\prime}<x
is similar. The sets
S(x),S(x^{\prime}),S^{*}(x),S^{*}(x^{\prime})
are closed intervals. We also observe that, since
x^{\prime}\notin S(x)
and
x\in S(x)
,
the point
\tilde{x}:=\max S(x)
satisfies
x<\tilde{x}<x^{\prime}
.
We also define
\overline{x}:=\max\{x,\min S(x^{\prime})\}<x^{\prime}
, since
x^{\prime}\in\text{int}(S(x^{\prime}))
.
We then write

\tilde{x}=\alpha x^{\prime}+(1-\alpha)x\quad{\rm and}\quad\overline{x}=\beta x+(1-\beta)x^{\prime},

(4.57)

for some
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\beta\leq 1
.
Note that
\overline{x}=x
and
\beta=1

if and only if
x\in S(x^{\prime})
.

Since
\tilde{x}
is the right end point of
S(x)
, one has

f(\tilde{x})=g(x)+u(\tilde{x}-x)
, and therefore, by the convexity
of
f
,

\alpha f(x^{\prime})+(1-\alpha)f(x)\geq f(\tilde{x})=g(x)+u(\tilde{x}-x).

(4.58)

Next, using that
x,x^{\prime}\in A_{t}
and (
4.58
), we obtain

\alpha g(x^{\prime})+(1-\alpha)g(x)\geq\alpha f(x^{\prime})+(1-\alpha)f(x)+t\geq g(x)+u(\tilde{x}-x)+t.

(4.59)

Using the convexity of
g
and since
\tilde{x}-x=\alpha(x^{\prime}-x)
,
we thus find that

t\leq\alpha(g(x^{\prime})-g(x))-\alpha u(x^{\prime}-x)\leq\alpha(u^{\prime}-u)(x^{\prime}-x),

(4.60)

which reveals a lower bound

u^{\prime}-u\geq\frac{t}{\alpha(x^{\prime}-x)}.

(4.61)

When
\beta<1
, we know that
\overline{x}
is the left endpoint of
S(x^{\prime})
, and we obtain by a similar
reasoning that

t\leq\beta(g(x)-g(x^{\prime}))+\beta u^{\prime}(x^{\prime}-x)\leq\beta(u^{\prime}-u)(x^{\prime}-x).

(4.62)

We thus have the improved lower bound

u^{\prime}-u\geq\frac{t}{x^{\prime}-x}\max\{\alpha^{-1},\beta^{-1}\},

(4.63)

which also holds when
\beta=1
, since it then reduces
to (
4.61
).

On the other hand, since
\tilde{x}\in S(x)

and
\overline{x}\in S(x^{\prime})
, and in view of the definition
of
S^{*}(x)
and
S^{*}(x^{\prime})
, one has for any
v
in their intersection the framing

u^{\prime}-\frac{t}{3\beta(x^{\prime}-x)}=u^{\prime}-\frac{t}{3(x^{\prime}-\overline{x})}\leq v\leq u+\frac{t}{3(\tilde{x}-x)}=u+\frac{t}{3\alpha(x^{\prime}-x)},

(4.64)

and therefore

u^{\prime}-u\leq\frac{t}{3(x^{\prime}-x)}(\alpha^{-1}+\beta^{-1})\leq\frac{2}{3}\frac{t}{x^{\prime}-x}\max\{\alpha^{-1},\beta^{-1}\},

(4.65)

which contradicts (
4.63
).
∎

Since the construction process of the
z^{i}
’s ensures that

z^{j}\notin S(z^{i})
if
i<j
, and all
z^{i}\in A_{t}
, the above lemma implies that

S^{*}(z^{i})\cap S^{*}(z^{j})=\emptyset,\quad i\neq j.

(4.66)

On the other hand, we know from (
4.47
) that if
x\in A_{t}
and
if
B(x,t/2)\subset\Omega
,
then
B(x,t/2)\subset S(x)
, or equivalently

B(0,t/2)\subset S(x)-x.

(4.67)

It follows that

(S(x)-x)^{\circ}\subset B(0,t/2)^{\circ}=B(0,2/t).

(4.68)

By rescaling and translating by
u
which has norm at most
1
(since
g
is
1
-Lipschitz),
we find that

S^{*}(x)\subset u+B(0,2/3)\subset B(0,5/3).

(4.69)

This containment is no longer guaranteed for those
x\in A_{t}
with distance less than
t/2

from
\partial\Omega
. For such
x
, we will use the fact that there exists a larger
convex set
\tilde{S}(x)\supset S(x)
such that

B(x,t/2)\subset\tilde{S}(x)\quad{\rm and}\quad|\tilde{S}(x)|=C|S(x)|,

(4.70)

where
C
depends only on
d
. We postpone the construction of
\tilde{S}(x)
to the end of the proof, and set

\tilde{S}^{*}(x):=u+(t/3)(\tilde{S}(x)-x)^{\circ}
,
so that we have the containment

\tilde{S}^{*}(x)\subset B(0,5/3).

(4.71)

By convention, we set
\tilde{S}(x):=S(x)
for those
x\in A_{t}
such that
B(x,t/2)\subset\Omega
.
Note that for all
x\in A_{t}
we have the upper bound

|\tilde{S}(x)|\leq C\frac{\log n}{n},

(4.72)

and therefore the lower bound

|\tilde{S}^{*}(x)|\geq ct^{-d}\frac{n}{\log n},

(4.73)

where
C
and
c
depend only on
d
.
Since we have
\tilde{S}^{*}(z^{i})\subset S^{*}(z^{i})
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, we find
by (
4.66
) that we also have

\tilde{S}^{*}(z^{i})\cap\tilde{S}^{*}(z^{j})=\emptyset,\quad i\neq j,

(4.74)

and therefore

ckt^{d}\frac{n}{\log n}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\tilde{S}^{*}(z^{i})|\leq C:=|B(0,5/3)|.

(4.75)

This yields an upper bound for
k
, namely,

k\leq Ct^{-d}\frac{\log{n}}{n},

(4.76)

where
C
depends only on
d
.

We conclude the proof by explaining the construction of
\tilde{S}(x)
from
S(x)
in the case
where
x\in A_{t}
is at distance less than
t/2
from
\partial\Omega
. We first observe that,
trivially
\|f-g\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 2
, so that
we may always assume that
t\leq 2
.
Since
x\in\Omega
,
we can always find a
x^{\prime}\in\Omega
such that

|x-x^{\prime}|=\min\{1,t\}/4\quad{\rm and}\quad{\rm dist}(x^{\prime},\partial\Omega)\geq\frac{\min\{1,t\}}{4\sqrt{d}}\geq\frac{t}{8\sqrt{d{}}}:=ct.

(4.77)

Observe that

B(x^{\prime},ct)\subset B(x,t/2)\cap\Omega\subset S(x).

(4.78)

We then define the desired set by

\tilde{S}(x):=x^{\prime}+\frac{3}{4c}(S(x)-x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}+6\sqrt{d}(S(x)-x^{\prime}),

(4.79)

which obviously contains
S(x)
since
6\sqrt{d}\geq 1
and whose
size is

|\tilde{S}(x)|=C|S(x)|,\quad C:=(6\sqrt{d})^{d}.

(4.80)

Finally, we find from (
4.78
) that
B(0,ct)\subset S(x)-x^{\prime}
, so that

B(x^{\prime},3t/4)\subset\tilde{S}(x)
, and therefore

B(x,t/2)\subset B(x^{\prime},3t/4)\subset\tilde{S}(x),

(4.81)

which confirms (
4.70
) and ends the proof of the construction claim.

Remark 4.8
.

In
[
14
]
, Mahler apparently only proved (
4.52
) for convex and centrally symmetric bodies. However, it follows easily from this that the same inequality holds for closed convex bodies containing
0
in their interior. Indeed, suppose that
S
is a closed convex body with
0\in\text{int}(S)
. Let
z^{1}
be the point in
S
with a largest norm
|z^{1}|
, and choose sequentially points
z^{i}\in S
whose distance from
\text{span}(z^{1},...,z^{i-1})
is maximized. Then
S
contains the simplex
\Delta
with vertices
z^{0}=0,z^{1},...,z^{d}
, whose volume is

|\Delta|=\frac{1}{d!}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathop{\rm dist}(z^{i},\text{span}(z^{1},...,z^{i-1})),

(4.82)

and therefore
|S|\geq|\Delta|
.
On the other hand, the symmetric convex hull

S_{\rm sym}:=\text{conv}(S\cup-S)\subset{\cal R}

is contained in a rectangle
{\cal R}
with side lengths
l_{i}=2\mathop{\rm dist}(z^{i},\text{span}(z^{1},...,z^{i-1}))
, and thus

2^{d}d!|\Delta|=2^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathop{\rm dist}(z^{i},\text{span}(z^{1},...,z^{i-1}))=|{\cal R}|\geq|S_{\rm sym}|

Hence, it follows that
|S|\geq\tilde{c}|S_{\rm sym}|

with
\tilde{c}:=(2^{d}d!)^{-1}
. On the other hand, since
S\subset S_{\rm sym}
, it follows from the definition that
S^{\circ}\supset S_{\rm sym}^{\circ}
. Hence

|S||S^{\circ}|\geq\tilde{c}|S_{\rm sym}||S_{\rm sym}^{\circ}|\geq\tilde{c}c,

(4.83)

where
c
is the constant from
[
14
]
for convex and centrally symmetric bodies.
We note that determining the optimal constant
c
, both in the case of general or centrally symmetric convex sets, is called Mahler’s conjecture, and is a well-known and exceptionally difficult open problem, which was solved by Mahler only in the case
d=2
.

5
Sharp upper bounds for the sampling numbers of
{\cal B}

In this section, for each fixed
d
, we show that the estimates in Theorem
4.1
for the class
{\cal L}:={\cal L}(\Omega)
,
\Omega:=\Omega_{d}
, are also valid for the
larger class
{\cal B}:={\cal B}(\Omega)
. Namely, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
1<p<\infty
. Then, for any
n\geq 2
, we have

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\leq C\kappa(n,p,d),

(5.1)

where
\kappa(n,p,d)
is defined in
(
4.1
)
and
C
depends only on
d
and
p
.

The main difference between Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
5.1
lies in the sampling strategy, which is more delicate for the class
{\cal B}
. We need to refine the grid near the boundary
\partial\Omega
in a similar way to how it was done in the case of a tensor product grid studied in §
3
.
Let us also recall that for the
L_{\infty}
norm one has

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp 1
, while

\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp n^{-1/d}
, and the case
p=1
has already been discussed in Theorem

3.1
, see (
3.4
), where there are no logarithms present.

Proof:.

Our strategy is to
decompose
\Omega
into
subdomains and apply Theorem
4.1
to each of them
after a proper rescaling that will place us in the class
{\cal L}
for each subdomain. We fix the values of
n\geq 1
, and
d\geq 1
and
1<p<\infty
.

We first define the partition in the univariate case.
We set

J_{0}:=[1/3,2/3],

and with
a_{l}:=\frac{1}{3}2^{-l}
,
l=0,1,\dots
, we define

J_{-l}:=\frac{1}{3}\cdot[2^{-l},2^{-(l-1)}]=:[a_{l},a_{l-1}]\quad{\rm and}\quad J_{l}:=[1-a_{l-1},1-a_{l}],\quad l=1,2,\dots.

(5.2)

The collection
\{J_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}
forms a
partition of
(0,1)
whose interiors are pairwise disjoint.
By tensorization we obtain a partition
\{R_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
of the interior of
\Omega
where

R_{k}:=J_{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times J_{k_{d}},\quad k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

(5.3)

Since
J_{l}
and
J_{-l}
have length
\frac{1}{3}2^{-l}
, the rectangle
R_{k}
has measure

|R_{k}|=3^{-d}\,2^{-(|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|)}.

(5.4)

We next define
\phi_{l}
as the univariate affine function that maps
[0,1]
onto
J_{l}
,
Therefore, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the transformation

\Phi_{k}:\Omega\to R_{k}
, defined as

x\mapsto\Phi_{k}(x):=(\phi_{k_{1}}(x_{1}),\dots,\phi_{k_{d}}(x_{d})),\quad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),

(5.5)

is a bijective map between
\Omega
and
R_{k}
.

For any given
n
, we build a sampling set
\Lambda_{n}^{*}

of cardinality at most
n
as follows. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
we define

n_{k}:=\lfloor cn2^{-\alpha(|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|)}\rfloor,\quad\hbox{where}\quad c:=\Bigl(\frac{2}{1-2^{-\alpha}}-1\Bigr)^{-d},

(5.6)

and where
\alpha>0
is fixed and small enough to ensure that

1-\alpha p\tilde{r}/d>0.

(5.7)

We pick sets
\Lambda^{*}_{n,k}
comprised of
n_{k}
points in
\Omega
according to Proposition
4.2
,
and therefore satisfying Property 1 and 2 with
n_{k}
in place of
n
. Note that, in view of the definition of
n_{k}
,
the set
\Lambda^{*}_{n,k}
is
non-empty only for finitely many values of
k
.
Using
\Phi_{k}
, we then map these points
into
R_{k}
and define

\Lambda_{n}^{*}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Phi_{k}(\Lambda^{*}_{n,k}).

(5.8)

The set
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
has cardinality at most
n
since

\#(\Lambda_{n}^{*})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}n_{k}\leq cn\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}2^{-\alpha(|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|)}=cn\Bigl(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-\alpha|l|}\Bigr)^{d}=cn\Bigl(\frac{2}{1-2^{-\alpha}}-1\Bigr)^{d}=n.

(5.9)

We now consider any two functions
f,g\in{\cal B}
that agree on the data sites
\Lambda^{*}_{n}
.
Similarly to the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we need to show that for some
C
large enough

\|f-g\|_{L_{p}(\Omega_{d})}\leq C\kappa(n,p,d).

(5.10)

In order to prove (
5.10
), we consider any
d
dimensional rectangle
R_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and derive a bound for
\|f-g\|_{L_{p}(R_{k})}
. We fix
R_{k}
and first observe that for any
f\in{\cal B}
, upon applying (
3.8
)
to the univariate convex functions

t\mapsto f(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},t,x_{j+1},\dots,x_{d}),

(5.11)

the coordinates
u_{j}
of
any
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})\in\partial f(x)

satisfy the bounds

x\in R_{k}\implies|u_{j}|\leq 6\cdot 2^{|k_{j}|},\quad j=1,\ldots,d,\quad\hbox{with}\quad u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})\in\partial f(x).

(5.12)

For a smooth function, this simply means that
\Big|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\Big|\leq 6\cdot 2^{|k_{j}|}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
.

These bounds hold for the subgradients of
f
and
g
. Given
f,g\in{\cal B}
,
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we denote by
f_{k}
and
g_{k}

the pullbacks of
f|_{R_{k}},g|_{R_{k}}
,

f_{k}(x):=(2\sqrt{d})^{-1}f(\Phi_{k}(x))\quad{\rm and}\quad g_{k}(x):=(2\sqrt{d})^{-1}g(\Phi_{k}(x)),\quad x\in\Omega,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

(5.13)

which are now functions defined on
\Omega
. Since
|\phi_{l}^{\prime}(t)|=2^{-l}/3
,
it follows from (
5.12
) that each component of their subgradient is bounded
by
(\sqrt{d})^{-1}
. Therefore,

f_{k},g_{k}\in{\cal L}
, and agree on the data sites
\Lambda^{*}_{n,k}
if
f
and
g
agree on
\Phi_{k}(\Lambda^{*}_{n,k})
. From Theorem
4.1
, we find that

\|f_{k}-g_{k}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\kappa(n_{k},p,d).

(5.14)

This estimate is only for the value
n_{k}\geq 2
, but in the case
n_{k}=0,1
, we simply have
\|f_{k}-g_{k}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C
since
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
are bounded by
1
.
This allows us to rewrite,
regardless of the value of
n_{k}
,

\|f_{k}-g_{k}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C(\max\{1,n_{k}\})^{-\tilde{r}/d}\left[\log(\max\{2,n_{k}\})\right]^{\gamma},\quad\tilde{r}:=\min\{2,1+d/p\},

(5.15)

where
\gamma
is the power of the logarithmic factor showing on the right side of (
5.1
), and
C
depends only on
d
and
p
. We note that

\max\{1,n_{k}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}cn2^{-\alpha(|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|)}\quad{\rm and}\quad\log(\max\{2,n_{k}\})\leq\log n,

(5.16)

where the first inequality follows from the definition of
n_{k}

and the second uses
n_{k}\leq n
and
n\geq 2
, and therefore

\|f_{k}-g_{k}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C2^{\alpha\tilde{r}/d(|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|)}n^{-\tilde{r}/d}(\log n)^{\gamma},\quad n\geq 2.

(5.17)

.

The
L_{p}
norm of
(f-g)
can then be decomposed according to

\|f-g\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|f-g\|_{L_{p}(R_{k})}^{p}=(2\sqrt{d})^{p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|R_{k}|\|f_{k}-g_{k}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}^{p}.

(5.18)

We then obtain from (
5.17
), (
5.4
) and the fact that
\beta:=1-\alpha p\tilde{r}/d>0
, the estimate

\|f-g\|_{L_{p}}^{p}\leq Cn^{-p\tilde{r}/d}(\log n)^{p\gamma}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}2^{-\beta(|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|)}=C\Bigl(\frac{2}{1-2^{-\beta}}-1\Bigr)^{d}n^{-p\tilde{r}/d}(\log n)^{p\gamma},

(5.19)

where
C
depends only on
d
and
p
. This concludes the proof.
∎

6
Recovery algorithms for the model classes
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}

In the previous sections of this paper, we have determined the asymptotic behavior (up to logarithm factors) of the sampling rates
\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{L_{p}}
for the model classes
{\cal K}={\cal L},{\cal B}
when the error is measured in
L_{p}
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
. For those results, the key issue was where to sample the functions. Given
n\geq 1
, we have described a set
\Lambda_{n}^{*}\subset\Omega=\Omega_{d}
of data sites so that sampling an
f\in{\cal K}
on
\Lambda_{n}^{*}
gives a data vector
w(f)
, which determines
f
to the prescribed accuracy in
L_{p}
.

We then noted that any mapping
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to L_{p}
that maps each data vector
w=w(f)
with
f\in{\cal K}
, to an element in the set

{\cal K}_{w}:=\{g\in{\cal K}:w(g)=w\},

(6.1)

gives a recovery algorithm with the near optimal performance of Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 5.1. In this section, we discuss how to numerically construct such a mapping
A
.

We shall work in the following setting. The model class
{\cal K}
will always be either
{\cal L}={\cal L}(\Omega)
or
{\cal B}={\cal B}(\Omega)
and
the set
\Lambda=\{x^{i},\ i=1,\dots,n\}\subset\Omega
of data sites can be arbitrary.
We shall construct a practical recovery algorithm
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to L_{\infty}(\Omega)
which, when
applied to any data vector
w=w(f)

with
f\in{\cal K}
,
constructs a function

A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w)=g_{w}\in{\cal K}_{w}.

(6.2)

This guarantees that
A
is near optimal in the sense that for each
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have

\|f-A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w(f))\|_{L_{p}}\leq 2\rho({\cal K},\Lambda)_{L_{p}},\quad f\in{\cal K}.

(6.3)

Let us remark on two noteworthy properties of the
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
we construct. The first is that it will not depend on the value
p
of the
L_{p}
space where we measure error. In addition,
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w)
will be defined for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, not just data vectors. This will be useful when treating noisy data.
When
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}^{*}
, the algorithm
A^{\cal K}_{\Lambda_{n}^{*}}
provides, via (
6.3
), the rates obtained in Theorem
4.1
(for
{\cal K}={\cal L}
) and Theorem
5.1
(for
{\cal K}={\cal B}
) when the data is not noisy.

Let us note that in optimal recovery in Banach spaces, constructing a recovery operator is well studied, but there is no numerical procedure that is known to work in a general setting.
In our case, we are successful because each of the model classes
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
has the following property:

Supremum Property
:
Each of the classes
{\cal K}={\cal L},{\cal B}
is closed under suprema. More precisely, if
{\cal M}
is a subset of
{\cal K}
,
then the function
g_{\cal M}
defined by

g_{\cal M}(x):=\sup_{g\in{\cal M}}g(x),\quad x\in\Omega,

(6.4)

also belongs to
{\cal K}
.

As a particular consequence of this property, let us note that if
w=w(f)
for some
f\in{\cal K}
, then the function

g_{w}^{\cal K}(x):=\sup_{g\in{\cal K}_{w}}g(x)\in{\cal K},

(6.5)

and even belongs to
{\cal K}_{w}
because
g_{w}^{\cal K}(x^{i})=w_{i}
for all
i=1,...,n
. In fact,
g^{\cal K}_{w}
is the largest function in
{\cal K}_{w}
. We show below that this function can be constructively found by using classical optimization techniques.

We now proceed to describing our proposed constructive recovery mapping. Our definition of
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
is based, in part, on the fact that any function
g\in{\cal K}
only takes values in the interval
[-1,1]
.
In particular, if
V
denotes the set of the
2^{d}
vertices of
\Omega
, then for any
g\in{\cal K}
, we have

g(v)\leq 1,\quad v\in V.

(6.6)

Recall that we want the recovery map
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
to be defined for any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\tilde{w}:=(\tilde{w}_{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{n}),\ {\rm where}\ \tilde{w}_{i}:=\max(-1,w_{i}),\ i=1,\dots,n,

(6.7)

and introduce the sets

\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}:=\{g\in{\cal K}:~g(x^{i})\leq\tilde{w}_{i},\ x_{i}\in\Lambda,\ {\rm and}\ g(v)\leq 1,\ v\in V\},\quad w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(6.8)

Note that
\tilde{\cal K}_{w}
is non-empty because it contains the function
g\equiv-1
.
We define our reconstruction mapping for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w):=\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K},\qquad\textrm{where for }x\in\Omega\qquad\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}(x):=\sup_{g\in\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}}g(x).

(6.9)

This mapping is well defined for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and due to the supremum property, the function
\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}
is always in
{\cal K}
.

The following lemma shows that
\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}
coincides with the previously defined
g_{w}^{\cal K}
when
w=w(f)
for a function in
f\in{\cal K}
, and therefore
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
is near optimal in the sense of (
6.3
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
{\cal K}
be either of the model classes
{\cal L}
or
{\cal B}.
Suppose that
f\in{\cal K}
and
w=w(f)
. Then, we have

\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}=g_{w}^{\cal K},

(6.10)

and therefore
g_{w}^{\cal K}\in{\cal K}_{w}
is a near optimal recovery of
f
from the data
w
.

Proof:.

Let us first observe that when
w=w(f)
, for some
f\in{\cal K}
, then
w_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i}
.
Since we clearly have
{\cal K}_{w}\subset\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}
,
it follows that

g_{w}^{\cal K}\leq\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}
.
On the other hand, each
g\in\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}
is in
{\cal K}
and satisfies
g(x^{i})\leq\tilde{w}_{i}=w_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. It follows that for any
g\in\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}
, the function
\bar{g}:=\max(f,g)\in{\cal K}_{w}
and
\bar{g}\geq g
. Therefore,
\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}\leq g_{w}^{\cal K}
.
∎

6.1
Evaluating
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w)
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

We have proven in Lemma
6.1
that the mapping
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
defined by (
6.9
) is a near optimal recovery and therefore achieves the accuracy

\rho({\cal K},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, for the model classes
{\cal K}={\cal L},{\cal B}
, regardless of the choice
\Lambda
of data sites. At this stage, we cannot claim that
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
is practical since we have not described how one can numerically
execute the optimization in (
6.9
). In this subsection, we simplify the optimization problem (
6.9
) that needs to be solved by reducing the set
\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}
over which maximization takes place. Namely,
we next show that the function
\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}=A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w)
can be found by solving
an optimization problem over the finite dimensional space of affine functions on
\Omega
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
denote the linear space of affine functions
\ell
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{L}:=\{\ell:\,\,\ell(\circ)=\zeta_{\ell}\cdot\circ+b_{\ell},\quad\zeta_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(6.11)

In the next lemma, we show that we can replace the sets
\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}
in (
6.9
) by
the following set of affine functions

{\mathbb{L}}_{w}^{\cal L}:=\{\ell\in\mathbb{L}:|\zeta_{\ell}|\leq 1\ {\rm and}\ \ \ell(x^{i})\leq\tilde{w}_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n,\ {\rm and}\ \ell(v)\leq 1,\ v\in V\},

(6.12)

and

{\mathbb{L}}_{w}^{\cal B}:=\{\ell\in\mathbb{L}:\ \ \ell(x^{i})\leq\tilde{w}_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n,\ {\rm and}\ \ell(v)\leq 1,\ v\in V\},

(6.13)

when
{\cal K}={\cal L}
and
{\cal K}={\cal B}
, respectively.
Both of these sets are defined for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and are non-empty since they contain the affine function
\ell\equiv-1
.
For each
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\cal K}={\cal L},{\cal B}
, we define

\ell_{w}^{\cal K}(x):=\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{w}^{\cal K}}\ell(x),\quad x\in\Omega.

(6.14)

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
{\cal K}
be either of the model classes
{\cal L}
or
{\cal B}
. For any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
x\in\Omega_{d}
, we have

\tilde{g}^{\cal K}_{w}(x)=\ell_{w}^{\cal K}(x).

(6.15)

Proof:.

Fix any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and fix any
x\in\Omega
. Let
g\in\tilde{\cal K}_{w}
and let
\zeta
be any element in
\partial g(x)
. Then the affine function
\ell(y):=g(x)+\zeta\cdot(y-x)
satisfies

\ell(y)\leq g(y)
,
y\in\Omega
, and so
\ell\in{\mathbb{L}}_{w}^{\cal K}
because
|\zeta|\leq 1
when
{\cal K}={\cal L}
. Since
g(x)=\ell(x)
, it follows that

\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}(x):=\sup_{g\in\tilde{\cal K}_{w}}g(x)\leq\sup_{\ell\in{\mathbb{L}}_{w}^{\cal K}}\ell(x)=:\ell_{w}^{\cal K}(x).

(6.16)

To prove the reverse inequality, let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{w}^{\cal K}
, and consider the function
g_{\ell}:=\max\{\ell,-1\}
. Then,
g_{\ell}
is a convex function which is in

\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}
. Since
\ell\leq g_{\ell}
on
\Omega
, we have

\ell_{w}^{\cal K}(x):=\sup_{\ell\in{\mathbb{L}}_{w}^{\cal K}}\ell(x)\leq\sup_{g\in\tilde{\cal K}_{w}}g(x)=:\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}(x),\quad x\in\Omega.

(6.17)

The last two inequalities prove the lemma.
∎

The above lemma shows that for each
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w)=\ell_{w}^{\cal K},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\ell_{w}^{\cal K}(x):=\sup_{\ell\in{\mathbb{L}}^{\cal K}_{w}}\ell(x),\quad x\in\Omega,\quad{\cal K}={\cal L},{\cal B}.

(6.18)

Moreover, for each
x\in\Omega
,
\ell^{\cal K}_{w}(x)
can be found by classical optimization. For the model class
{\cal K}={\cal B}
,
\ell_{w}^{{\cal B}}(x)
is found by linear programming, and when

{\cal K}={\cal L}
,
\ell_{w}^{{\cal L}}(x)
is found by optimization algorithms for conic programming due to the quadratic constraint on
\zeta
,
see, for example
[
3
]
.

The recovery algorithm
A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}
can be applied to any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and gives a near optimal recovery when
w=w(f)
for
f\in{\cal K}
.
The following theorem shows that this mapping is stable.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
{\cal K}
be either of the model classes
{\cal L}
or
{\cal B}
. For any
w,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has the stability estimates

\|A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(w)-A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(\eta)\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\|w-\eta\|_{\ell_{\infty}}.

(6.19)

Proof:.

We define
\delta:=\|w-\eta\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have

\tilde{w}_{i}:=\max\{-1,w_{i}\}\geq\max\{-1,\eta_{i}-\delta\}=\max\{-1,\tilde{\eta}_{i}-\delta\}.

We claim that for each
g\in\widetilde{\cal K}_{\eta}
, the function

\bar{g}:=\max\{-1,g-\delta\}\in\widetilde{\cal K}_{w}.

(6.20)

Indeed, this function is convex, takes values in
[-1,1]
, and in the case
{\cal K}={\cal L}
has its subgradients
u\in\partial\bar{g}(x)
,
x\in\Omega_{d}
, satisfying
|u|\leq 1
. Moreover,

\bar{g}(x_{i})=\max\{-1,g-\delta\}(x^{i})\leq\max\{-1,\tilde{\eta}_{i}-\delta\}\leq\tilde{w}_{i},

(6.21)

thereby verifying our claim.
Therefore, for every
g\in\widetilde{\cal K}_{\eta}
, we have

g-\delta\leq\bar{g}\leq\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K},

where we have used the definition of
\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}
. The latter inequality
implies that
\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}\geq\tilde{g}_{\eta}^{\cal K}-\delta
. Similarly, we find

\tilde{g}_{\eta}^{\cal K}\geq\tilde{g}_{w}^{\cal K}-\delta
, which proves the result.
∎

The following corollary, bounds the performance of
A
when the measurements of
f
are noisy.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
f\in{\cal K}
where
{\cal K}={\cal L}
or
{\cal B}
. Suppose that in place of the true measurement vector
w=w(f)
we have the noisy measurement vector
\eta
with

\|w-\eta\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq\delta
. Then, we have

\|f-A_{\Lambda}^{\cal K}(\eta)\|_{L_{p}}\leq 2\rho({\cal K},\Lambda)_{L_{p}}+\delta.

(6.22)

Proof:.

This follows directly from Theorem
6.3
.
∎

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have determined the rate of decay of the sampling numbers

\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{L_{p}}
for the model classes
{\cal K}={\cal L}
and
{\cal K}={\cal B}
, through
upper and lower bounds that match up to logarithmic factors.
We have also established
upper bounds on the linear sampling numbers
\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal K})_{L_{p}}

with matching lower bounds only in
the case
p=1,2,\infty
.
In the case
p=2
, the rate of decay of

\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal K})_{L_{2}}
is inferior to that of
\rho_{n}({\cal K})_{L_{2}}
. In contrast to classical model classes, this reveals an instance where non-linear recovery methods from point samples significantly outperform their linear counterparts.
One remaining open question is the complete determination of the rate of decay of
\rho_{n}^{\ell}({\cal K})_{L_{p}}
for values of

p\neq 1,2,\infty
.

We have considered classes of functions defined on the unit cube

\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
. A legitimate question is the validity of our results
for classes of convex functions, defined on more general domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We leave the extension of our results to this more general setting for future work.

As observed in the introduction of this paper, sampling
numbers have natural connections with Kolmogorov widths
and entropy numbers. These quantities have been studied for the two model classes
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
.

Unfortunately, the Kolmogorov widths of
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
are not completely known.
For
{\cal L}
, it was proven in
[
11
]
that for any dimension
d
,

d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{1}}\asymp n^{-2/d}.

(7.1)

We have also seen in the proof of Theorem
2.2
that

d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{2}}\asymp n^{-\frac{3}{2d}}.

(7.2)

In view of the comparison (
1.16
),
our results on linear sampling numbers
in Theorem
2.2
give the
general upper estimate

d_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d},\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty,\quad\hbox{where}\quad r:=1+\frac{1}{p},

(7.3)

which is sharp when
p=1
and
p=2
.

For the class
{\cal B}
, in the univariate case
d=1
, the rate of decay of the Kolmogorov widths has been determined for all
1\leq p<\infty
in Theorem 1.2 from
[
10
]
,
where it was shown that (again with
r=1+1/p
), we have

d_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\asymp\begin{cases}n^{-r},\quad 1\leq p\leq 2,\\
n^{-\frac{3}{2}},\quad\quad 2\leq p<\infty.\end{cases}

(7.4)

Our result in Theorem
3.1
gives the
general upper estimate in any dimension
d\geq 1

d_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\leq Cn^{-r/d},\quad 1\leq p<\infty,

(7.5)

which is again sharp when
p=1
and
p=2
since
{\cal L}\subset{\cal B}
, see (
7.1
) and (
7.2
).

The rates of decay of the entropy numbers of the two classes
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
in
L_{p}
are known. The first result on entropy of the class
{\cal L}
and all
d\geq 1
is

\varepsilon_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{\infty}}\asymp n^{-2/d},

(7.6)

which was shown in
[
4
]
, Theorem 6. Later, in
[
11
]
, it was proven that the entropy numbers satisfy

\varepsilon_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{1}}\asymp n^{-2/d}.

(7.7)

From this, one easily obtains that for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
,

\varepsilon_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-2/d}.

(7.8)

Finally, it was proven in
[
7
]

that the same result holds for the larger class
{\cal B}

\varepsilon_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}\asymp n^{-2/d},\quad 1\leq p<\infty.

(7.9)

Here
p=\infty
is excluded because, as noted in
[
7
]
, the class
{\cal B}
is not compact in
L_{\infty}
.

We have mentioned that sampling numbers are not expected to decay faster than the entropy numbers. It is interesting to note that our results from §
4
and §
5

show that both
\rho_{n}({\cal L})_{L_{p}}
and

\rho_{n}({\cal B})_{L_{p}}
decay like

n^{-2/d}
(up to logaritmic factors) when
1\leq p\leq d
, and therefore, up to logarithmic factors, provide the rate of decay of the entropy numbers for
{\cal L}
and
{\cal B}
.
