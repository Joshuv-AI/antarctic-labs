Optimizing early triage for subclinical cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: longitudinal associations, incremental value, and clinical utility of nine insulin resistance surrogates
Authors: Yingxiu Huang, Xiaoxiao Qu, Shenrui Xu, Peng Zhen, Mei Yao, Jiachao Luo, Ruyu Cui, Chi-E Qiu, Guosong Jiang, Jing Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome stage 3 represents a critical, yet highly insidious, window of subclinical target organ damage. We aimed to comprehensively compare nine baseline non-insulin-based surrogate indices of insulin resistance to predict progression to CKM stage 3 and determine their scenario-specific clinical utility.MethodsThis longitudinal study included 2958 adults initially at CKM stages 0–2 from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Independent associations were assessed using multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic splines. Discriminative ability was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Incremental predictive values over a basic clinical model were quantified via continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). The net clinical benefit was further evaluated through decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsDuring follow-up, 497 (16.8%) participants progressed to CKM stage 3. After adjusting for comprehensive covariates, all nine indices were independently associated with the outcome. Notably, composite indices coupling systemic glucolipotoxicity with central adiposity exhibited preferable predictive superiority. Triglyceride-glucose index combined with waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) and triglyceride-glucose index combined with waist circumference (TyG-WC) yielded the highest odds ratios per 1-SD increment (1.86 and 1.82, respectively). Incorporating them into the basic model significantly increased the AUC and provided the most substantial improvements in patient reclassification (NRI: 0.524 and 0.512, respectively; both P < 0.001) and discrimination (IDI). Furthermore, DCA confirmed that models augmented with TyG-WC or TyG-WHtR provided the highest net clinical benefit across varying threshold probabilities. This prognostic dominance remained robust across four stringent sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsTyG-WC and TyG-WHtR are the most robust, independent predictors of incident CKM stage 3. They offer a highly actionable, scenario-adaptable screening strategy: TyG-WC serves as an ultra-accessible “first-pass” filter for large-scale public health triage, while TyG-WHtR provides a calibrated prognostic anchor for high-threshold, individualized clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03266-1.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Our findings advocate a pragmatic, “dual-star” scenario-adaptable screening strategy: TyG-WC serves as an ultra-accessible, cost-effective “first-pass” filter for large-scale public health triage, whereas TyG-WHtR provides a calibrated prognostic anchor for high-threshold, individualized clinical decision-making (e.g., initiating pharmacotherapy). Integrating these non-invasive tools into routine electronic health record (EHR) systems will proactively shift global CKM syndrome management from late-stage reaction to early interception.

Background

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a conceptual framework recently delineated by the American Heart Association (AHA), represents a systemic pathophysiological cascade driven by metabolic derangements [1]. The transition to CKM stage 3—characterized by subclinical cardiovascular disease (CVD) or high-risk chronic kidney disease (CKD)—serves as the ultimate and most critical breakpoint for halting irreversible end-organ damage [2–4]. However, the precise diagnosis of CKM stage 3 relies heavily on complex laboratory assays or advanced imaging modalities (e.g., echocardiography and precise urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratios) [1, 5]. This high diagnostic threshold renders stage 3 highly insidious, particularly in resource-limited primary care settings. Furthermore, a substantial “survivor bias” exists in current epidemiological literature, where large-scale longitudinal cohorts frequently bypass stage 3 to investigate stage 4 (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke) simply because established hard endpoints are easier to capture via follow-up systems [6]. Consequently, identifying simple, cost-effective, and noninvasive biomarkers to accurately predict the insidious progression to CKM stage 3 remains an urgent unmet need in the field of CVD prevention.

Insulin resistance (IR) and central adiposity are the core engines propelling the CKM cascade [7–9]. Given the impracticality of the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp in routine practice, a myriad of non-insulin-based surrogate indices have emerged, such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, TyG combined with the waist circumference (TyG-WC), and TyG combined with the waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) [10–13]. Mechanistically, these indices capture various dimensions of cardiometabolic risk, ranging from systemic glucolipotoxicity (TyG) to a proinflammatory milieu induced by visceral adiposity (WC, WHtR) [11]. While previous studies have linked these individual surrogates to CVD or metabolic mortality [14–17], two evident knowledge gaps persist. First, there is a profound scarcity of rigorous “head-to-head” comparisons within high-quality longitudinal cohorts to determine which specific index (or combination) offers the optimal predictive hierarchy. Second, contemporary research increasingly focuses on the “long-term trajectories” or “cumulative exposure” of these indices [18, 19]. Trajectory analyses hold theoretical value in explaining chronic pathophysiological mechanisms; however, real-world clinical practice demands immediate point-of-care decision-making. Baseline indices are the clinically preferred tools for frontline bedside screening and risk stratification, owing to their instantaneous availability and high clinical operability.

To bridge these critical translational gaps, we conducted a comprehensive secondary analysis using the nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort. The primary objective was to perform a definitive head-to-head comparison of nine baseline non-insulin-based IR surrogates for predicting the longitudinal progression to CKM stage 3 among an initially at-risk population (stages 0–2). By integrating traditional epidemiological metrics (e.g., multivariable regression and net reclassification improvement [NRI]), we hypothesized that composite indices coupling systemic glucolipotoxicity with central adiposity would exhibit superior predictive discrimination and clinical net benefit, thereby offering a highly pragmatic tool for early CKM risk triage.

Methods

Data source

This secondary analysis was based on the CHARLS, an ongoing, nationally representative prospective cohort study. Initiated in 2011, CHARLS employed a multistage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size sampling design to recruit 17,708 adults aged ≥ 45 years from 28 provinces in mainland China [20]. The baseline survey achieved an 80.5% response rate, ensuring robust representativeness of the population. Participants were prospectively followed up every 2–3 years until 2020. Trained personnel collected standardized anthropometric measurements and venous blood samples. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Study population

The 2011 (wave 1) and 2015 (wave 3) CHARLS surveys were selected as the baseline and follow-up assessments, respectively, because the precise staging of early to intermediate CKM syndrome (stages 0–3) relies fundamentally on laboratory biomarkers (e.g., blood glucose levels, lipid profiles, and renal function), which were exclusively collected during these two specific waves. Of the 17,708 participants enrolled at baseline, individuals were sequentially excluded if they were < 45 years old, lacked age data (N = 648), or had missing essential baseline variables for CKM staging (including anthropometric indices, biomarker data, and CVD status; N = 7207). To establish a cohort strictly at early risk, we further excluded 4,675 participants who presented with advanced CKM (stages 3 and 4) at baseline. Among the remaining 5178 individuals in baseline CKM stages 0–2, we excluded those lacking follow-up CKM data at wave 3 (N = 1788) and those with extreme outlier values in the core parameters (N = 90). Notably, to maintain a homogeneous study endpoint and specifically target the critical prevention window of subclinical target organ damage, we excluded 342 participants who rapidly progressed to overt clinical CVD (CKM stage 4) by wave 3. The final analytical sample comprised 2958 participants (Fig. 1). We compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants to assess potential selection bias (Supplementary Table S1).

Exposure variables

The primary exposures in this study were the nine baseline non-insulin-based surrogate indices of IR. Conceptually, these indices were categorized into three dimensions: the fundamental metabolic index (TyG), the inflammation-integrated metabolic index (composite triglyceride index; CTI), and seven composite indices that coupled systemic glucolipotoxicity (TyG) with various anthropometric measures of general, regional, or central adiposity (TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, TyG-WWI, TyG-ABSI, TyG-BRI, and TyG-LAP). These anthropometric components represented the body mass index (BMI), WC, WHtR, weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), body shape index (ABSI), body roundness index (BRI), and lipid accumulation product (LAP). Notably, potential surrogates, such as the cholesterol-to-high-density lipoprotein index and its derivatives and TyG-related indices incorporating high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), were initially evaluated; however, they were excluded from the final analysis to avoid potential overfitting and statistical redundancy, as the HDL-C level is an intrinsic component of the CKM staging criteria. All constituent parameters essential for these calculations, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and standardized anthropometric measurements (height, weight, and WC), were rigorously obtained at the wave 1 baseline. Detailed mathematical formulas and original literature references for each index are provided in Supplementary Table S2. For statistical modeling, these indices were evaluated as standardized continuous variables (per 1-standard deviation [SD] increment) and categorical quartiles (with the lowest quartile [Q1] assigned as the reference group) to robustly capture both linear and potentially nonlinear risk trajectories.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of this study was the incident progression to CKM stage 3 at the 2015 follow-up (wave 3) among participants with CKM stages 0–2 at baseline (2011). In accordance with the AHA presidential advisory, the CKM stages were adjudicated based on clinical and laboratory criteria [1]. Stage 0 was defined as the absence of all CKM risk factors. Stage 1 was characterized by excess or dysfunctional adiposity, including overweight/abdominal obesity or impaired fasting glucose levels (100–124 mg/dL or glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] levels of 5.7–6.5%). Stage 2 included individuals with established metabolic risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, or moderate-risk CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] of 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m2). The study endpoint, CKM stage 3, was identified by the presence of subclinical CVD, indicated by a predicted 10-year CVD risk ≥ 20% (based on the Framingham Risk Score) or very high-risk CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2). To ensure a homogeneous focus on the progression of subclinical target organ damage, individuals who had already progressed to overt clinical CVD (CKM stage 4, including heart disease or stroke) by the wave 3 assessment were excluded from the primary analysis, as described in the study population section. The detailed diagnostic criteria for each CKM stage are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Covariates

To ensure the statistical independence of the observed associations, covariates were selected based on the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) (Supplementary Fig. S1). These variables were classified into three dimensions: (1) demographic and socioeconomic status, including age, sex, Han ethnicity, marital status, residence (urban or rural), and educational level; (2) lifestyle behaviors, including smoking and drinking status; and (3) clinical and metabolic parameters, such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), systolic blood pressure (SBP), self-reported chronic lung disease, and antidiabetic medication use. Notably, the eGFR was adjusted for as a baseline covariate. Although the eGFR was integrated into the CKM staging framework, its inclusion in our models primarily served as a quantitative marker of baseline renal reserve and systemic microvascular health. By adjusting for baseline eGFR, we aimed to calibrate the physiological starting point of each participant within the stages 0–2 CKM spectrum, thereby ensuring that the predictive value of IR-related indices for future CKM progression was independent of the initial renal function status.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study population were compared across CKM stages. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were analyzed using the Chi-square test.

Prior to formal inferential analyses, rigorous model validation diagnostics were conducted to verify the structural assumptions of the logistic regression models. To optimize the analytical efficiency and avoid methodological redundancy, representative indices from distinct conceptual dimensions (specifically, TyG, CTI, TyG-BMI, and TyG-WC) were selected for these diagnostic checks, thus confirming the overall reliability of the modeling framework (Supplementary Figs. S2, S3, S4 and S5).

Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the associations between baseline IR indices and incident CKM stage 3. Three models were fitted sequentially: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and nation; and Model 3 was fully adjusted for age, sex, nation, residence, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, education, LDL-C level, SBP, eGFR, antidiabetic drug use, and chronic lung disease. To explore potential nonlinear dose–response relationships, restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots were employed. Subgroup analyses accompanied by likelihood ratio tests for interactions were performed to verify the robustness of these associations across diverse populations.

For indices demonstrating significant independent associations, we quantified their incremental predictive values beyond the basic clinical model using the change in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), continuous NRI, and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Based on these discriminative metrics, three top-performing indices (TyG-WHtR, TyG-WC, and TyG-BMI) were advanced for further evaluation. Their model calibration was visually assessed using calibration plots, and their clinical utility was quantified using DCA to ascertain the net clinical benefit across various threshold probabilities.

To further ascertain the robustness and reliability of our primary findings, a series of rigorous sensitivity analyses were conducted across four distinct scenarios. First, to eliminate the potential masking effect of pharmacological interventions on metabolic and hemodynamic profiles, we excluded participants who were actively using antidiabetic or antihypertensive medications. Second, to strictly rule out the structural confounding of pre-existing subclinical renal impairment on CKM progression, the analysis was restricted to individuals with an eGFR ≥ 75 mL/min/1.73 m2. Third, a complete-case analysis was performed by excluding participants with missing covariate data, to assess the impact of missingness. In addition, to mitigate the disproportionate influence of extreme values, the dataset was truncated to the 1st–99th percentile range for all continuous variables. Finally, we conduct sensitivity analysis by redefining the composite endpoint As incident progression to either CKM stage 3 or stage 4. In each of these four sensitivity scenarios, both multivariable logistic regression and incremental predictive value metrics (AUC, continuous NRI, and IDI) were systematically reevaluated.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and the Free Statistics Analysis platform (version 2.4.0, Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) [21]. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value of < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics and incidence of CKM stage 3

The baseline characteristics of the 2958 eligible participants, stratified by their progression to CKM stage 3 at wave 3 follow-up, are summarized in Table 1. During the follow-up period, 497 patients (16.8%) progressed to CKM stage 3. Compared with non-progressors (stages 0–2), those who progressed to stage 3 were generally older (58.6 ± 6.1 vs. 54.3 ± 6.4 years, P < 0.001), with a significantly higher proportion of males (70.6% vs. 26.1%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the progressors exhibited a higher baseline prevalence of hypertension (52.1% vs. 38.3%, P < 0.001) and higher rates of current smoking and alcohol consumption (both P < 0.001).

Regarding clinical and metabolic parameters, the progressors displayed a more deteriorated cardiometabolic and renal profile at baseline, characterized by significantly elevated SBP and fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, CRP, and uric acid levels, coupled with a markedly lower eGFR (101.6 ± 10.9 vs. 108.6 ± 9.8 mL/min/1.73 m2, P < 0.001).

Notably, while there was no significant difference in general obesity, as measured by BMI, between the two groups (23.4 ± 3.6 vs. 23.5 ± 3.7 kg/m2, P = 0.801), progressors had a significantly larger WC (85.4 ± 9.6 vs. 83.8 ± 9.6 cm, P < 0.001). This paradoxical finding underscores the potential superiority of central adiposity over general adiposity in signaling early CKM risk. Consistent with this finding, among the evaluated surrogate indices of IR, the composite index TyG-WC was significantly elevated at baseline in the progression group (734.3 ± 111.1 vs. 719.6 ± 106.0, P = 0.005).

Notably, among the evaluated parameters, the composite indices coupling systemic glucolipotoxicity with central adiposity yielded the most pronounced effect sizes. Specifically, each 1-SD increase in TyG-WHtR and TyG-WC corresponded to an 86% (OR: 1.86, 95% CI 1.61–2.15) and 82% (OR: 1.82, 95% CI 1.59–2.08) elevated risk of CKM stage 3 progression, respectively. These effect magnitudes outperformed that of TyG-BMI (OR: 1.77, 95% CI 1.54–2.03) and substantially exceeded that of the fundamental metabolic index TyG (OR: 1.54, 95% CI 1.35–1.76) or CTI (OR: 1.49, 95% CI 1.31–1.69). This robust risk gradient was consistently preserved when the indices were categorized into quartiles. Compared with participants in the lowest quartile (Q1), those in the highest quartile (Q4) of TyG-WHtR (OR: 4.60, 95% CI 3.08–6.89) and TyG-WC (OR: 4.49, 95% CI 3.07–6.56) exhibited more than a four-fold increased risk of advancing to CKM stage 3 in the fully adjusted model.

To further elucidate the dose–response trajectories, RCS analyses were performed (Fig. 2). The RCS models revealed continuous, predominantly linear, positive associations across the spectrum of the top-performing indices (such as TyG-WHtR and TyG-WC; P for overall < 0.001, P for nonlinearity > 0.05), indicating a steady and proportional escalation of CKM progression risk in tandem with deteriorating composite IR profiles.

Subgroup analyses

To further validate the stability of these longitudinal findings, exploratory subgroup analyses were performed across the prespecified demographic and clinical strata (Fig. 3). Overall, the positive associations between the TyG-derived indices and the risk of incident CKM stage 3 were highly consistent across subgroups stratified by sex, age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), smoking status, and baseline CKM stage (0, 1, or 2). Crucially, for the top-performing composite index, TyG-WHtR, no significant effect modifications were observed across any of the evaluated strata (all P for interaction > 0.05), highlighting its universal prognostic applicability regardless of the initial metabolic status or demographic background. Although a marginal interaction with the residence was noted for TyG-WC (P for interaction = 0.033), the risk magnitude remained substantial and statistically significant in both urban and rural populations.

Incremental predictive value and clinical utility of TyG-derived indices

To evaluate whether baseline TyG-derived indices could enhance CKM risk stratification beyond established risk factors, we assessed their incremental predictive value by incorporating them into a comprehensive basic clinical model. This baseline model (comprising age, sex, nation, residence, marital status, smoking, drinking, education, LDL-C level, SBP, eGFR, lung disease, and antidiabetic medication use) demonstrated a robust baseline discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.857 (95% CI 0.841–0.873).

As detailed in Table 3 and illustrated by the ROC curves (Fig. 4A), the addition of any evaluated IR surrogate significantly improved the predictive performance of the base model. In a head-to-head comparison, composite indices integrating central adiposity, specifically TyG-WHtR and TyG-WC, exhibited the most substantial incremental prognostic value. The incorporation of TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR increased the AUC to 0.872 and 0.871, respectively. These two indices dramatically improved the patient reclassification, yielding the highest continuous NRI (0.5243 for TyG-WHtR and 0.5116 for TyG-WC; both P < 0.001) and IDI (0.0244 for TyG-WHtR and 0.0259 for TyG-WC; both P < 0.001). These improvements considerably outperformed those achieved using the fundamental TyG index (NRI: 0.4332; IDI: 0.0165) and CTI.

To ascertain the reliability of the top-performing models (augmented with TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, or TyG-BMI), we further evaluated their calibration and clinical utility. Calibration plots (Fig. 4B) revealed excellent agreement between the predicted probabilities and the actual observed frequencies of incident CKM stage 3, with curves closely tracking the ideal 45-degree diagonal reference line. Sensitivity analyses

The core findings of this study remained virtually unaltered across all the predefined restrictive scenarios. Specifically, the independent positive associations and superior incremental predictive values (assessed via continuous NRI and IDI) of the top-performing indices—TyG-WHtR and TyG-WC—were robustly preserved even after excluding participants actively using antidiabetic or antihypertensive medications (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5), restricting the cohort to individuals with preserved baseline renal function (eGFR ≥ 75 mL/min/1.73 m2) (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7), performing complete-case analysis by omitting missing covariates (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9), truncating continuous variables to the 1st–99th percentiles (Supplementary Tables S10 and S11), and redefining the composite endpoint as incident progression to either CKM stage 3 or stage 4 (Supplementary Tables S12 and S13). This remarkable consistency confirms that the prognostic superiority of coupling systemic glucolipotoxicity with central adiposity is highly stable and strongly suggests it is not an artifact driven by pharmacological masking, pre-existing subclinical renal impairment, missing data, or extreme biological outliers.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first and most comprehensive longitudinal investigation to conduct a head-to-head comparison of baseline non-insulin-based IR surrogates for predicting insidious progression to CKM stage 3 in a nationally representative cohort. Our principal finding is a valuable incremental advance: composite indices that strictly coupled systemic glucolipotoxicity with central adiposity, specifically TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR, exhibit robust and substantially superior prognostic value. Their predictive hierarchy profoundly surpassed that of isolated metabolic metrics (e.g., the fundamental TyG index or CTI) and indices that incorporated general adiposity (e.g., TyG-BMI). Crucially, this prognostic superiority is not merely a statistical artifact. It was rigorously cross-validated through traditional epidemiological metrics (yielding the highest NRIs), quantified for real-world clinical utility (via DCA).

Consistent with previous literature, our study confirmed that composite IR indices are powerful predictors of adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [22–26]. However, our investigation substantially advances the current knowledge base by addressing three critical conceptual and methodological limitations inherent in existing studies. First, whereas prior longitudinal cohort studies have predominantly focused on irreversible, hard clinical endpoints, such as overt cardiovascular events or mortality (corresponding to CKM stage 4) [14, 27, 28], we strategically targeted the insidious progression to CKM stage 3, directly addressing the AHA mandate to capture the critical, reversible window of subclinical target organ damage. Second, earlier head-to-head comparisons utilized HDL-dependent indices, such as the Chinese visceral adiposity index, which inevitably introduced structural confounding and mathematical overfitting because HDL-C is an intrinsic component of CKM staging; however, our deliberate exclusion of such parameters ensured absolute statistical independence [29, 30]. Finally, we transcended the conventional regression models and basic AUC comparisons by synergizing incremental predictive metrics (continuous NRI and IDI) and clinical utility analyses (DCA) with an advanced ML pipeline (encompassing Boruta feature selection and SHAP global interpretability). This multidimensional and exhaustive evaluation framework conclusively established TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR not only as statistically significant correlates but also as highly actionable and superior clinical tools.

Echoing partial findings from recent investigations, our results consistently demonstrated the prognostic supremacy of TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR [31–34]. Remarkably, this superiority was not isolated to a single metric but permeated the entire analytical pipeline, from traditional multivariable regression and nonlinear trajectory analyses to incremental reclassification improvements and ML-derived global feature importance. The predictive capabilities of these two composite indices can be elucidated using both biological and statistical methods. Biologically, progression to CKM stage 3 is fundamentally driven by a vicious pathophysiological cycle that involves visceral adiposity-induced chronic systemic inflammation and ectopic fat deposition-mediated IR [35, 36]. While the fundamental TyG index captures the biochemical dimension of systemic glucolipotoxicity (primarily reflecting hepatic and muscular IR), isolated morphological metrics such as the BMI fail to differentiate between subcutaneous and visceral fat [37–39]. By multiplying TyG by the central adiposity markers (WC or WHtR), the resulting composite indices mathematically synergize the two parallel disease engines. They act as holistic proxies for “visceral adiposity syndrome,” effectively mirroring the proinflammatory adipokine cascade (e.g., elevated levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6) that directly fuels endothelial dysfunction and microvascular target organ damage [40–42]. Statistically, coupling a circulating biochemical parameter with an anatomical measurement creates “orthogonal” information dimensions. This integration effectively reduces the collinearity and mathematical redundancy that often affect models that rely exclusively on multiple blood biomarkers, thereby maximizing the information entropy and predictive yield within a single composite variable.

While both TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR exhibited top-tier performance, a nuanced dissection of our data revealed that TyG-WC demonstrated a slight yet consistent advantage across the majority of traditional discriminative and reclassification metrics (e.g., marginally higher AUC and IDI). This subtle superiority likely stems from the fact that the absolute WC is the most direct and unadulterated anatomical proxy for visceral fat accumulation. However, it is crucial to recognize the unique demographic context of our aging cohort (CHARLS, adults aged ≥ 45 years). In elderly populations, age-related height loss due to osteoporosis, vertebral compression, or postural changes can introduce unavoidable physiological noise into the denominator of the WHtR calculation, occasionally perturbing its predictive stability compared with the raw measurement of WC [43].

Rather than pitting these two indices against each other as mutually exclusive competitors, our findings advocate for a pragmatic, “dual-star” scenario-based strategy in clinical translation. The TyG-WC has emerged as an optimal tool for large-scale public health screening and primary care triage [32]. It requires only the most rudimentary infrastructure involving standard blood testing (FBG and TG levels) and a tape measure. Its unmatched cost-effectiveness and computational simplicity make it the perfect “first-pass” filter to universally screen asymptomatic populations and flag those at high risk of hidden CKM stage 3 progression. Conversely, in the context of highly individualized clinical decision-making, TyG-WHtR stands out. As evidenced by the DCA in this study, TyG-WHtR frequently offered superior net clinical benefits at varying higher-stakes threshold probabilities. By incorporating height, the WHtR inherently normalizes risk assessments across diverse body frames and extreme statures [44]. Therefore, when specialists face high-threshold clinical decisions, such as whether to initiate aggressive pharmacological interventions (e.g., sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors or glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists) versus intensive lifestyle modifications, TyG-WHtR provides a more calibrated and safety-oriented prognostic anchor, minimizing the risks of both over- and under-treatment [45]. Together, these two indices constructed a seamless, cost-effective stratification cascade for the early prevention of CKM syndrome.

The strengths of this study were as follows. First, the use of the nationally representative, multistage prospective CHARLS cohort ensured the robust generalizability of our findings to a broader aging population. Second, by strategically anchoring our primary endpoint to the insidious progression to CKM stage 3, we addressed a critical clinical blind spot, providing an evidence-based rationale for the early detection of subclinical target organ damage. Third, this study used a highly rigorous and multidimensional methodological design. Rather than relying solely on conventional analytical methods, we synergized traditional incremental reclassification metrics (NRI and IDI). Another methodological consideration is that our primary analysis excluded 342 participants who progressed to CKM stage 4 (overt CVD) during follow-up, ensuring the endpoint strictly focused on subclinical lesions. This truncation of the high-risk population may introduce potential selection bias and compromise the model’s discriminative ability by reducing outcome heterogeneity. However, a sensitivity analysis merging stages 3 and 4 as a composite outcome (Supplementary Tables S12, S13) yielded highly consistent results, with TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR maintaining their predictive superiority. This indicates that despite potential selection bias, the relative rankings and core conclusions regarding the predictive value of the indices remain reliable. Finally, the stability of our conclusions was further reinforced by a rigorous battery of sensitivity analyses, which carefully controlled for potential structural confounders such as pharmacological masking and the baseline renal reserve.

Despite its multiple strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged, although rigorous methodological steps have been undertaken to mitigate their effects. First, due to the lack of imaging-based markers (such as coronary artery calcium or carotid intima-media thickness) in the CHARLS database, CKM stage 3 was operationally defined using an FRS ≥ 20% as a validated surrogate, consistent with previous cohort studies. Although FRS ≥ 20% denotes predicted high risk rather than confirmed subclinical disease, and may introduce non-differential misclassification that typically biases results toward the null, this pragmatic approach allowed us to evaluate advanced CKM risk in a nationwide population. Future studies with integrated imaging and biomarker data are warranted to refine these findings. Second, the observational nature of the CHARLS cohort precluded the establishment of definitive causal inferences. However, our primary objective was to predict risk stratification, rather than causal determination. Furthermore, we employed DAG-directed exhaustive covariate adjustments and strict sensitivity analyses to ensure that the observed associations were highly robust and independently predictive. Third, our analyses relied on a single baseline measurement of IR surrogates, which inherently could not capture the dynamic metabolic trajectories over time. Nevertheless, as emphasized earlier, this perceived limitation perfectly aligned with realistic primary care scenarios, where frontline clinicians demand instantaneous single-point assessments for rapid bedside triage rather than waiting years to establish a trajectory. Fourth, despite comprehensive adjustments, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., detailed dietary habits or genetic variations) could not be entirely ruled out. Therefore, our models were subjected to extreme “stress tests” through a battery of sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of participants on vasoactive or metabolic medications and those with diminished renal reserve and truncation of statistical outliers, indicating that our findings are not artifacts driven by masked or extreme biological states. Fifth, a substantial proportion of participants from the original CHARLS cohort were excluded due to missing baseline biomarkers or follow-up CKM data, resulting in a potential selection bias. As detailed in Supplementary Table 1, the included sample was systematically younger, had a higher proportion of females and rural residents, and possessed a more favorable baseline metabolic profile than those excluded. This ‘healthy cohort effect’ implies that our findings may underestimate the true strength of the associations between insulin resistance surrogates and CKM progression in the general population. Consequently, the generalizability of our results may be restricted to individuals who regularly adhere to clinical follow-ups and laboratory screenings. Future studies based on electronic health records with lower missing rates are required to replicate these rankings. Six, CKM staging was solely eGFR-based owing to unavailable UACR data, failing to detect early albuminuric renal injury that elevates cardiometabolic risk despite preserved filtration [46, 47]. Future staging schemes should combine UACR and eGFR for improved risk assessment. Finally, while the cohort was a nationally representative of Chinese adults, external validation in diverse multiethnic populations remains the logical next step in confirming the global applicability of these indices.

Conclusions

This nationally representative study established TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR as the most robust independent predictors of CKM stage 3 progression. By coupling systemic glucolipotoxicity with central adiposity, they significantly outperformed traditional metabolic or obesity parameters. Clinically, we advocate a scenario-adaptable strategy wherein TyG-WC serves as a cost-effective, ultra-accessible “first-pass” filter for large-scale primary screening, while TyG-WHtR provides a calibrated prognostic anchor for high-threshold, individualized decision-making. Integrating these noninvasive tools into electronic health record systems and primary care algorithms is crucial. Such widespread implementation can facilitate the early identification of subclinical target organ damage, ultimately shifting global CKM syndrome management from late-stage reaction to proactive interception.
