Epigenetic and immunological alterations in umbilical cord blood of overweight/obese women with gestational diabetes mellitus: insights into DNA methylation signatures and immune cell dysregulation
Authors: Jiawei Wang, Qiuling Chen, Anji Chen, Yunfan Zhang, Feng Yan, Le Wu, Xiang Hu, Changqiao You, Jun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common pregnancy complication associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Epigenetic modifications may reflect intrauterine metabolic exposure and contribute to immune and metabolic alterations. This study aimed to explore DNA methylation profiles in umbilical cord blood from overweight and obese women with and without GDM.MethodsUmbilical cord blood samples from 30 overweight/obese pregnant women (with and without GDM) were analyzed using the Illumina 850 K methylation array to identify differentially methylated positions (DMPs) and regions (DMRs). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to assess the functional relevance of methylation changes. Immune cell composition was estimated using deconvolution analysis and further examined in an independent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) cohort. Lasso regression was applied to identify CpG sites associated with GDM status and construct a preliminary methylation-based classification model.ResultsA total of 23,331 hypermethylated and 29,501 hypomethylated DMPs were identified between women with and without GDM, with hypomethylation predominating. Enrichment analyses indicated associations with neurodevelopmental pathways, metabolic processes, immune regulation, and epigenetic modification. Immune deconvolution analysis suggested reduced proportions of CD4+ T cells (p < 0.05) and a trend toward decreased NK cells in the GDM group, alongside increased CD8+ T cells and neutrophils. Seven CpG sites were selected for model construction and demonstrated strong discriminatory performance within this cohort.ConclusionThis exploratory study identifies distinct cord blood DNA methylation patterns associated with GDM in overweight/obese pregnancies. The findings suggest potential links between epigenetic alterations and immune cell composition in GDM-exposed offspring. The identified CpG signature warrants further validation in larger, prospective cohorts to determine its clinical applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09547-7.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Umbilical cord blood, Overweight/obesity, DNA methylation, CD4+ T cells

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) refers to glucose intolerance first diagnosed during pregnancy, characterized primarily by significant insulin resistance, leading to maternal hyperglycemia, glucose stress response, and systemic inflammation [1]. Globally, GDM affects approximately 14–16% of pregnancies, accounting for an estimated 18–20 million births annually [2], and its prevalence continues to rise in parallel with increasing rates of obesity and type 2 diabetes [3–6]. Importantly, both mothers and offspring face elevated long-term metabolic risks. Offspring of mothers with GDM have a significantly higher risk of macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and subsequent development of obesity and T2DM compared with offspring of mothers without GDM [7].

Epigenetics plays an increasingly important role in the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases [8], with DNA methylation being one of the most studied and well-characterized markers [9]. It has been shown to be closely related to changes in the maternal intrauterine environment and can influence fetal gene expression and phenotype [10]. Numerous studies have explored the epigenetic abnormalities associated with GDM and its offspring. For instance, DNA methylation changes in genes involved in key physiological processes, such as lipid metabolism, insulin secretion, cell differentiation, inflammatory response, antigen processing and presentation, vitamin D metabolism, cell growth and apoptosis, and myocardial contraction, have been observed in adipose tissue [11–15], peripheral blood [16–18], umbilical cord blood [15, 19–25] and placental tissue [21–23, 25–29]. Furthermore, abnormal DNA methylation of imprinted genes, including maternal imprints such as MEST, DLGAP2, and KCNQ1, and paternal imprints such as H19, has also been reported [30–32]. Although these studies employed candidate gene and genome-wide epigenetic approaches with similar sample sizes and designs, the identified differentially methylated genes and pathways rarely overlap, suggesting that factors other than GDM may be involved in the regulation of these changes. Among these, overweight/obesity, as a common and significant comorbidity, warrants particular attention.

Overweight/obesity has been recognized as a global health issue, and its high prevalence during pregnancy is closely related to a variety of adverse pregnancy outcomes and complications [33, 34]. Maternal overweight/obesity not only directly affects maternal health during pregnancy but may also alter fetal gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms, increasing the risk of offspring becoming overweight/obese or developing cardiovascular and metabolic diseases in adulthood [35, 36]. However, current research on GDM has primarily focused on normal-weight women with GDM, and there is limited systematic research on the epigenetic characteristics of overweight/obese women with GDM.

To address this gap, this study aims to investigate the DNA methylation characteristics in the umbilical cord blood of overweight/obese women with GDM and analyze its potential impact on immune function. We employed the 850 K methylation array to perform comprehensive screening of umbilical cord blood samples from overweight/obese pregnant women, with the goal of revealing unique epigenetic regulatory patterns related to GDM under the overweight/obesity context, particularly focusing on immune cell composition. Through this study, we hope to provide new insights into the epigenetic and immune regulatory mechanisms under the interaction of maternal overweight/obesity and GDM, and lay the theoretical foundation for developing provide insights.

Materials and methods

Data download and preparation

In this study, the 850 K methylation data of umbilical cord blood from overweight/obese pregnant women with GDM and overweight/obese pregnant women without GDM were obtained from the self-constructed hnsfy_methydata cohort. The 450 K methylation array data of umbilical cord blood from normal-weight pregnant women with GDM and normal-weight pregnant women without GDM were retrieved from the GEO database (GSE153219). Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of umbilical cord blood mononuclear cells (CBMCs) from normal-weight pregnant women with GDM and normal-weight pregnant women without GDM were obtained from the GEO database (GSE212309). Each dataset was processed and analyzed independently without cross-platform merging.

Recruitment and screening of the hnsfy_methydata cohort

This exploratory observational study was conducted at Changsha Maternal and Child Health Hospital (China) between June and July 2022. During this period, pregnant women attending routine antenatal care were consecutively recruited. The study focused specifically on overweight and obese pregnant women. According to the Chinese adult BMI classification criteria (overweight ≥ 24 kg/m², obesity ≥ 28 kg/m²) [37], women with a pre-pregnancy BMI < 24 kg/m² were excluded. Pre-pregnancy weight was obtained from hospital medical records at the first prenatal visit and was not self-reported.

Additional exclusion criteria included multiple gestation, chronic hypertension, kidney or liver disease, severe infection, psychiatric disorders, maternal age < 15 years, congenital or chromosomal fetal abnormalities, pregestational diabetes, other major systemic diseases, and adverse pre-pregnancy lifestyle factors.

After screening, 30 overweight/obese pregnant women were included in the final analysis, comprising 20 women with GDM and 10 women without GDM. As enrollment was restricted to overweight and obese women recruited from routine obstetric care, the relatively high prevalence of GDM observed in this cohort is consistent with the established association between elevated BMI and increased GDM risk.

GDM was diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria based on a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), defined as meeting at least one of the following plasma glucose thresholds: fasting glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L; 1-hour glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L; or 2-hour glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [38].

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changsha Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. 2021013). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Collection of umbilical cord blood samples

Umbilical cord blood was collected within one hour after delivery by a certified nurse using EDTA-anticoagulated tubes, with a total volume of 10 mL collected in two tubes. Immediately following collection, the samples were promptly stored at -80 °C for future analysis.

DNA methylation experiment

DNA was extracted from whole blood using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen). The purity and concentration of the DNA were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Only DNA samples meeting quality requirements were used for subsequent analysis. Next, 500 ng of DNA from each sample was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, USA). The bisulfite-converted DNA was then hybridized to 850 K BeadChips according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina) and processed by Sinotech Genomics Co., Ltd.

Chip data analysis and quality control

Genome-wide DNA methylation data from different cohorts were generated using either the Illumina Human methylation450K or Infinium MethylationEPIC (850 K) BeadChip platform. Each dataset was analyzed separately. Raw IDAT files were processed using the ChAMP package (version 2.21.1) in R [39]. Beta (β) values were used to represent the proportion of methylation at each CpG site.

Quality control steps included removal of probes with detection p value > 0.01, probes with fewer than three beads in ≥ 5% of samples, non-CpG probes, multi-hit probes, SNP-related probes, and probes located on chromosomes X and Y. After filtering, data were normalized using the Beta Mixture Quantile (BMIQ) normalization method [40] to adjust type I and II probe bias.

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) analysis was performed to assess potential batch effects related to BeadChip slide and array position [41]. Batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm implemented in the sva package [41]. Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) and regions (DMRs) were identified using the “champ.DMP” and “champ.DMR” functions. CpG sites with adjusted p-value (adj.P.Val) < 0.05 were considered DMPs, and no additional Δβ cutoff was applied. DMRs were defined using thresholds of |Δβ| ≥ 0.2 and p < 0.05. Annotation was performed based on the hg19 reference genome.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.8) [42] to identify enriched Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Enrichment results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Classification model construction

Core CpG sites were identified using the Lasso algorithm from the glmnet R package (version 4.1-8) [43]. A classification model was constructed using the caret R package (version 6.0) [44], The hnsfy_methydata cohort was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%) to evaluate the classification performance of the selected CpG markers within the cohort.

Evaluation of immune cell subtype distribution

Immune cell subtype distribution was evaluated using the EpiDISH package (version 2.12.0) in R [45]. The normalized methylation β-value matrix was used as input. Cell-type deconvolution was performed using the reference-based constrained projection approach implemented in EpiDISH. The reference matrix for cord blood immune cell types provided within the package was applied to estimate the proportions of major immune cell subsets, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, monocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils.

scRNA-seq data processing

The scRNA-seq, which had undergone upstream processing (including quality control and genome alignment), were analyzed using the Seurat R package (version 4.3) [46]. Cells were filtered according to the following criteria: UMI counts > 1,000, UMI counts below the 97th percentile of total UMI counts, gene counts > 200, and mitochondrial UMI percentage < 10%. Normalization of gene expression for each cell was performed using the “LogNormalize” method implemented in the NormalizeData function, where expression values were scaled by the total expression, multiplied by a scaling factor of 10,000, and log-transformed. Highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function, with the top 2,000 genes retained for downstream analysis. The selected genes were then centered and scaled using the ScaleData function, and dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA).

Based on the PCA results, a shared-nearest neighbour graph was constructed using the FindNeighbors function, and cells were clustered using the FindClusters function. To enhance visualization, the RunTSNE function was applied. Differentially expressed gene markers for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, which compares gene expression in a given cluster with that of all other clusters. To mitigate technical artifacts, DoubletFinder R package (version 2.0) [47] was used to identify and remove putative doublets, improving data quality. Batch effects across samples were corrected using the Harmony R package (version 1.2) [48], ensuring effective integration and consistency across data.

Cell type annotation

The SingleR R package (version 2.2) [49] was used to assign cell types to each cluster based on reference datasets.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.3). Unless otherwise specified, continuous data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test or Student’s t-test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 or adj.P.Val < 0.05 was considered statistically significant across all analyses. Ns, *, **, *** and **** indicate not Significant, p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001, respectively.

Distribution analysis of DMPs and DMRs across the genome

Analysis of the hnsfy_methydata cohort identified 23,331 hypermethylated and 29,501 hypomethylated DMPs between overweight/obese women with and without GDM. These DMPs were distributed across multiple chromosomes (Fig. 1A), although the distribution was uneven. No DMPs were detected in several chromosomal regions (chr1:120311587–144008937 bp, chr9:38672608–71115783 bp, chr13:0–19448912 bp, chr14:0–20502652 bp, chr15:0–21937575 bp, chr16:35043866–46615086 bp, chr21:0–10990893 bp, and chr22:0–17564994 bp) (Supplementary file 1: Figure S1). Chromosomes 1, 2, 6, 11, and 17 exhibited relatively higher numbers of DMPs. Across most chromosomes, hypomethylated DMPs were more abundant than hypermethylated DMPs (Fig. 1B).

In the analysis of gene functional regions (Fig. 1C), DMPs were primarily located in gene body regions and intergenic regions (IGR, regions between annotated genes). Fewer DMPs were detected in the TSS200 and TSS1500 regions, defined as 200 bp and 1500 bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS), respectively, accounting for approximately 20% of all DMPs. Hypomethylated DMPs were more frequent than hypermethylated DMPs across most functional categories.

Analysis of CpG annotation (Fig. 1D) showed that DMPs were mainly distributed in CpG islands, shores (within 2 kb of CpG islands), shelves (within 2 kb of shores), and open sea regions (genomic regions outside islands, shores, and shelves). Hypomethylated DMPs were more prevalent than hypermethylated DMPs in CpG island regions. Because DMP analysis identifies methylation differences at individual CpG sites, we further performed DMR analysis to detect clusters of adjacent CpG sites with consistent methylation changes.

DMR analysis identified 42 hypermethylated and 211 hypomethylated regions (Fig. 1E). Several imprinted genes, including HOXA4, MEST, PHPT1, FBRSL1, DLK1, and NAA60, were detected among hypomethylated DMRs. Compared with DMPs, DMRs showed differences in chromosomal distribution (Fig. 1F), with no hypermethylated DMRs observed on chromosomes 13, 16, 20, and 21. A total of 233 DMRs were primarily located in promoter regions (Fig. 1G).

Functional enrichment analysis of DMR-associated genes

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on genes associated with DMRs to characterize their functional annotations. GO analysis (Fig. 2A) identified significant enrichment in biological processes related to neurodevelopment, chromatin organization, immune-related processes, metabolic pathways, and stem cell differentiation.

(B) Network plot illustrating GO enrichment results for DMR-associated genes across chromosomes. Nodes represent enriched GO terms and genes, and edges indicate gene–term associations. (C) Network plot showing KEGG pathway enrichment results for DMR-associated genes across chromosomes

Enriched neurodevelopment-related GO terms included neuron development, dopamine metabolism, and behavioral regulation. Terms associated with chromatin organization and epigenetic processes included histone modification and protein localization. Immune-related enrichment included immune suppression and receptor signaling regulation. Metabolic-related terms included nitrogen metabolism and pyridine synthesis. Enrichment was also observed in processes related to hematopoietic progenitor cell differentiation and tissue development.

KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 2A) identified enrichment in pathways related to immune response (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway and antigen processing and presentation), metabolism (e.g., fructose and mannose metabolism, steroid biosynthesis), and cell signaling (e.g., Hippo signaling pathway).

To examine chromosomal distribution of functional enrichment, GO and KEGG analyses were conducted for DMR-associated genes on individual chromosomes. GO analysis (Fig. 2B) showed that genes on different chromosomes were enriched in distinct biological processes. For example, chromosomes 1, 7, and 8 were enriched in processes related to protein localization and lipid metabolism, whereas chromosomes 3, 9, and 10 were enriched in cell cycle-related and neurodevelopment-associated terms. Chromosomes 4, 12, and 14 showed enrichment in skeletal development and immune-related categories.

KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 2C) further demonstrated chromosome-specific enrichment patterns. For instance, chromosomes 1 and 17 were enriched in arachidonic acid metabolism pathways, chromosomes 2, 3, 10, 11, 14, and 19 in amino acid and glycolipid metabolism pathways, and chromosomes 6 and 22 in immune-related pathways such as antigen presentation.

Identification of core CpG sites and their correlation with clinical parameters associated with GDM

To identify CpG sites associated with GDM status in umbilical cord blood from overweight/obese women, we applied Lasso regression analysis (Fig. 3A, B) and identified seven CpG sites (Supplementary file 2: Table S1): cg19037167 (TLR2), cg22998811 (ZNF563), cg02249039 (ASZ1), cg15150348 (MEST), cg17537719 (GLS), cg26538349 (DDX60), and cg26792694 (FAM118A). Among these, cg17537719 and cg26792694 were hypermethylated, whereas the remaining five CpG sites were hypomethylated in women with GDM compared to women without GDM.

(A) Coefficient profiles of CpG sites as a function of log(λ) in the Lasso regression model. (B) Binomial deviance plot used to determine the optimal λ based on minimum deviance. (C) Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values for the classification model in the hnsfy_methydata cohort, illustrating discrimination between women with and without GDM within this cohort. (D) Correlation analysis between selected CpG sites and clinical/metabolic parameters in overweight/obese women and their newborns, with correlation coefficients (R) and p-values indicated

These CpG sites were distributed across different functional genomic regions, including 5′UTR-shore, 5′UTR-open sea, gene body-shelf, TSS200-island, and TSS1500-shore regions.

Based on these seven CpG sites, we constructed a methylation-based classification model (Fig. 3C) using logistic regression, linear regression, random forest, and additional algorithms. All models demonstrated AUC values exceeding 0.83 within the hnsfy_methydata cohort. The logistic regression model was selected for further analysis. The model formula is as follows:

logit(P) = 12.09–7.26 * cg19037167 + 0.16 * cg22998811–6.89 * cg02249039–6.22 * cg15150348 + 2.20 * cg17537719–11.46 * cg26538349–0.96 * cg26792694.

Further correlation analysis (Fig. 3D) showed associations between these CpG sites and maternal and neonatal characteristics. Parity was positively correlated with cg22998811 (R = 0.41, p = 0.02) and negatively correlated with cg26792694 (R = − 0.43, p = 0.02). Maternal height showed a non-significant positive correlation with cg19037167 (R = 0.34, p = 0.07). In addition, fasting, 1-hour, and 2-hour glucose levels were significantly correlated with all seven CpG sites.

Immune cell proportions and CpG Site associations in umbilical cord blood

Immune cell proportions were estimated from DNA methylation data using the EpiDISH package in R. Immune cell deconvolution analysis was performed in the hnsfy_methydata and GSE153219 cohorts. Multiple immune cell types were detected in umbilical cord blood, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, eosinophils, monocytes, neutrophils, and NK cells (Fig. 4A).

Compared with normal-weight women (Fig. 4C), overweight/obese women exhibited significantly lower proportions of B cells, CD4+ T cells, eosinophils, and NK cells (p < 0.05), and higher proportions of CD8+ T cells, monocytes, and neutrophils (p < 0.05). In the normal-weight cohort (Fig. 4D), women with GDM showed decreasing trends in CD4+ T cells and NK cells and increasing trends in neutrophils compared with women without GDM, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. In overweight/obese women (Fig. 4B), those with GDM exhibited lower CD4+ T cell proportions (p < 0.05) and similar directional trends in NK cells, neutrophils, and CD8+ T cells.

Methylation levels of CpG sites associated with immune-related functional scores (including T cell activation, cytotoxicity, interferon response, and inhibitory scores) are shown in Fig. 4G.

To further characterize immune cell composition, scRNA-seq data from seven CBMC samples (four from women with GDM and three from women without GDM) were analyzed. Clustering of 57,147 cells identified 12 distinct cell populations (Fig. 4F, H), including plasma cells, B cells, neutrophils, γδ T cells, macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, mast cells, NK T cells, and naïve T cells. Comparison of cell-type proportions between groups (Fig. 4I) showed similar directional changes in B cells, CD4+ T cells, monocytes, and neutrophils as observed in the GSE153219 cohort.

Correlation analysis between core CpG sites and immune cell proportions (Fig. 4E) revealed significant positive correlations between CD4+ T cell proportion and cg15150348 (R = 0.47, p = 0.009), cg19037167 (R = 0.56, p = 0.001), cg22998811 (R = 0.40, p = 0.02), and cg26538349 (R = 0.43, p = 0.01), and a significant negative correlation with cg17537719 (R = − 0.41, p = 0.02). Neutrophil proportion was significantly negatively correlated with cg15150348 (R = − 0.45, p = 0.01) and cg19037167 (R = − 0.43, p = 0.02). Additional correlation results are presented in Fig. 4E. Overall, these findings indicate associations between methylation levels at selected CpG sites and immune cell proportions within the cohort.

Discussion

Through differential DNA methylation analysis of umbilical cord blood, we identified genome-wide methylation differences between overweight/obese women with and without GDM, consistent with previous studies in conventional GDM cohorts [19, 50]. We also observed that DMPs showed non-uniform regional distribution patterns across the genome in this overweight/obese cohort. Several genes, including CASP8, HLA-F, ESR1, MEST, MTNR1B, NRIP2, and HIF3A, were identified among significant DMPs and DMRs.

CASP8 (caspase-8) is involved in apoptosis and has been reported to participate in immune-inflammatory signaling pathways, including Toll-like receptor signaling and NF-κB-related processes [51–53]. HLA-F, a major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecule, has been implicated in placental immune tolerance and maternal–fetal immune interactions [54]. ESR1 has been linked to glucose metabolism homeostasis and insulin sensitivity [55], while MEST, an imprinted gene, has been associated with fetal growth outcomes in GDM pregnancies [32, 56]. MTNR1B has been associated with insulin secretion and genetic susceptibility to GDM (e.g., rs10830963) [57, 58]. NRIP2 has been reported to be related to metabolic regulation and β-cell function in diabetes-related contexts [59, 60]. HIF3A has been linked to metabolic traits and has been discussed in the context of maternal metabolic factors and placental-related pathways [24, 61, 62]. Collectively, these findings highlight CpG methylation changes in genes previously implicated in metabolic and immune-related processes in GDM; functional studies are needed to clarify biological roles and clinical relevance.

Genes located on the same chromosome often exhibit cis-coexpression, a coexpression pattern that has been described in previous studies [63, 64]. Through chromosomal-level functional and pathway enrichment analysis, we observed that DMR-associated genes on specific chromosomes were enriched in particular biological processes. For instance, enrichment analysis suggested associations of Chr1 with lipid metabolism and neuronal-related pathways, Chr2 with feeding behavior and innate immune-related processes, Chr6 with antigen processing and presentation, Chr10 with sphingolipid metabolism and cardiac-related pathways, and Chr19 with glycosaminoglycan metabolism and immune-related processes. These findings indicate chromosome-specific enrichment patterns; however, enrichment analysis does not establish coordinated regulation.

Notably, Chr1 (containing genes such as G0S2, PM20D1, and CYP2J2) and Chr7 (containing the NPY gene) have previously been associated with lipid metabolism and energy balance in overweight/obesity. G0/G1 switch gene 2 (G0S2), an inhibitor of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), has been implicated in lipid metabolism regulation; previous studies reported no significant change in its placental expression in GDM [65, 66]. PM20D1 (Peptidase M20 Domain Containing 1) has been linked to lipid metabolism and inflammatory pathways, and altered expression has been reported in GDM contexts [67, 68]. Additionally, CYP2J2 participates in lipid metabolism, detoxification, and glucose-related physiological processes, and its genetic polymorphisms have been associated with metabolic abnormalities during pregnancy [69–72]. NPY (neuropeptide Y) on Chr7 is involved in appetite and metabolic regulation and has been reported to be altered in metabolic disorders, including type 2 diabetes [73].

Overall, the identification of DMR-associated genes within these chromosomal regions suggests potential overlap between regional epigenetic variation and pathways previously implicated in metabolic dysregulation. However, further functional and longitudinal studies are required to clarify the biological relevance of these chromosomal enrichment patterns in GDM.

By constructing a classification model, this study analyzed the correlations between maternal characteristics, metabolic parameters, immune cell proportions, and core CpG sites, identifying seven CpG sites—cg19037167 (TLR2), cg22998811 (ZNF563), cg02249039 (ASZ1), cg15150348 (MEST), cg17537719 (GLS), cg26538349 (DDX60), and cg26792694 (FAM118A)—that were included in the model. Among these, the number of previous births showed a positive correlation with cg22998811 (ZNF563) and a negative correlation with cg26792694 (FAM118A), suggesting opposite correlation patterns between these two CpG sites.

ZNF563 (zinc finger protein 563), a transcription factor reported to be associated with erythrocyte development, exhibits higher expression in adult erythrocytes compared to fetal erythrocytes [74]. FAM118A has been studied in immune-mediated diseases and certain cancers, where it has been discussed as a potential genetic marker [75, 76]. The correlations observed in the present study indicate possible associations between these CpG sites and maternal reproductive characteristics; however, functional implications require further investigation. Additionally, the proportion of CD4+ T cells showed significant positive correlations with cg15150348, cg19037167, cg22998811, and cg26538349, and a significant negative correlation with cg17537719. These findings indicate associations between CpG methylation levels and immune cell proportions. TLR2 (Toll-like receptor 2) has been widely reported to participate in immune activation and inflammatory signaling pathways [77, 78]. GLS (glutaminase), which catalyzes glutamine metabolism, has been implicated in cellular metabolic processes and has been studied in cancer and cardiovascular disease contexts [79–81].

The observed positive and negative correlation patterns among the identified CpG sites and immune cell proportions may reflect complex associations between epigenetic variation and immune-related phenotypes. However, given the cross-sectional design and the use of delivery samples, causal relationships cannot be established. In summary, this study identified associations between core CpG sites and maternal phenotypic characteristics, metabolic parameters, and immune cell proportions in overweight/obese pregnancies with GDM. Further validation in larger and prospective cohorts, as well as functional studies, is necessary to clarify biological relevance and potential clinical applicability.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may limit statistical power and generalizability. Although significant associations were identified, the findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, independent, and preferably multi-center cohorts. Second, the analysis was limited to umbilical cord blood collected at delivery. Other relevant tissues, such as placenta and maternal peripheral blood, were not included. Multi-tissue investigations may provide a more comprehensive understanding of epigenetic variation associated with GDM. Third, this study focused solely on DNA methylation. Other epigenetic layers, including histone modifications and non-coding RNA regulation, were not examined. Integrating multi-omics approaches may help further characterize molecular alterations associated with GDM. In addition, although associations were observed between CpG methylation levels and immune cell proportions, the cross-sectional design and reliance on correlation analysis do not allow causal inference. Functional experiments and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify biological mechanisms and temporal relationships.

In summary, larger prospective studies and independent validation cohorts are necessary to confirm the robustness and reproducibility of these findings.

Conclusions

This study identified differential DNA methylation patterns in umbilical cord blood associated with GDM in overweight/obese pregnancies. Hypomethylated DMPs were predominant and were enriched in genomic regions related to metabolic-, immune-, and developmental-related pathways. Differences in immune cell proportions, particularly reduced CD4+ T cells, were also observed. In addition, several core CpG sites were associated with maternal characteristics, metabolic parameters, and immune cell proportions within this cohort. These findings suggest potential links between epigenetic variation and immune-metabolic features in GDM. However, given the cross-sectional design and the use of delivery samples, further validation in larger and prospective studies is required to determine biological relevance and potential clinical applicability.
