Healthcare resource utilization and readmission outcomes in patients treated with ceftolozane/tazobactam: real-world insights from the multinational SPECTRA study
Authors: Emre Yücel, Alex Soriano, Florian Thalhammer, Stefan Kluge, Pierluigi Viale, Mike Allen, Brune Akrich, Jessica Levy, Huina Yang, Sunny Kaul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance poses a significant threat to global health, with Gram-negative pathogens contributing to morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs. While ceftolozane/tazobactam provides an effective treatment option against these pathogens, real-world data on healthcare resource use associated with ceftolozane/tazobactam, especially outside the USA, remain limited.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, multinational observational study includes 617 adult inpatients from Australia, Austria, Germany, Italy, Mexico, Spain and the UK who received ≥48 hours of ceftolozane/tazobactam. The outcomes included 30-day all-cause and infection-related readmission rates, hospital length of stay and post-ceftolozane/tazobactam length of stay. Subgroup analyses were conducted by rank of ceftolozane/tazobactam initiation, ICU admission and cystic fibrosis status.ResultsThe overall 30-day all-cause readmission rate was 10.2%. The infection-related readmission rate was 4.9%. The median hospital length of stay was 42 days (IQR 22–71), and median post-ceftolozane/tazobactam length of stay was 6 days (IQR 1–25). Among ICU patients (n = 298), the 30-day all-cause readmission rate was 3.4% and median length of stay was 59 days. In cystic fibrosis patients (n = 64), the 30-day all-cause readmission rate was 10.9% and the median length of stay was 31.5 days (IQR 16–48.5).ConclusionsThis is the largest multinational, real-world dataset on healthcare resource use associated with ceftolozane/tazobactam outside the USA. These descriptive findings show differences in readmission rates and length of stay across rank-of-initiation groups; however, no causal relationships can be inferred from these data. The results are hypothesis-generating for future adjusted and economic analyses. Further research is warranted for economic evaluations and to optimize the timing of ceftolozane/tazobactam initiation.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a top-10 threat to global health as identified by the World Health Organization.1 AMR risks undermining decades of medical progress by rendering common infections and procedures riskier and more costly.2 The research and development pipeline of new antibiotics is insufficient to deal with rising levels of AMR.2 Globally, the per-patient cost of resistant infections ranges from US$2371 to US$29 289 and causes an average excess length of stay of 7.4 days.3 The global direct costs associated with AMR are estimated at US$66 billion per year, projected to rise to US$159 billion per year by 2050.4 In addition to direct costs, AMR has significant economic costs. The World Bank estimates losses of US$1–3.4 trillion per year of GDP due to AMR by 2030.2

To justify the cost of new generation antibiotics, such as ceftolozane/tazobactam (C/T), particularly for formulary inclusion and stewardship decisions, real-world evidence (RWE) on healthcare resource utilization (HCRU)—including hospital length of stay (LOS) and 30-day readmissions—is critical. In the UK, a single bed for a single day on a general ward costs £586.59 and a single bed for a single day on an ICU costs >£1600.5 In the UK in 2020–2021, 15.5% of all hospital discharges resulted in an emergency readmission within 30 days.5 In 2010, all emergency readmissions cost the NHS £1.6 billion, a number likely to have grown substantially.6 Therefore, early and appropriate administration of new generation antibiotics, such as C/T, play a role in managing infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens, and has been associated with better outcomes.7 Delayed administration of appropriate antibiotics often reflects treatment failure or progression of infection, which is associated with poorer clinical outcomes including higher rates of readmission, prolonged hospital stays and increased morbidity.8 This would increase cost burden to the society and healthcare systems.9 Timely, appropriate therapy can improve patient recovery trajectories, reduce complications and potentially lower mortality.10

Particularly relevant are high-risk subgroups such as ICU patients and those with cystic fibrosis (CF), who frequently experience AMR-related complications and incur greater HCRU. Early, appropriate use of novel agents like C/T could result in improved clinical outcomes,11,12 reducing LOS, readmissions and cost. Yet, data on real-world C/T use outside the US remain sparse, especially across many countries and complex patient subgroups. The cost of a single day of stay in an ICU is significantly higher than the cost of admission to a general ward. Drivers of this cost disparity include requirements for organ support such as mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy, and greater staffing costs due to the need for 1:1 nursing.13 Cystic fibrosis (CF) leads to disordered mucociliary clearance and recurrent pulmonary infections that necessitate frequent courses of antibiotics, making those infections high risk for developing AMR. Drivers of HCRU among patients with CF include high rates of hospitalization and frequent clinical need for intravenous (IV) antibiotics.14

While cost-effectiveness models for C/T have been developed from randomized controlled trial data and show promising results, RWE is needed to demonstrate the real-world HCRU outcomes associated with C/T. Previously, most RWE studies on C/T have been based in the USA. Given regional variations in healthcare systems, RWE on HCRU outcomes for wider populations treated with C/T is vital to understand how those outcomes vary regionally across complex patient subgroups, including ICU and CF populations. This study addresses this evidence gap by examining HCRU outcomes from Study of Prescribing patterns and Effectiveness of Ceftolozane/Tazobactam Real-world Analysis (SPECTRA), a multinational retrospective study evaluating C/T use across seven countries. Our descriptive analysis explores variations in outcomes by rank of C/T initiation (order of antibiotic administration), ICU status and CF status, offering key insights for policy, stewardship, and economic planning.

Materials and methods

The SPECTRA was a multinational, multi-centre, retrospective study on adult inpatients treated with C/T designed to provide real-world data on outcomes associated with C/T use. Data were collected from patients in Australia, Austria, Germany, Italy, Mexico, Spain and the UK. In this paper we report outcomes related to HCRU. Clinical outcomes have been reported elsewhere.15–17

Study design

Patients were included if they were >18 years old, had received at least 48 hours of C/T and had received their last dose at least 30 days before the date of data extraction. In total, 617 patients were included. The study design has been reported previously.15–17

The study was conducted in compliance with the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices,18 the ethical principles arising from the Declaration of Helsinki,19 the European Union Good Pharmacovigilance Practices, European and national laws in terms of data protection, and all applicable local regulations. Before the enrolment of any patients, approval from the relevant local independent ethics committee or institutional review board was procured, where required by national laws and guidelines.

Measurements and outcomes

Sociodemographic, clinical and HCRU data were collected from the study population. The infection for which the patient was first treated with C/T is considered the index infection, and the hospitalization during which the patient received this treatment is considered the index hospitalization. Rank of C/T initiation was defined by chronological order of start dates of all anti-pseudomonal Gram-negative antibacterials given for the index infection. When two antibiotics were initiated on the same calendar date, rank was assigned using the antimicrobial spectrum hierarchy per the study statistical analysis plan (first line, second line, third line), with third-line agents considered a later rank than second line, and second line a later rank than first line. Pathogen data were available for 428 of the 617 patients; pathogen proportions reported are calculated among patients with available culture data.

In this paper we report on 30-day all-cause readmission rate (RR), 30-day infection-related readmission rate (IRR) and hospital LOS. RR was defined as readmission to the same hospital within 30 days of the last dose of C/T, and IRR was defined as readmission to the same hospital due to any infection within 30 days of the last dose of C/T. RR and IRR were calculated as the number of patients readmitted within 30 days divided by the total number of patients in the analysis population (n = 617). Patients who remained hospitalized at 30 days after the last C/T dose remained in the denominator and could not contribute an event of 30-day readmission; patients who died before 30 days remained in the denominator and were not considered at risk for readmission. Readmission captures were limited to the same hospital; readmissions at other hospitals were not captured.

The hospital LOS was the duration from the index hospitalization to discharge, or death for patients who died during the index hospitalization. The numbers of patients hospitalized 30 days after the last day of C/T are reported. The median LOS and post-C/T length of stay are calculated. For patients who remained hospitalized >30 days after the last dose of C/T, the post-C/T LOS was imputed as 31 days and the total LOS was calculated based on this. For patients admitted to ICU, the median ICU LOS was the duration from ICU admission due to index infection until ICU discharge. All LOS outcomes are reported as medians to avoid the results being skewed by uneven distributions, specifically that a small number of patients with abnormally long LOS would have a disproportionate impact on mean LOS. RR, LOS and numbers of patients hospitalized 30 days after the last day of C/T are further broken down by rank of C/T initiation. Subgroup analyses were carried out for overall hospitalized patients, patients with CF and patients admitted to an ICU.

Results

All patients

In total 617 patients were included in the final analysis, with the largest number of patients from Spain (n = 313, 50.7%), followed by 88 from the UK (14.3%), 59 from both Australia and Mexico (9.6%), 57 from Italy (9.2%), 29 from Germany (4.7%) and 12 from Austria (1.9%). The mean age was 57.4 years and 65.5% of patients were male. A total of 510 patients reported at least one comorbidity (82.7%), with a median number of 2.0 (IQR 1.0–3.0) comorbidities per patient reported. The most frequent comorbidities reported were immunocompromised status (n = 268, 43.4%), chronic pulmonary disease (n = 180, 29.2%) and organ transplant (n = 160, 25.9%). Microbiology results were available for 428 patients. Among those with cultures most patients were infected with Pseudomonas species (n = 378, 88.3%), Escherichia coli (n = 35, 8.2%) and Klebsiella species (n = 26, 6.1%). A full breakdown of baseline characteristics is available in Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

The overall RR was 10.2%, with the highest RR (18.2%) in the fifth-rank initiation group. The lowest RR was in the sixth-or-greater-rank initiation group (5%). The IRR was 4.9%. The highest IRR was in the fifth-rank initiation group (13.6%) while lowest was in the sixth-or-greater-rank initiation group (0%). When initiated as a first-rank therapy, the 30-day all-cause RR was 5.8% and IRR was 3.5% (Figure 1).

Median readmission rates for all patients by rank of C/T administration.

Overall, 87 patients (14.1%) remained hospitalized 30 days after the last day of C/T. The median LOS was 42 days (IQR 22–71 days). The greatest median LOS was among patients in the sixth-or-greater-rank initiation group (60 days) while the shortest LOS was among patients who were in the second-rank initiation group (30.5 days). The median post-C/T LOS was 6 days (IQR 1–25 days). The greatest median post-C/T LOS were among patients for whom C/T was initiated as fourth rank and sixth or greater rank (10 days); the smallest was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a third-rank therapy (5 days) (Table 1).

Patients in ICU

Among ICU patients (n = 298), the RR was 3.4%. This is considerably lower than among the general population. The greatest RR was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a fourth-rank therapy (4.7%); the smallest was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a first-rank therapy (1.4%). The overall IRR was 1.7%. The greatest IRR was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a fifth-rank therapy (3.8%); the smallest was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a first-rank therapy (1.4%) (Table 2).

In the ICU population, 64 patients (21.5%) remained hospitalized 30 days after the last day of C/T. Most patients who remained hospitalized were in the first four rank-of-administration groups. The median LOS was 59 days and median post-C/T length of stay was 15 days. The greatest median LOS was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a fifth-rank therapy (66 days); the shortest, 52 days, was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a second-line therapy. The greatest median post-C/T LOS was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a first-rank therapy (12 days) (Table 2). Admission to the ICU was related to the index infection in 125 (49.1%) of patients. In this group, the median ICU LOS was 19 days.

Patients with CF

There were 64 patients with CF included in the study population. The index infection was an exacerbation of a chronic respiratory infection in 36 patients (56.3%) and pneumonia in 15 patients (23.4%). Of the 64 patients with CF, 38 (59.4%) had received previous antibiotics or antifungals in the 30 days before the index date. Among patients with CF, the RR was 10.9%. The highest RR was 44.4%, among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a fifth-rank therapy. The IRR was 6.3%. The greatest IRR was among patients for whom C/T was initiated as a fifth-rank therapy (33.3%). There were no readmissions, infection-related or otherwise, recorded in the third-rank, fourth-rank or sixth-or-greater-rank initiation groups (Table 3).

Discussion

This descriptive analysis of the SPECTRA study provides important insights into HCRU associated with C/T use across a diverse patient population. Our findings demonstrate variations in HCRU by rank of C/T initiation. In addition, there were important differences in HCRU associated with the different patient subgroups—patients in the ICU, patients with CF and subgroups by rank-of-initiation—included in this descriptive analysis.

Across the overall cohort, later-rank initiation of C/T was associated with higher 30-day readmission rates and longer hospital stays. Notably, patients who received C/T as a fifth-rank therapy had the highest infection-related readmission rate (18.2%) and among the longest post-CT length of stay. These trends suggest that delayed initiation of C/T may reflect more complex clinical scenarios, including treatment failure or progression of infection, which in turn drives increased HCRU. However, due to the descriptive nature of this study and risk of confounding variables, no causal relationship can be established.

ICU patients in the ICU cohort had lower observed 30-day readmission rates but substantially longer median hospital and post-C/T LOS compared with the overall cohort. Several non-mutually exclusive explanations could account for this descriptive finding, including longer index hospital stays reducing the opportunity for post-discharge readmission to be captured within 30 days, higher in-hospital mortality (which precludes readmission) or differences in discharge planning and follow-up.20–22 In addition, ICU patients often receive increased antimicrobial stewardship monitoring due to additional parameters, which leads to earlier intervention and daily monitoring, which may contribute to improved infection control and clinical outcomes.23 We did not conduct adjusted analyses to evaluate these hypotheses and therefore cannot disentangle these factors. Together, these factors suggest that while ICU stays are longer and resource intensive, the quality and intensity of care delivered may effectively reduce short-term readmissions, highlighting the importance of tailored management strategies in critically ill populations.23

Among patients with CF there were significant variations in HCRU by rank of initiation. Notably, while 30-day all-cause readmission and 30-day infection related readmission were comparable to the general population, rates were significantly higher in the fifth-rank initiation group (44% and 33%, respectively) than in the general population (10.2% and 4.9%, respectively). A possible explanation is that each rank-of-initiation group within the CF subgroup was very small compared with the rank-of-initiation groups for the other subgroups. As a result, a small number of readmissions could have caused a disproportionate impact on the rate for that group. Patients with CF are repeatedly exposed to antibiotics throughout their lives, and therefore more likely to experience increasing levels of AMR during courses of treatment.24 These findings therefore contribute to the existing scientific evidence supporting use of novel therapeutics in this patient population, and indicate that C/T represents a treatment option associated with similar HCRU burden in both a general patient population and those with CF.

Further research is warranted which aims to elucidate the underlying drivers of HCRU variation, including clinical decision-making processes and resistance patterns. Economic evaluations of HCRU outcomes are also warranted to assess the cost-effectiveness of early versus delayed C/T initiation. Finally, interventional studies targeting reductions in readmission and length of stay could further highlight the value of C/T in real-world settings.

Conclusions

This descriptive real-world analysis from SPECTRA presents HCRU data related to real-world C/T usage including subgroup analyses of ICU patients and patients with CF. Importantly, variations in outcomes were observed on the basis of the timing of C/T initiation and across subgroups. The data demonstrate low rates of readmission and infection-related readmission across all populations, especially among patients in ICU. In the overall cohort, only 14.1% of patients remained hospitalized 30 days after the last C/T dose and the median post-treatment LOS was just 4 days, both indicators of relatively efficient resource use. However, since this was a descriptive study, care must be taken in drawing causal relationships relating to these conclusions. The findings support the need for continued research to optimize treatment sequencing and improve outcomes in patients with Gram-negative bacterial infections. A deeper understanding of HCRU patterns can inform antimicrobial stewardship strategies and guide healthcare planning for this complex and high-risk patient population. Future prospective studies and formal economic evaluations are needed to confirm these findings and guide optimal integration of C/T into antimicrobial stewardship frameworks.

Limitations

This real-world descriptive analysis of data from SPECTRA is the largest set of HCRU data associated with C/T prescribing outside the USA. The population included and subgroups analysed give valuable insight into the HCRU of populations which may not normally be included in interventional studies. However, as this was an observational study, no conclusions about causal relationships can be drawn from the data and unmeasured confounding factors, such as infection severity, comorbidities or microbiological data, may have influenced treatment decisions and outcomes.

Some subgroups, particularly those receiving later-rank therapy, had small sample sizes that may limit the generalizability of specific findings. Since the study was conducted soon after the introduction of C/T, infection specialists may have reserved C/T for higher rank interventions, potentially leading to an overall selection of more severely unwell patients than might otherwise be expected. This may have led to confounding, since the outcomes for higher rank-of-initiation groups may be worse due to a more severely unwell patient population.

The rank of initiation by C/T was considered as part of this study using an intentionally conservative approach. We focused on cases where C/T was used for a minimum of 48 hours. The investigators concentrated on GN infections—especially HABP/VABP, cUTI and cIAI—and reported the use under other comorbid situations such as CF and use of C/T in the ICU. Absolute timing data for C/T initiation were also not reported. Consequently, the rank of initiation reflects the sequence of antibiotic administration but does not distinguish between early and delayed initiation, which may have affected outcomes. Stratification by pathogen type or patient characteristics were also not available, which may affect rank of C/T administration, thus confounding these results.

Today, selecting appropriate clinical antibiotics is highly challenging, due to delays in test results and lack of proper antibiotic stewardship.25 More research is needed, particularly with a granular approach to evaluating antibiotics in the context of clinical and microbiological data. This would allow us to better understand how rank of initiation is associated with outcomes. While our study was focused only on C/T, we hope that this study will lead to more research investigating these possible associations. No adjusted or multivariable analyses were performed for HCRU outcomes; this paper reports only descriptive results. At present, re-analysis of the underlying dataset to perform adjusted modelling is not feasible within the present project due to data access constraints; such analyses are an important topic for future work.

The results of this study may be generalizable to similar populations presenting with Gram-negative bacterial infections; however, care should be taken when doing so as there is the possibility of biases in the dataset. As investigators were selected on a voluntary basis, it is possible that there was non-response selection bias. The list for the investigators contacted was provided by Merck Sharp & Dohme LLC, a subsidiary of Merck & Co., Inc., Rahway, NJ, USA, which is another possible source of investigator selection bias. As this was a retrospective study, it was reliant on information being accurately recorded in the patient’s records. Missing or incorrectly recorded information may result in estimates of associations that are susceptible to misclassification bias. Another limitation was the possibility of loss to follow-up, as only events occurring at the same hospital were captured in the data, excluding readmission at other facilities. In addition, data across centres was pooled for descriptive analysis meaning outcomes could not be assessed by centre type or level and no conclusions drawn for MDR organism rate or antimicrobial stewardship implementation by these stratifications. The study was designed to be as widely generalizable as possible, however, because of the risk of these biases, care should be taken in interpreting the results.
