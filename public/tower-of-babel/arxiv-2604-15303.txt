Abstract
. We prove a strong general-purpose bound for the diameter of a finite group depending only on the diameters of its composition factors and the maximal exponent of a normal abelian section. There are a number of notable applications: (1) if G G is a finite soluble group of exponent e e , diam ⁡ ( G ) ≪ e ​ ( log ⁡ | G | ) 8 \operatorname{diam}(G)\ll e(\log|G|)^{8} , (2) anabelian groups with bounded-rank composition factors have polylogarithmic diameter, (3) transitive soluble subgroups of S n S_{n} have diameter ≪ n 5 \ll n^{5} , and (4) Grigorchuk’s gap conjecture holds for any finitely generated group acting faithfully on a bounded-degree rooted tree. Additionally, conditional on Babai’s conjecture, (5) any transitive permutation group of degree n n has diameter bounded by a polynomial in n n (a folkloric conjecture), and (6) Grigorchuk’s gap conjecture holds for residually finite groups, and thus the conjecture reduces to the simple case.

1.
Introduction

If
G
is a group and
X\subseteq G
(usually a generating set), we write
\ell_{X}
for the length function with respect to
X
, i.e., if
g\in G
then
\ell_{X}(g)
is the length of the minimal representation of
g
as a product of elements of
X\cup X^{-1}
,
or
\infty
if none exists.
For a subset
S\subseteq G
we write
\ell_{X}(S)=\sup_{g\in S}\ell_{X}(g)
.
The
diameter
of a finite group
G
with respect to
X
is
\operatorname{diam}(G,X)=\ell_{X}(G)
, and we define

\operatorname{diam}(G)=\max\{\operatorname{diam}(G,X):X~\text{a generating set for}~G\}.

The most important conjectures about diameters of finite groups are the following two.
The first is folkloric, but seems to have first taken definite form in the early 1980s: it is implicit in Driscoll–Furst
[
16
]
, and explicit in Kornhauser–Miller–Spirakis
[
37
]
and McKenzie
[
45
]
.
The second is universally attributed to Babai, even though it first appeared in a joint paper with Seress
[
3
]
.

Conjecture 1

(folklore)
.

If
G\leq S_{n}
is transitive then
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{O(1)}
.

Conjecture 2

(Babai)
.

If
G
is a nonabelian finite simple group then
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll(\log|G|)^{O(1)}
.

Here and throughout the paper we are using standard big-
O
notation, as well as the Vinogradov notation
A\ll B
, which means
A\leq O(B)
.

There are plenty of examples of generating sets for
A_{n}
or
S_{n}
giving quadratic diameter, and there are no known transitive permutation groups having a Cayley graph with greater than quadratic diameter.
In both cases one may conjecture that the optimal exponent is
2
.

In the direction of
1
, Helfgott–Seress
[
31
]
proved that for any transitive group
G\leq S_{n}
,

(1.1)

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq n^{O((\log n)^{3}\log\log n)}.

This is the best known bound for
\operatorname{diam}(A_{n})
, so
2
remains open in this case, as for the other simple groups of unbounded rank.
Here and throughout the paper we use the term
rank
for the degree
n
in the case of
A_{n}
or the (untwisted) Lie rank in the case of a simple group of Lie type.
The rank of sporadic groups (or any finite subcollection) is unimportant and can be taken to be
1
by convention.
The simple groups of rank greater than
8
are alternating or classical.
The best known general bound for the diameter of a simple classical group
G\leq\operatorname{PGL}(V)
is

(1.2)

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq|V|^{O(\log\dim V)^{2}},

which is due to Halasi–Maróti–Pyber–Qiao
[
30
]
.

2
has been proved in bounded rank by Helfgott, Breuillard–Green–Tao, and Pyber–Szabó
[
11
,
51
]
.
Thus for a simple group
G
of rank
n
we know that

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq(\log|G|)^{c(n)}

for some function
c(n)
of the rank.
Recently, it was proved by Bajpai–Dona–Helfgott
[
5
]
that

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq(\log|G|)^{O(n^{4})}

for untwisted classical groups
G
of rank
n
defined over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p>n
, and there is considerable hope that this bound may be extended to the excluded cases.
At present, in full generality, or for groups defined over a small field, the best available bound for a simple classical group
G\leq\operatorname{PGL}(V)
is still (
1.2
).

The results in this paper are complementary to the case of simple groups.
We give a general polylog-type bound for the diameter of a group in terms of the diameters of its composition factors as well as the maximal exponent of a normal abelian section.
There are several notable applications.
Among them is the striking fact that
2
implies
1
.

To state our results it is useful to introduce some special notation.
Let
G
be a finite group.
The standard notation
\mu(G)
indicates the minimal degree of a faithful permutation representation
G\to S_{n}
.
Note that if
G
is simple then
\mu(G)
is just the minimal index of a proper subgroup of
G
.
Let
\mathcal{N}(G)
denote the set of nonabelian composition factors of
G
.
We define

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(G)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{\log\operatorname{diam}(T)}{\log\mu(T)}:T\in\mathcal{N}(G)\right\}\cup\{1\},

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(G)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{\log\operatorname{diam}(T)}{\log\log|T|}:T\in\mathcal{N}(G)\right\}\cup\{1\}.

The idea is that
\theta_{i}(G)
measures how much Conjecture
i
fails for the composition factors of
G
.
In particular, Conjecture
i
implies that
\theta_{i}(G)
is absolutely bounded.
Moreover, by the results quoted above,
\theta_{i}(G)
is in any case bounded by a function of the maximal rank of a nonabelian composition factor of
G
.
Note that
\theta_{1}(G)\leq 2\theta_{2}(G)
, since
|T|\leq\mu(T)!
.

Recall that the
exponent

\exp(G)
of a group
G
is the smallest integer
e
such that
g^{e}=1
for all
g\in G
.
Let

\varepsilon(G)=\max\{\exp(N/N^{\prime}):N\trianglelefteq G\}.

Here
N^{\prime}=[N,N]
is the derived subgroup of
N
.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

If
G
is a finite group, then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\varepsilon(G)(\log|G|)^{31+5\theta_{1}(G)}\max\{\operatorname{diam}(T):T\in\mathcal{N}(G)\}\cup\{1\}.

We list a number of corollaries and subresults.
To begin with, if
G
is soluble then
\mathcal{N}(G)=\emptyset
, so the theorem asserts that

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\varepsilon(G)(\log|G|)^{O(1)},

i.e., soluble groups with controlled exponent have polylogarithmic diameter.
In fact this is not just an important special case but also a crucial intermediate result, and we actually obtain the following explicit bounds.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite soluble group. Let
L
be the derived length and let

\varepsilon_{0}=\max\{\exp(G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)}):0\leq i<L\}\leq\exp(G).

Then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq\varepsilon_{0}4^{L-1}(\log_{2}|G|)^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{0}(4\log_{2}|G|)^{8}.

Next, since
\operatorname{diam}(T)\leq(\log|T|)^{\theta_{2}(G)}
for
T\in\mathcal{N}(G)
and
\theta_{1}(G)\leq 2\theta_{2}(G)
, the following corollary is immediate from
Theorem

1.1
.

Corollary 1.3
.

If
G
is a finite group then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\varepsilon(G)(\log|G|)^{31+11\theta_{2}(G)}.

A finite group
G
is called
anabelian
if it has no abelian composition factors.

Corollary 1.4
.

If
G
is anabelian then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll(\log|G|)^{31+11\theta_{2}(G)}.

Now let us turn to the case of transitive permutation groups and
1
, which is our primary motivation.

Corollary 1.5

(
2

\implies

1
)
.

If
G\leq S_{n}
is transitive then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{32+7\theta_{1}(G)}.

In particular,

1
holds if and only if the following two special cases hold:

(1)

\operatorname{diam}(A_{n})\leq n^{O(1)}
,

(2)

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq|V|^{O(1)}
for simple classical groups
G\leq\operatorname{PGL}(V)
.

The best known results in the direction of (a) and (b) are (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) respectively.
In view of the latter, we are tantalizingly close to reducing
1
to the case of
A_{n}
.
For now the best we can say is the following.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
G\leq S_{n}
be a transitive permutation group. Let
m\geq 5
be the largest integer such that
A_{m}\in\mathcal{N}(G)
, or else let
m=1
if
G
has no alternating composition factors.
Then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{O((\log\log n)^{2}+(\log m)^{3}\log\log m)}.

Corollary

1.5
implies in particular that if the nonabelian composition factors of
G\leq S_{n}
have bounded rank then
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{O(1)}
. In particular if
G
is soluble then
\operatorname{diam}(G)
is polynomially bounded. A direct application of
Corollary

1.5
gives
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{39}
, but in fact we can obtain
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{5}
by being more careful with our tools.

Theorem 1.7
.

If
G\leq S_{n}
is soluble and transitive then
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{5}
.

Let us briefly give the idea of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
as well as its corollaries.
The starting point is that there is a subadditive extension bound for lengths: for any normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
,

\ell_{X}(G)\leq\ell_{X}(N)+\ell_{X}(G/N).

Here
\ell_{X}(G/N)
is just the diameter of the quotient
G/N
with respect to the projection
XN/N
of the generators, so

\ell_{X}(G/N)\leq\operatorname{diam}(G/N).

Bounding the term
\ell_{X}(N)
is tricky, however, as
X
is a generating set for
G
, not
N
, but at least

\ell_{X}(N)\leq m\operatorname{diam}(N)

provided that
N
has a generating set
Y
with
\ell_{X}(Y)\leq m
.
In general a basic lemma of Schreier gives
m\leq 2\operatorname{diam}(G/N)+1
, and this implies

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\operatorname{diam}(N)\operatorname{diam}(G/N).

However, because the diameters are multiplied in this bound,
this is an expensive inequality that we must apply sparingly.
Our arguments therefore focus on constructing generating sets for small normal subgroups by more efficient means than Schreier’s lemma.

In the case of a soluble group, we achieve this by descending the derived series using commutators and conjugation in a natural way,
taking advantage of an old lemma of Milnor
(used in the proof of the Milnor–Wolf theorem).
We also take advantage of the fact that the derived length cannot be very large: the derived length of a soluble group
G
is bounded by
O(\log\log|G|)
by a result of Glasby
[
23
]
.
If
G
happens to be a soluble transitive permutation group of degree
n
then we have even sharper bounds for its derived length, and also for the exponent parameter
\varepsilon(G)
.

In the case of an arbitrary finite group, we first consider the quotient of
G
by its soluble radical.
The quotient embeds as a subdirect product of a finite collection of monolithic groups with nonabelian socles.
The prototypical example of such a group is the iterated wreath product

G=A_{5}\wr\cdots\wr A_{5}.

For such groups we use an inductive argument to bound the length of some nontrivial element of the socle.
For this we rely on a generalization of an argument of Wilson from
[
57
]

(in fact, the study of Wilson’s papers
[
56
,
57
]
motivated much of the present paper).

There is a well-known theorem of Pálfy–Wolf
[
50
,
58
]
that asserts that the order of a primitive soluble group
G\leq S_{n}
is bounded by a polynomial function of
n
; in fact
|G|\leq 24^{-1/3}n^{c}
, where
c\approx 3.25
.
This influential result was extended by Pyber
[
52
]
*Theorem 2.10 (see also Guralnick–Maróti–Pyber
[
28
]
), who showed that if
G\leq S_{n}
is primitive then the product of the orders of its abelian composition factors is bounded by the same function,
and moreover that the number of nonabelian composition factors is
O(\log n)
.
In the analysis of monolithic groups with nonabelian socles, it turns out, somewhat surprisingly, that we need the following natural generalization of these results.

Theorem 1.8

(Generalized Pálfy–Wolf Theorem)
.

If
G\leq S_{n}
is a primitive permutation group of degree
n
with composition series
G=G_{0}>\cdots>G_{\ell}=1
and factors
T_{i}=G_{i-1}/G_{i}
then

\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu(T_{i})<n^{5}.

This result may be of independent interest. For example, it implies the theorem of Babai–Cameron–Pálfy
[
2
]
with an exponent linear in the rank, since
\mu(T)\geq|T|^{c/r}
for simple groups of rank at most
r
. (This also follows from Liebeck–Shalev
[
41
]
*Theorem 1.4.)

Corollary 1.9

(Babai–Cameron–Pálfy with linear exponent)
.

There is a constant
C
such that the following holds.
If
G\leq S_{n}
is a primitive permutation group of degree
n
and
G
has no nonabelian composition factor of rank greater than
r
then
|G|\leq n^{Cr}
.

Theorem

1.8
and an iterative application of Schreier’s lemma give a bound on the diameter of a primitive permutation group in terms of
\theta_{1}(G)
. This special case of
Corollary

1.5
is used in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.
To deal with an arbitrary finite group, we ascend a normal series and alternately cover an insoluble normal subgroup or apply
Theorem

1.2
.

We now discuss important applications of our results
to the growth of residually finite groups.
If
G
is a group generated by a finite set
X
, the
growth function of
X
(or
of
G
, by abuse) is defined by

\gamma_{X}(n)=|B_{X}(n)|,\qquad B_{X}(n)=\{g\in G:\ell_{X}(g)\leq n\}.

Growth functions are conventionally compared in the following way: we write
f\preceq g
if there is a constant
C>0
such that
f(n)\leq Cg(Cn)
for all
n>0
.
This relation defines a preorder on the set of growth functions, and the equivalence class
[\gamma_{X}]
is an invariant of
G
, i.e., independent of the choice of generating set
X
; in fact it is a quasi-isometry invariant.
Growth functions of typical examples (abelian groups, soluble groups, linear groups, …) are generally either polynomially bounded or exponential.
A group
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
is said to have
intermediate growth
in all other cases, i.e., if
n^{d}\prec\gamma_{X}(n)\prec\exp(n)
for all
d
.

It is not difficult to prove that a virtually nilpotent group has polynomial growth, i.e.,
\gamma_{X}(n)\preceq n^{d}
for some integer
d
.
Gromov
[
27
]
famously proved the converse: if
G
has polynomial growth then
G
is virtually nilpotent.

Grigorchuk’s gap conjecture
predicts the existence of a constant
\beta>0
such that the same conclusion holds under the much weaker hypothesis that
\gamma_{X}(n)\prec\exp(n^{\beta})
.
The strong version asserts that one can take
\beta=1/2
.
See the introduction to
[
17
]
for more on the history of this question,
and see
[
17
]
*Section 5 for even stronger variants.

Conjecture 3

(Grigorchuk)
.

There is a constant
\beta>0
such that the following holds.
If
G
is a group generated by a finite set
X
and
\gamma_{X}(n)\prec\exp(n^{\beta})
then
G
is virtually nilpotent.

As observed by Grigorchuk (see
[
25
]
*Theorem 7.4), the main result of Bajorska–Macedońska
[
4
]
implies that the gap conjecture reduces to two extreme cases: residually finite groups and simple groups.
For further reduction theorems in this spirit, see
[
26
]
.
All the early examples of groups of intermediate growth were residually finite.
Non-residually-finite examples were first constructed by Erschler
[
20
]
and Bartholdi–Erschler
[
6
]
.
The first simple group of intermediate growth was constructed by Nekrashevych
[
48
]
(solving a long-standing open problem).

Using our methods we can show that Babai’s conjecture (
2
) implies Grigorchuk’s gap conjecture for residually finite groups.
We formulate the result in an unconditional way as follows.

Theorem 1.10

(
2

\implies

3
for residually finite groups)
.

For any
\theta>0
there is some
\beta=1/(20+10\theta)>0
such that the following holds.
If
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
is a finitely generated group such that

(1)

G
has a sequence of finite-index normal subgroups
N_{i}
such that
\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}N_{i}=1
and such that
\theta_{2}(G/N_{i})\leq\theta
for all
i\geq 1
, and

(2)

\gamma_{X}(n)\preceq\exp(n^{\beta})
,

then
G
is virtually nilpotent.

In particular, we have the following notable corollary, which applies to many natural branch groups, for example all spinal groups with finite rooted and directed parts as defined in
[
7
]
*Chapter 2.
By
[
7
]
*Theorem 10.9, this includes a rich class of groups of intermediate growth.

Corollary 1.11
.

For
d\geq 1
there is a constant
\beta=\beta(d)>0
such that the following holds.
If
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
is a finitely generated group such that

(1)

G
acts faithfully on an infinite rooted
d
-regular tree, and

(2)

\gamma_{X}(n)\preceq\exp(n^{\beta})
,

then
G
is virtually nilpotent.
In fact we can take
\beta(d)=1/(\log d)^{4}
for
d
sufficiently large.

2.
Basic diametry

The following easy lemmas are our most basic tools for bounding diameters. We will use them throughout the paper usually without explicit reference.

Lemma 2.1

(chain rule)
.

For
X,Y,Z\subseteq G
we have

\ell_{X}(Z)\leq\ell_{X}(Y)\ell_{Y}(Z).

Let
B_{X}(r)=(X\cup X^{-1}\cup\{1\})^{r}
denote the ball of radius
r
.
Generalizing the
\ell_{X}
notation, for any pair of subgroups
K\leq H\leq G
, let
\ell_{X}(H/K)
denote the smallest integer
r\geq 0
such that
H\subseteq B_{X}(r)K
.

Lemma 2.2

(extension rule)
.

Suppose
K\leq L\leq H\leq G
. Then

\ell_{X}(H/K)\leq\ell_{X}(H/L)+\ell_{X}(L/K).

Proof.

If
r_{1}=\ell_{X}(H/L)
and
r_{2}=\ell_{X}(L/K)
then

H\subseteq B_{X}(r_{1})L\subseteq B_{X}(r_{1})B_{X}(r_{2})K=B_{X}(r_{1}+r_{2})K.\qed

We often apply
Lemma

2.2
in the following iterated form.
Suppose
G=G_{0}>G_{1}>\cdots>G_{L}=1
and
X\subseteq G
. Then

\ell_{X}(G)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\ell_{X}(G_{i-1}/G_{i}).

Lemma 2.3

(Schreier)
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finite group and
N\trianglelefteq G
.

(1)

N=\left\langle Y\right\rangle
where
\ell_{X}(Y)\leq 2\operatorname{diam}(G/N)+1
.

(2)

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq 4\operatorname{diam}(N)\operatorname{diam}(G/N)
provided
1<N<G
.

Proof.

(Cf.
[
3
]
*Lemma 5.1 or
[
57
]
*Lemma 2.1.)
Schreier’s classical lemma states that if
T
is a transversal to the right cosets of
N
then

Y=\{t_{1}xt_{2}^{-1}:x\in X,t_{1},t_{2}\in T\}\cap N

generates
N
. A transversal of minimal
X
-length is
T=B_{X}(\operatorname{diam}(G/N))
. For this choice,
\ell_{X}(Y)\leq 2\operatorname{diam}(G/N)+1
, which proves
(1)
.
Part
(2)
follows from
(1)
and the previous two lemmas:

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\ell_{X}(G/N)+\ell_{X}(Y)\ell_{Y}(N)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{diam}(G/N)+(2\operatorname{diam}(G/N)+1)\operatorname{diam}(N)

\displaystyle\leq 4\operatorname{diam}(G/N)\operatorname{diam}(N).\qed

Remark 2.4
.

The previous lemma is valid without the normality hypothesis if
\operatorname{diam}(G/N)
is understood as the diameter of the Schreier graph.

3.
Soluble groups

The key to the proof of
Theorem

1.2
is the following short argument.
We refer to it as the
generalized Milnor lemma
, as it is similar to
[
47
]
*Lemma 1.
A closely related lemma was also critical in the recent paper
[
17
]
of the first two authors.

If
G
is a finite group, the
subgroup length
of
G
is the largest integer
\lambda(G)=\lambda
such that there is a chain of subgroups
1=H_{0}<H_{1}<\cdots<H_{\lambda}=G
.
We will use only the trivial bound
\lambda(G)\leq\log_{2}|G|
.

Lemma 3.1

(Generalized Milnor lemma)
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a group and let
N=\left\langle Y^{G}\right\rangle\trianglelefteq G
be a finite normal subgroup.
Then
N=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where

\ell_{X}(Z)\leq\ell_{X}(Y)+2\lambda(N).

Proof.

Let

Z_{i}=\{y^{g}:y\in Y,g\in B_{X}(i)\},\qquad N_{i}=\left\langle Z_{i}\right\rangle.

Then

\left\langle Y\right\rangle=N_{0}\leq N_{1}\leq N_{2}\leq\cdots\leq N=\left\langle Y^{G}\right\rangle,

so there is some
k\leq\lambda(N)
such that
N_{k}=N_{k+1}
.
Then
N_{k}\trianglelefteq G
, so
N_{k}=N
.
Thus
N=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where
Z=Z_{k}
and

\ell_{X}(Z)\leq\ell_{X}(Y)+2k\leq\ell_{X}(Y)+2\lambda(N).\qed

If a group
N
is generated by a subset
Y
,
in general its derived subgroup
N^{\prime}
is not generated
by commutators of elements of
Y
.
Hence the following result has nontrivial content.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
and let
N=\left\langle Y\right\rangle\trianglelefteq G
be a normal subgroup with
N^{\prime}
finite.
Then
N^{\prime}=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where

\ell_{X}(Z)\leq 4\ell_{X}(Y)+2\lambda(N^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let
Z_{0}=\{[y_{1},y_{2}]:y_{1},y_{2}\in Y\}
. Then
\ell_{X}(Z_{0})\leq 4\ell_{X}(Y)
and
Z_{0}
normally generates
N^{\prime}
.
Apply
Lemma

3.1
to
N^{\prime}=\left\langle Z_{0}^{G}\right\rangle
.
∎

The derived series of
G
is defined as usual by
G^{(0)}=G
and
G^{(i)}=(G^{(i-1)})^{\prime}
for
i\geq 1
. Recall that
G
is soluble if and only if
G^{(i)}=1
for some index
i
. The minimal such
i
is called the
derived length
of
G
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finite group. For each
i\geq 0
, there is a set
X_{i}\subseteq G^{(i)}
such that

G^{(i)}=\left\langle X_{i}\right\rangle,\qquad\ell_{X}(X_{i})\leq 4^{i}\log_{2}|G|.

Proof.

Applying
Lemma

3.2
inductively starting with
X_{0}=X
produces a generating set
X_{i}
for
G^{(i)}
such that

\ell_{X}(X_{i})\leq 4\ell_{X}(X_{i-1})+2\lambda(G^{\prime}).

Rewriting this inequality in the telescopic form

\frac{\ell_{X}(X_{i})}{4^{i}}-\frac{\ell_{X}(X_{i-1})}{4^{i-1}}\leq\frac{2}{4^{i}}\lambda(G^{\prime}),

we deduce that

\ell_{X}(X_{i})/4^{i}\leq 1+\frac{2}{3}\lambda(G^{\prime})\leq 1+\frac{2}{3}\log_{2}|G^{\prime}|\leq\log_{2}|G|

for
G\neq 1
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If
A
is a finite abelian group then

\operatorname{diam}(A)\leq\exp(A)\lambda(A)\leq\exp(A)\log_{2}|A|.

Proof.

Let
X
be a generating set for
A
. We may assume
X
is minimal. Then
|X|\leq\lambda(A)
and
A
is the set of products
\prod_{x\in X}x^{e_{x}}
with
0\leq e_{x}<\exp(A)
. Every such product has length at most
\exp(A)|X|\leq\exp(A)\lambda(A)
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In fact if
A\cong C_{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times C_{d_{n}}
where
d_{1}\mid\cdots\mid d_{n}
then

\operatorname{diam}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor d_{i}/2\right\rfloor.

See
[
56
]
*Section 3.

We can now prove our main theorem for soluble groups.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finite soluble group of derived length

.
By a result of Glasby
[

]
,

L<3\log_{2}\log_{2}|G|+9.

By
Lemma

,
G^{(i)}=\left\langle X_{i}\right\rangle
where
\ell_{X}(X_{i})\leq 4^{i}\log_{2}|G|
. Applying

,

, and

,

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\ell_{X}(G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\ell_{X}(X_{i})\operatorname{diam}(G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}4^{i}\log_{2}|G|\exp(G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)})\log_{2}|G^{(i)}:G^{(i+1)}|

4.
Generalized Pálfy–Wolf

If
T
is a finite simple group (possibly abelian), we denote by
\mu(T)
the minimal degree of a nontrivial permutation representation of
T
. Equivalently,

\mu(T)=\min\{|T:H|:H<T\}.

Now if
G
is an arbitrary finite group with composition series

G=G_{0}>G_{1}>\cdots>G_{\ell}=1,

with quotients
T_{i}=G_{i-1}/G_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
(the composition factors, with multiplicity),
we define

\mu_{\operatorname{cf}}(G)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu(T_{i}).

Note that
\mu_{\operatorname{cf}}(G)=|G|
if (and only if)
G
is soluble.
Let us also define
\mathcal{A}=\{i:T_{i}~\text{is abelian}\}
and

\mu_{\operatorname{ab}}(G)=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(T_{i})=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{A}}|T_{i}|,\qquad\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\prod_{i\notin\mathcal{A}}\mu(T_{i}).

Then

(4.1)

\mu_{\operatorname{cf}}(G)=\mu_{\operatorname{ab}}(G)\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G),

and, by the theorem of Pyber quoted in the introduction, if
G\leq S_{n}
is primitive then

(4.2)

\mu_{\operatorname{ab}}(G)\leq 24^{-1/3}n^{c},

where
c=1+c_{0}=1+\log_{9}(48\cdot 24^{1/3})\approx 3.44

as in Pálfy–Wolf.

The following two basic lemmas hold for each of
\mu_{\operatorname{cf}},\mu_{\operatorname{ab}},\mu_{\operatorname{na}}
, but we only need the versions for
\mu_{\operatorname{na}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
G
is a finite group and
N\trianglelefteq G
then

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(N)\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G/N).

Lemma 4.2
.

If
G
is a subdirect subgroup of
G_{1}\times\dots\times G_{t}
, then

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{i}).

Proof.

We may assume
t>1
.
Let
N=G\cap G_{1}
.
Then
N\trianglelefteq G
and
G/N
is subdirect in
G_{2}\times\cdots\times G_{t}
, so by
Lemma

4.1
and induction we have

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(N)\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G/N)\leq\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(N)\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{2})\cdots\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{t}).

Also,
N\trianglelefteq G_{1}
since
G
is subdirect, so
\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(N)\leq\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{1})
.
∎

We need the following standard result on the structure of primitive linear groups.
The main ideas are due to Suprunenko
[

,
54
]
, though Suprunenko only considered soluble groups;
more recent work tends to refer to Aschbacher
[

The statement below can be found for example in

Theorem 4.3

(Suprunenko)
.

Let
G\leq\operatorname{GL}_{n}(p)
be a primitive linear group, where
n\geq 1
and
p
is a prime.
Then there is a divisor
r\mid n
and a subgroup
H\trianglelefteq G
such that
G/H
is cyclic of order
n/r
,

Z(H)
is cyclic of order dividing
p^{r}-1
,
and the generalized Fitting subgroup
F=F^{*}(H)
is a central product

F=Z(H)\circ E_{1}\circ\cdots\circ E_{m}\circ U_{1}\circ\cdots\circ U_{s},

where

(1)

for
1\leq i\leq m
,
E_{i}\trianglelefteq H
is an extraspecial group of order
p_{i}^{1+2n_{i}}
for some prime divisor
p_{i}
of
p^{r}-1
and some
n_{i}\geq 1
,

p_{i}^{2n_{i}}\leq H/C_{H}(E_{i})\leq p_{i}^{2n_{i}}\cdot\operatorname{Sp}_{2n_{i}}(p_{i}),

and the projection of
H/C_{H}(E_{i})
to
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n_{i}}(p_{i})
is completely reducible;

(2)

for
1\leq j\leq s
,
U_{j}\trianglelefteq H
is a central product of some

copies of a quasisimple group
Q_{j}=Z(Q_{j})\cdot T_{j}
,

T_{j}^{u_{j}}\leq H/C_{H}(U_{j})\leq\operatorname{Aut}(T_{j})\wr\operatorname{Sym}(u_{j}),

the projection of
H/C_{H}(U_{j})
to
\operatorname{Sym}(u_{j})
is transitive,
and
Q_{j}
has an irreducible representation
Q_{j}\to\operatorname{SL}_{t_{j}}(p)
for some
t_{j}\geq 2
;

(3)

the product
p_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots p_{m}^{n_{m}}\cdot t_{1}^{u_{1}}\cdots t_{s}^{u_{s}}
is a divisor of
n/r
.

We are going to bound
\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)
for primitive groups.
We actually prove a more general statement which is suitable to an inductive argument.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
b_{1}=5^{1/4}\approx 1.495
and
c_{1}=\log_{8}(7b_{1})\approx 1.129
. Let
n\geq 1
.

(1)

If
G\leq S_{n}
is primitive (with
n\geq 2
) then
\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq b_{1}^{-1}n^{5/4}
.

(2)

If
G\leq S_{n}
then
\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq b_{1}^{n-1}
.

(3)

If
G\leq\operatorname{GL}_{n}(p)
is completely reducible then
\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq b_{1}^{-1}p^{c_{1}n}
.

Remark 4.5
.

All three bounds are sharp for infinitely many
n
. Let

G_{h}=A_{5}\wr\cdots\wr A_{5}

be the iterated wreath product of
h
copies of
A_{5}
(with the natural action).
Then

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{h})=5^{5^{h-1}+\cdots+5+1}=5^{(5^{h}-1)/4}.

There is a natural faithful transitive action of
G_{h}
on
n=5^{h}
points (imprimitive for
h>1
), which shows that the bound in
(2)
is sharp.
There is also a faithful primitive action of
G_{h}=A_{5}\wr G_{h-1}
on
n=5^{5^{h-1}}
points,
which shows that the bound in
(1)
is sharp.
Finally, the group
\operatorname{GL}_{3}(2)\wr G_{h}
acts faithfully and irreducibly on
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{n}
where
n=3\cdot 5^{h}
, and

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(\operatorname{SL}_{3}(2)\wr G_{h})=7^{5^{h}}5^{(5^{h}-1)/4}=b_{1}^{-1}2^{c_{1}n},

which shows that the bound in
(3)
is sharp.

Proof.

First let us describe the structure of the induction.
In the proof of each statement we assume that
(1)
–
(3)
hold for all positive integers
m<n
.
Additionally, in the proof of
(2)
we assume that
(1)
holds for
m=n
.
Finally, in the proof of
(3)
we assume that
(1)
and
(2)
hold for
m=n
,
and also that
(3)
holds for
m=n
and all smaller primes
p^{\prime}<p
.

(1)
Let
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)
be primitive, where
|\Omega|=n
.

Affine case.
If
G
has affine type then
G\cong VH
where
V=\mathbf{F}_{p}^{d}
may be identified with
\Omega
and
H\leq\operatorname{GL}(V)
is irreducible. Since
d<p^{d}=n
, by induction we have

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(H)\leq b_{1}^{-1}p^{c_{1}d}=b_{1}^{-1}n^{c_{1}}<b_{1}^{-1}n^{5/4}.

Non-affine case.

Now assume that
\operatorname{soc}(G)
is nonabelian, say
\operatorname{soc}(G)=T^{d}
where
T
is a nonabelian simple group.
Then

T^{d}\leq G\leq\operatorname{Aut}(T^{d})\cong\operatorname{Aut}(T)\wr S_{d}.

Since
G
is primitive,
T^{d}
is transitive on
\Omega
.
By
[
35
]
*5.2.7(ii) (and the fact that
\mu(T)\leq|T|^{1/2}

[
40
]
),
it follows that

5^{d}\leq\mu(T)^{d}\leq n.

In particular

.
Let
\bar{G}
be the image of

in

.
Now
\operatorname{Out}(T)=\operatorname{Aut}(T)/T
is soluble by the Schreier conjecture, so by induction

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\mu(T)^{d}\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(\bar{G})\leq nb_{1}^{d-1}\leq b_{1}^{-1}n^{5/4}

(with equality if
n=5
).

(2)
Let
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)
where
|\Omega|=n
. If
G
is intransitive then
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
where
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
are nonempty

Let
n_{i}=|\Omega_{i}|
and let

be the group induced on
\Omega_{i}
for

. Then

is subdirect in
G_{1}\times G_{2}
, so we are done by
Lemma

4.2
and induction:

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{1})\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{2})\leq b_{1}^{n_{1}-1}b_{1}^{n_{2}-1}<b_{1}^{n-1}.

Similarly, if
G
is imprimitive, consider an imprimitivity system
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{d}\}
for
G
, where
d\mid n
and
1<d<n
. Then
G
acts on
\mathcal{B}
with kernel
N\trianglelefteq G
say.
Let
N_{i}
be the group induced on

by

for
1\leq i\leq d
.
Then
N_{i}\cong N_{1}
for each

and
N
is subdirect in
N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}
, so by
Lemmas

and
4.2
and induction we have

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(N_{1})^{d}\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G/N)\leq(b_{1}^{n/d-1})^{d}b_{1}^{d-1}=b_{1}^{n-1}.

Hence we may assume that
G
is primitive. We may assume
n\geq 5
.
Then by
(1)
we have

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq b_{1}^{-1}n^{5/4}\leq b_{1}^{n-1}

(again equality holds if
n=5
).

(3)
Now let
G\leq\operatorname{GL}(V)
be a completely reducible linear group, where
V=\mathbf{F}_{p}^{n}
.
If
G
is reducible then
V=U_{1}\oplus U_{2}
for some
G

,
and
G
is subdirect in
G_{1}\times G_{2}
where

is the group induced by

on
U_{i}
.
By
Lemma

4.2
and induction it follows that

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)\leq\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{1})\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G_{2})\leq b_{1}^{-2}|U_{1}|^{c_{1}}|U_{2}|^{c_{1}}<b_{1}^{-1}|V|^{c_{1}}.

Next suppose that
G
is irreducible but imprimitive on
V
.
Then there is a
G
-invariant decomposition
V=U_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{d}
such that

,

for each

, and

transitively with kernel

say.
By Clifford’s theorem,

is a completely reducible

Let
N_{i}=N|_{U_{i}}
be the completely reducible linear group induced by

.
Then

for each

and
N
is subdirect in

.
Hence, by

4.1
and
4.2
and induction,

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(N)\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G/N)\leq{\left(b_{1}^{-1}|U_{1}|^{c_{1}}\right)}^{d}b_{1}^{d-1}=b_{1}^{-1}|V|^{c_{1}}.

Finally, assume that
G
is primitive on
V
.
By
Theorem

4.3
, there is a divisor
r\mid n
and a subgroup

such that

is abelian and

F=F^{*}(H)=Z(H)\circ E_{1}\circ\cdots\circ E_{m}\circ U_{1}\circ\cdots\circ U_{s},

and properties

Let

.
Since

, we have

and

\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{H}{C_{H}(E_{i})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{H}{C_{H}(U_{j})}.

Consider an index

.
We have a representation

\rho_{i}:H/C_{H}(E_{i})\to\operatorname{Sp}_{2n_{i}}(p_{i})

.
Moreover we have
2n_{i}\leq p_{i}^{n_{i}}\leq n/r\leq n
and

p_{i}^{2n_{i}}<p^{p_{i}^{n_{i}}}\leq p^{n},

Thus either

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(H/C_{H}(E_{i}))\leq b_{1}^{-1}p_{i}^{2c_{1}n_{i}}<b_{1}^{-1}p^{c_{1}p_{i}^{n_{i}}}.

Now consider an index

.
We have

T_{j}^{u_{j}}\leq H/C_{H}(U_{j})\leq\operatorname{Aut}(T_{j})\wr S_{u_{j}}

In particular

\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(H/C_{H}(U_{j}))\leq\mu(T_{j})^{u_{j}}b_{1}^{u_{j}-1}<p^{t_{j}u_{j}}b_{1}^{u_{j}-1}.

\displaystyle\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(G)=\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(H/Z)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(H/C_{H}(E_{i}))\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{\operatorname{na}}(H/C_{H}(U_{j}))

5.
Groups with trivial soluble radical

If
G
is a finite group we denote by
\operatorname{sol}(G)
the soluble radical of
G
, i.e., the largest soluble normal subgroup of
G
.
In this section we consider finite groups
G
with
\operatorname{sol}(G)
trivial.
The
socle
of
G
is the subgroup
\operatorname{soc}(G)
generated by the minimal normal subgroups.
If
\operatorname{sol}(G)=1
then
\operatorname{soc}(G)
is a direct product of nonabelian simple groups.

A critical subcase is the case in which
G
is monolithic with nonabelian socle.
A finite group
G
is said to be
monolithic
if it has a unique minimal normal subgroup.
A key motivating example is the iterated wreath product

G_{h}=A_{5}\wr\cdots\wr A_{5}\leq\operatorname{Sym}(5^{h}).

The following lemma is basic and well-known.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N_{1},\dots,N_{k}
be distinct nonabelian minimal normal subgroups of a group
G
.
Then

(1)

\left\langle N_{1},\dots,N_{k}\right\rangle=N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}
,

(2)

every
G
-normal subgroup of
N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}
has the form
\prod_{i\in I}N_{i}
.

In particular this holds if
N_{1},\dots,N_{k}
are nonabelian simple groups and
G=\left\langle N_{1},\dots,N_{k}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

Part
(1)
is proved by induction on
k
, using the fact that
[N_{i},N_{j}]\leq N_{i}\cap N_{j}=1
for
i\neq j
. This implies that
N_{k}
centralizes
\left\langle N_{i}:1\leq i<k\right\rangle
, so
N_{k}\cap\left\langle N_{i}:1\leq i<k\right\rangle=1
. This implies that

\left\langle N_{1},\dots,N_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle N_{1},\dots,N_{k-1}\right\rangle\times N_{k}=N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}

by induction.

For part
(2)
, suppose
N\leq N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}
and
N\trianglelefteq G
.
Let
I=\{i:N_{i}\leq N\}
. By quotienting out
\prod_{i\in I}N_{i}
we may assume
I=\emptyset
.
Then
[N,N_{i}]\leq N\cap N_{i}=1
for each
i
, so
N\leq Z(\prod N_{i})=1
.
∎

We now give a series of results that, given
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
and a normal subgroup
N
of
G
, try to locate a short nontrivial element in
N
.
The subgroup length
\lambda(N)
was defined in
Section

3
,
and recall that
B_{X}(r)=(X\cup X^{-1}\cup\{1\})^{r}
denotes the ball of radius
r
.
A subgroup
N\leq M
is called
self-centralizing
if
C_{M}(N)\leq N
.
The following lemma generalizes
[
57
]
*Lemma 3.4.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
k,m\geq 1
and let
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}
be groups such that, for each
i
,

M_{i}
has a self-centralizing minimal normal subgroup
N_{i}
and
\lambda(N_{i})\leq m
.
Let
M=\left\langle X\right\rangle\leq M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{k}
be a subdirect subgroup, and let
\pi_{i}:M\to M_{i}
be the natural epimorphism.
Let
r\geq 1
and assume that

\pi_{i}(B_{X}(r))\cap N_{i}\neq\{1\}\qquad(1\leq i\leq k).

Then

B_{X}(4k^{2}(r+2m))\cap(N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k})\neq\{1\}.

Proof.

We will show by induction on
k
that

B_{X}(a_{k})\cap(N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k})\neq\{1\}.

where the integer sequence
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is defined by the recurrence equation

a_{1}=r,\qquad a_{k}=4a_{\left\lceil k/2\right\rceil}+4m\qquad(k\geq 2).

It is straightforward to check by induction that

a_{k}=4^{i}(r+\tfrac{4}{3}m)-\tfrac{4}{3}m

where
i=\left\lceil\log_{2}k\right\rceil
is the unique integer such that
2^{i-1}<k\leq 2^{i}
; indeed, it suffices to note that
2^{i-2}<\left\lceil k/2\right\rceil\leq 2^{i-1}
.
This will prove the lemma since

a_{k}<4k^{2}(r+2m).

Note also that
a_{k}
is monotonic in
k
: this will be used in the proof.

The case
k=1
is trivial, so assume
k\geq 2
.
For any subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
, let
\pi_{I}
denote the projection map

\pi_{I}:M\to M_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}M_{i}.

Let
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
be a subset of cardinality
k_{1}=\left\lceil k/2\right\rceil<k
, and consider
\pi_{I}(M)=\left\langle\pi_{I}(X)\right\rangle\leq M_{I}
.
By induction, there is an element
x\in B_{X}(a_{k_{1}})
such that

1\neq\pi_{I}(x)\in\prod_{i\in I}N_{i}.

Let

J=\{j\in\{1,\dots,k\}:\pi_{j}(x)\notin N_{j}\}.

Then
I\cap J=\emptyset
, so
|J|\leq\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor\leq k_{1}
.
If
J=\emptyset
then we may take
g=x
.
Otherwise, by induction there is
y\in B_{X}(a_{k_{1}})
such that

1\neq\pi_{J}(y)\in\prod_{j\in J}N_{j}.

Let
j\in J
be an index such that
\pi_{j}(y)\neq 1
.
Then

\pi_{j}(x)\in M_{j}\setminus N_{j},\qquad\pi_{j}(y)\in N_{j}\setminus\{1\}.

Since
N_{j}
is minimal normal in
M_{j}
and
\pi_{j}(y)\neq 1
,

N_{j}
is the normal closure of
\pi_{j}(y)
, so
Lemma

3.1
gives

N_{j}=\left\langle\pi_{j}(y^{h}):h\in B_{X}(m)\right\rangle.

Since
C_{M_{j}}(N_{j})\leq N_{j}
and
\pi_{j}(x)\notin N_{j}
, there is some
h\in B_{X}(m)
such that

\pi_{j}([x,y^{h}])=[\pi_{j}(x),\pi_{j}(y^{h})]\neq 1.

Let
g=[x,y^{h}]
. Then
g
is a nontrivial element of
N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}
such that

\ell_{X}(g)\leq 2\ell_{X}(x)+2\ell_{X}(y)+4m\leq 4a_{k_{1}}+4m=a_{k}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finite monolithic group with nonabelian socle
N\cong T^{n}
, where
T
is a nonabelian simple group and
n\geq 1
.
Then

B_{X}(2n^{16+5\theta_{1}(G)}\log_{2}|T|)\cap N\neq\{1\}.

Proof.

We use induction on
n
.
If
n=1
then
T\leq G\leq\operatorname{Aut}(T)
.
By
Lemma

2.3
,
N=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where
\ell_{X}(Z)\leq 2|\operatorname{Out}(T)|+1
.
We have
|\operatorname{Out}(T)|\leq\log_{2}|T|-1
(see
[
36
]
or
[
46
]
*Lemma 2.3.2),
so indeed we have
\ell_{X}(Z)\leq 2\log_{2}|T|
, as required.

Hence assume
n\geq 2
.
Then
G\leq\operatorname{Aut}(N)\cong\operatorname{Aut}(T)\wr S_{n}
.
Let
\phi:G\to S_{n}
be the induced homomorphism.
Since
N
is minimal normal,
\phi(G)\leq S_{n}
is transitive.
Let
K=\ker\phi=G\cap\operatorname{Aut}(T)^{n}
, so
G/K\cong\phi(G)
.
Let
M/K
be a maximal normal intransitive subgroup of
G/K
.

Let
N_{1}
be a minimal normal subgroup of
M
.
Let the
G
-conjugates of
N_{1}
be denoted
N_{1},\dots,N_{k}
.
Then, by
Lemma

5.1
,

N=\left\langle N_{1}^{G}\right\rangle=N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}=T_{1}\times\cdots\times T_{n},

and each
N_{j}
is of the form
\prod_{i\in I}T_{i}
where
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
is an orbit of
\phi(M)
.
Also,
k>1
since
\phi(M)
is intransitive.

Now
G/M
permutes
N_{1},\dots,N_{k}
transitively.
Let
\Delta=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{s}\}
be a system of maximal blocks for this action, where
1<s\mid k
.
If
M_{1}
is the kernel of the action
G\to\operatorname{Sym}(\Delta)
then
M\leq M_{1}\trianglelefteq G
and
\phi(M_{1})
is intransitive, so
M=M_{1}
by maximality of
M
.
Thus
G/M
has a faithful primitive action of degree
s\leq k
.
Applying
Corollary

4.6
,

\operatorname{diam}(G/M)<\tfrac{1}{4}k^{10+5\theta_{1}},

where
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G)
.
By
Lemma

2.3
it follows that
M=\left\langle Y\right\rangle
where

\ell_{X}(Y)\leq k^{10+5\theta_{1}}.

Let
M_{i}=M/C_{M}(N_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
Then
M_{i}
is monolithic with socle
N_{i}C_{M}(N_{i})/C_{M}(N_{i})\cong N_{i}\cong T^{n/k}
,
and
M=\left\langle Y\right\rangle
is subdirect in
M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{k}
since

\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}C_{M}(N_{i})=C_{M}(N)=1.

Write
\pi_{i}:M\rightarrow M_{i}
for the natural projection.
By induction,

\pi_{i}(B_{Y}(r))\cap N_{i}\neq\{1\},

where
r=2(n/k)^{16+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|T|
.
Now by
Lemma

5.2

and the bound

\lambda(N_{i})\leq\log_{2}|N_{i}|\leq(n/k)\log_{2}|T|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}r,

there is a nontrivial element
g\in N=N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\ell_{Y}(g)\leq 4k^{2}(r+2m)\leq 2^{4}k^{2}(n/k)^{16+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|T|.

This implies that

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(g)\leq\ell_{X}(Y)\ell_{Y}(g)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{4}k^{12+5\theta_{1}}(n/k)^{16+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|T|

\displaystyle=2^{4}k^{-4}n^{16+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|T|

\displaystyle\leq n^{16+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|T|

since
k\geq 2
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

The following proposition is the main result of this section.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a nontrivial finite group with trivial soluble radical.
Let
S=\operatorname{soc}(G)
.
Then

B_{X}((\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}(G)})\cap S\neq\{1\}.

Proof.

Let
N_{1},\dots,N_{k}
be the minimal normal subgroups of
G
.
By
Lemma

5.1
,
S=N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}
.
Let
G_{i}=G/C_{G}(N_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Then each
G_{i}
is monolithic with socle
N_{i}\cong T_{i}^{n_{i}}
say and
G
is a subdirect in
G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{k}
.
Let
\pi_{i}
be the projection
G\to G_{i}
.
Then
G_{i}=\left\langle\pi_{i}(X)\right\rangle
.
By
Proposition

5.3
, we have

\pi_{i}(B_{X}(r))\cap N_{i}\neq\{1\}\qquad(1\leq i\leq k),

where

r=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}2n_{i}^{16+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|T_{i}|,\qquad\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G).

Let
m=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\log_{2}|N_{i}|
.
Then applying
Lemma

5.2
gives

B_{X}(4k^{2}(r+2m))\cap(N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k})\neq\{1\}.

Bounding crudely,

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\leq\log_{60}|S|=a^{-1}\log|S|,\qquad\text{where}~a=\log(60)\approx 4.1,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\leq 2(\log_{60}|S|)^{15+5\theta_{1}}\log_{2}|S|\leq 4a^{-20}(\log|S|)^{16+5\theta_{1}},

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}|S|\leq 2a^{-20}(\log|S|)^{16+5\theta_{1}},

so

4k^{2}(r+2m)<(\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}.\qed

To apply
Proposition

5.4
effectively,
we need to bound the diameter of a direct product of simple groups.
The following result is taken from Dona
[
15
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
G=T_{1}\times\cdots\times T_{n}
where
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
are nonabelian finite simple groups of rank at most
r
.
Then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{3}r\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\operatorname{diam}(T_{i}).

Remark 5.6
.

This result was first proved by Babai–Seress in
[
3
]
*Lemma 5.4 in the case of alternating groups,
and later generalized to all finite simple groups by Dona
[
15
]
.
See the comments in
[
32
]
*Section 4 for more commentary.
We give a proof below only to demonstrate that the use of Ore’s conjecture can be avoided.
The proof is similar to that of
Lemma

5.2
.

Proof.

Let
d=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\operatorname{diam}(T_{i})
.
Say
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
. Fix an index
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, without loss of generality
i=1
, and for any
J\subseteq\{2,\dots,n\}
let
\pi_{J}
denote the projection
G\to T_{1}\times\prod_{j\in J}T_{j}
.
For each index
j\neq 1
, Schreier’s lemma (
Lemma

2.3
) implies that
\pi_{\{j\}}(B_{X}(2d+1))
contains a nontrivial element of
T_{1}\times 1
.
By conjugation, it follows that
\pi_{\{j\}}(B_{X}(4d+1))
contains a nontrivial conjugacy class of
T_{1}\times 1
.

We claim by induction that for each subset
J\subseteq\{2,\dots,n\}
of cardinality
|J|\leq 2^{k}
, the set
\pi_{J}(B_{X}(4^{k}(4d+1)))
contains a nontrivial conjugacy class in
T_{1}\times 1^{J}
.
This holds for
k=0
by the previous paragraph, so assume
k>0

and let
J\subseteq\{2,\dots,n\}
be a set of cardinality
|J|\leq 2^{k}
.
Partition
J
into two sets
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
each of cardinality at most
2^{k-1}
.
By induction,
\pi_{J_{i}}(B_{X}(4^{k-1}(4d+1)))
contains a nontrivial conjugacy class of
T_{1}\times 1^{J_{i}}
for
i=1,2
.
By taking commutators, it follows that
\pi_{J}(B_{X}(4^{k}(4d+1)))
contains a nontrivial conjugacy class of
T_{1}\times 1^{J}
, as claimed.

Applying the inductive claim to
J=\{2,\dots,n\}
, it follows that
B_{X}(20n^{2}d)
contains a nontrivial conjugacy class of
T_{1}
.
By
[
15
]
*Proposition 2.4,
T_{1}
has diameter at most
8(5r+7)\leq 100r
with respect to any nontrivial conjugacy class, so
\ell_{X}(T_{1})\leq 2000n^{2}rd
.
The index
i=1
was arbitrary, so
\ell_{X}(T_{i})\leq 2000n^{2}rd
for
1\leq i\leq n
, and it follows that
\ell_{X}(G)\leq 2000n^{3}rd
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

Determining the order of growth of the sequence
\operatorname{diam}(A_{5}^{n})
is a fascinating open problem.
We have
n\ll\operatorname{diam}(A_{5}^{n})\ll n^{3}
.

At this point we can easily complete the proof of the main theorem in the anabelian case.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
G\neq 1
be a finite anabelian group. Then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll(\log|G|)^{23+5\theta_{1}(G)}\max_{T\in\mathcal{N}(G)}\operatorname{diam}(T).

Proof.

Let
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G)
and
d=\max_{T\in\mathcal{N}(G)}\operatorname{diam}(T)
.
Let
S=\operatorname{soc}(G)
.
Suppose that
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
.
By
Proposition

5.4
, there is a nontrivial element
g\in S
such that

\ell_{X}(g)\leq(\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}.

Let
N=\left\langle g^{G}\right\rangle
. Then
N=\left\langle Y\right\rangle
where

Y=\{g^{h}:\ell_{X}(h)\leq\log_{2}|S|\},\qquad\ell_{X}(Y)\ll(\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}.

Also
N
is a normal subgroup of
G
contained in
S=\operatorname{soc}(G)
, so by
Lemma

5.1
we have
N=\prod_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}
for some nonabelian simple groups
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
.
Applying
Theorem

5.5
,

\ell_{X}(N)\leq\ell_{X}(Y)\operatorname{diam}(N)\ll(\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}\cdot n^{3}rd,

where
r
is the maximum rank of
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
. Crudely bounding

n\ll\log|S|,\qquad r\ll\log|S|,

we have

\ell_{X}(N)\ll(\log|S|)^{22+5\theta_{1}}d\ll(\log|G|)^{22+5\theta_{1}}d.

Now we use

\ell_{X}(G)\leq\ell_{X}(N)+\ell_{X}(G/N)\leq\ell_{X}(N)+\operatorname{diam}(G/N)

(from
Lemma

2.2
)
and induction. We get that

\ell_{X}(G)\ll m(\log|G|)^{22+5\theta_{1}}d

where
m
is the composition length of
G
. Since
m\leq\log_{60}|G|
, we are done.
∎

6.
Arbitrary finite groups

In this section we consider arbitrary finite groups by combining our results for soluble groups and for groups with trivial soluble radical.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finite group.
If
G
is insoluble then there is an insoluble normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
such that

\ell_{X}(N)\ll\varepsilon(G)(\log|G|)^{30+5\theta_{1}(G)}\max_{T\in\mathcal{N}(G)}\operatorname{diam}(T).

Proof.

Define
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(G)
,
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G)
, and

d=\max_{T\in\mathcal{N}(G)}\operatorname{diam}(T).

Let
G^{*}=G/\operatorname{sol}(G)
and let
g\mapsto g^{*}
be the natural quotient map.
Applying
Proposition

5.4
to
G^{*}=\left\langle X^{*}\right\rangle
and
S=\operatorname{soc}(G^{*})
, there is an element
g\in G
with
1\neq g^{*}\in S
and

\ell_{X}(g)=\ell_{X^{*}}(g^{*})\leq(\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}.

Let
N=\left\langle g^{G}\right\rangle\trianglelefteq G
.
By
Lemma

3.1
,
N=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(Z)\leq\ell_{X}(g)+2\lambda(N)

\displaystyle\ll(\log|S|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}+\log|\operatorname{sol}(G)|

\displaystyle\ll(\log|G|)^{18+5\theta_{1}}.

Now
1\neq N^{*}\trianglelefteq S
, so
N^{*}
is a nontrivial direct product of nonabelian composition factors of
G
.
By
Theorem

5.5
, it follows that

\operatorname{diam}(N^{*})\ll(\log|N^{*}|)^{4}d\leq(\log|G|)^{4}d.

On the other hand
N\cap\operatorname{sol}(G)=\operatorname{sol}(N)
, so
N^{*}\cong N/\operatorname{sol}(N)
, and by
Theorem

1.2
we have

\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{sol}(N))\ll\varepsilon_{0}(\operatorname{sol}(N))(\log|\operatorname{sol}(N)|)^{8}\ll\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{8}.

Thus by Schreier’s lemma (
Lemma

2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(N)

\displaystyle\ll\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{sol}(N))\operatorname{diam}(N/\operatorname{sol}(N))

\displaystyle\ll\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{12}d.

Since
N=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
, it follows that

\ell_{X}(N)\leq\ell_{X}(Z)\operatorname{diam}(N)\ll\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{30+5\theta_{1}}d,

as claimed.
∎

We can now give the proof of
Theorem

1.1
in complete generality.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finite group.
Define
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(G)
,
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G)
, and

d=\max\{\operatorname{diam}(T):T\in\mathcal{N}(G)\}\cup\{1\}.

If
G
is insoluble then
Lemma

6.1
assures the existence of an insoluble normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
such that

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\ell_{X}(G/N)+\ell_{X}(N)\leq\ell_{X}(G/N)+c\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{30+5\theta_{1}}d,

where
c>0
is some constant.
If
G/N
is again insoluble, a similar bound applies to
\ell_{X}(G/N)
, and so on.
If
G
has
m
nonabelian composition factors, it follows by induction that there is a normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq G
with
G/N
soluble such that

\ell_{X}(G)\leq\ell_{X}(G/N)+cm\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{30+5\theta_{1}}d.

Note that
m\leq\log_{60}|G|\leq\log|G|
, while by
Theorem

1.2
we have

\ell_{X}(G/N)\leq\operatorname{diam}(G/N)\ll\varepsilon_{0}(G/N)(\log|G/N|)^{8}\leq\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{8}.

Thus

\ell_{X}(G)\ll\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{8}+\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{31+5\theta_{1}}d.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

7.
Permutation groups

To apply our results to permutation groups, we need to bound the exponent parameter
\varepsilon(G)
.
For this we have the following convenient lemma, which follows easily from results of Guralnick
[
29
]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(n)
be a transitive group and let
N\trianglelefteq G
.
Assume the orbits of
N
have length
m
.
Then
\exp(N/N^{\prime})\leq m
.
In particular
\varepsilon(G)\leq n
.

Proof.

Let
R
be the transitive group induced by
N
on one of its orbits. Then
N
is subdirect in
R^{n/m}
.
Let
\gamma(H)
denote the group invariant introduced in
[
29
]
*Section 2 (denoted there by
\mu(H)
).
By
[
29
]
*Lemmas 2.1, 2.3, and Theorem 4.1(a), we have

\exp(N/N^{\prime})=\gamma(N/N^{\prime})\leq\gamma(N)=\gamma(R)\leq m.\qed

The following lemma is well-known; see Kovács–Praeger
[
38
]
for a much more general result.

Lemma 7.2
.

If
T
is simple and
T\cong G/N
with
N\trianglelefteq G\leq S_{n}
then
\mu(T)\leq n
.

Proof.

Let
n
be the smallest integer such that
T\cong G/N
for some subgroups
N\trianglelefteq G\leq S_{n}
.
If
G_{x}N=G
then
G_{x}/G_{x}\cap N\cong G_{x}N/N=G/N\cong T
, contradicting the minimality of
n
, so
G_{x}N<G
.
It follows that
G_{x}N/N
is a proper subgroup of
G/N\cong T
of index
|G:G_{x}N|\leq|G:G_{x}|\leq n
.
∎

We can now prove the main corollary listed in the introduction on transitive permutation groups.

Proof of
Corollary

1.5
.

Let
G\leq S_{n}
be transitive. Let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(G)
and
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G)
. By
Theorem

1.1
,

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\varepsilon(\log|G|)^{31+5\theta_{1}}\operatorname{diam}(T)

for some
T\in\mathcal{N}(G)
, or else the same without the
\operatorname{diam}(T)
factor if
\mathcal{N}(G)=\emptyset
.
By
Lemma

7.1
,
\varepsilon\leq n
, and
\log|G|\leq n\log n
.
By
Lemma

7.2
,
\mu(T)\leq n
, so
\operatorname{diam}(T)\leq n^{\theta_{1}}
.
Thus

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\varepsilon(n\log n)^{31+5\theta_{1}}n^{\theta_{1}}\ll n^{32+7\theta_{1}}.

This proves the first statement.

Now assume that the following two statements hold:

(1)

\operatorname{diam}(A_{n})\leq n^{O(1)}
,

(2)

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq|V|^{O(1)}
for simple classical groups
G\leq\operatorname{PGL}(V)
.

In the latter case
\mu(G)\gg|V|^{1/2}
(see
[
35
]
*Theorem 5.2.2), while obviously
\mu(A_{n})=n
, so the two statements imply
\theta_{1}(T)\ll 1
for all finite simple groups of rank larger than
8
.
Since Babai’s conjecture is known for simple groups of bounded rank, it follows that
\theta_{1}(T)\ll 1
for all simple groups,
which implies that
\theta_{1}(G)\ll 1
for all finite groups.
Thus
1
holds.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

1.6
.

By
Corollary

1.5
we have
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{32+7\theta_{1}}
where
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(G)
.
Assuming
\theta_{1}>1
, let
T\in\mathcal{N}(G)
realize the maximum in the definition of
\theta_{1}(G)
.
If
T
has bounded rank then
\theta_{1}\ll 1
.
If
T\cong A_{m}
for some
m\geq 5
then

\theta_{1}\ll(\log m)^{3}\log\log m

by
Equation

1.1
.
If
T\leq\operatorname{PGL}(V)
is classical then by
Lemma

7.2
and
[
35
]
*Theorem 5.2.2 again we have
n\geq\mu(T)\gg|V|^{1/2}
, so

\theta_{1}(T)\ll(\log\dim V)^{2}\ll(\log\log n)^{2}

by (
1.2
).
∎

In particular if
G\leq S_{n}
is transitive and soluble then
\theta_{1}(G)=1
, so
Corollary

1.5
gives

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{39}.

In fact we can obtain

\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq n^{6.16}

by combining the bound
\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq\varepsilon(G)4^{L-1}(\log_{2}|G|)^{2}
from
Theorem

1.2
with
Lemma

7.1
and the following standard bounds for order and the derived length of a transitive soluble group:

|G|\leq 24^{(n-1)/3},\qquad L\leq(5/2)\log_{3}n.

(see
[
14
]
*Theorem 5.8B and Exercise 5.8.7).

Let us now invest some energy to reduce the exponent in the result for transitive soluble groups.
As usual,
\gamma_{3}(G)
denotes the subgroup generated by the triple commutators
[x,y,z]=[[x,y],z]
with
x,y,z\in G
.
If
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
then
\gamma_{3}(G)
is normally generated by the triple commutators
[x,y,z]
with
x,y,z\in X
.

Lemma 7.3

(Variant of
Lemma

3.2
)
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
and let
N=\left\langle Y\right\rangle\trianglelefteq G
be a normal subgroup with
\gamma_{3}(N)
finite. Then
\gamma_{3}(N)=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where

\ell_{X}(Z)\leq 10\ell_{X}(Y)+2\lambda(\gamma_{3}(N)).

Proof.

Let
Z_{0}=\{[y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}]:y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in Y\}
. The triple commutator word
[x,y,z]
has length
10
, so
\ell_{X}(Z_{0})\leq 10\ell_{X}(Y)
, and
Z_{0}
normally generates
\gamma_{3}(N)
. Apply
Lemma

3.1
to
\gamma_{3}(N)=\left\langle Z_{0}^{G}\right\rangle
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.7
.

Let
G\leq S_{n}
be soluble and transitive,
and let
H
be a maximal soluble subgroup of
S_{n}
containing
G
. Then
H=P_{h}\wr\cdots\wr P_{1}
for some primitive soluble groups
P_{j}\leq\operatorname{Sym}(d_{j})
where
n=d_{1}\cdots d_{h}
.
By
[
17
]
*Theorem 4.6(i), for each
j
there is a series of subgroups

P_{j}=P_{j,0}>P_{j,1}>\cdots>P_{j,\ell_{j}}=1

with the following properties:

(1)

for each
i=0,1,\dots,\ell_{j}-1
,
P_{j,i+1}
is either
(P_{j,i})^{\prime}
or
\gamma_{3}(P_{j,i})
,

(2)

if
m_{j}
is the number of nonabelian factors
P_{j,i}/P_{j,i+1}
then

\ell_{j}+m_{j}\log_{4}(5/2)\leq 3\log_{4}(d_{j}),

(3)

\ell_{j}\ll 1+\log\log_{2}d_{j}
by Zassenhaus–Newman
[
49
]
.

By stringing these series together for
j=1,\dots,h
we get a series

G=G_{0}>G_{1}>\cdots>G_{L}=1

with the following properties:

(4)

for each index
i=0,\dots,L-1
,
G_{i+1}
is either
(G_{i})^{\prime}
or
\gamma_{3}(G_{i})
,

(5)

for each index
i\geq\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{j}
,
G_{i}\leq(P_{h}\wr\cdots\wr P_{j+1})^{d_{1}\cdots d_{j}}
, so the orbits of
G_{i}
have length at most
d_{j+1}\cdots d_{h}=n/d_{1}\cdots d_{j}
.

Note in particular that
G_{i}\trianglelefteq G
for each
i
.

Now let
X
be a generating set for
G
and define sets
X_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
for
i=0\dots,L-1
inductively as follows, starting with
X_{0}=X
.
If
0<i<L
and
G_{i}=(G_{i-1})^{\prime}
, apply
Lemma

3.2
to obtain
X_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
such that
G_{i}=\left\langle X_{i}\right\rangle
and

\ell_{X}(X_{i})\leq 4\ell_{X}(X_{i-1})+2\log_{2}|G|.

Otherwise
G_{i}=\gamma_{3}(G_{i-1})
; apply
Lemma

7.3
to obtain
X_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
such that
G_{i}=\left\langle X_{i}\right\rangle
and

\ell_{X}(X_{i})\leq 10\ell_{X}(X_{i-1})+2\log_{2}|G|.

Define
a_{i}=4
in the first case and
a_{i}=10
in the second case.
Then

\frac{\ell_{X}(X_{i})}{a_{1}\cdots a_{i}}-\frac{\ell_{X}(X_{i-1})}{a_{1}\cdots a_{i-1}}\leq\frac{2}{4^{i}}\log_{2}|G|,

and we deduce by summing that

\frac{\ell_{X}(X_{i})}{a_{1}\cdots a_{i}}\leq 1+\frac{2}{3}\log_{2}|G|\ll n.

Thus

\ell_{X}(X_{i})\ll(a_{1}\cdots a_{i})n=4^{i+\log_{4}(5/2)r_{i}}n

for
0\leq i<L
, where
r_{i}
is the number of nonabelian sections
G_{j-1}/G_{j}
with
j\leq i
.

Fix an index
i
with
0\leq i<L
and let
j\in\{0,\dots,h-1\}
be the unique index such that

\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{j}\leq i<\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{j+1}.

By property
(5)
, the orbits of
G_{i}
have length some

n_{i}\leq d_{j+1}\cdots d_{h}=\frac{n}{d_{1}\cdots d_{j}}.

Now by properties
(2)
and
(3)

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(X_{i})\ll 4^{\sum_{s=1}^{j+1}(\ell_{s}+m_{s}\log_{4}(5/2))}n\ll(d_{1}\cdots d_{j})^{3}(\log_{2}d_{j+1})^{O(1)}n.

By
Lemma

7.1
,
\exp(G_{i}/G_{i}^{\prime})\leq n_{i}
and also
\exp(G_{i}^{\prime}/G_{i+1})\leq n_{i}
.
If
G_{i}/G_{i+1}
is abelian then

\operatorname{diam}(G_{i}/G_{i+1})\leq\exp(G_{i}/G_{i+1})\log_{2}|G_{i}:G_{i+1}|\leq n_{i}\log_{2}|G_{i}:G_{i+1}|

by
Lemma

3.4
as before.
If
G_{i}/G_{i+1}
is class-2 nilpotent then, since the commutator subgroup of a class-2 nilpotent group is generated by the commutators of the generators, by
Lemma

2.2
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}(G_{i}/G_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{diam}(G_{i}/G_{i}^{\prime})+4\operatorname{diam}(G_{i}^{\prime}/G_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq n_{i}\log_{2}|G_{i}:G_{i}^{\prime}|+4n_{i}\log_{2}|G_{i}^{\prime}:G_{i+1}|

\displaystyle\leq 4n_{i}\log_{2}|G_{i}:G_{i+1}|.

Now by
Lemma

2.2
again we have

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\ell_{X}(X_{i})\operatorname{diam}(G_{i}/G_{i+1})

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\ell_{X}(X_{i})n_{i}\log_{2}|G_{i}:G_{i+1}|

\displaystyle\ll n\max_{0\leq i<L}\ell_{X}(X_{i})n_{i}

\displaystyle\ll n^{3}\max_{0\leq j<h}(d_{1}\cdots d_{j})^{2}(\log_{2}d_{j+1})^{O(1)}

\displaystyle\leq n^{5}\max_{0\leq j<h}d_{j+1}^{-2}(\log_{2}d_{j+1})^{O(1)}\ll n^{5}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

A simplified version of the above argument using just the derived series gives the exponent
5.16
instead of
5
.
Key motivating examples are the iterated wreath products

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Sym}(4)\wr\cdots\wr\operatorname{Sym}(4)\leq\operatorname{Sym}(4^{h}),

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\operatorname{AGL}_{2}(3)\wr\cdots\wr\operatorname{AGL}_{2}(3)\leq\operatorname{Sym}(9^{h})

(the latter is the example achieving Dixon’s bound on the derived length of a soluble permutation group).

In the case of a nilpotent group, an easier argument suffices and obtains the exponent
4
.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
G\leq S_{n}
be a transitive nilpotent group of degree
n
. Then
\operatorname{diam}(G)\leq n^{4}
.

Proof.

Similar to the previous proof, but easier. Let

G=G^{(0)}>G^{(1)}>\cdots>G^{(L)}=1

be the derived series of
G
and let
n_{i}
be the length of the orbits of
G^{(i)}
. Since primitive nilpotent groups are abelian,
n_{i+1}<n_{i}
for each
i<L
.
It follows that
n_{i}\leq n/2^{i}
.
In particular
L\leq\log_{2}n
.
Also
\exp(G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)})\leq n_{i}
by
Lemma

7.1
.
Now suppose
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
. By
Lemma

3.3
,
G^{(i)}=\left\langle X_{i}\right\rangle
where

\ell_{X}(X_{i})\leq 4^{i}\log_{2}|G|.

Also, it is well-known that
|G|\leq 2^{n-1}
, so
\log_{2}|G|\leq n
.
Hence

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\ell_{X}(X_{i})\operatorname{diam}(G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)})

\displaystyle\leq n\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}4^{i}n_{i}\log_{2}|G^{(i)}:G^{(i+1)}|

\displaystyle\leq n^{3}\max_{0\leq i<L}2^{i}\leq n^{4}.\qed

8.
Residually finite groups

The arguments in this section are directly inspired by and reliant on Wilson’s papers
[
56
,
57
]
.

The rough idea is the following.
If
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
is a residually finite group then
G
is approximated by a sequence of finite quotients
G^{*}=G/N_{i}
, each of which has an induced generating set
X^{*}
, and
Corollary

1.3
applied to
G^{*}=\left\langle X^{*}\right\rangle
gives

\operatorname{diam}(G^{*})\leq\varepsilon(\log|G^{*}|)^{C\theta_{2}},

where
C\geq 1
is a constant,
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(G^{*})
, and
\theta_{2}=\theta_{2}(G^{*})
.
This implies that there is an integer
m\leq\varepsilon(\log|G^{*}|)^{C\theta_{2}}
such that

\gamma_{X}(m)\geq|G^{*}|\geq\exp((m/\varepsilon)^{1/(C\theta_{2})}).

We would essentially be done at this point were it not for
\varepsilon
, which is a nuisance.

We can avoid the dependence on the exponent parameter
\varepsilon
by arguing more circuitously.
In fact we will not apply our main diameter bounds directly but rather the results in
Section

5
.
We will argue that if
G
has controlled growth then
G
cannot have arbitrarily large nonabelian chief factors, and then deduce that
G
is virtually residually soluble.
We will then use the following result of Wilson for residually soluble groups, which in turn depends on the deep result of Grigorchuk–Lubotzky–Mann
[
24
,
42
]
on the residually nilpotent case (using group ring arguments and the Lazard analyticity criterion).

Theorem 8.1

(Wilson
[
57
]
*Theorem 1)
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a residually soluble group such that

\gamma_{X}(n)/\exp(n^{1/6})\to 0\qquad\text{as}~n\to\infty.

Then
G
is virtually nilpotent.

Remark 8.2
.

The exponent
1/6
was recently improved to
1/4-o(1)
in
[
17
]
.

Concretely, the extension to residually finite groups is based on the following observation.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a group with a finite quotient
G/K
such that
G/K
is monolithic with nonabelian socle
N=T^{n}
, where
T
is simple.
Let

0<\beta\leq(19+10\theta_{2}(G/K))^{-1}.

Then there is a constant
c=c(\beta)
and an integer
m\geq 0
such that

\gamma_{X}(m)\geq|N|\geq\exp(cm^{\beta}).

Proof.

There is no loss in assuming
K=1
. Let
N=\operatorname{soc}(G)=T^{n}
.
By
Proposition

5.3
and
Lemma

3.1
,
N=\left\langle Z\right\rangle
where

\ell_{X}(Z)\leq 2n^{16+5\theta_{1}(G)}\log_{2}|T|+2n\log_{2}|T|\ll n^{16+5\theta_{1}(G)}\log|T|.

By
Theorem

5.5
, it follows that

\displaystyle\ell_{X}(N)\leq\ell_{X}(Z)\operatorname{diam}(T^{n})

\displaystyle\ll n^{19+5\theta_{1}(G)}(\log|T|)^{2}\operatorname{diam}(T)

\displaystyle\leq n^{19+5\theta_{1}(G)}(\log|T|)^{2+\theta_{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leq(\log|N|)^{19+10\theta_{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leq(\log|N|)^{1/\beta}.

Let
C
be a suitable constant such that
\ell_{X}(N)\leq C(\log|N|)^{1/\beta}
.
Then the integer
m=\left\lfloor C(\log|N|)^{1/\beta}\right\rfloor
satisfies

\gamma_{X}(m)\geq|N|\geq\exp(cm^{\beta}),

where
c=C^{-\beta}
.
∎

Lemma 8.4
.

Suppose
G
is a finite group with a nonabelian chief factor
N/K\cong T^{n}
. Then there is a monolithic quotient of
G
with socle
T^{n}
.

Proof.

There is no loss in assuming
K=1
.
Then
N
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
.
Now consider
G^{*}=G/C_{G}(N)
.
Let
1\neq L^{*}\trianglelefteq G^{*}
and let
L
be the full preimage of
L^{*}
in
G
.
Since
L^{*}\neq 1
,
L
is not contained in
C_{G}(N)
, so
1\neq[L,N]\leq L\cap N
.
Since
N
is minimal normal in
G
,
N\leq L
, so
N^{*}\leq L^{*}
.
Thus
G^{*}
is monolithic with socle
N^{*}\cong N
.
∎

Now we can prove that Babai’s conjecture implies Grigorchuk’s gap conjecture for residually finite groups.

Proof of
Theorem

1.10
.

Given
\theta>0
, let

\beta_{0}=1/(19+10\theta),\qquad\beta=1/(20+10\theta).

Now let
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle
be a finitely generated group such that

(1)

G
has a sequence of finite-index normal subgroups
N_{i}
such that
\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}N_{i}=1
and such that
\theta_{2}(G/N_{i})\leq\theta
for all
i\geq 1
,

(2)

\gamma_{X}(n)\leq C\exp(Cn^{\beta})
for some constant
C>0
and all
n\geq 0
.

Let
i\geq 1
and suppose that
G/N_{i}
has a nonabelian chief factor
T^{n}
.
Then by
Lemmas

8.3
and
8.4
there is a constant
c_{0}=c(\beta_{0})
and an integer
m\geq 0
such that

\gamma_{X}(m)\geq|T^{n}|\geq\exp(c_{0}m^{\beta_{0}}).

On the other hand
\gamma_{X}(m)\leq C\exp(Cm^{\beta})
, so

c_{0}m^{\beta_{0}}\leq Cm^{\beta}+\log C,

which implies that
m
is
(\theta,C)
-bounded.
Since

|T^{n}|\leq\gamma_{X}(m)\leq C\exp(Cm^{\beta}),

it follows that
|T^{n}|
is also
(\theta,C)
-bounded.
Therefore
|\operatorname{Aut}(T^{n})|\leq a
for some integer
a
depending only on
(\theta,C)
.

Let
H
be the intersection of the subgroups of
G
of index at most
a
. Note particularly that
a
and
H
are independent of
i
.

Now again let
i\geq 1
. If
L/M\cong T^{n}
is a nonabelian chief factor as above of
G^{*}=G/N_{i}
then there is a natural map
G\to\operatorname{Aut}(T^{n})
. Since
|\operatorname{Aut}(T^{n})|\leq a
,
H
is contained in the kernel, so
H^{*}
centralizes
T^{n}
.
Let
G^{*}=G^{*}_{0}>G^{*}_{1}>\cdots>G^{*}_{\ell}=1
be a chief series of
G^{*}
. Then

H^{*}=G^{*}_{0}\cap H^{*}>G^{*}_{1}\cap H^{*}>\cdots>G^{*}_{\ell}\cap H^{*}=1

is a normal abelian series for
H^{*}
.
Indeed, each section
G^{*}_{i-1}\cap H^{*}/G^{*}_{i}\cap H^{*}\cong(G_{i-1}^{*}\cap H^{*})G^{*}_{i}/G^{*}_{i}
is
H^{*}
-isomorphic to a subgroup of
G^{*}_{i-1}/G^{*}_{i}
, which is either abelian or centralized by
H^{*}
.
Thus
H^{*}
is soluble.
Since
i
was arbitrary, it follows that
H
is residually soluble.

Finally, since
\beta<1/6
,
Theorem

8.1
implies that
H
is virtually nilpotent, and therefore so is
G
.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

1.11
.

Let
d\geq 1
and assume
G=\left\langle X\right\rangle\leq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{d})
,
where
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is an infinite rooted
d
-regular tree.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{d}^{h}
denote the
d
-regular tree of height
h
.
Let
N_{h}\trianglelefteq G
be the pointwise stabilizer of the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
at distance
h
from the root.
Then

G/N_{h}\leq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{d}^{h})=\overbrace{S_{d}\wr\cdots\wr S_{d}}^{h}.

If
T
is a nonabelian composition factor of

it follows that
T\hookrightarrow A_{d}
.
Therefore
\theta_{2}(G/N_{h})
is bounded by some
\theta=\theta(d)
.
Now let
\beta=\beta(\theta)
be as in
Theorem

1.10
.
Then
\gamma_{X}(n)\preceq\exp(n^{\beta})
implies that

is virtually nilpotent.

To give a more precise formula it suffices to bound

\theta_{2}(T)=\frac{\log\operatorname{diam}(T)}{\log\log|T|}

for nonabelian simple

.
Let
n=\mu(T)
. Then
5\leq n\leq d
and
T
is a primitive simple subgroup of
S_{n}
.
By
[

]
*Theorem 1.1, either

for some

In the second case, by the Helfgott–Seress bound (

Thus we may take

for

is some constant,
and therefore we can take

Finally,

Remark 8.5
.

Since growth functions are not totally ordered (trichotomy fails), there are multiple ways of formulating the gap conjecture.
We are focusing on the weakest version.
A stronger version of
3
(sometimes called
\operatorname{Gap}^{*}(\beta)
) predicts the existence of a constant
\beta>0
such that every non-virtually-nilpotent finitely generated group
G
satisfies
\gamma_{G}(n)\succeq\exp(n^{\beta})
(rather than
\gamma_{G}(n)\not\prec\exp(n^{\beta})
).
An even stronger version is stated in
[
17
]
*Section 5.

9.
Examples

The form of the bound in
Theorem

1.1
suggests the intriguing possibility of precisely characterizing the structure of finite groups of polylogarithmic diameter, assuming the validity of Babai’s conjecture (
2
).
For example, by
Corollary

1.4
, anabelian is sufficient.
In general, to what extent is the dependence of
Theorem

1.1
on the exponent parameter

\varepsilon(G)=\max\{\exp(N/N^{\prime}):N\trianglelefteq G\}

necessary? Can we say that only certain kinds of abelian sections are important?

In a similar spirit, Breuillard and Tointon
[
12
]
*Section 4 showed that the
polynomial
diameter condition
\operatorname{diam}(G)\geq|G|^{\varepsilon}
is equivalent to the presence of a cyclic section
H/N
with index
|G:H|\ll_{\varepsilon}1
and
|H:N|\gg_{\varepsilon}|G|^{\varepsilon}
.
If there is a similar condition that characterizes polylogarithmic diameter, it has to navigate the following examples.

Obviously cyclic groups have large diameter.
More generally, groups with large cyclic quotients have large diameter,
and dihedral groups are examples of groups with small abelianization but linear diameter due to a cyclic section.
More generally, if
H/N
is a nontrivial
k
-generated abelian section of
G
with
|G:H|\leq k
and
N\trianglelefteq G
,
then
G/N
is
(2k-1)
-generated by some set
X
,
there is a set
Y\subseteq H/N
of Schreier generators, where
|Y|\leq 2k^{2}
, and

(9.1)

\operatorname{diam}(G)\geq\operatorname{diam}(G/N,X)\geq\operatorname{diam}(H/N,Y)\gg|H/N|^{1/(2k^{2})}.

In particular any large cyclic section
H/N
with
N\trianglelefteq G
and
|G:H|
small forces the diameter of
G
to be large.

The presence of a large cyclic section
H/N
may force the diameter of
G
to be large even if the index
|G:H|
is not small.
Consider the extension
G=V:A_{n}
, where
V\cong C_{p}^{n-1}
is the standard deleted permutation module for
A_{n}
over
\mathbf{F}_{p}
,
p\nmid n
.
Then
\log|G|\sim n(\log p+\log n)

and Schreier’s
Lemma

2.3
immediately gives
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll pn\operatorname{diam}(A_{n})
.
However, if
v=(1,-1,0,\dots,0)\in V
then
X=\{v\}\cup A_{n}
is a generating set for
G
and it is easy to see that
\operatorname{diam}(G,X)\gg pn
.
Therefore

\max(pn,\operatorname{diam}(A_{n}))\ll\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll pn\operatorname{diam}(A_{n}).

It follows that
G
has polylogarithmic worst-case diameter only if
p
is very small, namely
p\leq n^{O(1)}
(and Babai’s conjecture holds for
A_{n}
).
It is interesting that the best-case (or perhaps even typical-case) diameter remains small for much larger
p
:
the first and third authors
[
18
]
recently showed that as long as
p\leq e^{O(n)}
there is a bounded-cardinality generating set
X\subseteq G
such that
\operatorname{diam}(G,X)\ll\log|G|
(in fact, such that the Cayley graph is an expander graph).

On the other hand, not every large abelian section is harmful to the diameter.
A simple example is the wreath product
G=C_{2}\wr C_{n}
, for which
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll n^{2}
by Schreier.
Another example is
G=\mathbf{F}_{p}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p)
, for which
\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll(\log p)^{O(1)}
.
This follows from the following variant of
Lemma

2.3
(2)
.
Let
V\trianglelefteq G
be a normal abelian subgroup of a group
G
. The
G
-normal diameter

\operatorname{diam}_{G}(V)
of
V
is defined to be the maximum value of
\operatorname{diam}(V,X)
taken over
G
-invariant generating sets
X\subseteq V
. Then

\operatorname{diam}(G)\ll\operatorname{diam}_{G}(V)\operatorname{diam}(G/V).

Conversely,
\operatorname{diam}_{G}(V)\leq\operatorname{diam}(G)
if
V
is complemented.

The previous examples may suggest that the critical thing is the maximal
G
-normal diameter of an abelian section, or possibly of a complemented abelian section, but neither is correct.
For example, let
p
be an odd prime and consider an extension of the form
p^{1+2}:\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p)
or
p^{1+4}:\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p)
with centre
Z\cong C_{p}
.
In this case
Z
is a large Frattini abelian subgroup of
G
, and
\operatorname{diam}_{G}(Z)=\operatorname{diam}(Z)\sim p/2
since
Z
is central, but one can check that
\operatorname{diam}(G)
is polylogarithmic.
On the other hand, there are arbitrarily large Frattini abelian extensions of
A_{5}
of the form
C_{2^{n}}^{5}\cdot A_{5}
,
and
\operatorname{diam}(G)\geq|G|^{c}
for any such group by (
9.1
)
(the existence of these extensions follows from the theory of universal Frattini extensions: see Fried–Jarden
[
21
]
*Chapter 25 and
[
22
]
*§II.C).

Let us now turn away from diameters of abstract finite groups and focus on transitive permutation groups, which is our primary motivation.
It is not known whether any transitive subgroup of
S_{n}
has greater than quadratic diameter.
In fact, apart from the giant cases
G=A_{n}
and
G=S_{n}
(and close relatives like
S_{n/2}\wr S_{2}
etc),
it is generally difficult to construct transitive groups with diameter greater than linear.

One way of doing so is to consider the congruence quotients of the famous Grigorchuk group
G
. Let us briefly recall the construction. The interested reader might refer to
[
13
]
*Chapter VIII or
[
43
]
*Chapter 10 or
[
7
]
.
Let
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\omega}
be the set of all infinite binary strings.
We define involutions
a,b,c,d\in\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)
by the following recursive rules:

\displaystyle(0w)^{a}=1w,

\displaystyle(1w)^{a}=0w

\displaystyle(0w)^{b}=0w^{a},

\displaystyle(1w)^{b}=1w^{c},

\displaystyle(0w)^{c}=0w^{a},

\displaystyle(1w)^{c}=1w^{d},

\displaystyle(0w)^{d}=0w,

\displaystyle(1w)^{d}=1w^{b}.

The
first Grigorchuk group
is
G=\left\langle a,b,c,d\right\rangle
.
Note that
G
may be identified with a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
, where
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is an infinite rooted binary tree,
so in particular
G
is residually a finite
2
-group.
It turns out that
G
is an infinite torsion group, and moreover
G
has intermediate growth.
In fact it was proved by Bartholdi
[
8
]
that

\gamma_{X}(R)\preceq\exp(R^{\beta}),

where
X=\{a,b,c,d\}
,
\beta=(1-\log_{2}\eta)^{-1}\approx 0.767
, and
\eta\approx 0.811
is the real root of the polynomial
X^{3}+X^{2}+X-2
.
Recently, a nearly matching lower bound was proved by Erschler and Zheng
[
19
]
:

\gamma_{X}(R)\succeq\exp(R^{\beta-o(1)}).

By pruning the tree we obtain the
congruence quotient

G_{h}\leq\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{T}_{2}^{h})
, which is a
2
-group acting faithfully and transitively on
n=2^{h}
points.
Let
X_{h}
be the image of
X
in
G_{h}
.
By
[
13
]
*VIII.D.41,

|G_{h}|=2^{5n/8+2}\qquad(h\geq 3).

From Bartholdi’s bound on the growth function it follows that

\operatorname{diam}(G_{h},X_{h})\gg n^{1/\beta},\qquad 1/\beta\approx 1.303.

Unfortunately, we do not get a nearly matching upper bound on
\operatorname{diam}(G_{h})
from Erschler–Zheng, as it may be that a disproportionate number of the elements in the ball
B_{X}(R)
are contained in the kernel of the map
G\to G_{h}
.
However, at least from
[
9
]
*Proposition 7 we have

\operatorname{diam}(G_{h},X_{h})\ll n^{1.939}

and, moreover, by
Remark

7.6
the worst-case diameter is

\operatorname{diam}(G_{h})\ll n^{2}.

Variations on the Grigorchuk group have been the subject of intense study for several decades.
For a particularly vast generalization, we refer to the concept of a
spinal group
as defined by Bartholdi–Grigorchuk–Šuniḱ
[
7
]
.
The defining data consists of two groups,
A
and
B
(typically finite), an integer sequence
(d_{i})_{i\geq 1}
with
d_{i}\geq 2
for each
i
, and a sequence of permutation representations

\alpha_{1}:A\to\operatorname{Sym}(d_{1}),\qquad\alpha_{i,j}:B\to\operatorname{Sym}(d_{i})\quad(i\geq 2,1\leq j<d_{i-1}).

Define

A_{1}=\alpha_{1}(A),\qquad A_{i}=\left\langle\alpha_{i,j}(B):1\leq j<d_{i-1}\right\rangle\quad(i\geq 2).

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an infinite spherically homogeneous rooted tree with degree sequence
(d_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.
We may identify the vertices of
\mathcal{T}
with the set
\Omega
of all finite strings
\omega=\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{h}
with
1\leq\omega_{i}\leq d_{i}
for each
i
,
and we may identify
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
with the subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)
stabilizing the prefix relation.
We define actions
A,B\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
as follows.
The action of
A
on
\mathcal{T}
is the obvious
rooted action
defined by

(\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{h})^{a}=\omega_{1}^{\alpha_{1}(a)}\omega_{2}\cdots\omega_{h}\qquad(a\in A).

The action of
B
on
\mathcal{T}
is the
directed action
defined by the rule

\displaystyle(d_{1}\cdots d_{i-2}j\omega_{i}\cdots\omega_{h})^{b}=d_{1}\cdots d_{i-2}j\omega_{i}^{\alpha_{i,j}(b)}\omega_{i+1}\cdots\omega_{h}

\displaystyle\hfill(b\in B,i\geq 2,1\leq j<d_{i-1}),

and
\omega^{b}=\omega
if
\omega=d_{1}\cdots d_{h-1}j
.
We assume both actions are faithful and we identify
A,B
with their images in
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
.
The group
G=\left\langle A,B\right\rangle
is called a
spinal group
if

(1)

(spherical transitivity)
A_{i}\leq\operatorname{Sym}(d_{i})
is transitive for each
i\geq 1
,

(2)

(strong faithfulness)
\bigcap_{i\geq k}\bigcap_{j=1}^{d_{i-1}-1}\ker\alpha_{i,j}=1
for all
k\geq 2
.

The subgroups
A
and
B
are the
rooted part
and the
directed part
of
G
.

Now assume
\alpha_{i,j}
is trivial whenever
i\geq 2
and
j\neq 1
, and let
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i,1}
for
i\geq 2
.
We say
G
is of
type
\mathrm{G}
if this holds and additionally

(3)

(covering condition)
\bigcup_{i\geq j}\ker\alpha_{i}=B
for all
j\geq 2
,

and, more strongly, of
type
\mathrm{G}_{r}
if

(
3_{r}
)

(
r
-uniform covering condition)
\bigcup_{i=j}^{j+r-1}\ker\alpha_{i}=B
for all
j\geq 2
.

For example, the first Grigorchuk group is a spinal group of type
\mathrm{G}_{3}
with

A=C_{2},\qquad B=C_{2}\times C_{2},

and a
3
-periodic sequence of homomorphisms
\alpha_{i}:B\to\operatorname{Sym}(2)
.

By
[
7
]
*Theorem 10.5–10.8, every spinal group of type
\mathrm{G}
with finite directed part has intermediate growth, and moreover the growth function satisfies

\gamma_{G}(n)\succeq\exp(n^{\beta_{1}}),\qquad\beta_{1}=(1+\log_{m}(2))^{-1},~m=\min d_{i}.

Furthermore, by
[
7
]
*Theorem 10.10, if
G
has type
\mathrm{G}_{r}
then

\gamma_{G}(n)\preceq\exp(n^{\beta_{2}}),\qquad\beta_{2}=(1+\log_{M}(1/\eta_{r}))^{-1},~M=\max d_{i},

where
\eta_{r}
is the positive root of
X^{r}+X^{r-1}+X^{r-2}-2
.

Consider an illustrative example. Take

A=C_{2},\qquad B=C_{2}\times C_{2}\times\operatorname{Alt}(5).

Let
(d_{i})_{\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\{2,5\}
such that
d_{1}=2
,
d_{i}=2
infinitely often,
d_{i}=5
infinitely often, and

d_{i}=5\implies d_{i+1}=d_{i+2}=d_{i+3}=2\qquad(i\geq 2).

If
d_{i}=2
then there are three choices for

\alpha_{i}:B\to C_{2}\times C_{2}\to\operatorname{Sym}(2),

which we take in a
3
-periodic fashion.
If
d_{i}=5
then we take the natural map

\alpha_{i}:B\to\operatorname{Alt}(5)\to\operatorname{Sym}(5).

The resulting spinal group is of type
\mathrm{G}_{4}
, so the growth function of
G
satisfies

\exp(n^{0.5})\preceq\gamma_{G}(n)\preceq\exp(n^{0.922}).

Now let
G_{h}
be the quotient of
G
by the level-
h
stabilizer subgroup,
and let
X_{h}
be the image of the generating set
X=A\cup B
in
G_{h}
.
Then
G_{h}
is a transitive subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(n)
where
n=d_{1}\cdots d_{h}
,
2^{h}\leq n\leq 5^{h}
,
every composition factor of
G_{h}
is isomorphic either to
C_{2}
or to
\operatorname{Alt}(5)
,
and the number of
\operatorname{Alt}(5)
factors tends to infinity as
h\to\infty
.
One can show using spinal group arguments as in
[
7
]
that the order of
G_{h}
is exponential in
n
.
Finally, from the slow growth of
G
it follows that

\operatorname{diam}(G_{h},X_{h})\gg n^{1.084}.

In the other direction, at least it follows from
Corollary

1.5
and the fact that
\operatorname{diam}(A_{5})=10
that

\operatorname{diam}(G_{h})\ll n^{43}.

Since
G
is not virtually residually soluble, it is a counterexample to a conjecture of Sarah Black
[
10
]
*Conjecture
2^{\prime}
that any finitely generated residually finite group of subexponential growth is virtually residually soluble.
However, it is abundantly clear that the slow growth in
G
is directly linked to the
C_{2}
factors,
while the
A_{5}
factors are only incidental.
The groups of Kassabov–Pak
[
33
]
are similar.
In fact we do not know examples of residually finite-anabelian groups of intermediate growth.

[
34
]
*21.44.

Question
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated dense subgroup of the infinite iterated wreath product
W=\cdots\wr A_{5}\wr A_{5}\wr A_{5}
.
Can
G
have subexponential growth?

Corollary

1.11
implies that there is a constant
\beta>0
such that no such group
G
has growth
\gamma_{X}(n)\preceq\exp(n^{\beta})
.
We may take
\beta=1/40
.
