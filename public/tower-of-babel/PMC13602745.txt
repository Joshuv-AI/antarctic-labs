Building and externally validating a prediction model for long COVID in severe and critical COVID-19 patients: a multi-center cohort study
Authors: Zhang Haojing, Kan Lin, Pan Dianzhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the risk factors of Long COVID and to construct a nomogram to predict the occurrence of Long COVID.MethodThis study was an observational study. Clinical data were collected from patients diagnosed with COVID-19 and hospitalized at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University Hospital from December 7, 2022, to February 1, 2023. The prediction model was constructed using a nomogram. The clinical data of patients in Panjin Central Hospital Hospital from December 7, 2022 to December 7, 2023 were used for external validation.ResultsIn the development cohort and the validation cohort of this study, 60.3% and 62.3% of the patients developed Long COVID, respectively. After Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, the final variables included in the prediction model were percentage of lymphocyte, the Charlson Comorbidity Index, Computed Tomography score, and oxygen requirement. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic for external validation of the model is 0.786, and the p value of Spiegelhalter test was 0.126. The p value of the Hosmer and Lemeshow chi-square statistic was 0.098. The decision curve analysis indicates that the model performs well.ConclusionThe prediction model developed in this study is useful for assessing the likelihood of developing Long COVID in hospitalized patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04409-6.Keywords: Long COVID, COVID-19, Risk factor, Nomogram, Forecast

Introduction

Since the first confirmed case of the novel coronavirus [1] to the latest mutant strain, JN.1 [2] ,Since its emergence in late 2019, Corona Virus Disease 2019(COVID-19) has circulated globally for over seven years [3]. As of February 1, 2024, COVID-19 has caused more than 770 million confirmed cases and over 7.03 million deaths globally. With the World Health Organization (WHO) declaring on May 5, 2023, that COVID-19 is no longer a global public health emergency, the global health crisis caused by the novel coronavirus infection has been effectively controlled. However, the long-term sequelae of acute infection continue to affect a considerable number of COVID-19 survivors.

After recovering from severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, patients worldwide have found it difficult to return to baseline health. Evidence from multiple channels [4, 5] has led healthcare workers to recognize that the impact of COVID-19 on their health is neither temporary nor confined to a single system. They quickly associated new discomfort symptoms and unexplained physical weakness with COVID-19 sequelae [6]. The WHO defines this condition as Long COVID syndrome [7], officially described as symptoms persisting in COVID-19 patients three months after the onset or diagnosis in asymptomatic individuals, lasting for at least two months and not attributable to other diagnoses. These symptoms significantly affect patients’ daily lives.

Long COVID syndrome is prevalent, affecting 9% to 63% of the COVID-19 population globally, and is six times [8] more common than with other coronaviruses. Prevalence rates by country include: China 49%-76%, Italy 5%-51%, and the USA 16%-53%. The incidence among hospitalized patients is notably higher than among non-hospitalized patients [9]. As waves of reinfections have occurred, the enduring challenges posed by COVID-19 have become increasingly apparent.

Studies have indicated that inpatients, outpatients, and patients treated at home report symptoms during the follow-up process, including persistent fatigue, muscle weakness, dyspnea, dry cough, palpitations, anxiety, depression, headache, cognitive impairment, olfactory and taste dysfunction, and decreased immunity [10]. Long-term novel coronavirus infection can also lead to structural and functional involvement of multiple organs.

COVID-19 has broad coverage and a lasting impact on patients. Given the vast population base affected by COVID-19, the resulting strain on healthcare resources and the associated economic burden have been substantial and difficult to quantify Consequently, COVID-19 has attracted unprecedented attention. The diagnosis, differentiation, and treatment of Long COVID remain pressing issues to address.

A significant amount of previous research has focused on studying the clinical manifestations of Long COVID [5], with few studies addressing the screening of high-risk groups and early intervention, which are crucial for predicting and managing Long COVID. A single-factor prediction model is often insufficient; a multi-factorial approach significantly improves prediction accuracy. This study aims to estimate the probability of Long COVID through retrospective data analysis, covering demographics, comorbidities, symptoms, admission details, vaccination status, laboratory parameters, treatment data, and clinical severity, by constructing a nomogram and externally validating the model to assess its predictive performance.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board(IRB) of our hospital ( IRB Number: KYLL20244279), and all patients provided written informed consent. Data of 289 patients hospitalized at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University Hospital from December 7, 2022 to February 1, 2023 and 1342 patients hospitalized at Panjin Central Hospital hospital from December 7, 2022 to December 7, 2023 were collected. Our research adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology(STROBE) guidelines.Cases from the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University hospitals were used as the development cohort, and cases from Panjin Central Hospital hospitals were used as the validation cohort.Inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; inpatient status; nasal swab reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test results positive for COVID-19; adherence to the diagnosis and treatment plan for severe and above patients with novel coronavirus infection (tenth edition). Exclusion criteria included patients who died during hospitalization and at follow-up; patients lost to follow-up; patients who lacked data on the first confirmed RT-PCR test, who lacked two consecutive negative RT-PCR tests or who lacked necessary Computed Tomography(CT)/High-Resolution Computed Tomography(HRCT)/Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography(PETCT) results. After screening, 141 datasets were fully included in the development cohort, and 818 in the validation cohort. The development cohort included a total of 141 patients, among whom 85 experienced the outcome event. A flow chart illustrating the patient selection process is detailed in Fig. 1A (development cohort) and 1B (validation cohort). As the proportion of missing values for key variables was low. Specifically, patients with missing outcome data at follow-up were excluded from the final analysis. No imputation methods were applied, as multiple imputation might introduce additional assumptions that could bias the results given the observational nature of the study.No AI tools were used in the writing, analysis, or figure generation of this manuscript.

Data collection

Demographics (age and sex), past medical history, admission symptoms, vaccination status, laboratory parameters, imaging findings, treatment-related data, and clinical severity were collected.

Past medical history included hypertension, cardiovascular disease (coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, rheumatic heart disease, old myocardial infarction, cardiac insufficiency), diabetes, chronic liver disease (fatty liver, hepatitis B, hepatitis C), chronic lung disease (bronchiectasis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, emphysema, tuberculosis, asthma), chronic kidney disease (chronic renal failure, chronic nephritis), rheumatic disease (systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma), nervous system disorders (cerebral infarction and hemorrhage), and malignancy. Vaccination status was divided into four groups: unvaccinated, partially vaccinated (one dose), fully vaccinated (two doses), and booster vaccinated (three doses or more). Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count, percentage of neutrophils, percentage of lymphocytes, percentage of eosinophils, troponin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, albumin, C-reactive protein, D-dimer, creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase, procalcitonin (PCT), and Interleukin 6 (IL-6). Imaging results covered chest CT and HRCT. Treatment-related data encompassed oxygen inhalation status, oxygen inhalation mode, hormone dosage, and the use of antiviral drugs.

All clinical variables, including demographic characteristics, past medical history, laboratory parameters, imaging findings, and treatment-related data, were collected at hospital admission or within 24 hours of admission as baseline data for prediction model development. Laboratory parameters were obtained from the first blood draw performed after admission. Chest CT scores were assessed based on the initial imaging examination performed upon admission. Oxygen demand and antiviral drug use were recorded based on the patient’s status during the early phase of hospitalization, reflecting the initial severity of illness and treatment strategy. Long COVID outcomes were assessed at least three months after hospital discharge through telephone follow-up or outpatient clinic visits.

Data wrangling

Total CT score [11, 12]

Each lung lobe is scored from 0 to 5, with 0 indicating no involvement, 1 for < 5%, 2 for 5–25%, 3 for 26–50%, 4 for 51–75%, and 5 for > 75% involvement. The overall CT score is the sum of each lobe score (0–25); CT scores are categorized as follows: 0–5 for category 1, 6–10 for category 2, 11–15 for category 3, 16–20 for category 4, and 21–25 for category 5.CT score were scored by two associate chief physicians who read the same CT images. If the results were inconsistent, a third chief physician will make the judgment.If the conclusion of the third chief physician was consistent with that of one of the previous two chief physicians, the conclusion was cited directly. In cases of disagreement between the two primary assessors, a senior radiologist was consulted, and the final score was reached by consensus.For patients who did not undergo standard chest CT, images from HRCT or PET-CT were used for scoring when available.

Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)

We use the CCI comprehensive assessment to evaluate the past medical history, with the range being 0 to 9 [13].

Clinical manifestations of long COVID

Long COVID was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition, as symptoms persisting in COVID-19 patients three months after the onset of symptoms or diagnosis in asymptomatic individuals, lasting for at least two months, and not attributable to other diagnoses. These symptoms significantly affect patients’ daily lives.

Long COVID symptoms can be classified into respiratory system symptoms (shortness of breath, breathing difficulties, chest pain, chest tightness, cough, expectoration, etc.), cardiovascular system symptoms (palpitations, hypotension, syncope, angina pectoris, etc.), neurological symptoms (cognitive impairment, thinking disorders, poor attention, imbalance, headache, etc.), psychological and social manifestations (fear, anxiety, sadness, depression, etc.), gastrointestinal manifestations (constipation, abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, etc.), metabolic manifestations (newly diagnosed diabetes, newly diagnosed hyperthyroidism, newly diagnosed hypothyroidism, newly diagnosed aldosteronism, etc.), musculoskeletal manifestations (joint pain, myalgia, etc.), and other manifestations (persistent fatigue, sweating, weakness, etc.).

Time to definitive diagnosis

For the Long COVID group occurred: time from acute COVID-19 diagnosis to the date when WHO criteria for Long COVID were first met. This is an event time.

For the No long COVID group occurred, we will further divide the study population into two categories:

Patients who remained completely asymptomatic throughout the follow-up period: Based on the existing evidence (that new long-term COVID symptoms rarely occur after 24 months), we defined the endpoint of the 24-month follow-up period as “Time to definitive diagnosis” [14].

Patients who remained completely asymptomatic throughout the follow-up period: Based on the existing evidence (that new long-term COVID symptoms rarely occur after 24 months), we defined the endpoint of the 24-month follow-up period as “Time to definitive diagnosis” [14].

Patients who had symptoms that resolved before meeting the WHO duration criteria:“Time to definitive diagnosis” was defined as the date of symptom resolution.

Patients who had symptoms that resolved before meeting the WHO duration criteria:“Time to definitive diagnosis” was defined as the date of symptom resolution.

These definitions allowed a comparable “Time to definitive diagnosis” for both groups.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 26.0) and R language (version 4.5.2) were utilized for statistical analysis, defining statistical significance as a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Normality tests for continuous variables were performed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Independent sample t-tests and χ2 tests were used for normally distributed variables, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to non-normally distributed variables. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, non-normally distributed continuous variables as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage).Performed missing value imputation using linear interpolation via SPSS (version 26.0), under the missing at random assumption.

Predictor variables were selected using Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator(lasso)regression in the development cohort [15]. The prediction model was then exported as a nomogram. External validation was performed on the validation cohort, evaluating the model’s consistency, discrimination, and clinical usefulness through AUROC, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis [16–18].

A multivariable Lasso regression model was used to construct the predictive model. The independent predictors included CCI (OR = 1.302, 95% CI: 1.037–1.658, p = 0.026), percentage of lymphocytes (OR = 0.962, 95% CI: 0.928–0.996,p = 0.031), oxygen demand (OR = 0.324, 95% CI: 0.167–0.549,p < 0.001), and total CT score (OR = 1.499, 95% CI: 1.058–2.160, p = 0.025) ( Fig. 3A and B).Variable selection was performed using LASSO regression. As shown in Fig. 3A, the coefficients of all features were gradually shrunk toward zero as the penalty parameter λ increased, with coefficients of variables weakly associated with the outcome shrinking to zero. Based on 10-fold cross-validation, the binomial deviance was plotted against log(λ) (Fig. 3B). The optimal λ was determined using the minimum cross-validation error criterion (i.e., the left vertical dashed line corresponding to lambda.min), which identified 4 predictors with non-zero coefficients for subsequent model construction.

Each clinical feature was assigned a specific point value. For instance, a Long COVID patient with a CCI score of 7 received 66 points, a CT score of 3 received 26 points, a lymphocyte percentage of 45 received 25 points, and an oxygen demand and of 2 received 0 points. This resulted in a total score of 117 points for the patient, suggesting an approximate 27% probability of developing Long COVID. This outcome can assist in clinical decision-making plans ( Fig. 3D).

The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristicarea(AUROC)of this development cohort was 0.797, with a 95% confidence interval of (0.725, 0.868) (P < 0.01), as shown in Fig. 4A. The area under the ROC curve of this validation cohort was 0.786, with a 95% confidence interval of (0.755, 0.817) (P < 0.01), as shown in Fig. 4B. This indicates that the model has a good discriminatory power for predicting Long COVID syndrome in both the modeling and validation sets. For practical application, the risk probabilities were truncated at the point farthest from the reference line in the development cohort, with a cutoff value of 69.3%, a sensitivity of 58.8%, and a specificity 89.3%, as shown in Fig. 4A.

The p value of the Hosmer and Lemeshow chi-square statistic was 0.097, and the p value of Spiegelhalter test was 0.126. The Brier score is an indicator used to evaluate the accuracy of binary classification model predictions. It is based on the difference between the predicted probabilities by the model and the actual labels. The closer it is to 0, the more accurate the model’s predictions. If Brier Score > 0.25, some literature considers it worthless. We have now calculated and reported the Brier score to assess the overall performance of the model. The parameters on the calibration side show that the Brier score of the validation set is 0.173, indicating that the model has good calibration and prediction accuracy. The calibration curve of this model indicates that when the estimated probability is lower than 45% or falls within the range of 60% to 90%, the prediction model tends to underestimate the incidence of long COVID symptoms; while when the estimated probability is within the range of 45% to 60% or exceeds 90%, the prediction model tends to underestimate the incidence of long COVID symptoms. Overall, the Calibration curve of the prediction model is close to the standard curve, indicating that the predicted probability of Long COVID syndrome is consistent with the actual probability, as shown in Fig. 4C.

As shown in Fig. 4D. When the predicted probability exceeds 0.1, the decision curve is above the two reference lines, indicating that there is a good net benefit within various threshold probability ranges, indicating that the model performs well in this range and can achieve more net benefits compared to the two extreme requirements. Within the range of 0 to 0.1, the Decision Curve Analysis(DCA) is close to the two ineffective lines, indicating that the model performs poorly in this range. Combined with the probability threshold of 69.3% in Fig. 1A for this prediction model, it shows that this prediction model has clinical utility. The “benefit” in our DCA represents the net benefit of using this diagnostic model to guide the above clinical decisions, compared with two extreme strategies: assuming all patients will develop Long COVID and intervening accordingly, or assuming none will develop Long COVID and providing no intervention. The DCA results demonstrate that within certain threshold probability ranges, our model provides superior net benefit, supporting its clinical utility. Discussion

The chronic effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection, including post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection symptoms and unexplained physical weakness that persists long-term, continue to affect a significant portion of COVID-19 survivors. In this research, we developed a prediction model utilizing clinical data from patients admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University Hospital for COVID-19 treatment, following China’s “Category B and B Management” implementation and the full societal reopening. Lasso regression analysis identified crucial factors associated with Long COVID, culminating in the selection and external validation of four clinical variables: CCI, CT score, oxygen requirement, and lymphocyte percentage, using the clinical data from Panjin Central Hospital Hospital.The outcome (Long COVID) was assessed at a prespecified, uniform minimum time point (≥ 3 months after discharge) for all patients.The time to definitive diagnosis did not differ significantly between groups.

CCI, combining age and comorbidities across all age groups and systemic conditions, was first proposed by Charlson in 1987 [14] to predict long-term mortality and has since been widely adopted for forecasting long-term prognosis and survival. Given its efficacy, simplicity, and applicability, and considering that the occurrence of Long COVID is also part of COVID-19’s prognosis study, CCI was introduced in this study. Statistical findings reveal that a higher CCI correlates with an increased likelihood of Long COVID.

While most published studies have focused solely on either age or comorbidities, we identified fourteen study sets that considered age, with twelve confirming the predictive value of age. This research encompasses data from China, Italy, the United States, the United Kingdom, and other countries [6, 7, 19–22], covering most designated medical centers for treating the novel coronavirus.

Regarding comorbidities, determining whether Long COVID-19 symptoms originate from pre-existing conditions, new symptoms caused by COVID-19, or a combination of both is challenging. Consequently, Long COVID’s definition emphasizes duration over distinguishing between these factors. A 2022 JAMA article [20] highlighted that an increased number of comorbidities elevates the risk of Long COVID. Specifically, a study from Italy noted the importance of an allergic constitution among various comorbidities. Another study in the BMJ from the United States pointed out that individuals with pre-existing interstitial lung disease are more likely to develop severe COVID-19.

The ongoing impact of coronavirus on T cell function leads to immune escape, allowing the virus to persist at low concentrations for extended periods. Data from past coronavirus outbreaks, such as Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome(SARS) and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome(MERS) [23, 24], show that some patients continue to suffer from lung damage after the infection has resolved. The novel coronavirus, characterized by its low virulence and viral load, does not typically cause systemic symptoms but can persist in certain organs, leading to specific clinical symptoms. A recent Nature study [25] demonstrated that most patients struggle to completely eliminate the virus during the acute phase, with about 0.1% − 0.5% of patients harboring the virus for at least 60 days. The virus’s continued presence weakens the immune system’s ability to clear it, particularly in individuals with a history of medical conditions, thus increasing the likelihood of long-term effects from the novel coronavirus.CCI inherently captures disease severity through its incorporation of comorbidities and age, both of which are associated with worse acute-phase outcomes.

The study by Falsetti directly investigated the relationship between the CCI and long COVID [26], finding that a CCI score ≥ 1 was associated with an approximately threefold increased risk of long COVID, while a CCI score ≥ 4 was associated with a sixfold increased risk. Honchar further confirmed that patients with persistent long COVID lasting 12 months had significantly higher CCI scores [27]. A large-scale cohort study from Denmark, which included 1,034,093 Danish patients diagnosed between March 1, 2020, and February 28, 2022, focused on elucidating the association between CCI and long COVID [28]. This study first demonstrated that higher CCI scores were associated with an increased risk of long COVID. Subsequently, age-stratified analyses were conducted, and this trend was observed in both the adult group aged 40–59 years and the older adult group aged ≥ 60 years. Finally, the study further decomposed the CCI into individual underlying comorbidities and found that chronic pulmonary disease and rheumatic disease were independent risk factors for long COVID across both age groups. Another important factor was diabetes with chronic complications; however, this factor did not perform well in the final multivariable analysis. The conclusions of the present study are broadly consistent with those of the Danish nationwide cohort study. The difference lies in the fact that the present study focused more on predicting the occurrence of long COVID and translated the aforementioned complex influencing factors into a readily applicable nomogram. However, whether this approach is suitable for cross-population generalization warrants further exploration on a larger scale and in more in-depth clinical investigations in the future.

In China, mainstream studies have often neglected to simultaneously consider both age and comorbidity. A nomogram developed by the National Infectious Disease Center team at Fudan University in Shanghai, China, included five risk factors [19]. Being over 75 years of age, having chronic kidney disease, and suffering from chronic lung disease were identified as the most significant risk factors. This highlights the particular importance of age and comorbidities in predicting the risk of severe COVID-19 cases in the Chinese population.

The total CT score, initially utilized to assess the prognosis of the interstitial pneumonia index [29], has been adopted for its quantitative analysis, high accuracy, and the ability of CT scans to provide rapid diagnostics. Many research teams have adapted and revised it [30] as a tool for real-time assessment of lung involvement severity in COVID-19 cases. Compared to other indicators of disease progression, the vast majority of research teams [31] recognize the total CT score as the most accurate index reflecting disease progression, with more severe COVID-19 cases being more likely to develop into Long COVID [21].

This study highlights the total CT score as the optimal indicator of disease progression. For the first time, the total CT score was applied to COVID-19 and included in the final prediction model through Lasso regression analysis. In the longitudinal follow-up of COVID-19, persistent imaging changes were often observed, closely correlating with the peak total CT score during the acute phase[32]. Current research primarily describes these persistent imaging changes, with pulmonary fibrosis-like changes being the most frequently observed. A follow-up study of the initial set of COVID-19 patients from Wuhan Jinyintan Hospital [32] statistically identified a total CT score exceeding 18 during the acute phase of COVID-19 as an independent risk factor for developing pulmonary fibrosis-like changes during follow-up. Pulmonary fibrosis is an irreversible structural change that reflects the long-term impact of COVID-19 on tissues and organs. These persistent imaging changes correlate with long-standing clinical symptoms, and Long COVID can be considered an umbrella term encompassing these clinical manifestations.The total CT score’s predictive power reflects its direct quantification of lung damage severity, a key driver of post-acute complications like pulmonary fibrosisl [32].

In 2022, Erkan et al. conducted a retrospective study involving 157 patients and found that the acute-phase CT severity score was an independent predictor of long COVID [33] (OR = 1.059, 95% CI: 1.002–1.119). This finding is consistent with the results of our study. Compared with their study, our study included a larger patient population, integrated other predictive factors on this basis, and developed a more readily applicable nomogram. Furthermore, external validation enhanced the robustness of our conclusions, providing clinicians with a more actionable tool for judgment.Another study explored the relationship between the total CT score and post-COVID neuroimaging findings, demonstrating that neuroimaging abnormalities in patients with long COVID were directly associated with clinical manifestations such as cognitive impairment, chronic insomnia, fatigue, and mood disorders [34]. Unfortunately, no significant association was found between the CT score and the occurrence of neuroimaging findings. This discrepancy suggests that the sequelae of long COVID may involve different pathological mechanisms across various organ systems. Therefore, the relationship between the total CT score and clinical manifestations in different systems warrants further investigation in the future.

According to the novel Coronavirus infection diagnosis and treatment plan (10th version), severe and critical patients are more focused on supportive treatments compared to those with ordinary and mild cases. Respiratory system support therapy, specifically oxygen therapy, is a primary treatment strategy. In a multicenter study including 2,433 cases [21], 71.6% of hospitalized patients received oxygen therapy, and 0.9% underwent mechanical ventilation. These studies have established a connection between oxygen therapy, mechanical ventilation, and the persistence of fatigue, which is one of the most common Long COVID symptoms [6]. Our research extended to Long COVID among hospitalized patients with acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, indicating that symptomatic oxygen support is provided based on the severity of the patient’s condition. Our findings suggest that a lower oxygen requirement increases the likelihood of developing Long COVID. This conclusion may be attributed to two factors: First, in conditions of insufficient blood oxygen saturation, oxygen is preferentially supplied to vital organs like the brain, heart, and lungs. Other organs may remain hypoxic, leading to long-term tissue decompensation, chronic cellular damage, and the persistence of symptoms and discomfort after discharge, which are recognized as clinical manifestations of Long COVID. Second, for patients with severe conditions, timely high-flow oxygen support is often provided. A significant proportion of these patients might die before discharge or during follow-up, excluding them from this study and introducing a bias. Therefore, the incidence of Long COVID appears to be lower among recipients of high-flow oxygen therapy. On the other hand, prolonged oxygen therapy can cause lung damage. Several post-COVID follow-up studies have observed [32] interstitial lung lesions in CT scans of patients with Long COVID. It remains unclear whether these damages are attributable to COVID-19 itself or to the side effects of oxygen therapy received during hospitalization. This ambiguity highlights the need for further research to understand the relationship between oxygen therapy and Long COVID fully. The association between oxygen requirement and Long COVID essentially reflects a mismatch between the oxygen demand of the body and the administration of oxygen therapy, which is highly subjective. This study, based on clinical data from two medical centers, has its limitations, and the relationship between oxygen therapy and Long COVID warrants additional investigation.

Our findings are consistent with global evidence that disease severity is the strongest predictor of prolonged COVID-19. In a large prospective cohort study, severity of illness during the acute phase (e.g., hospitalization, oxygen support, and radiographic pulmonary involvement) was most significantly associated with persistent lung dysfunction and respiratory symptoms at follow-up [35]. In our cohort, total CT score and oxygen demand, proxies of disease severity, were both independent predictors, further validating this relationship. Of note, one study showed that patients with a CT score > 18 or those requiring supplemental oxygen had 3.2-fold higher risk for prolonged COVID-19 than patients with milder disease [36, 37]. These results may be related to the mechanism that severe acute COVID-19 triggers prolonged inflammatory and fibrotic pathways, leading to long-term sequelae [20, 21, 32].

Blood conventional lymphocyte percentage is a quick indicator used to identify the source of infection, with lymphocyte percentages < 20% signaling viral infection. Lower lymphocyte percentages are associated with a higher likelihood of viral infection. Previous research has indicated that adults infected with COVID-19 exhibit lymphopenia, increased platelet counts, and elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, with a decrease in lymphocyte count in severe cases indicating a more severe disease and a higher risk of mortality [38]. In this study, lymphocyte percentage was included as an indicator of COVID-19 severity. This metric, derived from a routine blood test available to all patients, holds significant potential for widespread application.

The value of vaccination status in predicting long COVID has garnered increasing attention in predictive modeling. In this study, vaccination information was complete in both the development and validation cohorts, and the overall vaccination rate was relatively high. Although some studies have indicated that vaccination can reduce the risk of long COVID, vaccination status did not enter the final model as a strong independent predictor in our study. This result may be attributable to several factors. First, the high proportion of vaccinated patients in this study may have resulted in limited between-group variability. Second, the protective effect of vaccination against long COVID may be achieved indirectly by mitigating the severity of the acute illness, rather than by directly influencing the occurrence of long COVID. Therefore, when constructing predictive models for long COVID, the contribution of vaccination status may be partially superseded by variables such as total CT score and CCI. Future research should further explore the independent predictive value of vaccination for long COVID in populations with greater variability in vaccination coverage.

In this study, no significant predictive value of D-dimer or C-reactive protein (CRP) levels for the occurrence of long COVID was identified. Inconsistent findings have been reported in the existing literature: some studies have shown that elevated D-dimer levels during the acute phase can predict the risk of long COVID [39], while others have not confirmed its independent predictive role [40]. This heterogeneity may be attributable to differences in the timing of biomarker measurement, population characteristics, definitions of long COVID, and sample sizes. In the present study, the timing of biomarker assessment varied considerably, making it difficult to reflect the impact of acute-phase status on long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the pathogenesis of long COVID involves interactions across multiple systems, including immune, coagulation, and neurological pathways. Future research should focus on multi-biomarker panels and mechanism-based subtyping, while strictly standardizing the timing of measurements, to improve the accuracy and clinical applicability of predictive models.

Our nomogram provides a practical tool for identifying high-risk patients with prolonged COVID-19, enabling tailored follow-up strategies. The following are examples or scenarios where this nomogram may influence clinical decisions.Outpatient triage and follow-up intensity: A 65-year-old patient with COVID-19 (CCI 2, lymphocytes 12%, high-flow oxygen, CT score 18) receives a total score of 139 points on our nomogram, corresponding to a > 40% predicted probability of Long COVID. Based on this high-risk stratification, clinicians can schedule more frequent follow-up visits and proactive rehabilitation referrals.Resource allocation: In resource-limited settings, identifying high-risk patients enables efficient resource allocation. Those with predicted probability < 30% may receive standard follow-up, while those > 50% are prioritized for intensive monitoring and early intervention.

Available studies demonstrate the inefficiency of a one-size-fits-all follow-up approach, as only 15 to 20% of recovered patients require close monitoring. In contrast, risk stratification using markers such as CT score, CCI, and oxygen demand can optimize resource allocation. Prioritizing pulmonary rehabilitation and symptom management for the population predicted to develop SARS-CoV-2 can reduce unnecessary visits for low-risk patients, medical costs and patient burden. This is consistent with new guidelines advocating personalized post-COVID-19 care pathways [21, 32]. A large prospective study suggested that while most patients do not require intensive follow-up after COVID-19, certain subgroups may still benefit from tailored monitoring [41]. Healthcare systems could allocate targeted interventions—such as serial imaging or specialist referral—based on risk stratification, while minimizing overtreatment. The follow-up plan was based on the nomogram we developed.

This research utilized data from the first major outbreak to develop a prediction model for Long COVID syndrome in patients with severe COVID-19, offering a scientific basis for early identification with clinical relevance. Nonetheless, the study is subject to limitations: (1) These data are derived from only two centers, which may limit the general applicability of our research results. Both of these centers are large tertiary hospitals and may not fully represent long COVID patients in primary care settings or across different medical systems. Future studies need to be conducted in a multi-center environment, in different geographical regions, and under different medical levels, especially multi-center prospective studies to further confirm the general applicability of our model.(2) Due to the retrospective nature of the study, some patients were excluded. Although the sensitivity analysis showed that there was no significant difference in most baseline characteristics between the included and excluded populations, suggesting that the data were randomly missing. However, this still cannot fundamentally solve the problem of selection bias. The retrospective analysis is limited by the completeness of data recording, the consistency of measurements, potential confounding factors, and the non-standardized data collection, which may introduce information bias, measurement bias, and confounding bias, thereby affecting the reliability of causal inference.(3)It lacks an assessment of lung involvement in Long COVID severity, including further subgroup analysis predictions. (3) It does not include data from intensive care units, particularly from mechanically ventilated patients. (4) There is a lack of data on the duration of symptoms and their analysis. (5) CCI lacks consideration of some autoimmune diseases or mental health problems.(6) The main result of this study is the occurrence of “long-term COVID”, which is a binary endpoint (present or absent). Therefore, given the nature of the outcome variable, binary logistic regression is the most direct and appropriate modeling method. Currently, there are no time-dependent follow-up data. Therefore, binary logistic regression is the most direct and appropriate modeling method. Time-event models or other non-binary models may be more suitable for future studies with longitudinal follow-up data.

Conclusion

The prediction model developed in this study identifies CCI, CT score, oxygen requirement, and lymphocyte percentage as significant factors linked to Long COVID. These could serve as risk factors for the early detection and identification of Long COVID in clinical settings.
