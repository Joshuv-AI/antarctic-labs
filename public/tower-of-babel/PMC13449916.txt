Multimodality imaging features of primary mammary angiosarcoma: a case report with delayed osseous metastases
Authors: Bano Alsaleh, Ahmed Alanzi, Dawood Alatefi, Malik Alkabazi, Hamza Hamad Aldossary, Ruqaiyah Naji Al Hakeem, Mai Alsaeed, Ahmed Alsoorti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary angiosarcoma of the breast is an uncommon and aggressive vascular malignancy that typically affects young women and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We report the case of a 26-year-old nulliparous woman who presented with progressive left breast swelling over approximately 18 months. Clinical examination revealed diffuse tender swelling without skin discoloration, nipple changes, or palpable lymphadenopathy. Ultrasonography demonstrated a complex cystic mass with thick septations and increased vascularity, while mammography showed global asymmetry of the left breast without suspicious calcifications. Breast MRI revealed a large 8.4 cm heterogeneously enhancing mass in the upper outer quadrant with possible focal skin involvement, but no chest wall invasion or nodal disease. Initial staging with 18F-FDG PET/CT showed no distant metastases. The patient underwent left nipple-sparing mastectomy, and histopathological examination confirmed intermediate-grade primary mammary angiosarcoma, FNCLCC grade 2. Subsequent re-excision achieved clear surgical margins. She received adjuvant ifosfamide–epirubicin chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Initial follow-up imaging showed no residual or recurrent disease. However, 14 months after completion of treatment, follow-up 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated new FDG-avid lytic lesions in the sacral and iliac bones, and Breast MRI revealed a small enhancing sternal lesion, consistent with delayed osseous metastatic disease. The patient was treated with palliative radiotherapy and systemic therapy. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of primary breast angiosarcoma, its potential for delayed hematogenous osseous spread, and the value of multimodality imaging in diagnosis, staging, treatment planning, and surveillance.

Introduction

Angiosarcoma of the breast is an exceedingly rare, highly malignant vascular tumor, accounting for approximately 0.04%-0.05% of all breast malignancies [1]. It originates from malignant endothelial cells lining vascular channels [2]. Breast angiosarcoma may occur as a primary tumor arising de novo within the breast parenchyma or as a secondary tumor developing in previously irradiated skin or chest wall after treatment for breast cancer [1]. These 2 entities differ clinically. Primary breast angiosarcoma typically affects younger women, with a median age of 30-40 years, whereas secondary breast angiosarcoma usually occurs in older patients several years after radiotherapy [3,4].

Primary breast angiosarcoma commonly presents as a rapidly enlarging breast mass and may be associated with diffuse breast swelling or bluish skin discoloration. In contrast, secondary breast angiosarcoma more often manifests as violaceous skin nodules, plaques, or skin thickening in a previously treated breast [1]. Lymphatic spread is uncommon, reflecting the hematogenous dissemination pattern of this tumor [5].

Because the clinical and imaging findings are nonspecific, misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis may occur. Breast swelling may be attributed to benign inflammatory or cystic processes, and tumor-related skin discoloration may be mistaken for bruising [2]. Primary breast angiosarcoma has an aggressive clinical course with a high risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis. Common metastatic sites include the lungs and liver, although osseous metastases have also been reported [6,7]. The overall prognosis remains poor, with reported 5-year survival rates ranging from 28% to 54% [8].

Case presentation

A 26-year-old married nulliparous woman with no significant medical or surgical history presented with progressive left breast swelling over approximately 1 and a half years. The swelling had gradually increased in size and was associated with mild pain. She denied nipple discharge, nipple retraction, skin discoloration, fever, trauma, or constitutional symptoms. There was no family history of breast malignancy and no known risk factors, including prior chest irradiation, chronic lymphedema, or previous breast surgery.

Clinical examination demonstrated diffuse tender swelling involving nearly the entire left breast, measuring approximately 8 cm in diameter. The overlying skin appeared intact without dimpling or nipple retraction. No palpable axillary, supraclavicular, or infraclavicular lymphadenopathy was identified. Examination of the contralateral breast and axilla was unremarkable.

The patient subsequently underwent left nipple-sparing mastectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed primary mammary angiosarcoma, intermediate grade, FNCLCC grade 2, pathologic stage pT2 pNx pMx. Microscopically, the tumor demonstrated infiltrative vasoformative proliferation composed of variably sized and partly anastomosing vascular channels, with heterogeneous growth patterns ranging from open vascular lumina and slit-like spaces to focal solid endothelial proliferation. The neoplastic vascular channels dissected through mammary stroma and adipose tissue and were associated with stromal hemorrhage and blood lakes. Tumor cells showed mild-to-moderate endothelial atypia with focal endothelial tufting, rare mitotic figures, 1-3/10 HPF, and minimal spindle-cell and solid areas. A microscopic focus of necrosis was present but was insufficient for high-grade classification. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated diffuse CD31 and CD34 positivity with multifocal weak C-Myc expression. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 10%-15%. No dermal invasion or lymphovascular invasion was identified. The posterior surgical margin was involved, and the superior margin was extremely close, 0.06 mm. Subsequent re-excision demonstrated negative superior and deep margins without residual tumor.

Following multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, the patient received adjuvant chemotherapy with ifosfamide and epirubicin for 3 cycles, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, 60 Gy in 30 fractions. One additional chemotherapy cycle was administered after completion of radiotherapy.

Initial follow-up CT and 18F-FDG PET/CT examinations demonstrated postoperative and postradiation changes without evidence of residual or recurrent disease.

However, 14 months later, follow-up 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated newly developed FDG-avid lytic lesions involving the left sacral ala, SUVmax 6.5, and posterior left iliac bone, SUVmax 3.2, highly suspicious for osseous metastatic disease (Fig. 7). No locoregional recurrence or visceral metastatic disease was identified. No confirmatory biopsy of the osseous lesions was performed. However, the diagnosis was considered radiologically and clinically consistent with metastases based on the new FDG-avid lytic morphology, MRI correlation, multifocal osseous distribution, and the patient’s known history of angiosarcoma. Discussion

Rarity and clinical presentation

Primary breast angiosarcoma is a rare and aggressive vascular malignancy, accounting for only approximately 0.04%-0.05% of all breast malignancies [1]. It typically affects younger women and arises de novo within the breast parenchyma, in contrast to secondary breast angiosarcoma, which usually occurs after prior breast irradiation or chronic lymphedema [1,3,4]. In the present case, the patient had no recognized risk factors, including prior chest irradiation, chronic lymphedema, previous breast surgery, or family history of breast malignancy.

The clinical presentation is often nonspecific and may include progressive breast enlargement, pain, palpable mass, or skin discoloration [1,2]. These nonspecific features may delay diagnosis, as breast swelling can be mistaken for benign inflammatory, cystic, or traumatic conditions [2]. This diagnostic difficulty is reflected in our case, where the patient presented with progressive breast swelling over approximately 18 months before definitive diagnosis.

Role of multimodality breast imaging

Imaging findings of primary breast angiosarcoma are variable and not pathognomonic. Mammography may show global asymmetry, a noncalcified mass, architectural distortion, or may be inconclusive, particularly in young women with dense breast tissue [1,9,10]. Ultrasound commonly demonstrates a heterogeneous solid or complex mass, often with increased internal vascularity on Doppler imaging, reflecting the vascular nature of the tumor [[10], [11], [26]].

Breast MRI is particularly important for evaluating tumor extent, internal hemorrhage, enhancement pattern, skin involvement, pectoralis muscle involvement, and chest wall invasion [[9], [12], [13], [25]]. Typical MRI findings include high T2 signal intensity, heterogeneous enhancement, and possible T1 hyperintense foci related to intratumoral hemorrhage [[9], [11], [12], [25]]. In our case, Breast MRI helped define the large upper outer quadrant mass, demonstrated hemorrhagic foci and restricted diffusion, and showed no definite pectoralis muscle or chest wall invasion, supporting surgical planning. Nevertheless, primary breast angiosarcoma may occasionally be multifocal, with small discontinuous lesions that remain occult on preoperative imaging; this possibility should be considered when assessing tumor extent and planning the surgical approach [24].

Pathologic correlation and tumor classification

Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation, as imaging alone cannot reliably distinguish angiosarcoma from other malignant or benign vascular breast lesions [1,2]. Histologically, angiosarcoma is characterized by infiltrative vasoformative proliferation with irregular anastomosing vascular channels lined by atypical endothelial cells [14,15]. Higher-grade tumors may show increased cellularity, solid growth, marked atypia, mitotic activity, and necrosis [14,15].

In this case, the diagnosis was supported by infiltrative anastomosing vascular channels, slit-like spaces, stromal hemorrhage, blood lakes, mild-to-moderate endothelial atypia, focal endothelial tufting, and focal necrosis. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated diffuse CD31 and CD34 positivity, supporting endothelial differentiation, while the Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 10%-15%. The tumor was classified as intermediate grade, FNCLCC grade 2, with pathologic stage pT2 pNx pMx. This corrected terminology avoids the inaccurate use of “WHO TNM grading” and clearly separates tumor grade from TNM staging.

Treatment and prognostic considerations

Because primary breast angiosarcoma is rare, standardized treatment guidelines remain limited. Complete surgical excision with negative margins is considered the mainstay of treatment [[16], [17], [18]]. Axillary lymph node involvement is uncommon, consistent with the tumor’s predominantly hematogenous pattern of spread [5,19]. The role of adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy remains individualized and is usually determined by multidisciplinary assessment of tumor size, grade, margin status, and recurrence risk [1,16,18].

In the present case, the patient underwent nipple-sparing mastectomy followed by margin re-excision, which achieved negative superior and deep margins. Given the aggressive tumor biology and initial margin involvement, she subsequently received adjuvant ifosfamide–epirubicin chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

Delayed osseous metastases and musculoskeletal imaging relevance

Primary breast angiosarcoma most commonly metastasizes hematogenously, particularly to the lungs and liver, but bone metastases have also been described [6,7,[20], [21], [22]]. In a population-based analysis of metastatic angiosarcoma, osseous involvement was reported in a substantial proportion of metastatic cases [7]. Published case reports have described delayed skeletal metastases involving the pelvis, spine, sternum, and other osseous sites after treatment of primary breast angiosarcoma [[20], [21], [22]].

In our case, follow-up 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated newly developed FDG-avid lytic lesions in the left sacral ala and posterior left iliac bone, while Breast MRI showed a small enhancing sternal lesion. These lesions were not biopsy-proven; therefore, they are best described as radiologically suspicious for or consistent with osseous metastases rather than definitively proven metastases. The interpretation was supported by their interval development, FDG avidity, lytic morphology, multifocal osseous distribution, MRI correlation, and the patient’s known history of angiosarcoma. This point directly supports the relevance of the case not only to breast imaging but also to cross-sectional and musculoskeletal imaging.

Limitations of 18F-FDG PET/CT and need for multimodality surveillance

18F-FDG PET/CT is useful for whole-body staging and surveillance in angiosarcoma because it can detect metabolically active local recurrence and distant disease [23]. However, FDG avidity can be variable, and small marrow-based or early osseous metastases may be missed or underestimated on PET/CT [21]. Ariga et al. [21] reported angiosarcoma bone metastases that were evident on MRI but false-negative on 18F-FDG PET/CT, highlighting the limitation of relying on PET/CT alone for skeletal assessment.

Therefore, persistent symptoms, equivocal PET/CT findings, or high-risk clinical features should prompt targeted MRI or CT correlation [21]. The delayed osseous lesions in the present case emphasize the importance of long-term multimodality surveillance, even when initial staging and early follow-up imaging show no distant metastasis. This approach may improve detection of clinically significant metastatic disease and guide timely palliative or systemic treatment.

Summary of teaching point

This case highlights the diagnostic difficulty of primary breast angiosarcoma, the value of Breast MRI for local tumor characterization, and the importance of 18F-FDG PET/CT and cross-sectional imaging for systemic surveillance. It also demonstrates that delayed osseous metastases may occur despite initially negative staging and early disease-free follow-up imaging. A concise multimodality approach is therefore essential for diagnosis, treatment planning, and long-term follow-up of this rare aggressive tumor.
