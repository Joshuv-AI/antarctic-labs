Endoscopic endonasal resection of ectopic suprasellar pituitary neuroendocrine tumors: comparison of intra-stalk and extra-stalk tumors. Illustrative cases
Authors: Lan Yang, Xiaodi Han, Lin Zong, Zhizhong Jin, Zixun Wang, Sheng Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEctopic suprasellar pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (Pit-NETs) are exceptionally rare lesions that differ from intrasellar Pit-NETs in both imaging appearance and surgical management. Ectopic pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (Pit-NETs) develop outside the sella turcica and primarily demonstrate extrasellar growth.1 The first case of an ectopic Pit-NET was reported by Jakob Erdheim in 1909, and ectopic Pit-NETs remain rare.2 Reported cases indicate that typical locations for ectopic Pit-NETs include the sphenoid sinus, clivus, suprasellar region, nasopharynx, and cavernous sinus, with the suprasellar region being one of the most common ectopic sites.3 Ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs often lack distinctive imaging features, which complicates their differentiation from other suprasellar space-occupying lesions. This challenge is particularly pronounced with nonfunctioning ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs, which are especially difficult to diagnose because their endocrine test results typically appear normal, further hindering diagnosis. In this report, we categorize ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs into two types: the intra-stalk type, which originates from and grows within the pituitary stalk, and the extra-stalk type, which originates from the stalk but predominantly grows outside of it. The 2 cases reported by Tsukada et al.4 are the extra-stalk type. Based on our comprehensive series, we have determined that maintaining the integrity of the pituitary stalk is notably more challenging in cases of intra-stalk–type ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs compared to both extra-stalk–type ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs and conventional intrasellar Pit-NETs.

Herein, we present 2 cases of intra-stalk–type ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs alongside 2 cases of extra-stalk–type ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs. We detail their preoperative diagnostic processes, intraoperative findings, postoperative outcomes, and subsequent follow-up.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 41-year-old woman presented with a 1-month history of headaches accompanied by bilateral visual disturbances that developed 2 weeks prior to admission. Preoperative endocrine evaluations indicated that all hormone levels were within normal limits. CT and MRI revealed a mass-like lesion in the suprasellar region, exhibiting marked enhancement following contrast administration (Fig. 1 A–F). This lesion compressed the optic chiasm superiorly and extended into the third ventricle. Notably, the pituitary gland appeared normal in size and signal intensity, with no evidence of compression. The pituitary stalk could not be clearly identified on MRI. Establishing a definitive diagnosis based solely on preoperative imaging studies and laboratory findings posed a challenge, and the possibility of a retrochiasmatic craniopharyngioma could not be excluded.

B and C: Preoperative T1-weighted (T1W) (B) and T2-weighted (T2W) (C) MR images demonstrating the suprasellar tumor extending upward to compress the optic chiasm. The tumor invaded the third ventricle, and the pituitary stalk was poorly visualized. D–F: Preoperative contrast-enhanced T1W MR images showing the suprasellar tumor. Following the removal of the necessary bone, an incision was made in the dura of the tuberculum sellae and then expanded. During arachnoid dissection, an anomalous pituitary stalk was immediately identified, raising suspicion of an intra-stalk tumor. A longitudinal incision along the stalk revealed a grayish, mass-forming tumor that originated within the stalk and extended toward the third ventricle. The lesion was carefully resected in a piecemeal fashion. However, due to severe compression and resultant attenuation, the stalk was rendered exceedingly tenuous, complicating efforts to preserve its integrity (Fig. 1J and K). A vascular pedicle nasoseptal flap, a fascia lata graft, and autologous fat were utilized for skull base reconstruction to minimize the risk of postoperative CSF leakage.5 Details of the procedure are provided in Video 1. Histopathological examination confirmed a sparsely granulated corticotroph adenoma, with immunohistochemical analysis indicating positivity for CK8/18, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and P53, along with a Ki-67 index of 1% (Fig. 1I). Following the surgery, the patient experienced diabetes insipidus and pituitary insufficiency, for which standard hormone replacement therapy was administered. Over the 2-year follow-up period, the patient reported no new complaints, and no evidence of tumor recurrence was observed during this time (Fig. 1G and H).

Case 2

A 64-year-old female patient presented with a 2-month history of headaches, dizziness, and visual disturbances prior to admission. CT and MRI revealed a boot-shaped suprasellar mass extending upward and to the right, invading the third ventricle (Fig. 2A–F). The pituitary stalk was poorly visualized, and the optic chiasm was compressed. Preoperative endocrine tests were normal. We were hesitant to make a definitive preoperative diagnosis because we could not exclude a retrochiasmatic craniopharyngioma, which can exhibit similar imaging features and lesion locations.

B and C:Preoperative T1W (B) and T2W (C) MR images revealing a suprasellar mass compressing the optic chiasm, with poor visualization of the pituitary stalk. D–F: Preoperative contrast-enhanced T1W images showing the suprasellar tumor with heterogeneous enhancement. G and H: Postoperative MR images confirming the reconstruction of the skull base with no evidence of residual tumor. J:Following arachnoid mater opening, abnormally thickened pituitary stalk and a pituitary gland were visualized. K:Incision of the stalk showed an internal grayish-white tumor compressing the stalk, with extension of the tumor into the third ventricle.

After drilling away the necessary bone, an extended dural incision was made over the tuberculum sellae. Following arachnoid dissection, an abnormally thickened pituitary stalk and a normal pituitary gland were visualized. Based on the imaging findings, we hypothesized that the tumor might be located within the pituitary stalk. A longitudinal incision along the pituitary stalk revealed an intrinsic, grayish, mass-forming tumor. This lesion originated and expanded within the stalk, extending into the third ventricle and compressing adjacent tissues (Fig. 2J and K). After piecemeal resection of the tumor, we noted that the stalk was significantly thinned due to tumor compression, making preservation of its structural integrity challenging. The skull base was then reconstructed using a vascular septal mucosal flap, a fascia lata graft, and autologous fat.5 Details of the procedure are provided in Video 2.

Histopathological analysis revealed a sparsely granulated corticotroph adenoma with focal prolactin (PRL) and growth hormone (GH) expression. Immunohistochemistry results were positive for CK8/18, ACTH, T-PIT, PRL (focal), PIT-1 (focal), estrogen receptor (ER) (focal), and P53, with a Ki-67 index of 5% (Fig. 2I). Postoperatively, the patient developed diabetes insipidus and hypopituitarism, which were managed with standard hormone replacement therapy. At the 9-month follow-up, the patient remains free of new complaints or signs of recurrence (Fig. 2G and H).

Case 3

A 55-year-old male presented with a 20-day history of headache and dizziness prior to admission. The preoperative endocrine evaluation yielded unremarkable results. Imaging studies revealed a suprasellar mass that caused superior displacement of the optic chiasm and a rightward deviation of the pituitary stalk (Fig. 3A–D). The differential diagnoses based on radiological findings included tuberculum sellae meningioma and Pit-NET.

Case 3. A–D:Preoperative contrast-enhanced T1W images demonstrating a mass predominantly located in the suprasellar region, with extension toward the anterior cranial fossa. The lesion exhibited heterogeneous enhancement on contrast-enhanced scans. The pituitary stalk was identified in the preoperative images. The pituitary stalk was displaced to the left, and the optic nerves were compressed. E:Postoperative follow-up MR image showing a clearly defined pituitary stalk with no evidence of residual tumor. F: High-power H&E staining demonstrating typical Pit-NET histopathological features. G: The tumor was largely visualized in the suprasellar region. H: The tumor originating from the pituitary stalk and the suprasellar tumor were resected to relieve compression on the optic nerves. Following tumor removal, the integrity of the pituitary stalk was preserved.

During the surgical procedure, the sellar bone was drilled to gain access, followed by an incision in the dura that was extended toward the tuberculum sellae. After performing an arachnoid dissection, the suprasellar tumor became visible. Intraoperative observations confirmed that the tumor originated from the pituitary stalk. The tumor was grayish-white, soft, and predominantly located in the suprasellar region (Fig. 3G and H). It was resected in a piecemeal fashion using a suction device, while careful measures were taken to preserve the optic nerves, pituitary stalk, surrounding brain tissue, and adjacent arteries. Complete hemostasis was achieved before initiating skull base reconstruction. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of a Pit-NET, with immunohistochemical findings indicating it was a gonadotroph adenoma. The immunohistochemistry results were positive for CK (+), synaptophysin (+), SF-1 (+), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (+) and negative for PIT-1 (−), ER (−), and p53 (−), with a Ki-67 labeling index of 5% (Fig. 3F). The patient did not exhibit any symptoms of hypopituitarism or diabetes insipidus. During a 2-year follow-up, there was no indication of tumor recurrence (Fig. 3E).

Case 4

A 65-year-old female patient presented with a 3-year history of visual disturbances and frontal headaches. In the previous 2 months, her symptoms had worsened, and she also experienced dizziness and nausea. Preoperative imaging studies revealed a suprasellar lesion located superior and to the left of the pituitary gland, which was compressing the pituitary stalk and the left optic nerve (Fig. 4A–D). The preoperative differential diagnosis included Pit-NET and craniopharyngioma.

The pituitary stalk was displaced to the right, and the left optic nerve was compressed with partial encasement of the left internal carotid artery. After the necessary bone was removed, the dura was incised, and the opening was extended toward the tuberculum sellae. Following arachnoid dissection, a suprasellar tumor was visualized. This tumor appeared grayish-red and soft, predominantly located on the left side, near the left internal carotid artery (Fig. 4G and H). Intraoperative findings confirmed that the tumor originated from the pituitary stalk. It was resected in a piecemeal fashion, while ensuring that the optic nerves were well preserved and the integrity of the pituitary stalk was maintained. The skull base was reconstructed using a vascular septal mucosal flap, along with a fascia lata graft and autologous fat.5 Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of a Pit-NET, while immunohistochemical findings further supported the diagnosis of a gonadotroph adenoma. Immunohistochemical analysis was positive for synaptophysin (+), SF-1 (+), GATA-3 (+), and ER (+) and negative for CK (−), LH (−), and PIT-1 (−), with a Ki-67 labeling index of 2% (Fig. 4F). The patient exhibited no postoperative signs of hypopituitarism or diabetes insipidus. Over a 3-year follow-up period, there was no evidence of tumor recurrence (Fig. 4E).

Discussion

Observations

According to Zhu et al., ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs are classified into three distinct types (I, II, and III) based on whether they arise from the anterior pituitary gland with suprasellar extension, the pituitary stalk, or Rathke’s pouch and the suprasellar basal region, respectively.6,7 In all 4 cases reported, intraoperative findings confirmed that the tumors originated at the pituitary stalk. Consequently, these cases were categorized as type II. Based on a review of 18 previously reported cases of ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs along with the 4 cases presented here, 22 cases in total (Table 1),1,4,6–18 we observed that type II ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs can be subdivided into those originating from and growing primarily within the pituitary stalk and those originating from the pituitary stalk but growing predominantly outside it. We propose classifying the former as intra-stalk–type and the latter as extra-stalk–type ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs. Cases 1 and 2 are classified as intra-stalk type, while cases 3 and 4 are categorized as extra-stalk type.

In a cohort of 22 cases, there were 9 males (40.9%) and 13 females (59.1%), resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.44. The mean age at diagnosis was 43 years, with a median age of 43.5 years. Clinically, nonfunctioning Pit-NETs were the most common (10 cases, 45.5%), followed by ACTH-secreting (22.7%), PRL-secreting (13.6%), TSH-secreting (9.1%), and GH-secreting (4.5%) Pit-NETs. Additionally, preoperative serum hormone testing was not performed in 1 case. Among the 22 patients, preoperative symptoms included visual disturbances in 12 patients (54.5%), headaches in 8 (36.4%), Cushing’s syndrome in 6 (27.3%), hyperprolactinemia in 1 (4.5%), hyperthyroidism in 1 (4.5%), and dizziness in 1 (4.5%). Two patients (9.1%) were asymptomatic. Based on hormonal assays, histopathological findings, and immunohistochemical staining, 14 cases (63.6%) were monohormonal, with ACTH-secreting adenomas being the most common (6 cases, 27.3%), followed by TSH-secreting and PRL-secreting types (3 cases each, 13.6%). Four cases (18.1%) were plurihormonal, and in another 4 cases (18.1%), all hormonal stains were negative. Postoperative complications were most frequently diabetes insipidus (9 cases, 40.9%), followed by hypopituitarism (7 cases, 31.8%). No postoperative complications were observed in 8 cases (36.4%). One case experienced postoperative generalized tonic-clonic seizures, while others had homonymous hemianopia and transient worsening of visual acuity.

Regarding the potential etiologies of ectopic Pit-NETs, it is widely accepted that these tumors originate from residual pituitary cells that remain along the migratory route of Rathke’s pouch during embryonic development.19 At the 4th week of gestation, the ectoderm of the primitive oral cavity begins to invaginate upward, leading to the formation of Rathke’s pouch. This pouch subsequently comes into contact with the infundibular process, which extends downward from the neuroectoderm. Between weeks 6 and 8, Rathke’s pouch undergoes separation and differentiation, giving rise to the pars distalis, pars intermedia, and pars tuberalis of the adenohypophysis. Ultimately, the definitive pituitary gland finds its final position within the sella turcica. As the skull base closes, it creates a separation between Rathke’s pouch and the oropharyngeal ectoderm. During this process, some cells may persist along the migration pathway. Specifically, type II ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs, which lack continuity with the intrasellar gland, are believed to primarily originate from the pars tuberalis.20,21 Further embryological and histological studies are required to confirm this hypothesis.

Surgical approaches included transcranial resection in 8 cases (36.4%), transsphenoidal resection in 11 cases (of which 8 were endoscopic), and a combined transcranial and transsphenoidal approach in 3 cases (13.6%). All 3 patients who underwent the combined approach initially had transsphenoidal surgery, with one receiving a total hypophysectomy and another a partial hypophysectomy. Because of unresolved symptoms, these patients subsequently underwent transcranial surgery for tumor resection. Among the 18 cases of type II ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs in the cohort, 15 cases (83.3%) were of the extra-stalk type, all of which demonstrated preserved anatomical integrity of the pituitary stalk. The remaining 3 cases (16.7%) were of the intra-stalk type, indicating that the intra-stalk type is less common than the extra-stalk type.

It is noteworthy that in the case reported by Park et al.,18 intraoperative findings also revealed the tumor originating from and growing within the pituitary stalk, consistent with an intra-stalk ectopic suprasellar Pit-NET. Perhaps due to the intraoperative manipulation of the pituitary stalk, the patient developed hypopituitarism lasting for 6 months postoperatively. Given that the tumor size was relatively small, preserving the pituitary stalk was easier than in our cases involving larger tumors. However, based on a review of the literature and our own cases, we hypothesize that maintaining the integrity of the pituitary stalk in intra-stalk ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs is more challenging than in intrasellar Pit-NETs or extra-stalk ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs. Furthermore, the degree of difficulty may be correlated with tumor volume.

Intraoperative observations in all 4 reported cases indicated significant compression of the pituitary stalk by the tumor. We believe that preserving the integrity of the pituitary stalk during the resection of Pit-NETs is crucial. However, achieving this goal was particularly challenging in cases 1 and 2. Both cases experienced postoperative hypopituitarism despite having normal preoperative function. These findings suggest that, for asymptomatic patients, regular follow-up may be the more appropriate initial approach.

Surgical intervention is currently considered the first-line treatment for patients experiencing visual impairment due to adenoma compression of the optic nerves or chiasm.22 However, when a patient is diagnosed with a PRL-secreting Pit-NET and exhibits a clinically stable condition, pharmacological therapy is typically recommended as the first-line treatment. Surgical intervention is the primary approach for cases of rapid disease progression accompanied by severe compressive symptoms, such as significant visual impairment due to tumor apoplexy. It is also indicated when the patient cannot tolerate the side effects of medication.

The endoscopic endonasal approach has been widely used in Pit-NET surgery and has achieved promising results.6,7 Nevertheless, it is incontrovertible that in the management of certain exceptionally large or complex Pit-NETs, transcranial approaches can still prove to be highly beneficial. The gross anatomical positioning of the tumors in cases 1 and 2 was retrochiasmatic, aligning with the typical localization of retrochiasmatic craniopharyngiomas. In this anatomical location, the endoscopic endonasal approach is critical for attaining extensive surgical exposure while minimizing collateral damage.

Our 4 cases are exactly similar to the tumors reported by Tsukada et al.,4 which were successfully resected using an endoscopic endonasal approach. Therefore, we suggest that endoscopic endonasal surgery may be an excellent approach for resecting ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs that cause mass effects and exhibit clinical and radiological features similar to those in our reported cases. The overall surgical strategy should, however, be tailored to the individual patient.

Clinically, among ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs, ACTH-secreting Pit-NETs are the most frequently documented, followed by nonfunctioning Pit-NETs (Table 1). However, within our cohort, postoperative pathological reclassification has shown that a subset of preoperatively diagnosed nonfunctioning Pit-NETs are, in fact, silent ACTH-secreting Pit-NETs. Research by Portovvedo et al.23 and Langlois et al.24 suggests that silent ACTH-secreting Pit-NETs likely tend to show more aggressive biological behavior and pose greater technical difficulties during resection compared to other subtypes. Notably, the dissection between the tumor and pituitary stalk proved more challenging in our 2 cases than in most Pit-NET resections. Given these operative findings, we strongly suspect that cases 1 and 2 represent silent ACTH-secreting ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs. This potential association between ectopic suprasellar intra-stalk Pit-NETs and silent ACTH-secreting Pit-NETs warrants further investigation in future studies.

All 4 patients in our series presented with headache as their chief complaint; 3 also experienced blurry vision, and 1 experienced dizziness. No clinical evidence of hormone hypersecretion was observed, consistent with the characteristics of nonfunctioning Pit-NETs. Hormone-secreting Pit-NETs are typically identified at an early stage due to symptoms resulting from hormonal excess. In contrast, nonfunctioning Pit-NETs often remain clinically silent until mass effects, such as headaches and visual impairment, prompt medical consultation. These tumors are frequently inconspicuous on initial imaging.

Based on our analysis of the literature and our own reported cases, we have identified several technical challenges that surgeons may face during endoscopic endonasal surgery for type II ectopic Pit-NETs. The first challenge involves the extent of bone drilling required during the procedure. This must be customized according to the tumor’s size and extent to provide sufficient exposure for the resection of suprasellar tumors. In some instances, this may necessitate the removal of the tuberculum sellae and the planum sphenoidale.4,25 Second, it is important to note that all ectopic Pit-NETs reviewed in this study are situated in the suprasellar region, surrounded by complex neurovascular structures. This necessitates a meticulous microsurgical technique to preserve critical neural and vascular tissues, including the optic nerves, internal carotid arteries, superior hypophyseal arteries, and posterior communicating arteries. Third, for type II ectopic Pit-NETs, which originate from the pituitary stalk, maintaining the integrity of the stalk during tumor resection presents a significant technical challenge for surgeons. Compared with extra-stalk ectopic Pit-NETs, preserving the structural integrity of the pituitary stalk in intra-stalk ectopic Pit-NETs is particularly difficult, as the tumor completely occupies the interior of the stalk. When the stalk is entirely occupied by the tumor, maintaining its structural integrity becomes exceedingly challenging, often resulting in varying degrees of stalk injury. This can lead to postoperative hypopituitarism and DI in these patients, necessitating endocrinological treatment.18 Lastly, type II ectopic Pit-NETs with significant suprasellar invasion, particularly those with tumor extension into the third ventricle, such as cases 1 and 2 in our report, face an increased risk of postoperative CSF leakage. Based on data from other teams and our group’s surgical experience, a meticulous approach to skull base reconstruction utilizing a multilayer, overlapping technique with diverse materials, including biomaterials, free tissue grafts like fat and fascia lata, and vascular regional flaps, proves advantageous in minimizing the incidence of postoperative CSF leakage.25,26

Lessons

Ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs are rare entities. Type II ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs originate from the pituitary stalk, compressing the stalk and surrounding structures. Based on the predominant anatomical relationship between the tumor and the pituitary stalk, these tumors are classified as extra-stalk or intra-stalk types. We hypothesize that preserving the integrity of the pituitary stalk is more challenging in intra-stalk–type cases compared to extra-stalk–type cases. Preoperative diagnosis based solely on imaging is difficult for clinically nonfunctioning ectopic suprasellar Pit-NETs. The intra-stalk type may present as silent ACTH-secreting Pit-NETs, causing symptoms like headache and visual disturbances by compressing the optic nerve, even in the absence of excessive hormones. Given the high incidence of postoperative hypopituitarism in patients with the intra-stalk type, asymptomatic patients should primarily be managed with regular surveillance. Resection via an endoscopic endonasal approach is advisable when mass effect symptoms develop.
