Inter-arm Blood Pressure Difference is Associated With Both Coronary Artery Disease and Peripheral Artery Disease in People With HIV
Authors: Sávio Amaral, Moises Alberto Suarez-Zdunek, Andreas Dehlbæk Knudsen, Andreas Fuchs Larsen, Álvaro H Borges, Lars Valeur Køber, Thomas Benfield, Klaus F Kofoed, Carlos Brites, Susanne Dam Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh systolic inter-arm blood pressure difference (SIABPD) is associated with cardiovascular events. We evaluated the prevalence of high SIABPD and its association with coronary and peripheral artery disease in people with HIV. The prevalence of high SIABPD was 24%, and SIABPD was independently associated with extensive coronary and peripheral arterial disease.

High systolic inter-arm blood pressure difference (SIABPD) is associated with higher risk of cardiovascular events, peripheral artery disease (PAD), and mortality in people without HIV (PWoH) [1]. Consensus guidelines recommend further investigations in persons with an inter-arm blood pressure difference greater than 10 mm Hg [2, 3]. Studies of patients undergoing coronary angiography showed more than twice the odds of coronary artery disease (CAD) in PWoH with high SIABPD [4], suggesting that high SIABPD may be an easily assessed marker of CAD.

People with HIV (PWH) have twice the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases compared to PWoH [5]. The pathogenesis is multifactorial and includes chronic inflammation, coinfections, low CD4 counts, some antiretroviral therapy categories, and a higher prevalence of traditional risk factors [5]. In a previous study that evaluated cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA), we found that having HIV is independently associated with obstructive CAD and high-risk plaque features [6]. Traditional risk scores underestimate the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in PWH, and additional screening tools are needed.

The Women's Interagency HIV Study evaluated the SIABPD in women with and without HIV and demonstrated that 26% had a SIABPD over 10 mm Hg [7], but whether these findings can be reproduced in men with HIV and whether SIABPD is associated with CAD and PAD in PWH is unknown.

We aimed to evaluate the prevalence of high SIABPD and to determine if high SIABPD is associated with CAD and/or PAD in PWH.

METHODS

Overview

The Copenhagen Comorbidity in HIV Infection (COCOMO) study is a prospective cohort investigating comorbidities in PWH (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02382822), which included >40% of eligible PWH in Copenhagen between May 2015 and November 2016. All PWH aged ≥20 years followed at outpatient HIV clinics at Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet or Amager-Hvidovre were invited for physical examinations and research CCTA.

Data Collection

Participants underwent physical examinations including measurements of height, weight, and blood pressure. Participants sat for 15 minutes before blood pressure measurements in both arms, which were repeated at 60-second intervals, until the difference between measurements was ≤5 mm Hg or for a maximum of 4 times per arm. The mean systolic and diastolic blood pressures were recorded for the 2 closest measurements in each arm. The blood pressure measurements were conducted at the physical examination, and the CCTA was performed within a short time interval relative to the physical examination (median 27 [interquartile range: 39–57] days). We also measured the ankle blood pressure in both legs to calculate the ankle-brachial index (ABI) as previously described [8].

Concentrations of glycated hemoglobin, glucose, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, were measured at inclusion, and HIV RNA viral load, current CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell counts, CD4+ T-cell nadir, were recorded from the patient records. Participants provided self-reported data on demographics and lifestyle, including smoking and medical history [9].

Participants underwent CCTA according to the previously described protocol [6], which was analyzed for plaque volume and presence and degree of luminal stenosis [10].

Definitions

SIABPD was computed as the absolute difference of systolic blood pressure between arms. High SIABPD was defined as a SIABPD ≥10 mm Hg [11].

We defined any CAD as any coronary luminal stenosis, obstructive CAD as a maximum coronary luminal stenosis ≥50%, and extensive CAD as luminal stenosis of any degree involving at least 6 of 17 coronary segments [12]. We defined PAD as ABI ≤0.9.

Hypertension was defined as a blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg and/or current use of antihypertensives [3], dyslipidemia as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥3.0 mM (116 mg/dL) and/or current use of lipid-lowering agents [12], and diabetes mellitus as nonfasting plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dL) and/or glycated hemoglobin ≥ 48 mmol/L and/or use of antidiabetics [13]. The body mass index was categorized as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.99 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.99 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30 kg/m2) [14].

Statistical Analyses

We used SIABPD and high SIABPD as exposure variables. In analyses of any, obstructive, extensive CAD, and PAD, we used logistic regressions to calculate odds ratios (OR). Participants with ABI ≥1.4 were excluded (arterial noncompressibility). In analyses of total plaque volume, we used linear regressions to calculate regression coefficients (β) of exposure variables. Regression models were adjusted for confounders (model 1: adjusted for age, sex, and smoking; model 2: adjusted for age, sex, smoking, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia; model 3: adjusted for age, sex, smoking, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and body mass index).

We performed sensitivity analyses excluding participants with history of prior coronary or peripheral revascularization and hepatitis C infection, respectively, and we additionally adjusted model 3 for hypertension to account for a potential impact on SIABPD.

Two-sided P < .050 denoted statistical significance. We used R (version 4.3.2) for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

We included 646 PWH (Supplementary Figure 1), with a median age of 50.4 years, 89% male. Participants had a median time since HIV diagnosis of 12.8 years, the median CD4+ T-cell count was 670, and 96% had HIV RNA ≤50 copies/mL. Of all participants, 41% had hypertension, 19% had dyslipidemia, and 3% had diabetes mellitus (Table 1). The prevalence of high SIABPD was 24%. High SIABPD was associated with extensive CAD (OR 2.23; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.29–3.85; P < .01), PAD (OR 4.20 [95% CI, 2.21–8.01], P < .001), and more than 3 times higher total plaque volumes (3.02 [95% CI, 1.06–4.38] fold increase, P = .03) (Figure 1). High SIABPD remained independently associated with extensive CAD after adjustment for model 1 (adjusted OR [aOR] 2.17 [95% CI, 1.16–4.06], P = .01), model 2 (aOR 2.02 [95% CI, 1.06–3.84], P = .03), and model 3 (aOR 2.11 [95% CI, 1.10–4.04], P = .02), as well as with PAD adjusted for model 1 (aOR 4.03 [95% CI, 2.10–7.75], P < .001), model 2 (aOR 4.48 [95% CI, 2.28–8.82], P < .001), and model 3 (aOR 4.47 [95% CI, 2.26–8.84], P < .001) (Figure 1). These findings were unaffected by further adjustment for hypertension, history of prior coronary revascularization, and hepatitis C infection (Supplementary Table 2).

Similarly, each 10-mm Hg increase of SIABPD was associated with extensive CAD before (OR 1.92 [95% CI, 1.23–2.98], P < .01) and after adjustments (model 1: aOR 1.99 [95% CI, 1.19–3.33], P < .01, model 2: aOR 1.90 [95% CI, 1.13–3.19], P = .02, model 3: aOR 2.11 [95% CI, 1.24–3.59], P < .01), as well as with PAD before (OR 3.19 [95% CI, 1.93–5.28], P < .001) and after adjustments (model 1: aOR 3.09 [95% CI, 1.86–5.14], P < .001, model 2: aOR 3.21 [95% CI, 1.91–5.41], P < .001, model 3: aOR 3.08 [95% CI, 1.82–5.21], P < .001).

Neither high SIABPD nor increases in SIABPD were associated with any CAD, obstructive CAD, or plaque volumes after adjustments (Figure 1).

DISCUSSION

In this cohort of well-treated PWH, we found a prevalence of high SIABPD of 24%, and high SIABPD was independently associated with extensive CAD and PAD.

This prevalence of high SIABPD was higher than previously reported in PWoH. In the Framingham cohort older than 60 years, the prevalence of high SIABPD was 9% [1], and in a systematic review, the pooled prevalence was 7% in the general population, 11% among those with hypertension, and 7% among those with diabetes [15]. However, in the Women's Interagency HIV Study cohort, the prevalence was 26%, comparable to our findings [7]. These data suggest that PWH may have a higher prevalence of high SIABPD than PWoH, although this needs to be confirmed in studies with direct comparison. Nonetheless, high SIABPD in the Framingham cohort was associated with 38% higher risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular events [1].

We found an independent association of high SIABPD with extensive CAD and PAD, but not between high SIABPD and obstructive CAD. Rather than reflecting the severity of individual lesions, extensive CAD reflects diffuse coronary atherosclerosis that increases the risk of cardiovascular events similarly to obstructive disease [8, 12]. Because extensive CAD does not necessarily imply flow-limiting atherosclerosis, it is often asymptomatic, and the use of SIABPD may therefore potentially be a clinically useful add-on to assessment of asymptomatic patients [12]. Although this study was not aimed at determining the mechanistic impact of HIV on SIABPD, the HIV-specific relevance comes from the need of additional tools to identify people at high risk of CVD despite optimal treatment for traditional and HIV-related CVD risk factors. Currently, SIABPD is not part of routine CVD risk assessment in PWH despite uncomplicated assessment, and our findings suggests that it may have potential to identify PWH in whom the more time-consuming but prognostically validated CCTA may be warranted.

Some limitations of our study include the absence of a control group to assess if HIV status modifies the association between SIABPD, CAD, and PAD, the predominance of Caucasian men, and cross-sectional design, which prevents determination of causality and evaluation of SIABPD over time. However, this study is the first to evaluate the association between high SIABPD, CAD, and PAD in PWH, and the use of CCTA and ABI enabled detection of subclinical disease in a large sample of virologically suppressed participants.

High SIABPD was independently associated with extensive CAD and PAD. SIABPD may be used as an accessible tool for screening PWH for extensive CAD and PAD.
