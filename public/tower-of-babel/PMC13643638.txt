Biopsy of Iris and Anterior Chamber Tumours: Fine Needle, Minimally Invasive and Excision Techniques ‐ Review
Authors: Charles N. J. McGhee, Joevy Z. Lim, Peter W. Hadden, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeveral benign, inflammatory and malignant lesions can present as iris or anterior chamber masses, including iris naevi, cysts, adenomas, leiomyomas, nodules, vascular tumours, melanomas, metastases, as well as iris and pigment epithelium adenocarcinomas. Fortunately, benign iris lesions are much more common than malignant tumours and many can be diagnosed by clinical and contemporary non‐invasive analyses. However, differentiating between enlarging benign and malignant melanocytic lesions remains problematic. Anterior chamber and iris biopsy may include anterior chamber paracentesis and cytology, fine needle aspiration biopsy, vitreous aspiration cutter biopsy, micro‐forceps and punch techniques, minimally invasive excision with an anterior chamber device and conventional iridectomy or excision biopsy. However, there may be reluctance from both patient and physician to progress to iris biopsy or excision biopsy as part of a definitive, or staged, management approach for fear of damage to delicate anterior chamber structures, or inadvertent spread of a malignant tumour. This review provides a practical guide to available techniques, highlighting indications, complications and outcomes. Notably, the yield for cytological, histological and cytogenetic results is typically high, significant complications are uncommon, and no cases of external tumour spread have been reported with clear corneal biopsy techniques.

Introduction

Despite major advances in imaging of the eye and ocular tumours over the last 30 years, accurate diagnosis, especially prediction of metastatic potential and provision of informed prognosis for patients remains imperfect [1]. However, easy visualisation and access to the anterior chamber (AC) of the eye makes it suitable for relatively minimally invasive, diagnostic biopsies [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Tissue and aqueous humour sampling may better identify the diverse clinical presentations that may be seen in the AC, including: inflammation, infection, systemic malignancies and specifically primary and secondary iris tumours [10]. Contemporary prognosis of uveal melanoma, the most common malignant intraocular tumour, increasingly incorporates molecular prognostic markers in combination with histopathological assessment, highlighting the ongoing utility of cellular and tissue biopsies where possible [1, 11]. Therefore, in this review the authors attempt to provide a concise overview of approaches to fluid and tissue biopsy for AC tumours.

Method and Presentation of Review

To identify the relevant literature for this narrative review, an extensive Medline literature search was initially commenced on 7th July 2025 and thereafter updated until the 30th October 2025, using combinations of the keywords, iris, iris tumour, iris melanoma, uveal melanoma, AC tumours, biopsy, incisional biopsy, excisional biopsy, AC paracentesis, fine‐needle aspiration biopsy and liquid biopsy. Additional publications were obtained from review of the references from each publication or chapter considered. Only publications in English and specifically referring to management of iris or iridociliary lesions were retrieved (biopsy of choroidal and primarily cilio‐choroidal lesions were excluded). A total of 1959 publications of potential interest were identified, and after review of titles and abstracts, 181 of these publications were considered in greater detail. Many of these were single case reports, short case series (N < 5) or larger retrospective series in which there were insufficient and consistently reported comparable data for meta‐analysis, hence the narrative approach adopted in this review.

Discussion

Background

Primary malignant tumours of the eye are relatively uncommon, with uveal melanoma, the most common, occurring in only 5 to 13 cases per million population per year in predominantly fair skinned and light iris individuals [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Malignant tumours of the anterior segment, particularly the iris, are even less common, with ciliary body and iris representing only 6% and 4% of uveal melanomas, respectively [19], therefore there is a relatively limited clinical literature on which to base informed management decisions [20]. Fortunately, the majority of iris tumours are benign, or of relatively low metastatic potential, and therefore thorough clinical assessment and regular review is the preferred management in the majority of cases [21, 22].

Intraocular biopsy of the anterior and posterior segments of the eye is well established, with the first intraocular biopsy being attributed to Hirschberg in 1868 and, more than a century ago, the initial report of intraocular tumour excision attributed to Zirm in 1911 [23, 24, 25]. Stallard, Foulds and other leaders in the field introduced newer approaches to ocular tumour surgery in the 1960s and several small biopsy and excisional techniques now exist for both anterior and posterior segments [5, 20, 25, 26, 27].

However, due to the delicate nature of the corneal endothelium, AC angle, iris and crystalline lens, the limited space for instrument manoeuvres in the AC, and concerns over the representativeness of biopsies and extraocular tumour seeding, many tumours are treated without confirmatory tissue biopsy. These are primarily diagnosed based on clinical appearance and progression. Indeed, in a series of 54 patients with large, non‐resectable iris melanoma undergoing proton beam irradiation, Willerding et al. [28] performed biopsy pre‐operatively in only 14 (26%) cases and post‐irradiation in another 10 (18%) cases. These 24 biopsies from 54 eyes demonstrated 2 naevi, 1 melanocytoma, 17 spindle melanoma, 2 mixed, and 2 epithelioid melanoma [28]. In a smaller series of 10 iris tumour biopsies, Schalenbourg [29] noted that biopsies might provide ‘a falsely reassuring negative or an inconclusive result’ and concluded that with management advances in ultrasound and proton beam therapy, a biopsy has only a relative value in cases suggestive of iris melanoma.

A variety of benign and malignant lesions (Figure 1) may affect the iris and AC [8, 22, 30, 31]. Malignant lesions include iris melanoma, metastases to the iris, and iris pigment epithelium adenocarcinoma [22]. Benign iris lesions are much more common than malignant tumours. The most common are iris naevi but other lesions include iris cysts, iris pigment epithelium adenomas, iris leiomyomas, iris nodules and vascular tumours of the iris [8, 22, 30]. In the modern clinical setting, as the authors have highlighted in a recent review in this journal, clinical observation typically includes slit‐lamp microscopy/photography, gonioscopy, anterior segment Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), iris fluorescein angiography and high frequency ultrasound [8]. Newer modalities include magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), and so forth [20, 32, 33, 34, 35].

Melanocytic tumours of the iris are the most common iris abnormality. In a large, early retrospective series, Harbour et al. [2] reported on 285 patients with melanocytic iris tumours over a 15‐year period (1972–1986). The majority of iris lesions were simply observed; 36 lesions were initially excised, 89% of which were malignant melanomas [2]. Typically, these excised iris lesions were associated with tumour‐related symptoms, larger size (> 3.00 mm basal diameter), pigment dispersion, elevated IOP and increased vascularity. Two patients in this group subsequently died of metastatic disease [2].

Tumour enlargement was typically slow in the observed group of 249 patients (5‐year rate of 6.5%), but 10 lesions enlarged significantly, and, therefore, six were excised and confirmed as malignant melanomas on histology [2]. This confirmed the need to monitor tumours for growth and the increased risk of melanoma with significant enlargement. The authors observed that although the majority of iris melanocytic tumours are benign, even prompt local excision cannot avoid metastases in a small minority of patients [2]. Subsequently, in a major study of 1611 cases of iris naevi, Shields et al. [36] identified the risk factors for iris naevus growth into melanoma, elegantly summarised in their ‘ABCDEF’ rubric. These associations were defined as (A) Age young, (B) Blood (hyphaema), (C) Clock hour inferior, (D) Diffuse configuration, (E) Ectropion uveae, and (F) Feathery tumour margin [36].

In the contemporary management of growing tumours or those with changing morphology that may have greater malignant potential, simple observation and regular clinical documentation may be less satisfactory, especially with the typical approach to tumours elsewhere in the body being initial incisional or excisional biopsy with histopathological diagnosis to help guide further management. The rationale for ophthalmologists' resistance to more commonly performing intraocular biopsies includes the risks of surgery, possible subsequent visual compromise and the feared possibility of inadvertent extraocular tumour spread [37]. In contrast, the rationale for obtaining tissue biopsy includes, confirming diagnosis in suspected malignant tumour, refining diagnosis in cases of atypical clinical appearance, genetic analysis of tumours prior to treatment, and, of course, patient's and surgeon's desire for pathological confirmation before more invasive treatment [37]. Currently, such definitive treatment options may include local excision, enucleation, brachytherapy, stereotactic and proton beam radiotherapy [8, 20, 25].

Anterior Chamber Paracentesis and Cytology

Indications

The rationale for utilising AC paracentesis and cytology of the aspirate is typically uncertainty of clinical diagnosis, after non‐invasive investigations. Differential diagnoses might include malignant tumour, naevus, inflammatory lesions, atypical appearance, need for genetic analysis prior to treatment, and as previously noted, the patient's and surgeon's desire for definitive diagnosis before considering more invasive treatment.

Surgical Techniques, Results and Complications

The simplest approach to ‘anterior chamber biopsy’ is the AC tap, with paracentesis and aspiration of aqueous humour via a hypodermic needle for identification of infectious organisms and cytology. This approach has been widely adopted for more than three decades in the diagnosis of infectious uveitis [9, 38].

There are a number of approaches to this relatively simple procedure, either in the operating/procedure room or in the clinic room at the slit lamp. In a typical approach, following application of topical anaesthesia, the skin and conjunctival sac are prepared with dilute povidone iodine solution or chlorhexidine. The lids are retracted and the eye exposed using a fine lid speculum. The eye is stabilised with fine‐tooth forceps (or sterile cotton bud/spear) and a temporal approach is utilised via the peripheral cornea or limbal sclera with a fine gauge hypodermic needle (25‐30‐G) on a 1 mL tuberculin syringe. After the aqueous sample is obtained, the needle is carefully withdrawn with local counterpressure on the entry point, using a sterile spear or swab, to minimise discomfort and bleeding. AC tap enabled diagnosis of infectious uveitis in 25% of cases, with complications in approximately 6% of cases, when the majority of cases were performed at the slit lamp (75%), and where more than 2/3 of physicians were cornea or uveitis trained [9].

Finger et al. [39] used simple AC paracentesis and cytology as an alternative to vitreous biopsy in the diagnosis of intraocular lymphoma in four selected cases with cells in the AC. This provided confirmatory diagnosis of lymphoma in three cases and endophthalmitis in the fourth. Kitazawa et al. [40] also reported an AC paracentesis variant using a 30‐G needle and a custom disposable pipette, rather than a syringe. In the large series of 264 eyes investigated for viral or bacterial infection and intraocular tumours (n = 29), the authors noted no serious complications such as hyphaema, endophthalmitis, severe uveitis or crystalline lens injury [40].

An early variation of this AC paracentesis technique specifically for iris tumours termed ‘fine needle aspiration biopsy’ (note: non‐tumour‐penetrating) was described by Grossniklaus in 1992 [41]. This procedure can also be performed at the slit lamp with the eye and head stabilised by an assistant [41]. The needle is inserted via peripheral clear cornea, preferably at 45 to 90 degrees to the iris lesion, avoiding damage to the iris and the crystalline lens. The needle is gently swept, bevel down, over the surface of the tumour, and gentle aspiration is applied via the syringe to create a vacuum effect and thus obtain approximately 250–300 μL of fluid. In the series of nine consecutive patients, cytology/histopathology could be performed in all cases yielding the following diagnoses: primary uveal melanoma, metastatic melanoma, metastatic adenocarcinoma, leukaemic infiltrate, lymphocytic infiltrate, and epithelial ingrowth. The author concluded that the method was both safe and effective, with the main limitation being small tissue sample and cytological interpretation [41].

The Sheffield ocular oncology team reported a blunt instrument variation of AC biopsy in 2012, utilising a trans‐corneal fine‐cannula aspiration approach with 25‐G Rycroft cannulas under local anaesthesia in 12 patients [42]. Controlling aspiration with a 2 mL syringe to create a stronger vacuum, the cannula was passed repeatedly over the iris lesion, creating a groove in the tissue which was then aspirated [42]. Tissue specimens varied from approximately 1.0–1.5 mm with sufficient tissue to enable diagnosis by a histopathologist in all 12 cases, yielding the diagnosis of 10 iris melanomas, one metastatic tumour, and one pigmented adenoma [42]. Although this novel approach was reported as safe with 100% diagnostic success, there appear to be no further studies of the technique.

In relation to iris tumour biopsy techniques, these approaches are generally considered low risk, and provide a reasonable cell yield for cytology diagnosis. These minimally invasive approaches have a good safety profile, although potential complications may include insufficient sample, hyphaema, cataract, tract infection, endophthalmitis or, hypothetically, tumour extension via the incision site [40, 41, 42].

Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy (FNAB)

Indications

In a landmark 2006 study, Shields et al. [3] reported on 100 consecutive cases biopsied via limbal entry with a trans‐aqueous approach in a cohort of 1400 patients referred with iris tumours. This technique involves penetration of the tumour and taking a sample with a needle [3]. It was coincidentally also termed ‘fine needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB)’, the same name as the technique described by Grossniklaus in the last section [41]. However, in the previous approach, the tumour is not penetrated by the needle [41]. In this review, we will use the abbreviation FNAB to refer to the penetrating technique only, and use ‘fine needle aspiration biopsy (non‐penetrating)’ to refer to the Grossniklaus technique.

Shields et al. [3] reported that the biopsied iris tumours were typically large, with a median basal dimension of 9.0 mm and median thickness of 2.5 mm. The most common indication for FNAB was suspected melanoma or metastases, which was ultimately confirmed by cytology in 67% of eyes [3]. The authors specifically noted that FNAB sampling may be most suitable for tumours involving 6 clock hours of the eye with angle seeding (prior to brachytherapy or enucleation), whereas tumours of 3 clock hours or less, without angle seeding, might be best managed by local excision. An adequate tumour sample was obtained in almost all cases (99%), and the only acute complication was a degree of hyphaema in one third of the eyes [3].

Other indications for FNAB include: diagnostic uncertainty, an amelanotic mass in cases with a history of previous non‐ocular malignancy, patient refusal of treatment until confirmation of malignancy, suspected regrowth after treatment of an intraocular mass, requirement of prognostication, or being part of a clinical trial [43].

Surgical Technique

This technique is more involved than the AC paracentesis and cytology variants outlined in the preceding section, and therefore requires more planning, preparation and experience. Although this FNAB approach may yield more tissue, the volume is still likely to be very small and needs to be very carefully handled, conserved and analysed in conjunction with a specialist cytologist. Typically, this procedure is carried out in an operating room under aseptic conditions using peribulbar local anaesthesia techniques (avoiding penetration of the conjunctiva).

An excellent, highly detailed, account of the technique is provided by Shields et al. [3] (Figure 2). Briefly, their FNAB approach can be summarised as follows. A 25‐G needle is connected to a 10 mL syringe via 30–50 cm of clear polyethylene connector tubing to provide adequate suction and minimise transmission of any movement in the syringe to the needle tip [3]. The eye is entered via the peripheral cornea avoiding the conjunctiva, preferably temporally or infero‐temporally, at a 20°–30° angle to the iris plane. Bevel up, the needle is carefully passed via the AC, anterior and parallel to the iris plane, and then inserted into the tumour mass. Once securely within the tumour, the needle is gently moved back‐and‐forth (avoiding twisting or rotation of the needle) to separate cells from the tumour during aspiration. The full 10 mL aspiration is maintained for several seconds within the tumour, and as the needle is removed from the eye, to retain all cellular material. A crucial step at this stage is the flushing of all cellular material into the syringe [3]. Aqueous may be aspirated during or at the end of the procedure, thus the AC may shallow or collapse, and may need to be reformed using a new needle or cannula. The incision site can be hydrated if required to seal the AC [3].

Results

Overall, the biopsied tissue was sufficient for cytological diagnosis in 99% of cases [3]. One specimen was obtained in 74% of eyes, 2 in 24% and 3 in 2%. The most common diagnoses in these cases were: melanomas (47%), metastases (20%), iris stromal cysts (10%), melanocytomas (3%), leukaemias (3%), medulloepitheliomas (3%), granulomas (3%), juvenile xanthogranulomas (3%), and nonspecific inflammation (3%) [3]. Note that approximately two‐thirds of the cases were malignant in nature, highlighting the importance of accurate diagnosis to guide further management [3].

Historically there has been limited data on chromosomal abnormalities in iris tumours compared to other uveal tumours, since they typically represent only 4% of uveal melanomas, are relatively small, and studies often combine iris, ciliary body and choroidal melanomas. In a subsequent study, using their FNAB technique, Shields et al. [44] demonstrated significant success in cytogenetic analysis of 17 iris melanomas, prior to brachytherapy (n = 16) or enucleation. The 17 eyes exhibited moderately sized iris melanomas, with a mean thickness of 3.4 mm and basal extent of 6.8 mm. Chromosome 3 monosomy was assessed and reviewed, with disomy (29%), partial monosomy (41%), and complete monosomy (29%) being reported [44]. No intra‐ or post‐operative complications were reported. This study confirmed cytogenetic analysis was possible in all cases, and 71% exhibited partial or complete monosomy 3 that was correlated with age and had a non‐significant association with mixed/epithelioid cell types [44].

More recently, gene expression profiling has been used to characterise and prognosticate posterior uveal melanoma [45]. Whilst its utility in iris melanoma is yet to be established, Harbour et al. [46] showed that up to a third of 20 iris melanomas demonstrate class 2 gene expression profiles, which disputes the theory that iris melanomas have fewer genetic alterations and hence are less aggressive.

Complications

Intraoperative hyphaema (34%) was the most common complication of FNAB in Shields' initial 100 consecutive cases [3]. It typically resolved without intervention within 2 weeks. Hyphaema persisted beyond 2 weeks in 6%, with only a single case requiring an AC washout [3].

In all cases of pigmented iris tumours, pigmented cells were present in the corneal needle tract, but none showed any proliferation [3]. Notably, all tumours were treated by plaque brachytherapy, chemotherapy or enucleation [3]. Specifically, no cases of severe complications such as recurrent hyphaema, prolonged hypotony, lenticular damage, endophthalmitis, or extraocular tumour seeding along the needle tract were encountered [3]. In contrast, scleral extension of melanoma has been very rarely reported following trans‐scleral FNAB (0.2%) in cilio‐choroidal melanomas [47].

Small Tissue Biopsy Techniques

Indications

The indications for these techniques are similar to those previously highlighted in Sections 4 and 5. However, to obtain larger and thereby more representative tissue samples, several small series have been published highlighting techniques that lie somewhere between FNAB and formal, large iris biopsies.

Finger Iridectomy Technique (FIT)

Surgical Technique

As vitreoretinal surgery rapidly evolved in the last quarter of the twentieth century and came to be used in the management of choroidal tumours, initially by Peyman, Cohen and Charles and subsequently refined by Damato and others, vitrectomy techniques were also considered for AC and posterior segment tumours [48, 49, 50, 51]. Bechrakis et al. [52] employed a 20‐G vitreous cutter technique, using a two‐port corneal approach, or a three‐port pars plana vitrectomy approach, in 11 iris tumours and 23 choroidal tumours. A definitive histopathological diagnosis could be made in 97% of the entire cohort and iris melanoma was confirmed in 8 of the 11 iris tumours (74%) [52]. At a mean of 44 months, there was no increase in tumour related mortality [52].

The aspiration cutter‐assisted anterior segment tumour biopsy technique is now synonymous with the Finger Iridectomy Technique or FIT. This approach utilises a 1.0 mm, peripheral, clear corneal paracentesis approach to the AC using a 25‐G vitrectomy probe to obtain biopsies [53]. Typically, the single corneal incision is placed on the same side as the tumour to avoid crossing the pupil and the potential for lens touch [53]. The pupil is pharmacologically constricted with pilocarpine 1 h prior to surgery, putting the iris and tumour on stretch and limiting exposure of the crystalline lens surface. The AC is filled with an ophthalmic viscoelastic device (OVD) to maintain AC depth and stability [53].

The aspiration port is placed over the tumour to become partly occluded by it [53]. Using a starting suction of 300 mmHg and the lowest cut rate possible, biopsy of the tumour is performed [53]. Two to three partial thickness iris biopsies are typically acquired and the vitrectomy tubing is flushed into a syringe with 0.5 mL of saline for tissue preparation [53].

In 2011, the New York Eye Cancer Center group subsequently reported on their single centre retrospective study of 56 biopsies using the ‘finger iridectomy technique’ in 55 consecutive eyes, highlighting the utility of the technique [54]. The most common indications for biopsy were suspicion of anterior segment malignancy, atypical tumour presentation, and the desire for histopathological and genetic tumour analysis before definitive treatment [54]. The surgical approach is elaborated in detail and the authors note that two to three biopsies were typically required for diagnostic purposes, and that partial vitrector occlusion, rather than full occlusion (which may obtain more tissue), avoids creating a full thickness iridotomy [54]. Notably, a single, typically self‐sealing, clear corneal incision is made and the AC is filled with OVD rather than using an AC maintainer to avoid washing out tissue samples [54].

In 2017, the Finger group [55] published a variant of the FIT, the Multifocal Finger Iridectomy Technique (MFIT) in order to obtain several tissue biopsies in more diffuse iris tumours (Figure 3). This technique also utilises a single, 1.0 mm, clear corneal incision; the AC is also maintained with an OVD [55]. An aspiration cutter (25‐G) is used to create full and partial thickness biopsies (an average of five) and rotated via the single incision to different iris sites (effectively creating the clinical appearance of multiple peripheral iridotomies) [55].

Results

In the first series of 10 cases using FIT, it was noted that adequate specimen samples were obtained in nine (90%) [53]. One patient developed elevated IOP post‐surgery, but there were no other complications [53]. Diagnoses included malignant melanoma (4), melanocytoma (1), naevus (2), epithelial cyst (1) and normal iris (1) [53].

In the subsequent larger series of 56 biopsies (55 cases, 1 repeat biopsy) using FIT, enough biopsy material was available for a cyto‐pathological diagnosis in 98.2% of eyes [54]. Diagnoses included iris malignant melanoma (69.6%), iris naevus (7.1%), melanocytoma (7.1%), iris epithelial cyst (3.6%) and lymphoma (3.6%) [54]. Typically, these tumours were nodular and involved the iris (52.7%) or were iridociliary (41.8%). Notably, they were slightly smaller in size (median thickness 1.5 mm, median basal dimension 4.5 mm) compared to those biopsied by FNAB in Shields' study (median thickness 2.5 mm, median basal dimension 9.0 mm) as reported in Section 5 [3].

In the MFIT approach, sufficient tissue and cells were obtained for diagnosis in all seven cases assessed, with five diagnosed as diffuse melanomas and two as melanocytic proliferations of indeterminate nature [55].

Complications

In the large study of 56 biopsies using FIT, the authors specifically assessed the biopsy technique, complications and perioperative visual acuity [54]. The median follow‐up was 30.4 months and a minimum one‐month follow‐up was available for 94% of biopsies [54]. A minority of eyes (13.4%) required a suture to seal the AC, 11.5% experienced transient post‐operative elevation of IOP and there was one case of hyphaema with persisting elevation of IOP beyond 4 weeks postoperative. One case of melanocytoma had an asymptomatic pupillary defect. In terms of key outcomes, no patient lost vision (mean 20/50 before and 1 month post biopsy) and there were no cases of cataract, endophthalmitis or secondary tumour extension in the AC or at the corneal incision site. Emphasising their corneal approach in this biopsy technique, they also commented, ‘In a review of the literature, we could find no reported case of an iris or iridociliary tumour being disseminated by transcorneal biopsy’ [54].

When considering MFIT, one might reasonably presume there may be a greater risk of complications [55]. However, no instance of vision loss, secondary increase in IOP, hyphaema, infection or cataract development was reported [55]. Interestingly, considering the creation of multiple, full thickness, peripheral iris defects, there were no cases of glare or uniocular diplopia. As in the earlier series from this research group, no tumour dissemination was observed. Indeed, the authors reasonably concluded in this small study that, ‘Multifocal surgical iridectomy biopsy can be minimally invasive and effective allowing for partial and full‐thickness iris biopsy. Use of a 25‐G aspiration cutting probe allowed for small incision surgery, rapid rehabilitation, and no significant complications’ [55].

Small Incision Iris Tumour Biopsy Using Cavernous Forceps

In 2011, Akgül et al. [56] described a new technique using the Essen biopsy forceps (now called Akgül biopsy forceps), and a 3‐port 23‐G sutureless vitrectomy approach in the biopsy of 20 intraocular, subretinal tumours (Figure 4). In all cases, tissue biopsies were obtained, ranging from 0.3 to 2.1 mm in size [56]. This enabled histological diagnosis in 95% of cases, including choroidal melanoma (50%), choroidal naevus (15%) and choroidal metastases (15%). The authors concluded that this technique provided enough tissue for histology, with low risk of complications and might be especially useful in small tumours [56].

Subsequently, an alternative, minimally invasive, clear corneal biopsy approach under local anaesthesia was reported by Chronopoulos et al. [57] in a small series of seven iris tumours. Typically, two clear corneal incisions were required, one for the Essen 23‐G biopsy forceps and one for the 23‐G micro‐scissors [57]. Importantly, the Essen forceps have a somewhat conical interior that enables incorporation of a small biopsy with minimal manipulation and tissue damage. The tumour is grasped with the cavernous sampling forceps and separated from the iris using a bimanual micro‐scissor technique if required. The biopsy is contained in the closed forceps and removed from the eye to minimise artefactual damage to the tissue or spread of cells in the AC or at the incision site [57].

Seven patients underwent seven biopsies for iris tumours with a mean thickness of approximately 1.0 mm [57]. Visible biopsy material of approximately 1 mm by 2 mm was obtained in all cases [57]. In six of the seven (85%) pigmented iris lesions, a histological and immunohistochemical diagnosis could be made [57].

Complications

Only minor complications were reported in this small series including minimal bleeding at the biopsy site (3 cases) and one case with relative post‐operative pupil enlargement [57].

Iris Punch Biopsy

In 2007, Jacob Pe'er et al. [58] described a technique using a commercially available trabeculectomy punch (Kelly Descemet's membrane punch) in two cases (Figure 5). The punch has a 1.0 mm diameter head and can create a 0.75 mm‐deep bite into tissue [58]. A 3.2 mm, self‐sealing corneal incision is created adjacent to the tumour site and the AC is filled with OVD. The punch is manoeuvred over the centre of the lesion to be biopsied, pressed down firmly onto the lesion, and a bite taken by compressing the handle of the punch, which is then kept closed and removed with the tissue sample contained within. The authors report being able to obtain approximately 0.86 mm of tissue sample for histological analysis and immunostaining [58].

A diagnosis of iris melanoma was made in the two cases assessed and the only complications were an element of intraoperative bleeding (resolved with OVD tamponade) and elevation of IOP in one eye [58]. The authors concluded, ‘This technique is quick, simple to perform and requires non‐expensive and easily available equipment. The tissue obtained is of sufficient quality and quantity to enable routine and special stainings.’ [58] There appears to be no more extensive reports of this interesting approach.

Small Incision Iris Biopsy Techniques Using an AC Delivery Device

Indications

The two techniques highlighted in this section, both using small corneal incisions and a device to deliver the excised iris tumour from the AC, share a number of advantages compared to standard methods of obtaining larger iris biopsies/excisions. Smaller corneal incisions combined with OVD rather than AC fluid maintainers create a well‐formed and stable AC with protection of the endothelium during surgery. The employment of OVD also enables creation of space between the iris and crystalline lens and manipulation of the biopsy segment in order to minimise lens‐instrument touch. The use of a delivery device, either an IOL injector or modified tubing, minimises distortion or compression artefact of the iris biopsy on egress at the incision site and potentially eliminates the risk of tumour cell contact with the corneal incision or ocular surface. The requirement for sutures is limited and thus induced astigmatism is reduced and visual rehabilitation is more rapid. These techniques, explored in detail below, work best for excision of small iris tumours but can also be used for sub‐total excision biopsy of larger tumours.

Minimal Iris Touch Excision (MITE) Surgical Technique

Typically, this procedure is performed in the operating room under aseptic conditions with peribulbar or general anaesthesia (conjunctival incisions should be avoided to circumvent the risk of tumour seeding) [7]. After the AC has been filled with an OVD, four 1.1 mm peripheral clear corneal incisions are created and angled to enable two radial and two basal iris incisions around the tumour [7]. OVD is also injected between the iris tumour and the subjacent anterior capsule to elevate the iris and create a surgical space, whilst protecting the crystalline lens and zonules from instrument touch. The pupillary margin of the iris segment is grasped by non‐toothed micro‐forceps, avoiding direct tumour contact. Two radial iris incisions, with a 1.0 mm clear margin, are created from the pupil margin to iris root. Using the two oblique paracenteses, with the iris segment placed on tension, basal iris cuts are then performed to free the tumour and locate it in the mid‐AC [7].

A three‐step, 2.7 mm clear corneal (phaco‐style) incision is created, typically by extending one of the paracenteses opposite the excised iris segment, and a large open‐ended intraocular lens cartridge (Monarch A, Alcon Laboratories Ltd) primed with a cohesive OVD and balanced salt solution (BSS) is inserted 1.0–2.0 mm into the AC (Figure 6) [7]. The excised iris tissue tends to spontaneously move towards and into the IOL cartridge opening due to the difference in IOP and cartridge intraluminal pressure plus an element of capillary action. However, additional OVD or BSS can be introduced into the AC via a paracentesis to gently push the excised tissue towards the lumen of the cartridge [7].

Once fully within the IOL cartridge, the tumour can be removed without touching the internal corneal incision or the ocular surface. No aspiration is required, so the AC usually remains well formed, but additional OVD or BSS may be used to avoid shallowing or iris prolapse once the biopsy is removed. The primary corneal incision is typically closed with a single 10–0 nylon suture and the paracenteses sealed by hydration [7]. The biopsy is carefully ejected from the cartridge with BSS, unrolled flat, and sandwiched between two pieces of filter paper prior to fixation, to provide correct orientation for histology [7].

Results

In the initial report of three cases with small iris tumours (< 1.5 mm basal width) and a history of growth, sufficient tissue biopsy was obtained for histological diagnoses in all cases (1 naevus, 2 spindle B cell melanoma) [7]. One was noted to have a biopsy‐proven history of amelanotic iris melanoma with possible recurrence [7]. In two tumours, clear iris margins were obtained, but in the amelanotic tumour, the tissue margins were positive on histology and this subject elected to undergo no further treatment. There was no visible pigment within the clear corneal incisions and there were no ocular recurrence or metastases with greater than 40 months follow up in all cases [7]. The authors note that in the first 20 cases subsequently treated by MITE biopsy (unpublished data, McGhee 2025), sufficient tissue was obtained for diagnosis in all cases.

Complications

In the initial series of three subjects, there was minimal intraoperative bleeding (minimised by the tamponade effect of OVD in a relatively closed eye) [7]. One subject had transient elevation of IOP that was managed medically and the one patient with a clear crystalline lens at the time of biopsy remained cataract free [7].

The authors have used MITE in the management of iris tumours for more than a decade [59], and in the first 20 cases of MITE biopsy (unpublished data, McGhee October 2025) there was one case of significant intraoperative bleeding that did not prevent safe completion of surgery, and a separate single case of post‐operative hyphaema. Transient, self‐limiting, modest elevation of IOP was relatively common when dispersive OVDs were utilised despite aspiration at the end of procedures; therefore oral acetazolamide is routinely prescribed for 24 h post‐operatively. No cases of endophthalmitis or spread of tumour via corneal incisions were identified. There were no cases of acute cataract formation, though the majority of cases had cataract surgery with an intracapsular artificial iris as part of their planned and staged management [60].

Small Incision Guarded Hydroaspiration of Iris Lesions (SIGHIL)

In the treatment of iris lesions, Singh [6] postulated that the use of multiple small clear corneal incisions might avoid the potential complications associated with a single large incision, and that aspiration of an excised iris tumour from the AC whilst enclosed in tubing might also minimise potential tumour contamination of the wound. Thus, he developed a novel method of iris biopsy for the resection of small iris tumours in a series of 22 eyes, with a mean thickness of 1.3 mm (range 0.5–2.5 mm), mean basal diameter of 3.0 mm (range 1.5–5.0 mm) and a minimum of 2.0 mm of uninvolved peripheral iris beyond the tumour. The majority (95%) of tumours involved the pupil margin [6].

Surgical Technique

The SIGHIL procedure involves six key surgical steps (Figure 7): (1) A small self‐sealing, clear corneal, primary incision (2.0–3.0 mm) is created with a keratome in the quadrant opposite to the iris tumour, supplemented by additional corneal incisions as required (typically 2–3 incisions, 2.0–3.2 mm diameter, mean 2.7 mm) [6]. One of the corneal incisions is subsequently enlarged to 5.2 mm to accommodate the aspiration tube (diameter 3.5 mm) [6]. (2) Using these corneal incisions and straight (23‐G or 25‐G) vitrectomy scissors, two radial iris incisions are commenced at the pupil edge with 1.0 mm clear margins on either side of the tumour. The partially resected iris tissue is then manipulated by micro forceps or OVD to enable peripheral iris incisions with curved vitrectomy scissors. (3) Once the biopsy is free in the AC, one of the incisions is enlarged to 5.2 mm as noted, and clear, 3.5 mm diameter, plastic tubing (attached to a 3.0–4.0 mL syringe) is filled with OVD and inserted into the AC via this incision. The bevelled tip is approximated to the detached biopsy and by careful and slow retraction of the syringe plunger, relative vacuum is created, and the tumour is aspirated into the tube lumen before withdrawal. (4) The iris defect is closed with 10–0 prolene sutures. (5) The corneal incision(s) are closed with interrupted 10–0 nylon sutures. (6) The tumour sample is expressed from the plastic tubing onto filter paper for histopathological processing [6].

Results

The authors reported that all tumours were completely resected with clear margins in a series of 22 carefully selected small iris tumours with at least 2 mm of uninvolved healthy peripheral iris [6]. Interestingly, 16 (73%) lesions were confirmed on histopathology to be melanoma—one was borderline neoplastic, and five (23%) were either benign neoplastic (n = 2) or non‐neoplastic (n = 3). In those diagnosed with malignant tumours (73%), no local recurrences or metastases were reported at median follow up of 33 months [6].

Complications

A major risk in local iris tumour resection is intraoperative or postoperative bleeding; however, in this study, intraoperative hyphaema was minimal in 59% of eyes and absent in the remainder [6]. In no cases did AC bleeding interfere with the surgical procedure and no hyphaema persisted beyond 1 week post‐operatively. There were no cases of wound leak or hypotony [6].

At 1–3 months post‐surgery, the multiple small corneal incisions were associated with a mean corneal astigmatism of 1.0 D (range 0.14–2.99 D; median 0.8 D); however, the magnitude did not correlate with the number of incisions [6]. Three cases went onto cataract surgery, one planned prior to biopsy and the others at one and 4.5 years post‐operatively, though none required revision of the pupilloplasty for cataract surgery [6].

Conventional Large Incision Iridectomy or Biopsy Excision

Indications

These are more established and conventional surgical approaches to iris biopsy, and the indications are typically similar to those in previous sections, that is, to confirm diagnosis of an iris or AC mass by biopsy or excision biopsy. Compared to some of the preceding techniques, the advantages include that they require no specialist equipment, and the techniques should be within the surgical toolkit of most ocular oncologists and anterior segment surgeons. Disadvantages include larger incisions, potentially more tumour contamination of the incision and ocular surface and induced corneal astigmatism.

Conventional Iridectomy Biopsy/Excisional Biopsy

Conventional iridectomy involves removal of a triangular sector of the iris containing the tumour and preferably a margin of normal iris (Figure 8) [10]. Multiple variations in technique exist, but it typically involves the following steps [8, 62, 63]. First, a conjunctival peritomy may be created followed by formation of a partial‐thickness, sclero‐corneal or peripheral corneal incision 2 mm wider than the outside border of the iris tumour. A full thickness keratome incision is completed over the tumour to facilitate removal of tissue from the AC. Radial iris incisions are made from the periphery with at least a 1 mm margin surrounding the tumour. Closure of wounds is achieved with 10/0 nylon. The main complications of iridectomy are glare, hyphaema, cataract formation, infection, transient elevation of IOP and/or secondary glaucoma requiring further treatment [8, 62].

Variants on these conventional iridectomy techniques have used corneal paracentesis opposite the tumour site. Using micro‐scissors and forceps (23–25‐G), this produces more controlled radial iris incisions, from pupil margin to iris root, in a well‐formed AC filled with OVD (Figure 9). A peripheral, clear‐corneal, three step incision, extending approximately 1 clock‐hour beyond the radial incisions, is created to enable incision of the peripheral iris and delivery of the tumour onto the ocular surface. In 2011, Jacob Pe'er's group [65] described a modified technique of sector iridectomy in six cases, where, following a peripheral corneo‐scleral incision, the iris tumour was prolapsed externally through this incision using OVD. This enabled the tumours to be excised without any intraocular manipulation and without the need for specialist instrumentation. The authors noted that potentially this might decrease intraocular complications [65].

Liquid Biopsy

Liquid biopsy refers to the minimally invasive sampling and analysis of tumour‐specific biomarkers from various liquids in the body, including whole blood and intraocular fluids such as aqueous humour. It is an emerging technique for intraocular diagnosis and prognostication, especially for retinoblastoma, where direct intraocular sampling is largely contraindicated due to the risk of extraocular spread [66, 67], and more recently, it was applied to uveal melanoma [68, 69, 70]. Tumour biomarkers obtained for molecular analysis include cell‐free DNA (cfDNA), fragmented DNA freely circulating in body fluids which are derived from normal cell turnover, inflammation, or diseases [68, 69, 70]. Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) is a subset of cell‐free DNA which are derived from tumours, typically comprising between 0.1% to 10% of any cell‐free DNA sample depending on tumour status [71].

To minimise the tumour externalisation risk of intraocular biopsies, blood‐derived ctDNA analysis is desirable [71]. It may also complement intraocular biopsies with insufficient material [71]. Despite a lack of studies specific to iris melanoma, blood‐based ctDNA was detected in 0%–100% of primary uveal melanomas, with 4 out of 9 studies reporting no detection (0%) [71]. Metastatic uveal melanomas were understandably more readily detectable in blood (41%–100% detection rate) [71]. For primary tumours where there is a low tumour burden, such as iris tumours, technical advances are required to improve sensitivity for blood‐derived ctDNA analysis to be useful, but the future is promising [71].

Discussion

Despite the rapid expansion of and refinement in anterior segment diagnostic techniques since the millennium, the accurate diagnosis of iris and AC tumours, especially distinguishing between naevi and malignant melanoma when planning management remains a significant challenge [8]. However, there is often reluctance to biopsy in cases suggestive of malignancy due to potential risks to the ocular structures, visual function and extraocular spread [37]. As highlighted in this review, modern clear corneal biopsy methodologies demonstrate a robust safety profile and high diagnostic performance. Crucially, across all contemporary clear corneal clinical literature, no cases of extraocular tumour dissemination or incision‐site involvement have ever been reported [3, 7, 40, 41, 42, 54, 57, 58].

Although iris melanomas have a relatively low risk of metastatic spread, establishing a definitive histological diagnosis is vital for best management and prognostication [72]. Confirmed diagnosis of malignancy is especially important when some of the interventions, such as proton beam therapy, are associated with significant ocular morbidity [73]. Acknowledging that retrospective series reveal that between 10% and 20% (up to 50% in specific diagnostic subgroups) of highly suspicious iris masses are ultimately confirmed to be benign lesions—such as naevi, melanocytomas, or stable vascular anomalies—pre‐operative biopsy holds immense value [3, 6, 7, 41, 42, 52, 53, 54, 55]. Therefore, biopsy before definitive therapy might avoid unnecessary or over‐treatment in a reasonable number of cases. Furthermore, in a modern clinical landscape where patients are heavily informed by online media and peer‐led support groups, providing a clear pathological diagnosis offers vital psychological reassurance during management planning.

The optimal choice of surgical biopsy approach must be carefully tailored to the tumour's size, its geometric position, and whether it involves the anterior chamber angle structures, as summarised in Table 1.

Biopsy by AC paracentesis and cytology has been demonstrated to provide diagnostic data in only around 25% of presumed infective uveitis cases [9], but small series with fine‐needle, non‐tumour‐penetrating, or blunt Rycroft grooving variations of this technique have reported diagnostic results in the majority of iris lesions [39, 40, 41, 42]. These techniques are relatively straightforward but produce small cytological samples, and thus require expert analysis. Generally, the utility of the AC paracentesis approach in anterior chamber tumours does not appear to have been widely reported beyond such small studies.

The intra‐tumour FNAB technique, as elegantly defined by the Shields group, provided a high success rate in diagnostic cytology (99 of 100 cases), and in a subsequent study of 17 iris melanomas, enabled cytogenic analysis in 100% [3, 44]. Although the technique involves inserting a needle into the tumour and moving it back and forth under suction, reported complications were minimal beyond self‐limiting hyphaema in one‐third of eyes. However, FNAB may be best reserved for moderate to large tumours, and, as the group acknowledged, smaller tumours (< 3 clock hours without angle seeding) may still be best treated by excision biopsy [3].

The preceding biopsy techniques are limited to cellular and cytogenetic analysis, and a number of procedures have been developed to provide more tissue for analysis, including FIT, the Kelly punch adaptation and the Akgül (Essen) cavernous forceps biopsy [53, 54, 56, 57]. All appear to work and provide small tissue, rather than cellular, samples. In the small studies using the latter two techniques, 0.8 to 2.0 mm biopsies were obtained, enabling 100% diagnosis (N = 2) in the Kelly punch series and 85% diagnosis (N = 7) in the Essen biopsy series [56, 57, 58]. These techniques have not been widely reported elsewhere. However, FIT has been more widely utilised and reported in three series including 72 eyes [53, 54, 55]. In the largest series, the diagnostic biopsy success rate was 98% and there were no significant complications other than transient elevation of IOP; no eyes lost vision and there was no evidence of extraocular tumour spread [54]. For practitioners comfortable with aspiration vitrectomy, this appears to provide a good balance of adequate AC tissue sampling with low ocular morbidity.

Of course, a limitation of cellular and small tissue biopsy techniques is the risk of inadequate or unrepresentative sampling that might be of little benefit or falsely reassuring [29]. Small incision iris biopsy techniques using an AC delivery device enable complete excision of small iris tumours and subtotal excision of larger tumours. The MITE and SIGHIL techniques, from the McGhee and Singh groups respectively, have a number of elements in common [6, 7]. They both use multiple, small, corneal incisions and micro‐instruments to excise and free the iris tumour, coupled with a delivery device to remove the biopsy from the eye without contact with the wound edges or the ocular surface [6, 7].

The SIGHIL approach, as pioneered by Singh, initially reported on 22 carefully selected, small, iris tumours that typically involved the iris pupil margin with 2 mm of tumour free peripheral iris. All excised margins were tumour free and there was no evidence of recurrence or metastatic disease at a mean of 33 months [6]. The iris defect can be repaired by primary suture closure. Other than transient intraoperative hyphaema, there were no significant complications [6]. The significant benefits of this small corneal incision approach include less ocular surface contamination and reduced induced astigmatism. Singh calculated that, for the relatively small iris tumours excised using his technique, a conventional corneo‐scleral approach would require a larger 10.5 to 11.5 mm incision [6].

The MITE procedure, like the SIGHIL approach, can be used for complete excision of small iris tumours affecting the pupil margin, but may also be employed for excision biopsy of larger tumours that do not involve the AC angle, or sub‐total excision of those that may involve the angle [7]. Complications include intraoperative hyphaema and post‐operative elevation of IOP, but no cases of tumour involvement of the corneal incisions have been encountered. While enabling larger tumour biopsies, the extent of tissue excision, up to 4–6 clock hours of iris means that, unlike the SIGHIL technique, primary iris closure is often not possible. Therefore, patients are consented for a staged approach. This involves (A) excision or subtotal iris tumour removal, (B) depending on margins and histology, secondary tumour treatment by brachytherapy or proton beam therapy, and (C) reconstructive surgery if required, usually with lens extraction, IOL, and intracapsular artificial iris [60].

Conventional biopsy iridectomy and excision still has a role in that it can be performed without specialist instrumentation and is within the skill set of a well‐trained ophthalmic surgeon [8, 10, 62, 63]. However, drawbacks may include poorer control of the anterior chamber structures intra‐operatively with an open eye technique, bleeding and induced astigmatism.

As previously highlighted, biopsy of intraocular tumours is well established, although perhaps used less frequently than perceived, with Shields et al. [74] initially reporting FNAB in only 2.4% (N = 159) in a series of 6500 patients referred to their major oncology service. Char [75], reflecting on diagnosis before therapy, reported on 100 consecutive patients with FNAB with presumed uveal melanoma, recording 5 false negatives and importantly 9 cases with a diagnosis other than melanoma that resulted in correct management. A high level of cytological, histological and cytogenetic certainty is provided by the more recent series highlighted in this review [3, 6, 7, 42, 52, 53, 54, 55], coupled with low complication rates. Notably, there have been rare cases of extraocular tumour extension with FNAB of choroidal and ciliary body melanoma [47, 76], but there have been no reported cases of similar events with clear corneal biopsy of AC tumours.

Although iris melanoma is often diagnosed on the basis of clinical and non‐invasive investigations, differential diagnoses include several benign lesions which may be identified by biopsy prior to definitive treatment, as noted in the preceding sections. Despite this, suspected iris melanoma is often treated by brachytherapy or proton beam therapy without a diagnostic biopsy. Indeed, in three large series treating presumed iris melanoma using proton beam radiotherapy, with N = 36, N = 54, N = 107 cases respectively, only 19%, 26% and 21.5% underwent pre‐treatment tumour biopsy [28, 77, 78]. Local tumour control was extremely good in all series; however, complications were commonly reported, with 42%– 62% developing cataract and 6%– 56% glaucoma [28, 77, 78]. In a recent study of 226 cases of presumed iris melanoma treated by proton beam, the authors noted ‘Histologic confirmation was performed for a minority of patients; it was not considered mandatory when there was evidence of tumour growth, iris tissue distortion, or involvement of the pigment layers’ [73].

To understand the long‐term impact of surgical intervention, these findings must be viewed through the lens of the Zimmerman‐McLean‐Foster hypothesis, which suggests that primary ocular tumours may seed subclinical micro‐metastases long before clinical presentation. Singh et al. [79] noted in 2004 that mortality rates for uveal melanoma for similar sized tumours were comparable for interventions such as enucleation, resection, plaque and proton therapies, and that, at the time of micro‐metastases, the primary tumour is probably small, being about 3 mm in diameter and only 1.5 mm thick. This suggests that local ocular therapy may control local disease, but will have little effect on long term metastatic disease in many cases.

Fortunately, because of their position, iris tumours are typically diagnosed earlier and at a smaller size than choroidal or ciliary body melanomas [45, 62]. Crucially, in a pivotal 30‐year comparative analysis tracking 45 iris melanomas and 268 small choroidal melanomas, Sabazade et al. [80] concluded that there was no difference in the long term prognosis between the groups if they were adjusted for tumour diameter and thickness. So it may be that the widely recorded better prognosis for iris melanomas is largely due to diagnosis at an earlier stage and smaller size, possibly before micro‐metastases have occurred. Therefore, it is unlikely that debulking the tumour size by excision biopsy will alter the systemic course of the disease in larger tumours but may limit local ocular disease. In the case of subtotal biopsy by MITE, SIGHIL or conventional iridectomy approaches, where histological and cytogenetic factors are unfavourable, local brachytherapy or proton beam treatment of residual tumour remains preferable to enucleation where suitable. In a study from Shields et al. [81] of 32 eyes with residual or recurrent iris melanoma, using an Iodine 125 plaque, local disease control was obtained in 87% of eyes at 6 years after radiotherapy, with poor visual acuity in 9%, enucleation in 13%, metastasis in 3%, cataract in 53% and elevated IOP in 19%.

In conclusion, atypical iris and anterior chamber masses may still provide a diagnostic dilemma even with advanced clinical assessment, but there are a number of biopsy approaches as outlined in this review which may enable clarification. Surprisingly, since these approaches enable not only cytological, histological and cytogenetic information, the rate of diagnostic biopsy prior to definitive treatments such as radiotherapy remains relatively low. Recent advances in the understanding of uveal melanoma have implications for both prognosis and treatment [1, 82]. Although data on iris melanoma is limited due to its low incidence, cytological and prognostic data on uveal melanoma per se have been extrapolated (with reservations) to iris melanoma, and recent studies have shown similarities in relation to iris melanoma mutations [83, 84]. Ultimately, iris biopsies have a relatively low risk, may avoid more invasive treatment and subsequent complications, may be therapeutic if additional therapy is not required, and may enable more bespoke treatments for anterior chamber tumours [85]. The risk of tumour spread appears minimal with clear corneal approaches. Contemporary restorative surgery, such as intracapsular artificial iris prostheses, means that even large iris defects may be repaired to maintain function and yet enable easy ongoing visualisation and surveillance of anterior chamber and angle structures.
