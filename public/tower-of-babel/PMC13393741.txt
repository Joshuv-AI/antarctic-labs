Remdesivir from failed Ebola drug to first FDA-approved COVID-19 antiviral: The medico-legal integration
Authors: Tung-Hu Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRemdesivir became the first antiviral for COVID-19 to receive emergency use authorization (EUA) and, subsequently, full FDA approval. This article provides a statutory analysis of its development life cycle, tracing the contributions of nine US federal statutes from foundational coronavirus science through full approval, using a qualitative regulatory-science method that combines doctrinal and document analysis. Each statute is mapped onto a staged life cycle framework. The National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, under 42 U.S.C. § 282, designed and ran the pivotal the adaptive COVID-19 treatment trial (ACTT-1), generating EUA-supporting evidence in 14 weeks; Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority’s (BARDA) Other Transaction Authority enabled at-risk manufacturing before the EUA; the Project BioShield Act addressed the market failure of an intravenous, hospital-only antiviral; and the Defense Production Act supported domestic supply. Together, these interdependent statutes show how an integrated legal architecture compressed development into months. Sustaining this institutional infrastructure is the most consequential investment in pandemic preparedness.

The 95-day puzzle: How statutes shaped a pandemic first

On May 1, 2020, the US Food and Drug Administration granted emergency use authorization (EUA) for remdesivir (Veklury; Gilead Sciences, Inc.) for hospitalized COVID-19 patients—approximately 95 days after its formal identification as a SARS-CoV-2 candidate, compressing a process that, for a new molecular entity, has historically required several years of clinical development and regulatory review. On October 22, 2020, remdesivir became the first COVID-19 therapeutic to receive full FDA approval under the New Drug Application (NDA) process, a sequence interpreted here as demonstrating that the EUA pathway can serve as a scientifically credible bridge to standard regulatory authorization.1

Remdesivir’s trajectory is distinctive because the pivotal clinical evidence supporting its authorization was generated not by the drug’s manufacturer but by the US government. The adaptive COVID-19 treatment trial (ACTT-1), designed and operated by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) under 42 U.S.C. § 282, enrolled 1,063 patients at 68 sites across multiple countries and produced the interim efficacy data on which the EUA was based.2 This government-as-sponsor arrangement is uncommon in pharmaceutical development and reflects the capabilities that § 282’s statutory framework vests in the National Institutes of Health (NIH) independently of pharmaceutical industry cooperation.

Remdesivir’s origin as a broad-spectrum adenosine nucleotide analog, developed against Ebola and later evaluated in coronavirus models, provides critical scientific context.3 The compound had been characterized as a clinical candidate before SARS-CoV-2 emerged, and NIAID’s pre-pandemic coronavirus biology research—built through § 282-funded programs following SARS-CoV-1 in 2003—appears to have substantially shortened the target-identification phase, which for many first-in-class antivirals has extended development timelines by years.4

This article provides a comprehensive statutory analysis of remdesivir’s full development life cycle, tracing the contributions of nine US federal statutes from foundational coronavirus science through full NDA approval. Remdesivir’s unique status as the first authorized and approved COVID-19 antiviral, combined with its distinctive government-operated trial architecture, makes it the foundational case study for understanding how America’s pandemic medical countermeasure (MCM) legal system functions.5,6

While the pharmacokinetics and clinical efficacy of COVID-19 antivirals have been extensively characterized elsewhere, this Perspective focuses on the complementary medico-legal context that shaped the development, authorization, and deployment of these therapeutics. The policy implications of ongoing pandemic preparedness debates remain directly relevant, as statutory and institutional decisions on maintaining these mechanisms will determine whether the system’s demonstrated capabilities can be reliably replicated.7

Tracing the law behind the drug

This article uses a qualitative regulatory=science approach to analyze statutory contributions to remdesivir’s development across its entire life cycle. The analysis combined two complementary procedures. First, doctrinal legal analysis was used to identify the operative authorities, triggering conditions, and scope of each statute from its primary text and implementing regulations. Second, structured document (content) analysis of official records was used to map each statutory authority onto the documented administrative and clinical actions taken during remdesivir’s development. Statutes were included if a primary source (a statute, regulation, Federal Register notice, agency contract or press release, congressional document, or FDA authorization record) directly linked the authority to a specific action in remdesivir’s life cycle; general appropriations or authorities with no traceable, remdesivir-specific action were excluded. “Documentary evidence” in this article refers specifically to these primary federal sources: statutory and regulatory texts, Federal Register notices, FDA EUA and approval documents, biomedical advanced research and development authority (BARDA)/Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) contract and procurement records, and congressional and Government Accountability Office (GAO) reports, with peer-reviewed literature supplementing scientific and clinical claims.

An eight-stage life cycle lens

The analysis used an eight-stage life cycle framework: (1) scientific foundation, (2) intellectual property (IP) and technology transfer, (3) emergency contracting and at-risk manufacturing, (4) regulatory authorization, (5) institutional infrastructure, (6) liability-risk management, (7) fiscal appropriation, and (8) procurement guarantee. This staged framework is the author’s analytical construct, developed for this study by adapting the general product-development life cycle and regulatory-pathway concepts familiar in regulatory science and pharmaceutical policy scholarship; it is not a pre-existing, validated model and is used here as an organizing heuristic rather than as a tested theoretical instrument. For each stage, the operational contributions of the relevant statutes were traced through documentary evidence. Additionally, interactions with cooperative statutes were characterized, and their unique contributions were assessed relative to the pre-pandemic baseline.

Nine statutes, one integrated system

The legal journey of remdesivir began pre-pandemic under 42 U.S.C. § 282 and the Bayh-Dole Act (35 U.S.C. §§ 200–212), which provided the NIH research funding and IP framework necessary for its initial discovery. As the pandemic emerged in early 2020, the federal government entered an emergency response phase, using BARDA’s Other Transaction Authority (OTA) (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6b) to fast-track development funding and Section 564 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. § 360bbb-3) to authorize its use under the EUA. During the subsequent rollout and formal approval phase, the PREP Act (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d) provided essential liability protections for manufacturers, while the CARES Act and Pandemic and All-Hazards Preparedness Act (PAHPRA) (under Pub. L. No. 116–136 and 113-5) provided supplemental R&D funding and strengthened preparedness authorities. Finally, the Project BioShield Act (6 U.S.C. § 319F-2) facilitated the government’s procurement and distribution of the drug as a critical medical countermeasure. In parallel, the DPA (50 U.S.C. §§ 4501–4568) was invoked with respect to remdesivir: under authority delegated to the HHS Secretary by executive order early in the pandemic, HHS designated remdesivir among the scarce or threatened health and medical resources subject to the Act’s section 102 anti-hoarding measures, while its priorities-and-allocation authority remained available to help direct domestic supply as shortages persisted within the US.

Scientific foundation: 42 U.S.C. § 282 and the government-operated pivotal trial

42 U.S.C. § 282 is the statutory authority that enabled the NIH/NIAID to legally initiate research, allocate funds, and conduct the ACTT-1 clinical trials during the pandemic. Section 282 of Title 42 grants the NIH Director statutory authority to conduct and support research on the causes, diagnosis, treatment, control, and prevention of diseases.8 Its contribution to remdesivir spanned two dimensions: the pre-pandemic scientific foundation that made remdesivir a credible candidate and the direct design and execution of ACTT-1.

NIAID-funded coronavirus research following the 2003 SARS-CoV-1 outbreak established that (1) coronavirus RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, nsp12) is essential for viral replication and a viable antiviral target, (2) nucleoside and nucleotide analogs active against other RNA virus RdRps cross-react with coronavirus RdRp due to a conserved active-site architecture, and (3) adenosine analogs with specific C1′ ribose modifications show enhanced inhibitory activity against coronavirus RdRp.9,10 When remdesivir was evaluated against SARS-CoV-2 RdRp in January 2020, this body of § 282-funded knowledge provided the interpretive framework, substantially reducing the target-characterization work that, for many first-in-class antivirals, adds years to the development timeline.

The preclinical history that made remdesivir a credible SARS-CoV-2 candidate was itself a product of federally funded, multi-agency research. Remdesivir originated from a Gilead nucleoside-analog library screened for antiviral activity, and its early development was driven by collaboration among Gilead, the NIH/NIAID, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the US Army Medical Research Institute of Infectious Diseases (USAMRIID). In cell-culture and animal studies conducted in part by these government laboratories, the compound showed broad-spectrum activity against RNA viruses, including Ebola virus and, critically for later events, against the zoonotic and epidemic coronaviruses SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV. Its efficacy in the rhesus macaque model of MERS-CoV infection, together with in vitro potency against diverse coronavirus polymerases, established the mechanistic rationale that made it an immediately credible candidate when SARS-CoV-2 emerged.9,10 This research was enabled by overlapping statutory authorities: NIH/NIAID research under 42 U.S.C. § 282, CDC’s public-health research authority under 42 U.S.C. § 241, and Department of Defense (DoD) medical research conducted by USAMRIID under 10 U.S.C. § 4001 (formerly 10 U.S.C. § 2358). Independent accounts of these federal contributions—including the roles of CDC and USAMRIID alongside NIH—are provided by Krellenstein and Morten11 and by the US GAO.12 The GAO report additionally attempts a partial attribution of public versus private development investment, which informs the financial analysis below.

ACTT-1 represents § 282’s most operationally distinctive contribution. NIAID’s statutory authority enabled it to design a double-blind, placebo-controlled, adaptive trial; activate 68 international sites; enroll 1,063 patients; generate an interim efficacy analysis demonstrating a 31% improvement in time to recovery (11 versus 15 days); and provide these data to the FDA as the primary EUA evidentiary basis—all within approximately 14 weeks2,13 NIAID had pre-existing master clinical trial agreements with all 68 ACTT-1 sites, pre-established data safety monitoring board relationships, and direct FDA communication channels built through decades of NIAID-FDA collaboration on HIV, influenza, and prior coronavirus programs.14 Activating this pre-positioned infrastructure allowed ACTT-1 to proceed at a pace no sponsor-operated trial could match, regardless of financial resources.

IP framework: Bayh-Dole Act (35 U.S.C. §§ 200–212)

The Bayh-Dole Act of 1980 established the framework under which institutions receiving federal funding may retain patent ownership of inventions resulting from their federally funded research.15 Remdesivir’s IP landscape is structurally distinctive: Gilead holds primary patents based on internal drug discovery, asserting independent invention status outside Bayh-Dole’s coverage, while NIH- and USAMRIID-funded research programs contributed foundational scientific frameworks, and university partners whose NIH-funded structural biology data informed remdesivir’s optimization hold patents licensable under Bayh-Dole terms.16

The Bayh-Dole march-in rights controversy over remdesivir’s commercial pricing—approximately $3,120 per 5-day course for insured patients—sparked a major COVID-19 march-in petition, which established the policy framework for subsequent pricing debates over molnupiravir and Paxlovid.17 On August 4, 2020, a bipartisan coalition of 34 state and territorial attorneys general formally petitioned HHS, NIH, and the FDA to exercise march-in rights and license remdesivir to additional manufacturers.18 The agencies immediately pushed back: in a statement to the press, an HHS spokesperson clarified that the government can exercise march-in rights only when the underlying IP for the product as a whole was funded by the federal government.19 Because remdesivir’s core composition-of-matter patents were privately held, the agencies maintained they fell outside the statutory framework—a position later supported by a GAO analysis indicating that the core patents were not subject to the Bayh-Dole Act.12 The tensions here are therefore between, on one side, advocates and state officials who argued that substantial public investment in remdesivir’s scientific foundation justified price or licensing interventions, and, on the other, the agencies’ and Gilead’s position that the specific patents were privately owned and legally out of reach. Notwithstanding this dispute, the broader IP ecosystem enabled rapid global deployment: Gilead bypassed central clearinghouses like the Medicines Patent Pool and issued direct, voluntary, non-exclusive licenses to multiple generic manufacturers to supply 127 low- and middle-income countries. These outcomes illustrate the enabling role of the modern IP framework in facilitating global access, even when its most contested statutory intervention provisions were not formally triggered.

Emergency contracting: BARDA OTA (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6b)

While standard Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) procurement requires competitive solicitation, documentation, and approval chains that collectively take 12–18 months, OTA enables flexible, milestone-based arrangements outside the FAR.20 For remdesivir, the foundational statutory flexibility arose not during the pandemic but in 2016, when the DoD executed a prototyping OTA with Gilead to evaluate the drug’s efficacy against Ebola and other filoviruses.

BARDA’s 2020 OTA engagement with Gilead encompassed three parallel activities: co-investment in additional ACTT-1 clinical sites beyond those that NIAID could activate directly; funding for manufacturing process optimization to support large-scale production of the intravenous formulation; and drug-substance scale-up commitments executed before EUA issuance.21 The at-risk manufacturing concept warrants particular emphasis: without OTA authority, BARDA could not legally commit to funding the manufacture of an unapproved product, and given the commercial uncertainty of an unapproved compound, the likelihood that Gilead would have independently proceeded with commercial-scale manufacturing before EUA issuance was low.22

BARDA’s OTA agreements with Gilead for remdesivir manufacturing were executed within weeks of the pandemic declaration—a direct prerequisite for remdesivir supply to be available when the EUA was granted in May 2020.

Regulatory authorization: FD&C Act section 564 EUA

Section 564 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. § 360bbb-3) authorizes the FDA to issue EUAs for unapproved products when HHS declares that justifying circumstances exist, the FDA concludes that the benefits outweigh the risks, and no adequate alternatives are available. Remdesivir’s EUA process set four precedents that shaped all subsequent COVID-19 therapeutic authorizations.

First, ACTT-1 established that data from government-operated adaptive trials would serve as the primary basis for EUAs. Because NIAID had pre-established regulatory science relationships with the FDA, including pre-agreed endpoints and analytical methods, the FDA could accept NIAID’s statistical design choices without requiring independent verification, thereby reducing the regulatory science uncertainty typically associated with early EUA applications.23

Second, the FDA applied § 564’s “may be effective” standard to ACTT-1’s interim primary finding of a 31% improvement in time to recovery (median 11 versus 15 days), despite the absence of a demonstrated mortality reduction. This established that the statutory threshold accommodates outcomes-adjacent endpoints when the mortality effect is directionally positive but underpowered at the time of authorization.24 Subsequent evidence on mortality has remained mixed. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis reported a small mortality benefit in some hospitalized subgroups, particularly patients not requiring high-flow oxygen or mechanical ventilation;25 whereas other analyses found no mortality reduction among patients requiring high-flow supplemental oxygen or invasive mechanical ventilation.26

Third, the EUA-to-full-approval transition on October 22, 2020, approximately 5.5 months after EUA issuance, showed that EUA-standard evidence generated through randomized controlled methods can be compatible with the subsequent attainment of the ‘substantial evidence of effectiveness’ standard for full approval. This single transition is best read as an early, illustrative precedent for—rather than definitive empirical validation of—§ 564’s design as a temporary bridge, issued before the vaccine EUAs.

Fourth, the EUA indication expansion on August 28, 2020, to all hospitalized patients under § 564(g) demonstrated real-time adaptive regulatory flexibility without requiring full reauthorization,27 a mechanism used in virtually every subsequent COVID-19 EUA.

Institutional infrastructure: PAHPRA

The PAHPRA and its subsequent reauthorizations, most notably the 2013 PAHPRA and the 2019 Pandemic and All-Hazards Preparedness and Advancing Innovation Act (PAHPAIA), established BARDA’s organizational authorities and interagency coordination mechanisms, enabling other statutory interventions to operate at pandemic speed.28 By January 2020, this institutional investment had produced an emergency-response enterprise with experienced contracting and legal teams, established relationships with project officers at pharmaceutical manufacturers, and scientific staff with deep expertise in antiviral development. Without this accumulated institutional capacity across HHS and the DoD, the flexible contracting frameworks and allocation agreements for remdesivir would likely have taken substantially longer to negotiate.

Furthermore, PAHPAIA’s interagency coordination frameworks among HHS, NIH, the FDA, and the DoD provided the institutional infrastructure through which ACTT-1 data flowed seamlessly to the FDA’s review division, enabling the interim analysis to be completed in approximately 48 h. Crucially, the 2013 PAHPRA amendments to § 564’s EUA trigger provisions—which decoupled EUA declarations from the formal § 319 Public Health Emergency (PHE) declaration by allowing triggers based on “significant potential” for an emergency—granted the FDA the flexibility to prepare regulatory frameworks before formal PHE constraints were met.

Liability risk management: PREP Act (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d)

The Public PREP Act of 2005 provides immunity from tort liability to manufacturers, distributors, program planners, and qualified persons administering covered countermeasures under an applicable HHS Secretary declaration.29 The PREP Act does not simply transfer liability from manufacturers to the federal government; for covered claims other than willful misconduct, it forecloses ordinary tort suits entirely and channels eligible injured persons to a no-fault administrative remedy, the Countermeasures Injury Compensation Program, rather than to the courts. HHS Secretary Azar’s PREP Act declaration for COVID-19 MCMs—signed on March 10, 2020, and published in the Federal Register on March 17, 2020—categorically covered “any antiviral.” This broad definition provided immediate liability protection for the entire remdesivir administration chain—hospital formulary committees, pharmacy directors, nurses, physicians, and Gilead as the manufacturer—without requiring drug-specific declaration processes.30

Without PREP Act coverage, hospital formulary committees would have been required to individually assess institutional liability exposure to stocking EUA antiviral under an evidentiary standard explicitly lower than that required for standard formulary inclusion. The PREP Act’s automatic categorical coverage removed this institutional barrier and likely enabled remdesivir to reach hospitalized patients far more quickly than sequential, drug-specific formulary liability reviews would have permitted, though the exact time saved cannot be quantified relative to a controlled baseline.

Fiscal architecture: CARES Act emergency appropriations

The Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act (CARES Act, P.L. 116–136), enacted on March 27, 2020, provided the emergency appropriations needed to implement the government’s pandemic response.31 The CARES Act’s timing was critical: it was enacted approximately 7 weeks after NIAID identified remdesivir as a priority candidate and about 5 weeks before the ACTT-1 trial reached its interim analysis threshold. Rather than financing the federal bulk purchase of finished remdesivir doses or the manufacturing scale-up contracts (the latter funded through BARDA OTAs), which Gilead and the commercial market largely absorbed, CARES Act appropriations funded critical institutional infrastructure. This included the rapid expansion of NIAID’s ACTT-1 clinical networks, the logistical operations supporting the HHS supply allocation agreements, and emergency supplemental funding for the FDA to hire additional reviewers and expand concurrent COVID-19 review capacity.

Among COVID-19 antivirals, remdesivir’s entire development lifecycle—from CARES Act enactment to full NDA approval in October 2020—was completed within a single appropriations cycle of about 7 months. By contrast, subsequent oral antivirals (such as molnupiravir and Paxlovid) required the American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) to recapitalize the HHS to execute their decisive multibillion-dollar procurement agreements. This contrast highlights remdesivir’s temporal efficiency and, on this reading, illustrates what is termed a “single-appropriations-cycle” pattern: a development-to-approval pathway completed within a single emergency appropriation, without recourse to a later, separate appropriation.

Procurement guarantee: Project BioShield Act

While mechanisms such as the Project BioShield Act of 2004 established HHS’s authority to use the Special Reserve Fund to procure medical countermeasures, remdesivir’s market guarantee was structurally distinct.32 Remdesivir’s commercial situation exhibited features of market failure: as an intravenous, hospital-only antiviral, it lacked a commercial outpatient market, so its revenue potential depended entirely on the number of hospitalized COVID-19 patients—a quantity that was uncertain, highly variable with the epidemic’s trajectory, and subject to rapid obsolescence as other therapeutics emerged.33

Rather than procuring directly from the Special Reserve Fund, HHS addressed revenue uncertainty by entering into an unprecedented supply agreement in late June 2020. Following Gilead’s initial global donation of 1.5 million vials, HHS secured 100% of Gilead’s projected July production and 90% of August and September production, totaling over 500,000 treatment courses.34 Under this framework, HHS determined the allocation of the drug supply to state health departments, while US hospitals purchased the drug directly from Gilead’s sole authorized US distributor, AmerisourceBergen (now Cencora), rather than from the federal government. Pricing was set at a Wholesale Acquisition Cost of $3,120 per course for commercially insured patients, with a discounted rate of $2,340 for direct government purchasers such as the US Department of Veterans Affairs and the DoD.

While Gilead absorbed the upfront, at-risk manufacturing investment independently, this federal allocation strategy can be interpreted as a downstream market guarantee. It established a defined commercial pipeline, set a national pricing framework, and signaled the government’s commitment to prioritizing US supply chains, thereby organizing the hospital market and mitigating commercial volatility for the manufacturer.

Domestic supply prioritization: DPA (50 U.S.C. §§ 4501–4568)

Operating alongside the BioShield procurement framework, the DPA provides distinct statutory levers focused not on financing supply but on controlling its allocation and preventing its diversion. Title I of the DPA authorizes the President to require priority performance of contracts and to allocate scarce materials to promote the national defense, while section 102 (50 U.S.C. 4512) authorizes measures to prevent the hoarding of designated scarce or threatened materials,35 the authorities long understood to encompass public health emergencies. Early in the pandemic, the President delegated these authorities to the HHS Secretary by executive order: priorities-and-allocation authority under Executive Order 13909, and section 102 anti-hoarding authority under Executive Order 13910.36,37 The DPA was expressly invoked with respect to remdesivir. On July 23, 2020, HHS extended its March 25, 2020, designation of scarce or threatened materials to add the drug products then recommended by the NIH COVID-19 Treatment Guidelines Panel—specifically remdesivir and dexamethasone—thereby subjecting remdesivir to the Act’s prohibitions on accumulation in excess of reasonable needs and on resale above prevailing market prices.38 At the same time, the government did not rely on Title I priority-rating of Gilead’s contracts to secure the bulk of supply; instead, through a negotiated agreement with Gilead Sciences, it secured the bulk of remdesivir’s imminent domestic production,34 allowing HHS and state health departments to implement a data-driven distribution framework that directed the constrained early supply to US hospitals rather than to foreign markets. The relative weight of these instruments is not fully resolved in the public record: it is plausible that the negotiated supply agreement and the state-managed allocation framework were the operative mechanisms keeping remdesivir within the US, with the section 102 designation functioning as a complementary safeguard against hoarding and resale. Its role illustrates that deploying emergency countermeasures depends not only on developing and purchasing a product but also on the legal capacity to prioritize, retain, and channel a limited domestic supply.

What the remdesivir story reveals

Remdesivir as the foundational MCM case study

The present analysis establishes remdesivir as the foundational case study of the COVID-19 MCM statutory architecture, not because it was the most clinically effective COVID-19 antiviral authorized (in the EPIC-HR [evaluation of protease inhibition for COVID-19 in high-risk patients] trial, nirmatrelvir-ritonavir [Paxlovid] reduced the relative risk of COVID-19-related hospitalization or death by 89% versus placebo when started within three days of symptom onset in high-risk, unvaccinated outpatients,39 an endpoint not directly comparable to remdesivir’s recovery-time benefit in hospitalized patients), because it was the first authorized antiviral. Remdesivir’s development during the COVID-19 pandemic can be understood as the first real-world test of the core MCM legal mechanisms that subsequent antivirals relied on: the NIAID adaptive trial model, agile OTA preclinical frameworks, federal supply allocation agreements, CARES Act infrastructure funding, § 564 EUAs, PREP Act categorical liability coverage, and PAHPAIA interagency coordination.40 It is plausible that, without this early experience, subsequent developers and regulators would have faced greater uncertainty about how these mechanisms would function in a real-world pandemic.

The NIAID government-operated adaptive trial model represents the most institutionally significant contribution to remdesivir’s life cycle. The ACTT-1 design was accepted by the FDA without the extended negotiation typically associated with adaptive design submissions, because NIAID’s established regulatory science relationship provided the credibility for the FDA to accept NIAID’s statistical design choices without independent verification.41 The Accelerating COVID-19 Therapeutic Interventions and Vaccines (ACTIV) public-private partnership subsequently developed directly from this ACTT-1 experience, institutionalizing the government-operated adaptive trial model as a standing infrastructure capable of rapid activation for future pandemic therapeutics.14

Market failure correction and pricing legacy

Remdesivir illustrates with unusual clarity why pandemic MCM development requires government intervention to correct structural market failures that purely commercial development cannot address. Its intravenous, hospital-based formulation created a more severe market-failure profile than subsequent oral antivirals, with no commercial outpatient market, undefined insurance reimbursement pathways, and large manufacturing capital requirements, necessitating decisive federal intervention.

Remdesivir’s commercial pricing generated a major COVID-19 march-in rights petition and remains an unresolved policy legacy. The government’s substantial contributions—funding foundational RdRp research, executing the pivotal ACTT-1 trial, and organizing a guaranteed national allocation pipeline—created a level of public investment that many analysts argued justified imposing price constraints the government declined to impose.17 The commercial stakes were substantial. According to Gilead’s public financial disclosures, worldwide Veklury (remdesivir) sales were approximately $2.8 billion in 2020, rose to about $5.6 billion in 2021, and then declined to roughly $3.9 billion in 2022, about $2.2 billion in 2023, and about $1.8 billion in 2024, with continued sales thereafter cumulatively exceeding $16 billion in revenue within its first 5 years of availability.42 Gilead does not separately report product-level profit for remdesivir, so net margins cannot be stated precisely. The 2023 Biden administration march-in rights framework, which recognized pricing as a potential basis for march-in determination, represents a belated policy acknowledgment of this argument, though remdesivir itself was not subject to proceedings under the revised framework. Future pandemic preparedness legislation should address this gap by attaching pricing provisions to advance purchase or allocation agreements rather than relying on post hoc march-in proceedings. More recent policy has begun to move in this direction: the NIH Intramural Research Program Access Planning Policy (NOT-OD-25-136) attaches access and pricing-related planning provisions to licenses of NIH-owned IP, as commonly occurs when the NIH co-develops a medical product with a private partner, thereby embedding access conditions ex ante rather than relying on after-the-fact march-in determinations.43,44

Institutional maintenance requirements

Remdesivir’s 95-day bench-to-EUA timeline was achievable only because NIAID’s site networks, BARDA’s contracting capacity, and FDA’s collaborative review protocols were already operational when SARS-CoV-2 emerged. The historical pattern of post-pandemic legislative attention followed by inter-pandemic institutional neglect is well documented across HIV, anthrax, H1N1, and MERS response cycles.45 Based on this single-case analysis, the following is offered as a policy interpretation rather than a generalizable prediction: future pandemic MCM timelines are likely to be longer unless the institutional infrastructure that enabled remdesivir’s rapid development is actively sustained through consistent funding, legislative reauthorization, and operational exercises.46 Confirming this expectation would require a comparative study across multiple countermeasures and emergencies.

What a single-case analysis cannot show

This article is subject to several limitations. Most fundamentally, it is a single-case statutory analysis. Its findings are interpretive and illustrative rather than statistically generalizable, and the causal and comparative inferences it draws, particularly regarding timeline compression and institutional effectiveness, cannot be validated by a single-case counterfactual. The eight-stage framework is an organizing heuristic developed for this study rather than a pre-existing, externally validated model, and the selection of statutes, while governed by the inclusion criteria stated in the STAR Methods, necessarily reflects analytical judgment. The terms of the BARDA-Gilead OTA agreement are partially redacted in publicly available procurement disclosures, so reliance is placed on aggregate financial figures from HHS press releases and congressional testimony. The precise attribution of development costs between government investment and Gilead’s proprietary expenditure is not publicly available with full granularity; the most detailed public attempt at such an accounting is the 2021 GAO report, which is drawn on above.12 As noted by FDA reviewers, the emergency trial conditions of ACTT-1 may have introduced limitations not present in standard clinical settings.

Sustaining the architecture before the next emergency

Remdesivir’s journey from a failed Ebola candidate to an FDA-approved COVID-19 antiviral in about 10 months provides a foundational demonstration that America’s pandemic MCM legal architecture can function as an integrated system, compressing multi-year development timelines into months. Its statutory legacy rests on four policy innovations that this case illustrates: (1) the NIAID government-operated adaptive trial, a faster evidence-generation mechanism than sponsor-operated development; (2) OTA at-risk manufacturing, the legally viable mechanism for pre-authorization supply investment; (3) FD&C Act § 564 EUA, a scientifically credible and legally defensible bridge to full approval; and (4) BioShield guaranteed government purchase, the essential correction to pandemic intravenous antiviral market failure.

The nine statutes analyzed operate not as parallel, independent instruments but as a sequentially dependent, integrated system in which each statute’s output serves as the operational prerequisite for the next. NIAID’s § 282 science made remdesivir a recognizable candidate; Bayh-Dole’s IP framework made Gilead’s collaboration commercially rational; OTA made manufacturing available at authorization; BioShield made government purchase commitments viable; the DPA was invoked to help retain and protect the domestic supply against hoarding and diversion; the CARES Act provided fiscal capital for all of the above; § 564 translated evidence into patient access; the PREP Act eliminated institutional liability barriers; and PAHPRA’s institutional infrastructure enabled all other mechanisms to operate at pandemic speed. Without any single component, the system would have produced a materially slower outcome.

The most important lesson from remdesivir’s statutory history is the need for institutional maintenance. The speed achieved was determined by the infrastructure already in place when SARS-CoV-2 emerged. Future pandemic responses will replicate remdesivir’s speed only if these institutional capabilities are actively sustained during the inter-pandemic period through consistent funding, legislative reauthorization, and operational exercise. The statutory architecture that made remdesivir possible is not self-sustaining, and the most consequential pandemic-preparedness investment is ensuring it remains operational before the next emergency requires its activation.

The success of remdesivir’s full FDA approval for COVID-19 cannot be achieved through medical or legal efforts alone; rather, it requires integrating medical and legal approaches.
