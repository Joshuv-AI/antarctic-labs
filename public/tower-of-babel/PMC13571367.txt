Severe Biventricular Takotsubo Syndrome Triggered by Status Epilepticus With Pulmonary Embolism and Critical Illness Myopathy
Authors: Akash Dontamsetty, Usman Bashir Itoo, Rachita Kour, Jaison Lawrence Alexander Santhi, Muzamil Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Takotsubo syndrome is an acute syndrome of transient ventricular systolic dysfunction that often presents similarly to acute coronary syndrome (ACS), with chest pain, electrocardiographic abnormalities, biomarker elevation, and regional wall-motion abnormalities.1 Although historically associated with emotional stress, physical stressors are now commonly recognized, particularly in critically ill patients.2 Neurologic insults have emerged as an important context for Takotsubo syndrome, and the interaction between acute brain injury, autonomic dysregulation, and myocardial dysfunction is increasingly appreciated as part of a broader brain-heart axis.3 Status epilepticus is an especially relevant trigger because it can generate profound sympathetic discharge, with marked catecholamine release and downstream effects leading to myocardial stunning.1 Pulmonary embolism (PE) may provide an additional physiologic burden through hypoxemia, adrenergic activation, and acute right-sided hemodynamic stress, and has also been described in association with Takotsubo syndrome.4Take-Home Messages•Takotsubo syndrome should be considered in patients with status epilepticus who develop hemodynamic deterioration.•These patients need serial reassessment during recovery because severe neurologic illness may evolve into a prolonged multisystem critical illness.

We report a case of severe but reversible biventricular stress cardiomyopathy following status epilepticus in the setting of subsegmental PE, followed by prolonged recovery complicated by critical illness myopathy (CIM).

Case Presentation

History of presentation

An 81-year-old woman was found unresponsive at home. On arrival at the emergency department on February 12, 2026, she developed a generalized tonic-clonic seizure requiring intravenous lorazepam administration and endotracheal intubation for airway protection. Initial evaluation revealed severe leukocytosis, acute kidney injury and markedly elevated liver enzymes. CT scan results for the head, cervical spine and maxillofacial region were negative for acute intracranial abnormality, fracture or subluxation. Computed tomography angiography of the chest demonstrated subsegmental PE involving the right upper lobe and demonstrated no findings of right heart strain.

Past medical history

Past medical history included asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnoses included ACS, Coxsackie meningitis, myocarditis, and pheochromocytoma.

Investigations

In the intensive care unit (ICU), rapid encephalogram monitoring demonstrated a diffuse encephalopathy with highly epileptogenic foci and a 30% seizure burden, consistent with nonconvulsive status epilepticus.

Initial cardiac biomarkers were significantly elevated, with a peak troponin level of 3,956 ng/L and a brain natriuretic peptide level of 3,573 pg/mL. A limited initial transthoracic echocardiogram on February 11 reported normal left ventricular size and systolic function (ejection fraction approximately 60%), though it was technically difficult with no apical windows obtained. However, a follow-up echocardiogram on February 12 revealed deterioration with severe biventricular systolic dysfunction with a left ventricular ejection fraction of <20%. The left ventricle exhibited global hypokinesis with best-preserved wall motion in the basal segments of the lateral, posterior, and anterior walls—consistent with the apical ballooning variant of Takotsubo syndrome. Right ventricular systolic function was also severely reduced. Interventional cardiology assessed the presentation as most consistent with stress-induced biventricular cardiomyopathy triggered by status epilepticus.

A follow-up echocardiogram on February 19 demonstrated recovery with normalization of the left ventricular ejection fraction to 55% to 60% and resolution of the right ventricular dysfunction.

Management

She was started on heparin anticoagulation, loaded with levetiracetam, and treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics before admission to the ICU.

In the ICU, she was treated for recurrent generalized tonic-clonic seizures and status epilepticus with levetiracetam, lacosamide, and benzodiazepines.

Shortly after, she developed cardiogenic shock requiring aggressive vasopressor and inotropic support with norepinephrine, vasopressin, and milrinone. She was additionally managed with intravenous diuresis. Her cardiogenic shock resolved over the following 2 weeks, and vasopressors were discontinued by February 24. After successful extubation and resolution of her acute kidney injury and ischemic hepatopathy, on February 26, she was transferred out of the ICU and was managed on the medical floor. While on the medical floor, because of prolonged critical illness, she developed diffuse and symmetrical weakness. She was clinically diagnosed with CIM and transferred to an inpatient rehabilitation unit. After a 10-day rehabilitation course, she was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up

On April 21, she followed up with a brain natriuretic peptide level improved to 61 pg/mL, and was recommended to continue supportive management.

Discussion

This case is notable because it unifies several high-acuity events within a single hospitalization, including status epilepticus, severe, reversible biventricular systolic dysfunction, cardiogenic shock, subsegmental PE, and CIM. Among these events, status epilepticus is the most compelling precipitant of Takotsubo syndrome.4 Neurologic disease is increasingly recognized in association with Takotsubo syndrome, and contemporary cohort data suggest that these patients represent a higher-risk subgroup with more frequent in-hospital complications, cardiogenic shock, and worse short- and long-term outcomes.3 Therefore, this case fits within the broader concept of neurocardiogenic injury occurring along the brain-heart axis.

The most widely accepted pathophysiologic model for Takotsubo syndrome describes excessive sympathetic stimulation, triggering a catecholamine-mediated cascade that leads to myocardial stunning in a susceptible ventricle.1 Status epilepticus is a plausible trigger because prolonged seizure activity can generate autonomic discharge with profound hemodynamic fluctuations and marked neurohumoral stress.5 PE may also amplify neurocardiac stress through acute hypoxemia, heightened adrenergic activation, increased right ventricular afterload, and systemic hemodynamic strain.6 These symptoms can lead to transient hypoperfusion to the brain, producing cortical irritability and lowering seizure threshold. This patient had no seizure history and developed status epilepticus and PE. As such, it is possible that their concurrent PE, despite being subsegmental, may have led to subsequent seizure activity (Figure 1). At the same time, the discussion of seizure-associated Takotsubo syndrome should remain measured. Although case reports and small series clearly support an association between seizures, status epilepticus, and Takotsubo syndrome, the true prevalence of Takotsubo syndrome among patients with status epilepticus remains uncertain.5 This case’s significance is therefore not that it demonstrates a common complication, but rather that it illustrates a severe and reversible cardiac consequence of prolonged neurologic stress in an elderly patient.7 This distinction is important because overstated epidemiologic claims can undermine the credibility of an otherwise worthwhile case report.

The echocardiographic findings further strengthen the diagnosis (Figure 2 and 3). Severe depression of left ventricular systolic function to an ejection fraction of <20%, associated right ventricular dysfunction, cardiogenic shock, and subsequent recovery to normal systolic function are compatible with severe Takotsubo presentations described in the literature (Table 1).8 Biventricular involvement is clinically meaningful because it is associated with greater acuity and a more complicated in-hospital course. The initial limited echocardiogram, which failed to capture the apical ballooning, highlights the diagnostic challenges in critically ill patients and the necessity of comprehensive imaging once clinical deterioration occurs. Nevertheless, Takotsubo syndrome remains a diagnosis that must be distinguished from ACS, myocarditis, and sepsis-related cardiomyopathy. Of interest is the persistence of a dilated inferior vena cava (22.60 mm) on day 8 despite the apparent recovery of biventricular systolic function (Figure 1). This illustrates that systolic recovery often precedes complete hemodynamic normalization in Takotsubo syndrome. The dilated inferior vena cava in this patient likely reflects a combination of persistent severe diastolic dysfunction—a hallmark of the recovery phase of stress cardiomyopathy—and the residual afterload burden from the concurrent subsegmental PE. The delayed resolution of diastolic dysfunction and right atrial pressure elevation highlights the importance of comprehensive echocardiographic assessment beyond simple ejection fraction recovery.

The patient's later profound weakness requiring rehabilitation adds a longitudinal dimension to the report. Research describes CIM as diffuse, symmetrical weakness arising after ICU admission and emphasizes its association with prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer ICU stay, and adverse functional outcomes.9 This framework is more defensible than attributing skeletal muscle injury solely to catecholamine excess. Although a shared stress-mediated mechanism may be hypothesized, the more evidence-based interpretation is that the patient's weakness most likely emerged from the cumulative burden of prolonged critical illness.

This case also has practical clinical implications. In patients with status epilepticus who develop unexplained shock, pulmonary edema, troponin elevation, or electrocardiographic abnormalities, early cardiac evaluation with echocardiography should be considered. Failure to recognize stress-induced cardiomyopathy in this setting may delay appropriate hemodynamic support. Beyond echocardiography, this can be accomplished via modalities such as the InterTAK prognostic score. This method does not require imaging and uses data that can be collected in the emergency department, demonstrating its effectiveness in quickly identifying Takotsubo syndrome. If these measures were to be integrated in-patient, it would undoubtedly lead to improved clinical outcomes.

As patients recover from the acute phases of illness, clinicians should reassess for post-ICU complications such as profound weakness, impaired mobility, and respiratory muscle dysfunction, all of which may affect long-term recovery.10 Limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this is a single case, mechanistic conclusions remain inferential. Second, the extent to which PE directly precipitated Takotsubo syndrome, rather than simply coexisting with it, cannot be definitively established. Severe neurologic stress may coincide with reversible cardiomyopathy and a prolonged multisystem critical illness course that requires careful serial reassessment.

Conclusions

Status epilepticus can trigger Takotsubo cardiomyopathy via the brain-heart axis, along with biventricular dysfunction and shock. Rapid deterioration followed by recovery on serial echocardiography helps distinguish it from other causes of acute cardiac dysfunction. Concurrent stressors such as PE may worsen the clinical course. Early cardiac evaluation, diagnostic scoring tools, and attention to post-ICU complications such as CIM are all essential.
