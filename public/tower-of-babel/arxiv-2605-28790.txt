Multiscale Vectorial Determination of Magnetic Order Parameters using Electron Magnetic Linear Dichroism Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jan Hajduček, Jáchym Štindl, Ján Rusz, Vojtěch Uhlíř

Abstract
We demonstrate electron magnetic linear dichroism as a quantitative probe of vectorial magnetic order parameters with nanometer resolution in transmission electron microscopy. Explicit inclusion of vectorial core-level exchange splitting into mixed dynamic form factor simulations accounting for dynamical diffraction enables direct reconstruction of the magnetic spin axis from momentum-resolved electron energy-loss spectra. The resulting dichroic signal is intrinsically separable from nonmagnetic anisotropy, exhibits a well-defined dependence on the Néel vector or magnetization orientation, and remains robust down to the atomic scale. Applied to the collinear antiferromagnetic and ferromagnetic phase of cubic FeRh, this approach allows quantitative real-space mapping of the magnetic vector. These results open a pathway to nanoscale spectroscopy and imaging of antiferromagnets and altermagnets where the generalized approach to electron dichroism provides direct access to different magnetic order parameters.



Introduction:
Nanoscale reconstruction of magnetic order remains a central challenge in spintronics and quantum materials, particularly for compensated magnetic phases such as antiferromagnetic (AF)

A complementary route to magnetic dichroism is provided by inelastic electron scattering in a transmission electron microscope (TEM), commonly used for atomic-scale materials characterization. Momentum-resolved electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) enables detection of electron dichroism: energy loss resolves electronic transitions, while momentum transfer encodes the chirality required for circular dichroism

While the circular form of electron dichroism is now well established for high-resolution magnetic characterization, the linear form of electron dichroism (ELD), sensitive to anisotropic transitions, has so far been explored mainly in nonmagnetic systems for directions transversal to the electron beam

Origin of EMCD and EMLD:
The scattering geometry underlying EMD is shown in Fig.
1
(a). An incident electron beam undergoes elastic and inelastic scattering in the FeRh crystal, forming a diffraction pattern in the TEM back focal plane, with inelastic intensity localized around Bragg reflections. By selecting energy and momentum transfer in this plane, specific electronic transitions are probed. In transition metals, these correspond to core-level excitations (e.g.,
2p\rightarrow 3d
at the
L_{2,3}
edges), where the unoccupied
3d
states carry magnetic information. Spin–orbit coupling and core-level exchange splitting lead to anisotropic transition probabilities, projecting as EMLD, while spin-dependent asymmetry projects as EMCD.

We consider a geometry with magnetic moments perpendicular to the incident beam (in-plane). EMCD is accessed via conjugate aperture positions
\Omega^{+}
and
\Omega^{-}
in the diffraction plane, typically under two- or three-beam conditions that enhance sensitivity to chiral transitions
Song et al. 2017
;
Song and Dunin-Borkowski 2021
. In contrast, the linear dichroic component (EMLD/ENLD) is obtained by comparing intensities along orthogonal momentum-transfer directions,
\Omega^{\parallel}
and
\Omega^{\perp}
, analogous to XMLD. Aperture positions are guided by calculated dichroic signal maps, while their size is maximized to ensure sufficient count rates without compromising momentum selectivity.

Hébert and Schattschneider 2003
;
Schattschneider et al. 2006
. Because valence-state polarization is present in the FM phase but absent in the AF phase, EMCD is detectable only in the FM phase, provided the electron interaction volume extends beyond individual atomic columns
Song et al. 2026

Structural anisotropy, in turn, manifests in EELS as ENLD
Hébert and Schattschneider 2003
. In cubic metallic magnets with weak structural anisotropy, such as FeRh, the ENLD response is small in both magnetic phases, unlike systems with strong orbital anisotropy, including transition-metal oxides, AF insulators, layered magnets, and compounds with large magnetocrystalline anisotropy
Gong et al. 2017
;
Meer et al. 2023
. By including the exchange splitting, the degeneracy of spin-dependent states is lifted, giving rise to EMLD signal. As exchange splitting is present in both AF and FM phases, EMLD can be detected in both, as is the case for XMLD
Stöhr and Siegmann 2006
;
Kuch et al. 2007
. Inclusion of exchange splitting is formalized in END MATTER. Although EMLD and ENLD are accessed within the same aperture geometry, their magnitudes differ significantly, and they can be experimentally separated from each other by exploiting their distinct physical origins. Pure ENLD can be isolated by suppressing magnetic order (e.g., heating above the Néel temperature, where EMLD vanishes). Conversely, pure EMLD can be obtained by reversing or rotating the magnetic axis while keeping the detection geometry fixed, for which ENLD remains invariant. This separation is fully analogous to XMLD-based protocols for disentangling magnetic and structural anisotropy
Guo et al. 1994
;
Stöhr and Siegmann 2006
.

Overall, we show that magnetic dichroic components can be separated via aperture selection, as demonstrated for EMCD

;
Song and Dunin-Borkowski 2021
and EMLD. Quantitative separation of the EMCD and EMLD contributions is evaluated in Supp. Note S1
EML

Vectorial EMLD simulations:
The inclusion of the exchange splitting effectively introduces a uniaxial anisotropy tied to the magnetic axis and as a result, the EMLD signal becomes explicitly sensitive to the orientation of the magnetic moment. Correct treatment of the directional dependence is implemented into the simulation procedure from Refs.
Schattschneider et al. 2006
;
Rusz et al. 2007a

(i) accepts first-principles transition matrix elements from DFT, (ii) rotates of the spin and orbital reference frame via Euler transformations, and (iii) constructs the mixed dynamic form factor (MDFF) combined with dynamical diffraction for arbitrary spin axis. Different components of the magnetic vector can thus be selectively probed using tailored aperture geometries. As demonstrated in Refs.
Yuan and Menon 1997
;
van Aken and Lauterbach 2003

Complementarily, sensitivity to in-plane (IP) components is obtained by employing off-center aperture positions in the diffraction plane, enabling discrimination between orthogonal IP directions, as illustrated in Fig.
1
for the AF phase of FeRh.

Taken together, this unified approach enables consistent calculation and interpretation of both EMCD and EMLD signals for arbitrary orientations of the magnetization or Néel vector. It thereby provides a direct and general route to vectorial magnetic reconstruction in both AF and FM systems.

Discussion:
The framework presented for isolating the magnetic circular, linear, and natural linear electron dichroism components, highlighting the vectorial reconstruction, opens the possibility for experimental reconstruction of the spin axis in various magnetic orderings.

A key advantage of using EMLD for vectorial reconstruction is its robustness across scales and parameter space. First, EMLD persists over a broad range of crystal and electron beam orientations. Unlike EMCD, which requires near-ideal two- or three-beam conditions
Schattschneider et al. 2006
(see Supp. Note S3
EML
), the EMLD signal remains present across an extended angular phase space of incident directions
\vec{k}_{i}
(see Fig.
4
). Although the magnitude and sign of EMLD vary continuously with crystal orientation (Fig.
4
(a) for IP component), the presented simulation framework allows its full predictive control. Notably, a signal instability occurs for beam incidence along the zone axis due to dynamical diffraction, which motivates the use of a small tilt, as adopted throughout this work. In contrast, the OOP component remains stable over the same angular range (Fig.
4
(b)). Second, the EMLD signal is robust with respect to sample thickness, retaining both its sign and spectral shape (see Supp. Note S4
EML
). Third,
we show that EMLD retains sensitivity to AF order and enables vectorial reconstruction over a wide range of electron probe sizes from the µm-sized parallel beam down to the atomically sized probes with large convergence angles (for details on convergent beam EMLD simulations see Supp. Note S5

EML
). In contrast, EMCD in AF becomes detectable only with atomically confined probes capable of spatially resolving sublattice asymmetry, thus placing stringent demands on experimental
detection of AF order using EMCD
.

Additionally, EMLD reproduces the characteristic
\sin^{2}\varphi
dependence known from XMLD
Arenholz et al. 2006
;
Stöhr and Siegmann 2006
and previous EMLD experiments
Yuan and Menon 1997
;
van Aken and Lauterbach 2003
. The agreement between our spectral simulations and the dielectric-tensor formalism (Supp. Note S5
EML
) establishes a direct conceptual connection between electron- and photon-based linear dichroism. This correspondence enables the transfer of established XMLD methodologies to the TEM, opening routes toward quantitative evaluation of magnetocrystalline anisotropy and, in combination with EMCD, toward probing altermagnetic order at the nanoscale.

Conclusion
: Our results identify EMLD as a practical and conceptually unified approach for resolving compensated magnetic order in the TEM, rooted in the anisotropic response of core-level excitations. The demonstrated vectorial sensitivity, combined with robustness against probe size and alignment, places experimental realization within the reach of modern aberration-corrected microscopes equipped with high-brightness sources and advanced EELS instrumentation. High-resolution EMLD offers a pathway to map local magnetic anisotropy, interfacial and two-dimensional magnetism, and symmetry breaking induced by strain, providing access to emergent phenomena in antiferromagnets and altermagnets at the nanoscale.

Implementation of vectorial EMLD
: This section summarizes the computational implementation used to evaluate EMLD for arbitrary orientations of the Néel (or magnetization) vector within the MDFF formalism. A consistent transformation of both the spin population and the core-level
j,j_{z}
basis is required to capture the vectorial nature of the exchange-induced anisotropy.

Spin states from DFT
Blaha et al. 2020
are typically obtained for magnetization
\parallel[001]
and are represented as
|ss^{\prime}\rangle
states separately for each sublattice in the AF case or combined for the FM case. Rotation of the spin space is accomplished through the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
Wigner
D
-matrix,
D^{(1/2)}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. In the absence of valence spin–orbit coupling (SOC), the spin population can be expressed as a diagonal operator
\hat{M}=\text{diag}(M_{\uparrow\uparrow},M_{\downarrow\downarrow})
, which allows a simple rotation:

\hat{M}^{\prime}_{ss^{\prime}}=D^{(1/2)}_{ss^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\,\hat{M}_{ss^{\prime}}\,D^{(1/2)\dagger}_{ss^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).

(A1)

To ensure consistency between the rotated spin populations and the angular-momentum quantization axis, we also rotate the
|jj_{z}\rangle
basis into
|jj_{z}\rangle_{\hat{z}\parallel\vec{M}}
. This transformation is achieved by expressing the core-to-valence transition elements from real-space basis states
|\vec{r}s\rangle
into total-angular-momentum space through Clebsch–Gordan coefficients
C_{lm\tfrac{1}{2}s}^{jj_{z}^{\prime}}
and a Wigner
D
-matrix
D^{(j)}{j_{z}^{\prime}j_{z}}
:

\displaystyle\langle\vec{r}s|jj_{z}\rangle_{\hat{z}\parallel\vec{M}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{z}^{\prime}}\langle\vec{r}s|jj_{z}^{\prime}\rangle\langle jj_{z}^{\prime}|jj_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{z}^{\prime}}C_{lm\tfrac{1}{2}s}^{jj_{z}^{\prime}}\,R_{js}(\vec{r})\,Y_{lm}(\hat{r})\,D^{(j)}_{j_{z}^{\prime}j_{z}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).

(A2)

Here, the Euler angles
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
align the local

\vec{N}

\Delta E_{j,j_{z}}=\lambda_{j}j_{z}

(A4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}}{\partial\Omega\,\partial E}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{j,j_{z}}\frac{S^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{j,j_{z}}(\vec{q},\vec{q}^{\prime},E-\Delta E_{j,j_{z}})}{q^{2}q^{\prime 2}}.

(A5)

S_{j,j_{z}}

\vec{N}
