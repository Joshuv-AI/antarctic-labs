Development and Validation of an XGBoost‐SHAP Model for Predicting Adverse Outcomes in Elderly Cardiovascular Patients With Polypharmacy: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Kun Wang, Dandan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to develop and validate an interpretable machine learning model using Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to predict adverse outcomes in elderly cardiovascular patients with polypharmacy. This retrospective cohort study included 1200 patients aged ≥ 65 years with cardiovascular disease and polypharmacy (≥ 5 medications) from The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2021 and December 2024. Data were split into training (60%), validation (20%), and test (20%) sets. The primary outcome was adverse drug events (ADEs); secondary outcomes included 30‐day readmission, 90‐day readmission, and 1‐year mortality. XGBoost models were developed and compared with logistic regression, random forest, and support vector machine algorithms. Model interpretability was enhanced using SHAP values to identify key predictive features and their contributions. The cohort had a mean age of 76.34 ± 8.12 years, with 18.92% experiencing ADEs during follow‐up. The XGBoost model demonstrated strong discriminative ability for ADE prediction (AUC 0.857, 95% CI: 0.790–0.910) compared to logistic regression (AUC 0.795, p < 0.001). The model also performed well for secondary outcomes: 30‐day readmission (AUC 0.843, 95% CI: 0.779–0.899), 90‐day readmission (AUC 0.848, 95% CI: 0.784–0.895), and 1‐year mortality (AUC 0.873, 95% CI: 0.807–0.929), all significantly superior to logistic regression (all p < 0.001). At optimal thresholds, the model achieved sensitivity ranging from 62.71% to 90.24% and specificity from 67.84% to 87.85% across outcomes. SHAP analysis identified age (importance: 0.196), estimated glomerular filtration rate (0.181), medication count (0.159), serum albumin (0.147), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (0.138) as the most influential predictors. The model maintained good calibration (Brier scores: 0.156–0.164) and consistent performance across patient subgroups. SHAP threshold analysis identified eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 and serum albumin < 3.5 g/dL as key risk boundaries associated with markedly elevated adverse drug event risk. The XGBoost‐SHAP model provides accurate, interpretable prediction of adverse outcomes in elderly cardiovascular patients with polypharmacy. This approach may facilitate personalized risk assessment and targeted interventions to improve medication safety in this vulnerable population.Keywords: adverse drug events, cardiovascular disease, elderly, interpretable artificial intelligence, machine learning, polypharmacy, risk prediction, SHAP, XGBoost

Introduction

Polypharmacy, commonly defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications, has become increasingly prevalent among elderly patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD), affecting up to 82% of this population [1, 2]. The aging demographic, coupled with the rising burden of multimorbidity, has led to complex medication regimens that aim to manage multiple chronic conditions simultaneously [3, 4]. While guideline‐directed medical therapy is essential for cardiovascular risk reduction, the accumulation of medications introduces substantial risks, particularly in older adults with age‐related physiological changes and comorbidities [5, 6, 7].

The clinical implications of polypharmacy in elderly cardiovascular patients are profound. Previous studies have demonstrated that polypharmacy significantly increases the risk of adverse drug events (ADEs), with reported incidence rates ranging from 15% to 35% in hospitalized elderly patients [8, 9, 10]. These ADEs encompass a spectrum of drug‐related problems, including medication errors, drug–drug interactions, adverse drug reactions, and therapeutic failures [11, 12]. Beyond direct medication‐related complications, polypharmacy has been associated with increased hospital readmission rates (17%–25% at 30 days) and elevated mortality risk (hazard ratio 1.28–1.85 for all‐cause mortality) [13, 14, 15]. The economic burden is equally concerning, with polypharmacy‐related complications accounting for substantial healthcare expenditures and resource utilization [16].

Despite the well‐recognized risks, current approaches to polypharmacy management remain suboptimal. Traditional risk assessment tools, such as the Beers Criteria and STOPP/START criteria, provide general guidance on potentially inappropriate medications but lack individualized risk quantification [17, 18]. These tools do not account for the complex interactions between patient‐specific factors, disease severity, and medication combinations that collectively determine adverse outcome risk. Furthermore, clinicians often face challenges in identifying high‐risk patients who would benefit most from intensive medication review and deprescribing interventions [19, 20].

Machine learning (ML) approaches offer promising solutions to these challenges by leveraging large‐scale clinical data to identify complex, non‐linear relationships between predictive variables and outcomes [21, 22]. Recent studies have demonstrated the superior performance of ML algorithms, particularly tree‐based ensemble methods like XGBoost, in cardiovascular risk prediction compared to traditional statistical models [23, 24, 25]. XGBoost's ability to handle non‐linear relationships, missing data, and high‐dimensional feature spaces makes it particularly well‐suited for clinical prediction tasks [26, 27]. However, the “black box” nature of complex ML models has limited their clinical adoption, as healthcare providers require transparent, interpretable predictions to guide clinical decision‐making [28, 29].

The development of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods, particularly SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), has addressed the interpretability challenge [30, 31, 32]. SHAP values, derived from cooperative game theory, provide a unified framework for quantifying each feature's contribution to individual predictions while maintaining mathematical consistency [33, 34]. Recent applications of SHAP in clinical prediction models have demonstrated its utility in enhancing model transparency and facilitating clinical understanding of ML‐based predictions [35, 36, 37]. The integration of XGBoost with SHAP analysis offers a powerful combination of predictive accuracy and interpretability, potentially bridging the gap between advanced ML techniques and clinical practice [38, 39, 40].

Despite the growing evidence supporting ML applications in healthcare, specific studies examining interpretable ML models for polypharmacy risk prediction in elderly cardiovascular patients remain limited. Most existing studies have focused on general polypharmacy prevalence or single outcomes, without comprehensive risk assessment across multiple clinically relevant endpoints [41, 42, 43]. Furthermore, few studies have incorporated advanced interpretability methods to elucidate the mechanistic relationships between patient characteristics and adverse outcomes [44, 45].

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable machine learning model using XGBoost with SHAP analysis to predict multiple adverse outcomes—including ADEs, hospital readmissions, and mortality—in elderly cardiovascular patients with polypharmacy. We hypothesized that the XGBoost‐SHAP model would: (1) demonstrate superior predictive performance compared to traditional statistical methods and alternative ML algorithms; (2) provide clinically meaningful interpretation of risk factors through SHAP value analysis; and (3) maintain consistent performance across diverse patient subgroups, thereby supporting its potential clinical utility for personalized risk stratification and intervention targeting.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, a tertiary academic medical center in Suzhou, Jiangsu Province, China. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study utilizing de‐identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Study Population

We identified all patients aged ≥ 65 years who were admitted to the cardiology department between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2024, with a primary or secondary diagnosis of cardiovascular disease. Cardiovascular disease was defined as the presence of at least one of the following conditions: coronary artery disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or hypertension requiring hospitalization. Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of five or more regular medications at hospital discharge, consistent with current definitions in geriatric medicine literature [46, 47].

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 65 years at index hospitalization; (2) documented cardiovascular disease; (3) discharge with ≥ 5 regular medications; and (4) availability of complete baseline clinical data and at least 12 months of follow‐up data.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) terminal illness with life expectancy < 6 months as documented in the medical record; (2) transfer to another facility within 48 h of admission; (3) enrollment in palliative care only; (4) missing data for > 20% of key variables; and (5) patient or family declined follow‐up participation prior to hospital discharge.

Data Collection

Clinical data were extracted from the hospital's electronic health record (EHR) system using standardized data collection protocols. All data were de‐identified prior to analysis. Trained research coordinators reviewed medical records to ensure data accuracy and completeness.

Demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status.

Comorbidities were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes and confirmed through medical record review. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated for each patient as a measure of overall comorbidity burden [48].

Laboratory parameters collected at index hospitalization included: estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated using the CKD‐EPI equation, serum creatinine, hemoglobin, serum albumin, white blood cell count, sodium, and potassium. Laboratory values were extracted from results obtained within 24 h of hospital admission.

Medication data were obtained from discharge medication lists. Each medication was categorized according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system. The total number of regular medications (excluding as‐needed medications) was recorded. Specific attention was paid to high‐risk medication classes including anticoagulants, antiplatelets, proton pump inhibitors, psychotropic medications (benzodiazepines, antidepressants, antipsychotics), opioid analgesics, and systemic corticosteroids.

Outcome Definitions

Primary Outcome

Adverse drug events (ADEs) were defined as any unintended injury or complication related to medication use, including adverse drug reactions, medication errors, therapeutic failures, and drug–drug interactions resulting in clinical consequences. ADEs were identified through: (1) readmission with ICD‐10 codes related to drug toxicity or adverse effects; (2) emergency department visits attributed to medication‐related problems; (3) documentation in outpatient notes of medication‐related symptoms requiring intervention; and (4) laboratory abnormalities consistent with drug toxicity (e.g., bleeding events in anticoagulated patients, acute kidney injury in patients on nephrotoxic agents). All potential ADEs were adjudicated by two independent clinical pharmacists, with disagreements resolved by a third senior pharmacist.

Sample Size Determination

Sample size was calculated using the pmsampsize package in R, [49] based on the following parameters: anticipated ADE prevalence of 20%, 12 candidate predictors, expected Cox‐Snell R 2 of 0.30, and desired shrinkage factor of 0.90. This yielded a minimum required sample size of 892 patients. Accounting for potential data quality issues and to ensure adequate representation in validation and test sets, we targeted a sample size of 1200 patients [50].

Model Development

Data Splitting

The entire cohort was randomly divided into three mutually exclusive sets using stratified sampling to maintain outcome prevalence: training set (60%, n = 720), validation set (20%, n = 240), and test set (20%, n = 240). The random seed was set to 2024 for reproducibility.

XGBoost Model

The optimal hyperparameters were selected based on maximum area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC) in cross‐validation. Early stopping was implemented with a patience of 20 rounds to prevent overfitting. Additional regularization was incorporated through the hyperparameter search: L1 (alpha) and L2 (lambda) penalization, gamma (minimum loss reduction for further node splitting), instance subsampling (subsample), and feature subsampling (colsample_bytree) collectively provided layered protection against overfitting. Feature space was further constrained to 12 pre‐specified clinical predictors, limiting model complexity relative to the available sample size.

Model Evaluation

Discrimination

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC). The DeLong test was used to compare AUC values between models. Additional metrics included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1‐score. The optimal classification threshold was determined using the Youden index (maximum of sensitivity + specificity−1).

Calibration

Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots, calibration slope, calibration‐in‐the‐large, and the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. Well‐calibrated models demonstrate agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies.

Clinical Utility

Decision curve analysis (DCA) was considered to evaluate net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities [51]. Given the retrospective, single‐center design and primary focus on model development and internal validation, DCA was deferred to future prospective implementation research; Youden index‐derived classification thresholds should accordingly be regarded as exploratory rather than prescriptive for real‐world clinical deployment.

Subgroup Analysis

To assess model generalizability, we evaluated performance across predefined subgroups: age ≥ 75 years vs. < 75 years, male vs. female, presence vs. absence of diabetes, presence vs. absence of chronic kidney disease, and hyperpolypharmacy (≥ 10 medications) vs. polypharmacy (5–9 medications).

SHAP Analysis for Model Interpretability

To enhance model interpretability and provide clinically meaningful insights, we employed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to individual predictions, based on cooperative game theory principles. For each prediction, SHAP values indicate the magnitude and direction of each feature's impact on the model output. SHAP analysis was implemented using the shap library (v0.42) with the SHAP. TreeExplainer class, which computes exact Shapley values for tree‐based ensemble models without Monte Carlo approximation [52]. Global feature importance was quantified as mean absolute SHAP value averaged across all test‐set observations.

Statistical Analysis

Variables with missing data (all below the 20% exclusion threshold) were imputed prior to model development using median values for continuous variables and modal values for categorical variables; imputation parameters were derived exclusively from the training set to prevent data leakage. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range) for skewed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between patients with and without adverse outcomes were performed using independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9 (Python Software Foundation) with scikit‐learn 1.2, XGBoost 1.7, SHAP 0.42, and pandas 1.5 libraries, and R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with packages including tableone, pROC, rms, and pmsampsize. Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For multiple comparisons in subgroup analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between January 2021 and December 2024, we screened 1856 elderly patients with cardiovascular disease. After applying exclusion criteria, 1200 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). The mean age was 76.34 ± 8.12 years, and 52.33% were male (Table 1). The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension (82.17%), coronary artery disease (64.25%), and chronic kidney disease (52.75%). The mean number of medications at discharge was 8.42 ± 2.76, with 68.33% having polypharmacy (5–9 medications) and 31.67% having hyperpolypharmacy (≥ 10 medications).

Outcome Incidence

During the follow‐up period, 227 patients (18.92%) experienced at least one ADE. The 30‐day readmission rate was 17.25% (n = 207), the 90‐day readmission rate was 24.83% (n = 298), and the 1‐year mortality rate was 12.58% (n = 151).

Baseline Characteristics by Outcome Status

Patients who experienced ADEs were significantly older (78.91 ± 8.54 vs. 75.67 ± 7.89 years, p < 0.001), had lower eGFR (42.35 ± 18.76 vs. 52.18 ± 17.93 mL/min/1.73 m2, p < 0.001), lower hemoglobin (12.08 ± 1.83 vs. 13.04 ± 1.71 g/dL, p < 0.001), and lower albumin (3.38 ± 0.57 vs. 3.76 ± 0.44 g/dL, p < 0.001) compared to those without ADEs (Table 1). Patients with ADEs also had higher comorbidity burden (CCI: 5.74 ± 2.34 vs. 4.41 ± 2.03, p < 0.001) and were taking more medications (9.76 ± 2.95 vs. 8.11 ± 2.63, p < 0.001). The prevalence of hyperpolypharmacy was significantly higher in the ADE group (44.49% vs. 28.67%, p < 0.001).

Model Performance for ADE Prediction

The XGBoost model demonstrated strong discriminative ability for predicting ADEs across all data sets (Table 2, Figure 2A). In the test set (n = 240), the model achieved an AUC of 0.857 (95% CI: 0.790–0.910), significantly outperforming logistic regression (AUC 0.795, DeLong test p < 0.001). At the optimal threshold of 0.500 determined by Youden's index, the model achieved sensitivity of 80.00%, specificity of 75.90%, positive predictive value of 43.37%, negative predictive value of 94.27%, accuracy of 76.67%, and F1‐score of 0.562. The model demonstrated good calibration with Brier score of 0.159. The prediction distribution showed clear separation between patients with and without ADEs, though with expected overlap in the intermediate risk range (Figure 2A, middle panel). Calibration plots confirmed reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies (Figure 2A, right panel).

Performance for Secondary Outcomes

The XGBoost model also demonstrated strong performance for secondary outcomes (Table 2, Figure 2B–D).

For 30‐day readmission, the model achieved an AUC of 0.843 (95% CI: 0.779–0.899), significantly superior to logistic regression (AUC 0.786, DeLong test p < 0.001). At the optimal threshold of 0.380, sensitivity was 90.24% and specificity was 67.84%, indicating a threshold that prioritizes sensitivity to capture most readmission events. The model achieved an accuracy of 71.67% and F1‐score of 0.521, with a Brier score of 0.164 (Figure 2B).

For 90‐day readmission, the AUC was 0.848 (95% CI: 0.784–0.895), with optimal threshold of 0.657 yielding balanced performance: sensitivity of 62.71% and specificity of 87.85%. This higher threshold reflects the model's conservative approach for longer‐term readmission prediction, achieving accuracy of 81.67% and F1‐score of 0.627. Calibration remained robust with Brier score of 0.159 (Figure 2C).

The model performed best for 1‐year mortality prediction, achieving AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.807–0.929), significantly outperforming logistic regression (AUC 0.808, p < 0.001). At the optimal threshold of 0.583, the model achieved sensitivity of 76.67%, specificity of 82.38%, and accuracy of 81.67%, with F1‐score of 0.511. The good calibration (Brier score 0.156) and evident separation in prediction distributions (Figure 2D, middle panel) demonstrated meaningful risk stratification for mortality.

All models significantly outperformed logistic regression across all outcomes (all DeLong test p < 0.001), with AUC improvements ranging from 0.057 to 0.069. Bootstrap confidence intervals indicated stable performance across resampled test sets. Patient‐level visualization in ROC space revealed clear clustering of high‐risk and low‐risk patients, with optimal operating points effectively balancing sensitivity and specificity (Figure S1).

SHAP analysis revealed that age was the most influential predictor of ADEs (mean absolute SHAP value: 0.196), followed by eGFR (0.181), medication count (0.159), albumin (0.147), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (0.138) (Figure 3). Other important features included hemoglobin (0.089), chronic kidney disease (0.067), heart failure (0.054), diabetes mellitus (0.046), and psychotropic medication use (0.041). Sex and BMI had relatively minor contributions (0.024 and 0.019, respectively).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study developed and validated an interpretable machine learning model for predicting adverse outcomes in elderly cardiovascular patients with polypharmacy. The XGBoost‐SHAP model demonstrated strong predictive performance (AUC 0.843–0.873) across multiple clinically relevant outcomes while providing transparent, clinically meaningful interpretation of risk factors through SHAP analysis. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to systematically integrate advanced machine learning with comprehensive explainability methods specifically for polypharmacy risk assessment in the elderly cardiovascular population.

The consistent outperformance of XGBoost over traditional logistic regression (AUC improvement of 0.057–0.069 across all outcomes) underscores the value of machine learning approaches in capturing complex, non‐linear relationships inherent in polypharmacy risk assessment. The model achieved good discrimination (AUC 0.843–0.873) for all outcomes, with particularly strong performance for 1‐year mortality prediction (AUC 0.873). These results represent an incremental advance over traditional statistical methods, suggesting that ML‐based risk stratification may offer added value in this clinical context.

Several key findings emerged from our analysis. First, age demonstrated the strongest individual contribution to ADE risk, with a non‐linear relationship showing accelerated risk after age 80. This finding aligns with geriatric pharmacology principles, as older patients experience age‐related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, reduced physiologic reserve, and increased susceptibility to medication‐related complications [6, 7]. The observed threshold effect at age 80 suggests that this population may warrant particularly intensive medication review and monitoring.

Second, renal function (eGFR) emerged as a critical predictor, with a clear threshold effect at eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, corresponding to CKD stage 3b. This finding has important implications for medication management, as many cardiovascular medications require dose adjustment or are contraindicated in moderate‐to‐severe renal impairment [15, 41]. The sharp risk increase below this threshold suggests that systematic medication review should be triggered when patients cross this eGFR boundary.

Third, the medication count itself was a strong independent predictor, with our SHAP analysis revealing that hyperpolypharmacy (≥ 10 medications) conferred disproportionately higher risk than simple polypharmacy (5–9 medications). This supports recent calls for more aggressive deprescribing in patients with extensive medication regimens [17, 19]. Importantly, the medication count effect was amplified in patients with higher comorbidity burden, suggesting that the “right” number of medications may vary based on individual patient complexity.

Fourth, serum albumin emerged as a powerful predictor, with a threshold effect at < 3.5 g/dL. Low albumin reflects both nutritional status and acute illness severity, both of which influence drug metabolism, distribution, and sensitivity to adverse effects [44]. This finding suggests that albumin could serve as a readily available marker for identifying high‐risk patients requiring enhanced medication safety monitoring.

Our findings extend previous research on polypharmacy in elderly cardiovascular patients in several important ways. The observed ADE rate of 18.92% falls within the range reported in recent systematic reviews (15%–35%) [13, 14] but our study provides more precise risk quantification for individual patients. Recent studies by Yamashita et al. [2] and Tamargo et al. [7] highlighted the prevalence and general risks of polypharmacy in cardiovascular populations but lacked individualized risk prediction capabilities.

The predictive performance of our XGBoost model (AUC 0.843–0.873 across four clinical outcomes) compares favorably with recent ML applications in cardiovascular medicine. Li et al. [19] reported AUC of 0.84 for cardiovascular risk prediction using irregular EHR data, while Liu et al. [30] achieved AUC of 0.87 for heart failure mortality using multiple ML algorithms. Our model's performance is comparable to these benchmarks while addressing a previously understudied area—polypharmacy‐related risks.

The integration of SHAP analysis represents an important methodological advance over previous studies. While recent work by Yi et al. [33] and Tanaka et al. [34] demonstrated SHAP applications in Alzheimer's disease and acute heart failure, respectively, our study is among the first to apply comprehensive SHAP‐based interpretation to polypharmacy risk assessment. The dependence plots revealed clinically actionable threshold effects (eGFR < 45, albumin < 3.5, age > 80, medication count ≥ 10) that could inform clinical protocols and decision support systems.

Our findings regarding medication count align with dose–response relationships observed by Huang et al. [42] who demonstrated monotonic increases in mortality risk with increasing medication numbers. However, our SHAP analysis extends these findings by revealing non‐linear acceleration of risk at ≥ 10 medications and identifying important interactions with comorbidity burden and renal function.

A key strength of our approach is the comprehensive interpretability analysis using SHAP values. The “black box” criticism of machine learning models has been a major barrier to clinical implementation [28, 29]. By providing transparent, feature‐level explanations for individual predictions, SHAP analysis bridges the gap between algorithmic accuracy and clinical trust. Clinicians can understand not only a patient's risk level but also which specific factors drive that risk, enabling targeted interventions.

For example, a patient identified as high‐risk primarily due to low eGFR and high medication count might benefit from nephrology consultation and systematic medication review, while a patient whose risk is driven by low albumin and high age might require nutritional intervention and more conservative medication management. This individualized insight represents a paradigm shift from population‐level risk scores to person‐centered risk assessment.

The SHAP dependence plots revealed several clinically actionable patterns. The threshold effects observed for eGFR and albumin could be incorporated into clinical decision support systems to trigger automatic alerts for medication review when patients cross these thresholds. The accelerated risk at ≥ 10 medications supports awareness of this as a potential high‐risk indicator in clinical practice, consistent with recent expert consensus statements [9, 20]. Importantly, SHAP values characterize feature contributions to model predictions and reflect descriptive patterns of model behavior rather than causal or mechanistic relationships. The identified thresholds should not be interpreted as intervention‐ready clinical decision boundaries; prospective validation of their actionability is required before translation into formal clinical protocols.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the large sample size (n = 1200) and comprehensive 4‐year study period provide robust data for model development and validation. Second, the rigorous three‐way data split (train‐validation‐test) with stratified sampling ensures unbiased performance estimates. Third, the inclusion of multiple clinically relevant outcomes (ADEs, readmissions, mortality) provides comprehensive risk assessment. Fourth, the integration of SHAP analysis offers unprecedented interpretability for a polypharmacy prediction model. Fifth, the use of readily available clinical variables enhances real‐world applicability.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, single‐center design may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings or geographic regions. Subgroup robustness observed within a single‐center should not be equated with external validity; prospective external validation in geographically and ethnically diverse, multicenter cohorts is required to establish generalizability before clinical deployment. Second, while we adjusted for major confounders, unmeasured confounding from factors not captured in the EHR cannot be excluded. Third, ADE ascertainment relied on medical record documentation and may underestimate true incidence if patients sought care elsewhere or events went unreported. Fourth, we did not analyze specific drug–drug interactions or medication appropriateness using tools like STOPP/START criteria, which could provide additional predictive information. Fifth, the study period (2021–2024) includes the COVID‐19 pandemic, which may have influenced prescribing patterns and outcomes, though sensitivity analyses (not shown) suggested minimal impact.

Sixth, while SHAP values provide feature‐level interpretation, they do not explicitly model causal relationships. The observed associations, while clinically plausible, require validation through prospective intervention studies. Seventh, the model does not account for medication adherence, which likely influences outcomes but is challenging to measure retrospectively. Eighth, our definition of polypharmacy (≥ 5 medications) is widely used but somewhat arbitrary; different thresholds might yield different results. Finally, while we included psychotropic medications as a high‐risk category, we did not examine all potentially inappropriate medications or conduct detailed medication‐level analysis. Additionally, the logistic regression comparator model incorporated linear terms only; inclusion of non‐linear transformations (e.g., restricted cubic splines for continuous predictors such as age and eGFR) or clinically plausible interaction terms could potentially improve LR performance, and the magnitude of XGBoost's incremental advantage may be reduced with a more flexible comparator. Formal decision curve analysis was not conducted in the current study, limiting quantification of net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities; this represents an important gap to address in future prospective validation work.

Several avenues for future research emerge from this work. First, prospective external validation in diverse healthcare settings is essential to confirm generalizability. Multicenter studies incorporating different practice patterns, patient populations, and healthcare systems would strengthen the evidence base. Second, prospective implementation studies are needed to assess whether model‐guided interventions improve outcomes. Randomized trials comparing ML‐guided medication review versus usual care could establish clinical utility. Third, incorporating additional data sources—such as pharmacy dispensing records, patient‐reported outcomes, and genomic data—might further enhance predictive performance.

Fourth, developing real‐time clinical decision support systems integrating the XGBoost‐SHAP model into EHR workflows could facilitate point‐of‐care risk assessment. Such systems could automatically identify high‐risk patients, generate alerts for medication review, and provide personalized risk reports with SHAP‐based explanations. Fifth, investigating the impact of specific medications or medication classes would enable more granular risk assessment and targeted deprescribing recommendations. Sixth, longitudinal modeling approaches that account for time‐varying medication exposures and clinical status could provide dynamic risk updates.

Finally, combining the predictive model with intervention frameworks—such as structured deprescribing protocols or enhanced medication management programs—would create comprehensive care pathways for high‐risk patients identified by the model. Cost‐effectiveness analyses of ML‐guided polypharmacy management would inform resource allocation decisions and health policy.

The XGBoost‐SHAP model has several potential applications in clinical practice and health systems. At the patient‐level, the model could enable personalized risk assessment during hospital discharge planning, outpatient visits, or transitions of care. High‐risk patients could be targeted for intensive interventions, including comprehensive medication review by clinical pharmacists, close follow‐up, and enhanced monitoring.

At the health system level, the model could inform population health management strategies by identifying high‐risk patient cohorts for proactive outreach. Healthcare organizations could use risk stratification to allocate resources efficiently, directing intensive services to those most likely to benefit. Quality improvement initiatives could track outcomes in high‐risk patients and assess the impact of polypharmacy management programs.

From a policy perspective, this work supports incorporating ML‐based risk prediction into quality metrics and reimbursement structures. Value‐based care models could incentivize proactive identification and management of high‐risk polypharmacy patients. Educational initiatives for healthcare providers should emphasize the importance of identified risk factors (particularly eGFR, albumin, medication count) and promote evidence‐based deprescribing.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that an XGBoost‐SHAP model provides accurate, interpretable prediction of adverse outcomes in elderly cardiovascular patients with polypharmacy. The model's favorable performance relative to traditional statistical approaches, combined with transparent risk factor interpretation, positions it as a potentially useful tool for supporting personalized medication management decisions. Key risk factors identified through SHAP analysis—particularly medication count (≥ 10 medications), renal function (eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2), and nutritional status (albumin < 3.5 g/dL)—offer opportunities for threshold‐informed, targeted clinical interventions. Implementation of this model in clinical practice could facilitate earlier identification of high‐risk patients, guide personalized medication management strategies, and ultimately improve outcomes in this vulnerable population. Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and assess the clinical impact of ML‐guided polypharmacy management.
