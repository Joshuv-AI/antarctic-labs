Executive Summary

 This deliverable reports FY26 progress in advancing a neural-network-based Green’s-function
framework for reconstructing neutron-flux distributions from ex-core measurements and for translating
reconstruction requirements into a practical detector-layout strategy. Building on the FY25 formulation,
the present work had two main objectives: (1) refine and re-evaluate the reconstruction methodology on
an updated Purdue University Reactor Number One (PUR-1) model, and (2) develop a systematic,
Green’s-function-guided procedure for boundary detector placement.

 Figure 1. Schematic of the physics-directed workflow for learning an effective Green’s kernel.

 Figure 2. Reconstructed three-dimensional neutron-flux field (left) and fractional
 reconstruction error (right) across axial planes of the core active region.
 4

 The reconstruction workflow was improved through targeted updates to the neural-network
representation of the effective Green’s kernel, including sinusoidal activation layers, nonnegativity
enforcement, and improved kernel regularization (Figure 1). These changes were evaluated using a more
geometrically representative OpenMC model of PUR-1. Boundary flux and flux-gradient were sampled at
282 boundary locations, while training used flux values at 112 interior anchoring voxels selected from a
6 × 6 × 8 voxelized domain. For a control-rod configuration not used during training, the refined model
reconstructed the three-dimensional neutron-flux field with mean fractional errors of 4.25% in the active
core region and 5.48% over the full core, demonstrating that the FY25 framework remains effective when
transferred to a more realistic reactor representation (Figure 2).

 A second major outcome of this work is the development of a quantitative methodology for boundary
detector placement. In the proposed approach, the sensing system is represented as a set of detector strings,
consistent with practical implementations such as self-powered neutron detectors (SPNDs). The key
design choices therefore correspond to the number of sensitive volumes distributed along each string and
the number and azimuthal placement of the detector strings around the reactor boundary. The learned
Green’s function is used to construct noise-weighted sensitivity matrices, and candidate detector strings
are ranked using a Fisher-information-based D-optimal criterion (Figure 3a).

 Axial and azimuthal sampling requirements are first used to determine the minimum density of
boundary sensors that is needed to resolve the spatial structure of the boundary field; a greedy selection
procedure is then applied to identify informative and nonredundant string locations. For the simplified
PUR-1 demonstration case, the axial coherence analysis yielded an axial coherence length of 29.8 cm,
corresponding to a minimum requirement of 7 detectors per string, while the azimuthal coherence analysis
yielded a coherence angle of 0.94 rad, corresponding to a minimum requirement of 14 detector strings
 5

around the core perimeter (Figure 3b). The subsequent greedy D-optimal selection showed that, for the
reference 5 × 5 × 5 representation, this configuration captures more than 70% of the total recoverable
information, thereby supporting a two-stage deployment approach in which dense instrumentation is
adopted during calibration and a reduced subset of detector strings is retained for routine operation.

 Overall, the results reported here advance the project from proof-of-concept flux reconstruction toward
a more deployable sensing-and-reconstruction architecture. The FY26 work demonstrates both that
accurate boundary-to-interior reconstruction can be achieved on the updated PUR-1 model with limited
interior measurement, and that the same learned Green’s-function representation can be used to derive
quantitatively justified detector layouts. This provides a foundation for future work on higher-fidelity
geometries, more realistic detector models, and experimental validation of the selected boundary
instrumentation strategy on PUR-1.

1. Introduction
 Reconstructing the neutron-flux distribution inside a reactor core from measurements acquired at or
near the core boundary is a challenging inverse problem. Ex-core measurements provide only an indirect
signature of the interior neutron field, and the information they carry depends strongly on both the
reconstruction model and the way the boundary is instrumented. Yet this capability is important for reactor
monitoring, diagnostics, and the development of practical sensing strategies in environments where
extensive in-core instrumentation may be limited by survivability, geometric constraints, cost, or
operational complexity. In the proposed physics-directed formulation, the relationship between boundary
observables and the interior flux distribution is treated as an operator-learning problem: an effective
Green’s kernel is identified for a reference reactor condition and then reused to reconstruct neutron-flux
fields under new operating states. This formulation is attractive for nuclear applications because it embeds
physical structure directly into the learning problem and is therefore better suited than purely empirical
approaches to the small-data conditions that often characterize reactor analysis and experimental
campaigns.

 The present deliverable builds on the neural-network-based reconstruction methodology established in
FY25 milestones. The objective is to advance this approach through refinement of the neural-network
implementation, evaluation on a more geometrically representative model of PUR-1, and development of
a systematic methodology for boundary-detector placement. A practical sensing architecture must satisfy
two complementary requirements. First, the detector network must sample the field with sufficient spatial
resolution to capture the dominant axial and azimuthal variations in neutron flux at the core boundary. If
the measurement density is too low, relevant spatial features may be aliased or lost, limiting the ability of
any reconstruction method to recover the interior flux distribution accurately. Second, once adequate
sampling has been established, the detector locations must be selected so that they provide complementary
rather than redundant information about admissible interior perturbations. In other words, the sensing
system must be designed not only to observe the boundary field, but also to maximize the independent
information available for inferring the reactor-core state. The methodology developed in this work is
intended to support a two-stage deployment strategy. In an initial calibration phase, a relatively dense
sensor configuration can be used to characterize the Green’s function with the highest practicable fidelity
and to establish the spatial information content available at the boundary. That characterization can then
be used to identify a reduced set of detector locations that preserves the information most relevant to
reconstruction, thereby enabling a more practical configuration for subsequent testing or operational
deployment. In this way, the proposed approach provides a structured path from proof-of-concept model
development to a deployable sensing-and-reconstruction architecture grounded in both physical sampling
requirements and information-based sensor selection.

 This report is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the FY26 refinements to the reconstruction
framework and presents updated results for neutron-flux reconstruction on the PUR-1 case. Section 3
introduces the Green’s-function-guided methodology for boundary-detector placement. Section 4
 7

demonstrates that methodology on the PUR-1-derived test problem. Section 5 concludes the report and
identifies priorities for future work.

2. Neural-Network Enhancements for Neutron-Flux Reconstruction
2.1. Workflow Overview
 The workflow for reconstructing the interior neutron flux from ex-core measurements separates into
two stages: (i) a training stage, in which the Green’s kernel is identified as a multi-layer neural network
and the coefficients are learned, and (ii) a test stage, in which the learned kernel is reused to reconstruct
flux fields for previously unseen control-rod configurations. The main steps are described below. For
further details, the reader may refer to [1][2].

 unknown 𝑢𝑢 = 𝜙𝜙 − 𝜓𝜓, which satisfies homogeneous Dirichlet BCs.

 the domain boundary 𝑆𝑆 and use it to evaluate the corresponding boundary data for 𝑢𝑢 via ∇𝑢𝑢 =
 ∇𝜙𝜙 − ∇𝜓𝜓 on 𝑆𝑆.

3. Green’s Function-Guided Method for Boundary Detector Placement
3.1. Sensor-Placement Objectives and Overall Workflow
 The sensor-placement strategy is guided by a simple physical consideration: an ex-core detector is
useful only to the extent that it captures a sufficiently strong and repeatable signature of the neutron leakage
field. Because boundary detectors intercept only a fraction of the neutrons leaving the core, the detector
layout directly affects both measurement quality and the recoverability of the interior neutron state. This
leads to two complementary requirements.

 • The boundary must be sampled densely enough in both the axial and azimuthal directions to resolve
 the relevant spatial structure of the boundary field. During the testing phase, the neural-network
 infers the neutron-flux distribution from the collected boundary measurements. For this
 reconstruction to be reliable, distinct internal states of the system should produce distinct sets of
 external measurements. If two different core states are perceived identically by the ex-core
 detectors, the inverse problem becomes ambiguous, and the network may reconstruct the wrong
 internal state. This can be interpreted as spatial aliasing: insufficient boundary sampling causes
 the higher-spatial-frequency features of the boundary field to be misrepresented or folded into
 lower-frequency measurement patterns. The issue is mitigated by increasing the number of sensors
 until the boundary field is sampled with adequate spatial resolution. In the present work, a spatial
 analogue of the Nyquist criterion is used to define the minimum admissible sensor density.

 • The sensor set should maximize observability with respect to the admissible interior perturbations.
 To support this objective, the proposed methodology introduces an auxiliary finite-dimensional
 representation of the interior state, described by the coefficient vector 𝑎𝑎⃗, for quantifying
 observability and comparing candidate sensor layouts. The assumption that the Green’s function
 remains invariant across the reference operating conditions provides the physics-based basis for
 sensor-placement optimization. The noise-weighted sensitivity matrix derived from the learned
 Green’s function is then used to rank and select sensor locations sequentially, while enforcing any
 spacing or coverage constraints.

 Most sensor-placement methodologies are formulated as optimization problems, in which a cost
functional or performance metric is introduced to rank candidate sensor configurations and guide the
search toward increasingly favorable layouts. Representative examples include the mutual-information
framework [10], which casts sensor placement as a combinatorial optimization problem over Gaussian-
process models, and the convex-relaxation strategy [11], which selects sensor subsets by approximately
minimizing estimation uncertainty through convex optimization. In the nuclear context, a reactor-specific
methodology based on the Generalized Empirical Interpolation Method (GEIM), where sensor placement
is linked to reduced-order reconstruction and the stable recovery of the underlying field, was proposed
[12].
In this Section, a methodology for determining a detector configuration that provides sufficient
 14

information to the neutron network for boundary-to-interior flux reconstruction while avoiding
unnecessary instrumentation is presented. The output of the sensor-placement procedure is threefold: (1)
the minimum number of detectors per axial string (𝑁𝑁𝑧𝑧min ), (2) the minimum number of azimuthal detector
strings (𝑁𝑁𝜃𝜃min ), and (3) the final selected subset of detector locations, chosen from a set of candidate
boundary positions, using an information-based criterion. The methodology is organized in the following
steps:

 1. Establish the minimum axial sampling requirement required to resolve the axial content of the
 boundary field.

 2. Establish the minimum azimuthal sampling requirement required to resolve its circumferential
 content.

 3. Construct a candidate detector grid that is sufficiently dense relative to these minimum
 requirements.

 4. Select the most informative detector subset by applying a quantitative layout criterion to the
 candidate set.

 5. Terminate the selection procedure once the sampling requirements are satisfied and the
 incremental benefit of additional sensors becomes negligible.

The remainder of this section develops this framework progressively. We first introduce the measurement
and uncertainty model used to compare candidate layouts. We then define the finite-dimensional
representation adopted to quantify observability and relate detector responses to the learned Green’s-
function representation. On this basis, a quantitative placement criterion is formulated, followed by the
construction and selection procedure used to determine the final detector configuration.

4. Application of the Sensor-Placement Procedure to a Simplified Model
 of PUR-1
 The sensor-placement workflow described in Section 3 was demonstrated on the simplified PUR-1
geometry (Figure 4-1a) previously adopted as a proof-of-concept test bed [4]. Owing to the capabilities of
the Keras environment, the weights and biases of the fully connected layers were extracted, thereby
making the effective Green’s kernel explicitly accessible. The trained neural network was therefore used
not only for neutron-flux reconstruction, but also as a practical surrogate for evaluating the learned kernel
at arbitrary boundary-interior point pairs. This capability is central to the placement methodology, since
the sensitivity matrices and corresponding Fisher-information metrics are assembled directly from
tabulated values of 𝐺𝐺� .
 Before applying the placement algorithm, the extracted kernel was examined for basic physical
consistency (Figure 4-1b). The learned effective Green’s function was found to be approximately
symmetric with respect to interchange of source and field points, nonnegative over the sampled domain,
and strongly peaked near the diagonal, that is, when the source and field locations coincide or are very
close. In the discretized representation adopted here, this near-diagonal amplification appears as a finite
but sharply localized peak, consistent with the expected regularized singular structure of the Green’s
function.

 The number of detector strings suggested by the cumulative Fisher-information curve depends on the
 30

adopted discretization of the reactor interior domain. In the present formulation, the Fisher matrix is
constructed for a finite-dimensional representation of the core state, so the selected number of strings
reflects how much boundary information is needed to explain a chosen fraction of the recoverable Fisher
information for that specific discrete representation. Accordingly, the threshold inferred from Figure 4-2
should not be interpreted as a grid-independent measure of reconstruction accuracy, but rather as an
indication of how many boundary measurements are needed to capture a prescribed fraction of the
recoverable information for the 5 × 5 × 5 voxel discretization of the studied domain.
 This dependence is illustrated by comparison with the finer 10 × 10 × 10 voxel discretization (Figure
4-4). As the interior grid is refined, both the dimension of the finite-dimensional interior representation
and the sampling of the sensitivity field are altered. Consequently, the cumulative-gain curve changes, and
the threshold number of detector strings obtained from the cumulative Fisher-information analysis may
also shift. Thus, as the spatial resolution of the interior representation increases, additional detector strings
may be needed to achieve comparable observability. The threshold should therefore be interpreted together
with the adopted grid, rather than as an absolute or universally applicable detector count.

 At the same time, the axial and azimuthal sampling requirements derived from the coherence analysis
are generally less sensitive to the interior discretization. In the present implementation, the correlation
measures used to estimate the axial coherence length (𝑙𝑙𝑧𝑧 ) and the azimuthal coherence angle (𝑙𝑙𝜃𝜃 ) are
evaluated on a uniform interior grid with normalized source weights. Under these conditions, the discrete
inner products entering the correlation metric behave as averages over the sampled domain. Refining the
grid therefore acts mainly to improve the quadrature of an underlying continuum correlation measure,
rather than redefine the quantity itself. Once that approximation has converged sufficiently, the inferred
coherence scales change only weakly with further refinement. Besides, the final detector counts are
obtained through thresholding and ceiling operations, and the changes in discretization may not be large
enough to change the corresponding integer requirements (𝑁𝑁𝑧𝑧min and 𝑁𝑁𝜃𝜃min ). For these reasons, the
coherence-based sampling requirements can appear nearly unchanged across different grids, even though

 31

the cumulative Fisher-information curves differ more visibly. The coherence analysis estimates spatial
scales of variation of the Green’s kernel, whereas the Fisher-information analysis quantifies how
informative a given detector set is for a specific finite-dimensional reconstruction problem.

 Taken together, these observations help clarify the role of spatial discretization in the proposed
placement methodology. The coherence-based analysis provides a relatively stable estimate of the
sampling density needed to resolve the dominant axial and azimuthal structure of the boundary response,
while the Fisher-information analysis determines how many detector strings are needed to make the chosen
discrete interior representation observable. The former tends to stabilize quickly under refinement in the
present implementation; the latter remains explicitly tied to the adopted state dimension and sensitivity
sampling. For this reason, detector-placement results should be interpreted in two layers: the sampling
requirements indicate the spatial resolution that the boundary instrumentation should support, whereas the
cumulative Fisher-information threshold reflects the information demand of the particular discrete inverse
problem used for reconstruction.

5. Conclusions
 This deliverable reports FY26 progress in advancing a previously established Green’s-function-based
framework for reconstructing three-dimensional neutron-flux distributions from ex-core measurements.
Building on the FY25 methodology, the work presented here focused on two objectives: refining and re-
evaluating the reconstruction approach on an updated PUR-1 model and developing a systematic
procedure for boundary detector placement. The updated reconstruction results confirm that the framework
remains effective on the improved reactor model, with mean fractional errors of 4.25% in the active core
region and 5.48% over the full domain for an unseen control-rod configuration.

 As for the Green’s-function-guided methodology for boundary-detector placement, the approach
combines physical sampling criteria with a Fisher-information-based D-optimal selection procedure and
identifies detector layouts that are both informative and non-redundant. Unlike sensor-placement strategies
that rely on generating large ensembles of scenario-dependent training datasets, the present method
operates directly on the learned Green’s-function representation of boundary-to-interior sensitivity. Once
this representation is available, detector selection can be performed through the associated noise-weighted
sensitivity matrix. Although the K–H formulation is formally posed in terms of boundary conditions, the
results show that a discrete sampling strategy is sufficient in practice. Applied to a simplified PUR-1
geometry used for demonstration, the method produced a detector configuration that is quantitatively
justified and consistent with the spatial information required for boundary-to-interior flux reconstruction.
Specifically, a configuration of 14 detector strings, each containing 7 detectors, was found to provide the
neural-network model with the information required to reconstruct the neutron-flux distribution during
testing, thereby supporting an unambiguous mapping between the collected measurements and the internal
core state. Overall, these results advance the project from proof-of-concept reconstruction toward a more
practical sensing-and-reconstruction framework.

 Future work will emphasize experimental demonstration of the proposed placement procedure on
PUR-1 by collecting neutron-flux and neutron-flux-gradient measurements at the selected boundary
locations. These measurements will provide a basis for validating the detector-layout methodology under
representative conditions and for assessing reconstruction performance with a practically deployable
sensor set. The validated framework will then be extended to core-diagnostics applications, where
boundary measurements will be used to support the identification and localization of abnormal in-core
conditions.
