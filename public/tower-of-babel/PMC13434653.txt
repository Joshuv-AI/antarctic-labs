Optical access of spin-polarized excited states in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals
Authors: Yu Zhang, Haidi Liu, Shuchun Zhang, Zhengwei Yang, Qingda Chang, Lixin Xu, Yongli Yan, Chuang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interplay of slow spin relaxation (long τS) and fast radiative recombination (short τR) is essential for using spin-polarized excited states in lead halide perovskites towards spintronics and quantum-optics technologies. Herein, we report on the observation of simultaneously long τS (59.8 ps) and short τR (3.4 ns) in the colloidal nanocrystals of high-entropy perovskites, Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, which enables the optical access and spin manipulation of excited states at room temperature. The incorporation of non-Pb metals spatially separates the [PbBr6]4− framework into smaller domains below the Bohr radius of excitons, and the size confinement leads to blue-shifted bright photoluminescence from short-lived photoexcitation species. Moreover, the distortion of [PbBr6]4− lattice is significantly enhanced in high-entropy structures, which enlarges the Rashba spin splitting and consequently prolongs τS over an order of magnitude. Optical write-in and read-out of spin states via circularly polarized light are achieved for both photoluminescence and amplified spontaneous emission from Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. Optical Hanle effect is demonstrated and allows for very efficient spin manipulation (positive/negative polarizations ~25.8%) under a perpendicular magnetic field, showing the great potential of high-entropy perovskites for opto-spintronic applications.Subject terms: Magneto-optics, Optical spectroscopy, Ultrafast photonics

Introduction

Lead halide perovskites (LHPs) provide a promising platform for various optoelectronic applications due to their excellent properties and chemical versatility1–3. Colloidal nanocrystals of LHPs have been proposed and studied for their potential in quantum information technologies based on optically accessible spin-polarized excited states (SPESs)4,5. Similar to III-V semiconductors such as GaAs6, LHPs show two SPESs (σ⁺ and σ⁻), corresponding to two spin-selective optical transitions (ΔJ = +1 and −1) from the recombination between electrons (J = ±1/2) and holes (s = ±1/2). The Rashba spin splitting, due to the coexistence of inversion symmetry breaking and strong spin-orbit coupling in LHPs, prolongs the spin relaxation time (τS) to a comparable level with the radiative decay lifetime (τR)7–9. It allows for the optical write-in and read-out of SPESs in LHPs with circularly polarized light, which to date still requires cryogenic temperature operations to obtain a measurable degree of polarization10,11. Incorporating organic ligands with alkyl chains may enhance the structural distortion of [PbX6]4− octahedra and thus Rashba spin splitting for a further prolonged τS12–15; however, the splitting in the conduction band introduces an indirect bandgap feature16,17, and results in long-lived photoexcitation that shows a low photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY). This, in turn, limits the efficiency for optically reading out SPESs, especially at room temperature, which hinders the use of spin states in LHPs for information technologies.

Very recently, high-entropy LHPs have been synthesized where the Pb sites are partially substituted by a series of non-Pb metals (M) that cannot be solely embedded in the lattice18,19. Colloidal nanocrystals of high-entropy LHPs are prepared through liquid-phase ion exchange and stabilized by entropic contributions to free energy20,21. The structural diversity of high-entropy LHPs provides unique photophysical properties such as blue-shifted and brighter light emission compared with their pristine Pb counterparts22–24. We propose that the colloidal nanocrystals of high-entropy LHPs hold great opportunities for optical access of SPESs, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The [MX6]4− octahedra spatially isolate the [PbX6]4− phase into smaller domains, leading to the size confinement effect that facilitates the radiative decay of excited states (τR decreases). More importantly, the surrounding [MX6]4− octahedra introduces a lattice distortion of [PbX6]4− structure to enlarge the inversion symmetry breaking in high-entropy LHPs. The Rashba effect is thus expected to be more profound and bring a prolonged τS. The interplay of long τS and short τR allows for a significantly enhanced steady-state spin polarization P in the colloidal nanocrystals of high-entropy LHPs, providing a versatile yet promising platform for efficient optical write-in and read-out of SPESs.

Schematic illustration of high-entropy LHP colloidal nanocrystals.

The structural features of mixed phases and lattice distortions lead to strong exciton confinement and large Rashba splitting, respectively. The spin polarization P is thus improved by the combination of short τR and long τS in high-entropy LHPs.

In this work, we synthesize the colloidal nanocrystals of high-entropy LHPs, Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, via a modified ion exchange method, and demonstrate their unique advantages of both short τR and long τS for optically accessible SPESs. The high-entropy Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 contains near-equimolar [PbBr6]4– and three other [MBr6]4– (M = Mg, Zn and Cd) octahedra and shows a good structural stability due to entropic contributions to free energy. The [MBr6]4– phases spatially separate the small domains of [PbBr6]4− phase, which are even smaller than the exciton Bohr radius, and meanwhile result in strong structural distortions. The [PbBr6]4− domains serve as the luminescent centers with a strong confinement of excitons, and the blue-shifted light emission shows a high PLQY ~ 98.5% and a fast decay of τR down to several nanoseconds. Moreover, the lattice distortions of [PbBr6]4− domains in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 colloidal nanocrystals lead to a large Rashba splitting, and prolong the spin relaxation time of excitons τS from 4.6 ps to 59.8 ps at room temperature. The relatively long τS enables the excitation and emission of SPESs with circularly polarized light in high-entropy Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. The optical Hanle effect is demonstrated to manipulate SPESs using a perpendicular magnetic field, and the circular polarization is up to 25.8% at room temperature for the amplified spontaneous emission from Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals. Optical write-in/read-out and multi-level control of SPESs are facilely achieved by varying the excitation polarization and external field strength, highlighting the role of high-entropy perovskite materials in future opto-spintronic applications.

Results

Colloidal nanocrystals of high-entropy Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 are synthesized through a modified ion-exchange method on pre-prepared CsPbBr3 nanocrystals, to overcome the limitation of solubility differences among various precursors (see experimental procedures in the “Methods”)24. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 exhibits cube-shaped crystals with an edge length ranging from ~8 nm to ~16 nm (Fig. 2a). Compared to that of CsPbBr3, no significant morphological changes are observed, indicating that the ion-exchange process occurs slowly under mild conditions (Fig. S1). High-entropy M-site substitution does not cause lattice collapse in CsPbBr3, and Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 still maintains high crystallinity according to the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns. The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) results confirm that Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 crystallizes in the orthorhombic system (Pnma space group) at room temperature (Fig. 2b)25, and no phase transitions occur at cryogenic temperatures (Fig. S2). The primarily exposed crystal planes being (101), which remain unchanged from the CsPbBr3 nanocrystals (Fig. S3)26,27. It means that the incorporation of non-Pb metals partially substitutes Pb-sites, and forms a stable and uniform structure where the mixed phases of [PbBr6]4− and [MBr6]4− octahedra coexist. Configuration entropy drives the introduction of various +2 metal elements (Mg, Zn, Cd) on M-sites in the orthorhombic structure. In this case, the reduced ionic radii of M-site elements (Mg2+ 0.72 Å, Zn2+ 0.74 Å, Cd2+ 0.95 Å, Pb2+ 1.19 Å) lead to the lattice contraction and distortion, as indicated by the broader XRD peaks shifted to higher angles in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. The replacement of Pb with smaller B-site cations allows for a strong lattice distortion in the rigid CsPbBr3 lattice. The large mismatch in ionic sizes precludes single-metal high-level doping28, but a mixture of these non-Pb ions (Mg, Zn, Cd) can be simultaneously introduced with both structural stability and lattice distortions. As shown in Fig. 2c, high-angle annular dark-field TEM (HAADF-TEM) and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping images reveal the spatial distribution of Pb, Mg, Zn and Cd in nanocrystals, which proves the formation of a high-entropy-stabilized structure containing mixed phases. The preparation of high-entropy perovskite nanocrystals is highly reproducible with very narrow distributions on metal elemental compositions (Fig. S4).

The atomic-level M-site substitution is further verified by the contrast analysis of atomic numbers in the zoomed-in HAADF-TEM image of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 colloidal crystals (Fig. 2d). Compared to the orderly periodic arrangement of Pb atoms in the CsPbBr3 lattice (Fig. S5), the M-sites are partially substituted by non-Pb metals with lower atomic numbers in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. In this case, the original corner-sharing [PbBr6]4− framework is separated into smaller domains, providing an approach to realize the quantum confinement effect below the exciton Bohr radius (~5 nm) in high-entropy LHPs. Note that it remains very difficult to prepare monodispersed and robust sub-5 nm quantum dots of LHPs via traditional colloidal synthesis methods29. Note that the size and spatial distribution of [PbBr6]4− domains are highly random due to the high-entropy nature of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 structure (Fig. S6). Moreover, the high ratio (up to ~50%) of M-site substitution with a variety of ion radii causes a large amount of lattice distortions in the original [PbBr6]4− framework30,31, which is clearly seen by comparing the atomic inverse fast Fourier transform filtered images of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and CsPbBr3 (Fig. 2e). Raman spectroscopy further reveals the structural distortion of [PbBr6]4− octahedra, where the shifts in torsional (37.2 cm−1 to 30.8 cm−1) and octahedral vibration (66.9 cm−1 to 77.2 cm−1, 146.2 cm−1 to 137.35 cm−1) peaks indicate changes in octahedral connectivity and distortion such as Pb-Br bond bending or symmetry alterations (Fig. S7)32,33. The high-entropy nature of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 results in the formation of small [PbBr6]4− domains within the strongly distorted coordination environment, which may influence both the photo-physical and spin-physical properties of high-entropy LHPs.

As shown in Fig. 3a, the Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 colloidal nanocrystals exhibit a brighter cyan emission, compared to the green emission from the CsPbBr3 sample under UV lamp excitation. With the increase in the substitution ratio of M metals on Pb-sites, the [PbBr6]4− framework is gradually separated into smaller domains with stronger distortions (Fig. S8). Meanwhile, the central wavelength of PL bands shifts from ~530 nm to ~515 nm (Fig. 3b), and the radiative recombination rate increases obviously (Fig. S9). The excitation/emission spectra of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 recorded at different wavelengths show the same shape and features, without the appearance of additional impurity peaks (Fig. S10). It indicates that excitons are photo-generated and strongly localized in the [PbBr6]4− domains of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, and the quantum confinement effect occurs depending on the domain size and causes an increase in the bandgap. The optical properties of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals are reproducible (Fig. S11) and relatively stable to ambient environments (Fig. S12). The optical absorption band edge shifts from 2.31 eV for CsPbBr3 to 2.40 eV for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 (Fig. S13), and the PLQY dramatically increases from 30.5% for CsPbBr3 to 98.5% for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 (Fig. S14). The k-vector difference between the valence band (VB) and the conduction band (CB) governs the PL emission efficiency, and spin-orbit coupling combined with inversion symmetry breaking can induce spin splitting in both VB and CB. The Rashba splitting may align the VB maximum and CB minimum in k-space34, and brighten the excited state in high-entropy nanocrystals. In Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, the short M-Br bonds strengthen the orbital hybridization and drive the electronic states associated with the [MBr6]4− octahedra deep into the valence/conduction bands. It may passivate the interface of small [PbBr6]4− domains and thus benefit from quantum confinement of excited states in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, which reduces the band dispersion and relaxes the momentum selection rule for radiative recombination35. The energetically and spatially confinements also limit the dissociation or non-radiative decay of excitons, giving rise to the blue-shifted PL with a high PLQY observed in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. As shown in Fig. 3c, the relation between emission intensity (I) and excitation laser power density (L) is analyzed with the equation of I = nLk where n is the fitting parameter36. The I–L relation is sub-linear for CsPbBr3 (k = 0.76), while it becomes perfectly linear for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 (k = 1.0), indicating that the non-radiative process at high excitation density is eliminated in the high-entropy LHPs.

To understand the changes in electronic structures caused by high-entropy doping, we use VASP software to construct a high-entropy model where the [PbBr6]4− domain is surrounded by other [MBr6]4− octahedra. It shows that the band gaps at the Γ point (without spin-orbit coupling) for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and CsPbBr3 have similar values of 2.39 eV and 2.29 eV, respectively, which closely align with their optical absorption edges (Figs. 3d, S15). Calculations on density of states (DOS) reveal that the valence band maximum of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is still composed of Pb 6 s and Br 4p orbitals, while the conduction band minimum is primarily contributed by Pb 6p and Br 4p orbitals. The energy band contribution of [MBr6]4− components is located at deep energy levels, which have negligible effects on the optical absorption and emission processes. It verifies that the photogenerated excited states are strongly localized in the [PbBr6]4− domains of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. The temperature-dependent (6–300 K) PL spectra are measured to extract the activation energy (Ea) for exciton dissociation and exciton-phonon coupling strength in CsPbBr3 and Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 (Fig. S16)37,38. By fitting the temperature dependence of PL intensity with a single-term Arrhenius equation, we obtain the Ea values of CsPbBr3 and Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 to be 18 meV and 74 meV, respectively (Fig. 3e). It further explains the very high PLQY of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, as the dissociation process is not activated by the thermal energy (~25 meV) at room temperature. Exciton-phonon coupling is significantly enhanced after high-entropy doping (longitudinal mode γLO from 27 meV to 247 meV, transverse mode ELO from 11 meV to 63 meV), as the photo-excitations are locally confined within the [PbBr6]4− domains (Fig. S17)39. More importantly, as shown in Fig. 3f, the PL lifetime τR of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is obviously shorter than that of CsPbBr3. At room temperature, the lifetime of CsPbBr3 exhibits biexponential decay, with the short-lifetime component τ1 (4.1 ns, 57.9%) attributed to the geminate recombination of photon-generated excitons, and the long-lifetime component τ2 (23.6 ns, 42.1%) associated with the non-geminate recombination of carriers upon exciton dissociation. At a low temperature, the thermally activated process is suppressed, leading to an increase in the τ1 component to >80% in CsPbBr3 (Fig. S18, Table S1). Consequently, the spin polarization of excitons can be better preserved in CsPbBr3 at cryogenic temperatures, as the non-geminate process suffers from spin relaxation of both excitons and carriers during a relatively long period (tens of nanoseconds). In contrast, the short-lifetime component becomes dominated in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 even at room temperature (3.4 ns, 97%), as the non-geminate process related to exciton dissociation is suppressed due to the large Ea. Note that τ1 in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is shorter than that in CsPbBr3 in a wide range of temperatures, in spite of the existence or absence of an exciton dissociation pathway. The unique optical properties can be attributed to the confinement of excited states in the [PbBr6]4− domains of high-entropy structure, and thus, benefit for the high-efficiency optical access of SPESs if the spin polarization of excitons could be maintained in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3.

As shown in Fig. 4a, we evaluate the lattice distortion of the [PbBr6]4− framework surrounded by other [MBr6]4− octahedra in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 by constructing the M-site doping models. Based on the refined crystal structures, the bond angle variance σ2 and the bond length variance Δd are calculated for the distortion of [PbBr6]4− framework40,41. The σ2 drastically increases from 0.22 to 29.1 after replacing a portion of Pb sites with M metals in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, and consequently, Δd rises from 0 to 0.00315, which is due to the difference of bond length among Pb-Br and various M-Br. This significantly breaks the octahedral symmetry of the crystal structure in the localized [PbBr6]4− domains formed by high-entropy M-site doping. The impact of incorporating Mg, Zn and Cd is further investigated, revealing that the structural distortion is related to the number and species of M elements (Fig. S19, Table S2). The high-entropy co-doping of Mg, Zn and Cd results in the highest degree of structural distortion and thus the most pronounced breaking of inversion asymmetry. The complete electronic band structures of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and CsPbBr3 are calculated using the GGA/PBE method with spin-orbit coupling (Figs. 4a, S20)42. Compared with the original band structure for octahedral CsPbBr3, the breaking of inversion symmetry in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 causes the enhanced Rashba spin splitting, i.e., the degenerate spin-up and spin-down bands to shift oppositely in the k-space43, which is experimentally validated by circular photogalvanic effect measurements (Fig. S21)44. The Rashba effect is characterized by both an energy splitting (ER) and a momentum offset (kR), which together determine the spin texture of the band-edge states. The energy splitting ER rises from 0.068 eV for CsPbBr3 to 0.274 eV for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3; more importantly, the momentum offset kR for the band minimum drastically increases from 0.053 Å−1 for CsPbBr3 to 0.305 Å−1 for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br345. Spin relaxation in CsPbBr3 and other lead-halide perovskites is typically controlled by the phonon-assisted spin relaxation pathways, which are mainly determined by kR rather than ER in the Rashba spin splitting46,47.

The spin dynamics of photogenerated excited states are profiled by the ultrafast transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy (Fig. S22), using circularly polarized (σ+/σ−) pump and probe lights on CsPbBr3 and Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 for spin injection and detection, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4b, the rapid rising of ground-state photobleaching (PB) band at ~516 nm indicates that excitons are formed immediately after photoexcitation48, and its decay extends to several nanoseconds and exhibits a redshift corresponding to the phonon-assisted thermal relaxation of high-energy hot excitons in CsPbBr3. The dynamics consist of a fast decay from the geminate recombination of excitons, and a slow decay associated with the exciton dissociation into free carriers (Fig. S23), which is consistent with the PL lifetime analysis. By using the σ+/σ− pump and probe lights, the spin relaxation of SPESs can be obtained by the difference between σ+σ+ and σ+σ− configurations, as shown in Fig. 4c. The PB signal for σ+σ+ increases at the expense of that for σ+σ− (Fig. S24), which is a typical signature of the exciton spin-flip process. τS of CsPbBr3 is measured to be ~4.6 ps at room temperature, similar to the average values from previous reports49–51. In Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, the PB band blue-shifts to ~506 nm, and the phonon-assisted thermal relaxation becomes less obvious (Fig. 4d). Also, the fast geminate recombination governs the decay dynamics of photogenerated excitons in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. More importantly, τS is determined to be as long as ~59.8 ps at room temperature, as shown in Fig. 4e. The prolonged spin relaxation is not observed in single-metal doping samples (Fig. S25), as they cannot be prepared at comparable substitution levels. It should be attributed to the large Rashba spin splitting in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, in which the large k-space splitting inhibits spin flips via phonon-assisted scattering processes. Note that the long τS in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 surpasses the previously reported spin relaxation time in LHPs52 (Table S3), which highlights the unique advantages of the high-entropy doping strategy for perovskite spintronics.

As shown in Fig. 5a, we use a home-built optical setup to examine the circularly polarized PL of SPESs in CsPbBr3 and Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. Similar to TA measurement, the σ+/σ− excitation is adopted for the generation of SPESs (optical write-in of spin state); instead, the optical read-out of SPESs is realized by measuring the circularly polarized (σ+/σ−) PL (Fig. S26). Therefore, the spin polarization (P) is defined as the difference of exciton populations on σ+/σ− sublevels (Fig. S27), which is determined by the competition between radiative recombination τR and spin relaxation τS, P~11+τR/τS 53. Taking τR = 4.1 ns and τS = 4.6 ps for CsPbBr3, P should be less than 0.1% and further decreased by the exciton dissociation and non-geminate recombination of carriers. As shown in Fig. 5b, the difference between σ+ and σ− PL spectra is indeed very limited in CsPbBr3; however, a decent P-value of ~2.1% is observed in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 at room temperature. It is reasonable according to the estimation from τR = 3.4 ns and τS = 59.8 ps, and the near unity PLQY minimizes any additional losses of SPESs in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. The exciton confinement and Rashba spin splitting optimize τR and τS simultaneously in high-entropy structure and lead to the spin polarization of excitons at room temperature, which is typically observed in other LHPs only at cryogenic temperatures10,11.

To verify that the circularly polarized PL is from the recombination of SPESs rather than optical anisotropy, a perpendicular magnetic field Bz is applied on Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 to accelerate the spin relaxation, i.e., decreasing τS (Fig. S28). This is known as the optical Hanle effect, a powerful tool to examine the physical origin of circular polarization54. In the presence of Bz, P(Bz)=PPL(0)1+(ωLT)2 where ωL=μBgexBz/ℏ is the Larmor frequency, and T=1/(1τR+1τS) is the effective lifetime of exciton spin polarization11,53. The exciton g-factor gex are measured to be ~1.30 for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and ~1.22 for CsPbBr3 (Fig. S29)53. As shown in Fig. 5c, P decreases with the increase of Bz on Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, and is diminished at the field strength of ~500 mT. It means that the circular polarization of PL from Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is solely attributed to the spin polarization of excitons, and an external field can be used for the spin manipulation of SPESs. By switching the σ⁺/σ− excitation on two spin sublevels for the write-in of SPESs, an opposite mirror-like Hanle curve is obtained with the same magnitude and line shape. Note that T is very close to τS when τS « τR, and the Hanle curves in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 can be well fitted using the τS parameter obtained above. This correlates and confirms the consistency among experimental results of PL spectroscopy, TA spectroscopy and optical Hanle effect measurements.

We further measure the Hanle curves at cryogenic temperatures, which offers a facile way to obtain the temperature dependence of τS values for both Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 (Fig. 5d) and CsPbBr3 (Fig. S30). At all measured temperatures, τS in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is nearly one order of magnitude longer than that of CsPbBr3, and approaching ~1 ns when the phonon-assisted process is suppressed at the lowest temperature of 6 K. The spin relaxation in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and CsPbBr3 appears to follow a combination of Elliott-Yafet (EY) and D’yakonov-Perel (DP) mechanisms46,47,52, where the EY mechanism should be dominated at room temperature and the DP mechanism seems more pronounced at liquid helium temperature. They are both related to phonon-assisted scattering, and possibly with the contributions from recombination/trapping and inhomogeneous broadening in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and CsPbBr3 nanocrystals. SPESs are optically generated using circularly polarized excitation, and the circular polarization of PL is determined by the competition between radiative recombination (τR) and spin relaxation (τS). As τR of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is consistently shorter than that of CsPbBr3 and their variations are relatively small for a broad temperature range, and the temperature dependence of P follows the same trend as that of τS in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 and CsPbBr3, as shown in Fig. 5e. In particular, P reaches as high as ~29.1% in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 at 6 K, which is greatly improved compared with P ~ 4.4% in CsPbBr3 (Fig. S31). It should be mentioned that the large P at 6 K can still be fully diminished by the optical Hanle effect under a Bz field of ~500 mT, which indicates its origin of SPESs and further allows for spin state manipulation (Fig. S32). Despite the fact that the exciton dissociation in CsPbBr3 becomes negligible at cryogenic temperatures, its τR/τS remains longer/shorter than those for Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. It proves that SPESs in high-entropy structures are intrinsically suitable for optical write-in and read-out of spin states.

A more feasible way to achieve a large P-value at room temperature is to decrease τR by nonlinear optical processes such as amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) under high excitation density55,56. Benefiting from its high optical gain, Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 shows a transition from broadband PL to narrowband ASE at a threshold of ~3.8 μJ/cm2 (Fig. S33), and τR is shortened to 0.2 ns for ASE above the threshold (Fig. S34). As shown in Fig. 6a, when Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is pumped with σ+ laser pulses, its ASE in σ+ polarization is obviously stronger than that in σ− polarization, with a P value as high as ~25.8% at room temperature. It is due to the very short τR for ASE compared to that for PL, as the spin polarization of excitons can be better maintained for the radiative recombination. Note that the P value for ASE from CsPbBr3 at room temperature is negligibly small (Fig. S35). As shown in Fig. 6b, the P value of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is constantly ~2% for PL under low pump fluences, and it sharply increases and reaches a saturation of >25% at the pump fluences exceeding the ASE threshold. The ASE circular polarization remains relatively stable during a long period (Fig. S36), which is an intrinsic property of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. Moreover, when switching the polarization of the pump laser from σ+ to σ−, the circular polarization of ASE in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 changes its polarity accordingly (Figs. S37, S38).

The optical access of SPESs is enabled in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 at room temperature, by using σ+/σ− light for writing-in and reading-out spin states. As shown in Fig. 6c, the polarity of light sources (σ+/σ−) determines the initial spin polarization of excitons and thus the polarity of light emissions (σ+/σ−) from Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3. In addition, a perpendicular field Bz is applied for the in-situ control of P values (spin polarization) based on the optical Hanle effect. Under the σ+/σ− laser pump and the field strengths of 0, 50, 100, 200 mT, the reading-out of SPESs using P values can be obtained on 8 distinguishable levels. Note that these levels of P values are highly reproducible (Fig. S39), and the magnetic field, as a non-contact gate control, enables the entire spin manipulation process to be fully reversible and nearly lossless (the loss of P < 0.1%). Thus, we believe that the high-entropy Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 offers a promising platform for optical access of SPESs and holds great potential in advanced opto-spintronic devices.

Discussion

In summary, we report the photo-physical (τR) and spin-physical (τS) properties of a high-entropy material Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, for the optical access of SPESs using circularly polarized lights. The isolated and distorted [PbBr6]4− domains in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 not only bring highly emissive short-lived excitons upon photoexcitation, but also slow their spin relaxation, especially at room temperature (τS = 59.8 ps) due to the Rashba effect. Transient and steady-state spectroscopies are adopted to verify the long spin lifetime of SPESs in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3, and the optical Hanle effect is observed to demonstrate the spin polarization of excitons for circularly polarized PL. The polarization of ASE from Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 is as high as 25.8%, providing the possibilities for multi-level optical access of SPESs by σ+/σ− write-in and read-out at room temperature. The compositional and structural engineering of high-entropy perovskites may further overcome current limitations in key spin-related parameters and help clarify the spin relaxation mechanisms in LHPs. This would benefit the construction of room-temperature spintronic devices for both optical and electronic categories based on LHPs. Future studies using continuous-wave pumping or electrical injection are essential for the practical device applications of spin-polarized lasers based on perovskite materials57,58. In addition, incorporating the high-entropy nanocrystals into microcavity or waveguide platforms may be useful for the manipulation of spin-polarized states on integrated photonic chips towards quantum information processing. The unique high-entropy structure of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 opens up a promising route to addressing photo-physical and spin-physical properties simultaneously, making it worthy of attention in the field of opto-spintronic materials.

Methods

Materials

Lead (II) bromide (PbBr2, 98%), Magnesium (II) bromide (MgBr2, 98%), Zinc (II) bromide (ZnBr2, 98%), Cadmium (II) bromide (CdBr2, 98%, anhydrous), Cesium (I) bromide (CsBr, 98%) were purchased from InnoChem. Toluene (>99.8%), N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, >99.8%, anhydrous), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99.8%, anhydrous), Ethanol (EtOH, >99.8%), Methyl acetate (MeOAc, 99%), 3-Methyl-2-butanone (98%), Acetonitrile (MeCN, >99.5%), Oleic acid (OLA, 90%) and Oleylamine (OAM, approximate C18-content 80–90%) came from Aladdin. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of CsPbBr3 nanocrystals

In a typical synthesis of CsPbBr3 nanocrystals, CsBr (0.4 M) was dissolved in ethanol (EtOH), while PbBr2 (0.4 M) was dissolved in a 9:1 mixture of DMF and DMSO. OLA (1.5 mL) and OAM (100 μL) were combined with 35 mL of toluene in a 100 mL round-bottom flask. Following this, the perovskite precursor solutions, PbBr2 (1.5 mL) and CsBr (1.5 mL), were added dropwise while stirring vigorously. The reaction was stirred for 2 min at room temperature and under ambient conditions, leading to the formation of a precipitate. The precipitate was separated from the reaction mixture by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 6797 g, 8 min). It was then redispersed in 6 mL of fresh toluene and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The resulting supernatant containing the colloidal CsPbBr3 nanocrystals was filtered using a syringe filter (pore size = 0.22 μm). To wash the nanocrystals, 2.5 mL of MeOAc was added to 5.5 mL of the CsPbBr3 colloidal dispersion, and the mixture was immediately centrifuged (8000 rpm, 6797 g, 8 min). The precipitate was then redispersed in 5 mL of fresh toluene and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The purified CsPbBr3 solution was stored for subsequent experiments.

Synthesis of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals

In a typical synthesis of Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals, within a nitrogen-filled glovebox, MgBr2 (30.3 mg, 0.165 mmol), ZnBr2 (37.2 mg, 0.165 mmol), and CdBr2 (44.9 mg, 0.165 mmol) were dissolved in a mixture of toluene (600 μL), OLA (150 μL), OAM (150 μL), and 3-methyl-2-butanone (150 μL), and stirred briefly. This suspension was then combined with the CsPbBr3 colloidal dispersion (1.8 mL) and stirred at room temperature for about 12 h. The reaction mixture was separated by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 6797 g, 8 min). The supernatant containing the dispersed Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals was filtered using a syringe filter (pore size = 0.22 μm). Then, a 2:1 (v/v) mixture of MeOAc and MeCN (900 μL) was added to 1.8 mL of the Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 colloidal dispersion, which caused a precipitate to form. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (10000 rpm, 10621 g, 12 min) and redispersed in a smaller volume of toluene (~200 μL). The colloidal dispersion was then centrifuged (8000 rpm, 6797 g, 8 min). The purified Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br3 nanocrystals were collected from the supernatant without filtration and stored for future experiments.

Characterizations

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded using a PANalytical B.V. Empyrean diffractometer with Cu Kα (1.54 Å) radiation. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was conducted using a field emission TEM (JEOL JEM-2100). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra, photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were obtained by an Edinburgh Instruments FLS 1000, and the temperature-dependent PL spectrum was measured by its temperature control system. Optical Hanle effect measurements were carried out using a home-built optical system, as shown in Fig. S24. The diffuse reflectance spectra of the microcrystalline powders were recorded on a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Hitachi, UH4150), calibrated by simultaneous measurement of the substance (BaSO4 powder), and then converted to absorbance using the Kubelka-Munk theory. Femtosecond transient absorption (TA) characterizations were carried out using a commercial ultrafast TA spectroscopy system (Harpia-TA, Light Conversion). Circularly polarized TA spectroscopy was modified on the TA system, as shown in Fig. S22. The Raman spectra were recorded using a HORIBA microconfocal laser Raman spectrometer.

Details for theoretical calculation

First-principles calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), grounded in density functional theory (DFT) and employing the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method. To further investigate the electronic structure, we conducted hybrid functional calculations using the Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) approach, incorporating spin-orbit coupling (SOC). The plane-wave basis set was truncated at an energy cutoff of 350 eV. Structural optimizations were performed using the Quasi-Newton algorithm, with convergence criteria set to a total energy variation below 10−5 eV and atomic forces less than 10−3 eV/Å. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of 4 × 2 × 4. To address the well-known underestimation of band gaps by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), we employed the Tran-Blaha modified Becke-Johnson (TB-mBJ) potential in a perturbative manner for band structure and density of states (DOS) computations.
