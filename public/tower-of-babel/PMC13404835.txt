Relative strength of major determinants of quality of life in rheumatoid arthritis: an observational, cross-sectional study in tertiary rheumatology centres
Authors: Lumie Sabanai Shintaku, Cleandro Pires Albuquerque, Ana Paula Monteiro Gomides Reis, Rina Dalva Neubarth Giorgi, Sebastião Radominski, Leticia Rocha Pereira, Ivânio Alves Pereira, Maria Fernanda Brandão Resende Guimarães, Alisson Pugliesi, Paulo Louzada Junior, Maria de Fátima Lobato Da Cunha Sauma, Karina Rossi Bonfiglioli, Claiton Viegas Brenol, Licia Maria Henrique da Mota, Geraldo da Rocha Castelar Pinheiro, REAL Study
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the relative strength of clinical and sociodemographic predictors of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) under real-life conditions.MethodsThis cross-sectional, observational study included adult patients fulfilling the 2010 American College of Rheumatology-European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria for RA, recruited from 11 public tertiary rheumatology centres in Brazil. To reflect real-life clinical practice, no restrictions were applied regarding disease stage or treatment status. HRQoL was assessed using the 12-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12). Physical disability was measured by the Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI). Clinical and epidemiological variables were evaluated as candidate predictors. Associations were explored using bivariate analyses and multiple linear regression models. Standardised beta coefficients (zβ) were used to estimate the relative strength of independent predictors.Results1079 patients were included; 89.4% were women and 56.0% were white, with a mean (SD) age of 57.0 (11.4) years and a median (IQR) disease duration of 152 (84.0–242.0) months.In multivariate analysis, the following remained independently associated with physical score: HAQ-DI score (β=−3.92, 95% CI (−4.56 to −3.28), zβ=−0.44, p<0.001), non-white race (β=−0.97, 95% CI (−1.80 to −0.14), zβ=−0.71, p=0.022) and absence of regular physical activities (β=0.56, 95% CI (0.04 to 1.09), zβ=−0.07, p=0.036).For mental score, remaining independent predictors were HAQ-DI (β=−5.00, 95% CI (−6.17 to −3.83), zβ=−0.31, p<0.001), fibromyalgia (β=−5.52, 95% CI (−7.76 to −3.26), zβ=−0.15, p<0.001), white race (β=3.32, 95% CI (1.79 to 4.84), zβ=0.13, p<0.001), current smoking (β=−4.95, 95% CI (7.42 to −2.48), zβ=−0.12, p<0.001), female biological sex (β=3.91, 95% CI (−6.36 to −1.46), zβ=−0.10, p=0.002) and Simplified Disease Activity Index (β=−0.91, 95% CI (−0.17 to −0.02), zβ=−0.09, p=0.018).ConclusionPhysical disability was the strongest determinant of impaired HRQoL in both physical and mental domains among participants with RA. Other relevant determinants of poorer physical HRQoL in descending order of relative strength were non-White race and absence of regular physical activity, while for poorer mental HRQoL, fibromyalgia, race, smoking status, female sex and disease activity. These findings reinforce functional preservation as a key therapeutic target in routine RA care.Keywords: RHEUMATOLOGY, Quality of Life, Chronic Disease, Patient Reported Outcome Measures

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease that primarily affects peripheral joints, leading to inflammation of the synovial membrane and periarticular soft tissues. Additionally, it is associated with extra-articular manifestations.1 2 RA can result in persistent joint pain, irreversible deformities and functional disability.3–5 Moreover, it is closely linked to depression and anxiety.6–8

The WHO defines quality of life as an individual’s perception of their position in life within the context of their culture and value system, in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns.9 Quality of life is often compromised in patients with RA.10–13 Several factors have been identified as predictors of poorer health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including female sex, advanced age, presence of comorbidities, functional disability, higher disease activity and subjective pain.14–17

Identifying factors associated with poorer HRQoL allows clinicians to focus on the most relevant aspects during consultations. Consequently, this approach can enhance therapeutic decision-making, improve clinical management and promote early interventions. A more personalised and comprehensive approach to patient care may ultimately mitigate the impact of the disease on overall well-being.18 19 However, despite the recognition of these contributing factors, the relative strength of different predictors associated with poorer HRQoL remains unclear.

This study aimed to evaluate the relative strength of several clinical characteristics potentially related to HRQoL in patients with RA under real-world conditions.

Methods

Study design

This observational, cross-sectional study corresponds to a subanalysis of data derived from the multicentre Rheumatoid Arthritis in Real Life (REAL) study.20 This was a multicentre prospective cohort study conducted in 11 Brazilian public university hospitals across multiple geographic regions of the country, between August 2015 and April 2016, providing a nationwide real-life representation. The REAL study has been described in detail elsewhere20 and has generated multiple publications exploring clinical characteristics, treatment patterns and patient-reported outcomes in Brazilian patients with RA.21–23

Patient cohort

Participants meeting the 2010 American College of Rheumatology-European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria for RA24 were enrolled. The subanalysis included all participants from the REAL study who had data available on HRQoL and disability, as assessed using the 12-item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12) and the Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI), respectively.

Given the real-world nature of this study, we deliberately included a patient population that reflects routine clinical practice, encompassing the heterogeneity of clinical presentations, comorbidities, associated factors and disease duration. Therefore, we did not restrict the inclusion of participants to specific time points during the course of the disease.

Collected variables

Sociodemographic and lifestyle data were collected through a structured interview. The variables included biological sex, race, body mass index (BMI), current smoking status, practice of regular physical activity, marital status, education level and socioeconomic classification. Economic classification was determined according to the Brazilian Economic Classification Criterion that categorises individuals into socioeconomic strata (A–E) based on a scoring system derived from household assets, availability of public services and the education level of the household head.25 For the purpose of this study, the strata were aggregated as upper (wealthiest, A and B) and lower (poorer, C, D and E) classes.

Data about medical history and RA diagnosis and treatment details were also collected, such as total disease duration, time from the symptoms’ onset to the first disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD), rheumatoid factor (RF) titres, presence of erosive disease, fibromyalgia diagnosis, other comorbid diagnoses and current use of corticosteroid, methotrexate and biological DMARD (bDMARD). Delayed treatment (non-early treatment) was defined as initiation of DMARD therapy more than 6 months after symptom onset.

Disease activity was assessed using the Simplified Disease Activity Index (SDAI). Pain intensity was measured using a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 to 100 mm. Functional capacity was evaluated through the HAQ-DI, while HRQoL was assessed using the SF-12 physical and mental subscales. In the SF-12, lower scores indicate poorer HRQoL, whereas higher scores reflect better perceived health status.

Statistical analysis

Initially, data were analysed using descriptive statistics, including measures of central tendency and dispersion (for continuous and discrete numerical variables) and absolute and relative frequencies (for categorical variables).

In bivariate analyses, the differences in SF-12 scores between groups regarding several dichotomised clinical characteristics were assessed using Student’s t-test, while associations between SF-12 scores and numerical clinical predictors were evaluated through Pearson’s correlation.

Subsequently, only the predictors identified as significantly associated with the outcomes of interest (SF-12 physical and mental domains) in the previous bivariate analyses were carried forward to the multivariate models. This two-step approach was adopted as a strategy to identify potentially relevant associations preliminarily and reduce the final number of independent variables in the multivariate models. Then, multiple linear regression models were employed so that the multiple covariates were adjusted for each other’s effects, aiming at identifying the predictors independently associated with HRQoL. The standardised β-coefficients (zβ) from the regression models were used to determine the relative strength of those predictors. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05. Correction for multiple testing in the bivariate analyses was not applied because that step was intended for higher sensitivity, that is, to spot all potentially relevant candidate predictors, even at the cost of lower specificity (higher risk for type 1 error).

Considering the standardised coefficients (zβ), the HAQ-DI score was identified as the strongest predictor of HRQoL on both SF-12 physical and mental subscales, such that higher disability (HAQ-DI) indicated poorer HRQoL (SF-12). For the SF-12 physical score, the next strongest predictors of poorer HRQoL were non-White race, followed by the absence of regular physical activity. In contrast, for the SF-12 mental score, additional significant predictors of poorer HRQoL, in order of strength, were fibromyalgia, White race, higher joint pain intensity, female biological sex and current smoking (tables 5 and 6).

Discussion

This study evaluated the relative strength of key clinical characteristics associated with poor HRQoL in patients with RA under real-world conditions. The predictors related to poorer physical HRQoL, in order of strength, were disability, non-White race and absence of physical activities. While for poorer HRQoL, they were disability, fibromyalgia, White race, higher joint pain intensity, female biological sex and current smoking.

In our study, female biological sex was associated with poorer mental HRQoL. Other studies have reported worse scores in HRQoL questionnaires among women in RA, despite erosive disease and extra-articular manifestations being more common in men.27–30 The worse HRQoL finding among females might be explained by factors such as delayed referral to rheumatology care, a higher prevalence of comorbidities and lower treatment response rates to DMARDs observed among female patients.27 31 32 The association between poorer mental HRQoL and female sex may be explained by the greater frequency of depression and anxiety in women, which are the most common psychiatric disorders.33 This is consistent with other studies that show that female patients with RA have lower levels of psychological well-being.27 34 Effective strategies to combat psychiatric disorders, such as timely diagnosis, accessible pharmacological and psychological therapies and psychoeducation, are important to reduce their impact on HRQoL.35

Interestingly, the White race was related to a worse mental HRQoL, but the non-White race was associated with a worse physical HRQoL in our study. Prior research has associated non-White race with worse HRQoL,36–38 largely attributed to healthcare disparities and underlying genetic characteristics.39 40 Furthermore, HRQoL questionnaires may be influenced by reading levels, language translation, cultural norms, preference values and cognitive processing, which might cause a non-equivalent assessment of the HRQoL in different ethnicities.41 However, psychiatric disorders, especially mood disorders, seem to be more prevalent among the White race,42 43 which could contribute to poorer mental HRQoL in this subgroup.44 45

Current smoking was identified as a predictor of poorer mental HRQoL, consistent with previous studies.40 46 Smoking is associated with psychiatric disorders such as depression, anxiety and substance use disorders.38 It is a major extrinsic risk factor for RA development and severity due to its role in promoting oxidative stress and inflammation, inducing autoantibody formation and triggering epigenetic modifications.47 48 It can also influence the risk of infections and have a negative impact on treatment response.41 49 Combating smoking relies on integrated approaches that combine education, behavioural therapy, pharmacological support and ongoing follow-up.38

We also found that regular physical activity was related to better HRQoL, in line with other studies showing reduced pain perception and improved HRQoL in patients with RA who engage in physical exercise.45 50 51 Physical activity may promote an anti-inflammatory effect by an initial increase in interleukin (IL) 6, which subsequently stimulates the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1 receptor antagonist and IL-10 and soluble tumour necrosis factor receptors.52 Besides, it seems to increase self-esteem, alleviate symptoms of depression, improve sleep quality and decrease pain perception.51 The implementation of educational initiatives, motivational strategies and supervised and accessible programmes is important to improve adherence to physical activity in RA patients.53 54

Fibromyalgia emerged as a predictor of worse mental HRQoL. This condition has been associated with a higher pain level, increased composite disease activity scores and worse psychological status and HRQoL in patients with RA.37 39 55 56 The coexistence of fibromyalgia might contribute to persistent pain through alterations in central nervous system pain-processing mechanisms.36 38

Disability was the most important predictor of poorer HRQoL, both physical and mental. Several investigations in diverse populations have consistently shown a relationship between disability and HRQoL in patients with RA.57–60 Three studies specifically identified disability as the main predictor of poorer HRQoL.4 18 61 This association has likewise been observed in other rheumatic diseases such as spondylarthritis, osteoarthritis, mixed connective tissue disease, systemic lupus erythematosus and systemic sclerosis.62 In patients with interstitial lung diseases, respiratory symptoms-related disability was also associated with poorer HRQoL in RA, as well as in systemic sclerosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.63 Limitations in physical function were closely linked to reduced independence and greater disease burden, contributing significantly to a decline in perceived health status.18 64 This finding emphasises the importance of strategies to prevent physical disability, such as early diagnosis and treatment, as well as approaches to address established damage, including rehabilitation, occupational therapy and psychoeducation.65 66

Another important finding was that disease activity was not associated with either physical or mental components of HRQoL. This aligns with other studies suggesting that measurements of disease activity alone may not fully capture the patient’s HRQoL experience.12 15 67 68 Physicians tend to prioritise disease activity in clinical assessments, whereas patients are more likely to value pain and disability.69 As a matter of fact, joint pain was associated with worse mental HRQoL in our study, in line with other research that has identified pain as a contributor to lower overall HRQoL.57 58 70 71 Consequently, it is important to aim for adequate pain control in these patients through pharmacological, behavioural, neuromodulator and psychological approaches.72

We should note some limitations of our current study. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between the identified predictors and HRQoL. Although significant associations were observed, it is not possible to determine whether certain factors directly influence poorer HRQoL or if they result from it. Patients included in this study were recruited from public, specialised rheumatology centres, which may not fully represent individuals in primary care or private healthcare settings. Additionally, as with any study based on self-reported measures, there is a possibility of recall bias, particularly regarding pain perception and functionality. Future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings and better understand the causal pathways influencing HRQoL in patients with RA.

Conclusion

Physical disability emerged as the strongest determinant of poorer HRQoL, in both physical and mental domains, among patients with RA. Other relevant predictors in order of strength were, for physical HRQoL, non-White race and the absence of regular physical activity; for mental HRQoL, fibromyalgia, White race, joint pain, female and current smoking. This finding reinforces the critical role of physical function in patients’ overall well-being and highlights the need for healthcare teams to prioritise strategies aimed at preserving and improving functional capacity. Early rehabilitation, pain management and daily living activities adaptations, which are better attained through a multidisciplinary approach, should be emphasised in seeking to mitigate the impact of RA on HRQoL.
