Self-assessed Traditional Chinese Medicine nursing competency profiles among clinical nurses in a tertiary hospital in China: a cross-sectional latent profile analysis
Authors: Ming Jin, Yongping Lu, Jing Yang, Zhiqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo describe the overall level of self-assessed Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing competency among clinical nurses, identify latent competency profiles, and examine demographic and training-related attitude factors associated with profile membership.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from January to October 2024 at a tertiary Grade A hospital in China. Eligible nurses were recruited voluntarily from multiple clinical departments in which TCM nursing services were routinely provided. A total of 457 valid questionnaires were analyzed. Data were collected using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Nursing Competency Questionnaire and a questionnaire assessing training-related attitudes and needs among TCM nursing staff. Latent profile analysis was used to identify competency profiles, and multinomial logistic regression was performed to examine factors associated with profile membership.ResultsThe overall level of self-assessed TCM nursing competency was moderate. Scores were higher for TCM nursing techniques but lower for advanced clinical practice, indicating relatively limited advanced-practice and integrative application capacity. Latent profile analysis supported a three-profile solution (entropy = 0.846): low self-assessed competency (12.3%), moderate self-assessed competency (64.3%), and high self-assessed competency (23.4%) profiles. Profiles differed significantly in years of service, professional title, educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training. In multinomial logistic regression, educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training were consistently associated with membership in higher self-assessed competency profiles. Professional title was significant only in the high-profile contrast, whereas years of service was not independently associated with profile membership after adjustment.ConclusionsClinical nurses working in departments where TCM nursing services were routinely provided showed heterogeneous patterns of self-assessed TCM nursing competency, with most nurses classified into the moderate self-assessed competency profile and advanced clinical practice emerging as a relative weakness. Profile-informed, tiered training strategies focusing on contextualized practice and clinical application may be warranted. However, given the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data, the identified profiles should be interpreted as patterns of perceived competency rather than developmental stages or evidence of progression over time. Longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to further verify these associations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing competency, Self-assessed competency profile, Latent profile analysis (LPA), Clinical nurses, Cross-sectional study, Intention to participate in training, Training needs, Multinomial logistic regression

Background

The trend of population aging and an increasing burden of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) and other chronic health conditions requires health systems to prioritize long-term management and continuous, integrated care rather than episodic treatment of acute illness [1]. According to projections and statistics provided by the World Health Organization (WHO), the percentage of the aged population is on the rise and the number of deaths caused by NCDs is high [2–4]. One systematic review approximated that a considerable proportion (46%) of older individuals have multimorbidity (more than two chronic illnesses) [5], and multimorbidity requires more person-centered integrated care [6, 7]. Continuity of care is also highly dependent on nurses, who serve as a pillar of continuity, and their competence is directly associated with the quality of chronic disease management and the effectiveness of health promotion [8, 9]. As such, defining and consolidating key nursing competencies in long-term care settings is a significant driver of chronic disease management and healthy aging.

In the context of the Healthy China 2030 strategy, China has gone further to conserve and develop traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) [10]. One of the national strategic plans in the development of TCM (2016–2030) points to health promotion initiatives based on the concept of preventive treatment of disease [11]. The National Plan of the Development of Nursing Services (2021–2025) recommends competency-oriented training systems, and enhancement of standards and specifications regarding TCM nursing skills, procedures, and practice [12]. In China, TCM is embedded in the national healthcare system and may be delivered not only in specialized TCM hospitals but also in general hospitals and departments where TCM-related nursing services are available. For nurses, exposure to TCM nursing may occur through multiple pathways, including entry-level nursing education, hospital orientation, department-based in-service training, and continuing professional development [13]. However, the depth, standardization, and clinical emphasis of TCM nursing education and training may vary across educational institutions, hospital levels, departments, and local service models. Therefore, TCM nursing competency should not be understood as a uniform requirement for all nurses in all clinical settings, nor as equivalent to the full competency framework of nurses who have completed formal TCM nursing specialist training. Rather, in the present study, it refers to the TCM-specific knowledge, techniques, and clinical application abilities expected of clinical nurses working in departments where TCM nursing services are routinely provided.

In the Chinese clinical nursing context, TCM nursing practice generally refers to nursing care informed by TCM theories and delivered within the nursing scope of practice and institutional protocols. In departments where TCM nursing services are provided, such practice may include TCM-related health assessment and patient education, syndrome-differentiation-informed nursing care, dietary and lifestyle guidance based on TCM concepts, and selected non-pharmacological TCM nursing techniques or interventions, such as acupoint application, acupressure, moxibustion, cupping, scraping, auricular acupressure, herbal fumigation, or TCM foot bathing, depending on hospital regulations and departmental routines. These activities are usually implemented as part of nursing care and do not imply independent medical diagnosis, prescription of Chinese herbal medicines, or physician-level TCM treatment decision-making. Accordingly, in the present study, TCM nursing competency is conceptualized as a TCM-specific domain of nursing competence, referring to nurses’ self-assessed ability to apply TCM-related knowledge, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM-oriented clinical practice in patient care. TCM nursing competency in geriatric, rehabilitation, and chronic disease care should encompass a solid theoretical foundation, including pattern differentiation, proficiency in TCM nursing techniques, and the ability to integrate these competencies into clinical practice. Nevertheless, there is heterogeneity in access to training, opportunities for clinical application, and transfer of training to practice among clinical nurses, implying substantial individual differences in this domain-specific competency [14]. Therefore, evaluation based solely on mean levels may be insufficient for guiding training-resource allocation.

TCM nursing competency measurement models are beginning to emerge, such as an indicator system of core competencies among nurses using TCM specialty based on evidence synthesis [15], and the creation and testing of a competency scale of clinical care in TCM hospitals among nurses [16]. However, most studies have relied on variable-centered approaches that emphasize average levels and may obscure latent subgroups [17]. Benner’s novice-to-expert model highlights that nursing competency is context-dependent and may vary across levels of clinical experience and practice exposure [18], providing a theoretical rationale for examining heterogeneity in competency patterns. In the present study, this perspective was used only to support the expectation that nurses may differ in their TCM-specific nursing competency. The latent profiles identified in this study were therefore interpreted as patterns of self-assessed competency rather than as formal developmental stages or evidence of progression over time. More specifically, the construct examined here refers to differences in self-assessed TCM-related knowledge, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM-oriented clinical practice. Person-centered latent profile analysis (LPA) can identify latent classes that differ in competency patterns within a population. Emerging evidence supports LPA for identifying heterogeneity in nurses’ professional characteristics, competencies, and training needs and indicates that training experiences and educational attainment are associated with class membership [19–21]. However, in TCM nursing, empirical studies remain scarce—particularly those integrating training-related attitude indicators with competency profiles. Accordingly, analytic strategies that foreground population heterogeneity are needed to identify TCM nursing competency profiles and explore their correlates.

Self-assessed competency may also be related to nurses’ attitudes toward continuing learning and professional training. Self-determination theory posits that the fulfillment of the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness can support high-quality motivation and sustained engagement in learning [22, 23]. Studies on nurses’ continuing professional development likewise suggest that intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, together with organizational support, influence participation in continuing education [24]. In the present study, self-determination theory was used only as a broad interpretive lens for understanding why nurses’ intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training may be associated with self-assessed TCM nursing competency profiles. However, the study did not directly measure the core constructs of self-determination theory, such as autonomy, competence, relatedness, or different forms of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Therefore, these variables are referred to as training-related attitudes rather than direct indicators of self-determined motivation. In this context, nurses may differ not only in their self-assessed TCM nursing competency but also in their intention to participate in training and in the value they attach to such training. However, empirical evidence on how these factors relate to latent competency profiles in TCM nursing remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to (1) describe the overall level of self-assessed TCM nursing competency among clinical nurses; (2) identify latent profiles of TCM nursing competency using latent profile analysis; and (3) examine whether demographic characteristics and training-related attitudes, including intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training, were associated with profile membership. By adopting a person-centered approach, this study sought to generate evidence to support profile-informed training and workforce development of the TCM nursing workforce.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center cross-sectional survey conducted from January to October 2024 at a tertiary Grade A hospital in Guangyuan, Sichuan Province, China. In the Chinese hospital system, a tertiary Grade A hospital represents the highest level of comprehensive hospital care, with relatively strong clinical, teaching, and specialist service capacity. With coordination from the Nursing Department, questionnaires were distributed hospital-wide to eligible on-duty clinical nurses, and participation was voluntary; therefore, a convenience sample was obtained. A total of 489 questionnaires were distributed, and all questionnaires were returned, yielding a response rate of 100.0%. After quality-control screening, 32 questionnaires were excluded as invalid, including 7 questionnaires completed in less than 3 min, 22 questionnaires with patterned or highly similar responses, and 3 questionnaires with failed logical consistency checks. Finally, 457 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding an effective questionnaire rate of 93.5%.

Participants were recruited from multiple clinical departments in which TCM nursing services and TCM nursing interventions were routinely provided. Accordingly, the study population consisted of clinical nurses working in departments with routine exposure to TCM nursing practice within a tertiary-hospital setting. These participants were general clinical nurses with exposure to TCM nursing services; completion of formal TCM nursing specialist training or possession of a certificate of completion for TCM nursing specialist training was not required as an eligibility criterion. Participants represented different professional ranks and clinical backgrounds. Data were collected once during the same survey period. Because this was a cross-sectional survey based on convenience sampling within a single institution, the sample size was determined by the accessible study population during the survey window rather than by an a priori sample size calculation.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) on-duty clinical nurses employed at the study hospital; (2) able to understand and complete the questionnaire independently; and (3) working in a department that provided TCM nursing services and delivered TCM nursing interventions.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-nursing personnel; (2) nurses who were on leave or unavailable because of off-site training or further education; (3) nurses on continuous sick leave or maternity leave for ≥ 3 months; and (4) nurses undertaking short-term clinical rotation in the department, because their temporary placement might not reflect stable exposure to the department’s TCM nursing practice environment.

Questionnaires were excluded as invalid if they met any of the predefined quality-control criteria: (1) completion time of less than 3 min; (2) patterned, highly similar, or one-dimensional responses across questionnaire items; or (3) failed logical consistency checks.

Measurement instruments

General information questionnaire

A general information questionnaire was developed by the research team through a literature review and expert consultation to assess participants’ demographic and work-related characteristics. Items included sex, age, years of nursing experience, professional title, employment type, highest educational attainment, graduating institution, and whether the participant’s department provided TCM nursing services and offered TCM nursing techniques/interventions. In the Chinese nursing career system, professional title reflects professional rank, level of responsibility, and clinical seniority. In general, nurses with junior titles mainly provide frontline clinical care, those with intermediate titles usually assume greater independent clinical responsibilities, and those with associate senior titles or above are more likely to undertake advanced clinical, teaching, supervisory, or management roles.

Traditional Chinese Medicine nursing competence questionnaire

TCM nursing competency was assessed using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Nursing Competency Questionnaire developed by Wang Dan et al. (2018) [25]. The instrument comprises 37 items across three dimensions: basic knowledge of TCM nursing (12 items), TCM nursing techniques (18 items), and advanced TCM nursing clinical practice (7 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale of mastery/familiarity (1 = very unfamiliar, 2 = unfamiliar, 3 = moderate, 4 = familiar, 5 = very familiar). Higher scores indicate greater TCM nursing competency. Total scores range from 37 to 185; dimension mean scores (1–5) may also be used for cross-dimension comparisons. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the three dimensions were 0.91, 0.88, and 0.96, respectively, and the corresponding content validity index (CVI) values were 0.83, 0.91, and 0.80, respectively.

Training needs questionnaire for TCM nursing staff in public hospitals

The Training Needs Questionnaire of the TCM Nursing Staff in Public Hospital created by Ye Meixia et al. [26] was used to measure training needs. In the present study, “TCM nursing training” was defined as continuing professional development or in-service educational activities related to TCM nursing knowledge, TCM nursing techniques, and TCM-oriented clinical application. Such training may include theoretical instruction, technical skills training, case-based learning, bedside demonstration, supervised practice, and department-based or hospital-level continuing education, depending on institutional arrangements. This study did not evaluate a standardized training intervention delivered by the research team; instead, training was assessed from the perspective of nurses’ perceived training needs and training-related attitudes. The questionnaire includes six domains: intention to participate in training, perceived importance, training frequency, modalities of theoretical training, modalities of skills training, and training content. Among these domains, intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training were used in the present study as training-related attitude variables, because they were directly aligned with the study aim of examining factors associated with latent competency profile membership. Accordingly, the term “training-related factors” in this study mainly refers to nurses’ attitudes toward TCM nursing training rather than the effect of participation in a specific standardized training programme. A pilot survey and psychometric testing were conducted; the cumulative variance explained was 62.1%, and the overall Cronbach’s α was 0.84.

Analytical framework

In this study, the three dimension scores of the Traditional Chinese Medicine Nursing Competency Questionnaire—basic knowledge of TCM nursing, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM nursing clinical practice—served as continuous indicators in the latent profile analysis. These three dimension-level indicators were selected because they correspond to the validated structure of the questionnaire and represent the main domains of the TCM nursing competency construct measured in this study: knowledge, techniques, and advanced clinical practice. Using dimension-level scores also helped maintain model parsimony and facilitated interpretation in a sample of 457 nurses. Given the use of dimension-level indicators, the resulting profiles were interpreted primarily as level-based patterns of self-assessed TCM nursing competency and were therefore labeled as low, moderate, and high self-assessed competency profiles. In the subsequent multinomial logistic regression, latent profile membership was treated as the dependent variable. Variables included in the multinomial logistic regression were selected a priori based on the study aims, the conceptual focus on TCM nursing competency and continuing professional development, and previous literature suggesting potential associations of work experience, professional title, educational attainment, and training-related attitudes with nursing competency. Years of work experience, professional title, educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training were entered as explanatory variables.

Data quality control

Quality control processes were applied during both questionnaire administration and data processing to improve data accuracy, completeness, and analyzability. A standard set of questions and scoring procedures was tested before questionnaire distribution. The questionnaire was self-administered, and respondents were given standardized instructions regarding the study purpose and confidentiality to reduce social desirability bias and nonresponse. Questionnaires were screened for excessive missing data, inconsistent response patterns, and one-dimensional response profiles across the full set of items. Data were coded using a standardized codebook and entered into the database with verification procedures. Logical range checks and consistency checks were conducted, and implausible values and potential outliers were checked against the original records and corrected where necessary. Missing data were handled according to the requirements of each analysis. Specifically, available-case analysis was used where applicable for descriptive and inferential analyses, whereas full information maximum likelihood (FIML) was used in the latent profile analysis. Common method bias (CMB) (Harman’s single-factor test) and internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) were evaluated to support subsequent latent profile analysis (LPA) and regression modeling.

Data analysis

Valid questionnaires (n = 457) were included in the analyses. Descriptive statistics and latent profile analysis (LPA) were conducted. Item scores were summed according to the scoring rules to obtain three continuous dimension scores representing basic knowledge of TCM nursing, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM nursing clinical practice. To assess common method bias (CMB), Harman’s single-factor test was conducted; exploratory factor analysis of all items extracted six factors, and the first unrotated factor explained 40.284% of the variance (< 50%). Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as n (%). For descriptive statistics, χ² tests, and multinomial logistic regression, analyses were conducted using available cases.

In Mplus (version 8.11), LPA was conducted by fitting 1- to 5-profile models using the three dimension scores as continuous indicators. Models were estimated using maximum likelihood (ML), with full information maximum likelihood (FIML) used to handle missing data. Multiple random starts were specified to reduce the risk of local maxima and to obtain a stable best log-likelihood solution. Model selection was not based solely on the lowest information criteria, because AIC, BIC, and aBIC may continue to decrease as additional profiles are extracted in mixture modeling. Instead, the final profile solution was selected by jointly considering statistical fit indices, entropy, likelihood ratio tests, profile size, average posterior classification probabilities, model parsimony, and substantive interpretability. In particular, models with very small profiles, non-significant improvement over the model with one fewer profile, unstable or poorly separated classes, or additional profiles that only subdivided an existing level-based pattern without clear clinical or theoretical meaning were considered less preferable. This strategy was used to reduce the risk of over-extraction and to identify a profile solution that was both statistically acceptable and clinically interpretable. Specifically, model selection considered the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT), and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), together with profile proportions and substantive interpretability. Participants were assigned to profiles based on maximum posterior probability, and average posterior probabilities were examined to evaluate classification quality and profile separation.

Between-profile differences were tested using χ² tests (two-sided, α = 0.05). Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with profile membership, with the low self-assessed TCM nursing competency profile serving as the reference group; results are reported as B, standard error (SE), Wald statistic, p value, odds ratio (OR), and 95% confidence interval (CI). Predictors were coded as years of experience (1–5 for 0–5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–20, and ≥ 21 years), professional title (1–3 for junior, intermediate, and associate senior or above, corresponding to increasing professional rank and responsibility in the Chinese nursing career system), educational attainment (1–3 for technical secondary school, junior college, and bachelor’s degree or above), intention to participate in training (1–5 from very unwilling to very willing), and perceived importance of training (1–5 from not important at all to very important). Because these predictors had clear ordinal structures and clinically interpretable rank orders, they were entered into the multinomial logistic regression model as ordered predictors rather than as nominal dummy variables. This modeling strategy was used to preserve ordinal information, maintain model parsimony, and reduce potential parameter instability that could arise from sparse cells if multiple category-specific dummy variables were created. Accordingly, the ORs for these predictors represent the change in the odds of membership in the moderate or high self-assessed competency profile versus the low profile for each one-category increase in the ordered predictor. This approach assumes an approximately monotonic association between increasing predictor levels and the log-odds of profile membership; therefore, the results should be interpreted as ordinal trend associations rather than category-specific contrasts. Before fitting the regression model, profile sizes and cross-tabulated distributions of predictors by profile membership were reviewed. Because some response categories contained relatively small numbers, no category-specific dummy-variable estimates were used for these ordinal predictors. This approach was intended to avoid overparameterization and to improve the stability and interpretability of the regression model in the available sample.

Ethics approval and informed consent

This cross-sectional study involved no intervention. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of respect, justice, and beneficence outlined in the Belmont Report. Participation was voluntary; participants were informed of the study objectives and their right to withdraw at any time. To protect privacy, the questionnaire was completed anonymously, and data were used only for academic research. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangyuan Central Hospital (approval no. GYZXLL2024010). Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 489 questionnaires distributed during the survey period, all were returned, yielding a response rate of 100.0%. After quality-control screening, 32 questionnaires were excluded as invalid, including 7 completed in less than 3 min, 22 with patterned or highly similar responses, and 3 with failed logical consistency checks. Finally, 457 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, corresponding to an effective questionnaire rate of 93.5% (Table 1). Participants were predominantly female (95.6%), with males accounting for 4.4%. Age was mainly 20–30 years (42.5%) and 31–40 years (36.8%), followed by 41–50 years (17.5%) and ≥ 51 years (3.3%). Years of work experience were 0–5 (26.0%), 6–10 (27.1%), 11–15 (25.6%), 16–20 (5.9%), and ≥ 21 years (15.3%). Professional title was junior (51.6%), intermediate (34.6%), and associate senior or above (13.8%); 74.2% held non-established positions. Educational attainment was bachelor’s degree or above (69.8%), junior college (27.4%), and secondary technical school (2.8%).

Instrument reliability and common method bias assessment

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α in SPSS 26.0. The TCM nursing competency scale demonstrated high reliability across the three dimensions: Basic knowledge of TCM nursing (12 items; α = 0.944), TCM nursing techniques (18 items; α = 0.913), and Advanced TCM nursing clinical practice (7 items; α = 0.912). All α values exceeded 0.90, indicating satisfactory internal consistency. Harman’s single-factor test was used to assess common method bias (CMB). Exploratory factor analysis of all items extracted six factors, and the first unrotated factor explained 40.284% of the variance, which was below the conventional 50% threshold.

Latent profile analysis of self-assessed TCM nursing competency

Model selection

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using three continuous indicators of TCM nursing competency—Basic knowledge of TCM nursing, TCM nursing techniques, and Advanced TCM nursing clinical practice—by fitting one- to five-profile models. Model selection was based on Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT), and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), together with profile proportions and substantive interpretability. Overall, the information criteria decreased as the number of profiles increased. The two-profile model showed improved fit over the one-profile model, and the three-profile model further improved fit, with lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, higher entropy (0.846), significant LMRT (p = 0.003) and BLRT (p < 0.001), and interpretable profile proportions (64.3%, 12.3%, and 23.4%). Although the four- and five-profile models showed lower information criteria, the four-profile solution had a non-significant LMRT (p = 0.253) and a small profile (5.7%), whereas the five-profile solution also yielded a small profile (7.2%) and reduced interpretability. Therefore, the three-profile solution was retained as the optimal model (Table 3).

Interpretation and naming of latent profiles

Based on the model-selection results, the three-profile solution was retained for subsequent interpretation. Inspection of the profile plot showed that the three profiles were largely parallel across the three competency dimensions, indicating that the retained solution mainly reflected differences in the overall level of self-assessed TCM nursing competency rather than qualitatively distinct competency configurations. Therefore, the solution was interpreted primarily as a level-based pattern of perceived TCM nursing competency. In other words, the profiles should not be understood as distinct competency types such as “knowledge-dominant,” “technique-dominant,” or “practice-dominant” patterns, but rather as low, moderate, and high levels of self-assessed competency across the same three domains. Accordingly, the three profiles were labeled as the low self-assessed TCM nursing competency profile, moderate self-assessed TCM nursing competency profile, and high self-assessed TCM nursing competency profile.

As shown in Fig. 1, the low self-assessed competency profile had the lowest scores across all three dimensions, the moderate self-assessed competency profile showed intermediate scores, and the high self-assessed competency profile had the highest scores. This low–moderate–high pattern is consistent with the use of broad dimension-level indicators, including basic knowledge of TCM nursing, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM nursing clinical practice. Classification quality was further supported by the average posterior probabilities shown in Table 4, which ranged from 0.878 to 0.950 and indicated acceptable profile separation for the retained three-profile solution. Taken together, these findings suggest that TCM nursing competency among clinical nurses is characterized by meaningful heterogeneity in self-perceived competency levels. However, because the profiles were level-based and derived from cross-sectional self-report data, they should not be interpreted as evidence of longitudinal development, progression over time, or sharply separated competency typologies.

Between-group differences in study variables

A chi-square test (χ² test) was used to compare the distributions of study variables across the three self-assessed TCM nursing competency profiles: low, moderate, and high self-assessed competency profiles (Table 5). Statistically significant differences in distribution were observed for years of work experience (χ² = 33.544, p < 0.001), professional title (χ² = 74.069, p < 0.001), educational attainment (χ² = 50.898, p < 0.001), intention to participate in training (χ² = 109.836, p < 0.001), and perceived importance of training (χ² = 83.340, p < 0.001). Specifically, the proportion of participants classified into the high self-assessed competency profile increased with greater work experience and higher professional title. Higher educational attainment was more common in the moderate and high self-assessed competency profiles. In addition, the high self-assessed competency profile had higher proportions selecting “willing/very willing” and “important/very important” for intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training, respectively.

Factors associated with latent profile membership

With the low self-assessed competency profile as the reference category, a multinomial logistic regression model was fitted including years of work experience, professional title, educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training (Table 6). As described in the Methods section, these variables were entered as ordered predictors because they had clear rank orders. Therefore, the reported ORs should be interpreted as the change in odds associated with each one-category increase in the corresponding ordered predictor, rather than as separate comparisons between each category and a single reference category. The retained three-profile solution provided acceptable subgroup sizes for regression analysis, with 56 participants in the low self-assessed competency profile, 294 in the moderate self-assessed competency profile, and 107 in the high self-assessed competency profile. In addition, the average posterior probabilities for the three-profile solution ranged from 0.878 to 0.950, supporting acceptable classification quality. However, some predictor categories contained relatively small numbers in specific profiles, as shown in Table 5. Therefore, the regression model was specified parsimoniously by treating ordinal predictors as ordered variables, and the findings should be interpreted as trend-based associations across ordered levels rather than as precise category-specific estimates. The regression coefficients indicated different association patterns for membership in the moderate and high self-assessed competency profiles. Educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training were statistically significant in both contrasts.

Discussion

Main findings

Based on 457 nurses from a Grade A tertiary hospital, latent profile analysis identified three level-based profiles of self-assessed Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing competency: low, moderate, and high self-assessed competency profiles. Importantly, the competency examined in this study refers to a TCM-specific domain of nursing competence—covering TCM-related knowledge, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM-oriented clinical practice—rather than the full range of general nursing competence. The decision to retain the three-profile solution was based on both statistical and substantive considerations. Although models with more profiles showed lower information criteria, the additional profiles were small, less stable, or did not provide clear additional clinical meaning. In contrast, the three-profile model showed acceptable entropy, significant likelihood ratio tests, reasonable profile proportions, and acceptable average posterior classification probabilities, while also providing a parsimonious and clinically interpretable low–moderate–high pattern. Therefore, the retained three-profile solution should be viewed as the most statistically acceptable and substantively meaningful representation of heterogeneity in this sample, rather than as the only possible classification structure.

The three profiles showed a largely parallel gradient across the three competency dimensions, suggesting that the identified profiles mainly reflected differences in the overall level of perceived TCM nursing competency rather than qualitatively distinct competency configurations. Accordingly, these profiles should not be interpreted as separate competency types, developmental stages, or evidence of progression over time. Instead, they represent level-based patterns of self-assessed competency derived from broad dimension-level indicators. Most nurses were classified into the moderate self-assessed competency profile, indicating that many nurses had acquired a certain foundation in TCM nursing but may still need further support to strengthen advanced clinical application.

The relatively high proportion of the moderate self-assessed competency profile is consistent with previous latent profile studies in nursing, in which intermediate or level-based groups are commonly identified and profile-based findings are used to inform targeted training and management strategies [27–30]. Descriptive analyses also showed that scores were relatively higher for technical competence and foundational knowledge, whereas scores for advanced clinical practice were lower. This pattern suggests that the main challenge may not be limited to learning individual TCM nursing techniques, but may also involve translating TCM-related knowledge and procedures into complex, context-sensitive nursing practice. In particular, advanced TCM-oriented clinical practice may require clinical reasoning, integration of syndrome-differentiation-informed nursing approaches, and context-sensitive decision-making in complex situations [27]. Therefore, the moderate profile should be viewed as a key target group for profile-informed training, especially for strengthening advanced clinical practice and integrative application capacity. However, because the retained profiles were based on cross-sectional self-report data, training implications should be interpreted as exploratory and context-specific rather than as evidence that nurses move sequentially from the low to the moderate and high profiles.

Associations of educational attainment and years of work experience with profile membership

Using the low self-assessed competency profile as the reference category, multinomial logistic regression showed that educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training were positively associated with membership in both the moderate and high self-assessed competency profiles. Professional title was significantly associated only with membership in the high self-assessed competency profile, whereas years of work experience was not independently associated with profile membership after adjustment. Because educational attainment, professional title, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training were modeled as ordered predictors, these findings should be interpreted as associations across increasing ordinal levels rather than as category-specific comparisons. For example, the OR for educational attainment reflects the change in odds associated with moving upward by one educational category, not the direct contrast between bachelor’s degree or above and technical secondary school. Similarly, the ORs for intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training reflect ordinal trend associations across increasing levels of willingness or perceived importance. These findings suggest that perceived TCM nursing competency may be related more closely to educational preparation, professional role, and learning-related factors than to tenure alone.

Higher educational attainment may facilitate the acquisition and integration of theoretical knowledge, abstract reasoning, and evidence-informed clinical judgment, thereby supporting higher levels of perceived TCM nursing competency [16, 27, 31]. The association between professional title and the high self-assessed competency profile may reflect differences in role complexity and opportunity structures. Nurses with higher professional titles are more likely to undertake responsibilities such as preceptorship, quality improvement, specialty procedures, and complex case management, which may provide richer opportunities for applying TCM nursing knowledge and techniques in practice [32–34]. By contrast, the absence of an independent association between years of work experience and profile membership is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that competency does not increase automatically with tenure, but instead depends on structured learning, meaningful clinical exposure, and supportive learning and training-transfer environments [27, 34, 35]. In this sense, years of service alone may be an insufficient indicator of perceived TCM nursing competency and should be interpreted alongside education, professional title, and practice opportunities.

Training-related attitudes and possible motivational explanations

The significant associations of intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training with profile membership suggest that self-assessed TCM nursing competency was related not only to demographic and professional characteristics, but also to nurses’ attitudes toward TCM nursing training. In contrast, perceived sufficiency of previous TCM nursing training did not differ significantly across profiles. This pattern may indicate that general perceptions of whether previous training was sufficient are not, by themselves, enough to explain differences in self-assessed competency profiles. Rather, nurses’ intention to engage in future training and the value they attach to such training may be more closely linked to their perceived competency level. Nevertheless, because this study did not measure the actual duration, content, quality, intensity, or clinical transfer of previous training, these findings should be interpreted as associations between training-related attitudes and competency profiles rather than as evidence of training effects.

Self-determination theory may help explain why training-related attitudes were associated with profile membership; however, the present study did not formally test this theory. From this perspective, nurses who report stronger intention to participate in training and greater perceived importance of training may be more likely to view TCM nursing learning as meaningful, relevant to their professional role, and beneficial for clinical practice [22–23, 36]. These attitudes may indicate a stronger perceived value of TCM nursing training, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of autonomous motivation or internalized motivation. Because autonomy, competence, relatedness, intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and organizational support were not directly measured, the present findings only suggest that training-related attitudes were associated with latent profile membership.

These findings have practical implications for the design of TCM nursing training. Evidence from continuing professional development and training-transfer research suggests that training outcomes are shaped by multiple factors, including motivation, organizational support, workload, training-transfer climate, and the relevance and accessibility of educational opportunities [24, 34, 35, 37]. Therefore, training programmes should not focus solely on increasing participation rates. Greater attention should also be paid to enhancing the perceived relevance of training, providing timely feedback, incorporating deliberate practice and reflection, and creating supportive clinical learning environments that facilitate the application of TCM nursing knowledge and techniques in practice [33]. Future studies should incorporate direct measures of motivational constructs and more detailed indicators of training exposure to examine how training-related attitudes are associated with competency profiles and training transfer over time.

Implications for profile-informed training and competency assessment

The identification of distinct self-assessed competency profiles has practical implications for nursing management and workforce development [38]. Profile-informed strategies may help institutions tailor training pathways according to nurses’ perceived competency levels and learning needs rather than relying on uniform educational approaches. This is also consistent with recent efforts to develop standardized and systematic refresher training systems for TCM nursing staff, in which training objectives, curriculum content, core competencies, training methods, and training effectiveness evaluation are integrated into a structured framework [39]. For nurses in the low self-assessed competency profile, training may prioritize foundational TCM knowledge and core technical skills, supported by scenario-based instruction and preceptorship to reinforce standardized practice. For nurses in the moderate self-assessed competency profile, who constituted the largest subgroup, training may focus on strengthening advanced clinical practice, including the integration of TCM techniques into complex care situations, syndrome-differentiation-informed decision-making, and risk management. Deliberate practice, competency checklists, structured feedback, and opportunities for supervised application may be particularly valuable for this group [27, 32, 33]. For nurses in the high self-assessed competency profile, continued development may emphasize specialization, clinical teaching, evidence translation, quality improvement, and leadership roles, thereby enabling these nurses to serve as mentors and facilitators within the TCM nursing workforce [34, 38].

In addition, competency-oriented assessment systems should extend beyond self-report measures in future research and practice. Self-assessed competency is valuable for understanding nurses’ perceived familiarity, confidence, and training needs, but it can be complemented by more objective or multi-source evidence. Combining scale-based assessment with structured clinical competency assessments, observed performance, skill checklists, supervisor or peer evaluation, and process or outcome indicators may provide a more comprehensive understanding of TCM nursing competency [16, 38]. At the policy and institutional levels, ongoing efforts to strengthen TCM nursing service capacity in China could be supported by evidence-based standards, consensus statements, and clinical guidance that promote more consistent training and clinical implementation [40, 41]. Further development of TCM nursing specialty posts and nurse-led service models may also provide role platforms for nurses with higher perceived competency, while future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to examine whether profile-informed training can improve both perceived competency and clinical application outcomes [42].

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of latent profile analysis (LPA) to characterize unobserved heterogeneity in nurses’ self-assessed TCM nursing competency. Compared with traditional mean-based comparisons, LPA more directly identifies subgroups with distinct perceived competency patterns, thereby providing a more informative basis for profile-informed training and training-resource allocation. Incorporating training-related attitude variables into profile comparisons and multinomial logistic regression, including intention to participate in training and perceived importance of training, allowed us to examine factors associated with profile membership in a way that is meaningful for hospital training management. A standardized questionnaire with strict data quality control procedures was utilized in the study, and internal consistency was satisfactory. Common method variance (CMV) was also assessed and did not indicate a serious problem. Managerial implications of the findings are also noteworthy, because profile-specific characteristics and perceived competency gaps can be translated into practical training pathways and workforce development strategies.

There are a number of limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot establish temporal relationships between training-related attitudes and self-assessed TCM nursing competency profiles. Therefore, the identified profiles should not be interpreted as developmental stages or evidence of progression over time. Second, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary Grade A hospital in which TCM nursing services were routinely provided, and participants were clinical nurses with exposure to TCM nursing practice rather than a nationally representative sample of all clinical nurses or nurses who had completed formal TCM nursing specialist training. Accordingly, the findings may be most applicable to similar tertiary-hospital settings with established TCM nursing service provision. In addition, information on whether participants had obtained a certificate of completion for TCM nursing specialist training, the exact duration and intensity of previous TCM nursing training, the frequency of performing specific TCM nursing techniques, and the level of TCM nursing specialization was not collected. Future studies should include these exposure- and training-related variables to better characterize participants’ professional profiles and clarify their relationships with TCM nursing competency profiles. Third, training-related attitudes and TCM nursing competency were measured using self-report instruments. This approach is commonly used in nursing competency, training needs, and latent profile research, particularly when the aim is to understand nurses’ perceived competency, learning needs, and professional development characteristics at the group level. In the present study, self-assessed TCM nursing competency was appropriate for identifying perceived competency patterns and generating profile-informed implications for training management. Nevertheless, self-assessment mainly reflects nurses’ perceived familiarity, confidence, and readiness related to TCM nursing practice, and may not fully capture all aspects of observed clinical performance. Nurses’ ratings may also be influenced by individual confidence, departmental expectations, and opportunities to apply TCM nursing interventions in practice. Therefore, the identified profiles should be interpreted as meaningful patterns of perceived TCM nursing competency, while future studies may further examine how these profiles correspond to observed clinical performance and clinical application outcomes. Although common method variance was assessed, residual bias cannot be fully excluded. Harman’s single-factor test provided preliminary support that common method variance was not a serious concern, but it cannot fully address all potential limitations inherent in self-reported competency assessment. In addition, both measurement instruments were developed and applied within the Chinese TCM nursing context, and their applicability to other healthcare systems or cultural settings requires further validation. Fourth, the latent profile analysis was conducted using three dimension-level indicators: basic knowledge of TCM nursing, TCM nursing techniques, and advanced TCM nursing clinical practice. Although these indicators were consistent with the validated structure of the questionnaire and supported a parsimonious and interpretable model, they may not fully capture more detailed within-domain patterns, technique-specific competencies, or item-level differences. Future studies could use item-level indicators, domain-specific modeling, or mixed-method approaches to explore more nuanced structures of TCM nursing competency. Lastly, the regression models mainly included demographic and training-related attitude variables and did not fully account for other potentially important determinants, such as organizational support, departmental practice context, workload, actual training exposure, and opportunities for clinical application. Although the retained latent profiles had acceptable subgroup sizes and classification quality, several predictor categories contained relatively small numbers in specific profiles. To reduce potential instability caused by sparse cells, ordinal predictors were modeled as ordered variables rather than as multiple category-specific dummy variables. This approach improved model parsimony and supported interpretation of the findings as ordinal trend associations. However, it also assumes approximately monotonic associations between predictor levels and profile membership and may not fully capture non-linear or category-specific effects. Therefore, the regression estimates should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for predictors with uneven category distributions, and should be verified in larger multicenter samples with more balanced subgroup structures. Future research could build on the present findings by incorporating multi-source or performance-based assessments, such as structured clinical competency assessment, skill checklists, supervisor or peer ratings, case-based evaluation, or indicators related to the implementation of TCM nursing interventions. These approaches would complement self-report measures and further strengthen the evidence base for profile-informed TCM nursing training strategies.

Future directions

Future research should include multicenter studies across different regions and institutions in China, particularly hospitals with varying levels of TCM nursing service provision, to further examine the stability, distribution, and generalizability of the identified self-assessed competency profiles. Longitudinal or cohort designs are also needed to examine whether profile membership changes over time and to clarify how educational attainment, professional role, clinical exposure, and training-related attitudes are associated with changes in self-assessed and observed TCM nursing competency.

In terms of measurement, future studies should incorporate objective or multi-source indicators of competency in addition to self-report, and should further refine exposure-related variables such as training participation, training quality, duration and intensity of training, and opportunities for clinical application. These improvements may strengthen the interpretation of profile characteristics and reduce the limitations associated with single-method assessment.

From an intervention perspective, profile-informed and tiered training programmes should be designed and piloted, particularly those targeting the relative weakness in advanced clinical practice. Such programmes may emphasize scenario-based learning, structured feedback, deliberate practice, and support for training transfer, while evaluating post-training changes in perceived competency, observed performance, sustainability of improvement, and clinical application outcomes. Future work could also examine how training value endorsement, learning engagement, and training transfer are associated over time, thereby providing stronger evidence to inform hospital strategies for tiered workforce development and training-resource allocation.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study examined self-assessed Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing competency among clinical nurses in a tertiary Grade A hospital in China where TCM nursing services were routinely provided. Overall, nurses reported a moderate level of self-assessed TCM nursing competency. Scores were relatively higher for TCM nursing techniques but lower for advanced clinical practice, suggesting that the main area for improvement may lie in the integration of TCM nursing knowledge and techniques into more complex clinical care.

Latent profile analysis identified three level-based profiles of self-assessed TCM nursing competency, namely low, moderate, and high self-assessed competency profiles, with the moderate profile representing the largest subgroup. This finding suggests that many nurses had established a basic foundation in TCM nursing but may benefit from further support in advanced clinical application. Profile membership differed significantly by years of service, professional title, educational attainment, intention to participate in training, and perceived importance of training. In the multinomial logistic regression, educational attainment and training-related attitudes were consistently associated with membership in higher self-assessed competency profiles, whereas years of service was not independently associated after adjustment. These findings indicate that perceived TCM nursing competency may be more closely related to educational preparation, professional role, and learning-related attitudes than to accumulated tenure alone.

The findings may inform profile-informed and tiered workforce development strategies. For nurses in the low self-assessed competency profile, training may focus on foundational knowledge and core TCM nursing techniques. For those in the moderate self-assessed competency profile, training may emphasize advanced clinical practice, scenario-based learning, and the transfer of training into routine care. For nurses in the high self-assessed competency profile, continued development may focus on specialization, preceptorship, clinical teaching, and leadership roles. Training strategies may also consider nurses’ training-related attitudes to enhance learning engagement and support the translation of training into clinical practice. However, given the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported competency, the identified profiles should be interpreted as patterns of perceived competency rather than developmental stages or evidence of progression over time. Because the profiles were largely parallel across broad competency dimensions, they should also be understood as overall level-based differences rather than qualitatively distinct competency configurations. Future longitudinal, multicenter, and intervention studies incorporating objective or multi-source competency assessments are needed to verify the stability of these profiles, clarify causal relationships, and evaluate the effectiveness of profile-informed TCM nursing training strategies.
