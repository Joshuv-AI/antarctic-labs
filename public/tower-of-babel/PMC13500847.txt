Non-invasive characterization of the relationship between skin microrelief and dermal–epidermal junction topography using line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT)
Authors: Ken Uda, Kazuchika Iwami, Naoko Yoshida, Toshiyuki Homma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-invasive technologies for observing internal skin structures without damaging the surface have advanced in recent years. However, an integrated understanding of internal structures together with the morphology of surface “microrelief”—the surface pattern consisting of furrows and ridges (i.e., sulci cutis and cristae cutis)—remains limited. We elucidated the relationship between internal skin structure and microrelief by combining line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT) with a proprietary AI system. We applied a semantic segmentation-based image AI system to LC-OCT data from 11 adults. The system extracted the coordinates of sulci cutis, stratum corneum thickness, viable epidermal thickness, and dermal–epidermal junction (DEJ) topography. The analysis revealed that the skin surface and DEJ in sulci cutis were nearly parallel. The DEJ undulation index in cristae cutis correlated with epidermal thickness, stratum corneum multilayering rate, and ceramide subclass parameters. We verified the relationship between the skin surface and the DEJ structure by incorporating positional information of the microrelief. LC-OCT combined with AI-based analysis provides a non-invasive approach to link age-related changes in DEJ structure with alterations in microrelief and related biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56985-6.Keywords: Microrelief, Skin aging, Dermal–epidermal junction (DEJ), Artificial intelligence, Line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT)Subject terms: Biological techniques, Biomarkers, Medical research, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Measuring skin biomarkers in vivo provides valuable insights into physiological changes related to both intrinsic (such as aging and genetic predisposition) and extrinsic factors (such as photoaging and environmental influences). Several techniques based on physical measurements of the skin and the resulting data have been developed to deepen our understanding of skin physiology. Dermatologists clinically assess skin conditions, including age-related changes and macroscopic signs, through visual inspection and palpation of target areas. Instrumental evaluation using established tools, such as stratum corneum hydration meters, transepidermal water loss meters, and skin viscoelasticity meters, is widely used1. Furthermore, high-resolution photography and dermoscopy enable visualization and quantification of several visible signs on the skin. These include skin cancers such as melanoma, atopic dermatitis, dry skin, wrinkle formation, changes in pigmentation, such as lentigines and aging spots, and actinic keratosis2–5. Models predicting the differences in aging rates between internal and external body surfaces, extending beyond surface observations, have been constructed recently. Moreover, information from human facial images, such as perceived age, is being used as a biomarker to infer systemic physiological functions6.

Intradermal imaging techniques capable of visualizing deeper structures within the skin have been developed over the past few decades. These are characterized by their performance in terms of spatial resolution, axial resolution, and depth of analysis, in addition to the choice of illumination source. They are based on different principles and modalities. For example, principles of reflective confocal microscopy (RCM), multiphoton microscopy (MPM), and in vivo collagen second harmonic generation microscopy (SHG) have been used to non-invasively examine and quantify several structural changes associated with skin diseases and aging across diverse populations7–9. Furthermore, measurement techniques suited to size and resolution based on the depth of the target object using OCT and high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) technologies have been established10–12.

Recently, line-field confocal OCT (LC-OCT) has been developed. LC-OCT can analyze depths up to 500 μm, including the superficial dermis of human skin, while achieving an isotropic spatial resolution of approximately 1 µm13. Its unique capability to image both vertical cross-sections (B-scans) and frontal cross-sections (horizontal or C-scans) enables rapid and comprehensive three-dimensional (3D) examination of living skin. It can observe tissue microstructures down to the micrometer level and provide detailed observations at the single-cell level14,15.

The skin is the largest organ in the human body and plays diverse roles in protecting the organism from harmful external factors while maintaining internal homeostasis. Skin homeostasis is maintained by physical and chemical barrier functions, immune defense mechanisms, water retention, and temperature regulation—achieved through the coordinated action of the multilayered structure comprising the epidermis, basement membrane, dermis, and subcutaneous tissue16–18. The basement membrane maintains skin homeostasis. Located at the dermal–epidermal junction (DEJ), it is primarily composed of type IV collagen, laminin, and glycoproteins19. Type IV collagen and laminin interact with integrin receptors on the epidermal keratinocyte surface, contributing to skin homeostasis by activating the extracellular signaling pathways. Furthermore, degeneration or damage to the basement membrane or defects in its constituent genes underlie intractable skin diseases and tumor progression20,21. Thus, the basement membrane is crucial for controlling the microenvironment governing epidermal keratinocyte adhesion, differentiation, and proliferation. Furthermore, it is crucial for maintaining the structural stability of the skin and the repair following injury. When observed in a vertical section, the structure of the basement membrane, known as dermal papillae, extends toward the epidermis at the DEJ zone or elongates toward the dermis as a wavy rete ridge. This structure enhances the skin’s mechanical strength and increases the contact area with capillaries. Several studies have investigated the basement membrane from the perspective of skin aging. It degrades and breaks down, and that rete ridges flatten with age22,23. These changes decrease skin homeostasis and function, leading to impaired epidermal turnover and reduced intercellular interactions, resulting in several effects associated with ultraviolet (UV) exposure and aging, such as the development of spots and wrinkles, loss of firmness, and dry skin24.

Collagen and elastin, the primary constituent molecules of the ECM in the dermis, which forms the foundation of the epidermis, have been extensively studied.18 Aging, UV exposure, and lifestyle factors can structurally degenerate and functionally impair the dermal matrix, thereby affecting skin homeostasis. Furthermore, dermal capillaries near the DEJ undergo atrophy with aging, and disruption of the papillary dermal niche structure is correlated with reduced epidermal stem cell maintenance function25–28. Thus, the layered structure of the skin, from the epidermis to the dermis, is crucial for maintaining homeostasis in each layer. Skin tissue is an extremely complex system, with studies conducted not only on individual components but also on the interactions within and between tissues. These studies are highly significant, leading to the development of therapeutic strategies targeting specific components of skin diseases, applications in regenerative medicine, and the development of anti-aging products and cosmetics for healthy skin through the identification of aging mechanisms.

This report focuses on the relationship between the basement membrane and skin surface, which has previously been discussed separately. The new focus is on “microrelief.” Microrelief consists of an outermost surface pattern composed of furrows and ridges. Healthy skin ridges form a regular triangular pattern. Skin furrows can become disrupted or lose their defined lines due to dryness-induced roughness or aging29. This study capitalizes on LC-OCT’s ability to simultaneously observe the microrelief and the internal structure immediately beneath it. Furthermore, recent studies have elucidated the relationship between the quantity and types of ceramides, constituting the majority of intercellular lipids, and conditions such as atopic dermatitis, dryness, and sensitive skin. The stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the skin, primarily consists of denucleated corneocytes and intercellular lipids (a brick-and-mortar structure)30–32. Although research investigating surface ceramide types, a parameter related to skin barrier function, is underway, the relationship between the uneven structure of the basement membrane and the surface condition of the skin remains unknown.

We investigated the forearm skin in healthy Japanese individuals using LC-OCT 3D imaging and performed correlation analyses between different internal skin structures and instrumental skin measurement parameters (some basic parameters were also measured on the face). The acquired LC-OCT 3D images were used to calculate the thickness of the stratum corneum and viable epidermis, as well as the degree of irregularity of the dermal surface (evaluating the undulating structure of the DEJ, termed the “DEJ undulation Index”). Although the LC-OCT image data were enormous, these were analyzed using AI-assisted support. Additionally, skin measurements included an assessment of stratum corneum hydration, stratum corneum area, multilayering rate, and compositional analysis of stratum corneum ceramide species, enabling detailed examination and analysis. LC-OCT is a non-invasive technique that uses light interference to acquire cross-sectional images of the skin. Compared to conventional microscopy, it enables high-resolution imaging deep into the skin and is well-suited for analyzing 3D skin structures. Furthermore, real-time observation is possible, allowing the acquisition of large amounts of data while reducing the burden on the subjects. We quantitatively analyzed the skin’s internal structure, including DEJ topography, using LC-OCT and elucidated the relationship between these structural parameters and surface condition of the skin (hydration, stratum corneum cells, and stratum corneum ceramide species composition).

Results

Spatial correspondence between sulci cutis and DEJ topography

Using the AI-based segmentation system described in the Materials and Methods section, we analyzed LC-OCT datasets to investigate the spatial relationship between surface microrelief and DEJ topography and to examine their correlations with the acquired parameters. First, we summarized the LC-OCT images and skin structures that could be classified using a single flowchart (Fig. 1). Because the analyzed skin furrows fell within the measurement range of the LC-OCT system (approximately 1,200 μm × 500 μm in the x–y plane), simultaneous observation of skin furrows and the underlying DEJ structure was feasible.

Identification of parameters correlated with the DEJ undulation index

We defined the topography index obtained using LC-OCT as the DEJ UI. Although this topography index is sometimes referred to as an “interdigitation index33,” we adopted the more commonly used term “undulation.” The specific calculation formula is presented in Fig. 3. First, we provided dot plots of the DEJ UI and other parameters across the entire measurement area (Fig. 4). We examined the relationships between the DEJ UI and age, microrelief S, stratum corneum area, stratum corneum multilayering rate, epidermal thickness, stratum corneum hydration, and different ceramide species in the stratum corneum. Weak correlations were observed with stratum corneum area, epidermal thickness, and ceramide EOP (r = − 0.37, 0.48, and − 0.34, respectively), whereas microrelief line density (Fig. 4b) showed the strongest negative correlation (r = − 0.50). This finding is counterintuitive to the consensus that microrelief density and stratum corneum condition deteriorate with age. Therefore, we focused on the z-axis coordinates of the sulci cutis and cristae cutis.

The index value was approximately 1 for sulci cutis, whereas it increased significantly for cristae cutis while calculating the index values separately for regions corresponding to the z-axis (vertical) positions of the skin surface shape (Fig. 5a). Thus, although the stratum corneum and DEJ are parallel structures within sulci cutis, they exhibit a more pronounced undulating shape within cristae cutis (Fig. 5b). Therefore, we hypothesized that the index corresponding to cristae cutis (abundant dermal papillae) significantly reflects internal structure changes.

Next, following the above approach, we separately extracted only the sulci cutis and cristae cutis and performed the same analyses (Figs. 6 and 7). For the sulci cutis, the skin microrelief line density remained unchanged and showed a negative correlation with the DEJ UI (r = − 0.50) (Fig. 6b), with several other parameters exhibiting similar trends. Surprisingly, epidermal thickness alone demonstrated no correlation (r = − 0.08) (Fig. 6e). In contrast, the cristae cutis region was positively correlated between the DEJ UI and microrelief line density (r = 0.32), consistent with the conventional consensus (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, epidermal thickness displayed a strong positive correlation with the DEJ UI (r = 0.80) (Fig. 7e). Thus, the high DEJ topography immediately beneath the dermal papillae is an important biomarker indicating the characteristics of young, thick (i.e., homeostatic), and healthy skin.

Analysis results of parameters correlated with the DEJ UI (cristae cutis region). DEJ UI, dermal–epidermal junction undulation index.

Another interesting finding was the discovery of correlations between DEJ UI in the cristae cutis and stratum corneum multilayering rate (r = − 0.54), ceramide NDS (r = − 0.44), and ceramide EOP (r = 0.48) (Fig. 7d,g,h). We adopted the DEJ UI as a biomarker reflecting skin homeostasis and demonstrated consistent correlations with all measured parameters that aligned with the changes typically observed in skin aging.

The index value for the sulci cutis region was close to 1 (Fig. 5), indicating that the stratum corneum was parallel to the DEJ in these areas. This indirectly suggests, through a comparison of whole-area and region-specific analyses, that this value is influenced and essentially canceled out in global assessments, indicating that the DEJ structure at positions parallel to the sulci cutis has limited significance as a biomarker.

This leads to a new hypothesis: Independent of the sulci cutis, where the DEJ runs essentially parallel to the skin surface, the cristae cutis region, with its abundant dermal papillae and vascular network with an undulating DEJ structure, creates a specialized niche for the maintenance of skin homeostasis. Details of parameters obtained in addition to other than DEJ UI correlation analyses are summarized in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Discussion

Figure 8 shows a schematic summarizing the findings. The DEJ structure flattens with age. A study demonstrated that microrelief morphology, composed of sulcus cutis and crista cutis, changes with age. However, no previous studies have thoroughly examined the relationship between these two aspects. Our findings revealed that sulci cutis, which is a visible indicator of skin conditions, exhibits structural similarities with DEJ. Furthermore, analyses separating sulci cutis and cristae cutis indicated that the DEJ structure directly beneath the cristae cutis was a key determinant of skin homeostasis. Altogether, these results point to a potential new aging mechanism: the surface microrelief simultaneously becomes attenuated and eventually disappears as the DEJ topography flattens with age.

Summary of the study.

RCM images more clearly reflect DEJ and capture the age-related reduction in the DEJ structure28. Furthermore, although clinical trials using RCM to assess morphological changes associated with skin aging have revealed epidermal keratinocytes and dermal collagen29, details regarding the DEJ remain unclear. LC-OCT, which has been developed recently, can diagnose skin lesions, such as carcinomas and nevi13,34, malignant melanomas35, and actinic keratoses36. A combination of LC-OCT and artificial intelligence (AI) can investigate complex structures in healthy skin37–39. Thus, although its utility has been reported in limited contexts such as specific ethnicities and age ranges, LC-OCT research is still in its infancy. Regarding specific research findings on aging, one study used deep learning to elucidate the correlation between structural features of the subepidermal layer and aging40. Another study reported facial skin aging characteristics by combining LC-OCT with an AI-based image analysis protocol in 100 healthy Caucasian female volunteers41. Recently, attempts have been made to develop systems capable of distinguishing stem cell properties by measuring epidermal stem cell morphology using LC-OCT42. Thus, LC-OCT can precisely capture age-related structural changes in the skin. However, despite the high expectations for knowledge advancement through high-performance imaging technologies, current research remains largely limited to evaluating the characteristics of healthy skin and the effects of aging, with relatively few reports available to date. Furthermore, LC-OCT is a powerful tool for addressing several structural and functional differences between skin types (e.g., differences between Caucasians and Asians) and is attracting considerable interest in both the dermatology and cosmetics industries.

We focused on sulci cutis observed in the outermost layer as a visible indicator of skin conditions. Identifying sulci cutis using histological examination of the excised skin (conventional method) is difficult. In this context, the combination of LC-OCT and proprietary AI-based image analysis proved to be highly useful and represents a major contribution of this study. The excised biopsy specimens and their histological sections were no longer subjected to in vivo mechanical tension. This possibly differs substantially from the mechanical and physical conditions of the intact living skin. LC-OCT imaging enables direct confirmation, in vivo, of the correspondence between the internal DEJ topography and surface structure, and can identify multiple parameters contributing to maintaining this structural relationship. Our region-specific analyses of sulci cutis and cristae cutis revealed numerous skin parameters correlating with DEJ topography in the cristae cutis region. This is particularly interesting in the context of local structural organization and differences in mechanical tension within the skin.

Furthermore, it is crucial to focus on non-lesional skin areas. Because the skin is an epithelial tissue, its physiological mechanisms must be considered separately from those driven by somatic genetic mutations, such as those observed in skin cancer. Although we could not eliminate the influence of UV exposure (photoaging), the DEJ structure was analyzed uniformly on the inner forearm, an area with minimal sun exposure, and is therefore considered to strongly reflect intrinsic aging effects. The human DEJ structure varies markedly depending on the body site. Compared to the forearm, we demonstrated that the facial region exhibited an almost flat DEJ (data not shown). Conversely, DEJ undulation decreased with advancing age in the forearm region, consistent with previous reports indicating that the DEJ flattens as its constituent components of the basement membrane decrease or degradation43,44. We analyzed the forearm region, where sulci cutis are clearly observed, to uncover a novel finding of structural similarity between sulci cutis and the underlying DEJ topography. This may capture an aspect of global skin aging that is more strongly influenced by intrinsic aging than photoaging. In contrast, flattening of the DEJ in the facial region appears to occur early in life, potentially obscuring interdependence with sulci cutis. Considering that previously published findings are largely limited to analyses of non-sun-exposed arm and buttock regions, this is an intriguing observation. DEJ morphology and microrelief are known to vary by anatomical site. Our preliminary observation that the facial region exhibited an almost flat DEJ contrasts with the clearly defined microrelief and undulating DEJ observed on the forearm. These regional differences limit the direct generalizability of our findings and highlight the need for future multi-site studies.

The DEJ undulation decreases and flattens with aging22,23,43. Furthermore, the mechanisms by which aging and UV exposure adversely affect the basement membrane components have been widely studied24. However, this study may represent the first report to confirm a direct correlation between DEJ undulation and epidermal thickness using non-invasive measurements in human skin. Additionally, the stratum corneum multilayering rate, a known indicator of immature turnover, negatively correlated with DEJ undulation, as expected. Thus, the DEJ UI measured using LC-OCT could estimate skin condition changes associated with aging. Among the parameters examined, the DEJ undulation index (DEJ UI) in the cristae cutis region and viable epidermal thickness appear to be the most promising candidates for clinical application. DEJ UI can be calculated automatically from LC-OCT 3D datasets using our AI system, supporting its high reproducibility and practical feasibility. The strong positive correlation between DEJ UI and epidermal thickness (r = 0.80) in the cristae cutis region further supports its potential clinical relevance as a non-invasive structural biomarker.

Studies examining the relationship between the internal and surface structures of the skin are scarce. Regarding fingerprints, the internal structures of the basement membrane and dermis exhibit ridged surfaces with elevations and depressions, with sweat glands forming on the ridges and grooves in the depressions.45 The surface ridges and grooves of fingerprints can correspond to the primary and secondary ridges within the DEJ structure. These structures are formed during fetal development in parallel with sweat gland induction, with Wnt signaling as the initiating factor46. According to a recent report, in human skin with reduced hair density, rete ridges form via mechanisms independent of hair follicle and sweat gland development and distinct from those underlying fingerprint ridge formation47. Thus, sulci cutis may also develop through a complex mechanism.

Furthermore, because sulci cutis become disordered with aging, lose their random orientation, and tend to run predominantly in a single direction29, incorporating directionality into future analyses could reveal additional correlations with internal structures over time from an aging perspective, potentially elucidating more detailed mechanisms. However, once formed, the sulci cutis are susceptible to epigenetic regulation, mechanical stress associated with skin stretching and contraction due to body shape and movement, and aging-caused tissue degradation, increasing their susceptibility to disappear more quickly than fingerprints. In contrast, as indicated by basic research in mice, we hypothesized that regions subjected to repeated facial expressions would become fixed, thereby contributing to wrinkle formation via a similar mechanism48,49. Although sulci cutis are surface structures of healthy skin and have no direct link to disease, they represent a crucial focal point for inferring the mechanisms of skin aging.

However, further investigation is necessary to elucidate the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying skin aging. Although we identified a coordinated change between DEJ flattening and loss of sulci cutis, the causal sequence of these events remains unclear. Previous in vitro studies using inserts simulating the DEJ structure in reconstituted skin models or altering the basal surface shape have reported effects such as loss of stemness and impaired epidermal differentiation, leading to skin aging50,51. However, no relationship with the surface structure was observed. Vitamin C (VC) increases dermal papillary density52. However, because VC has rarely been reported to improve sulci cutis, topical agents may have limited potential to simultaneously influence DEJ topography and surface texture. Constructing skin models that recapitulate the primary and secondary ridges, as characterized in this study, will advance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms. The functional role of the sulci cutis region, whose significance is questioned here, in maintaining skin homeostasis cannot be excluded. This was a pilot study with a limited sample size (n = 11), and the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Larger, adequately powered cohorts are required to confirm these correlations and to establish their generalizability. Our cohort (age range, 23–52 years) did not include elderly individuals (> 70 years), in whom the most pronounced DEJ flattening has been reported. Future studies should include older participants to capture the full spectrum of age-related structural changes.

Although this study was conducted in a Japanese population to avoid biases stemming from racial differences, this approach also limits its generalizability. Several reports have indicated racial differences in the visible signs of aging. Caucasians tend to develop fine lines and deep wrinkles earlier and more prominently than Asians53. In contrast, Asian skin is generally thicker than Caucasian skin, making it less prone to sagging and fine wrinkles. However, it is more susceptible to UV-induced pigmentation disorders (such as melasma and hyperpigmentation)54,55. Thus, the effects of race on skin aging are complex and involve interactions between genetic predispositions and environmental factors56. To understand the underlying physiological mechanisms, including histological features, in vivo clinical studies in other racial groups should be conducted using this experimental design. Reports analyzing the differences between Caucasians and Asians using 3D LC-OCT have already emerged57. The range of measurable parameters is also expandable, and LC-OCT images can assess dermal collagen density, size of epidermal spinous cells, and melanin content. We plan to extend these analyses in future studies. This study analyzed only the volar forearm, an area with minimal sun exposure. From an additional perspective, an intra-individual comparison with the dorsal forearm (sun-exposed site) would enable differentiation of intrinsic aging from photoaging effects and should be incorporated in future studies.

LC-OCT, recently introduced and based on a combination of the principles of RCM and OCT, has a shallower imaging depth than conventional OCT but achieves greater imaging depth than RCM13. Furthermore, its image resolution surpasses that of conventional OCT and approaches that of RCM. LC-OCT generates real-time vertical and horizontal cross-sections to generate 3D images within seconds. This study focused on the microrelief and DEJ of healthy skin and reported their structural similarities and correlations with different biophysical and biochemical parameters. We believe that this study, particularly the separate analysis of the previously underexplored spatial relationship between sulci cutis and cristae cutis, will provide clues for elucidating new mechanisms of skin homeostasis related to the often-overlooked DEJ topography.

However, it is important to consider the trade-offs between LC-OCT and other high-resolution non-invasive imaging techniques. Multiphoton microscopy (MPM) offers intrinsic biological specificity through second harmonic generation (SHG) for collagen and fluorescence for melanin, enabling segmentation based on molecular signatures rather than morphological features. In contrast, our LC-OCT-based approach relies on manual labeling and AI-driven pattern recognition rather than molecule-specific signals, which may limit the biological specificity of segmentation. However, LC-OCT offers several practical advantages: it is easier to implement in clinical and industrial settings, provides a larger field of view (approximately 1,200 μm × 500 μm), generates real-time vertical and horizontal cross-sections, and is available as a handheld probe that can access virtually any body site. This balance between molecular specificity (MPM) and clinical practicality (LC-OCT) positions LC-OCT as a complementary modality, particularly well suited for large-scale screening and longitudinal studies of skin aging.As a non-invasive device, LC-OCT has been miniaturized into an ergonomically designed handheld probe, allowing easy access to virtually any skin area on the human body. It is an imaging modality with an excellent resolution and acquisition speed. Numerous studies have reported LC-OCT-based observations of different skin lesions, including skin cancer. Furthermore, changes in the internal structure induced by topical interventions can be captured by conducting appropriately designed, randomized, double-blind, and controlled clinical trials to assess their effects on improvement58. Recent retrospective studies have demonstrated the clinical accuracy and usefulness of AI-assisted LC-OCT in the diagnosis of skin diseases. LC-OCT is emerging as a new modality under the concept of “digital biopsy,” which is non-invasive and does not require tissue excision59. With ongoing advances in AI algorithms well-suited for image analysis, skin imaging technologies are poised for rapid development. Such collaborative efforts between imaging and AI research should form the foundation for LC-OCT’s transition from a specialized research tool to a routine clinical modality. Future studies comparing healthy skin, tumor-adjacent skin, and tumor tissue using LC-OCT could help disentangle aging-related structural changes from tumor-associated alterations at the DEJ. Such comparisons could also test whether DEJ UI serves as a differential diagnostic or prognostic marker in dermatological oncology.

Conclusion

We developed a non-invasive skin analysis system that combines LC-OCT with AI-based image processing to elucidate the influence of internal skin structures on surface microrelief. The system classifies LC-OCT images into stratum corneum, epidermal live cell layer, dermis, and sulci cutis, enabling 3D visualization.

Using this system, we examined DEJ topography, including the spatial relationships of surface sulci cutis, and performed quantitative correlation analyses with various acquired parameters. The results revealed that sulci cutis are essentially structures that run parallel to, and closely resemble, the DEJ, whereas multiple correlated parameters were confined to the cristae cutis region. To our knowledge, this is the first study to spatially resolve the DEJ–microrelief relationship by separately analyzing sulci cutis and cristae cutis regions in vivo, revealing that global (whole-area) DEJ undulation indices may obscure biologically meaningful structural variation localized to the cristae cutis region. These findings suggest that age-related flattening of the DEJ, including the disappearance of dermal papillary vessels, progresses in coordination with changes in surface sulci cutis. In addition, in the cristae cutis region, this flattening is associated with reduced epidermal thickness, which in turn contributes to altered skin homeostasis and barrier function.

Our results indicate a novel structural relationship between DEJ topography and surface microrelief, which is involved in maintaining skin homeostasis and may provide useful insights into suppressing skin aging and improving skin barrier function. Furthermore, this study contributes to the establishment of new evaluation metrics for future skin wound treatments and skincare product development.

Materials and methods

Study participants

LC-OCT measurements and different skin measurement tests were performed on 11 Japanese subjects (six males and five females; age range, 23–52 years). The study was conducted with the approval of the Fujifilm Life Science Ethics Review Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were required to be healthy adults aged 18 years or older. The following exclusion criteria were applied based on a pre-study questionnaire and a self-administered patch test: (i) currently receiving treatment or medication for atopic dermatitis; (ii) currently receiving treatment or medication for food or drug allergies; and (iii) any visible skin abnormalities, lesions, or scars on the measurement sites. Eligibility was determined by the principal investigator. Prior to measurement, the sites were cleansed and acclimatized for a minimum of 20 min under controlled conditions.

Study design and measurement flow

On the first day, the measurement site was cleaned, thoroughly dried, and examined by LC-OCT. On the following day, the same skin area, identified using a skin marker, was imaged using a dermatoscopic camera to assess the skin appearance. In addition, the stratum corneum hydration was measured. Subsequently, tape stripping was performed to analyze stratum corneum morphology and ceramide composition.

LC-OCT data acquisition

The LC-OCT measurements were performed using a commercially available system (DeepLive system; DAMAE Medical, France). Images were acquired from the inner forearms of the volunteers. Before the measurement, dedicated mineral oil (liquid paraffin) was used. A fixture capable of stabilizing the probe head was used to ensure that both the probe and the skin remained stationary during measurement. Measurements were performed in the high-resolution mode using three-dimensional vertical confocal imaging.

AI analysis and acquired parameters

An image recognition AI model was constructed by training it with labeled data derived from the LC-OCT images, as described in the following subsections. After the segmentation of the skin layers, the AI reconstructed the data in 3D, enabling the calculation of different parameters, including the spatial relationships of the microrelief. Thus, we analyzed the stratum corneum thickness, epidermal thickness, and DEJ undulation index (DEJ UI) derived from LC-OCT data using AI. Macro-CCD images of the observation area were acquired simultaneously using the same device. Heatmap visualization and three-dimensional image reconstruction were performed using MATLAB (version R2022a, MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA; https://www.mathworks.com/). with the Image Processing Toolbox and the Computer Vision Toolbox.

Image AI system construction

We extracted the skin, stratum corneum, epidermal cell layer, and dermis from cross-sectional skin images acquired using LC-OCT. The raw data measured and acquired using LC-OCT consisted of two-dimensional (2D) images stored in a combined file format. Therefore, analyzing each parameter required laborious labeling and classification of layers across numerous images. Thus, we developed an image AI system by training machine learning models on manually labeled data to calculate parameters, such as the thickness and topography of each skin layer, and generate 3D visualizations of images. The raw data comprised two types of 2D images: cross-sectional x–z axis images (B-scans) acquired from the side of the skin and x–y axis images (C-scans) acquired perpendicular to the image plane. The technique for extracting regions from two-dimensional images is called segmentation, and multiple methods are available. We developed a method to accurately segment the skin furrows (sulci cutis), stratum corneum, viable epidermis, and dermis. We employed U-Net (U-Net: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation)60, a convolutional neural network developed for biomedical image labeling, which is known for its high pixel-level classification accuracy.

Calculation of interlayer boundary prediction accuracy

Although IoU can express the overlap (matching) rate between the ground-truth image and inference result, we additionally used the interlayer boundary prediction accuracy, expressed as the root mean squared error (RMSE), to comprehensively evaluate the inferred boundary shape, because we focused on the boundary shape between the epidermal cell and dermal layers. RMSE was calculated by dividing the sum of the squared differences between the true boundary coordinates Px and the predicted boundary coordinates Qx for any two layers by the number of samples N and subsequently taking the square root. A smaller RMSE indicates a smaller prediction error. The two evaluated layer boundaries were defined as (i) the dermis–epidermal cell layer and (ii) the stratum corneum–epidermal cell layer. We confirmed that the RMSE did not increase as we verified segmentation accuracy using IoU. The RMSE for the dermis–epidermal cell layer boundary was 9.2 μm, and that for the stratum corneum–epidermal cell layer boundary was 3.7 μm, indicating that the boundary prediction accuracy was sufficient for reliable thickness measurements.

The boundary lines and layer thicknesses were calculated from the AI-identified skin layers as follows: For the boundary line calculation, the boundary regions of adjacent layers (e.g., stratum corneum and underlying layer) were extracted as point clouds. Because the extracted boundary segments could be discontinuous, isolated point clusters were removed first, followed by smoothing. The layer thickness was calculated by obtaining the difference between the Y-coordinate sequence of the lower boundary line in the skin-depth direction and that of the upper boundary line for the layer of interest. We calculated the thickness of the stratum corneum and epidermal cell layer.

Measurement of stratum corneum hydration

The hydration of the stratum corneum was measured using a corneometer (CM825; Courage + Khazaka Electronic GmbH, Germany). The measurement area corresponding to the LC-OCT field-of-view was recorded using skin markers. On the day following the LC-OCT measurement, the same site (1 cm × 2 cm) was measured five times using a stratum corneum hydration probe, and the mean value was used for analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1). The skin hydration is expressed as the electrical capacitance of the skin surface (arbitrary units, a.u.).

Microrelief measurement

Skin surface conditions were captured using multiple cameras (CASIO DZ-D100; iPhone SE with a derma camera attachment, DL1; DermaLite). Sulci cutis were assessed by visually labeling distinct skin furrows in images acquired using DermaLite. The microrelief line density was calculated by extracting single-pixel-wide lines from the labeled furrow images using skeletonization processing (ImageJ, Fiji), and subsequently determining the percentage of the line area relative to the total area (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Stratum corneum tape strip method

On the day after the LC-OCT measurement, two stratum corneum samples were collected from three locations on the forearm. For the first sample, the stratum corneum was collected on an adhesive tape (Cello Tape; Nichiban Co., Ltd., Japan) and transferred to a glass slide by immersion in xylene solution. The stratum corneum cells were subsequently stained with a BG solution (0.5% brilliant green, 1% gentian violet R; FUJIFILM Wako, Japan), and images were acquired using an optical microscope (BZ-X710, Keyence, Japan). We used the Cell Count Module BZ-H3C (Keyence) analysis software to calculate the area (µm²) of individual stratum corneum cells and the percentage (%) of multilayered stratum corneum, based on pigment intensity differences (Supplementary Fig. S3). In addition, the sebum tape (Skinchecker S100, SmartChip) collected in the second step pooled the stratum corneum samples from the three forearm sites. These samples were stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis of stratum corneum ceramide species (commissioned by Nicoderm Research).

Acquisition and correlation analysis of skin measurement parameters

The DEJ topography obtained via LC-OCT and different skin parameters were analyzed. Scatter plots were created for each subject’s measurement data to evaluate the relationship between the two continuous variables, and a correlation analysis was performed. Assuming that the variables followed a normal distribution, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to calculate the correlation. The results of the correlation analyses, including the correlation coefficients (r), are presented in the figures.

Statistics

Given the exploratory nature of this pilot study with a small sample size, p-value adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied, as such corrections would be overly conservative and could mask potentially meaningful trends in a hypothesis-generating context. All reported correlations should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in adequately powered studies.
