Abdominal binders versus semi-rigid corsets after open ventral hernia repair: early postoperative tolerance and patient preference in a randomized cross-over study
Authors: Kryspin Mitura, Małgorzata Pajer, Laura Kacprzak, Piotr Niecikowski, Marta Wojcik, Lidia Mitura, Małgorzata Sawicka, Iwona Witowska, Krystian Kisielewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of abdominal supports after ventral hernia repair remains common practice despite inconsistent evidence and lack of clear recommendations. Concerns persist that more rigid devices may be poorly tolerated in the early postoperative period.AimTo compare early postoperative patient-reported health state, functional outcomes, global preference, and willingness to recommend an elastic abdominal binder and a semi-rigid corset.MethodsIn this prospective randomized within-subject cross-over study, 23 patients following open sublay repair of large ventral hernias were sequentially fitted with an elastic abdominal binder and a semi-rigid corset on postoperative day 2–3. Each device was worn for approximately three hours during routine postoperative activities. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using VAS and EQ-5D-5 L questionnaires. Global preference and willingness to recommend each device were recorded.ResultsVAS scores were comparable between devices (p = 0.36), indicating no deterioration in perceived health state with corset use. No EQ-5D-5 L domain was rated worse with the corset. Significant improvements were observed for usual activities and anxiety/depression. A majority of patients preferred the corset (69.6%), and significantly more patients recommended it compared to the binder (95.7% vs. 65.2%, p = 0.039).ConclusionsSemi-rigid corsets were not associated with worse early postoperative tolerance and were more frequently preferred and recommended by patients. Early tolerance of semi-rigid supports may influence patient acceptance of postoperative abdominal support strategies, although long-term adherence was not assessed in the present study. These findings challenge the assumption that more rigid supports are poorly tolerated and support considering semi-rigid corsets as a viable option after open ventral hernia repair.

Introduction

Ventral hernias represent a common surgical problem with a substantial impact on patient quality of life [1–4]. Complex abdominal wall reconstruction procedures, including open sublay repair, are associated with a risk of postoperative complications and recurrence, particularly when postoperative recommendations are not followed [5].

The use of abdominal supports during postoperative recovery is common practice, although perioperative recommendations regarding their routine use remain inconsistent [6–9]. In clinical practice, limited attention is often paid to the specific type of support prescribed, and elastic abdominal binders are typically recommended more frequently than semi-rigid corsets. Both devices are intended to improve comfort, support wound healing, and facilitate recovery [6, 10, 11]. However, both surgeons and patients frequently express concerns that more rigid devices may be excessively tight or uncomfortable and therefore poorly tolerated in the early postoperative period [6]. To date, no studies have evaluated early patient-reported tolerance and preference between supports of different rigidity within the same patients in the immediate postoperative period. Early patient perception of abdominal supports may be critical, as initial tolerance may influence adherence to postoperative recommendations [12].

The aim of this study was therefore to compare early postoperative patient-reported health state, functional outcomes, global preference, and willingness to recommend an elastic abdominal binder and a semi-rigid lumbar corset following open sublay repair of large ventral hernias, using a randomized within-subject cross-over design.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, randomized, within-subject cross-over study was conducted at a tertiary referral center specializing in abdominal wall surgery. The study was designed as an exploratory pilot study. A sample size estimation was performed to inform the number of participants required for a paired within-subject comparison of patient-reported health state assessed using a visual analogue scale (VAS). Assuming a clinically relevant within-patient difference of 15 points, a standard deviation of paired differences of approximately 25 points, a two-sided α level of 0.05, and 80% power, 20 patients were required. To account for potential incomplete paired assessments, the target sample size was set at 23–25 patients. Patients were evaluated on postoperative day 2 or 3 following open sublay repair of large ventral hernias between February and December 2025. Each patient tested both an elastic abdominal binder and a semi-rigid lumbar corset, with the order of device application randomized. Patients were excluded if they were unable to comply with study procedures or if abdominal circumference fell outside manufacturer-recommended size ranges for the available products. Abdominal supports

Two commercially available abdominal supports were evaluated: one elastic abdominal binder and one semi-rigid corset. The elastic abdominal binder used in the study was the Abdominal Binder “24” (SIGVARIS S.A., St. Gallen, Switzerland), while the semi-rigid support was the lumbosacral stabilizing corset “26/2” (SIGVARIS S.A., St. Gallen, Switzerland). Each device was applied and secured using Velcro fastening closures according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Two sizes of each product were used according to manufacturer recommendations based on waist circumference. The elastic abdominal binder relied primarily on stretchable textile construction, whereas the semi-rigid corset incorporated reinforced structural elements designed to limit circumferential elongation and provide increased trunk stabilization.

Patient-reported outcomes

The primary endpoint was patient-reported health state assessed using a visual analogue scale (VAS) during device use. Each device was tested once per patient during a single postoperative evaluation period. Following completion of the first assessment, the alternative device was applied according to the randomized sequence and evaluated using the same protocol. The approximately three-hour evaluation period was intentionally selected to assess immediate functional tolerance under clinically relevant postoperative conditions, including mobilization, respiration, eating, and routine daily activities. This duration was considered sufficient for patients to reliably assess differences in tightness, comfort, breathing effort, and perceived stabilization between devices while maintaining feasibility of the within-subject cross-over design in the immediate postoperative setting. After each application period, patients completed the EQ-5D-5 L questionnaire assessing mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression (each domain scored from 1 to 5). Patients also rated their overall perceived health state while wearing the device using a visual analogue scale (VAS; 0 = worst imaginable health state, 100 = best imaginable health state) and indicated whether they would recommend the device to other patients. After testing both devices, participants were asked to indicate which device they considered overall better.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using paired within-subject comparisons reflecting the cross-over study design. VAS and EQ-5D-5 L outcomes were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Binary outcomes were compared using the exact McNemar test. Data are presented as median (interquartile range) or number (percentage), as appropriate. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Twenty-three patients completed paired assessments for all outcome measures and were included in the final analysis. The study population consisted predominantly of patients with large ventral hernias undergoing open sublay repair, with baseline demographic and clinical characteristics summarized in Table 1. No patients discontinued device testing once enrolled, and paired data were available for all primary and secondary outcomes.

Patient-rated health state (VAS)

Median VAS scores were 70 (IQR 60–90) for the elastic binder and 80 (IQR 60–80) for the semi-rigid corset. Paired analysis showed no statistically significant difference between devices (p = 0.36). Recommendation to other patients

The elastic abdominal binder was recommended by 15 of 23 patients (65%), whereas the semi-rigid corset was recommended by 22 of 23 patients (96%) as shown in Fig. 2B. In paired within-subject comparison, patients were significantly more likely to recommend the corset than the binder (p = 0.039), indicating higher patient acceptance of the corset when both devices were tested under identical postoperative conditions (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study provides clinically relevant insight into early patient experience with abdominal supports following ventral hernia repair. Early tolerance may represent an important contributor to postoperative acceptance and continued use of abdominal supports, as discomfort during initial use is a common reason for discontinuation in clinical practice. Despite common concerns that more rigid devices may be poorly tolerated, semi-rigid corsets were not associated with worse patient-reported health state and were more frequently preferred and recommended compared with elastic abdominal binders. [13, 14]

Our data challenge the widespread assumption that greater device stiffness necessarily translates into poorer patient tolerance in the immediate postoperative period. Patients’ evaluations reflected not only perceived health state but also their ability to perform basic daily activities, postoperative well-being, and confidence in the effectiveness of the applied support.

Functional outcomes were comparable between devices, with small but consistent improvements observed with the corset in selected domains, particularly usual activities and anxiety/depression. Although these differences were modest, they suggest that patients may experience greater functional reassurance and confidence in abdominal support when using a device perceived as providing stronger stabilization during early mobilization. Importantly, device evaluation was performed during routine postoperative activities, suggesting that patient preference reflects real-life functional experience rather than passive device tolerance alone.

Early tolerance of an abdominal support may play a key role in determining patient adherence. In clinical practice, discomfort during the first hours of use is a common reason for premature discontinuation, despite recommendations for prolonged daily use over several weeks. Therefore, the initial period of device use may represent a critical window influencing long-term compliance. Patients who perceive a device as comfortable and supportive during the first hours of use may be more willing to continue wearing it during the postoperative period. However, long-term adherence was not directly evaluated in the present study and requires confirmation in future longitudinal investigations.

In this context, the higher preference and recommendation rates observed for semi-rigid corsets may have implications for long-term adherence, although this was not directly assessed in the present study.

Importantly, patient preference was not determined solely by comfort. Some patients preferred the corset despite reporting increased tightness, suggesting that perceived stability and confidence in abdominal wall protection may play a significant role in postoperative device acceptance. Some patients also expressed concern that more elastic supports might provide insufficient abdominal stabilization, potentially compromising perceived surgical protection, which further influenced device preference.

The results should be interpreted in the context of previously reported biomechanical differences between abdominal supports. Semi-rigid corsets have been shown to provide greater circumferential stabilization of the abdominal wall compared with elastic binders under physiologically relevant loading conditions, while not impairing respiratory function [12]. This enhanced stabilization may translate into a greater subjective sense of support and safety during mobilization, which may explain the observed patient preference patterns.

This observation has practical implications for postoperative care. Selection of abdominal supports is often guided by routine practice, product availability, or patient habit rather than by comparative evidence or consideration of mechanical properties and patient acceptance [6, 8, 9, 15]. The present results suggest that semi-rigid corsets should not be dismissed solely because of presumed intolerance. Instead, both comfort and perceived functional support should be considered when prescribing postoperative abdominal supports, as patient adherence and confidence in the device may influence recovery behavior during the early postoperative period.

Recommendation rates further highlight the clinical relevance of device acceptance, as willingness to recommend a device likely reflects an integrated patient judgment encompassing comfort, perceived effectiveness, and confidence in protection of the abdominal wall. Devices that patients are willing to recommend may also be more acceptable for prolonged postoperative use, although this relationship requires confirmation in future longitudinal studies. Future studies should determine whether greater patient confidence and acceptance of more rigid supports translate into clinically relevant outcomes, including earlier return to daily activities or differences in postoperative recovery trajectories.

The role of abdominal supports may extend beyond the immediate postoperative period. In our clinical practice, abdominal supports are also frequently used preoperatively in patients with large ventral hernias. The rationale for such use includes improving abdominal wall support, limiting progressive enlargement of the hernia, and facilitating physiological adaptation to reduced abdominal compliance expected after abdominal wall reconstruction. By increasing external abdominal support and partially restoring abdominal domain, these devices may increase diaphragmatic workload and respiratory effort prior to surgery, potentially contributing to perioperative respiratory conditioning. Whether such preoperative conditioning influences postoperative adaptation, respiratory function, or progression of loss of domain remains to be determined and warrants further investigation. However, these potential effects remain speculative and require dedicated investigation.

Our results suggest that device selection may influence not only patient comfort but also patient acceptance of postoperative abdominal support strategies. Both mechanical properties and patient acceptance should therefore be considered, as early patient experience may play a role in determining continued postoperative use.

Strengths and limitations

The within-subject randomized cross-over design represents a key strength, allowing direct comparison of both abdominal supports under identical clinical conditions while minimizing inter-individual variability. Importantly, devices were evaluated during routine postoperative activities, enhancing the real-world relevance of patient-reported outcomes rather than reflecting passive device tolerance alone. Assessment in the early postoperative period is particularly relevant, as this phase is critical for patient mobilization, confidence, and acceptance of postoperative support.

An additional strength is the focus on patient-reported outcomes and global preference, which reflect an integrated patient experience beyond isolated measures of discomfort or functional limitation and are directly relevant for postoperative adherence. The results may have practical implications for postoperative care, where device selection is often based on routine rather than patient-centered evidence.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single specialized center and included a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Outcomes were restricted to the early postoperative period, and long-term tolerability as well as effects on recovery trajectories were not assessed. The study was not designed to evaluate clinical endpoints such as postoperative complications or hernia recurrence. The study assessed short-term tolerance after approximately three hours of use and therefore does not allow conclusions regarding long-term adherence to the recommended six-week postoperative use.

The study did not include formal cost analysis of the evaluated abdominal supports. In local healthcare setting, both devices are commercially available at comparable cost, and therefore economic comparison was not considered a major differentiating factor within the present study. However, pricing and reimbursement policies may differ substantially between healthcare systems, and future studies may further evaluate cost-effectiveness in broader clinical settings.

A formal washout period between device applications was not feasible in the immediate postoperative setting; however, randomization of device order was applied to minimize potential sequence effects. Preoperative use of abdominal supports was not evaluated and remains an area for future research. Finally, although mechanical properties of abdominal supports have been previously characterized, detailed biomechanical analysis was beyond the scope of the present study, which intentionally focused on patient-reported experience. Future studies should determine whether early tolerance of abdominal supports predicts long-term adherence and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Semi-rigid lumbar corsets were not associated with worse patient-rated health state compared with elastic abdominal binders in the early postoperative period after open sublay repair. On the contrary, corsets were more often preferred and recommended by patients. These findings challenge the assumption that more rigid supports are poorly tolerated and support considering semi-rigid corsets as a viable postoperative option after open ventral hernia repair.
