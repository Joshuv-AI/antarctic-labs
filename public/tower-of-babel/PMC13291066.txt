Liquid biopsies for BRAF V600E assessment and monitoring in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma: a real-world study of a tertiary cancer center
Authors: Elena Colombo, Elisa Ortolan, Stefano Cavalieri, Elena Tamborini, Federica Perrone, Iolanda Capone, Luca Agnelli, Biagio Paolini, Massimo Milione, Giuseppina Calareso, Monica Salvetti, Francesca Platini, Cristiana Bergamini, Salvatore Alfieri, Carlo Resteghini, Arianna Ottini, Francesca Caspani, Imperia Nuzzolese, Achille Bottiglieri, Giacomo Massa, Daria Maria Filippini, Giancarlo Pruneri, Lisa Licitra, Maria Grazia Daidone, Laura Deborah Locati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRelapsed/metastatic anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (R/M ATC) is associated with poor prognosis. Approximately 40% of ATCs harbor the BRAFV600E mutation, for which targeted therapy is available. However, the clinical characteristics of BRAFV600E-mutant versus wild-type ATC and the role of liquid biopsy (LB) for molecular assessment and monitoring remain incompletely defined.MethodsConsecutive patients with R/M ATCs treated at a tertiary referral center were stratified as BRAF wild-type (Cohort A) or BRAFV600E-mutant (Cohort B). Patients received chemotherapy, dabrafenib and trametinib (BRAF/MEKi), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) or best supportive care. Tumor tissue (TB) was analyzed by RT-PCR and/or next-generation sequencing (NGS). Circulating cell-free DNA was assessed by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) and/or a 52-gene NGS panel on LB at baseline in both cohorts and longitudinally in Cohort B. Clinical characteristics, treatment outcomes, and concordance between TB and LB were evaluated.ResultsBetween 2018 and 2021, 24 patients were included (13 Cohort A, 11 Cohort B). BRAFV600E tumors more frequently presented with metastatic disease at diagnosis (81.8% vs. 15.4%, p = 0.003). Overall, 79% received first-line therapy and 41.6% second-line treatment. All patients in Cohort B received BRAF/MEK inhibitors, achieving a median progression-free survival of 3.2 months (95% CI 1.2–17.5). Concordance for BRAFV600E status between TB and LB assessed by ddPCR was 93.7% (specificity 100%, sensitivity 85.7%). Among 12 paired TB/LB NGS analyses, overall mutation concordance was 30%. In longitudinal assessment, LB anticipated disease progression in 3 of 6 cases and, in one case, identified emerging secondary alterations during BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy.ConclusionsBRAFV600E-mutant ATC displays distinct clinical features and improved survival when treated with targeted therapy; ddPCR-based liquid biopsy provides a rapid and sensitive method for BRAFV600E detection and may support timely therapeutic decision-making. Serial LB analysis may contribute to disease monitoring and detection of resistance mechanisms in selected patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12020-026-04672-x.Keywords: Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, BRAF V600E, Liquid biopsy, Digital droplet PCR, Next generation sequencing, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) accounts for 2% of all thyroid cancers (TCs) [1, 2]. In historical series, 46% of ATCs present with unresectable disease, median overall survival (OS) is 4 months and 1-year survival falls below 20% [3, 4]. ATCs can harbor gene mutations in BRAF, RAS, TP53 and TERT promoter, gene fusions are rare [5, 6]. High tumor mutational burden and PD-L1 expression are frequently present [6–9].

Phase II ROAR study demonstrated the efficacy of BRAF and MEK inhibitors (BRAF/MEKi) dabrafenib and trametinib in relapsed/metastatic (R/M) BRAF-mutant ATC, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 56% and 1-year progression free survival (PFS) and OS of 43.2% and 51.7%, respectively [10]. Phase II study with PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) spartalizumab reported higher ORR for PD-L1 positive ATC compared to the whole cohort (29% vs. 19%) [11]. Further studies are investigating the association of anti-PD-(L)1 with anti-CTLA4 [12, 13], VEGFRi [14, 15], BRAF/MEKi [16] or chemotherapy. Nevertheless, the accessibility to innovative therapies for ATC patients in Europe is still an unmet clinical need [17].

The International guidelines issued by European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) [18], American Thyroid Association (ATA) [19] and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [20] recommend: (i) to assess BRAF status upfront at diagnosis on tissue biopsies (TB), either by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR); (ii) to use liquid biopsies (LB) if tumoral tissue is not promptly available; (iii) to perform NGS analysis in BRAFWT to identify other potentially actionable alterations; (iv) to start first-line systemic therapy with BRAF/MEKi for R/M BRAF-mutant ATC; (v) to include ATC patients in clinical trials.

In the last years, the detection of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and the quantification of genomic material released by tumor cells as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) by liquid biopsy (LB) allowed to detect driver mutations, monitor treatment response, anticipate recurrence, and investigate mechanisms of resistance to targeted therapy in several tumor types [21, 22]. Droplet-digital/digital PCR (dd/dPCR)–based technologies and cfDNA Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) can assess the BRAF status through a minimally invasive, repeatable approach [23–25]. Previously, the molecular profiling of a single-center ATC series has been evaluated at diagnosis by NGS on LB and TB, showing a 92.9% concordance rate of BRAFV600E mutations [26]. The impact of BRAF status on the clinical behavior of ATC and the utility of cfDNA NGS/ddPCR on LB performed in longitudinal samples across systemic therapy for BRAFV600E mutant ATCs are still unreported.

This real-world study aimed at analyzing the features and outcomes of consecutive patients with R/M ATC treated in a referral cancer center, and to compare the characteristics of two ATC subgroups stratified by BRAF status. In addition, we investigated (i) the concordance in BRAFV600E status at baseline between TB and LB by PCR-based and NGS-based assays; (ii) the kinetics of ctDNA assessed by serial LB in the BRAFV600E cohort, exploring the role of LB as a potential tool to inform therapeutic decisions together with clinical and radiological re-evaluations.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted at Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Nazionale Tumori in Milan, Italy, in compliance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The protocol was approved by the institutional Ethical Committee (INT 84/20). This retrospective observational study was conducted according to STROBE principles and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligible adult patients with R/M ATC entered this study from April 2018 to October 2021. BRAF assessment led to stratify cases in 2 Cohorts: BRAFWT (A) and BRAFV600E (B). Cohort A received either chemotherapy (carboplatin AUC 5-paclitaxel 175 mg/sqm IV every 3 weeks (Q3W)) or ICIs (Nivolumab 240 mg IV Q2W or Pembrolizumab 200 mg IV Q3W). Cohort B received either dabrafenib (150 mg BID)-trametinib (2 mg daily) or chemotherapy or ICI, as described above. ICIs and dabrafenib-trametinib were supplied upon nominal request to AIFA 5% Fund and Managed Access Program (open between 2020 and 2022), respectively. The best objective response (OR) to each treatment was evaluated by computer tomography scan and classified according to RECIST v.1.1.

Stage at diagnosis was defined according to the UICC/AJCC TNM 8th edition classification at initial presentation. The presence of distant metastases was instead assessed at the time of first oncological evaluation prior to initiation of systemic therapy, which could occur after locoregional treatment.

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples obtained during diagnostic biopsy or thyroidectomy were used to evaluate BRAF status and the molecular profiling. Hematoxylin and eosin slides were internally reviewed, and ATC tumor area was marked for macrodissection. The corresponding areas were scraped from 10 serial FFPE sections of 8 μm thickness to obtain a minimum tumor cellularity of 40%. DNA extracted by tumor samples was considered positive for BRAFV600E if detected either by direct gene sequencing RT-PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Life Technologies, 3500 DX Genetic Analyzer, with certification CE-IVD) or targeted NGS (Ion AmpliSeq Cancer Hotspot Panel v2 CHPv2 consisting of 207 amplicons covering hot-spot mutations of 50 genes). Targeted NGS was performed through Personal Genome Machine with IonTorrent technology (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Life Technologies). Amino acidic substitution and variant allele frequency (VAF) were annotated. PD-L1 expression was assessed through CE-IVD PDL-1 IHC 22C3 Pharm DXX (Dako 22C3 Clone developed in Autostainer Link 48) and quantified as Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) and Combined Positive Score (CPS) (Appendix 1).

LB was performed at first oncological evaluation to assess BRAF status concordance with TB. In Cohort B, LBs were performed at each outpatient visit up to the end of treatment to monitor the VAF of BRAFV600E gene and the emergence of new mutations. Circulating BRAFV600E was assessed for each patient on cfDNA by using a custom designed assay and ddPCR™ technology, three negative controls were included in every run. cfDNA sequencing using the cfDNA NGS panel was performed at baseline and throughout therapy. ctDNA was assessed using Oncomine Pan-Cancer Cell-Free assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a 52-gene NGS panel covering 272 amplicons from 52 cancer-related genes, hereafter referred to as the cfDNA NGS panel.

Mutations found by plasma cfDNA NGS panel were validated by ddPCR technology using custom designed assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific custom SNP genotyping assay tool or Bio-Rad Mutation Detection Assay). Turnaround times were approximately 24–48 h for ddPCR on liquid biopsy, 3–5 days for tissue RT-PCR, and 10–14 days for targeted NGS panels (tissue or plasma).

Descriptive statistics were performed in the two cohorts. Contingency tables were analyzed with Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variables, distributions of age, tumor diameter and time from symptoms onset to diagnosis were compared with Mann-Whitney U test. Data cutoff was September 1, 2023. The disease-free interval (DFI) was defined as the time from the date of surgery of the primary ATC to the occurrence of locoregional recurrence or distant metastatic disease and was calculated exclusively in patients who underwent locoregional treatment (surgery ± RT). Patients presenting with metastatic disease at diagnosis who did not undergo surgery were not included in DFI calculation. Median PFS with dabrafenib-trametinib and ICI, received either in first line (1 L) or second line (2 L), was defined as the interval time from the start of therapy and the end for any reason. OS was defined as the interval time from either upfront Best Supportive Care (BSC) or start of 1 L therapy and the event. PFS and OS curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with log-rank test. Hazard Ratio (HR) for death was assessed with Cox proportional hazard methods. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism v.5.02 was used for contingency table analyses, SAS University Edition for survival ones (Suppl.Materials).

An oncoplot was generated using custom scripts in Python (version 3.9) using the matplotlib library. Mutation data were derived from targeted NGS panels performed on tumor tissue and plasma samples. For visualization, alterations were aggregated at the gene level. When multiple variants were detected in the same gene and sample, the variant with the highest variant allele frequency (VAF) was selected for visualization.

Results

Twenty-four patients entered the study: 13 in Cohort A, 11 in Cohort B (Table 1). Loco-regional treatments were administered in 38.5% of patients in the Cohort A, and in 45.5% of the Cohort B. Three BRAFWT non-operated patients (ID9, ID10, ID12) received palliative external beam radiotherapy for symptom control prior to systemic therapy. Median disease-free interval was 3.4 months (95% CI 1.019–5.46; 1.3 vs. 4.0 months in Cohort B vs. A). The most prevalent histological variant was pleomorphic, squamous was found mainly in Cohort B and rhabdoid only in Cohort A. Cohort B patients were diagnosed more frequently at Stage IVC (81.8% vs. 15.4%, p = 0.003), and presented higher prevalence of distant metastases at the start of systemic treatment (91% vs. 38.5%, p = 0.013). A concomitant minor component of DTC was identified in 25% of patients, all from Cohort A (p = 0.002). At first oncological evaluation, ECOG PS ≥ 2 was observed in 66.7% of the overall population (53.8% and 81.8% of Cohort A and B patients, respectively).

BRAF status was assessed on tissue by RT-PCR in 5 cases (4 BRAFWT, 1 BRAFV600E) and RT-PCR plus NGS in 19 cases, with full concordance of PCR/NGS results. TP53 mutations were found in 70% of BRAFV600E and in 22.2% of BRAFWT ATC (p = 0.07), RAS mutations in 33.3% of BRAFWT and in 10% of BRAFV600E. Other mutations found are reported in Supplementary Table 2. The median number of pathogenic mutations detected per patient was 1.5 (range 0–3) in BRAFWT subgroup and 2 (range 1–4) in BRAFV600E. PD-L1 expression was available for 14 cases (6 in Cohort A, 8 in Cohort B): 7 were moderately positive (TPS score 1–49%), 5 strongly positive (TPS > 49%); 2 had negative TPS, but 1 positive CPS.

Circulating BRAFV600E was assessed by ddPCR. LB was available for 17 out of 24 patients (70.8%), 11 BRAFWT and 6 BRAFV600E. Comparing baseline LB results versus BRAF test on tumor tissue at the same time (16 concurrent pairs), either by RT-PCR or NGS, the ddPCR on LB showed a concordance of 93.7% (15/16) in the overall population, 9/9 in the BRAFWT and 6/7 in the BRAFV600E groups, with a specificity and sensitivity of 100% and 85.7%, respectively. Baseline LB-NGS of cfDNA was available for 14/24 patients (58.3%), 9 BRAFWT and 5 BRAFV600E. Twelve concurrent pairs of TBs and LBs were available (11 sampled at baseline and 1 during treatment, within 4 weeks of distance and without therapeutic change in between): in 2/12 pairs no mutation was detected by NGS, either on TB or LB. In 10/12 pairs, globally 20 mutations were detected: 6/20 both in TB and LB (30% concordance), 9/20 only in TB and 5/20 only in LB. The highest rates of concordance were observed for BRAFV600E (4/7, 57.1%). Median value of BRAF VAF was higher on TB (15%; range, 1–51) compared to LB (0.8%; range, 0.1–13.9) (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This study highlights relevant differences between BRAFWT and BRAFV600E ATCs by analyzing clinical aspects, tissue and plasma features:

Metastatic pattern. BRAFV600E ATC presented more frequently with distant metastases, in agreement with previous data on papillary TC [27]. Liver metastases were observed prevalently in Cohort B; previous observations reported a possible hepatic tropism of BRAF-mutant cancers [28, 29].

Metastatic pattern. BRAFV600E ATC presented more frequently with distant metastases, in agreement with previous data on papillary TC [27]. Liver metastases were observed prevalently in Cohort B; previous observations reported a possible hepatic tropism of BRAF-mutant cancers [28, 29].

PD-L1, TP53 mutation, ICI responses. PD-L1 expression was independent of BRAF status. In a recent work, TP53/BRAF co-mutation predicted response to ICI across multiple cancer types, while TP53 mutation alone was related to the worst outcomes [30]. In our study, 3 out of 4 patients treated with ICI showed PR (all had TPS ≥ 49%). One out of four patients had TP53/BRAF co-mutation and showed a profound response to ICI (ID22 17 months) compared to the PFS of 2 patients with TP53/BRAFWT ATC who received ICI (ID3 1.28, ID10 4.2 months). The benefit of adding ICI to BRAF/MEKi has been recently reported in a retrospective study [31].

The benefit of adding ICI to BRAF/MEKi has been recently reported in a retrospective study [31].

Survival outcomes. BRAFV600E patients who received treatment showed better survival outcomes than BRAFWT despite a higher rate of metastatic spread at diagnosis and PS ≥ 2. Overall mPFS and mOS were similar to previous real-world series [32, 33]. Cohort B 1-year OS was 33%, between the results reported by a previous real-world series (25%) [33] and the ROAR study (51.7%) [10]. Differences in baseline performance status and access timelines to targeted therapy may partly explain this discrepancy compared with clinical trial populations. Nevertheless, this study confirms the pivotal role of BRAF/MEKi to achieve disease control in BRAFV600E ATC, consistent with the tissue-agnostic activity of BRAF/MEK inhibitors across BRAFV600-mutant tumors [34], and the controlled comparison between BRAF-mutant and BRAFWT tumors strengthens the biological interpretation of the findings.

The prevalence of BRAFV600E in our cohort (45.8%) is consistent with the reported range of 25–50% across published series, though variability exists across populations. The French ENDOCAN-TUTHYREF multicenter cohort [35] reported a BRAFV600E prevalence of approximately 25% among 360 ATC patients. This discrepancy may reflect differences in referral patterns, testing strategies (systematic BRAF assessment at our center vs. 59% testing rate in the ENDOCAN-TUTHYREF cohort), and population characteristics such as geographic and ethnic variability.

The high sensitivity and specificity in detecting circulating BRAFV600E through ddPCR was consistent with previous results [25]. The concordance in NGS results between concurrent pairs of TBs/LBs was 57.1% for BRAF mutation, 30% overall. This may be due to (i) incomplete gene and codon overlap between NGS panels; (ii) temporal heterogeneity in paired TB/LB tests, and (iii) lower sensitivity of cfDNA-based NGS compared to tissue-based NGS. Plasma NGS, detecting gene alterations at low VAF thresholds (≥ 0.1%), can identify a broad spectrum of variants. However, not all variants detected at low frequency represent true somatic mutations, thus low-VAF gene alterations require mandatory validation by allele-specific assays (e.g., ddPCR). Conversely, ddPCR offers superior sensitivity and specificity for predefined targets such as BRAFV600E, and a positive ddPCR result can be considered reliable without further confirmation. These results support ddPCR as a minimally invasive tool to quickly assess the presence of BRAFV600E mutation (turnaround time approximately 24–48 h) and rapidly address appropriate treatment, especially when TB is not feasible. If ddPCR test is negative, BRAF status needs to be confirmed also on FFPE tissue. In clinical practice, a positive ddPCR result on liquid biopsy may allow prompt initiation of BRAF/MEKi while awaiting tissue confirmation, particularly in rapidly progressive cases. These findings align with recent efforts to standardize molecular assessment algorithms in thyroid cancer and to optimize the use of precision medicine platforms with different panel extensions [36].

Disease progression to BRAF/MEKi occurs via reactivation of the MAPK pathway [37]. A case series of four ATC patients treated with BRAF/MEKi reported the detection of acquired mutations in RAS pathway, and only one was case diagnosed by LB [38]. Here, in one patient we identified acquired mutations in NRAS and in PIK3CA, possibly related to mechanisms of secondary resistance, as described in melanoma [39]. Both alterations could be targeted in clinical trials. LB deserves to be explored as a potential tool to anticipate progression and to evaluate the dynamic changes in ctDNA, and further investigations are needed in reference to LB RECIST [40]. NGS gene panel sizes can differ significantly, and a comprehensive genomic profiling would maximize the probability of finding actionable targets. NGS assays of LB can play a role in providing access to basket trials, as recently reported in the PRISM study [41]. Plasma ctDNA NGS analysis still requires technical refinements to improve sensitivity and specificity, and the results should be critically interpreted by clinicians. These findings suggest that tissue and plasma NGS should be considered complementary tools rather than interchangeable approaches in ATC.

A limit of this study was related to the sample size; however, we are dealing with an extremely rare and aggressive cancer without easily accessible therapeutic options. The complex procedures to access BRAF/MEKi and ICIs required weeks/months to be completed: this could have had an impact on the therapeutic choices and, consequently, on patients’ outcomes. Another limit was the lack of LB longitudinal sampling in BRAFWT cohort. Recently, in some countries the association of pembrolizumab-lenvatinib was included as therapeutic option for ATC regardless of BRAF mutation, and LB could add new information by comparing the dynamic changes across time in both BRAF-mutant and wt ATCs.

BRAF mutation in ATCs is not a mere molecular finding, but it implies different clinical features, treatments and prognosis. LB is a promising and non-invasive tool to detect rapidly BRAF mutation by ddPCR. Structured studies on TB/LB molecular genotyping are needed to further advance the precision oncology research in ATC.
