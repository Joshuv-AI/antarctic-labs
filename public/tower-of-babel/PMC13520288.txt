Nurses’ perspectives on the influence of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols on postoperative recovery after emergency laparotomy: a qualitative study
Authors: Fatmah Ahmed Alamoudi, Mohammed Nasser Albarqi, Mohammed Yousef Almulhim, Mai Adel Albana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency laparotomy carries high postoperative morbidity and mortality, requiring coordinated perioperative care to optimize recovery. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols improve outcomes in elective surgery; however, their implementation in emergency settings remains complex. Limited evidence explores how nurses perceive and operationalize ERAS in high-acuity contexts. This study aimed to examine nurses’ perspectives on the influence of ERAS protocols on postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy in tertiary care.MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was employed, grounded in a constructivist-informed, descriptive epistemological orientation. Twenty registered nurses from surgical wards, intensive care units [ICU], high-dependency units [HDU], and post-anesthesia care units [PACU] in two tertiary government teaching hospitals in the Al-Ahsa region, Saudi Arabia, participated in semi-structured interviews. Participants had direct experience caring for patients undergoing emergency laparotomy and exposure to ERAS related practices. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. The study adhered to SRQR guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Faisal University.ResultsFour interrelated themes were identified: (1) ERAS as a structured recovery roadmap providing measurable milestones; (2) balancing protocol and patient instability through adaptive modification; (3) nursing advocacy and clinical judgment as central mediators of implementation; and (4) system constraints, including staffing and institutional variability, shaping consistency. These findings informed the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model, conceptualizing ERAS enactment as a dynamic, nurse-mediated and context-dependent process.ConclusionsERAS implementation in emergency laparotomy is adaptive rather than checklist-driven. Its effectiveness depends on nursing leadership, interdisciplinary collaboration, and organizational support. Strengthening these elements may enhance sustainable recovery pathways in high-acuity surgical settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04861-z.

Introduction

Emergency laparotomy remains one of the highest-risk surgical procedures worldwide, associated with significant postoperative morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stay [1]. Patients undergoing emergency abdominal surgery often present with physiological instability, sepsis, bowel obstruction, perforation, or intra-abdominal bleeding, all of which compound surgical stress and impair recovery trajectories [2]. Unlike elective surgery, emergency laparotomy limits opportunities for preoperative optimization and structured perioperative planning, thereby increasing the complexity of postoperative care [3]. Consequently, improving recovery outcomes following emergency laparotomy remains a major clinical priority for nursing teams.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols were developed to mitigate surgical stress responses, reduce complications, and to enhance and expedite functional postoperative recovery through evidence-based, multimodal perioperative care pathways [4]. Initially implemented in colorectal surgery, ERAS has demonstrated reductions in length of stay, postoperative complications, and healthcare costs across multiple elective surgical specialties [5, 6]. Core ERAS components include early mobilization, optimized pain management with opioid-sparing strategies, early enteral nutrition, fluid balance optimization, and standardized postoperative monitoring [7]. These interventions aim to promote physiological stability, reduce catabolism, and support earlier return to baseline function [8, 9].

While ERAS pathways are well-established in elective settings, their application in emergency surgery - particularly emergency laparotomy - remains less standardized and more challenging [10]. The unpredictable nature of emergency presentations, hemodynamic instability, and higher baseline risk profiles can hinder full protocol adherence [10]. Nonetheless, emerging evidence suggests that adapted ERAS pathways in emergency laparotomy may improve postoperative outcomes, including reduced complications and shorter hospital stays [11]. However, successful ERAS implementation is highly dependent on multidisciplinary collaboration and frontline clinical engagement, especially among nurses who coordinate and deliver much of postoperative care [12, 13].

Nurses play a central role in operationalizing ERAS principles. They are directly responsible for monitoring hemodynamic status, managing analgesia, facilitating early mobilization, encouraging nutritional intake, educating patients and families, and detecting early signs of complications [14]. In emergency laparotomy, where patients often require intensive monitoring and rapid clinical decision-making, nursing judgment and coordination are critical determinants of recovery [15]. Moreover, adherence to ERAS elements such as early ambulation or timely feeding frequently relies on nursing advocacy and patient engagement [16].

Despite the centrality of nursing practice in ERAS implementation, much of the existing literature focuses on surgical or institutional outcomes rather than the experiences and perspectives of nurses [17]. Quantitative studies have evaluated protocol compliance rates, complication indices, and cost-effectiveness; however, fewer studies have explored how nurses perceive the influence of ERAS on patient recovery in high-acuity emergency contexts [18]. Understanding nurses’ perspectives is essential because their clinical interpretations, perceived barriers, and experiential insights directly shape protocol adherence and sustainability [19]. Furthermore, nurses’ perceptions of ERAS effectiveness directly influence their adherence to protocol elements and their engagement with patients in recovery planning, thereby shaping postoperative outcomes through motivated, informed nursing practice.

Emergency laparotomy presents unique contextual challenges that may influence ERAS application, including staffing constraints, fluctuating patient acuity, limited preoperative education, and organizational variability [11]. Nurses must often adapt standardized protocols to unstable or deteriorating patients, balancing evidence-based guidelines with clinical pragmatism [20]. These adaptive processes remain underexplored in qualitative literature. Without insight into how nurses interpret, prioritize, and enact ERAS principles in emergency settings, efforts to optimize implementation may overlook key practical realities [21].

Furthermore, qualitative inquiry is particularly valuable in examining complex clinical interventions such as ERAS, where protocol success depends not only on technical compliance but also on professional culture, interprofessional communication, and contextual judgment [22]. By exploring nurses’ perspectives, researchers can illuminate the mechanisms through which ERAS influences recovery, identify barriers to consistent implementation, and generate contextually grounded recommendations for practice refinement [23].

Given the high-risk nature of emergency laparotomy and the pivotal role of nursing care in postoperative management, there is a critical need to examine how nurses perceive the influence of ERAS protocols on patient recovery in this setting. Such exploration may reveal how protocol components translate into clinical outcomes from the standpoint of those most intimately involved in bedside care. Additionally, it may uncover tensions between protocol expectations and real-world practice, thereby informing more responsive and sustainable ERAS adaptations for emergency surgery.

Therefore, this study aims to explore nurses’ perspectives on the influence of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols on postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy. By generating in-depth qualitative insights, this research seeks to contribute to the refinement of ERAS implementation strategies and to strengthen nursing-led approaches to optimizing recovery in high-acuity surgical contexts. The study employed semi-structured interviews to generate rich, experience-based accounts of how nurses interpret and enact ERAS principles in this challenging clinical context.

Specifically, this study addresses two interrelated research questions. The primary question asked: “How do nurses perceive the influence of ERAS protocols on postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy in tertiary care settings?” A secondary question guided the inquiry: “What contextual, clinical, and organizational factors shape ERAS implementation in emergency laparotomy?”

Method

Design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design to explore nurses’ perspectives on how Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols shape postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy in a high-acuity tertiary-care context. Qualitative description was selected because it is well-suited to generating practice-near, clinically actionable accounts of how complex care pathways are implemented and experienced by frontline clinicians, without imposing heavy theoretical interpretation beyond participants’ meanings [24]. This approach supports a clear, contextualized understanding of “what is happening” in routine care and how nurses interpret its influence on patient recovery processes such as mobilization, nutrition, pain control, complications surveillance, and discharge readiness. Qualitative description is particularly appropriate when the study aim is to inform service improvement and implementation refinement in real-world healthcare settings [25]. The study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) to enhance transparency, coherence, and methodological rigor [26]. Epistemologically, the study is grounded in a descriptive, constructivist-informed orientation: it does not seek to impose an interpretive theory on participants’ accounts, but rather to construct a faithful, practice-near understanding of their experiences and perceptions as frontline clinicians. The researcher’s positioning as a nursing academic with perioperative knowledge is acknowledged and addressed through reflexive practice (see Section "Rigour and reflexivity").

Study setting and recruitment

The study was conducted in two tertiary healthcare government teaching hospitals in the Al-Ahsa region, Saudi Arabia, where emergency general surgery services provide round-the-clock care for patients undergoing emergency laparotomy. These tertiary hospitals manage high volumes of acute abdominal presentations and deliver perioperative care through multidisciplinary surgical, anesthesia, critical care, and nursing teams. Postoperative recovery commonly occurs across multiple units (e.g., post-anesthesia care unit [PACU], surgical wards, high-dependency units [HDU], and intensive care units [ICU]), creating variability in ERAS implementation depending on acuity, staffing, and workflow. The inclusion of participants from both hospitals and from diverse postoperative care areas enabled exploration of nurses’ perspectives on ERAS implementation across varying levels of acuity, patient complexity, and care requirements. This setting enabled exploration of ERAS influence within a context characterized by rapid clinical transitions, time-sensitive decision-making, and frequent deviations from elective pathways.

A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit nurses with direct experience in postoperative care for emergency laparotomy patients and routine exposure to ERAS-related practices (e.g., early mobilization, early oral intake, multimodal analgesia, lines/tubes management, and standardized monitoring). Recruitment occurred through unit-based announcements and direct invitation facilitated by a designated nursing liaison who was not involved in data collection. Interested nurses contacted the research team directly to reduce perceived coercion. Sampling continued until information power was achieved - operationalized as the point at which additional interviews yielded no substantively new insights relevant to the study aim [27]. Efforts were made to ensure variation in participants’ clinical areas (surgical ward, ICU/HDU, PACU), years of experience, and professional roles (staff nurse, charge nurse). This variation was operationalized through purposive matrix sampling: at the outset, the research team identified the dimensions of variation considered most clinically relevant (unit type, years of experience, and professional role) and mapped these as a sampling grid. As recruitment progressed, the nursing liaison actively sought participants from under-represented cells in the matrix. For example, if early recruits were predominantly from surgical wards, subsequent invitations were targeted toward ICU/HDU and PACU staff. This approach was continued iteratively until information power was achieved across all dimensions of variation.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews designed to elicit rich, experience-based narratives of how nurses perceive ERAS-related practices influencing recovery after emergency laparotomy. Semi-structured interviewing was chosen for its ability to explore complex clinical processes while allowing participants to prioritize what they considered most meaningful, including contextual constraints and tacit clinical judgment [28]. Interviews were conducted between September and October 2025 in private meeting rooms within the hospital to support confidentiality and minimize interruptions; where shift demands prevented release, interviews were scheduled immediately before or after shifts in a quiet staff room.

The lead interviewer was a nursing academic trained in qualitative methods and not part of the participants’ managerial reporting line, which helped reduce social desirability bias and encourage candid accounts. Before each interview, brief demographic information was collected (age range, gender, unit, role, years of nursing experience, years in current unit, and typical exposure to emergency laparotomy cases). Field notes were documented after each interview to capture contextual details (e.g., unit pressures, interruptions avoided, nonverbal cues) and initial analytic reflections.

Interview structure and conduct

An interview guide was developed based on ERAS principles and implementation literature, then refined through consultation with two senior perioperative nurses to ensure clinical relevance and cultural appropriateness (see supplementary file 1). The guide was piloted with two nurses (not included in the final analysis) to optimize clarity, sequencing, and probing depth. Interviews began with broad prompts to establish participants’ roles and experiences, then moved to targeted questions about ERAS elements and recovery indicators in emergency laparotomy. Example prompts included:

Probes (e.g., “What happened next?”, “How did you decide?”, “What made that challenging?”) were used to deepen accounts and surface implicit reasoning. Interviews lasted 45–92 min (mean ~ 65 min). The interviewer used reflective summarizing during interviews to confirm understanding and to invite correction or elaboration. A total of 20 individual interviews were conducted, one per participant. No repeat or follow-up interviews were carried out; participant member reflection was conducted separately as part of the trustworthiness strategy (see Section "Rigour and reflexivity").

Recording and transcription

All interviews were audio-recorded with written permission. Recordings were transferred immediately to a password-protected, access-restricted university device and deleted from the recording device after secure transfer. Interviews were transcribed verbatim. For Arabic interviews, transcripts were prepared in Arabic first to preserve linguistic nuance, then translated into English for analysis and reporting where needed. Translation accuracy was supported through bilingual review by the research team, with attention to maintaining culturally embedded expressions and clinical terminology. A subset of transcripts was cross-checked against recordings to confirm fidelity and completeness. Specifically, eight transcripts (40% of the total dataset) were selected for cross-checking using a purposive approach that prioritized the longest interviews, interviews conducted in Arabic (which required bilingual translation), and the first three interviews (to verify transcriptionist accuracy early in the process). This subset was considered sufficient to confirm systemic transcription fidelity without duplicating the full verbatim process. No material discrepancies were identified during cross-checking.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach: familiarization, initial coding, generating themes, reviewing themes, defining/naming themes, and producing the report [29–31]. This method was selected because it supports systematic pattern identification while allowing attention to context, meaning, and variation across clinical areas. Data management was supported using Atlas.ti (a qualitative data management software program; ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany).

Coding began with line-by-line semantic coding to capture explicit content (e.g., “mobilization barriers,” “pain-control trade-offs,” “feeding readiness,” “communication with surgeons”), followed by more interpretive coding to identify underlying mechanisms (e.g., “negotiating protocol vs acuity,” “risk containment,” “nursing advocacy,” “micro-adaptations”). Themes were refined through iterative comparison across transcripts, constant movement between coded extracts and the full dataset, and development of a thematic map that linked ERAS elements to perceived recovery outcomes and contextual moderators (e.g., staffing, ICU transfer timing, patient stability, family engagement). Analytic memos documented decisions and emerging interpretations throughout. The analytic approach was primarily inductive: codes emerged from close reading of participants’ accounts rather than being imposed from an a priori coding framework. While the study aim was informed by awareness of ERAS implementation literature, this theoretical context was used to sensitize, not predetermine, the analytic process. Coding was conducted collaboratively across the team: the lead analyst (MAA) undertook initial coding independently, after which coded transcripts were reviewed by a second team member (MNA). Discrepancies in interpretation or coding were resolved through discussion among the full authorship team until consensus was reached. No formal inter-rater reliability coefficient was calculated, consistent with reflexive thematic analysis principles, which prioritize interpretive consensus over quantified agreement.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at King Faisal University, Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia(KFU-2026-ETHICS4166). All participants received an information sheet explaining study aims, voluntary participation, confidentiality measures, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. To protect anonymity, participants were assigned codes (e.g., N01, N14), and identifying details (unit-specific incidents, names, unique roles) were removed or generalized. Audio files and transcripts were stored on encrypted, password-protected devices accessible only to the research team, consistent with institutional data protection requirements.

Rigour and reflexivity

Trustworthiness was established using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria [32, 33]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the topic, iterative questioning, and member reflection, whereby a subset of participants reviewed brief thematic summaries for resonance and accuracy. Specifically, six participants (30% of the sample) were invited to review a one-page written summary of the four themes after preliminary thematic analysis was complete. Participants were selected to reflect variation in clinical unit, years of experience, and role (two from surgical wards, two from ICU, and two from HDU/PACU, spanning staff nurses and charge nurses). Written summaries were shared via email, and participants returned written comments or confirmed resonance within two weeks. All six participants confirmed that the themes reflected their experiences. Three participants provided minor clarifications regarding terminology and the distinction between ERAS delay due to clinical judgment versus resource constraints; these were incorporated into the final theme descriptions and illustrative narrative. No participants requested substantive revision or challenged the overall thematic structure. Dependability was supported by maintaining an audit trail (interview guide versions, recruitment logs, coding framework evolution, and analytic memos). Confirmability was strengthened through peer debriefing within the research team and documentation of analytic decisions. Transferability was supported by providing rich description of the Al-Ahsa tertiary-care context, emergency laparotomy care pathways, and unit-level influences on ERAS enactment.

Reflexivity was addressed through a reflexive journal maintained by the lead researcher to consider how professional background, assumptions about ERAS, and perceptions of “ideal recovery” might influence interviewing, coding, and interpretation. Regular team discussions were used to challenge premature closure and ensure themes remained grounded in participants’ accounts.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 20 registered nurses working in tertiary healthcare hospitals in the Al-Ahsa region, Saudi Arabia, participated in the study (Table 1). All participants had direct clinical experience caring for adult patients undergoing emergency laparotomy and were routinely involved in postoperative monitoring and recovery processes aligned with ERAS principles. Participants’ ages ranged from 24 to 47 years (M = 32.6, SD = 6.1). Clinical experience ranged from 2 to 22 years (M = 9.4, SD = 5.8), with between 1 and 15 years of experience specifically within surgical, high-dependency, or intensive care units managing emergency abdominal cases.

The sample included nurses from multiple postoperative care settings: surgical wards (n = 9), intensive care units (n = 6), high-dependency units (n = 3), and post-anesthesia care units (n = 2). This variation allowed exploration of ERAS implementation across different acuity levels and recovery phases. Twelve participants were female and eight were male. Most participants held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (n = 17), while three held a master’s degree.

Regarding professional roles, the majority were staff nurses (n = 14), four were charge nurses, and two held clinical educator or senior nurse positions. Exposure to ERAS-related protocols varied: eleven participants reported formal ERAS orientation or structured training sessions, while nine indicated that their knowledge was primarily acquired through unit-based experience and multidisciplinary discussions rather than standardized institutional training.

Participants described frequent involvement in ERAS-related components, including early mobilization (n = 20), multimodal pain management (n = 20), early enteral feeding (n = 18), fluid balance monitoring (n = 20), and early removal of invasive lines and tubes (n = 17). Most nurses (n=?) reported managing between 2 and 6 emergency laparotomy cases per month, depending on unit type and seasonal fluctuations in admission volume.

Thematic findings

Thematic analysis generated four interconnected themes that illuminate how nurses perceive the influence of ERAS protocols on postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy. These themes capture the dynamic interplay between protocol-driven care, clinical judgment, patient acuity, and contextual realities within tertiary emergency settings. Collectively, the findings reveal that nurses do not view ERAS as a rigid checklist but as a negotiated recovery framework - one that requires adaptation, advocacy, and relational engagement to translate into meaningful patient outcomes. Themes, sub-themes, and descriptions

ERAS as a structured recovery roadmap

This theme captures how nurses described ERAS as providing a coherent, goal-oriented structure within the inherently unstable context of emergency laparotomy. Participants consistently framed emergency abdominal surgery as “high-risk,” “unpredictable,” and “clinically volatile,” where patient trajectories could change rapidly within hours. In this environment, ERAS was perceived not merely as a protocol but as a recovery roadmap—a structured sequence of priorities that transformed postoperative care from reactive monitoring into proactive progression.

Creating predictability in a high-risk context

Participants described emergency laparotomy as one of the most clinically demanding surgical scenarios, frequently involving critically ill patients with uncertain prognoses. Within this high-risk environment, ERAS was perceived as introducing an element of predictability that reduced uncertainty for both staff and patients.

Nurses explained that having defined postoperative goals—such as early mobilization within 24 h, prompt removal of nasogastric tubes, and progression to oral intake—created shared expectations across multidisciplinary teams. This predictability fostered confidence and improved coordination.

In emergency cases, everything feels urgent and unstable. ERAS gives us something stable to hold onto. It tells us what we are aiming for. (RN14, ICU, 12 years).

In emergency cases, everything feels urgent and unstable. ERAS gives us something stable to hold onto. It tells us what we are aiming for. (RN14, ICU, 12 years).

Participants also noted that ERAS facilitated smoother handovers. Clear documentation of recovery milestones helped incoming staff understand what had been achieved and what remained pending. Several nurses described ERAS as a “common language” that aligned surgical, nursing, and anesthesia teams around recovery targets.

However, predictability was described as conditional rather than absolute. Nurses highlighted that emergency patients often required deviation from timelines due to pain, hypotension, or complications. In such situations, ERAS served as a reference point rather than an obligation.

We don’t follow it blindly. It guides us, but the patient condition decides the pace. (RN3, HDU, 6 years).

We don’t follow it blindly. It guides us, but the patient condition decides the pace. (RN3, HDU, 6 years).

Importantly, participants expressed that this structured predictability also benefited patients psychologically. When nurses explained recovery steps—why early movement was encouraged or why feeding was introduced sooner—patients appeared more engaged and less fearful.

When we tell them, ‘This is part of your recovery plan,’ they feel there is a plan. It reduces anxiety. (RN9, PACU, 4 years).

When we tell them, ‘This is part of your recovery plan,’ they feel there is a plan. It reduces anxiety. (RN9, PACU, 4 years).

Thus, ERAS was experienced as providing both clinical direction and emotional reassurance in a context typically marked by uncertainty.

Early mobilization and feeding as turning points

Among all ERAS components, early mobilization and early enteral feeding were most frequently described as visible and symbolic turning points in recovery. Nurses characterized these interventions as moments when patients transitioned from critical illness toward functional restoration.

Early mobilization was often described as the first tangible sign that a patient was stabilizing. Sitting out of bed, standing, or taking a few steps were interpreted not only as physical achievements but also as psychological breakthroughs.

The first time they sit in the chair, you see the difference. They look more alert, more hopeful. It’s like recovery starts moving. (RN1, Surgical Ward, 5 years)

The first time they sit in the chair, you see the difference. They look more alert, more hopeful. It’s like recovery starts moving. (RN1, Surgical Ward, 5 years)

However, mobilization was rarely straightforward. Participants described resistance due to pain, fear of wound dehiscence, dizziness, or fatigue. Nurses emphasized that achieving this milestone required encouragement, reassurance, and sometimes negotiation.

They are afraid the stitches will open. We explain, we support them physically, sometimes two nurses help. It’s teamwork. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

They are afraid the stitches will open. We explain, we support them physically, sometimes two nurses help. It’s teamwork. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

Similarly, early feeding was described as both clinically beneficial and emotionally significant. Nurses observed that tolerating oral fluids or light meals often marked a shift from dependency on invasive support toward physiological normalization.

When they start drinking and don’t vomit, it gives everyone confidence—the patient and us. (RN18, ICU, 11 years)

When they start drinking and don’t vomit, it gives everyone confidence—the patient and us. (RN18, ICU, 11 years)

Participants noted that early feeding required careful assessment of bowel sounds, nausea, and overall stability. In emergency cases, gastrointestinal dysfunction was common, requiring cautious progression. Nonetheless, nurses associated successful feeding initiation with reduced complications, improved energy levels, and faster mobilization.

Importantly, both mobilization and feeding were described as relational processes. Nurses invested time in explaining rationale, addressing misconceptions, and building trust. Without this relational engagement, ERAS milestones were more difficult to achieve.

Across narratives, early mobilization and feeding symbolized forward momentum. They were not merely checklist items but embodied markers of recovery progression. As one participant summarized:

Once they move and eat, you feel the recovery is real—not just numbers on the monitor. (RN12, ICU, 20 years)

Once they move and eat, you feel the recovery is real—not just numbers on the monitor. (RN12, ICU, 20 years)

Balancing protocol and patient instability

This theme reflects how nurses described navigating the tension between the structured expectations of ERAS protocols and the physiological instability characteristic of emergency laparotomy patients. While participants valued ERAS as a guiding framework, they consistently emphasized that emergency surgery rarely unfolds linearly or predictably. Hemodynamic fluctuations, sepsis, comorbidities, and postoperative complications frequently disrupted standardized timelines. As a result, nurses portrayed ERAS implementation not as rigid adherence, but as a process of continuous clinical calibration.

Adapting ERAS to clinical reality

Participants repeatedly emphasized that emergency laparotomy patients often arrived in critical condition, with sepsis, bowel perforation, shock, or multi-organ compromise. In such cases, strict adherence to early mobilization or feeding timelines was not always feasible. Nurses described modifying ERAS elements in response to blood pressure instability, oxygen requirements, ongoing pain, or delayed gastrointestinal function.

The protocol says mobilize early, but if the patient is on vasopressors, we cannot push them out of bed. Safety comes first. (RN12, ICU, 20 years)

The protocol says mobilize early, but if the patient is on vasopressors, we cannot push them out of bed. Safety comes first. (RN12, ICU, 20 years)

Adaptation was described not as abandoning ERAS, but as pacing it differently. Nurses spoke about delaying mobilization for several hours or a day until vital signs stabilized, or initiating feeding more gradually when nausea or ileus was suspected.

Sometimes we start with sips only, then observe carefully. ERAS is still there, but we go step by step. (RN3, HDU, 6 years)

Sometimes we start with sips only, then observe carefully. ERAS is still there, but we go step by step. (RN3, HDU, 6 years)

Participants also highlighted the unpredictability of pain levels in emergency cases. Unlike elective surgery, where patients are prepared and optimized preoperatively, emergency patients often experienced severe discomfort that hindered early movement. Nurses described intensifying pain management strategies before attempting mobilization.

If pain is not controlled, you cannot expect them to walk. We adjust analgesia first, then try again. (RN9, PACU, 4 years)

If pain is not controlled, you cannot expect them to walk. We adjust analgesia first, then try again. (RN9, PACU, 4 years)

These narratives demonstrate that ERAS functioned as a flexible scaffold rather than a rigid sequence. Nurses described interpreting patient cues—skin color, fatigue patterns, respiratory effort, wound appearance—before advancing recovery steps. Adaptation required vigilance and situational awareness.

Importantly, participants noted that deviations were sometimes misunderstood as lack of adherence by external auditors or documentation systems. However, from the nurses’ perspective, thoughtful modification was a marker of expertise rather than failure.

On paper, it may look delayed. But clinically, it was the right decision. (RN14, ICU, 12 years)

On paper, it may look delayed. But clinically, it was the right decision. (RN14, ICU, 12 years)

Thus, ERAS was enacted through dynamic adjustment, shaped by patient acuity and contextual realities.

Clinical judgment over checklist compliance

While ERAS introduced structured recovery milestones, nurses consistently emphasized that bedside assessment and professional judgment ultimately determined implementation timing. Participants resisted the notion of treating ERAS as a mechanical checklist detached from patient condition.

Several nurses described moments when documentation prompts encouraged early feeding or line removal, yet their clinical intuition suggested caution.

The system reminds us to remove the nasogastric tube early. But if I see abdominal distension and the patient looks uncomfortable, I wait and discuss with the team. (RN7, Surgical Ward, 8 years)

The system reminds us to remove the nasogastric tube early. But if I see abdominal distension and the patient looks uncomfortable, I wait and discuss with the team. (RN7, Surgical Ward, 8 years)

Experienced nurses, in particular, highlighted the importance of tacit knowledge—subtle indicators not always captured by formal criteria. They described noticing changes in breathing patterns, anxiety levels, or wound tension that influenced their decisions.

Sometimes the numbers are stable, but the patient doesn’t look ready. Experience tells you that. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

Sometimes the numbers are stable, but the patient doesn’t look ready. Experience tells you that. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

Participants framed clinical judgment as complementary to ERAS rather than oppositional. The protocol established goals, but judgment determined timing and sequencing. Younger nurses reported relying more heavily on ERAS checklists for reassurance, while senior nurses described integrating protocol guidance with experiential knowledge.

For junior staff, ERAS gives confidence. For senior nurses, it’s one tool among many. (RN2, ICU, 10 years)

For junior staff, ERAS gives confidence. For senior nurses, it’s one tool among many. (RN2, ICU, 10 years)

Importantly, nurses expressed concern that excessive emphasis on compliance metrics could inadvertently undermine individualized care. They cautioned against reducing recovery to documentation targets without contextual interpretation.

Recovery is not just ticking boxes. It’s about reading the patient. (RN11, Surgical Ward, 9 years)

Recovery is not just ticking boxes. It’s about reading the patient. (RN11, Surgical Ward, 9 years)

Despite these tensions, participants did not reject ERAS. Instead, they advocated for a balanced approach in which protocol adherence and clinical reasoning coexist. Successful recovery was described as emerging from this interplay rather than from rigid compliance.

Nursing advocacy as the engine of ERAS

This theme reflects how nurses positioned themselves as the central driving force behind the practical implementation of ERAS in emergency laparotomy care. While ERAS protocols are institutionally endorsed and multidisciplinary in design, participants consistently described nursing advocacy as the “engine” that sustains recovery momentum. Nurses portrayed themselves as continuity figures across shifts, units, and rotating surgical teams—monitoring progress, prompting action, and reinforcing recovery goals when competing priorities threatened to delay them.

Negotiating with the multidisciplinary team

Participants frequently described ERAS implementation as a collaborative yet sometimes contested process. While most surgical teams supported ERAS principles in theory, differences in risk tolerance, communication styles, and clinical preferences occasionally created tension. Nurses described their role as mediating between protocol recommendations and team hesitations.

One recurring example involved early removal of nasogastric tubes or urinary catheters. Nurses reported assessing readiness and prompting discussions with physicians when they believed removal would support mobilization and comfort.

Sometimes the surgeon is cautious and prefers to keep the tube longer. We present our observations—‘The output is minimal, the abdomen is soft’—and suggest reviewing it. (RN14, ICU, 12 years)

Sometimes the surgeon is cautious and prefers to keep the tube longer. We present our observations—‘The output is minimal, the abdomen is soft’—and suggest reviewing it. (RN14, ICU, 12 years)

Similarly, decisions about advancing oral intake were often negotiated collaboratively. Nurses described presenting objective data—bowel sounds, absence of nausea, stable vitals—to advocate for earlier feeding initiation.

We monitor closely, so we see when the patient is ready. We share that with the team to move forward. (RN3, HDU, 6 years)

We monitor closely, so we see when the patient is ready. We share that with the team to move forward. (RN3, HDU, 6 years)

In some cases, advocacy involved addressing delays caused by workflow fragmentation. For example, physiotherapy referrals or mobilization plans could be postponed due to scheduling constraints. Nurses described stepping in to initiate mobilization when safe.

If physio is delayed, we don’t wait. We start sitting the patient out of bed ourselves. (RN1, Surgical Ward, 5 years)

If physio is delayed, we don’t wait. We start sitting the patient out of bed ourselves. (RN1, Surgical Ward, 5 years)

Participants emphasized that effective advocacy required relational trust. Units where interdisciplinary communication was open and respectful facilitated smoother ERAS implementation. In contrast, hierarchical barriers could slow recovery progression.

When there is mutual respect, ERAS works better. If communication is rigid, everything slows down. (RN12, ICU, 20 years)

When there is mutual respect, ERAS works better. If communication is rigid, everything slows down. (RN12, ICU, 20 years)

Through these negotiations, nurses described themselves as safeguarding recovery trajectories—ensuring that ERAS principles were not overlooked amid competing clinical priorities.

Educating and motivating patients

Beyond interdisciplinary negotiation, participants highlighted patient education and motivation as fundamental to ERAS success. Emergency laparotomy patients often experienced fear, fatigue, and uncertainty, which could limit engagement in early mobilization or feeding. Nurses described investing significant emotional and communicative effort to encourage participation.

They are scared to move. They think something will tear inside. We explain why movement prevents complications. (RN6, Surgical Ward, 7 years)

They are scared to move. They think something will tear inside. We explain why movement prevents complications. (RN6, Surgical Ward, 7 years)

Education was framed as both informational and relational. Nurses explained the rationale behind ERAS elements in simple, reassuring language—connecting early mobilization to reduced pneumonia risk or early feeding to faster bowel recovery. This explanation transformed recovery tasks into purposeful actions.

When they understand it’s for their benefit—not just hospital rules—they cooperate more. (RN10, HDU, 11 years)

When they understand it’s for their benefit—not just hospital rules—they cooperate more. (RN10, HDU, 11 years)

Motivation often required repeated encouragement. Participants described using incremental goals—first sitting at the edge of the bed, then standing, then walking short distances. Celebrating small achievements reinforced patient confidence.

We tell them, ‘Today just sit in the chair. Tomorrow we walk a few steps.’ Small steps build courage. (RN9, PACU, 4 years)

We tell them, ‘Today just sit in the chair. Tomorrow we walk a few steps.’ Small steps build courage. (RN9, PACU, 4 years)

Family involvement also emerged as an important facilitator. Nurses described engaging relatives to support mobilization efforts and reinforce nutritional intake, particularly in culturally family-centered contexts.

When the family encourages them, they feel stronger. It makes a big difference. (RN18, ICU, 11 years)

When the family encourages them, they feel stronger. It makes a big difference. (RN18, ICU, 11 years)

However, participants noted that motivational work was time-intensive. In busy units, sustained encouragement was challenging. Nevertheless, nurses viewed this relational investment as central to recovery.

You cannot implement ERAS without talking to the patient. It’s not just clinical—it’s motivational. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

You cannot implement ERAS without talking to the patient. It’s not just clinical—it’s motivational. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

Across narratives, patient engagement was described as the point where ERAS moved from protocol to lived practice. Without education and emotional support, checklist elements risked becoming mechanical and ineffective.

System constraints shaping implementation

This theme captures how nurses situated ERAS implementation within broader organizational and systemic realities. While participants consistently endorsed the value of ERAS in supporting postoperative recovery after emergency laparotomy, they emphasized that its enactment was strongly shaped - sometimes limited - by structural factors beyond individual control. Staffing levels, workload intensity, unit culture, and variability in surgical leadership were described as powerful determinants of how consistently ERAS principles could be applied.

Staffing and workload pressures

Participants consistently identified staffing levels and workload intensity as central challenges affecting ERAS implementation. Emergency laparotomy patients often required close monitoring, frequent reassessment, assistance with mobilization, and sustained encouragement—all of which were time-intensive. When nurse-to-patient ratios were high or units were experiencing surges in admissions, ERAS activities were sometimes delayed despite nurses’ intentions.

Early mobilization takes time - sometimes two nurses. If we have many unstable patients, it becomes difficult to prioritize. (RN6, Surgical Ward, 7 years)

Early mobilization takes time - sometimes two nurses. If we have many unstable patients, it becomes difficult to prioritize. (RN6, Surgical Ward, 7 years)

Several nurses explained that mobilizing a postoperative patient safely required coordination, equipment preparation, and monitoring for dizziness or hypotension. In under-resourced shifts, these processes were postponed until staffing allowed.

We want to follow ERAS, but if you have three critical patients and one emergency admission, you focus first on immediate risks. (RN2, ICU, 10 years)

We want to follow ERAS, but if you have three critical patients and one emergency admission, you focus first on immediate risks. (RN2, ICU, 10 years)

Workload pressures also influenced documentation and communication. Nurses described situations where ERAS milestones were achieved but not thoroughly documented due to time constraints, creating discrepancies between practice and recorded compliance.

Sometimes we do the mobilization, but documentation is delayed because we are busy. It may look like we didn’t follow ERAS. (RN13, Surgical Ward, 8 years)

Sometimes we do the mobilization, but documentation is delayed because we are busy. It may look like we didn’t follow ERAS. (RN13, Surgical Ward, 8 years)

In addition to staffing numbers, participants highlighted skill mix as important. Units with experienced nurses were perceived as better equipped to implement ERAS efficiently, whereas frequent rotation of junior staff required additional supervision and slowed recovery processes.

When the team is experienced, ERAS flows naturally. When there are many new staff, everything takes longer. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

When the team is experienced, ERAS flows naturally. When there are many new staff, everything takes longer. (RN16, Surgical Ward, 15 years)

Despite these constraints, nurses described making deliberate efforts to preserve ERAS principles whenever feasible. However, they acknowledged that sustained high workload environments risked reducing ERAS to a secondary priority rather than an integrated recovery philosophy.

Variability in surgical and institutional support

Beyond staffing pressures, participants described variability in surgical and institutional endorsement of ERAS as a significant influence on implementation consistency. While some surgical teams actively championed early mobilization, feeding, and catheter removal, others adopted more conservative or individualized approaches.

Some surgeons are very proactive—they ask about mobilization and feeding every day. Others prefer a slower approach. (RN14, ICU, 12 years)

Some surgeons are very proactive—they ask about mobilization and feeding every day. Others prefer a slower approach. (RN14, ICU, 12 years)

This variability sometimes created uncertainty among nursing staff regarding how assertively to advance recovery milestones. Nurses described navigating differing preferences while attempting to maintain ERAS-aligned care.

We adjust depending on the surgeon. The protocol is there, but practice changes according to leadership. (RN3, HDU, 6 years)

We adjust depending on the surgeon. The protocol is there, but practice changes according to leadership. (RN3, HDU, 6 years)

Institutional culture also emerged as influential. Units where ERAS was integrated into orientation programs, electronic documentation prompts, and regular audit discussions were perceived as more consistent in application. Conversely, in areas where ERAS training was informal or inconsistently reinforced, implementation relied heavily on individual initiative.

If ERAS is embedded in the system—like reminders in the chart—it becomes routine. If not, it depends on who is on shift. (RN9, PACU, 4 years)

If ERAS is embedded in the system—like reminders in the chart—it becomes routine. If not, it depends on who is on shift. (RN9, PACU, 4 years)

Participants also noted that visible support from hospital leadership strengthened commitment. When recovery outcomes were discussed in quality meetings and ERAS adherence was recognized, nurses felt their efforts were validated.

When management talks about recovery outcomes and supports ERAS, it motivates us. It feels like a shared goal. (RN18, ICU, 11 years)

When management talks about recovery outcomes and supports ERAS, it motivates us. It feels like a shared goal. (RN18, ICU, 11 years)

Conversely, absence of institutional reinforcement could weaken sustainability. Nurses described initial enthusiasm following ERAS rollouts that gradually diminished without ongoing monitoring or structured feedback.

At the beginning, there was training and attention. Over time, without follow-up, practices slowly return to old habits. (RN7, Surgical Ward, 8 years)

At the beginning, there was training and attention. Over time, without follow-up, practices slowly return to old habits. (RN7, Surgical Ward, 8 years)

Thus, ERAS implementation was described as deeply influenced by leadership alignment, interprofessional consensus, and system-level integration rather than solely by bedside intention.

Conceptual model: the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model

The findings informed the development of the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model, which conceptualizes how ERAS protocols influence postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy within tertiary care settings (Fig. 1). The model was developed inductively through the thematic analysis process: as the four themes were progressively defined and named, the research team identified conceptual relationships among them that warranted visual representation. Specifically, the team observed that the themes were not independent but formed a coherent explanatory structure in which (1) ERAS protocol structure provides the directional scaffold; (2) patient clinical instability creates adaptive demands on that scaffold; (3) nursing advocacy and clinical judgment serve as the active mediating layer translating the protocol into individualized care; and (4) system and organizational context moderates the overall process. This thematic map was discussed, refined, and agreed upon across the full authorship team. The model was alsodiscussed in relation to the member reflection process: the six participants who reviewed written thematic summaries (not the visual model diagram itself) confirmed that the themes accurately represented their clinical experiences; as the model was inductively derived from these themes, participant endorsement of the thematic structure provides indirect support for the model’s conceptual representativeness. It is important to note that the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model represents a preliminary conceptual framework derived from qualitative findings in a single regional context. It has not yet been empirically validated and should be understood as a theoretically informed tool to guide future inquiry and implementation design rather than a tested predictive model. The model positions ERAS as a structured recovery framework that provides direction through standardized milestones such as early mobilization, early feeding, multimodal analgesia, and optimized line management. However, ERAS does not function through rigid adherence. Instead, its influence unfolds through dynamic interaction with patient clinical instability, including hemodynamic fluctuations, sepsis, pain variability, and comorbidities common in emergency surgical contexts. These factors necessitate adaptation rather than strict protocol compliance.

At the center of the model is nursing advocacy and clinical judgment, which mediate the translation of ERAS principles into individualized care. Nurses interpret patient readiness, negotiate multidisciplinary decisions, adjust pacing of recovery milestones, and motivate patient engagement. Through bedside assessment and relational support, they ensure safety while sustaining recovery momentum. Without this mediating role, ERAS risks becoming procedural rather than responsive.

The broader system and organizational context - including staffing levels, workload pressures, surgical leadership support, and institutional reinforcement - acts as a moderating influence, either strengthening or constraining implementation consistency. Postoperative recovery outcomes therefore emerge not from protocol adherence alone, but from the adaptive interplay among structured ERAS guidance, patient instability, nursing mediation, and systemic conditions. This model reframes ERAS implementation in emergency laparotomy as a context-sensitive, nurse-driven process embedded within complex healthcare systems. Throughout this manuscript, the term “implementation” is used as the primary descriptor for the process by which ERAS protocols are applied in clinical practice. Where terms such as “enactment,” “operationalization,” or “translation” appear, they refer specifically to the situated, adaptive dimensions of that process — i.e., how nurses actively interpret and apply ERAS in response to individual patient conditions and contextual constraints — rather than constituting distinct theoretical constructs. The model was developed inductively, emerging directly from the four themes generated through reflexive thematic analysis rather than being derived from or mapped onto pre-existing theoretical frameworks. Following finalization of the thematic structure, the research team engaged in a series of analytic discussions to identify the conceptual relationships among themes. These discussions established that the themes were not independent constructs but formed an interrelated system: ERAS protocols provide a structural foundation (Theme 1); patient instability creates adaptive pressure (Theme 2); nursing advocacy and clinical judgment constitute the central mediating mechanism (Theme 3); and systemic and organizational factors function as contextual moderators (Theme 4). These relationships were depicted in a diagrammatic model that was reviewed, refined, and agreed upon by all authors through iterative team discussion. The model was not subjected to external participant validation beyond the member reflection process described in section "Rigour and reflexivity". Accordingly, it is presented as a preliminary conceptual framework derived from qualitative findings in one regional context and has not yet been empirically validated across settings.

Discussion

This study explored nurses’ perspectives on the influence of ERAS protocols on postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy in tertiary healthcare settings. The findings reveal that ERAS is perceived not as a rigid compliance framework but as a structured yet adaptive recovery pathway mediated by nursing advocacy and shaped by system-level conditions. The development of the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model offers a conceptual contribution by reframing ERAS implementation in emergency contexts as a dynamic, nurse-driven, and context-sensitive process rather than a linear protocol application.

Consistent with existing literature, participants recognized ERAS as a structured recovery roadmap that enhances coordination and promotes measurable postoperative milestones such as early mobilization and early feeding [34]. Previous quantitative studies have demonstrated that ERAS pathways reduce complications, shorten length of stay, and improve functional outcomes in elective surgery [35]. However, emergency laparotomy presents greater physiological instability and unpredictability, limiting direct translation of elective models [36]. Our findings extend this evidence by illustrating how nurses operationalize ERAS principles in high-acuity contexts where patient trajectories are often nonlinear.

A key contribution of this study lies in highlighting the mediating role of nursing advocacy and clinical judgment. While ERAS provides standardized recovery targets, its effective enactment depends on nurses’ ability to interpret patient readiness, negotiate interdisciplinary decisions, and adapt pacing according to evolving clinical conditions. This aligns with implementation science literature emphasizing that successful adoption of evidence-based interventions requires active sense-making and relational work at the frontline [37]. In emergency laparotomy, where patients frequently present with sepsis, hemodynamic instability, or multiple comorbidities, strict protocol adherence without contextual interpretation may compromise safety [38]. Nurses in this study described adaptation not as deviation but as responsible clinical calibration, reinforcing the importance of professional expertise in sustaining recovery momentum.

Early mobilization and early enteral feeding emerged as particularly powerful turning points in recovery. Participants described these interventions as both physiological milestones and psychological catalysts, signaling a transition from acute crisis toward restoration. This perception is supported by evidence linking early mobilization to reduced pulmonary complications and improved functional independence [39], and early feeding to enhanced gastrointestinal recovery and reduced infection risk [40]. However, our findings underscore that achieving these milestones in emergency settings requires substantial motivational effort, reassurance, and interdisciplinary coordination—tasks primarily led by nurses.

The model further demonstrates that ERAS implementation is moderated by system-level factors, including staffing adequacy, workload intensity, and variability in surgical leadership support. These findings resonate with broader research indicating that organizational culture and resource availability significantly influence the sustainability of perioperative care pathways [41]. Nurses described workload pressures and skill mix variability as barriers to consistent ERAS enactment, particularly when mobilization required additional personnel or when documentation systems failed to reinforce recovery targets. Without structural anchoring - such as electronic prompts, audit feedback, or leadership endorsement - ERAS risks becoming dependent on individual initiative rather than institutional integration.

Importantly, the findings also illuminate interprofessional dynamics in emergency surgical care. While ERAS is inherently multidisciplinary, nurses frequently assumed the role of continuity agents across rotating surgical teams [42]. Their advocacy - through presenting clinical observations, prompting tube removal discussions, or initiating mobilization when physiotherapy was delayed - was central to maintaining recovery progression. This aligns with literature recognizing nurses as key implementation leaders in complex care environments [43]. However, variability in surgeon preferences and hierarchical communication structures sometimes constrained this role, highlighting the need for shared governance and collaborative cultures to optimize ERAS sustainability [44].

The Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model, therefore, conceptualizes postoperative recovery as an emergent outcome arising from the interaction between structured protocol guidance, patient instability, nursing mediation, and organizational context. This perspective aligns with complexity-informed approaches to healthcare implementation, which argue that interventions evolve through adaptive interactions rather than fixed linear pathways [45, 46]. In emergency laparotomy, recovery cannot be reduced to compliance metrics; rather, it reflects negotiated, relational, and context-dependent processes. While the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model was inductively derived rather than mapped onto a pre-existing framework, it bears conceptual resonance with established implementation theories such as Normalization Process Theory (NPT) and the PARIHS framework, both of which foreground the roles of context, relational work, and professional sense-making in implementation success; the Adaptive ERAS Model extends these perspectives by centering patient clinical instability as a distinct, dynamic moderator specific to emergency surgical settings. It is important to acknowledge that the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model represents a preliminary conceptual contribution grounded in qualitative findings from a single regional context. It has not been empirically validated, and its transferability to other healthcare systems with differing institutional cultures, staffing structures, or ERAS maturity levels remains to be established. Future research should empirically test and refine the model’s components and their interrelationships across diverse settings. Notably, the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model represents a preliminary conceptual framework derived from qualitative findings within a single regional context. As such, it has not yet been validated through quantitative testing or replication across diverse healthcare systems. Future empirical work should be directed toward testing the model’s components, examining whether the identified relationships among protocol structure, patient instability, nursing mediation, and organizational context predict measurable postoperative outcomes in broader and more varied settings [47, 48].

Implications for practice

A note on terminology: throughout this manuscript, the term “implementation” is used as the primary descriptor for the process by which ERAS protocols are put into practice at the bedside. Where the terms “enactment,” “translation,” or “operationalization” appear, they are used as contextually appropriate synonyms to avoid repetition and do not signal distinct theoretical constructs. All such terms refer consistently to the practical, real-world application of ERAS protocol elements by nurses in emergency surgical care contexts.

The findings have several implications for nursing practice and perioperative service design. First, ERAS training programs in emergency settings should explicitly incorporate adaptive decision-making frameworks, emphasizing clinical judgment rather than checklist compliance. Second, leadership strategies should address staffing adequacy and skill mix optimization to ensure feasibility of early mobilization and feeding targets. Third, integrating ERAS prompts into electronic documentation systems may strengthen continuity across shifts and reduce reliance on memory-based adherence. Finally, fostering interdisciplinary dialogue and shared accountability may enhance consistency in recovery pacing across surgical teams.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides in-depth qualitative insights from nurses working in tertiary emergency surgical care, offering a contextualized understanding of ERAS implementation in a Middle Eastern healthcare setting. The inclusion of participants across surgical wards, ICU, HDU, and PACU enhanced transferability across postoperative phases. However, the study was conducted within a single regional context, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems with different staffing models or institutional cultures. Additionally, perspectives from surgeons and allied health professionals were not included; future research incorporating multidisciplinary viewpoints could provide a more comprehensive understanding of ERAS enactment.

Future research

Future studies may quantitatively test elements of the Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model, examining how staffing ratios, interdisciplinary communication quality, or nursing experience levels moderate recovery outcomes in emergency laparotomy. Longitudinal and mixed-methods designs could further evaluate how institutional reinforcement strategies influence ERAS sustainability over time.

Conclusion

This study provides qualitative insight into how nurses perceive the influence of ERAS protocols on postoperative recovery following emergency laparotomy in tertiary care settings. The findings demonstrate that ERAS is not enacted as a rigid checklist but as a structured yet adaptable recovery framework shaped by patient instability, nursing clinical judgment, and organizational conditions. Nurses emerged as central mediators of ERAS implementation, translating protocol milestones into individualized care through advocacy, interdisciplinary negotiation, and patient engagement.

The Adaptive ERAS Implementation Model developed from these findings conceptualizes recovery as an emergent outcome of dynamic interaction among protocol structure, clinical variability, nursing expertise, and system-level support. In high-acuity emergency contexts, successful ERAS implementation depends not only on guideline availability but on sustained nursing leadership, adequate staffing, and institutional reinforcement.

Optimizing postoperative recovery after emergency laparotomy therefore requires recognition of nursing advocacy as a critical driver of recovery progression and investment in organizational structures that enable adaptive, context-sensitive implementation. Strengthening these elements may enhance consistency, safety, and patient-centered recovery in complex surgical environments.
