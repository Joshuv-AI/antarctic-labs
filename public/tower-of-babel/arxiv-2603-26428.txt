Classical and continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distances
K. V. Semenov, A. A. Tuzhilin

Abstract
Starting from the definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance via distortion of correspondences, we add the requirement of semicontinuity of each correspondence and its inverse. It turns out that in the case of lower semicontinuity we obtain the same classical Gromov–Hausdorff distance, while for upper semicontinuity we are able to prove coincidence with the classical one only in cases where the spaces are either totally bounded or boundedly compact. Keywords : metric spaces, Hausdorff distance, Gromov–Hausdorff distance, set-valued maps, lower semicontinuous and upper semicontinuous set-valued maps.



Introduction

The famous Gromov–Hausdorff distance
[
1
,
2
]
measures the degree of non-isometry of metric spaces: for isometric spaces the distance is zero, and the more “dissimilar” the spaces are, the larger this distance is. In the classical definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, additional structures that metric spaces may be equipped with are not taken into account. Even the topology induced by the metric is ignored by this distance. In
[
3
]
, a modification of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance that takes continuity into account was proposed. It was noted that when comparing spheres in Euclidean space equipped with the standard intrinsic metric using the classical Gromov–Hausdorff distance, the result differs from comparison using the continuous analogue of this distance. In
[
4
]
, another version of the continuous Gromov–Hausdorff distance was proposed for comparing dynamical systems and solutions of partial differential equations. However, the approach of these authors leads to a significant complication of the technique, since their version of the distance does not satisfy the triangle inequality.

We consider several modifications of the classical Gromov–Hausdorff distance that take continuity into account in one way or another. Namely, in the definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance via distortion of correspondences, we will additionally require that these correspondences and their inverses, which are essentially surjective set-valued maps, be either lower semicontinuous or upper semicontinuous. We will show that under additional assumptions the result is the same Gromov–Hausdorff distance. Thus, we narrow the family of correspondences, which, we hope, may in some cases simplify the computation of the classical Gromov–Hausdorff distance between specific classes of metric spaces.

Basic definitions and preliminary results

We provide the definitions and results necessary for what follows; details can be found in
[

]
, see also
[

]
. For convenience, the distance between points
x
and

will be denoted by
|xy|
.

Let
X
be an arbitrary metric space,
x\in X
, and

and

be real numbers. Denote by
U_{r}(x)=\bigl\{y\in X:|xy|<r\bigr\}

|xA|=|Ax|=\inf\bigl\{|xa|:a\in A\bigr\}
and
|AB|=|BA|=\inf\bigl\{|ab|:a\in A,\,b\in B\bigr\}
. Next, define the
open
r
-neighborhood
and

s
-neighborhood of a set

by setting respectively

U_{r}(A)=\bigl\{x\in X:|xA|<r\bigr\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ B_{s}(A)=\bigl\{x\in X:|xA|\leq s\bigr\}.

Definition 1.1
.

For nonempty subsets
A
and
B
of a metric space

, the
Hausdorff distance from

to

is the quantity

d_{H}(A,B)=\max\bigl\{\sup_{a\in A}|aB|,\,\sup_{b\in B}|Ab|\bigr\}=\\
=\inf\bigl\{r>0:A\subset U_{r}(B)\ \text{and}\ U_{r}(A)\supset B\bigr\}=\\
=\inf\bigl\{s\geq 0:A\subset B_{s}(B)\ \text{and}\ B_{s}(A)\supset B\bigr\}.

If

and

are isometric metric spaces, we denote this fact by
X\approx Y
.

Definition 1.2
.

The
Gromov–Hausdorff distance
between nonempty metric spaces

is the quantity

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf\bigl\{d_{H}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}):X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\subset Z,\,X^{\prime}\approx X,\,Y^{\prime}\approx Y\bigr\},

where the infimum is taken over all metric spaces

and all isometric embeddings of

and

into

.

Definition
1.2
is ill-suited for concrete computations. There is an equivalent definition given in terms of correspondences. We provide the necessary concepts and results.

For an arbitrary set

, denote by
\mathcal{P}_{0}(Z)
the family of all nonempty subsets of
Z
. In particular,
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
is the set of all nonempty relations between
X
and
Y
.

Definition 1.3
.

For any nonempty metric spaces
X
,
Y
and a (nonempty) relation
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
, its

\operatorname{dis}\sigma=\sup\Bigl\{\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|:(x,y),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\sigma\Bigr\}.

The following results follow immediately from the definition of distortion.

Proposition 1.4
.

If
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
and
\tau\subset\sigma
, then
\operatorname{dis}\tau\leq\operatorname{dis}\sigma
.

Proposition 1.5
.

If
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X\times Y)
,
\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(Y\times Z)
and
\tau\circ\sigma\neq\emptyset
, then
\operatorname{dis}(\tau\circ\sigma)\leq\operatorname{dis}\sigma+\operatorname{dis}\tau
.

Recall that a
set-valued map
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
from a set

is any map

[
7
]

In what follows we will identify a set-valued map

with the subset
\cup_{x\in X}\bigl(\{x\}\times f(x)\bigr)
in
X\times Y
.

A set-valued map

if
\cup_{x\in X}f(x)=Y
. A relation
R\subset X\times Y
between sets
X
and
Y

R\colon x\mapsto R(x)=\{y\in Y:(x,y)\in R\}

. The set of all correspondences between

and

is denoted by
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
5
]
)
.

For any nonempty metric spaces
X
and
Y
,

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

Proposition 1.7
.

Nonempty metric spaces
X
and
Y
are isometric if and only if there exists
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
such that
\operatorname{dis}R=0
. All such

are exactly all isometries between
X
and
Y
(we identify maps and their graphs).

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty metric spaces,
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
and
{\bar{R}}\subset X\times Y
be the closure of
R
. Then
\operatorname{dis}{\bar{R}}=\operatorname{dis}R
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{dis}R=\infty
, then also
\operatorname{dis}{\bar{R}}=\infty
, since
R\subset{\bar{R}}
, and in this case the equalities are proved.

Now suppose
\operatorname{dis}R<\infty
. Choose arbitrary
(x,y),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\bar{R}}
, then for each
\varepsilon>0
there exist
(x_{1},y_{1}),\,(x^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{1})\in R
such that
|xx_{1}|<\varepsilon
,
|yy_{1}|<\varepsilon
,
|x^{\prime}x^{\prime}_{1}|<\varepsilon
,
|y^{\prime}y^{\prime}_{1}|<\varepsilon
, but then
|x_{1}x^{\prime}_{1}|-2\varepsilon<|xx^{\prime}|<|x_{1}x^{\prime}_{1}|+2\varepsilon
and
|y_{1}y^{\prime}_{1}|-2\varepsilon<|yy^{\prime}|<|y_{1}y^{\prime}_{1}|+2\varepsilon
, therefore

\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|<\bigl||x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}|-|y_{1}y^{\prime}_{1}|\bigr|+4\varepsilon,

whence

\operatorname{dis}{\bar{R}}=\sup_{(x,y),\,(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\bar{R}}}\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|\leq\sup_{(x_{1},y_{1}),\,(x^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{1})\in R}\bigl||x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}|-|y_{1}y_{1}^{\prime}|\bigr|+4\varepsilon=\operatorname{dis}R+4\varepsilon,

and since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
\operatorname{dis}{\bar{R}}\leq\operatorname{dis}R
. But
R\subset{\bar{R}}
, so we also have the opposite inequality, and with it the desired equality.
∎

Denote by
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(X,Y)
the set of all closed correspondences
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
. From Proposition
1.8
and Theorem
1.6
we immediately obtain the following result.

Corollary 1.9
.

For any nonempty metric spaces
X
and
Y
,

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

Proposition 1.10
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty metric spaces,
\varepsilon>0
,
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
and
R_{\varepsilon}=U_{\varepsilon}(R)\subset X\times Y
. Then
\operatorname{dis}R=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{dis}R=\infty
, then also
\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}=\infty
for all
\varepsilon
, since
R\subset R_{\varepsilon}
, and in this case the equalities are proved. If
\operatorname{dis}R<\infty
, then
\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}
is a monotonically increasing function of
\varepsilon
, so
\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}
. The rest repeats verbatim the proof of Proposition
1.8
.
∎

We recall the necessary definitions from the theory of set-valued maps. Details can be found in
[
7
]
.

Let
X
and
Y
be topological spaces, then a set-valued map
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
is called

•

upper semicontinuous at a point
x\in X
if for any neighborhood
U^{f(x)}
of
f(x)
there exists a neighborhood
V^{x}
of
x
such that for all
x^{\prime}\in V^{x}
we have
f(x^{\prime})\subset U^{f(x)}
, i.e.
f(V^{x})\subset U^{f(x)}
;

•

lower semicontinuous at a point
x\in X
if for any open set
U\subset Y
such that
f(x_{0})\cap U\neq\emptyset
, there exists a neighborhood
V^{x}
of
x
satisfying the following condition: for all
x^{\prime}\in V^{x}
we have
f(x^{\prime})\cap U\neq\emptyset
;

•

continuous at a point
x\in X
if it is both upper and lower semicontinuous at
x
.

A set-valued map is
upper semicontinuous, lower semicontinuous, or continuous
if the corresponding condition holds for all points
x\in X
.

Remark 1.11
.

If
f
is a single-valued map, each of the three definitions just given is equivalent to ordinary continuity at a point. Thus, every continuous single-valued map is upper semicontinuous, lower semicontinuous, and continuous in the sense of set-valued maps. In particular, the identity map, considered as a set-valued map, is continuous.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
and
g\colon Y\rightrightarrows Z
be set-valued maps that are either both upper semicontinuous, both lower semicontinuous, or both continuous. Then their composition is also such.

Proof.

First, let
f
and
g
be upper semicontinuous. Take an arbitrary point
x\in X
and consider a neighborhood
U:=U^{g(f(x))}
, then for each point
y\in f(x)
we have
g(y)\subset g(f(x))\subset U
, so
U
is also a neighborhood of
g(y)
. Since
g
is upper semicontinuous at
y
, there exists a neighborhood
V^{y}
such that
g(V^{y})\subset U
. Set
V=\cup_{y\in f(x)}V^{y}
, then
g(V)\subset U
, and
V
is a neighborhood of
f(x)
, therefore, by the upper semicontinuity of
f
, there exists a neighborhood
W^{x}
such that
f(W^{x})\subset V
, whence
g\bigl(f(W^{x})\bigr)\subset U
, so
g\circ f
is upper semicontinuous at
x
and, by the arbitrariness of
x
, upper semicontinuous overall.

Now let
f
and
g
be lower semicontinuous. Take an arbitrary point
x\in X
and consider an open set
U\subset Z
such that
g\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\cap U\neq\emptyset
. Since
g\bigl(f(x)\bigr)=\cup_{y\in f(x)}g(y)
, there exist
y\in f(x)
for which
g(y)\cap U\neq\emptyset
. Denote the set of such
y
by
Y^{\prime}
. Since
g
is lower semicontinuous, for each
y\in Y^{\prime}
there exists a neighborhood
V^{y}
such that for all
y^{\prime}\in V^{y}
we have
g(y^{\prime})\cap U\neq\emptyset
. Set
V=\cup_{y\in Y^{\prime}}V^{y}
, then
V
is an open set, and for all
y^{\prime}\in V
we have
g(y^{\prime})\cap U\neq\emptyset
and, moreover, since
\emptyset\neq Y^{\prime}\subset f(x)
and
Y^{\prime}\subset V
, we have
f(x)\cap V\neq\emptyset
. Since
f
is lower semicontinuous, there exists a neighborhood
W^{x}
such that for all
x^{\prime}\in W^{x}
we have
f(x^{\prime})\cap V\neq\emptyset
. But then
g\bigl(f(x^{\prime})\bigr)\cap U\neq\emptyset
for all
x^{\prime}\in W^{x}
, so
W^{x}
satisfies the lower semicontinuity condition for
g\circ f
at
x
, and, by the arbitrariness of
x
, this map is lower semicontinuous overall.

For continuous set-valued maps, the proof is obtained by concatenating the two previous items.
∎

Proposition 1.13
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty topological spaces,
Z\subset Y
be a nonempty subset, and
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
be an upper semicontinuous (lower semicontinuous, continuous) set-valued map. Then the restriction
f|_{Z}
is also such.

Proof.

In the definition of semicontinuity of
f
at a point
x\in X
, it is asserted that if some property holds for
f(x)
, then there exists a neighborhood
U^{x}
such that the same property also holds for
f(x^{\prime})
for all points
x^{\prime}\in U^{x}
. But for
f|_{Z}
, semicontinuity requires the property to hold for all
x^{\prime}\in U^{x}\cap Z
, which, of course, also holds.
∎

For metric spaces
X
and
Y
, set

•

\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)=\{R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y):\text{$R,\,R^{-1}$ are upper semicontinuous}\}
;

•

\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)=\{R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y):\text{$R,\,R^{-1}$ are lower semicontinuous}\}
;

•

\mathcal{R}_{rc}(X,Y)=\{R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y):\text{$R,\,R^{-1}$ are continuous}\}
.

The following obvious inclusions hold:

(1)

\mathcal{R}(X,Y)\supset\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)\cup\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)\supset\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)\cap\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)=\mathcal{R}_{rc}(X,Y).

Many of the facts presented below hold simultaneously for all the families of correspondences introduced above, so for brevity, when referring to one of these families, we will denote it by
\mathcal{R}_{gen}
, i.e.
\mathcal{R}_{gen}\in\{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}_{us},\mathcal{R}_{ls},\mathcal{R}_{rc}\}
. For example, we prove the following general result.

Proposition 1.14
.

Each family
\mathcal{R}_{gen}
is nonempty.

Proof.

Note that the correspondence
R:=X\times Y
is continuous, so
\mathcal{R}_{rc}(X,Y)
, and together with it, by inclusions (
1
),
\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)
,
\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)
and
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
are always nonempty.
∎

Furthermore, Remark
1.11
and Proposition
1.12
imply

Corollary 1.15
.

For any nonempty metric spaces
X
,
Y
and
Z
,

•

if
\nu\colon X\to Y
is a homeomorphism (in particular, an isometry), then

\nu\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)\cap\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)\cap\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)\cap\mathcal{R}_{rc}(X,Y);

•

for each
\mathcal{R}_{gen}
,

\mathcal{R}_{gen}(Y,Z)\circ\mathcal{R}_{gen}(X,Y)\subset\mathcal{R}_{gen}(X,Z).

We define the following modifications of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, namely,

•

d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)\bigr\}
is called the
upper semicontinuous
GH
-distance
;

•

d^{ls}_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)\bigr\}
is called the
lower semicontinuous
GH
-distance
;

•

d^{rc}_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{rs}(X,Y)\bigr\}
is called the
R
-continuous
GH
-distance
.

Example 1.16
.

If
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
is an arbitrary set-valued map, it is continuous. Indeed, for any point
x
, we can choose
U^{x}:=\{x\}
as a neighborhood of
x
, and then for each point
x^{\prime}\in U^{x}
we have
f(x^{\prime})=f(x)\subset f(x)
and
f(x^{\prime})\cap f(x)=f(x)\neq\emptyset
, therefore for any open set
V\subset Y
that either contains
f(x)
or intersects
f(x)
, the same property also holds for all
x^{\prime}\in U^{x}
, which proves the continuity of
f
. It follows that
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)=\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)=\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)=\mathcal{R}_{rc}(X,Y)
, hence
d_{GH}(X,Y)=d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y)=d^{ls}_{GH}(X,Y)=d^{rc}_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Thus, for discrete
X
and
Y
, all the Gromov–Hausdorff distances defined above are equal.

From inclusions (
1
) we immediately obtain the following result.

Proposition 1.17
.

For any metric spaces
X
and
Y
,

•

d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq\min\bigl\{d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y),\,d^{ls}_{GH}(X,Y),\,d^{rc}_{GH}(X,Y)\bigr\}
;

•

d^{rc}_{GH}(X,Y)\geq\max\bigl\{d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y),\,d^{ls}_{GH}(X,Y)\bigr\}
.

2
General properties of modified
GH
-distances

From the definitions of the distances under consideration, we immediately conclude that they are all nonnegative, equal to zero between each space and itself, and symmetric. It turns out that all these distances also satisfy the triangle inequality. By analogy with how we treated the sets of correspondences of different types, we denote by
d_{GH}^{gen}
any of the variants of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance introduced above, i.e.
d_{GH}^{gen}\in\{d_{GH},d^{us}_{GH},d^{ls}_{GH},d^{rc}_{GH}\}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

All the Gromov–Hausdorff distances defined above are generalized pseudometrics.

Proof.

It suffices to verify the triangle inequality. Choose arbitrary nonempty metric spaces
X
,
Y
,
Z
and show that
d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Z)\leq d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Y)+d_{GH}^{gen}(Y,Z)
. If one of
d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Y)
and
d_{GH}^{gen}(Y,Z)
is infinite, the inequality holds. Now suppose both are finite. Choose an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and find correspondences
R_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{gen}(X,Y)
and
R_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{gen}(Y,Z)
such that
2d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Y)\geq\operatorname{dis}R_{1}-\varepsilon
and
2d_{GH}^{gen}(Y,Z)\geq\operatorname{dis}R_{2}-\varepsilon
, then
R:=R_{2}\circ R_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{gen}(X,Z)
by Corollary
1.15
, and by Proposition
1.5
,

2d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Z)\leq\operatorname{dis}R\leq\operatorname{dis}R_{1}+\operatorname{dis}R_{2}\leq 2d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Y)+2d_{GH}^{gen}(Y,Z)+2\varepsilon,

from which we obtain the required result by the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.
∎

Furthermore, Corollary
1.15
and Proposition
1.7
lead to

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty isometric metric spaces, then
d_{GH}^{gen}(X,Y)=0
.

In what follows, by
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
we denote the proper class in the sense of von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory
[
8
,
9
]
consisting of all metric spaces considered up to isometry. From Corollary
2.2
it follows that all four variants of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance under consideration are well-defined on
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
. The proper class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
is called the
Gromov–Hausdorff class
. The subclass of
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
consisting of all bounded metric spaces is denoted by
\mathcal{B}
, and the subclass of
\mathcal{B}
of all compact metric spaces is denoted by
\mathcal{M}
. Note that the class
\mathcal{B}
is proper. The class
\mathcal{M}
, however, is a set, called the
Gromov–Hausdorff space
.

For a metric space
X
, denote by
\operatorname{diam}X
its
diameter
:

\operatorname{diam}X=\sup\bigl\{|xy|:x,y\in X\bigr\}.

For a one-point metric space we reserve the notation
\Delta_{1}
. If
\lambda>0
, then
\lambda X
denotes the metric space obtained from
X
by multiplying all distances by
\lambda
. If
X
is bounded, set
0X=\Delta_{1}
. Note that
\operatorname{diam}(\lambda X)=\lambda\operatorname{diam}X
.

In the following proposition, we show that the main properties of the ordinary Gromov–Hausdorff distance (see
[
5
]
) also hold for its continuous analogues.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
,

(1)

2d^{gen}_{GH}(\Delta_{1},X)=\operatorname{diam}X
;

(2)

2d^{gen}_{GH}(X,Y)\leq\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\}
;

(3)

if the diameter of
X
or
Y
is finite, then
\bigl|\operatorname{diam}X-\operatorname{diam}Y\bigr|\leq 2d^{gen}_{GH}(X,Y)
;

(4)

if the diameter of
X
is finite, then for any
\lambda\geq 0
,
\mu\geq 0
,
2d^{gen}_{GH}(\lambda X,\mu X)=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X
, from which it immediately follows that the curve
\gamma(t):=t\,X
is a shortest path between any of its points, and the length of such a segment of the curve equals the distance between its endpoints;

(5)

for any
\lambda>0
,
d^{gen}_{GH}(\lambda X,\lambda Y)=\lambda\,d^{gen}_{GH}(X,Y)
, and if
X
and
Y
are bounded, the equality also holds for
\lambda=0
;

(6)

if
X
and
Y
are discrete metric spaces, then
d^{gen}_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y)
;

(7)

if
X_{1},\,X_{2},\ldots
is a sequence of metric spaces converging with respect to
d^{gen}_{GH}
to a metric space
X
, then it also converges with respect to
d_{GH}
to this
X
.

Proof.

(
1
) Note that the set
\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{1},X)
consists of exactly one correspondence
R:=\Delta_{1}\times X
, and since the remaining
\mathcal{R}_{gen}(\Delta_{1},X)
contained in this set are always nonempty by Proposition
1.14
, they all coincide with
\{R\}
. It remains to note that
\operatorname{dis}R=\operatorname{diam}X
.

(
2
) Since each
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
is contained in the correspondence
X\times Y
, by Proposition
1.4
we have
\operatorname{dis}R\leq\operatorname{dis}(X\times Y)
. It remains to note that
\operatorname{dis}(X\times Y)=\max\{\operatorname{diam}X,\operatorname{diam}Y\}
.

(
3
) If
\operatorname{diam}X=\operatorname{diam}Y
, the inequality holds. Let
\operatorname{diam}X>\operatorname{diam}Y
, then the diameter of
Y
is finite. Choose arbitrary
R\in\mathcal{R}_{gen}(X,Y)
and consider sequences
(x_{n},y_{n}),\,(x^{\prime}_{n},y^{\prime}_{n})\in R
such that
|x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|\to\operatorname{diam}X
as
n\to\infty
. Then, starting from some
n
, we have
|x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|>\operatorname{diam}Y\geq|y_{n}y^{\prime}_{n}|
, whence for all such
n
,

\bigl||x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|y_{n}y^{\prime}_{n}|\bigr|=|x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|y_{n}y^{\prime}_{n}|\geq|x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-\operatorname{diam}Y\to(\operatorname{diam}X-\operatorname{diam}Y)\ \text{as $n\to\infty$},

so
\operatorname{dis}R\geq\operatorname{diam}X-\operatorname{diam}Y=|\operatorname{diam}X-\operatorname{diam}Y|
. By the arbitrariness of
R
, we obtain the required result.

(
4
) By item (
3
) we have
2d^{gen}_{GH}(\lambda X,\mu X)\geq\bigl|\operatorname{diam}(\lambda X)-\operatorname{diam}(\mu X)\bigr|=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X
. To prove the reverse inequality, let
R\colon\lambda X\to\mu X
be the identity map, then, by Corollary
1.15
, this
R
is contained in all four
\mathbb{R}_{gen}(X,X)
. It remains to note that
\operatorname{dis}R=|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X
.

(
5
) For each
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
denote by
R_{\lambda}
the same correspondence, but considered as an element of
\mathcal{R}(\lambda X,\lambda Y)
. Then
\operatorname{dis}R_{\lambda}=\lambda\operatorname{dis}R
, from which the statement of this item follows.

(
6
) This follows from the fact that all maps between
X
and
Y
, both single-valued and set-valued, are continuous.

(
7
) This follows from the inequality
d_{GH}\leq d^{gen}_{GH}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Analysis of the facts we used in the proofs of Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.3
(except item
6
) allows us to obtain the following generalization. For each pair
(X,Y)
of metric spaces, denote by
\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,Y)
a
nonempty
subset of
\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
and require that the following properties hold:

•

for any metric space
X
, the identity map
{\operatorname{id}}
is contained in
\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,X)
;

•

for any metric spaces
X
,
Y
and
Z
,
\mathcal{R}_{g}(Y,Z)\circ\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,Y)\subset\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,Z)
;

•

for any metric spaces
X
,
Y
,
\mathcal{R}_{g}(Y,X)=\bigl[\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,Y)\bigr]^{-1}
.

Note that the first two conditions define a
category
whose
objects
are metric spaces and whose
morphisms
are correspondences from
\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,Y)
.

Define the Gromov–Hausdorff
g
-distance
by the formula

d^{g}_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\bigl\{\operatorname{dis}R:R\in\mathcal{R}_{g}(X,Y)\bigr\}.

Then
d^{g}_{GH}(X,Y)
satisfies Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.3
(except possibly item
6
), where
\mathcal{R}_{gen}
is replaced by
\mathcal{R}_{g}
.

3
Coincidence of the semicontinuous
GH
-distance with the classical one

We begin with the case of the lower semicontinuous distance.

3.1
Lower semicontinuous
GH
-distance

Let
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
be a set-valued map of sets. The
full preimage
of a set
D\subset Y
is the set

f_{-}^{-1}(D)=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)\cap D\neq\emptyset\bigr\}.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
be a set-valued map of topological spaces. Then
f
is lower semicontinuous if and only if for any open set
U\subset Y
, the set
f_{-}^{-1}(U)
is open in
X
.

Recall that a map
f\colon X\to Y
of topological spaces is called
open
if the image of every open set is open. In what follows, we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be topological spaces, and
\pi_{X}\colon X\times Y\to X
be the standard projection. Then
\pi_{X}
is an open map.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be topological spaces, and
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
be an open subset of
X\times Y
. Then
R\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
and
R^{-1}\colon Y\rightrightarrows X
are lower semicontinuous set-valued maps. In other words,
R\in\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

We prove lower semicontinuity for
R
(the proof for
R^{-1}
is analogous). Choose an arbitrary open
U\subset Y
, then
R_{-}^{-1}(U)=\pi_{X}\bigl[(X\times U)\cap R\bigr]
, and since
X\times U
is open and
R
is open,
(X\times U)\cap R
is open. By Lemma
3.2
,
\pi_{X}\bigl[(X\times U)\cap R\bigr]
is open, so
R
is lower semicontinuous by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty metric spaces, then
d_{GH}(X,Y)=d^{ls}_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, for each
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
,
\varepsilon>0
and
R_{\varepsilon}:=U_{\varepsilon}(R)
we have
R_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)
. On the other hand, by Proposition
1.10
,
\inf_{\varepsilon}\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{dis}R
, so

2d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)}\operatorname{dis}R=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y),\,\varepsilon>0}\operatorname{dis}R_{\varepsilon}\geq\inf_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{ls}(X,Y)}\operatorname{dis}R=2d^{ls}_{GH}(X,Y).

It remains to apply Proposition
1.17
.
∎

We now consider the case of the upper semicontinuous distance.

3.2
Upper semicontinuous
GH
-distance

Let
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
be a set-valued map of sets. The
small preimage
of a set
D\subset Y
is the set

f_{+}^{-1}(D)=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)\subset D\bigr\}.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
be a set-valued map of topological spaces. Then
f
is upper semicontinuous if and only if for any closed set
W\subset Y
, the set
f_{-}^{-1}(W)
is closed in
X
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty compact topological spaces, with
X
also Hausdorff, and let
f\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
be a set-valued map whose graph
f\subset X\times Y
is closed. Then
f
is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

Choose an arbitrary closed
W\subset Y
and denote by
\pi_{X}\colon X\times Y\to X
the standard projection. Then

f_{-}^{-1}(W)=\bigl\{x\in X:f(x)\cap W\neq\emptyset\bigr\}=\bigl\{x\in X:\exists y\in W,\,y\in f(x)\bigr\}=\pi_{X}\bigl[(X\times W)\cap f\bigr].

Since
X\times W
is closed in
X\times Y
,
(X\times W)\cap f
is also closed, and hence compact due to the compactness of
X\times Y
. Since the projection
\pi_{X}
is continuous, and the continuous image of a compact set is compact, the set
f_{-}^{-1}(W)\subset X
is compact and, therefore, closed because
X
is Hausdorff. Thus, by Theorem
3.5
, the set-valued map
f
is upper semicontinuous.
∎

From Theorem
3.6
we immediately obtain the following result.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty compact Hausdorff topological spaces. Then every closed correspondence
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
, together with its inverse, is upper semicontinuous. In other words,
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(X,Y)\subset\mathcal{R}_{us}(X,Y)
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty compact metric spaces. Then

d_{GH}^{us}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y).

Proof.

By Corollary
3.7
, we have
d_{GH}(X,Y)\geq d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y)
. The opposite inequality is contained in Proposition
1.17
.
∎

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
A\subset X
and
B\subset Y
be nonempty subsets of metric spaces, and
R\subset A\times B
be closed in
A\times B
. Let
(a_{n},b_{n})\in R
be a sequence such that
a_{n}\to a\in A
and
b_{n}\to b\in B
. Then
(a,b)\in R
.

Proof.

Suppose the contrary, i.e.,
(a,b)\not\in R
. Since
R
is closed in
A\times B
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\bigl(U_{\varepsilon}(a)\cap A\bigr)\times\bigl(U_{\varepsilon}(b)\cap B\bigr)
does not intersect
R
. But for sufficiently large
n
, we have
a_{n}\in U_{\varepsilon}(a)\cap A
and
b_{n}\in U_{\varepsilon}(b)\cap B
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
Y\subset X
be a nonempty subset of a compact metric space
X
. Then
d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

For arbitrary
R^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}(Y,X)
and
\varepsilon>0
, extend
R^{\prime}
to
R\in\mathcal{R}(\bar{Y},X)
as follows: for each point
y^{\prime}\in\bar{Y}\setminus Y
,

•

consider an arbitrary sequence
y^{\prime}_{n}\in Y
,
y^{\prime}_{n}\to y^{\prime}
;

•

for each
n
, choose
x^{\prime}_{n}\in X
such that
(y^{\prime}_{n},x^{\prime}_{n})\in R^{\prime}
;

•

using the compactness of
X
, choose a subsequence from
x^{\prime}_{n}
converging to some
x^{\prime}\in X
;

•

without loss of generality, assume that
x^{\prime}_{n}\to x^{\prime}
;

•

add all such obtained
(y^{\prime},x^{\prime})
to
R^{\prime}
.

Next, let
{\bar{R}}\subset\bar{Y}\times X
be the closure of
R
in
\bar{Y}\times X
. By Corollary
3.7
, the set-valued map
{\bar{R}}\colon\bar{Y}\rightrightarrows X
is upper semicontinuous. By Proposition
1.13
, the restriction
{\bar{R}}|_{Y}
is also upper semicontinuous.

We show that
{\bar{R}}|_{Y}=R^{\prime}
. Choose an arbitrary point
(y,x)\in{\bar{R}}|_{Y}
, then there exists a sequence
(y_{n},x_{n})\in R
converging to
(y,x)
. By construction, for each
y_{n}
there are two possibilities:

•

either
y_{n}\in Y
, then we remain it unchanged;

•

or
y_{n}\in\bar{Y}\setminus Y
, but then, by construction, there exists
y^{\prime}_{n}\in Y
such that
(y^{\prime}_{n},x^{\prime}_{n})\in R^{\prime}
and
|y^{\prime}_{n}y_{n}|<1/n
; in this case, we replace the pair
(y_{n},x_{n})
with
(y^{\prime}_{n},x^{\prime}_{n})
.

Note that for the reconstructed sequence
(y_{n},x_{n})
we have
(y_{n},x_{n})\in R^{\prime}
for all
n
,
y_{n}\to y
,
x_{n}\to x
. Since
y\in Y
and
R^{\prime}
is closed, by Lemma
3.9
,
(y,x)\in R^{\prime}
.

Thus, we have shown that
\overline{\mathcal{R}}(Y,X)\subset\mathcal{R}_{us}(Y,X)
, which completes the proof.
∎

From Theorem
3.10
we immediately obtain the following result.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
Y\subset X
be a dense subset of a nonempty compact metric space. Then
d_{GH}^{us}(X,Y)=0
.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
X
and
Y
be totally bounded metric spaces. Then
d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{X}
and
\bar{Y}
be the completions of
X
and
Y
, respectively. Then
\bar{X}
and
\bar{Y}
are compact, and
X
and
Y
are their dense subsets. It remains to use Theorem
3.8
, Corollary
3.11
and the triangle inequality for
d^{us}_{GH}
.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
X
and
Y
be boundedly compact metric spaces (i.e., all closed balls in them are compact) with
d_{GH}(X,Y)<\infty
. Then
d^{us}_{GH}(X,Y)=d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proof.

It suffices to choose an arbitrary closed correspondence
R\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}(X,Y)
with
\operatorname{dis}R<\infty
and show that for any
x\in X
, the set-valued map
R
is upper semicontinuous at
x
.

Consider arbitrary open and closed balls
U:=U_{r}(x)
and
A:=B_{r}(x)
, respectively. Since
\operatorname{dis}R<\infty
, the set
R(A)
is bounded, so there exists an open ball
V:=U_{s}(y)\subset Y
such that
R(A)\subset V
. Set
B=B_{s}(y)
. Since
X
and
Y
are boundedly compact,
A
and
B
are compact. The set-valued map
R|_{A}
maps the metric compact
A
into the metric compact
B
. Since
R
is closed in
X\times Y
,
R|_{A}=(A\times B)\cap R
is closed in
A\times B
. By Theorem
3.6
, the set-valued map
R|_{A}
is upper semicontinuous.

Let
W\subset Y
be an arbitrary open set in
Y
containing
R(x)
. Since
R(x)\subset R(A)\subset V
, we have
R(x)\subset V\cap W\subset B
, and
V\cap W
is an open set in
Y
and, therefore, also an open set in the topology induced on
B
. Since
R|_{A}
is upper semicontinuous, there exists a neighborhood
W^{x}
in
A
such that for all
x^{\prime}\in W^{x}
,
R(x^{\prime})=R|_{A}(x^{\prime})\subset V\cap W
. Note that the set
W^{x}\cap U
is open in
X
, so it is an open neighborhood of
x
in
X
as well. Thus, we have constructed a neighborhood in
X
of
x
such that for all points
x^{\prime}
in it, the set
R(x^{\prime})
is contained in an arbitrarily chosen neighborhood in
Y
of
R(x)
, which proves the upper semicontinuity of the set-valued map
R
at
x
.
∎
