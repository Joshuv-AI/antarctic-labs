Nonlocal Teams and Information Structures
Drishti Baruah, Sachin Teli, Ankur A. Kulkarni

Abstract
We look at Bell inequalities 2 from the lens of information structures in stochastic teams. We consider the usual CHSH game and a dynamic variant of the same to study how various classes of strategies, classical, projective and quantum, behave under team theoretic solution concepts. We find that projective strategies (where each player performs projective measurements) enjoy important properties in the usual CHSH game, but they do not carry over to its dynamic version. These results shed light on the delicate interplay of information structure in quantum strategies and the fragility of some well known ideas under changes of information structure.

Introduction

Bell’s theorem
2
, asserting that quantum theory’s predictions cannot be explained by any local theory, stands as a milestone in the foundations of physics, revealing the inherent conflict between quantum mechanics and the principle of locality. This theorem demonstrated that no local hidden variable theory can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics.

Viewing Bell inequalities as games offers an intuitive understanding, as exemplified by the CHSH game.
Bell nonlocal games
4
involve multiple agents who receive inputs and produce actions to minimize a cost that depends on their actions and a state of nature that is correlated with their inputs, but not fully known to either agent.
Strategies utilising quantum resources such as shared entanglement and quantum measurements often outperform classical strategies in these games, providing an advantage via the deployment of such resources.

This paper uncovers new structural phenomena in nonlocal games through the lens of team theory. Bell nonlocal games can also be thought of as (stochastic)
team
problems. In such a problem, agents receive partial information of an underlying state, and must jointly design a strategy that minimizes a cost that depends on their collective actions and the unknown state. The
information structure
of a team is a description of
who knows what
in the team. An information structure is
static
if no agent can affect the underlying state of a system; else it is
dynamic
. In a dynamic
classical
information structure, all agents have access to the information of agents that have acted prior to them whereas in a non-classical information structure, this assumption does not hold. Problems with non-classical information structures are known to defy commonly expected properties (as first observed by Witsenhausen
13
in the context of Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian control problems), and their discrete versions are known to be NP-complete
11
. On the other hand problems with classical information structure admit tractable formulations and recursive algorithms
3
;
8
. In this paper, when we refer to dynamic information structure, we mean its non-classical variant.

Since information structure is known to have such a dramatic effect on stochastic teams, a natural curiosity arises in understanding the effect of the information structure on the phenomenon of the quantum advantage. Bell nonlocal games in their usual formulation can be viewed as static team problems and exhibit a quantum advantage as evidenced in the CHSH inequality. We ask – what is the character of this advantage if the information structure is dynamic and non-classical?

1.1
Main findings

The usual CHSH game is a static team problem.
To make a comparison sensible between the static and dynamic information structure, we construct a dynamic team problem with the same cost as a Bell nonlocal game, but where the agents act in a sequence, and a noise-corrupted version of the first agent’s action is available to the second agent, while the first agent’s information is not. We find that there is a clean one-to-one correspondence between the static and dynamic team: the source and channel noise in the dynamic problem can be mapped to the inputs in the static problem. Under this mapping the team optimal cost under classical strategies is the
same
for static and dynamic problems.

We then study the quantum advantage that the agents can obtain.
Our aim is to compare and contrast the structural properties of the optimal strategies with the change of information structure. Our main finding is that projective strategies, which are known to be optimal in the usual CHSH game, cease to be always optimal when the information structure is dynamic. In fact, for certain values of the problem parameters, projective strategies yield a strict quantum
disadvantage
over classical strategies when the information structure is dynamic.

We then analyse two other solution concepts or properties arising in game theory: person-by-person optimality and best response strategies. A person-by-person optimal strategy is one that players can be expected to play in the absence of any pre-play communication. Each player picks a strategy that is optimal given the other player’s strategy; in other words a Nash equilibrium
9
;
1
;
7
.
The best response refers to the optimal strategy chosen by a player in response to the actions of the other player.
We investigate these properties from the vantage of strategy classes, and ask if classical or projective strategy classes enjoy the property that contains a person-by-person optimal or best response. Here too we find that the information structure is critical. For example, projective strategies are person-by-person optimal for the static CHSH game, but they need not be so for the dynamic one. Comprehensive summary of all results is found in Table
2
.
Our results shed light on the subtle and fascinating role played by the information structure of a stochastic team with reference to quantum strategies and serve as instructive extensions of the landmark CHSH game.

We first lay out some preliminary concepts required for our study beginning with a formulation of a CHSH game. We will be using

{\mathbb{P}}(y_{1},y_{2})

y_{1},y_{2}

2.1
The CHSH Game

Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt
6
devised a thought experiment to show how strategies that exploit shared quantum entanglement can violate a particular Bell inequality which later came to be known as the CHSH inequality. The setting that forms the backdrop of this inequality has often been referred to as the
CHSH game
.

The CHSH game comprises of two agents Arjuna and Bhima.
Arjuna receives an observation of a random variable
y_{1}\in\{0,1\}
and Bhima receives
y_{2}\in\{0,1\}
.
In the standard CHSH game,
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are taken to be uniformly random and independent of each other. Arjuna then chooses
u_{1}\in\{0,1\}
based on the observation
y_{1}
, and Bhima chooses
u_{2}\in\{0,1\}
by observing
y_{2}
. They win the game if
u_{1}\oplus u_{2}=y_{1}\cdot y_{2}
and lose otherwise. The highest winning probability that the players can achieve classically is
75\%
. In this context, “classically” means that the players are restricted to play strategies based solely on local randomness (i.e., independent randomization by each player) or shared classical common randomness (i.e., players have access to a random variable generated independently of
y_{1},y_{2}
). On the other hand, if the players choose actions resulting from a measurement performed on a Bell state, then the best winning probability increases to about
85\%
. This increase reflects the advantage provided by quantum correlations.

In this paper we view the CHSH game through the lens of team theory.
Arjuna and Bhima form a
team
in the sense of Radner and Marschak
12
– decision makers with asymmetric information but a common goal, in this case maximizing the winning probability. Arjuna and Bhima are referred to as
agents
or
players
.
Maximizing the winning probability is equivalent to the minimization of the expected value of a
cost function

\mathcal{B}_{1}
, defined as:

\mathcal{B}_{1}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2}):=u_{1}\oplus u_{2}\oplus y_{1}\cdot y_{2}.

This function vanishes when the players win the game (
u_{1}\oplus u_{2}=y_{1}\cdot y_{2}
) and is unity when they lose (
u_{1}\oplus u_{2}\neq y_{1}\cdot y_{2}
). As a result, the expected value
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}_{1}]
represents the probability of
losing
the game.

A key part of the theory of teams is
information structure
which describes “who knows what” in a team. A team theoretic view of the CHSH game now opens the possibility of studying the impact of information structures on the players’ strategies and winning probabilities. The goal of this paper is to pursue this direction to its logical conclusion. We aim to understand the impact of the information structure, and team theoretic solution concepts on the outcomes of the CHSH game.

The above game belongs to a broad class of games known as XOR games
4
. Players win if the XOR of their actions,
u_{1}\oplus u_{2}
, equals
c=c(y_{1},y_{2})
, some binary function of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. Varying the function
c
yields eight distinct XOR games, each of which corresponds to a version of the CHSH inequality. In our team theoretic view, this yields eight distinct cost functions, numbered
\mathcal{B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{8}
, and thus eight distinct team problems.
We refer to these cost functions as “Bell costs”. The results of our analysis are qualitatively independent of the choice of the specific cost function; we show this in Section
2.4
. Thus we work with
\mathcal{B}_{1}
for convenience.

2.2.2
Dynamic Information Structure

In the dynamic information structure, Bhima’s input depends on Arjuna’s action. Arjuna observes a source whose probability distribution is given by

{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(y_{1}=0)=p_{s}\quad{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(y_{1}=1)=1-p_{s}=:\widetilde{p_{s}}.

The distribution of

{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(v=0)=p_{c}\quad{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(v=1)=1-p_{c}=:\widetilde{p_{c}}.

Arjuna and Bhima must choose actions
u_{1},u_{2}

by observing
y_{1},y_{2}

For the dynamic information structure, denote
\mathsf{I}=\mathsf{D}

\mathsf{A}

In the dynamic information structure the action of the first acting player has a secondary, indirect, effect – the primary effect of the action is on the cost – by influencing the strategies available to the second acting player. Indeed this secondary effect is present even in the absence of any primary effect (as seen for example, in a communication problem). This phenomenon is known as the
dual effect
in the theory teams and decentralized control, and is known to render decentralized decision problems computationally intractable
11

13

8

Notice that the dynamic problem reduces to a static one when the channel is maximally noisy (
p_{c}=\tfrac{1}{2}
), which severs the causal dependence of
y_{2}
on
u_{1}
and recovers independent observations for the two agents. This property has fascinating implications, as we will see later in the paper.

2.3
Strategy Classes

This section describes the various strategy classes that players can adopt.

2.3.1
Classical Strategies

A general mixed classical strategy is given by stochastic mappings
\mathbb{P}(u_{i}|y_{i})
,
u_{i},y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Formally, a mixed classical strategy is a pair of functions
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
, where

\Gamma_{1}:\{0,1\}\rightarrow[0,1],\quad\Gamma_{2}:\{0,1\}\rightarrow[0,1]

specify the probability of choosing action
0

\mathbb{P}(u_{1}=0|y_{1})=\Gamma_{1}(y_{1})
and
\mathbb{P}(u_{2}=0|y_{2})=\Gamma_{2}(y_{2})
. Thus under a classical strategy

{\mathbb{P}}(u_{1},u_{2}|y_{1},y_{2})={\mathbb{P}}(u_{1}|y_{1}){\mathbb{P}}(u_{2}|y_{2}).

In a static information structure
y_{2}
is independent of
u_{1}
, whereas in a dynamic information structure

{\mathbb{P}}(y_{2}|u_{1})={\mathbb{P}}(u_{1}+y_{1}+v|u_{1}).

However, since the expected cost
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}_{1}]

{\mathbb{P}}(u_{1}|y_{1})
and

Under these strategies, players choose actions as a deterministic function of their observations.

\displaystyle:\{0,1\}\rightarrow\{0,1\},\quad\gamma_{2}:\{0,1\}\rightarrow\{0,1\},

2.3.2
Quantum Strategies

To define a quantum strategy, assume that players conduct measurements on a bipartite quantum state
\ket{\Psi}
lying in a 4-dimensional Hilbert space with the basis
\{\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}\}
. We take
\ket{\Psi}

\ket{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{00}+\ket{11}\right).

In a quantum strategy the players choose a positive operator-valued measure (POVM) as a function of their observations

\ket{\Psi}

. This we have that the probability

{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1}|y_{1})=\Tr((E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes I)\rho)
. The post-measurement state at this stage is given by

\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{E^{u_{1}}_{y_{1}}}\otimes I\rho\sqrt{E^{u_{1}}_{y_{1}}}\otimes I}{{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1}|y_{1})}.

After this, Bhima performs his measurement on
\rho^{\prime}
, and chooses action
u_{2}=m_{2}
. We thus have,

{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{2}|y_{1},u_{1},y_{2})=\Tr((I \otimes F^{u_2}_{y_2})\rho')=\frac{\Tr((I \otimes F^{u_2}_{y_2}) (E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes I)\rho)}{\Tr(E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes I \rho)}=\frac{\Tr(E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes F^{u_2}_{y_2} \rho)}{{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1}|y_{1})}.

Hence,

{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2})=\Tr(E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes F^{u_2}_{y_2} \rho){\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(y_{2}|u_{1},y_{1}){\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(y_{1}).

Here, note that
{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(y_{2}|u_{1},y_{1})
is either
p_{c}
or
1-p_{c}
since
y_{2}=y_{1}\oplus u_{1}\oplus v
.
The corresponding team optimal costs are defined as

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\min_{\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}}J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})

(9a)

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{D*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{s},p_{c})

\displaystyle=\min_{\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}}J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{s},p_{c}),

(9b)

where the minimization is over all possible POVMs
\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}
.

2.3.3
Parameterisation of Quantum Strategies

For a general quantum strategy, we express the POVM elements as a linear combination of the Pauli matrices. Let

E_{i}=e^{0}_{i}I+\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad F_{j}=f^{0}_{j}I+\mathbf{f}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

where
e_{i},f_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
is the Pauli vector. The vectors are defined as
\boldsymbol{e_{i}}=(e_{i}^{x},e_{i}^{y},e_{i}^{z}),

\boldsymbol{f_{j}}=(f_{j}^{x},f_{j}^{y},f_{j}^{z}).

Positivity of the POVM elements requires

|\mathbf{e}_{i}|\leq\min(e_{i},1-e_{i}),\qquad|\mathbf{f}_{j}|\leq\min(f_{j},1-f_{j}).

For the maximally entangled Bell state
\rho=\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}
, a crucial property is

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\,\sigma_{\alpha}\otimes\sigma_{\beta})=\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\{x,y,z\},

where
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
is the Kronecker delta (which equals
1
if
\alpha=\beta
and
0
otherwise), reflecting perfect correlations along each axis and vanishing cross-correlations.
Using this, the traces simplify naturally:

\Tr E_{i}=2e^{0}_{i},\qquad\Tr F_{j}=2f^{0}_{j},

(10)

\Tr(E_i \otimes F_j \rho)=e^{0}_{i}f^{0}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{j}}.

(11)

Proposition 2.2
.

For both the static and dynamic information structure, for each classical strategy, there exists a quantum strategy
(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}})
attaining the same joint distribution on
u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
.

Proof.

1.

Static information structure:
First consider the static information structure.
We have the following relations

\displaystyle{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1}|y_{1})

\displaystyle=\Tr(E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes I \rho)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr E^{u_{1}}_{y_{1}},

(12a)

\displaystyle{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{2}|y_{2})

\displaystyle=\Tr(I \otimes F^{u_2}_{y_2} \rho)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr F^{u_{2}}_{y_{2}},

(12b)

where the second equality holds because the Bell state’s reduced density matrix is
{\textstyle{1\over 2}}I
.

Now, consider a classical mixed strategy

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
given by

\mathbb{P}(u_{1}=0|y_{1}=i)=\Gamma_{1}(i)=:\alpha_{i},\text{and}\mathbb{P}(u_{2}=0|y_{2}=j)=\Gamma_{2}(j)=:\beta_{j},

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in[0,1]
. It can be seen that the quantum strategy (with the Bell state) given by

E_{0}=\alpha_{0}I,E_{1}=\alpha_{1}I,F_{0}=\beta_{0}I,F_{1}=\beta_{1}I,

(13)

is equivalent to this classical strategy, as it satisfies the above relations as per (
12a
) and (
12b
).

2.

Dynamic information structure:
For this information structure we have

\displaystyle{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1}|y_{1})

\displaystyle=\Tr(E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes I \rho)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr E^{u_{1}}_{y_{1}},

(14a)

\displaystyle{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{2}|y_{2},u_{1},y_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr(E^{u_1}_{y_1} \otimes F^{u_2}_{y_2} \rho)}{\frac{1}{2}\Tr E^{u_{1}}_{y_{1}}},

(14b)

thanks to (
8
).
As in the static case, consider a classical mixed strategy,
{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{1}=0|y_{1}=i)=\alpha_{i},{\rm I\kern-1.69998ptP}(u_{2}=0|y_{2}=j)=\beta_{j}
. The same quantum strategy
(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}})
given in (
13
) satisfies (
14a
) and (
14b
) .

Hence, any classical strategy can be expressed in terms of POVMs.

Due to this proposition, we consider the set of classical strategies (upto isomorphisms) as a subset of the set of quantum strategies (with a Bell state), that is, we have the relation
\mathsf{C}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
.

2.3.4
Projective Quantum Strategies

Projective quantum strategies are a specific subset of quantum strategies that restrict players to executing projective measurements. In these strategies, the measurement operators
\{E_{0},E_{1},F_{0},F_{1}\}
are characterized by being rank-1 projective matrices, meaning that
E^{2}_{i}=E_{i}
and
F^{2}_{j}=F_{j}
, for all
i,j
. These measurements essentially project the quantum state onto specific states and their orthogonal complements. This projection can be parameterized as follows. Define,

P(\theta,\phi)=\ket{\theta,\phi}\bra{\theta, \phi}.

Here,
\ket{\theta,\phi}=\cos{\theta}\ket{0}+e^{\iota\phi}\sin{\theta}\ket{1}
represents a pure quantum state on the Bloch sphere
10
, with
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
,
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
, and where
\iota
is the imaginary unit.
A projective quantum strategy is a quantum strategy with the POVM given by

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=:P(\theta_{10},\phi_{10}),

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=:P(\theta_{11},\phi_{11}),

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=:P(\theta_{20},\phi_{20}),

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=:P(\theta_{21},\phi_{21}).

Let
\perp
be the set of projective quantum strategies. Additionally, let

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{10},\theta_{11},\theta_{20},\theta_{21}),\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{10},\phi_{11},\phi_{20},\phi_{21}).

So, a projective strategy is parametrized by
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
. The expected cost under projective quantum strategies is denoted by

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi};p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2}}[\mathcal{B}_{1}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2})]

(15a)

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi};p_{s},p_{c})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2}}[\mathcal{B}_{1}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2})]

(15b)

where the joint distribution of
(u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
is given by (
7
) in (
15a
) and (
8
) in (
15b
) for the static and dynamic information structure respectively.
The optimal costs are given by:

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\perp}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi};p_{1},p_{2}),

(16a)

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{D*}}_{\perp}(p_{s},p_{c})

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi};p_{s},p_{c}).

(16b)

Clearly,
\mathsf{\perp}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
. However, unlike general quantum strategies, all classical strategies do not have equivalent projective quantum strategies as defined above.

2.4
Invariance of Bell Costs

Let a general Bell cost be denoted by

\mathcal{B}_{k}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2})=u_{1}\oplus u_{2}\oplus\alpha_{k}(y_{1},y_{2}),

(17)

where
\alpha_{k}
is a binary function of
y_{1},y_{2}
.
There are 16 such binary functions
\alpha_{k}
, out of which we have listed the 8 non-trivial ones that give rise to a violation of a corresponding Bell inequality. The remaining 8 functions are considered trivial because they can be perfectly decoupled into local functions of the form
\alpha_{k}(y_{1},y_{2})=g(y_{1})\oplus h(y_{2})
. For these trivial cases, players can always achieve a zero expected cost (a
100\%
winning probability) using strictly classical deterministic strategies by simply playing
u_{1}=g(y_{1})
and
u_{2}=h(y_{2})
, meaning no quantum advantage can exist.

\begin{array}[]{ |c|c|c|c|c|c| }\hline\cr\mathcal{B}_{k}&\alpha_{k}(y_{1},y_{2})&\alpha_{k}(0,0)&\alpha_{k}(0,1)&\alpha_{k}(1,0)&\alpha_{k}(1,1)\\
\hline\cr\mathcal{B}_{1}&y_{1}\cdot y_{2}&0&0&0&1\\
\mathcal{B}_{2}&y_{1}\cdot\overline{y_{2}}&0&0&1&0\\
\mathcal{B}_{3}&\overline{y_{1}}\cdot y_{2}&0&1&0&0\\
\mathcal{B}_{4}&\overline{y_{1}}\cdot\overline{y_{2}}&1&0&0&0\\
\mathcal{B}_{5}&\overline{y_{1}}+\overline{y_{2}}&1&1&1&0\\
\mathcal{B}_{6}&\overline{y_{1}}+y_{2}&1&1&0&1\\
\mathcal{B}_{7}&y_{1}+\overline{y_{2}}&1&0&1&1\\
\mathcal{B}_{8}&y_{1}+y_{2}&0&1&1&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Table 1
:
Cost Functions Associated With All Bell’s Inequalities.

In this paper we consider the well known CHSH game which is derived from the CHSH inequality. The CHSH game corresponds to the cost
\mathcal{B}_{1}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2})=u_{1}\oplus u_{2}\oplus y_{1}\cdot y_{2}
. But in general, one can look at general “Bell costs”
\mathcal{B}_{k}
which can be derived from other similar Bell inequalities. We show in Appendix
A.2
that the expected quantum costs vary only upto a simple mapping across Bell costs. Moreover, the optimal cost under all strategy classes remains the same regardless of which Bell cost one chooses. As such, fixing
\mathcal{B}_{1}
leads to no loss of generalisation due to this invariance across Bell costs.

3
Team Optimal Cost under varying Information Structures

We define our first solution concept by which we will study these strategy classes
\mathsf{C}
,
\mathsf{\perp}
and
\mathsf{Q}
. Recall that we have already established that
\mathsf{C},\mathsf{\perp}\subseteq\mathsf{Q}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix
(p_{1},p_{2})
and
(p_{s},p_{c})
and let
\mathsf{A}
be a strategy class. A strategy pair
(\textbf{{E}}^{*},\textbf{{F}}^{*})\in\mathsf{Q}
is called the team optimal for the static information structure if

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}}^{*},\textbf{{F}}^{*};p_{1},p_{2})\leq J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})\quad\forall(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}})\in\mathsf{Q},

and for the dynamic information structure if

J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}}^{*},\textbf{{F}}^{*};p_{s},p_{c})\leq J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{s},p_{c})\quad\forall(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}})\in\mathsf{Q}.

If for some
p_{1},p_{2}
(resp.,
p_{s},p_{c}
) there exists a team optimal solution
(\textbf{{E}}^{*},\textbf{{F}}^{*})\in\mathsf{A}
, then we say the strategy class
\mathsf{A}
is team optimal for the static (dynamic) information structure for this
p_{1},p_{2}
(resp.,
p_{s},p_{c}
).

If a strategy class
\mathsf{A}
is team optimal for all
p_{1},p_{2}
(resp.,
p_{s},p_{c}
), then we say that the strategy class
\mathsf{A}
is team optimal for the static (dynamic) information structure.

We explore the team optimal strategies and costs associated with the cost function
\mathcal{B}_{1}
in the static and dynamic information structure. First we look at the team optimal when players are restricted to play deterministic classical strategies and then proceed to find the team optimal when players are allowed to play quantum strategies. Finally, we compare both the team optimal to find out the existence and extent of quantum advantage.

The following is our result for classical strategies and the two information structures.

Proofs are included in Appendix

3.2.2

We now ascertain the team optimal cost under quantum strategies.
We do a detailed step-by-step analytical optimization of the above expressions over the POVMs

and obtain closed form expressions for the optimal cost for static and dynamic information structures.

Theorem 3.5
.

For a static information structure, the optimal expected cost under quantum strategies is given by

J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})=\begin{cases}{\textstyle{1\over 2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\{p_{1}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2}\}\{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}\}},&\text{if }\left|(2p_{1}-1)(2p_{2}-1)\right|\leq 4p_{1}\widetilde{p_{1}}\,p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}},\\

(23)

Projective strategies are team optimal for static information structure.

Classical strategies are team optimal for
p_{1},p_{2}
satisfying
\left|(2p_{1}-1)(2p_{2}-1)\right|\geq 4p_{1}\widetilde{p_{1}}\,p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}

Proof.

Let

\textbf{{E}}=(E_{0},E_{1}),\qquad\textbf{{F}}=(F_{0},F_{1}),

where, for each setting, the binary POVM is
\{E_{i},I-E_{i}\}
for Arjuna and

\{F_{j},I-F_{j}\}
for Bhima. Recall from Lemma
3.3
that

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(p_{1}p_{2}\langle A_{0}B_{0}\rangle+p_{1}\widetilde{p_{2}}\langle A_{0}B_{1}\rangle+\widetilde{p_{1}}p_{2}\langle A_{1}B_{0}\rangle-\widetilde{p_{1}}\widetilde{p_{2}}\langle A_{1}B_{1}\rangle\Big),

(24)

where

A_{i}=2E_{i}-I,\qquad B_{j}=2F_{j}-I.

Since
\rho
is the Bell state, its reduced density matrices are
\frac{1}{2}I
.
Hence, by (
20
),

\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle=1+4\Tr[(E_{i}\otimes F_{j})\rho]-\Tr E_{i}-\Tr F_{j},

whereby the RHS of (
24
) is linear in
\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}
.

Now let

\mathcal{E}:=\{M\in\mathbb{H}_{2}:M\succeq 0,I-M\succeq 0\}.
This is a compact convex set. We claim that the
extreme points of
\mathcal{E}
are precisely

0,I\text{and the rank-one projections.}

Indeed, suppose
M\in\mathcal{E}
has spectral decomposition

M=\lambda_{1}P+\lambda_{2}(I-P),\qquad 0\leq\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\leq 1,

where
P=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
is a rank-one orthogonal projection on
\ket{\psi}
.
Then
M
is extreme only when
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\{0,1\}
; otherwise one
can perturb the eigenvalues slightly in opposite directions and write
M
as a
nontrivial convex combination of two distinct elements from
\mathcal{E}
. Thus the only extreme
points are
0
,
I
, and rank-one projections. Recall that the POVM
{0,I}
corresponds to classical strategies.

Since the RHS of (
24
) is affine in
\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}
the minimum of

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})
over
\mathcal{E}
is attained where each of

E_{0},\ E_{1},\ F_{0},\ F_{1}

is an extreme point of
\mathcal{E}
. Therefore an optimal static quantum strategy may be
chosen so that each player plays either projective or classical strategies.

Using (
10
) and (
11
) in (
20

\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle=1+4\Tr[(E_{i}\otimes F_{j})\rho]-\Tr E_{i}-\Tr F_{j}=1+4(e^{0}_{i}f^{0}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{j}})-2e^{0}_{i}-2f^{0}_{j}.

Substituting this into (
24

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{p_{1}}\widetilde{p_{2}}-2p_{1}\bigl(p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}+\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-2\widetilde{p_{1}}\bigl(p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}-\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{e_{1}}+p_{1}e^{0}_{0}+\widetilde{p_{1}}(p_{2}-\widetilde{p_{2}})e^{0}_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+p_{2}f^{0}_{0}+\widetilde{p_{2}}(p_{1}-\widetilde{p_{1}})f^{0}_{1}-2\Big((p_{2}f^{0}_{0}+\widetilde{p_{2}}f^{0}_{1})p_{1}e^{0}_{0}+(p_{2}f^{0}_{0}-\widetilde{p_{2}}f^{0}_{1})\widetilde{p_{1}}e^{0}_{1}\Big).

(25)

To analyse this expression we now consider two cases.

Case 1: all four are rank-one projections.

Suppose
E_{0},E_{1},F_{0},F_{1}
are all rank-one projections. Using the
parametrization from Section
2.3.3
, write

E_{i}=e^{0}_{i}I+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad F_{j}=f^{0}_{j}I+\boldsymbol{f_{j}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad i,j\in\{0,1\}.

Since each of
E_{i}
and
F_{j}
is a rank-one projection, its
eigenvalues are
1
and
0
. Hence

\Tr E_{i}=\Tr F_{j}=1,

i,j\in\{0,1\}
.
Using (
10

e^{0}_{i}=f^{0}_{j}=\frac{1}{2},

i,j\in\{0,1\}.

Now the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix of the form

are
a\pm|\mathbf{v}|
. Therefore the
eigenvalues of
E_{i}=e^{0}_{i}I+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
are

e^{0}_{i}\pm|\boldsymbol{e_{i}}|=\frac{1}{2}\pm|\boldsymbol{e_{i}}|
.
Hence

|\boldsymbol{e_{i}}|=\frac{1}{2},i\in\{0,1\}.

Similarly,

|\boldsymbol{f_{j}}|=\frac{1}{2},j\in\{0,1\}.

Thus

A_{i}=2E_{i}-I=2\,\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad B_{j}=2F_{j}-I=2\,\boldsymbol{f_{j}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

Also, by (

\Tr[(E_{i}\otimes F_{j})\rho]=e^{0}_{i}f^{0}_{j}+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{j}}=\frac{1}{4}+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{j}}.

Substituting this into (
20

\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle=1+4\Tr[(E_{i}\otimes F_{j})\rho]-\Tr E_{i}-\Tr F_{j}=1+4\left(\frac{1}{4}+\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{j}}\right)-1-1=4\,\boldsymbol{e_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{j}}.

Hence (
24

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(4p_{1}p_{2}\,\boldsymbol{e_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}}+4p_{1}\widetilde{p_{2}}\,\boldsymbol{e_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}+4\widetilde{p_{1}}p_{2}\,\boldsymbol{e_{1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}}-4\widetilde{p_{1}}\widetilde{p_{2}}\,\boldsymbol{e_{1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-2\Big(p_{1}\,\boldsymbol{e_{0}}\cdot(p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}+\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}})+\widetilde{p_{1}}\,\boldsymbol{e_{1}}\cdot(p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}-\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}})\Big).

We now minimize the above under the constraints

e^{0}_{0}=e^{0}_{1}=f^{0}_{0}=f^{0}_{1}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad|\boldsymbol{e_{0}}|=|\boldsymbol{e_{1}}|=|\boldsymbol{f_{0}}|=|\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|=\frac{1}{2}.

For fixed
\boldsymbol{f_{0}},\boldsymbol{f_{1}}
, since
|\boldsymbol{e_{0}}|=|\boldsymbol{e_{1}}|=\frac{1}{2}
, the minimum is attained when

\boldsymbol{e_{0}}
is aligned with
p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}+\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{e_{1}}
is aligned with

p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}-\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}}
. Hence

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})=\frac{1}{2}-p_{1}|p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}+\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|-\widetilde{p_{1}}|p_{2}\boldsymbol{f_{0}}-\widetilde{p_{2}}\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|.

Now let

t:=4\,\boldsymbol{f_{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\in[-1,1].

Since
|\boldsymbol{f_{0}}|=|\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|=\frac{1}{2}

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(p_{1}\sqrt{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}+2p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}\,t}+\widetilde{p_{1}}\sqrt{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}-2p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}\,t}\Big).

Thus it remains to minimize the above
over
t\in[-1,1]
.

Therefore the interior optimum is attained precisely under the above symmetric
condition.

We see that critical point
t=t^{*}
satisfies

\frac{p_{1}}{\sqrt{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}+2p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}t^{*}}}=\frac{\widetilde{p_{1}}}{\sqrt{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}-2p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}t^{*}}},

which yields

t^{*}=\left(\frac{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}}{2p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{p_{1}^{2}-\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2}}{p_{1}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2}}\right).

t^{*}
is feasible if and only if

\left|\left(\frac{p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2}}{2p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{p_{1}^{2}-\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2}}{p_{1}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2}}\right)\right|\leq 1.

Squaring both sides and rearranging, this is equivalent to

\left|(2p_{1}-1)(2p_{2}-1)\right|\leq 4p_{1}\widetilde{p_{1}}\,p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}}.

If this condition holds, then substituting for
t^{*}
gives

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(p_{1}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2})(p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2})}.

(26)

If
t^{*}\notin[-1,1]

points
t=\pm 1

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})=\begin{cases}\widetilde{p_{1}}\widetilde{p_{2}},&p_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2},\\[2.84526pt]
\widetilde{p_{1}}p_{2},&p_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2},\end{cases}

whereas for

p_{1}p_{2},&p_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Thus, in the boundary case,

J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})=\min\{p_{1}p_{2},\widetilde{p_{1}}p_{2},p_{1}\widetilde{p_{2}},\widetilde{p_{1}}\widetilde{p_{2}}\}=J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{C}}(p_{1},p_{2}),

from Theorem

Therefore, in this case,

\min_{\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}}J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(p_{1}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{1}}^{2})(p_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{2}}^{2})},&\text{if}\left|(2p_{1}-1)(2p_{2}-1)\right|\leq 4p_{1}\widetilde{p_{1}}\,p_{2}\widetilde{p_{2}},\\[8.61108pt]
J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{C}}(p_{1},p_{2}),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(27)

where the minimum is over
\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}
satisfying Case 1. Notice that the RHS of (

) no greater than
J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{C}}(p_{1},p_{2})

We now examine if projective strategies are team optimal for the dynamic information structure.

Theorem 3.6
.

For a dynamic information structure, the optimal expected cost under quantum strategies is given by

J^{\mathsf{D*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{s},p_{c})=\min\left\{{\textstyle{1\over 2}}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\sqrt{p_{s}^{2}+\widetilde{p_{s}}^{2}},J^{\mathsf{D*}}_{\mathsf{C}}(p_{s},p_{c})\right\}.

p_{s},p_{c}

4
Other Solution Concepts

We have seen that for both static and dynamic information structures, when we optimize over the space of general quantum strategies, the optimal quantum strategy always turns out to be either projective or classical in nature. We now analyse two further solution concepts, formally defined below, concerning these classes for the static and dynamic information structures.

Definition 4.1
.

For a strategy class
\mathsf{A}
, the property of best response over
\mathsf{A}
holds for the static information structure, if for all
p_{1},p_{2}
the following holds:

•

For any
\textbf{{E}}\in\mathsf{A},\exists\textbf{{F}}^{*}\in\mathsf{A}
such that

•

For any
\textbf{{F}}\in\mathsf{A},\exists\textbf{{E}}^{*}\in\mathsf{A}
such that

J_{\mathsf{Q}}^{\mathsf{S}}(\textbf{{E}}^{*},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})\leq J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})\quad\forall\textbf{{E}}\in\mathsf{Q},

That is, for each player
i
, and each strategy belonging to
\mathsf{A}
of this player, there is an optimal response of the other player over strategies in
\mathsf{Q}
, also belonging to
\mathsf{A}
. We say
\mathsf{A}
has the property of best response for the dynamic information structure if the above holds with the replacement
\mathsf{S}\mapsto\mathsf{D}
and
(p_{1},p_{2})\mapsto(p_{s},p_{c})
.

Definition 4.2
.

For some strategy class
\mathsf{A}
, the property of person-by-person optimality over
\mathsf{A}
holds for the static information structure, if for all
p_{1},p_{2}
there exists
\textbf{{E}}^{*},\textbf{{F}}^{*}\in\mathsf{A}

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we explored the CHSH game through the lens of information structures in stochastic teams. Our main findings, summarized in Table
2
, highlight how the choice between a static and dynamic information structure significantly alters the landscape of optimality.
In the static setting, projective strategies are team optimal for all probability distributions. In contrast, for the dynamic information structure, the region of quantum advantage is notably smaller because the optimal cost achieved by projective strategies is independent of the channel noise. Most importantly, we found that there exist certain probability distributions in the dynamic game where projective strategies perform worse than classical ones, creating a region of strict quantum disadvantage.

Furthermore, we investigated the properties of person-by-person optimality and best response over strategy classes. While projective strategies enjoy these properties in the static game, we demonstrated that they do not universally carry over to the dynamic variant. Ultimately, our results shed light on the subtle and fascinating role played by the information structure, demonstrating the fragility of well-known quantum advantages when the flow of information between agents changes.

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}+\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}f^{0}_{1}.

Thus

J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{s},p_{c})=-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}+\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{0}}+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}f^{0}_{1}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}.

(43)

Now

\left|{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\right|\leq{\textstyle{1\over 2}},

since
|\boldsymbol{e_{0}}|=|\boldsymbol{e_{1}}|={\textstyle{1\over 2}}
. Also, for any POVM element
F_{1}
,

|\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|\leq\min\{f^{0}_{1},1-f^{0}_{1}\}\leq f^{0}_{1}.

Hence, by Cauchy-Schwartz,

{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\leq\left|{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\right|\,|\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|\leq{\textstyle{1\over 2}}|\boldsymbol{f_{1}}|\leq{\textstyle{1\over 2}}f^{0}_{1}.

Therefore

{\textstyle{1\over 2}}f^{0}_{1}-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(\boldsymbol{e_{0}}-\boldsymbol{e_{1}})\cdot\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\geq 0.

So, for fixed
E_{0},E_{1}
, the
F_{1}
-dependent part of
J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{s},p_{c})
is always nonnegative, and is
minimized by taking

f^{0}_{1}=0,\qquad\boldsymbol{f_{1}}=0,

that is,

F_{1}=O.

Thus, for every projective strategy of Arjuna, there is an optimal response of Bhima with
F_{1}=O
,
and hence that response is not projective.

Therefore, when
p_{s}={\textstyle{1\over 2}}
and
p_{c}=1
, there does not exist a pair of projective strategies
that are person-by-person optimal. Hence, the property of person-by-person optimality over
projective strategies does not hold for the dynamic information structure.

A.2
Invariance of Bell Costs

As claimed in Section
2.4
, we now show that the team optimal costs do not change across the different Bell costs. So our analysis done for
\mathcal{B}_{1}
does not need to be repeated for the other costs, as the same results hold.

Now, for a general
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, the expressions for all Bell costs will be the same as (
5a
) and (
5b
) but for the corresponding cost.

Definition A.1
.

The expected costs under quantum strategies for the static and dynamic information structure, for a general Bell cost
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, are:

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})(\mathcal{B}_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2}}[\mathcal{B}_{k}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2})]

(44a)

\displaystyle J^{\mathsf{D}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{s},p_{c})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2}}[\mathcal{B}_{k}(u_{1},u_{2};y_{1},y_{2})],

(44b)

where the joint probability distributions are given earlier in
2.1
(Equations
7
and
8
).

We show that the optimal quantum cost is invariant across the eight Bell costs

\mathcal{B}_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,8
.

Static information structure.

The Bell costs
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2},\mathcal{B}_{3},\mathcal{B}_{4}
are obtained from each
other by relabeling the inputs
y_{1},y_{2}
. For example,
\mathcal{B}_{2}(u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},y_{2})=\mathcal{B}_{1}(u_{1},u_{2},y_{1},\overline{y_{2}})
. These relabelings correspond to exchanging

E_{0}
with
E_{1}
, or
F_{0}
with
F_{1}
, and replacing
p_{1}
by
\widetilde{p_{1}}
or
p_{2}
by
\widetilde{p_{2}}
.
Since the optimization in (
21
) is over all admissible strategies, and the expression
J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}
is invariant with replacing
p_{1}
by
\widetilde{p_{1}}
or
p_{2}
by
\widetilde{p_{2}}
,
the optimal value
remains unchanged. Hence

J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{1}]=J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{2}]=J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{3}]=J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{4}].

The remaining Bell costs are complements of these four. For example
{\cal B}_{5}=\overline{{\cal B}}_{1}
and therefore
J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})[{\cal B}_{5}]=1-J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})[{\cal B}_{1}]
. Replacing
F_{j}

I-F_{j}
in

\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}}

\displaystyle 1-J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{1}](E_{0},E_{1},F_{0},F_{1},p_{1},p_{2}))=J^{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\textbf{{E}},\textbf{{F}};p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{1}](E_{0},E_{1},I-F_{0},I-F_{1},p_{1},p_{2})

J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{1}]=J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{2}]=\cdots=J^{\mathsf{S*}}_{\mathsf{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2})[\mathcal{B}_{8}].

Dynamic information structure.

22
). Relabeling
y_{1}
and
y_{2}

with
E_{1}

F_{0}

F_{1}

by

or

A.3
POVMs are sufficient to capture all strategy functions

In all the above considerations we assumed that the players choose their actions directly based on the measurement outcome
m_{i}
of the measurements, that is
u_{i}=m_{i}
. It is important to check if searching over POVMs automatically captures choosing action
u_{i}=f(m_{i},y_{i})
for all
f:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
(as mentioned in
2.3.2
). Suppose Arjuna plays the strategy
E_{0},E_{1}
for
u_{i}=m_{i}
, then for all such functions, the equivalent POVMs would be as given by the table below

\begin{array}[]{ |c|c|c| }\hline\cr f(m_{1},y_{1})&\text{ equivalent }E_{0}&\text{ equivalent }E_{1}\\
\hline\cr 0&I&I\\
1&0&0\\
y_{1}&I&0\\
\overline{y_{1}}&0&I\\
m_{1}&E_{0}&E_{1}\\
\overline{m_{1}}&I-E_{0}&I-E_{1}\\
m_{1}\oplus y_{1}&E_{0}&I-E_{1}\\
\overline{m_{1}\oplus y_{1}}&I-E_{0}&E_{1}\\
m_{1}+y_{1}&E_{0}&0\\
m_{1}+\overline{y_{1}}&0&E_{1}\\
\overline{m_{1}}+y_{1}&I-E_{0}&0\\
\overline{m_{1}}+\overline{y_{1}}&0&I-E_{1}\\
m_{1}y_{1}&I&E_{1}\\
m_{1}\overline{y_{1}}&E_{0}&I\\
\overline{m_{1}}y_{1}&I&I-E_{1}\\
\overline{m_{1}}\overline{y_{1}}&I-E_{0}&I\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Thus all equivalent
E_{0},E_{1}
are valid POVMs. Hence our POVM optimization also optimizes over all such functions of
m_{1}
and
y_{1}
.
