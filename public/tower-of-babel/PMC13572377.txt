Age-related differences in alpha power for distractor inhibition during visual working memory
Authors: Sabrina Sghirripa, Lynton Graetz, John G Semmler, Rebecca Sutton, Ellen E R Williams, Mitchell R Goldsworthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs working memory (WM) is limited in capacity, neural resources must be directed towards task-relevant, and away from task-irrelevant information. Alpha oscillations (8–12 Hz) have been implicated in distractor inhibition during WM retention in younger adults, but it is unclear if alpha oscillations also support distractor inhibition in older adults. We recorded electroencephalography while 24 younger (aged 18–35) and 24 older (aged 60–86) adults completed a modified delay match-to-sample task where distractors of varying strength appeared during the retention period. We found: (1) strong distractors impaired WM performance in both age groups, but only older adults were impaired by weak distractors, relative to the no distractor condition; (2) younger adults demonstrated robust increases in alpha power from baseline during retention, while older adults showed little evidence for a change from fixation; (3) both age groups showed lower alpha power when anticipating distractors compared to no-distractor trials, but younger adults more strongly modulated this response based on distractor strength; and (4) higher pre-distractor alpha power was associated with better task performance in younger adults. Our results suggest age-related differences in both WM retention strategies and anticipatory distractor processing, each of which may contribute to older adults’ greater susceptibility to distraction during visual WM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59189-0.Keywords: Alpha oscillations, Cognitive ageing, Distractor inhibition, EEG, Working memorySubject terms: Cognitive ageing, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Visual working memory (WM) is severely limited in capacity1, highlighting the importance of encoding and retaining task-relevant, and ignoring task-irrelevant or distracting information. An inability to inhibit distracting information is one suggested basis for age-related reductions in WM performance2, but the neural mechanisms underlying age-related deficits in ignoring distractions during WM are unclear.

Neural oscillations in the alpha (8–12 Hz) frequency range have been implicated in distractor inhibition during WM performance in younger adults3–5. While it was initially thought that increases in alpha power reflected ‘cortical idling’6, the modulation of alpha power has now been proposed to dynamically gate sensory input to task-relevant brain regions7. Evidence linking alpha power to distraction inhibition during visual WM was derived from tasks where participants attend to a task-relevant and inhibit a task irrelevant hemifield. These paradigms are associated with decreases in alpha power in the task-relevant, and increases in alpha power in the task-irrelevant hemifield, implicating alpha power in both the facilitation of visual WM performance and inhibition of task-irrelevant information4.

While the above evidence comes primarily from spatially lateralised visual WM paradigms, alpha modulation has also been investigated in the context of distractor anticipation, largely using verbal WM tasks. During the retention period of verbal WM tasks where participants can anticipate the onset of a distractor, anticipation of strong distractors has been associated with greater alpha power prior to the onset relative to alpha power in anticipation of a weak distractor3. Using a verbal WM paradigm, we have previously reported an increase in alpha power that did not differ based on distractor type5, while others using visual WM tasks have reported lower alpha power in the presence of strong distractors present for the entire retention period8.

Alpha oscillations reduce in power and frequency with age9, and given that age-related deficits in distractor inhibition may account for age-related deficits in WM performance2, a link may exist between age-related changes in alpha oscillatory activity and deficits in distractor inhibition during WM. In a study employing a lateralised task to investigate age-differences in alpha power for the suppression of irrelevant information during WM, it was observed that while suppression of visual processing was associated with modulation of alpha power in younger adults, older adults did not modulate alpha power, despite performance indicating that suppression of distractors had occurred10.

However, older adults demonstrate greater performance declines when distractors match the same category of stimulus as the memory set, suggesting that the strength of the distractor is important in age-related declines in distractor inhibition11–13. Despite studies in younger adults reporting differences in alpha power modulation with distractor strength3, no study has investigated whether older adults also modulate alpha power in anticipation of distractors of different strength.

Here, we employed a modified delay match-to-sample task with strong, weak and no distractor conditions to determine whether alpha power is modulated in anticipation of distractors of varying strength in younger and older adults. As older adults have been shown to demonstrate poorer performance under higher WM loads relative to younger adults14, we aimed to isolate and directly test the effect of distractor strength across age groups by individualising WM load to each participant, ensuring that any observed differences in distractor inhibition cannot be attributed simply to task difficulty. We hypothesised that: (1) WM performance in the presence of distractors would be more impaired by strong and weak distractors in older, compared with younger adults, (2) that younger adults would show stronger alpha power before the onset of strong, compared with weak distractors, while older adults would show no differences in alpha power between conditions, and (3) that stronger alpha power in the lead up to the distractor would be associated with better WM performance in the presence of distractors.

Method

Participants

24 healthy younger (mean age: 23.36 years, SD: 5.83 years, range:18–35, 22 female) and 24 healthy older adults (mean age: 69.96 years, SD: 6.94 years, range: 60–86, 17 female) participated in the study. The samples in each group were similar for years of education (median difference = 0.62 years). All older adults were without cognitive impairment as assessed by Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination III (ACE-III) (score > 82)15.

Exclusion criteria involved a history of neurological/psychiatric disease, use of central nervous system altering medications, history of alcohol/substance abuse and uncorrected hearing/visual impairment (including colour blindness). All participants gave informed written consent before the commencement of the study, and the experiment was approved by the University of Adelaide Human Research Ethics Committee (H-2019-154). All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Working memory task

Participants first completed a WM load adjustment task consisting of 20 trials at each of load-2, load-3, load-4, load-5 and load-6 with no distractors present. This task was identical to the no distractor condition in the main task, therefore training the participant on the task and allowing us to adjust the WM load for each participant individually before partaking in the EEG task. The load for each participant was chosen based on the load where ~ 80% accuracy was achieved. By calibrating each participant to ~ 80% accuracy in the no-distractor condition, we created comparable baseline WM performance levels between groups. This approach allowed us to isolate the specific effects of distractors on WM performance from baseline capacity differences, ensuring that any observed age-related differences in distractor cost reflect differences in distractor suppression mechanisms rather than age differences in task difficulty. This methodological decision is consistent with approaches used in previous studies investigating age differences in attentional processes during visual WM16.

The distractor visual WM task used stimuli presented by PsychoPy17 (Fig. 1A). Each trial began with the participant fixating on a cross in the centre of the screen for 2 s. A memory set consisting of six squares then appeared for 0.5 s to maintain equal sensory input for each WM load. The position of squares did not change within trials but varied between trials. The number of coloured squares depended on the WM load chosen in the load adjustment task, and unfilled squares were indicated with a black outline. Each square subtended 0.65° × 0.65° of visual angle, and there were 12 unique colours in the stimulus set (blue, green, red, orange, yellow, pink, purple, turquoise, magenta, brown, beige, black). Following a consolidation period of 0.25 s, a pattern mask consisting of coloured static appeared for 0.15 s in the same location as each item presented in the memory set. We chose to include a mask and consolidation period to disrupt residual sensory trace left by the memory set from influencing oscillatory power during the retention period18. Following the mask, a 4 s retention period began. In the distractor blocks, 2 s into the retention period a strong (4 coloured squares not in the memory set) or weak distractor was shown (4 light grey squares) for 0.5 s in a fixed position, followed by the 1.5 s remainder of the retention period. Grey squares were chosen to provide equivalent low-level visual input whilst minimising interference with the memory set. In the no distractor condition, only the fixation cross was present for 4 s. A single probe coloured square then appeared in the location of a randomly selected square from the memory set. Participants were instructed to respond with the right arrow key if the probe square was the same colour in the memory set and respond with the left arrow key if the square was a different colour. In each block, 50% of the trials required a right arrow key response (same colour), and 50% required a left arrow key response (different colour). The probe remained on the screen until the subject responded.

In the task, there were 5 blocks with strong distractors, 5 blocks with weak distractors and 5 blocks with no distractor. The order of the blocks was randomised between participants. Blocks consisted of 20 trials (total = 100 trials per distractor condition), with the blocked design allowing participants to anticipate the strength of the distractor within blocks. Distractors were never part of the memory set or probe, and participants were explicitly asked to ignore the distractor. Short breaks were allowed between blocks.

EEG data acquisition

EEG data were recorded from a 64-channel cap containing Ag/AgCl scalp electrodes arranged in a 10–10 layout (Waveguard, ANT Neuro, Enschede, The Netherlands) using a Polybench TMSi EEG system (Twente Medical Systems International B.V, Oldenzaal, The Netherlands). Conductive gel was inserted into each electrode using a blunt-needle syringe to reduce impedance to < 5 kΩ. The ground electrode was located at AFz. Signals were amplified 20x, online filtered (DC-553 Hz) and sampled at 2048 Hz. Due to the lack of data from the mastoids, data were referenced to the average of all electrodes. EEG was recorded during each block of the distractor visual WM task.

EEG data pre-processing

EEG data were pre-processed using EEGLAB19, TMS-EEG Signal Analyser (TESA v1.1.1)20 and custom scripts using MATLAB (R2020b, The Mathworks, USA). Each block of EEG data was merged, and incorrect trials were flagged for removal at a later stage. Unused channels were removed, data were downsampled to 256 Hz and then band-pass (0.1–100 Hz) and band-stop (48–52 Hz) filtered using the EEGLAB ‘eegfiltnew’ function. Data were epoched − 2 to 6 s relative to the onset of the memory set. Channels and trials were then visually inspected and removed if contaminated with residual artifacts (e.g. muscle activity or non-stereotypical artifacts). An average of 1 channel was removed from the younger adult group, and 2 were removed from the older adult group (range young: 0–7, range old: 0–6). Independent component analysis (ICA) was then completed using the FastICA algorithm21, with the ‘symmetric approach’ and ‘tahn’ contrast functions selected. Components corresponding to eye-blinks and persistent muscle activity were detected using the TESA20 ‘compselect’ function and were manually inspected before removal from the data. Missing channels were then interpolated, and data were re-referenced to the common average. Epochs were then split into distractor types and correct and incorrect trials were separated.

For younger adults, an average of 76 trials for the no distractor condition, 76 for the weak distractor condition and 72 for the strong distractor condition were accepted for further analysis. For older adults, an average of 73 trials for the no distractor condition, 72 for the weak distractor condition and 67 for the strong distractor condition were accepted for further analysis.

Spectral analysis

Spectral analysis was performed using FieldTrip toolbox22. Data were converted to the time–frequency domain using a multi-taper transformation based on multiplication in the frequency domain (cfg.method = ‘mtmconvol’). A time window of 3 cycles was used for each frequency (0.5 Hz steps between 3 and 45 Hz) and time point (50 ms steps) from the start of fixation until the probe (− 2 s to 5 s). Spectral power was calculated for individual trials before being averaged over trials for each distractor condition in each age group. Data were baseline corrected − 1 to − 0.25 s relative to the onset of the memory set.

Following spectral analysis, we visually inspected the grand average time–frequency representation of power (averaged across all participants, conditions, and electrodes) to identify the most relevant time–frequency window for our hypothesised alpha power modulation during distractor anticipation. This allowed us to select relevant time and frequency ranges for subsequent statistical analyses without biasing our statistical analysis based on condition differences.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1. Bayesian linear mixed effects models were used to analyse both behavioural and EEG data, implemented using rstanarm (version 2.32.6) (Goodrich et al. 2020) and bayestestR (version 0.14.0)23. The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method was used to estimate the posterior distribution, and all models were run using the default priors in the rstanarm package. A total of 80,000 iterations on 4 chains were run with 40,000 discarded for burn-in in each chain, yielding 40,000 posterior samples per chain. Convergence was assessed using the potential scale reduction factor (Rhat), with values < 1.01 indicating successful convergence 24. Post-hoc comparisons were investigated using emmeans (version 1.10.3) which derived contrasts from the posterior distribution of the fitted model, and all 95% credible intervals were calculated using the highest density interval (HDI) method.

We report two complementary measures of evidence strength that allowed us to separately evaluate whether (1) the effects exist in a certain direction, and (2) whether they are large enough to be meaningful. First, the probability of direction (pd) quantifies the certainty that an effect exists in a particular direction (i.e. positive or negative). This measure correlates with frequentist p-values, with a pd value of 97.5% approximately corresponding to a frequentist p-value of 0.0523. The following reference values for interpreting the strength of effect existence were used: pd > 97.5%, a likely effect; pd > 99%, a probable effect; and pd > 99.9%, a certain effect25.

Second, we evaluated the magnitude of effects by examining whether they exceeded a minimal threshold for practical importance. We defined a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) as ± 0.1*SD of the outcome variable23, representing a threshold below which effects were considered negligible in the context of behavioural science26. Effects were then classified based on the proportion of their 95% HDI overlapping this ROPE. Effects with more than 99% of their HDI within the ROPE were considered trivial, and those exceeding 97.5% overlap were considered probably trivial. Effects with less than 1% of their HDI within the ROPE were considered non-trivial, and those with less than 2.5% overlap were considered probably non-trivial. Effects where 2.5–97.5% of the HDI fell within the ROPE were classified as inconclusive, indicating that the data neither confirmed nor ruled out a meaningful effect size25. Importantly, an inconclusive result reflects uncertainty about the effect’s magnitude rather than evidence for its absence. For example, an effect might show strong evidence for existence (pd > 99%) but uncertainty as to whether the magnitude of the effect is large enough to be meaningfully important. (e.g., 15% in ROPE).

Data are presented as median ± 95% HDI in figures and median [95% HDI] in text, unless indicated otherwise.

Analysis of behavioural data

Behavioural models included age group and distractor condition as fixed effects, with participant as a random effect. Separate models were run with accuracy and reaction time (RT) as outcome variables. To assess group differences in working memory capacity, we conducted a Bayesian ordinal regression on the individualised load levels (3, 4, or 5 items) using stan_polr with default priors.

Analysis of EEG data

Initial analyses of EEG data were performed using the FieldTrip toolbox22, using cluster-based permutation tests to control for the type-1 error rate when comparing across multiple channels, times, and frequencies27. Detailed methods and results from the cluster-based permutation tests can be found in supplementary material.

To model the full factorial design (rather than relying on difference scores for interactions) and quantify the strength and certainty of effects, Bayesian mixed effects models were applied to the EEG data. For these analyses, alpha power was quantified as the average across the 9–12 Hz frequency range and the 0.75 s prior to distractor onset at each electrode, which was based on examining the time–frequency representation of power averaged across both age groups and all distractor conditions from all participants, and the average of all electrodes. For Bayesian mixed effects models, electrodes were grouped a-priori into three defined regions of interest (ROI) determined according to the 10–10 layout of the EEG cap: frontal (FP1, FPz, FP2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, AF7, AF3, AF4, AF8, F5, F1, F2, F6, FT7, FT8), central (FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6, T7, C3, Cz, C4, T8, CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6, FC3, FCz, FC4, C5, C1, C2, C6, CP3, CPz, CP4, TP7, TP8), and parieto-occipital (P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, POz, O1, Oz, O2, P5, P1, P2, P6, PO5, PO3, PO4, PO6, PO7, PO8).

The primary model included age group, distractor condition, and ROI as fixed effects, with participant and electrode as random effects, and baseline corrected alpha power as the outcome. Two additional models were also run, the first of which used raw alpha power as the outcome, age group, distractor condition, ROI, and time segment (fixation or pre-distractor) as fixed effects and the same random effect structure, to determine age differences at fixation and whether changes in pre-distractor alpha power from fixation existed. An additional model examining correct versus incorrect trials included response type, age group, distractor type and ROI as fixed effects, with the same random effects structure.

A comparison of the results obtained using both cluster-based permutation tests and Bayesian mixed effects models can be found in section S2.5. Additionally, to assess whether the observed results could be attributed to differences in individualised WM load, sensitivity analyses were conducted in the subset of participants completing the task at load-4 (9 younger, 13 older adults) and are reported in supplementary section S2.6.

Results

Behavioural data

WM load adjustment

Bayesian ordinal regression revealed strong evidence that younger adults performed the task at higher loads than older adults (median difference = 2.32 [1.00, 3.72], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE), confirming that the individualised load levels appropriately captured age-related differences in working memory capacity. In the younger adult group, 9 participants completed the task at load-4 and 15 at load-5, while in the older adult group, 8 participants completed the task at load-3, 13 at load-4, and 3 at load-5.

RT

The model predicting RT revealed that younger adults were faster to respond than older adults in the no distractor condition (median difference = − 0.49 s [− 0.65, − 0.34], pd = 100%), however there was little evidence that this difference was modulated by distractor condition for either strong (pd = 60.52%, 81.14% in ROPE) or weak distractors (pd = 70.53%, 75.86% in ROPE). Post-hoc comparisons collapsed across age group revealed that RT was slower in the strong distractor compared to no distractor condition (median difference = 0.08 s [0.05, 0.11], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE), and slower in the weak compared to no distractor condition, although the magnitude was inconclusive (0.04 s [0.01, 0.07], pd = 98.95%, 56.94% in ROPE). RT was also slower in the strong compared to weak distractor condition, although inconclusive in magnitude (0.05 s [0.02, 0.08], pd = 99.91%, 23.84% in ROPE).

Accuracy

The model predicting accuracy revealed no age difference in the no-distractor condition (median difference = 0.68% [− 3.71, 5.07], pd = 62.11%, 28.64% in ROPE). The model provided some evidence that the cost of strong distractors on accuracy was smaller in younger relative to older adults (median difference = 3.33% [− 0.29, 6.94], pd = 96.17%, 6.11% in ROPE), but this did not meet the threshold for a likely effect. There was little evidence for a comparable interaction with weak distractors (median difference = 2.45% [− 1.19, 6.06], pd = 90.91%, 15.37% in ROPE) (Fig. 1B).

Given our a-priori hypothesis that ageing specifically impacts the cost of distraction, and the successful matching of baseline WM performance through individual load adjustment, we examined distractor effects within each age group relative to their no-distractor baseline. Older adults showed a 7.67% decrease in accuracy [5.11, 10.25] (pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE) in the strong distractor condition compared to the no-distractor condition, while younger adults showed a 4.34% decrease [1.80, 6.93] (pd = 99.93%, 0% in ROPE). In weak distractor trials, older adults showed an accuracy decline of 3.25% [0.62, 5.74] (pd = 99.30%, 0% in ROPE) relative to the no-distractor condition, whereas younger adults showed little evidence of a behavioural effect of the weak distractor (median difference = − 0.79% [− 3.38, 1.73], pd = 72.95%, 5.51% in ROPE) (Fig. 1C). Finally, accuracy was lower in the strong compared with weak distractor condition for both younger (median difference = − 3.55 [− 6.06, − 0.95], pd = 99.6%, 0% in ROPE) and older adults (median difference = − 4.42 [− 6.96, − 1.87], pd = 99.9%, 0% in ROPE).

EEG analysis

Our a-priori hypothesis was that increases in alpha power would be present prior to the onset of distractors. Visual inspection revealed that the largest alpha power was observed in the 0.75 s prior to the onset of the distractor in the 9–12 Hz frequency range (Supplementary Fig. 1). We used this predefined time–frequency window of interest for Bayesian mixed effects models, and cluster-based permutation tests (reported in Supplementary Material S2). The two approaches largely converged and key points of comparison are noted throughout (for full cluster-based permutation results, see Supplementary Material S2).

Fixation period and changes from baseline

To ensure age-group analyses using baseline corrected data were not biased by age-related differences in oscillatory power, we first sought to determine whether age-differences in oscillatory power were present in the fixation period used for baseline correction. The model revealed that age-related differences in alpha power during the fixation period approached but did not reach our threshold for a likely effect (pd = 94.62%, 12.58% in ROPE).

However, the model revealed a certain effect of age group on the change in alpha power from fixation to pre-distractor period in the parieto-occipital, relative to the central region (median difference = 4.97 [2.98, 6.88], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE). Post-hoc contrasts demonstrated strong evidence for increased alpha power from baseline in all conditions in the parieto-occipital region in younger adults (strong: median difference = 5.57 [4.57, 6.76]; weak: median difference = 5.21 [4.15, 6.32]; no: median difference = 5.57 [4.57, 6.76]; all pd = 100%; all 0% in ROPE). Conversely, in older adults, there was little evidence for a difference in alpha power between fixation and the pre-distractor period (all pd < 96%, > 63% in ROPE).

Age and distractor effects on anticipatory alpha power

The model with baseline-corrected alpha power as the outcome revealed evidence for overall age-related differences, specifically in the parieto-occipital ROI, where younger adults (median = 0.57 [− 0.03, 1.17]) demonstrated higher alpha power than older adults (median = − 0.65 [− 1.25, − 0.06]; pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE). The model also revealed evidence for an interaction between age group and distractor type, with younger adults showing greater decreases in alpha power in anticipation of weak distractors, relative to the no distractor condition, than older adults (median difference = − 0.21 [− 0.36, − 0.06], pd = 99.69%), although the effect magnitude was inconclusive (31.66% in ROPE) (Fig. 2A). Cluster-based permutation tests similarly confirmed significant age differences in alpha power across all distractor conditions (all p ≤ 0.02), though an interaction between age group and distractor type was not detected (see Supplementary S2.2–2.3).

Given the presence of age-related effects that varied by ROI and condition, we conducted post-hoc comparisons to understand the differences across conditions and regions both between and within age groups.

Between age groups, older adults showed the strongest evidence for reduced alpha power relative to younger adults in the parieto-occipital region (no distractor: median difference = − 1.21 [− 2.03, − 0.37]; strong distractor: median difference = − 1.35 [− 2.18, − 0.52]; weak distractor: median difference = − 1.12 [− 1.96, − 0.30]; all pd > 99.48%; all 0% in ROPE) (Fig. 2B-C-F). In the frontal and central regions, despite the probability of direction trending towards possible effects (pd range: 89.88–97.05%), the magnitude of age differences was inconclusive (all % in ROPE > 4.47%) (Fig. 2D-E).

Within younger adults, alpha power was higher in the no distractor compared to the weak distractor condition in all ROIs (parieto-occipital: median difference = 0.48 [0.36, 0.60]; central: median difference = 0.47 [0.37, 0.58]; frontal: median difference = 0.39 [0.27, 0.51]; all pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE) (Fig. 2G-H-I). Alpha power was also higher in the no, compared to the strong distractor condition in the parieto-occipital region (median difference = 0.13 [0.01, 025], pd = 98.45%) and central regions (median difference = 0.20 [0.10, 0.31], pd = 98.45%), but the magnitude of these effects was inconclusive (both > 30.8% in ROPE). However, the effect approached but did not meet the threshold for existence in the frontal region (median difference = 0.11 [− 0.01, 0.24], pd = 96.15, 86.25% in ROPE). Finally, alpha power was higher in the strong compared to weak distractor condition in all ROIs (parieto-occipital: median difference = 0.35 [0.23, 0.47], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE; central: median difference = 0.27 [0.16, 0.37], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE; frontal: median difference = 0.28 [0.16, 0.41], pd = 100%, 2.32% in ROPE). Cluster-based permutation tests corroborated the significant difference between strong and weak distractor conditions in younger adults (p = 0.03; Supplementary Material S2.3).

Within older adults, alpha power was higher in anticipation of no relative to strong distractors across all regions, although effect magnitude was inconclusive in all ROIs (parietal-occipital: median difference = 0.28 [0.16, 0.40], frontal: median difference = 0.19 [0.06, 0.31]; central: median difference = 0.21 [0.10, 0.32]; all pd > 99.86%; all > 2.80% in ROPE) (Fig. 2G-H-I). Like younger adults, alpha power was higher in the no distractor compared to weak distractor condition across all regions (all pd = 100%). The magnitude of these effects was non-trivial in the parieto-occipital (median difference = 0.39 [0.27, 0.51], 0% in ROPE) and frontal regions (median difference = 0.36 [0.24, 0.49], 0% in ROPE) but inconclusive in the central region (median difference = 0.26 [0.15, 0.37], 3.62% in ROPE). Unlike younger adults, the difference in alpha power between strong and weak distractor conditions did not meet the threshold for effect existence and were of either trivial or inconclusive magnitude in older adults across the central (median difference = 0.05 [− 0.06, 0.15], pd = 80.05%, 100% in ROPE) and parieto-occipital regions (median difference = 0.11 [− 0.01, 0.24], pd = 96.71%, 85.75% in ROPE). A probable effect was observed in the frontal region (median difference = 0.17 [0.05, 0.30], pd = 99.67%, 51.67% in ROPE), but the magnitude was inconclusive. Cluster-based permutation tests also did not find evidence for a difference between the strong and weak distractor condition in older adults (Supplementary S2.3).

Alpha power and task performance

We then examined the relationship between alpha power and task performance by comparing alpha power in anticipation of distractors on correct and incorrect trials. The model revealed that the effect of response type on alpha power differed by age group (median difference = − 0.48 [− 0.73, − 0.24], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE), and the effect of ROI also differed by age group (parieto-occipital vs central: median difference = 0.47 [0.27, 0.68], pd = 100%, 0% in ROPE). There was also probable evidence for an interaction between age group, response type, and distractor condition, which was most evident for the strong distractor condition (median difference = 0.37 [0.11, 0.66], pd = 99.5%), but the magnitude of effect was inconclusive (7.24% in ROPE). No statistically significant differences were found using cluster-based permutation tests (all p > 0.07; Supplementary S2.4).

Post-hoc comparisons revealed that in younger adults, correct trials were associated with higher anticipatory parieto-occipital alpha power than incorrect trials. This pattern was non-trivial in both the strong (median difference = 0.40 [0.24, 0.55]) and weak distractor conditions (median difference = 0.38 [0.22, 0.53]; both pd = 100%, both 0% in ROPE), and but of uncertain magnitude in the no-distractor condition (median difference = 0.28 [0.13, 0.43], pd = 99.95%, 9.68% in ROPE) (Fig. 3G). In the frontal ROI, effects were strong for both the weak and no-distractor conditions (median differences = 0.45 [0.29, 0.60] and 0.35 [0.19, 0.51], respectively; pd = 100%, < 1% in ROPE for both), but of inconclusive magnitude for the strong distractor condition (median difference = 0.25 [0.09, 0.41], pd = 99.88%, 19.79% in ROPE) (Fig. 3C). In the central region, certain effects in the same direction were observed across all conditions (strong distractor: 0.29 [0.15, 0.42]; weak distractor: 0.29 [0.14, 0.42]; no-distractor: 0.38 [0.24, 0.51]; all pd = 100%), though effect magnitude varied (6.92%, 5.29%, and 0% in ROPE, respectively) (Fig. 3E).

In contrast, in older adults the pattern reversed, and correct trials were preceded by lower alpha power than incorrect trials in the parieto-occipital ROI. This was evident for the no-distractor (median difference = − 0.19 [− 0.35, − 0.04], pd = 99.25%) and weak distractor conditions (median difference = − 0.18 [− 0.33, − 0.03], pd = 98.83%), although effect magnitude was inconclusive in both (all > 48.39% in ROPE). There was little evidence for an effect in the strong distractor condition (pd = 87.38%, 92.21% in ROPE) (Fig. 3H). A similar pattern was observed in the frontal ROI during the no-distractor condition (median difference = − 0.20 [− 0.37, − 0.06], pd = 99.52%, 43.13% in ROPE) (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, this reversed pattern was not observed in the central ROI during strong distractor trials, where older adults instead showed higher alpha power preceding correct relative to incorrect trials, though the effect size was inconclusive (median difference = 0.18 [0.05, 0.32], pd = 99.55%, 52.84% in ROPE) (Fig. 3F).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated age-related differences in alpha oscillatory power in anticipation of distractors of varying strength during the visual WM retention period. Our findings revealed that (1) older adults showed performance decrements for both strong and weak distractors, while younger adults were only impacted by strong distractors, relative to the no distractor condition; (2) each age group demonstrated distinct patterns of alpha power modulation during retention: younger adults showed consistent increases in alpha power from fixation, while older adults demonstrated little evidence of change from fixation; (3) the age groups differed in their anticipatory response to distractors, with younger adults modulating alpha power based on distractor strength, particularly in parieto-occipital regions, while older adults showed no practically important differences between distractor types, despite evidence for an effect in the frontal region; and (4) the relationship between alpha power and performance differed between groups, with younger adults demonstrating higher alpha power in correct, relative to incorrect trials.

We found that while both age groups showed performance decrements in the presence of strong distractors, only older adults demonstrated a practically important decline in performance in the weak distractor condition. Importantly, these differences in distractor cost emerged when WM load was individualised to each participant, suggesting that susceptibility to distractors cannot be fully explained by reduced WM capacity alone or differing WM resource demands between age groups. These findings provide support for extensive literature detailing the inhibitory deficit hypothesis, which suggests that older adults experience WM deficits due to an inability to inhibit task-irrelevant information2. Our results theoretically align with the “deletion” facet of the inhibitory deficit hypothesis that suggests older adults are more likely to allow distractors to enter the memory store, and then fail to delete the distractions even when they are aware that the information is irrelevant, such as when the distractors are predictable in strength and temporal onset like in our task2. Therefore, when the contents of the distractor directly compete with the memory store (i.e. strongly distracting), this decline in WM performance is greater.

To understand whether age differences in alpha power during the task can explain these behavioural differences, examining the no-distractor condition provides a contextual control for how age groups differ in their neural strategies during visual WM retention. In this condition, younger adults demonstrated robust increases in alpha power from fixation, while older adults showed a tendency toward decreased alpha power, with little evidence for a meaningful change from fixation. These opposing patterns demonstrate that younger and older adults potentially used different neural strategies for maintaining visual information in WM, independent of differences in distractor anticipation.

In younger adults, the increase in alpha power during no-distractor trials may reflect a transition from initial visual processing to a more direct maintenance strategy while maintaining top-down control over external visual processing. This interpretation aligns with studies demonstrating increases in alpha power during the retention period of verbal WM tasks, which is suggested to reflect inhibition of the visual stream even in the absence of visual distractors28–31. Alternatively, it is also possible that younger adults engaged a verbal rehearsal process during the task, leading to patterns of alpha power modulation like those seen in verbal paradigms.

In contrast, older adults appear to remain at fixation-level alpha power during visual WM retention, failing to show the recovery and subsequent increase seen in younger adults, potentially reflecting a weakening of top-down control mechanisms that would normally support a transition to active WM maintenance, or alternatively a compensatory strategy. These results are consistent with the findings of studies employing lateralised delay match-to-sample tasks, in which older adults demonstrate diminished alpha lateralisation16,32. Supporting this interpretation of different maintenance strategies or compensatory mechanisms, we found evidence that successful performance in the no-distractor condition was associated with lower alpha power in older adults, particularly in parieto-occipital regions, although this effect was of uncertain practical importance. However, this remains speculative and requires further investigation, potentially by repeating a non-lateralised visual delay match-to-sample task between age groups and manipulating load to understand the pattern of alpha modulation during visual WM maintenance.

These findings should also be considered in the context of ongoing debates about whether visual WM retention is characterised by increases or decreases in alpha power, with a recent systematic review demonstrating a ratio of studies finding 60/40% of increasing to decreasing alpha power with load during visual WM retention in younger adults33. Therefore, further work should be done to better characterise both the modulation of alpha power during visual WM and its functional relevance. Likewise, as all conditions were presented within the same session, it is possible that participants adopted a consistent maintenance strategy across all trial types, which may have influenced alpha power in the no-distractor condition. Further research separating the no-distractor condition into a separate session is required to clarify whether these patterns reflect WM retention strategies or are influenced by the broader task context involving distractor anticipation.

The alpha suppression observed during retention, if reflecting sustained visual processing, may help explain older adults’ greater vulnerability to both types of distractors. For example, older adults’ sustained visual engagement throughout retention, potentially due to an inability to recover from initial encoding alpha suppression or alternate compensatory strategies16, may leave their visual processing more susceptible to incoming visual information. However, a second key finding was that both age groups showed lower alpha power before weak distractors compared to no-distractor trials, suggesting that anticipating any visual input during WM retention induces some degree of alpha suppression. However, the groups diverged in how they modulated this response based on distractor strength.

In younger adults, we found that alpha power in anticipation of distractors was higher in the strong relative to the weak distractor condition, particularly in parieto-occipital region, with robust evidence linking higher alpha power to better WM performance on distractor trials. Our results align with those of Bonnefond and Jensen3, who found functionally relevant increases in alpha power in occipito-temporal areas in anticipation of strong, compared with weak distractors. While behavioural interference from strong distractors was still evident, the analysis of correct versus incorrect trials suggests that when successfully employed, higher alpha power was conducive to better performance in this condition. The ability to modulate alpha power based on anticipated distractor strength, and for this to support WM performance, suggests that younger adults may have effectively engaged anticipatory top-down control mechanisms to regulate the influence of distractors during visual WM retention34.

In contrast, older adults demonstrated decreases in alpha power in anticipation of distractors and showed less evidence for conclusive differences in alpha power between distractor types. This pattern suggests two possible, though not yet conclusively demonstrated, mechanisms that may contribute to increased distractor susceptibility in older adults. First, the potential inability to recover from encoding-related alpha suppression beyond fixation levels, which also is a task period associated with visual expectation, may leave older adults in a state of sustained visual engagement, as seen in the no-distractor condition. Second, they show further decreases in alpha power relative to the no distractor condition when expecting visual input35,36 but are unable to modulate this response based on the strength of expected distractors to a practically useful degree. These patterns conceptually align with the inhibitory deficit hypothesis2, extensive literature detailing deficits in top-down suppression37,38 and partially with the findings of Vaden et al.10, who reported that older adults did not modulate alpha power to suppress distractors. However, it is also possible that declines in alpha modulation and distractor filtering represent independent processes that both happen to covary with age, and further work is required to disentangle the two.

Interestingly however, in the central region during strong distractor trials, older adults showed directional evidence for higher alpha power on correct versus incorrect trials (like younger adults), and there was evidence for differences in alpha power existing between distractor types in frontal, but not parieto-occipital regions. This aligns with the posterior-anterior shift in ageing (PASA) model39, potentially reflecting compensatory recruitment of more anterior regions when posterior mechanisms are compromised. However, as both effects were inconclusive in magnitude, further research is needed to determine whether this pattern truly represents successful compensation in the context of distractor inhibition.

While our findings highlight important age-related differences in distractor suppression, an unexpected pattern in the context of existing literature emerged when comparing both distractor conditions to the no-distractor condition. As previously mentioned, both age groups showed lower alpha power before weak distractors compared to no-distractor trials, and a tendency towards lower alpha power in the strong distractor condition. While previous studies have compared alpha modulation between strong and weak distractors3,5, our inclusion of a no-distractor condition provides novel insights into how anticipatory alpha is modulated relative to typical WM retention states.

Our results suggest that alpha modulation in anticipation of distractors may reflect an interplay between WM retention states and preparatory visual processing. In the no-distractor condition, alpha power likely reflects the natural maintenance state during visual WM. When distractors are anticipated at temporally consistent intervals, alpha suppression occurs due to expected visual input35,36, but the extent of this suppression appears to be modulated based on anticipation of distractor strength. This interpretation may explain why alpha power was highest in no-distractor trials (no preparation for visual input needed), lowest before weak distractors (less strict control over suppression as the weak distractor is less “distracting”), and intermediate before strong distractors (tighter control over suppression to prevent distraction). However, further work should be done to understand whether these patterns are affected by features of the distractor, such as the number of distractors, spatial location and colour salience.

Together, these findings demonstrate that a combination of sustained visual engagement indexed by lower alpha power during visual WM retention, and reduced ability to modulate alpha power based on anticipated distractor strength, may explain older adults’ greater susceptibility to distractors during visual WM. Future work should now investigate how different maintenance strategies might influence susceptibility to distractors, as well as better characterise age-related differences in alpha power during visual WM and its functional relevance.
