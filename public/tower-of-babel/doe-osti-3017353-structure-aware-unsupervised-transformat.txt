The major goal of this project is to develop machine learning (ML) methods to enable improved
predictive power on real drug discovery for novel targets. More specifically, we planned to
demonstrate the capability and effectiveness of ML tools utilizing unlabeled large-volume
protein-ligand datasets. We investigated multiple pre-training approaches for 3D protein-ligand
structure-based foundation models, without relying on experimental binding data. We also
addressed scenarios in which crystal structures are unavailable or binding data are limited. We
also planned to develop a complete pipeline to screen novel compounds as well as to
demonstrate the capability and effectiveness of the developed methods by testing on a realistic
drug discovery task such as SARS-CoV-2. While the major goals and milestones remain
consistent with the original proposal, certain technical details have been adjusted, based on the
experimental results and related outcomes.

1. 3D complex structure data curation and release

One of the main goals of this project is to develop unsupervised or self-supervised machine
learning (ML) framework for 3D protein-ligand complex data. For this, we curated 3D complex
structure data using our in-house and publicly available datasets. We also created a benchmark
dataset for virtual screening use cases.

1.1 PDBspheres docking structures

PDBspheres is an in-house dataset of protein structure regions (spheres) adjacent to
complexed ligands derived from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). We extracted biologically
relevant targets, resulting in approximately 400K sphere complexes. Then, we filtered out
incorrect ligand positions, irrelevant ligands, and compounds containing Boron and heavy
metals (Mercury, Zinc). Then we used AutoDock Vina, orchestrated through in-house high-
throughput docking pipeline, ConveyorLC, to generate 10-20 docking poses per complex
structure entry. We used Molecular Operating Environment (MOE), and Amber for formal
charge calculation, Protein receptor cleaning, respectively. The docking process was performed
on Livermore Computing (LC), the high-performance computing environment at Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL). The entire process includes ligand protonation, formal
charge calculation, protein preparation, docking, RMSD calculation of each docking pose,
interaction feature extraction, and data conversion for ML training.

Once we curated the first version of the PDBspheres dataset with molecular docking structures,
we found several issues in this dataset such as duplicates due to multiple chains in a single
sphere, multiple ligands per sphere, and crystallization ingredients (crystallization additive,
surfactants). To resolve the issues, we re-curated the database by filtering out the incorrect
ligands as described above, resulting in 101,553 protein-ligand spheres with 2,029,998 docking
structures. Despite the reduction, the dataset remains approximately 5 times larger than the
latest PDBbind database. Figure 1 shows two examples of PDBspheres co-crystal structures
and their corresponding VINA docking poses.

We conducted unsupervised/semi-supervised model training using this database on LC,
including the pose classification and protein-ligand interaction feature prediction. Multiple model
evaluations indicated that ML models trained on this dataset perform worse than those trained
on the PDBbind dataset. These results suggest that additional curation – particularly to address
docking-related errors is required before the dataset can be made publicly available.
Accordingly, we curated the recently released SAIR dataset for additional pre-training.

1.2 SAIR featurization

It is essential to have large-scale 3D protein-ligand complex data to conduct unsupervised or
self-supervised model training. Due to the error and poor performance of the PDBspheres
dataset, we additionally curated Structurally Augmented IC50 Repository (SAIR) dataset for our
unsupervised ML training. SAIR is a public dataset of protein-ligand 3D structures paired with
binding potency measurements, sourced from the ChEMBL and BindingDB databases and
cofolded using the Boltz model. Given each structure entry, we used Open Drug Discovery
Toolkit (ODDT) and Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) to calculate the interaction
features between the protein and the ligand, such as hydrogen and hydrophobic bonds, as well
as their close contact distances. The extracted atomic and interaction features were then stored
as an atomic graph representation in the PyTorch geometric format. Together with our 3D
structure-based self-supervised ML approach, we are currently preparing a manuscript for
journal or workshop submission.

1.3 LLNL-vHTS: benchmark dataset for virtual screening use cases

Robust evaluation of trained ML models requires appropriate benchmark datasets. Although
several benchmarks are available for compound-only data such as MoleculeNet, relatively few
structure-based datasets with 3D information are available. We generated a new benchmark
dataset of 3D protein-ligand complex structures. This benchmark consists of 5 biologically
relevant single-target cases, each of which contains varying number of, but relatively few

 3

compound instances with experimental binding affinities (pIC50): ACHE, Mu-opioid, NMDA,
Sars-CoV-2 main protease, and Zika main protease.

For each target, we first used AlphaFold3 to predict the 3D structure of the protein receptor from
its amino acid sequence. We curated compound libraries with MOE to generate chemically valid
3D structures, including appropriate protonation states based on predicted pKa values. Docking
was then performed using AutoDock Vina, orchestrated through ConveyorLC. This end-to-end
workflow reflects common virtual high-throughput screening practices, from sequence-based
protein structure modeling through multi-receptor docking.

We provide the scaffold split for each target to mitigate structural leakage and emphasize out-of-
distribution generalization. We also provide results of relatively simple ligand-based ML models
as baseline approaches for comparative analysis.

We are currently in the preparation of the manuscript and the dataset for public release:
Stevenson et al., LLNL-vHTS: A Suite of Benchmarks for Evaluating Models in Realistic Virtual
Screening Use Cases

2. Structure-based unsupervised/self-supervised approaches

In this study, we developed or extended structure-based methods for large-scale 3D protein-
ligand complex data, particularly in settings where the experimental labels are limited or
unavailable.

2.1 Pose classification

The main idea of the pose classification approaches (PECAN) is to distinguish between correct
and incorrect 3D ligand docking poses within protein binding pockets. These methods leverage
crystal structures and their corresponding docking poses for training, but do not rely on
experimentally measured binding labels. In our experiment, we define that ligands extracted
from the crystal structures or docking poses with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of less
than 2.5 Å are categorized as correct poses whereas docking poses whose RMSD is greater
than 6 Å are labeled as incorrect poses.

We extended and improved upon the existing pose classification approaches by employing a
recent equivariant graph neural network architecture (EGNN) and training on our large-scale
complex structure dataset, PDBspheres. Additionally, protein-ligand atomic interaction features,
including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, were used as self-supervised training
objectives. We observed that utilizing the additional interaction feature improved pose
classification accuracy. Figure 2 illustrates a schematic overview of this approach.

3. Structure-based multi-instance learning approach utilizing docking pose
structures

Despite recent advances and improved predictive performance, most structure-based ML
methods including our pose classification primarily rely on utilizing co-crystal complex structures
and experimentally measured binding affinities. However, co-crystal structures are not readily
available in many cases. Alternative molecular docking poses for protein-ligand structures can
often degrade predictive performance, due to inaccuracies in the docking poses compared to
the crystal structures. We developed a novel structure-based inference method utilizing multiple
molecular docking poses, without the need for complex crystal structures, which increases
applicability across various drug targets. This method employs multi-instance learning with an
attention network to predict binding affinity from a collection of docking poses given a protein-
ligand structure entry. Figure 5 illustrates the overall schematic of this approach.

4. Simultaneous labeling and binding affinity prediction for 3D structures

In the high-throughput screening, a large number of compounds are labeled inactive through
single-concentration screens without measuring binding affinities. These inactive compounds,
along with the active ones, can be used to train binary classification models, while regression
models are trained using compounds with binding affinities only. However, the classification and

 9

regression tasks are often handled separately, without sharing the learned feature
representations. We developed a novel model architecture, SLAB: simultaneous labeling and
binding affinity prediction for protein–ligand structures, which jointly performs regression and
classification objectives, aiming to maximize data utilization and improve predictive performance
by leveraging two complementary tasks. In our setup, the regression yields the binding affinity,
whereas the classification task yields the label as active or inactive, as shown in Figure 8.

  Figure 8: Overview of the proposed method using simultaneous labeling and binding affinity
 prediction

We demonstrated our method using compound databases for Dengue type-2 and Zika main
protease receptors. Given that the dataset contains 20 docking poses for each compound, we
reported the results using the average predicted binding affinity across all docking poses. In
Table 4, we present the outcomes of both the standard regression task and our approach. With
the addition of substantial inactive compounds in the training set, we observe that our method
outperforms significantly across all metrics. Specifically in case of EGNN-SLAB, we notice a
significant improvement in all metric values against both of the SGNN models and EGNN
without the proposed framework.

5. Binding pocket clustering-based ligand featurization and unsupervised data
creation

In this study, we developed a ligand-based featurization method using clustering of protein
binding pocket regions to identify potential drug interactions. This method can be used for
unsupervised data generation, particularly when few or no compounds are available for a
specific protein target of interest.

5.1 Ligand featurization using binding pocket clustering

Effective extraction of representative atomic features such as interactions between proteins and
ligands is crucial for accurate prediction of protein-ligand binding affinity in drug discovery and
virtual high-throughput screening. In this work, we developed a novel ligand-based featurization
and mapping of human protein pockets to identify closely related protein targets and to project
novel drugs into a hybrid protein-ligand feature space to identify their likely protein interactions.
Using structure-based template matches from Protein Data Bank (PDB), protein pockets are
featured by the ligands that bind to their best co-complex template matches. The simplicity and
interpretability of this approach provide a granular characterization of the human proteome at
the protein-pocket level instead of the traditional protein-level characterization by family,
function, or pathway, as shown in Figure 9. We demonstrated our method by clustering a subset
of the human proteome and evaluating the predicted cluster associations of over 7000
compounds. This method is useful in the case of unsupervised or self-supervised ML platforms
without the availability of experimental data labels for training.

5.2 Unsupervised data generation

When experimental binding affinity data is unavailable or not sufficient for training, data-driven
ML methods become impractical, due to the need for substantial training data. To address this,
we developed an alternative unsupervised method to generate compounds for ML training. This
method complements our structure-based unsupervised models by leveraging our recently
developed protein structure clustering technique. It utilizes ligand featurization and binding
pocket clustering to extract ligand data from similar protein pocket regions within our
PDBspheres dataset, facilitating the creation of training data for ML, as illustrated in Figure 10.

Figure 10: Unsupervised data generation method using our ligand featurization method

We demonstrated our unsupervised data generation method using SARS-CoV-2 main protease
(mpro) dataset (2176 training compounds and 571 evaluation compounds using the scaffold

evaluate our method. We compared three cases: 1) No fine-training (zero-shot) of existing

6.2 Few-shot learning for limited data

To tackle transfer learning in new chemical spaces or new target receptors with limited
experimental data, we applied a few-shot learning technique and conducted initial experiments
on a small-size dataset. Our method utilizes deep Brownian distance covariance (BDC) within a
few-shot learning framework. We validated this method by performing experiments on a small
subset of the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (mpro) dataset, showing its potential for adapting to
new conditions with minimal data.

We demonstrated our BDC-based few-shot framework using a small subset of compounds from
the mpro dataset, sourced from the PostEra and GoStar datasets. To facilitate transfer learning
for new chemical spaces within this dataset, we used a scaffold split method to partition the data
into support (training) and query (test) sets. Each set contains both positive (binder) and
negative (weak or no-binder) compounds, based on a binding affinity threshold (pIC50) of 6.
