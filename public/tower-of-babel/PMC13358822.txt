Canine radiography and echocardiography sensor fusion for deep learning-based detection and classification of myxomatous mitral valve disease
Authors: Kobkarn Thepsupornkul, Sirilak Disatian Surachetpong, Alisa Kunapinun, Matthew N. Dailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly recognition of myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) is crucial for extending the lifespan and improving the quality of life in dogs. Timely diagnosis allows clinicians to initiate medical therapy before the onset of congestive heart failure. However, early-stage MMVD is challenging to identify on radiographs alone, and while echocardiography is highly informative, its interpretation remains highly operator-dependent. This paper describes the development and validation of GGNet, an automated multimodal deep learning framework that synthesizes echocardiographic and thoracic radiographic data for accurate MMVD staging.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center study, diagnostic images collected between June 2014 and January 2024 were evaluated. The final cohort comprised 902 dogs (n=902), distributed across stages: normal (n=232), stage B1 (n=243), stage B2 (n=190), and stage C (n=237). Imaging modalities included two-dimensional echocardiograms [right parasternal short-axis at left atrium (LA)/aortic root (Ao) level] and thoracic radiographs [right lateral (RL) and ventrodorsal (VD) projections]. We evaluated GGNet, a dual-input (Echo-RL) architecture employing a high-level feature fusion strategy and a selective partial unfreezing fine-tuning protocol [L2–L4, fully connected (FC) layers], on this dataset. Model performance was evaluated using a five-fold cross-validation approach.ResultsThe final GGNet configuration achieved an overall accuracy of 80.3%±2.8%. Crucially, for the clinical task of distinguishing stage B2, the threshold for initiating pimobendan therapy, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 82.1%±7.5% and a positive predictive value (PPV) of 91.1%±4.0%, effectively minimizing both false negatives and false positives.ConclusionsThe proposed GGNet framework provides a reliable diagnostic aid for MMVD staging, reducing subjective variability in image interpretation and offering practical decision support for veterinary cardiology. However, its clinical efficacy inherently relies on standardized, high-quality image acquisition by the operator. Furthermore, as a single-center study utilizing internal cross-validation, future external validation on diverse, multi-center datasets is required before broader clinical deployment.

Key findings

• The proposed GGNet multimodal deep learning framework successfully fuses canine radiography and echocardiography for myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) staging, achieving an overall accuracy of 80.3% and areas under the curve ranging from 0.92 to 0.96 across clinical stages.

• Explainable AI techniques (e.g., Grad-CAM) confirmed that the model’s decision-making process aligns with clinically relevant cardiac regions, enhancing model transparency and supporting clinical trust.

What is known and what is new?

• Accurate staging of canine MMVD typically requires specialized veterinary cardiology expertise to separately evaluate and interpret different imaging modalities.

• This study introduces a novel sensor fusion approach that simultaneously processes both radiographs and echocardiograms, effectively translating a comprehensive clinical diagnostic workflow into an automated deep learning pipeline.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The GGNet framework demonstrates potential as an evidence-based decision-support tool, assisting general veterinary practitioners in screening and staging MMVD when specialized cardiology expertise is limited.

• Veterinary clinical workflows could benefit from integrating multimodal AI aids to enhance the accessibility and consistency of cardiac assessments, though future multicenter validation will further support its widespread clinical adoption.

Introduction

Myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) is the most prevalent cardiac disorder in dogs, accounting for approximately 75% of canine heart disease cases in clinical practice (1). The condition is characterized by progressive degeneration of the mitral valve, leading to chronic volume overload and cardiac remodeling. According to the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ACVIM) consensus guidelines 2019, accurate staging is critical for clinical management. A key diagnostic challenge is differentiating stage B1 (asymptomatic dogs with structural disease but no significant cardiac enlargement, requiring no medical treatment) from stage B2 (asymptomatic dogs with significant cardiac enlargement) (1). Accurate identification of stage B2 is important, as these patients are eligible for the early administration of pimobendan, an intervention proven by the EPIC trial to delay the onset of congestive heart failure (CHF) and extend survival (2). Misclassifying stage B1 as B2 leads to unnecessary medication and potential side effects, while misdiagnosing stage B2 as B1 may deprive the patient of crucial early intervention, potentially accelerating disease progression (3). Likewise, timely identification of progression to stage C is essential for guiding therapeutic management, improving quality of life, and considering alternative treatments, such as mitral valve repair (1,4).

Thoracic radiography and echocardiography are the principal imaging modalities for MMVD diagnosis and staging. However, manual interpretation is time-consuming, heavily reliant on operator expertise and experience, and thus susceptible to inter-observer variability, particularly among general practitioners who have less experience and specialized training compared to veterinary cardiologists. To mitigate these limitations, recent studies have applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) using single-modality data for staging. While these unimodal approaches utilizing thoracic radiographs (5,6), clinical features (7), or phonocardiograms (8-10) have shown promise in identifying healthy or severe cases, they consistently demonstrate low accuracy when distinguishing between adjacent stages, particularly the subtle but clinically vital threshold between stages B1 and B2.

The inherent limitations of unimodal AI systems, which cannot fully capture the multidimensional staging criteria defined by the ACVIM guidelines, constitute a gap in the current research. While echocardiography provides crucial internal measurements [e.g., left atrium (LA):aortic root (Ao)], and radiography assesses global cardiac enlargement, unimodal approaches lack this complete clinical picture, compromising their accuracy in classifying adjacent stages. To address this gap, this study introduces GGNet, an automated multimodal framework that integrates high-level feature representations from right-lateral (RL) radiographs and two-dimensional echocardiographic images. By leveraging this multimodal fusion strategy, a paradigm increasingly recognized for mitigating single-modality limitations in medical imaging (11-13), GGNet provides a comprehensive and clinically robust approach for MMVD staging.

In clinical practice, manual staging of MMVD using the ACVIM guidelines relies on precise metrics [e.g., LA:Ao ≥1.6, normalized left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVIDdN) ≥1.7] to differentiate stage B1 and B2. However, this process is subject to high inter-observer variability, particularly regarding LVIDdN measurements, which are frequently underestimated by general practitioners compared to experienced veterinary cardiologists (14). Therefore, the proposed GGNet framework is positioned primarily as a standardization tool for less experienced operators and as a second reader. By functioning as a clinical decision support system, GGNet aims to reduce subjective measurement errors, increase diagnostic confidence for general practitioners, and improve clinical workflow efficiency.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

For this retrospective study, we collected a dataset from a group of canine patients treated at the Chulalongkorn University Small Animal Teaching Hospital in Bangkok, Thailand between June 2014 and January 2024. To ensure a comprehensive and unbiased dataset, a consecutive sampling approach was applied, in which all eligible patients presenting within the specified timeframe and meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled without omission. Permission to use the hospital’s data for medical data and imaging assessment was granted under a project license (No. MED258/2566). As this study involved only the retrospective review of existing clinical records, formal ethical approval was not required.

Image acquisition protocol

Imaging data were collected according to a standardized thoracic imaging protocol, integrating both radiographic and echocardiographic examinations to ensure accurate and reproducible disease staging. Because the patients inherently varied in body size and weight, radiographic and echocardiographic settings were individualized to ensure optimal diagnostic image quality, reflecting standard clinical protocols.

Thoracic radiographs consisted of right lateral (RL) and ventrodorsal (VD) projections, acquired using a digital radiographic system (Brivo DR-F, GE Healthcare, USA) with exposure settings typically ranging from 60–70 kVp and 2.5–3.1 mAs, adjusted for patient size and interpreted by a board-certified veterinary diagnostic radiologist to ensure diagnostic consistency.

Two-dimensional (2D) transthoracic echocardiography was performed using an ultrasound machine (Mindray M9, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a 4–10 MHz phased-array transducer, with depth, focus, and gain settings optimized per individual subject. Examinations were performed by an experienced veterinary cardiologist following the 2019 ACVIM consensus guidelines for MMVD diagnosis and staging (1,2). Standardized echocardiographic views included the right parasternal short-axis at LA/Ao level, which allows measurement of the LA:Ao ratio in early diastole, a validated indicator of left atrial enlargement in MMVD (15,16). This view was selected exclusively because it provides an intrinsic anatomical ratio, with the aorta serving as an internal reference for normalizing left atrial size, making it well-suited for image-based deep learning without requiring external scaling variables.

Although ACVIM guidelines recommend re-evaluation every 6–12 months, most image sets were acquired within a three-month window. While the model was trained on 902 cases, 1 case was excluded specifically from the temporal analysis due to incomplete date records. For the remaining 901 cases, the median interval between radiographic and echocardiographic acquisitions was 41 days [interquartile range (IQR), 4–246 days; range, 0–2,513 days].

Reference standard and data labeling

To establish the reference standard and ensure strict adherence to both inclusion and exclusion criteria, the assessment of image quality, the presence of obscuring pathologies, and the ground-truth staging were performed through a rigorous, blinded, multi-specialist review process. The panel consisted of a board-certified radiologist evaluating the radiographs, an imaging specialist evaluating the echocardiograms, and a senior board-certified veterinary cardiologist independently verifying the ACVIM stages. Crucially, strict blinding was enforced; no evaluator had access to the findings of the others, and the veterinary expert panel established the ground truth without any knowledge of the model’s predictions. Staging was strictly based on objective criteria outlined in the ACVIM consensus guidelines [e.g., defining Stage B2 using left atrium-to-aorta ratio (LA:Ao) ≥1.6 and normalized left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVIDdN) ≥1.7 for echocardiograms, and vertebral heart scale (VHS) >10.5 for radiographs]. As noted above, cases with obscured cardiac silhouettes or inadequate image quality were entirely excluded. To establish a definitive ground truth for model training, unanimous agreement among all three specialists was a strict prerequisite for inclusion. Therefore, the dataset consists entirely of concordant cases; any case presenting ambiguous or discordant diagnostic findings among the panel was not enrolled in the study.

In clinical studies involving temporal intervals between different imaging acquisitions, protocols frequently specify stability criteria to ensure the patient’s disease stage remains unchanged between examinations. Regarding the stability criteria for stage C, as established in our inclusion criteria (“Study design and patient selection), our methodology did not utilize subjective temporal tracking of clinical metrics (e.g., respiratory rate or CHF treatment modifications) across the examination interval. Instead, diagnostic stability was established by requiring a definitive, objectively confirmed clinical diagnosis of CHF at the exact time of the matched radiographic and echocardiographic acquisitions. As previously stated, cases were included strictly based on the objective ACVIM imaging criteria uniformly applied at that specific diagnostic time point, ensuring the model was trained on unambiguous morphological features rather than variable clinical histories.

Based on the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, no data imputation techniques were performed. From the initial pool of 956 cases, a total of 54 cases were excluded using this strict approach: 51 due to inadequate radiographic quality and 3 due to echocardiographic issues (two cardiac masses, one poor image quality). Only dogs with complete, high-quality RL, VD, and echocardiographic images were retained. The final dataset included 2,706 diagnostic images from 902 dogs, each contributing exactly one RL radiograph, one VD radiograph, and one echocardiogram, totaling 1,804 thoracic radiographs and 902 echocardiographic images. To maintain data consistency, each dog in the final cohort was represented by a single, unique image per modality (one RL radiograph, one VD radiograph, and one echocardiogram). In cases where multiple images met the inclusion criteria, the multi-expert panel strictly selected the most optimal image, defined by superior anatomical clarity and diagnostic contrast, to represent the patient in the final dataset of 902 dogs. To ensure a balanced distribution for robust model training, the final cohort comprised approximately equal proportions across the four adapted stages: normal (n=232, 25.7%), stage B1 (n=243, 27.0%), stage B2 (n=190, 21.0%), and stage C (n=237, 26.3%).

Notably, we defined a ‘stage N’ (Normal) cohort to serve strictly as an imaging baseline. This cohort comprises dogs that presented to the hospital for either routine annual health check-ups or pre-anesthetic cardiovascular assessments prior to elective, non-systemic procedures. The unifying clinical definition for our stage N group is the multi-expert confirmed absence of heart murmurs, structural heart disease (via normal echocardiography), and concurrent thoracic or systemic abnormalities (via unremarkable radiography), independent of ACVIM stage A breed predisposition criteria.

Demographic data (breed, age, body weight, and sex) were extracted from clinical records to characterize the study cohort and support the evaluation of potential confounding factors. The dataset included 902 canine patients, predominantly small breeds, with the top three being Chihuahua (10.2%), Mixed breed (9.5%), and Pomeranian (8.3%). Implausible retrospective records (e.g., body weight or age recorded as zero) were treated as missing data. Age and weight distributions were available for the vast majority of the cohort.

Robustness and sensitivity analyses

To assess potential confounding biases related to patient size, a stratified analysis was performed by dichotomizing the test cohort into two standard subgroups based on body weight: small dogs (<10 kg) and medium-to-large dogs (≥10 kg).

Furthermore, to assess the potential impact of temporal mismatch and wide examination intervals on model performance, a sensitivity analysis was performed. A subset of patients with a strictly limited examination interval of ≤30 days between radiography and echocardiography (n=420) was extracted. The model’s performance on this subset was evaluated and compared against the overall dataset using an independent t-test on the 5-fold cross-validation accuracies to determine if the inclusion of cases with wider intervals introduced significant label noise. Results of the analysis are presented in “Robustness and sensitivity analyses” section of the Results.

Image preprocessing and de-identification

To protect patient privacy and minimize the impact of non-anatomical artifacts, all images underwent a standardized de-identification pipeline. Original thoracic radiographs, initially stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format (17), were converted to JPEG to match the format of two-dimensional (2D) echocardiographic images. All input images were initially center-cropped and resized to 1,000×1,000 pixels to standardize spatial dimensions and minimize truncation of the cardiac anatomy while preserving diagnostically relevant structures. The Generative Remove tool was specifically selected after testing open-source alternatives, which failed to preserve the sharp edge definition required for accurate MMVD staging. Therefore, Generative Remove was ultimately chosen for its ability to eliminate unwanted visual elements while maintaining morphological authenticity and diagnostic quality (18). Generative edits were strictly limited to non-anatomical elements such as text, logos, and overlays, with diagnostic morphological integrity strictly preserved. Importantly, we strictly verified that no original images were utilized if the primary ROI (the heart or lungs) was inherently corrupted or irreversibly obscured by text or graphical overlays prior to preprocessing. This generative inpainting procedure was applied to 100% of the images in our dataset prior to model training to ensure complete de-identification.

Image augmentation protocol

Following image preprocessing and de-identification, the input transformation pipeline uniformly resized all images to 500×500 pixels to meet the input dimensional requirements of the downstream neural networks. To mitigate model overfitting and ensure generalization under the limited sample size typical of veterinary datasets, we implemented a structured data augmentation protocol.

The core augmentation pipeline applied random spatial and pixel-level transformations during training with a probability of 0.5. The specific transformations included: (I) random rotation within a small angular range; (II) horizontal and vertical flipping; (III) Gaussian blurring; (IV) random pixel dropout; (V) brightness cariation; and (VI) random sharpness adjustment. These strategies were specifically designed to mimic realistic variability in image acquisition parameters and patient positioning (19,20).

Crucially, the application of this augmentation protocol was architecture-dependent to accommodate the distinct clinical characteristics of each modality:

❖ Stage I (unimodal fine-tuning): augmentation was applied exclusively to the radiographic modalities. The unimodal echocardiographic baseline was purposefully evaluated without augmentation to preserve diagnostic fidelity. Proposed GGNet architecture

The proposed multimodal fusion architecture (Figure 7) consists of three core components:

❖ Modality-specific backbones: ResNet-18 and ResNet-50 architectures (23) pretrained on ImageNet for feature extraction. Additional backbone variants (ResNet-34, 101, 152) were evaluated; however, ResNet-18 and ResNet-50 consistently demonstrated superior performance for radiographic and echocardiographic modalities, respectively. Specifically, ResNet-18 was employed for radiographic inputs (computing a 512-dimensional final feature vector), while ResNet-50 was utilized for echocardiography (computing a 2,048-dimensional final feature vector) (Figure 8). Prior to multimodal integration, these backbones were independently validated using modality-specific classification heads (Figure 9).

Hierarchical training strategy

To ensure robust feature learning, the framework implemented two sequential training stages. All experiments were conducted under identical computational conditions to ensure reproducibility. Training and evaluation were performed on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU using standard 32-bit precision (FP32) without Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP), utilizing open-source frameworks (PyTorch 2.6.0+cu124, Torchvision 0.21.0+cu124, Pandas 2.3.3, NumPy 2.2.6, Pillow 12.1.1, scikit-learn 1.7.2, and Matplotlib 3.10.8). All models were optimized using the Adam optimizer with a constant learning rate, as applying a global decay schedule to the very low base learning rates required for fine-tuning could lead to learning stagnation. Furthermore, an automated early stopping policy was omitted in favor of a fixed-epoch and dynamic model checkpointing strategy. During training, the model weights were automatically updated and saved whenever the validation loss reached a new minimum. This approach ensured that the final evaluated models were properly optimized and not overfitted, serving as a practical alternative to early stopping while preserving the complete training trajectory.

❖ Unimodal model fine-tuning: modality-specific classifiers were initially optimized independently (Figure 9). During this stage, radiographic inputs underwent the augmentation protocol described previously. To improve regularization under limited-data conditions, Dropout2d layers (dropout rate =0.2) were inserted into the ResNet-18 residual layers 2–4, and the classification head was modified to a linear-ReLU-dropout (0.5)-linear architecture. Each radiographic network was fine-tuned for 1,500 epochs, with residual layer 4 and the fully connected layer unfrozen, using a constant learning rate of 1×10−5, a weight decay of 0.001, and a batch size of 64. This prolonged training, paired with a higher learning rate and more aggressive weight decay, was necessary to extract subtle cardiac features from radiographs, which inherently present complex anatomical superimposition. This configuration facilitates effective exploration of the noisy loss landscape and reduces overfitting to spurious features. Conversely, no image augmentation was applied to the echocardiographic inputs. The ResNet-50 backbone was fully fine-tuned (all layers unfrozen) for 100 epochs using a constant learning rate of 1×10−6, a weight decay of 0.0001, and a batch size of 16. The lower learning rate was selected to fine-tune the deeper backbone without disrupting the pre-trained weights, and the 100 epochs limit was sufficient due to the more direct structural representation of cardiac morphology in echocardiographic images.

Performance evaluation

To ensure unbiased performance estimation and experimental reproducibility, all models were evaluated using a five-fold cross-validation protocol, with the random seed strictly fixed at 434 across all standard libraries (NumPy, PyTorch, and random). Dataset splitting was performed strictly at the patient level to prevent data leakage. During the testing phase of the 5-fold cross-validation, the deep learning model (index test) predicted the MMVD stages based solely on the input images from the test set, completely blinded to the corresponding ground truth labels. Model performance across the folds is reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

To comprehensively evaluate models’ multi-class classification performance, standard statistical metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity (recall), specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1-score, were computed. To explicitly handle class imbalance, a comparable number of samples for each class was selected during the initial dataset curation. Consequently, both macro-averaged and weighted-averaged metrics were calculated; given the balanced nature of the dataset, evaluating these metrics ensures equal representation and prevents evaluation bias toward any frequently occurring class. Because GGNet is designed to serve as a clinical decision support system, assessing the reliability of its predicted probabilities is crucial. To evaluate the clinical reliability of the model’s predicted probabilities, a calibration assessment was performed using Reliability Diagrams and the Expected Calibration Error (ECE). Furthermore, for comprehensive multi-class discrimination, per-class metrics, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) were calculated using a One-vs-Rest (OvR) approach. Crucially, both the ROC/AUC and calibration analyses were evaluated based on the combined predictions from the pooled test sets across the 5-fold cross-validation (n=902 images).

To demonstrate the model’s utility as a clinical decision support system, specifically for the critical threshold of initiating pimobendan therapy, an explicit binary classification analysis (“B2 vs. not-B2”) was conducted to evaluate performance tradeoffs across various operating thresholds.

Statistical analysis

Formal hypothesis testing was conducted to determine the statistical significance of performance differences among the various architectural, fusion, and fine-tuning configurations. Due to the limited statistical power of fold-level tests (n=5), McNemar’s tests were applied to the paired predictions across the pooled combined test set (n=902 images). To control the family-wise error rate across multiple preplanned comparisons, the critical P values were adjusted using Bonferroni corrections. Additionally, for the temporal sensitivity analysis (comparing the overall dataset against a subset with examination intervals ≤30 days), an independent t-test was employed on the cross-validation accuracies. All statistical analyses were performed using the scikit-learn library, with statistical significance defined according to the Bonferroni-adjusted alpha levels.

Explainable multimodal classification via gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)

To enhance the interpretability of the proposed GGNet framework, we adopted Grad-CAM to visualize the spatial regions that most strongly influenced the model’s predictions during MMVD stage classification (24). Grad-CAM generates class-specific heatmaps by propagating the gradients of target predictions back to the final convolutional feature maps, thereby highlighting diagnostically salient regions without requiring architectural modification. This approach provides an intuitive and model-agnostic visualization strategy suitable for multimodal deep learning frameworks in veterinary imaging.

We applied Grad-CAM across the imaging modalities comprising the final GGNet architecture (echocardiography and right lateral thoracic radiographs) to identify the specific features driving the model’s decisions. For each correctly classified case, activation maps were computed from the last convolutional layer of the corresponding backbone (ResNet-18 or ResNet-50) and superimposed on the original image for visual interpretation. To demonstrate the consistency of these activation patterns beyond representative examples, we provide extended Grad-CAM visualizations across multiple cases per class (n=20 random cases per class from the test set) in Figures S1-S4.

To assess clinical plausibility, the resulting activation maps were qualitatively evaluated for their spatial association with established stage-specific pathophysiological signs of MMVD. Specifically, we examined whether the model’s attention aligned with recognized diagnostic hallmarks, in accordance with ACVIM consensus guidelines (1) and veterinary reference standards (25,26). The detailed stage-specific qualitative evaluations of these spatial associations, along with an explicit assessment of model limitations, are presented in the “Results” and “Discussion” sections.

Quantitative performance evaluation and statistical analysis

As summarized in Table 5, the multimodal configurations consistently outperformed the unimodal baselines across all evaluation metrics. The progression of model optimization demonstrates the systematic selection of imaging modalities, fusion strategies, and fine-tuning protocols, with pairwise statistical comparisons evaluated using McNemar’s test and Bonferroni corrections (Table 6).

❖ Unimodal baselines and augmentation strategy: initially, unimodal baselines were established, with the Single Echo model achieving the highest unimodal accuracy of 0.715±0.041. Notably, an empirical ablation study confirmed that applying comprehensive data augmentation to these isolated echocardiographic frames severely degraded performance (mean accuracy decreasing to 0.415±0.013, P<0.001; Table 7). Prior to architectural optimization, the multimodal data augmentation strategy was evaluated. Unlike the unimodal baseline, applying augmentation uniformly to both modalities in the proposed GGNet architecture yielded substantially improved performance (mean accuracy of 0.803±0.028) compared to augmenting only the radiographic branch (mean accuracy of 0.635±0.052, P<0.001; Table 8).

❖ Multimodal modality selection: among the subsequent multimodal combinations, the dual-input Echo–RL model achieved the highest overall performance in the modality selection phase (Table 5), with an accuracy of 0.769±0.026, a precision of 0.775±0.032, a sensitivity of 0.771±0.021, a specificity of 0.922±0.008, and an F1-score of 0.771±0.025. McNemar’s tests on the pooled test set confirmed that this dual-input model significantly outperformed both the RL-only baseline (P<0.001) and the Echo-only baseline (P=0.001, adjusted α =0.0167) (Table 6). This establishes that the integration of RL radiographic features provides crucial complementary information for MMVD staging. By contrast, the dual RL-VD and Echo-VD configurations exhibited lower performance, indicating that the VD view contributes minimal complementary value. While the triple-input model (Echo-RL-VD) achieved comparable accuracy (0.758±0.014), the difference between the dual- and triple-input models was not statistically significant (P=0.37). Thus, the Echo-RL configuration was selected as the primary configuration for subsequent ablation studies due to its optimal accuracy and superior computational efficiency, Specifically, despite a marginally higher GPU memory requirement, the dual-input architecture reduced the average training time per fold by approximately 68% and significantly decreased inference latency compared to the triple-input model (Table 9).

❖ Feature fusion optimization: subsequent ablation studies optimized this dual-input Echo-RL architecture. Among the evaluated fusion paradigms, high-level feature fusion achieved an accuracy of 0.769±0.026. Statistical analysis indicated a significant improvement over low-level feature fusion (P<0.001, adjusted α =0.0125). As demonstrated in Table 10, while mid-level feature fusion consumed slightly less memory (3.81 vs. 4.37 GB), it suffered from prolonged training times (121.74 minutes per fold). Conversely, decision-leve and attention-based fusion approaches incurred significantly higher computational burdens, taking 143.46 and 231.27 minutes per fold, respectively, without proportional efficiency gains. ❖ Fine-tuning depths and computational cost: finally, an evaluation of fine-tuning depths revealed that progressive unfreezing of deeper convolutional layers generally improved predictive performance. The configuration unfreezing the fully connected layer and the last three convolutional blocks (FC + L4 + L3 + L2) achieved the highest accuracy of 0.803±0.028, a sensitivity of 0.804±0.028, a specificity of 0.934±0.010, and an F1-score of 0.806±0.026. This configuration demonstrated statistically significant improvements compared to both the FC-only baseline (P<0.001) and the FC + L4 + L3 configuration (P<0.001, adjusted α =0.0125). Notably, selectively unfreezing only down to L3 resulted in marked performance degradation and increased variance, a phenomenon consistent with the “fragile co-adaptation of features” theory, where mid-level representation shifts disrupt the hierarchical feature extraction pipeline. To justify this strategy, computational costs were compared against a fully unfrozen approach (Table 11). While full training of all layers yielded comparable accuracy (P=0.12), it drastically increased the computational burden, extending training time by approximately 27% and nearly doubling the peak GPU memory requirement from 18.15 to 34.02 GB. Given these severe constraints on hardware without proportional predictive benefits, the partial unfreezing approach was designated as the final proposed GGNet model (previously detailed in Table 2). For reference, detailed fold-by-fold performance metrics, computational costs, and exact P values for all evaluated configurations are provided in Tables S1-S8.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated GGNet, an automated multimodal deep learning framework designed to stage MMVD by integrating echocardiographic and thoracic radiographic data. Our findings demonstrate that fusing these heterogeneous imaging modalities significantly improves staging accuracy, particularly in differentiating the critical stage B2 treatment threshold, compared to unimodal baseline models.

Comparative evaluation with prior studies

To contextualize our findings, Table 20 compares the performance of the proposed GGNet (Echo-RL configuration) with published baseline models. Overall, GGNet achieved higher accuracy (80.3%) and precision (81.5%) than the baseline models (5-7), as well as higher AUC scores across all corresponding stages compared to radiograph-only networks (5,6). While the model integrating clinical anamnesis with physical examination (7) achieved a higher per-class accuracy for the normal stage (N), GGNet outperformed it in the remaining disease stages. Specifically, GGNet provided a robust classification for the intermediate stages, achieving a per-class accuracy of 94.5% for stage B2, compared to only 62.2% in model (7). This improvement is highly clinically relevant, as accurate identification of stage B2 is the crucial threshold for initiating medical therapy.

(8) utilized acoustic data for binary classification between stages B1 and B2 (AUC 0.861), their approach is most valuable as a pre-echocardiographic screening tool. (9) reported an accuracy of 94.1%, but their methodology relies on the MINE grading system, which is not aligned with the ACVIM B1/B2 clinical thresholds. Despite the inherent difficulty of the four-class ACVIM classification, GGNet achieved an overall sensitivity of 80.4% and an overall specificity of 93.4%, with AUC scores of 0.92 or higher across all stages. This indicates effective differentiation across the full spectrum of disease severity, demonstrating that multimodal integration enhances diagnostic confidence for therapeutic decision planning.

Methodological justifications: data augmentation and generative inpainting

Our empirical findings validate the architecture-specific data augmentation strategy employed in this study. As demonstrated in “Quantitative performance evaluation and statistical analysis” section, applying geometric augmentations to isolated echocardiograms severely degraded performance, supporting the clinical rationale that arbitrary transformations disrupt standardized acquisition planes and signal fidelity in ultrasound imaging. Conversely, the significant performance improvement observed when comprehensive augmentation was applied to the multimodal GGNet confirms that symmetric augmentation effectively regularizes complex networks. It encourages the model to learn invariant, cross-modal features rather than memorizing modality-specific details, thereby mitigating the risk of overfitting inherent in multimodal architectures.

A notable limitation of our methodology involves the use of a proprietary generative inpainting tool (Adobe Lightroom Generative Remove). However, this approach is justified for two primary reasons. First, while several open-source Python-based inpainting methods were initially tested, every attempt resulted in a significant loss of critical anatomical details. The Generative Remove tool was ultimately selected specifically for its superior ability to eliminate unwanted visual elements while strictly maintaining morphological authenticity and diagnostic quality. Second, the generative edits were confined solely to masking explicit text and non-anatomical artifacts, leaving the underlying ROI fundamentally preserved. Additionally, this procedure is empirically justified through Grad-CAM results. In early attempts using raw, non-preprocessed images, significant model bias was observed where the network improperly focused on irrelevant labels. After thoroughly removing these artifacts with the preprocessing pipeline, the final Grad-CAM analysis clearly demonstrates that the model does not attend to these peripheral edited regions. Instead, it appropriately focuses on the anatomically relevant regions within the ROI, confirming that this method does not introduce any new generative bias.

Robustness across demographics and temporal intervals

Regarding the study cohort, systematic differences in age, body weight and sex distribution were observed in our dataset. This accurately reflects the natural pathophysiology and well-documented epidemiological reality of MMVD which predominantly affects older, small-breed, and male dogs, consistent with the ACVIM 2019 consensus guidelines. Importantly, our stratified analysis confirmed that GGNet maintained high accuracy across different body sizes. This demonstrates that the model’s staging capability is rooted in pathological morphological features rather than confounding size-related proxies.

Furthermore, our temporal sensitivity analysis provides important methodological validation. As reported in “Robustness and sensitivity analyses” section, there was no significant difference in performance (P=0.34) when comparing the tightly restricted subset (interval ≤30 days) to the overall dataset. This indicates that the inclusion of cases with wider examination intervals did not significantly alter the model’s predictive performance, thereby confirming the robustness of our strict diagnostic inclusion criteria. By anchoring the ground-truth to a definitive, objectively confirmed clinical state at the exact time of imaging, we effectively minimized label noise without unnecessarily shrinking the dataset size.

Model limitations and trustworthy interpretation of Grad-CAM evaluation

The spatial concordance between the observed Grad-CAM activation patterns and established diagnostic landmarks (as detailed in “Explainable multimodal classification” section) provides crucial clinical plausibility to the proposed framework. For instance, in the normal stage, the radiographic focus aligns with the clinical assessment of the straight caudal cardiac waist (i.e., the absence of the “backpack sign”, a localized caudodorsal bulge indicative of left atrial enlargement) and the absence of dorsal cardiac border elevation, which are key landmarks for confirming the absence of cardiomegaly. In stage B1, the model’s echocardiographic focus on the LA and Ao regions aligns with clinical reasoning, as LA/Ao-based measurements are central to early MMVD staging. As the disease progresses to stages B2 and C, it can be observed that the echocardiographic activation expands to cover a broader area, reflecting its association with global chamber structural remodeling, while the radiographic attention shifts toward perihilar opacities, which are commonly associated with pulmonary edema. This interpretation suggests that the model’s predictions rely primarily on anatomically relevant features, although some influence from non-anatomical image textures is still present.

Model limitations and susceptibility to artifacts

Furthermore, our comprehensive qualitative evaluation (via Figures S1-S4) revealed distinct model limitations related to shortcut learning. In the echocardiographic modality, Grad-CAM visualizations identified activation at the ultrasound cone apex across all disease stages. While the exact reason for the model’s consistent focus on the upper apex of the echocardiograms remains undetermined, we hypothesize that it may be related to image contrast normalization. The model might extract information from this region to estimate contrast or to learn the differentiation between the background and the interior of the scan. Consequently, this apex region could be serving as global “reference information” that the model utilizes when processing the entire image.

Despite this artifact, the model consistently identifies relevant features in both echocardiograms and radiographs simultaneously for Normal and stage B1 cases. In contrast, for stages B2 and C, this dual-modality consistency decreases; the model frequently focuses on relevant features in only one modality, while its attention in the other modality disperses toward irrelevant aspects. For example, in the radiographic modality during the transitional stage B2 and the congestive stage C, the model occasionally anchored on skeletal structures (e.g., thoracic spine and sternum) rather than the cardiac silhouette or perihilar region.

However, in the context of a multimodal framework, even if the model’s attention deviates toward artifacts in one modality (e.g., radiographs in stages B2 and C), the overall predictive performance may still be sustained if it maintains focus on the correct structures in the complementary modality. These observations demonstrate a fundamental vulnerability of deep learning architectures to spurious correlations in medical imaging and represent a limitation of the current version of GGNet, which cannot yet extract definitive visual evidence from stage B2 and C images as effectively and consistently across both modalities as it does for Normal and stage B1 cases.

Clinical implications, limitations, and future directions

The identification of these limitations has significant clinical implications. For example, the model’s focus on the apex of the echocardiograms indicates that clinicians should not over-rely on GGNet predictions and informs them about specific scenarios where the model’s focus might be spurious. To mitigate such vulnerabilities in future iterations, we propose implementing an instance segmentation technique to precisely locate the heart, ensuring the model processes only the cardiac region and its immediate surrounding tissues. Furthermore, for echocardiograms, applying a predefined mask (such as a fan-shaped mask) could effectively constrain the model’s focus area. These approaches would reduce the occurrence of artifacts in irrelevant regions, directing the model to focus primarily on clinically relevant areas. This finding underscores the necessity of utilizing interpretability tools not merely to validate successes, but to transparently expose vulnerabilities and guide future model refinements for trustworthy clinical deployment.

In terms of practical deployment, GGNet is strictly designed to function as a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) for primary care settings, rather than a standalone diagnostic replacement. Strictly adhering to the multidimensional ACVIM guidelines can be challenging, and manual measurements are susceptible to inter-observer variability. Therefore, GGNet serves as a standardization tool and a “second reader” to assist general practitioners who may evaluate complex cardiac cases less frequently than board-certified cardiologists. By providing an objective, automated assessment to corroborate manual staging, the tool aims to increase diagnostic confidence, improve workflow efficiency, and support appropriate treatment protocols, such as the timely initiation of pimobendan.

Additionally, a notable technical limitation is the absence of LVIDdN as a direct model input. While LVIDdN is a fundamental parameter in the ACVIM guidelines, and was strictly evaluated by multi-expert consensus to establish the clinical ground-truth staging for all cases in our dataset, it requires allometric scaling based on the patient’s body weight, an external metadata variable not present in the image pixels. Therefore, the proposed model relies solely on the LA/Ao short-axis view, which is a self-contained relative ratio that is invariant to the dog’s absolute size or breed. The lack of direct LVIDdN integration may limit the model’s ability to replicate the full multidimensional assessment used by human experts in borderline stage B1 vs. B2 cases. Furthermore, a significant limitation of this study, and of clinical implementation in general, is that image acquisition, especially echocardiographic image acquisition, is fundamentally operator-dependent. GGNet cannot compensate for poor image acquisition, incorrect anatomical planes, or significant user-generated artifacts. Therefore, rigorous quality control of the input images by the operator remains a mandatory and crucial step in the diagnostic workflow prior to utilizing this model.

Finally, a critical limitation of this study pertains to the inherent circularity in our model evaluation and its generalizability. The ground truth (ACVIM stage) in the dataset was determined using imaging criteria that overlap directly with the model’s input modalities. The model therefore primarily supports standardized imaging-based staging rather than providing a completely independent clinical disease diagnosis. Furthermore, the proposed framework was trained and evaluated on a strictly concordant, retrospective dataset curated from a single veterinary institution. Because cases with inadequate image quality or obscuring pathologies were excluded, and unanimous multi-expert agreement was strictly required to ensure ground-truth purity, the reported performance metrics may be inflated compared to a general clinical population where atypical, ambiguous, or discordant presentations are prevalent. Additionally, the model’s capacity to generalize across different ultrasound machines, radiographic exposure protocols, and broader demographic variations remains unverified. Future research must prioritize multi-center, prospective validation to capture real-world clinical heterogeneity and thoroughly assess the utility of explainable artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted veterinary cardiology.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated multimodal deep learning strategies for MMVD staging in dogs, emphasizing the diagnostic advantages of combining echocardiographic and radiographic data. The proposed dual-input Echo-RL model, optimized through high-level feature fusion and selective fine-tuning of higher convolutional layers (L2–L4 and FC), achieved improved accuracy and stability, surpassing unimodal and triple-input baselines while maintaining computational efficiency. These results confirm that targeted multimodal integration enhances classification performance and robustness within the evaluated cohort.

Beyond quantitative performance, the incorporation of Grad-CAM-based interpretability confirmed that the model extracts diagnostically relevant anatomical features, particularly in early disease stages. Crucially, the application of these interpretability tools also exposes the model’s susceptibility to shortcut learning and non-anatomical artifacts. This highlights the necessity of visual explanations not just for validating successes, but for ensuring safe, informed, and transparent clinical deployment.

From a clinical perspective, GGNet provides a robust automated framework for distinguishing between all four ACVIM stages. By reliably identifying the critical stage B2 threshold, the model facilitates timely treatment initiation and offers practical decision support for general practitioners. However, its real-world efficacy relies on standardized, high-quality image acquisition by the operator, and the absence of size-dependent metrics (e.g., LVIDdN) necessitates its use as an assistive diagnostic tool rather than a standalone replacement for veterinary cardiologists.

Future work could explore extending the GGNet framework by incorporating automated instance segmentation and predefined masking to explicitly mitigate artifact susceptibility. Furthermore, validation on diverse, multi-center datasets and the integration of temporal echocardiographic sequences represent valuable next steps to further enhance model robustness and clinical applicability in veterinary cardiology.
