Women’s empowerment and experience of marital control in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Richard Gyan Aboagye, Augustus Osborne, Joshua Okyere, Mainprice Akuoko Essuman, Yaa Boahemaa Gyasi Aderoju, Blessing Jaka Akombi-Inyang, Bright Opoku Ahinkorah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study examined the association between women’s empowerment (social independence, decision-making, and attitudes to violence) and experience of marital control in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsA weighted sample of 82,208 was extracted from the most recent Demographic and Health Surveys conducted in 19 countries in sub-Saharan Africa. We used multinomial and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses to examine the association between the dimensions of women’s empowerment and marital control. All the analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0.ResultsWomen with high attitudes to violence (adjusted relative risk ratio [aRRR] = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.68, 0.77) were less likely to experience medium marital control than those with low attitudes to violence. Women with medium (aRRR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.74, 0.86) and high (aRRR = 0.49; 95% CI = 0.46, 0.52) attitudes to violence were less likely to experience high marital control. Women with high social independence were less likely to experience medium and high levels of marital control. Women with medium decision-making capacity were less likely to experience medium marital control. Additionally, women with medium and high decision-making capacity had a reduced likelihood of experiencing high marital control. Partner alcohol consumption, women’s exposure to interparental violence, place of residence, wealth index, women’s age, and media exposure were the covariates associated with marital control.ConclusionThis study highlights an association between women’s empowerment and marital control. It emphasises the importance of prioritising women’s empowerment initiatives to address controlling behaviour by spouses. Governments in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in Eastern African countries, should focus on providing women with quality education and skills development programmes to enhance their economic independence and decision-making capacity.Keywords: Attitude towards violence, Autonomy, Decision-making, Demographic and Health Survey, Intimate partner violence, Marital control, Social independence, sub-Saharan Africa, Women’s empowerment

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) remains a major, pervasive, yet avoidable public health issue, with over 27% of women of reproductive age globally experiencing physical, sexual, or both forms of IPV [1]. IPV is prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where about 33% of women reported that they have experienced at least one form of IPV in their lifetime [2]. Along with sexual, physical, emotional or psychological abuse against women, controlling behaviour is one of the forms of IPV [3]. Controlling behaviours include actions that are used to frighten, isolate, degrade, and gain compliance over an intimate partner and could result in pain or injury that goes beyond what is physically visible [4, 5]. Spousal control behaviours include acts to constrain women’s mobility, ability to work, or access to friends and relatives [6]. In most cases, these controlling behaviours are expressed through jealousy over talking to other men, accusations of unfaithfulness, denial of permission to meet friends, restrictions on the partner’s contact with family, and constant insistence on knowing the partner’s whereabouts [7]. These controlling behaviours may result in trust issues, frequent disagreements, and conflict, which may then result in physical, sexual, and/or emotional violence [8, 9]. A woman’s physical and social freedom is often restricted by controlling behaviours, leading to isolation, a loss of autonomy, and decreased productivity [10].

Controlling behaviours are becoming an increasingly common practice and, because of their negative impact, require the needed attention to address them. A relatively high prevalence of partner control behaviour has been reported: 64% in England and 65% in Brazil [11]; 81.7% in Türkiye [3]; 32.1% in Vietnam [12]; and 31.8% in Pakistan [13]. Nearly three-quarters (71.34%) of women in SSA who participated in a multi-country study reported that their husbands exhibit controlling behaviours against them, with prevalence above two-thirds in countries such as Cameroon, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Sierra Leone, and Zambia [14]. Male partners’ controlling behaviours are a prelude to IPV, as evidenced by a World Health Organization (WHO)'s multi-country study in 2011, which reported that male partners are more likely to use violence than less controlling ones [15].

Any form of violence violates the fundamental human rights of its victim-survivors. Violence has the potential to become an obstacle to equality, development, and peace. Additionally, victim-survivors may experience one or more physical, mental, sexual and reproductive health problems, including but not limited to sexually transmitted infections, physical injuries and deaths, substance abuse, depression, and suicidal ideations [16]. Therefore, it is crucial to emphasise the need to strengthen commitments towards eliminating any form of violence against women, including controlling behaviours, in line with Sustainable Development Goal 5, target 5.2 [17]. To do this, it is necessary to examine and assess the vast range of social and economic factors that drive IPV and to focus on advocacy and intervention.

Earlier studies have found age, rural setting, justification of violence, being afraid of intimate partners, level of education, income contribution, and exposure to violence as some major predictors of partner control behaviours [3, 14]. Perhaps the most convincing underlying risk factor of all forms of IPV, such as those expressed as controlling behaviours, is gender-based power disparities [18]. In SSA, cultural traditions are mostly based on patriarchal expectations for the family and conventional gender roles [19, 20]. Men frequently hold a dominant position both within and outside the home, while women handle the majority of childcare and household responsibilities. Female disempowerment is fuelled by gender inequity and patriarchal cultural norms, which may cause males to act in an excessively dominating manner. Research has revealed that egalitarian decision-making may be a prelude to violence against women despite the dearth of studies on male dominance and IPV [21].

Although not widely explored, women’s empowerment may influence the occurrence of IPV, as reported in earlier studies [22–24]. Kabeer [25] defines empowerment as “the expansion of people’s ability to make strategic life choices in a context where this ability was previously denied to them.” Gender inequality is the root cause of violence against women, including controlling behaviours, which may be lessened via the empowerment of women [23, 26–29]. Earlier studies have indicated that various aspects of empowerment, such as access to paid employment, household income contribution, decision-making ability, and education, may influence the occurrence of IPV [23, 26–29]. However, most of these studies were conducted outside SSA. Also, none of the aforementioned studies examined controlling behaviour as a form of IPV, leaving an evidence gap this study seeks to fill.

This study utilises the Theory of Gender and Power to examine the nuances between women’s empowerment and marital control [30]. The theory posits that gender-based divisions of labour, authority, and normative expectations shape interpersonal dynamics, including the extent to which men exert control over their partners. Within this theoretical framework, women’s empowerment - evidenced by education, economic independence, and decision-making autonomy - serves as a challenge to traditional hierarchies that legitimise male dominance [30]. As women acquire access to resources and agency, they are potentially better equipped to resist or negotiate marital control, thereby diminishing their vulnerability to coercive behaviours. However, evidence indicates that shifts in gender power dynamics may provoke resistance, as men might reassert control to sustain dominance in contexts where patriarchal norms are deeply rooted [31]. This phenomenon is particularly pertinent in SSA, where sociocultural norms often reinforce male authority within intimate relationships. Empirical data demonstrate that, although empowerment can function as a protective factor, it may concurrently be associated with an increased risk of marital control in environments characterised by rigid gender norms and substantial sanctions against empowerment [32]. Nevertheless, limited evidence at the subregional level exists to elucidate the association between women’s empowerment and marital control.

Given that many women are experiencing partner control behaviours [33, 34] in SSA, it is important to study how empowering women could help curtail this problem. In the absence of studies in SSA that have comprehensively investigated the specific dimensions of empowerment that may influence controlling behaviours against women, this study examined the association between women's empowerment - specifically in the domains of social independence, decision-making, and attitudes to violence - and experience of partner marital control behaviours. To achieve this, we used the survey-based women’s empowerment (SWPER) index [35, 36], which is one of the unique instruments used to define African women’s empowerment. The findings of this study provide empirical data needed to formulate policies and interventions targeted at eliminating controlling behaviours against women by empowering them.

Methods

Data source and design

Data were obtained from the most recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) of 19 countries in SSA published from 2015 to 2021. Specifically, data from the individual recode (women’s file) in each of the 19 countries were merged for analysis. The list of countries, sample, and corresponding survey years, are listed in Table 1. The DHS is a nationwide survey conducted in over 90 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [37]. DHS employed a cross-sectional design, with respondents sampled using a two-stage cluster sampling procedure [37]. Structured questionnaires that had been pretested and validated were utilised to obtain data from the women. This study comprised 82,208 women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who were in sexual unions – married or cohabiting (Table 1). The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed in writing this paper [38].

Variables

Experience of marital control was the outcome variable. Women who were in a sexual union were asked if they had experienced any of the six dominating behaviours: jealous or angry if she talks to other men; frequently accuses her of being unfaithful; does not permit her to meet her female friends; tries to limit her contact with her family; insists on knowing where she is at all times; and does not trust her with money [39]. However, the majority of the countries’ datasets did not include the last question: Does not trust her with money? To ensure uniformity across all countries, we used only the remaining five in our final study [39]. We utilised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to derive a composite variable, marital control. The obtained values were classified as “low,” “medium,” and “high.”

The SWPER index, developed, tested, and validated for use in LMICs, was the key explanatory variable in the study [35]. SWPER has been used to study various health and social issues, including maternal and child health and reproductive health [35, 36, 40]. The SWPER index was derived from fourteen DHS variables and divided into three dimensions of women’s empowerment: attitudes toward violence, social independence (autonomy), and decision-making [36]. These dimensions were included in our study to examine their association with women's experience of marital control. We adopted the WHO’s coding methodology to create the subcategories for each dimension [41]. For attitude towards violence, the coding for each category was low (score <- 0.700), medium (score > -0.700 and ≤ 0.400), and high (score > 0.400). The high category denotes strong disagreement or rejection of attitudes toward violence (positive), whereas the low group emphasises strong acceptance of violence against women (negative). Low (score ≤ -0.559), medium (score > -0.559 and ≤ 0.293), and high (score > 0.293) were the coding and classification categories for the social independence dimension. The categories of decision-making were low (score ≤-1.000), medium (score >-1.000 ≤ 0.600), and high (score > 0.600) [41]. We controlled for seven variables in the study. These variables were selected based on their association with IPV and marital control from the literature [14, 39, 42] and their availability in the DHS dataset. The covariates included women’s age, exposure to mass media [43], partner alcohol consumption, exposure to interparental violence, household wealth index, place of residence, and geographic sub-region. Statistical analyses

Stata software, version 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA), was used for all the analyses. The proportion of women who experienced marital control was presented using percentages, as shown in Table 1. We examined the distribution of marital control across the dimensions of the SWPER index and the covariates using a bivariate analysis. The Pearson chi-square test of independence was used to determine the variables significantly associated with the experience of marital control. Next, we used a multinomial regression analysis to examine the association between the dimensions of the SWPER index and marital control, adjusting for the covariates. The results were presented as adjusted relative risk ratios (aRRR) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Later, a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between the SWPER dimensions and the individual variables used to assess marital control, while adjusting for covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CI were used to present the results. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Since the DHS domestic violence module was administered to a randomly selected subsample of women, all analyses were weighted using the domestic violence weight variable (d005) divided by 1,000,000 to obtain the survey weight (dv005_pw). We weighted the data at the individual-country level before pooling. To pool data across the 19 countries, the country-level weights were denormalised using the formula: dv005_pwpool = dv005_pw × (total female population aged 15–49 at the time of the survey / total sample size after applying eligibility restrictions). The denormalised pooled weight variable (dv005_pwpool) was used to weight all estimates in the pooled analysis [39].

Ethical considerations

No ethical approval was required for this study, since the DHS dataset is readily available in the public domain for usage. Rather, authorisation was acquired from the Monitoring and Evaluation to Assess and Use Results of Demographic and Health Surveys (MEASURE DHS) before using the dataset. The ICF Institutional Review Board and the applicable Institutional Review Boards in the survey nations approved the DHS survey methodology and questions. Before collecting data, respondents and/or their parents or guardians provided informed consent.

Results

Proportion of women who experienced marital control in sub-Saharan Africa

Figure 1 shows the prevalence of marital control in SSA. The results indicate that 48.3% of women reported their partners experienced jealousy if they talked to other men, 42.0% had partners who insisted on knowing their whereabouts, and 20.4% had partners who accused them of unfaithfulness.

Prevalence of marital control variables among the women

Table 1 presents the prevalence of marital control among women in SSA, categorised into low, medium, and high levels across 19 countries. The results showed significant variation in the prevalence of marital control, with countries such as Burundi (67.8%) and Rwanda (50.3%) reporting the highest prevalence of low marital control, while Liberia (18.4%) and Sierra Leone (20.8%) reported the lowest prevalence. Moderate marital control was prevalent in Malawi (44.6%) and Tanzania (40.5%), whereas high marital control was particularly prominent in Liberia (56.8%) and Sierra Leone (56.0%). Overall, the pooled prevalence of low, medium, and high marital control was 38.5%, 31.7%, and 29.8%, respectively.

Distribution of marital control across women’s empowerment index and the covariates

Table 2 shows the distribution of marital control across the explanatory variables. The results show a significant association between each explanatory variable and marital control at p<0.05. Women with a high attitude to violence had the highest prevalence of low marital control (47.1%) and those with low attitude to violence reported the highest prevalence of high marital control (36.3%). Among women in the high social independence category, the prevalence of low marital control was 43.8%, whereas those in the medium social independence category reported the highest proportion of high marital control (29.8%). The prevalence of low marital control among women with medium decision-making capacity was 44.3%. Also, 30.5% of women in the low decision-making category reported experiencing high marital control. Additionally, women aged 45–49 had the highest prevalence of low marital control (50.3%, while those aged 20–24 reported the highest proportion of high marital control (31.9%. The prevalence of low marital control was 44.4% among women who were not exposed to mass media, whereas those exposed to mass media showed the highest proportion of high marital control (28.9%). Furthermore, 45.2% of women whose partners did not consume alcohol experienced low marital control, whereas the prevalence of high marital control among women whose partners drank alcohol was 35.9%. Women unexposed to interparental violence reported the highest proportion of low marital control (45.0%), whereas those exposed reported the highest proportion of high marital control (36.6%). Also, women from Eastern Africa reported the highest prevalence of low marital control (46.6%), while women from Central Africa had the highest proportion of high marital control (30.6%).

Association between women’s empowerment and marital control in sub-Saharan Africa

Table 3 presents the results of the association between women’s empowerment and marital control. Women with high attitudes to violence (aRRR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.68, 0.77) were more likely to experience medium marital control than those with low attitudes to violence. Women with medium (aRRR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.74, 0.86) and high (aRRR = 0.49; 95% CI = 0.46, 0.52) attitude to violence were less likely to experience high marital control than those with low attitude to violence. Women with high social independence (aRRR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.84, 0.96) were less likely to experience moderate marital control than those with low social independence. Similarly, women who had high social independence (aRRR = 0.92; 95% CI = 0.86, 0.99) were less likely to experience high marital control relative to those with low social independence. Women with medium decision-making capacity (aRRR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80, 0.92) were less likely to experience moderate marital control than those with low decision-making capacity. Additionally, women with medium (aRRR = 0.80; 95% CI = 0.75, 0.86) and high (aRRR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.79, 0.92) decision-making capacity had a lower risk of experiencing high marital control than those with medium decision-making capacity.

Women with high social independence had lower odds of experiencing marital control: spouse gets jealous if respondent talks with other men (aOR = 0.91; 95% CI = 0.86, 0.96), spouse accuses respondent of unfaithfulness (aOR = 0.88; 95% CI = 0.82, 0.94), spouse tries to limit respondent’s contact with family (aOR = 0.86; 95% CI = 0.79, 0.94), and spouse insists on knowing where respondent is (aOR = 0.92; 95% CI = 0.87, 0.97). Women with high decision-making had lower odds of experiencing marital control: the spouse became jealous if the respondent talked with other men, and the spouse tried to limit the respondent’s contact with family. However, the odds of experiencing accusation of unfaithfulness (aOR =1.08; 95% CI = 1.01, 1.17) was higher among women with high decision-making capacity compared with those in the decision-making category. Compared to women with low decision-making, those with medium decision-making had lower odds of experiencing all the indicators of marital control.

Discussion

This study examined the association between women’s empowerment (social independence, decision-making, and attitudes to violence) and experience of marital control in SSA. Moderate and high decision-making capacity were associated with lower likelihood of experiencing medium and high marital control. Women with high social independence were less likely to experience medium and high marital control. High attitudes to violence were associated with a lower risk of experiencing medium and high marital control, whereas medium attitudes to violence were associated with a lower risk of experiencing high marital control.

Lower risk of experiencing marital control were reported among women with high autonomy compared to those with low scores in autonomy. This was consistent across four of the five individual indicators of marital control. Our findings align with previous studies [3, 44] that have found autonomy to be associated with a lower risk of IPV, including marital control. Women with high social independence may have strong self-confidence, established social networks, and financial independence. This self-assuredness makes them more assertive and intolerant of controlling behaviours from their partners. Moreover, social independence often correlates with higher levels of education and awareness about gender equality and healthy relationship dynamics [45, 46]. As such, highly independent women may be more knowledgeable about the signs of controlling behaviours and take action to prevent their occurrence. However, the likelihood of partners not permitting women to meet female friends was high among those with medium autonomy scores. This finding may stem from the perception that the woman’s friends could influence her to be more autonomous and resist the man’s authority.

Corollary to autonomy, the study indicates that marital control was significantly lower among women who scored medium to high in decision-making. The pattern of association was consistent across all five indicators of marital control, except for the husband or partner accusing the respondent of unfaithfulness, where those with high decision-making had higher odds of experiencing marital control. Similar findings have been reported in Türkiye [3]. Likewise, the study by Navedand and Persson [47] corroborates our findings, asserting that women who possess decision-making abilities and actively engage in household decision-making are better equipped to withstand oppression and less likely to accept authoritarian behaviour from their partners. In most sub-Saharan African countries, the prevailing social norms and expectations encourage overt control of women by their spouses. However, women with high decision-making capacity can challenge these male-dominated norms and insist on doing whatever they want. Women with limited decision-making capacity may fear negative consequences, such as divorce or abandonment, if they were to challenge or resist marital control. This fear may lead them to comply with their spouses’ wishes to avoid these potential repercussions.

Consistent with previous studies [48, 49] that suggest favourable attitudes toward violence exacerbate marital control, we found that women with high scores in terms of their rejection of violence were less likely to experience marital control. A similar pattern of association was observed across the disaggregated indicators of marital control. This association is expected, as women who are intolerant of violence are likely to exit relationships that exhibit signs of violence. Given that controlling behaviours are known acts constituting IPV, it is expected that women with high scores on attitudes to violence would be less likely to experience marital control.

Evidence from an earlier study has shown that previous experiences with violence often lead to the internalisation and normalisation of violent acts, including controlling behaviours from partners [50]. Consistent with this, our findings revealed a higher likelihood of marital control among women who had witnessed interparental violence. Also, we found rural-urban variation in marital control, with rural-dwelling women being less likely to experience controlling behaviours. This finding is inconsistent with existing literature [3], which suggests that rural-dwelling women are more exposed to controlling behaviours due to their adherence to patriarchal cultural norms that promote submission to one’s partner. However, our study’s findings align with a recent study conducted in SSA [51], which also reported that controlling behaviours were less common among rural-dwelling women than among their urban counterparts.

Consistent with existing literature [6, 50, 52], we found a higher likelihood of marital control among women whose partners consumed alcohol, an association that was evident across all five indicators of marital control. Alcohol consumption may impair judgment and reduce inhibitions, making individuals more prone to engage in controlling behaviours that they might otherwise avoid when sober [53]. In addition, exposure to media was associated with an increased prevalence of marital control. These findings align with Gavin and Kruis’ [54] study, which reported a significant positive association between media exposure and violence, including controlling behaviours. Primarily, the observed association is best explained from the perspective that media platforms often portray women as objects to be controlled by their romantic partners [55, 56], with depictions of pleasurable violence perpetuating the normalisation of such behaviours in relationships [54]. This normalisation reinforces the prevalence of controlling behaviours in romantic relationships.

Strengths and limitations

This study was based on large and nationally representative data, which allows for its findings to be generalised to the broader population of sub-Saharan African women in sexual relationships. In addition, the use of SWPER indicators offers a more comprehensive approach to measuring women’s empowerment. Another notable strength is the disaggregation of components of marital control, providing valuable insights into the specific actions involved. Nevertheless, causality cannot be established because the data were collected in a cross-sectional study. Also, since the data were self-reported, there is a possibility of recall or social desirability bias. Additionally, the absence of data on “does not trust her with money” in most of the country’s DHS data limits our study’s ability to assess the interplay between the economic aspects of marital control and women’s empowerment. Further, the DHS does not collect data on men’s perspectives on social issues, including marital control, sociocultural norms and beliefs, and women’s personal agency. Hence, the findings in our study should be interpreted with caution, as inferences made should be based on the variables included in the study.

Conclusion

Women's empowerment is associated with marital control, such that higher levels of empowerment are associated with lower odds of experiencing marital control. This finding emphasises the need to prioritise women's empowerment initiatives as a core strategy to reduce the prevalence of controlling behaviour by spouses. Hence, governments across the SSA region should provide women with access to quality education and skills development programmes to enhance their economic prospects and decision-making capacity, especially among women from low socioeconomic backgrounds and at younger ages. Recognising the association between witnessing interparental violence and experiencing marital control, policymakers should invest in early interventions and awareness campaigns aimed at breaking the cycle of violence. Reducing the prevalence of controlling behaviour can be achieved by providing support and resources to individuals who have witnessed violence in their homes to help prevent the normalisation of such behaviours. Future studies should be conducted at the country level to clearly understand, using qualitative approaches, the mechanisms through which women's empowerment influences marital control.
