Bilateral tympanic membrane perforation during bronchoscopy in an intensive care unit patient: a case report
Authors: Hejing Bao, Chaofeng Wu, Yanna Wu, Nemin Liu, Canjian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchoscopy is a fundamental and generally safe interventional procedure in respiratory medicine, with recognized complications being predominantly confined to the airways. Traumatic tympanic membrane perforation, while a common form of barotrauma, is exceptionally rarely associated with bronchoscopy. This report describes a case of bilateral tympanic membrane perforation complicating bronchoscopy, aiming to enhance clinicians’awareness of this uncommon iatrogenic injury.Case presentationA 77-year-old man presented with cough and shortness of breath. Emergency investigations revealed a large left-sided pleural effusion, bilateral pneumonia, significantly elevated inflammatory markers, and type I respiratory failure. Following initial stabilization in the intensive care unit, bedside bronchoscopy was performed for microbiological diagnosis. During the procedure, the patient experienced sudden onset of bilateral otalgia and ear bleeding. Urgent otolaryngology consultation confirmed bilateral central tympanic membrane perforations. Conservative management with observation was adopted due to the small size of the perforations. Subsequent etiological investigation indicated a mixed pulmonary infection. The patient was discharged in a stable condition after targeted antimicrobial therapy, and both tympanic membrane perforations healed spontaneously without residual hearing deficit.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that bronchoscopy can, in rare instances, lead to bilateral traumatic tympanic membrane perforation, a presentation potentially more severe than the unilateral injury previously reported. This complication likely results from procedure-induced cough generating abrupt pressure changes transmitted via the Eustachian tubes and may occur more frequently in elderly, critically ill, or less cooperative patients. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for this potential complication. Optimizing sedation-analgesia protocols should be considered for high-risk individuals to aid prevention. When it occurs, small perforations can often be managed successfully with conservative measures, underscoring the importance of timely multidisciplinary collaboration.

Introduction

The tympanic membrane (TM) is a delicate membranous structure that separates the external auditory canal from the middle ear and is highly susceptible to pressure-related injury [1, 2]. Traumatic TM perforation most commonly results from direct mechanical trauma or sudden pressure changes within the ear canal, such as those associated with slap injuries or blast exposure [3]. Although many acute perforations heal spontaneously, they can be complicated by conductive hearing loss, tinnitus, and middle ear infection [4–8].

Flexible bronchoscopy is an essential diagnostic and therapeutic procedure in modern respiratory medicine [9, 10]. It is widely used for airway inspection, microbiological sampling, and tissue acquisition [11–14]. While generally considered safe, bronchoscopy may provoke complications such as cough, hypoxemia, bleeding, and other procedure-related adverse events [15–18]. However, TM perforation associated with bronchoscopy, particularly bilateral perforation, has not been reported in the English-language literature. This report describes a rare case of bilateral TM perforation triggered by violent coughing during bedside bronchoscopy in an intensive care unit (ICU) patient and discusses its potential underlying mechanisms and clinical implications.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old man with a history of hypertension and gout was admitted following a one-week history of persistent non-productive cough and exertional dyspnea. His condition acutely worsened one day prior to admission, with the onset of severe dyspnea accompanied by orthopnea and wheezing, necessitating emergency evaluation. Chest computed tomography demonstrated a massive left-sided pleural effusion causing compressive atelectasis of the underlying lung, alongside bilateral scattered inflammatory opacities (Fig. 1). Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis with neutrophilia, a markedly elevated C-reactive protein level, and evidence of mild renal dysfunction. Arterial blood gas analysis performed on supplemental oxygen showed mild hypoxemia in the context of respiratory alkalosis. On admission, the patient was hypertensive and hypoxemic, sitting upright due to marked respiratory distress, with tachypnea and audible wheezing. Breath sounds were diminished over the left hemithorax. His oxygenation and hemodynamic status improved after initiation of non-invasive ventilatory support combined with antimicrobial therapy, bronchodilators, expectorants, and antihypertensive medications.

To expedite etiological identification, flexible bronchoscopy was subsequently performed at the bedside in the ICU, followed by drainage of the pleural effusion. The procedure was carried out using an Olympus BF-P40 fiberoptic bronchoscope (outer diameter 5.0 mm, working channel 2.2 mm), a standard flexible instrument. Considering the patient's type I respiratory failure and the risk of respiratory depression associated with sedatives, no intravenous sedatives or analgesics were administered. Airway anesthesia was achieved with topical 2% lidocaine solution. Following initial oropharyngeal spray, a "spray-as-you-go" technique was employed via the bronchoscope working channel to apply supplemental anesthesia to the larynx and airways, with a total estimated dose of approximately 200 mg. During the procedure, the patient received oxygen via nasal cannula at a flow rate of 5 L/min. The non-invasive ventilation (mode: S/T; settings: IPAP 10 cm H₂O, EPAP 5 cm H₂O, FiO₂ 60%), which was in use prior to the procedure, was temporarily suspended.

During the passage of the bronchoscope through the nasal cavity and past the glottis, the patient developed intense, paroxysmal irritative coughing accompanied by physical resistance and trismus, necessitating a brief procedural pause. After the patient's response subsided somewhat and supplemental topical lidocaine was applied to the larynx, the bronchoscope was advanced into the trachea. Shortly after this initial episode of severe coughing, the patient complained of bilateral ear pain, and the operator noted blood in the external auditory canals. The procedure was halted immediately, and an urgent otolaryngology consultation was requested.

The medical team assessed the otologic complication as a localized, stable acute injury. However, the life-threatening underlying issue—the etiological diagnosis of severe pneumonia—remained unresolved, and a delay could compromise subsequent targeted therapy. Therefore, it was decided to proceed swiftly to complete the essential diagnostic steps. Upon entering the main trachea and during subsequent suctioning and lavage, the patient's cough reflex markedly diminished. Examination revealed congested and edematous bronchial mucosa with mild luminal narrowing of the left bronchus. The entire procedure, from insertion to completion of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid retrieval, lasted approximately 8–10 min.

An immediate bedside otoscopic examination by the otolaryngologist confirmed fresh, small central perforations of the pars tensa in both tympanic membranes, accompanied by minor bleeding (Fig. 2). Due to the small size of the perforations and the absence of acute symptoms such as vertigo, bedside pure-tone audiometry was not performed. The patient and his family denied any history of Eustachian tube dysfunction, chronic otitis media, or prior ear surgery. In accordance with specialist recommendations, a conservative management plan was implemented, including keeping the ears dry and preventing infection, along with arrangements for close follow-up. Subsequent next-generation sequencing of the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid detected Mycobacterium tuberculosiscomplex, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and Aspergillus fumigatus. SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid testing was also positive. Targeted anti-tuberculosis therapy, antiviral treatment, and supportive care were initiated. Follow-up otologic examination showed progressive healing of both tympanic membranes without residual hearing impairment.

Discussion

To ascertain previous reports of this complication, a systematic literature search was conducted in March 2026.​ The databases PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Google Scholar, and Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang Data) were searched from inception until March 2026. The search strategy employed the following key term combinations: (bronchoscopy OR fiberoptic bronchoscopy) AND (tympanic membrane perforation OR barotrauma OR ear injury)and (iatrogenic) AND (tympanic membrane perforation) AND (cough OR Valsalva). The search yielded only one relevant publication: a Chinese report from 1991 describing unilateral middle ear hemorrhage and tympanic membrane perforation during bronchoscopy [19]. To the best of our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported in the English-language literature of bilateral traumatic tympanic membrane perforation attributed to flexible bronchoscopy.

While bronchoscopy is widely regarded as a routine and relatively safe procedure [20], the present case demonstrates that clinically significant adverse events can occur at sites anatomically remote from the airways when procedure-related physiological stress is sufficiently intense. Thus, this event is not merely a rare complication; it also serves as a reminder that the safety profile of bronchoscopy depends not only on the risk of direct technical injury to the airways but also on the magnitude of systemic physiological responses it may provoke.

In this patient, several features likely converged to create an exceptionally vulnerable state. The presence of severe pneumonia, a massive pleural effusion, hypoxemic respiratory failure, and significant airway irritation are all known to heighten cough reflex sensitivity. Furthermore, the procedure was performed at the bedside in the ICU, an environment that may offer less controlled conditions compared to a dedicated bronchoscopy suite. While sedation and analgesia might be limited due to hemodynamic instability or respiratory compromise, inadequate cough suppression can precipitate sudden and dramatic swings in intrathoracic pressure [21, 22]. Under such circumstances, bronchoscopy may act as the trigger for a pressure-transmission cascade rather than the site of direct injury.

Several alternative explanations for the perforations were considered and deemed less likely. The patient was receiving non-invasive ventilation (NIV) prior to bronchoscopy, but the settings were a low-pressure support mode (Inspiratory Positive Airway Pressure(IPAP) 10 cm H₂O, Expiratory Positive Airway Pressure(EPAP) 5 cm H₂O). More importantly, the perforations occurred at the moment when NIV had been suspended and the patient was taken off the mask for the procedure, making a direct causal role of NIV pressure improbable. The patient had no history of ear disease, hearing loss, ear surgery, or recurrent otitis media, and no related symptoms or signs were reported. The urgent otoscopic examination revealed no stigmata of chronic ear pathology other than the acute perforations. The presentation—sudden, bilateral, and immediately following violent coughing—is more consistent with acute barotrauma than an infectious or inflammatory process. Direct external trauma and spontaneous perforation were also ruled out. Although these factors warrant consideration in theory, the temporal relationship, patient history, and examination findings collectively support the conclusion that the most probable and direct explanation is an inside-to-out barotrauma resulting from bronchoscopy-induced violent coughing.

Middle ear barotrauma typically results from a pressure imbalance across the tympanic membrane. Classic causes include an external-to-internal pressure gradient (e.g., diving, aviation) and an internal-to-external pressure gradient generated by forceful actions (e.g., nose blowing, the Valsalva maneuver). In medical practice, positive pressure ventilation is a well-recognized iatrogenic cause. A similar internal-to-external mechanism has been mentioned in isolated cases of violent vomiting during gastroscopy or coughing upon laryngeal mask airway removal, though these are not direct analogies [23]. A case reported by Li YX described unilateral middle ear hemorrhage and tympanic membrane perforation following fiberoptic bronchoscopy [19], indicating that such procedure-related otologic injuries have been mentioned, albeit extremely rarely, in the historical literature. However, the bilateral and symmetrical perforation presented in our case carries distinct clinical significance. A unilateral injury could stem from local factors or asymmetric pressure changes [24], whereas simultaneous bilateral occurrence strongly supports a systemic, symmetrical, and abrupt pressure surge affecting both middle ears concurrently.

We therefore postulate that the mechanism in our case stems from a reverse Valsalva effect. The violent cough induced by bronchoscopic stimulation generated a sudden and significant increase in intrathoracic and nasopharyngeal pressure. This high pressure was transmitted through the Eustachian tube system, creating a transient internal-to-external pressure gradient within the middle ear cavities that exceeded the tensile limit of the tympanic membranes, resulting in bilateral perforation. This fundamentally differs in the direction of the pressure gradient from traditional middle ear barotrauma seen in diving or aviation, which is caused by external-to-internal pressure changes. Consequently, this case not only represents the first detailed report of bilateral perforation but also provides new evidence for understanding the systemic physiological stress—extending beyond the local airway—that bronchoscopy may potentially trigger.

This mechanism is clinically plausible because the tympanic membrane, particularly the pars tensa, is highly sensitive to sudden pressure differentials. Under normal conditions, the Eustachian tube equilibrates middle ear pressure during swallowing or yawning. However, a rapid and forceful pressure surge can overwhelm this compensatory mechanism, especially when coughing is severe and repetitive. In our patient, the presence of a massive pleural effusion may have further compromised respiratory mechanics, increased the work of breathing, and amplified cough-related pressure fluctuations. The combination of airway inflammation, pleural compression, and procedural stimulation likely created a permissive condition for barotrauma.

From a practical standpoint, this case broadens the recognized spectrum of bronchoscopy-related complications. While most clinicians are vigilant about hypoxemia, bleeding, bronchospasm, arrhythmia, and post-procedure infection, remote otologic complications may not be immediately considered. This case demonstrates that tympanic membrane perforation should be included in the differential diagnosis if a patient develops sudden otalgia, aural fullness, tinnitus, hearing change, or external ear bleeding during or shortly after bronchoscopy. Early recognition is important, as prompt otolaryngologic evaluation can distinguish traumatic perforation from other causes of ear bleeding and guide appropriate conservative management.

This case holds significant implications for ICU practice, as bedside bronchoscopy is frequently performed on critically ill patients who often have a heightened cough reflex and may be unable to cooperate fully. In such high-risk settings, any factor causing a sharp rise in intrathoracic pressure can lead to injury in atypical sites. Clinicians should therefore include otologic complications in the list of rare but possible differential diagnoses for bronchoscopy, particularly when a patient complains of sudden ear pain. For high-risk patients, such as those represented by this case with active infection, airway hyperreactivity, severe cough, or pleural effusion, reinforced airway management is key to preventing such rare complications. Employing a "spray-as-you-go" technique allows for more precise and real-time topical anesthesia, effectively suppressing airway stimulation. If a vigorous response is anticipated, pre-procedure consideration of superior laryngeal nerve block can provide deeper anesthesia to the supraglottic region. Under close monitoring, the use of ultra-short-acting, titratable sedative-analgesic drugs (e.g., a combination of low-dose remifentanil and target-controlled infusion of propofol) to achieve an adequate depth of sedation and cough suppression may offer benefits that outweigh the theoretical risk of respiratory depression. Therefore, this case supports a more individualized risk assessment prior to performing bedside bronchoscopy in unstable patients.

The favorable outcome in this patient is clinically reassuring. Small traumatic tympanic membrane perforations often heal spontaneously, and conservative management with dry ear precautions and observation is typically sufficient in the absence of infection or ossicular chain injury. The complete healing observed in our patient aligns with the natural history of small central perforations and underscores that timely recognition can prevent unnecessary intervention.

Given that this is a single-case report, its observational nature precludes establishing a definitive causal relationship between the bronchoscopy and the tympanic membrane perforation. Although the strong temporal association and plausible pathophysiology support a link, this conclusion requires further validation in larger populations. As with many rare complications, the value of this report lies not in its frequency but in pattern recognition: once a rare event is identified and its mechanism understood, it often becomes preventable. For bronchoscopists and ICU clinicians, this case reinforces that even routine procedures can yield unexpected consequences when performed on patients with marked physiological vulnerability.

Key points

Bronchoscopy can, in rare instances, precipitate barotrauma at sites remote from the respiratory tract.

Sudden bilateral tympanic membrane perforation during bronchoscopy should prompt consideration of cough-induced intrathoracic pressure transmission via the patent Eustachian tubes.

Patients with severe pneumonia, airway hyperreactivity, pleural effusion, or inadequate sedation may be at increased risk of pressure-related complications during bedside bronchoscopy.

Early recognition of otologic symptoms—such as ear pain, bleeding, tinnitus, or hearing change—enables prompt otolaryngologic evaluation and appropriate conservative management.

Meticulous cough suppression, gentle airway manipulation, and individualized procedural risk assessment are crucial for enhancing the safety of bronchoscopy in critically ill patients.

Conclusion

Bilateral tympanic membrane perforation is an exceptionally rare and previously unreported complication of bronchoscopy. This case suggests that bronchoscopy-induced coughing can generate sufficient intrathoracic pressure to cause bilateral middle ear barotrauma via the Eustachian tubes. In critically ill patients with severe infection, pleural effusion, and heightened airway reactivity, bronchoscopy should be undertaken with careful risk stratification, optimized cough suppression, and gentle technique. Awareness of this potential complication may facilitate earlier diagnosis, appropriate management, and safer procedural practice.
