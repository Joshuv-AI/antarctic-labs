Higher dietary antioxidant quality is linked to reduced diabetic nephropathy risk in type 2 diabetes: a case-control study
Authors: Fatemeh Jafarieh, Leila Mahmoudieh, Behnood Abbasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress plays an important role in the pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy (DN). The dietary antioxidant quality score (DAQS) indicates overall dietary antioxidant intake. We conducted this case-control study on 206 adults aged 18–65 with diabetes (103 cases with DN and 103 controls without DN) who attended the Labafinezhad clinic in Tehran, Iran, during 2023–2024. DN was defined as persistent albuminuria (≥ 30 mg/g creatinine) or reduced eGFR (< 60 ml/min/1.73 m2). General characteristics were collected, and a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) containing 147 items was used to assess dietary intakes. Data from dietary intakes of vitamins C, E, A, selenium, and zinc were used to evaluate DAQS. The associations between DAQS and DN were assessed by logistic regression models. An inverse association between DAQS and the odds of DN was found (OR: 0.667; 95% CI: 0.466–0.954; P = 0.027). This association was significant even after controlling for possible confounding variables, including sex, age, BMI, blood pressure, waist circumference, diabetes duration, smoking, physical activity, HbA1c, and hypertension medications. Our results may be used to develop potential dietary recommendations to delay diabetic nephropathy development and to use DAQS as a probable predictor of DN.

Introduction

One of the “fastest growing global health emergencies of the 21st century” is diabetes, which is a global health challenge1. According to the latest data, the number of patients with diabetes from 200 million in 1990 reached 830 million in 2022, and in 2021, over 2 million deaths were caused by diabetes and diabetic kidney disease2. Diabetes causes approximately one trillion USD in direct health expenditures, and it will increase by 20301. Although diet is one of the modifiable risk factors of diabetes complications3, there is limited evidence regarding the association between the dietary antioxidant quality score (DAQS) and diabetic nephropathy (DN). This gap underscores the need for further investigation.

A significant microvascular complication associated with diabetes is DN4, which occurs in 20–40% of patients with diabetes, finally leading to kidney transplantation and end-stage renal disease (ESRD)5. Multiple factors, including hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension, are involved in DN pathogenesis. At the same time, oxidative stress is the principal and integral factor that connects them and ultimately leads to diabetic kidney disease (DKD)4,6,7.

To realize the total intake of dietary antioxidants, we calculated the DAQS. DAQS is a score that considers intake of selected dietary antioxidants, including vitamin C, vitamin A, vitamin E, zinc, and selenium8. A probable association between different anti-inflammatory and antioxidant dietary patterns and diabetic kidney disease has been studied but findings are inconsistent: lower risk of DN has been associated with higher adherence to the mediterranean (Med) diet9,10, dietary approaches to stop hypertension (DASH)10,11, plant-based dietary index (PDI)12, dietary phytochemical index (PI)13, dietary total antioxidant capacity (DTAC)14, dietary flavonoid intake15 and oxidative balance score (OBS)16 while in other studies there was no significant association between Med diet adherence and cardiovascular risk factors and renal function in DN patients17 and among type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients, there was no association between DTAC and chronic kidney disease (CKD)18.

It has been shown that antioxidant vitamins, such as vitamin C, vitamin E, and selenium, can play a role in reducing systemic inflammation, preserving endothelial function, and regulating oxidative stress19. In addition, the protective role of zinc against oxidative stress has been noted20. In previous studies, higher DAQS has been associated with lower systolic blood pressure in adults21,22. Among adults with diabetes, higher DAQS has been associated with lower risk of death23, in patients with CKD, higher DAQS was associated with lower mortality24, and in the context of T2DM risk, higher DAQS was associated with a lower risk of developing T2DM25, but research on the relationship between DAQS and DN is currently lacking. As we know, there is currently insufficient evidence considering the association between DAQS and DN. Therefore, this research aimed to investigate the relationship between DAQS and DN. This study uses the same dataset as Jafarieh et al. (2025)26, which focused on anthropometric indicators of DN. The current study hypothesizes that higher DAQS is associated with a lower risk of DN.

Methods

Study population

This case-control study was conducted among adult patients aged 18–65 with diabetes attending Labafinezhad Hospital in Tehran, Iran, during 2023–2024. We utilized a convenience sample method, which is a nonprobability sampling technique, to select the participants. The sample size was calculated using Fleiss formula with a confidence level of 95% and a power of 80%. We excluded 24 participants due to incomplete biochemical data, 4 were misdiagnosed, two had incomplete dietary data, and two reported energy intakes of more than 4200 kcal per day. In total, 103 diabetic patients with nephropathy as the case group, and 103 patients with diabetes without diabetic nephropathy from the same center as the control group were enrolled in the study. Cases and controls were matched for age and gender. Frequency matching was done according to age and sex between cases and controls in order to ensure that both parameters would be comparable among cases and controls. The process involved ensuring that the controls would have the same distribution of age and sex as the cases. Diabetes and nephropathy were diagnosed by an endocrinologist and nephrologist, based on the American Diabetes Association’s27 and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) diagnostic criteria: persistent albuminuria (≥ 30 mg/g creatinine) or reduced eGFR (< 60 ml/min/1.73 m2)28. This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We described the aim of the study to all individuals, and informed consent was obtained to enroll in this study. The National Committee for Ethics in Biomedical Research, Iran Ministry of Health and Medical Education, approved the study protocol with the following identification: (IR.IAU.SRB.REC.1401.393).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 18–65 with type 2 diabetes mellitus were included in the study. Participants with a background of autoimmune disorders, cancer, chronic liver disease, gastrointestinal disorder, coronary artery bypass grafting, stroke, dialysis, or if they were pregnant or lactating, were not included in the study. Also, we excluded those participants whose response to the FFQ was less than 80% or whose reported total energy intake was < 800 or > 4200 kcal per day29.

Data collection

General variables, including sex, age, education level, smoking status, diabetes duration, and past medical and medication history, were recorded. Participants’ body weight (kg) was measured while they were dressed in light clothing and not wearing shoes. A non-stretchable tape was used to measure height and waist circumference (WC). We measured height in a standing position without shoes and WC between the top of the pelvic bone and the bottom of the ribs. We divided weight by the square of height (kg/m²) to calculate the body mass index (BMI). Blood pressure was measured in a sitting position using a sphygmomanometer. To evaluate the physical activity level of the participants, a valid physical activity questionnaire was used30. Biochemical variables, including serum creatinine and HbA1c, and the medication history were obtained from the patient’s medical records. Their HTN medication was categorized as: no HTN medication (none), taking one class, two classes, three classes, or more than three classes of HTN medication. HTN medication classes were (1) Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), (2) Angiotensin-converting-enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, (3) mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, (4) calcium channel blockers, (5) loop diuretics, (6) thiazide diuretics, (7) Potassium-sparing diuretics. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine Eq. (2021 CKD-EPI eGFRcr) was used to calculate eGFR31.

Dietary intake assessment

A semi-quantitative FFQ consisting of 147 items, which is both valid and reliable32, was used by a trained dietitian through direct interviews to evaluate the dietary intake of the participants. We used household measures to convert the frequency of consumption for each food to grams33. The Nutritionist IV program, designed for Iranian dietary practices, was utilized to transform the frequency of each food item in the FFQ into its equivalent weight in grams per day.

Assessment of the dietary antioxidant quality score

DAQS was calculated using the Tur method8. The daily intake of selected antioxidants (vitamins C, A, E, zinc, and selenium) was compared to their recommended daily intake (RDI). According to the tur method8, we considered a value of 0 when the intake fell below 2/3 of the RDI, while intakes exceeding 2/3 of the RDI it was considered 1. The total DAQS varied from 0 (indicating very low antioxidant quality) to 5 (representing high antioxidant quality).

Statistical analyses

We reported qualitative variables as frequency (%) and were assessed between case and control groups using the Chi-square test. Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and their normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For variables following a normal distribution, the independent t-test, and for those that were not normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney-U test was utilized to examine the quantitative variables among groups. The association between DAQS and DN was evaluated using binary logistic regression. In the crude model, we did not adjust for any confounders. In model 1, sex, age, and BMI, and in model 2, sex, age, BMI, Bp, WC, diabetes duration, smoking, PA, HbA1c, and HTN medication were adjusted. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27 (SPSS, IBM, USA). When the P-value was less than 0.05, the analysis was considered significant.

Results

General characteristics of participants

Overall, the study was conducted on 206 subjects, including 103 cases and 103 controls. The mean ± SD of age in cases and controls were (58.12 ± 5.702) and (56.53 ± 5.916), respectively. 50.5% and 54.4% of cases and controls were female. The mean ± SD of waist circumference in cases was (102.42 ± 12.147) vs. controls (97.38 ± 10.466), and diabetes duration in cases was (16.09 ± 8.593) vs. controls (10.78 ± 8.496), which was significantly higher in cases (p < 0.05). Cases had lower eGFR (mean ± SD: 41.88 ± 18.210 vs. 82.30 ± 11.951) and physical activity than controls (p < 0.05). We did not find any significant differences in age (p = 0.052), weight (p = 0.062), height (p = 0.655), BMI (p = 0.115), HbA1c (p = 0.242), sex (p = 0.577), education level (p = 0.314), blood pressure (p = 0.130) and smoking status (p = 0.497) between cases and controls. The differences seen in the studied variables between the case and control groups in relation to the duration of diabetes, waist circumference, eGFR, physical activity, and antihypertensive medication can be explained by the physio-pathological differences seen in patients with DN. DN is known to occur after a prolonged period of diabetes, and patients have greater central obesity, leading to progression of the disease. Variations seen in the eGFR and antihypertensive medication can be attributed to the difference in the degree of disease severity and approaches used in managing the patients. Therefore, we included the above-mentioned variables in the multivariate models to adjust for them. The association between DAQS and diabetic nephropathy

The association between DAQS and DN was examined using binary logistic regression in crude and adjusted models. The odds ratios with a 95% confidence interval in the crude and adjusted models are shown in Table 2. We found a significant association between DAQS and DN in the crude model (OR: 0.667; 95% CI: 0.466–0.954; P = 0.027). In other words, higher DAQS was associated with 34% lower odds of DN. In model 1, a significant association was observed between higher DAQS and the odds of DN after adjusting for sex, age, and BMI (OR: 0.685; 95% CI: 0.476–0.986; P = 0.042), which was associated with a reduced risk of DN by 32%. In model 2, after adjusting for sex, age, BMI, Bp, WC, diabetes duration, smoking, PA, HbA1c, and HTN medication, we also found a reverse association between DAQS and the odds of DN (OR: 0.618; 95% CI: 0.400–0.956; P = 0.031). It should be mentioned that total energy intake was not adjusted. Considering that the DAQS captures food choices and the antioxidant quality of the diet rather than energy or antioxidant quantity, we believe unadjusted estimates better reflect the association of interest, and that over-adjustment, in light of the qualitative nature of the DAQS, may cause the relationship to become non-significant.

Discussion

The association between DAQS and DN in T2DM patients was investigated in this study. DAQS is a score that compares the daily intake of selected antioxidants (vitamins C, A, E, selenium, and zinc) to their recommended daily intake (RDI), which is used to estimate the overall dietary antioxidant intake. In previous studies, the relationship between individual antioxidants and DN was mainly assessed, and investigations on the potential correlation between various antioxidants are limited. Therefore, assessing the association between overall dietary antioxidants and DAQS as a certain dietary score and DN is important. The findings of the current study suggest that the likelihood of DN in T2DM patients was associated with decreasing DAQS. These findings support our hypothesis that higher DAQS is associated with a lower risk of DN among T2DM patients. After multiple confounding variables, such as sex, age, BMI, blood pressure, waist circumference, diabetes duration, smoking, and physical activity, were adjusted, the DAQS remained negatively associated with the risk of DN.

In line with our results, previous studies that investigated the association between dietary scores and DN found that a higher composite dietary antioxidant index (CDAI), including zinc, selenium, vitamin E, vitamin C, vitamin A, and carotenoids, could significantly reduce DKD risk 26% and mortality risk of 33% after multivariate adjustment34. In a cohort study on 7077 T2DM patients, there was a significant association between higher healthy lifestyle adherence and lower risk of microvascular complications35. Studies by Mirzababaei et al. were conducted on 210 women with diabetes, and they found that higher PDI, DASH adherence, PI, and Med diet (all representing higher intakes of antioxidant sources such as fruits and vegetables) were associated with lower odds of DN in women with type 2 diabetes9–13. In another study, high adherence to patterns of fat-soluble vitamins and minerals were associated with decreased DN risk by 47% and 49%, respectively, but there was no relationship between patterns of water-soluble vitamins and DN risk36. In a cross-sectional study on 5389 patients with diabetes, a reduced risk of low-eGFR, albuminuria, and DKD was associated with higher OBS and dietary OBS, which is an indicator of lifestyle factors and dietary components that assess pro-oxidants and antioxidants exposure16.

In contrast to our findings, results of a randomized trial found no significant association between Med diet and DN37. A case-control study conducted on 210 T2DM patients found no association between DTAC and CKD in these patients18. These discrepancies may be due to several methodological differences, including variations in population demographics (elderly patients as well as adults were included in this study, although we did not include patients older than 65 years old), different criteria to define nephropathy (considering only eGFR and not albuminuria state), different equations to estimate eGFR [Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) vs. CKD-EPI 2021], and different methods to assess antioxidant quality of the diet (DTAC vs. DAQS). Also, another cross-sectional study on 270 patients with DN did not find any significant association between a Med diet adherence and cardiovascular risk factors and renal function among DN patients17. These contrary results may be due to variations in the study design (cross-sectional vs. case-control), DN diagnosis criteria (serum creatinine vs. eGFR and albuminuria), equations to estimate eGFR (MDRD vs. CKD-EPI 2021), and dietary intake assessment method (168-item FFQ vs. 147-item FFQ).

To the best of our knowledge, there is relatively scare research investigating the association between overall dietary antioxidants and the risk of DN, but favorable effects of individual antioxidants on diabetic nephropathy and biomarkers of kidney function in patients with diabetes have been explored; for example, it has been shown that in T2DM patients who have albuminuria, short-term vitamin E and C in pharmacological doses could lower albumin excretion rate38. Another study concluded that the effect of vitamin C and E in combination could significantly lower the urinary albumin excretion rate (UAER) and HbA1c in patients with diabetic nephropathy39. Results of a clinical trial on 69 T2DM patients revealed that 200 mg vitamin C and 100 IU vitamin E supplement in combination (P = 0.034), and also 200 mg vitamin C, 100 IU vitamin E, 200 mg Mg, and 30 mg Zn (P = 0.005) could significantly reduce UAER40. A nationwide study concluded that elevated serum concentrations of vitamin C were associated with a lower likelihood of albuminuria, low eGFR, and CKD41. In a cohort study on 1692 participants, higher intakes of vitamin C and E lowered the risk of incident CKD42.

It has been demonstrated that 12 weeks of supplementation with oral high-dose vitamin E could be protective against biomarkers of kidney damage, inflammatory markers, and oxidative stress in DN patients43. 12 weeks of 800 IU/day vitamin E could induce positive effects on glutathione level and lipid profile in DN patients44. Results of a randomized controlled trial revealed that 12 weeks of supplementation with 400 mg/day Tocovid (tocotrienol-rich vitamin E) could significantly improve renal function, as assessed by serum creatinine and eGFR in DN patients, which remained significant 6–9 months post-washout45. In another trial, 400 mg/day tocovid could significantly reduce serum creatinine and significantly improve eGFR after 8 months46, and supplementation with 200 mg Tocovid twice a day for 8 weeks could significantly reduce serum creatinine47. In a prospective clinical study on 64 patients with type 1 and 64 patients with type 2 diabetes, vitamin E supplementation could gradually and significantly decrease total cholesterol, diastolic blood pressure, and post-prandial blood sugar, and it was beneficial for diabetes complications as well as CVD, retinopathy, and foot ulcer, but not statistically significant improvements in nephropathy48. Results of a cross-sectional study revealed that there was an association between higher dietary vitamin E intake and reduced prevalence of CKD49. It has been shown in animal models that vitamin E can prevent DN by inhibiting the protein kinase C pathway and normalizing cellular levels of diacylglycerol50.

Insufficient intake of β-carotene, as a precursor of vitamin A, in CKD patients has been reported51. Higher retinol intake has been associated with a 26% reduction in CKD incidence in T2DM, and lower retinol intake has been attributed as a risk factor for CKD onset52. There was an association between higher dietary carotenoid intake and reduced CKD prevalence in women53. In cell culture and animal model studies, retinoids reduced inflammation and interstitial fibrosis50.

The results of a clinical trial on 60 DN patients demonstrated that 12 weeks of selenium supplementation (200 µg/day) had beneficial effects on plasma glutathione peroxidase (GPx), insulin resistance markers, as well as HOMA-IR, serum insulin levels, and HOMA-B, and some inflammatory and oxidative stress54,55. In a study on DN patients, 12 weeks of 50 mg elemental zinc as zinc sulfate supplementation could be beneficial in decreasing blood glucose, TG, UAER, and inflammation56. Results of a crossover trial on 39 patients with diabetes and microalbuminuria showed that supplementation with 30 mg elemental zinc for 3 months could significantly decrease albumin excretion in these patients57.

The association between DAQS and diabetic nephropathy risk has not been explored in previous studies. Sampling and selection of cases and controls were in the same period and from the same hospital. To assess the dietary intakes, we used a widely applied and valid FFQ that could reflect long-term dietary intake, and a trained dietitian interviewed patients to gather the food frequency data so that we minimized any possible errors as compared to administering by patients themselves. We faced some limitations that might influence the results. Due to the case-control design of this study, we were unable to establish a causal relationship between DAQS and DN. It should be considered that FFQ is memory-based, and possible under/over-reporting, as well as recall bias, portion size estimation error, and cognitive limitations should be taken into account. Another limitation of the current study is the lack of information regarding other possible confounders, which include medication adherence, co-morbidities, and dietary patterns beyond the DAQS, that may have affected the results. It is important to consider these confounders in future research. One of the limitations of our analysis is the fact that there was a significant difference in the duration of diabetes between groups, with the case group having a relatively longer duration of diabetes than the controls. As the length of diabetes is highly correlated with decreasing eGFR and increasing the risk of developing DN, such disparity might have introduced some form of confounding bias, leading to an overestimation of the relationship between the exposures and DN. While we did adjust for the duration of diabetes in our multivariable models, some forms of confounding might still be present.

The present study added new evidence to the relatively scarce explorations on the association between dietary antioxidants and the risk of DN. In summary, our results indicated that in T2DM patients, higher DAQS is associated with lower odds of DN. In other words, DAQS may be a possible predictor score of DN risk in T2DM patients, considering that its calculation is simple, inexpensive, and quick. To confirm these findings, more prospective studies are suggested.
