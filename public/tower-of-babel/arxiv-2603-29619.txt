The Euler system of gas dynamics
Eduard Feireisl

Abstract
This is a survey highlighting several recent results concerning well/ill posedness of the Euler system of gas dynamics. Solutions of the system are identified as limits of consistent approximations generated either by physically more complex problems, notably the Navier-Stokes-Fourier system, or by the approximate schemes in numerical experiments. The role of the fundamental principles encoded in the First and Second law of thermodynamics in identifying a unique physically admissible solution is examined.

Introduction

The
Euler system of gas dynamics
is a mathematical model describing the time evolution of an ideal gas in the framework of continuum
mechanics. From the mathematics viewpoint, the problem represents an iconic example of a system of non–linear conservation laws, see
Part IV of the monograph by Benzoni-Gavage and Serre
[
8
]
or Dafermos
[
17
]
. The system consists of three field equations
reflecting the underlying physical principles of

\displaystyle\bullet\ \mbox{\bf mass conservation:}\hskip 221.93158pt\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}\bm{m}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\bullet\ \mbox{\bf momentum balance:}\hskip 122.34692pt\partial_{t}\bm{m}+{\rm div}_{x}\left(\frac{\bm{m}\otimes\bm{m}}{\varrho}\right)+\nabla_{x}p

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\bullet\ \mbox{\bf energy balance:}\hskip 176.407pt\partial_{t}E+{\rm div}_{x}\left[\Big(E+p\Big)\frac{\bm{m}}{\varrho}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(1.3)

where
\varrho=\varrho(t,x)
is the
mass density
,
\bm{m}=\bm{m}(t,x)=\varrho\bm{u}(t,x)
the
momentum
with the corresponding bulk velocity
\bm{u}
,

p
the pressure, and
E
the
total energy
. For the sake of simplicity, we suppose the gas occupies a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
,
with an impermeable rigid boundary,

\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\ \bm{n}\ \mbox{the outer normal vector to}\ \partial\Omega.

(1.4)

In this study, we focus mostly on the physically relevant multi–dimensional setting
d=3
.

1.1
The Second law of thermodynamics, entropy

The Euler system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) supplemented with the boundary condition (
1.4
) is
thermodynamically closed
, meaning
neither mass nor energy are exchanged with the “outer world”. The asymptotic behaviour of closed systems
is characterized in the most flagrant way by the celebrated statement of Clausius:

The energy of the world is constant; its entropy tends to a maximum.

While the time evolution of the energy is governed by equation (
1.3
), the entropy can be identified only through a suitable

equation of state
relating the pressure
p
and the
internal energy

\varrho e=E-\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\bm{m}|^{2}}{\varrho}

to the density
\varrho
and the
absolute temperature

\vartheta
. The existence of
entropy

s
follows by imposing
Gibbs’ law

\vartheta Ds=De+pD\left(\frac{1}{\varrho}\right).

(1.5)

Formally, meaning under the assumption of smoothness of all quantities in question, the entropy
s
satisfies a transport equation

\partial_{t}(\varrho s)+{\rm div}_{x}(s\bm{m})=0\ \mbox{or}\ \partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{x}s=0.

(1.6)

In particular, the entropy remains constant if constant initially, which gives rise to a reduced
isentropic
Euler system. Here, we focus
on the general case with non-constant entropy.

Note that (
1.6
) is clearly at odds with Clausius’ statement quoted above as the entropy obeying
(
1.6
) is a
conserved quantity
. In the context of gas dynamics, however, solutions of the Euler system
are known to develop singularities - the so–called shock waves - in a finite time for a fairly general class of the initial states.
Accordingly,
global-in-time
solutions, as long as they exist, satisfy the Euler system in the weak (distributional) sense. The entropy
equation (
1.6
) is relaxed to the inequality

\partial_{t}(\varrho s)+{\rm div}_{x}(s\bm{m})\geq 0,

meaning the entropy is produced in accordance with the Second law of thermodynamics.
This can be viewed as a ghost effect of dissipative terms in the vanishing viscosity limit, where the Euler system is understood
as an asymptotic state of the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system with vanishing dissipation.

Introducing
the
total entropy

S=\varrho s
we write the entropy inequality in the form of

\partial_{t}S+{\rm div}_{x}\left(S\frac{\bm{m}}{\varrho}\right)\geq 0

(1.7)

usually interpreted as an
admissibility criterion
in the class of weak solutions of the Euler system.
Moreover, by means of Schwartz representation theorem, we may interpret (
1.7
) as

\bullet\ \mbox{\bf entropy inequality:}\hskip 170.71652pt\partial_{t}S+{\rm div}_{x}\left(S\frac{\bm{m}}{\varrho}\right)=\sigma,

(1.8)

with the
entropy production rate

\sigma
– a non–negative measure.

We consider a
polytropic equation of state
:

p=(\gamma-1)\varrho e,\ \mbox{with the adiabatic exponent}\ \gamma>1.

(1.9)

Apparently,
the equation of state (
1.9
) is
incomplete
, in particular, the temperature
\vartheta
is not uniquely determined. Indeed Gibbs’ equation
(
1.5
), together with (
1.9
), yield

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}}\right),

(1.10)

and,

\displaystyle e(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\vartheta}{\gamma-1}\frac{\vartheta^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}}{\varrho}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}}\right),

\displaystyle s(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle=S\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}}\right),\ S^{\prime}(Z)=-\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\frac{\gamma P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z^{2}},

(1.11)

for an arbitrary function
P
. Thus the absolute temperature
\vartheta
is determined by
\varrho
and
e
modulo the function
P
, see Cowperthwaite
[
15
]
, Müller and Ruggeri
[
44
]
, or
[
34
, Chapters 2,3]
. To fix this problem, we consider
P(Z)=Z
obtaining the standard

Boyle–Mariotte law

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=\varrho\vartheta,\ e=c_{v}\vartheta,\ c_{v}=\frac{1}{\gamma-1},\ s(\varrho,\vartheta)=c_{v}\log(\vartheta)-\log(\varrho).

(1.12)

1.2
State–of–the art

Let us briefly summarize the available results concerning well–posedness of the Euler system.

1.2.1
Strong/classical solutions

As is well known, the Euler system is well posed, locally in time, for sufficiently regular initial data. The relevant result
was obtained by Schochet
[
46
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
Short time existence).

Suppose that

•

the thermodynamic functions
p=p(\varrho,\vartheta)
,
e=e(\varrho,\vartheta)
,
s=s(\varrho,\vartheta)
are three times continuously
differentiable for
\varrho>0
,
\vartheta>0
, satisfy Gibbs’ law (
1.5

\frac{\partial p(\varrho,\vartheta)}{\partial\varrho}>0,\ \frac{\partial e(\varrho,\vartheta)}{\partial\vartheta}>0\ \mbox{for all}\ \varrho>0,\vartheta>0.

•

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded domain with
C^{\infty}-
boundary;

•

the initial data belong to the class

\varrho_{0},\ \vartheta_{0}\in W^{3,2}(\Omega),\ \bm{u}_{0}\in W^{3,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}),\ \varrho_{0}>0,\ \vartheta_{0}>0\ \mbox{in}\ \overline{\Omega};

•

the compatibility conditions

\partial^{k}_{t}\bm{u}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0

hold for
k=0,1,2
.

Then there exists
T>0
such that
the Euler system (
1.1
) – (
1.3
), with the boundary condition (
1.4
), and the initial data

\varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0},\ \bm{u}(0,\cdot)=\bm{u}_{0},\ \vartheta(0,\cdot)=\vartheta_{0},

admits a classical solution in
(0,T)\times\Omega
,

\varrho(t,\cdot),\ \vartheta(t,\cdot)>0\ \mbox{in}\ \overline{\Omega}\ \mbox{for}\ t\in[0,T).

By classical solution we mean that all functions
\varrho
,
\vartheta
, and
\bm{u}
are continuously differentiable up to the boundary
and the equations hold pointwise in
(0,T)\times\Omega
. As a matter of fact, the solutions are constructed in the
Sobolev class

\cap_{k=0}^{3}C^{3-k}([0,T];W^{k,2}(\Omega)),

whence the desired regularity follows from the Sobolev embedding
W^{2,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

1.2.2
Weak solutions

It is well known that the life span of classical/strong solutions may be finite for a large class of initial data, see e.g. the monograph by Smoller

[
47
]
. Thus if the Euler system is to be retained as a mathematical model of the gas in a large time lap, the class of solutions must be extended.
In view of the character of the available results we want to discuss, we consider the space periodic boundary conditions replacing
\Omega
by the flat torus

\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\ \Big|\ x_{i}\in[0,1]|_{\{0;1\}},\ i=1,\dots,d\right\},\ d=1,2,3.

Adopting the equation of state (
1.12
) we say
a triple
(\varrho,\vartheta,\bm{u})
is
weak solution
of the Euler system in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
emanating from the initial
data
(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0},\bm{u}_{0})
if the following holds:

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Big(\varrho\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varrho_{0}\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(1.13)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
;

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Big(\varrho\bm{u}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}+\varrho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}:\nabla_{x}\bm{\varphi}+p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}\bm{\varphi}\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varrho_{0}\bm{u}_{0}\cdot\bm{\varphi}(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(1.14)

for any
\bm{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\varrho|\bm{u}|^{2}+\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta)\right)\partial_{t}\varphi+\left(\frac{1}{2}\varrho|\bm{u}|^{2}+\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta)+p(\varrho,\vartheta)\right)\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varrho_{0}|\bm{u}_{0}|^{2}+\varrho_{0}e(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})\right)\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(1.15)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

In addition, we say that a weak solutions is (entropy) admissible, if the entropy inequality

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Big(\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\leq-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varrho_{0}s(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(1.16)

holds for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
\varphi\geq 0
.

1.3
Well posedness for the Euler system?

Let us finish the introductory section by formulating the first and still largely open problem:

Open problem I:

Despite the large number of recent results concerning the existence of wild data and infinitely many weak solutions to the Euler
system, the existence of an entropy admissible solution for arbitrary, say bounded, initial data remains open.
Specifically, does the Euler system admit a globally defined entropy admissible weak solution for any finite energy initial data?

Introducing the class of weak solutions seemingly did not have any positive impact on the problem of well posedness of the Euler system.
As we have seen above, even the entropy admissible ones are not uniquely determined by the (initial) data, and, moreover,
the class of weak solutions is possibly not large enough to guarantee existence. In this survey, we summarize some recent efforts
to restore well posedness for the Euler and possibly similar systems in fluid dynamics.

2
Dissipative measure–valued (DMV) solutions

The idea of the concept of
measure–valued solution
to systems of conservation law goes back to the pioneering paper by
DiPerna
[
23
]
, and was developed in the context of the incompressible Euler system in a series of papers of DiPerna and Majda
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
,

[

]
. The Euler system is interpreted as an inertial range of turbulence, specifically as a limit of a possibly oscillatory sequence of

]

Adopting the standard equation of state (
1.12
)
we set

p(\varrho,S)=(\gamma-1)\varrho e(\varrho,S)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varrho^{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{S}{c_{v}\varrho}\right)\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho>0,\\
0\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho=0,\ S\leq 0,\\
\infty\ \mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

It is easy to check

\frac{\partial\varrho e(\varrho,S)}{\partial S}=\vartheta>0\ \mbox{whenever}\ \varrho>0,

(2.2)

meaning

is a strictly increasing function of total entropy

Similarly, we define the kinetic energy

\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\bm{m}|^{2}}{\varrho}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\bm{m}|^{2}}{\varrho}\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho>0,\\
0\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho=0,\bm{m}=0.\\
\infty\ \mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

Now, by virtue of (
2.1
), (

), the total energy

expressed in terms of the variables

is a convex l.s.c. function in

ranging in
[0,\infty]
, strictly convex on its domain.
Note that convexity of the total energy is equivalent to

of the system, cf.

[
7
]

[

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\cdot\bm{\varphi}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=\tau_{1}-}^{t=\tau_{2}-}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\mathds{1}_{\varrho_{\varepsilon}>0}\left(\frac{\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}}{\varrho_{\varepsilon}}+p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},S_{\varepsilon})\mathbb{I}\right):\nabla_{x}\bm{\varphi}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{[\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}{\rm d}{e}^{3}_{\varepsilon}[\bm{\varphi}],\ \bm{m}_{\varepsilon}(0-,\cdot)\equiv\bm{m}_{0},

\displaystyle{e}^{3}_{\varepsilon}[\bm{\varphi}]

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{M}[0,T],\ \int_{[0,T]}{\rm d}|{e}^{3}_{\varepsilon}[\bm{\varphi}]|\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0

(2.6)

for any
0\leq\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq T
,
\bm{\varphi}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};R^{d})
,
\bm{\varphi}\cdot\bm{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
;

•

entropy minimum principle:

S_{\varepsilon}\geq\underline{s}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \mbox{a.a. in}\ (0,T)\times\Omega

(2.7)

for a certain
\underline{s}\in R
;

•

approximate entropy inequality:

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\Omega}S_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\varphi\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=\tau_{1}-}^{t=\tau_{2}-}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\mathds{1}_{\varrho_{\varepsilon}>0}\left(S_{\varepsilon}\frac{\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}}{\varrho_{\varepsilon}}\right):\nabla_{x}\varphi\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+\int_{[\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}{\rm d}e^{4}_{\varepsilon}[\varphi],

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}(0-,\cdot)

\displaystyle\equiv S_{0},

\displaystyle e^{4}[\varphi]

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{M}[0,T],\ \int_{[0,T]}{\rm d}|e^{4}[\varphi]|\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0

(2.8)

holds for any
0\leq\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq T
, and any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
,
\varphi\geq 0
.

Here, the
consistency errors

e^{i}[\varphi]
,
i=2,3,4
are signed measures on the compact time interval
[0,T]
.
In particular, the quantities

t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\varphi\ \,{\rm d}{x},\ t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{\varphi}\ \,{\rm d}{x},\ t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}S_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\varphi\ \,{\rm d}{x}

can be interpreted as BV functions defined in
[0,T]
.

It is worth noting that we have replaced the energy equation (
1.3
) by the integrated total energy balance (
2.4
) and added the entropy inequality
(
2.8
). Consistent approximations arise as solutions of suitable numerical schemes, several examples are discussed in the monograph

[
31
, Part III]
. Another, more physical example of a consistent approximation is the sequence obtained
in the vanishing viscosity/dissipation limit, see
[
11
, Section 3.4]
,
[
29
]
. From this point of view, the asymptotic behaviour
of a sequence of consistent approximation examined below is closely related to turbulence.

2.2
Asymptotic limits of consistent approximations

It follows from the energy inequality (
2.4
) combined with the minimum entropy
principle (
2.7
) that

\displaystyle\varrho_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\ \mbox{weakly-(*) in}\ L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to\bm{m}\ \mbox{weakly-(*) in}\ L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\Omega;R^{d})),

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to S\ \mbox{weakly-(*) in}\ L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(\Omega))

(2.9)

at least for a suitable subsequence, see
[
31
, Chapter 7]
. Moreover, by the same token, we may assume

E(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\bm{m}_{\varepsilon},S_{\varepsilon})\to\overline{E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)}\ \mbox{weakly-(*) in}\ L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega})),

(2.10)

where, in view of convexity of
E
,

0\leq\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(\tau,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}\overline{E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)}\leq\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\equiv\mathcal{E}_{0}\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ \tau\in(0,T).

(2.11)

By analogy with the theory of (deterministic) turbulence, we may interpret
\mathcal{E}_{0}
as the total (conserved) energy,

\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}
as the mean flow energy, and their difference

\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}
as turbulent energy. We say the consistency limit is
turbulent
if

\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\neq 0
. As the weak solutions of the Euler system conserve the total energy,
we immediately observe the following implication:

\mbox{{\it consistency limit is turbulent}}\ \Rightarrow\ \mbox{{\it the limit}}\ (\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \mbox{{\it IS NOT a weak solution of the Euler system.}}

(2.12)

2.2.1
Dissipative solutions–limits of turbulent consistent approximations

In view of the convergence (
2.9
), it is possible to associate to
the sequence
(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\bm{m}_{\varepsilon},S_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
a parametrised (Young) measure
(\mathcal{V}_{t,x})_{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega}
,

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{t,x}:(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d+2}),\ \mathcal{V}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm weak-(*)}((0,\infty)\times\Omega;\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d+2})),

(2.13)

where
\mathfrak{P}
denotes the set of Borel probability measures defined
on the space of “dummy” variables

\mathbb{R}^{d+2}=\left\{(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\Big|(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\right\},

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}F(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}=\left({\rm weak-(*)}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}F(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\bm{m}_{\varepsilon},S_{\varepsilon})\right)(t,x)\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ (t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega

(2.14)

for any
F\in BC(\mathbb{R}^{d+2})
. Note this step requires passing to a new subsequence as the case may be.

Possible concentrations are captured by
the concentration measure

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}

\displaystyle={\rm weak-(*)}\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\left[\frac{\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\bm{m}_{\varepsilon}}{\varrho_{\varepsilon}}+p(\varrho_{\varepsilon},S_{\varepsilon})\mathbb{I}\right]-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}_{\rm weak-(*)}(0,\infty;\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\rm sym})),

(2.15)

where
the symbol
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\rm sym}))
denotes the set of all matrix-valued positively semi-definite finite Borel measures on
the compact set
\overline{\Omega}
.

The limit
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)
, together with the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, is termed
a dissipative (measure–valued) (DMV) solution of the Euler system.

Definition 2.1

(
DMV solution).

A trio
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)
, together with the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is called
dissipative measure–valued (DMV) solution
of the Euler system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
), (
1.4
) in
[0,\infty)\times\Omega
, with the initial data

(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})

if there exists a parametrized measure
\{\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\}
and
a concentration measure
\mathfrak{C}
such that the following holds:

•

compatibility:

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\ {\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ (t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega;

(2.16)

•

equation of continuity:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho(t,x)\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}\widetilde{\bm{m}}{\rm d}\ \mathcal{V}_{t,x}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi(t,x)\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t=-\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}(x)\varphi(0,x)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.17)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
;

•

momentum equation:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\bm{m}(t,x)\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}(t,x)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{\varrho}>0}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\bm{m}}\otimes\widetilde{\bm{m}}}{\widetilde{\varrho}}+p(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{S})\mathbb{I}\right){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\right):\nabla_{x}\bm{\varphi}(t,x)\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\nabla_{x}\bm{\varphi}(t,x):{\rm d}\mathfrak{C}(t,x)-\int_{\Omega}{\bf m}_{0}(x)\cdot\bm{\varphi}(0,x)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.18)

for any
\bm{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\bm{\varphi}\cdot\bm{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
;

•

entropy inequality:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[S(t,x)\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{\varrho}>0}\widetilde{S}\frac{\widetilde{\bm{m}}}{\widetilde{\varrho}}\ {\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi(t,x)\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Omega}S_{0}(x)\varphi(0,x)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.19)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
,
\varphi\geq 0
;

•

energy compatibility:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{0}\geq\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\ {\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}+r(d,\gamma)\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\ {\rm d}{\rm trace}[\mathfrak{C}](t,x),

\displaystyle r(d,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2};\frac{d\gamma}{\gamma-1}\right\}

(2.20)

for a.a.
t\in(0,\infty)
.

The concept of DMV solution was introduced in
[
10
]
and later elaborated in the monograph
[
31
, Chapter 5]
. Kröner and Zajaczkowski
[
42
]

proposed a different class of measure–valued solution to the Euler system postulating entropy
equation
rather than inequality (
2.19
). Unfortunately,
a physically admissible weak solution with a shock that produces entropy does not fit in this class.

2.2.2
Basic properties of DMV solutions

The reader may consult
[
31
, Chapter 5]
for
a detailed discussion of the concept of DMV solutions to various problems in fluid mechanics and their use in numerical analysis.
Here, we record their basic properties:

1.

Global existence.
The Euler system admits global–in–time DMV solutions for any choice of the initial data
(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})
belonging to the convex set

X_{D}=\left\{(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\ \Big|\ \int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ S_{0}\geq\underline{s}\varrho_{0}\right\}.

(2.21)

see
[
9
, Proposition 3.8]
.

2.

Weak continuity.

The functions
\varrho
,
\bm{m}
belong to the class

\varrho,\ \bm{m}\in BC_{\rm weak}([0,\infty);L^{\gamma}(\Omega));

(2.22)

the total entropy admits one-sided limits

\lim_{t\to\tau\pm}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\phi\ \,{\rm d}{x},\ \lim_{t\to 0+}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\phi\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \mbox{for any}\ \phi\in L^{\gamma^{\prime}}(\Omega).

(2.23)

In particular
S(t\pm)
can be identified with a function in
L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
, where we set
S(0-)=S_{0}
. There holds

S(t-)\leq S(t+)\ \mbox{for any}\ t\geq 0,\ \|S(t\pm)\|_{L^{\gamma}(\Omega)}\leq\overline{S}\ \mbox{for all}\ t\geq 0,

(2.24)

see
[
31
, Chapter 5, Section 5.2]
.

3.

Compatibility with strong solutions.
If

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in C^{1}([T_{1},T_{2}]\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\inf_{[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\overline{\Omega}}\varrho>0
, and

\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ t\in(T_{1},T_{2}),

then
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)
is a classical solution of the Euler system
in
[T_{1},T_{2}]
, see
[
31
, Chapter 5, Theorem 5.7]
.

4.

Weak–strong uniqueness.

The dissipative solution coincides with the strong
W^{1,\infty}
solution of the Euler system as long as the latter solution exists,
[
31
, Chapter 6, Theorem 6.2]
.

5.

Convexity and compactness.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]=

\displaystyle\left\{(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \Big|\ (\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \mbox{is a dissipative solution in}\ (0,\infty)\times\Omega\right.

\displaystyle\mbox{emanating from the data}\ (\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})\in X_{D}\Big\}

(2.25)

denote the solution set associated to the data
(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})

Then the following holds:

•

The set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is non–empty and convex.

•

The set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is closed
and bounded
in the weighted Lebesgue space

L^{q}_{\omega}((0,\infty)\times\Omega;R^{d+2})\ \mbox{with the measure}\ {\rm d}\omega=\exp(-t)\,{\rm d}t\times\,{\rm d}{x}

(2.26)

for any
1\leq q\leq\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}
.

•

The set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is compact in the space

L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d+2})),\ \ell>3,

(2.27)

for any
1\leq q<\infty
.

For the proof, see
[
31
, Chapter 5, Theorem 5.2]
.

2.3
Dissipative vs. weak solutions

As observed, the set of all DMV solutions
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
emanating from given initial data is convex and compact in the separable Banach space

L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d+2}))
,
\ell>3,q>1
finite.
Let

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}].

By virtue of Choquet’s generalization
of Krein–Milman theorem, see e.g. Phelps
[
45
]
, there exists
a Borel probability measure
\mu
supported on the
extremal points of the convex set

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
such that

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mu.

(2.28)

A celebrated conjecture formulated originally by DiPerna
[
23
]
reads:

Open problem II (DiPerna’ s conjecture on extremal points):

The extremal points of the set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
are weak solutions solutions of the Euler system.

Remark 2.2
.

Here, the class of weak solutions is understood in a slightly larger sense than in Section
1.2.2
. They satisfy

\mathcal{V}_{t,x}=\delta_{[\varrho(t,x),\bm{m}(t,x),S(t,x)]},\ \mathfrak{C}=0,

together with the following version of the Euler system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}\bm{m}

\displaystyle=0,\ \varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0},

(2.29)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{m}+{\rm div}_{x}\left(\frac{\bm{m}\otimes\bm{m}}{\varrho}\right)+\nabla_{x}p(\varrho,S)

\displaystyle=0,\ \bm{m}(0,\cdot)=\bm{m}_{0},

(2.30)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S+{\rm div}_{x}\left(S\frac{\bm{m}}{\varrho}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\ S(0+,\cdot)=S_{0}

(2.31)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=0,\ \int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(0+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x},

(2.32)

in the sense of distributions.

3
The second law of thermodynamics

Having solve the problem of existence, albeit in a larger class of DMV solutions, we focus on the choice of a suitable
admissibility conditions to restore uniqueness of DMV solutions for given initial data. We aim to solve the problem by imposing
some sort of
maximality
of the entropy production.

3.1
DiPerna’s maximality criterion

In his pioneering work
[
23
]
, DiPerna introduced the following
partial ordering
of global-in-time DMV solutions belonging to the set

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
, namely

\displaystyle(\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1},\mathcal{E}_{0})\prec_{\rm DiP}(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2},\mathcal{E}_{0})\ \Leftrightarrow\ \int_{\Omega}S^{1}(t-,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\int_{\Omega}S^{2}(t-,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \mbox{for any}\ t>0.

(3.1)

Open problem III (DiPerna’ s conjecture on maximal entropy production):

Any DMV solution in
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
that is
maximal
with respect to the order relation

\prec_{\rm DiP}
is a weak solution of the Euler system in the sense of (
2.29
)–(
2.32
).

Although the
existence
of
\prec_{\rm DiP}
-maximal solutions was shown in
[
9
]
, DiPerna’s conjecture remains open.

Let us introduce the
energy defect

D_{E}(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})(t\pm)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \mbox{for}\ t>0.

(3.2)

As pointed out in Section
2.2
, DMV solutions with small energy defect are “close” to weak solutions.
The following results can be therefore interpreted as asymptotic validity of DiPerna’s conjecture for
t\to\infty
, see
[
30
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Theorem 3.1

(
Asymptotic regularity of maximal solutions).

Let
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)
be a
\prec_{\rm DiP}
maximal solution in the set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
.

Then

D_{E}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t\pm)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty.

The next result shows that any set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
contains a solution with arbitrarily small energy defect,
see
[
32
, Theorem 7.1]
.

Theorem 3.2

(
DMV solutions with small defect).

Let the initial data
(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})
satisfy

\mathcal{E}_{0}=\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

Then for any
\delta>0
, there exists a DMV solution
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

such that

0\leq D_{E}(t)=\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\delta\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ t\in(0,\infty).

We point out that the proof of Theorem
3.2
is non–constructive based on Zorn’s lemma (Axiom of Choice). The possibility of effectively computing
such a solution remains dubious.

3.2
Dafermos’ maximality criterion

It follows from the entropy inequality (
2.19
) that the total entropy

t\in[0,\infty)\to\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

can be identified with a non–decreasing (càglàd) function, with
S(0-,\cdot)=S_{0}
. In particular,

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{{\rm d}^{+}}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\infty\ \mbox{for any}\ t\geq 0,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{+}}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}<\infty\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ t\in(0,\infty).

(3.3)

Dafermos
[
16
]
proposed a refined admissibility criterion based on an order relation
\prec_{\rm Daf}

between two solutions of the Euler system :

\displaystyle(\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1},\mathcal{E}_{0})

\displaystyle\prec_{\rm Daf}(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mbox{there exists}\ \tau\geq 0\ \mbox{such that}\ (\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1})(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle=(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2})(t,\cdot)\ \mbox{for all}\ t\leq\tau,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{+}}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}S^{2}(\tau,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle>\frac{{\rm d}^{+}}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}S^{1}(\tau,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

(3.4)

In contrast with DiPerna’s criterion based on (
3.1
), Dafermos’ admissibility criterion is of local character, in particular,
it is independent of the behaviour of the solutions after the time
\tau
. We report the following result, see
[
32
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 3.3

(
Regularity of maximal DMV solutions).

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
, any
\prec_{\rm Daf}
- maximal DMV solution in

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

is a weak solution of the Euler system in the sense of (
2.29
)–(
2.32
).

The fact that a criterion based on maximality of the entropy production selects a weak solution of the Euler system seems quite surprising.
Unfortunately, the
existence
of a
\prec_{\rm Daf}
- maximal solution is an open problem.

Open problem IV (Existence of maximal solutions in the sense of Dafermos):

Does the Euler system admit a
\prec_{\rm Daf}
- maximal DMV solution for any finite energy initial data?

The answer to Open problem IV may be negative as the following result suggests, see
[
32
, Corollary 3.3]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let the initial date be as in Theorem
3.2
.

Then either the Euler system admits a unique weak solution in the sense specified in (
2.29
)–(
2.32
),
or there
is an infinite sequence of DMV solutions such that

(\varrho^{n},\bm{m}^{n},S^{n})\prec_{\rm Daf}(\varrho^{n+1},\bm{m}^{n+1},S^{n+1}),\ n=1,2,\dots

4
Solution semigroup

Our ultimate goal is to identify a
solution semigroup
for the Euler system based on DMV solutions. First, we recall the definition of the

phase space
(formerly data space):

\displaystyle\ X_{D}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})\Big|(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \mbox{measurable in}\ \Omega,\ S\geq\underline{s}\varrho,\ \int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle\subset W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})\times[0,\infty),\ \ell>d.

(4.1)

Given
\underline{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, the phase space
X_{D}
is a non–empty convex compact subset of the Hilbert space

W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})\times\mathbb{R}
.

A solution semigroup is a family of mappings

\bm{U}:\Big(t,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)\in[0,\infty)\times X_{D}\mapsto X_{D}

enjoying the following properties:

•

\bm{U}\Big(0,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)=[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}];

(4.2)

•

semigroup property:

\bm{U}\Big(t+s,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)=\bm{U}\Big(t,\bm{U}\Big(s,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)\Big)

(4.3)

for any
t,s\geq 0
;

•

problem compatibility:

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto\bm{U}\Big(t,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\times\{\mathcal{E}_{0}\}

(4.4)

•

measurability:

\mbox{the mapping}\ [\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\in X_{D}\mapsto\bm{U}\Big(t,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)\in X_{D}

(4.5)

is Borel measurable for any fixed
t\geq 0
.

In addition, keeping in mind the interpretation of limits of consistent approximations as inertial range of turbulence we require

D_{E}(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})(t\pm)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty

(4.6)

whenever
\Big[(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}\Big]=\bm{U}\Big(t,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big)
,
t\geq 0
.
The physical interpretation of (
4.6
) is vanishing of the turbulent energy for large times.
We recall that
property (
4.6
) is automatically satisfied if the semigroup solution if
\prec_{\rm DiP}-
maximal.

Unfortunately, we are not able to guarantee the selected semigroup to be
continuous
with respect to the initial data.
However, in view of the Borel measurability property (
4.5
),
we may use an abstract version of Egorov (Lusin) theorem
(cf. Wisniewski
[
49
]
) to deduce the following conclusion:

Let
\mu
be a complete Borel probability measure on
X_{D}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\tau>0
there exists a closed set
K\subset X_{D}
,
\mu[K]>1-\varepsilon
such that

\bm{U}(\tau,\cdot)|K\to X_{D}\to X_{D}

is continuous. As a matter of fact, Wisniewski
[
49
]
proves a stronger result, namely there is a sequence of
continuous
mappings

(\bm{U}_{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot))_{\varepsilon>0}
such that

\bm{U}_{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)\to\bm{U}(\tau,\cdot)\quad\mu-\mbox{almost surely.}

Although the definition of DMV solution involves
a priori
undetermined quantities, specifically the parametrized measure
\mathcal{V}_{t,x}

and the concentration measure
\mathfrak{C}
, the semigroup solution depends only on the
data
(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0})
and the (constant) value of the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{0}
.
As the DMV solutions are in general not uniquely determined by the data, the semi-group solution must be obtained by applying a proper
selection

process.
In the remaining part of this section, we discuss several selection processes that give rise to a semigroup solution
enjoying the properties (
4.2
)–(
4.6
).

4.1
A selection process by Krylov, Cardona, and Kapitanski

We start by specifying the topology on the solution space
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
. There are two natural choices:

•

STRONG topology

X_{\rm STRONG}=L^{q}((0,\infty)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2}),\ {\rm d}\omega=\exp(-t)\,{\rm d}t\times\,{\rm d}{x},\ 1<q\leq\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1};

•

WEAK topology represented by the weighted Hilbert space

X_{\rm WEAK}=L^{2}_{\omega}(0,\infty;W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})),\ \ell>d;

cf. (
2.26
), (
2.27
).

The selection strategy, proposed in the context of stochastic equations by Krylov
[
43
]
and later adapted to the deterministic
setting by Cardona and Kapitanski
[
12
]
, is based a set of axioms to be satisfied by a multivalued solution set

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
, cf.
[
9
]
.

•

[A1] Compactness.
The solution set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is a non–empty

compact
subset of the separable Hilbert space
X_{\rm WEAK}
, cf. Section
2.2.2
, notably (
2.27
).

•

[A2] Measurability.
The mapping

[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\in X_{D}\mapsto\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\in{\rm comp}\Big[X_{\rm WEAK}\Big]

is Borel measurable, where
{\rm comp}
denotes the metric space of all compact subsets of the Hilbert space
X_{\rm WEAK}

endowed with the Hausdorff topology, see
[
9
, Section 4, Lemma 4.5]
.

•

[A3] Time–shift.
For any
T\geq 0
and any

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in X_{\rm WEAK}
, we define

\mathcal{S}_{T}[\varrho,\bm{m},S](t,\cdot)=(\varrho(t+T),\bm{m}(t+T),S(t+T)),\ t\geq 0.

We require for any

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

to satisfy

\mathcal{S}_{T}[\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}]\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho(T,\cdot),\bm{m}(T,\cdot),S(T-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}]

for any
T\geq 0
, see
[
9
]
.

•

[A4] Concatenation.

For

(\varrho^{i},\bm{m}^{i},S^{i})\in X_{\rm WEAK},\ i=1,2,\ T>0,

we define

(\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1})\cup_{T}(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2})=(\varrho,\bm{m},S),

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1})(t,\cdot)\ \mbox{for}\ 0\leq t\leq T,\\
\\
(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2})(t-T,\cdot)\ \mbox{for}\ t>T\end{array}\right.

For any

(\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

and

(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho^{1}(T,\cdot),\bm{m}^{1}(T,\cdot),S^{1}(T-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}],

we require

(\varrho^{1},\bm{m}^{1},S^{1})\cup_{T}(\varrho^{2},\bm{m}^{2},S^{2})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}],

see
[
32
, Lemma 5.1]
.

If axioms [A1]–[A4] are satisfied by a multivalued solution mapping, the selection procedure consists of a
successive
minimization of
a countable family of cost functionals

\mathcal{F}_{n,m}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda_{n}t)\left[G_{m}\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot)\Big)\right]\,{\rm d}t,

(4.7)

where
(\lambda_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a dense set in
(0,\infty)
, and
(G_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
a family of continuous (bounded) functionals separating
points in
X_{D}
. Specifically, given
(n,m)
, we define
a restricted solution set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{n,m}\circ\mathcal{U}=

\displaystyle\left\{(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{n,m}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\leq\mathcal{F}_{n,m}(\varrho,\bm{m},{S})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{for all}\ (\varrho,\bm{m},{S})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big\}

(4.8)

It is possible to show that the restricted solution set
\mathcal{U}_{n,m}\circ\mathcal{U}
complies with the axioms [A1]–[A4]. Consequently, applying this
minimization process successively for a dense family
(n_{k},m_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
,

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]=\cap_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{U}_{n_{k+1},m_{k+1}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{n_{k},m_{k}}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{U}_{n_{1},m_{1}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{n_{k},m_{k}}\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

the resulting set is a singleton yielding the desired solution semigroup.

The above delineated procedure has been successfully applied
in
[
9
]
. The main shortcoming of the process is the fact that the selected solution may sensitively depend on the choice/order
of the sequence
\lambda_{n}
as well as the functionals
G_{m}
. Reducing the number of cost functionals may not only simplify the selection
procedure but also facilitate its eventual numerical implementation.

4.2
Two step selection procedure based on convexity

In
[
30
]
, we proposed a simplified two–step selection process based on
convexity
of the solution set

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
.

4.2.1
First selection

Following the strategy of
[
9
]
we introduce the

first selection functional

\mathcal{F}_{S}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t

which is a bounded linear form defined on the solution set

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
endowed with the topology of the Banach space
X_{\rm STRONG}
.

Similarly to the preceding section, we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm argmax}\mathcal{F}_{S}\Big[\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big]

\displaystyle=\left\{(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{S}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\geq\mathcal{F}_{S}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\mbox{for any}\ (\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big\}.

(4.9)

As shown in
[
30
]
, the restricted solution set
\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]
enjoys the following properties:

•

Convexity.
The set
\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is a nonempty convex set, compact in
X_{\rm WEAK}
and closed
in
X_{\rm STRONG}
.

•

Measurability.
The set valued mapping

\mathcal{U}_{S}:(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})\in X_{D}\mapsto{\rm argmax}\,\mathcal{F}_{S}\Big[\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big]\in{\rm comp}\Big[X_{\rm WEAK}\Big]

(4.10)

is Borel–measurable. Moreover, the same mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{S}:(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})\in X_{D}\mapsto{\rm argmax}\,\mathcal{F}_{S}\Big[\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big]

\displaystyle\in{\rm closed}\Big[X_{\rm STRONG}\Big]

(4.11)

is Borel measurable with respect to the metrisable Wijsman topology on closed convex subsets of
X_{\rm STRONG}
. We recall
the convergence in the Wijsman topology:

\mathcal{A}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{A}\ \Leftrightarrow\ {\rm dist}_{L^{q}_{\omega}}[y,\mathcal{A}_{n}]\to{\rm dist}_{L^{q}_{\omega}}[y,\mathcal{A}]\ \mbox{for any}\ y\in L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2}).

•

Maximal dissipation rate.
All solutions belonging to the set

\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
are maximal with respect to the partial ordering
\prec_{\rm DiP}
. In particular, they satisfy (
4.6
).

4.2.2
Second selection

If the set
\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
fails to be a singleton,
we propose the second selection based on maximisation of the energy defect (the so–called turbulent energy). More specifically, we consider the functional

\mathcal{F}_{E}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t.

The selection procedure is then closed by setting

\mathcal{U}_{ES}(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})={\rm argmin}\,\mathcal{F}_{E}\Big[\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big].

As the energy functional
E
is strictly convex in its domain,

\mathcal{U}_{ES}(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})
is a singleton, which finishes the selection process.
Intuitively, the second choice prefers “turbulent” solutions. A similar selection process was proposed by
Klingenberg, Markefelder, and Wiedemann
[
40
]
.

As shown in
[
30
]
, the mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{ES}:(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in X_{D}\mapsto{\rm argmin}\,\mathcal{F}_{E}\Big[\mathcal{U}_{S}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0}]\Big]\in X_{\rm STRONG}

(4.12)

is Borel measurable. This observation is based on the construction
of a Moreau–Yosida regularisation of the
functional
\mathcal{F}_{E}
on the Banach space

X_{\rm STRONG}
proposed by
Bacho
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}_{E}

\displaystyle(\varrho,\bm{m},S)

\displaystyle=\inf_{(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\in L^{q}_{\omega}((0,\infty);L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2}))}\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\|_{L^{q}_{\omega}((0,\infty);L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})}^{q}+\mathcal{F}_{E}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}))\right]

(4.13)

for
\varepsilon>0
, see
[
30
]
for details.

Finally, it is easy to see that the instantaneous values of dissipative solution belong to the set
X_{D}
,

\Big[\mathcal{U}_{ES}(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0});\mathcal{E}_{0}\Big](\tau-,\cdot)\in X_{D}\ \ \mbox{for any}\ \tau>0.

Moreover, the entropy
S
can be identified with a càglàd mapping ranging in the space
W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega)
. In particular,
the entropy
S
belongs to the Skorokhod space of càglàd mappings,

S\in D([0,\infty);W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega)).

We refer to
[
35
, Appendix A1]
for basic properties of the space
D([0,\infty);W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega))
.

We conclude that the two-step selection process specified above yields a solution semigroup

t\mapsto\bm{U}(t,[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}])=\Big[\mathcal{U}_{ES}(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}),\mathcal{E}_{0}\Big]

possessing all properties specified in (
4.2
)–(
4.6
).

4.3
Second Law of Thermodynamics - a single step selection

As we show below, the selection can be based on a single cost functional as long as the latter is strictly convex on its domain.
Motivated by Clausius’ statement quoted in Section
1.1
, we propose a single step selection criterion based
on minimization of a distance between a DMV solution and the background equilibrium state.

4.3.1
Background equilibrium state

It is a direct consequence of the equation of continuity (
2.17
) that the total mass

\int_{\Omega}\varrho(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=M_{0}>0

(4.14)

is conserved for any DMV solution. Given the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, the background (stable)
equilibrium density-temperature is given by the constant state

(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})
,

\overline{\varrho}=\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\,{\rm d}{x},\ \mathcal{E}_{0}=c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\varrho}\overline{\vartheta}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ \overline{\vartheta}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{c_{v}M_{0}}.

Next, let us introduce the Bregman distance (divergence) associated to the convex energy
E
,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\left(\varrho,\bm{m},S\Big|\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}\right)

\displaystyle=E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\frac{\partial E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})}{\partial\varrho}(\varrho-\widetilde{\varrho})-\frac{\partial E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})}{\partial\bm{m}}\cdot(\bm{m}-\widetilde{\bm{m}})-\frac{\partial E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})}{\partial S}(S-\widetilde{S})

\displaystyle\quad-E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}).

We refer to
[
31
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed discussion of the concept of Bregman divergence associated to the (convex) total energy
E
.
At this point, it is enough to observe that
E\left(\varrho,\bm{m},S\Big|\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}\right)
represents a “metric” in the sense that

E\left(\varrho,\bm{m},S\Big|\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}\right)\geq 0,\ E\left(\varrho,\bm{m},S\Big|\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}\right)=0\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\varrho,\bm{m},S)=(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S}).

Evaluating the Bregman distance of a DMV solution
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)
to the equilibrium solution

(\overline{\varrho},0,\overline{\varrho}s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}))
, and integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}E\left(\varrho,\bm{m},S\Big|\overline{\varrho},0,\overline{\varrho}s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S]\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\overline{\vartheta}M_{0}s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})-\mathcal{E}_{0},

(4.15)

cf.
[
31
, Chapter 5]
.
This motivates the choice of the cost functional

\mathcal{F}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot)-\overline{\vartheta}S(t,\cdot)\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t,\overline{\vartheta}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{c_{v}M_{0}},

(4.16)

cf.
[
30
]
.

Similarly to the preceding part, we say that
(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
is
admissible
DMV solution to the Euler system with the initial data

(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in X_{D}

if

(\varrho,\bm{m},S)={\rm argmin}_{\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t\right],

(4.17)

meaning
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})-\overline{\vartheta}\widetilde{S}\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t

(4.18)

for all
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
.
As the function
(\varrho,\bm{m},S)\mapsto E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S
is strictly convex on its domain, the admissible solution is identified in a single
step.

Although the admissible DMV solution are (not known to be)
\prec_{\rm DiP}
maximal, their energy defect vanishes as
t\to\infty
,
see
[
27
, Proposition 3.2]
.

Theorem 4.1

(
Vanishing energy defect).

Let
(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
be an admissible

Then

D_{E}(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})(t\pm)\equiv\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty.

In view of Theorem

4.3.2
Absolute entropy maximizers?

The reader will have noticed that we have deliberately fixed
\lambda_{n}=1
in the cost functional (
4.7

There is a legitimate question to ask what would be the result of the minimization process if we consider

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t,\cdot)-\overline{\vartheta}S(t,\cdot)\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t,\ \lambda>0,

Apparently, minimizing (

4.2
)–(

In addition, we claim the following result concerning possible uniqueness of the selected solution.

Theorem 4.2

(
Absolute entropy maximizer).

Suppose there exists

(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})={\rm argmin}_{\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t\right]

(4.20)

for all
\lambda\geq\overline{\lambda}>0
.

Then

D_{E}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S},\mathcal{E}_{0})(t+,\cdot)(t+)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0

for all

. In particular,
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S},\mathcal{E}_{0})
is a weak solution of the Euler system in the sense specified

Proof.

Step 1: Positive energy defect and negative jumps of the cost functional

Let us set

\mathcal{G}(\varrho,\bm{m},S)=\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

Consider a DMV solution
(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
. We recall
We recall that dissipative solutions

\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ (\varrho,\bm{m},S)(0-,\cdot)\equiv(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0}),

(4.21)

S(t\pm,\cdot)\geq\underline{s}\varrho.

(4.22)

Moreover,
it follows from (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) that

\displaystyle\varrho(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\ \Rightarrow\ \bm{m}(t,x)=0\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\varrho(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\ \Rightarrow\ S(t\pm,x)=0\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ x\in\Omega

(4.23)

Suppose that

D_{E}(\tau+)>0\ \mbox{for some}\ \tau>0.

(4.24)

Our aim is to show that the solution
(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
can be extended beyond
the time
\tau
in such a way that the functional
\mathcal{G}
evaluated at
\tau
experiences a negative jump proportional
to
D_{E}(\tau+)
.

First, by virtue of (

S(\tau+,\cdot)=\mathds{1}_{\varrho(\tau,\cdot)>0}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\Big(c_{v}\log(\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot))-\log(\varrho(\tau,\cdot))\Big).

Next, we introduce

\widehat{\vartheta}(\tau,\cdot)=(1+\varepsilon)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot)\ \mbox{whenever}\ \varrho(\tau,\cdot)>0

where
\varepsilon>0
will be fixed below.

Let us denote

E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)=c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot).

the associated internal energy.
We have

\widehat{{E}}_{\rm int}(\tau,\cdot)\equiv c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\widehat{\vartheta}(\tau,\cdot)=E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)+\varepsilon c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot),

(4.25)

and

\widehat{S}(\tau,\cdot)\equiv c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(\widehat{\vartheta}(\tau,\cdot))-\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(\varrho(\tau,\cdot))=S(\tau+,\cdot)+c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(1+\varepsilon).

(4.26)

Thus we fix
\varepsilon>0
so that

\widehat{D}_{E}(\tau)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},\widehat{S})(\tau,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0,

(4.27)

meaning

\varepsilon c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=D_{E}(\tau+)\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon=\frac{D_{E}(\tau+)}{\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}}.

(4.28)

The relations (
4.27

4.28
) imply that we may continue the original solution
(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
by concatenating it at the time
\tau

a new solution
(\widehat{\varrho},\widehat{\bm{m}},\widehat{S},\mathcal{E}_{0})
starting from the data

(\varrho(\tau,\cdot),\bm{m}(\tau,\cdot),\widehat{S}(\tau,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}),

with the energy defect
\widehat{{D}}_{E}(\tau+)=0
.

Next, we evaluate the functional
\mathcal{G}
at the time
\tau
for the concatenated solution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},\widehat{S})(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},{S})(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\overline{\vartheta}c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(1+\varepsilon)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(4.29)

In addition, using (

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle-\overline{\vartheta}c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(1+\varepsilon)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=d_{E}(\tau)-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{D_{E}(\tau+)}{\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}}\right).

(4.30)

Finally, observe that

\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\int_{\Omega}E\Big(\varrho,\bm{m},S)(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\mathcal{E}_{0}-D_{E}(\tau+);

whence

D_{E}(\tau+)-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{D_{E}(\tau+)}{\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}}\right)\leq d_{E}(\tau)-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{D_{E}(\tau+)}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-D_{E}(\tau+)}\right).

(4.31)

Introducing the function

G:y\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{y}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-y}\right)-y=\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-y}\right)-y=\mathcal{E}_{0}\log(\mathcal{E}_{0})-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log(\mathcal{E}_{0}-y)-y

(4.32)

we observe that

G(0)=0,\ G^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-y}-1>0\ \mbox{as long as}\ y>0.

(4.33)

Thus we may infer

\widehat{{D}}_{E}(\tau)=0,\ \mathcal{G}\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},\widehat{S})(\tau,\cdot)\Big)\leq\mathcal{G}\Big((\varrho,\bm{m},{S})(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)-G(D_{E}(\tau+))

(4.34)

for the concatenated solution,
where
G
is defined in (
4.32
). We conclude that the functional
\mathcal{G}
evaluated at the concatenated solution
experiences a negative jump proportional to
D_{E}(\tau+)
.

Suppose that for some

\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,\bm{m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t\right]<\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\bm{m}},\widetilde{S})-\overline{\vartheta}\widetilde{S}\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t\right]

4.4
Solution semigroup - conclusion

The above discussion revealed two possible rather different approaches to the problem of well posedness for the Euler system. On the one hand,
the Euler system can be seen as an inertial range of
turbulence
. Admissible solutions are identified with limits of consistent approximations exhibiting
oscillatory behaviour and/or possible concentrations. They are “truly” measure valued, whereas their energy exhibits
priori
undetermined turbulent component.
On the other hand, the entropy maximization in the spirit of Dafermos or DiPerna seems to prefer the standard weak solutions. The question
which approach yields a “correct” admissible solutions should be possibly decided by experiments rather than by mathematical arguments.

4.4.1
Turbulent scenarios

In the context of turbulent DMV solutions, the open question remains what is the origin of the underlying mechanism creating oscillations and/or concentrations
in the sequence of consistent approximation. One possible scenario, supported by the numerical analysis performed by the group around Fjordholm, Mishra, Tadmor et al.

[
36
]
,
[
37
]
,
[
38
]
(see also
[
31
]
), is a generation of the associated Yong measure directly by oscillations in the
approximate sequence. An alternative, suggested again by numerical experiments performed by Elling
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
, is a strong
(non–oscillatory) convergence of consistent approximations to a
connected set
of solutions of the Euler system. Apparently, the second scenario
is in line with the existence of infinitely many solutions revealed recently by the method of convex integration.

4.4.2
Reducing the set of eligible DMV solutions, computable solutions

It is clear that
convexity
of the set of all DMV solutions emanating from given initial data plays a crucial role in the selection process.
To reduce the solution set, it would be desirable to focus only on
computable
ones, meaning those that can be identified as limits
of consistent approximations.

Open problem VI:

Let the initial data
(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})
be given.
Is the set of (weak) limits of consistent approximations convex? Does the set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
of all DMV solutions coincide
with the set of all limits of consistent approximations?

An (affirmative) answer to the above questions in the context of the incompressible Euler system was given by Székelyhidi, and Wiedemann

[
48
]
.

Another possibility of reducing the set of relevant DMV solutions would be to take into account only
statistical limits
of consistent
approximations. More specifically, we would consider strong limits of Cesàro (empirical) averages

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\varrho_{n},

\displaystyle\bm{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{m}_{n},

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}S_{n}

(4.35)

of sequences of consistent approximation
(\varrho_{n},\bm{m}_{n},S_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. In accordance with the celebrated version
of the Banach-Saks theorem by Komlós
[
41
]
, any sequence
(v_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
bounded in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)

admits a subsequence
(v_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that there holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{n_{k}}\to v\ \mbox{a.a. in}\ (0,T)\times\Omega.

(4.36)

As a matter of fact, the convergence (
4.36
) remains valid also for any subsequence of
(v_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
. The limit in
(
4.36
) coincides with the so-called biting limit on the sequence
(v_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
.

The theory of parametrized (Young) measures based on Komlós theorem was elaborated by Balder
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
.
Converting weak convergence to strong has been applied in the numerical analysis of the specific form of Young measures in
[
33
]
.

Inspired by (
4.35
), we may introduce a new class of
computable solutions

\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
,

\displaystyle(\varrho,\bm{m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\varrho=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\varrho_{n},\ \bm{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{m}_{n},\ S=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}S_{n}\ \mbox{a.a. in}\ (0,\infty)\times\Omega,

for some sequence

\displaystyle(\varrho_{n},\bm{m}_{n},S_{n},\mathcal{E}_{0})\ \mbox{of consistent approximations of the Euler system.}

(4.37)

It is not difficult to show the following properties of the set
\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
:

•

convexity:

for any initial data
(\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})
, the set of computable solutions

\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is a non-empty closed convex subset of
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
;

•

compatibility

any weak solution of the Euler system is computable.

In view of convexity of the set
\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
, the selection procedures specified in Sections

4.2
,
4.3
may be directly applied to the set of computable solutions yielding a solution semigroup. Our last open problem reads:

Open problem VII:

We have

\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]\subset\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}].

Is it true that

\mathcal{U}_{\rm comp}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]=\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},\bm{m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]

at least for a specific class of initial data?

Needless to say that considering empirical averages of numerical approximations is strongly reminiscent of the Monte Carlo method, where, however, the average is taken
over the set of
statistical solutions
emerging from i.i.d. initial data. One could therefore conjecture that non–uniqueness
of the limits generated by consistent (numerical) approximations could be created by certain “random” errors during the computation process.
