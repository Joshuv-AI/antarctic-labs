Revisited Pharmacokinetic Profiles of Methylprednisolone in Plasma and Urine After Single and Multiple Oral Administrations: Relevance in Sports Drug Testing
Authors: Sergi Coll, Claudia Bressan, Núria Monfort, Ana Aldea-Perona, Marcel·lí Carbó, Rosa Ventura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pharmacokinetic profiles of methylprednisolone (MP) in plasma and urine were evaluated after single and multiple oral doses. MP is prohibited in sports competitions when administered systemically (oral, injectable, or rectal administrations) whereas other routes remain permitted for therapeutic purposes.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to characterize the urinary MP excretion after oral administration to assess whether the current World Anti-Doping Agency minimum reporting level of 30 ng/mL in urine is appropriate for distinguishing permitted from prohibited administrations, as well as whether the recommended washout period is adequate. Another aim was to better define MP elimination kinetics and evaluate its systemic effects.MethodsSixteen healthy male participants were enrolled and divided into two groups. One group received a single oral dose of 12 mg of MP, while the other received 12 mg daily for 3 consecutive days. Urine and blood samples were collected before, during, and after administration. Urine samples were analyzed for MP and 15 metabolites using a sensitive and selective liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry method. Plasma samples were analyzed for unchanged MP and cortisol using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. A pharmacokinetic analysis was performed using a one-compartment model with first-order absorption, combining plasma concentrations and urinary excretion data.ResultsMP represented only a small proportion of the administered dose, confirming that metabolism is the main elimination pathway. After a single oral dose, urinary MP concentrations above 30 ng/mL were observed up to 12 h, while all samples collected later were below this concentration. After multiple dosing, most samples above 30 ng/mL were also found within 12 h, although a few participants still showed concentrations above the minimum reporting level in the 12 to 24 h period. All samples collected more than 24 h after the last dose were below 30 ng/mL. In plasma, MP reached peak concentrations around 1.6–2.3 h after administration and was not detectable 24 h after the last dose. Cortisol concentrations were markedly suppressed after dosing, with recovery to baseline within 24–48 h depending on the regimen.ConclusionsThis study confirms that the current urinary minimum reporting level of 30 ng/mL is appropriate for detecting oral MP use while minimizing the risk of false adverse analytical findings. The results also support the adequacy of the current 3-day washout period. Modern liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis and combined plasma-urine pharmacokinetic modeling provided robust evidence that MP is rapidly eliminated, extensively metabolized, and associated with transient cortisol suppression after oral administration.Clinical Trial RegistrationEudraCT number 2020-004596-41.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01665-x.

Key Points

The study confirms that a minimum reporting level of 30 ng/mL is suitable for detecting oral methylprednisolone misuse in sports drug testing.A 3-day washout period for oral methylprednisolone is appropriate to avoid false adverse analytical findings due to legitimate out of competition treatments close to competitions.Modern liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis and combined plasma-urine pharmacokinetic modeling provided robust evidence that methylprednisolone is rapidly eliminated, extensively metabolized, and associated with transient cortisol suppression after oral administration.

Introduction

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive agents broadly used for numerous medical conditions. In sports, they are frequently prescribed for managing injuries and general health issues. Nevertheless, GCs are included in the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) Prohibited List owing to potential health risks, mainly related to the suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [1, 2], and because of evidence suggesting performance-enhancing effects [3–9]. Over time, GCs have been subjected to various regulations. Since 2022, WADA has prohibited the use of GCs during competitions when administered via systemic routes (including all injectable, oral, and rectal routes) [10]. Conversely, local routes (inhaled, dermatological) with therapeutic purposes remain permitted.

GCs come in diverse pharmaceutical forms and can be delivered through various routes with either local or systemic effects and, for that reason, it is important to distinguish between these in the context of doping control. Therefore, minimum reporting levels (MRLs), defined as the concentration above which an analytical result is reported as adverse analytical findings (AAF), are required to distinguish between permitted and prohibited administrations of GCs. A single MRL of 30 ng/mL was applied across all GCs to differentiate legal therapeutic use from prohibited use up to 2021. However, emerging data highlighted the need for a more nuanced, compound-specific approach [10–28]. To address this, WADA introduced individualized MRLs for certain GCs, such as betamethasone, budesonide, triamcinolone acetonide, prednisone, and prednisolone, starting in 2022. These MRLs were established based on findings from controlled administration studies [10] and reflect urinary concentrations following permitted use, ensuring greater specificity and reducing the risk of therapeutic use being misclassified as an AAF. In addition, defined washout periods for prohibited administration routes were recommended [10]. The washout periods eliminate the risk of false AAFs when athletes receive legitimate GC treatments out of competition but close to an in-competition testing window.

Methylprednisolone (MP) is one of the most frequently detected GCs in sports drug testing. Its pharmacokinetics has been studied following various administration regimens [29–37]. However, sports drug testing relies primarily on urine as the biological matrix, and very limited data on urinary concentrations of MP are available [12, 38, 39]. Furthermore, the available pharmacokinetic studies were published several decades ago, and plasma concentrations of MP were measured using bioanalytical methods with significantly lower sensitivity and a lack of information regarding selectivity than those of current analytical instrumentation. The limit of quantitation of the analytical method employed plays a major role in the estimation of pharmacokinetic parameters, and insufficient selectivity may result in an overestimation of the plasma concentrations.

With respect to urine, in a previous study conducted by our group, the unchanged drug and 15 metabolites were detected in urine (Fig. S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]) [38]. In a subsequent study, the urinary profile of MP and its metabolites was evaluated in a limited number of participants following oral and dermatological administrations [12]. Although certain metabolites (M8 and M11, Fig. S1 of the ESM) offered slightly better discrimination between oral and dermatological administration compared to unchanged MP, their use would require a full synthesis for complete structural identification. Therefore, MP was ultimately selected as the preferred marker to distinguish allowed and forbidden administrations because of the simplicity and practicality of its analysis in antidoping laboratories. Another study described urinary concentrations after oral and intra-articular administration of MP in a reduced number of participants [39]. Despite these studies, the data available were very limited, and the MRL for MP was maintained at 30 ng/mL pending further studies to support its validation [10].

The primary aim of the current study was to investigate the urinary excretion pattern of MP following both single and repeated oral administration to determine whether the current MRL of 30 ng/mL is appropriate and, also, to assess whether the established washout period is sufficient for therapeutic oral use of GCs administered out of competition. To support this, MP and 15 of its urinary metabolites were analyzed (Fig. S1 of the ESM). A secondary aim was to measure plasma concentrations of both MP and cortisol (CORT) and reassess their profile in order to evaluate the elimination kinetics of MP and gain further insight into its systemic impact on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, using up-to-date analytical methodologies.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

MP, CORT, and cortisol-9,11,12,12-d4 (CORT-d4) were obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). Methylprednisolone-9,11,12,12-d4 (MP-d4) was purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, ON, Canada). The β-glucuronidase preparation (type Escherichia coli K12) was purchased from Roche Diagnostics GmbH (Mannheim, Germany). Analytical grade di-sodium hydrogen phosphate, sodium hydrogen phosphate, ethyl acetate, ammonium chloride, acetonitrile, methanol (liquid chromatography gradient grade), and formic acid (liquid chromatography grade) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Milli-Q water was obtained using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore Ibérica, Barcelona, Spain). Human blank plasma was provided by Hospital del Mar (Barcelona, Spain).

Clinical Studies

A clinical study that involved single-dose and multiple oral dose administration of MP to healthy Caucasian subjects, and collection of urine and blood samples, were performed at the Clinical Research Unit of Hospital del Mar Research Institute (Barcelona, Spain) (protocol code IMIMFTCL/DACORSIN4). Ethical approval of the study was granted by the local research ethical committee (Comitè Ètic d’Investigació Clínica CEIC-Parc de Salut Mar, Barcelona, Spain) and the Spanish Medicines Agency (EudraCT protocol number 2020-004596-41).

In total, 16 male participants were recruited, and eight participants were allocated in each dose administration group of MP. All participants gave their written informed consent prior to inclusion and underwent a general physical examination, routine laboratory test, urinalysis, urine drug test, breathalyzer, and a 12-lead electrocardiogram with results within normal values. All participants declared not to consume drugs that could alter GC metabolism such as steroids, prohormones, contraceptives, or diuretics. In both dose administration groups, recommended therapeutic dosages were used.

A single oral dose of 12 mg of MP (Urbason, Hoechst Marion Roussel) was administered to eight participants (aged 31.0 ± 9.9 years; 71.7 ± 5.2 kg; 23.7 ± 2.0 kg/m2 of body mass index). Urine samples were collected before administration and after administration from 0 to 4 h, 4–8 h, 8–12 h, and 12–24 h on day 1, from 0 to 12 h and from 12 to 24 h on day 2, and from 0 to 24 h on days 3–5. Blood samples were collected before administration and 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h after administration.

An oral dose of 12 mg of MP (Urbason, Hoechst Marion Roussel) was daily administered for 3 consecutive days to eight male participants (aged 26.4 ± 5.9 years; 72.7 ± 6.8 kg; 23.2 ± 2.5 kg/m2 of body mass index). Urine samples were collected before administration and after administration from 0 to 4 h, 4–8 h, 8–12 h, and 12–24 h on the 3 treatment days and day 1 post-treatment (days 1–4), and from 0 to 24 h on days 2–4 post-treatment (days 5–7). Blood samples were collected before, during, and after administration at the following collection times: 0, 1.5, and 3 h on days 1 and 2; 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 12 h on day 3; post-treatment: 24, 48, 72, 120, and 240 h.

Urine samples were collected in sterile containers and were pooled during the specified time periods. Preliminary measurements, including specific gravity (SG) and pH, were performed. Specific gravity was determined using an Atago UG-α Refractometer (Atago, Tokyo, Japan), and pH was measured by a Crison GLP 21 pH meter (Crison, Alella, Spain). Urine samples were stored at – 20 °C until further analysis.

Blood samples were collected with K2EDTA tubes (BD Vacutainer® spray-coated K2EDTA Tube) according to WADA blood collection guidelines. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation (10 min, 1400g, 4 °C), and was immediately transferred to low-binding tubes and stored at – 20 °C until analysis.

Clinical safety assessments were performed at regular intervals for each treatment period throughout both dose administration groups and included monitoring of adverse events, clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, electrocardiograms, and physical examinations.

Sample Preparation

Urine Samples

The analysis of urine samples was performed using an extraction protocol previously described by our group [12]. Briefly, 50 μL of internal standard (ISTD) solution (50 ng of MP-d4) were added to 5 mL of urine, followed by the addition of 1.5 mL of phosphate buffer 1M pH 7. Then, β-glucuronidase from E. coli was added (50 µL) and hydrolysis was carried out for 1 h at 55 °C. The buffered solution was alkalinized with 500 µL of 25% potassium carbonate solution to pH 8–9 and the steroids were extracted with 6 mL of ethyl acetate. After centrifugation (5 min at 1400g), the organic layer was transferred into a new tube and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen stream in a water bath at 50 °C. The residue was reconstituted into 100 µL of a mixture of water:acetonitrile (9:1, v/v), and 10 μL were injected into the system.

Plasma Samples

The analysis of plasma samples was performed using an extraction procedure previously described by our group to analyze CORT and other GCs [19, 24]. Twenty-five microliters of an ISTD mix (50 ng of CORT-d4 and 2.5 ng of MP-d4) were added to 1 mL of plasma. Then, 1 mL of acetonitrile was added in order to precipitate proteins, and compounds were extracted with 5 mL of tert-butyl-methyl ether. After centrifugation (10 minutes at 1400g), the organic layer was transferred into a new tube and evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream in a water bath at 40 °C. The residue was reconstituted into 150 µL of a mixture of water to acetonitrile (1:1, v/v), and 10 μL were injected into the system.

Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry Analyses

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analyses were carried out using a triple quadrupole (Xevo TQ MS) mass spectrometer provided with an orthogonal Z-spray-electrospray interface (Waters Associates, Milford, MA, USA) coupled to an Acquity ultra high-performance liquid chromatography system (Waters Associates) for the chromatographic separation. Nitrogen and argon (both with purity ≥ 99.999%) were used as desolvation gas and as collision gas, respectively. The desolvation gas flow was set to approximately 1200 L/h and the cone gas flow to 50 L/h. The nitrogen desolvation temperature was set to 450 °C and the source temperature to 120 °C. Capillary voltage of 3.5 kV was used in positive ion mode.

Liquid chromatographic separations were performed using an Acquity BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm internal diameter, 1.7 µm particle size) (Waters Associates). Two different liquid chromatographic methods were used. To analyze urine samples, the mobile phase solvents A and B were water and acetonitrile, respectively, both with formic acid (0.01%). The percentage of organic solvent was linearly changed as follows: 0 min, 10% B; 0.6 min, 10% B; 5 min, 20% B; 12 min, 30% B; 13 min, 90% B; 13.5 min, 90% B; 13.6 min, 10% B, and 15.5 min, 10% B at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and at 45 °C. To analyze plasma samples, water (solvent A) and methanol (solvent B) both with 1 mM of ammonium formate and formic acid (0.01%) were selected as mobile phase solvents. The percentage of organic solvent was linearly changed as follows: 0 min, 30% B; 0.5 minutes, 30% B; 3 min, 43% B; 12.5 min, 48% B; 13.5 min, 65% B; 16.5 min 95% B, 16.6 min 30% B, and 18.5 min, 30% B at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and at 55 °C. The injection volume in both chromatographic methods was 10 µL.

Mass spectrometric analysis were performed in selected reaction monitoring mode in the conditions described in Table S1 of the ESM. All compounds were ionized in positive mode at the conjugated 3-keto function to form the [M+H]+.

Methods Validation

In the present work, an analytical method was validated to quantify MP in plasma. This method uses the same conditions as the method previously validated by our group to quantify CORT [19, 24]. Thus, both MP and CORT were quantified.

The selectivity and specificity of the analytical method were verified for all metabolites by analyzing different 25 blank samples. Calibration samples at six different concentration levels (0.5, 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 ng/mL) were prepared by adding the appropriate volume of stock solutions of the compound to blank plasma samples. The peak area ratios between the analyte and the ISTD (MP-d4) were plotted against the concentration. Peak area ratios were subjected to a proportional weighted least-square regression analysis and calibration curves were not forced through the origin.

Quality-control (QC) samples at three concentration levels (2, 15, and 60 ng/mL) were prepared to evaluate the precision and accuracy of the method. Accuracy is expressed as the relative error (%) in the estimation of each concentration for the QC samples. Intra-assay precision of the method is expressed as the relative standard deviation (%) of the estimated concentrations obtained for three replicates of the QC samples analyzed the same day, at each concentration level. Inter-assay precision is given as the relative standard deviation (%) of the estimated concentrations obtained for all replicates analyzed along 4 different days (n = 12).

Limit of quantitation (LOQ) was defined as ten times the noise level. The standard deviation of the estimated concentration of the lowest calibration point for each analyte (n = 4) was used as a measure of the noise.

The extraction recovery was calculated by analysis of four replicates of blank samples spiked at three different concentrations of the compounds and four replicates of blank samples to which the same concentrations of analytes were added after extraction of the blank matrix. The ratios of the peak areas between the analytes and the ISTD obtained from the extracted spiked samples were compared with the ratios obtained in the samples in which the analytes were added to extracted blank samples (representing 100% of extraction recovery).

The matrix effect was studied by the analysis (n = 4) of four different blank plasma samples spiked with MP and the ISTD at 5 ng/mL. The signal in matrix was compared with that obtained from the analysis of water samples spiked at the same concentration.

Results

Validation of the Quantitation of MP in Plasma

Two LC-MS/MS methods were used in this study. The method used for the analysis of the urine sample was previously validated by our group [12], and allows for the quantitation of MP and the qualitative determination of 15 urinary metabolites (Fig. S1 of the ESM). Regarding plasma samples, the method was previously validated by our group to quantify CORT [19, 24]. In the present work, the method was validated to quantify MP in plasma and, therefore, the method was used to quantify both MP and CORT in that matrix.

A summary of the validation results for MP in plasma is shown in Table 1. The assay was linear (r2 > 0.99) for MP in the studied range of concentrations. No significant interferences were observed at the retention times of the analytes and their ISTDs in the chromatograms of the corresponding ion transitions in drug-free samples. The LOQ was estimated in 0.5 ng/mL.

Urine Elimination Profile of MP After Single and Multiple Oral Administrations

MP and 15 metabolites described in our previous study [12, 38] (Fig. S1 of the ESM) were measured in urine samples collected after single and multiple oral administrations. MP was quantitatively determined, and the rest of metabolites were qualitatively determined as they were not commercially available as standards. The applied sample preparation allows for the comprehensive detection of free and glucurono-conjugated metabolites.

MP concentrations measured after single-dose and multiple-dose oral administrations are presented in Fig. 1. MP concentrations were corrected by SG when it was greater than 1.018, using the equation described previously, as routinely performed in sports drug testing to account by urine concentration. The amounts of the qualitative determined metabolites are summarized in Table S2 of the ESM.

MP accounted for 2.4 ± 0.8% of the administered dose during the first 24 h, calculated from the urine concentrations and the volume of the different fractions. Concentrations greater than 30 ng/mL were observed up to 12 h after a single dose and after that time all concentrations were below 30 ng/mL (Fig. 1A). In the multiple-dose study, the results were similar, with most of the samples collected up to 12 h after each administration with concentrations above 30 ng/mL. However, some samples above 30 ng/mL were also detected in the period 12–24 h post-dose, as can be seen in Fig. 1B. These differences between single-dose and multiple-dose studies are due to two participants of the multiple-dose study (participants 505 and 507) with concentrations significantly higher compared with the other participants in the period 12–24 h. As already mentioned, participants of the single-dose study were different from those of the multiple-dose study. Samples collected more than 24 h after the last dose were all below 30 ng/mL.

The excretion rates of MP following single and multiple administrations are shown in Fig. 2. Overall, comparable excretion profiles were observed after a single dose and across the multiple-dose study.

The 15 qualitatively determined metabolites were consistently detected in all samples collected during the first 24 h after single-dose administration, as well as in all samples collected on treatment days during the multi-dose treatment. The relative signals of the metabolites normalized to the ISTD are presented in Table S2 of the ESM. Metabolites M1, M2, M3, M8, and M11 were the most abundant metabolites both after single-dose and multiple-dose samples. Following in relative abundances were metabolites M5, M7, M9, and M12. In the single-dose study (Table 2A), individual Cmaxobs plasma concentrations reached mean value of 90.8 ng/mL (confidence interval of 95% [IC95%] 76.7–104.9) detected at Tmaxobs 1.6 hours (IC95% 1.1–2.0). MP plasma concentrations remained for the first 8 h after administration (mean 13.7 ng/mL; IC95% 8.8–18.6 ng/mL) and it was not detectable in the following samples, collected 24 h after administration (Fig. 3A).

In the multiple-dose study (Table 2B), Cmaxobs after the last dose was 82.7 ng/mL (IC95% 65.4–100.0 ng/mL), similar to the single-dose study. Tmaxobs 2.25 hours (IC95% 1.4–3.1 h) was also similar with a slightly wider IC95%. As can be seen in Fig. 4A, according to the pharmacokinetic model, the steady-state concentrations were reached soon after the first dose. At 8 and 12 h after administration, mean plasma concentrations were 20.7 ng/mL (IC95% 10.4–31.1 ng/mL) and 7.3 ng/mL (IC95% 2.5–9.8 ng/mL), respectively. No MP concentrations were detected in the next sample collected at 24 hours after the last day of administration.

The calculated mean AUC0–∞ (477.6 ng·h/mL) and AUC0–last (460.1 ng·h/mL) after the single dose (Table 2A) were similar to AUC0–∞ (478.7 ng·h/mL) and AUC0–last (473.2 ng·h/mL) obtained after the last dose of the multiple dosing study (Table 2B). These results indicate no accumulation of the drug. The calculated elimination constant Ke was similar in the single dose study (0.86 h−1) and the multiple-dose study (0.67 h−1), as well as the t1/2 (0.83 and 1.07 h, respectively) (Table 2).

The V obtained after single dose and after multiple dosing were similar (Table 2). Plasma clearance Clp showed similar mean values in the multiple dosing assay (31.7 L/h) and after intake as a single dose (40.1 L/h). After MP oral administration, systemic elimination can be mainly attributed to the CLm (39.5 L/h, in the single-dose study; 30.2 L/h, in the multiple-dose study) leading a urinary excretion of unchanged MP (Fel) of 2.1 and 4.9%, in the single-dose and multiple-dose studies, respectively, which are in agreement with the value calculated from urinary data.

Interindividual variability was observed especially in some participants of the multiple dose study that showed t1/2 greater than the mean (Table S4 of the ESM). Particularly, participants 504, 505, and 507 showed a t1/2 of 1.5, 1.3, and 1.5 h higher than the mean of the group. All three subjects also shown a Cmax higher than the mean. For participant 504, with a significantly higher percentage of the dose excreted in urine as unchanged MP (Fel = 17.5%), the high t1/2 can be explained by the lower metabolism (Clm = 22 L/h). Participants 505 and 507 showed the highest AUC0–last and AUC0–∞, and the higher t1/2 can be also explained by the lower metabolism (Clm = 28 L/h and 16 L/h, respectively), although not completely reflected in the Fel value obtained. In agreement with their t1/2, participants 505 and 507 also showed significantly higher concentrations in urine in the period 12–24 h after administration (Fig. 1).

In Figs. 3B and 4B, CORT levels are shown as percentage of change relative to basal concentrations, owing to the high interindividual variability in basal samples. Both single and multiple oral administrations of MP resulted in a rapid and reversible suppression of CORT levels, consistent with the inhibition of the HPA axis. Following a single oral dose (Fig. 3B), the minimum CORT concentrations were detected 8 h after dosing, and CORT levels were recovered to baseline in the next sample collected 24 h after administration. In the multiple-dose treatment (Fig. 4B), CORT levels decreased after each daily dose, showing minimum levels 12 h after administration (observed in point 60 h in Fig. 4B). CORT levels were recovered at approximately 60% 24 h after each dose (points 24, 48, and 72 h in Fig. 3b). For most participants, CORT levels were recovered to basal values 48 h after the last dose (point 120 h in Fig. 4B).

Discussion

In this study, urinary and plasma concentrations of MP and metabolites following oral administration were measured in healthy male participants using state-of-the-art LC-MS/MS methodologies. Although the pharmacokinetics of MP have been previously investigated under various dosing regimens [29–36], earlier studies relied on analytical methods with limited sensitivity (LOQ > 5 ng/mL) and insufficient characterization of selectivity, particularly regarding chromatographic separation from co-eluting metabolites. In contrast, the methods employed in the present study provided substantially enhanced sensitivity (LOQ of 0.5 ng/mL in plasma and 0.1 ng/mL in urine) and improved selectivity through optimized chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection (Table 1) [12]. These analytical advancements may explain the differences observed in pharmacokinetic parameters compared with previous studies.

Urine remains the primary biological matrix in anti-doping control. However, data on MP urinary excretion have been limited because of small sample sizes in previous studies [12, 39]. Consequently, when WADA revised GC regulations in 2022, the MRL for MP was maintained at 30 ng/mL [10]. In parallel, washout periods were introduced to support therapeutic use while minimizing the risk of false AAFs, with a 3-day washout period recommended for oral GC administration [10].

The present work provides additional evidence to support the current criteria for detecting MP misuse in sport. A 12-mg oral dose was selected based on estimates indicating that it represents the lowest dose associated with a high risk of performance-enhancing effects during acute administration [10]. Together with previously studied doses (4, 8, and 40 mg) [12, 39], the investigated range reflects clinically relevant dosing regimens (4–40 mg) commonly used for pulmonary conditions, which are among the main indications for MP use in athletes.

The study population consisted of healthy male Caucasian participants. MP was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events or any events that led to the withdrawal of participants from the study. Reported adverse events were mild (seven events in six participants) and unrelated to the intervention (headache, odynophagia, heartburn, epigastric pain, and increased transaminases).

The results demonstrated that the current MRL of 30 ng/mL is appropriate for detecting MP misuse. Concentrations exceeding the MRL were observed for up to 12 h following both single and multiple oral administrations of 12 mg. In some individuals, MP remained above the MRL between 12 and 24 h, whereas all samples collected in the 24- to 36-h interval were below the MRL. Consistent with previous findings, the detection window appears to be dose dependent [12]. After an oral dose of 4 mg, concentrations exceeded the MRL for up to 12 h, while after 40 mg, they remained above 30 ng/mL for up to 24 h. A separate study reported concentrations exceeding 30 ng/mL for up to 30 h after 8-mg oral administration [39]. While these data warrant consideration, the absence of detailed validation data for the analytical method limits the reliability of these findings.

MP represents a minor fraction of the administered dose in urine, reflecting extensive metabolism. This observation is consistent with previous work [12] and is supported by the predominance of metabolic clearance over renal clearance. Systemic metabolism of MP accounted for approximately 98.5% and 95.2% of the total plasma clearance, following single and multiple dosing, respectively. In agreement with this, multiple metabolites were detected in urine, with 15 metabolites identified in addition to the parent compound (Fig. S1 of the ESM) [38]. Although only a qualitative assessment was possible owing to the absence of reference standards, the most abundant metabolites were associated with C20 reduction (M2 and M3) and C11 oxidation (M1), followed by C16 hydroxylation (M8) or further C20 reduction (M11). These findings confirm that MP metabolism is primarily mediated by 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenease and 20-ketosteroid reductases, although the quantitative contribution of each pathway remains unclear.

Despite its low urinary abundance, MP was selected as the preferred marker for distinguishing permitted from prohibited use owing to its analytical robustness and ease of implementation in routine antidoping workflows. Although metabolites such as M8 and M11 demonstrated slightly improved discriminatory potential, their use would require unequivocal structural confirmation [12].

The low fraction of unchanged drug excreted in urine contributes to substantial interindividual variability. This variability was particularly evident in the multiple-dose group, where the fraction excreted (Fel) ranged from 1.6% to 17.5% (mean ± standard deviation, 4.9 ± 5.4%) [Table S4 of the ESM]. Additional variability related to other factors not evaluated in this study cannot be discarded (gender, ethnicity). Although the present data suggest that a shorter washout period (e.g., 48 h) might be feasible following oral intake, the observed variability, together with the existing literature and the need for harmonized recommendations across GCs, supports maintaining the current 3-day washout period.

MP was detectable in plasma samples at least up to 12 h after administration, according to the data obtained in the multiple-dose study. In the next samples collected 24 h after oral intake, it was not detected. Plasma data confirmed the systemic effects of oral MP, as evidenced by suppression of CORT.

A pharmacokinetic model integrating plasma concentrations and urinary excretion data was developed for both dosing regimens. The model accurately described the individual plasma concentrations with good agreement between predicted and observed concentrations (Figs. S3 and S4 of the ESM). The mean observed concentrations at each timepoint were also in agreement with the mean predicted values (Figs. 2 and 3).

Key pharmacokinetic parameters (Table 2), including the AUC, V, and Clp, were consistent with previous studies investigating oral administrations of MP [30, 32, 35, 37]. However, the elimination rate constants (Ke) were slightly higher, resulting in shorter t1/2 values than previously reported. Earlier studies reported a t1/2 that ranged from 1.78 to 2.4 hours [29, 32, 35, 36], and a recent meta-analysis estimated a t1/2 of 2.6 h using a pharmacokinetic model constructed using plasma data from these earlier studies [37]. In contrast, the present study yielded lower values (0.83 ± 0.15 h and 1.07 ± 0.35 h in single and multiple dosing groups, respectively). Our findings were confirmed using an independent method, namely the rating urinary excretion calculation method described by Gibaldi and Perrier [41]. The Ke and their corresponding t1/2 values obtained with this second method were in agreement with those derived from the compartmental model for all participants, supporting the robustness of our data.

The sampling design was adequate for pharmacokinetic modeling. Following single-dose administration, extrapolated area under the concentration–time curve from 8 h to infinity remained below 10% in all participants, indicating reliable estimation despite the absence of intermediate sampling points. In the multiple-dose study, carried out in eight different participants, additional sampling at 12 h further strengthened model accuracy.

The shorter t1/2 values observed in this study compared with earlier publications may be explained by several factors. First, the interindividual variability in metabolism can play an important role, as already observed in our volunteers, as MP is extensively metabolized by cytochrome P450 3A4, an isozyme of the cytochrome P450 superfamily with high interindividual variability in their expression. Second, limitations in earlier analytical methods including insufficient selectivity and reliance on ultraviolet detection may have led to inaccurate quantitation of MP, potentially including contributions from metabolites. Finally, incomplete reporting of demographic variables (e.g., sex, age, body weight) in earlier studies may also contribute to discrepancies, as these factors influence MP pharmacokinetics. For example, higher clearance and a shorter t1/2 have been reported in women compared with men [34]. Taken together, these findings support the robustness and reliability of the pharmacokinetic parameters obtained in the present study, and reinforce the suitability of the current MRL and washout recommendations.

Conclusions

Urinary and plasma concentrations of MP and its metabolites were evaluated following both single and multiple oral administrations using sensitive and selective LC-MS/MS technology. Based on the results, the MRL of 30 ng/mL is confirmed as an appropriate limit for detecting oral MP use while minimizing the risk of false AAF when adhering to the minimum 3-day washout period for oral GCs. Modern LC-MS/MS analysis and combined plasma-urine pharmacokinetic modeling provided robust evidence that MP is rapidly eliminated, extensively metabolized, and associated with transient CORT suppression after oral administration.
