A bibliometric analysis of executive functions in ADHD mapping trends and hotspots
Authors: Qingmin Zhou, Changsheng Chen, Junyi Tong, Peng Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Executive functions (EFs) represent a key domain in neuropsychological research, attracting attention gradually in studies of ADHD. Despite the growing body of literature on EFs in ADHD, a comprehensive bibliometric analysis mapping the intellectual structure, research hotspots, and evolutionary trends in this field remains lacking. Given the importance of EFs and ADHD, the study aimed to map research trends, and identify the research hotspots on EFs in ADHD over the past decade.MethodsAll literature related to EFs in ADHD from 2015 to 2024 was collected from the Web of Science Core Collection. Our research utilizes bibliometric tools to construct relevant knowledge maps for national, institutional, author, journal, reference, and keyword analysis, and summarizes trends and research hotspots in the field.Results2898 papers about EFs in ADHD were accessed in the Web of Science Core Collection over the last decade. Data analysis revealed that the USA dominated in publication volume; King’s College London was the institution with the most publications; Kofler, Michael J was the most published researcher; the Journal of Attention Disorder was the main journal for the study of EFs in ADHD. Seven clusters were identified and five hotspots were analyzed.ConclusionOur study summarizes the research trends and research hotspots in the field of EFs in ADHD research. Future research could focus on the mechanisms underlying the relationship between ADHD core symptoms and EFs, and studies on comorbidities associated with ADHD based on EF deficits. Research in this field regarding diagnosis, neuroimaging, and treatment remains to be conducted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00495-4.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) describes a neurologically based developmental condition characterized by developmentally inappropriate symptoms of inattention, impulsivity, and/or hyperactivity [1]. It is generally believed to result from a combination of genetic and other contributing factors [2]. The global prevalence of ADHD is approximately 5–7% among children, and in most cases, symptoms persist into adulthood, making it a significant public health concern [3, 4]. In clinical practice, ADHD typically presents with predominant hyperactivity and impulsivity in childhood, while in adolescence and adulthood, symptoms of inattention tend to become more prominent [5, 6]. Beyond its core symptoms, ADHD commonly co-occurs with conditions like autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and mood disorders, significantly impacting both families and society [7, 8]. Although ADHD diagnosis adheres to rigorous criteria, the process largely relies on subjective reports from parents, teachers and doctors, and the diagnosis continues to be questioned [9]. In terms of treatment, traditional pharmacotherapy has many side effects [10], and emerging treatments still lack strong evidence [11]. With the emergence of “biopsychosocial medicine”, neuropsychological approaches to ADHD have gained prominence and are anticipated to offer fresh theoretical insights into the underlying causes, symptom manifestations, diagnostic processes, and therapeutic interventions for ADHD [11].

Among the various cognitive theories of ADHD, the executive function (EF) deficit theory has become one of the most influential research frameworks in recent years due to its strong explanatory power for the heterogeneity of ADHD symptoms and its high comorbidity with other conditions. EFs have received much attention as a hot topic in neuropsychological research [12, 13], and the crucial function of EFs has been consistently demonstrated across multiple studies [14, 15]. EFs are a set of top-down functions, at least partially independent, that support goal-directed action. EFs have traditionally been thought to be associated with the prefrontal lobe, but are now also closely associated with posterior frontal and subcortical areas [16]. Core EFs include inhibitory control (IC), working memory (WM), and cognitive flexibility (CF), as well as higher-order functions such as reasoning, planning, and problem-solving [17]. More pronounced EF deficits have been documented in children with ADHD relative to typically developing children, which are closely related to their behavioral and academic challenges [18]. However, there are still many research gaps in the field of research related to ADHD and EFs. On one hand, not all individuals with ADHD exhibit the same EFs profiles, highlighting the need for further exploration of the heterogeneous manifestations of EFs in ADHD [12]. On the other hand, while current pharmacological treatments provide significant clinical benefits, they may not fully address executive function impairments in ADHD, highlighting the urgent need for more targeted interventions [19]. Furthermore, a review of the literature revealed that scholarly output has risen at an exponential rate in recent years [20, 21]. While numerous empirical studies have explored the relationship between ADHD and EFs, a comprehensive and systematic synthesis of the overarching intellectual landscape, research hotspots, and evolutionary trends in this domain is still lacking. Specifically, no bibliometric analysis has yet been undertaken to quantitatively map the global research output on ADHD and EFs, leaving the structural dynamics of this critical field largely unexplored.

These aforementioned phenomena increase the need for literature review and management, making it necessary for this study to conduct a further bibliometric analysis (BA). As a research method, BA applies mathematical and statistical techniques to the study of publications pertaining to a particular research topic across a specified timeframe [22]. With the growing volume of scientific output, bibliometric research has become an important tool for retrieving and analyzing information related to specific research fields [23]. In addition to assessing the scientific contributions and influence of countries, institutions, and individual researchers, bibliometrics can also serve as a mapping tool to identify relevant studies, author collaborations, and institutional networks within a research domain [24]. Currently, applying BA provides a synthesis of the status quo, emerging trends, and focal points in a given disease or research area, thereby suggesting innovative avenues and orientations for subsequent research [25].

To date, researchers have not published any bibliometric studies comprehensively analyzing hotspots addressing the intersection of ADHD and EFs. Given the distinctive features of BA research and the limited existing literature in this area, this study conducts a BA of publications over the past decade related to ADHD and EFs, focusing on publication trends, geographical distribution of research, and major contributing entities. The goal is to summarize the current state and developmental trajectory of this field, identify research hotspots and challenges, and ultimately offer fresh perspectives to advance future investigations.

Materials and methods

Database and search strategy

All data for this study were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) on February 2, 2025, covering the period from 2015 to 2024. To ensure scientific rigor and reproducibility, we followed established practices in bibliometric research [26, 27] and selected WoSCC as the exclusive data source. As a widely recognized and standardized database for global academic literature, WoSCC enables high-quality data retrieval and comprehensive coverage of peer-reviewed research [28]. Specifically, we restricted our search to the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-E) and Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI) to enhance the representativeness and academic quality of the included publications [29].

The diagnostic criteria for ADHD underwent significant revisions in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), published in 2013. These changes prompted updates to corresponding Screening Scales [30], stimulated a new wave of empirical research [31] and etc., thereby shaping subsequent research trajectories in the field. As such, the DSM-5 is widely regarded as a landmark development in ADHD research. Considering the lag between guideline updates and the dissemination of academic findings, we defined the study period as January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024. This timeframe ensures that the retrieved literature adequately captures the research trends and practical implications emerging after the DSM-5 revision.

The search strategy was applied to identify relevant publications (Fig. 1). “TS” refers to the topic field, which includes titles, abstracts, and keywords. The first TS group included keywords related to ADHD, while the second included terms related to EFs. ADHD-related keywords were formally defined using MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms in PubMed. Data cleaning

Based on the search strategy described above, a total of 3193 publications were retrieved from WoSCC for the period spanning January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024. Duplicate records were initially removed using CiteSpace. The remaining articles were then screened according to the following exclusion criteria: (1) non-article document types (e.g., book chapters, book reviews, corrections, editorial materials, letters, meeting abstracts, proceedings papers, retracted publications, and retractions); (2) articles not formally published; (3) articles irrelevant to the research topic; and (4) publications not written in English. After this screening process, 2898 articles were deemed eligible for further analysis. The full records and cited references of these articles were exported in plain text format. A flowchart detailing the literature search and selection procedure is presented (Fig. 1).

Data analysis

Data analysis and scientific knowledge mapping were performed using CiteSpace (version 5.7.R5) and VOSviewer (version 1.6.18). To ensure data integrity, keyword cleansing was conducted independently by two authors, with any unresolved discrepancies adjudicated by a senior author. Subsequently, VOSviewer (version 1.6.18) was employed to organize the included data, which were then imported into Scimago (Beta 1.0.48) to generate a geographical visualization of the national collaboration network. Basic charts were generated using Microsoft Excel and Origin to complement the bibliometric analyses.

VOSviewer was employed to conduct multidimensional bibliometric analyses of institutions, authors, journals, and keywords. Unlike conventional bibliometric software, VOSviewer places particular emphasis on the graphical representation of bibliometric data, enabling the visualization of large-scale scholarly networks in an intuitive and interpretable manner. The software was developed primarily to construct and visualize bibliometric networks, thereby facilitating a comprehensive understanding of the structural composition and evolutionary dynamics of scientific research [32]. The clustering algorithm implemented in VOSviewer is based on the association strength metric and employs a modularity-based optimization technique. Entities (such as a country, institution, journal, author, or keyword) were grouped according to their co-occurrence frequency and link strength. In the generated visualizations, each node corresponds to an entity. Clusters represent groupings of co-occurring entities generated by the clustering algorithm. The thickness of the connecting lines between nodes represents the strength of collaborative relationships or co-citation linkages [32]. In maps depicting the average annual publication year, color gradients are used to represent the temporal distribution of items.

As a bibliometric method for assessing the degree of relationship between academic articles, co-citation refers to the phenomenon where two or more documents are simultaneously cited by one or more subsequent papers. Such co-citation implies a conceptual or thematic connection between the cited works. Because each cited reference inherently contains information about its authors and the journal in which it was published, co-citation analysis can also be extended to examine relationships among authors or journals. In a co-citation network visualization, the size of each node is proportional to the citation frequency of the respective document, author, or journal. Similarly, by identifying terms or phrases that co-occur in the titles, abstracts, or keywords of scholarly papers, co-occurrence analysis can reveal clusters of interconnected entities, uncover the academic interests of a domain, and detect nascent research topics.

CiteSpace, originally developed by Chaomei Chen, is a widely adopted bibliometric analysis tool designed to uncover and visualize latent knowledge patterns within scientific literature. Grounded in the disciplines of scientometrics and data visualization, this software enables the exploration of intellectual structures and emerging trends in research [29]. In this study, CiteSpace was employed to perform a journal dual-map overlay analysis, as well as to generate keyword timeline maps and detect keyword bursts. The keyword burst map was used to identify references and keywords characterized by surging citation activity over time. The journal dual-map overlay illustrated the citation relationships between citing and cited journals, while a keyword co-occurrence network perspective revealed the temporal evolution of research themes.

Meanwhile, this study also systematically analyzes the developmental characteristics of the field of ADHD and EFs by applying multiple bibliometric indicators and laws: Lotka’s Law and Price’s Law are used to analyze the distribution of author productivity and identify core researchers; Garfield’s Law (used to analyze citation concentration trends and the influence of core literature) is employed to reveal research trends and knowledge evolution mechanisms of this field from multiple dimensions. The distinguished scholar Lotka made foundational contributions to understanding the distribution of scientific productivity [33]. Accordingly, authors with five or more publications are classified as core contributors. In addition to author productivity analysis, Garfield’s Law was employed to examine citation concentration patterns and identify core journals. Garfield’s Law posits that a small core of journals accounts for the majority of cited references in a given discipline, reflecting the uneven distribution of scientific impact. To examine this pattern, we adopted a commonly used approach: ranking all journals in descending order by their total citation counts and calculating the cumulative percentage of total citations contributed by the top N% of journals (e.g., top 5%, 10%, and 25%). This method allows for the identification of core journals that exert disproportionate influence in the field, and helps to reveal the degree of citation concentration, thereby validating the applicability of Garfield’s Law to the research domain under investigation.

Research hotspots represent the focal points of scholarly inquiry within a discipline, and keywords serve as concise encapsulations of these thematic directions. In a given research field, keywords with high frequency of occurrence often indicate prevailing areas of interest. Consequently, co-occurrence analysis of keywords enables the identification of core research content and facilitates the elucidation of key thematic trends within the field [28]. In general, the top few hotspots with the highest frequency of keywords are selected for analysis.

Result

Basic quantitative information and trend of publication outputs

According to our search strategy, a total of 2989 articles related to ADHD and EFs were published during 2015–2024. These papers came from 11,697 authors from 3166 organizations in 83 countries, published in 666 journals, and were cited 101,241 times in 17,478 journals. Over the past decade, although the annual number of publications has fluctuated, it has always exceeded 250 and reached a peak of 349 in 2022, reflecting the intensity of research in this area of ADHD and EFs (Fig. 2). Publications on EFs in ADHD are projected to exhibit a continued upward trajectory in the last decades. Countries and institutions

In the WOS database, 2989 articles in the field of ADHD and EFs were published in 83 countries around the world (Fig. 3). Among these countries, the USA topped the list with 41.12% of the total publications, followed by the UK (15.96%), China (9.83%), Canada (8.30%), and Germany (6.39%). Publications originating in the USA accumulated 30,717 citations, with an average citation of 26.17 per year which is slightly lower than the UK’s average of 27.39 but remarkably higher than China’s 14.63. Moreover, the USA attained the highest H-index (73), significantly surpassing the UK (56), Canada (36), and Germany (35). Journals and co-cited journals

Our findings confirm Garfield’s Law, demonstrating that a small core of journals accounts for a disproportionate share of citations. The top 5% of journals contributed 30.4% of total citations, while the top 25% accounted for over half (52.9%), underscoring the central role of key journals in shaping the field. To further explore the intellectual connections and collaborative patterns among these influential journals, we conducted dual-map overlay and co-citation analyses using CiteSpace and VOSviewer. To illustrate the relationship between citing journals and cited journals, we used CiteSpace to overlay the two layers. The analysis of journal citation patterns reveals a clear disciplinary distinction between citing and cited journals. Discussion

Global research status and trends

Since 2015, articles concerning ADHD and EFs have shown a fluctuating upward trend, peaking in 2022, suggesting that the field has been receiving attention gradually. A survey of the literature identified EFs as a neuropsychological mechanism that plays an important role in the pathogenesis of ADHD [35].

With regard to the country of publication, a total of 70 countries and regions worldwide have conducted research in the field of ADHD and EFs. The top five contributing countries were the USA, the UK, China, Canada, and Germany, which together accounted for approximately 80% of total publications. In the map of collaborations among countries, countries such as China, and Australia and regions such as South America demonstrated relatively limited international cooperation. This highlights the importance of strengthening collaboration among major contributing countries to break down academic barriers and promote global knowledge exchange.

The USA accounted for half of the top ten institutions, with China, Sweden, Canada, Netherlands, and the UK making up the rest. Although only one UK institution, King’s College London, was in the top ten, it had the highest number of papers as well as citations. The Chinese institution, Peking University, has the lowest citations in this listing and the weakest links to other institutions, which might be related to the late start of research in this area, indicating a need to consider collaborating with more organizations in order to increase their influence.

Among the most distinguished contributors to this field of research, American scholars constituted a majority and had a strong connective strength, which revealed the emergence of a cohesive and influential community of authors across the United States. Notably, many of these highly co-cited authors were scholars who have made foundational contributions in the fields of psychiatry, behavioral sciences, psychology, etc., such as C. Keith Conners, the creator of the Conners’ Adult ADHD Rating Scales, and David Wechsler, the developer of the Wechsler Intelligence Scales.

Research hotspots

Keywords encapsulate the core thematic content and research scope of scholarly publications, and the analysis of high-frequency terms has been widely employed to uncover research focal points and evolving trends within specific disciplines. To further elucidate the structural characteristics of these hotspots and the relationships among them, we conducted a co-occurrence cluster analysis of keywords using VOSviewer. Based on the intrinsic associations and co-occurrence frequencies among terms, keywords were grouped into distinct thematic clusters, thereby delineating the primary research directions in the field of ADHD and EFs. This clustering approach not only highlights the core topics of inquiry but also reveals the interconnections between different research themes, as reflected by the linkages among keywords across clusters.

Based on the co-occurrence network analysis of literature keywords, seven core knowledge clusters were identified (details can be found in the supplementary materials). Through manual inspection of keyword composition and representative literature, two of the seven clusters were found to have substantial thematic overlap and were therefore merged by author consensus, resulting in five dominant research hotspots. Hotspot 1 (ADHD Core Symptoms Mediating Academic Performance Deficits and Mechanisms from an EF Perspective) primarily integrates Cluster 2 (focusing on “EF” deficits and specific components such as “working memory” and “inhibitory control” as well as their association with symptoms like “inattention” and “hyperactivity/impulsivity”) and Cluster 3 (focusing on functional outcomes such as “performance” and “academic achievement”). Together, these clusters form a complete research framework of “symptoms–cognitive mechanisms–functional impairment”. Hotspot 2 (Comorbidity Research on ADHD Based on EF Deficits) directly corresponds to Cluster 6, which centers on “ASD” and “comorbidity”, representing an independent and active branch of research on neurodevelopmental comorbidities. Hotspot 3 (Diagnostic Challenges and Clinical Features in Adult ADHD) derives from the tightly connected subnetwork within Cluster 1 (formed by keywords such as “adult ADHD”, “diagnosis”, and “assessment”) and from Cluster 7 (formed by “adolescence”, “childhood”, etc.), highlighting the unique clinical issues across the lifespan. At the same time, it also extends to the discussion of related clinical features in adult ADHD that are associated with childhood, as well as diagnostic markers representing current hot topics and challenges. Hotspot 4 (Neuroimaging and Pharmacotherapy in ADHD EFs) corresponds to Cluster 4, where keywords are clearly divided into two intertwined themes: neurobiological mechanisms (e.g., “fMRI”, “prefrontal cortex”) and pharmacological interventions (e.g., “methylphenidate”, “atomoxetine”), representing the research pathway from biological mechanisms to core treatments. Hotspot 5 (Non-Pharmacological Interventions Enhancing EFs in ADHD) corresponds to Cluster 5, which centers on “interventions” and distinctly points to non-pharmacological approaches such as “cognitive training” and “physical activity”, reflecting a significant and growing area of research on complementary treatment strategies. These five hotspots, data-driven and thematically distinct yet complementary, collectively encompass the complete knowledge structure of the field, ranging from foundational theories to clinical applications.

Hotspot 1: ADHD core symptoms mediating academic performance deficits and mechanisms from an EF perspective

The core symptoms of ADHD—inattention, impulsivity, and/or hyperactivity—are significantly associated with EF deficits. A series of studies by Biederman et al. found that individuals with EF deficits are associated with aggravated ADHD symptoms, specifically inattention, greater emotional dysregulation, and higher levels of associated psychopathology [36]. Notably, inattention symptoms demonstrate stronger predictive value and show a closer association with EF deficits [37]. Research dissecting EF components reveals heterogeneity: prevalence rates for WM impairments ranges from 30 to 37%, while deficits in inhibitory control range from 21 to 46%. WM impairment in children correlates with increased severity of both inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms [38]. The core symptoms of ADHD are collectively mediated by EFs, particularly WM and inhibitory control [39], suggesting that EF deficits are multidimensional.

EF deficits are also supposed to be closely linked to academic performance deficits. Several studies confirm that students diagnosed with ADHD demonstrate significantly poorer academic performance compared to neurotypically developing peers [40, 41]. In individuals with ADHD, inattention, particularly among pediatric populations, has been identified as the core symptom most strongly linked to poorer academic performance [42]. Furthermore, there is a consensus in the literature that EF, as an important predictor of academic achievement in childhood and adolescence, is positively associated with academic performance levels, with stronger EF linked to higher achievement outcomes [43]. Carrasco et al. pointed that although ADHD symptom severity may diminish over time, academic difficulties often persist or even worsen with age, suggesting EF deficits may serve as a mediating factor in the ADHD-academic achievement relationship [44]. Beyond these core academic deficits, learning difficulties, such as handwriting difficulties, dyslexia, and mathematical difficulties, are highly comorbid with ADHD [45–47].

At the theoretical level, Barkley’s response inhibition model emphasizes that inhibition deficits disrupt other EF components [48]. The dual pathway model accounts for ADHD symptoms by integrating cognitive dysfunctions (e.g., EFs deficits) and motivational dysfunctions (e.g., delay aversion) [49]. Consistent with this framework, Thorell [50] proposed that in ADHD, inattention manifests as a result of abnormal self-regulatory functioning due to inhibition deficits, whereas impulsive traits may stem from situationally conditioned motivational deficits. Progress has also been made in neuroanatomical studies based on the dual pathway model [51]. “Cold” EFs are thought to operate in an emotionally neutral environment, whereas “hot” EFs involve more emotional/motivational involvement [52]. Deficits in both “cold” and “hot” EFs are associated with ADHD [53], but their impact on EFs varies. “Cold” EFs are linked to inattention, while “hot” EFs are related to hyperactivity/impulsivity [54]. Recent research has increasingly emphasized the neurodevelopmental significance of “hot” EFs and motivational pathways [55].

Hotspot 2: comorbidity research on ADHD based on EF deficits

The DSM-5 reclassified ADHD from the disruptive behavior disorders into the neurodevelopmental disorders, while explicitly permitting comorbid diagnosis with ASD [56]. This shift has deepened the understanding of ADHD’s clinical traits. The comorbidity rate of ADHD among individuals with ASD is approximately 28–50% [57], while about 21% of individuals with ADHD are also diagnosed with ASD [58]. Core EF deficits in ADHD primarily involve inhibitory control and sustained attention, manifesting as hyperactivity and distractibility; in contrast, ASD is more strongly associated with deficits in CF [59].

Notably, children with comorbid ASD and ADHD exhibit more pronounced EF impairments compared to those having either disorder alone [59]. Ninety-two percent of children with comorbid ASD and ADHD are found to have EFs deficits, compared with 47% and 63% of children with ASD or ADHD, respectively [60]. This superimposed effect suggests that comorbidities may exacerbate EF impairments through different neuropsychological mechanisms, and thus systematic studies are urgently needed to clarify their clinical implications. A systematic review indicated that children with comorbid ASD and ADHD had significantly more severe deficits in WM, planning and organization, and IC compared to single diagnosed children, although deficits in attention were similar to those of the ADHD children [61]. Research indicates a relationship between ADHD symptoms and EFs in children with ASD [62]. Further evidence indicated that comorbid ADHD/ASD was associated with broader EF deficits than ADHD alone, and the comorbid group’s EF profile more closely mirrored that of ADHD than ASD [63]. This pattern may reflect a common dysfunction of basal ganglia circuits [64], and elucidating the underlying mechanisms will require additional neuroanatomical studies.

Despite growing recognition of this comorbidity, research specifically examining comorbid ASD and ADHD remains limited, as many previous studies have treated ADHD and ASD as mutually exclusive groups. Among the existing comorbidity studies, the majority have focused on the default mode network (DMN) [65, 66]. The PFC has important functions in EFs [67], and recent study found, compared to those with ASD alone, children with comorbid ASD and ADHD exhibited altered gray matter volume asymmetries in prefrontal regions linked to executive function [68]. However, from a neuroimaging perspective, the precise nature of the relationship between ADHD and ASD in terms of executive dysfunction remains unclear—specifically, whether the two conditions interact to exacerbate impairments or whether their co-occurrence should be viewed as a distinct neurobiological entity requiring an independent framework.

Hotspot 3: diagnostic challenges and clinical features in adult ADHD

ADHD, a prevalent childhood-onset neurobiological disorder, affects approximately 6–10% of children and persists in 4.4% of adults [69]. Indeed, approximately 60% of those diagnosed with ADHD as children retained symptomatic expression into adulthood [70]. Although there is widespread recognition of ADHD’s presence across the life span and its potential adverse impacts, research and clinical focus have historically centered on childhood populations [71]. This gap is especially significant due to the greater complexity involved in diagnosing adults with ADHD compared to diagnosing children.

While the DSM-5 states that ADHD diagnostic criteria apply equally to adults and children, clinical evidence indicates that adults tend to demonstrate more varied impairments in higher-level EF and emotional regulation compared to younger populations [70]. Adults with ADHD frequently encounter substantial difficulties fulfilling expected social roles across various life areas, which can result in considerable emotional distress [72]. Deficits in the emotional domain mean that many adults with ADHD experience feelings of being overwhelmed, impatience, and difficulties regulating intense emotions [73]. These emotion impairments may consequently exacerbate ADHD core symptoms, executive dysfunction, psychiatric comorbidities, and even higher rates of criminal convictions [74]. The persistence of ADHD is most consistently predicted by lower Intelligence Quotient in childhood and more severe symptoms [75].

Moreover, evidence from multiple studies points to the possibility that ADHD symptoms manifest initially during adolescence or adulthood in certain cases, though this conclusion is not without contention [76, 77]. Regarding the similarity between adult ADHD (apparent onset after age 12) and childhood-onset ADHD, more research has supported that childhood-onset becoming apparent later due to scaffolding/compensation [78, 79]. Additionally, Faraone and Biederman pointed out that youth with ADHD who possess strong intellectual abilities or live in supportive, well-structured childhood environments may compensate during early life through such scaffolding, but can develop the full ADHD syndrome when these supports are removed [80].

To comprehend the complexities of identifying ADHD in adults, a close examination of the diagnostic criteria is required. Diagnosis relies on a detailed clinical interview, which remains the gold standard for both children and adults [30, 81]. In adult ADHD, DSM-5 modifications to enhance applicability to adult ADHD notably include: lowering the symptom threshold to ≥ 5/9 criteria per domain for individuals aged ≥ 17 (versus ≥ 6/9 previously), while retaining DSM-IV’s 18 core symptoms verbatim but adding adult-specific manifestation examples for each item [77].

However, even though the criteria have been adjusted to better suit adults, diagnosing adult ADHD is still much harder than diagnosing ADHD in childhood. Evidence shows a general decline in ADHD symptomatology with age, characterized by a shift in the presentation of core symptoms (impulsivity, hyperactivity, inattention) from childhood to adulthood, usually toward more subtle expressions [82]. Moreover, adults with ADHD may develop compensatory strategies for lifelong difficulties. These include pursuing fast-paced, varied, or physically demanding careers, or relying on a partner to manage organizational deficits. Additionally, some individuals may self-medicate undiagnosed symptoms using substances such as caffeine, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, or amphetamines [83]. Another factor is that when asked about childhood, many adults with ADHD often cannot recall specific details about their school-related behaviors, which is also a contributing factor to the misdiagnosis of adult ADHD [84]. Lastly, due to the considerable overlap of symptoms between ADHD and other disorders, practitioners may mistakenly attribute ADHD behaviors to mood disorders, depression or mania in clinical practice [85, 86]. Beyond symptom overlap, the actual comorbidity of conditions like major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorder, etc. significantly hinders accurate adult ADHD detection, as the often more acute manifestations of comorbid conditions typically become the assessment focus, potentially obscuring the ADHD component [77].

Hotspot 4: neuroimaging and pharmacotherapy for EFs in ADHD

Attention-deficit, hyperactivity, and impulsivity symptoms in ADHD patients stem from a synergistic combination of structural and functional alterations across multiple brain regions. Structural abnormalities include reduced gray matter volume in the PFC, basal ganglia, anterior cingulate gyrus, parietal lobe, and limbic system [87–89]. In terms of functional abnormalities, fMRI studies have shown inconsistent results due to methodological heterogeneity (e.g., statistical methods, sample characteristics, etc.) [90], and it is worth noting that the results of many early fMRI studies may have yielded false positive rates as high as 70% due to inappropriate statistical thresholds [91]. Current fMRI studies have increasingly focused on functional abnormalities in networks such as the DMN, the PFC, the fronto-parietal region, the striatum, and the cerebellum—and their association with EF dysfunction [92–94]; however, most of these studies are limited by small sample sizes or rely on meta-analyses that incorporate a substantial number of early, methodologically flawed studies. From a neurodevelopmental perspective, regions in large-scale brain networks gradually develop from initially sparsely connected to tightly connected during typical development [95], whereas patients with ADHD exhibit a significant and specific maturation lag in DMN internal connectivity and its connectivity with the ventral attentional network and frontal-parietal network [96]. EFs are synergistically regulated by multiple brain regions including PFC, thalamus, basal ganglia, parietal lobe, and temporal lobe [97]. Notably, EFs are not entirely dependent on a single brain region, but rather on the dynamic interaction of the prefrontal lobe with other networks [97]. For example, disturbed structural connectivity of the frontal striatal circuit in children with ADHD is significantly associated with executive dysfunction [98]. EEG-related studies have also been more popular in the area of ADHD and EFs, with ADHD patients having EF deficits accompanied by abnormalities in event-related potentials [99–101], and EEG-related studies have shown that components of the late ERP (e.g., the P300) are thought to correlate with EFs [99, 101].

Building on these neurobiological insights, pharmacological studies have sought to determine whether medications targeting these systems can ameliorate EF deficits. There have been many studies demonstrating that methylphenidate and atomoxetine alleviate ADHD core symptoms [102, 103], but their effects on EFs remain to be investigated. Stimulants are used as first-line medications, with methylphenidate (MPH) being a prime example. A meta-analysis showed that MPH treatment was associated with improvements in multiple aspects of EFs (e.g., IC, WM, planning) in individuals with ADHD [104], and another similar study indicated that medication only improved EF deficits in children but was not effective in adults [105]. Moreover, improvements in self-reported EFs have also been found in studies of the effects of MPH on EFs, and performance-based EFs don’t show any differences [102]. The potential reasons for this discrepancy require further study. Non-stimulant medications are used as second-line treatments, with atomoxetine being a representative example. Although meta-analyses have shown that long-term use of both stimulants (e.g., MPH) and nonstimulants (e.g., atomoxetine) alleviates ADHD behavioral symptoms and improves EF [106], some studies have shown that EFs deficits persist after drug treatment [107, 108]. Taken together, while existing studies have addressed aspects of EF, there remains a lack of large-scale, multicenter trials specifically designed to investigate the precise impact of pharmacological treatments on executive functions and the underlying mechanisms.

Hotspot 5: non-pharmacological interventions enhancing EFs in ADHD

Emerging evidence demonstrates that physical activity (PA) interventions enhance domains including attention, EFs (e.g., IC, WM), motor skills, cognitive function, and social functioning in pediatric populations with ADHD [109–111]. PA encompasses skeletal muscle-generated bodily movements that induce measurable energy expenditure [112]. PA interventions are considered a promising addition to conventional treatment approaches for ADHD [113]. Among various non-pharmacological strategies targeting EF enhancement in ADHD-e.g., physical exercises, cognitive training (CT), neurofeedback, cognitive-behavioral therapy-targeting EFs enhancement in ADHD populations, PA-based interventions have been shown to demonstrate superior efficacy in improving global EFs [114]. Lubans et al. proposed a framework outlining three pathways through linking PA to improved cognitive outcomes in youth: neural mechanisms (e.g., cerebral perfusion, gray matter volume), psychosocial dimensions (e.g., autonomy, social connectivity, self-concept), and observable behaviors (e.g., motor coordination, sleep patterns, academic achievement) [115]. Consistent with these proposed pathways, empirical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of specific PA modalities. Based on randomized controlled trials, a network meta-analysis by Li et al. revealed perceptual-motor training demonstrated superior efficacy for motor proficiency and WM, whereas aquatic exercise achieved the highest efficacy for attention deficits and CF [109]. Recent meta-analyses indicate that single bout of moderate-to-vigorous PA [116] and chronic PA [111] elicit significant improvements in some of EFs among pediatric ADHD populations. Further, A meta-analytic review by Shi et al. identified that structured PA sessions (30–50 min, 3–7 times weekly, spanning ≤ 10 weeks) significantly enhanced EFs outcomes in atypical children [117].

Beyond PA, CT represents another prominent non-pharmacological approach for enhancing EFs in ADHD. CT, particularly scaffolding-based approaches, has been extensively implemented to enhance EFs and may reduce impulsivity and inattention symptoms in preschoolers with ADHD, according to recent research findings [118]. While traditional CT approaches typically involve pen-and-paper exercises and necessitate direct clinician guidance, contemporary research has demonstrated growing empirical support for computerized CT targeting EFs enhancement in individuals with ADHD [119]. Chen et al. conducted a meta-analysis and found that multi-domain CT improves attention and EFs in ADHD [120]. A recent study conducted by Dehnabaei et al. found that computerized CT demonstrates greater EFs improvements in ADHD adolescents compared to non-ADHD peers [121]. Wu et al. reported comparable enhancements in ADHD symptoms and EF following both targeted and generalized CT approaches, noting superior outcomes from ADHD-focused training for milder presentations [119]. In a systematic review concluded that among nine trials conducting follow-up assessments, seven indicated sustained therapeutic benefits, supporting CT’s efficacy as an adjunctive ADHD intervention for youth [122]. However, Robledo-Castro et al. found that although computer-based CT shows potential in improving WM and IC in ADHD children, mixed outcomes and methodological heterogeneity across studies warrant further validation [123].

Limitations

Firstly, although we fully considered the time lag between the publication of DSM-5 and its practical implementation, and therefore set the time span to 2015–2024, we did not verify whether each included study actually used DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. There may still be a possibility that some studies did not employ DSM-5 criteria, and we did not exclude such articles. This may have introduced bias into our results. Besides, our bibliometric study only included the English literature from 2015 to 2024, which may have led to the omission of some relevant studies. The language-based inclusion criteria may have overlooked the research contributions of non-English-speaking scholars. Meanwhile, restricting the scope to SCI/SSCI-indexed journals and document types (i.e., reviews and articles) might have excluded some meaningful research findings. Secondly, the academic influence of high-quality literature published in recent years (e.g., 2023–2024) could be underestimated due to the short citation accumulation period. Additionally, studies from 2025 were not included, which might have led to the omission of emerging research frontiers.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of research on EFs in ADHD from 2015 to 2024. The annual number of publications showed a general upward trend, reaching a peak in 2022, indicating sustained and growing interest in this field. Our study mapped the trends in the research field from different perspectives, including countries, institutions, authors, journals, references, and keywords. Keyword co-occurrence analysis revealed several prominent research hotspots, including the mechanisms linking EF deficits to core ADHD symptoms and academic performance, ASD, diagnostic challenges in adult ADHD, neuroimaging correlates of EF impairments, and both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions targeting EFs. Our findings suggest a shift from merely describing EF deficits toward exploring their complex relationships with clinical presentations and underlying neural substrates. Notably, while neuroimaging and treatment-related topics have gained increasing attention, many studies in these areas are limited by small sample sizes or methodological concerns, and large-scale, well-controlled trials remain scarce. In conclusion, this bibliometric analysis maps the evolving landscape of research on EFs in ADHD, identifies current focal points, and provides a reference for researchers entering this field.
