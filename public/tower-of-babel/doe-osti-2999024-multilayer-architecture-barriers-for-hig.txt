 Abstract

 Multilayer Architecture Barriers for High Temperature
 Nuclear Applications

Innovative thin film multilayer metal/ceramic barrier coatings have been developed by Argonne
National Laboratory to protect fuel cladding against different phenomena that can be encountered
under the harsh operating conditions of advanced nuclear reactors. One phenomenon is Fuel-
Cladding Chemical Interaction (FCCI), which can represent a challenge to the performance of
steel-clad metallic fuels in sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs), especially when operating to high
burnups at elevated cladding temperatures, and long fuel residence time. Another phenomenon is
the corrosion of zirconium-based cladding under prototypic boiling water reactor (BWR)
conditions and associated cladding during off normal conditions, including loss of coolant
accidents (LOCA).
This work provides details of the development and testing of a thin film (few microns) multilayer
metal/ceramic (CrY/Y2O3) coatings on steel cladding and its performance against FCCI. The
coating architecture evolved through systematic mechanical testing, radiation tolerance
assessments, and high-temperature diffusion studies from a preliminary multilayer concept to an
optimized high-performance design. The optimized structure leverages stoichiometric Y2O3 for its
exceptional radiation stability, chemical inertness, and ultra-low fission product diffusivity (e.g.,
Ce diffusivity ~10⁻²⁰ m²/s in irradiated Y2O3 at 650 °C). This is paired with ductile chromium-rich
interlayers (Cr₉₅Y₅), which significantly enhance fracture toughness and mechanical resilience.
Design refinements, including increased ceramic layer thickness and optimized Cr–Y interlayer
composition, eliminated fracture propagation and preserved coating integrity under extreme
mechanical stress (>10× typical reactor conditions) and prolonged radiation exposure (~300 dpa).
Those conditions are well beyond expected conditions during normal operations in SFR, and cover
some of the transient conditions as well. In addition, the coating deposition was demonstrated to
be applied uniformly to the inner surface of steel tubes representing prototypic SFR cladding.
Another phenomenon under consideration in this work is the corrosion of zirconium-based
cladding under prototypic boiling water reactor (BWR) conditions and associated off normal
conditions including loss of coolant accident (LOCA). Another multilayer thin film coating is
considered for mitigation of this phenomenon, where a coating of yttrium aluminum and yttrium
aluminum ceramic/metal thin film coating. Preliminary results on the coating performance during
high temperature thermal cycling (up to 1000 C) have shown promising performance of the
coating. On-going optimization of the coating will continue in FY26.

 1 INTRODUCTION
 Metallic-fuel sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs) are key components of advanced nuclear
development in the United States, supported by federal programs and growing private-sector
interest. Reliable operations at higher operating temperatures and burnups, and at extended fuel
residence time (in some of the designs), can potentially improve plant efficiency and overall
reactor economics. In particular, the impact of increasing the reactor core outlet temperature,
above typical outlet temperatures (e.g., 510oC) have been investigated [1,2]and shown to have
potential economic benefits, provided that safety requirements are satisfied. In the case of metallic
fuel cores, a key limiting factor to increasing the outlet flow temperature and associated increase
in fuel cladding temperature, is the fuel cladding chemical interaction (FCCI) phenomenon. Thus,
mitigation of this phenomenon in steel-clad SFR metallic fuel can be of importance to future
generations of those advanced reactors that seek further improvements in efficiency and economics.
 FCCI occurs when lanthanide fission products generated within typical metallic U–Zr fuel
diffuse into surrounding iron-based cladding (e.g., HT-9) and associated transport of cladding
components into the fuel, causing cladding embrittlement, and degradation of its mechanical
properties. These effects are exacerbated with increasing cladding temperatures (e.g.,
temperatures > 600oC). To address possibilities for operations at high-temperatures, advanced
claddings such as oxide dispersion-strengthened (ODS) steels and high-entropy alloys (HEAs) are
under active development. ODS steels, with nanoscale oxide dispersions and ferritic or ferritic-
martensitic matrices, provide excellent high-temperature strength, creep resistance, and swelling
resistance under irradiation. HEAs offer high-temperature strength, corrosion resistance, and
promising irradiation tolerance. That said, ODS steels retain a ferrous matrix and therefore remain
susceptible to FCCI, and HEAs, depending on composition, are not expected to be fully immune
to FCCI in the SFR environment. Traditional FCCI mitigation approaches have focused on
metallic diffusion barriers (e.g., Cr, Ti, Mo, V, Zr) [6–11], which offer high melting points and
low solubility in U-Zr fuel. For example, electroplated Cr coatings on HT-9 cladding have shown
promise based on recent in-reactor available data, at 550 °C cladding temperature and burnups of
~8.7 at% [12]. However, post-irradiation examinations reveal Cr depletion via chemical
interaction with fuel and fission products, leading to brittleness and cavity formation. These
outcomes indicate that with higher operational temperatures simply increasing metal-based coating
thickness may not be a sustainable solution. Alternatively, ceramic coatings such as YSZ, CrN,
ZrN, TiO₂, and TiN, which offer superior chemical and thermal stability [13–17] can provide an
answer, but are limited by their low fracture toughness, thermal expansion mismatch with steel
substrates, and fabrication challenges in achieving dense, defect-free films. Furthermore, most
existing diffusion studies (500–650 °C, 24–100 hours) do not fully capture long-term degradation
or defect-assisted diffusion pathways. A viable FCCI barrier must therefore combine chemical and
thermal stability with mechanical resilience, radiation tolerance (>100 dpa), and reliability under
both steady-state and transient reactor conditions.

 9

 Yttrium oxide (Y2O3) has emerged as a promising FCCI barrier due to its fluorite-derived cubic
structure, high melting point (~2450 °C), phase stability (~2325 °C), moderate thermal
conductivity (~7 W/m·K), and exceptional chemical inertness [18–22]. Its strong Y–O bonds
suppress chemical reactivity and interdiffusion with uranium and fission products, and its highly
negative Gibbs free energy of formation enhances thermodynamic stability [23]. In this study, we
systematically evaluate Y2O3’s barrier performance, including thermodynamic stability, resistance
to irradiation-induced phase transformation, and lanthanide diffusion kinetics—with specific
attention to distinguishing grain boundaries and bulk diffusion mechanisms. Despite these
strengths, single-layer Y2O3 coatings on HT-9 substrates have demonstrated limited
thermomechanical compatibility, with thermal stress–induced cracking exacerbated under
irradiation. To address this, we developed a multilayer architecture incorporating alternating Y₂O₃
and Cr–Y alloy interlayers. Cr–Y alloys offer excellent high-temperature mechanical properties,
chemical stability with uranium, and enhanced interfacial adhesion [24–27]. Two compositions
(Cr₅₀Y₅₀ and Cr₉₅Y₀₅) were evaluated for their effects on ceramic–metal bonding, fracture
resistance, irradiation response, and overall multilayer hardness. Optimizing interlayer
composition is essential for improving crack tolerance and preserving barrier function under
extreme thermal and irradiation conditions.
 Based on these findings, an optimized multilayer design employing stoichiometric Y2O3 and
Cr₉₅Y₀₅ interlayers was fabricated and tested. This configuration exhibited robust mechanical and
thermal stability and demonstrated superior irradiation tolerance, satisfying key FCCI mitigation
criteria for advanced SFRs [27]. Notably, this work establishes that radiation-resistant,
mechanically tough FCCI barriers can be achieved using relatively thin ceramic films [28]. By
addressing critical knowledge gaps in ceramic barrier performance and minimum functional
thickness, this multilayer approach offers a promising path for next generation cladding materials
(e.g., ODS, HEA), as well as existing claddings such as HT-9, for utilization in fast reactors
operating under prolonged, high-temperature service conditions. Meanwhile, we recognize that
more testing, including integral in-reactor irradiation will be needed in the future to demonstrate
performance and longevity of this multilayer approach as a viable FCCI solution.
 Also, demonstration of the ability to deposit this coating uniformly over the inside surface of
steel tubes representative of SFR cladding is also presented. This multilayer approach represents
a transformative step in FCCI barrier technology allowing for additional enhancement in metal
fuel performance in fast reactors.

 2 FCCI BARRIER EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

2.1 FCCI barrier coating preparation

 Y2O3 coatings were fabricated using two different approaches. In Case (1), magnetron
sputtering was performed using a three-inch diameter (Y2O3 target (PVD model: Nano 36, Kurt J.
Lesker) under RF plasma in an O₂-diluted Ar atmosphere. The process employed a base pressure
of ~10⁻³ Torr, 3 sccm Ar flow, and ~140 W power (~20 W/in²) applied to the target, yielding a
deposition rate of ~9 nm/min, calibrated via SEM cross-section analysis on Si wafers. HT-9 steel
discs (1 cm diameter, 0.5 mm thick) served as substrates, resulting in a dense, dark bluish Y2O3
coating. In Case (2), metallic Y was first deposited via RF sputtering under similar conditions and
subsequently thermally oxidized at 700 °C for 2 hours to form a stoichiometric Y2O3 layer,
yielding a nearly transparent coating with a slight greenish hue. The oxidation regime was selected
based on established oxidation kinetics of Y metal and O₂ diffusion through the developing Y2O3
layer, as discussed in references [29,30]. Cr–Y alloy interlayers were fabricated via co-sputtering
of Cr and Y targets, using DC power for Cr and RF power for Y. The base pressure (~10⁻³ Torr)
and Ar flow (3 sccm) remained consistent with the ceramic deposition conditions. Two metal
compositions were produced:
•      Composition A: >50 at.% Y and <50 at.% Cr, using 180 W (~25 W/in²) for Y and 150 W
       (~21 W/in²) for Cr.
• Composition B: >95 at.% Cr and <5 at.% Y, using 200 W (~21 W/in²) for Cr and 100 W
       (~14 W/in²) for Y.
These compositions were selected to evaluate the influence of Y incorporation into the Cr matrix

2.2 Thermal Cycling Studies

 Multiple thicknesses of single layer Y2O3 coatings, developed through direct sputtering and
thermal oxidation of metallic Y, and later Y2O3-CrY multilayers were deposited on polished HT-
9 discs and thermally tested at 750 °C via thermal cycling (TC) under high vacuum conditions.
The purpose of this testing was to evaluate the coatings' robustness against thermal stresses that
may arise from brief high-temperature exposures during reactor off normal conditions. A total of
10 thermal cycles were performed, each ranging from room temperature to 750 °C and back, with
each cycle lasting 10 hours.

2.3 High energy ion implantation

 The Y2O3–CrY multilayer barrier, deposited on HT-9 discs and capped with a ~1.0 μm Ce
layer, was irradiated using a 32.5 MeV Fe ion beam at the ATLAS Material Irradiation Station
(AMIS), located at the ATLAS accelerator facility at Argonne [32]. The coated sample was

 11

mounted horizontally on a copper block with high-temperature silver paste, ensuring the coated
surface faced the incident beam. Irradiation was performed at ~650 °C, representative of in-reactor
conditions, achieved via beam heating and supplemented with a PID-controlled cartridge heater
(Heat Wave Labs) to ensure uniform temperature distribution (Figure 1a, b).
The Fe ion beam exhibited an approximately 2D Gaussian profile (σ ≈ 2.1 mm) and was
maintained at a beam current of ~36 particle nA for ~72 hours, resulting in a peak fluence of
~5.59 × 10¹⁷ ions/cm². SRIM simulations (Figure 1c) predict that peak damage (~300 dpa)
occurred at ~5.5 μm depth, just before the Fe deposition maximum within the HT-9 substrate. The
inclusion of a Ce capping layer allowed assessment of fission product interactions with Y₂O₃ under
irradiation. Fe ions were selected due to their relevance as a primary constituent of steel cladding.
Irradiation temperature was monitored via two K-type thermocouples placed several millimeters
from the sample surface.

2.4 Mechanical Properties

 A micro-indentation technique was employed to evaluate the mechanical resilience of the
developed FCCI barrier coatings, providing insights into their structural integrity under extreme
mechanical loading conditions anticipated during reactor operation or off-normal scenarios.
Indentation testing was conducted using a Struers Vickers micro-indentation device, applying
loads ranging from 0.01 Hv to 0.2 Hv on the coating surface with a 10-second dwell time, aiding
in determining optimized ceramic layer thicknesses for enhanced mechanical stability. These

 12

evaluations are crucial for understanding the barrier response to stresses arising from internal gas
pressure buildup in the fuel pin plenum region and direct mechanical contact with the swelling
metallic fuel, especially at high burnups where fuel cladding mechanical interaction (FCMI) is
effective. Additionally, the collected indentation data served to assess the mechanical effects
introduced by varying CrY alloy compositions on adjacent Y2O3 layers, as well as to examine how
high-temperature irradiation impacts the overall structural integrity of the multilayer design.

2.5 Diffusion couple studies

 To investigate chemical interactions between fission products and steel, a diffusion couple
test was performed using a misch metal disc in contact with HT-9 steel. The misch metal, procured
from American Elements, primarily contains Ce (~45 at.%), La (~26 at.%), with minor fractions
of Pr and Nd [33]. Note: The present diffusion screening is Ce-weighted and therefore
conservative. Variations in La, Nd, and Sm fractions may shift thermodynamic activity and
diffusivity margins. Future studies will evaluate fission-product alloys formulated to more closely
match reactor-generated compositions. Both misch metal and HT-9 discs were fabricated with a
10 mm outer diameter and ~0.5 mm thickness. The contact surfaces were mirror-polished,
beginning with SiC grinding and followed by sequential polishing using 9 μm, 3 μm, and 1 μm
polycrystalline diamond suspensions. The diffusion couple was assembled as shown in Figure 2
(a, d), comprising two graphite discs, one coated or uncoated HT-9 disc, one polished misch metal
disc, two steel gaskets, and Swagelok® VCR® male/female fittings. Graphite and steel gaskets
ensured uniform pressure across contact surfaces while isolating the misch metal from surrounding
steel hardware. The graphite also plays a role as a compliant thermal spreader within the sealed
fixture to ensure uniform contact across the couple. Lanthanides can react vigorously with carbon
at high temperatures, particularly in their molten state, but in these experiments the misch metal
remained solid (650 °C base with occasional excursions to 750 °C, i.e., below the ~800–1000 °C
melting range for this Ce-rich alloy) and did not observe evidence of severe reaction at the misch-
metal/graphite interface. The entire assembly was enclosed within 316 stainless steel Swagelok®
VCR® fittings (Figure 2b). To prevent metallurgical bonding between the VCR, plug and cap
during high-temperature exposure (>600 °C), both components were coated with a 100 nm Al₂O₃
layer.
The assembled diffusion couples were initially tightened to a finger-tight state, followed by an
additional ~¼ turn using adjustable wrenches to ensure sufficient contact without overloading the
HT-9/misch metal interface. The Swagelok fittings were then placed inside a custom-designed
graphite crucible (Figure 2 c) to promote uniform heating. The crucible was loaded into a
cylindrical furnace under a clean inert atmosphere. Diffusion tests were conducted at 650 °C, with
some trials including brief thermal excursions up to 750 °C. Exposure durations ranged from 1 to
14 days. Upon completion, the assemblies were removed from the furnace, air-cooled, and
disassembled for post-exposure characterization (Figure 2 e).

entire “stack” is handled as an integrated laminate. Layer‐by‐layer moduli differences, thickness

3D simulations, provided each sublayer remains elastic under the loads considered. Furthermore,
because the HT-9 substrate is much thicker and expected to be stiffer than the thin coating, it is

 often treated as a rigid or semi‐infinite foundation rather than an elastic layer within the laminate.
 This assumption negates the need for substrate thickness or properties in the lamination
 calculations, since its deformation under the relatively small indentation load is negligible. Thus,
 the base material details are not required in the laminate stack: the laminate approach only models
 the coating layers themselves, while the HT-9 acts essentially as an immovable boundary at the
 coating–substrate interface. This simplification is both standard and appropriate in cases where
 the substrate’s flexure or strain is minimal compared to that of the coating layers. Meanwhile, we
 acknowledge that sliding tests are essential to capture frictional shear and wear as fuel–cladding
 contact in service involves sliding and friction. We intend to perform tribometry and nano-scratch
 in future work over this developed FCCI barrier design as follow-on work to quantify friction
 coefficients, wear rates, and transfer layers. In the present manuscript, indentation results are used
 only to screen mechanical robustness and interfacial cohesion of multilayer architecture
 (particularly focused on the metal layer composition effect), which complement (rather than
 substitutes for) future sliding studies.

3.2 Single layer Y2O3 coating performances as a potential FCCI barrier.

 3.2.1 High temperature thermal cycling performance at 750 °C
 Single-layer Y₂O₃ coatings were fabricated using two distinct manufacturing approaches
on HT-9 alloy discs: (a) direct sputter coating and (b) controlled thermal oxidation of a metallic
yttrium layer. Detailed information about the process parameters is provided in the experimental
section (2.1). Coating thicknesses selected for this study were (a) 100 nm, (b) 200 nm, (c) 500 nm,
and (d) 1000 nm, based on established ceramic coating examples reported in the literature [17].
For the Y2O3 layer produced via direct sputtering, achieving a 1000 nm film proved challenging
due to spallation during heat treatments aimed at confirming film stability. Thus, the maximum
tested thickness using this method was limited to 500 nm. In contrast, coatings created by thermal
oxidation demonstrated superior adhesion to the HT-9 substrate, though actual thickness slightly
exceeded the intended targets due to volume expansion from yttrium oxidation. Specifically,
thinner coatings exceeded nominal thickness by a few tens of nanometers, while the 1000 nm
coatings exceeded their target by more than 200 nm. These coated discs underwent thermal cycling
at 750 °C (details in section 2.2) to assess their thermal stability. Results indicated that the 500 nm
direct sputter-coated Y2O3 layer experienced significant surface cracking and complete
delamination, as illustrated in Figure 3(a), whereas coatings of other thicknesses remained intact.
Figure 1(b) presents a representative surface of the approximately 1000 nm thermally generated
Y2O3 coating, showing good adherence and absence of surface cracks. Consequently, achieving a
stable thermomechanical, several hundred nanometer-thick Y2O3 coating via direct sputtering
remains challenging.

ceramic layer alone may not offer sufficient protection for extended service or under severe

transients. This underscores the potential of multilayer barrier architectures to ensure long-term
FCCI resistance in advanced reactor cladding applications. The failure of all thickness ranges for
this Y2O3 single-layer preparation method can be attributed to several factors:
 (a) the difficulty in achieving strong adhesion between the ceramic and metal substrate
 using sputter coating particularly with Y2O3, which is chemically inert to most metals
 often results in interface delamination even under relatively low mechanical stress. The
 lower thickness of the surviving Y2O3 coating, coupled with a weak interface bond,
 makes the layer highly susceptible to fracture and prone to developing through-
 thickness cracks.
 (b) c challenges in producing a uniform and stoichiometric Y2O3 layer via plasma
 sputtering, as maintaining the correct oxygen partial pressure and plasma composition
 is difficult. Non-stoichiometric coatings typically exhibit poor diffusion barrier
 performance due to the formation of lattice defects that allow diffusing species to
 bypass the barrier.[41];
 (c) Despite its overall thermo-mechanical stability, the thermally grown Y2O3 layer
 exhibited multiple Ce penetration sites. STEM–TEM analysis of the diffusion cross-
 section (Figure 5) revealed localized zones at the Y–Fe interface with significantly
 deeper interaction, forming dome-shaped regions, highlighted by the yellow split line
 and confirmed by Fe EDS maps. These features are consistent with intermetallic phase
 formation, as suggested by the Y–Fe phase diagram [42]. Such dome-shaped interfacial
 regions likely induce localized stress concentrations, which can serve as crack initiation
 sites under mechanical or thermal loading. While the polycrystalline Y₂O₃ structure can
 resist crack propagation through grain boundary deflection, repeated thermal cycling
 may facilitate crack growth. These microstructural defects appear to have allowed solid
 fission products to penetrate the barrier, contributing to the non-uniform and
 disproportionate FCCI observed in specific regions.
Consequently, a single layer of Y2O3, whether prepared by sputtering or thermal oxidation, is
inadequate for long-term performance, necessitating a more robust barrier architecture to achieve
effective FCCI protection.

3.3 Multilayer metal-ceramic architecture approach

 To enhance barrier performance and mitigate micro-fracture formation—a known failure
mode in thermally grown Y2O3 coatings, a multilayer architecture incorporating alternating
ceramic and metallic layers was developed. The metallic interlayers serve to absorb thermal stress
generated during high-temperature operation or cycling, thereby arresting crack propagation. This
configuration also increases the total diffusion path length for fission products, improving long-
term resistance under elevated reactor conditions.

interactions with Ce, confirming formation of stoichiometric oxide layers. (c) STEM image of the

 23

 Similar metal–ceramic architectures have been successfully employed in thermal barrier
coatings for turbine blades [43] and in our group’s prior work on high-temperature hydrogen and
oxidation barriers for nuclear applications [44,45]. The multilayer design consists of three Y2O3
layers separated by two Cr–Y alloy interlayers. This tri-ceramic structure introduces redundancy,
ensuring continued protection even if one ceramic layer is compromised. The use of Cr–Y alloys
instead of pure Cr addresses the challenge of poor interfacial bonding with Y2O3—an inherently
inert ceramic. Yttrium’s high oxygen affinity promotes chemical interaction with Y₂O₃, improving
adhesion and interfacial toughness. To evaluate the effect of Y content, two alloy compositions
were selected: Cr₅₀Y₅₀ (lower interlayer) and Cr₉₅Y₅ (upper interlayer). This approach allows
investigation into the influence of Y concentration on bonding, ceramic stability, and potential
phase segregation within the metal during deposition or thermal exposure (~650 °C). To maintain
comparable overall Cr content, the Cr₅₀Y₅₀ layer was deposited thicker, ensuring a chromium-rich
phase persists even with interdiffusion. The microstructure of these metal layers plays a crucial
role in mechanical integrity, particularly in impeding crack propagation between ceramic layers
under extreme thermal or mechanical stress. To detect thermally activated micro- or nano-cracks,
a thin cerium (Ce) layer was deposited atop the Y2O3 surface, followed by a 100 nm TiN capping
layer to inhibit Ce oxidation. The complete stack was subjected to thermal cycling at 750 °C,
following the same protocol applied to single-layer coatings. The results of thermal, irradiation,
diffusion, and mechanical tests are presented in the following sections to support the performance
optimization of this multilayer FCCI barrier.
 Figures 6(a, b) show FIB-SEM/EDS cross-sections of the tri-layer structure, confirming
enhanced mechanical stability compared to single-layer coatings. Notably, no fractures or
interfacial delamination were observed; the CrY interlayers exhibited strong bonding with adjacent
Y2O3 layers, validating the design rationale. To investigate microstructure, phase stability, and
potential interdiffusion, STEM and TEM analyses were conducted. Figure 6(c) presents a cross-
sectional STEM montage of the entire multilayer, identifying four key interfacial zones: (A) Ce–
Y2O3, (B) Cr₉₅Y₅– Y2O3, (C) Cr₅₀Y₅₀– Y2O3, and (D) Y2O3–Fe. Contrast in the STEM images,
reflective of mass density variations, reveals minimal interaction in Zone A, with Ce diffusion
confined to a few tens of nanometers and no evidence of grain boundary diffusion, indicating a
dense, high-quality Y₂O₃ microstructure. In Zones B and C, localized reactions between excess Y
and adjacent Y₂O₃ layers are observed, as expected. All metal–ceramic interfaces remain intact
and well-bonded, with no signs of cracking or separation. At the Fe– Y2O3 interface (Zone D), two
distinct interactions were observed: (1) Fe diffusion into Y2O3, likely occurring during thermal
oxidation of the Y-rich layer, and (2) non-uniform interface features attributed to localized Fe–Y
interactions. Figure 6(d) shows a bright-field TEM image from the central multilayer region
(highlighted in Figure 6(c)), capturing Cr₉₅Y₅, Y2O3, and Cr₅₀Y₅₀ layers. SADPs collected from
Areas 1–4 (circled) reveal that the Cr-rich layers maintain a BCC polycrystalline structure, with
minor Y segregation to the grain boundaries—further promoting chemical bonding with adjacent
Y₂O₃. The diffraction pattern from the Cr50–Y50 region (Area 2) displays a mixture of Y (hexagonal
crystal structure) [46] and body centered cubic (BCC) Cr reflections [47], confirming a

 24

predominantly nanocrystalline microstructure and elemental segregation within the Y-rich layer.
Similar to Cr, Y elemental segregation has been observed elsewhere too [24]. In Area 3, the SADP
reveals cubic polycrystalline Y2O3 [48] with grain sizes ranging from approximately 200 nm to
1.0 μm, while Area 4 shows a sharp interface between cubic Y2O3 and HT-9. Collectively, the
STEM and TEM analyses demonstrate that the multilayer system has developed a complex
microstructure, with all layers achieving crystalline order. These observations indicate that the
various metal–ceramic interfaces not only formed but also withstood extensive high-temperature
thermal cycling at 750 °C. Moreover, the metal–ceramic multilayer architecture successfully
endured the thermal stress, with both CrY alloy compositions providing mechanical support to
their neighboring Y₂O₃ layers while maintaining strong interfacial adhesion.
 3.3.1 Multilayer response to high temperature radiation damage simulated with high energy
 ion implantation studies.
 In addition to thermal stability, FCCI barrier coatings must withstand significant radiation
damage over their service life to effectively protect steel cladding from fuel and fission product
interactions. Positioned near the fuel surface, these coatings are exposed to high damage rates—
typically 300–400 dpa after just 5 at% burnup—due to fission product recoil and proximity to the
fuel. Assessing the radiation tolerance of new multilayer architectures under high-temperature
conditions is therefore essential. As a first step in material screening and optimization, ion
irradiation is employed as a surrogate for neutron and fission product-induced damage (see Section
2.3). This method offers a cost-effective, accelerated, and controlled alternative to neutron
irradiation, allowing replication of displacement damage and defect evolution using selected ion
species. It enables rapid evaluation of microstructural stability, phase evolution, and overall
coating performance under reactor-relevant conditions.
 Before estimating the displacement per atom (dpa) generated within the Y2O3-CrY structure,
using SRIM calculations, the approximate threshold displacement energy (TDE) for the Y₂O₃ and
CrY systems needs to be determined. This evaluation is a key part of the overall dpa calculations,
since the displacement process only produces a stable atomic displacement if the incident energy
exceeds the threshold. At finite temperatures, thermal fluctuations in atomic positions and
velocities can significantly affect the recombination probability. Experimental studies [49–54] and
analytical models have demonstrated that the TDE in several metals and ceramics gradually
decreases with increasing temperature. This behavior is attributed to a combination of reduced
elastic stiffness, additional kinetic energy contributions from lattice vibrations to the primary
knock-on atom (PKA), and a reduction in the spontaneous recombination distance due to these
vibrations. Therefore following Stoller et al.'s guidance [55], the displacement energy for
individual elements Ce, Cr, Y, O, and Fe are the following; EdCe = 45 eV [56], EdCr = 43 eV [57],
EdY = 65 eV [58], EdO = 15 eV [59], and EdFe = 40 eV [60] estimated at room temperature or near
273K are not appropriate to be used for this study. Based on available examples for ceramic and
metal alloy systems, the average decrease in Ed values from room temperature to approximately
900 K typically ranges from 20% to as much as 50% in ceramic oxides (such as MgO and Al 2O3

 25

[51,53]) and around 30–40% in BCC and FCC crystalline metals [54] . Therefore, as a simple
approximation, we have assumed a ~30% reduction in the Ed values for the respective elements in
this multilayer design when irradiated at ~920 K. The adjusted Ed values used for estimating the
vacancies generated per ion interaction with the coating are: EdCe = 31 eV, EdCr = 30 eV, EdY =
41 eV, EdO = 11 eV, and EdFe = 28 eV. Although molecular dynamics (MD) simulations would
have been ideal, the generated data provides sufficient support for the values used, as later
validated with phase change of Y2O3. The densities for each material phases used for the
calculations are the following: Y2O3: 5.01 g/cc [61], Cr50Y50: 5.19 g/cc (estimated using rule of
mixture for the alloy system, ρ = Mavg/Vm, avg., where Mavg is the average atomic weight and Vm, avg
is the average molar volume.), Cr95Y5: 6.84 g/cc , and finally HT-9: 7.86 g/cc [62]. Based on these
parameters it was found from SRIM estimations that close to 300 dpa damage is achieved within
the multilayer structure, where an ion dose in the order of ~5E17 ions/cm2 is required. Additionally,
in the max flux zone, the lowest damage achieved towards the top layers of the multilayer coating
amounted to ~30-50 dpa, sufficient to evaluate radiation-enhanced diffusion occurrence (if any) at
the Ce-Y2O3 interface.

 3.3.2 Micromechanical response of the multilayer coatings before and after radiation with
 micro-indentation studies.
 In sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs), the stainless-steel cladding onto which the FCCI
barrier is applied—typically forms a thermal bond with the inserted metallic fuel rod (slug) via
liquid sodium [67]. This sodium fills the annular gap between the fuel and cladding, which closes
at approximately 1–2% burnup, initiating direct contact between the metallic fuel and the cladding.
Modern fuel designs intentionally set this initial gap to allow interconnected porosity within the
fuel to form at the time of contact, enabling the release of fission gases to a plenum located above
the fuel slug. This mechanism alleviates mechanical loading at the cladding–fuel interface,
leaving hoop stresses arising from gas pressure accumulation as the primary mechanical concern.
 However, at higher burnups (>10 at%), porosity within the fuel is reduced due to solid
fission product buildup, increasing mechanical contact between the fuel and cladding. This gives
rise to Fuel Cladding Mechanical Interaction (FCMI) [68], where radial hydrostatic stresses
exceeding 130 MPa may develop within the cladding, potentially leading to its failure [69–71].
To assess the mechanical robustness of the developed multilayer FCCI barrier under such stress
conditions, micro-indentation testing was employed before and after ion irradiation. Unlike nano-
indentation which probes only surface properties micro-indentation applies higher loads (0.25–
0.5 N), generating stress fields that penetrate the entire multilayer thickness. This simulates
through-thickness loading conditions analogous to those at the cladding–fuel interface, capturing
realistic failure modes such as interfacial cracking and delamination.
 A tri-layer Y2O3 and dual-layer CrY multilayer stack was used, with Cr₅₀Y₅₀ and Cr₉₅Y₅
interlayers. To maintain fidelity with irradiated samples, a 1.5 μm Ce layer and 100 nm TiN
capping layer were added atop the outer Y2O3 surface. Mechanical properties of each sublayer
were incorporated into classical laminate stress models (Table 1), using rule-of-mixtures for CrY
alloys. Y₂O₃ properties were adjusted to reflect the cubic (0 dpa) and monoclinic (irradiated)
phases, with thickness variations accounted for via STEM/TEM cross-sectional imaging (Figures
7).

 4 OPTIMIZATION OF THE FCCI BARRIER ARCHITECTURE
 The multifaceted results presented above from single-layer Y2O3 coating failures to the
improved performance of our multilayer Y2O3/CrY architectures under thermal cycling,
mechanical loads and high-dose irradiation, offers a valuable foundation for understanding and
optimizing FCCI barrier strategies. In the following sections, we critically examine how the
interplay between ceramic phase stability, interfacial adhesion, and radiation-enhanced
mechanical properties drives the robust behavior of these multilayer systems. .

4.1 Yttrium content in the Cr-Y alloy layers.

 4.1.1 Y role in increasing metal-ceramic layer adhesion

 While pure chromium may appear ideal as a metal interlayer in ceramic–metal multilayer
structures given the low solubility between Cr and Y up to ~1200 °C (Cr–Y phase diagram [76]),
its chemical inertness toward Y2O3 poses a limitation. Based on the Ellingham diagram, yttrium
has a significantly higher oxygen affinity than chromium, forming more stable Y–O bonds. This
disparity inhibits the formation of strong Cr–O bonds at the interface, since Cr must rely on limited
free oxygen within the Y2O3 lattice, resulting in weak or inconsistent adhesion. In contrast,
alloying Cr with Y promotes the formation of Y-rich phases that chemically bond with Y₂O₃
boundaries, facilitating robust Y–O interfacial linkages [77]. These Y-containing phases also retain
strong metallic bonding with chromium, thereby improving overall interface cohesion. The dual
benefit of strong Cr–Y and Y–O interactions lead to a chemically and mechanically stable interface
that is resistant to delamination or spallation.
 Experimental observations show no significant difference in adhesion performance
between Cr₉₅Y₀₅ and Cr₅₀Y₅₀ alloys when paired with Y2O3; both compositions formed equally
robust interfaces. This suggests that within the investigated compositional range, yttrium
concentration does not adversely impact interfacial strength. Therefore, Cr–Y alloys offer a
superior alternative to pure chromium for achieving durable, high-temperature adhesion to Y2O3,
making them especially suited for FCCI barrier coatings in fast reactor environments.
 4.1.2 Yttrium role in metal layer resistance against fracture propagation

 Before analyzing the individual behavior of the Cr₅₀Y₅₀ and Cr₉₅Y₀₅ layers, it is important to
first understand the stress states induced within the multilayer structure during Vickers indentation,
as these dictate the resulting fracture modes. Figure 10(a) schematically illustrates the stress
distribution in a multilayer coating under indentation. The indenter applies a vertical compressive
load, inducing bending (elastic and plastic) in the multilayer. This causes the upper and lower
ceramic–metal interfaces to experience lateral shear forces, while tensile stresses develop
particularly in the lower Y2O3 layer due to bending-induced outward stretching. Meanwhile, the
central metal layers (Cr₉₅Y₀₅ and Cr₅₀Y₅₀) are subjected to compressive forces at their upper
boundaries and mild tensile stresses at their lower interfaces beneath the indenter. Fracture

 35

behavior under these conditions varies based on the state of the local stress. In compressive regions,
radial or cone cracks dominate, propagating outward from the indent due to high compressive
stress. In tensile zone, especially below the neutral axis circumferential cracks tend to initiate,
potentially leading to axial crack growth and interfacial delamination, as the tensile forces promote
separation between layers.

 Figure 10(b) presents FIB cross-sections and simplified schematics illustrating fracture

Type 1 (in the 0 dpa condition) and Type 3 (in the max dpa condition). Both are circumferential

entering the Cr₅₀Y₅₀ layer. In several cases, these cracks arrest at the upper Interface 2, while others
follow it before re-penetrating into the metal. These observations suggest that crack resistance is
lower within the Cr₅₀Y₅₀ layer or at its interfaces than within the ceramic, facilitating crack
deflection into the metal. This reduced resistance is attributed to the significant elastic mismatch
between Cr₅₀Y₅₀ and Y2O3, which intensifies interfacial shear stresses—consistent with predictions
from Dundurs’ parameters [78]. The microstructural analysis provided by SAED (as shown in
Figure 3) confirms distinct elemental segregation within the Cr₅₀Y₅₀ layer, specifically revealing
uniformly distributed pure chromium grains surrounded by softer, yttrium-rich phases containing
minor dissolved chromium content. Mechanically, yttrium (modulus ~60.9 GPa [79]) is
significantly softer than chromium (modulus ~248 Gpa [80]), enabling fracture propagation
through Y-rich regions due to their reduced local fracture resistance.
 Fracture propagation typically halts upon reaching Cr-rich regions, which enhances local
fracture resistance either by direct obstruction from hard chromium grains or through localized
plastic deformation within the chromium matrix. This behavior corresponds to Type 1 fractures
observed at low (0 dpa) doses. At higher doses, Type 3 fractures are more prevalent, where cracks
deflect sharply at the upper metal–ceramic interface. This suggests a substantial improvement in
interfacial toughness and/or an increase in the fracture resistance of the adjacent Y2O3 layer. In
such cases, the local strain energy release rate (Gdriving force) at the crack tip is insufficient to exceed
the critical fracture energy (Gc) of the upper interface or ceramic, thus blunting further crack
growth, a desirable mechanical feature of irradiated systems. Conversely, at lower doses, Type 2
fractures dominate, with cracks traversing the entire metal layer into the adjacent Y2O3. This
suggests that, at these doses, defects such as weak CrY–Y₂O₃ bonding or pre-existing nano-cracks
formed during Y oxidation reduce the interfacial fracture resistance. The local (Gdriving force) in these
regions exceeds (Gc) allowing cracks to propagate across multiple layers. In contrast, cracks in the
Cr₉₅Y₀₅ layer behave differently. SEM and schematic analyses confirm that cracks are consistently
deflected at "Interface 3" with no through-thickness fracture, even at maximum irradiation. This
improved resistance is attributed to the near-pure Cr composition, which has a higher elastic
modulus (~248 GPa) compared to cubic (~179 GPa) or monoclinic (~143 GPa) Y2O3. As a result,
the Cr₉₅Y₀₅ layer and its adjacent interfaces possess superior fracture resistance, confining damage
to ceramic sublayers and preventing delamination. This behavior is consistent across both 0 dpa
and max-dose conditions, making Cr₉₅Y₀₅ a superior choice over Cr₅₀Y₅₀ for multilayer barrier
design.
 Overall, these observations highlight the critical role of Cr–Y composition in modulating
fracture behavior and stress accommodation within multilayer ceramic–metal systems. Optimizing
metal interlayers not only enhances mechanical toughness but also ensures long-term durability
under irradiation and thermal stress.

4.2 Enhanced mechanical resilience of the irradiated multilayer architecture.

 The surface hardness of the ion-irradiated multilayer coatings was nearly double that of the
0-dose samples, accompanied by a clear enhancement in resistance to fracture propagation. This
hardness increase is attributed to microstructural modifications induced by ion irradiation (see
Section 3.3). Ion irradiation, which mimics radiation damage from fission fragments or neutrons,
typically induces atomic rearrangements, element migration, and a reduction in the material's free
volume. According to the free volume model [81], hardness (H) increases as free volume decreases
and the activation barrier for defect migration (ΔG) rises. Elastic collisions from incoming ions
displace atoms into energetically favorable configurations, reducing free volume and tightening
atomic spacing. This densification raises the barrier to localized plastic deformation, thereby
increasing hardness [82]. In our multilayer system, these effects are particularly evident in the
Y₂O₃ layers, where irradiation drives a phase transformation from cubic to the denser monoclinic
structure. This transformation leads to volumetric contraction, elevated internal stress, and higher
atomic packing density, collectively contributing to increased hardness and stability. Moreover,
the volumetric change at the ceramic–metal interfaces introduce additional stress states, which if
effectively accommodated by the irradiated, hardened metallic layers can suppress crack initiation
or arrest propagation. For example, as seen near the Cr₅₀Y₅₀ interface (Figure 9(b″)), cracks may
be deflected or blunted due to insufficient local strain energy release rates (G) to sustain
propagation into harder, irradiated regions.
 Thus, radiation-induced atomic rearrangements, free volume reduction, and phase
transformations synergistically enhance the mechanical resilience and fracture toughness of the
multilayer barrier, enabling improved performance under irradiation.

4.3 Y2O3 as an effective FCCI barrier.

 A significant knowledge gap persists regarding the performance of both cubic and
monoclinic Y₂O₃ coatings against solid fission products, which are more reactive and have lower
melting points than uranium across all relevant temperatures. Advanced Y2O3-based FCCI barrier
designs require detailed evaluation of their effectiveness against fission product diffusion under
elevated temperature and irradiation conditions. In this study, cerium (Ce) one of the most
chemically aggressive lanthanide fission products was selected as a surrogate to represent solid
fission product behavior. A Ce layer was deposited via physical vapor deposition (PVD) atop the
multilayer coating architecture to assess: (a) the thermal stability of the coating at elevated
temperatures (650 °C–750 °C) over extended durations, and (b) the barrier’s performance under
concurrent irradiation and high-temperature exposure (650 °C). Figure 11 (a–c) shows the Ce–
Y2O3 interface under three conditions: as-prepared, post-annealing at 650 °C for 72 hours (0 dpa),
and post-irradiation at 650 °C for 72 hours (~30–40 dpa at the interface).
 STEM images reveal the interfacial condition and provide direct insight into interdiffusion
phenomena through contrast variations. In the as-prepared state, the Ce– Y2O3 interface is sharply
defined. After thermal exposure, a limited reaction zone forms with a diffusion width of

 38

approximately 30 ± 5 nm. In contrast, following irradiation, the interaction zone expands to
~150 ± 25 nm, marked by a broader region of interdiffusion delineated by the red dotted lines in
the images. These results indicate that elevated temperature alone causes minor interfacial
reactions, while irradiation significantly accelerates diffusion processes at the Ce– Y2O3 interface.
These findings help resolve prior uncertainties regarding the radiation tolerance and structural
stability of monoclinic Y2O3 under extreme conditions.

was intentionally conservative because, under actual reactor conditions, the coating would not be
exposed to a 100% Ce flux, but rather a mixture of Ce with other, generally less diffusive, fission

ceramic multilayer architecture for enhanced structural integrity The following is a summary of

 5 ZIRCALOY CORROSION BARRIER
5.1 Introduction.

 The safety and reliability of nuclear power plants are critically dependent on the
performance of zirconium (Zr)-based fuel claddings, which serve as the first barrier to fission
product release. Zr alloys such as Zircaloy-2, Zircaloy-4, ZIRLO™, and M5® have long been
favored in light water reactors (LWRs) due to their low neutron absorption cross-section, adequate
mechanical strength, and reasonable corrosion resistance under normal operating conditions
[84,86]. However, their vulnerability becomes evident during off-normal and accident scenarios.
Under loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) conditions, Zr rapidly reacts with high-temperature steam,
forming ZrO₂ and releasing hydrogen. This exothermic process accelerates core temperature rise
and poses risks of hydrogen detonation, as demonstrated during the Fukushima Daiichi accident
in 2011 [86]. In response, the concept of accident-tolerant fuels campaign (ATFs) was introduced,
focusing on claddings that can withstand higher temperatures, reduce hydrogen generation, and
extend coping time during transients [85, 86].
 Two broad ATF strategies exist: (a) entirely new cladding systems (FeCrAl alloys, SiC
composites, MAX phases, multilayer Mo), and (b) protective surface coatings on conventional Zr
alloys. The latter represents a near-term, cost-effective solution compatible with existing fuel
assembly designs, making it particularly attractive for boiling water reactors (BWRs) [85, 86]. A
wide range of coatings has been evaluated, including metallic, ceramic, and multilayer systems.
Ion implantation of light elements (N, O, C, Al) produced shallow hardened layers but failed at
high temperature [85]. Carbon- and silicon-based coatings improved oxidation resistance modestly,
though porosity, hydration, and poor adhesion limited durability [85].
 Among metallic candidates, Cr has emerged as the most promising, forming dense Cr₂O₃
scales that reduce hydrogen pickup and extend steam oxidation resistance to ~1200 °C [85–89].
Yet, Cr₂O₃ is unstable in oxygenated BWR coolant: at high electrochemical potential (ECP) it
dissolves oxidatively to chromates, producing porous nanocrystalline films and spallation [90–96].
FeCrAl and related alloys offer high-temperature stability but suffer eutectic degradation with Zr
above ~900 °C and hydroxide dissolution issues [86]. Ceramic coatings (Al₂O₃, ZrO₂, SiC, TiN,
CrN) have also been investigated [85, 86]; however, Al₂O₃ hydrates to boehmite, Y₂O₃ converts to
Y(OH)₃, and SiC films are prone to cracking and poor adhesion under thermal cycling [85, 85].
To rationalize these behaviors, Pourbaix diagrams (E–pH stability maps) have been employed. At
BWR temperatures (288–360 °C) and elevated dissolved oxygen (>100–300 ppb), protective oxide
stability fields shrink, while hydroxo complexes expand, explaining the poor persistence of single
oxides. Under hydrogenated chemistry (low ECP, as in PWRs), protective fields enlarge,
accounting for the improved durability of Cr₂O₃ and Al₂O₃ in PWR autoclave tests [96–101].
Elevated-temperature Pourbaix studies for Cr, Al, and Ti consistently confirm strong
thermodynamic drives toward hydroxide formation under oxidizing conditions [96-98].

 51

 Mixed or complex oxides provide a promising pathway to address these shortcomings. By
combining multiple cations, they distribute bonding environments and reduce the free energy of
hydration relative to binary oxides [87-90]. Y–Al complex oxides (e.g., YAlO₃ perovskite,
Y₂Al₂O₅ garnet) are especially attractive, as they hydrate less readily than Al₂O₃ or Y₂O₃ alone,
while offering higher lattice rigidity and diffusion resistance. Unlike Ti–Al or Cr–Al oxides, which
inherit the instability of TiO₂ and Cr₂O₃, Y–Al phases are chemically inert to radiolytic oxidants
in BWR coolant and suppress oxygen/hydrogen transport [87-90]. Their high melting points and
sluggish diffusion kinetics further enhance barrier performance. However, direct in situ formation
in BWR water is unreliable: Y₂O₃ and Al₂O₃ form first and hydrate rapidly [96-98]. Therefore, Y–
Al complex oxides must be deposited directly or developed by controlled high-temperature
oxidation [88, 89]. Additional challenge is to determine the stability of the prepared coating over
the Zirc-4 substrate due to thermo-mechanical mismatch between brittle Y–Al oxides (CTE ~7–
8.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹) and Zircaloy-4 (~6 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹) generates stresses up to 250–350 MPa under reactor
transients, risking cracking or delamination. So, we have implemented a multilayer architecture to
address this by introducing a compliant metallic Cr interlayer with a CTE close to Zircaloy-4,
capable of forming self-healing Cr₂O₃ in steam and arresting cracks, while the Y–Al oxide outer
layer resists hydroxylation.
 Through this report, we describe the preparation of Cr–Y and Cr–Y–Al multilayers to
probe how compositional complexity and layered architectures affect oxidation resistance,
adhesion, and phase stability under dry heat and humid heat. These early studies provide insight
into multilayer strategies that couple chemical robustness with mechanical compliance, offering a
pathway toward reliable ATF cladding protection in BWRs.

5.2 Materials and Barrier coating Architecture evolution.

 5.2.1 Identification of stable material in BWR chemistry (potential advantages of utilizing
 mixed or complex Oxide forms):

 Mixed oxides represent a rational strategy for improving coating performance in aggressive
reactor environments because they combine multiple cation chemistries within a single lattice,
which can reduce the overall free energy of hydration and dissolution relative to simpler binary
oxides [96,105-107]. By distributing bonding environments and oxygen sublattice connectivity
across different cation sites, mixed oxides often exhibit greater thermodynamic stability against
hydroxylation consistent with high-temperature Their structural complexity can also slow cation
diffusion and ionic transport, providing an intrinsic barrier to the penetration of oxidizing species
and hydrogen and thereby suppressing dissolution/reprecipitation cycles that degrade film
continuity in hot water [106, 107]. In practice, this means that a well-designed mixed-oxide coating
can persist longer in oxygenated, high-temperature aqueous environments, maintaining protective
continuity where single-oxide films tend to hydrate, dissolve, or spall [96,97,106,107]. The
approach thus reflects a broader design principle: leveraging multi-component oxide chemistry to

 52

achieve a balance of thermodynamic robustness, mechanical resilience, and diffusion resistance
that single oxides cannot provide on their own [103-105].
 We have identified Y-Al mixed oxides (such as YAlO₃ (perovskite) and Y₂Al₂O₅ (garnet-
like aluminates)) as the potential barrier material over other complex oxides primarily it offers
distinct advantages over other aluminates such as Ti–Al or Cr–Al systems when considered for
long-term stability in BWR water. This arises from several factors, (a) their thermodynamic drive
for hydroxylation is weaker: Y-Al perovskites hydrate less readily than Al₂O₃ or Y₂O₃ alone, while
Ti–Al and Cr–Al systems inherit the strong hydroxo-instability of TiO₂ and the oxidative
dissolution vulnerability of Cr₂O₃ based on the enthalpy estimates (discussed later) [96, 97, 105],
see Figure 19 for YAlO₃ case and figure 5 for both Y₂Al₂O₅ and YAlO₃. The estimated Pourbaix
diagram (Figure 19) for YAlO₃–H₂O at ~300 °C highlights a broader and more stable
electrochemical window for the persistence of the crystalline perovskite oxide compared to typical
binary oxides such as TiO₂, Cr₂O₃, Al₂O₃, and SiO₂. In Ti–H₂O systems, TiO₂ stability is
constrained at elevated potentials where hydrolysis to Ti(OH)4 dominates, resulting in dissolution
and film destabilization under oxygenated BWR conditions. Cr₂O₃ similarly shows only a narrow
passivation domain, readily oxidizing to soluble Cr(VI) species (e.g., HCrO₄⁻) at high
electrochemical potentials, which explains the spallation and porosity observed in autoclave
studies. Al₂O₃, while intrinsically stable at low temperatures, transitions toward boehmite (AlOOH)
and Al(OH)₃ under hot aqueous environments, severely compromising durability. SiO₂ exhibits
strong thermodynamic stability across a broad pH range but is prone to irradiation-assisted
dissolution and poor adhesion when interfaced with Zr-based claddings, limiting its mechanical
reliability. By contrast, YAlO₃ occupies a wider stability envelope in the estimated Pourbaix map:
the reducing boundary lies well below neutral potentials, while the hydroxylation boundary shifts
upward with pH, indicating reduced driving force for hydration compared to Y₂O₃ or Al₂O₃ alone.
Thus, relative to binary oxides, YAlO₃ offers enhanced chemical persistence in oxygenated, high-
temperature aqueous environments, making it a superior candidate for protective multilayer
coatings in BWR conditions.

redox equilibria. The hydroxylation boundary marks the potential above which hydrated species
(e.g., Y(OH)₃, Al(OH)₃) are thermodynamically favored; both oxides display increasingly positive
boundary potentials with rising pH, reflecting stronger hydroxide stability in alkaline conditions.
Overall, the Y₂Al₂O₅ curves lie slightly above those of YAlO₃, indicating marginally greater
resistance to both reduction and hydroxylation under identical conditions, while the close spacing
of all four curves suggests comparable aqueous stability envelopes for the two mixed oxides.

 54

  Figure 20: Estimated Pourbaix-Type Stability Boundaries for YAlO₃ and Y₂Al₂O₅ at 300 °C

(b) the structural rigidity of Y–Al perovskites (corner-sharing AlO₆ octahedra with Y–O
polyhedral) provides higher lattice stability and stronger resistance to crack propagation than the
more defect-prone Ti–Al spinels or Cr–Al solid solutions [105, 106], (c) Y–Al phases are expected
to be more chemically inert to radiolytic oxidants in high-ECP water, whereas Ti–Al and Cr–Al
systems can generate soluble Ti(OH)₄ or chromates under oxygenated conditions, undermining
barrier persistence [106-107]. Finally, the high melting points and sluggish diffusion
characteristics of Y–Al complex oxides further suppress interfacial transport and promote long-
term barrier continuity [105, 106]. Collectively, these factors make Y–Al oxides uniquely suited
as coolant-facing layers in multilayer coatings, whereas Ti–Al and Cr–Al compounds struggle with
aqueous stability under BWR operating conditions.
 5.2.2 Motivation for a metal/ceramic multilayer approach:

 Directly developing a dense Y–Al mixed oxide (e.g., Y–Al perovskite/aluminate) on
Zircaloy-4 addresses durability in BWR coolant, but it introduces a thermo-mechanical mismatch
that a single ceramic layer cannot reliably tolerate over reactor-relevant thermal excursions. The
central issue is coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch and the associated biaxial
thermal stress in a thin ceramic film bonded to a much thicker metallic substrate. Zircaloy-4 has a
coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of ~5.5–6.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ [108], while Y–Al mixed oxides

 55

typically expand at ~7.0–8.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ [109]. This CTE gap of ~2 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ translates to a strain
mismatch of ≈0.1% over a 500–600 K thermal swing, generating stresses on the order of 250–350
MPa in the oxide film. Such stress magnitudes are sufficient to induce microcracking, edge
spallation, or interfacial delamination, especially under cyclic thermal loading. Therefore,
introducing a compliant metal layer along with the ceramic layer should help distribute the thermal
and deposition related residual stresses.
 Therefore, we have developed a Cr-YAlO₃ based multilayer system strategically combines
the strengths of metallic Cr and Y-based perovskite ceramics while leveraging the architectural
reliability of multilayers to overcome the weaknesses observed in single-layer coatings. A thin Cr
layer will be ductile where its CTE is close to Zircaloy-4 and high toughness sits between substrate
and ceramic. It should be able to redistribute the thermal strain, blunts cracks, and greatly increase
work of adhesion. In practice, this can drop the effective peak tensile stress in the ceramic by a
large fraction and convert through-thickness cracks into arrested, deflected microcracks. In
addition, layered interfaces act as hydrogen/oxygen diffusion baffles and crack arresters. If the
outer Y–Al oxide locally hydrates or defects under extreme conditions, the inner metallic (Cr) and
any intermediate sub-oxide/Cr-aluminate layers preserve barrier continuity, preventing a one-
defect-to-failure cascade before the next Y-Al oxide layer is exposed. Therefore, this architecture
couple’s chemical durability with mechanical compliance, delivering reliability that a single
ceramic layer cannot achieve on Zircaloy-4.

5.3 Experimental Details.

 5.3.1 Fabrication of multilayer BWR barrier architecture:

 To systematically evaluate the thermo-mechanical and aqueous stability of yttrium- and
yttrium–aluminate–containing multilayers, two distinct coating architectures were fabricated on
Zircaloy-4 substrates, see Figure 21 (a,b). These designs were selected to provide baseline
validation of Pourbaix-predicted stability trends and to assess the effectiveness of complex oxide
phases under high temperature thermal exposures in BWR-LOCA relevant environments.

 Case A (Y/Cr–Y multilayers, baseline design): In this design, alternating Y and Cr–Y
layers were deposited by magnetron sputtering. The multilayer structure was chosen to serve as a
baseline system, aimed at understanding the combined effects of Y incorporation into metallic Cr
and the role of repeated interfaces in mitigating thermal stresses and limiting oxygen/hydrogen
diffusion. Following the multilayer FCCI design principles, each Y layer acts as a reservoir that
can oxidize to Y₂O₃ under exposure, while the Cr-rich layers contribute ductility, adhesion, and
the formation of self-healing Cr₂O₃ in steam. The Case A coatings thus provide a platform to test
the thermodynamic predictions derived from extended Pourbaix diagrams, specifically the
tendency of Y₂O₃ and Cr₂O₃ toward hydroxylation under oxygenated water, and to validate
whether a multilayer arrangement can delay spallation and maintain film continuity under dry heat

 56

and humid steam environments. Alternating Y and Cr–Y layers were deposited by magnetron
sputtering (PVD system: Nano 36, Kurt J. Lesker) to establish a baseline response under dry heat
and humid heat (steam-like) conditions and to test the Pourbaix-based expectation that Y₂O₃/Cr₂O₃
are susceptible to hydroxylation in oxygenated water. The chamber base pressure was maintained
at ~1×10⁻³ Torr with an Ar flow of 3 sccm (O₂-diluted Ar only when growing oxides). RF power
was used for Y, DC for Cr.

Figure 21. Schematic representation of multilayer coating architectures tested in this study: (A)
baseline Y/Cr–Y multilayer design and (B) YAl top-layer/Cr–Y multilayer design.

 Case B (YAlO₃-top/Cr–Y multilayers, complex-oxide enhanced design): In the second
design, the multilayer framework was capped with an outer Y-Al based layer, with an aim to
directly integrate a complex perovskite oxide predicted to offer superior stability over binary
oxides. The motivation for this design stems from thermodynamic analyses showing that YAlO₃
has a reduced driving force for hydroxylation compared to Y₂O₃ or Al₂O₃ alone and provides
improved chemical resistance against radiolytic oxidants in high-ECP BWR water. The Cr–Y
multilayers beneath serve the same role as in Case A, providing ductility, adhesion, and hydrogen
diffusion resistance, while the outer YAlO₃ functions as a chemically robust shield. This Case B
system therefore allows direct testing of whether complex oxides, when integrated into a
multilayer framework, outperform simple binary oxides and whether the predictions of the

schematic Pourbaix diagrams (broader stability fields for YAlO₃ compared to Cr₂O₃, TiO₂, and
Al₂O₃) can be experimentally validated. We have taken the metallurgical route (in-situ Y–Al
formation) After depositing the terminal Y layer, a thin Al cap layer was sputtered (Al target ~150

conversion toward Y–Al mixed oxide (target phases YAlO₃ / Y₂Al₂O₅) before any humid heat
exposure was performed. We have not taken the direct ceramic phase development route in the
preliminary development (direct complex-oxide deposition), from a ceramic YAlO₃ target by RF
sputtering in O₂-diluted Ar to form a dense, near-stoichiometric Y–Al oxide surface, as it requires
more effort and time to identify the right parameters.
 Together, these two multilayer configurations provide complementary insights: Case A as
a baseline verification of multilayer performance with binary oxide formers and Case B as an
advanced design that leverages complex oxide chemistry. Their comparative performance under
controlled dry and humid heat testing directly addresses the central question of whether multilayer
design and compositional complexity can overcome the limitations of monolithic coatings in
BWR-relevant environments.

 5.3.4 High-Temperature Air Exposure Study at 800 °C :

 To evaluate the oxidation resistance and thermal stability of the developed coatings, a
comparative study was conducted between uncoated Zircaloy-4 tubes and coated tubes fabricated
with the two multilayer architectures (Case A: Cr–Y multilayer; Case B: Y–Al/Cr–Y multilayer).
The samples, approximately 5 cm in length, were subjected to a 10-hour high-temperature
exposure at ~800 °C in air.
 The experiment was performed under air atmosphere with repeated thermal cycling. After
insertion into the furnace, the tubes were exposed to ~800 °C for fixed intervals, then removed to
room temperature (RT) to allow natural air quenching, see Figure 24. Following cooling, the tubes
were reintroduced into the furnace for the next cycle. This procedure was repeated multiple times
over the total 10-hour duration. The cycling protocol was chosen to simulate off-normal transients,
where claddings can undergo rapid heating and cooling events, and to assess coating resilience
against thermal shock, oxide scale evolution, and adhesion degradation. Representative images of
the heating and cooling stages are shown on Fig. 24, highlighting the sample arrangement inside
the furnace, the red-hot exposure state, and subsequent surface appearance after cooling.

 5.3.5 Humid Heating Exposure at 900 °C:

 Following the initial dry-air oxidation study at 800 °C, the same multilayer-coated Zircaloy-4
tubes were subjected to humid heating exposures at 900 °C to simulate a more aggressive accident-
relevant environment. The experiment consisted of two thermal cycling runs, where the tubes were
exposed to high-temperature steam and then quenched back to room temperature before reinsertion.
This testing protocol was designed to evaluate coating stability and performance under severe
oxidative stress, mimicking boiling water reactor (BWR) transient conditions or loss-of-coolant
accident (LOCA)-like steam events.
 Steam at 900 °C presents a far more aggressive challenge than dry air, due to the higher
oxygen chemical potential and enhanced transport of hydrogen and hydroxyl radicals. Under these
conditions, uncoated Zircaloy-4 would rapidly undergo breakaway oxidation, forming porous ZrO₂
accompanied by accelerated hydrogen generation. In contrast, multilayer-coated tubes provide
valuable insight into:
 • Oxide stability – whether Cr₂O₃ layers remain intact or transition to porous chromates, and
 whether Y–Al mixed oxides preserve continuity rather than hydrating to Y(OH)₃ or
 AlOOH.
 • Interfacial adhesion – steam cycling amplifies stresses associated with thermal expansion
 mismatch; delamination or spallation would reveal mechanical weaknesses.
 • Potential hydrogen suppression – multilayers act as both oxygen and hydrogen diffusion
 barriers; their integrity directly correlates with reduced hydrogen ingress into the Zr
 substrate.
 • Comparative performance of multilayer designs – Case A (Cr–Y multilayers) versus
 Case B (Y–Al/Cr–Y multilayers) under conditions where Pourbaix-type predictions
 indicate that mixed oxides should outperform binary Cr₂O₃ or Y₂O₃ phases.

 While dry-air oxidation primarily highlights scaling resistance and thermal mismatch, steam
exposure at 900 °C directly probes the chemical durability of protective oxides under coolant-

 62

relevant conditions. It also enables validation of thermodynamic predictions: if Y–Al perovskite-
like oxides exhibit reduced hydroxylation compared to binary oxides, Case B coatings should
retain structural integrity and continuity longer than Case A. Ultimately, these data provide critical
benchmarks for assessing multilayer coating viability in reactor accident scenarios, where coatings
must remain protective not only under steady-state operation but also during rapid excursions into
high-temperature steam environments.

 5.4.1 High-Temperature Dry Air Exposure at 800 °C:

samples were exposed for 10 hours at 800 °C in dry air under thermal cycling conditions. After
cooling to room temperature, the surface states of the tubes were examined (Fig. 25 a-c).

 The uncoated tube developed a thick white oxide scale that appeared coarse-grained and

phases formed during exposure provide enhanced stability compared to binary oxides (e.g., Cr₂O₃,

 5.4.3 Assessment of Oxidation-Induced Strain and Biaxial Stress at 800 °C:

 Strain estimation: The thermal cycling of Zircaloy-4 (Zr-4) tubes at 800 °C in dry air revealed
significant differences in dimensional stability between the uncoated control and the two
multilayer-coated cases. The original tube length was 5.9 cm (59 mm). After 10 hours of exposure,
the uncoated Zr-4 tube expanded axially by 1.2 mm, corresponding to a strain of:
                  ε = ΔL / L₀ = 1.2 / 59 ≈ 0.0203 (2.03%)
By comparison, Case A (Cr–Y multilayer, ~5 µm) expanded by 0.5 mm, yielding:
                  ε = ΔL / L₀ = 0.5 / 59 ≈ 0.0085 (0.85%)
Case B (Y–Al/Cr–Y multilayer, ~5 µm) expanded by 0.3 mm, giving:
                  ε = ΔL / L₀ = 0.3 / 59 ≈ 0.0051 (0.51%)
These results indicate that the multilayer coatings effectively suppressed macroscopic swelling,
with Case B providing nearly a 60–75% reduction in strain compared to the uncoated control. The
much larger expansion of the uncoated tube is consistent with uncontrolled ZrO₂ formation, which
has a high Pilling–Bedworth ratio (≈1.56). It should be noted that in all cases, the inner surface of
the tubes remained uncoated, meaning oxygen ingress and oxidation could proceed internally.
Thus, the measured length expansion and stress estimates reflect a combination of internal
oxidation-induced swelling and external barrier performance.
 5.4.3.1 Biaxial stress estimation:
 We treat the coating as a thin membrane on a much thicker substrate (plane-stress in the
film). The biaxial stress in the film is approximated by:
 σf ≈ Mf × εeff, Mf = Ef / (1 − νf)
 εeff = εaxial + εmismatch

        • Cr–Y (metal-rich stack): Ef ≈ 200 GPa, νf ≈ 0.30 ⇒ Mf ≈ 286 GPa

 5.4.3.2 Consolidated Interpretation of Strain–Stress Behavior and Observed
 Performance:
 The uncoated Zircaloy-4 tube in Figure 11 and 12 displayed the highest axial strain (2.03%)
and the largest estimated substrate stress (~2.93 GPa). This high strain originates from rapid
growth of ZrO₂ with a high Pilling–Bedworth ratio, leading to volume expansion, crack formation,
and spallation. The microstructural images confirm this, Figure 12 (a) the surface is covered with
thick, crumbly oxides and widespread fracture networks, consistent with excessive oxidation-
induced swelling and stress accumulation. Case A (Cr–Y multilayer) significantly reduced both
strain (0.85%) and substrate stress (~1.22 GPa). The coating partially accommodated oxidation-
induced swelling, but the calculated biaxial film stresses (1.14–2.75 GPa) indicate that the Cr–Y
layers experienced substantial tensile loads. These stresses explain the limited edge spallation and
surface cracks observed: while the multilayer effectively suppressed bulk oxide growth, the Cr-
rich outer layers were still susceptible to stress-assisted cracking and local delamination under
repeated thermal cycling, see Figure 12 (b). Case B (Y–Al/Cr–Y multilayer) achieved the lowest
strain (0.51%) and substrate stress (~0.73 GPa), along with the lowest biaxial film stress (1.09–
2.37 GPa). The reduction in both strain and film stress explains why this coating remained fully
intact with no through-thickness fracture. The superior performance of Case B stems from the
stabilizing effect of the Y–Al oxide phase, which resists fracturing and maintains higher rigidity,
thereby distributing stress more uniformly across the multilayer. This synergy between chemical
robustness and mechanical compliance resulted in the best coating integrity after cycling.

 5.4.4 High-Temperature Oxidation Performance at 900 °C in Humid Atmosphere:

 Following the 800 °C dry air cycling studies, the uncoated Zircaloy-4 (Zr-4) tubes and the
two multilayer-coated designs (Case A: Cr–Y multilayer; Case B: Y–Al/Cr–Y multilayer) were
subjected to additional thermal exposure under more aggressive conditions: 900 °C in a humid
atmosphere, with two complete thermal cycles between room temperature and the target
temperature, see Figure 28. This follow-up study was intended to evaluate the coatings’
performance under accelerated oxidation and hydration conditions, which are particularly relevant
to off-normal boiling water reactor scenarios.
 Uncoated Zr-4 Tubes : The uncoated Zr-4 tube showed catastrophic oxidation after cycling
at 900 °C in humid air. The surface exhibited thick, friable, and spalled ZrO₂ scales, consistent
with rapid oxygen ingress and extensive volume expansion from the Pilling–Bedworth ratio of
zirconia (~1.56). Severe crack networks formed across the tube surface, indicating poor scale
adherence and high tensile stress generated during thermal cycling. The morphology underscores
the inherent instability of Zr-4 in steam at elevated temperatures.
 Case A (Cr–Y Multilayer): The Case A tubes (Cr–Y multilayers) showed improved
resistance compared to uncoated Zr-4, but significant degradation was still observed after exposure
to humid 900 °C conditions. The Y₂O₃-rich layers oxidized to porous scales with high ionic
conductivity, enabling fast inward diffusion of O²⁻ ions and outward transport of cations. This
facilitated accelerated fracture development and spallation, similar in appearance to the uncoated
case but somewhat delayed in onset. The persistence of fractures highlights the intrinsic weakness
of binary Y₂O₃ as a terminal barrier under high humidity, where hydration and transformation to
Y(OH)₃ further destabilize the protective layer.

 68

Figure 28: Comparative Oxidation Behavior of Uncoated, Y₂O₃-Coated (Case A), and YAlO₃-
Based Multilayer-Coated (Case B) Zr-4 Tubes After Exposure to 900 °C Humid Thermal Cycling

 Case B (Y–Al/Cr–Y Multilayer): In contrast, Case B tubes with the Y–Al mixed oxide
(perovskite/garnet-like YAlO₃/Y₃Al₅O₁₂) top layer demonstrated significantly enhanced oxidation
resistance. The coatings remained mostly adherent but evidence of some surface fracture was
noticed, after two thermal cycling. The improved performance of Case B coatings can be attributed
to the intrinsic chemical stability of Y–Al complex oxides such as YAlO₃ (perovskite) and
Y₃Al₅O₁₂ (garnet-like) compared to binary Y₂O₃. Several mechanisms have likely contributed to
this superiority in performance:
 1. Reduced Hydroxylation Tendency
 o Y₂O₃ readily hydrates in high-temperature steam, forming Y(OH)₃, which is porous
 and structurally weak. This hydration–dehydration cycle promotes spallation and
 accelerates oxygen ingress.
 o In contrast, YAlO₃ has a lower Gibbs free energy of hydration due to the strong
 covalent character of Al–O bonds. The Al–O sublattice stabilizes the oxygen
 framework, suppressing the formation of hydroxides even in oxidizing and humid
 environments.

 69

 2. Lower Oxygen Ionic Conductivity and Diffusivity
 o Y₂O₃ exhibits relatively high O²⁻ ion mobility at elevated temperatures, especially
 along grain boundaries, allowing rapid inward diffusion of oxygen.
 o The Y–Al perovskite lattice has corner-sharing AlO₆ octahedra coupled with Y–O
 polyhedra, which creates a more rigid oxygen network. This reduces oxygen
 vacancy mobility and diffusivity, thereby significantly lowering the oxygen ionic
 conductivity compared to Y₂O₃. This directly translates to slower oxidation kinetics.
 3. Structural Rigidity and Crack Resistance
 o Y₂O₃ is cubic and relatively brittle, with poor resistance to fracture once hydrates
 form.
 o YAlO₃’s perovskite structure, with mixed cation sublattices, imparts greater
 mechanical rigidity and higher fracture toughness. It resists crack propagation
 under thermal cycling stresses, as confirmed by the absence of through-thickness
 cracks in Case B.
 4. Synergistic Cation Chemistry
 o The combination of Y³⁺ and Al³⁺ cations distribute lattice strain and reduces the free
 energy driving force for hydrolysis.
 o The dual-cation environment also slows dissolution kinetics, making Y–Al oxides
 less soluble in high-temperature aqueous environments than either Y₂O₃ or Al₂O₃
 alone.
 Together, these factors explain why Case B (YAlO₃/Cr–Y) maintained surface integrity
and oxidation resistance even after two cycles at 900 °C in humid air, while Case A (Y₂O₃/Cr–Y)
degraded severely. The lower O²⁻ transport and suppressed hydroxylation in Y–Al oxides directly
mitigated the oxidation-driven swelling and stress accumulation observed in Case A. This aligns
with the earlier strain (0.51% vs 0.85%) and stress (~1.1–2.4 GPa vs 1.1–2.7 GPa) differences,
demonstrating that chemical robustness and stress accommodation act synergistically in Case B.
 5.4.5 Characterization of the coated Zirc-4 tested at 900 °C with case A and case B designs:

 Case A: High-resolution TEM and EDS mapping provide direct microstructural
confirmation of the degradation processes previously inferred from the 800 °C and 900 °C thermal
cycling studies. The as-deposited Case A coating (Fig. 29 a, top) consisted of alternating layers of
hexagonal yttrium (Layer 1) and BCC chromium (Layer 2), with well-defined interfaces and dense
microstructure. The diffraction patterns confirm the crystalline phases, while EDS elemental

 70

mapping shows sharp compositional contrast between Y, Cr, and the Zr substrate, consistent with
a multilayer design intended to act as a thermal and oxidation barrier.
 After exposure to 900 °C in humid conditions (Fig. 29 b, bottom), significant structural
changes were observed. The yttrium layer transformed into a cubic Y₂O₃-like phase with evidence
of porosity, suggesting oxidation and possible hydroxide intermediate formation. The chromium
layer exhibited transformation to a CrOₓ phase, consistent with enhanced oxidation in the humid
environment. TEM cross-sections reveal the formation of a thin, porous surface layer at the
coating–gas interface, which significantly reduces barrier effectiveness. This porosity likely arises
from hydroxylation–dehydration reactions, known to destabilize Y₂O₃ at elevated temperatures in
water-containing atmospheres.
 Most critically, the Zr-4 substrate beneath the coating shows pronounced cracking and
oxygen ingress. EDS oxygen maps reveal deep penetration across the coating–substrate interface,
with fracture features extending into the Zr-4. These observations correlate with the large axial
expansion and high estimated biaxial stresses (~2.75 GPa upper bound) recorded for Case A at
800 °C, confirming that oxidation-induced strain at the interface leads to fracture and coating
debonding under thermal cycling.
 Together, these TEM results corroborate the macroscopic findings: Case A suffers from
accelerated oxidation and coating breakdown due to the relatively high oxygen ionic conductivity
of Y₂O₃, the susceptibility of Y₂O₃ to hydroxylation in humid conditions, and the stress-driven
fracture within the underlying Zr-4. The presence of a porous Y₂O₃-rich layer after exposure
explains the poor oxidation resistance observed experimentally, highlighting the necessity of
modifying the top layer (as in Case B, with YAlO₃) to mitigate oxygen transport and improve
barrier durability.

 • Oxygen mapping reveals significantly reduced oxygen penetration through the coating

 • Unlike Case A, no evidence of thin, porous hydroxide or degraded Y₂O₃ layers is observed.

 • Cross-sectional imaging shows only minor microcracking in the Zr-4 substrate, with no

 microstructural integrity.
 The TEM findings provide mechanistic validation of the macroscopic results. At 800 °C, Case
 B displayed minimal strain accumulation and no visible spallation. At 900 °C with humid
 cycling, the coating retained continuity and suppressed oxidation, whereas Case A failed due
 to Y₂O₃ instability and porosity formation. The superior behavior of Case B thus arises from
 both chemical stability (YAlO₃’s resistance to hydroxylation and low O²⁻ diffusivity) and
 mechanical integrity (multilayer design distributing stress and avoiding brittle fracture).

5.5 Future Work.

 Building on the current findings demonstrating the performance advantage of the
multilayer coating systems under dry air and humid high-temperature cycling, several critical
directions are proposed for future work:
(a) Optimization of Multilayer Architectures for Thermal Shock Resistance
Future efforts will focus on iterating the individual layer thicknesses within the multilayer coating
design to further improve adhesion and resistance to thermal shock. These iterations will be guided
by careful analysis of the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch between constituent
materials (Cr, Y, Al, YAlO₃, and their oxides) and Zircaloy-4. By tailoring the thickness ratio of
ductile metallic versus stiff ceramic layers, we aim to mitigate stress localization, delay crack
initiation, and improve cycling stability under reactor-relevant thermal gradients.
(b) Autoclave and LOCA Testing for Long-Term and Accident-Relevant Performance in
collaboration with ORNL. While dry air and humid 900 °C cycling tests provide an accelerated
screening method, the long-term performance of these coatings in reactor-simulated environments
requires more extensive validation. Autoclave exposures under boiling water reactor (BWR)
chemistry conditions will allow evaluation of corrosion resistance, hydrogen ingress suppression,
and coating adhesion under steady-state service. Additionally, loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA)
simulation studies will be carried out to probe the response of the coated Zircaloy tubes under
transient overheating, rapid quenching, and steam-rich conditions. These tests will establish the
coating’s ability to withstand accident-level stresses and verify whether the Y–Al–O-based
multilayer maintains its barrier functionality under extreme conditions.
 Together, these ongoing and planned studies aim to refine the multilayer design into a
reliable accident-tolerant cladding coating solution for advanced reactor applications.

5.6 Conclusions.

 This study demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of advanced multilayer coatings to
improve the oxidation resistance and thermal stability of Zircaloy-4 claddings under boiling water
reactor (BWR)-relevant conditions. Comparative testing at 800–900 °C under dry and humid
atmospheres revealed the inherent limitations of monolithic or binary oxide systems and the
significant performance gains achieved through compositional and architectural design.
 The uncoated Zircaloy-4 tubes exhibited extensive ZrO₂ growth, spallation, and severe
dimensional swelling, driven by the high Pilling–Bedworth ratio of zirconium oxidation and rapid
oxygen ingress. In contrast, multilayer coatings provided robust protection: Case A (Cr–Y
multilayer) delayed oxidation but showed porosity and microcracking consistent with the known

 76

aqueous instability of Y₂O₃ and the susceptibility of Cr₂O₃ to oxidative dissolution. Case B, which
incorporated a Y–Al oxide top layer above the Cr–Y stack, significantly outperformed Case A.
The dense YAlO₃-based layer exhibited lower oxygen ionic conductivity and greater
thermodynamic stability in humid, high-temperature environments, suppressing fracture formation
and limiting oxygen ingress. These improvements translated into reduced axial strain, lower
calculated biaxial stresses, and intact barrier continuity after thermal cycling.
 Microstructural characterization via TEM confirmed these trends: Case A coatings
developed porous, degraded oxide layers with evidence of oxygen penetration into the substrate,
while Case B coatings maintained a compact morphology with no porous phase formation and
limited substrate oxidation. Together, these results establish Y–Al complex oxides as promising
outer-barrier candidates, particularly when integrated into multilayer architectures that balance
chemical robustness with mechanical compliance.
 Overall, this work underscores the necessity of combining thermodynamic insights from
high-temperature Pourbaix analysis with multilayer engineering to design coatings that resist
hydroxylation, spallation, and thermal mismatch stresses. The Cr–Y–Al multilayer approach
represents a viable pathway toward durable accident-tolerant cladding protection in BWR systems,
with future optimization and in-reactor testing needed to validate long-term applicability.
