Health Utility Values Measured by EQ-5D in Breast Cancer From Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocol
Authors: Lynn Jia Tay, Siew Chin Ong, Lyn Xuan Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

However, patients living with breast cancer in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face distinct burdens compared with those in high-income countries (HICs), driven by profound disparities in resources, infrastructure, and cultural context. High reliance on HUVs from HICs risks misrepresenting the specific disease burden in LMICs, introducing uncertainty in local healthcare resource allocation. This protocol outlines the methods for a planned systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize available evidence from LMICs to generate pooled EuroQol-5 Dimensions HUV estimates that are contextually appropriate for local decision making.MethodsBy following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines, this protocol is registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251208371). The anticipated findings will establish a critical evidence base for decision making in resource-constrained settings. By generating contextually relevant estimates, this will minimize parameter uncertainty and support more equitable resource allocation. Background

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are critical for capturing the direct impact of disease and treatment on the lives of patients and caregivers [1]. Through standardized instruments such as the EuroQoL-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D) and the Short Form 36-item Health Survey (SF-36), patient responses can be converted into health utility values (HUVs) using established valuation methodologies [2, 3]. As a quantitative measure of health states where 1 represents perfect health and 0 represents dead, HUVs serve as an essential parameter for quality-adjusted life year (QALY) calculations in economic evaluations and health technology assessments (HTA) [4]. Compared with other preference-based tools, EQ-5D is widely recognized in HTA agencies such as NICE, HTAi and INAHTA due to its validity, sensitivity, reliability and the availability of country-specific value sets [5]. With a descriptive system consisting of five health dimensions, it offers a comprehensive framework that encompass both physical and psychological aspects of health-related quality of life.

Breast cancer represents a primary and complex global public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), it was the second most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide in 2022, with over 2.31 million new cases [6]. Among females specifically, breast cancer was the leading incident cancer in 164 countries and territories, where it ranked as the primary cause of cancer mortality followed by tracheal, bronchus and lung cancer and cervical cancer [7]. Besides, breast cancer resulted in 778,000 deaths and a loss of 24.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2023, an increase of approximately 107.7–111.1% compared with statistics from 1990 [7].

Despite the global prevalence of breast cancer, significant disparities in burden persist across regions and country income groups. The mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR), a key indicator of health system performance, is notably higher in low Human Development Index (HDI) countries compared with high HDI nations such as the United States and Germany. Previous studies observed a two-fold difference in MIR for females (0.69 vs 0.30) between these groups [8]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), patients often face late-stage diagnosis, limited health literacy, and restricted access to care, resulting in poorer prognostic outcomes than their counterparts in high-income countries (HICs) [9, 10]. Consequently, economic evaluations in LMICs often face a methodological dilemma which is the heavy reliance on HUVs derived from HIC populations.

Previous systematic reviews mapped utility decrements associated with advanced disease stages and treatment modalities (e.g., chemotherapy, surgery, radiation), while pointing out the EQ-5D as the most utilized preference-based instrument [11]. Similarly, another review revealed a steep, progressive disutility in breast cancer patients as the disease advances from localized to metastatic stages (Stage IV) [12]. However, while these reviews establish foundational utility estimates, the included studies in these reviews were mostly conducted in populations from HICs. Due to the absence of country-specific value sets, HUVs from HICs were commonly utilized in economic evaluations conducted in LMICs. As preference-based evaluation tools are anchored in subjective health perceptions, they are highly sensitive to a population's specific cultural, socioeconomic, and environmental context. Different populations have distinct expectations regarding health and employ varying approaches and coping mechanisms to manage the multidimensional challenges of disease. Therefore, the profound cultural heterogeneity between HICs and LMICs severely limits the transferability of such data, potentially misrepresenting the true disease burden and the subjective lived experiences of patients in LMICs. While the use of country-specific HUVs is the gold standard to reflect local preferences, many LMICs lack comprehensive primary utility data or validated local value sets across the breast cancer continuum. In this context of data scarcity, pooling HUVs from LMICs provides a pragmatic and methodologically superior alternative to the default practice of transferring HUVs from HICs.

To mitigate these biases and ensure allocative efficiency, locally relevant parameters are indispensable. In the current healthcare landscape, scarcity of healthcare resources imposes great challenges for stakeholders in determining the value of healthcare from multiple perspectives [13]. For breast cancer, the most common cancer in women globally, these utility data are scattered and inconsistent, particularly in LMICs. Therefore, we planned to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the pooled HUVs of patients with breast cancer residing in LMICs by considering evidence from various study designs. The research findings presented in this analysis provide reliable HUV estimates in evaluating new and existing treatments and interventions in breast cancers in these countries and make meaningful cross-country comparisons. These estimates are urgently needed to inform local HTA, guide resource allocation, and improve the quality of economic evaluations in LMICs.

Methods/Design

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to systematically gather, assess and synthesize the pooled estimate of health utility values (HUVs) measured by EQ-5D in people with breast cancer residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Design

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review Protocols (PRISMA-P) recommendations will be applied to ensure the transparency of this protocol for conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis [14]. The protocol has been designed based on a practical guideline [15] and registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251208371).

Study Eligibility

Studies that reported HUVs measured by EQ-5D instruments from both male and female patients with breast cancer residing in LMICs will be sought from various databases using the Population, Intervention Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework in electronic supplementary material (ESM) S1. Their full eligibility into the systematic review and meta-analysis will be assessed based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria as shown in ESM S2.

Literature Search and Data Sources

A literature search will be conducted using Cochrane CENTRAL, PubMed, Web of Science core collection and Scopus. The search will use the following keywords and Medical Subject Headings alone and in combination: ‘EQ-5D’, ‘breast cancer’, and ‘LMICs’ (as described in ESM S3). To ensure that there is a substantial body of literature, the reference lists of the included articles will be examined (known as reference snowballing) and relevant papers will be assessed for inclusion. As the standardized EuroQol Valuation Technology protocol was introduced to derive national value sets in 2014 [16], published studies from January 1, 2015 to May 31, 2026 will be sought in the search and we plan to complete the search by August 2026.

Study Selection

After the initial database searches, the citations will be imported to Endnote and duplicate records will be removed before title and abstract screening. Two reviewers (LJT and SCO) will independently examine study titles and abstracts for study inclusion. Next, the authors will gain access to full texts to confirm eligibility. If there is any discrepancy during the entire screening process, it will be resolved through discussion with the third reviewer (LXT). To ease understanding, a PRISMA flow diagram will be utilized to demonstrated the entire screening process from initial database search to final study inclusion.

Data Extraction

A data extraction form will be created in Microsoft Excel to record the study’s characteristics and key findings. By confirming the number of final studies included, two reviewers (LJT and SCO) will extract information independently of each other from the full text to meet the proposed objectives. The author(s) name, publication year, study design, setting, sample size, population age and gender, clinical characteristics of cancer (e.g. stage, type), EQ-5D version used, value set used, mode of administration, distribution of responses at each domain, mean utility score and 95% confidence intervals will be extracted from the full text of each study. For studies reporting median HUVs, established statistical algorithms will be utilized to estimate the sample mean and standard deviation, provided the sample size and a measure of dispersion (range or interquartile range) are available [17]. This approach promotes data inclusion from resource-constrained settings with various reporting standards. If dispersion data is not sufficient, these studies will be excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis but retained in the qualitative narrative synthesis. Any disagreement in the extraction process will be resolved via discussion with the third reviewer (SCO).

Data Synthesis

Data cleaning and verification of the extracted data will be conducted to ensure consistency. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize the characteristics of the included studies, such as author name, publication year, country, country income level, sample size, disease type, mean age, EQ-5D utility scores, EQ-VAS scores and proportions with problems in the five dimensions, value set used and administration method. Any discrepancies or inconsistencies will be addressed through discussion among the authors.

Study Quality and Risk-of-Bias Assessment

Risk of bias will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias assessment tool 2 (ROB-2) for randomized controlled trials (RCT), and the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [18]. Ranging from 0 to 9 stars, the NOS comprises 8 items that are further categorized into selection, comparability and outcome or exposure (depending on the study design) [19]. The ROB-2 tool is a structured instrument to guide risk-of-bias assessment through five distinct domains from randomization process, intervention deviations, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. The result of the ROB-2 tool is reported in three levels: low, some concerns and high risk of bias [20].

Meta-Analysis

According to international recommendations, meta-analysis is considered feasible if two or more studies reported utility for people with breast cancer [21, 22]. The mean EQ-5D utility value from observational studies and pre-intervention baseline values from RCTs will be pooled in both EQ-5D-5L and EQ-5D-3L versions. Next, meta-analysis of single proportions (metaprop) will be conducted from the R meta package (version 4.5.2) to provide point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for fixed and random effects models by using the inverse variance method and Der Simonian-Laird method, respectively [23, 24]. Heterogeneity is evaluated using the I2 statistic where I2 >25% reveals heterogeneity [25]. Subgroup analysis will be performed to investigate the influence of different factors such as breast cancer subgroups based on the clinical staging system with Tumor, Node, Metastases (TNM), treatment stages (newly diagnosed, receiving treatment, survivors), country income group, age group, self- or proxy reporting, and study design where applicable.

Patient and Public Involvement

This systematic review and meta-analysis will not involve patients or the general public.

Ethics and Dissemination

As this study will only include publicly available publications, approval from an institutional review board is deemed not compulsory for this systematic review and meta-analysis. We intend to disseminate our research findings by means of presentations at national and international conferences as well as publications in peer-reviewed, open-access journals.

Amendments

The protocol for this study will be amended as necessary. Any changes to this protocol will be included explicitly in the future manuscript.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will aggregate HUVs of people living with breast cancer from LMICs and stratify HUVs into different subgroups that are valuable in conducting economic evaluations such as cost-effectiveness or cost-utility analysis. By synthesizing EQ-5D utility values specific to breast cancer in LMICs, this research will provide the essential data infrastructure for evidence-based decision making. It will enable more accurate health economic modelling, support equitable resource allocation, and ultimately contribute to health systems that are responsive not just to the biological presence of cancer, but to the holistic well-being of the patients and families it affects.

With the emergence of new pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, reliable estimates of HUVs provide important implications in assessing the value of breast cancer care at different clinical stages. With that, it has profound implications for decision making across multiple levels of the health system. For government and third-party payers, updated HUVs reduce uncertainty when balancing financial impact, clinical efficacy and utility gains. In LMIC settings where management of side effects (e.g., neutropenia, nausea) is suboptimal due to resource constraints, the disutility of toxicity may be greater than in HICs. Therefore, reliable HUVs play a crucial role in informing Health Technology Assessment (HTA) and resource allocation decisions to prioritize equally effective regimens that minimize the occurrence of complications and adverse effects as a whole. Moreover, stratified HUVs based on country income group incorporate context-specific considerations reflecting the subjective well-being of the populations living with breast cancer, from those newly diagnosed to survivors. With these research findings, the approach of ‘transferring’ utility values from HICs due to the absence of data or country-specific value sets could be avoided as it could introduce bias in reflecting preferences that are dependent on cultural values, health expectations, and access to health and supportive care.
