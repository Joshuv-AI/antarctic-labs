Comparative effectiveness of inspiratory muscle training approaches within intensive care unit–acquired weakness prevention and rehabilitation strategies: a systematic review and component network meta-analysis
Authors: Li Xueyi, Wang Xue, Li Ying, Li Yao, Song Jinhui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study compared the relative effectiveness of different inspiratory muscle training (IMT) modalities within intensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) prevention and rehabilitation strategies using network meta-analysis (NMA) and component network meta-analysis (CNMA).MethodsA systematic search of electronic databases in both Chinese and English was conducted to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) enrolling adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). Eligible studies evaluated strategies for the prevention and rehabilitation of ICU-AW, including inspiratory muscle training IMT-related interventions, systemic physical rehabilitation, and their combinations. The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB2) tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included RCTs, and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was applied to evaluate the quality of the evidence. Heterogeneity was initially assessed using conventional pooled analyses and was not used as the sole criterion for model selection in the subsequent NMA. Outcomes with substantial heterogeneity were further analyzed using random-effects NMA, for which 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were additionally reported. For outcomes with low apparent heterogeneity, fixed-effect NMA was applied, with heterogeneity interpreted in light of clinical and methodological diversity across studies. Component network meta-analysis (CNMA) was additionally conducted to quantify the independent treatment effect of each intervention component. For continuous outcome measures (Medical Research Council [MRC] score, maximal inspiratory pressure [MIP], and duration of mechanical ventilation [MV]), results were presented as mean differences (MDs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For binary outcome measures (incidence of ICU-AW and weaning success rate), treatment effects were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs.ResultsA total of 23 eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in this analysis, which evaluated 11 distinct intervention strategies. These strategies were categorized into 5 multicomponent interventions and 6 single-component interventions. The 6 single-component interventions included 4 IMT modalities, a systemic physical rehabilitation intervention, and routine care, which was designated as the reference control group. Compared with the routine care reference group, mechanical threshold showed a non-statistically significant trend toward lower ICU-AW incidence (OR = 0.19, 95% CI [0.04, 1.01]); hence, the best-ranked results based on SUCRA should be interpreted as exploratory findings only. The combination of systemic physical rehabilitation and routine inspiratory muscle training was associated with improved MRC scores and higher weaning success rates. For MIP, the combination of systemic physical rehabilitation and mechanical threshold achieved the highest ranking. CNMA further indicated that the addition of systemic physical rehabilitation to mechanical threshold was associated with incremental therapeutic benefit. For duration of MV, combined strategies incorporating systemic physical rehabilitation and IMT also ranked favorably; however, this outcome was associated with a higher degree of uncertainty and should be interpreted with caution. According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence was rated as low for the incidence of ICU-AW, moderate for the MRC score, weaning success rate, and MIP, and very low for the duration of mechanical ventilation.ConclusionWhile combined interventions incorporating systemic physical rehabilitation and IMT ranked highest across multiple outcomes, evidence supporting the superiority of any specific IMT regimen over another remains limited. For MIP, CNMA further demonstrated that the addition of systemic physical rehabilitation to mechanical threshold was associated with a statistically significant incremental therapeutic benefit compared with mechanical threshold alone, although definitive isolation of the independent effects of most individual components was not possible. Overall, these findings provide a more differentiated assessment of IMT-related strategies within ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation. However, the overall certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to very low across all outcomes according to the GRADE framework, highlighting the need for further high-quality randomized controlled trials to validate these findings. Background

Intensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) is a common neuromuscular complication in critically ill patients and is clinically characterized by diffuse, symmetrical muscle weakness [1]. Its occurrence is particularly frequent in patients requiring mechanical ventilation [2–4]. Epidemiological studies have shown that the risk of ICU-AW increases with the duration of mechanical ventilation. The reported incidence ranges from 26% to 65% after 5–7 days of ventilation and rises to 67% when ventilation exceeds 10 days [5, 6]. These findings suggest that mechanically ventilated patients constitute a population at particularly high risk of developing ICU-AW. A key pathophysiological mechanism underlying this increased risk is ventilator-induced inspiratory muscle dysfunction, particularly diaphragmatic disuse. When mechanical ventilation is continued for more than 24 h, the physiological contractile activity of the diaphragm is largely replaced by ventilatory support, leading to profound muscle inactivity and near-complete electrophysiological silence [7]. This state of disuse accelerates proteolysis and impairs protein synthesis in diaphragmatic fibers, thereby contributing to rapid diaphragmatic atrophy [8, 9]. As inspiratory muscle function declines, spontaneous breathing becomes less effective, and weaning from mechanical ventilation becomes increasingly difficult. At the same time, prolonged mechanical ventilation and immobilization further exacerbate peripheral muscle disuse, thereby worsening generalized weakness, prolonging ICU stay, and increasing the risks of weaning failure and mortality [10, 11]. Thus, prolonged mechanical ventilation and ICU-AW may reinforce each other, thereby forming a vicious cycle.

To break this vicious cycle involving prolonged mechanical ventilation, inspiratory muscle dysfunction, and generalized weakness, early preventive and rehabilitative strategies are needed for mechanically ventilated ICU patients. In current clinical practice, ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies generally rely on multicomponent approaches rather than any single intervention, with systemic physical rehabilitation and IMT serving as two key components [12–14]. Within this framework, IMT should be regarded as one component of a broader rehabilitation strategy rather than as an isolated treatment. IMT is a targeted intervention that applies controlled inspiratory resistance to improve inspiratory muscle strength and endurance, thereby potentially delaying or reversing disuse-related inspiratory muscle dysfunction [15, 16]. It can be delivered in several forms, including routine inspiratory muscle training, targeted flow-resistive loading, mechanical threshold loading, and device-assisted inspiratory muscle pacing [17]. Previous studies have emphasized the clinical importance of early recognition and intervention for inspiratory muscle dysfunction in critically ill patients [18]. Existing clinical studies and conventional meta-analyses further suggest that IMT, when evaluated as an overall intervention, may improve inspiratory muscle performance and may provide benefits over usual care for some respiratory- and weaning-related outcomes [19–24]. However, these studies have generally examined IMT as a single intervention category, rather than distinguishing between different IMT modalities or considering IMT within the broader framework of ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies. As a result, it remains unclear whether different IMT-containing strategies have differential effects on ICU-AW-related and respiratory-related outcomes, and how specific IMT components or combinations may relate to variations in clinical outcomes. Consequently, several important clinical and research questions remain unresolved: (1) What are the relative effects of different IMT-containing interventions on ICU-AW-related and respiratory-related outcomes? (2) How are specific IMT components or combinations associated with differences in clinical outcomes?

Against this background, network meta-analysis (NMA) offers a suitable methodological approach for addressing these unresolved issues. By integrating both direct and indirect comparative evidence, NMA enables simultaneous comparison of the relative effectiveness of multiple interventions within a single statistical model, thereby providing a more comprehensive and hierarchical evidence framework than traditional meta-analysis [25]. As an extension of NMA, component network meta-analysis (CNMA) is well suited to deconstructing the complex multicomponent interventions examined in this study. By decomposing the overall effect of a composite intervention into the contributions of individual components, CNMA can help identify the key active components that primarily drive therapeutic effects [26].

Therefore, this study aims to systematically identify relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and apply both conventional NMA and CNMA to address these key objectives. Through rigorous methodological design and comprehensive statistical analysis, this study is expected to inform the development of optimized, evidence-based inspiratory muscle training strategies in clinical practice and to provide high-quality evidence for reducing the incidence of ICU-AW and improving patients’ long-term prognosis.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension statement for network meta-analysis (PRISMA-NMA). The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under registration number CRD420251208302. Databases were searched from inception to 25 October 2025, and the complete database-specific search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Material. In the primary database search, broader IMT-related terms were used to maximize search sensitivity, because specific IMT modalities are not always described consistently in titles, abstracts, and indexing records. To reduce the risk of missing relevant studies reported mainly using modality-specific terminology, supplementary screening was conducted through reference-list screening and citation tracking of included studies and relevant reviews, with targeted supplementary checking of modality-specific IMT terms where appropriate. No additional primary database search was conducted after 25 October 2025; subsequent procedures included deduplication, study selection, quality assessment, and supplementary snowballing. The search included five English-language databases—Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and MEDLINE—and five Chinese-language databases—China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, the VIP Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database, the Chinese Medical Journal Full-Text Database, and SinoMed.

Study selection

Eligibility criteria were developed according to the PICOS framework, comprising participants, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and study design, as detailed below:

Participants (P)

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU and mechanically ventilated for at least 24 h.

Interventions (I)

Prevention and rehabilitation strategies of ICU-AW [27], including systemic physical rehabilitation; 4 IMT approaches, specifically: Routine inspiratory muscle training, targeted flow-resistive loading, mechanical threshold, and device-assisted inspiratory muscle pacing. The standardized definitions of each component are as follows: (1) Systemic physical rehabilitation was defined as a separate non-IMT rehabilitation strategy initiated at any time during MV or within 7 days after ICU admission and included passive and active interventions such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), bedside cycling, or range-of-motion exercises [28];(2) Routine inspiratory muscle training was defined as an IMT approach including pursed-lip breathing, diaphragmatic breathing, and abdominal sandbag compression training [12]; (3) Targeted flow-resistive loading was defined as a pre-set, quantifiable airflow resistance load designed to achieve specific training effects. Common tools include adjustable-resistance respiratory trainers, resistance masks, balloon/bottle-blowing devices, etc. Studies using professional devices with precise resistance adjustment were prioritized [29]; (4) Mechanical threshold was defined as a pre-set minimum mechanical pressure value that the patient’s inspiratory muscles must generate to allow airflow passage and complete breathing movements [29]; (5) Device-assisted inspiratory muscle pacing was defined as a diaphragmatic pacing technique that stimulates the phrenic nerve using functional pulsed current to enhance diaphragmatic contractile function [30].

Comparators (C)

Studies were included if they compared any of the above inspiratory muscle training components, or their combined regimens, with routine care. Routine care referred to the standard care provided in the ICU without structured systemic physical rehabilitation or specific inspiratory muscle training [28]. In this network meta-analysis, routine care served as the control condition and was not classified as an active intervention targeting inspiratory function.

Outcomes (O)

Studies were included if they reported at least one of the following outcome measures: incidence of ICU-AW, Medical Research Council (MRC) score, weaning success rate, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and duration of mechanical ventilation (MV). For each outcome measure, only the latest assessment data obtained during ICU hospitalization or before discharge were extracted. ICU-AW was diagnosed according to a total Medical Research Council muscle strength score ≤ 48, with a full score of 60.

Study design (S)

Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included.

Language and screening process

Only articles published in Chinese or English were included. Two researchers independently conducted the database searches, removed duplicates, screened titles and abstracts, and assessed full-text eligibility. Both researchers received training in literature quality and evidence evaluation before conducting independent assessments. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer until consensus was reached.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted data from the included studies, and the extracted data were cross-checked to ensure accuracy and completeness. The extraction framework included the following items: (1) basic study characteristics, including the first author’s name, country where the study was conducted, and publication year; (2) participant characteristics, including the duration of mechanical ventilation and time of admission to the ICU; (3) details of intervention and control regimens, including the specific types of inspiratory muscle training; and (4) outcome indicator including the incidence of ICU-AW, MRC score, weaning success rate, MIP, and duration of MV.

Risk of bias assessment

To ensure the internal validity of the included studies, two researchers independently assessed the methodological quality of the included RCTs using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (Risk of Bias 2.0, RoB 2) [31]. This tool covers five core bias domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from the intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of outcomes; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result.

The risk of bias for each domain and for the overall study was classified into one of three categories: “low risk of bias”, “some concerns”, or “high risk of bias”. Before assessment, the two reviewers received unified training on ROB2 and then performed the evaluations independently. Any disagreements were resolved through full-text rechecking and group discussion until consensus was reached. If consensus could not be reached, a third researcher was consulted for further review and discussion.

Data integration and analysis

NMA was conducted within a frequentist framework using STATA software, version 18.0. Outcome-specific network plots were generated to illustrate the geometry of the available evidence. NMA served as the primary analytical framework for estimating relative treatment effects, constructing league tables, assessing inconsistency and heterogeneity, performing sensitivity analyses, evaluating small-study effects, and calculating treatment rankings using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).

In Stata 18.0, network meta-analysis was performed using the network meta-analysis command suite, including network setup, network convert, network meta, netleague, network rank, and sucra. Conventional pooled analyses and Egger’s test were performed using metan and metabias, respectively. For outcomes analyzed using random-effects NMA, 95% prediction intervals were additionally obtained using intervalplot with the pred option. (1) Heterogeneity was initially assessed using conventional pooled analyses, and the I² statistic was interpreted as a preliminary guide rather than as the sole determinant for model selection in the subsequent NMA. Outcomes with greater heterogeneity in the preliminary assessment were further analyzed using random-effects NMA, whereas fixed-effect NMA was applied to outcomes with low apparent heterogeneity. For fixed-effect NMA, in addition to the observed statistical heterogeneity, clinical and methodological diversity across studies was also considered; for outcomes analyzed using random-effects NMA, model-based heterogeneity parameters and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were additionally reported. (2) For binary outcomes, statistical significance was considered when the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the odds ratio (OR) did not include 1. For continuous outcomes, statistical significance was considered when the 95% CI did not include 0, quantitative synthesis of continuous outcomes was based on mean and standard deviation (SD) data. In this review, no continuous outcome included in the final quantitative pooling was reported solely as median and interquartile range. If such data had been reported without directly available or reliably estimable mean/SD values, they would not have been pooled quantitatively to avoid introducing additional uncertainty from statistical conversion. (3) Local inconsistency was assessed using the node-splitting method when closed loops were present in the evidence network. A P value > 0.05 was considered to indicate no statistically significant inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence. (4) Ranking of intervention effects: The surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was used to quantitatively evaluate the effectiveness of different inspiratory muscle training modalities. SUCRA values range from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating a higher probability that the intervention has a better therapeutic effect in improving the outcome indicators. (5) Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using funnel plots in combination with Egger’s test when at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome. When fewer than 10 studies were included, formal quantitative assessment of publication bias was not performed because of limited statistical power. The review was reported in accordance with PRISMA-NMA recommendations.

Python 3.11 was used to prepare the analytical dataset, and component network meta-analysis (CNMA) was conducted as a complementary analysis to evaluate the component-level and incremental effects of different elements within multicomponent rehabilitation interventions. In Python 3.11, data preprocessing and component-level analyses were performed using the pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, and Matplotlib libraries. File handling was performed using Python’s built-in os module. The CNMA was based on the additive model assumption, whereby the effect of a combined intervention is represented as the sum of the effects of its individual components. Potential interaction terms between components were considered conceptually, but were not incorporated into the primary CNMA because the available dataset, particularly the limited number of relevant multi-arm trials, did not provide sufficient information for stable and reliable estimation of component interactions. Accordingly, the additive model was used as the primary CNMA approach in this study. Under this framework, binary outcomes, including weaning success and ICU-AW incidence, were analyzed using a binomial generalized linear model, with the odds ratio (OR) as the effect measure. Continuous outcomes, including MIP, MRC score, and duration of mechanical ventilation, were analyzed using weighted least squares (WLS) regression with inverse-variance weighting to estimate incremental mean differences (IMDs/effect estimates). Study was included in the model as a fixed-effect parameter to account for study-specific differences across trials. CNMA results were used to report component-level effect estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For binary outcomes, statistical significance was considered when the 95% CI did not include 1 for OR; for continuous outcomes, statistical significance was considered when the 95%CI did not include 0.

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)

The certainty of the evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE framework [32], which evaluates five core methodological domains for RCTs: (1) Risk of bias was assessed using the ROB2 tool, with downgrading considered when most of the contributing evidence had some concerns or a high risk of bias in key domains; (2) Inconsistency was evaluated based on heterogeneity estimates, including I² and τ², together with clinical and methodological considerations. Downgrading was considered when important unexplained inconsistency was present; (3) Directness was judged by the alignment of study populations, interventions, comparators, and outcomes with the review question. Downgrading was considered if important indirectness was identified; (4) Imprecision was assessed based on the width of the 95% confidence intervals and the adequacy of sample size. Downgrading was considered if the confidence intervals were wide, crossed the null effect, or if the total sample size was insufficient; and (5) Publication bias was assessed, where feasible, using approaches such as Egger’s test when the number of studies was sufficient. Downgrading was considered if potential selective publication was suspected. Two independent reviewers rated the certainty of evidence for each outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low. The certainty rating started as high for RCTs and was downgraded by one level for serious limitations or by two levels for very serious limitations. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third senior reviewer.

Results

The detailed study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. After full-text screening and eligibility assessment, 23 studies were included [33–55]. Among these studies, 13 reported the duration of mechanical ventilation, 9 reported the incidence of ICU-AW, 5 reported MRC score, 6 reported weaning success rate, and 6 reported MIP values. All eligible studies were incorporated into the primary analysis. Across these studies, a total of 11 interventions were identified, including 6 single-component interventions: routine care, systemic physical rehabilitation, routine inspiratory muscle training, targeted resistance, mechanical threshold, and device-assisted inspiratory muscle pacing. Heterogeneity test

For the outcome of ICU-AW incidence, the results showed almost no statistical heterogeneity among the included studies, with good consistency across results (I² = 0.0%, P = 0.497); for MRC muscle strength score, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the observed heterogeneity. The original analysis showed substantial statistical heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 81.5%, P < 0.001). After exclusion of the study by Liu et al. [38], heterogeneity decreased substantially (I² = 13.4%, P = 0.326), suggesting that this study was the main contributor to the overall heterogeneity; for weaning success rate, no substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² = 0.0%, P = 0.851); for MIP, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² = 92%, P < 0.05). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially removing each study, and heterogeneity remained high throughout the analysis; for duration of MV, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed among the included studies in the original analysis (I² = 91.6%, P < 0.001). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially removing each study, and the heterogeneity estimates showed no obvious changes. Based on the preliminary heterogeneity assessment, MIP and MV duration were further evaluated using random-effects NMA, and 95% PIs were additionally reported for these two outcomes. Random-effects NMA was retained for quantitative synthesis to explore the overall direction and magnitude of the effects. Heterogeneity analysis under the random-effects NMA model indicated the following: for MIP, no substantial heterogeneity was observed (τ² = 0, I² = 0%, P = 0.6452); whereas for MV duration, substantial between-study heterogeneity persisted (τ² = 1.5769, I² = 74.7%, P = 0.0001).

Loop inconsistency test

As the evidence networks for weaning success rate and MIP contained closed loops, loop inconsistency tests were performed for these two outcomes. The loop inconsistency test for weaning success rate showed that, in the two closed loops involving mechanical threshold–targeted flow resistance–routine care inconsistency factor (IF) (IF = 0.81, 95% CI [0.00,3.16]) and mechanical threshold–routine inspiratory muscle training–routine care (IF = 0.10, 95% CI [0.00,1.95]), the 95% CIs for both comparisons included 0, indicating no statistically significant inconsistency. In addition, τ² = 0.000 further supported the absence of inconsistency in these two closed loops. Overall, the loop inconsistency test showed good agreement between direct and indirect evidence for weaning success rate, indicating that no correction was required. The loop inconsistency test for MIP showed only one closed loop, involving mechanical threshold–targeted flow resistance–routine care (IF = 2.03, 95% CI [0.00,7.55]). The 95% CI included 0, indicating no statistically significant inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence within this closed loop and suggesting negligible heterogeneity within the loop. In addition, the closed loop involving systemic physical rehabilitation + device-assisted inspiratory muscle pacing, systemic physical rehabilitation, and routine care consisted solely of multi-arm trials, and its inherent structure ensured good consistency of evidence. Overall, evidence from direct and indirect comparisons was generally consistent across all closed loops for this outcome, supporting the appropriateness of the consistency model for effect size pooling. Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots or Egger’s test. The funnel plots were generally symmetric, and all P values from Egger’s test were > 0.05, suggesting no significant publication bias.

For duration of MV (n = 13), Egger’s test was performed to assess small-study effects. The result showed a P value of 0.046, suggesting significant small-study effects. As small-study effects may be associated with publication bias, this finding suggested the possible presence of publication bias for this outcome. In accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and standard meta-analytical guidelines [56], publication bias was assessed using funnel plot visualization or Egger’s regression test only for outcomes with 10 or more included studies. Publication bias was therefore not assessed for ICU-AW incidence (n = 9), MRC score (n = 5), MIP (n = 6), or weaning success rate (n = 6), as fewer than 10 eligible RCTs were available for each of these outcomes. Because statistical tests for publication bias and visual interpretation of funnel plots have limited statistical power and methodological reliability when applied to a small number of studies, we did not perform these assessments for these outcomes, thereby maintaining the rigor and validity of our analysis.

Standard NMA suggested that combined interventions had the highest SUCRA rankings for MRC score, weaning success rate, MIP, and MV duration. Discussion

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have generally suggested that IMT may be associated with improvements in inspiratory muscle strength and selected weaning-related outcomes in critically ill patients. For example, Alonso-Pérez et al. [12] reported that IMT was associated with improvements in MIP, although a significant benefit for weaning success was not demonstrated. Similarly, Andrade-Rebolledo et al. [23] suggested that high-intensity IMT may improve extubation success and shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation, although substantial variability across protocols and patient populations limited the generalizability of these findings. In a network meta-analysis of physical therapy interventions, Worraphan et al. [14] further suggested that combined strategies involving IMT and conventional physical therapy may be beneficial for weaning-related outcomes, although heterogeneity remained an important concern. In addition, Farley et al. [22] reported that the effect of IMT on broader physical function in critically ill adults remained uncertain.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have mainly evaluated IMT as a single overall intervention compared with routine care, with primary attention given to inspiratory muscle performance and weaning-related outcomes. Overall, these studies suggested that IMT may have potential benefits in these domains, although conclusions were not entirely consistent across reviews. By contrast, evidence on ICU-AW-related outcomes and other broader clinical outcomes remains limited. In this context, the present study extends the existing literature by evaluating IMT within the broader framework of ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies. Rather than simply reassessing whether IMT is beneficial overall, we used NMA and CNMA to compare the relative effects of different IMT-related strategies and to explore which IMT-related components were more likely to be associated with differences in clinical outcomes.

Interpretation of core results

In this study, NMA and CNMA were applied to examine the effects of different IMT approaches within ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies. Overall, the findings suggest that IMT-related interventions may be associated with beneficial effects on several clinically relevant outcomes. In addition, CNMA provided a complementary component-level analysis of the relative effects of different IMT components and combinations.

High heterogeneity was observed across studies for some of the included outcomes. This heterogeneity may be attributable to substantial differences across the included studies in intervention implementation details and baseline patient characteristics. For the total MRC score, the study by Liu et al. [37].was identified as the main source of heterogeneity. Compared with the other included studies, this trial additionally restricted participants to those with a Barthel Index score > 70, indicating a relatively higher baseline level of self-care ability and potentially better baseline muscle function. This difference may have introduced clinical heterogeneity and contributed to systematically higher total MRC scores in this subgroup, thereby increasing the pooled effect estimate for MRC score. By contrast, no apparent statistical heterogeneity was detected for ICU-AW incidence or weaning success in the preliminary analyses, and fixed-effect and random-effects models yielded materially similar estimates. Accordingly, no additional sensitivity analysis was undertaken for these outcomes, although the absence of a detected heterogeneity signal should not be interpreted as evidence of absolute homogeneity.

For MIP, the random-effects NMA showed that the 95% PIs for all interventions versus routine care did not cross the null value. Taken together, these findings suggest that the overall direction of the effect on MIP may be relatively stable across comparable settings, although the results should still be interpreted cautiously. In contrast, for MV duration, although some interventions showed statistically significant effects based on 95% CIs, only systemic physical rehabilitation + routine inspiratory muscle training remained statistically significant after incorporation of the 95% PIs, whereas the prediction intervals for the remaining interventions crossed the null value. This suggests that the true effects on MV duration may vary across clinical settings. Accordingly, the findings for MIP, and particularly for MV duration, should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

For ICU-AW incidence, the league table results suggested that IMT-related interventions were generally more favorable than routine care; however, the available pairwise comparisons did not provide sufficient evidence to establish the clear superiority of any single IMT regimen over the others. Given that the diagnosis of ICU-AW commonly relies on the total MRC score, the corresponding MRC results should also be interpreted cautiously. Although systemic physical rehabilitation + routine inspiratory muscle training had the most favorable SUCRA ranking, the evidence mainly showed statistically significant differences versus routine care for selected combined interventions, whereas evidence supporting superiority over other active interventions remained limited. Taken together, these findings suggest that intervention selection in clinical practice should not rely solely on ranking results. Although combined regimens tended to have favorable rankings, the current evidence does not establish that any single regimen is definitively superior across all patients or clinical settings. Therefore, intervention strategies should be selected flexibly according to individual patient characteristics, such as respiratory function, disease severity, and comorbidities, rather than recommended solely on the basis of SUCRA rankings.

Regarding key weaning-related outcomes, including weaning success rate, MIP, and MV duration, several combined strategies involving systemic physical rehabilitation and IMT ranked favorably in the SUCRA analyses. For MIP, systemic physical rehabilitation + mechanical threshold ranked highest, and complementary component-level comparisons suggested that adding systemic physical rehabilitation to mechanical threshold was associated with a statistically significant additional benefit compared with mechanical threshold alone. For weaning success rate, combined strategies also showed relatively favorable rankings, suggesting a potentially beneficial pattern for weaning-related outcomes. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The ranking results and pairwise comparisons mainly suggest that some combined strategies may perform better than routine care, but they do not establish that two-component interventions are consistently superior to all single-component interventions. In addition, for MV duration, the prediction interval analysis indicated substantial uncertainty across future comparable settings. Therefore, although combined strategies involving systemic physical rehabilitation and IMT may represent a promising direction within ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies, the current evidence does not support a definitive conclusion regarding the superiority of any specific combined regimen.

The CNMA results suggest that, except for MIP, the theoretically optimal combination therapies did not demonstrate statistically significant advantages over their individual components across the other outcomes. This may suggest that variations in component combination patterns, intervention timing, and intervention intensity across different therapeutic approaches could obscure potential synergistic effects among individual components.

Mechanisms of action

Mechanically ventilated ICU patients are prone to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) due to factors such as trauma, infection, and ventilator-associated lung injury (VILI). This inflammatory response triggers the massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). These cytokines accelerate skeletal muscle protein degradation by activating the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and inhibit muscle protein synthesis, ultimately leading to muscle atrophy and reduced muscle strength [58–60].

In the present study, both single IMT and combined regimens showed statistically significant advantages over routine care, which is consistent with previous findings [61–64]. A possible shared mechanism is that IMT may improve inspiratory muscle contractile efficiency and pulmonary ventilation, thereby reducing the activation of inflammatory pathways induced by hypoxia and carbon dioxide retention [65]. Meanwhile, regular inspiratory muscle contraction may promote local blood circulation, facilitate the clearance of inflammatory factors, and reduce the systemic inflammatory burden, thereby helping to prevent further progression of ICU-AW [66]. This effect may be primarily related to regulation of the inflammatory response, an important initiating pathway in the development of ICU-AW. Regarding ICU-AW incidence, mechanical threshold showed a potentially favorable ranking, which may be attributed to its role in protecting inspiratory muscles through precise load regulation [67]. The core advantage of mechanical threshold lies in its ability to provide individualized resistance loading according to the patient’s respiratory function status, thereby enabling precise and progressive IMT. On the one hand, precise loading may help prevent secondary injury caused by insufficient training or excessive loading. By enhancing inspiratory muscle strength and endurance, it may reduce deterioration in inspiratory function induced by respiratory muscle fatigue, thereby lowering the risk of disuse muscle atrophy associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation [68]. On the other hand, because inspiratory muscles constitute one of the body’s core muscle groups, improvements in inspiratory muscle function may influence systemic muscle function through the neuromuscular regulatory network [69]. Mechanical threshold may activate motor regulatory pathways in the central nervous system, promote the release of neurotrophic factors, protect neuromuscular junction function, and reduce motor neuron damage, thereby potentially contributing to the prevention of ICU-AW [70].

As established indicators of systemic muscle strength (MRC score) and weaning-related outcomes (MIP, weaning success rate, and duration of MV), improvements in these measures directly reflect the effects of interventions on muscle function recovery in ICU patients. The potential advantage of combined interventions may mainly derive from synergistic enhancement, which targets the core pathological pathways underlying muscle strength decline.

On the one hand, systemic physical rehabilitation involves early mobilization within 7 days of ICU admission, including active/passive limb training and joint range-of-motion exercises. This approach may effectively improve systemic blood circulation and reduce disuse atrophy of skeletal muscles, and its safety and effectiveness have been confirmed in previous studies [71]. Prolonged bed rest in mechanically ventilated patients tends to reduce satellite cell activity and decrease myofiber synthesis. In contrast, systemic physical rehabilitation may activate the proliferation and differentiation of satellite cells, promote muscle protein synthesis, and simultaneously inhibit ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS)-mediated muscle protein degradation, thereby delaying or reversing systemic muscle atrophy [72]. On the other hand, as a fundamental form of IMT, routine inspiratory muscle training specifically enhances the strength of inspiratory muscles, including the diaphragm and intercostal muscles [73]. In mechanically ventilated patients, prolonged bed rest and ventilatory support can readily lead to ventilator-induced diaphragmatic disuse atrophy and impaired inspiratory muscle function. Routine inspiratory muscle training may partially reverse this process by enhancing spontaneous breathing capacity, reducing respiratory load, and improving readiness for weaning [74]. Mechanical threshold provides specific, graded loading stimuli to the diaphragm and inspiratory intercostal muscles, directly enhancing the contractile force and endurance of the core inspiratory muscles and thereby establishing a localized functional basis for improving MIP [67]. However, mechanical threshold alone may be limited by a “ceiling effect” or by individual tolerance to the imposed threshold pressure [75]. In contrast, systemic physical rehabilitation may address the underlying contributors to inspiratory muscle weakness by restoring limb muscle strength, improving neural conduction, and reducing the depth of sedation [71]. Crucially, the combination of these two modalities may produce a synergistic effect. Threshold loading provides localized and targeted stimulation to the inspiratory musculature, whereas systemic physical rehabilitation may improve thoracic compliance, enhance whole-body circulatory efficiency, and reduce extraneous workloads imposed on the inspiratory muscles. Together, these effects may facilitate fuller expression of intrinsic inspiratory muscle contractile capacity and ultimately contribute to significant improvement in MIP. These two components may therefore form a comprehensive regulatory system involving prevention and control of systemic muscle atrophy, local enhancement of inspiratory muscle function, and protection of neural function. However, this study demonstrates that even when the potentially optimal combined therapy was compared with single-component therapies, statistically significant differences were difficult to establish. This finding suggests that single intervention measures may be insufficient to fully address multiple clinical goals, including reducing ICU-AW incidence, improving MRC score, shortening the duration of mechanical ventilation, increasing weaning success rate, and enhancing inspiratory muscle strength. This may reflect the multifaceted synergistic mechanisms involved in improving ICU-AW-related outcomes. ICU-AW results from interactions among multiple pathways, including inspiratory muscle function, systemic endurance, inflammatory status, and neural regulation. A single intervention component may regulate only one specific pathway and may therefore be insufficient to cover the full range of physiological conditions required for successful weaning.

Clinical implications

Although different IMT interventions vary in form, they share common therapeutic targets, including enhancing inspiratory muscle function, reducing the occurrence of ICU-AW, and improving respiratory regulation [76]. Based on our findings, clinicians should prioritize the implementation of some form of IMT over usual care alone in mechanically ventilated patients. For improving inspiratory muscle strength specifically, the combination of mechanical threshold and systemic physical rehabilitation appears particularly promising. This interpretation is consistent with the component-level comparison showing that adding systemic physical rehabilitation to a protocol already including mechanical threshold was associated with additional benefit for MIP. However, for other major outcomes, statistically significant differences between active intervention strategies were generally not observed. Therefore, regimen selection may reasonably be guided by local resources, clinical expertise, and patient-specific factors, rather than by assuming the clear superiority of one active protocol over another.

From the perspective of ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies, the present findings suggest that inspiratory muscle strength and weaning-related outcomes may represent clinically relevant upstream targets in the recovery process. Because MRC score is commonly used in the assessment of ICU-AW, interventions that improve inspiratory muscle function and facilitate weaning may be clinically meaningful within the broader rehabilitation pathway. Better inspiratory muscle function may support earlier weaning, while earlier liberation from mechanical ventilation may reduce prolonged inactivity and weakness-related complications, thereby potentially contributing to a lower risk of ICU-AW and better overall muscle function. Within this framework, IMT may be useful not only for respiratory rehabilitation itself but also as part of a broader strategy to support ICU-AW prevention and recovery [77, 78].

At the same time, these relationships should be interpreted cautiously. The current dataset does not allow direct confirmation that improvements in MIP occur in parallel with changes in MRC score or ICU-AW incidence. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal chain across these outcomes. Rather, this suggest that combined interventions incorporating systemic physical rehabilitation and IMT may have greater clinical relevance than isolated approaches alone. In particular, some combined strategies showed favorable results for MRC score, and complementary component-level analysis suggested that adding systemic physical rehabilitation to mechanical threshold may provide an incremental benefit for MIP. However, these findings do not establish the universal superiority of any single combined regimen; therefore, intervention strategies should remain individualized according to patient characteristics, clinical expertise, and available resources.

Limitations and prospects

Although this study used NMA and CNMA to systematically synthesize the effects of various IMT approaches on relevant clinical outcomes within ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the applicability of the findings.

Firstly, as the anchor comparator in this network meta-analysis, routine care exhibits implementation variability across included RCTs, which represents a potential source of heterogeneity. In the included studies, the specific practices of routine care varied substantially. In some hospitals, routine care mainly referred to standard ICU management, such as vital sign monitoring, regular repositioning, and general supportive care; in others, limited passive limb movement or occasional bedside activity may also have been included. However, these activities were generally not described as structured, protocolized, or rehabilitation-targeted interventions and were therefore not comparable to the predefined systemic physical rehabilitation strategies included in this review. Despite these implementation differences, the quality assessment of the included RCTs and analysis of baseline characteristics indicated that key baseline indicators were generally well balanced between the intervention and control groups, with no clear evidence of systematic bias. This suggests that variability in routine care was unlikely to compromise baseline comparability across studies or introduce baseline-level bias into the estimation of intervention effects. Therefore, such variability more likely reflects differences in the intensity and standardization of background care. Such variability may have influenced the magnitude of absolute treatment effects but is unlikely to have substantially invalidated the relative comparisons across intervention strategies. Nevertheless, this limitation suggests that future clinical studies should further standardize the definition and implementation details of routine care and report its specific components in detail, thereby improving comparability across studies and providing more homogeneous control data for subsequent meta-analyses.

Second, CNMA employed an additive model to explore the contribution of individual components to outcomes, assuming that the effects of each intervention component are independently additive. However, this approach cannot capture potential interactions between components, and the inability to model such interactions may have contributed to the largely null findings observed in some component-level comparisons. Furthermore, the limited number of included multi-arm trials [57] restricted the statistical power of our study to fit an interaction model. Therefore, the additive model was a reasonable analytical choice given the current study design. Future multi-arm clinical trials are warranted to adequately evaluate interaction effects between components and provide more precise evidence for optimizing clinical combination interventions.

Third, because the diagnosis of ICU-AW usually relies on the total MRC score, which requires patient cooperation, its assessment may be limited in deeply sedated, delirious, or unconscious ICU patients, potentially leading to measurement bias or underestimation of ICU-AW incidence [79]. In many critically ill patients, sedation or impaired consciousness may result in missing post-intervention total MRC scores and ICU-AW-related data. Consequently, researchers may need to rely on per-protocol analyses or imputation of missing data, which may further affect the robustness of the results. In addition, the total MRC score is assessed using manual muscle testing, which may introduce assessor subjectivity. Therefore, for non-cooperative patients, objective measurement methods, such as electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS), may be important for improving the accuracy of ICU-AW detection [80].

Fourth, this study combined various systemic physical rehabilitation interventions into a single category for analysis. The specific forms of systemic physical rehabilitation in the included studies included neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), bedside cycling exercise training, passive and active range-of-motion training, posture management, and out-of-bed activity training [27]. Because of the limited number of eligible randomized controlled trials and the sparsity of the intervention network, further stratification of systemic physical rehabilitation into specific subtypes was not feasible in the present CNMA. As a result, the estimated effect of this component should be interpreted as an average effect across heterogeneous forms of systemic physical rehabilitation rather than as evidence supporting any specific subtype. This within-component heterogeneity may have reduced the precision of the pooled estimates and obscured potentially important differences among individual rehabilitation modalities. Therefore, our findings regarding systemic physical rehabilitation should be interpreted at the category level rather than at the level of specific techniques. Future studies with more standardized intervention protocols and adequately powered head-to-head comparisons are needed to determine whether different forms of systemic physical rehabilitation differ meaningfully in efficacy. Meanwhile, based on the integrated logic of “upstream enhancement–downstream prevention and control” constructed in this study, actionable clinical pathways can be developed, together with supporting training manuals and quality control standards, to improve the homogeneous implementation of interventions under different medical resource conditions.

Fifth, for MV duration, Egger’s test suggested potential publication bias or small-study effects (P = 0.046). This finding raises the possibility that smaller studies reporting larger reductions in MV duration were more likely to be published, whereas studies with more modest or null effects may be underrepresented in the available literature. As a result, the pooled estimate for MV duration may have been inflated, and the apparent benefit of interventions over routine care in shortening MV duration may have been exaggerated. Therefore, conclusions related to MV duration should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should prioritize trial registration and the more comprehensive inclusion of unpublished or gray literature to reduce this bias.

Sixth, among the included studies, MIP and MRC score were not jointly reported in a sufficient number of studies to allow direct comparison between these outcomes. This limitation prevented us from determining whether improvements in inspiratory muscle strength, as reflected by MIP, occurred in parallel with changes in overall muscle strength or ICU-AW incidence within the same study populations. This limitation is important for interpreting the clinical meaning of the MIP findings. Although MIP directly reflects inspiratory muscle strength and is physiologically relevant to inspiratory muscle training, the current dataset does not allow us to determine whether improvements in MIP were accompanied by corresponding changes in MRC score or ICU-AW incidence. Therefore, the relevance of MIP in this review should be interpreted as mechanistic and clinically supportive, rather than as direct evidence of concordance with MRC-based or ICU-AW-based outcomes.

Seventh, according to the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence was not uniformly high across outcomes, reflecting both methodological limitations of the included RCTs and the complexity of rehabilitation research in critically ill populations. In particular, the certainty of evidence was lower for ICU-AW incidence and especially for MV duration, whereas the evidence for MRC score, weaning success, and MIP was of moderate certainty. These ratings indicate that some observed effects, particularly those for outcomes with greater uncertainty, should be interpreted cautiously. For MV duration, the very low certainty further suggests that the true effect may differ substantially from the pooled estimate. To strengthen the evidence base and support more robust clinical decision-making, future high-quality RCTs with larger sample sizes, clearer intervention definitions, more standardized rehabilitation protocols, and more transparent reporting of methodological details are needed. Greater consistency in the design and reporting of IMT and multicomponent rehabilitation interventions would also improve comparability across studies and help clarify the relative effects of specific rehabilitation strategies for ICU-AW prevention and recovery.

Eighth, blinding was difficult in some of the included studies. Because the interventions evaluated in this review required patient cooperation to complete the training procedures, participants could often directly perceive the intervention process, making patient-level blinding difficult to achieve. In addition, researchers were commonly involved in intervention delivery, operational guidance, supervision, and progress monitoring, which also limited the feasibility of personnel blinding. This issue reflects a practical characteristic of this type of rehabilitation intervention rather than a purely avoidable design flaw. Nevertheless, the lack of double blinding may still have introduced performance bias. Although some studies implemented relatively rigorous quality control measures, including randomization, blinded outcome assessment, and data integrity procedures, the potential influence of incomplete blinding cannot be fully excluded and should be taken into account when interpreting the findings of the present review. Future studies should, where feasible, further optimize blinding-related designs and strengthen blinded outcome assessment and standardized intervention procedures to minimize the potential influence of performance bias.

Finally, this study is subject to potential language bias, as only studies published in English or Chinese were included, whereas studies published in other languages were excluded. This may have led to the omission of relevant research, particularly intervention studies conducted in non-English- or non-Chinese-speaking regions, thereby potentially influencing the estimation of outcome effects. This language restriction resulted in an evidence base that may not fully encompass global research on rehabilitation interventions; therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the findings of this study. Future network meta-analyses should consider including multilingual literature to more comprehensively assess the true effects of interventions and mitigate the impact of language bias.

Conclusions

This study used NMA and CNMA to systematically synthesize the effects of various IMT approaches on clinically relevant outcomes within ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation strategies, including ICU-AW incidence, MRC score, MIP, weaning success rate, and duration of mechanical ventilation. Potentially optimal IMT regimens were identified for each outcome: The potentially optimal regimen for reducing ICU-AW incidence was mechanical threshold. Systemic physical rehabilitation + mechanical threshold was identified as the potentially optimal regimen for improving MIP. Systemic physical rehabilitation + routine inspiratory muscle training was identified as the potentially optimal regimen for improving MRC score, increasing weaning success rate, and shortening the duration of mechanical ventilation.

Although combined interventions encompassing systemic physical rehabilitation and conventional IMT ranked highest across multiple key outcomes, evidence supporting the superiority of any specific IMT regimen over another remains limited, with no statistically significant differences observed between most active interventions. CNMA further indicated that adding systemic physical rehabilitation to mechanical threshold may represent a promising strategy for improving MIP, although the current analysis cannot definitively isolate the independent effects of individual intervention components.

Compared with routine care, several combined strategies involving mechanical threshold, systemic physical rehabilitation, and routine inspiratory muscle training tended to rank favorably across outcomes related to ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation, inspiratory muscle strength, and weaning. However, evidence supporting the clear superiority of any specific active inspiratory muscle training regimen remains limited, and findings for the duration of mechanical ventilation should be interpreted cautiously. Taken together, these results suggest that inspiratory muscle training, especially when integrated with systemic physical rehabilitation, may represent a promising component of ICU rehabilitation. In clinical practice, the choice of specific inspiratory muscle training components should be individualized according to local resources, staff expertise, and patient-specific factors, rather than based on the assumption of universal superiority of any single regimen.

This study provides a more differentiated assessment of inspiratory muscle training–related strategies within ICU-AW prevention and rehabilitation, suggesting that combined respiratory rehabilitation strategies may be beneficial for some functional outcomes in ICU patients; however, the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to very low across outcomes. Its core contribution lies in clarifying the role of IMT as a key link connecting respiratory function improvement and weakness prevention, while explicitly acknowledging the lack of statistically significant differences between most active IMT regimens and the inconclusive nature of the CNMA findings regarding individual component effects. Although these insights may help lower the decision-making threshold for implementing IMT-based interventions in clinical practice, they should be interpreted as flexible, actionable pathways for precision rehabilitation rather than as definitive standards, given the nuanced limitations of the current evidence base. Despite limitations related to the quality and quantity of the included studies, this review supports the core value of IMT throughout the prevention–treatment–rehabilitation continuum for ICU patients. Future studies should further strengthen the evidence base through high-quality research, promote the standardization and clinical translation of interventions, and ultimately improve patient prognosis.
