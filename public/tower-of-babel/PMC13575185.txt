Interpretability of multimodal neural networks for prediction of visual acuity in patients with branch retinal vein occlusion
Authors: Soyoun Won, Kiyoung Kim, Youngseob Won, Samra Irshad, Sungyoung Lee, Seung-Young Yu, Seong Tae Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBranch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) can cause persistent visual impairment, and predicting long-term best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) after anti-vascular endothelial growth factor treatment remains clinically challenging. This retrospective proof-of-concept study developed multimodal neural networks to predict 12-month BCVA classes using retinal images and clinical metadata from treatment-naive BRVO eyes. The best internal model used OCT-horizontal scans, OCTA images, baseline BCVA, central subfield thickness, age, and sex. We evaluated performance using adjacent accuracy, exact accuracy, and mean absolute error under five-fold cross-validation, and we analyzed attribution localization using Pathway Attribution. We additionally performed a supplementary exploratory cross-disease feasibility analysis using an available diabetic macular edema (DME) OCT cohort with 24-month visual acuity outcomes. This analysis was interpreted only as supplementary exploratory feasibility evidence and not as disease-matched BRVO external validation. Overall, the results suggest that multimodal imaging and clinical metadata may provide complementary prognostic information after BRVO, but the modest predictive performance, retrospective single-center design, lack of disease-matched external validation, and limited attribution reliability require cautious interpretation and further prospective validation.Subject terms: Machine learning, Eye diseases, Vision disorders

Introduction

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) is the second most common cause of vision impairment due to retinal vascular disorders1. The etiology of BRVO implicates both compression of the retinal vein and endothelial wall injury, potentially culminating in thrombus formation2. Visual loss in BRVO typically arises from macular ischemia and edema. Clinical evidence demonstrates that sustained anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy can effectively manage macular edema and improve best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) among patients with BRVO3. Nevertheless, while some individuals experience prompt stabilization of macular edema, others present with refractory forms necessitating increased anti-VEGF injections and prolonged treatment duration. Furthermore, not all patients derive equivalent benefits from treatment, and sometimes BCVA fails to improve despite notable improvement in macular edema, indicating that multiple factors influence final visual outcomes irrespective of macular edema treatment. Numerous studies have been conducted to discern predictive factors for visual function after treatment. These predictors include age, baseline BCVA, ischemic areas, hemorrhage under the fovea, and baseline optical coherence tomography (OCT) findings4. However, the causal relationships between these factors are complex, making it challenging for clinicians to predict individual patient prognoses.

This study aimed to develop a deep neural network model to predict the BCVA of BRVO patients 12 months after the initial treatment. Machine learning may help identify predictive patterns in multimodal imaging and clinical variables associated with future visual acuity after BRVO. Predicting specific clinical outcomes may help clinicians plan follow-up and treatment more efficiently, potentially reducing patient anxiety and fostering trust in the treatment process.

Recently, deep learning has been widely used for analyzing retinal images5,6. However, the limited interpretability of deep neural networks is one important barrier to clinical translation, along with generalizability, calibration, prospective validation, regulatory considerations, and workflow integration7. In this study, we examined whether model attribution maps spatially corresponded to clinician-annotated BRVO-related regions, such as edematous or ischemic areas. Feature attribution methods offer insight into the model’s decision by highlighting the specific areas in the input image that the model critically relies on. However, the direct application of feature attribution methods in retinal datasets often produces confusing attribution maps with noise and uninformative pixels. To alleviate this issue and provide more interpretable attribution maps, we analyzed the model based on critical pathways8, which prune out unimportant neurons and apply gradient feature attribution to produce the attribution map.

We built upon Pathway Gradients, originally proposed by Khakzar et al.8, and presented Pathway SmoothGrad to compare the interpretability of the developed networks. For simplicity, we use the term “Pathway Attribution” to refer to Pathway Gradients and Pathway SmoothGrad. We use these methods as exploratory post hoc localization tools rather than as evidence of causal model reasoning.

Before assessing the models’ localization capabilities via Pathway Attribution, we first built the predictive models. We investigated the optimal combination of retinal imaging modalities for predicting post-treatment BCVA. Next, we evaluated how clinical metadata influenced the performance of the best model. We also examined Multimodal CutMix as an auxiliary augmentation strategy for multimodal input data. Because the revised analysis showed that the CutMix effect was metric-dependent, we interpreted this augmentation experiment cautiously and did not present it as a statistically robust overall performance-improving component. Materials

Dataset

We used an in-house dataset collected at a single center through a retrospective review of the medical records of patients with BRVO examined in the Department of Ophthalmology, Kyung Hee University Medical Center, from March 2018 to September 2023. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyung Hee University Medical Center (2022-10-059).

Multimodal retinal imaging is crucial for the ophthalmologic evaluation, treatment, and follow-up of patients with BRVO. The dataset contained three imaging modalities and four clinical metadata variables for each patient. Imaging data included color fundus photography (CFP), OCT in two orientations, and OCTA. Clinical metadata included baseline BCVA, central subfield thickness (CST), age, and sex. Eligible eyes were treatment-naive BRVO eyes with macular edema. Eyes were included only when reduced visual acuity was considered to be primarily attributable to BRVO-related macular edema or ischemic changes rather than unrelated ocular disease. Eyes were excluded if they had ocular comorbidities that could independently reduce BCVA or confound the attribution of visual outcome to BRVO-related pathology, including clinically significant cataract or media opacity, other macular diseases such as diabetic macular edema, age-related macular degeneration, epiretinal membrane with tractional change, macular hole, or myopic maculopathy, glaucoma or optic neuropathy, amblyopia, or previous retinal vascular occlusion other than BRVO. We excluded eyes with a history of retinal laser treatment (any type) or cataract surgery within 12 weeks prior to baseline in the study eye, as well as any prior history of vitrectomy or submacular surgery. Lastly, eyes were excluded if they presented with poor-quality imaging, defined as an OCT or OCTA signal strength of less than 7, that precluded reliable analysis.

All included eyes completed 12 months of follow-up and had available BCVA measurements at the 12-month visit. Eyes without BCVA data within ±1 month of the 12-month endpoint or with incomplete follow-up were excluded from the final analysis. Therefore, no imputation of missing 12-month outcome labels was performed. After the initial anti-VEGF injection, all subjects followed a pro re nata (PRN) regimen without a loading phase. Eyes with persistent macular edema were examined at 1-month intervals. In stable cases where macular edema had resolved, follow-up examinations were conducted at intervals of up to 3 months without further injection. Anti-VEGF therapy was administered using bevacizumab, ranibizumab, or aflibercept, and switching between these agents was permitted at the discretion of the treating physician based on clinical response. At each follow-up visit, BCVA measurements and OCT imaging were acquired on the same day. When an intravitreal injection was administered, these assessments were performed at the same visit before the injection.

CFPs, introduced in the 1950 s, offer fundus images mirroring fundoscopic findings9. Retinal hemorrhages, venous engorgement, venous sheathing, and retinal neovascularization are considered pathologic findings in BRVO patients. Previous works utilized CFPs with deep learning algorithms by constructing DNN models and applying them to BRVO screening and lesion segmentation10,11. In this study, CFP data were obtained using the VISUCAM 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec).

Today, OCT is the modality of choice for the diagnosis of macular edema. The morphology of the outer retinal layer evaluated in OCT images has demonstrated a correlation with visual function. Recent reports have explored the use of machine learning to predict outcomes following intravitreal anti-VEGF injections based on OCT images in various retinal diseases12. Macular ischemia can be confirmed through angiography, traditionally with fluorescein angiography (FA) and now with OCTA as well. In this study, spectral-domain OCT images were captured using the Cirrus 5000 (Carl Zeiss Meditec) at baseline.

OCTA facilitates non-invasive assessment of the retinal vasculature, including evaluation of the retinal capillary microvasculature, without the need for invasive dye. OCTA metrics serve as valuable biomarkers for identifying and monitoring macular ischemia, offering insights for visual prognosis in BRVO13. Capillary nonperfusion, seen as areas devoid of visible perfused capillaries, is one of the important quantitative OCTA metrics for assessing retinal ischemia in BRVO; prior OCTA analysis reported that macular ischemic changes were associated with visual and anatomical outcomes in BRVO14. All swept-source OCTA scans included in this work were acquired using the PLEX Elite 9000 (Carl Zeiss Meditec) device.

BCVA was measured at baseline and during subsequent visits following the initial treatment. All measurements were documented using a decimal visual acuity chart. CST is defined as the average thickness in the central 1 mm diameter circle of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinal Study group grid. PRN injections were administered when there was evidence of macular edema or subretinal fluid at the fovea in the OCT images.

In this study, we included 119 eyes of 119 patients who met the eligibility criteria. OCTA images were available for 107 eyes, and 12 eyes without OCTA were excluded from OCTA-based experiments. The average number of anti-VEGF injections during the 12-month follow-up was 3.5 ± 1.8. To further describe treatment burden, we additionally report the distribution of injection counts: the median number of injections was 3.0 (range, 1–8). Specifically, the proportion of eyes receiving 1, 2–3, 4–5, and ≥6 injections was 10.1%, 42.0%, 36.1%, and 11.8%, respectively.

For the supplementary exploratory cross-disease feasibility analysis, we used an available non-BRVO diabetic macular edema (DME) OCT cohort obtained from Kyung Hee University Medical Center. This cohort included baseline vertical and horizontal OCT images, baseline clinical metadata, and 24-month visual acuity outcomes. Annotation

For the attribution-map analysis, images were manually annotated, including CFPs, horizontal and vertical OCT B-scans, and SS-OCTA images. One clinician first manually annotated the images, and the masks were subsequently reviewed independently by two retina specialists. The experts manually delineated retinal hemorrhage regions on CFPs, intraretinal and subretinal fluid on OCT scans, and abnormal nonperfusion regions on SS-OCTA images. Any disagreement regarding lesion boundaries or the inclusion of abnormal regions was resolved through discussion and consensus among the retina specialists. Formal inter-grader agreement was not calculated, which we acknowledge as a limitation of the interpretability analysis.

Methods

Multimodal neural networks

An overview of the proposed neural network is shown in Fig. 1. In this study, we aimed to build a model to predict the BCVA of BRVO patients 12 months after the first treatment. We formulated 12-month BCVA prediction as a five-class ordinal classification task to reduce instability associated with small numerical differences in decimal BCVA measurements. However, this discretization is a methodological compromise: it reduces the resolution of the original continuous endpoint and depends on class thresholds. We therefore report exact accuracy and mean absolute error in addition to adjacent accuracy, and we interpret adjacent accuracy cautiously. Ordinal classification is beneficial when the order of each class is more significant than the numeric difference15; this is often relevant in clinical measurement settings where small numerical differences may be less meaningful than ordered severity categories16. We divided the range of BCVA into five classes with intervals of 0.2. BCVA values from 0.0 to 0.2, inclusive, are placed in Class 0. Values greater than 0.2 and up to 0.4 are assigned to Class 1; those between 0.4 and 0.6 are classified as Class 2; values ranging from 0.6 to 0.8 are assigned to Class 3; and values exceeding 0.8 are included in Class 4.

The architecture of multimodal neural networks used in this study. ResNet-18 is used as an encoder, and a feed-forward neural network is used as a metadata encoder. Output features from each modality are concatenated in the multimodal fusion step.

We used two image modalities, OCT-horizontal and OCTA, and four types of clinical metadata, baseline BCVA, CST, age, and sex. The image encoder is based on ResNet-1817. Each encoder was trained using a batch size of 2 over 60 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001. The metadata encoder consists of a feed-forward neural network with a rectified linear unit activation function. Finally, the multimodal model predicts outputs across five classes.

SmoothGrad SmoothGrad19 adds Gaussian noise to the input x. The procedure is repeated n times and averages the resulting maps to produce the final SmoothGrad map.

Critical Pathway The fundamental idea of the Critical Pathway is to select the critical pathway of neurons that contribute to the network’s output. Critical Pathway uses the Shapley value20 to select the critical neurons by their contribution. Because of the high computational cost, a Taylor approximation is used to calculate the Shapley value. Pathway Gradients uses Gradients to generate the attribution map on the pruned network. In addition to the original method, we considered SmoothGrad to form Pathway SmoothGrad. We define Pathway Attribution as a generic term for Pathway Gradients and Pathway SmoothGrad.

Related works

This study aimed to interpret neural networks that predict the treatment outcome of BRVO patients. To this end, we first used multimodal images and clinical metadata as inputs and then visualized the attribution map after ranking the importance score of the network’s neurons and pruning out unimportant neurons. Recently, a few studies have been conducted with a similar objective. Zhang et al.21 aimed to predict the treatment outcome of BRVO patients based on fundus fluorescein angiography and achieved an accuracy of 88.63% and F1 score of 0.89. However, they defined the treatment outcome as either good or bad based on macular edema or central macular thickness and conducted binary classification. Gallardo et al.22 used clinical metadata and OCT to predict anti-VEGF treatment demand in treat-and-extend regimens for retinal diseases including RVO-associated macular edema. In contrast, our target outcome is ordinal visual acuity, and our analysis focuses on multimodal retinal images and post hoc localization behavior. In our work, we provide both quantitative and qualitative analyses of interpretability. We interpret these localization results cautiously because attribution maps are post hoc explanations and do not establish causal model reasoning.

Results

In this section, we present experimental results of the models trained on various sets of modalities and clinical metadata. Given the small size of the dataset, each model was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation. The training and validation sets were divided at the patient level. Inputs from the same patient were matched before being provided to the network. Among the 119 eyes included in the study, OCTA images were available for 107 eyes. Therefore, all experiments involving OCTA were conducted on the 107-eye OCTA-available subset, excluding 12 eyes without OCTA data. Non-OCTA modality experiments were conducted using the corresponding available samples. We assessed each model based on adjacent accuracy (AAcc), exact accuracy (Acc), and mean absolute error (MAE). Adjacent accuracy is reported together with exact accuracy and MAE because the task is ordinal; we interpret adjacent accuracy cautiously and do not rely on it alone.

Modality selection

We considered three imaging modalities: CFP, OCT scans, and OCTA scans. OCT scans were further divided into OCT-horizontal and OCT-vertical. OCT-horizontal refers to the OCT dataset where the cross-section direction is from nasal to temporal, while OCT-vertical refers to the OCT dataset where the cross-section direction is from inferior to superior. The performance of single-modality and multimodal models is presented in Table 4. Overall, multimodal models trained on two modalities tended to enhance the performance of unimodal models by incorporating additional modalities, particularly regarding adjacent accuracy and MAE. However, including more modalities does not guarantee better performance. The model trained on OCT-horizontal and OCTA demonstrated the highest adjacent accuracy, the second-best accuracy, and the third-best MAE. These results suggest that multimodal inputs may improve prediction of post-treatment BCVA in patients with BRVO.

Supplementary exploratory cross-disease feasibility analysis using a DME cohort

We additionally performed a supplementary exploratory cross-disease feasibility analysis using an available non-BRVO diabetic macular edema (DME) OCT cohort with baseline OCT images and 24-month visual acuity outcomes. Baseline vertical and horizontal OCT images combined with baseline clinical metadata achieved an adjacent accuracy of 0.730 ± 0.070, exact accuracy of 0.387 ± 0.074, and MAE of 0.966 ± 0.168. This was higher than the clinical metadata-only baseline, which achieved an adjacent accuracy of 0.584 ± 0.123, exact accuracy of 0.174 ± 0.030, and MAE of 1.496 ± 0.372. These results suggest that baseline OCT images may add prognostic information beyond clinical metadata in long-term VA prediction, while direct disease-matched external validation in independent BRVO cohorts remains necessary.

Multimodal CutMix

We introduce Multimodal CutMix to perform data augmentation across different modalities. CutMix23 is a mixed sample data augmentation technique that combines two samples to generate augmented data. Prior work on mixed-sample data augmentation has shown that augmentation effects can depend on the evaluation target, including attribution or localization behavior24. CutMix showed higher adjacent accuracy but did not consistently improve exact accuracy or MAE. Interpretability analysis

We first evaluated models for predicting 12-month BCVA using various combinations of imaging modalities and clinical metadata, with Multimodal CutMix considered as an auxiliary augmentation strategy. We compared model performance using adjacent accuracy, exact accuracy, and MAE. In this section, we evaluate the models based on their interpretability.

Motivation

Although the networks were trained with retinal images and clinical metadata of patients with BRVO, it remains unclear whether clinically relevant regions used by clinicians are also important for the model’s prediction. To test the spatial alignment between clinician annotations and model attributions, we visualized the attribution map of the model and compared it to the annotations in Fig. 2. The attribution map is somewhat challenging to interpret as the gradients from the noisy background influence it as well.

Results

We calculated the Intersection over Union (IoU) between the binarized attribution maps created using Pathway Attribution and ground-truth annotations to measure the alignment between the critical area for the model and that for the clinicians. The binarized maps were generated by thresholding attribution values at 0.1.

We examined whether model attribution maps spatially overlapped with clinician-annotated BRVO-related regions, including intraretinal/subretinal fluid on OCT and abnormal nonperfusion regions on SS-OCTA. We quantified this spatial correspondence by computing the IoU between binarized attribution maps and annotation masks. This analysis was intended to provide an exploratory quantitative reference for attribution localization, not to prove that the model learned causal disease mechanisms.

The results on OCT-horizontal are shown in Table 8. Although some attribution maps showed partial spatial correspondence with annotated lesions, the IoU values were generally low, and the OCTA attribution maps were often diffuse. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence that the model reliably identifies clinically actionable pathological regions. The attribution maps provide a qualitative and quantitative reference for model localization behavior, but they do not establish causal reasoning by the model or clinical validity of the highlighted regions.

Discussion

This study suggests that multimodal fusion of imaging and clinical metadata may provide complementary prognostic information for 12-month BCVA prediction after BRVO in our retrospective cohort. For retinal specialists, such a model could eventually support risk stratification and follow-up planning by identifying patients who may require closer monitoring. However, given the retrospective single-center design and the modest predictive performance, the present model should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept research tool rather than a clinically deployable decision-support system.

We evaluated various retinal imaging modalities and identified OCT-horizontal scans, OCTA scans, and clinical metadata as the best-performing combination in our internal cohort. The supplementary exploratory DME cohort analysis further suggested that baseline OCT images can add prognostic information beyond clinical metadata in long-term VA prediction, but it should not be interpreted as disease-matched external validation of the BRVO model.

Because OCTA-based and non-OCTA-based experiments were performed using different effective sample sizes (107 and 119 eyes, respectively), direct comparisons between settings with and without OCTA should be interpreted with caution. The modality comparison should therefore be understood as an exploratory analysis of available modality combinations rather than a fully controlled comparison under identical sample sizes.

The interpretability analysis has several limitations. First, Pathway Attribution and related gradient-based attribution maps provide post hoc localization estimates and may not fully reflect the causal features used by the model. Second, the overlap between attribution maps and clinician annotations was modest, and OCTA attribution maps were particularly difficult to interpret. Third, the effect of Multimodal CutMix was metric-dependent and was not consistently observed across exact accuracy and MAE. Accordingly, we have revised the manuscript to avoid overstating the reliability or clinical meaningfulness of the attribution results.

The statistical analysis is limited by the small retrospective cohort and the five-fold cross-validation design. We therefore report fold-wise variability and interpret small differences between augmentation settings cautiously. In particular, the additional CutMix analysis did not demonstrate consistent improvement across exact accuracy and MAE, and the original statement regarding statistical significance has been removed or moderated. Future studies with larger independent cohorts are needed to determine whether augmentation strategies such as Multimodal CutMix provide reproducible performance or interpretability benefits.

Pruning less important neurons before applying an attribution method increased the plausibility and alignment of the attribution map on the OCT-horizontal image. However, the same procedure for OCTA did not produce a comparable improvement (Fig. 5). Even after pruning, the attribution maps from OCTA remain difficult to interpret because the attributions are scattered. OCTA images do not contain clearly irrelevant features in the same way as OCT-horizontal images, and every pixel may contain information related to flow-void signal and capillary nonperfusion. This raises the need for explanation methods tailored to different imaging modalities.

This study is also limited by the small retrospective single-center cohort and five-fold cross-validation design. A suitable publicly available BRVO dataset with the same 12-month endpoint and matched multimodal imaging/metadata configuration was not available; therefore, disease-matched external validation could not be performed in this revision. The supplementary exploratory DME cohort analysis was included only as supplementary exploratory cross-disease feasibility evidence and does not replace validation in independent BRVO cohorts. In addition, standardized ischemic versus non-ischemic BRVO classification was not available for all retrospective cases, which limited subgroup analysis of disease severity and cohort representativeness. Future studies should perform prospective validation using same-disease, same-endpoint external BRVO cohorts and identical imaging modalities. Because this cohort was treated with an initial anti-VEGF injection followed by a PRN regimen without a loading phase, the predicted 12-month BCVA endpoint may reflect both baseline disease characteristics and the specific treatment pattern used in this study. The generalizability of the model to cohorts managed with different loading regimens, retreatment criteria, or follow-up schedules should be evaluated in future studies.

Conclusion

This study presents a proof-of-concept multimodal deep learning framework for predicting 12-month visual acuity class after BRVO using retinal imaging and clinical metadata. In the internal cohort, OCT-horizontal, OCTA, and four clinical metadata variables provided the numerically best predictive performance in the internal cohort. A supplementary exploratory cross-disease DME OCT analysis suggested that baseline OCT images provide prognostic information beyond clinical metadata alone for 24-month VA prediction; however, this analysis was not disease-matched BRVO external validation. Pathway Attribution provided exploratory localization evidence, but the modest IoU values, diffuse OCTA attribution maps, small cohort size, retrospective single-center design, and lack of disease-matched external validation require cautious interpretation. Further prospective validation in larger independent BRVO cohorts with matched endpoints and imaging protocols is required before clinical deployment.
