Double intussusception: a rare case report and review of the literature
Authors: Rojan Singh Dangol, Bimarsh Acharya, Bijay Panday, Mandeep Dutta Joshi, Kushal Subedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Intussusception is one of the most common abdominal emergencies in childhood, characterized by the telescoping of a proximal bowel segment into the adjacent distal segment. While single-site intussusception is frequently encountered, double or simultaneous intussusception is exceedingly rare. Delay in diagnosis and management can lead to catastrophic complications, including bowel ischemia, necrosis, and perforation.Case presentation:We report a rare case of double intussusception involving jejunojejunal and ileoileal segments in a male adolescent presenting with acute abdominal pain, vomiting, bloody mucoid stool, and a palpable abdominal mass. Radiological evaluation with ultrasonography and computed tomography suggested small bowel intussusception. Exploratory laparotomy revealed two distinct intussusceptions at separate sites of the small intestine, both associated with pedunculated polypoid masses acting as pathological lead points. Surgical management included reduction, resection of the proximal polyp-bearing segment with primary anastomosis (5 cm), and a barrel loop ileostomy for the edematous distal segment (30 cm) without primary resection. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on day 12 with plans for stoma reversal and endoscopic surveillance.Clinical discussion:Double intussusception is clinically indistinguishable from single intussusception and is often diagnosed intraoperatively. Radiological modalities aid in early diagnosis; however, meticulous intraoperative exploration of the entire bowel is mandatory. The presence of pathological lead points, particularly intestinal polyps, necessitates surgical management.Conclusion:Double (simultaneous) intussusception is a rare but life-threatening condition. A high index of suspicion, thorough radiological assessment, and prompt surgical intervention, along with evaluation for pathological lead points, are essential to achieve favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Intussusception is defined as the telescoping of one segment of the intestine into an adjacent segment, leading to intestinal obstruction[1]. It is a common pediatric emergency, most frequently seen in children aged 2 months–2 years, with a male predominance[2–5]. In contrast, double or simultaneous intussusception is an exceptionally rare entity, with only a limited number of cases reported worldwide. Early recognition and intervention are critical, as delayed management is associated with poor outcomes due to ischemia, perforation, peritonitis, and shock[4]. Case presentation

A 17-year-old male presented with the sudden onset of abdominal pain since the morning of admission. The pain was severe, initially intermittent (lasting 10–15 minutes), and progressively increased in intensity and duration. He reported similar episodes intermittently over the preceding 6 months. This was associated with multiple episodes of vomiting and one episode of bright red blood mixed with mucus in the stool. There was no history of fever or recent gastrointestinal or respiratory infection.

On examination, the patient was anxious, restless, ill-looking, and dehydrated. Pathognomonic pigmentation was noted on the lips and fingertips, with similar findings reported in his father. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen with a palpable intra-abdominal mass measuring approximately 15 × 5 cm, extending from the epigastric to the right lumbar region. The mass was described as soft to firm and doughy. Bowel sounds were hyperdynamic.

Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (white blood cells count 20 430/cu mm) and a hemoglobin level of 10.4 g/dl. A plain abdominal X-ray demonstrated multiple air-fluid levels with dilated bowel loops. Ultrasonography revealed a “bowel-within-bowel” appearance in the right lower abdomen, measuring 12.8 cm in length and showing a “target sign” on cross-section.

Axial and coronal CT imaging confirmed the presence of telescoping bowel loops, consistent with small bowel intussusception at two distinct locations: one in the proximal jejunum and another in the distal ileum. Mesenteric fat stranding and mild proximal bowel dilation were noted. No definitive “double target sign” was identified preoperatively (Fig. 1). Surgical management included manual reduction of both intussusceptions. For the proximal segment (5 cm with three polyps), en bloc resection with primary end-to-end anastomosis was performed due to the high risk of recurrence from multiple polyps serving as pathological lead points. For the distal segment (30 cm with two polyps), significant mural edema and mild serosal contamination were present. Given the length of involvement and concern for an anastomotic leak in the edematous bowel, a barrel loop ileostomy was constructed without primary resection. The polyps in the distal segment were left in situ, with plans for definitive management at the time of ileostomy reversal.

Examination of the resected bowel segments revealed the lead points for the intussusception to be multiple pedunculated hamartomatous polyps (Fig. 3). Based on the triad of mucocutaneous pigmentation, family history (father with similar findings), and multiple hamartomatous polyps, a clinical diagnosis of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) was made. Genetic testing for the STK11 mutation was advised but not performed due to the lack of local availability and high cost (testing requires referral to India). The patient was referred for endoscopic surveillance and long-term oncology follow-up per clinical PJS guidelines.

The patient’s postoperative course was stable, with routine monitoring, intravenous antibiotics, and total parenteral nutrition (TPN). The patient tolerated gradual advancement to enteral feeds by postoperative day 4. TPN was discontinued on day 5. The ileostomy began functioning on day 2, with no evidence of high output or skin breakdown. Intravenous antibiotics were continued for 7 days. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 12, with plans for stoma reversal in 8–10 weeks. At the 4-week follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with good weight gain, no recurrent abdominal pain, and stable stoma function. The patient and his father expressed relief at the resolution of the chronic pain episodes.

Discussion

Intussusception is a form of bowel obstruction caused by invagination of the intussusceptum into the intussuscipiens, leading to venous congestion, edema, and eventual arterial compromise if untreated[1]. It is one of the most common abdominal emergencies in childhood and the second leading cause of intestinal obstruction after pyloric stenosis[4]. Although most cases in infants and young children are idiopathic, older children and adults are more likely to have secondary intussusception associated with a pathological lead point such as polyps, Meckel’s diverticulum, tumors, or lymphoid hyperplasia[5–8]. Double or simultaneous intussusception represents an even rarer entity, with very few cases reported in the literature.

Normal gastrointestinal peristalsis depends on the coordinated contraction and receptive relaxation of the bowel[9]. Any pathological lead point can disrupt this coordination, acting as a focus for abnormal peristalsis and initiating intussusception[4,10]. In rare situations, one intussusception itself may act as a lead point for another, resulting in compound or double intussusception[10,11]. Viral infections, tropical climates, younger age, good nutritional status, and rotavirus vaccination have also been implicated as predisposing factors[1,2,5]. In our case, multiple pedunculated intestinal polyps served as pathological lead points, explaining the occurrence of intussusception at two separate small-bowel sites.

This case meets the clinical diagnostic criteria for PJS (pigmentation + hamartomatous polyps + family history), although genetic confirmation remains pending due to resource limitations. PJS-associated polyps are a well-recognized lead point for intussusception in adolescents and young adults[12].

Clinically, intussusception presents with a wide spectrum of symptoms, including abdominal pain, vomiting, irritability, abdominal distension, and bloody mucoid stool. The classical triad of colicky abdominal pain, palpable abdominal mass, and red currant jelly stool is inconsistently present and unreliable for diagnosis[2,3]. Double intussusception is clinically indistinguishable from the single variant, and diagnosis often relies on imaging or intraoperative findings. Ultrasonography is the investigation of choice, demonstrating the characteristic target sign, while double intussusception may show a double target sign[13,14]. Computed tomography is superior in detecting pathological lead points and defining anatomy[7]. Surgical intervention is indicated in cases with lead points, failed non-operative reduction, bowel ischemia, or perforation, and careful inspection of the entire bowel is essential to avoid missing a second lesion[13,15,16]. Non-operative reduction with barium enema and clinical scoring systems have also been described, though they are less relevant in small-bowel intussusception with pathological lead points[17,18].

Resection of viable but polyp-bearing bowel in double intussusception is justified to prevent recurrence, as polyps themselves are pathological lead points[8]. Ileostomy is preferred over primary anastomosis when edema, the length of the involved bowel, or contamination raises the risk of an anastomotic leak[13].

Most published reports describe pediatric patients, and the majority are associated with an identifiable pathological lead point[10,11]. Reported lead points include Meckel’s diverticulum, intestinal duplication cysts, polyps, lymphoid hyperplasia, and postoperative changes[7,8,11]. Idiopathic double intussusception is exceedingly uncommon.

Yu et al described a case of double simultaneous intussusception caused by Meckel’s diverticulum and intestinal duplication, emphasizing the diagnostic difficulty and the intraoperative nature of diagnosis in such cases[11]. Gupta et al reported double-site intussusception in an infant, highlighting the importance of complete bowel exploration during surgery to avoid missing a second lesion[10]. Radiological literature has also described the characteristic “double target sign” on ultrasonography as a clue to preoperative diagnosis, although this finding is inconsistently present[14,19].

Across reported cases, surgical intervention remains the definitive management, particularly in the presence of pathological lead points or bowel compromise[13,15,16,17]. Resection and anastomosis are commonly required, and careful handling is advised to prevent tumor dissemination or iatrogenic perforation[8]. Our case adds to the existing literature by describing a rare anatomical variant with two distinct small-bowel intussusceptions associated with multiple pedunculated polyps and represents one of the earliest such reports from Nepal.

Limitations

This report describes a single patient with limited long-term follow-up (only 4 weeks). The rarity of the condition limits comparative analysis. Genetic testing for the STK11 mutation to confirm PJS was not performed due to the lack of local facilities and the high cost of referral to India.

Conclusion

Double (simultaneous) intussusception is an exceptionally rare surgical emergency, often associated with pathological lead points such as intestinal polyps. High clinical suspicion, appropriate imaging, meticulous intraoperative bowel exploration, and timely surgical intervention are crucial to prevent life-threatening complications and ensure good outcomes. In resource-limited settings, a clinical diagnosis of PJS is acceptable when the classic triad is present, and genetic testing can be deferred for research or specialized referral purposes.
