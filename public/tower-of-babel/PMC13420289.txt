Successful Diagnostic Confirmation of Malignant Pleural Mesothelioma With Extensive Pleural Adhesions Using Pleural Cryobiopsy Under Local Anaesthesia
Authors: Shotaro Otaki, Mikito Suzuki, Kaito Yano, Tomohiro Imoto, Reiko Shimizu, Tsunekazu Hishima, Kazuo Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLocal anaesthesia thoracoscopy using flex‐rigid thoracoscope is useful for diagnosing pleural lesions, but it is generally not indicated in cases with extensive pleural adhesions. We report a case of malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) with extensive pleural adhesions that was safely diagnosed using pleural cryobiopsy under local anaesthesia. A 65‐year‐old man with a history of asbestos exposure presented with chest pain and significant weight loss. A very small amount of pleural effusion, narrowing of the left lower intercostal spaces on chest computed tomography (CT) and negative lung sliding sign on transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) indicated significant pleural adhesions. Considering the high risk of general anaesthesia, biopsy under minimally invasive local anaesthesia was chosen. Precise localization of the lesions using positron emission tomography‐CT and TTE enables making the incision over the tumour. After minimal adhesion dissection, pleural cryobiopsy was performed and a definitive diagnosis of epithelial MPM was made.Keywords: flex‐rigid thoracoscope, local anaesthesia thoracoscopy, malignant pleural mesothelioma, pleural adhesions, pleural cryobiopsy

Introduction

Local anaesthesia thoracoscopy (LAT) is a useful technique for diagnosing pleural lesions. However, it is contraindicated in cases of severe pleural adhesions, [1] and surgical biopsy under general anaesthesia is selected instead. We report a case of malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) complicated by extensive adhesions where diagnosis was established via pleural cryobiopsy under local anaesthesia.

Case Report

A 65‐year‐old male construction worker with a history of asbestos exposure presented with a 10 kg weight loss over 3 months and chest pain. His performance status was one, requiring a cane for walking. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed multiple calcified plaques in the bilateral parietal pleura and diffused irregular thickening of the left parietal pleura without mass formation, raising suspicion of Stage III MPM. Furthermore, a very small amount of pleural effusion and narrowing of the left lower intercostal space were presented (Figure 1a). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed absence of the lung sliding sign throughout the left chest, indicating severe intrapleural adhesions. Pulmonary function test revealed restrictive impairment with percent forced vital capacity of 40.7%. Considering the recent rapid functional decline, and high risk associated with general anaesthesia, we first opted for the less invasive pleural cryobiopsy under local anaesthesia.

(a) Chest computed tomography showing diffuse irregular thickening of the left parietal pleura with minimal pleural effusion. Compared with the right side, narrowing of the lower intercostal spaces is observed, suggesting extensive pleural adhesions. A 2‐cm skin incision was made on the midaxillary line at the eighth intercostal space (arrow). (b) Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography demonstrating intense uptake in the left pleural lesion (a peak maximum standardized uptake value of 9.6). The arrow corresponds to the entry point into the pleural cavity shown in (a). The biopsy site is indicated by an arrowhead. (c) Intraoperative thoracoscopic view revealing a whitish tumour on the parietal pleura (arrow). The visceral pleura shows marked anthracosis (arrowhead), and extensive intrathoracic adhesions are present.

The tumour location was identified using preoperative CT, fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT (Figure 1b), and TTE. Sedation and analgesia were achieved via intravenous administration of midazolam, fentanyl and dexmedetomidine. Under right lateral decubitus position, a 2‐cm skin incision was made on the midaxillary line at the eighth intercostal space directly over the tumour, and subcutaneous tissue and intercostal muscles were bluntly dissected with Pean forceps to reach the parietal pleura. The intercostal space was narrowed, and gently being retracted using a retractor hook. Incision of the parietal pleura with Pean forceps revealed extensive adhesions as expected. Minimal blunt dissection of the adhesions between the visceral and parietal pleura, performed digitally and with a cotton swab (Naruke Thoraco Cotton, Kenzmedico Co. Ltd., Saitama, Japan), revealed anthracosis on the visceral pleura. A whitish tumour on the parietal pleura was identified beneath the incision using a flex‐rigid thoracoscope (LTF‐H290, Olympus, Japan) (Figure 1c). Cryobiopsy was performed using a 2.4‐mm cryoprobe (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany), activating for 10–14 s. As the biopsy site was directly beneath the incision, compression with a cotton swab was applied immediately after the cryobiopsy that a bleeding was kept minimal. Given the extensive pleural adhesions, a chest tube was not inserted. The intraoperative frozen section diagnosis indicated a malignant tumour. The surgical procedure lasted 58 min (including a 24‐min wait for the frozen diagnosis). The patient was able to walk 2 h postoperatively and was discharged the following day. Macroscopically, the specimen was measured up to 22 × 12 × 4 mm. Histologically, cuboidal tumour cells with enlarged round nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm proliferated, forming crowded glandular and papillary structures (Figure 2). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated calretinin and WT1 positivity, CEA and TTF‐1 negativity, loss of BAP1 and MTAP expression, confirming the diagnosis of epithelial MPM. After the initiation of ipilimumab and nivolumab on postoperative Day 22, the patient has remained progression‐free for 8 months.

Discussion

In this case, stage III MPM was suspected. However, the patient had experienced a weight loss, decreased performance status, and severe restrictive ventilatory impairment, indicating a high risk for general anaesthesia surgery. Notably, restrictive ventilatory impairment and poor performance status were known as poor prognostic factors for MPM after surgery [2]. Combined with recent clinical trial results, [3] systemic therapy was planned. Lack of a distinct mass precluded percutaneous needle biopsy. To avoid further deterioration of the patient's general condition and to initiate systematic therapy promptly, a minimally invasive diagnostic procedure under local anaesthesia was selected.

Generally, advanced pleural adhesions are considered a contraindication for LAT using flex‐rigid thoracoscope [1]. However, the procedure might be indicated for patients with a pleural space that can be accessed with minimal adhesion dissection, and where target lesions can be reached within that pleural space. Additionally, it is essential to employ safety methods that can obtain sufficient tissue samples.

In this case, a small amount of pleural effusion was thought to be an important finding for safe entry into the pleural space. Additionally, preoperative CT, PET‐CT, and TTE accurately identified the lesion, allowing a skin incision to be placed directly over it. When performing LAT in cases of severe adhesions, it is critically important to create the incision directly over the lesion. To obtain tissue samples, we considered using cryobiopsy. These factors ultimately led us to choose LAT over general anaesthesia.

Cryobiopsy is valuable in the diagnosis of MPM, providing larger tissue samples with minimal crush artefacts [4]. While previous reports on pleural cryobiopsy often had freezing times up to 6 s, we employ freezing times of over 10 s by ensuring haemostasis using cotton swabs [5]. In this case, the lesion was located directly beneath the incision, allowing for easy haemostasis under direct visualization. Consequently, we performed prolonged freezing up to 14 s and obtained large specimens.

LAT using cryobiopsy can be feasible for patients with severe adhesions if safe access to the pleural space is achievable and sufficient tissue of target lesions can be obtained within that area.
