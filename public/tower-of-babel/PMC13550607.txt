Effect of targeted wall shape thickness on flow response and recovery for engineering design
Authors: Yaren Dincoglu, Suyash Verma, Arman Hemmati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The effect of pipe-insert thickness on turbulent pipe flow response and recovery is numerically investigated at a Reynolds number of 25, 000. Three perturbation thicknesses are assessed, referred to as Fourier pipe-inserts, h=0.05D, 0.1D, and 0.15D. The relationship between insert thickness and recovery of turbulent kinetic energy and mean axial velocity is assessed to evaluate the impact of perturbation thickness. Increasing the perturbation strength (thickness) enhances flow turbulence due to increased geometric contraction. Excess turbulence is transported away from the wall, following an empirical power law that scales with h. Global parameters, such as skin friction and pressure loss, are analyzed for future design considerations. All cases demonstrate a localized skin-friction reduction that returns to equilibrium at varying distances downstream. The thickest perturbation (h=0.15D) has the largest skin-friction reduction and exhibits the largest pressure gradient within the pipe-insert, with an overall increase in pressure loss by 3.1%. These results demonstrate that insert thickness is a critical design parameter for Fourier pipe-inserts with significant implications for flow recovery and drag reduction.

Introduction

Moderate to high Reynolds number turbulent flows that are subjected to abrupt changes in geometry or surface condition fall into a class of perturbed or non-equilibrium flows that cause long-lasting changes in flow behavior. Some examples of this type of flow are a step change in roughness (smooth-to-rough)1, an axisymmetric square bar roughness2, and a sudden onset of wall curvature3. The response and recovery dynamics of perturbed turbulent flows are complex and require a deep understanding of flow mechanisms. However, a commonality among perturbed turbulent flows is a slow recovery to equilibrium. Persistent changes in turbulent flow behavior, particularly in pipe flow, can be used as a form of passive flow control device for industrial applications, such as designing transportation pipelines. Specifically, changes in flow near the pipe wall and in the core can cause drag reduction4 and enhance mixing5, thereby influencing heat and mass transfer. Such design enhancements can be particularly useful in closed-loop geothermal energy systems, where limiting heat loss to surrounding formations can help maintain production efficiency. Near-wall flow structures account for 80% of the energy in turbulent fluctuations, and the bursting events of energetic coherent structures increase turbulent drag6. Beyond the implications for friction drag and heat loss reductions, understanding the response and recovery of perturbed pipe flows are useful to predict long-term effects in pipelines. Favorable pressure gradients cause acceleration in bulk flow and have shown to increase turbulent mixing in the pipe core7. This has strong implications for applications for mixing, where large non-uniform concentrations need to be reduced or chemical reactions need to be enhanced8.

Early work on perturbed or non-equilibrium boundary layer flows, such as a step change in roughness (smooth-to-rough) at a height aligned with the smooth surface9, demonstrated a flow response that formed an internal layer containing the flow affected by the new boundary condition. This response was independent of the outer boundary layer, leaving it undisturbed. Smits, Young & Bradshaw10 investigated the effects of concave and convex bends of several angles, which can be classified as stronger perturbations, and found that this type of perturbation led to a prolonged recovery to equilibrium, extending up to 60-boundary-layer thicknesses downstream. Interestingly, the Reynolds stresses in the outer layer of the concave bend collapsed to well below the level of entry before returning to equilibrium, exhibiting a non-monotonic response to the perturbation. Smits, Young & Bradshaw10 stated that the strong spanwise variations, presumably due to longitudinal vortices, further complicated the flow past a concave bend. A similar non-monotonic flow response was reported by Antonia & Luxton9 in a turbulent boundary layer subjected to a change in roughness (rough-to-smooth).

Extending this work to pipe flows, Van Buren et al.1 conducted an experimental study on turbulent pipe flow encountering a change in surface roughness, transitioning from a rough surface to a smooth surface, with the flow response involving an overshoot in the velocity distribution and turbulence. This was consistent with previous studies9,11. This study also1 conducted a detailed investigation into the effect of a step change in roughness by examining terms in the Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equation and the transport equations of Reynolds stresses. The wall-normal gradient of the mean streamwise velocity was discovered to be the dominant term, governed by the wall-normal gradient of the Reynolds shear stress in the recovery process. Furthermore, it was determined that the flow response could be dictated by the disturbance in the outer region of the boundary layer, particularly by the shape and amplitude of the radial flow profile downstream of the perturbation. More recently, Smits et al.3 advanced this concept by examining the relaxation process downstream of a square bar roughness element in a pipe. It was found that redistribution of turbulence was the defining mechanism in the initial response of the flow, confirming that turbulent convection in the wall-normal direction played a critical role in the recovery process of perturbed turbulent pipe flows. When excess turbulence was produced near the perturbation, it was spread through turbulent convection in the wall-normal direction. Compared to boundary layer flows, where the convection process continued to the edge of a boundary layer, pipe flow experienced convection that stops before it reaches the centerline. This was demonstrated by Van Buren et al.1, Ding et al.2, and Ding et al.12.

Perturbation strength has been characterized by the wall-normal height of geometric perturbations13,14, and while there exists previous work on separation and reattachment over forward- and backward-facing steps of varying sizes15, there is less focus on the long-term recovery of flow past such perturbations. Moreover, relatively few studies have examined how the flow evolves past the reattachment point, despite it having important implications for turbulent transport and flow control3. A few of these studies2,12,16,17 have investigated the turbulent flow response and recovery downstream of perturbations with varying geometries, focusing on the transport and relaxation of Reynolds shear stress during the recovery process. Ding et al.12 evaluated the mean velocity and Reynolds shear stress distribution in turbulent pipe flow passing over three bodies of revolution with diameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d = D/3, \sqrt{2}D/3, \text { and } \sqrt{3}D/3$$\end{document} that were placed on the centerline. The largest body exhibited the strongest global acceleration, and the recovery was expected to persist for a long distance. Smits et al.3 also experimentally investigated a fully developed turbulent pipe flow perturbed by single square bar roughness elements of two heights (h/D = 0.05, 0.1) that were mounted on the wall of a pipe. The reattachment lengths were compared and the rate of turbulent transport was assessed, which revealed that the two cases followed the same power law trend (power of 0.6) in the near field (x/h < 30) when scaled with h. Yet the transport of Reynolds shear stress was twice as fast for the larger bar height than for the smaller one. The turbulence arising from the square-bar perturbation was more intense for the case of h/D = 0.1, and so was the rate of turbulent transport. Goswami et al.16 found similar results when numerically investigating square bar elements of equivalent height using RANS simulations. It was found that the thicker bar led to larger turbulent production and increased turbulence transport, making this a consistent observation in both experimental and numerical (RANS) studies. It is widely agreed1,2,13 that the overall recovery behavior of turbulent pipe flow past a perturbation is associated with diminishing oscillatory behavior exhibited in the mean velocity gradient and Reynolds shear stress.

More recently, Masoumifar et al.18 found that introducing targeted wall shapes that follow Fourier modes produced flow response and recovery dynamics similar to those observed in perturbed flows such as flows past square-bar elements and abrupt changes in surface roughness. These wall shapes are unique in their design such that they aim to target energetic modes that are self-similar in turbulent pipe flow19. Turbulent pipe flow can be decomposed in the azimuthal direction and ranked by the turbulent kinetic energy that each mode contains20 so that it can be described by a relatively small number of energetic modes. By targeting these modes, turbulent flow can potentially be controlled to reduce drag or induce mixing through the suppression of certain modes and enhancement of others. Three targeted shapes that followed distinct modes were assessed: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m=3, m=15, \text { and } m = 3+15$$\end{document}, each with different perturbation amplitudes. Here, m denotes the azimuthal Fourier mode number (i.e., the number of circumferential repetitions/lobes of the corrugation around the pipe). A unique feature exhibited by each targeted wall shape was the distinct regions of high rotation that correlated with the shape of the respective mode. These high rotation regions lasted downstream of the physical pipe-inserts and corresponded to the geometric feature of each case, where the maximum perturbation amplitude was located. The latter two cases, associated with the higher Fourier mode (m=15), exhibited a second-order response in mean and turbulent flow properties. This hinted at a relatively higher impact on the boundary layer structures compared to the case corresponding to azimuthal mode m=3.

The long-term response and recovery dynamics observed in literature hint at a potential turbulent flow control strategy12. In particular, manipulating mean axial velocity near the wall and core region of a pipe associates with a reduction in frictional drag and an increase in the bulk flow rate, making it useful for engineering design in energy or flow transportation applications. Drag reduction can also have an analogous impact on heat transfer, reducing the heat loss and making targeted wall shapes a functional technique for geothermal systems, where specific design factors are necessary to efficiently utilize high temperatures below the ground surface21. The current study expands on existing literature related to the flow dynamics over square roughness elements3,16 and Fourier inserts18,22 by exploring how increasing the thickness of gradual wall (surface) perturbation impacts turbulent pipe flow dynamics. These cases did not motivate flow separation, contrary to roughness elements. They represented geometrical variations in thickness of perturbation, contrary to studies of Fourier pipe-inserts, where the wall shape was varied with the same thickness and length. This constitutes a novel study with meaningful understanding of what thickness and length are most appropriate for drag reduction and additional mixing, while maintaining minimum pressure loss. This study provides the necessary information for further design of such passive flow manipulation in energy systems.

The paper is organized such that Section 2 describes the problem as well as the verification and validation of the results. Section 3 presents the main findings, including the response and long-term recovery of mean and turbulent flow parameters, and the effect of perturbation thickness on skin-friction and pressure losses. Section 4 summarizes the main conclusions.

Methodology

Effect of perturbation thickness on turbulent pipe flow response and recovery are investigated by numerically examining wall-based perturbations of three thicknesses (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h = 0.05D, 0.1D, \text { and } 0.15D$$\end{document}) using OpenFOAM. We select m=15 to remain consistent with prior targeted wall-shape pipe-insert studies and to ensure a sufficiently strong and persistent downstream response for quantifying thickness effects22. The influence of mode selection (lower/higher m) is treated as a separate design variable and is left for future work. Bulk flow Reynolds number is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Re = \rho U_\infty D/\nu = 25,000$$\end{document}, consistent with sub-surface flow conditions in closed-loop geothermal applications, which is relevant for future industrial use. Wall-based perturbations are referred to as Fourier pipe-inserts that can be installed between subsurface tubing pipe segments to influence turbulent flow behavior. Pipe-insert was originated from a study conducted by Van Buren et al.19, where a segment of pipe was reshaped to manipulate prominent structures in turbulent flow, particularly to excite and suppress targeted modes already present in pipe flow. Subsequently, long-term response and recovery of the pipe wall modification22 revealed that targeted shapes with higher Fourier modes resulted in a second-order mean and turbulent flow response that delayed the overall recovery back to equilibrium. Here, we use the term second-order (non-monotonic) flow response to denote an overshoot/undershoot recovery process, i.e., the flow does not relax monotonically to the fully developed state. This behavior has been linked to an imbalance between mean shear and turbulent shear stress during recovery and has been observed in perturbed wall-bounded flows, including pipe flows22. Hence, the pipe-insert in the current study is based on Fourier mode of m=15. The cross-sectional geometry of the Fourier pipe-insert follows a sinusoidal shape in the axial direction and has distinct corrugations in the azimuthal direction, as illustrated in the three views on the right of Figure 1. The azimuthal corrugations are repeated 15 times about the circumference because the insert targets the azimuthal Fourier mode m=15 (i.e., the number of circumferential repeats equals the selected mode number), and the maximum corrugation thickness is varied as h=0.05D, 0.10D, and 0.15D for the inserts labeled Case T1, Case T2, and Case T3, respectively. We keep the azimuthal mode fixed to isolate the effect of insert thickness on response and recovery. Prior work22 shows that mode selection (e.g., m=3 vs m=15) can significantly change the persistence and recovery length of the downstream disturbance. Therefore varying m is treated as a separate design variable and is left for future work. Maximum thickness occurs at the center of the pipe-insert. The cross-sectional shape of the pipe-insert changes from a smooth pipe by gradually increasing to the maximum thickness, h, then symmetrically decreasing toward the end of the pipe-insert. Domain of the simulated pipe containing the pipe-insert is shown in Figure 1, where the pipe domain extends from -27D to +92D in the axial direction, and the pipe-insert itself extends from -2D to +2D, for all three cases. Numerical grid is shown in Figure 2. The grid distribution and quality for three cases fit the requirements of standard k-\varepsilon model. The base case contains \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$9.86 \times 10^6$$\end{document} structured elements, with the radial and axial directions expanding at a rate of 1.03 and 1.2, respectively.

At the inlet boundary, a three-dimensional fully developed turbulent inflow condition is applied, by mapping a fully developed outlet profile of a 220D length pipe. It is important to mention that all simulations are performed under a constant bulk flow-rate (constant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_b$$\end{document}) condition for each case, and the streamwise pressure gradient adjusts to satisfy the imposed flow rate. A Neumann boundary condition (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial \phi / \partial n = 0$$\end{document}, where ϕ is any flow variable) is implemented for both velocity and pressure at the outlet boundary, and a no-slip wall boundary condition is imposed on the pipe wall.

All pipe flows are modeled using OpenFOAM, a finite volume-based open-source program. The Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) Standard k-\varepsilon model is utilized for all cases. Second-order spatial and temporal discretizations methods are used. Simple-Implicit-Pressure-Linked-Equation (SIMPLE) is utilized to couple pressure and velocity fields.

Verification and validation

Reliability of simulation results with respect to both domain and grid is ensured using independent sensitivity studies. Final results are validated with available experimental data. In this study, we use flow over a sinusoidal wavy wall in a channel to validate simulations of the pipe-inserts. This flow problem is chosen due to the comparable features between flow over a wavy channel wall and the Fourier pipe-insert, including their induced axial flow behavior that contributes to the transport of momentum. In the absence of detailed quantitative experimental data on the flow over Fourier pipe-inserts, we opt to validate numerical results in three steps: (1) qualitative comparison with experiments of Van Buren et al.19, which was reported by Masoumifar et al.18, (2) existing performance studies on modeling flow over square bar roughness elements16, and (3) modeling turbulent flow past gradual wall (surface) variation or perturbation using wavy channel flow experiments of Hudson et al.23 and DNS results of Errico & Stalio24. Combining these efforts, we believe that the numerical results are sufficiently accurate for comparative assessment of the wall-perturbation thickness effect on bulk flow response and recovery observed in the case of Fourier pipe-inserts. Results and discussion

Effect of pipe-insert thickness on performance

For geothermal systems that require high heat loads, specific design factors such as fluid flow rate and well depth are critical in achieving efficient energy extraction and minimized heat losses21,29. Smits et al.3 described the periodic introduction of wall modifications as a potential method to minimize pressure losses and transport stress concentrations away from the wall to reduce drag. Our study thus far shows that stresses (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \overline{u_xu_r}/ u_{\tau }^2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \overline{u_xu_x}/ u_{\tau }^2$$\end{document}) move away from the pipe wall with a power-law trend, such that the rate of transport increases with pipe-insert thickness. This signifies a possible technique for frictional drag reduction that can last downstream of the pipe-insert, making it a useful strategy for energy transport pipelines, as well as possibly retaining heat in geothermal systems. Furthermore, the increased turbulence with a thicker pipe-insert, as demonstrated in the axial profile of k in Figure 7, suggests improved mixing and potential heat transfer in the pipe core with increased pipe-insert thickness. However, the increased geometric contraction by a thicker pipe-insert can lead to minor losses in flow, thereby impacting overall energy loss. This section relates the effect of pipe-insert thickness on response and recovery analysis to industrial application through axial evolution of time-average skin-friction coefficient, pressure gradient, and overall pressure losses with the addition of a pipe-insert.

To evaluate the performance of pipe-inserts at varying thicknesses, we first investigate the relationship between insert thickness and time-averaged skin-friction coefficient. An increase in insert thickness introduces a local resistance in the flow that affects the overall pressure distribution. Figure 16(b) presents the axial evolution of pressure gradient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\frac{dP}{dx}}/{(\frac{dP}{dx})_b}$$\end{document}) for varying thicknesses of the Fourier pipe-insert, normalized by its far downstream value at x/D = 50. This can be explained by the severity of geometric transition and velocity gradient, as shown in Figure 10(c). As the pipe-insert thickness decreases, the geometric transition becomes more gradual, resulting in weaker pressure gradients. In a qualitative sense, the pipe-insert produces a localized contraction–expansion effect that resembles the acceleration–recovery behavior of a mild nozzle/diffuser section, where the accelerated bulk flow yields a favorable mean pressure gradient (dP/dx<0) within/near the insert, and the magnitude of this pressure-gradient excursion increases with insert thickness (Fig. 18(b)). However, unlike a converging–diverging nozzle optimized for static-pressure recovery, the present geometry is designed for targeted flow manipulation. Thus the net outcome is a trade-off between localized acceleration/redistribution and a finite overall pressure-loss penalty. Consistent with this interpretation, the overall pressure-drop increase remains modest but grows monotonically with thickness.

For energy transport or geothermal systems, one of the main priorities is the reducing of the pump power requirement33. For example, utilizing passive elements, such as vortex generators, has been previously assessed for enhancing convective heat transfer and mixing in pipes for different industries5. However, these devices are accompanied by a significantly increased pressure loss, and require design assessment to manage this drawback. The pressure drop (Δ P) is measured between sections upstream and downstream of each pipe-insert and compared to an undisturbed pipe to determine the effect of insert thickness on pressure drop. The percent increase in Δ P for Case T1, Case T2, and Case T3 are 0.49%, 1.6%, and 3.1%, respectively, confirming that the enhanced turbulence with increased thickness results in higher pressure loss.

Conclusion

This study numerically investigates the effect of thickness on turbulent pipe flow response and recovery, with three thicknesses examined: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h = 0.05D, 0.1D, \text { and } 0.15D$$\end{document}. The immediate response is evaluated through radial profiles of several flow parameters, such as Reynolds stresses, mean axial velocity, and mean axial velocity gradient. Magnitude of Reynolds shear stress increases by a factor of ∼ 4 from Case T1 to Case T2, and ∼ 2 from Case T2 to Case T3. A similar pattern is shown for the normal stresses, where an increase by a factor of ∼ 3.9 is observed from Case T1 to Case T2, and ∼ 2 from Case T2 to Case T3. A higher rate of transport is observed for a thicker pipe-insert, such that all cases follow a consistent power-law redistribution trend.

Compared to the effect of varying the perturbation length, the present results confirm that thickness influences the immediate turbulence radial transport, whereas insert length primarily extends the downstream relaxation process. This identifies the insert thickness (h) and pipe radius (R) as the empirically determined near-field and far-field length scales, respectively. Specifically, this study provides more insights into how the rate of transport scales with pipe-insert thickness in case of gradual wall variations, represented by the Fourier pipe-insert shape. However, it remains constant with insert length, as shown in Dincoglu, Y27.. This finding suggests that the insert thickness primarily controls the rate at which the increased Reynolds shear stress moves away from the wall. It is important to mention that the present results are also obtained at a fixed bulk Reynolds number and therefore should be interpreted as Reynolds-number specific. Nonetheless, Reynolds-number dependence for the same targeted Fourier-mode pipe-insert concept has been quantified by Masoumifar et al.18 over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Re \approx 5\times 10^{3}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.58\times 10^{5}$$\end{document}, showing that recovery varies systematically with Reynolds number, exhibits an asymptotic trend for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Re \gtrsim 7.5\times 10^{4}$$\end{document}, and scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_r/D \sim Re^{4}$$\end{document} for both mean velocity and turbulence kinetic energy. They also report power-law trends for key response features (e.g., peak locations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p/D \sim Re^{4/3}$$\end{document}) and a downstream decay of peak Reynolds shear stress approximately as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x/D)^{-1/3}$$\end{document}. Based on these established Reynolds-number trends, we expect Reynolds number to similarly modulate the magnitude and streamwise extent of the thickness-induced response reported here; a detailed Re–thickness parametric study is left for future work.

Turbulent flow behavior past Fourier pipe-inserts of varying thicknesses is consistent with previously studied perturbed cases at varying heights. The thickest pipe-insert (h = 0.15D) demonstrates the highest overall velocity gradient along the radial profile downstream of the insert, indicating a sustained acceleration in the core region that is accompanied by a deceleration near the wall. The thickest pipe-insert demonstrates the highest maximum skin-friction reduction at 16.4%,which persists furthest downstream, recovering at an axial location of x/D = 4.2.

These results demonstrate that the Fourier pipe-insert can be optimized for future applications to promote efficient flow transportation and reduced heat losses in the energy industry. A greater insert thickness amplifies pressure gradient within the pipe-insert, resulting in pressure losses of 3.1% higher than an undisturbed pipe. Thus, a strategic design must balance between skin friction reduction and added pressure drag introduced by thicker pipe-inserts. The findings support design efforts for developing an effective flow manipulation in geothermal energy systems, such that we may reduce wall frictional drag and impose local mixing, while minimizing pressure losses.
