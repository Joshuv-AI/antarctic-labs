κ-Carrageenan-Based Hydrogels Support Viability and Odontogenic Differentiation of Stem Cells From the Apical Papilla
Authors: Muneera A. Alajlan, Rhodanne Nicole A. Lambarte, Terrence S. Sumague, Abdurahman A. Niazy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and AimsImmature teeth with pulp necrosis remain challenging because conventional root canal treatment does not restore pulp-dentin vitality. Hydrogel scaffolds for regenerative endodontics should support stem cell viability and provide a microenvironment favourable for dentin-pulp regeneration. This study evaluated κ-Carrageenan (κC)-based hydrogels, with and without gelatin, as scaffolds for stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAP), and examined how formulation-dependent properties influenced SCAP viability and odontogenic differentiation.MethodsHydrogels were prepared as 1% gelatin, 1.5% κC, 2.5% κC, 1.5% κC+gelatin (κCG) and 2.5% κCG, with cells cultured without hydrogel as a control. Physicochemical characterisation included scanning electron microscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, rheology, degradation and swelling at days 1, 3, 7 and 14. SCAP were isolated and characterised using flow cytometry. Cell viability, metabolic activity, spreading, migration, alkaline phosphatase activity, Alizarin Red S staining and qPCR of differentiation-related markers were also assessed.ResultsThe hydrogels showed formulation-dependent microstructure, degradation, swelling and viscoelastic properties. κC-containing formulations behaved as viscoelastic solids, and 2.5% κCG exhibited the highest storage modulus and lowest Tan (δ). SCAP remained predominantly viable in all groups. Gelatin-containing hydrogels improved long-term cytocompatibility and greater cell spreading compared to lower-concentration κC alone. Migration was comparable across groups. During differentiation, κC-containing formulations showed higher alkaline phosphatase activity and mineral deposition, with increased late-stage mineralisation observed in 2.5% κCG. Gene expression analysis indicated early upregulation of RUNX2 and BSP and increased late-stage expression of OPN, OCN, DMP1 and DSPP, with a more evident maturation-associated response in 2.5% κCG.ConclusionκC-based hydrogels supported SCAP viability and promoted odontogenic differentiation in vitro. Gelatin incorporation improved late-stage mineralisation and odontogenic marker expression, with 2.5% κCG demonstrating the most favourable overall response.Clinical RelevanceκC-gelatin hydrogels may serve as promising scaffold candidates for dentin-pulp regenerative strategies by supporting SCAP survival and odontogenic maturation within a tunable hydrogel microenvironment.

Introduction

Dental pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis are the predominant causes of tooth morbidity. Although conventional root canal treatment effectively reduces or eliminates intracanal infection and prevents healing of apical disease while preserving the tooth in function, it does not restore the biological vitality, sensory function, immune defense or regenerative capacity of the pulp-dentin complex.[1], [2], [3] This limitation is particularly significant in immature permanent teeth, where thin dentinal walls and an open apex increase the risk of root fracture and compromise long-term prognosis.4 Regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) have been developed to address this unmet need by facilitating apical healing and continued root development through biologically-driven regeneration rather than inert obturation.4,5 Recent studies have shown growing interest in bioactive materials that can modulate dental pulp cell behaviour, and support biologically based pulp repair.6

From a tissue-engineering perspective, successful pulp-dentin regeneration requires the coordinated action of resident or delivered stem and progenitor cells, bioactive signals and a permissive three-dimensional extracellular matrix (ECM)-like scaffold.7,8 In regenerative endodontics, cell homing approaches are particularly appealing because they aim to recruit endogenous stem/progenitor pools and may reduce regulatory complexity compared to cell transplantation.7,9 Among potential cell sources, stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAP) are highly relevant due to their origin in developing apical tissues involved in root maturation and can contribute to odontoblast-like differentiation and hard tissue formation under appropriate microenvironmental signals.10,11 Therefore, biomaterials capable of establishing a supportive physicochemical niche for related stem/progenitor cells adhesion, spreading, survival, migration and odontogenic differentiation are central to improving the consistency of next-generation REPs strategies.[11], [12], [13]

Hydrogels are particularly promising as endodontic scaffolds because injectable systems can conformally fill complex root canal anatomy while providing a hydrated ECM-mimetic environment that supports nutrient diffusion and cell infiltration.14,15 Biofunctional hydrogel strategies further aim to control the regenerative microenvironment without necessarily requiring transplanted cells, underscoring the importance of scaffold-driven structure-function effects.16 Recent work has also shown that hydrogel systems can be combined with additional bioactive components to provide antibacterial and angiogenic benefits relevant to dental pulp regeneration.17 Notably, the immunomodulatory features of scaffold materials are increasingly recognised as essential, as regenerative success depends on balancing antimicrobial and inflammatory control with pro-healing signalling that supports angiogenesis and odontoblastic differentiation.18

Gelatin-based biomaterials represent a practical scaffold class for dental tissue engineering because gelatin is collagen-derived, biocompatible and provides cell-adhesive motifs that can support attachment and spreading.19,20 Recent pulp-focused studies demonstrate how functional additives can further enhance the performance of gelatin-based biomaterials, including bioactive peptide/hormone loading to stimulate endogenous pulp regeneration, extracellular vesicle integration to promote odontogenesis, and composite strategies that couple ionic signalling with hydrogel mechanics to support functional repair.[21], [22], [23] Nonetheless, gelatin-dominant networks can exhibit limitations relevant to endodontic applications, including mechanical weakness and susceptibility to rapid enzymatic degradation, motivating composite designs to improve stability while retaining bioactivity.20

Marine sulfated polysaccharides represent a promising avenue for enhancing the biofunctionality of hydrogels, because their anionic sulfate chemistry can mimic certain characteristics of native glycosaminoglycans and influence hydration, ionic interactions and protein binding.24 κ-Carrageenan (κC), a linear sulfated galactan, forms physically crosslinked gels and has been extensively reviewed as a versatile biomaterial for drug delivery and tissue-engineering applications.24,25 Although the biomedical application of κ-Carrageenan has been most advanced in wound healing and delivery systems, its gelation behaviour, hydrophilicity and sulfate-bearing backbone provide a strong rationale for investigating κC-containing matrices as ECM-interactive scaffolds in mineralising tissues.26 Importantly for dental applications, κC has emerged in dentin-pulp regeneration research as a component of multipolymer scaffolds, supporting the feasibility of κC-based biomimetic microenvironments for pulp-related applications.27

These findings justify further investigation of κC-gelatin composite hydrogels as potential endodontic scaffolds, where gelatin contributes cell-adhesive bioactivity and κC introduces sulfate-rich polysaccharide chemistry with tunable mechanics and hydration. However, direct evidence linking κC concentration and κC-gelatin blending to outcomes relevant to SCAP such as viability, spreading, migration and odontogenic differentiation, remains limited. Systematic structure-property-function comparisons are necessary to guide the formulation selection for potential clinical translation. Therefore, the present study was designed to fabricate κ-Carrageenan hydrogels and κ-Carrageenan+gelatin composite hydrogels across defined polymer concentrations, characterise their formulation-dependent physicochemical and rheological properties, and evaluate SCAP responses with regards to cytocompatibility, cell spreading, migration and odontogenic/dentinogenic differentiation using functional assays and relative gene expression markers.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethical Committee of King Saud University, College of Medicine, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (Ref. No. 23/0062/IRB; Research Project No. E-22-7438). Written informed consent was obtained from all donors and/or their legal guardians prior to collection of freshly extracted mandibular third molars with immature roots and attached apical papilla tissue for SCAP isolation.

Hydrogel fabrication

κ-Carrageenan (κC) and gelatin powders (Sigma-Aldrich; κC: Catalog no. 22048; gelatin: from bovine skin, Type B, ∼225 Bloom, Catalog no. G9391) were used to prepare hydrogels using established methods with minor modifications.28,29 κC was dissolved in Milli-Q grade distilled water under continuous stirring at 60°C for 30 min to create homogeneous solutions at final concentrations of 1.5% and 2.5% (w/v). These concentrations were selected to enable comparison of lower and higher polymer content within a practical range for injectable hydrogel preparation, with expected differences in network density and structural stability.30 Gelatin was prepared at 1% (w/v) and added as a constant bioactive component to improve cell-material interaction, while κ-Carrageenan concentration served as the main formulation variable.27,31 For composite formulations, gelatin and κC were combined at the same final concentrations used in the single-polymer groups and mixed under continuous stirring to obtain homogeneous κCG formulations. The experimental groups were as follows: 1% gelatin, 1.5% κC, 2.5% κC, 1.5% κC + gelatin (κCG), 2.5% κCG, and control group (cells cultured without hydrogel). Hydrogel solutions were sterilised by autoclaving at 121°C for 30 min to ensure sterility and transferred into sterile 1 mL syringes for handling and accurate dispensing, then cooled at 4°C for 24 h before use.

Hydrogel physicochemical characterisation

Hydrogel microstructures were assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JEOL JSM- 6360LV; JEOL Ltd.). The samples were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series for 10 min at each concentration, followed by 15 min in absolute ethanol. The dried specimens were mounted on aluminium stubs and sputter-coated with gold using a JEOL JFC1100 sputter instrument (JEOL Ltd.) to enhance conductivity. SEM images were acquired at 10 to 20 kV and magnifications of × 1000 to evaluate the surface morphology, porosity and network architecture.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed on κ-Carrageenan and gelatin in powder form, as well as on the hydrogel samples evaluated in their hydrated state. Spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) diamond crystal over the range of 4000 to 600 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm−¹ with 32 scans per sample.32

Hydrogel degradability was evaluated by incubating preweighed hydrogel samples of each formulation in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4; Gibco Invitrogen) at 37°C. Samples were collected on days 1, 3, 7 and 14, gently blotted to remove surface water, and weighed. The percentage mass loss was calculated as the percentage of mass loss relative to the initial mass using the formula: Degradation rate (%) = [(W0 - Wt) / W0)] × 100, where W₀ represents the initial sample weight and Wₜ represents the sample weight at the respective time point.

Swelling behaviour was assessed by immersing preweighed hydrogel samples in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37°C. Samples were collected on days 1, 3, 7 and 14 to capture both the early water-uptake phase and subsequent stabilisation of the hydrogel network over the first 2 weeks, consistent with commonly used hydrogel characterization intervals.[33], [34], [35] At each time point, the samples were removed, gently blotted and reweighed. The swelling ratio (SR) was calculated as: Swelling ratio (%) = ([Wt - W0] / W0) × 100, where Wt is the weight of the swollen hydrogel at a given time t, and W0 is the initial dry weight of the hydrogel.

Rheological properties were measured using an MCR-72 rheometer (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) equipped with a 25 mm parallel plate geometry and 1 mm gap at 25°C. Frequency sweep tests were conducted in the linear viscoelastic range (0.1-100 rad/s) at constant strain, as described previously.25,36 The storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), complex viscosity (η*) and loss tangent (tan δ = G″/G′) were recorded to assess the viscoelastic behaviour.

Isolation and characterisation of SCAPs

Freshly extracted sound mandibular third molars with immature roots and attached apical papilla tissue were obtained from the Oral Surgery Clinic at the Dental University Hospital after informed consent was obtained. Apical papilla tissues were collected from 4 independent donors (n = 4). Teeth were placed in transport medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 2% antibiotic-antimycotic (all from Gibco, Invitrogen) and transported on ice. Under a sterile laminar flow cabinet (LabGard Class II, Type A/B3 laminar flow cabinet; NuAire), the apical papilla tissue was isolated using a modified explant protocol.37 The tissue was washed 3 times with 500 μL PBS, gently separated from the root tip, minced into approximately 3-mm fragments, and immersed in DMEM containing 2% antibiotic-antimycotic. Tissue fragments were transferred to 6-well culture plates (Greiner Bio-One GmbH), and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, and cultures were maintained for 2 to 3 weeks. Medium changes were performed every 3 days until cells reached approximately 80% confluency. Cells were subsequently subcultured and used between passages 4 and 6.38

Cell characterisation was performed using flow cytometry at the Stem Cell Unit of the Department of Anatomy, College of Medicine, King Saud University. Approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL at passage 3 were harvested, washed with PBS and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 1% FBS). For immunophenotyping, approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells were allocated for staining with each antibody. Cells were incubated for 30 min on ice with antibodies against CD146, CD73, CD29, CD34, CD14 and HLA-DR (Dako, Agilent Technologies) at a 1:10 dilution in FACS buffer.39 Appropriate FITC-, PE- and APC-conjugated isotype controls were included. After incubation and washing, a minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired using a FACSCalibur Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analysed using CellQuest Pro software version 6.0 (BD Biosciences), and histograms were generated to determine marker expression profiles.

SCAP cytocompatibility, metabolic activity and cell spreading on hydrogels

Cell metabolic activity was assessed using the AlamarBlue assay (Invitrogen™) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SCAPs were seeded onto hydrogel samples at a density of 0.25 × 105 cells/cm2 in 48-well culture plates (SPL Life Sciences) and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. On days 1, 7 and 14, the culture medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium containing 10% AlamarBlue reagent. The plates were then incubated in the dark at 37°C for 4 h. Background correction was performed using blank wells containing only the culture medium and AlamarBlue reagent. Fluorescence intensities were measured using a BioTek Synergy HT microplate reader (BioTek Inc.) at 530 nm excitation and 590 nm emission.

The Live/Dead assay was performed to assess SCAP viability and cytotoxicity at days 2, 7 and 14 using the Calcein AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Elabscience®). At each time point, the wells were gently rinsed with PBS, followed by the addition of the staining solution containing Calcein AM and propidium iodide (PI), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were incubated for 20 min at room temperature and protected from light. After incubation, the cells were imaged using a Nikon C2 confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc.). Multiple fields per sample across 4 regions were captured at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels using dual-channel acquisition to detect green fluorescence (live cells) and red fluorescence (dead cells). Images were captured using NIS-Elements Advanced Research Software (version 4.0, Nikon). Image analysis and quantification were performed using ImageJ (version 1.50i, NIH).40 Cell viability was calculated as the percentage of live cells relative to the total number of live and dead cells. Furthermore, relative cell spreading area was also evaluated from the CLSM images of the hydrogel groups.

SCAP migration assay

SCAP migration capacity was evaluated using a Transwell migration assay with minor modifications of previously described methods.23,41,42 A schematic illustration of the assay setup is presented in Figure 3D. Briefly, 24-well Transwell inserts with 8-μm pore size membranes (NEST Scientific) were placed in culture plates. SCAPs were suspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per insert in the presence of the respective hydrogel formulation. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with FBS to establish a chemotactic gradient. After 48 h of incubation at 37°C and 5% CO₂, nonmigrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using cotton swabs. Cells that had migrated to the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich), and imaged using an inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss Axiovert 40C). The number of migrated cells was quantified in multiple random fields per membrane by 2 independent researchers.

SCAP culture in hydrogels and osteogenic induction

Sterile hydrogel aliquots of each formulation were dispensed into 48-well culture wells and pre-equilibrated in complete DMEM at 37°C to allow hydration and stabilisation before cell incorporation. SCAPs were harvested, resuspended in growth medium, and gently introduced into the hydrogels to promote uniform cell distribution throughout the constructs. The final cell density was adjusted to 0.1 × 106 cells/mL hydrogel. The resulting cell-laden constructs were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and fresh medium was added carefully to avoid disrupting the hydrogel structure.38,43

For osteogenic induction, the culture medium was replaced with induction medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid (Wako Chemicals GmbH), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 nM calcitriol (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich). The induction medium was refreshed every 2 to 3 days throughout the culture period. Cell-laden hydrogels were maintained for up to 21 days, and constructs were collected at predetermined time points for further analysis.

Mineralisation-associated and gene expression assays

During induction, morphological changes and the appearance of mineralised nodule-like deposits were monitored qualitatively by phase-contrast microscopy at days 7, 14 and 21 using an inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss Axiovert 40C). Representative images were captured from each group at the predetermined time points to document changes in matrix deposition and the progressive appearance of calcified nodular structures.44,45

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was evaluated at days 7, 14 and 21 using the BioVision ALP Activity Colorimetric Assay Kit (BioVision) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At each time point, samples were rinsed with PBS and incubated with p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) substrate for 60 min at room temperature. The reaction product was quantified by measuring absorbance at 405 nm using a microplate reader, and ALP activity was normalised to total protein content.

Mineralisation and calcium deposition were evaluated at days 14 and 21 using Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. At each time point, samples were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 2% Alizarin Red S for 60 min. Excess dye was removed by thorough washing with deionized water, and mineralised nodules were imaged using an inverted bright-field microscope. For quantitative analysis, the bound ARS dye was extracted using 10% acetic acid, neutralised, transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader.

Expression of mineralisation-associated genes (RUNX2, BSP, OPN and OCN) and dentin-related genes (DMP1 and DSPP) was evaluated at days 7, 14 and 21 by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted using the NZY Total RNA Isolation Kit (NZYtech), and cDNA was synthesised from 500 ng total RNA using the NZY First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (NZYtech). Real-time qPCR was performed using the ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with NZYSupreme qPCR Green Master Mix (2 ×). Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using 4 independent biological donors (n = 4). Each experimental condition was performed with a minimum of 3 technical replicates per donor, unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 6.00, GraphPad Software). For outcomes measured across multiple time points, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test was used. For single time-point comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc testing was applied. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

FTIR spectra identified the characteristic bands and functional groups of the constituent polymers (Figure 1B). Gelatin-containing formulations displayed the expected amide-associated bands, whereas κC-containing formulations showed characteristic sulfate- and polysaccharide-associated peaks. In the composite κCG formulations, peaks attributable to both components were present.

All hydrogel formulations showed time-dependent gradual degradation over 14 days (Figure 1C). κC-only hydrogels exhibited greater early mass loss, whereas gelatin-containing and κCG formulations retained a higher proportion of their original mass over time. Swelling increased over 14 days in all groups (Figure 1D). The 1% gelatin group showed the lowest swelling, κC-only groups showed the highest swelling, and κCG groups showed intermediate swelling behaviour.

Rheological testing showed formulation-dependent viscoelastic properties (Figure 1E–G). In κC-containing formulations, the storage modulus (G′) remained higher than the loss modulus (G″) across the tested range. All groups showed decreasing viscosity with increasing shear rate (Figure 1F). Among the tested hydrogels, 2.5% κCG exhibited the highest G′ and the lowest Tan (δ).

SCAP cytocompatibility, metabolic activity and cell spreading

Live/Dead staining demonstrated predominantly viable cells in all groups at days 2, 7 and 14 (Figure 2B). Quantitative analysis confirmed high percentages of live cells in most groups across all time points (Figure 2D). A reduction in viability was observed in the 1.5% κC group at day 14 compared with the other groups.

Metabolic activity measured by AlamarBlue was higher than the control at day 1 in all hydrogel groups and declined over time (Figure 2C). By day 14, values in all hydrogel groups were close to, or slightly below, the control.

Cell spreading showed differences among formulations (Figure 2E). Larger relative cell areas were observed in the control, 2.5% κC and 2.5% κCG groups, whereas 1.5% κC showed the smallest spreading area among the tested formulations.

SCAP differentiation-associated responses under osteogenic induction

Phase-contrast microscopy showed morphological changes and increasing nodule-like mineralised deposits over time in all groups during osteogenic induction (Figure 3B). These deposits were more evident at days 14 and 21 than at day 7 and appeared more prominent in the κC-containing formulations, particularly 2.5% κC and 2.5% κCG. Quantitative ALP activity remained within a similar overall range relative to the control, with higher values observed in several κC-containing groups at days 7 and 14 and maintained activity through day 21 (Figure 3F).

Alizarin Red S staining showed mineralised deposition by day 14, which increased further by day 21 (Figure 3C). Quantitative analysis showed greater mineralisation in κC-containing formulations than in 1% gelatin, with the highest mineral deposition observed in 2.5% κCG at day 21 (Figure 3G).

RT-qPCR showed time- and formulation-dependent expression of mineralisation-associated and dentin-related markers (Figure 4). RUNX2 expression was highest at day 7, with greater levels in 2.5% κC and 2.5% κCG, and lower expression at days 14 and 21. BSP was markedly increased at day 7 in κC-containing groups and remained elevated at day 14, with the highest expression in 1.5% κCG and 2.5% κCG. OPN increased over time and reached its highest expression at day 21, particularly in 1.5% κCG and 2.5% κCG. Discussion

Regenerative endodontics require scaffolds that are injectable or conformable to complex root canal geometry, support survival and function of dental stem and progenitor cells, and provide biological signals that favour dentin-pulp regeneration rather than nonspecific calcification or fibrous repair.15,47 In this study, κ-carrageenan hydrogels, with and without gelatin, were evaluated as candidate scaffolds for stem cells from the apical papilla. The findings indicate that κC-containing formulations were cytocompatible and supported odontogenic differentiation, whereas the 2.5% κCG formulation provided the most favourable overall profile, combining improved viscoelastic properties, moderated swelling and degradation, and stronger late-stage mineralisation and odontogenic gene expression. These results suggest that blending κC with gelatin creates a more favourable microenvironment for SCAP maturation.

The physicochemical data provide a useful framework for understanding these biological outcomes. FTIR analysis showed preservation of characteristic bands of both gelatin and κC in the composite formulations, supporting successful polymer blending rather than chemical degradation of either component. This is relevant because the 2 polymers contribute different forms of bioactivity. κC, as a sulfated polysaccharide, provides a negatively charged environment of glycosaminoglycan-rich extracellular matrix that may influence ion interactions, protein adsorption and mineral nucleation, whereas gelatin contributes collagen-derived peptide sequences that support cell attachment and cell-matrix interactions.19,[48], [49], [50] Previous work on κC-gelatin systems has similarly shown that blending increases intermolecular contacts, including electrostatic pairing and hydrogen bonding, and can increase network packing and viscoelastic performance compared with gelatin alone.44 The structural and hydration data were also consistent with formulation-dependent network behaviour. SEM indicated clear differences in microarchitecture among groups, while swelling and degradation assays showed that κC-only formulations were associated with higher swelling and more rapid mass loss than gelatin-containing groups. These observations are consistent with the known hydrophilic and ionically responsive behaviour of κC, in which sulfate groups interact with surrounding ions and contribute to coil-to-helix transitions and helix aggregation that stabilise the gel network.41 From a regenerative endodontic perspective, these parameters can directly influence the biological microenvironment experienced by cells. The higher swelling at days 1 and 3 likely reflects early water uptake by the hydrophilic sulfate-rich κ-Carrageenan network, whereas the smaller changes observed at days 7 and 14 suggest stabilisation of the hydrogel structure toward equilibrium hydration.33,51 This pattern is relevant because swelling influences scaffold porosity, transport conditions and structural integrity, which in turn can affect the microenvironment experienced by cells. Within this context, the 2.5% κCG formulation appeared to provide a more balanced profile, with lower swelling than 2.5% κC, improved mass retention, and stronger solid-like viscoelastic behaviour. This more favourable degradation profile should, however, be interpreted with some caution in relation to clinical regeneration. In pulp-dentin regeneration, scaffold degradation should be sufficiently slow enough to support early cell accommodation and matrix deposition, but not so prolonged that it later restricts vascular or neural ingrowth from the apical tissues.52 In the present study, the 21-day assay reflects early in vitro scaffold stability only and does not determine whether degradation remains well matched to the longer biological course of pulp regeneration in vivo. This will require longer-term and in vivo studies, particularly because meaningful regenerative outcomes are typically assessed over substantially longer periods.5,53

Rheological findings were particularly relevant to the observed cell response. κC-containing formulations behaved as viscoelastic solids, with G′ exceeding G″, and 2.5% κCG exhibited the highest storage modulus and lowest Tan (δ). This suggests a more elastic and mechanically stable network with less viscous energy dissipation. Such behaviour is biologically meaningful because dental mesenchymal stem cell fate is sensitive to stiffness and viscoelasticity. Qu et al. demonstrated that high-stiffness nanofibrous gelatin scaffolds favoured DPSC spreading and mineralised tissue formation, whereas low-stiffness scaffolds supported more pulp-like soft tissue formation.13 Similarly, higher-concentration GelMA hydrogels have been reported to enhance SCAP spreading and increased odontogenic differentiation compared with nonpatterned controls.11 The present data are consistent with this concept and indicate that the more solid-like mechanics of 2.5% κCG may have contributed to stronger late-stage odontogenic outcomes.

The isolated SCAP population exhibited an expected mesenchymal phenotype, with high expression of MSC-associated markers and minimal expression of hematopoietic and immune-associated markers. The presence of a CD146-positive subset is notable because previous reports linking CD146-positive SCAP or related dental MSC subpopulations to enhanced regenerative and mineralising potential.10,54,55 Establishing this phenotype is important because it supports the biological relevance of the subsequent viability and differentiation findings.

All hydrogel groups maintained predominantly viable cells throughout the culture period, indicating overall cytocompatibility. The early increase in metabolic activity in κC-containing formulations, followed by convergence toward control values at later time points, may reflect a transition from initial metabolic activity toward lineage commitment, a pattern commonly observed as progenitor cells progress from proliferative states toward differentiation.56 The reduction in viability observed in the 1.5% κC group at day 14 suggests that κC alone, particularly at lower concentration, may provide less favourable long-term support than the blended formulations. A likely explanation is that gelatin improves cell–matrix interactions through adhesive motifs that are absent in κC alone.19,57 This interpretation is supported by the spreading data, in which larger cell areas were observed in 2.5% κC and 2.5% κCG, and the addition of gelatin improved spreading at the lower κC concentration. These findings indicate that both matrix mechanics and biochemical adhesion contributed to SCAP behaviour. This agrees with the broader literature indicating that dental stem cells integrate matrix adhesion, surface interactions and stiffness-dependent signals to regulate lineage fate and mineralisation outcomes.11,13

By contrast, migration did not differ markedly among groups, suggesting that the tested formulations supported SCAP motility under the present conditions. From a practical perspective, this is relevant because scaffold materials intended for regenerative endodontics should ideally support cell accommodation without restricting recruitment-related behaviour. Although κC has been reported to enhance migration and adhesion in other mineralising cell systems, such effects are likely context dependent and may differ between soluble exposure and crosslinked hydrogel presentation.26 The absence of migration differences also suggests that the κC/κCG hydrogels potentially support cell accommodation and directing lineage behaviour after seeding, rather than directly enhancing migratory activity, but additional chemokine loading could be explored if enhanced cell homing is a goal.9

During odontogenic differentiation, κC-containing formulations resulted in increased ALP activity over time at key time points. Interestingly, ALP activity exhibited formulation- and time-dependent peaks, such as increased early ALP in 1.5% κCG and increased day-14 ALP in higher κC formulations. This is potentially expected that ALP often increases during early matrix maturation and may plateau or shift as mineralisation becomes dominant. This suggests that κC formulations promoted ALP-associated maturation without compromising overall cytocompatibility. These align with prior reports showing that κC can enhance mineralising activity in nondental osteogenic models, where in MC3T3-E1 preosteoblasts, κ-Carrageenan increased ALP activity and markedly enhanced at later time points.26 More importantly, Alizarin Red S quantification showed significantly increased mineral deposition in κC-containing formulations, with the highest mineralisation content observed in 2.5% κCG at day 21, indicating that the blended scaffold can potentially generate a hydrogel niche that sustains mineral maturation beyond the initial differentiation phase.

The gene expression findings further support the view that these hydrogels acted as instructive matrices rather than passive substrates for calcification. Early upregulation of RUNX2 and BSP in κC-containing groups is consistent with lineage priming and early matrix-associated mineralisation events. RUNX2 serves as a central regulator of mineralising lineage commitment and integrates both biochemical and mechanical inputs, while BSP is closely associated with matrix organisation and mineral nucleation.58 As culture progressed, increased OPN and OCN expression indicated advancement toward more mature mineralising phenotypes. Most importantly, the later increase in DMP1 and DSPP, particularly in 2.5% κCG, is relevant to regenerative endodontics because these markers are more closely associated to odontoblast-like differentiation and dentin matrix biology than with osteogenic mineralisation.57,58 The pattern of early RUNX2 and BSP upregulation followed by later DSPP and DMP1 expression therefore supports the interpretation that the scaffold promoted a dentin-oriented differentiation program. However, because protein-level confirmation of DSPP and DMP1 was not performed, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of early odontogenic/dentinogenic differentiation-associated responses rather than definitive proof of terminal odontoblastic maturation. Because a hydrogel-in-basal-medium control was not included, these findings should be interpreted as showing enhanced odontogenic/dentinogenic differentiation-associated responses under osteogenic induction conditions, rather than independent inductive activity of the scaffold itself.

These findings are also broadly consistent with previous scaffold studies in dental tissue engineering. Loukelis et al. reported that κC-containing scaffolds increased ALP activity, calcium deposition and DSPP expression in dental pulp stem cells, supporting a positive role for κC in odontogenic differentiation.27 Likewise, Qu et al. showed that nanofibrous gelatin-based scaffolds support odontogenic gene expression and that further incorporation of bioactive components can amplify this effect.12

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted entirely in vitro and therefore does not fully replicate the full biological complexity of the root canal environment, including dentin wall interactions, restricted nutrient and oxygen gradients, and the requirements for vascular and neural integration. Second, odontogenic differentiation was evaluated primarily through ALP activity, mineral staining and mRNA expression, without protein-level confirmation of key markers such as DSPP, DMP1. Therefore, the dentin-specific quality and maturation status of the formed matrix cannot be fully established. Third, inflammatory conditions relevant to necrotic pulps and apical periodontitis were not modelled, and scaffold-immune interactions were not assessed. Finally, clinically relevant handling parameters, including injectability, gelation behaviour in canal-like geometries and direct interactions with dentin, were not evaluated.

Future work should therefore validate odontogenic differentiation at the protein and matrix level, evaluate scaffold performance in dentin-interface and root-segment models, assess angiogenic and neurogenic compatibility, and test the lead formulation under inflammatory or infection-relevant conditions. Longer-term in vivo studies are needed to confirm whether the favourable in vitro profile of 2.5% κCG translates into functional dentin-pulp regeneration. Lastly, comparison with clinically established regenerative endodontic scaffold materials will be important to further clarify the relative biological performance of κ-Carrageenan-based hydrogels within the context of pulp-dentin complex regeneration. Such comparisons may include clinically used blood clot or platelet concentrate approaches, as well as commercially available hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel systems.38,59

In conclusion, κC-containing hydrogels supported SCAP viability and odontogenic differentiation, and blending κC with gelatin improved the overall scaffold profile. Among the tested formulations, 2.5% κCG demonstrated the most favourable balance of physicochemical stability, solid-like viscoelastic behaviour and late-stage odontogenic response. These findings identify κC+gelatin hydrogels as a promising and tunable scaffold platform for regenerative endodontic applications.

Conclusion

κC-based hydrogels supported SCAP viability and enhanced odontogenic/dentinogenic differentiation-associated responses in vitro. Incorporation of gelatin improved late-stage mineral deposition and expression of known dentin-related markers, with the 2.5% κ-Carrageenan+gelatin hydrogel formulation showing the most favourable overall response. These findings support further investigation of κC-gelatin hydrogels as candidate scaffolds for dentin-pulp regenerative applications.
