Pneumothorax and Hemothorax: Practical Approaches for Radiologists
Authors: Woo Hyeon Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThoracic trauma, particularly pneumothorax and hemothorax, requires rapid and accurate radiologic assessment to reduce morbidity and mortality. Although supine chest radiography remains the initial screening tool, its diagnostic sensitivity is limited in evaluating patients with trauma, necessitating the use of alternative diagnostic tools such as chest ultrasonography. CT serves as the reference standard examination, providing definitive evaluation of the whole thorax and identifying pseudoaneurysms or active contrast extravasation in patients with suspected bleeding. Recently, AI has been developed and integrated into clinical workflows to reduce diagnostic errors. This review discusses standardized radiologic approaches for pneumothorax and hemothorax, focusing on distinctive imaging features, common interpretive pitfalls, structured reporting formats, and the evolving roles of advanced imaging modalities and AI in emergency trauma settings.Keywords: Trauma, Pneumothorax, Hemothorax, Imaging, Artificial Intelligence

INTRODUCTION

Thoracic injuries occur in approximately one-fifth of patients with trauma and are an important cause of trauma-related morbidity and mortality (1). Thoracic trauma encompasses a wide spectrum of injuries, including pneumothorax, hemothorax, pulmonary contusion or laceration, aerodigestive tract injuries, and thoracic skeletal injuries (2,3). Among these, pneumothorax and hemothorax are common findings and may lead to rapid clinical deterioration, particularly when tension pneumothorax or massive hemothorax compromises cardiopulmonary function (3,4,5).

In this review article, the mechanisms of injury are first discussed, since radiologists and clinicians should understand the injury mechanisms and characteristic injury patterns that can often be predicted and evaluated using imaging (2,3,4,6,7). The roles of major imaging modalities in the evaluation of traumatic pneumothorax and hemothorax are also reviewed. Familiarity with imaging findings obtained in the supine position—particularly chest radiography (CXR)—is important because supine CXRs may differ from erect CXRs in terms of imaging appearance and detectability of these abnormalities (8,9). Chest ultrasonography (USG) has recently played an important role in trauma settings because it enables rapid bedside detection of pneumothorax and hemothorax (10,11). Although CT is considered the gold standard for detecting pneumothorax and hemothorax, radiologists should also know that its use may be limited in hemodynamically unstable patients with trauma (6,7,12). This review also proposed structured reporting formats because such formats prompt radiologists to document not only the presence and amount of pneumothorax or hemothorax, but also associated injuries and red flag findings that may affect urgent management. Finally, this review summarizes recent advancements in the use of AI in patients with trauma, focusing on its applications for detecting and quantifying pneumothorax and hemothorax.

MECHANISMS OF TRAUMA

Thoracic trauma mechanisms can be broadly categorized according to the type of trauma (i.e., blunt, penetrating, or blast injury), the biomechanical forces involved (e.g., compression or deceleration), and the energy of the impact (i.e., high-energy vs. low-energy trauma) (Supplementary Material 1) (2,3,4,6,7).

Blunt and penetrating injuries typically damage organs through direct mechanical force, whereas blast injuries may cause damage through multiple mechanisms, including blast pressure waves, projectiles generated by the explosion, and displacement of the body caused by the blast (3,4). Because they involve multiple mechanisms acting simultaneously, blast injuries often result in complex injury patterns (13,14). In contrast, penetrating injuries tend to produce localized damage along the trajectory of the penetrating object (4,15,16).

Trauma mechanisms can also be classified on the basis of the direction of forces applied to the thorax. Compressive forces usually represent direct impact injuries and may result in contusion, laceration, or rupture of thoracic organs. In contrast, deceleration forces arise from inertial effects and are often associated with shearing injuries, such as traumatic aortic injury, tracheobronchial injury, or vertebral injury (3,4).

The severity of trauma is related to the amount of kinetic energy transferred to the body. High-energy trauma mechanisms, such as high-speed motor vehicle collisions or falls from heights, are associated with an increased risk of severe thoracic injuries such as pulmonary contusion, vascular injury, and diaphragmatic rupture (3,4).

IMAGING ASSESSMENT OF PNEUMOTHORAX

Disruption of the pleura, which is usually caused by penetrating injury or laceration, may result in pneumothorax. On CXR and CT, pneumothorax can be identified by the presence of air between the chest wall and the visceral pleura (Fig. 1, Table 1) (17). On supine CXRs, pneumothorax may manifest as the deep sulcus sign, abnormal deepening of the costophrenic angle caused by accumulation of intrapleural air in the anteroinferior space, or hyperlucency over the affected hemithorax (Fig. 1, Table 1) (8).

On chest USG, the absence of lung sliding (i.e., the absence of to-and-fro movement of the visceral pleura during respiration) and the absence of B-lines (i.e., the absence of vertical hyperechoic reverberation artifacts arising from the pleural line) may suggest pneumothorax (Fig. 2, Table 1, Supplementary Materials 2, 3) (10,18,19). Identification of the lung point, which represents the transition zone where normal lung sliding alternates with absent sliding, is considered a highly specific finding for pneumothorax (Fig. 2, Supplementary Materials 4, 5) (10,18,19). The lung point is typically detected along the anterior chest wall, such as near the midclavicular line, particularly in patients examined in the supine position (10,18,19).

Occult pneumothorax, which is defined as pneumothorax that is not visible or only barely visible on chest radiography but detected on CT, is relatively common in patients with trauma (20). In patients with occult pneumothorax, associated imaging clues such as subcutaneous emphysema or rib fractures may increase diagnostic confidence (Table 1). Although occult pneumothorax is relatively common in patients with trauma, it may not always be clinically significant, since many cases can be managed conservatively without chest tube placement (21,22). When pneumothorax is associated with a check-valve mechanism, tension pneumothorax may develop, resulting in compromised systemic venous return (5). Key imaging findings of tension pneumothorax include mediastinal shift to the contralateral side, diaphragmatic depression, widening of the intercostal spaces, and compression of the contralateral lung (Fig. 3, Table 1) (2,3,5). When a large pneumothorax with a persistent air leak despite chest tube placement is present, the possibility of tracheobronchial injury should be considered (Fig. 3) (3,5).

On CXRs, pneumothorax size is commonly estimated by measuring the distance between the lung margin and the chest wall. For example, the British Thoracic Society guideline defines a large pneumothorax as a distance of ≥2 cm at the level of the hilum, whereas the American College of Chest Physicians guideline uses a threshold of ≥3 cm at the apex (23,24). Rhea et al. (25) also proposed a method for estimating pneumothorax volume by using interpleural distances at three levels (apex, upper half, and lower half of the lung). The Rhea method performs reasonably well for small pneumothoraces but tends to underestimate pneumothorax volume in large pneumothoraces (26). However, attempts to estimate pneumothorax size on supine CXRs—which are more relevant in trauma settings—remain limited (27).

Collins et al. (28) proposed a CT-based formula using interpleural distances. The maximal radial pleural separation at the largest air pocket can also be used as a practical surrogate for pneumothorax size (29). A cutoff of >3.5 cm has been suggested as a threshold favoring chest drainage in hemodynamically stable patients with trauma (29).

Despite the inherent limitations of chest USG in estimating pneumothorax volume, several studies have explored semi-quantitative approaches for assessing pneumothorax size (30,31,32). One of the key assumptions underlying these approaches is that more posteriorly located lung points indicate a larger pneumothorax. However, caution is required with this approach because lung point–based approaches estimate the extent of the pleural surface involved rather than the true pneumothorax volume (33). In addition, the lung point may not be visible in patients with massive pneumothorax (33).

IMAGING ASSESSMENT OF HEMOTHORAX

Hemothorax may occur when thoracic vascular or parenchymal injuries cause bleeding into the pleural cavity. On CXRs, hemothorax typically appears as pleural effusion and is often associated with rib fractures (Table 2) (34,35). Because pleural fluid accumulates in dependent regions, pleural effusion may not be readily apparent on supine CXRs. Dense or asymmetric hemithoracic opacity, apical capping, or loss of the diaphragmatic contour may indicate pleural effusion on supine CXRs (Fig. 4, Table 2) (9).

CT is highly sensitive for detecting hemothorax, even when the volume of pleural fluid is small. Hemothorax typically manifests as a hyperattenuating pleural fluid (mean Hounsfield unit >20) or the hemorrhage–fluid level (i.e., hematocrit sign) and may be accompanied by associated findings such as rib fractures, vascular injury including pseudoaneurysm formation, or active contrast extravasation (Fig. 5, Table 2) (1,3,34). Identification of pseudoaneurysm or active contrast extravasation on CT is important because these findings may indicate the need for transcatheter arterial embolization (36). The most common source of bleeding in traumatic hemothorax is injury to the intercostal arteries, which frequently occurs in association with rib fractures (34,35). Other potential sources include the internal thoracic artery and pulmonary vessels, and, although uncommon, injury to major thoracic vessels such as the thoracic aorta may result in massive hemothorax (Table 2) (34,36).

Estimation of pleural effusion volume on CXRs is challenging, particularly on supine CXRs, since pleural fluid layers posteriorly. As a result, pleural effusion may not be detectable on supine CXRs until a relatively large volume of fluid accumulates, and its detectability is highly variable depending on patient positioning and radiographic technique (approximately 175–525 mL) (9,37). In contrast, USG allows more accurate estimation of pleural effusion volume using quantitative or semi-quantitative methods (19,38,39). Similar to pneumothorax assessment, the interpleural distance measured on ultrasound plays a key role in estimating pleural effusion volume.

On CT, pleural effusion volume can be estimated using a simple formula using the greatest anteroposterior depth and the greatest craniocaudal length of pleural effusion (40). However, this formula provides only an approximate estimate and should be interpreted with caution. In traumatic hemothorax, the patient’s hemodynamic status and evidence of ongoing bleeding should take precedence over volume estimation when determining clinical severity and management.

PITFALLS IN THE INTERPRETATION OF PNEUMOTHORAX AND HEMOTHORAX

Although identification of pneumothorax and hemothorax is usually straightforward, radiologists should be aware of several findings that may mimic these conditions. For example, skin folds may simulate pleural lines on supine CXRs (Fig. 7). However, the presence of lung markings beyond the line and extension of the line outside the thoracic cavity help distinguish skin folds from true pneumothorax (2,8).

On CT, dependent atelectasis may mimic hyperattenuating pleural fluid, and delayed images should be carefully evaluated to avoid overlooking active contrast extravasation or misinterpreting vascular structures as active bleeding (Fig. 7) (2).

Although loss of lung sliding on chest USG may indicate pneumothorax in most cases, this finding can also occur in other conditions, including atelectasis or cases with a history of pleurodesis (19). In addition, subcutaneous emphysema may obscure pleural visualization and reduce the diagnostic accuracy of chest USG for detecting pneumothorax (10).

IMAGING APPROACHES FOCUSED ON PNEUMOTHORAX AND HEMOTHORAX IN PATIENTS WITH TRAUMA

Portable CXR in the supine position is a core imaging modality in the initial evaluation of patients with trauma, regardless of hemodynamic stability (Fig. 8, Table 3) (6,7,41,42,43,44). In patients with unstable vital signs, trauma series radiography, including portable CXR and pelvic radiography, should be considered initially (6). These examinations aim to detect urgent life-threatening conditions such as tension pneumothorax and hemothorax. Supine CXR has shown high specificity (pooled specificity, 0.99–1.00) for detecting traumatic pneumothorax, although its sensitivity is limited (pooled sensitivity, 0.46–0.54) (42,45,46). Similarly, a meta-analysis reported a pooled sensitivity of 0.54 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.33–0.75) and pooled specificity of 0.99 (95% CI, 0.94–1.00) for detecting hemothorax using supine CXR (47).

Imaging approach for traumatic pneumothorax and hemothorax.

Hemodynamic stability is the key determinant of imaging selection in patients with trauma. In hemodynamically unstable patients, portable CXR and eFAST are performed as bedside imaging examinations to rapidly detect life-threatening conditions. In hemodynamically stable patients, contrast-enhanced CT provides comprehensive evaluation of thoracic injuries.

Because of the limited sensitivity of CXRs for detecting pneumothorax or hemothorax, complementary imaging modalities are often required in traumatic settings. In this context, chest USG performed as a part of the Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (eFAST) has emerged as a valuable bedside tool for detecting pneumothorax, hemothorax, and pericardial effusion (Fig. 8, Table 3) (12). Chest USG generally demonstrates improved sensitivity for detecting pneumothorax (pooled sensitivity, 0.69–0.89) and hemothorax (pooled sensitivity, 0.60–0.67) (47,48,49,50,51). For example, a meta-analysis including 1,271 patients with trauma in the emergency department (410 with traumatic pneumothorax) showed similar specificity between chest USG and CXR (0.99 [95% CI, 0.97–1.00] vs. 1.00 [95% CI, 0.97–1.00]), whereas USG showed higher sensitivity (0.91 [95% CI, 0.85–0.94] vs. 0.47 [95% CI, 0.31–0.63]) (46). Accordingly, several guidelines have emphasized the role of chest USG in the evaluation of patients with trauma, particularly those with unstable vital signs (12,41,42,43,44,52,53). However, chest USG should not be used as a standalone rule-out test, since it may yield false-negative results owing to its limited sensitivity in certain situations (12,41,42,43). In addition, the diagnostic performance of chest USG is operator-dependent and may be compromised in under-trained operators (41).

Chest CT, often performed as part of whole-body trauma assessment, plays a central role in evaluating thoracic injuries in patients with stabilized vital signs (Fig. 8, Table 3) (6,7,12,41,42,43,44,54). CT provides a comprehensive assessment of high-energy thoracic trauma, including lung parenchymal injury, mediastinal and vascular injury, thoracic spine fractures, diaphragmatic rupture, and chest wall injuries (12,20,54,55). This accurate evaluation may also alter clinical management. For example, Kea et al. (20) reported that CT could detect additional injuries, including rib fractures, lung contusions, and occult pneumothorax, in 18% of patients (106/589; 95% CI, 15%–21%) with normal initial CXRs. Among these 106 patients, 12 had injuries requiring subsequent intervention (20).

In hemodynamically stable patients with suspected thoracic injury, contrast-enhanced chest CT or whole-body CT with intravenous contrast in polytrauma is generally preferred because it allows comprehensive evaluation of pneumothorax, hemothorax, pulmonary injury, mediastinal injury, diaphragmatic injury, and associated chest wall injuries (6,7,41,43). CT angiography should be performed when vascular injury or active bleeding is suspected, such as in patients with active contrast extravasation or pseudoaneurysm on initial imaging, expanding chest wall or mediastinal hematoma, hemothorax with suspected arterial bleeding, blunt traumatic aortic injury, or penetrating trauma with a transmediastinal, precordial, periclavicular, or vascular-adjacent trajectory (6,7,41,43). Delayed-phase imaging may be added when persistent or slow bleeding is suspected or when differentiation between vascular structures and active extravasation is required (Fig. 5) (6,7,41,43). Non-contrast chest CT has a limited role in the acute trauma setting and may be considered when intravenous contrast is contraindicated or when the primary clinical concern is limited to pleural air, skeletal injury, or retained foreign bodies (6,7,41,43).

An associated aerodigestive tract, cardiac, or great-vessel injury should be suspected when pneumothorax or hemothorax is accompanied by a persistent air leak despite thoracostomy tube drainage, massive pneumomediastinum, airway-wall discontinuity, mediastinal fluid or hematoma, hemopericardium, active contrast extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, abrupt vascular contour abnormality, or an injury trajectory adjacent to the esophagus, heart, or great vessels (1,2,16,56). In addition, pulmonary contusion or laceration and diaphragmatic injury should be actively assessed on CT, particularly in patients with high-energy blunt trauma, lower thoracic penetrating injury, or combined thoracoabdominal injury (2,3,4).

In patients with penetrating injuries, injury patterns are largely determined by the trajectory of the penetrating object; therefore, focused evaluation along the trajectory by using CT is particularly important (Fig. 9) (7,16). In hemodynamically stable patients with penetrating thoracic trauma, the CT protocol should be tailored to the wound trajectory. Chest CT with intravenous contrast or CT angiography of the chest is appropriate when the injury is limited to the thorax, whereas chest–abdomen–pelvis coverage should be considered when the trajectory is uncertain or transdiaphragmatic injury is suspected (7,16). CT angiography is especially useful when great-vessel injuries are suspected (15,16).

CLINICAL MANAGEMENT IN PATIENTS WITH TRAUMA

Tension pneumothorax and large or enlarging pneumothorax generally require tube thoracostomy for immediate decompression (29,57). In hemodynamically stable patients with traumatic pneumothorax, drainage is favored when the pneumothorax involves more than 20% of the hemithorax on CXRs or when the maximal radial pleural separation on CT is greater than 3.5 cm (29,57). In contrast, smaller pneumothoraces, including those with a maximal radial pleural separation of 3.5 cm or less on CT, may be managed with close clinical monitoring and follow-up imaging (29).

For traumatic hemothorax, tube thoracostomy has traditionally been considered the initial treatment, particularly in patients with a moderate-to-large hemothorax, respiratory compromise, or ongoing bleeding (58,59). However, hemodynamically stable patients may show a small hemothorax, and recent evidence suggests that a CT-estimated volume of approximately 300 mL may be a practical threshold for considering drainage (59). Active contrast extravasation or pseudoaneurysms should prompt consideration of transcatheter arterial embolization or surgical management, depending on the bleeding source and clinical status (36,60). Surgical management should be considered when active bleeding is associated with hemodynamic instability, suspected cardiac or major vascular injury, or massive or persistent hemothorax after tube thoracostomy (57).

SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS IN PEDIATRIC TRAUMA

Imaging approaches in pediatric patients with trauma should differ from those in adults, given the anatomic and physiologic differences as well as differences in radiation susceptibility between children and adults (61). The high compliance of the chest wall and greater mobility of mediastinal structures in children are associated with less frequent rib fracture and cardiovascular injuries, but more frequent parenchymal injuries, even in the absence of rib fractures (61). Additionally, the tracheal characteristics of children (i.e., short, narrow, and more compressible airways) and their physiologic features predispose them to early hypoxia (61). The risk–benefit balance of radiation exposure from imaging must also be carefully considered because of the higher radiosensitivity in pediatric patients (62).

In pediatric patients with blunt trauma, CXR should be considered as the initial imaging examination (63). Ugalde et al. (64) highlighted the usefulness of CXR as an initial screening tool, given its acceptable sensitivity of 0.58 (95% CI, 0.55–0.61) and specificity of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.87–0.93). Chest USG has emerged as an adjuvant, non-irradiating imaging modality to screen pneumothorax, hemothorax, and pericardial effusion at the bedside, but its variable diagnostic performance limits its use as a standalone rule-out test (61,63,65,66,67). Similar to its application in adult patients, CT can identify more injuries—including pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax, rib fractures, and diaphragmatic rupture—than CXR in pediatric blunt trauma (64). However, CT is typically reserved for selected cases with abnormal CXRs, high-energy trauma mechanisms, persistent respiratory symptoms, or suspected mediastinal or diaphragmatic injuries (42,61,68,69).

AI UTILIZATION IN PATIENTS WITH TRAUMA

AI has demonstrated promising performance in detecting pneumothorax on CXRs, achieving diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of physicians (70). However, the imaging conditions in trauma settings differ from those used for routine CXR, since CXRs are often obtained in the supine position and with anteroposterior projection rather than erect posteroanterior projection. Therefore, the performance of AI algorithms on supine CXRs is particularly relevant in patients with trauma.

Hillis et al. (71) reported that AI successfully detected pneumothorax on supine CXRs (sensitivity, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–0.99]; specificity, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.82–0.92]) as well as on erect CXRs (sensitivity, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.90–0.96]; specificity, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92–0.97]). In addition, Gipson et al. (72) specifically evaluated the diagnostic performance of a commercial AI algorithm on supine CXRs obtained in trauma settings. In 1,404 patients, including 172 patients with pneumothorax (12%), AI demonstrated higher sensitivity for detecting pneumothorax (0.39 [95% CI, 0.32–0.47]) than radiologists (0.33 [95% CI, 0.26–0.41]), with CT used as the reference standard (72). Similarly, the potential of AI for detecting pleural effusion on CXRs has also been explored, and promising results have been reported in several studies (73,74,75). However, studies specifically evaluating the performance of AI for detecting pleural effusion on supine CXRs remain limited (73,74,75).

More recently, advances have been reported in the application of AI to chest USG for detecting pneumothorax (76,77,78). For example, AI algorithms designed for real-time detection of the absence of lung sliding—a key sonographic sign suggestive of pneumothorax—have demonstrated acceptable diagnostic performance in a prospective study (sensitivity, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.79–0.97]; specificity, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.74–0.86]) (76). Moreover, AI-assisted analysis of chest USG may help mitigate the operator dependency that is inherent to USG examinations (79).

Recent studies have also explored the use of AI-based segmentation algorithms to automatically quantify the volume of pneumothorax and pleural effusion on CT images (80,81). These approaches can allow automated estimation of pneumothorax size and pleural fluid volume, thereby facilitating clinical decision-making and monitoring of disease progression.

AI can also be applied to detect rib fractures, which may serve as ancillary imaging findings supporting the diagnosis of traumatic pneumothorax and hemothorax. For example, a commercially available AI model appeared to show limited performance for detecting rib fractures on supine CXRs when CT was used as the reference standard (Cohen’s κ, 0.39, 95% CI, 0.33–0.44) (72). However, this limitation may partly reflect the inherent limitations of supine CXR, since the agreement between the AI model and CT did not differ substantially from that between radiologists and CT (72). AI-based rib fracture detection on CT has also shown promising performance, with reported per-examination sensitivity of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.81–0.92) and per-examination specificity of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.88–0.94) (82). Nevertheless, its per-lesion sensitivity was lower (0.66, 95% CI, 0.62–0.69), indicating that it may still miss subtle or individual rib fractures (82). Therefore, AI-based rib fracture detection may assist radiologists in trauma imaging, but careful image review is necessary.

In brief, AI has the potential to improve the detection of pneumothorax and hemothorax. However, further development and validation of AI models specifically tailored for trauma imaging environments are warranted.

CONCLUSION

Imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and management of traumatic pneumothorax and hemothorax. While supine CXR and chest USG allow rapid bedside evaluation in patients with an unstable status, CT offers a comprehensive assessment of thoracic injuries in stabilized patients. Understanding the strengths and limitations of each imaging modality is essential for appropriate imaging selection and timely management. Radiologists should also be aware that the typical findings seen on erect CXRs may be absent on supine CXRs and should be familiar with supportive findings that indicate traumatic thoracic injuries. Structured reporting may facilitate comprehensive and systematic assessment of traumatic pneumothorax, hemothorax, and associated injuries. Emerging AI applications may further improve detection and quantification in trauma settings, although appropriate validation is required before clinical implementation.
