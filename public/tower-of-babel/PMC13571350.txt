Topsy-turvy heart is an exceedingly rare congenital malformation characterized by a 90° clockwise rotation of the heart along its longitudinal axis. This results in a superoinferior alignment of the ventricles, with the morphologic right ventricle situated superiorly and the left ventricle (LV) inferiorly. The segmental anatomy, including the atrioventricular (AV) and ventriculoarterial connections, is generally preserved. The rotated cardiac axis may cause significant distortion of the head and neck vessels, and the airways also may be affected.1,2 Elongation and compression of the trachea and bronchi may result in critical airway obstruction, bronchomalacia, and severe respiratory compromise in the neonatal period. The presence of an aortopulmonary window (AP window) is an associated finding with pulmonary overcirculation and pulmonary hypertension, which may further worsen respiratory symptoms.2, 3, 4

Fetal echocardiography enables prenatal diagnosis of topsy-turvy heart.2 More recently, fetal cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) has been used to improve characterization of complex cardiac conditions and to depict associated extracardiac anomalies. Postnatally, diagnostic imaging should include all the modalities enabling precise delineation of both cardiac and airway abnormalities. The prognosis of patients with topsy-turvy heart is often poor, particularly in the presence of significant airway anomalies and when surgical intervention is high risk.

We present the case of a neonate with topsy-turvy heart, a large AP window, and severe bronchial malformations, which were diagnosed prenatally. We discuss the use of pre- and postnatal multimodality imaging for assessing risk and prognosis and for facilitating clinical decision-making.

History of Presentation

A fetus was referred at 24 weeks’ gestation for evaluation of a suspected complex congenital heart defect. The parents were consanguineous (first cousins) and trio exome sequencing identified as homozygous c.1441C>T (p.Arg481) variant in the PDLIM5 gene. Although this gene defect has not been definitely recognized as disease-causing, some cases with cardiomyopathies and congenital heart defects have been reported.5

Prenatal Investigations

Fetal echocardiography showed a situs solitus, levocardia, and concordant AV and ventriculoarterial connections. The position of the ventricles was abnormal, with a predominantly horizontal and superoinferior orientation; the morphological right ventricle was located superiorly and the LV was located inferiorly. The LV appeared smaller and presented with an elongated outflow tract (Figure 1). Follow-up echocardiography performed at 28 weeks described a mismatch in the size of the great arteries; the pulmonary valve was significantly larger than the aortic valve and a large AP window was detected (Figure 2).

A fetal CMR was performed at 33 weeks of gestation. CMR illustrated the superoinferior and horizontal orientation of the ventricles (Figure 3), the elongated and horizontally aligned outflow tracts with a very low mediastinal position of the aortic arch, as well as the large AP window (Figure 4). CMR also revealed marked elongation of the supra-aortic vessels, caused by a flattened course of the aortic arch. Airway malformations and abnormal lung development were suspected due to inhomogeneous lung signal, an overinflation of the right lung, and a secondary flattening of the right hemidiaphragm (Figure 5).

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis comprised congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries and criss-cross heart; however, step-by step analysis of the segmental anatomy and topography confirmed the diagnosis of topsy-turvy heart.

Postnatal Presentation and Medical History

The newborn was delivered via caesarean section at 34 weeks because of placenta previa. Birth weight was 2,440 kg. During neonatal adaptation (APGAR score 1/7/9), after a temporary improvement, the newborn very quickly developed severe respiratory distress, hypercapnia, and eventually respiratory failure requiring intubation. Chest radiography was compatible with hyaline membrane disease.

During the subsequent hours, the patient remained severely hypoxemic despite maximization of ventilatory support. Attempts to achieve optimal airway recruitment led either to lung hyperinflation or airway collapse. A lung-protective ventilation strategy that also effectively opened the airways could not be reliably achieved. At the same time, progressive and eventually massive pulmonary overcirculation occurred, and increasing inotropic support was necessary.

Postnatal Investigations

Echocardiography showed the anatomy of topsy-turvy heart with a large AP window (5 × 7 mm) (Figure 6). Further advanced imaging was timely scheduled for completing diagnosis and evaluation of the prenatally suspected airways malformation, and included computed tomography (CT), neonatal CMR, and bronchoscopy (Figures 7 and 8, Video 1). All the exams were performed in a ventilated and sedated patient in critical clinical condition. Severe bronchomalacia and a complete collapsing of the elongated and stretched main bronchi were diagnosed at bronchoscopy (Videos 2 and 3).

Management and Outcome

Different therapeutic options, including airway stenting and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, were discussed interdisciplinary among the caring teams. Literature is limited to a few case studies and reports a mortality rate that can exceed 50%; even after correction of the AP window, mortality and morbidity have not been shown to significantly improve, and the airways condition remains the main determinant for prognosis. In our case, extensive bronchial stenting in a newborn was not found to be reasonable and tracheostomy and home ventilation would have inevitably caused development of lung emphysema and progressive pulmonary hypertension.3 Thus, the dramatic clinical deterioration of the patient, the presence of an AP window in severely abnormal outflow tracts, which may have represented a far more neonatal complex cardiac surgery than usual, and the severity and extension of bronchomalacia were the key factors for the team for estimating the prognosis as dismal and for recommending redirection of care. The parents understood the high risk of mortality and morbidity and agreed on redirection of care.

Discussion

This case illustrates the value of multimodality imaging in the diagnosis and management of a complex and rare congenital heart disease such as topsy-turvy heart. The superoinferior orientation of the ventricles, the horizontal configuration of the outflow tracts, and the inferior and posterior displacement of the great arteries were recognized on fetal echocardiography and led to the prenatal diagnosis. Pre- and postnatal CMR were used to confirm this rare cardiac lesion. CT added valuable information about the elongation of the brachiocephalic vessels and the extreme stretching of the airways along with the concomitant abnormalities of the pulmonary parenchyma, all secondary effects of the cardiac malrotation.2, 3, 4,6,7 Moreover, cross-sectional imaging enabled clear differentiation from a criss-cross heart. In presence of similar superoinferior position of the ventricles, the topsy-turvy heart consists of an organo-axial rotation around the base-apex axis of the heart; the cardiac segments remain concordant and the AV inflows parallel. In contrast, a criss-cross heart involves a twisting of the ventricular mass resulting in a nonparallel opening (cross) of the AV valves. The ventricular septum is curved and the atrial septum angled. Moreover, airways anomalies are usually normal in a criss-cross heart. Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries was excluded by step-by-step analysis of the cardiac segments, because the current lesion presented with AV and ventriculoarterial concordance and not AV and ventriculoarterial discordance.

A large AP window is among the most frequent associated anomalies in topsy-turvy heart. According to the literature, AP windows are present in most postnatal survivors and frequently contribute to pulmonary overcirculation and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn.3,4,7 In our case, the AP window resulted in a critical perfusion mismatch leading to insufficient systemic output. Also, severe airway anomalies, including stretching and/or compression of the trachea and bronchi progressing to severe bronchomalacia, have been described. In our patient, these additional morbidities were detected by CMR and CT and further investigated by bronchoscopy, which confirmed the degree and extension of bronchomalacia and airways distortion. These postnatal findings were helpful for understanding some features of prenatal CMR; the atelectatic and overinflated lung segments were caused by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors resulting in bronchomalacia.

The totality and quality of anatomical and functional information obtained by combining several noninvasive imaging modalities was crucial for multidisciplinary case discussion on therapeutic options and expected patient outcome. The comprehensive imaging assessment allowed for comparison of our case with the limited number of cases reported in the literature. Ultimately, this information was essential for clinical decision-making.

By combining fetal echocardiography and fetal CMR, advanced diagnostics can be initiated prenatally. Diagnosis as precise as possible, even in the most complex cases, is essential for parental counseling and for planning perinatal management. Moreover, this can help in preconceiving the required postnatal exams, which can be timely scheduled with the smallest burden for the patient. Availability of the diagnosis for both cardiac and extracardiac lesions may be particularly relevant in patients with a rapid clinical deterioration.

This approach enables an informed clinical decision-making and helps to avoid unnecessary delays when redirection of care is considered.

Conclusions

Even in complex and rare congenital heart disease such as topsy-turvy heart, detailed diagnostic imaging can be initiated prenatally and continued after birth by combining appropriate imaging modalities. Fetal findings can guide planning of timely neonatal diagnostic examinations and enable prompt clinical decision-making and appropriate parental counseling.
