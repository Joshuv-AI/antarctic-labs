Retrograde Recanalization of Occluded Pulmonary Arteries via Percutaneous Transluminal Pulmonary Angioplasty
Authors: Jian Xu, Jing-jing Wang, Lan Wang, Sugang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplete occlusion of pulmonary artery branches is challenging for percutaneous transluminal pulmonary angioplasty (PTPA) in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) patients.Case SummaryWe report 2 CTEPH patients (a 30-year-old woman and a 63-year-old woman) with complete occlusion of right A8a and left A7 branches, respectively. Background

Percutaneous transluminal pulmonary angioplasty (PTPA) has emerged as a key treatment modality for inoperable patients or those with a residual distal disease with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).1 While systemic-to-pulmonary collateral flow via hypertrophied bronchial arteries is well-documented in CTEPH, intrapulmonary arterial collateral communications are less frequently described as a therapeutic pathway.2 We present 2 cases demonstrating the successful use of retrograde wiring techniques, via such intrapulmonary collaterals, to recanalize occluded pulmonary artery branches during PTPA.

Case Presentation

Case report 1: complete retrograde crossing

A 30-year-old woman diagnosed with CTEPH was referred for PTPA. Pulmonary artery angiography demonstrated a complete occlusion of the right A8a segmental branch (Figure 1A). Through the 6F guide catheter, a 0.014-inch basic Sion guidewire (Asahi Intecc), characterized by superior flexibility and a reduced risk of vascular perforation, was carefully advanced. Leveraging the retrograde flow, the wire was navigated from the distal A8b branch, through the collateral channel, and into the distal true lumen of the occluded A8a. After several manipulations, the wire was successfully advanced retrogradely across the occlusion to re-enter the common A8 trunk (Figure 1C).

The occluded lesion was serially dilated using balloon catheters. Final angiography confirmed successful recanalization, with the reappearance of the right A8a branch, now filling antegradely (Figure 1D).

Case report 2: retrograde landmark-guided antegrade crossing

A 63-year-old woman with CTEPH underwent PTPA for a complete occlusion of the left A7 pulmonary artery branch, visualized on left pulmonary artery angiography (Figure 2A). Following the same initial setup, a 0.014-inch basic Sion guidewire was advanced retrogradely from the left A8 branch into the distal aspect of the occluded left A7. However, despite multiple attempts, the wire could not be manipulated to cross the chronic total occlusion and re-enter the proximal left A7 trunk antegradely (Figures 2D and 2E).

To confirm the wire's position, we used an ASAHI Corsair Pro torqueable microcatheter (135 cm length, 2.6F distal outer diameter). During advancement, the patient was instructed to hold a deep inspiration to stabilize the vessel, and the catheter was gently rotated back and forth (never exceeding 10 turns in 1 direction) while following the guidewire. Microcatheter advancement was smooth, with successful navigation into the distal A7 branch; no parenchymal or pleural hemorrhage was observed. The guidewire was removed, and a retrograde angiogram was performed. This confirmed the catheter was within the A7 branch and revealed 2 small, distally flowing branches arising from its proximal portion, validating the target vessel (Figure 2F, red arrows).

The retrograde guidewire was then re-advanced and left in place as a clear intraluminal landmark. Under the guidance of the retrograde wire, a stiff Pilot 50 guidewire (Abbott) was advanced antegradely through the guide catheter to probe for the occluded ostium of the left A7. Using the retrograde wire as a target, the antegrade wire was successfully maneuvered into the distal true lumen, resulting in a classic "kissing" wire configuration (Figure 2G). The occlusion was then dilated with balloon catheters. Final angiography demonstrated successful recanalization, with the previously occluded left A7 branch now fully opacified (Figure 2H).

Outcome and Follow-Up

According to current clinical practice, right-heart catheterization—including the measurement of cardiac output—is routinely performed approximately 6 months after the completion of the final PTPA session to assess hemodynamic outcomes. In the 2 cases reported in this paper, all PTPA procedures have been completed only recently; therefore, the scheduled 6-month follow-up right-heart catheterization has not yet taken place. In case 1, mean pulmonary arterial pressure, obtained via guiding catheter, decreased from 36 mm Hg to 32 mm Hg following the present PTPA session. And the patient's World Health Organization functional class improved from III to II within 48 hours after the procedure, accompanied by subjective relief of dyspnea. Six-minute walk distance increased from 280 m to 320 m before discharge. In case 2, the mean pulmonary arterial pressure decreased from 42 mm Hg to 37 mm Hg. The World Health Organization functional classification improved from Class Ⅲ to Class Ⅱ. And 6-minute walk distance increased from 250 m to 300 m. No oxygen supplementation was required after the procedure.

Discussion

These 2 cases illustrate the feasibility and utility of retrograde wiring techniques for managing occluded pulmonary arteries in CTEPH, leveraging under-recognized intrapulmonary arterial collaterals. While systemic-to-pulmonary anastomoses, particularly via hypertrophied bronchial arteries, are a hallmark of CTEPH, distal anastomoses between adjacent pulmonary arterial branches can also develop distal to a complete occlusion.2 These connections provide a potential retrograde conduit to the distal true lumen of an occluded vessel.

Potential complications of the retrograde approach include vessel perforation, pulmonary hemorrhage, hemoptysis, pneumothorax, guidewire/microcatheter fracture, distal embolization, and embolism. In our series, no such complications occurred. To minimize risks, we recommend: 1) strict case selection (well-visualized, nontortuous collaterals); 2) the use of a torqueable microcatheter (eg, Corsair Pro) and a soft guidewire (eg, Sion); 3) gentle back-and-forth rotation (≤10 turns) and breath-hold during inspiration while advancing the microcatheter; 4) immediate availability of embolization materials and thoracic drainage.
