Modalities of Combined Medical and Surgical Treatment in Hidradenitis Suppurativa: A Systematic Review of Efficacy and Safety
Authors: Laura Mateu-Arrom, Alejandro Molina-Leyva, Eva Vilarrasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by recurrent nodules, abscesses, and sinus tracts that lead to significant morbidity and impaired quality of life. Although both biologic therapies and surgical interventions are effective when used independently, their combined use has gained increasing attention. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining immunomodulatory therapy with surgical procedures in HS. A conceptual framework was applied that distinguished three modalities of combined treatment according to the temporal relationship between biologic therapy and surgery: pre-biologic surgery, post-induction surgery, and concomitant approaches. A comprehensive search identified eight eligible studies, including 508 patients. Most studies were retrospective and of low methodological quality. Across studies, combination therapy (particularly with adalimumab, secukinumab, or bimekizumab) was generally associated with improved clinical outcomes compared to biologic monotherapy, including higher response rates (Hidradenitis Suppurativa Clinical Response [HiSCR]; 55% reduction in the International Hidradenitis Suppurativa 4 [IHS4-55]), greater reductions in pain, disease flares, and Dermatology Life Quality Index scores. Safety profiles were comparable between groups, with no consistent increase in postoperative complications or serious adverse events. However, key outcomes such as wound healing time were poorly reported. Substantial heterogeneity in study design, treatment protocols, and timing of interventions limited comparability and precluded meta-analysis. Overall, the available evidence preliminarily supports combined medical and surgical treatment as a potentially beneficial and well-tolerated strategy for moderate-to-severe HS, in line with current expert practice. However, given the low quality and substantial heterogeneity of the included studies, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in higher-quality prospective studies that adopt standardized definitions of combination therapy and report wound healing as a core outcome.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by recurrent nodules, abscesses, and tunnels that frequently lead to permanent and disfiguring scarring, predominantly affecting intertriginous and gluteal areas [1]. The disease is associated with intense pain and discomfort, resulting in a substantial negative impact on patients’ quality of life, social relationships, and occupational performance [2].

Current therapeutic strategies for HS include antibiotics, anti-inflammatory agents, biologic immunomodulators, and surgical interventions, in addition to supportive measures such as pain control, smoking cessation, and weight reduction [1, 3]. The marked clinical heterogeneity of HS [4], its variable progression patterns [5], and the wide range of available medical and surgical options [3], complicate the selection of optimal treatment. While both medical and surgical approaches have demonstrated efficacy when used independently, increasing attention has been directed toward combined treatment strategies, particularly when there is structural damage, to improve disease control, reduce recurrence rates, and enhance quality of life [3]. However, evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of this combined approach has not yet been systematically evaluated.

The objective of this systematic review was to assess the clinical efficacy, safety, and tolerability of combined immunomodulatory therapy and surgical or interventional procedures in patients with hidradenitis suppurativa.

Methods

Protocol

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [6]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under identification number CRD420261331420.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in the Medline and Scopus databases, including all publications available up to February 2026, applying a search strategy using the following keywords: (‘hidradenitis suppurativa’ OR ‘hidradenitis’) AND (‘biologics’ OR ‘immunomodulators’ OR ‘adalimumab’ OR ‘anti-TNF’ OR ‘secukinumab’ OR ‘bimekizumab’) AND (‘surgery’ OR ‘excision’ OR ‘surgical’ OR ‘interventional procedures’ OR ‘CO2 laser’ OR ‘deroofing’ OR ‘setons’).

Reference lists of eligible articles were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, retrospective studies, observational studies, and case series including at least ten participants. Study Selection

Two authors (LMA and EV) independently screened titles and abstracts. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were subsequently reviewed for eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author (AML).

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

Risk of bias and methodological quality were independently assessed by two authors (LMA and EV) using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool [7] for randomized trials and the ROBINS-I version 2 [8, 9] for non-randomized studies. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (AML).

Data Collection

The following data were extracted independently by two reviewers: authors, publication year, country, study design, sample size, demographic characteristics, inclusion criteria, treatment protocols, follow-up duration, outcome measures, efficacy results, and adverse events.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Basic descriptive statistics were extracted and summarized. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean and standard deviation (SD), and qualitative variables as percentages. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS® v22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Given the marked clinical heterogeneity (including variation in pharmacological treatments and surgical techniques) and methodological heterogeneity (combining RCTs and retrospective studies), a meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate in order to avoid biased results, and was therefore not performed.

To address the absence of a standardized definition of “combined medical and surgical treatment” in HS and to allow a structured qualitative synthesis of the included studies, we applied a conceptual framework that distinguished three modalities of combined treatment according to the temporal relationship between biologic therapy and surgery: (1) pre-biologic surgery, in which surgery preceded biologic initiation; (2) post-induction surgery, in which surgery was scheduled after a defined period of biologic induction (e.g., 12–16 weeks); and (3) concomitant approaches, in which biologic therapy and surgical procedures were delivered in parallel without a pre-specified temporal sequence. Each included study was classified into one of these modalities by two reviewers (LMA and EV) based on the timing of surgery relative to the initiation of biologic therapy as reported by the authors; disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (AML). When the timing of surgery was not explicitly described, the study was conservatively classified as a concomitant approach. Results

Study Characteristics

A total of 179 records were identified (172 through database searches and 7 through manual reference screening). After a review of titles and abstracts, 48 articles underwent full-text assessment, and eight studies ultimately fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Flowchart of the study selection process

Flowchart of the study selection process

Of these studies, four evaluated adalimumab combined with surgery [10–13], two assessed secukinumab [14, 15], one investigated bimekizumab [16], and one included multiple biologic therapies [17]. Surgical approaches consisted of excision in two studies [12, 17] and deroofing in one study [13]. The surgical technique was not specified in three studies [10, 14, 16], while two studies included multiple surgical modalities [11, 15]. Two studies assessed a combination of biologic therapy and post-induction surgery [11, 12], five studies adopted a concomitant approach [10, 13, 15–17], and one study involved pre-biologic surgery [14].

Five studies were retrospective observational [10, 14–17], two were RCTs [11, 12], and one was a prospective non-controlled study [13]. The studies were published between 2016 and 2025 and included a total of 508 patients with HS. The main outcomes of these eight studies are summarized in Table 1.

Risk of Bias and Quality of Included Studies

Overall, one study was rated as good quality [12], two studies as fair quality [11, 16], and five as poor quality [10, 13–15, 17] due to significant risks of bias, related mainly to lack of blinding, confounding, and selection bias (Figs. 3, 4). Patient Characteristics

Across all studies, 508 patients were included: 192 received adalimumab plus surgery, 131 received adalimumab alone, 36 received secukinumab plus surgery, 15 received secukinumab alone, 37 received bimekizumab plus surgery, 41 received bimekizumab alone, and 56 received surgery alone. Follow-up ranged from 3 to 12 months. Most patients had moderate-to-severe disease: Hurley stage I (n = 16 patients), Hurley stage II (n = 250), and Hurley stage III (n = 242).

Adalimumab Monotherapy Versus Adalimumab Combined with Surgery

Efficacy

In a RCT, patients receiving adalimumab combined with surgery achieved significantly higher HiSCR rates than patients treated with placebo plus surgery at week 12, with a difference of 14% (95% confidence interval [CI] 0–27; p = 0.049). The proportion of patients achieving HiSCR excluding the surgical site was not significantly higher in the combination group at week 12. However, surgery was performed at week 12 of treatment, and this outcome reached statistical significance at week 24 (difference + 20%; 95% CI 7–34; p = 0.003) [12]. The intention-to-treat analysis also showed significantly greater improvements in the PGA, DLQI, HS Impact Assessment, and HS Symptom Assessment scores at weeks 12 and 24 in the adalimumab plus surgery group. No significant differences were observed between groups for other secondary endpoints, including surgical surface area or the proportion of patients requiring less extensive surgery than initially planned or no surgery at week 12 [12].

In a subsequent randomized trial in a real-world setting, HiSCR rates were observed at 12 months in 38% in patients treated with adalimumab alone and 59% in those receiving adalimumab combined with surgery, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.22). Patients in the combination group showed a significantly greater reduction in IHS4 compared with those receiving adalimumab monotherapy (− 19.1 ± 11.3 vs − 7.8 ± 11.8; p < 0.001) [11]. Higher response rates were also observed for the IHS4-55 (87% vs 32%; p < 0.001) and ≥ 2-point PGA improvement (58% vs 18%; p < 0.001), together with greater improvement in DLQI scores (− 8.2 ± 6.2 vs − 4.0 ± 7.7; p = 0.02). A significant reduction in pain was observed only in the combination group (p = 0.044). HS flares (defined in this trial as a sustained increase in inflammatory lesions requiring rescue therapy or unscheduled medical attention) occurred in 22% of patients treated with adalimumab plus surgery compared with 35% of those receiving adalimumab monotherapy [11].

In a single-center retrospective study, HiSCR50 (≥ 50% reduction in inflammatory lesion count/HiSCR count) was achieved in 78.9% of patients receiving combined adalimumab and surgery, compared with 71.4% of patients treated with adalimumab alone (the time point was not reported). In a study of 42 patients undergoing surgery for HS (13 treated with adalimumab and 1 with secukinumab), older age (p = 0.05) and Hurley stage III disease (p < 0.05) were inversely associated with the probability of achieving HiSCR at week 16 of treatment [10].

Safety

Bechara et al. reported adverse events (AEs) in 72% of patients treated with adalimumab combined with surgery (65% in patients treated with placebo combined with surgery). The most frequent events were nasopharyngitis (18%), worsening of HS (14%), procedural pain (14%), headache (13%), and arthralgia (6%). Only one serious AE was recorded in the combination group. Postoperative complications included one wound infection and one postoperative hemorrhage in both the adalimumab-plus-surgery and placebo-plus-surgery groups [12].

Aarts et al. observed a significantly higher incidence of AEs in patients receiving adalimumab monotherapy (94%) compared with those treated with adalimumab plus surgery (68%; p = 0.01), mainly due to a higher frequency of HS flares. No serious treatment-related adverse events were reported. The most common adverse events were HS flares (more frequent in the monotherapy group), viral or bacterial infections, injection-site hematomas, postoperative pain, and mild surgical-site bleeding [11].

Vu et al. evaluated outcomes following deroofing with or without adalimumab treatment. Postoperative infection requiring antibiotics occurred in 2.2% of patients, postoperative pain in 18%, and poor cosmetic outcomes in 2%. No comparison was performed between patients receiving biologic therapy and those who were not [13]. Finally, Malvaso et al. reported postoperative complications in 19% of patients (11% wound infection, 4% wound dehiscence, and 4% both). No increase in complications was observed in patients treated with surgery combined with biologics (adalimumab or secukinumab) compared with those receiving surgery alone or surgery with conventional therapies [17].

Secukinumab Monotherapy or Secukinumab with Surgery

Efficacy

Two retrospective studies evaluated secukinumab combined with surgery [14, 15]. Liu et al. reported HiSCR50 response rates of 76.2% (16/21), 71.4% (16/21), and 81% (17/21) at weeks 16, 32, and 48, respectively. Significant reductions from baseline were also observed in IHS4, DLQI, and visual analogue scale (VAS) scores at all time points [14]. Xu et al. compared secukinumab combined with surgery with secukinumab monotherapy. After 48 weeks of follow-up, the combination group achieved higher HiSCR50 rates (100% [15/15] vs 71.4% [10/14]; p = 0.042) and higher IHS4-55 response rates (100% [15/15] vs 35.7% [5/14]; p < 0.001). Greater reductions in abscesses, inflammatory nodules, IHS4, and DLQI were also observed in the combination group compared to secukinumab monotherapy. HS flares (defined in this trial as the appearance of at least 25% more abscesses or inflammatory nodules, with a minimum increase of two lesions, relative to the baseline lesion count) occurred in 35.7% (5/14) of patients receiving secukinumab monotherapy and in 13.3% (2/15) of those receiving combination therapy [15].

Safety

In the study by Liu et al., only one adverse event (4.7%), consisting of mild eczema, was reported [14]. Xu et al. documented one case (7.1%) of injection-site hematoma and one case (7.1%) of wound infection in the monotherapy group, and one case (6.6%) of scar contracture in the combination group. No serious adverse events were observed [15].

Bimekizumab with or Without Surgery

One multicenter retrospective study evaluated bimekizumab in patients with HS [16]. Among 78 included patients, 37 underwent surgery; however, outcomes were not reported separately for surgical and non-surgical groups. IHS4-55 response rates at weeks 16, 24, and 48 were 46.2%, 67.9%, and 82.1%, respectively. At week 48, the IHS4-100 response rate was 23.2%. Significant reductions were observed in VAS scores (from 7.4 to 2.7; p < 0.001) and in monthly flare frequency (from 2 to 0.5; p < 0.001). Adverse events occurred in 43.6% of patients and mainly consisted of mild-to-moderate fungal infections (26.9%) and eczematous reactions (9%) [16].

Discussion

This systematic review proposes a conceptual framework that classifies combined medical and surgical treatment of HS into three modalities according to the temporal relationship between biologic therapy and surgery—pre-biologic surgery, post-induction surgery, and concomitant approaches—and synthesizes the available evidence within this framework. Across modalities, the included studies suggest overall that the combination of biologic therapy (adalimumab, secukinumab, or bimekizumab) with surgical procedures may be associated with improved clinical outcomes compared with biologic monotherapy. Although the available evidence is limited and predominantly derived from retrospective observational studies, a trend toward higher rates of HiSCR and IHS4-55 responses, along with greater reductions in pain, disease flares, and DLQI scores, was observed in patients receiving combination therapy. Importantly, safety profiles appeared comparable between combination therapy and biologic monotherapy, with no increase in postoperative complications or serious AEs among patients treated with biologics in conjunction with surgery. Taken together, this early evidence is consistent with expert opinion and current clinical practice, which support combined medical and surgical therapy as the approach most likely to achieve optimal disease control in patients with moderate-to-severe HS, while acknowledging that the certainty of the underlying evidence remains low.

HS is characterized by a progressive disease course, with a transition from inflammatory lesions to irreversible tissue destruction that often becomes refractory to medical therapy, partly due to the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures associated with epithelialized tunnels, which sustain local immune activation and limit the response to systemic therapy [3, 18]. Medical and surgical treatments in HS have distinct but complementary objectives: surgery provides definitive removal of localized lesions, whereas medical therapy primarily controls systemic inflammation, although it does not ensure sustained disease control, particularly after treatment discontinuation [3]. Therefore, their combination offers several theoretical advantages, including limiting the extent of surgical intervention, improving surgical outcomes by reducing local recurrence, and enhancing the effectiveness of systemic therapy by eliminating persistent inflammatory foci [3, 19].

Current European guidelines consider surgery to be an essential component in the management of HS and frame the combination of surgery and medical therapy as a holistic therapeutic approach [3]. Nevertheless, evidence supporting the clinical benefit of this combined strategy remains limited. Although the available evidence is still of low quality, the available studies included in this systematic review suggest a consistent trend toward improved outcomes with combination therapy. Among biologic agents, adalimumab has the most robust evidence base. It is the only drug evaluated in RCTs [11, 12], with over 300 reported patients receiving either adalimumab monotherapy or adalimumab in combination with surgery [10–13, 17]. This combination has demonstrated superiority over monotherapy in terms of higher HiSCR rates at 12 weeks post-surgery [12], as well as improvements in IHS4, PGA, and DLQI scores at 12 months [11]. Evidence for secukinumab in combination with surgery is emerging [20]. However, only one retrospective case series included in this review directly compared secukinumab monotherapy with combination therapy, showing significantly higher HiSCR and IHS4-55 response rates at week 48 in the combination group [15]. As expected, given the more recent approval of bimekizumab for the treatment of HS, no studies to date have directly compared bimekizumab monotherapy with its use in combination with surgery. Notably, the only study evaluating bimekizumab included in this review did not report outcomes separately for patients who underwent surgery and those who did not, which precludes any assessment of the additive effect of surgery in patients receiving this agent [16]. This represents a relevant gap in the current evidence that future studies should specifically address.

Although a general trend toward improved clinical outcomes is observed with combination therapy, the current body of evidence has several interrelated limitations that should be interpreted as a whole and that justify a cautious, exploratory reading of our findings. Several limitations of the studies included in this review preclude drawing definitive conclusions. First, the overall quality of the included studies was low, with most of the studies retrospective in design and at high risk of selection, indication, and confounding bias. Second, only a minority of studies directly compared combination therapy with biologic monotherapy, while others used different comparators (adalimumab with surgery compared to surgery alone) [12] or lacked control groups entirely [13, 14, 16, 17]. Third, there was substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, which precluded quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis. Outcomes measured, follow-up time points, and biologic dosing regimens varied considerably. Fourth, surgical and wound-related endpoints—particularly wound healing time, which is a clinically critical outcome when biologic therapy is maintained perioperatively—were either inconsistently reported or absent from most studies, representing a major gap in the current evidence. Fifth, the only published study evaluating bimekizumab combined with surgery did not report outcomes stratified according to whether surgery was performed, which precluded any direct estimate of the effect of combination therapy with this agent and limited our ability to extend conclusions to interleukin (IL)-17A/F inhibition. Sixth, patient-related factors that are known or plausible modifiers of response (Hurley stage, anatomical location, smoking, body mass index, prior treatments, comorbidities, and prior surgical history) were rarely incorporated into stratified or multivariable analyses, so predictors of response to combined medical–surgical treatment remain insufficiently characterized despite their clear clinical relevance. An important consideration when interpreting the results of this systematic review is the substantial heterogeneity in what constitutes “combination therapy” across the included studies. The timing of surgery relative to biologic initiation varied considerably: in the trial by Bechara et al., surgery was performed at week 12 of adalimumab treatment, allowing a period of inflammatory control prior to the intervention [12]; in contrast, Liu et al. performed surgery 1 week before initiating secukinumab, representing a fundamentally different therapeutic sequence [14]. In several other studies, the timing of surgery was not specified. Similarly, the surgical techniques employed ranged from wide excision and limited excision [12, 17] to deroofing [13], with some studies including multiple modalities [11, 15] and others not specifying the technique used [10, 14, 16]. These differences are clinically meaningful, as operating on tissue where inflammation has been pharmacologically controlled may yield different outcomes than operating on actively inflamed tissue prior to biologic therapy. While this heterogeneity was appropriately identified as a reason for not performing a meta-analysis, its implications for the interpretation of individual study results and for the design of future comparative studies deserve emphasis. In response to this conceptual ambiguity, we propose a working classification of combined medical and surgical treatment in HS into three modalities according to the temporal sequence between biologic therapy and surgery: (1) pre-biologic surgery, in which surgery precedes biologic initiation, typically aimed at debulking established tunnels or scarring before pharmacological control; (2) post-induction surgery, in which surgery is performed after a defined period of biologic induction (e.g., 12–16 weeks), targeting residual structural lesions in tissue with reduced inflammation; and (3) concomitant approaches, in which biologic therapy and limited surgical procedures (such as deroofing of individual tunnels) are delivered in parallel as part of an ongoing medical–surgical strategy. Adopting and reporting against this classification, together with the recently proposed expert Delphi consensus on standardized procedural definitions for HS surgery by Bui et al. [21, would substantially improve comparability across studies and support the development of evidence-based perioperative protocols.

Current guidelines do not establish consensus regarding the optimal timing of surgery in patients with HS [3]. The concept of a “window of surgical opportunity” has been proposed, suggesting that earlier surgical intervention in patients with structural lesions may be associated with improved clinical outcomes [22]. Supporting this, a retrospective study not included in this systematic review, which involved 80 patients with moderate-to-severe HS undergoing wide excision and secondary intention healing, found that longer disease duration, higher Hurley stage (II–III), and higher IHS4 scores were associated with more extensive surgical interventions and higher recurrence rates [22]. Similarly, guidelines do not specify how biologic therapy should be managed perioperatively in patients undergoing surgery [3]. Based on the studies included in this review, no clear increase in AEs was observed with combination therapy. However, important parameters such as wound healing time were poorly reported. Only one study provided data on healing time after deroofing procedures, without stratifying results according to biologic treatment [13]. This is a particularly relevant gap, as biologic agents—especially anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) therapies—have a theoretical impact on wound healing that remains a source of concern among surgeons and patients [23]. Establishing wound healing time as a standardized secondary outcome in future studies would help address this uncertainty. Moreover, the predominantly retrospective and non-protocolized design of most included studies, in which postoperative complications were typically captured from chart review rather than ascertained prospectively, raises the possibility that minor surgical or wound-related adverse events were inconsistently recorded; this should be considered when interpreting the apparently favorable safety profile.

Several patient-related factors, such as smoking and elevated body mass index, are known to exacerbate HS [24, 25]. Other factors, including the number of affected regions [26], groin or perineal involvement, smoking status, and psychiatric comorbidities [27], have been associated with poorer surgical outcomes or treatment refractoriness. However, it remains unclear whether these factors modify outcomes in patients receiving combined surgical and biologic therapy. Notably, only one study included in this review evaluated predictors of response to combination therapy [17]. In that retrospective cohort of 42 patients undergoing surgery, younger age at the time of surgery was the only factor associated with achieving HiSCR at week 16, while other variables such as obesity and smoking were not significantly associated with clinical improvement [17].

The combination of biologic therapy with surgical procedures is not unique to HS and has been more extensively studied in other chronic inflammatory diseases, which may offer useful parallels. In perianal Crohn’s disease, the combination of surgical drainage with anti-TNF therapy has become a cornerstone of management, with evidence suggesting that combined approaches achieve higher fistula closure rates than either modality alone [28, 29]. Similarly, in rheumatology, considerable experience has been accumulated regarding the perioperative management of biologic therapies, with guidelines from multiple societies addressing the timing of treatment interruption and resumption around surgical procedures [30]. While the pathophysiology and surgical requirements of HS differ from those of these conditions, the general principle that combining targeted immunomodulation with surgery may yield synergistic benefits appears to be shared [31]. Drawing on this broader evidence base when designing future studies in HS could help standardize perioperative protocols and outcome assessment.

There is a clear lack of prospective studies specifically designed to assess the efficacy and safety of combined surgical and biologic treatment in HS. Although such studies are challenging due to the heterogeneity of HS presentations, future research comparing combination therapy with surgery alone or biologic therapy alone at standardized time points is needed. Given the ethical and practical difficulties of randomizing patients to surgery versus no surgery, pragmatic study designs—such as multicenter prospective cohort studies with standardized outcome measures and predefined time points—may represent a more feasible approach than classical RCTs. Collaborative registries capturing real-world data with sufficient granularity regarding surgical technique, timing, pre-surgical imaging tests, perioperative biologic management, and patient characteristics would also be valuable. Subgroup analyses incorporating patient characteristics (e.g., comorbidities), disease features (e.g., anatomical location, severity), and treatment variables (e.g., type of surgery, biologic agent) would provide important insights. Additionally, establishing consensus definitions of treatment response specific to combination therapy would facilitate comparisons across studies and contribute to a more robust evidence base.

Finally, the decision to combine biologic therapy with surgery in clinical practice is inherently individualized and depends on multiple factors, including disease severity and phenotype, patient preferences, tolerance of surgical risk, and treatment expectations. Several of the studies included in this review assessed patient-reported outcomes such as DLQI, which indirectly capture the patient perspective. The available evidence and current guidelines provide the basis for shared decision-making at the bedside, which should be actively encouraged in clinical practice so that the implementation of combined medical and surgical strategies reflects not only clinical evidence but also patient values and priorities.

Conclusion

The available evidence may suggest that combining biologic therapy with surgical procedures in patients with moderate-to-severe HS may offer clinical benefits beyond those achieved with biologic monotherapy, without an apparent increase in AEs. While these findings support the potential role of combined medical and surgical management in selected patients, the overall certainty of the evidence remains low. Most of the available data originate from retrospective studies with heterogeneous methodologies, small sample sizes, and varying definitions of combination therapy. Consequently, these results should be interpreted with caution. A key opportunity for future work is to define what constitutes combined medical and surgical treatment in HS, distinguishing pre-biologic surgery, post-induction surgery, and concomitant approaches as separate modalities. At present, the decision to combine these modalities should be individualized based on disease characteristics and patient preferences. Well-designed prospective studies with standardized surgical and pharmacological protocols, predefined outcomes, including wound healing parameters, and adequate follow-up are needed to define the optimal implementation of this combined approach and to identify which patient subgroups are most likely to benefit.
