The image of functor morphing
Ehud Meir

Abstract
. Functor morphing provides a method to translate complex representations of automorphism groups of finite modules over finite rings to representations of automorphism groups of functors in some abelian category. In this paper we give an explicit criterion for a representation to be in the image of functor morphing using the action of parabolic subgroups. We then demonstrate this criterion on Borel groups of finite fields.



1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a finite module over a finite ring
R
. In
[
CMO4
]
Tyrone Crisp, Uri Onn, and the author, introduced a new technique called functor morphing to study the representation theory of
\Aut_{R}(M)
. Assume that
M=M_{1}^{a_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{n}^{a_{n}}
is the decomposition of
M
into a direct sum of non-isomorphic indecomposable modules. Functor morphing associates to every irreducible representation
V
of
\Aut_{R}(M)
an additive functor
F:\langle M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\rangle\to Ab
and an irreducible representation
\widetilde{V}
of
\Aut(F)
. Here
\langle M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\rangle
stands for the full additive subcategory of
R-\,\mathrm{mod}\,
generated by
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
.
In most cases the procedure of functor morphing reduces the numerical complexity of the representation
V
. The functor
F
is the minimal (with respect to the subquotient relation) functor that satisfies
\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),V)\neq 0
, and the representation
\widetilde{V}
is given by
\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),V)
, where
\Aut(F)
acts on
KF(M)
in the natural way. The fact that
F
is uniquely defined and that
\widetilde{V}
is irreducible follows from a construction of the author of symmetric monoidal categories using invariant theory
[
M1
]
. The study of the representation theory of the groups
\Aut_{R}(M)
stems from the representation theory of the groups
\GL_{n}(\mathfrak{o}/(\pi^{l}))
, where
\mathfrak{o}
is a discrete valuation ring with uniformizer
\pi
and finite quotient field. One of the main philosophical consequences of functor morphing is the fact that the representation theory of these group contains in fact the representation theory of
\Aut_{R}(M)
for very general
R
and
M
. See also
[
CMO1
,
CMO2
,
CMO3
]
for a study of the representation theory of
\GL_{n}(\mathfrak{o}/(\pi^{l}))
using generalizations of Harish-Chandra functors and Clifford Theory.

Functor morphing thus gives a new way to study the representation theory of a very large class of finite groups.
We get a stratification
\Irr(\Aut_{R}(M))\cong\sqcup_{F}\Irr^{M,fm}(\Aut(F))
, where
\Irr^{M,fm}(\Aut(F))
stands for the irreducible representations of
\Aut(F)
that arise from functor morphing of representations of
\Aut_{R}(M)
. This raises the question of what irreducible representations actually appear in
\Irr^{M,fm}(\Aut(F))
. The goal of the present paper is to answer this question, and provide concrete examples for the family of upper triangular groups
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

To state our main result, we introduce some terminology. Let
M=\bigoplus_{i}M_{i}^{a_{i}}
,
F
, and
\mathcal{C}=\langle M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\rangle
, be as above. We say that
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
is a minimal presentation of
F
if

\Hom_{R}(Y,-)\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}\Hom_{R}(X,-)\to F\to 0

is a minimal resolution of
F
in the category
\mathcal{C}_{+1}=Fun(\mathcal{C},\text{Ab})
.
We have already seen in
[
CMO4
]
the connection between the minimal resolution of
F
and functor morphing. In Section
3
we prove the following:

Lemma 1.1

(See Lemma
3.18
)
.

A necessary condition for representations of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
to appear as functor morphing of representations of
\Aut_{R}(M)
is that if
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
is a minimal presentation of
F
then
X
is a direct summand of
M
.

If
G\subseteq F
is a subfunctor, we write
Z_{G}
for the unique object in
\mathcal{C}
such that
\Hom_{R}(Z_{G},-)
is the projective cover of
G
.
We write
P_{G}\subseteq\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
for the subgroup
\{\varphi\in\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)|\text{Im}(\varphi-\text{Id}_{F})\subseteq G\}
.

In Section
4
we prove our main result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
,
\mathcal{C}
, and
F
be as above. Assume that
F
has a minimal presentation
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
and that
M=X\oplus Z
.
Let
V
be an irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
. Then
V
is the functor morphing of an irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
if and only if the following condition holds: for every semisimple subfunctor
G\subseteq F
such that
Z_{G}
is not a direct summand of
Z
, it holds that
\Hom_{P_{G}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
.

The main result has the following corollary, characterizing the situations in which all the irreducible representations of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
arise from functor morphing:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
M=X\oplus Z
and let
(X\xrightarrow{f}Y)
be a minimal resolution for
F
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

Every irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is the functor morphing of an irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.

(2)

The trivial representation
\mathbf{1}
of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is the functor morphing of some irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.

(3)

If
\Hom(X_{1},-)
is the projective cover of
soc(F)
then
X_{1}
is a direct summand of
Z
.

Finally, in Section
5
we give examples. We first consider the family of groups
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. By
[
Z1
]
we know that these groups give rise to a positive self-adjoint Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}
. We explain the connection between our main result and the structure of
\mathcal{H}
.
The second family of examples that we give is of the family of groups
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
of upper triangular matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We show how our result translates to a combinatorial criterion, and we show how this gives a new enumeration of the irreducible representations of
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
when
n\leq 4
.
A long standing conjecture of Higman from 1960
[
H1
]
states that for every
n
the number of conjugacy classes in the unipotent radical
U_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
of
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a polynomial in
q

[
VA1
,
So1
]
. Since the number of conjugacy classes is the same as the number of irreducible representations, and since
U_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a normal subgroup of
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
with an abelian quotient, this suggests that there are deep connections between enumeration of irreducible representations of
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and Higman’s conjecture. It is our hope that, following this paper, the technique of functor morphing will be further used to study this conjecture.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Functor morphing

Let
R
be a finite ring and let
M
be an
R
-module. Functor morphing is a tool that was introduced in
[
CMO4
]
to study the representation theory of
\Aut_{R}(M)
. We recall here the details. Write
M=M_{1}^{a_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{n}^{a_{n}}
for the decomposition of
M
into non-isomorphic indecomposable modules. Write
\mathcal{C}=\langle M_{1},\ldots M_{n}\rangle\subseteq R-\,\mathrm{mod}\,
for the full subcategory of finite
R
-modules of the form
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{b_{i}}
and write
\mathcal{C}_{+1}=Fun(\mathcal{C},Ab)
for the category of additive functors from
\mathcal{C}
to the category of finite abelian groups. The isomorphism classes of the objects in
\mathcal{C}_{+1}
are partially ordered, where
F\preccurlyeq G
if and only if
F
is isomorphic to a subquotient of
G
. For every
F\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
it holds that
F(M)
is an
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\times\Aut_{R}(M)
-set in a natural way. As a result, the linearlization
KF(M)=span\{u_{v}\}_{v\in F(M)}
is an
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\times\Aut_{R}(M)
-representation.

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem A,
[
CMO4
]
)
.

Let
V
be an irreducible representation of
Aut_{R}(M)
. There is a
unique (up to isomorphism) functor
F\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
such that
\Hom_{Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),V)\neq 0
and
\Hom_{Aut_{R}(M)}(KG(M),V)=0
for every
G\prec F
.

This theorem shows that to every irreducible representation
V
of
\Aut_{R}(M)
we can attach a functor
F\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
.
In fact, more is true. Write
\overline{KF(M)}
for the largest subobject of
KF(M)
that satisfies

\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\overline{KF(M)},KG(M))=0

for every
G\prec F
.
The
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
-action on
KF(M)
restricts to an action on
\overline{KF(M)}
and we have an algebra morphism

\Phi_{F}(M):K\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\to\End_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\overline{KF(M)}).

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem B,
[
CMO4
]
)
.

The map
\Phi_{F}(M)
is surjective. If the numbers
a_{i}
that appear in the decomposition
M_{i}=\bigoplus_{i}M_{i}^{a_{i}}
are big enough, then
\Phi_{F}(M)
is an isomorphism.

This theorem enables us to attach to
V
an irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
. Indeed, we can decompose
\overline{KF(M)}
as an
\Aut_{R}(M)
-representation and write
\overline{KF(M)}=\bigoplus_{j}U_{j}\otimes V_{j}
, where
U_{j}
are vector spaces and
V_{j}
are non-isomorphic
\Aut_{R}(M)
-representations. The action of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
commutes with the action of
\Aut_{R}(M)
, and is thus given by an action on the vector spaces
U_{j}
. The fact that
\Phi_{F}(M)
is surjective implies that
U_{j}
is an irreducible
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
-representation.

Definition 2.3
.

We call
(F,U_{j}^{*})
the functor morphing of
(M,V_{j})
, and we write
(M,V_{j})\leadsto(F,U_{j}^{*})
.

Remark 2.4
.

(1)

By the first theorem we see that for every irreducible representation
V
of
\Aut_{R}(M)
there is a unique (up to isomorphism) functor
F
such that
V
appears in
\overline{KF(M)}
. So we can talk freely about
the
functor morphing of
V
.

(2)

The reason we take
U_{j}^{*}
instead of
U_{j}
in the definition is that we want to make sure that in the special case
F=\Hom_{R}(M,-)
it holds that representations functor morph to themselves under the identification
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\cong\Aut_{R}(M)
.

(3)

In the paper
[
CMO4
]
the ring
R
was an
\mathfrak{o}_{l}:=\mathfrak{o}/(\pi^{l})
-algebra, where
\mathfrak{o}
is some discrete valuation ring with uniformizer
\pi
, and
\mathcal{C}_{+1}
was the category of
\mathfrak{o}_{l}
-linear functors from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathfrak{o}_{l}-\Mod
. It can easily be seen that this category is equivalent to the category of additive functors from
\mathcal{C}
to
Ab
. In one direction we can just compose with the forgetful functor
\mathfrak{o}_{l}-\Mod\to Ab
, and in the other direction we can use the fact that if
F:\mathcal{C}\to Ab
is an additive functor, then
F(X)
has a canonical
\mathfrak{o}_{l}
-module structure for every
X\in\mathcal{C}
. In this paper we will just use the definition
\mathcal{C}_{+1}=Fun(\mathcal{C},Ab)
that does not require to refer to a discrete valuation ring.

2.2.
Hom-spaces

We begin with the following definition.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
F,G\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
and let
H\subseteq F\oplus G\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
. We define

T_{H}\in\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),KG(M))

u_{v}\mapsto\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v^{\prime}\in G(M)\\
(v,v^{\prime})\in H(M)\end{subarray}}u_{v^{\prime}}.

It is easy to see that
T_{H}
is indeed a morphism of
\Aut_{R}(M)
-modules.
The following is Proposition 2.23 in
[
CMO4
]

Proposition 2.6
.

The set
\{T_{H}\}_{H\subseteq F\oplus G}
spans
\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),KG(M))
.

Since
KF(M)
is a permutation module it holds that
(KF(M))^{*}\cong KF(M)
as
\Aut_{R}(M)
-representations.
We thus have a natural isomorphism

(2.7)

\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},K(F\oplus G)(M))\cong\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},KF(M)\otimes KG(M))\cong\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),KG(M)).

Remark 2.8
.

The trivial one-dimensional representation
\mathbf{1}
is isomorphic to
K0(M)
, where
0
is the zero functor from
\mathcal{C}
to
Ab
. It can easily be seen that under the isomorphism of Equation
2.7
the element
T_{H}\in\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(K0(M),K(F\oplus G)(M))
corresponds to the element
T_{H}\in\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(KF(M),KG(M))
. We will use this in what follows, when we study the linear relations the elements
T_{H}
satisfy.

The next definition will appear often in our study of functor morphing:

Definition 2.9
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
. We say that
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
is a presentation of
F
if
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
, and
F
has a resolution

\Hom_{R}(Y,-)\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}\Hom_{R}(X,-)\to F\to 0.

The presentation is called minimal if the resolution is minimal.

By Lemma 4.7. in
[
CMO4
]
we know that if
F
has a minimal resolution
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
and
\overline{KF(M)}\neq 0
then
X
is a direct summand of
M
. We will prove this again in Lemma
3.18
below. For more background on homological algebra in the category
\mathcal{C}_{+1}
, see Appendix A in
[
CMO4
]
.

3.
linear relations in hom-spaces

By Equation
2.7
we see that it is enough to study the linear relations among the elements

\{T_{H}\}_{H\subseteq F}\subseteq\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},KF(M))

for an arbitrary functor
F\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{C}
and
F\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
. We say that
F
is
M
-redundant if it holds that

F(M)=\bigcup_{H\subsetneq F}H(M).

If
F
is not
M
-redundant we say that
F
is
M
-irredundant.

Definition 3.2
.

For every
G\in\mathcal{C}_{+1}
we write

X_{G}(M)=G(M)\backslash\cup_{G^{\prime}\subsetneq G}G^{\prime}(M).

Thus,
G
is
M
-redundant if and only if
X_{G}(M)=\varnothing
.

Definition 3.3
.

For every
H\subseteq F
we define
L_{H}=\sum_{v\in X_{G}(M)}u_{v}\in\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},KF(M))

Lemma 3.4
.

It holds that

H(M)=\bigsqcup_{G\subseteq H}X_{G}(M).

Proof.

We first notice that that we have
X_{G_{1}}(M)\cap X_{G_{2}}(M)=\varnothing
for
G_{1}\neq G_{2}
. Indeed, if
v\in X_{G_{1}}(M)
and
v\in X_{G_{2}}(M)
then

v\in G_{1}(M)\cap G_{2}(M)=(G_{1}\cap G_{2})(M)
. But
G_{1}\cap G_{2}\subsetneq G_{1},G_{2}
, which implies that
v\notin X_{G_{1}}(M)
and
v\notin X_{G_{2}}(M)
, a contradiction.

It is clear that
X_{G}(M)\subseteq H(M)
for every
G\subseteq H
.
For
v\in H(M)
, write
Y_{v}=\{G|v\in G(M)\}
. Then
Y_{v}
is ordered by inclusion, and it contains a single minimal element. Indeed, if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are two minimal functors in
Y_{v}
, then
G_{1}\cap G_{2}\in Y_{v}
is minimal and
G_{1}\cap G_{2}\subseteq G_{1},G_{2}
.
Take
G
to be the unique minimal element in
Y_{v}
. Then it holds that
v\in G(M)\backslash\bigcup_{G^{\prime}\subsetneq G}G^{\prime}(M)=X_{G}(M)
, which implies the lemma.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

For
H\subseteq F
it holds that
T_{H}=\sum_{G\subseteq H}L_{G}
.

Proof.

Using the equality from the previous lemma we get

T_{H}=\sum_{v\in H(M)}u_{v}=\sum_{G\subseteq H}\sum_{v\in X_{G}(M)}u_{v}=\sum_{G\subseteq H}L_{G}.

∎

The last corollary shows us how to write the elements
T_{H}
as linear combinations of the elemenets
L_{G}
.
We can also go the other way around.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
H
be a functor, and let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}
be the maximal proper subfunctors of
H
.
It holds that

(3.7)

L_{H}=T_{H}-\sum_{i}T_{H_{i}}+\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}T_{H_{i_{1}}\cap H_{i_{2}}}-\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}}T_{H_{i_{1}}\cap H_{i_{2}}\cap H_{i_{3}}}+\cdots+(-1)^{r}T_{H_{1}\cap H_{2}\cap\cdots\cap H_{r}}.

Proof.

It holds that
X_{H}(M)=H(M)\backslash\bigcup_{H^{\prime}\subsetneq H}H^{\prime}(M)=H(M)\backslash\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}(M)
.
The equality now follows from applying the Inclusion-exclusion principle.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

We could have also phrased the last lemma using all the subfunctors of
H
, instead of just the maximal ones. However, there will be a huge advantage in using only the maximal ones. Indeed, For every
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{s}
it holds that
H/(H_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap H_{i_{s}})
is a direct sum of simple functors. This will enable us to reduce some calculations in Section
4
to the familiar playfield of the groups
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, as any automorphism group of a semisimple functor is of the form
\prod_{i}\GL_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}})
.

Proposition 3.9
.

If
H
is
M
-redundant then
L_{H}=0
. The set
\{L_{H}\}
for
H

M
-irredundant is a basis for
\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},KF(M))
.

Proof.

Corollary
3.5
shows that we can write the elements
T_{H}
as linear combinations of the elements
L_{H}
. This implies that
\{L_{H}\}_{H\subseteq F}
spans
\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},KF(M))
. If
H
is
M
-redundant then
X_{H}(M)=\varnothing
and the definition then gives that
L_{H}=0
. On the other hand, the supports of
L_{H}
for a functor
H
that is
M
-irredundant is exactly
X_{H}(M)
. Since
X_{H}(M)\cap X_{H^{\prime}}(M)=\emptyset
for
H\neq H^{\prime}
by Lemma
3.4
, the set
\{L_{H}\}
where
H
is an
M
-irredundant subfunctor of
F
is linearly independent, and we get a basis for
\Hom_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\mathbf{1},KF(M))
.
∎

We thus see that the concept of
M
-redundancy is fundamental to understanding the hom-spaces and endomorphism algebras. The next proposition gives an explicit criterion for a functor to be
M
-redundant.

Proposition 3.10
.

Assume that
F
has a minimal presentation
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
. Then
F
is
M
-redundant if and only if
X
is not a direct summand of
M
.

Proof.

Write
p:\Hom_{R}(X,-)\to F
.
Assume first that
F
is
M
-irredundant. Take
v\in F(M)\backslash\bigcup_{G\subsetneq F}G(M)
. Take
\varphi\in p_{M}^{-1}(v)
. Then
\varphi\in\Hom_{R}(X,M)
.
In particular, it induces a natural transformation
\varphi^{*}:\Hom_{R}(M,-)\to\Hom_{R}(X,-)
. We then have that
\text{Im}(p\varphi^{*})=G
is a subfunctor of
F
. It holds that
p\varphi^{*}(\text{Id}_{M})=p(\varphi)=v\in G(M)
. But
v
is not contained in any
H(M)
for
H
a proper subfunctor of
F
, so
G=F
. Thus means that
\text{Im}(p\varphi^{*})=F
, and so
p\varphi^{*}
is surjective. Since
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
is a minimal presentation of
F
, this implies that
\varphi^{*}:\Hom(M,-)\to\Hom_{R}(X,-)
is surjective. In particular,
\varphi^{*}:\Hom_{R}(M,X)\to\Hom_{R}(X,X)
is surjective, and so there is a
\psi:M\to X
such that
\varphi^{*}(\psi)=\psi\varphi=\text{Id}_{X}
. That is-
X
is a direct summand of
M
.

In the other direction, assume that
X
is a direct summand of
M
and that
F
is
M
-redundant. Let
\varphi:X\to M
be a split injection. Then
p(\varphi)\in F(M)
, and since
F
is
M
-redundant, there is a proper subfunctor
G\subsetneq F
such that
p(\varphi)\in G(M)
.
Let
\Hom_{R}(Z,-)
be the projective cover of
G
. We get the following commutative diagram:

Lemma 4.2

For

Assume first that

Assume now that

This shows that
a_{m}=q^{m(m-1)/2}(-1)^{m}
satisfies the above linear recursion. It follows that
a_{m}\neq 0
as desired.
∎

We now deduce some corollaries.

Definition 4.9
.

For a subfunctor
G\subseteq F
we define the subgroup

P_{G}=\{\varphi\in\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)|\text{Im}(\varphi-\text{Id}_{F})\subseteq G\}.

Remark 4.10
.

If
F=G\oplus G^{\prime}
then we can think of
P_{G}
as the group of all automorphisms that have the block diagonal form

\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}.

This is the reason we chose the terminology
P_{G}
, as it is related to parabolic subgroups.

Now, we have see that if
H=H_{\text{Id}_{F}}\oplus(0\oplus G)
is an
M
-redundant functor with
G
semisimple, then up to the non-zero scalar
a_{\text{Id}_{F}}
it holds that

\overline{L_{H}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\\
H_{\beta}\subseteq H\end{subarray}}\overline{T_{H_{\beta}}}.

By Lemma
4.2
we have

\overline{L_{H}}=\sum_{\beta\in P_{G}}\overline{T_{H_{\beta}}}.

If
H=H_{\alpha}\oplus(0,G)
is an
M
-redundant functor with
G
-semisimple and
Z
-redundant functor, and
\alpha\in\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
, then we can apply the automorphism
(\alpha,1)
that sends
H_{\alpha}
to
H_{\text{Id}_{F}}
to reduce to the case where
\alpha=\text{Id}_{F}
. Indeed, in this case we will get that

\overline{L_{H}}=\sum_{\beta\in P_{G}}\overline{T_{H_{\beta}}T_{H_{\alpha}}}.

Notice that it holds that for
g\in\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
we have
gP_{G}g^{-1}=P_{g(G)}
.
We summarize all of this in the following:

Proposition 4.11
.

Assume that
M=X\oplus Z
and that
F
has a minimal presentation
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
. Consider the homomorphism

\Phi:K\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\to\End_{\Aut_{R}(M)}(\overline{KF(M)})

\alpha\mapsto T_{H_{\alpha}}.

Then the kernel of
\Phi
is the two-sided ideal generated by the idempotents
e_{P_{G}}
, where
G\subseteq F
is a semisimple
Z
-redundant subfunctor.

Finally, we can state the main theorem:

Theorem 4.12
.

Assume that
M=X\oplus Z
and that
F
has a minimal presentation
(X\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}Y)
. An irreducible representation
V
of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is the functor morphing of an irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
if and only if the following condition holds: whenever
G\subseteq F
is a semisimple subfunctor with a projective cover
\Hom(Z_{G},-)
such that
Z_{G}
is not a direct summand of
Z
we have
\Hom_{P_{G}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
.

Proof.

In a semisimple algebra
\bigoplus\End(V_{i})
, the two-sided ideal generated by an idempotent
e
contains exactly the direct sum of those
\End(V_{i})
such that
e\cdot V_{i}\neq 0
. But for an irreducible representation
V
of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
,
e_{P_{G}}\cdot V=0
if and only if
\Hom_{P_{G}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
M=X\oplus Z
and let
(X\xrightarrow{f}Y)
be a minimal resolution for
F
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

Every irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is the functor morphing of an irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.

(2)

The trivial representation
\mathbf{1}
of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is the functor morphing of some irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.

(3)

If
\Hom_{R}(X_{1},-)
is the projective cover of
soc(F)
then
X_{1}
is a direct summand of
Z
.

Proof.

It is clear that 1 implies 2. If there is a semisimple functor
G\subseteq F
with a projective cover
\Hom_{R}(Z_{G},-)
such that
Z_{G}
is not a direct summand of
Z
, then the theorem shows that
\mathbf{1}
is not the functor morphing of an irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
. So if
\mathbf{1}
is the functor morphing of a representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
, this cannot happen, and so every module
X_{2}
that appears in the projective cover of any semisimple subfunctor
G\subseteq F
must be a direct summand of
Z
. This is true in particular for
G=soc(F)
, which shows that 2 implies 3.
Finally, if 3 holds then the conditions of the theorem hold trivially, and so every irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is the functor morphing of an irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.
∎

We record the following lemma, which will be useful to analyse some of the examples in the next section.

Lemma 4.14
.

If
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
are semisimple functors then
P_{G_{1}}\subseteq P_{G_{2}}
. As a result, when applying Theorem
4.12
we only need to consider the minimal functors (with respect to inclusion) in
\{G|Z_{G}\text{ is not a direct summand of }Z\}
.

Proof.

The subgroup
P_{G_{1}}
contains all automorphisms of the form
\varphi=1+\psi
, where
\psi:F\to G_{1}\to F
. Since
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
, such automorphisms are clearly contained in
P_{G_{2}}
as well.
This implies that if
\Hom_{P_{G_{1}}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
then
\Hom_{P_{G_{2}}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
, and the result follows.
∎

5.

5.1.
The groups
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Assume that
R=\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the finite field with
q

M=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. It holds that
\Aut_{R}(M)=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
The functors in
\mathcal{C}_{+1}
are all of the form

\mathcal{C}_{+1}\cong\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}

X=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}

if and only if

. In this case we write
M=X\oplus Z
where

Next, we notice that all subobjects of

G
of

\Hom_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},-)
for some

are two isomorphic subfunctors then they are conjugate under the action of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\cong\GL_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
It holds that
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}

We thus see that if

\GL_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-m+1}

We have

P_{G}=\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}|A\in\text{M}_{k\times k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\,B\in\text{M}_{k,m-k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\}\subseteq\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F).

To understand what representations
V

\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)

\Hom_{P_{G}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
using PSH-algebras from
[
Z1
]
.

Write

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{a\geq 0}K_{0}(\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q})).

Then
\mathcal{H}
has a structure of a positive self-adjoint Hopf algebra (or PSH-algebra), where multiplication is graded and given by by Harish-Chandra (parabolic) induction

K_{0}(\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\otimes K_{0}(\GL_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\cong K_{0}(\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\GL_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}K_{0}(P_{a,b})\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}K_{0}(\GL_{a+b}(\mathbb{F}_{q})),

and comultiplication is the dual operator, given by Harish-Chandra (parabolic) restriction

K_{0}(\GL_{a+b}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}K_{0}(P_{a,b})\mathrel{{\mathop{\to}\limits}}K_{0}(\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\GL_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\cong K_{0}(\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\otimes K_{0}(\GL_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{q})).

Here

P_{a,b}=\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&C\end{pmatrix}|A\in\text{M}_{a\times a}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),B\in\text{M}_{a\times b}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),C\in\text{M}_{b\times b}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\}

U_{a,b}=\{\begin{pmatrix}I&B\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\}\subseteq P_{a,b}

P_{a,b}\to\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\GL_{b}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

0&C\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(A,C).

Notice that the group

is the subgroup of all matrices in

.

The algebra
\mathcal{H}
has a natural

-basis given by irreducible representations. The term positive self adjoint refers to the fact that all the structure constants with respect to this basis are non-negative integers, and that the multiplication is dual to the comultiplication.
Zelevinsky showed that there is a unique (up to rescaling of the grading)
universal
PSH-algebra, and that every PSH-algebra splits as a tensor product of universal PSH-algebras in a unique way. The universal PSH-algebra is
Zel=\bigoplus_{a\geq 0}K_{0}(S_{a})

.
Zelevinsky showed that

a=a
. The element

is given by
\Delta(x_{c})=\sum_{a+b=c}x_{a}\otimes x_{b}
.

Going back to the algebra

\mathcal{H}=\bigotimes_{\rho}\mathcal{H}_{\rho},

where the tensor product is taken over all cuspidal elements in
\GL_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

This means in particular that for every irreducible representation

where for every cuspidal
\rho

is a partition of some natural number, and

is the irreducible representation in
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}
that corresponds to

under the isomorphism
H_{\rho}\cong Zel
. The numbers

are zero for almost all
\rho

Going back to the group
P_{G}

is equivalent to

for some

, where
\mathbf{1}_{k}
stands for the trivial representation of
\GL_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\langle x_{k}^{*}([V]),[W]\rangle=\langle[V],x_{k}\cdot[W]\rangle\neq 0,

x_{k}^{*}([V])=x_{k}^{*}(w_{\varepsilon,\lambda_{\varepsilon}})\prod_{\rho\neq\varepsilon}w_{\rho,\lambda_{\rho}}.

Summarizing, we see that

[
Z1

(\lambda_{\varepsilon})_{1}<k

(the formula in
[
Z1
]

We summarize this discussion.

Proposition 5.1
.

An irreducible representation
V
of
\GL_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
comes from functor morphing of an irreducible representation of

if and only if the following condition holds:
in the decomposition

(\lambda_{\varepsilon})_{1}<n-m+1

In
[
CMO4
]

where

it is easy to deduce the above proposition from this description of functor morphing.

If there is no index

Assume then that there is an index

It holds that

\varphi=1+\psi
is invertible if and only if
x\neq-1
. The resulting group
P_{G}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\ltimes\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The group
P_{G_{1}}
is then the subgroup
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
of
P_{G}
, and the group
P_{G_{2}}
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Let now
V
be an irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
. We consider its restriction to
P_{G}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\ltimes\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We would like to prove that
\Hom_{P_{G_{1}}}(\mathbf{1},V)\neq 0
or that
\Hom_{P_{G_{2}}}(\mathbf{1},V)\neq 0
. This will show that
V
cannot arise as the functor morphing of a representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
. By decomposing
V
into irreducible
P_{G}
representations, it will be enough to prove the claim for an irreducible
P_{G}
-representation

So assume that
\Hom_{P_{G_{2}}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
. Since
\Hom_{P_{G_{2}}}(\mathbf{1},V)=0
, it must hold that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
acts on
V
by non-trivial characters. The group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
acts freely on the set of non-trivial characters of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so by Clifford Theory we get
V=\Ind_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}^{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\ltimes\mathbb{F}_{q}}\widetilde{V}
for some representation
\widetilde{V}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. But this means that
V
is a regular
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
-representation, which means that in particular
\Hom_{P_{G_{1}}}(\mathbf{1},V)\neq 0
.

This covers all the cases in which
a_{i}=a_{j}
, and it shows us that when it happens no irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
appears as the functor morphing of any irreducible representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.
∎

The last two lemmas tell us that only functors of the form
F=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{l}F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
where
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
and
b_{i}\neq b_{j}
when
i\neq j
can arise in functor morphing from representations of
\Aut_{R}(M)
. The projective cover of such a functor
F
is thus
\Hom_{R}(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{l}M_{b_{i}},-)
, and the socle of
F
is

soc(F)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{l}F_{a_{i},a_{i}}.

We thus have
X=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{l}M_{b_{i}}
and
Z=\bigoplus_{s\in S}M_{s}
where
S=[n]\backslash\{b_{i}\}
.
The semisimple functors
G
that we will have to consider are thus all of the form
\bigoplus_{i\in T}F_{a_{i},a_{i}}
, where
T\subseteq[l]
satisfies that for some
i\in T
it holds that
a_{i}\in\{b_{j}\}
.
By Lemma
4.14
we only need to consider functors of the form
G=F_{a_{i},a_{i}}
where
a_{i}=b_{j}
for some
j
.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
G=F_{a_{i},a_{i}}
and
a_{i}=b_{j}
then
P_{G}
is a normal subgroup of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
. If
i\neq j
then
P_{G}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}
and if
i=j
then
P_{G}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

Proof.

We write
F=\bigoplus_{k}F_{a_{k},b_{k}}
. The only
k
such that
\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{k},b_{k}},F_{a_{i},a_{i}})\neq 0
is
j
. The only
k
such that
\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{i},a_{i}},F_{a_{k},b_{k}})\neq 0
is
i
.
We distinguish now between two cases.

If
i=j
then
a_{i}=b_{i}
. There are no non-zero morphisms
F_{a_{i},b_{i}}\to F_{a_{k},b_{k}}
and
F_{a_{k},b_{k}}\to F_{a_{i},a_{i}}
when
k\neq i
. The ring
\End_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
splits as
\End_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{i},a_{i}})\oplus\End_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(\bigoplus_{k\neq i}F_{a_{k},b_{k}})
, and as a result

\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)=\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{i},a_{i}})\times\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(\bigoplus_{k\neq i}F_{a_{k},b_{k}}).

Since
G=F_{a_{i},a_{i}}
and
\End_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{i},a_{i}})=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we get that
P_{G}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is a direct summand of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
, and in particular normal.

If
i\neq j
then
I=\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{j},b_{j}},F_{a_{i},b_{i}})\subseteq\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(\bigoplus_{k}F_{a_{k},b_{k}},F_{a_{k},b_{k}})=\End_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
is a nilpotent two-sided ideal.
This follows from the fact that for any
k\neq i
any composition
F_{a_{j},b_{j}}\to F_{a_{i},b_{i}}\to F_{a_{k},b_{k}}
vanishes, because
a_{i}\neq a_{k}
and so
b_{j}=a_{i}<a_{k}
, and for any
k\neq j
the composition

F_{a_{k},b_{k}}\to F_{a_{j},b_{j}}\to F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
vanishes due to a similar reason. The group
P_{G}
is then isomorphic to
1+I
, which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
∎

We summarize this discussion

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
F:=\bigoplus_{i}F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
. Then
F
arises in functor morphing of representations of
\Aut_{R}(M)
if and only if
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
and
b_{i}\neq b_{j}
for
i\neq j
. When this happens, an irreducible representation
V
of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
comes from functor morphing of a representation of
\Aut_{R}(M)
if and only if the following condition holds: if
a_{i}=b_{i}
then the subgroup
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cong\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{i},a_{i}})<\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
acts non-trivially on
V
, and if
a_{i}=b_{j}
for
i\neq j
then the subgroup
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong 1+\Hom_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{a_{j},b_{j}},F_{a_{i},b_{i}})
acts non-trivially on
V
.

Proof.

The only new thing in the proposition is the fact that if indeed
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
and
b_{i}\neq b_{j}
for all
i\neq j
then such a representation exists. But this is straightforward, by considering the product of all the
P_{G}
subgroups, which is again abelian in this case.
∎

We can get rid of the singletons among the intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
without too much hassle.
For a given functor
F=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
we can assume, by reordering the intervals, that
[a_{1},b_{1}],\ldots,[a_{k},b_{k}]
are not singletons and
[a_{k+1},b_{k+1}],\ldots,[a_{m},b_{m}]
are singletons (i.e.
a_{k+1}=b_{k+1},\ldots,a_{m}=b_{m}
). Write
F^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
. Then
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)\cong\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F^{\prime})\times(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{m-k}
. Every irreducible representation
V
of
F
can be written uniquely as
V=V^{\prime}\otimes K_{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m-k}}
, where
\psi_{j}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to K
is a one-dimensional character and
V^{\prime}\in\Irr(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F^{\prime}))
.
Recall that
\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F))
are the irreducible representations of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
that arise from functor morphing of irreducible representations of
\Aut_{R}(M)
.
The above proposition gives the following:

Corollary 5.6
.

It holds that
V\in\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F))
if and only if
V^{\prime}\in\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(\Irr(F^{\prime}))
and all the characters
\psi_{j}
are non-trivial.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
F^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
where
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
for all
i
. Write
l=|[n]\backslash\{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{k}\}|
.
Then there is a one-to-one correspondence

\bigsqcup_{F}\Irr^{M,fm}(F)\cong\Irr^{M,fm}(F^{\prime})\times\Irr((\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{l}),

where the union on the left hand side is taken over all functors
F
of the form
F=F^{\prime}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}F_{c_{j},c_{j}}
for
c_{j}\notin\{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{k}\}
.

Proof.

The previous corollary shows how a representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F)
for
F=F^{\prime}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}F_{c_{j},c_{j}}
gives a representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F^{\prime})\times(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{r}
. We can extend this to a representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F^{\prime})\times(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{l}
by letting the extra factors of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
act trivially. In the other direction, if we have a representation
V=V^{\prime}\otimes K_{(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{l})}\in\Irr^{M,fm}(F^{\prime})\times\Irr((\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{l})
then this can easily be seen to come from the functor
F=F^{\prime}\bigoplus_{c:\psi_{c}\neq\varepsilon}F_{c,c}
.
∎

To summarise this discussion, in order to study the image of functor morphing for
B_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
it is enough to study functors
F=\bigoplus F_{a_{i},b_{i}}
such that
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
for all
i
, and
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
and
b_{i}\neq b_{j}
for
i\neq j
. We finish with examples for small values of
n
.

When
n=2
the only functors which do not contain singletons are
0
and
F_{1,2}
. We cannot add singletons to
\{[1,2]\}
and since
1\neq 2
every irreducible representation of
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,2})\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
arises by functor morphing. For the 0 functor we have
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(0)=\{1\}
. We can add the singletons
[1,1]
and
[2,2]
and we get
\Irr^{M,fm}(0)\sqcup\Irr^{M,fm}(F_{1,1})\sqcup\Irr^{M,fm}(F_{2,2})\sqcup\Irr^{M,fm}(F_{1,1}\oplus F_{2,2})\cong\Irr((\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2})
. This example was given in Section 6 of
[
CMO4
]
.

Consider now the case
n=3
. The only new example that we get here, that does not arise from an order preserving embedding
[1,2]\to[1,3]
, is
F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3}
. Indeed, the case of
F_{1,2}
,
F_{1,3}
,
F_{2,3}
and functors that involve singletons can be deduced from the case
n=2
.
We have

\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3})=\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}|a,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times},b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}.

An irreducible representation
V
comes from functor morphing of
\Aut_{R}(M)
if and only if the normal subgroup
N:=\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
b&1\end{pmatrix}|b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
acts on it non-trivially. Using Clifford theory for
N
we get
\Irr^{M,fm}(F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3})\cong\Irr(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})
, because there is only one non-trivial
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(\mathbb{F}_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3})
-orbit in
\Irr(N)
, and its stabilizer in
\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3})/N
is given by
\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}\}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

For
n=4
the only cases that do not arise from an order preserving embedding
[1,3]\to[1,4]
are
F_{1,2}\oplus F_{3,4}
,
F_{1,3}\oplus F_{2,4}
,
F_{1,4}\oplus F_{2,3}
,
F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3}\oplus F_{3,4}
. In the first three cases the end points of the intervals do not intersect, and therefore functor morphing poses no restriction. We get
\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,2}\oplus F_{3,4}))\cong\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,4}\oplus F_{2,3}))\cong(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{2}
and
\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,3}\oplus F_{2,4}))\cong B_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, because there is a non-trivial morphism
F_{1,3}\to F_{2,4}
.
By using Clifford Theory in a similar way to the case
F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3}
when
n=3
we get that
\Irr^{M,fm}(\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{+1}}(F_{1,2}\oplus F_{2,3}\oplus F_{3,4}))\cong\Irr(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})
.
