Coordinated transcriptional shifts in microglia and oligodendrocytes from neurodevelopment to inflammaging
Authors: Yi-Shian Peng, Sen-Yung Hsieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glial cells shape brain development and aging, yet whether distinct glial lineages undergo coordinated lifespan transitions remains unclear. Although microglial aging states and oligodendrocyte vulnerability in neurodegeneration are well described, it remains unclear whether these programs evolve independently or in a temporally related manner. Here, we analyzed Mouse Cell Atlas single-cell RNA-seq data spanning embryogenesis to 24 months. Microglia and oligodendrocytes were the most transcriptionally dynamic glial populations, whereas astrocyte module activity was primarily observed during development. Microglia and oligodendrocytes exhibited similar late-life increases in inflammatory and senescence-associated modules, supported by cross-correlation (Pearson r ≈ 0.98; Max_CCF ≈ 0.98), dynamic time warping (DTW ≈ 6.16), and a large effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 11.08). These patterns were also supported by mixed glial contexts (p = 1.85 × 10⁻³). Together, these results identify a parallel transcriptional shift in microglia and oligodendrocytes from neurodevelopment to inflammaging and suggest that shared temporal programs across glial lineages may contribute to neurodegenerative vulnerability. This quantitative framework for assessing temporal similarity may be broadly applicable to other biological systems involving dynamic cellular state transitions.

Introduction

The central nervous system (CNS) consists of neurons and glial cells, both of which play critical roles during brain development1 and aging2. While neurons have traditionally been the primary focus in neuroscience research, glial cells, including astrocytes3, oligodendrocytes4, and microglia5, have emerged as key regulators of neural function, development, and homeostasis. Recent research has begun to uncover the diverse and dynamic functions of glial cells across the lifespan6,7, including during brain maturation8 and senescence9. Glial cells participate in essential processes such as synaptic pruning10, myelination11, neuroinflammation12, and maintenance of energy and functional homeostasis13. Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have revealed extensive heterogeneity among glial cells, identifying diverse subtypes and context-dependent transcriptional states across regions, developmental stages, and disease conditions14–17. Despite these insights, the dynamic regulatory programs that glia cells orchestrate the brain development and aging, particularly at high temporal resolution across the lifespan, remain incompletely understood. This gap limits our ability to fully elucidate how glial phenotypes are established, maintained, or altered in response to physiological aging and pathological insults.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS, are particularly notable for their unique ontogeny and context-dependent plasticity18; they originate from the embryonic yolk sac, rather than from the neuroectodermal lineage that gives rise to neurons and other glial cells19–21. During early development, they contribute to neural circuit formation via synapse remodeling and synaptogenesis22,23. In aging, microglia adopt reactive states and have been implicated in neurodegenerative pathologies24. Their transcriptional programs are highly dynamic and responsive to developmental, environmental, and pathological stimuli25. Recently, single-cell analyses revealed that microglia and astrocytes undergo age-related immune activation and homeostatic disruption, indicating that aging involves not only global transcriptional changes but also exhibits strong regional specificity26. Spatial profiling showed aging glial signatures are enriched in regions such as the corpus callosum and hypothalamus, with white matter astrocytes and microglia displaying pronounced activation and altered interactions27. Despite growing interest in glial biology28–30, a detailed understanding of the transcriptional networks that regulate glial cell behavior across time remains limited. In particular, few studies have examined glial gene co-expression dynamics at single-cell resolution across multiple developmental and aging stages.

To address this gap, we applied high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA), a recently developed tool for identifying co-regulated gene modules from high-dimensional transcriptomic data31. Previous applications of hdWGCNA have yielded insights into transcriptional networks in neurodegeneration32, metabolic disease33, and psychiatric disorders34. However, a life-span across different cell lineage profiling remains to be explored.

In this study, we analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data from mouse brains across eight stages spanning development to aging (E14.5–M24)35,36. We found that, unlike astrocytes, which were largely restricted to early developmental activity, microglia and oligodendrocytes remained transcriptionally dynamic throughout the lifespan. To examine temporal relationships between these lineages, we applied co-expression module clustering and eigengene correlation analyses, defining a “temporal coordination metric” to align the module trajectories across developmental, maturation, and senescence stages. This metric was further assessed using cross-correlation, dynamic time-warping, and effect sizes. We observed that microglia and oligodendrocytes exhibit similar temporal shifts in gene expression programs from neurodevelopmental states to late-life inflammatory-enriched profiles. This finding suggests a temporal concordance between the two lineages during aging, contrasting with previously described largely independent cellular aging trajectories.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic landscape of the mouse brain across the lifespan

To investigate transcriptional programs in brain cells across the lifespan, we analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from the Mouse Cell Atlas, spanning embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) to 24 months of age (M24; Table 1). To capture dynamic gene expression changes associated with brain development and aging, we selected the 2000 most variable genes across eight distinct stages. This approach enabled us to identify the predominant cell types exhibiting high transcriptional activity at each time point. The lower cell count observed at M12 likely reflects reduced representation of highly variable genes at this intermediate stage, rather than technical integration failure, as integration metrics remained stable across stages. Batch-correction performance was first evaluated using LISI scores, which indicated effective overall and stage-specific mixing, with values ranging from 2 to 3 for most cell types (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). As expected, LISI scores were below 2 for Neutrophils and Radial glial cells, which are restricted to specific developmental windows. In contrast, Microglia and Oligodendrocytes exhibited values above 2 (Supplementary Fig. 1c), consistent with effective cross-stage integration. To further assess robustness using an orthogonal metric, we calculated cell type–specific average silhouette widths for batch (ASW_batch). Across major cell types, ASW_batch ranged from −0.191 (Oligodendrocytes) to −0.014 (Radial glial cells), with most lineages showing negative values, indicating good mixing of cells from different stages in PCA space while preserving biological structure (Supplementary Fig. 1d). To support these observations, CytoTRACE2 was applied in Fig. 2 to infer the differentiation potential of four major glial populations across development and aging stage. Among glial lineages, microglia (MCG) and oligodendrocytes (ODCs) were consistently represented across all stages (Fig. 2), and were selected for downstream analysis, while other glial populations were further examined for stage-dependent transcriptional dynamics.

Temporal glial modules reveal lifespan dynamics

To investigate the biological significance of ODCs dynamics across the lifespan, we performed high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA), identifying six ODC-specific transcriptional modules (ODC-M1 to ODC-M6), each with distinct temporal and functional signatures (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Data 2,3; Supplementary Fig. 6). Correlation analysis among modules revealed that ODC-M2, M3, M4, and M5 were positively correlated; suggesting shared regulatory mechanisms or overlapping biological functions (Fig. 3b). ODC-M1, enriched for ribosomal genes, peaked between P10 and P21, marking early-stage differentiation. ODC-M2 and ODC-M4 were active through young adulthood (P10–P56) and associated with cholesterol biosynthesis and Wnt signaling, key pathways for membrane production and axon-glia interaction during myelination (Fig. 3c–e; Supplementary Data 2, 3). ODC-M3, most active from P42 to P56, reflected mature oligodendrocyte’s identity and myelin maintenance, as evidenced by hub genes such as Trf, Apod, and Pmp22 (Fig. 3f). In contrast, ODC-M5 and ODC-M6 were upregulated during aging (M18–M24), linked to neurodegenerative genes (App, Apoe) and immune activation (C1qa, Ctss), respectively, suggesting an emerging inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 3g; Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Data 2, 3). Glial module clustering reveals lifespan patterns of temporal coordination

To further delineate the temporal coordination among glial cell types, we performed two-way hierarchical clustering of module eigengenes derived from microglia, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes. This analysis resolved three major clusters of co-expression modules that reflect distinct stages of the lifespan (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Data 9; Supplementary Fig. 11a, b): Cluster 1 (Developmental): MCG-M1, ODC-M1, and AST-M1, peaking during embryonic and neonatal stages (Fig. 5b). Cluster 2 (Maturation): MCG-M2, ODC-M2 to ODC-M5, and AST-M2, active from postnatal to young adulthood, coinciding with peak myelination (Fig. 5c). Cluster 3 (Aging): MCG-M3 and ODC-M6, associated with immune activation and stress responses in aged brains (Fig. 5d). To quantify temporal similarity between module eigengene trajectories across lifespan stages, we applied a set of complementary metrics to assess concordance, including (i) Pearson correlation, (ii) maximal cross-correlation function (CCF), (iii) minimal dynamic time warping (DTW) distance, and (iv) Cohen’s d as an effect size measure. In the developmental and maturation stages (Clusters 1 and 2), microglial and oligodendrocyte modules exhibited strong concordance. For example, MCG-M1 and ODC-M1 showed a high correlation (Pearson r ≈ 0.83; Max_CCF ≈ 0.83), with a Cohen’s d of 3.03, indicating a large effect size. Similarly, MCG-M2 and ODC-M3 exhibited nearly perfect concordance (Pearson r ≈ 0.99; Max_CCF ≈ 0.99), with a Cohen’s d of 14.66, demonstrating an extremely strong association during peak myelination (Supplementary Data 10).

In the aging stage (Cluster 3), MCG-M3 and ODC-M6 also exhibited high concordance (Pearson r ≈ 0.98, Max_CCF ≈ 0.98), with a Cohen’s d of 11.08, reflecting a very large effect size in immune activation and stress responses in aging brains. Notably, the lag value between modules in the developmental and maturation stages was minimal, with MCG-M2 and ODC-M3 showing no lag (Lag = 0). Similarly, during the aging phase, MCG-M3 and ODC-M6 also showed no lag (Lag = 0), indicating synchronous expression across both cell types. Additionally, Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distances were calculated to quantify the temporal alignment between module eigengene expression patterns. The DTW distance between MCG-M3 (microglia) and ODC-M6 (oligodendrocytes) was found to be 6.16, which is the smallest distance among all pairwise comparisons. This minimal DTW distance signifies the closest temporal alignment observed among all cross-lineage comparisons, indicating the highest temporal similarity among cross-lineage comparisons during aging (Fig. 5e, f) (Supplementary Data 10).

For the MCG-M3 and ODC-M6 comparison, the p-value for the Pearson correlation was 9.94 × 10⁻⁶, with the permutation p-value ≈ 0, was significantly higher than expected by chance (Fig. 5e, f). These results highlight the robust temporal alignment of microglial and oligodendrocyte activity during aging. Together, these quantitative analyses support stage-dependent similarities in glial module trajectories across the lifespan, with relatively higher similarity observed between microglial and oligodendrocyte modules during aging (Supplementary Data 10).

Multicellular context reveals differences in aging-associated module signals

To examine the functional context of aging-associated inflammatory programs in MCG-M3 (microglia) and ODC-M6 (oligodendrocytes), we conducted single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) on three glial transcriptomic models: (i) purified microglia (immune response–centric), (ii) purified oligodendrocytes (non-immune glial cells), and (iii) a mixed glial model encompassing microglia, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) to approximate the complex multicellular glial environment (Fig. 2).

We focused on two gene modules: MCG-M3 (from microglia) and ODC-M6 (from oligodendrocytes), both enriched for inflammatory response (GO:0006954) and microglial activation (GO:0001774) pathways (Fig. 6a). Strikingly, correlations between these GO terms and module eigengenes were significantly stronger in the mixed glial context (MCG-M3: r = 0.837; ODC-M6: r = 0.856) than in the isolated cell models (microglia: r = 0.505/0.527; oligodendrocytes: r = 0.453/0.530). While the aging-associated oligodendrocyte module ODC-M6 was associated with inflammatory gene expression in the multicellular glial model, the broader oligodendrocyte population showed lower SASP-related activity. These results suggest that SASP-associated transcriptional changes are more pronounced in microglia, and that inflammatory-associated signals are more detectable in multicellular glial contexts. The enhanced activation of aging modules MCG-M3 and ODC-M6 in multicellular settings, paired with limited overlap with oxidative stress pathways, leads to the hypothesis that age-related glial inflammation is more closely associated with intercellular regulatory patterns than with cell-intrinsic stress responses.

Discussion

Herein, we present a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic spanning embryonic to aged mouse brains revealing a temporally coordinated, glia-specific transcriptional architecture that shapes brain function across the lifespan. We identify three temporally organized gene programs: developmental, maturation, and aging that are shared across microglia, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes. Among these, microglia and oligodendrocytes exhibit distinct but concordant temporal trajectories, transitioning from early developmental support and peak myelination to late-life inflammatory activation and aging-associated states. These temporally resolved cross-lineage modules are also partially conserved in human glial populations and provide a framework for understanding how glial transcriptional states evolve across aging and may relate to neurodegenerative vulnerability.

Notably, previous aging atlases provide valuable insights but have distinct limitations. Allen et al.26 primarily characterized cell-type-specific aging signatures, focusing on microglia, oligodendrocytes, or astrocytes independently, without examining cross-lineage interactions. Wu et al.27 mapped region-specific spatial transcriptional changes atlases of the brains of young and old mice, but lacked multi-temporal variations. Sankowski and Prinz25 described microglial state continua, highlighting progressive changes in microglial activation over time, but do not assess their relationship with other glial populations. In contrast, our study uses hdWGCNA to reveal co-expression modules synchronized across microglia, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes, highlighting inter-glial temporal coordination and its relevance to brain development, aging, and neurodegeneration—an aspect not captured by prior atlases. Previous studies have highlighted microglia’s role in phagocytosis37, synaptic pruning38, and oligodendrocyte maturation39, while oligodendrocytes contribute to axonal insulation40 and trophic support41. Our results extend this by showing that microglia and oligodendrocytes engage in temporally synchronized gene programs that align with key biological transitions42, such as myelination and metabolic support. Recent studies further highlight the role of glial cells in linking mitochondrial dysfunction to neurodegenerative processes43. Notably, ODC-M3, a maturation-associated oligodendrocyte module enriched for lipid metabolism44 and axonal support genes40, strongly overlaps with MCG-M2, a microglial module linked to myelin facilitation45 and glia-neuron interactions46. This convergence suggests coordinated functional engagement during the postnatal maturation window47.

Importantly, dysregulation of these modules may have pathological consequences. ODC-M3 and AST-M2, for instance, have been implicated in early-onset epilepsy48,49. Astrocyte dysfunction, including impaired potassium buffering, glutamate clearance, and disrupted synaptic homeostasis, can drive neural hyperexcitability. Concurrently, impaired myelination due to ODC-M3 deregulation may compromise signal propagation, exacerbating seizure susceptibility48,49. These findings emphasize that coordinated glial maturation is essential not only for myelination but also for the stabilization of neural circuits and seizure control50.

Astrocytes share developmental programs with microglia and oligodendrocytes51, as evidenced by the co-activation of AST-M1 and AST-M2 with early microglial and oligodendrocyte modules52,53. However, we did not detect age-associated astrocyte modules comparable to MCG-M3 or ODC-M6 in the present dataset. This observation is consistent with a reduced representation of astrocyte transcriptional activity in late-life stages within this analytical framework. Whether this reflects true biological differences or technical limitations remains unresolve.

Our analysis uncovers two oligodendrocyte modules (ODC-M5 and ODC-M6) that are progressively upregulated in aging and enriched for genes implicated in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and neuroinflammation. ODC-M5 includes genes associated with amyloid-β (Aβ) aggregation and tau pathology54,55, suggesting a pathogenic role in impairing neural transmission and promoting cognitive decline. ODC-M6, in turn, harbors inflammatory markers and shares transcriptional features with microglia-associated neuroinflammation seen in neurodegenerative conditions56.

This dual nature, whereby oligodendrocytes can either support neural integrity via ODC-M3 or contribute to degeneration via M5 and M6, highlights their plasticity and central involvement in the transition from brain health to disease. These findings align with recent studies showing that oligodendrocyte dysfunction exacerbates axonal degeneration and glial reactivity in aging57 and AD models58,59.

SASP-related trajectories and enrichment analyses further support an association between microglia and age-associated inflammatory signaling60,61. However, the increased signal of both MCG-M3 and ODC-M6 in mixed glial models suggests that glial inflammaging is not exclusively attributable to cell-intrinsic programs, but may also reflect influences from multicellular context. Together, these observations are consistent with the view that microglia and oligodendrocytes exhibit related transcriptional dynamics rather than acting as fully independent cellular programs during brain aging61,62. We further note that the mixed glial model, while approximating the in vivo glial environment, may introduce shared signals from co-aggregated cell types, potentially inflating observed correlations and warranting cautious interpretation.

Our study demonstrates the power of hdWGCNA applied to mouse scRNA-seq data in unraveling glial-specific, temporally dynamic gene co-expression networks across development and aging. Compared to bulk RNA-seq or microarray approaches (Table 2), our method offers higher resolution, enabling identification of temporally, cell-type–specific transcriptional modules aligned with biological processes. Despite providing new insights into inter-glial temporal coordination, our study has several limitations. The mouse and human datasets lack brain-region specification, and only eight developmental and aging stages are included, potentially missing transient or intermediate cellular states. Analyses rely on Microwell-Seq single-cell transcriptomics without spatial context, which limits both resolution and the ability to localize cells within brain regions. In addition, the current annotations are derived from unsupervised clustering and therefore likely represent broad cellular classes rather than fully resolved subtypes or functional states, which is particularly relevant in the context of aging-associated heterogeneity. Furthermore, compositional estimates are based on relative proportions from integrated datasets with uneven sampling and a subset of highly variable genes; thus, reported changes should be interpreted as relative enrichment rather than absolute cellular abundance. The apparent increase in neural proliferating cells at M24 is biologically unexpected and may reflect compositional effects, annotation uncertainty, and/or sampling imbalance rather than true proliferative expansion. Thus, this work should be viewed as a strategy for identifying cross-lineage temporal coordination rather than definitive conclusions.

Our findings highlight the temporal coordination of microglial and oligodendrocyte inflammatory modules in late-life glia, reflecting stage-specific temporal alignment of transcriptional programs. This perspective provides a basis for future studies to investigate whether there is an inter-lineage temporal interplay, which contribute to age-associated neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. In addition, the quantitative metric introduced here to assess temporal alignment across cell states may be adaptable to other biological systems, offering a general approach for characterizing concordant transcriptional programs during development, aging, and disease progression.

Methods

Data sources and cells filtering

All mouse brain single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets used in this study were obtained from the Mouse Cell Atlas (MCA)database (https://bis.zju.edu.cn/MCA/)35,36 and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)66 repository (GSE176063, GSE198832). The MCA dataset includes samples from 10 different mouse tissues across eight developmental and aging stages: E14.535 (embryonic day 14.5), P035 (neonatal), P1035 (postnatal day 10), P2135 (postnatal day 21), Adult35(42–56 days), M1236 (12 months), M1836 (18 months), and M2436 (24 months).

For this analysis, brain samples from all eight stages were selected, as summarized in Table 1. Data processing was performed using RStudio (version 2026.01.1 + 403) and R (version 4.3.2, released October 31, 2023). The “Seurat” package (version 5.0)67 was used for preprocessing and exploration of the scRNA-seq data. During quality control, the initial Seurat object was created using the “CreateSeuratObject” function, retaining cells with ≥200 detected genes (min.features = 200) and genes expressed in ≥3 cells (min.cells = 3) to exclude low-quality cells and low-abundance genes. Additional filtering removed cells with <500 or >2500 detected genes (nFeature_RNA) to eliminate low-quality cell. The number of high-quality cells retained at each stage is summarized in Table 1.

Integration of single-cell data and cell-type annotation

Single-cell RNA-seq datasets spanning eight developmental and aging stages (E14.5, P0, P10, P21, Adult, M12, M18, and M24) were processed using the Seurat (v5.0) in R environment. Data were first split by stage, and each subset was normalized with “SCTransform”68 (“vst.flavor” = “v2”, method = “glmGamPoi”69, “ncells” = 5000, “variable.features.n” = 3000) to standardize sequencing depth and model sparse gene expression. For each stage, PCA was performed using 20 principal components.

To facilitate cross-stage integration, the top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs; Supplementary Data 16) were selected using “SelectIntegrationFeatures”. Integration anchors were identified using “FindIntegrationAnchors” (dims = 1:20), and datasets were merged with “IntegrateData”, implementing SCT-based integration to harmonize stage-specific datasets while preserving biological variability. The integrated object was subsequently scaled and re-embedded using PCA (npcs = 20). Integration performance was evaluated using Local Inverse Simpson’s Index (LISI) and average silhouette width for batch labels (ASW_batch), providing complementary assessments of batch mixing and biological conservation. Cell clustering was performed using “FindNeighbors” (dims = 1:20) and “FindClusters” (resolution = 0.2), enabling identification of major cell populations across developmental and aging stages. All downstream analyses were performed using the integrated assay unless otherwise specified.

Cell type annotation was guided by the ACT (Annotation of Cell Types) framework (http://xteam.xbio.top/ACT/), using marker genes curated in the CellMarker 2.0 database70 for brain tissues (http://www.bio-bigdata.center/). To further enhance annotation accuracy, automated tools “ScType”71 and “SingleR”72 were employed. “ScType” incorporated tissue-specific marker sets from the brain, immune system, and placenta to increase the specificity of classification. This combined approach allowed precise annotation of major brain cell populations, including glial and immune cells. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the “RunTSNE”73 and “RunUMAP”74 functions for visualization. This integration strategy effectively corrected stage-associated batch effects and sequencing depth differences, ensuring that downstream co-expression module comparisons reflected biological rather than technical variation.

Immune activity scoring and trajectory inference

To assess glial aging and inflammatory status, immune activity was evaluated using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) based on 12 curated gene sets representing the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)75 (Supplementary Data 14). The gene sets included terms and pathways associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and cellular senescence, include Microglial Cell Activation and Inflammatory Response. To model the developmental and aging progression of glial cells, “CytoTRACE2”76 was used to infer cell differentiation potential. Unlike traditional pseudotime tools, “CytoTRACE” requires no prior lineage assumptions, making it ideal for heterogeneous populations. Pseudotime trajectories were generated across microglia (MCG), oligodendrocytes (ODC), astrocytes (AST) and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPC) to assess maturation and aging dynamics.

Gene co-expression network analysis

SCTransform-normalized Seurat objects were used as input for gene co-expression network analysis. Metacells were generated separately for each cell type using the “MetacellsByGroups” function implemented in hdWGCNA (v0.4.03)31, within R (v 4.3.2), built on Seurat (v5.0). Genes expressed in at least 5% of cells within each cell type were retained for network construction. Soft-thresholding powers were selected using the “TestSoftPowers” function to approximate scale-free topology (ODC = 9, R² > 0.85; MCG = 5; AST = 3; OPC = 6; NER = 5; NPC = 6). Weighted gene co-expression networks were constructed using the hdWGCNA framework with signed adjacency matrices and Pearson correlation. Networks were built using the “ConstructNetwork” function with the following parameters: “minModuleSize” = 50, “mergeCutHeight” = 0.2, and “detectCutHeight” = 0.995. Hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree cutting was applied to identify co-expression modules, resulting in six ODC-related modules, three MCG-related modules, and two AST-related modules. Module eigengenes—defined as the first principal component of each module’s gene expression matrix—were computed to summarize overall expression profiles and assess temporal expression dynamics and developmental stage specificity across ODC, MCG, and AST.

To examine cell type–specific dynamics, additional analyses compared module eigengene correlations between MCG and other cell types (NER, NPC, OPC) with respect to early-stage modules.

Computation of temporal similarity metrics

Cross-correlation analysis was performed using the ccf function in R to examine the alignment of module eigengene expression patterns between microglia (MCG), oligodendrocytes (ODC), and astrocytes (AST). Pearson correlations were calculated at Lag = 0, representing no temporal shift between modules, while lag values ranging from −5 to +5 were also considered to capture potential lead–lag relationships. For each pairwise module comparison, the maximum cross-correlation (Max CCF) was computed to quantify the strongest correlation across all lags, representing the peak temporal alignment between modules. A Pearson correlation coefficient (r) greater than 0.8 at Lag = 0 was considered indicative of strong temporal similarity.

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) was applied using the dtw R package to further quantify temporal alignment of eigengene trajectories. The DTW distance was calculated for each module pair, with smaller distances indicating stronger temporal coordination. Statistical significance was further evaluated using permutation testing (1000 permutations), in which one time series was randomly shuffled to generate a null distribution of correlation coefficients. Together, these metrics provide a multidimensional characterization of temporal relationships between gene expression trajectories. All pairwise module similarity metrics, including Pearson correlation, permutation p-values, Max CCF, lag, DTW distance, and Cohen’s d, are provided in Supplementary Data 10.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO)77 and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)78 pathway enrichment analyses were performed for MCG, ODC and AST modules using the “clusterProfiler” package79 in R. Significantly enriched terms were identified using hypergeometric testing with multiple testing correction applied via the Benjamini–Hochberg method (p-value adjustment). Terms with both p-values and adjusted p-values (q-values) below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To investigate aging-related stress responses in glial cells, we applied single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) using curated GO gene sets related to ischemia, hypoxia, ROS, inflammation, and microglial activation (Supplementary Data 13,14). ssGSEA scores were computed across microglia, oligodendrocytes, and glial cells model. The mixed glial model was designed to approximate the multicellular and interactive glial environment characteristic of the aging brain. Pearson correlations between ssGSEA scores and MCG and ODC-derived module eigengenes were calculated to identify functionally enriched modules. Visualization of correlations highlighted key modules (e.g., ODC-M6, MCG-M3) associated with microglial activation during aging, offering insight into stress-linked transcriptional programs across glial subtypes.

Aging biomarker identification via LASSO regression

To identify gene signatures predictive of cellular aging in oligodendrocytes, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression80 was applied to stage-specific gene expression data. The “glmnet” package81 in R was used for model fitting. Genes with non-zero coefficients in the final penalized model were considered age-predictive markers. The model was trained on developmental and aging stages to generate the correlation coefficient for ODC-specific aging signatures. Predictive positively correlated genes were further functionally annotated to evaluate their biological significance.

Comparison association between mouse and human brain

Human single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from the AgeAnno database (https://relab.xidian.edu.cn/AgeAnno/# /)82, which provides transcriptomic profiles of brain cells throughout the human lifespan. Donors were categorized into three age groups based on World Health Organization guidelines: youth (0–19 years), middle-aged (20–59 years), and elderly (60–100 years). Gene expression trends of human ODC were analyzed across these age groups. ODC signature genes exhibiting consistent directional expression patterns in both species were identified as conserved aging markers. This cross-species association underscores the biological relevance of the candidate gene modules and offers translational insights into glial aging.
