Hospital pharmacists’ perceptions, needs, barriers, and facilitators of pharmacy practice research in China: a qualitative study
Authors: Chu Zhang, Yanping Wu, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPharmacy practice research has received increasing international attention but remains in its early stages in China. Understanding pharmacists’ engagement in such research is essential for advancing pharmaceutical care and strengthening evidence-based practice.AimThis study aimed to explore hospital pharmacists’ perceptions of pharmacy practice research and identify the factors influencing their engagement in such research.MethodThis qualitative study employed semi-structured interviews with 21 pharmacists from Grade A tertiary hospitals across 14 provinces and municipalities in China. Participants were purposively sampled to ensure variation in their professional roles, experience, and geographic region. The interviews were conducted online, audio recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis informed by the COM-B framework and were managed using NVivo 12. Coding was performed independently by two researchers, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion to enhance the analytical rigor.ResultsThree overarching themes and 18 subthemes were identified. Most participants recognized the importance of pharmacy practice research and expressed a strong willingness to engage, particularly when research activities were closely aligned with their clinical practices. The key motivations included professional fulfillment, career advancement, and personal research interests. The identified research needs focused on optimizing pharmaceutical service content and models and strengthening information system support to facilitate data access and decision-making. Institutional support, a positive research culture, and interdisciplinary collaboration were important facilitators of engagement. However, major barriers include insufficient time and staffing, limited research resources, and inadequate research competence.ConclusionHospital pharmacists in China generally recognize the value of pharmacy practice research; however, their engagement is shaped by capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related factors, including research competence, organizational support, resource availability, and interdisciplinary collaboration. Strengthening research training, institutional support, and collaborative research environments may enhance pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-026-02197-x.

Introduction

As the primary providers of pharmaceutical care, pharmacists play a critical role in optimizing medication therapy and improving service delivery. Pharmacy practice research serves as an important mechanism for advancing evidence-based practice and enhancing patient outcomes [1]. Such research significantly advances the field of pharmaceutical care. As an integral component of health services research [2], pharmacy practice research has gained increasing international recognition as a distinct field, demonstrating significant value in clinical, economic, and humanistic outcomes [3, 4]. In addition to advancing the pharmacy profession, it contributes to both theoretical and practical frameworks for addressing global healthcare challenges [4].

Pharmacy practice research has been increasingly recognized as a scientific discipline. However, inconsistencies in terminology and the limited application of theory-informed approaches continue to hinder its development and impact [5]. Recent international consensus efforts, such as the Granada Statements, have emphasized the need to strengthen methodological rigor, improve conceptual clarity, and enhance research output quality in this field [5]. As defined by the European Society of Clinical Pharmacy, clinical pharmacy aims to optimize medication use to achieve person-centered and public health outcomes, encompassing professional practice and research in drug selection, administration, and monitoring [6].

Despite its importance, implementing pharmacy practice research is challenging. Common barriers include insufficient time, limited research skills or experience, lack of institutional support, and inadequate funding [7–9]. These challenges have been consistently reported across different healthcare systems [5].

In China, ongoing population aging and rising multimorbidity have increased the prevalence of polypharmacy and the need for long-term medication management, driving a greater reliance on specialized clinical pharmacy services [10, 11]. However, existing evidence indicates that hospital clinical pharmacy services in China still face several challenges, including insufficient organizational and financial support and limited patient awareness of the value of these services [12, 13]. Similar barriers have been reported in other middle-income countries such as Malaysia [14]. The lack of reimbursement mechanisms for clinical pharmacy services and limited patient awareness have hindered the routine implementation and sustainability of these services [14]. Therefore, pharmacy practice research is needed to generate evidence that may inform strategies for addressing the current challenges in healthcare institutions and strengthening the delivery of clinical pharmacy services.

Internationally, pharmacy practice research has evolved into a recognized field that contributes to the optimization of pharmaceutical care, expansion of pharmacists’ roles, and improvement of medication-related outcomes [4, 8, 15, 16]. However, pharmacy practice research remains heterogeneous across countries and healthcare systems, and barriers to research engagement continue to be reported internationally [7–9]. In China, pharmacy practice research remains an emerging area, with available evidence largely derived from single-center, small-sample studies [17, 18]. Furthermore, systematic research examining hospital pharmacists’ awareness and engagement in pharmacy practice research in China is lacking. Understanding these factors is essential for strengthening evidence-based pharmaceutical practices.

Aim

This study aimed to explore hospital pharmacists’ perceptions of pharmacy practice research and identify the factors influencing their engagement in such research.

Method

Study design and research team

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design within an interpretivist paradigm to explore hospital pharmacists’ perceptions, needs, and engagement in pharmacy practice research in China. Given the limited evidence in this area, semi-structured interviews were considered appropriate for exploring participants’ experiences and perspectives in depth. The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model [19] informed the analytical framework used to examine the factors influencing pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice research. This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [20].

The research team comprised investigators from the School of Pharmacy at Nanjing Medical University. The core researchers, C.Z. and Y.P.W., were postgraduate students majoring in clinical pharmacy with prior experience in hospital pharmacy settings and contributed to the interview design, data collection, and analysis.

Participant recruitment and sampling

A combination of purposive and snowball sampling was used to recruit the participants. Purposive sampling sought variation in geographical region, hospital location, professional role, academic qualifications, and years of practice. The initial participants were identified through professional networks, and the participants subsequently recommended additional candidates. Further participants were identified through publicly available information on the websites of major pharmacy schools and tertiary hospitals to enhance sample diversity.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Bachelor’s degree or above, (2) professional title of supervising pharmacist or higher, and (3) at least 10 years of professional experience in hospital pharmacy practice. These criteria were selected to ensure that the participants had substantial experience in pharmacy service delivery, management, and research activities. Recruitment continued iteratively until thematic saturation was achieved, as indicated by the absence of new themes or subthemes in successive interviews, and the data were considered sufficiently rich to address the study aims [21]. A total of 45 potential participants were contacted, of whom 21 completed the interview.

Participants were recruited from Grade A tertiary hospitals, the highest level of hospital accreditation in China. These hospitals are large, comprehensive institutions that provide advanced clinical care, education, and research. To maximize the variation in perspectives, participants were drawn from 14 provinces and municipalities across all seven major geographical regions of China, providing broad geographical representation and diversity in hospital pharmacy practice settings.

Data collection

Data were collected between June and August 2025 through one-on-one semi-structured interviews conducted on Tencent Meeting. Each interview lasted between 30 and 60 min and was audio recorded.

The interview guide was developed based on a review of pharmacy practice research literature and discussions with the research team. This study explored participants’ perceptions of pharmacy practice research, perceived needs, motivations, facilitators, barriers, and recommendations for future development. Pilot interviews with two pharmacists were conducted to refine the guide, resulting in minor revisions to enhance its clarity and relevance. To enhance the credibility of the findings, the interviewer used open-ended questions and follow-up probes to encourage participants to elaborate on their experiences and perspectives. Key interview questions

Data collection was conducted iteratively. Following each interview, the research team reviewed field notes and interview transcripts to identify emerging concepts and refine the interview prompts where necessary while maintaining consistency with the core interview guide. This iterative process enabled a deeper exploration of issues relevant to the study aims and supported thematic saturation. Prior to each interview, the participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire that included gender, age, professional title, years of professional experience, job position, and geographic region of work. All participants met the eligibility criteria, and recruitment was conducted to ensure broad geographical representation across the country.

Researcher reflexivity

All interviews were conducted by C.Z., a researcher with a pharmacy background and formal qualitative research training. The interviewer had no prior professional relationships with any of the participants. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout data collection and analysis to document methodological decisions, emerging interpretations, and researchers’ reflections on how their perspectives and assumptions might influence data interpretation.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed in Chinese. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis based on the COM-B model. Although the COM-B framework guided the organization and interpretation of codes, the coding process remained primarily data-driven, allowing themes to emerge inductively from the participants’ accounts. The codes were iteratively reviewed and mapped to the COM-B domains to ensure conceptual alignment while preserving the original data meanings.

Two researchers (C.Z. and Y.P.W.) independently familiarized themselves with the transcripts and conducted the initial coding. Coding was primarily data-driven, allowing concepts to emerge inductively from the data. The resulting codes were organized into the three COM-B domains and iteratively reviewed to develop themes and subthemes. Regular discussions among the research team were conducted to resolve discrepancies, refine interpretations, and ensure alignment between the data, emerging themes and the COM-B framework. Data management and coding were supported by NVivo 12 software.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Donghai County People’s Hospital (Approval Date: May 29, 2025; Approval Number: 2025-KY-110). All participants provided written informed consent prior to the study’s commencement. This study adhered to ethical principles, including confidentiality and voluntary participation of the subjects.

In this study, “clinical pharmacists” and “dispensing pharmacists” refer to hospital pharmacists with different professional roles in China. Clinical pharmacists directly participate in patient care activities, including medication review, therapeutic monitoring, and consultation, whereas dispensing pharmacists are primarily responsible for medication dispensing, prescription review, and medication supply. Theme 1. capability-related influences on engagement in pharmacy practice research

Understanding of pharmacy practice research

Participants generally perceived pharmacy practice research as encompassing a wide range of practice areas closely aligned with their routine professional activities. Many indicated that their daily work fell within the scope of pharmacy practice. Some participants had prior experience in this field, most commonly in clinical pharmacy services, while others described research in pharmaceutical administration, policy-related areas, and medication use management. Overall, pharmacists’ research topics were closely integrated into their routine practice.

“Clinical pharmacists usually do not have sufficient time to conduct basic research. If your research is closely linked to your daily work, pharmacy practice research is a more suitable direction.”—Participant 5, Clinical Pharmacist, East China.

These findings suggest that pharmacists’ understanding of pharmacy practice research is largely grounded in clinical pharmacy services, pharmaceutical administration and policy research, and medication use management.

Research capability

Another major barrier was insufficient research competence. Some pharmacists reported limited training in research methodology, including study design, statistical analysis, and academic writing, which made it difficult to develop research questions and implement studies effectively.

“Various problems arise during research. If the initial design is not well developed, you may realize midway that it is flawed or find that the results do not reach statistical significance.”—Participant 7, Clinical Pharmacist, East China.

These findings suggest that limited research capabilities may restrict pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice research.

Research capacity development priorities

Drawing on their professional experience, the participants identified several areas in which additional knowledge, competencies, and research capacity are required to advance pharmacy practice research, particularly in pharmaceutical care, service delivery models, and information system development.

Participants emphasized that pharmaceutical care should focus on evaluating medication effectiveness, optimizing treatment regimens, and providing individualized patient services. However, its implementation remains a challenge. Achieving precision in medication use requires a comprehensive assessment of patient characteristics, including medication history, age, comorbidities, hepatic and renal functions, genetic factors, and concomitant medications. This highlights the need for evidence-based research to support precision pharmaceutical care and individualized medication management for patients.

“We place great emphasis on precision in personalized medication. Pharmaceutical services also require precision because patients have different needs.”—Participant 9, Clinical Pharmacist, Central China.

Participants also noted that current service models have limitations in terms of coverage, coordination efficiency, and accessibility, particularly in primary care settings, among community-based patients, and among vulnerable populations, such as older adults and patients with chronic diseases. Expanding service models through “Internet + pharmacy” approaches, including remote consultation and integrated care systems, is a potential strategy for improving accessibility and continuity of care.

“As policies evolve and service scenarios expand—such as dual-channel pharmacies, community pharmacies, and home-based pharmaceutical services for older adults—we can explore these areas further.”—Participant 18, Dispensing Pharmacist, East China.

Participants further highlighted that underdeveloped information systems limit both service efficiency and research capacity. Strengthening intelligent pharmaceutical systems is essential for advancing pharmacy practice. Some participants described emerging efforts to use big data and artificial intelligence to support medication decision-making and risk assessment.

“For example, big data and AI can be used to predict medication risks and analyze real-world data; our hospital is currently exploring this.”—Participant 20, Clinical Pharmacist, Northeast China.

These findings indicate the need to strengthen pharmacists’ research capacity in emerging pharmacy practice areas, including precision pharmaceutical care, innovative service models, and intelligent pharmaceutical systems.

Theme 2. opportunity-related influences on engagement in pharmacy practice research

Organisational support

Most participants reported a positive research atmosphere in their institutions and recognized the value of pharmacy practice research in their institutions. These participants were primarily from comprehensive teaching hospitals in economically developed regions, which typically have strong academic environments and established research cultures. This suggests that pharmacists’ engagement is strongly influenced by the institutional research culture. Supportive environments appear to enhance motivation, making participation in research a natural extension of professional practice rather than an additional burden.

“I think that the research atmosphere is very important. For example, in our clinical pharmacy research group, everyone is highly motivated and competitive, which naturally boosts motivation. If you were in a dispensing department where no one conducted research, you might not engage in research either. Team cohesion is very important.”—Participant 15, Clinical Pharmacist, East China.

Key facilitating factors include institutional research requirements, the availability of support and incentive mechanisms, and effective teamwork. In some institutions, research outputs are incorporated into performance evaluation systems with incentives such as recognition, awards, and performance-based rewards, which effectively promote participation in research.

“First, it is related to performance, which is crucial. Second, there are requirements for professional title promotion, as pharmacists must demonstrate research achievements at each stage of advancement.”—Participant 15, Clinical Pharmacist, East China.

Institutional support was identified as an important facilitator of research engagement. Some hospitals provide research infrastructure, establish dedicated research management departments, and offer external training opportunities, thereby creating supportive research environments.

These findings indicate that organizational culture, research assessment requirements, and institutional support mechanisms create opportunities for engaging in pharmacy practice research to be conducted.

Collaborative environment

The participants emphasized the importance of teamwork. Interdisciplinary collaboration, particularly with clinicians and information specialists, is essential for identifying research questions, designing robust studies, and accessing relevant data.

“Cooperation is very important. When data are limited or incomplete, collaboration with colleagues who understand both clinical practice and data analysis becomes essential.”—Participant 16, Clinical Pharmacist, East China.

Resource constraints

Nearly all participants indicated that a heavy clinical workload limited their ability to conduct research. Dispensing pharmacists are primarily responsible for medication dispensing and prescription review, whereas clinical pharmacists often undertake additional administrative responsibilities. Consequently, research activities are frequently treated as additional work, often requiring overtime to complete.

“I want to conduct research, but I can only do it in my spare time. There is simply no time during working hours.”—Participant 10, Clinical Pharmacist, South China.

In addition, limited research resources, including inadequate funding, underdeveloped information systems, and a lack of specialized equipment, such as therapeutic drug monitoring systems, hinder research activities.

“Research funding is generally a major challenge in our region.”—Participant 9, Clinical Pharmacist, Central China.

These findings indicate that resource constraints are important opportunity-related barriers to pharmacy practice research in China. Heavy workloads, limited staffing, and insufficient research resources reduce opportunities for pharmacists to engage in and sustain research activities alongside their routine professional responsibilities.

External implementation challenges

Low patient awareness and limited cooperation in clinical research also pose challenges for data collection and project implementation. Additionally, the participants noted that translating research findings into routine practice often requires substantial time and effort.

“For instance, challenges may emerge due to poor patient compliance during drug monitoring or outpatient follow-ups.”—Participant 6, Clinical Pharmacist, East China.

These findings indicate that external environmental factors may limit pharmacy practice research opportunities.

Theme 3. motivation-related influences on engagement in pharmacy practice research

Professional motivations

All participants recognized the positive value of pharmacy practice research. They indicated that such research could help address clinical problems, provide professional medication guidance and interventions, improve patient outcomes, enhance the quality of pharmaceutical care, and support the transformation of pharmacy practices. Engaging in research was also perceived to strengthen pharmacists’ roles in clinical decision-making and increase recognition of their professional value among healthcare teams and patients. The sense of professional accomplishment derived from these contributions emerged as the key motivation for participation in this pharmacy practice research.

Motivation for participation was driven by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including professional fulfillment, a strong interest in research, and a desire to enhance professional competitiveness. Many participants expressed a strong interest in research and willingness to actively engage in such activities. Participation in research was viewed as an opportunity to expand professional knowledge, develop research thinking, and enhance innovation capacity, thereby further strengthening professional competitiveness. Career advancement and professional title promotion were also frequently cited as important motivations, as research achievements are often required for professional progression in the academic field.

“Being able to solve clinical problems and help patients through practice-based research—that’s where I find a great sense of accomplishment.”—Participant 10, Clinical Pharmacist, South China.

“Personally, it is closely related to my interest in research, and I hope to achieve breakthroughs within my professional field.”—Participant 21, Dispensing Pharmacist, East China.

These findings indicate that both intrinsic motivation and external professional incentives play important roles in driving pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice research.

Organisational motivations

In contrast, a minority of participants reported low engagement in pharmacy practice research among their peers. These participants indicated that the overall depth of pharmacy practice in their institutions was limited, with services largely restricted to basic tasks such as medication dispensing, counseling, and prescription review. This lack of advanced pharmacy practice activities and insufficient opportunities for longitudinal patient management may limit data accumulation, opportunities for in-depth research, and motivation to participate in pharmacy practice research.

“In our region, it is relatively difficult to conduct in-depth pharmacy practice. Overall, the level of practice is quite superficial.”—Participant 2, Clinical Pharmacist, North China.

These findings indicate that organizational culture and the maturity of pharmacy practice environments may influence pharmacists’ motivation to engage in pharmacy practice research.

Discussion

Pharmacy practice research has gained international attention, particularly in developed healthcare systems [22]. Pharmacist-led research plays a critical role in optimizing medication use and improving patient outcomes [23, 24]. In contrast, pharmacy practice research in China is still in its early stages. This study found that pharmacists’ research topics were closely aligned with their routine professional activity. Most participants recognized the necessity of conducting such research and expressed a strong willingness to engage. The key motivations included professional development, institutional evaluation requirements and personal research interests. Supportive institutional environments and effective team collaboration were identified as important facilitators of this process. However, insufficient time and staffing, limited research resources, and inadequate research competence remain significant barriers to this.

As pharmacy practice research continues to develop in hospital settings in China, greater conceptual clarity regarding its scope will be beneficial. Although often embedded within routine professional practice, pharmacy practice research should be distinguished from other practice improvement approaches, such as clinical audits, service evaluations, and quality improvement initiatives. Clarifying these distinctions may help establish a clearer understanding of its aims, methodological boundaries, and contributions to evidence-based pharmaceutical care.

The COM-B framework provides a useful lens for understanding pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice research. Participants described capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related influences, suggesting that research engagement is shaped by factors beyond individual interest. Although motivation was generally high, limited methodological competence, insufficient resources, and lack of protected research time frequently restricted participation. Consistent with the COM-B model, strengthening research engagement may require addressing capability- and opportunity-related barriers, alongside motivation [19]. Recent guidance has similarly emphasized the value of behavioral theories and frameworks in understanding pharmacy practice and research engagement [25, 26].

Motivation is a prerequisite for initiating research activities but is insufficient to sustain long-term engagement [15]. Pharmacists are more likely to recognize the value of research and actively participate when research activities are closely aligned with clinical needs and can directly improve patient outcomes [7, 15]. However, relying on motivation alone cannot ensure sustained engagement in research. Some participants suggested decreased motivation after achieving career advancement goals, highlighting the need for supportive structural conditions to maintain long-term participation in pharmacy practice research.

Within the COM-B framework, capability and opportunity are essential for translating motivation into behavior [27]. Strengthening pharmacists’ research capabilities through structured training and competency development is essential to support sustainable engagement in pharmacy practice research [28]. This study suggests that insufficient research competence and limited institutional support significantly hinder hospital pharmacists’ research participation. In particular, the lack of protected research time and insufficient staffing reflect a structural imbalance between clinical workload and research expectations. In addition, limited access to research resources, such as funding, information systems, and specialized equipment, further constrains participation in research. Even when initial motivation is strong, persistent opportunity-related barriers may limit sustained engagement in research.

Organizational culture plays a critical role in shaping research engagement. This finding is consistent with international evidence showing that institutions with strong research orientations and leadership support are more likely to provide protected research time, funding, and training opportunities [7, 8, 29–31]. Participants from large teaching hospitals generally reported stronger research cultures and greater institutional support, highlighting the importance of research infrastructure and organizational resources in fostering research engagement [30, 32]. In contrast, limited institutional support and weak incentive mechanisms may restrict pharmacists' participation in the program. Therefore, strengthening leadership awareness and establishing appropriate incentive systems are important strategies for promoting research engagement among pharmacists.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a key factor in facilitating this process. Collaboration with clinicians and information specialists can help address knowledge gaps, improve study design, and enhance data accessibility, thereby promoting the translation of research into clinical practice [33–35]. However, pharmacists must demonstrate their professional competence and clearly communicate their value in clinical decision-making to ensure effective collaboration [36, 37]. This highlights the dynamic interaction among capability, opportunity, and motivation, rather than a simple linear relationship.

Although pharmacists face multiple barriers, emerging research areas may provide new opportunities. Participants frequently highlighted precision medicine and pharmacogenomics as important directions for advancing individualized medication management, with evidence supporting their roles in improving medication safety and efficacy [38]. In addition, digital technologies such as artificial intelligence, big data, and data-driven decision systems are increasingly being applied to optimize prescribing and support clinical decision-making [39, 40]. As pharmacy practice continues to evolve, pharmacists are increasingly encountering new clinical and service-related challenges that require evidence to guide decision-making. Therefore, these emerging practice areas may provide opportunities for pharmacy research.

Although this study was conducted in China, similar barriers have been reported internationally, including heavy workloads, limited technical capacity, and insufficient funding [7–9, 36]. These findings are consistent with international evidence, where lack of time, limited research skills, and insufficient institutional support have been identified as major barriers to clinical pharmacy research engagement [41]. Together, these findings suggest broader relevance across healthcare systems. Therefore, structured research training, protected research time, and interdisciplinary collaboration are essential to enhance pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice research and support evidence-based pharmaceutical care.

This study had several limitations. First, all participants were recruited from Grade A tertiary general hospitals in China and were primarily senior pharmacists with considerable experience. While this enabled the collection of information-rich perspectives, their views may differ from those of junior pharmacists or early career practitioners, who may face different barriers and support needs when engaging in pharmacy practice research. Therefore, the findings may be most transferable to similar hospital settings, and caution should be exercised when applying them to other healthcare contexts. Second, this study focused exclusively on hospital pharmacists and did not include pharmacists working in other practice settings, such as primary care, community pharmacies, or industry. Future research should explore a broader range of pharmacy practice environments to better understand how organizational and contextual factors influence pharmacy practice research engagement.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into hospital pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy practice research in China. The findings suggest that engagement is shaped by capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related factors, including research competence, organizational support, resource availability, and interdisciplinary collaboration. These findings highlight the need for enhanced research training, institutional support, and collaborative practice environments to strengthen pharmacists’ engagement in pharmacy research in the future. As this study reflects the perspectives of hospital pharmacists working in tertiary hospitals, the findings should be interpreted within this specific organizational and professional context of tertiary hospitals.
