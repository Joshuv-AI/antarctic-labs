The Legend of Masaki Kashiwara and Algebraic Analysis To the memory of Professor Mikio Sato
Kiyoshi TAKEUCHI

Abstract
This survey paper offers a concise introduction to Kashiwara’s work on 𝒟 \mathcal{D} -modules, microlocal analysis and related subjects. In this way, we explain his role in the development of algebraic analysis.



1
Introduction

As one of many mathematicians who have benefited greatly from the works and
ideas of Masaki Kashiwara, I am very pleased to
take on the role of conveying my enthusiasm for
his remarkable achievements in mathematics to the reader.
Because of the rapid development of different fields of mathematics,
nowadays it is becoming harder and harder to move
across several subjects, as historical giants such as
Euler, Gauss, and Hilbert did centuries ago.
Nevertheless, this is not the case with Masaki Kashiwara.

One of the keys to his success lies in his
systematic use of category theory.
Arguments involving categories and functors
may appear too general at first glance, but they allow us to transmit ideas
between fields in a highly efficient way.
In my opinion, Kashiwara is one of the very few mathematicians
who have succeeded in making full use of this strength of category theory
to obtain decisive results in various distinct branches of
mathematics.

In particular, he developed
the theory of derived categories, introduced by
Grothendieck and Mikio Sato in the 1960’s, to
its full potential, so that it can be applied to a broad
range of problems. As a consequence, in the monumental
paper
[
177
]
, Sato, Kawai and Kashiwara constructed
a general theory for systems of linear PDEs, of a kind that had never
been envisioned before.
Moreover in
[
84
]
and
[
87
]
,
Kashiwara established the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence,
by which problems in analysis (regular holonomic

\mathcal{D}
-modules) can be translated into problems
in geometry (perverse sheaves), and used it
to settle the Kazhdan-Lusztig conjecture in spectacular fashion
with Brylinski in
[
21
]
.

In this way, Kashiwara and his coauthors
tied together analysis and algebra,
which had seemed far removed from each other,
and created a completely new field called “algebraic
analysis”. Soon after its emergence in the 1970’s,
it became clear that the methods and ideas developed in this field
were extremely powerful and applicable to many areas in mathematics,
such as algebraic geometry, representation theory,
singularity theory, number theory, and mathematical
physics. Just as analysis was profoundly
influenced by functional analysis a century ago,
algebraic analysis transformed it once again at a fundamental level.

In this revolution of mathematics,
Kashiwara and others also used the theory of derived
categories to create new fields and problems,
greatly enlarging our vision of mathematics.
In particular, Kashiwara’s books
[
100
]
and
[
104
]

on derived categories, written in collaboration
with Schapira, have had an enormous impact on mathematics
and are widely regarded as foundational texts.
Indeed, after Kashiwara, the mathematical landscape
has been profoundly changed. Like great art or music,
everybody who gets in touch with Kashiwara’s work
is struck by its beauty and elegance. In this sense, he has enriched human
culture itself,
gifting us a remarkable collection of intellectual masterpieces.
Undoubtedly, his influence will continue to spread over the generations.

The aim of this paper is to give a concise introduction to
this rapidly growing area of mathematics, with
special emphasis on Kashiwara’s works, and assuming only that the
reader is familiar with some basic definitions from sheaf theory.
Given the extraordinary breadth of Kashiwara’s contributions,
I have selected only a few important topics that I am
most familiar with. Each of these will be discussed separately in the
subsequent sections. Other important topics will be treated by the other
authors in this special volume dedicated to
Masaki Kashiwara.

The author thanks Ren Fernandes, Kazuki Kudomi and
Hajime Tsuchiya for reading the manuscript
carefully. He is also grateful to Professor
Alan Stapledon and the anonymous referee
for many useful suggestions on this paper.

\mathcal{D}
-modules

The theory of
\mathcal{D}
-modules was initiated by Kashiwara
[
91
]

and Bernstein
[
9
]
independently.
Whereas Kashiwara’s master thesis
[
91
]
treats analytic
\mathcal{D}
-modules
on complex manifolds, in Bernstein’s paper
[
9
]

algebraic
\mathcal{D}
-modules i.e. modules over Weyl algebras are treated.
Here we mainly restrict ourselves to Kashiwara’s theory of
analytic
\mathcal{D}
-modules.
For the details of this section, see e.g.

[
13
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
74
]
,

[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
.
Let
X
be a complex manifold and denote by
\mathcal{O}_{X}
the sheaf
of holomorphic functions on it.
Then we define a sheaf
\mathcal{D}_{X}
of (non-commutative) rings on
X

by assigning to each open subset
U\subset X

the ring of differential operators on
U
with coefficients in
\mathcal{O}_{X}
.
More precisely, we construct
\mathcal{D}_{X}
as a subsheaf of rings of

\mathcal{E}nd_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}(\mathcal{O}_{X})={\mathcal{H}}om(\mathcal{O}_{X},\mathcal{O}_{X})
and for two sections

P,Q\in\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)
on an open subset
U\subset X

their product
PQ\in\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)
is defined to be the composition
P\circ Q

of the
\mathbb{C}_{X}
-linear sheaf homomorphisms

\mathcal{O}_{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Q\times}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\times}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{U}
.
If the open subset
U\subset X
is non-emptry, connected and
endowed with a holomorphic coordinate

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, then we have

\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)=\biggl\{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}}a_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\,\biggm|\,a_{\alpha}(x)\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(U)\biggr\},

where we set
\mathbb{Z}_{+}\coloneqq\{m\in\mathbb{Z}\,|\,m\geq 0\}
,
the sums
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}}
are finite, and for

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
the standard
notation
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{n}}
is used.
In this situation, the products in the ring
\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)
are described
by the classical Leibniz rule.
For example, we have the relation

[\partial_{x_{i}},x_{j}]=\delta_{ij}\ (1\leq i,j\leq n)
.
For a differential operator
P=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}}a_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)
on such
U\subset X

we set

\textrm{ord}P\coloneqq\textrm{max}\{|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}\,\big|\,a_{\alpha}(x)\not\equiv 0\}

and call it the order of
P
.
It turns out that this local definition does not depend on the choice of the local coordinate

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and hence by setting

F_{i}\mathcal{D}_{X}\coloneqq\{P\in\mathcal{D}_{X}\,\bigm|\,\textrm{ord}P\leq i\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{X}\qquad(i\in\mathbb{Z})

we obtain a filtration
\{F_{i}\mathcal{D}_{X}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mathcal{D}_{X}
.
Then by the relation

[\partial_{x_{i}},x_{j}]=\delta_{ij}\,(1\leq i,j\leq n)
, for the classes

\xi_{i}\coloneqq[\partial_{x_{i}}]\in F_{1}\mathcal{D}_{X}/F_{0}\mathcal{D}_{X}\,(1\leq i\leq n)
the sheaf of graded rings

\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}\Bigl(F_{i}\mathcal{D}_{X}/F_{i-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}\Bigr)

associated to it is locally isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}_{X}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}]
.
This in particular implies that
\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}
is commutative.
Building upon the coherency of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
proved by K.Oka and using this
commutative
\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}
, Kashiwara overcame the difficulty arising from
the non-commutativity of
\mathcal{D}_{X}
and proved that the
sheaf
\mathcal{D}_{X}
of (non-commutative) rings is coherent.
This implies that a (left)
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
is coherent over
\mathcal{D}_{X}
if
and only if it
is locally finitely presented over
\mathcal{D}_{X}
i.e.
for any point
p\in X
there exist its neighborhood
U\subset X
,
non-negative integers
N_{0},N_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and an exact sequence

\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow 0

of (left)
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules on
U
.
As for such
U\subset X
there exists an
N_{1}\times N_{0}
matrix

P=(P_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq N_{1},1\leq j\leq N_{0}}\in M\bigl(N_{1},N_{0};\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)\bigr)
of differential operators

P_{ij}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)
on
U
such that the
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-linear morphism
\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}

is given by

\Phi(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{N_{1}})=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{N_{1}})P\qquad\bigl((Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{N_{1}})\in\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{1}}(U)\bigr),

we obtain an isomorphism
\mathcal{M}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}/\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{1}}P
on
U\subset X
.
In the early 1960’s,
in his faculty talk at the University of Tokyo,
Mikio Sato proposed a new program to study general systems of
linear partial differential equations by regarding
them as coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules.
Let us explain his brilliant idea briefly.
First, note that the sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{X}
is naturally a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module.
Next, apply the left exact functor

{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\,\cdot\,,\mathcal{O}_{X})
to the exact sequence

\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\times P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow 0

on
U\ \Bigl(P\in M\bigl(N_{1},N_{0};\mathcal{D}_{X}(U)\bigr)\Bigr)
.
Then one obtains an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\times}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{1}}

and hence an isomorphism

{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})\simeq\bigl\{\vec{u}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{0}}\,\bigm|\,P\vec{u}=\vec{0}\bigr\}\qquad\subset\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{0}}.

We thus find that the sheaf of the holomorphic solutions to the
differential equation
P\vec{u}=\vec{0}
is isomorphic to the hom
sheaf
{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})
. If
X
is a complexification of
a real analytic manifold
M\subset X
, the same is true also after replacing

\mathcal{O}_{X}
by any other left
\mathcal{D}_{X}|_{M}
-module of
classical function spaces such as (complex-valued)
C^{\infty}
-functions,
distributions and Sato’s hyperfunctions etc. on
M
.
For a coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
on
X
, then it is natural
to consider not only the solution sheaf

{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})
but also the solution complex
\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})
defined as follows. Let

\cdots\cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\times P_{k}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{k-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\times P_{k-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\times P_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}

be a (locally defined) free resolution of
\mathcal{M}
over
\mathcal{D}_{X}
. Then we call the complex

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{1}\times}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{2}\times}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{k-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{k}\times}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{X}^{N_{k}}\longrightarrow\cdots\cdots

obtained by applying the functor
{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\,\cdot\,,\mathcal{O}_{X})
to
it the solution complex of
\mathcal{M}
and denote it by
\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})
.
If we consider it as an object of the derived category
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)

(for the definition, see e.g.
[
74
]
,
[
100
]
,

[
104
]
) consisting of
bounded complexes of
\mathbb{C}_{X}
-modules on
X
, this definition does
not depend on the choice of the resolution of
\mathcal{M}
.
Namely, we set
\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})=\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})
.
Obviously it satisfies the condition

H^{0}\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})\simeq{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X}).

In order to study deeper structures of coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules, Kashiwara
defined their characteristic varieties as follows. Let

\pi_{X}\colon T^{*}X\rightarrow X
be the (holomorphic) cotangent
bundle of X. Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a local coordinate of
X

and
(x,\xi)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
that of
T^{*}X
associated to it.
Then for
P=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}}a_{\alpha}(x)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
of order
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

we define a holomorphic function
\sigma(P)
of
(x,\xi)
by

\sigma(P)(x,\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{|\alpha|=m}a_{\alpha}(x)\xi^{\alpha}

and call it the principal symbol of
P
. We can easily check that it defines a function on
T^{*}X
.
More precisely
\sigma(P)
is a section of the subsheaf

\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{X}[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}]\quad\subset{\pi_{X}}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}

of the direct image sheaf
{\pi_{X}}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}
of
\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}
by
\pi_{X}
.
Recall that in classical analysis for a single equation

Pu=0\,(P\in\mathcal{D}_{X})
(which corresponds to the coherent

\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{D}_{X}/\mathcal{D}_{X}P
) the complex hypersurface

\{\sigma(P)=0\}\subset T^{*}X
in
T^{*}X
is called the characteristic
variety of the differential operator
P\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
and plays an
important role in studying the properties of its solutions. However,
it was not until Kashiwara’s master thesis
[
91
]

that we knew how to define characteristic varieties for general systems

P\vec{u}=\vec{0}
of linear partial differential equations.
Namely, it was Kashiwara who first set up a solid basis for the
systematic study of linear partial differential equations.
For the definition of the characteristic variety of a coherent

\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
we proceed as follows.
First, by the (locally defined) surjective morphism
\Psi\colon\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{M}

we define a filtration
\{F_{i}\mathcal{M}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mathcal{M}
by

F_{i}\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\Psi\bigl((F_{i}\mathcal{D}_{X})^{N_{0}}\bigr)\subset\mathcal{M}\qquad(i\in\mathbb{Z}).

Then the graded module

\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}(F_{i}\mathcal{M}/F_{i-1}\mathcal{M})

associated to it is naturally a
\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module. Moreover,
we can show that it is coherent over
\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}
.
Then by the ring homomorphisms

\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}\longrightarrow\pi_{X}^{-1}{\pi_{X}}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}

we define a coherent
\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}
-module
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
on
T^{*}X
by

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}_{T^{*}X}\otimes_{\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{D}_{X}}\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathrm{gr}^{F}\mathcal{M}

and set
\ch\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\supp\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\subset T^{*}X
.
We can show that this definition of the complex analytic
subset
\ch\mathcal{M}\subset T^{*}X
does not depend on the choice of the
surjective morphism
\Psi\colon\mathcal{D}_{X}^{N_{0}}\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{M}
with the help of the theory of
good filtrations (see
[
91
]
).
We call
\ch\mathcal{M}\subset T^{*}X
thus obtained the characteristic variety of
\mathcal{M}
.
As in the case of a single equation

Pu=0
, the characteristic variety
\ch\mathcal{M}\subset T^{*}X
is conic i.e.
invariant under the standard action of
the multiplicative group
\mathbb{C}^{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
on
T^{*}X
. Recall that
T^{*}X
is endowed with
the canonical symplectic structure defined by the non-degenerate holomorphic 2-form

\sigma_{T^{*}X}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}\wedge d\xi_{i}\in\Omega_{T^{*}X}^{2}
.
Then the following theorem obtained by Sato, Kawai and Kashiwara in
the monumental paper
[
177
]

reveals a striking feature of
characteristic varieties.

Theorem 2.1
.

The characteristic variety
\ch\mathcal{M}\subset T^{*}X
of a coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
is involutive with respect to
the canonical symplectic structure of
T^{*}X
. In particular, for
any irreducible component
\Lambda
of
\ch\mathcal{M}
we have

\dim\,\Lambda\geq\frac{1}{2}\dim T^{*}X=\dim X.

In the algebraic case, the inequality

\dim\,\ch\mathcal{M}\geq\dim X
was proved also by
Bernstein
[
9
]
and we call it Bernstein’s inequality.
Later, a purely algebraic proof of Theorem
2.1
was
given by Gabber
[
55
]
.
For a geometric proof of this “involutivity theorem”
see Kashiwara-Schapira
[
100
, Theorem 6.5.4]
,
in which to our surprise the involutivity
of the micro-supports of general sheaves is proved. Among coherent

\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules, the following ones are of particular importance.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that a coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
is holonomic if its
characteristic variety
\ch\mathcal{M}\subset T^{*}X
is Lagrangian
i.e.
\dim\,\ch\mathcal{M}\leq\dim X
.

Let
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
be the abelian category of coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules on
X
and

\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})\subset\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
its full
subcategory consisting of holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules.
Then it turns out that the category
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
is again
abelian. It was Mikio Sato who first noticed
the importance of holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules and suggested Kashiwara
and Kawai to study them very precisely.
Indeed, as in the (quite fruitful) case of ordinary differential
equations on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
in classical analysis, the holomorphic solution complex

\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})=\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
is “finite dimensional”
in the following sense. Recall that for
a complex manifold
Z
a
\mathbb{C}_{Z}
-module
L
is a local system if it is locally
free of finite rank over
\mathbb{C}_{Z}
. For the complex manifold
X
, then we
say that a
\mathbb{C}_{X}
-module
K
is (complex) constructible if there exists a (complex analytic)
stratification
X=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in A}X_{\alpha}
of
X
such that

K|_{X_{\alpha}}
is a local system for any
\alpha\in A
.
More generally, we say that an object
F\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
of the
derived category of
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
of
\mathbb{C}_{X}
-modules is constructible if
its cohomology sheaves are constructible in the above sense. The following theorem, which is now called
Kashiwara’s constructibility theorem, was proved in his Ph.D. thesis
[
81
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For a holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
its solution
complex
\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})=\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}\\
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{O}_{X})\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
is constructible.

For a holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
we set

\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\mathcal{M})\coloneqq\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\mathcal{O}_{X},\mathcal{M})[\dim X]

and call it the de Rham complex of
\mathcal{M}
. In
[
81
]
Kashiwara also
proved that
\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\mathcal{M})\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
is constructible.
Let
{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)\subset\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
be the full subcategory of
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)

consisting of constructible objects.
Recall that the Verdier dual functor
\mathrm{D}_{X}\colon\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)\longrightarrow\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
defined by

\mathrm{D}_{X}(F)\coloneqq\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}(F,\mathbb{C}_{X}[2\dim X])\qquad\bigl(F\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)\bigr)

preserves the constructibility. Then the relation between
\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})

and
\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\mathcal{M})
is explained by the isomorphism

\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\mathcal{M})\simeq\mathrm{D}_{X}\bigl(\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})[\dim X]\bigr)

proved also in Kashiwara
[
81
]
. Although the notion of perverse
sheaves appeared only after the paper
[
8
]
of Beilinson,
Bernstein and Deligne in the 1980’s, in the Ph.D. thesis

[
81
]
Kashiwara already proved that
for a holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
the constructible sheaves

\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})[\dim X]
,
\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\mathcal{M})\in{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)

satisfy the conditions of perversity defined as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that
F\in{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
is a perverse sheaf if it satisfies the following two conditions.

(i)

\dim\,(\supp H^{j}F)\leq-j\qquad(j\in\mathbb{Z})
,

(ii)

\dim\,\bigl(\supp H^{j}\mathrm{D}_{X}(F)\bigr)\leq-j\qquad(j\in\mathbb{Z})
.

Denote by
\mathrm{Perv}(\mathbb{C}_{X})\subset{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
the full subcategory
of
{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
consisting of perverse sheaves. Then it turns out
that the category
\mathrm{Perv}(\mathbb{C}_{X})
is abelian. Consequently, we obtain a functor

\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\,\cdot\,)\colon\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Perv}(\mathbb{C}_{X}).

However it is not an equivalence of categories in general except for
the trivial case of
\dim X=0
. A remedy for
this problem is to construct a suitable subcategory of
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
to have an equivalence.
Generalizing the classical notion of regular singular points of
ODEs, in
[
97
]
Kashiwara and Oshima defined the regularity of holonomic
\mathcal{D}
-modules.
Then in
[
95
]
Kashiwara and Kawai developed the theory of
regular holonomic
\mathcal{D}
-modules and proved their basic properties.
Finally in
[
84
]
and
[
87
]

Kashiwara established the following
Riemann-Hilbert correspondence. Let
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
be the
full subcategory of
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
consisting of regular holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules.
We denote by
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})^{\mathrm{op}}
its opposite category.

Theorem 2.5
.

The functors

\displaystyle\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\,\cdot\,)\colon

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Perv}(\mathbb{C}_{X})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\,\cdot\,)[\dim X]\colon

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Perv}(\mathbb{C}_{X})

induce equivalences of categories.

Let
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
be the derived category of bounded complexes of
(left)
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules and
{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
its full subcategory consisting
of bounded complexes of
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules whose cohomology sheaves are regular holonomic.
Then, more generally in
[

]
Kashiwara proved also that there
exist equivalences of categories

\mathrm{DR}_{X}(\,\cdot\,)\colon{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X),\quad\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\,\cdot\,)\colon{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})^{\mathrm{op}}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X).

This gives an ultimate solution to (the higher-dimensional generalization of)
the 21st problem of Hilbert, raised in the early 20th century.
Recall that for regular meromorphic connections
\mathcal{M}\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})

on
X
a satisfactory answer to it was given previously by Deligne
[

]
.
In order to prove the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, Kashiwara
constructed an inverse
\RH_{X}(\,\cdot\,)\colon{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)\longrightarrow{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})^{\mathrm{op}}
of
the solution functor
\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\,\cdot\,)\colon{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow{\bf D}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
explicitly.
His proof was completed around 1980 (see
[
84
]
).
Then the full proof was published in
[
87
]
.
Later, a different proof was given by Mebkhout in

[
135
]
. The Riemann-Hilbert correspondence has also a nice
functoriality with respect to morphisms of complex
manifolds as follows. First, note that for a morphism

f\colon Y\longrightarrow X
of complex manifolds
the right
f^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module

\mathcal{D}_{Y\rightarrow X}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}_{Y}\otimes_{f^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{X}}f^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}

is naturally endowed with a structure of a left

\mathcal{D}_{Y}
-module, commuting with the action of
f^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}
.
Namely
\mathcal{D}_{Y\rightarrow X}
is a

(\mathcal{D}_{Y},f^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X})
-bimodule and for

\mathcal{M}\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
we define its inverse image

\mathrm{D}f^{\ast}\mathcal{M}\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{D}_{Y})
by

\mathrm{D}f^{\ast}\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}_{Y\rightarrow X}\overset{L}{\otimes}_{f^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}}f^{-1}\mathcal{M}\qquad\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{D}_{Y}).

Since this operation preserves the regular holonomicity,
we obtain a functor

\mathrm{D}f^{\ast}(\,\cdot\,):{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})\longrightarrow{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{Y})

(see
[
83
]
and
[
95
]
).
Moreover, for any
\mathcal{M}\in{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
there exists
an isomorphism

f^{-1}\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathcal{M})\simeq\mathrm{Sol}_{Y}(\mathrm{D}f^{\ast}\mathcal{M}).

Similarly, if
f
is proper for
\mathcal{N}\in{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{Y})

we can define its direct image

\mathrm{D}f_{\ast}\mathcal{N}\in{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathrm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
and obtain
an isomorphism

\mathrm{R}f_{\ast}\mathrm{Sol}_{Y}(\mathcal{N})[\dim Y]\simeq\mathrm{Sol}_{X}(\mathrm{D}f_{\ast}\mathcal{N})[\dim X].

Via the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, problems in analysis (
\mathcal{D}
-modules)
can be interpreted to those in geometry (constructible sheaves).
In this way, many problems were settled and beautiful theories
have emerged, advancing the state of science of mathematics tremendously.

3
Microlocal analysis

The idea of symplectic geometry and microlocal analysis goes back to
the age of Hamilton and Jacobi.
It is Hamilton who first considered ordinary differential equations
on the cotangent bundle
T^{\ast}\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in order to
study those on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
more elegantly.
Such systems of ODEs are called Hamilton’s canonical equations
and are basic in analytical mechanics and classical analysis.
Jacobi used Hamilton’s ODEs to construct solutions of partial
differential equations of first order.
We call it the method of Hamilton and Jacobi.
The idea of microlocal analysis of Mikio Sato is to see
the singularities of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
locally
at each point of the cotangent bundle
T^{\ast}\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
extend the above mentioned beautiful results in classical analysis
to any system of linear partial differential equations,
at least on the singularities of their solutions.
In collaboration with Kawai and Kashiwara, his dream was
realized in the so-called SKK paper
[
177
]
,
revealing numerous astonishing results.
In order to explain some of their great achievements in it,
let
M
be a real analytic manifold and
X
its complexification.
We denote by
T_{M}^{*}X\simeq\sqrt{-1}T^{*}M
the conormal bundle of
M
in
X
.
By removing the zero section
T_{M}^{\ast}X\cap T_{X}^{\ast}X\simeq M\subset T_{M}^{\ast}X

from it we set
\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X\coloneq T_{M}^{\ast}X\setminus T_{X}^{\ast}X
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{M}\coloneq\mathcal{O}_{X}|_{M}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}_{M}
) be
the sheaf of real analytic functions (resp. Sato’s hyperfunctions) on
M
.
Then in 1969, Sato introduced the sheaf
\mathcal{C}_{M}
of microfunctions on

\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X

2
2

2

In fact, in
[
177
]

the sheaf of microfunctions was defined on the
sphere bundle
S_{M}^{\ast}X=\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X/\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
which fits into the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathring{\pi}_{\ast}\mathcal{C}_{M}\longrightarrow 0

where
\mathring{\pi}\colon\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X\to M
is the projection.
For this purpose, he defined the microlocalization functor

\mu_{M}(\cdot):\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)\longrightarrow\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(T^{*}_{M}X)

along
M\subset X
, which is now a basic tool in sheaf theory.
Moreover, Kashiwara proved that the sheaf
\mathcal{C}_{M}
is conically flabby.
For a hyperfunction
u\in\mathcal{B}_{M}(U)
on an open subset
U\subset M
,
by the morphism
\mathcal{B}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathring{\pi}_{\ast}\mathcal{C}_{M}

we thus obtain a section
v\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(\mathring{\pi}^{-1}(U))
of
\mathcal{C}_{M}
on

\mathring{\pi}^{-1}(U)\subset\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X
.
Its support is nothing but the analytic wave front set

{\rm WF}(u)
of
u\in\mathcal{B}_{M}(U)
.
Then by the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{M}(U)\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{M}(U)\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{M}(\mathring{\pi}^{-1}(U))

we see that
u\in\mathcal{A}_{M}(U)\iff{\rm WF}(u)=\emptyset
. This
shows that the microfunction
v\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(\mathring{\pi}^{-1}(U))
can be
regarded as the “singularity” of
u\in\mathcal{B}_{M}(U)
.
In
[
177
]
the authors also introduced the sheaf
\mathcal{E}_{X}
of
(non-commutative) rings of microdifferential operators on
the complex cotangent bundle

T^{\ast}X
of
X
such that for a coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
we have

\ch\mathcal{M}=\supp\left(\mathcal{E}_{X}\otimes_{\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}}\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{M}\right).

Moreover they showed that its restriction
\mathcal{E}_{X}|_{\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X}

to
\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X\subset T^{\ast}X
acts on the sheaf

\mathcal{C}_{M}
of microfunctions.
For a coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}

we define a coherent
\mathcal{E}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}
on
T^{\ast}X
by

\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}\coloneq\mathcal{E}_{X}\otimes_{\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}}\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{M}.

Then we obtain an isomorphism

\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{E}_{X}}(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M},\mathcal{C}_{M})\simeq\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{M},\mathcal{C}_{M}),

where the restrictions
(\,\cdot\,)|_{\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X}
to

\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X\subset T^{\ast}X
are omitted.
Hence, if the coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
is elliptic i.e.
if it satisfies the condition

\supp(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M})\cap\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X=\ch\mathcal{M}\cap\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X=\varnothing

we can easily see that there exists an isomorphism

\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}|_{M}}(\mathcal{M}|_{M},\mathcal{A}_{M})\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{D}_{X}|_{M}}(\mathcal{M}|_{M},\mathcal{B}_{M}).

Namely, in this case, all the hyperfunction solutions to
\mathcal{M}
are real analytic.
Without the sheaf
\mathcal{C}_{M}
, it would be impossible to prove
such a very general regularity theorem for systems of linear PDEs.
We call it Sato’s fundamental theorem.
More generally, if a hyperfunction
u\in\mathcal{B}_{M}(U)
on an open
subset
U\subset M
is a solution of a coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
, its
analytic wave front set
{\rm WF}(u)
is contained
in the set
\ch\mathcal{M}\cap\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X
.
Then the next task of the authors of
[
177
]
was
to study the structures of the microfunction solutions

\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\mathcal{E}_{X}}(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M},\mathcal{C}_{M})\simeq\mathrm{R}{\mathcal{H}}om_{\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{X}}(\pi_{X}^{-1}\mathcal{M},\mathcal{C}_{M})

of general coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules
\mathcal{M}
.
First in
[
177
]
, they developed a theory of quantized contact transformations
of coherent
\mathcal{E}_{X}
-modules i.e.
microdifferential systems and proved that by a (locally defined)
homogeneous symplectic transform of
T^{\ast}X

(and microdifferential operators of infinite order)
the coherent
\mathcal{E}_{X}
-module

\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}
can be locally transformed to a partial de Rham system
\partial_{x_{1}}u=\cdots=\partial_{x_{d}}u=0

in general.
Furthermore, they showed that by a (locally defined)
“real” homogeneous symplectic transform
of
T^{\ast}X
preserving
T_{M}^{\ast}X\subset T^{\ast}X
,

\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}
is locally
transformed to a mixture of partial de Rham, partial Cauchy-Riemann
and Lewy-Mizohata systems in general.
It is surprising that we have such a general structure theorem
for generic microdifferential systems.
From this, we see in particular that
if
V\coloneq\ch\mathcal{M}\cap\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X\subset\mathring{T}_{M}^{\ast}X

is an involutive submanifold of
T_{M}^{\ast}X\simeq\sqrt{-1}T^{\ast}M

and
\mathcal{M}
has real simple characteristic along
V
at a point of
V

then the microfunction solutions
of
\mathcal{M}
are constant along the bicharacteristic leaves of
V
,
which means that the singularities of the solutions of
\mathcal{M}
propagate along them.
In the special case where
\mathcal{M}
is the classical wave equation,
this gives the first mathematical
explanation to the particle nature of waves,
which is a traditional guiding principle in physics.
Note that inspired by Sato’s ideas, in
[
72
]
and
[
48
]

Hörmander introduced the wave front sets of distributions
and developed a theory of Fourier integral operators, which is
parallel to the one of QCT of SKK, in collaboration with Duistermaat.

4
Microlocal study of sheaves

After the microlocal study of hyperbolic
\mathcal{D}
-modules in
[
98
]
,
Kashiwara and Schapira gradually noticed that in order to obtain
the main results on the microfunction and hyperfunction solutions
to a coherent
\mathcal{D}
-module the only thing that they have to keep in mind
is the set of the codirections (in the cotangent bundle) in which
the cohomology groups of its solution complex do not propagate.
First, by abstracting the notion of characteristic varieties
of coherent
\mathcal{D}
-modules,
for any bounded complex of sheaves
F
on a manifold they succeeded in defining
a closed conic subset in its cotangent bundle and called it the micro-support
of
F
. Note that this definition was motivated also by
the previous works by Bony-Schapira
[
16
]

and Zerner
[
201
]
.
Then in
[
99
]
and
[
100
]
, they studied the functorial properties of the
micro-supports of sheaves on manifolds and developed the whole theory in a short period.
It turned out that this theory is extremely useful in many branches of contemporary
mathematics.
In this section, without getting into the details of the theory, we shall
introduce the main results in
[
99
]
and
[
100
]
and explain related results.
For the sake of simplicity,
in this section we assume that
\mathbf{k}
is a commutative field
of characteristic zero and for a (real and
C^{\infty}
) manifold
X

we denote by
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
the derived category of bounded complexes of
sheaves of
\mathbf{k}_{X}
-modules on it.
Let
\pi_{X}\colon T^{\ast}X\longrightarrow X
be the projection.

Definition 4.1
.

For an object
F\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
we define a subset
\MS(F)\subset T^{\ast}X

by:

For a point
p\in T^{\ast}X
we have

\displaystyle p\notin\MS(F)\iff\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{There exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $p$ in $T^{\ast}X$ such that}\\
\text{for any point $x\in\pi_{X}(U)\subset X$ and any $C^{\infty}$-function
$\phi$ defined}\\
\text{on a neighborhood of $x$
in $X$ satisfying the conditions}\\
\begin{aligned} \phi(x)=0,\quad\mathrm{d}\phi(x)\in U\end{aligned}\\
\text{we have the vanishing}\\
\begin{aligned} \mathrm{R}\Gamma_{\{\phi\geq 0\}}(F)_{x}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}0.\end{aligned}\end{array}\right.

We call
\MS(F)\subset T^{\ast}X
the micro-support of
F
.

By this definition, it is clear that
\MS(F)\subset T^{\ast}X
is a closed subset
of
T^{\ast}X
and conic i.e. invariant by the standard action of the multiplicative
group
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\coloneq\{t\in\mathbb{R}\mid t>0\}
on
T^{\ast}X
.
For a closed subset
Z\subset X
of a manifold
X
and the inclusion map

i_{Z}\colon Z\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X
we denote

\mathrm{R}i_{Z}{}_{\ast}(\mathbf{k}_{Z})\simeq i_{Z}{}_{\ast}(\mathbf{k}_{Z})\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)

simply by
\mathbf{k}_{Z}\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
.

Example 4.2
.

For the constant sheaf
\mathbf{k}_{X}\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
on
X
,
its micro-support
\MS(\mathbf{k}_{X})\subset T^{\ast}X
is equal to
the zero section
T_{X}^{\ast}X\simeq X\subset T^{\ast}X
of
T^{\ast}X
.
More generally, for a closed submanifold
M\subset X
of
X
and the sheaf

\mathbf{k}_{M}\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
on
X
associated to it, its micro-support

\MS(\mathbf{k}_{M})\subset T^{\ast}X
is equal to the conormal bundle

T_{M}^{\ast}X\subset T^{\ast}X
of
M
in
X
.

With micro-supports of sheaves at hand, we can extend various results in
Morse theory.
For instance, together with Kashiwara’s
non-characteristic deformation lemma (see
[
100
, Proposition 2.7.2]
),
for
F\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
and two open subsets
U,V\subset X
in
X
such that
V\subset U
,
under some suitable conditions on
\MS(F)\subset T^{\ast}X
we obtain an
isomorphism

{\mathrm{R}}\Gamma(U;F)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{R}}\Gamma(V,F).

Note that in the theory of
stratified Morse theory developed by Goresky and MacPherson

[
61
]
similar results were obtained for complex constructible sheaves.
The micro-supports of sheaves enjoy several nice functorial properties
with respect to morphisms of manifolds.
For instance, if
f\colon Y\longrightarrow X
is a morphism of manifolds
and we take an object
G\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(Y)
such that
f
is proper on its support

\supp G\subset Y
, then the micro-support

\MS\left(\mathrm{R}f_{\ast}G\right)\subset T^{\ast}X
of the direct image

\mathrm{R}f_{\ast}G\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
is estimated from above as follows.
Let

T^{\ast}Y\underset{\rho_{f}}{\longleftarrow}Y\times_{X}T^{\ast}X\underset{\varpi_{f}}{\longrightarrow}T^{\ast}X

be the natural morphisms induced by
f\colon Y\longrightarrow X
.
Then we have

\MS(\mathrm{R}f_{\ast}G)\subset\varpi_{f}\rho_{f}^{-1}\left(\MS(G)\right).

The theory of micro-supports plays a powerful role also in studying morphisms
in derived categories.
Indeed, many natural morphisms in them are not isomorphisms.
But with micro-supports, we obtain conditions under which they are actually isomorphisms.
For instance, for a morphism
f\colon Y\longrightarrow X
of manifolds and
F\in\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(X)
,
Kashiwara and Schapira proved in
[
100
, Corollary 6.4.4]

that if
f
is non-characteristic for

\MS(F)\subset T^{\ast}X
(see
[
100
, Definition 6.2.7]
for the definition)
the natural morphism

f^{-1}F\otimes_{\mathbf{k}_{Y}}f^{!}\mathbf{k}_{X}\longrightarrow f^{!}F

in the derived category
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{b}}(Y)
is an isomorphism.
As we see in the theorem below, the notion of micro-supports is
a generalization of that of characteristic varieties.
If
X
is a complex manifold, then we denote by
X_{\mathbb{R}}

(resp.

manifold underlying

(resp. the complex cotangent bundle

T^{\ast}X
of

) and identify the cotangent bundle
T^{\ast}X_{\mathbb{R}}

of

with
(T^{*}X)_{\mathbb{R}}
as follows.
For a covector
\mathrm{d}\phi(x)\in T_{x}^{\ast}X_{\mathbb{R}}
at a point

x\in X_{\mathbb{R}}
defined by a real-valued
C^{\infty}
-function
\phi

on a neighborhood of
x
in
X_{\mathbb{R}}
, by the decomposition

\mathrm{d}\phi(x)=\partial\phi(x)+\bar{\partial}\phi(x)

we obtain an element
\partial\phi(x)\in T_{x}^{\ast}X
of
T_{x}^{\ast}X
.
Then we see that there exists an isomorphism
T^{\ast}X_{\mathbb{R}}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}(T^{\ast}X)_{\mathbb{R}}
(see
[
100

for a more intrinsic construction of this isomorphism)
and have the following result (see
[

, Theorem 10.1.1]
).

Theorem 4.3

(Kashiwara-Schapira
[
99
]
)

The theory of

-modules and constructible sheaves plays a prominent role

b

Let

P(s)f^{s+1}\equiv b(s)f^{s}\quad\mathrm{on}\ U\setminus f^{-1}(0)

by

b_{f}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

is a complex polynomial, the existence of

[
10

6
Irregular Riemann-Hilbert correspondence

Recall that in the classical analysis of (complex) ordinary differential equations
on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
not only regular singular points
but also irregular ones were studied precisely.
Unlike the case of regular singular points,
around an irregular singular point of a linear ordinary differential equation

Pu=0
it is almost always impossible to describe its holomorphic solutions explicitly.
Instead, on each sufficiently narrow sector along it
the asymptotic expansions of their holomorphic solutions can be calculated.
In the middle of the 19th century,
Stokes observed that the asymptotic expansion of a holomorphic solution may change
if we move the direction of the sector.
Namely, he discovered the so-called Stokes phenomenon.
In the middle of the 20th century, Hukuhara, Turrittin and others found methods
to calculate the formal structure of an ordinary differential equation
at an irregular singular point and hence the asymptotic expansions
of its holomorphic solutions.
We call it the Hukuhara-Turrittin theorem.
In order to describe the formal structure at an irregular singular point
p\in\mathbb{C}
,
we take a ramified covering (ramification)
\rho\colon\widetilde{U}\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow U

of an open neighborhood
U
of
p
in
\mathbb{C}
and consider the pull-back of
the differential equation by it (see e.g.
[
199
]
for the details).

In the early 21th century, in
[
166
]

Sabbah proposed a program
to obtain a higher-dimensional analogue of the Hukuhara-Turrittin theorem.
Let
X
be a complex manifold and
\mathcal{M}\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
a meromorphic connection on it
along a divisor
D\subset X
.
Then in
[
166
]
Sabbah conjectured that there exists a proper
morphism
f\colon Y\longrightarrow X
of complex manifolds inducing an isomorphism
Y\setminus f^{-1}(D)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}X\setminus D
such that the divisor
f^{-1}(D)\subset Y
is normal crossing
and for each point
p\in f^{-1}(D)
of it there exists a ramification

\rho\colon\widetilde{U}\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow U
of its open neighborhood
U
in
Y

along the normal crossing divisor
f^{-1}(D)\cap U\subset U
for which
the meromorphic connection

\mathrm{D}\rho^{\ast}\left((\mathrm{D}f^{\ast}\mathcal{M})|_{U}\right)\quad\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{U}})

on
\widetilde{U}

along the normal crossing divisor
\rho^{-1}(f^{-1}(D)\cap U)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}f^{-1}(D)\cap U

in
\widetilde{U}
has a normal form (in the formal sense).
This conjecture was proved by Sabbah
[
166
]
in dimension 2 i.e.
in the case
\mathrm{dim}X=2
.
After some important contributions by André in
[
2
]
,
it was finally settled by Kedlaya
[

,
[

and Mochizuki

This far-reaching result in mathematics can be considered as an analogue
for meromorphic connections of Hironaka’s celebrated theorem on the resolutions of
singularities of algebraic varieties. For its applications to

see

,
