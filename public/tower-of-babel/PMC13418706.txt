Pathogenicity and virulence of Streptococcus suis serotype 2: Emerging insights and persistent knowledge gaps
Authors: Lihua Fang, Yueru Yao, Jinmei Wu, Jie Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus suis serotype 2 is a major zoonotic pathogen causing meningitis, septicemia, streptococcal toxic shock-like syndrome (STSLS) in swine and humans. This review synthesizes discoveries over the past decade, organized around the infection progression, colonization, bloodstream survival, immune evasion, blood-brain barrier disruption, excessive inflammation culminating in STSLS. We dissect regulatory architecture including two-component systems, Ser/Thr kinase STK, small RNAs, toxin-antitoxin modules, and stringent response. We analyze functional effectors enabling niche adaptation, including capsule, pili, metabolic enzymes, metal acquisition systems, complement evasins, and suilysin. A dedicated section addresses critical controversies, including roles of CovR, phenotypes of SsnA and IdeS_suis, and dual functions of Fpr2. We evaluate translational implications, highlighting vaccine candidates and antivirulence targets. Knowledge gaps remain, including unknown targets of regulatory systems, unexplored post-translational modifications, discrepancies between mouse models and natural porcine infections. Future studies must prioritize natural-route infection models in pigs and systems-level approaches to translate discoveries into interventions.

Introduction

Streptococcus suis is a Gram-positive coccus and a major swine pathogen worldwide, causing substantial economic losses in the pork industry [1]. Among its 29 described serotypes, serotype 2 (SS2) is the most frequently isolated from diseased pigs and the predominant cause of human infections [2]. SS2 is a zoonotic agent that infects humans through direct contact with infected pigs or consumption of contaminated pork products, causing meningitis, septicemia, endocarditis, and the frequently fatal streptococcal toxic shock-like syndrome (STSLS) [3]. The case-fatality rate for STSLS exceeds 20%, and survivors often suffer from permanent neurological sequelae including hearing loss and cognitive impairment [4]. Large-scale outbreaks in China during 1998 and 2005 resulted in over 200 human cases with high mortality, establishing SS2 as an emerging zoonotic threat [5]. Sporadic cases continue to occur across Southeast Asia, with increasing reports from Europe and South America, reflecting the global expansion of this pathogen [6]. The zoonotic potential of SS2 is further underscored by the identification of epidemic sequence type 7 (ST7) strains that exhibit enhanced virulence in humans compared to the classical ST1 strains prevalent in pigs [7].

The pathogenesis of SS2 is multifactorial, involving a complex array of virulence factors that enable the bacterium to adhere to host epithelial surfaces, survive in blood and other hostile environments, evade innate and adaptive immunity, cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and trigger excessive inflammatory responses [8]. Early research identified classical virulence markers including the capsular polysaccharide (CPS) [9], suilysin (SLY) [10], muramidase-released protein (MRP) [11], and extracellular protein factor (EF) [12]. However, genomic and proteomic advances over the past decade have dramatically expanded the known virulence repertoire to more than 200 genes and proteins, ranging from global regulators to moonlighting metabolic enzymes [13]. Yet this expansion has not been matched by mechanistic depth: many studies rely on mouse models using intraperitoneal injection of unnaturally high bacterial doses, which may not faithfully recapitulate infection in the natural porcine host. The direct molecular targets of most two-component systems [14], serine/threonine kinases [15], and small non-coding RNAs [16] remain unknown. Post-translational modifications, particularly phosphorylation and ubiquitination, are largely unexplored at the systems level. Moreover, conflicting results from different laboratories highlight the influence of strain background and experimental model on virulence phenotypes, a problem that undermines the field’s translational credibility.

This review provides a critical synthesis of recent advances in SS2 virulence research, with emphasis on studies published over the past decade (Table 1). We organize the review around the central theme of niche-specific adaptation, following the natural infection progression from nasopharyngeal colonization through systemic dissemination to central nervous system invasion. For each stage, we synthesize key findings, identify under-investigated areas, and address critical controversies that require resolution. Nasopharyngeal colonization and adhesion

Adhesion to host epithelial surfaces of the upper respiratory tract (nasopharynx and tonsils) is the initial and critical step in SS2 pathogenesis. The bacterium must attach to and colonize these surfaces before disseminating systemically. Recent advances have identified multiple adhesins and colonization factors, including fimbria-like structures, LPXTG-anchored proteins, and moonlighting metabolic enzymes. Importantly, the capsular polysaccharide (CPS), which is essential for systemic survival, is downregulated during colonization to expose underlying adhesins (Figure 1). Pili and fimbria-like structures

Pili are long, filamentous surface structures that mediate adhesion to host cells. In SS2, two distinct pilus clusters have been characterized: the SrtBCD pilus cluster (encoding SBP1–SBP4 and class C sortases SrtB, SrtC, SrtD) and the SecY2/A2-secreted fimbria-like protein SssP1. SssP1 is a 4,647-amino acid serine-rich repeat glycoprotein identified in the novel serotype Chz strain CZ130302. It is transported by the SecY2/A2 secretion system and assembles into fimbria-like structures extending from the bacterial surface [17]. Deletion of sssP1 or disruption of the SecY2/A2 system severely attenuated virulence in mice (LD50 increased from 2.89 × 106 to > 5 × 108 CFU). The ΔsssP1 mutant showed dramatically reduced adherence to HEp-2 cells and human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs). Mechanistically, SssP1 contains two Ig-like subdomains that bind to sialic acid on host cells. Subsequently, the same group showed that SssP1 binds directly to host vimentin, and this interaction promotes bacterial penetration of the blood-brain barrier [18]. A critical limitation is that a complementation strain could not be constructed due to the large size of sssP1; thus, the ΔsssP1 phenotypes were only partially validated using a point mutation strain (SssP1/T182A). Whether SssP1 is the only effector of the SecY2/A2 system contributing to virulence remains unclear, an ambiguity that weakens the causal inference.

The SrtBCD pilus cluster contains four putative pilin genes (sbp1–sbp4) and three class C sortase genes (srtB, srtC, srtD). Using comparative proteomics between an attenuated mutant (ΔpepT) and a hypervirulent mutant (ΔrfeA), SBP2 (also called SBP2') was identified as a novel virulence-associated factor [12]. Deletion of sbp2 significantly reduced adherence to HEp-2 cells, and recombinant SBP2 bound to fibronectin and laminin. PCR analysis of 25 SS2 strains revealed that sbp2 was present in all highly virulent strains but absent in low-virulence strains, suggesting it is a superior virulence marker compared to classical markers (mrp, ef, sly). This claim, however, rests on a small sample size and lacks prospective validation. In contrast, SBP1 (another pilin in the same cluster) was found to be dispensable for systemic virulence [19]. Although Δsbp1 showed reduced adherence to HEp-2 cells and recombinant SBP1 bound fibronectin and laminin, the mutant exhibited no defects in invasion, phagocytosis resistance, or virulence in zebrafish and mouse models. Moreover, flow cytometry failed to detect SBP1 on the bacterial surface, suggesting low surface expression or poor accessibility, a reminder that binding assays do not necessarily predict virulence phenotypes.

Class C sortases (SrtB, SrtC, SrtD) are responsible for polymerizing and anchoring pilus subunits. In a mouse model, deletion of all three sortases (ΔsrtB-D) significantly reduced virulence (50% survival vs. 0% for wild-type), whereas single gene deletions had no effect, indicating functional redundancy [20]. This redundancy is not merely a biochemical curiosity; it has profound translational implications: single-sortase vaccines may be ineffective. Importantly, a study using the natural host (pig) and the natural route of infection (intranasal) found that both ΔsrtB and ΔsrtF mutants were completely avirulent in pigs, whereas in Galleria mellonella larvae the same mutants showed no attenuation [21]. This stark discrepancy underscores the critical importance of using appropriate infection models, a theme that recurs throughout this review. In pigs, the ΔsrtB and ΔsrtF mutants colonized tonsils at reduced levels and were cleared by day 15, with no clinical signs or systemic infection. Western blot confirmed that SrtF is required for Sfp1 polymerization, while SrtA (the housekeeping sortase) anchors Sfp1 to the cell wall. The specific substrates sorted by SrtB and SrtF that mediate virulence have not been identified.

Adhesins targeting host extracellular matrix and cell surface receptors

A diverse array of surface-associated and secreted proteins mediate SS2 adhesion to host cells by binding to extracellular matrix components (fibronectin, fibrinogen, collagen, laminin) or cell surface receptors (E-cadherin, integrins, glycolipids). SadP (streptococcal adhesin P) is a surface-anchored protein that binds to galabiose (Galα1-4 Gal)-containing glycolipids. Two major subtypes exist, SadP_PN (associated with systemic isolates) and SadP_R (associated with respiratory isolates). The molecular basis for this subtype specificity was resolved by structural biology [22]. The N-terminal galabiose-binding domain of SadP_PN contains a single amino acid residue (Asn285) that mediates specific binding to globotetraosylceramide (Gb4). Mutation of Asn285 to Asp (N285D) switched the binding preference to globotriaosylceramide (Gb3). Isothermal titration calorimetry and molecular dynamics simulations confirmed this switch. A separate study showed that SadP1 (present in zoonotic clonal complex 1 strains) binds to the Gb3/CD77 receptor on human intestinal epithelial cells (Caco-2) and porcine IPEC-J2 cells, and deletion of sadP1 reduced adhesion and translocation across polarized Caco-2 monolayers [23]. This supports the role of the gastrointestinal tract as an entry site for zoonotic infection. The virulence of SadP mutants has not been directly assessed in animal models, a significant gap given its proposed role in systemic dissemination.

FBPS is an anchorless protein that lacks an LPXTG motif but is retained on the bacterial surface. Structural studies determined the crystal structures of the N-terminal (FBPS-N) and C-terminal (FBPS-C) halves and elucidated the overall architecture using small-angle X-ray scattering [24]. FBPS-N mediates attachment to the bacterial surface, while FBPS-C binds to fibronectin and host cells. The C-terminal half interacts with the N-terminal 30-kDa fragment of fibronectin with a Kd of 370 nM (SPR). Functionally, FBPS promotes adherence to HEp-2 cells and contributes to IL-6 and IL-8 production via β1 integrin-dependent activation of ERK and p38 MAPK pathways. The virulence of a Δfbps mutant has not been directly assessed in an animal model, limiting conclusions about its contribution to pathogenesis.

Enolase (Eno) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) are classical glycolytic enzymes that are also found on the bacterial surface, where they function as adhesins and plasminogen receptors. Their roles in BBB disruption are discussed in Enolase as a moonlighting invasin. Their surface localization is regulated by STK/Stp1 [25] and the chaperone PrsA [26]. The regulatory logic governing their surface translocation remains poorly understood, representing a key knowledge gap.

The type II histidine triad protein HtpsC (SSU05-1577) was identified as an invasin that mediates bacterial entry into epithelial cells through interaction with the host receptor E-cadherin [27]. In human (Hep-2, A549, Caco-2) and porcine (IPEC-J2) epithelial cells, anti-E-cadherin antibodies significantly reduced wild-type invasion but had no effect on the ΔhtpsC mutant. Direct biochemical evidence for HtpsC-E-cadherin binding is lacking, and the virulence of the ΔhtpsC mutant has not been evaluated in an animal model.

Cba is a collagen-binding adhesin identified in SS9 strain SS9-P10. Deletion of cba reduced biofilm formation, adhesion to PK-15 cells, and resistance to phagocytosis, and attenuated virulence in mice (25% mortality vs. 100% for wild-type) [28]. However, immunization with recombinant Cba protein induced a strong antibody response but unexpectedly caused antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), leading to higher mortality and more severe organ lesions upon challenge. This finding raises a cautionary note for vaccine development targeting certain adhesins, antibody titers do not guarantee protection and may paradoxically enhance disease.

Using a proteomics-based screening approach, HP0487 was identified as a surface protein that binds to neutrophils [29]. Deletion of hp0487 significantly reduced adhesion to mouse, porcine, and human neutrophils, increased susceptibility to phagocytosis by macrophages and neutrophils, and attenuated virulence in mice (100% survival vs. 0% for wild-type at 2 × 108 CFU). The specific ligand or receptor on neutrophils that HP0487 binds to has not been identified, leaving the mechanism unresolved.

Most adhesion studies were performed using immortalized cell lines rather than primary porcine respiratory epithelial cells, and the natural route of infection is rarely used in mouse models. Future studies should prioritize primary porcine cells and intranasal infection models to validate adhesin functions in the natural host.

Autolysins and cell division proteins with adhesive functions

Autolysins are peptidoglycan hydrolases that cleave bonds in the cell wall, enabling cell separation, remodeling, and turnover. Several autolysins have been implicated in SS2 virulence. AtlAss contains a GW cell wall-anchoring domain and an N-acetylmuramidase domain. In the hypervirulent strain CZ130302 (which naturally exhibits very short chains), deletion of atlAss dramatically increased chain length, reduced autolysis, decreased biofilm formation, and attenuated virulence in mice (LD50 increased from 2.89 × 106 to 1.13 × 107 CFU) [30]. Immunofluorescence and immunogold EM revealed that AtlAss localizes predominantly at the division septum. AtlAss also bound directly to fibrinogen and fibronectin (far-western blotting). The specific catalytic residues and the mechanism by which AtlAss binds to host matrix proteins are unknown. The autolysin Atl (encoded by atl) was shown to bind directly to host vimentin via its GBS Bsp-like domain [31]. This interaction induces redistribution of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1) via MLCK-dependent phosphorylation of myosin light chain, leading to increased tracheal epithelial permeability. In vimentin knockout mice, bacterial loads in blood, lung, brain, spleen, and liver were significantly lower after intranasal infection, confirming that vimentin is required for systemic dissemination. Whether vimentin targeting is a viable therapeutic strategy remains speculative given the lack of pharmacological validation.

LytR is a member of the LCP (LytR-CpsA-Psr) family involved in cell wall polymer attachment. Deletion of lytR resulted in aberrant septum placement (asymmetric septa) and reduced survival in whole blood and PMN killing assays. The ΔlytR mutant was attenuated in mice [32]. RNA-seq revealed downregulation of pyrimidine biosynthesis genes and TCSs. MapZ is a homolog of the pneumococcal cell division protein; in SS2 strain 10, mapZ was found to be conditionally essential for survival in pig blood. Deletion of mapZ resulted in irregular cell shape, mispositioned septa, and formation of minicells and cell clusters [33]. The conditional essentiality of MapZ in pig blood but not in standard laboratory media suggests that cell division requirements differ between host environments, a nuance often overlooked in in vitro studies.

Capsular polysaccharide dynamics: A trade-off between colonization and immune evasion

The CPS is a major virulence determinant that protects SS2 from phagocytosis and complement killing. However, a thick capsule masks surface adhesins, reducing the bacterium’s ability to attach to host cells. Recent studies have revealed that SS2 dynamically regulates CPS thickness in a niche-specific manner. A landmark study provided direct in vivo evidence that SS2 isolates from the nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of infected mice have a thinner CPS layer, which facilitates adherence to respiratory epithelial cells and rapid transmigration into the CNS [34]. In contrast, bacteria isolated from the bloodstream (5 h post-infection) displayed a significantly thicker capsule, enhancing resistance to blood clearance. Passive antibody transfer experiments showed that anti-CPS antibodies restricted bacterial survival in the blood but had no impact on early nasal colonization or CNS invasion, whereas antibodies against non-CPS virulence factors blocked bacterial translocation from the nasal cavity to the CNS. These findings challenge the assumption that CPS is a static virulence factor and suggest that anti-CPS vaccines may fail to prevent initial colonization.

Systematic deletion of four glycosyltransferase genes (cps2E, cps2G, cps2J, cps2L) in strain SC19 revealed that each contributes to CPS structural integrity [35]. Δcps2E resulted in near-complete loss of the capsule, while deletions of cps2G, cps2J, and cps2L led to a thinner or incomplete capsule. All mutants showed enhanced adhesion to and invasion of Hep-2 cells, increased susceptibility to phagocytosis by porcine neutrophils and alveolar macrophages, and increased complement C3b deposition. The Δcps2E mutant was rapidly cleared from the bloodstream in a mouse model. The tyrosine kinase Cps2C directly phosphorylates key glycosyltransferases (Cps2F, Cps2H), linking this regulatory kinase to the capsule biosynthesis pathway [36]. Deletion of cps2C resulted in reduced CPS thickness, enhanced adherence to epithelial cells, decreased resistance to phagocytosis, and severe attenuation in mice. The discovery of Cps2C as a CPS regulator is significant, but the signals triggering its kinase activity remain unknown, an important gap for therapeutic targeting.

By swapping the entire cps gene cluster of a serotype 2 strain with those from other clinically relevant serotypes (3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 14), a landmark study demonstrated that the composition of the CPS itself is a key determinant of virulence [37]. In a mouse model, the serotype 8-switched mutant (SS2 to 8) was hypervirulent (100% mortality, cytokine storm), while the serotype 4-switched mutant (SS2 to 4) was avirulent. These differences correlated with resistance to phagocytosis and induction of pro-inflammatory responses in dendritic cells. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a small number of mutations outside the cps locus in the switched mutants, which could have contributed to the observed phenotypic differences. This caveat is important: serotype switching may introduce compensatory mutations that confound virulence attribution.

The host pentraxin PTX3 was shown to enhance the inflammatory response and bacterial clearance in a CPS-dependent manner [38]. Exogenous PTX3 enhanced IL-6 production, neutrophil recruitment, and macrophage-mediated bacterial clearance only in the presence of bacterial CPS. However, excessive PTX3 led to over-exuberant inflammation and increased mortality, highlighting the delicate balance required for immunomodulation. The therapeutic window for PTX3-based interventions appears narrow, and the lack of pig model data limits translational optimism.

Bloodstream survival and stress resistance

Once SS2 penetrates the mucosal barrier and enters the bloodstream, it must withstand a barrage of hostile conditions: oxidative stress from reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced by phagocytes, metal ion limitation imposed by host nutritional immunity, acidic pH in phagolysosomes, and heat stress during fever. To survive, SS2 deploys an array of stress response systems and metabolic adaptations. Remarkably, many classical metabolic enzymes have been co-opted as moonlighting virulence factors, localizing to the bacterial surface or being secreted to interact with host components (Figure 2). Oxidative stress defense systems

Superoxide dismutase and NADH oxidase

SodA is a manganese-dependent superoxide dismutase that converts superoxide to H2O2 and O2−. Deletion of sodA resulted in slower growth, increased susceptibility to phagocytosis, and attenuated virulence in mice (20% mortality vs. 100% for wild-type) [39]. Mechanistically, ΔsodA induced less activation of the caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptosis pathway in RAW264.7 macrophages, with reduced levels of GSDMD-N, cleaved caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18, suggesting that SodA promotes virulence not only by scavenging ROS but also by modulating the host inflammatory response. Earlier work showed that SodA also suppresses autophagy in macrophages by scavenging ROS [40]. Most recently, SodA was shown to promote evasion of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs); the ΔsodA mutant induced significantly more NETs than wild-type [41]. The dual role of SodA in ROS scavenging and inflammasome modulation suggests that antioxidant therapies may have unintended pro-inflammatory consequences.

Nox (NADH oxidase) is a flavoprotein that reduces O2 to H2O2 or H2O, regenerating NAD+ and maintaining the NAD+/NADH balance under aerobic conditions. Deletion of nox in strain SC19 resulted in severe growth impairment under high oxygen and in the presence of H2O2 or SIN-1 [42]. In a mouse intraperitoneal infection model, Δnox caused 0% mortality vs 80% for wild-type, and bacterial loads were undetectable. Strikingly, in a pig intravenous infection model (natural host), Δnox caused no clinical signs and all pigs survived, while wild-type caused severe symptoms and 80–100% mortality. Point mutations that abolished enzymatic activity only partially restored virulence, suggesting additional functions beyond catalysis. The partial restoration of virulence by catalytically inactive Nox raises the possibility of moonlighting functions, an important consideration for target-based drug design that assumes enzymatic inhibition is sufficient.

Thioredoxins and glutathione systems

Thioredoxins TrxA and TrxC are small redox proteins. ΔtrxA showed slower growth, reduced tolerance to high temperature, and reduced adhesion to IPEC-J2 cells; it also increased NO production and upregulated pentraxin 3 (PTX3) expression, and knockdown of PTX3 increased phagocytosis, indicating that TrxA promotes survival by inhibiting PTX3 [43]. ΔtrxC caused increased sensitivity to H2O2 and paraquat, induced higher NO production and upregulated autophagy-related genes via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, and upregulated MSR1 (macrophage scavenger receptor 1); MSR1 knockout reduced phagocytosis and increased bacterial survival [44]. Glutathione (GSH) is a major cellular antioxidant. SS2 can synthesize GSH via GshAB and import it via the ABC transporter GshT. Deletion of gshAB or gshT reduced intracellular GSH levels, increased sensitivity to H2O2, and attenuated virulence in mice [45]. The double deletion mutant could not be constructed, suggesting synthetic lethality. The synthetic lethality of the gshAB-gshT double mutant suggests that antioxidant pathways are non-redundant under infection conditions, but this has not been tested in the natural host.

Peroxide sensing and manganese acquisition

PerR is a peroxide-sensing transcriptional repressor. In SS2, manganese supplementation increased H2O2 tolerance, and RNA-seq identified the troABCD operon [46] (encoding an ABC manganese transporter) as downregulated by Mn2+. EMSA showed that PerR binds directly to the troABCD promoter. Deletion of troA, troB, troC, or troD reduced intracellular manganese content, impaired growth in Mn-depleted medium, and reduced H2O2 survival. ΔperR increased H2O2 survival 2.18-fold and elevated intracellular Mn content. The virulence of these mutants was not assessed in animal models, a recurring omission that limits mechanistic conclusions. Aagp is an atypical aquaglyceroporin that facilitates H2O2 efflux. Deletion of aagp in serotype 9 strain GZ0565 resulted in 50-fold higher intracellular H2O2 accumulation and severely reduced survival under oxidative stress [47]. Δaagp also showed impaired growth in the presence of glycerol, suggesting it may also transport glycerol. In a mouse model, Δaagp had significantly reduced bacterial loads in blood and multiple organs. The cadDX operon encodes a cadmium efflux system. Deletion of cadDX [48] in strain GZ0565 reduced survival under H2O2 and decreased resistance to Cd2+. Transcriptome analysis revealed that CadDX affects expression of genes involved in fatty acid biosynthesis, PTS transport systems, and FeoA. In a mouse model, ΔcadDX had reduced bacterial loads in blood and organs. Transferring cadDX into S. agalactiae conferred cadmium resistance, oxidative stress resistance, and increased virulence, demonstrating horizontal transfer potential. The horizontal transfer potential of cadDX raises concerns about the spread of resistance determinants among streptococci, though its clinical relevance remains speculative.

Metal ion homeostasis as a virulence determinant

Manganese acquisition

Manganese is critical for oxidative stress defense (as a cofactor for SodA). The TroABCD system is an ABC transporter for Mn2+ uptake. Deletion of troA, troB, troC, or troD resulted in reduced intracellular Mn, increased sensitivity to paraquat and calprotectin (a host Mn-chelating protein), and attenuated virulence in mice (100% survival vs 43% for wild-type) [49]. EMSA confirmed that the repressor TroR binds directly to the troABCD promoter in a Mn2+ -dependent manner. The MntE protein is a Mn2+ -specific efflux pump; deletion of mntE increased sensitivity to Mn2+ toxicity and to diamide-induced oxidative stress, and reduced colonization in a mouse competitive-infection model [50]. An elegant study on the interplay between copper and manganese homeostasis showed that inactivation of the TroABCD system protects against copper toxicity in a ΔcopA background [51]. The cross-talk between manganese and copper homeostasis is intriguing but has not been explored in the natural host, where metal availability may differ substantially from laboratory media.

Zinc acquisition

Zinc is essential for many enzymes but is tightly controlled by host calprotectin. The AdcA and AdcAII lipoproteins are zinc-binding proteins regulated by the AdcR repressor. Deletion of both adcA and adcAII (but not single deletions) impaired growth under zinc-deficient conditions, increased sensitivity to manganese, and attenuated virulence in mice (100% survival vs 40–60% for wild-type) [52]. EMSA confirmed AdcR binding to adcA and adcAII promoters. Another study showed that Lmb (laminin-binding protein, also called AdcAII) functions primarily as a zinc acquisition factor rather than a laminin-binding adhesin [53]. The Δlmb mutant showed impaired growth in zinc-restricted conditions, reduced cytokine activation, and attenuated virulence in mice (80% survival vs 40% for wild-type), but did not contribute to laminin binding or adhesion. A third study demonstrated that the double mutant ΔadcAΔlmb exhibited cellular damage under zinc-restricted conditions, reduced biofilm formation, increased sensitivity to cell wall-targeting antibiotics, and complete attenuation in mice (100% survival vs. 20% for wild-type) [54]. The functional overlap between AdcA and AdcAII/Lmb suggests that dual-targeting approaches may be required to disrupt zinc acquisition, but no such inhibitors have been described.

Copper and iron homeostasis

Copper is toxic in excess. CopA is a P1B-type ATPase that exports copper. ΔcopA showed severe growth defects in the presence of copper and accumulated significantly higher intracellular copper, but did not affect virulence in a mouse model [55]. PmtA is a P1B4-type ATPase that functions as an Fe(II) and Co(II) efflux pump. ΔpmtA showed growth inhibition under excess Fe(II) or Co(II) and accumulated higher intracellular iron and cobalt, but showed no significant difference in mouse survival compared to wild-type [56]. For iron/heme acquisition, SntA is a cell wall-anchored heme-binding protein. Deletion of sntA reduced growth in iron-restricted medium, and this defect was rescued by hemin supplementation [57]. Recombinant SntA bound heme in a 1:1 stoichiometry and interacted with host antioxidant protein AOP2 (peroxiredoxin 6), promoting oxidation of hemoglobin to methemoglobin. The lack of virulence defects for copper and iron efflux mutants in mouse models suggests functional redundancy or model-specific limitations, but this has not been tested in pigs where iron sequestration may be more stringent.

Nutrient transporters with moonlighting virulence roles

SS2 must acquire essential nutrients from the host environment. Several ABC transporters and their substrate-binding proteins have been linked to virulence. PnuC is a putative nicotinamide mononucleotide transporter present only in virulent SS2 strains; deletion of pnuC impaired growth under high oxygen and increased H2O2 sensitivity, and was significantly attenuated in zebrafish and mouse models [58]. MetQ is the substrate-binding protein of a methionine ABC transporter; ΔmetQ showed impaired growth under methionine-restricted conditions, reduced intracellular survival, increased H2O2 sensitivity, enhanced biofilm formation, and reduced dissemination to internal organs in a mouse intranasal model [59]. PotD is the substrate-binding protein of a polyamine ABC transporter; deletion of potD reduced arcA expression and attenuated SS2-induced downregulation of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin in brain endothelial cells, and the ΔpotD mutant showed reduced biofilm formation, reduced bacterial loads, and increased mouse survival (80% vs. 0% for wild-type) [60]. TrpX is the substrate-binding protein of a tryptophan ABC transporter, regulated by a T-box riboswitch [61]; its virulence has not been assessed. UgpE is a component of a glycerol phosphate ABC transporter; deletion resulted in a thinner capsule, shortened chains, increased adhesion to hCMEC/D3 cells, reduced phagocytosis resistance, and attenuated virulence [62]. MsmK is an ATPase that powers multiple carbohydrate ABC transporters; deletion resulted in longer chains, reduced phagocytosis resistance, paradoxically increased adhesion, and attenuated virulence [63]. ShgH is a substrate-binding protein that binds histidine and glutamine; deletion impaired growth under histidine or glutamine-limiting conditions, reduced capsule production, and caused a defect in systemic dissemination in a mouse intranasal model [64]. The functional overlap between transporters and the lack of pig model validation limit current translational potential.

Metabolic enzymes repurposed as virulence factors

A growing paradigm in bacterial pathogenesis is the moonlighting function of metabolic enzymes that localize to the bacterial surface or are secreted to interact with host components. SS2 exemplifies this paradigm with multiple glycolytic and metabolic enzymes serving dual roles in virulence.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) is a multienzyme complex that converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA. Deletion of pdh resulted in reduced resistance to heat, osmotic, and oxidative stress, reduced biofilm formation, reduced adhesion, and increased susceptibility to phagocytosis, with a 17.26-fold increase in LD50 [65]. A companion study showed that PDH interacts directly with the cell division protein FtsZ and regulates expression of multiple division-related genes [66]. Pyruvate formate lyase (PflB) is a key enzyme in mixed-acid fermentation under anaerobic conditions. Deletion of pflB impaired growth when galactose was the sole carbon source under anaerobiosis, affected CPS content in a carbon-source-dependent manner, and attenuated virulence in both intravenous and intranasal mouse models [67] Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) is essential for growth and virulence. Deletion of gdh in strain 1933 resulted in reduced cell size, slower growth, and increased sensitivity to heat, acid, alkaline, and high salinity [68]. In a pig intraperitoneal infection model (natural host), the Δgdh mutant was completely avirulent: all 10 pigs survived with no clinical signs or lesions, whereas all 10 pigs inoculated with wild-type died within 24–48 h. The complete avirulence of Δgdh in pigs is striking, but the mechanism linking glutamate metabolism to virulence remains unclear, complicating its development as a live vaccine.

Ornithine carbamoyltransferase (OTC) is a key enzyme of the arginine deiminase system (ADS). Deletion of otc reduced biofilm formation, reduced adhesion to HEp-2 cells (56% reduction), downregulated several adhesion-related genes, and increased the LD50 10-fold in mice [69]. Formate-tetrahydrofolate ligase (Fhs) is involved in one-carbon metabolism. Deletion of fhs had no effect on in vitro growth but dramatically attenuated virulence in mice (12.5% mortality vs. 87.5% for wild-type) and in a piglet competitive infection model (competitive index < 1 for all tissues) [70]. O-acetyl-homoserine sulfhydrylase (OAHS) is involved in methionine biosynthesis. Deletion of oahs resulted in shortened chains, reduced survival in whole blood and PMNs, reduced BBB disruption, and complete avirulence in mice (0% mortality vs 100% for wild-type) [71]. Notably, enolase expression was significantly reduced in Δoahs. Immunization with Δoahs provided 83.3% protection against homologous challenge and against another serotype 2 strain, making OAHS a promising vaccine candidate. FabG is a fatty acid biosynthesis enzyme (3-oxoacyl-ACP reductase). A deletion mutant could not be constructed due to essentiality, so recombinant protein and antibody inhibition approaches were used [72]. FabG was found on the bacterial surface, bound to host epithelial cells and plasminogen, and recruited plasminogen to the bacterial surface, leading to degradation of extracellular matrix components via plasmin activation. FabG also bound complement C3 and enhanced serum survival. Enolase (Eno) and GAPDH, as described in Adhesins targeting host extracellular matrix and cell surface receptors, are additional examples of moonlighting enzymes with roles in adhesion and BBB disruption (Enolase as a moonlighting invasin). The mechanisms underlying their surface localization and the relative contributions of enzymatic versus non-enzymatic functions to virulence remain poorly understood, representing a fundamental gap in the field.

Acid and heat stress adaptation mechanisms

Arginine deiminase system

The arginine deiminase system (ADS, encoded by arcABC) converts arginine to ornithine, producing ammonia and ATP, thereby raising intracellular pH under acidic conditions. ADS is regulated by multiple layers: the oxygen-sensitive activator FlpS [73], the XtrSs repressor [74], and the FadR activator phosphorylated by STK [75]. Deletion of ADS components attenuates virulence, while deletion of the repressor xtrSs causes hypervirulence due to ADS derepression [74]. The multi-layered regulation of ADS suggests evolutionary pressure for precise control, but the environmental signals triggering each layer remain poorly defined.

Heat shock proteases

Heat stress (fever) induces the expression of FtsH and ClpP proteases via relief of CtsR-mediated repression [76]. Deletion of ftsH or clpP impaired growth at 42°C, increased sensitivity to H2O2 and acidic pH, reduced survival in macrophages, and attenuated virulence in mice (20–40% mortality vs. 100% for wild-type). FadR is a transcriptional regulator phosphorylated by STK at Thr230. Phosphorylation enhances FadR binding to the adi promoter, increasing ADI expression and acid resistance [75]. The phosphomimetic mutant FadR-T230E showed increased intracellular survival in macrophages and hypervirulence in mice (0% survival vs. 30% for wild-type at 2 × 108 CFU). The integration of heat and acid stress responses through shared regulatory mechanisms (STK-FadR-ADI) suggests that combination therapies targeting multiple stress pathways may be more effective than single-target approaches, though this hypothesis remains untested.

Immune evasion strategies

Once SS2 enters the bloodstream, it must evade a sophisticated host immune system that includes complement, antibodies, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). SS2 has evolved a remarkable array of immune evasion mechanisms (Figure 3). Complement evasion

Factor H recruitment

Factor H (FH) is a host complement regulator that inactivates C3b. By binding FH, SS2 can recruit this regulator to its surface and prevent C3b deposition. Fhb (factor H-binding protein) was the first identified FH binder in SS2 [77]. Deletion of fhb significantly reduced bacterial traversal across an in vitro human BBB model. Mechanistically, Fhb binds to globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) on brain microvascular endothelial cells, activating the Rho/ROCK pathway and inducing phosphorylation of myosin light chain 2 (MLC2), thereby increasing endothelial permeability. In a mouse model, Gb3-deficient (A4galt-/-) mice exhibited significantly reduced mortality and lower brain bacterial loads [78]. The crystal structure of the Fhb receptor-binding domain in complex with Gb2 was solved at 2.7 Å resolution, revealing that aromatic residues (Tyr198, Phe283, Phe318) are critical for binding. AdcA, a zinc-binding lipoprotein, was also shown to bind FH; deletion of adcA or point mutations in the predicted FH-binding site increased C3b deposition and reduced serum survival [79]. A comprehensive 2D-Far-western blotting and proteomics study identified 14 potential FH-binding proteins on the SS2 surface, including enolase, EF-Tu, PK, GAPDH, FBA, FBPS, KAR, and MRP [80]. The diversity of FH-binding proteins suggests functional redundancy, which may limit the efficacy of single-target therapies, a consideration often overlooked in antivirulence drug design.

Direct C3b cleavage and inhibition

Instead of merely recruiting FH, some SS2 factors directly cleave or inhibit C3b. SsPepO (endopeptidase O) is a plasminogen-binding protein that, upon activation to plasmin, cleaves C3b into 68 kDa and 38 kDa fragments [81]. The ΔSsPepO mutant showed reduced plasminogen binding, impaired adherence to brain endothelial cells, increased serum susceptibility, and attenuated virulence in mice (80–100% survival vs. 0% for wild-type). ApuA (amylopullulanase) is a bifunctional enzyme that also binds C3b; deletion of apuA increased C3b deposition and MAC formation, reduced serum survival, and attenuated virulence in mice (100% survival vs 50% for wild-type) [82]. HylS', a truncated fragment of hyaluronidase, binds directly to C3b, reducing C3b deposition and MAC formation; the ΔhylS′ mutant exhibited increased C3b/MAC deposition and attenuated virulence in mice (12.5% mortality vs 100% for wild-type) [83]. The multiplicity of C3b-targeting factors suggests that combination therapies may be needed to restore complement-mediated killing, but no such combinations have been tested in vivo.

Anaphylatoxin inhibition

C3a and C5a are anaphylatoxins that recruit and activate immune cells. SspA-1 and SspA-2 (subtilisin-like serine proteases) interact with human C3a and C5a but do not cleave them; both recombinant proteins significantly inhibited THP-1 cell chemotaxis toward C3a and C5a [84]. Deletion of sspA-1 or sspA-2 reduced bacterial survival in swine blood and increased opsonophagocytosis. SLY (suilysin) was also identified as a C3aR- and C5aR-binding protein that inhibits monocyte chemotaxis [85]. The multifunctional roles of SLY in complement evasion, BBB disruption, and inflammasome activation are discussed in Anaphylatoxin inhibition and Mechanisms of blood-brain barrier disruption and tissue invasion, .1, and 6.1. SntA (a heme-binding protein with an LPXTG motif) binds to complement C1q and inhibits the classical pathway. ΔsntA showed reduced survival in human blood, increased C3 deposition and MAC formation, and attenuated virulence in mice (2/8 deaths vs 8/8 for wild-type) [86]. SntA bound to individual C1q subunits and inhibited C1q binding to immune complexes, and also directly activated the classical and lectin pathways, leading to complement consumption. The paradoxical activation of complement by SntA, while simultaneously inhibiting C1q, illustrates the complexity of bacterial immune evasion that simplistic loss-of-function studies may miss.

Immunoglobulin degradation

SS2 can degrade host immunoglobulins to evade opsonophagocytosis and antibody-mediated immunity. IgdE (immunoglobulin G-degrading enzyme of S. suis) is a secreted cysteine protease that specifically cleaves porcine IgG in the hinge region, just N-terminal of the inter-heavy chain disulfide bond cysteines [87]. Site-directed mutagenesis identified the catalytic triad as Cys-302, His-333, and Asp-348. All 15 tested SS2 strains possess IgdE activity. No animal infection experiments with ΔigdE have been performed, leaving its contribution to virulence speculative. IdeS_suis is an IgM-degrading enzyme. A point mutation (C195S) in the putative active site abolished enzymatic activity. In a pig intranasal infection model (natural host), there were no significant differences in mortality, morbidity, or meningitis incidence between wild-type, ΔideS_suis, and the catalytically inactive point mutant [88]. However, the complemented strain (with full IgM cleavage activity) had an unintended thinner capsule, which may have confounded the results. This capsule defect in the complemented strain is a critical caveat that undermines the conclusion that IdeS_suis is dispensable for virulence. In ex vivo assays, IdeS_suis reduced IgM and C3 labeling on the bacterial surface and improved survival in porcine blood. A follow-up study using recombinant IdeS_suis protein showed that even the cleavage-deficient mutant (C195S) bound to the IgM BCR on porcine B cells and caused long-lasting impairment of B cell activation and IgM secretion [89]. This non-enzymatic immunomodulatory function suggests that IdeS_suis may contribute to virulence through mechanisms beyond IgM cleavage, challenging the simplistic interpretation of the pig infection data.

The variable region of MRP (designated MRP-D1) from a high-virulence ST7 strain was shown to bind to porcine IgG, human fibronectin, factor H, and fibrinogen, whereas MRP-D1 from a low-virulence strain showed weaker or no binding [90]. Immunization with MRP-D1 conferred 50% protection in mice. The strain-specific binding profile of MRP-D1 raises questions about its utility as a universal vaccine antigen.

Resistance to antimicrobial peptides

AMPs such as LL-37 (human) and PR-39 (porcine) are produced by epithelial cells and neutrophils. SS2 produces proteases that degrade these peptides. ApdS is a cysteine protease that cleaves the N-terminal LLG tripeptide from LL-37, generating a truncated peptide with reduced α-helical content, impaired bactericidal activity, and diminished chemotactic ability [91]. Deletion of apdS increased sensitivity to LL-37, human blood, and NETs, but no in vivo animal model was used. The lack of in vivo validation is a significant limitation given the importance of AMP resistance in mucosal survival. PepO (endopeptidase O) also cleaves LL-37 (and mouse mCRAMP) into short peptides that lack bactericidal activity and immunomodulatory functions [92]. The ΔpepO mutant was more sensitive to LL-37, and LL-37 treatment further reduced bacterial loads in ΔpepO-infected mice but had no effect on wild-type-infected mice. Dipeptidylpeptidase IV (DPPIV) from SS2 degrades the porcine AMP PR-39, neutralizing its antibacterial activity and immunomodulatory effect [93]; no DPPIV knockout mutant has been constructed. The specificity of ApdS for human LL-37 and DPPIV for porcine PR-39 suggests host-adapted evasion mechanisms, but whether this adaptation facilitates cross-species transmission remains untested.

Evasion of neutrophil extracellular traps

NETs are web-like structures of DNA, histones, and antimicrobial proteins released by neutrophils to trap and kill bacteria. SsnA is a cell wall-anchored DNase. Deletion of ssnA in strain GD01 significantly reduced adherence to HEp-2 cells and attenuated virulence in mice (LD50 ~14-fold higher than wild-type), and immunization with ΔssnA provided 80% protection [94]. However, a subsequent study in the natural porcine host found that ΔssnA showed similar virulence to wild-type in piglets, with comparable mortality (1/9 vs 3/9), brain lesion scores, and bacterial re-isolation from tissues [95]. NETs were detected in cerebrospinal fluid of both infection groups, and host DNase activity (including DNase 1 in the choroid plexus) was detected, potentially compensating for the loss of SsnA. This is a major discrepancy between mouse and pig models that calls into question the translational relevance of mouse-based NET evasion studies. SodA also suppresses NETosis, as described in Superoxide dismutase and NADH oxidase [41]. The model-dependent phenotype of SsnA highlights the critical importance of validating virulence findings in the natural host, a principle that applies broadly to SS2 research but is rarely followed.

Adenosine-mediated immunosuppression

Extracellular adenosine signals through host adenosine receptors to suppress immune responses. SS2 produces 5'-nucleotidases that convert AMP to adenosine. Ssads (5'-nucleotidase) hydrolyzes AMP to adenosine at the bacterial surface, activating host A1 adenosine receptors on brain endothelial cells and leading to BBB disruption (detailed in 5'-nucleotidase and adenosine-driven barrier leakage) [96]. SS2 encodes four cell wall-anchored 5'-nucleotidases (SntA-D). SntB and SntC exhibit high AMP hydrolysis activity, while SntA and SntD have low activity. The quadruple deletion mutant (Δsnts) was significantly more susceptible to killing in whole blood and PMN assays; exogenous adenosine rescued survival, and the A2a receptor antagonist ZM241385 also reduced survival of wild-type, indicating that adenosine acts via A2a [97]. The 5'-nucleotidase from serotype 9 strain GZ0565 also converts dAMP to deoxyadenosine (dAdo), which triggers caspase-3-dependent death of RAW264.7 macrophages and causes monocytopenia in mice [98]. A purely biochemical study characterized the core domain of SntA as a broad-specificity phosphohydrolase that hydrolyzes cyclic dinucleotides including c-di-AMP, suggesting it may dampen STING-dependent type I interferon responses [99]. The immunomodulatory functions of SntA extend beyond adenosine production, but these diverse activities have not been integrated into a coherent model of SS2 immune evasion.

Capsule-based immune shielding

The capsular polysaccharide (CPS) is the most classical immune evasion factor of SS2. Its thickness and composition are dynamically regulated (Capsular polysaccharide dynamics: a trade-off between colonization and immune evasion). A thick CPS reduces phagocytosis and complement deposition by masking underlying surface components. CPS serotype switching can dramatically alter immune evasion capacity [37]. The PTX3-CPS axis coordinates inflammatory responses; PTX3 enhances bacterial clearance only in the presence of CPS [38]. CPS synthesis genes are regulated by multiple TCSs and STK (Section “Global regulatory networks controlling virulence” ). The molecular mechanism by which CPS thickness is sensed and adjusted in response to host environments remains largely unknown, a critical gap given the therapeutic potential of targeting this dynamic regulation.

Mechanisms of blood-brain barrier disruption and tissue invasion

Meningitis is the most severe manifestation of SS2 infection. To reach the central nervous system (CNS), SS2 must cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a highly selective endothelial barrier. Recent advances have identified three major pathways by which SS2 disrupts BBB integrity: (1) the pore-forming toxin suilysin (SLY), (2) the moonlighting protein enolase (Eno), and (3) the 5'-nucleotidase Ssads that produces adenosine (Figure 4). Suilysin: A multifunctional pore-forming toxin

SLY is a cholesterol-dependent cytolysin related to pneumolysin and streptolysin O. It activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis: SLY forms pores causing K+ efflux and ROS production, activating NLRP3; Δsly mutants are significantly attenuated in mice, and Nlrp3-/-, Caspase-1/11-/-, and Gsdmd-/- mice are protected [100]. High expression of functional SLY enables the non-epidemic ST1 strain P1/7 to cause STSLS in an NLRP3-dependent manner [101]. The fact that non-epidemic strains can acquire STSLS-causing capacity through SLY overexpression challenges the notion that epidemic strains possess unique virulence determinants. The roles of SLY in complement evasion and monocyte chemotaxis inhibition are discussed in Anaphylatoxin inhibition and Factor H recruitment and , respectively. SLY disrupts BBB via the PLA2G3/TNF-α pathway: sub-hemolytic SLY stimulates brain endothelial cells to release TNF-α (TLR4-dependent), which upregulates PLA2G3, leading to endothelial apoptosis and increased permeability [102]. SLY triggers ARF6-dependent apoptosis: using TurboID proximity labeling, ARF6 was identified as a host protein interacting with SLY; ARF6 overexpression enhances SLY-induced p38 MAPK phosphorylation and apoptosis [103]. SLY lyses red blood cells, releasing IL-33, which suppresses claudin-5 via MMP-9 and promotes BBB disruption [104]. SLY also induces platelet aggregation through a pore-dependent Ca2+ influx mechanism [105] and stimulates release of heparin-binding protein (HBP) from neutrophils, causing vascular leakage [106]. In an in vitro porcine tracheal epithelial model, SLY compromises the epithelial barrier by disrupting occludin and ZO-1 [107]. The pleiotropic effects of SLY on multiple host pathways suggest that targeting this single toxin may be insufficient for therapeutic intervention, yet most vaccine strategies still focus on SLY as a primary antigen.

Enolase as a moonlighting invasin

As introduced in Section 2.2, enolase is a glycolytic enzyme found on the bacterial surface that functions as an adhesin and plasminogen receptor. Its role in BBB disruption has been extensively characterized. Using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, four SS2 enolase mutants with amino acid substitutions at predicted plasminogen-binding sites were generated; all showed significantly reduced binding of plasminogen and plasmin, and addition of plasmin plus tPA enhanced translocation of wild-type but not the mutants in an in vitro BBB model [108]. Purified Eno induces IL-8 secretion from a porcine BBB co-culture model, and IL-8 receptor antagonist G31P reduces Eno-induced BBB disruption [109]. The host receptor for Eno was identified as 40S ribosomal protein SA (RPSA) on brain microvascular endothelial cells; Eno binding activates p38/ERK MAPK, leading to eIF4E phosphorylation, HSPD1 upregulation, and caspase-8/3 activation, resulting in apoptosis and increased BBB permeability [110]. The identification of RPSA as a host receptor is significant, but the physiological relevance of this interaction in the intact BBB remains uncertain given that most studies use monocultures rather than physiologically relevant co-culture systems.

A follow-up study uncovered a novel mechanism: Eno induces liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) of RPSA; the intrinsically disordered region of RPSA forms liquid-like condensates that translocate to the cell surface, and Eno promotes co-condensation of RPSA with vimentin [111]. Another study showed that Eno promotes translocation of HSPD1 from mitochondria to the cytoplasm, where HSPD1 interacts with β-actin, leading to increased mitochondrial membrane permeability and apoptosis [112]. The LLPS mechanism is intriguing but has not been validated in primary brain endothelial cells or in vivo models, raising questions about its physiological relevance.

5'-nucleotidase and adenosine-driven barrier leakage

As introduced in Section 4.5, Ssads is a surface-associated 5'-nucleotidase that hydrolyzes AMP to adenosine. This adenosine activates host A1 adenosine receptors on brain endothelial cells, leading to decreased cAMP, activation of ERK1/2 and NF-κB, actin cytoskeleton remodeling, and redistribution of ZO-1, thereby increasing BBB permeability and promoting paracellular translocation [96]. Deletion of ssads did not affect blood bacterial loads but significantly reduced brain bacterial loads (by ~ 10-fold) and normalized brain morphology in a mouse hematogenous meningitis model. Using A1 AR-knockout cells and mice, the study demonstrated that the pro-translocation effect is strictly dependent on host A1 AR. Other meningeal pathogens (GBS, S. aureus, S. epidermidis) also possess surface 5'-nucleotidase activity, suggesting a conserved mechanism. The conservation of this pathway across meningeal pathogens suggests broad therapeutic potential for A1 adenosine receptor antagonists, but none have been tested in porcine models of SS2 meningitis.

Additional invasins and BBB-targeting factors

SsPepO is a metallo-endopeptidase that binds human fibronectin, which subsequently engages integrins on brain microvascular endothelial cells, promoting bacterial adherence and increasing BBB permeability; the ΔSsPepO mutant exhibited significantly reduced brain bacterial loads in a mouse model of hematogenous meningitis [113]. Fhb binds to Gb3 on brain endothelial cells, activating Rho/ROCK and inducing MLC2 phosphorylation, thereby increasing endothelial permeability [77]. PotD regulates the arginine deiminase system; deletion of potD reduced arcA expression and attenuated SS2-induced downregulation of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin in brain endothelial cells [60]. RfeA is an RTX family exoprotein that induces pyroptosis in brain endothelial cells; it is internalized via caveolae/lipid raft-dependent endocytosis and interacts with the mitochondrial protein VDAC1, leading to mitochondrial ROS production, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and pyroptosis [114]. EF-P (elongation factor P) is phosphorylated by STK at Ser148 and Thr176; phosphorylated EF-P enhances translation of a serine protease that degrades ZO-1 [115]. STK also promotes degradation of claudin-5 via the E3 ubiquitin ligase HECTD1 [116]. The convergence of bacterial factors on tight junction disruption and endothelial apoptosis suggests that protecting barrier integrity may be a viable therapeutic strategy, but the lack of in vivo validation in porcine models limits enthusiasm.

Modulation of host inflammatory responses

SS2 infection triggers robust inflammatory responses that can be either protective (clearing the bacteria) or pathogenic (causing tissue damage, septic shock, and meningitis). The outcome depends on the delicate balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signals (Figure 5). Inflammasome activation and pyroptosis

As detailed in Section 5.1, SLY and RfeA activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to caspase-1 activation, GSDMD cleavage, and release of IL-1β and IL-18 [100], [114]. High expression of SLY alone is sufficient to enable a non-epidemic strain to cause STSLS in an NLRP3-dependent manner [101]. A detailed mechanistic study in primary mouse peritoneal macrophages identified two host scaffold proteins, RACK1 and NEK7, as essential for SS2-induced pyroptosis. RACK1 interacted with NF-κB p65 and promoted its phosphorylation, driving transcription of Nlrp3 and pro-IL-1β; NEK7 facilitated NLRP3 inflammasome assembly downstream of RACK1 [117]. SodA deficiency reduces pyroptosis, as described in Section 3.1.1 [39]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation is a double-edged sword: while it promotes bacterial clearance, excessive activation drives tissue damage and STSLS. Selective NLRP3 inhibitors may represent a therapeutic strategy for severe SS2 infections, but their efficacy in pig models has not been tested.

Autophagy manipulation by bacterial factors

The ABC-type fucose operon (FCS) upregulates host fucosyltransferases (FUT1, FUT4, FUT7, FUT8), which activate the AMPK/mTOR pathway to inhibit autophagy, thereby promoting bacterial survival in macrophages [118]. SodA scavenges ROS to suppress autophagy [40]. The chicken cathelicidin CATH-2 (tested in mouse macrophages) induces autophagy via the mTOR pathway, which suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine production [119]. The cross-talk between autophagy and inflammasome activation is complex, and manipulating one pathway may have unintended consequences for the other, a consideration for therapeutic development that is rarely addressed in preclinical studies.

Balancing pro- and anti-inflammatory signals

The formyl peptide receptor 2 (Fpr2) plays opposite roles depending on the infection site. In a model of STSLS (intraperitoneal infection), Fpr2 deficiency significantly reduced mortality, bacterial load, and systemic inflammatory responses by enhancing neutrophil recruitment and NET formation [120]. Treatment with Fpr2 agonists (AnxA1, LXA4) impaired neutrophil recruitment and increased bacterial loads. In contrast, in a meningitis model (intracisternal infection), Fpr2 played a protective role; Fpr2-/- mice were more susceptible to SS2 meningitis, exhibiting increased mortality and higher brain bacterial loads [121]. Administration of exogenous annexin A1 (AnxA1) reduced neutrophil invasion and brain damage in wild-type mice but not in Fpr2-/- mice. The dual role of Fpr2 in different infection sites highlights the context-dependent nature of inflammatory responses and cautions against blanket anti-inflammatory strategies that may be beneficial in one setting but harmful in another.

The host E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM32 drives hyperinflammation; Trim32-/- mice showed significantly lower bacteremia, reduced serum cytokine levels, and improved survival after high-dose infection [122]. Vimentin promotes inflammation in airway epithelial cells during SS2 infection; in vimentin knockout mice, lung injury, neutrophil infiltration, and pro-inflammatory cytokines were significantly reduced after intranasal infection, and vimentin promoted transcription of NOD2 and subsequent phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 [123]. SS2 produces extracellular vesicles (EVs) that suppress macrophage immunity: pre-incubation of RAW264.7 macrophages with EVs suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines, increased IL-10, downregulated MHC-II genes, and reduced adhesion molecules; EV supplementation in mice increased bacterial loads and exacerbated histopathological damage [124]. Host microRNA miR-7a-5p directly targets the 3'UTR of NLRP3 mRNA; overexpression of miR-7a-5p mimics reduced NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β levels, and in a mouse model, miR-7a-5p mimics significantly reduced histopathological damage [125]. MicroRNA-based therapies represent an emerging approach for inflammatory modulation, but their delivery to the CNS and potential off-target effects remain major hurdles.

Cytokine storm in streptococcal toxic shock-like syndrome

STSLS is characterized by a massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ) leading to septic shock and multi-organ failure. TstS is a stand-alone transcriptional regulator located within the 89K pathogenicity island of epidemic ST7 strains. tstS is highly upregulated in vivo and deletion of tstS significantly reduced cytokine production, attenuated virulence in mice (40% survival vs. 10% for wild-type), and reduced bacterial loads in blood; introduction of tstS into the non-STSLS-causing European strain P1/7 conferred increased virulence, higher bacteremia, and elevated cytokine production [126]. SspA-1 is a subtilisin-like protease secreted through the type IV secretion system (T4SS) encoded in the 89K pathogenicity island. RNA-seq of SspA-1-stimulated macrophages revealed that type I interferon signaling was the primary enriched pathway; SspA-1 directly bound to TLR2 (Kd = 24.3 nM), and TLR2 knockdown abolished cytokine production [127]. SspA-1 is the first identified T4SS effector in a Gram-positive bacterium; GST pull-down confirmed direct interaction with VirD4-89K, and deletion of sspA-1 attenuated virulence in mice (40% survival vs. 0% for wild-type) and reduced serum cytokine levels; immunization with SspA-1 provided 60% protection [128]. The cytokine storm in STSLS represents an uncontrolled inflammatory response that causes more damage than the pathogen itself. Targeting host inflammatory pathways (e.g. TstS, SspA-1) rather than the bacterium may be a viable strategy for severe infections, but this approach risks immunosuppression that could exacerbate bacterial dissemination.

Global regulatory networks controlling virulence

SS2 encounters diverse host environments, nasopharynx, blood, cerebrospinal fluid, each presenting distinct challenges. To adapt, the bacterium employs a sophisticated regulatory architecture that senses environmental signals and adjusts virulence factor expression accordingly. This section reviews the major regulatory systems in SS2, including two-component systems (TCSs), the eukaryotic-like Ser/Thr kinase STK and its cognate phosphatase Stp1, transcriptional regulators, small non-coding RNAs (sRNAs), toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems, and the stringent response mediated by (p)ppGpp (Figure 6). Two-component systems as virulence switches

TCSs are the primary signal transduction mechanism in bacteria, typically consisting of a membrane-bound histidine kinase (HK) and a cytoplasmic response regulator (RR). SS2 encodes more than a dozen TCSs, several of which have been functionally characterized. The orphan response regulator CovR (without a cognate HK in its immediate vicinity) was first studied in the human epidemic ST7 strain 05ZYH33, where it acts as a negative regulator of virulence. In that strain, covR deletion led to increased capsule thickness, enhanced resistance to phagocytosis, and hypervirulence [129]. Surprisingly, a later study in the porcine ST1 strain SC19 found the opposite: ΔcovR exhibited a thinner capsule, reduced adhesion, and attenuated virulence [130]. This discrepancy is striking and remains unresolved, a sobering reminder that regulatory logic can be strain-specific, complicating the generalization of findings from single strains. The authors of the SC19 study performed RNA-Seq and found that CovR positively regulated metabolic pathways (fructose/mannose metabolism, PTS systems) rather than classical virulence genes. Whether this reflects true strain-specific differences (ST7 vs ST1) or differences in infection models requires direct side-by-side comparison. This controversy is discussed in detail in Section “Controversies and discrepant findings”.

The VraSR TCS, initially characterized in S. aureus as a cell wall stress sensor, also plays a key role in SS2. Deletion of vraSR resulted in a thinner capsule (reduced sialic acid and downregulation of cps2B/C and neuB/C), increased susceptibility to phagocytosis and whole-blood killing, and attenuated virulence in mice [131,132]. Importantly, VraR did not directly bind to cps promoters, indicating an indirect regulatory mechanism. VraSR has been proposed as an antivirulence target [133], but the intermediate factor(s) linking VraSR to capsule synthesis remain unknown. The unidentified intermediate factors represent a major obstacle for drug design, as targeting VraSR without understanding its downstream effectors may have unpredictable consequences. The CiaRH TCS, conserved in streptococci, connects TCS signaling to antibiotic resistance. In SS2, ΔciaRH mutants showed reduced susceptibility to fluoroquinolones due to upregulation of the SatAB efflux pump, and CiaR directly represses the satRAB operon [14]. However, the environmental signal sensed by CiaH has not been identified. The lack of identified environmental signals for most TCSs is a fundamental gap that limits our understanding of when and why these systems are activated during infection.

A novel anti-phagocytic axis was discovered through the Ihk/Irr TCS. Deletion of ihk or irr reduced bacterial survival in RAW264.7 macrophages and in whole blood [134]. Mechanistically, phosphorylated Irr binds to the pepO promoter, upregulating the endopeptidase PepO. PepO then interacts with host TLR2, leading to activation of p38/ERK/MAPK pathways and subsequent reduction in F-actin polymerization, which impairs macrophage phagocytosis. This pathway links a TCS to a specific anti-phagocytic effector. Other TCSs, including TCS09HKRR and 1910HK/RR, have also been characterized. ΔTCS09HKRR mutants had thinner capsules and were completely avirulent in mice [135], while the 1910HK/RR system was essential for virulence in both mouse and pig models, with deletion mutants exhibiting dramatically reduced adhesion and invasion [136]. For all these TCSs, the signals that activate the histidine kinase and their direct downstream targets remain largely unknown, a recurring limitation that undermines mechanistic understanding and therapeutic targeting.

The eukaryotic-like Ser/Thr kinase STK and phosphatase Stp1

STK and Stp1 constitute a eukaryotic-like signaling module that controls a wide range of bacterial processes through protein phosphorylation. In SS2, STK has emerged as a central regulator of virulence, integrating signals from diverse environmental cues to coordinate multiple virulence traits. Several direct substrates of STK have been identified through phosphoproteomics and targeted assays. DivIVA (Thr199) is a cell division protein; STK-mediated phosphorylation is required for proper Z-ring formation and septation, and ΔdivIVA mutants form elongated, misshapen cells and are highly attenuated [137,138]. GlmM (Ser101) is a phosphoglucosamine mutase involved in peptidoglycan synthesis; the phosphoablative mutant GlmM-S101A had a thinner capsule, reduced peptidoglycan content, and attenuated virulence [139]. GntR (Ser41) is a transcriptional regulator; STK phosphorylation reduces its DNA-binding activity, leading to decreased transcription of nox (NADH oxidase) and reduced oxidative stress resistance, and the phosphomimetic GntR-S41E mutant was attenuated in mice [140]. FadR (Thr230) regulates the arginine deiminase gene adi; phosphorylation enhances FadR binding to the adi promoter, increasing acid resistance and intracellular survival in macrophages [75]. CcpS (Thr4, Thr7) is a small protein that coordinates capsule synthesis; non-phosphorylated CcpS binds to and inhibits CpsB phosphatase, thereby promoting CpsD-dependent capsule production, and STK phosphorylation of CcpS relieves this inhibition [141]. EF-P (Ser148, Thr176) is an elongation factor; phosphorylation enhances translation of a serine protease that degrades ZO-1, contributing to BBB disruption [115]. The identification of specific phosphorylation sites provides a framework for understanding bacterial adaptation, but the lack of identified upstream signals and incomplete substrate mapping limit therapeutic targeting.

While STK is a kinase, Stp1 is its cognate phosphatase. Comparative analysis of Δstk and Δstp1 mutants revealed opposing effects on capsule thickness (Δstk: thinner; Δstp1: thicker) and on the surface translocation of GAPDH and SLY [25]. Δstk was more attenuated than Δstp1, indicating that the kinase activity is more critical for virulence [15]. Despite these advances, the full phosphoproteome of SS2 under infection-mimicking conditions has not been mapped, and how STK/Stp1 activity is itself regulated by environmental signals remains unknown [142]. The absence of infection-mimicking phosphoproteomics is a critical methodological gap, as laboratory conditions may not recapitulate the phosphorylation events that occur in the host.

Transcriptional regulators of virulence traits

Beyond TCS response regulators, SS2 encodes numerous stand-alone transcriptional regulators that control virulence. Rex is a redox-sensing repressor that responds to the NADH/NAD+ ratio; Δrex mutants showed reduced tolerance to H2O2 and acidic pH, decreased survival in whole blood, and attenuated virulence in mice, and Rex directly binds to promoters of rex, adhE, arcA, frdA, dpr, ldh, and treR [143]. CodY is a pleiotropic regulator of metabolism and virulence that binds to the relA promoter in a GTP-independent manner and positively regulates (p)ppGpp synthesis [144]. XtrSs is an XRE family regulator that represses the arginine deiminase system (ADS); deletion of xtrSs led to increased ADS activity, enhanced acid resistance, and hypervirulence in mice (LD50 >10-fold lower) [74], a rare example of a regulator whose inactivation increases virulence. TstS, located within the 89K pathogenicity island of epidemic ST7 strains, is highly upregulated in blood and drives cytokine production, contributing to STSLS [126]. Other regulators such as NrtR [145], FlpS [73], GlpR [146], and SrtR [147] link NAD+ homeostasis, oxygen sensing, glycerol metabolism, and oxidative stress to virulence. For most of these regulators, direct DNA-binding sites have not been comprehensively mapped, and the signals that modulate their activity are poorly defined, limitations that hinder the construction of predictive regulatory models.

Small non-coding RNAs in post-transcriptional virulence control

sRNAs have emerged as important post-transcriptional regulators in bacteria. Several sRNAs have been characterized in SS2, though direct mRNA targets remain identified for only a minority. rss03 (303 nt) is highly conserved in epidemic ST7 strains; it binds to the coding region of glpF mRNA (encoding an aquaporin) and protects it from RNase J1 degradation, thereby increasing GlpF-mediated H2O2 efflux and oxidative stress resistance, and Δrss03 mutants had reduced survival in blood and brain [16]. rss04 represses the transcriptional regulator CcpA, leading to decreased capsule production and increased biofilm formation; Δrss04 mutants showed reduced brain bacterial loads and attenuated virulence in a meningitis model [148]. sRNA23 binds to fructose diphosphate aldolase (FBA) and ribosomal protein L2 (rplB), affecting adhesion and biofilm formation [149]. sRNA34 deletion resulted in elongated chains and increased phagocytosis, but its molecular targets remain unknown [150]. For most sRNAs, direct mRNA targets have not been identified, and the mechanisms by which sRNAs recognize their targets (base-pairing vs protein-mediated) are largely unexplored, a gap that limits both mechanistic understanding and therapeutic exploitation.

Toxin-antitoxin systems: beyond plasmid maintenance

TA systems are typically involved in stress responses, persistence, and plasmid maintenance. Several TA systems have been implicated in SS2 virulence and antibiotic resistance. ParDE is a type II TA system; ΔparDE mutants showed reduced survival in blood and macrophages, increased sensitivity to oxidative stress, enhanced biofilm formation, and attenuated virulence in mice [151]. SezAT and AbiE, encoded on ICESuHN105, stabilize the integrative and conjugative element by binding to the oriT and attL sites, thereby preventing ICE excision; double deletion of the antitoxins led to complete ICE loss and reduced multidrug resistance [152]. Deletion of the antitoxin xress in the Xress-MNTss system resulted in a thinner capsule, enhanced biofilm formation, and attenuated virulence [153]. The direct targets of the toxins (e.g. ParE, SezT, MNTss) in SS2 have not been identified, and whether TA systems contribute to bacterial persistence during chronic infection or antibiotic therapy remains unknown, an important consideration given the rising concern about antibiotic resistance in SS2.

The stringent response mediated by (p)ppGpp synthetases RelA/relQ

The stringent response, mediated by the alarmone (p)ppGpp, allows bacteria to adapt to nutrient starvation by reprogramming transcription, translation, and metabolism. In SS2, deletion of both relA and relQ (generating a (p)ppGpp0 mutant) resulted in markedly elongated bacterial chains, reduced adhesion to and invasion of HEp-2 cells, increased susceptibility to whole-blood killing and phagocytosis by macrophages, and complete avirulence in a mouse infection model (100% survival vs 0% for wild-type) [131]. Under glucose starvation, RelA was identified as the sole functional (p)ppGpp synthetase, mediating classical stringent responses including inhibition of protein synthesis, DNA replication, and cell division, as well as metabolic reprogramming such as downregulation of glycolysis and upregulation of alternative carbon source utilization genes [154].

Beyond starvation adaptation, (p)ppGpp signaling integrates with other regulatory systems. The pleiotropic transcriptional regulator CodY binds directly to the relA promoter in a GTP-independent manner and positively regulates relA expression; a ΔrelAΔrelQΔcodY triple mutant exhibited an even more severe phenotype than the (p)ppGpp0 single mutant, with a thicker but rough capsule and complete avirulence [144]. Despite these advances, the direct molecular targets of (p)ppGpp in SS2 have not been identified, and the cross-talk between the stringent response and other global regulators (e.g. CodY, STK) remains to be fully defined, a fundamental gap that limits our understanding of how SS2 coordinates virulence gene expression under stress.

Controversial or discrepant findings in SS2 virulence research: causes and proposed resolutions.

CovR: strain-specific regulatory logic

CovR acts as a negative virulence regulator in the human epidemic ST7 strain 05ZYH33 but as a positive regulator in the porcine ST1 strain SC19 [130]. Possible causes include true strain-specific differences in regulatory networks between ST7 and ST1, different infection models (intraperitoneal mouse vs pig), polar effects of deletion mutations on adjacent genes, and differences in capsule biosynthesis gene clusters between strains. Proposed resolutions include CRISPR-based allelic replacement to swap covR loci between isogenic backgrounds, transcriptomic comparison under identical infection-mimicking conditions, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) to map direct DNA-binding sites in both strains, and testing in the natural porcine host using intranasal infection. The failure to resolve this controversy for over a decade reflects a broader problem in the field, the reluctance to perform rigorous, side-by-side comparisons using standardized methods and appropriate infection models.

SsnA: model-dependent essentiality

SsnA is essential for virulence in mice (LD50 ~14-fold increase) but dispensable in piglets (no difference in mortality or lesions) [94]. Possible causes include host DNase activity in pig CSF (including DNase 1 in the choroid plexus) compensating for bacterial DNase loss, differences in NET composition between species, and alternative NET degradation pathways in pigs. Proposed resolutions include using the pig model as the gold standard for translational relevance, testing in DNase-deficient pigs or using DNase inhibitors, comparing NET composition and host nuclease activity between mouse and pig, and evaluating SsnA as a vaccine candidate in both models. The SsnA discrepancy is perhaps the most compelling evidence that mouse models can mislead, yet the field continues to rely heavily on mouse data for virulence attribution, a practice that undermines translational credibility.

IdeS_suis: in vitro activity vs in vivo phenotype

IdeS_suis cleaves porcine IgM and reduces complement deposition in vitro, but shows no virulence defect in piglets [88]. Possible causes include an unintended capsule defect in the complemented strain confounding results, high IgM turnover in pigs masking the effect, and compensatory immune evasion mechanisms. Proposed resolutions include generating a clean point mutant (C195S) without capsule defect, testing in immunodeficient pigs or using IgM-depleted serum, and evaluating long-term B cell impairment as a more relevant phenotype than acute virulence. The confounding capsule defect in the complemented strain is a critical methodological flaw that should have been caught during peer review, highlighting the need for rigorous complementation controls in virulence studies.

Fpr2: context-dependent host protection

Fpr2 is detrimental in STSLS (suppresses neutrophil recruitment) but protective in meningitis (limits excessive inflammation) [120]. Possible causes include different infection sites (blood vs CNS) with distinct immune cell populations, different cell types involved (peripheral neutrophils vs brain endothelium), and differential expression of Fpr2 ligands in different tissues. Proposed resolutions include cell-specific knockout mice (neutrophil-specific vs endothelial-specific), use of selective Fpr2 agonists/antagonists in different infection models, and single-cell transcriptomics of infected tissues to identify cell-type-specific Fpr2 signaling. The dual role of Fpr2 illustrates the danger of extrapolating findings from one infection site to another, a common practice in SS2 research that may lead to erroneous therapeutic conclusions.

Sortases: host-specific essentiality

Single deletion mutants of SrtB and SrtF are attenuated in pigs but not in mice or Galleria mellonella [21]. Possible causes include that mouse and Galleria are not natural hosts, pig has different immune pressures and colonization requirements, and functional redundancy among sortases in non-natural hosts. Proposed resolutions include prioritizing pig infection models with intranasal route for translational validation, generating triple sortase deletion mutants to test redundancy, and identifying SrtB/SrtF-specific substrates responsible for pig-specific virulence. The sortase discrepancy reinforces the principle that virulence factors required in the natural host may be dispensable in non-natural hosts, yet most preclinical screening continues to use mice, a cost-saving measure that may sacrifice translational relevance.

SLY overexpression: plasmid artifacts vs native regulation

High SLY expression enables non-epidemic strains to cause STSLS, but natural epidemic strains may have additional virulence factors [101]. Possible causes include plasmid-based overexpression not reflecting native regulation, copy number effects, and lack of coordinated regulation with other virulence factors. Proposed resolutions include using native promoter replacement or CRISPR-aided upregulation, evaluating coordinate regulation of SLY with other 89K pathogenicity island genes, and testing in the pig model to assess STSLS capacity. The reliance on plasmid-based overexpression to assign virulence functions is problematic, as it bypasses the native regulatory context that may modulate toxin expression in vivo, a concern that applies broadly to virulence factor studies in SS2.

Vaccine and therapeutic potential

Despite the wealth of information on SS2 virulence mechanisms, translation into effective vaccines and therapeutics remains limited [155]. This section evaluates promising candidates and identifies barriers to clinical development.

Vaccine candidates

Metabolic enzyme deletion mutants that are avirulent in animal models represent promising live vaccine candidates. Δgdh is completely avirulent in pigs (100% survival vs 0% for wild-type) [68]. Δoahs shows complete avirulence in mice (0% mortality vs 100% for wild-type) and immunization provided 83.3% protection against homologous and heterologous challenge [71]. ΔssnA provided 80% protection in mice [94]. However, the model-dependent phenotype of SsnA (dispensable in pigs) highlights the need for validation in the natural host before clinical development. Recent systematic reviews have consolidated evidence that multi-gene deletion strategies, such as the quintuple mutant Δsly-ΔscpA-ΔssnA-Δfhb-Δssads, achieve 70–100% cross-sequence type protection while retaining immunogenic CPS expression [156], and metabolic auxotrophs (Δaro) consistently achieve 100% homologous protection in swine. The reliance on mouse protection data for vaccine development is concerning given the repeated discrepancies between mouse and pig outcomes; regulatory authorities should require pig efficacy data before clinical trials.

Subunit vaccines offer alternative approaches. The Sao protein, a highly conserved surface-anchored antigen (>94.2% homology across serotypes), induces robust OPA when delivered via engineered OMVs (Lpp-SaoA fusion, 100% survival in mice without external adjuvant) [157] or live-attenuated Salmonella vectors (rSC0016/rSC0012, conferring homologous and heterologous SS7 protection in pigs) [158]. However, monovalent SaoA showed limited cross-serotype efficacy (only 20% survival against SS9 despite high OPA titers), underscoring the disconnect between antibody titers and functional protection [159]. The IgM protease IdeS_suis represents another promising candidate, with rIde vaccination achieving 100% survival in piglets against lethal serotype 9 challenge and serotype-independent protection against group A IgM protease-expressing strains (ST1, ST2, ST9, ST14) [160]. The serotype-independent protection of IdeS_suis is notable, but the mechanism remains unclear, whether protection is mediated by enzymatic activity or immunogenicity is unresolved.

SBP2 is present in all highly virulent strains and absent in low-virulence strains, making it a superior marker compared to classical virulence genes [12]. SssP1 is a large fimbria-like protein; its size complicates recombinant production but point mutation validation partially confirms its role [17]. SspA-1 immunization provided 60% protection in mice and represents the first identified T4SS effector in Gram-positive bacteria [128]. MRP-D1 immunization conferred 50% protection in mice and binds multiple host proteins [90]. Multi-antigen formulations may overcome single-subunit limitations: a dual-antigen SaoA-Enolase cassette delivered via Salmonella vector achieved 80–100% protection against serotypes 2, 7, 9, and 1/2 in mice [159], and a trivalent protein vaccine (JointS) combined with TLR4 agonist MPLA provided complete protection against SS2 in mice and good protection in piglets [161]. The superiority of multi-antigen formulations over single-subunit vaccines is consistent across studies, yet many academic groups continue to pursue monovalent approaches, a strategy that may be scientifically interesting but clinically naïve.

Cautionary notes are warranted. Immunization with recombinant Cba caused antibody-dependent enhancement, leading to higher mortality [28]. Similarly, recombinant Enolase and DPPIV subunit vaccines failed to protect against lethal SS2 challenge in outbred mice regardless of adjuvant, demonstrating that adjuvant optimization alone cannot rescue weak antigens [162]. A multicomponent vaccine of six conserved immunogens also failed in piglets [163]. These failures underscore that antigen selection and delivery platform are as critical as adjuvant formulation. Anti-CPS antibodies restrict bacterial survival in blood but do not block early colonization or CNS invasion [34]. Combination approaches targeting both CPS and surface adhesins may be required for effective protection. The repeated failures of subunit vaccines in piglets should temper enthusiasm for translational claims based solely on mouse data; the field needs more rigorous preclinical validation before advancing to field trials.

Antivirulence therapeutic targets

Regulatory systems represent attractive antivirulence targets because they govern the coordinated expression of multiple virulence determinants rather than a single factor, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development via compensatory pathways [164]. VraSR has been proposed as an antivirulence target [133]; however, unidentified intermediate factors limit current drug design. CiaRH connects signaling to antibiotic resistance, suggesting potential for combination therapies [14]. STK is a central regulator of multiple virulence traits; however, eukaryotic-like kinases may have off-target effects in host cells. The off-target liability of STK inhibitors is a significant concern that has not been adequately addressed in preclinical studies; selective bacterial kinase inhibitors remain an unmet need.

Immune evasion factors offer additional therapeutic opportunities. Blocking Fhb-Gb3 interaction or SsPepO-plasminogen binding could restore complement-mediated killing [77,81]. Adenosine receptor antagonists such as ZM241385 reduce wild-type survival in blood and PMN assays [97], suggesting adjunctive therapy for meningitis. Targeting SodA or SsnA could restore neutrophil-mediated killing [41]. BBB protective strategies may prevent neuroinvasion. A1 adenosine receptor antagonists prevent Ssads-mediated BBB disruption without direct antibacterial activity [96]. RPSA interaction inhibitors block Eno-induced endothelial apoptosis [110]. MLCK inhibitors prevent Atl-mediated tight junction disruption [31]. Beyond pharmacological intervention, host-directed strategies that reinforce barrier integrity, such as enhancing claudin-5 expression or modulating Rho/ROCK signaling, may offer synergistic protection against bacterial translocation [104]. A major translational challenge is that most BBB-protective candidates have been validated only in immortalized endothelial monocultures [165] or mouse models [166]; their efficacy in the natural porcine host, where SS2 exhibits distinct tropism for cerebrovascular endothelium, remains entirely unexplored, a gap that must be addressed before clinical development.

Barriers to clinical development

Despite promising preclinical findings, several barriers limit translation. Most targets are validated only in mouse models, which poorly predict pig and human outcomes. Multiple evasion mechanisms for complement, AMPs, and NETs suggest that single-target therapies may be ineffective. Many virulence factors are also essential for bacterial survival under stress, limiting therapeutic windows. The natural host remains underutilized for preclinical validation, and the lack of standardized pig infection protocols hampers comparative assessment. These barriers are not merely technical challenges; they reflect a systemic failure in the field to prioritize translational relevance over experimental convenience. Without a fundamental shift toward natural host validation, the gap between basic discovery and clinical application will continue to widen.

Discussion and future perspectives

The past decade has witnessed an explosion in the number of factors implicated in SS2 virulence, yet the field now faces a paradox of plenty, more than two hundred genes and proteins have been assigned virulence functions, but the direct molecular targets of most regulatory systems remain unknown, and the causal link between many in vitro phenotypes and actual infection outcomes is surprisingly weak. This disconnect between inventory and mechanism is the central challenge facing the field; simply cataloging more virulence-associated factors without establishing causal mechanisms is unlikely to advance therapeutic development.

A recurring theme across this review is the critical but often overlooked influence of experimental models. Mouse studies, typically using intraperitoneal injection of unnaturally high bacterial doses, have dominated the literature. However, when the same mutants were tested in pigs, the natural host and the only relevant model for vaccine development, striking discrepancies emerged: the DNase SsnA was essential for virulence in mice but dispensable in pigs [94]; the class C sortases SrtB and SrtF were completely avirulent in pigs [21], yet only mildly attenuated in mice; and the IgM protease IdeS_suis, despite cleaving porcine IgM and reducing complement deposition in vitro, showed no virulence defect in piglets [88]. These sobering results call into question the translational relevance of mouse-based virulence claims and underscore the urgent need to validate key findings in the natural porcine host, using the intranasal or oral route of infection. The continued dominance of mouse models in SS2 research is not merely a scientific preference but a strategic liability that undermines the field’s credibility with regulatory agencies and industry partners.

Another layer of complexity arises from strain-specific and context-dependent regulatory logic. The orphan response regulator CovR acts as a negative virulence regulator in the human epidemic ST7 strain 05ZYH33 but as a positive regulator in the porcine ST1 strain SC19 [130], a difference that has not been mechanistically resolved. The host receptor Fpr2 plays a detrimental role in STSLS by suppressing neutrophil recruitment [120] yet is protective in meningitis by limiting excessive inflammation. Such dualism is not a mere academic curiosity; it has direct implications for therapeutic targeting. Similarly, the immunization with the collagen-binding adhesin Cba induced antibody-dependent enhancement rather than protection [28], a cautionary tale for vaccine development that targets single adhesins without understanding their immunological context. These findings demand a more nuanced approach to therapeutic development that accounts for strain background, infection site, and host species, factors that are routinely ignored in preclinical studies.

Beyond model and strain variability, fundamental knowledge gaps persist at the molecular level. For nearly all two-component systems, the environmental signals that activate the histidine kinase have not been identified. The eukaryotic-like Ser/Thr kinase STK regulates multiple virulence traits (capsule, cell division, acid resistance, oxidative stress), but its own regulation and the full spectrum of its phosphorylation substrates remain incompletely mapped [15]. Small non-coding RNAs have emerged as important post-transcriptional regulators, yet only two of them (rss03 [16] and rss04 [148]) have known direct mRNA targets. Toxin-antitoxin systems are clearly involved in virulence [131] and antibiotic resistance, but the intracellular targets of the toxins (ParE, SezT, MNTss) are completely unknown. The stringent response alarmone (p)ppGpp is essential for full virulence, yet how it selectively reprograms gene expression in SS2 has not been studied at the molecular level. These gaps are not merely academic; they represent missed opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Without understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern virulence gene expression, target-based drug discovery will remain largely empirical.

Looking forward, the field must embrace a paradigm shift from inventory to mechanism, from correlation to causation. The natural pig model should no longer be an optional validation step but the primary platform for assessing virulence, particularly for mutants that show promising attenuation in mice. Systems-level approaches, CRISPR-based functional genomics, single-cell transcriptomics of infected tissues, and quantitative phosphoproteomics under infection-mimicking conditions, are urgently needed to move beyond the current catalog of “virulence-associated factors” and build a dynamic, network-based understanding of SS2 pathogenesis. This shift will require substantial investment in pig infection facilities and standardized protocols, but the alternative, continued reliance on inadequate models, risks perpetuating a cycle of translational failure.

Moreover, the contradictory findings highlighted throughout this review should not be dismissed as technical artifacts; they are valuable signals that point to genuine biological complexity, including strain-specific adaptations, niche-specific regulation, and host-pathogen co-evolution. Only by embracing this complexity, and by committing to rigorous, model-appropriate experimentation, can we translate basic discoveries into effective vaccines and therapeutics against this deadly zoonotic pathogen. The field’s willingness to confront these complexities will determine whether the next decade brings meaningful clinical progress or merely more virulence factors without therapeutic applications.
