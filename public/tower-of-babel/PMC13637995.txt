Crystal structures of products from the BBr3-promoted de­methyl­ation of 3-(meth­oxymeth­yl)-6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zine

Jona Voss, Jan-Hendrik Lamm, M. Weil

Abstract

A (meth­oxy­meth­yl)-substituted 1,2,4,5-tetra­zine was treated with BBr3 to obtain both the (hy­droxy­meth­yl)- and (bromo­meth­yl)-substituted 1,2,4,5-tetra­zines; all three compounds were characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction measurements, which revealed the formation of inter­molecular π–π stacks for all tetra­zines, as well as hydrogen-bonded dimers of the tetra­zine alcohol.
The BBr3-promoted de­methyl­ation of 3-(meth­oxy­meth­yl)-6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zine, C10H10N4O, resulted in the alcohol (6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetrazin-3-yl)methanol, C9H8N4O, and the bromide 3-(bromo­meth­yl)-6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zine, C9H7BrN4. All three compounds were analysed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy in solution and by single-crystal X-ray diffraction using non-spherical scattering factors for structure refinement. The substrate as well as the corresponding bromide form π–π inter­actions between anti­parallel stacks of mol­ecules. In contrast, due to the inter­molecular hydrogen bonds present, which lead to the formation of a bent dimer, the alcohol forms only very weak parallel π–π stacks. Corresponding O—H⋯N hydrogen bonds are established between the hy­droxy group and an N atom of a neighbouring tetra­zine mol­ecule, an only rarely explored structural feature of 1,2,4,5-tetra­zines.

Chemical context

1,2,4,5-Tetra­zines, first described by Pinner (1893), are electron-deficient heterocycles of particular relevance in fast and chemoselective cyclo­addition reactions (Zhao et al., 2022). While structurally simple 1,2,4,5-tetra­zines (herein further abbreviated as tetra­zines) are well-known in the literature, for many modern applications, tetra­zines substituted at the 3- and 6-positions are needed (Yim et al., 2026). In particular, tetra­zines modified with a hy­droxy­methyl group at the 3-position and derivatives thereof are frequently used as marker mol­ecules in biochemical applications (Baalmann et al., 2019; Tu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). Early reports on the synthesis and derivatization of such tetra­zines were provided by Lang et al. (1975) through the synthesis of a panel of 3,6-disubstituted tetra­zines. Out of this library, 3-(meth­oxy­meth­yl)-6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zine (1) was synthesized (Tu et al., 2019), and the BBr3-promoted de­methyl­ation re-investigated to obtain the deprotected alcohol (2) and the bromide (3) as a side product (Fig. 1). In the scope of the present investigations, tetra­zines (1)–(3) were characterised by NMR spectroscopy in solution and by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.[e-82-01221-scheme1.webp]

Structural commentary

While the structures of tetra­zine (1) and alcohol (2) were clearly confirmed by NMR spectroscopy in solution with 13C{1H} NMR shifts of the methyl­ene groups at 72.3 and 63.0 ppm, respectively, the strong upfield shift of the bromo­methyl group of bromide (3) to 27.8 ppm hints to the introduction of a strongly deshielding substituent. However, unambiguous structural proof was given by the results of single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

Both the substrate (1) and the bromide (3) crystallize in the triclinic space group P with one mol­ecule in the asymmetric unit each (Fig. 2). In contrast, alcohol (2) crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/n with a hydrogen-bonded dimer in the asymmetric unit (Fig. 3). The two crystallographically independent mol­ecules (2A/2B) show close conformational similarity with a root-mean-square deviation of 0.169 Å (Fig. 3, right). The phenyl units of all tetra­zines (1)–(3) show similar bond lengths for all C—C bonds [1.391 (2)–1.401 (2) Å] and all C—N and N—N bonds of the tetra­zine units [1.308 (1)–1.353 (1) Å], indicative of their aromaticity with an extended co-planar arrangement of the biaryl moieties. The dihedral angles between the phenyl and tetra­zine rings are 3.63 (4)° (1), 2.29 (4)° (2A), 8.47 (5)° (2B), and 2.72 (1)° (3). All bond angles agree with values obtained from structurally similar disubstituted tetra­zines containing a phenyl and substituted methyl substituents (for details see Section 4). The meth­oxy­methyl group of tetra­zine (1) and the bromo­methyl group of bromide (3) accommodate the largest atoms (O and Br), resulting in the smallest torsion angles with 73.17 (8)° for the N1—C2—C1—O1 connection and −74.63 (17)° for the N4—C2—C1—Br1 connection, likely due to repulsion with the N-centered lone pairs of the tetra­zine units. Inter­estingly, the N1—C2—C1—O1 torsion angles of alcohol (2) are different from those of (1) and (3) with −162.92 (7)° for (2A) and 16.06 (9)° for (2B), an observation that may be explained by the inter­molecular hydrogen-bonding inter­actions between hy­droxy groups and tetra­zine-N atoms (see Section 3).

Supra­molecular features

All of the tetra­zines investigated here show π–π inter­actions, the strongest between anti­parallel stacked mol­ecules of tetra­zine (1) and bromide (3) with centroid-to-centroid distances of 3.724 (1) Å [slippage of 1.676 (1) Å] between the phen­yl/tetra­zine rings for (1) and of 3.716 (1) Å [slippage of 1.643 (3) Å] for (3)], respectively (Fig. 4).

Two hydrogen bonds between hy­droxy groups and tetra­zine N atoms of mol­ecules (2A) and (2B) form a bent dimer of alcohol (2), as shown in Fig. 5, bottom. The drastic reduction in the N1—C2—C1—O1 torsion angle compared with tetra­zine (1) may be explained by the reduced electron density at the neighbouring N atom, which is attributable to its role as a hydrogen-bond acceptor (Table 1). Similar hydrogen-bonding inter­actions were described previously for hy­droxy­methyl substituted tetra­zines (Voss et al., 2025).

Inter­estingly, the inter­molecular hydrogen bonds of alcohol (2) cause the formation of weakly inter­acting parallel stacks of mol­ecules without contacts between phen­yl/tetra­zine ring centroids but with comparably elongated phen­yl/phenyl and tetra­zine/tetra­zine centroid-to-centroid distances of 4.557 (1) Å [slippage of 3.245 (1) Å] and 4.622 (1) Å [slippage of 3.311 (1) Å], respectively (Fig. 5, top). The observation of stronger inter­actions between anti­parallel stacks of tetra­zine (1) and the bromide (3) as compared to parallel stacks of alcohol (2) might be favoured by the increased dipole moment between phenyl and tetra­zine rings in anti­parallel stacks (Huber et al., 2014; Voss et al., 2025).

Database survey

A search of the Cambridge Structural Database (CSD version 6.01, November 2025; Groom et al., 2016) using ConQuest (version 2026.1.0) revealed eight reports for 3-aryl-6-alkyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zines, including CSD refcodes VEMZUM (Voss et al., 2025), CICPOU (Hu & Xu, 2008) and REWDUT (Xu & Hu, 2007). Out of these, the lengths of all aromatic C—C, C—N and N—N bonds are identical within a 3σ margin of error. With dihedral angles between the phenyl and tetra­zine rings of tetra­zines (1)–(3) in the range 2.29 (4) to 8.47 (5)°, similar values between the two rings are also reported for these structurally similar tetra­zines.

Synthesis and crystallisation

Materials and methods

Solvents and starting materials were used without further purification, unless noted otherwise. Di­chloro­methane for reactions was dried by distillation over CaH2. The solvents used for extraction and chromatography were of technical grade and were distilled prior to use. Column chromatography was performed with silica gel 60 M (Acros, 0.04–0.063 mm). Reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) carried out on silica gel plates (Merck, F254) using UV light (254 nm) for visualization. 3-(Meth­oxy­meth­yl)-6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zine (1) was used as starting material and synthesised following a literature protocol (Tu et al., 2019).

Analytical devices

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectra were recorded on an Avance 500 spectrometer (Bruker) at 298 K using the residual protonated solvent signal [δ(1H of CHCl3) = 7.26 ppm, δ(13C of CDCl3) = 77.36 ppm] (Fulmer et al., 2010) as inter­nal standard. Electrospray ionisation (ESI) mass spectra (MS) were acquired with an Esquire 3000 ion trap mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonik) equipped with a nano-ESI source.

De­methyl­ation of 3-(meth­oxy­meth­yl)-6-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetra­zine (1)

This reaction was performed under an argon atmosphere. Tetra­zine (1) (593 mg, 2.93 mmol) was dissolved in dry CH2Cl2 (8 ml) and the resulting pink solution cooled to 258 K. A solution of BBr3 (0.70 ml, 7.38 mmol) in dry CH2Cl2 (15 ml) was added dropwise to this solution over the course of 10 min while maintaining the temperature. The resulting orange solution was stirred at 258 K for 105 min after which a small part of the solution (50 μl) was withdrawn, added to H2O (0.3 ml), and the organic layer analysed by TLC (CH2Cl2), which indicated full consumption of tetra­zine (1). After a total of 155 min, the reaction mixture was poured onto ice (50 g) and CH2Cl2 (100 ml) was added consecutively. After the ice had melted, the layers were separated and the aqueous layer extracted with CH2Cl2 (1×80 ml). The combined pink organic layers were washed with brine (40 ml), dried over MgSO4, filtered and evaporated to obtain a pink solid (481 mg). Purification by column chromatography (diameter = 4.5 cm, length = 23 cm) using CH2Cl2/MeOH (95:5, v/v) to firstly obtain bromide (3) (90.0 mg, 358 μmol, 12%) as a pink solid. Secondly, tetra­zine (1) (42.3 mg, 209 μmol, 7%) was re-isolated as a pink solid. Note that since analysis by TLC indicated complete consumption of tetra­zine (1) a small amount of it must have been formed during the work-up, likely by methyl­ation of alcohol (2) through in situ formed bromo­methane. Thirdly, a mixture of different compounds and alcohol (2) (333 mg, Rf (CH2Cl2/MeOH, 95:5, v/v) = 0.22–0.07) was obtained. Purification of the third fraction by column chromatography (diameter = 4.5 cm, length = 33 cm) using CH2Cl2/MeOH (97.5:2.5, v/v) obtained alcohol (2) (291 mg, 1.55 mmol, 53%) as a pink solid. In Lang’s pioneering work (Lang et al., 1975), alcohol (2) was described as ‘relatively unstable’, a finding which we are unable to confirm – all the compounds isolated here remained stable in solid form at room temperature for over a year.

Analytical data of tetra­zine (1): 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3) δ [ppm]: 8.63 (m, 2H, o-Car­ylH), 7.67–7.57 (m, 3H, m-/p-Car­ylH), 5.10 (s, 2H, CH2), 3.64 (s, 3H, CH3). 13C{1H}NMR (125 MHz{500 MHz}, CDCl3) δ [ppm]: 166.3 (Car­ylCH2), 165.2 (Car­ylPh), 133.1 (p-Car­yl), 131.7 (Car­yl attached to tetra­zine), 129.5 (m-Car­yl), 128.4 (o-Car­yl), 72.3 (CH2), 59.8 (CH3). MS (ESI, positive): m/z = 225.0 [C10H10N4O+Na]+, 427.1 [2·(C10H10N4O)+Na]+. Rf (CH2Cl2/MeOH, 95:5, v/v) = 0.25). Tetra­zine (1) was crystallized by slow evaporation from a CH2Cl2/MeOH solution at room temperature.

Analytical data of alcohol (2): 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3) δ [ppm]: 8.62 (m, 2H, o-Car­ylH), 7.66 (m, 1H, p-Car­ylH), 7.61 (m, 2H, m-Car­ylH), 5.34 (d, 3J = 6.2 Hz, 2H, CH2), 3.12 (t, 3J = 6.3 Hz, 1H, OH). 13C{1H}NMR (125 MHz{500 MHz}, CDCl3) δ [ppm]: 167.4 (Car­ylCH2), 165.6 (Car­ylPh), 133.2 (p-Car­yl), 131.6 (Car­yl attached to tetra­zine), 129.5 (m-Car­yl), 128.4 (o-Car­yl), 63.0 (CH2). MS (ESI, negative): m/z = 186.9 [C9H8N4O–H]−. Rf (CH2Cl2/MeOH, 97.5:2.5, v/v) = 0.20). Alcohol (2) was crystallized by slow evaporation from a CH2Cl2/cyclo­hexane solution at room temperature.

Analytical data of bromide (3): 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3) δ [ppm]: 8.63 (m, 2H, o-Car­ylH), 7.66 (m, 1H, p-Car­ylH), 7.61 (m, 2H, m-Car­ylH), 5.00 (s, 2H, CH2). 13C{1H}NMR (125 MHz{500 MHz}, CDCl3) δ [ppm]: 167.4 (Car­ylCH2), 164.3 (Car­ylPh), 133.4 (p-Car­yl), 131.4 (Car­yl attached to tetra­zine), 129.6 (m-Car­yl), 128.6 (o-Car­yl), 27.8 (CH2). MS (ESI): the compound was not ionisable. Rf (CH2Cl2/MeOH, 95:5, v/v) = 0.79. Bromide (3) was crystallized by slow evaporation from a CH2Cl2/MeOH solution at room temperature.
