Trapping and commutative Boolean networks
Maximilien Gadouleau

Abstract
A Boolean network is a transformation of the set of Boolean configurations of a given length. Trapspaces of Boolean networks have been garnering attention due to their theoretical and applicative significance. A trapspace is a subcube (i.e. a set of configurations defined by fixing certain components) invariant by the Boolean network; a principal trapspace is the smallest trapspace containing a given configuration; a minimal trapspace is one that does not contain any smaller trapspace. In an unrelated development, commutative Boolean networks have been introduced as those networks where all local updates commute. In this paper, we relate those two aspects of Boolean network theory. We make five main contributions. First, we introduce the trapping graph and the trapping closure of a Boolean network. We also define trapping networks as the networks with transitive general asynchronous graphs and we prove that those are exactly the trapping closures. Second, we show that two Boolean networks have the same collection of (principal) trapspaces if and only if they have the same trapping closure. As such, trapping networks are a normal form for the study of trapspaces, for the trapping closure is the network with the most asynchronous transitions for a given collection of trapspaces. We then characterise the collections of (principal) trapspaces of Boolean networks. We finally give analogous results for the collections of minimal trapspaces. Third, we prove that commutative networks are a particular class of trapping networks, and we classify the collections of principal trapspaces of commutative networks. Fourth, we focus on bijective commutative networks, which we refer to as Marseille networks. We provide several alternative definitions for Marseille networks, and we classify them as special commutative or trapping networks. Fifth, we focus on idempotent commutative networks, which we refer to as Lille networks. We provide several alternative definitions for Lille networks, we classify them as special commutative or trapping networks, and we relate them to globally idempotent networks. Lille networks are the globally idempotent commutative networks; we then prove that globally idempotent networks are trapping, and we provide alternative definitions for those networks as well. Our investigations of Marseille and Lille networks also highlight relations amongst the asynchronous, general asynchronous, and trapping graphs of Boolean networks, as well as the structure of trapping networks in general.

Boolean networks

Boolean networks are a fundamental framework for addressing complex systems, with prominent applications in biology, ecology, and social sciences

A Boolean network represents a network of
n

has a Boolean state
x_{i}\in\mathbb{B}
, which evolves over time according to a deterministic function
f_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

Mathematically, a Boolean network is simply a mapping

as input and returns

Applying

to

Of course, different entities may update their state according to different schedules, yielding (general) asynchronous updates. Since the original works by Kauffman
[
12
]
and Thomas
[
20
]
, asynchronous updates have been widely studied, both in terms of modelling purposes and of dynamical analysis (see
[
5
,
3
]
and references therein). The update of a subset

of entities can be represented by
f^{(S)}
, where
f^{(S)}(x)=(f_{S}(x),x_{-S})
, and updating
S
and
T
successively can be represented by

. In the fully asynchronous case, only updates of the form
f^{(i)}
for some
i\in[n]
occur. All the general asynchronous transitions of the form
x\to f^{(S)}(x)
are collected in the general asynchronous of
f
, while the asynchronous graph of
f
only considers asynchronous transitions of the form
x\to f^{(i)}(x)
.

Trapspaces

Arguably the most well studied property of Boolean networks is their fixed points, i.e.
x

f(x)=x
(see
[
8
,
1

2
,
17
]

Beyond their intrinsic significance as “stable states” of the modelled network, fixed points have an important theoretical property: they are immune to changes in the update schedule.
Indeed, if

is also a fixed point of

for any
S\subseteq[n]
.

A trapspace of a Boolean network can be viewed as a “localised” fixed point, where only part of the network is fixed.
More formally, a trapspace is an invariant subcube: it is a set of the form
X=\{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}:x_{S}=0,x_{T}=1\}
obtained by fixing some states which satisfies
f(X)\subseteq X
, so that those states remain fixed
[
13
]
.
Trapspaces have garnered a lot of interest due to their significance in biological applications. Moreover, like fixed points, they are also immune to changes in the update schedule, which makes them theoretically important.

A trapspace is minimal if it does not contain any smaller trapspace. Minimal trapspaces of networks have been notably studied for their relation with limit (ultimately
periodic) configurations: each minimal trapspace necessarily contains at least one limit configuration

[

,
15
]

Preliminaries

Boolean configurations and subcubes

We denote the Boolean set by
\mathbb{B}=\{0,1\}
and for any positive integer
n
, we denote
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. A
configuration
is
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. For any
S\subseteq[n]
, we denote
x_{S}=(x_{s}:s\in S)
and
x_{-S}=(x_{t}:t\notin S)
, and we use the notation
x=(x_{S},x_{-S})
. We shall identify an element
i\in[n]
with the corresponding singleton
\{i\}
, so that
x=(x_{i},x_{-i})
for instance. For any two configurations
x,y\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, we denote the set of positions where they differ by
\Delta(x,y)=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}
and their Hamming distance by
d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)=|\Delta(x,y)|
. For any Boolean variable
a\in\mathbb{B}
, we denote its negation by
\neg a=1-a
; we extend this notation to configurations of any length by componentwise negation:
\neg x=(\neg x_{1},\dots,\neg x_{n})
.

A
subcube
of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is any
X\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{n}
such that there exist two disjoint sets of positions
S,T\subseteq[n]
with
X=\{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}:x_{S}=0,x_{T}=1\}
. For any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{n}
, the principal subcube of
A
, denoted by
[A]
, is the smallest subcube containing
A
. If
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
, we also denote
[A]=[a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
. If
X
is a subcube and
x\in X
, then there is a unique
y\in X
such that
X=[x,y]
; we refer to
y
as the
opposite
of
x
in
X
, and we denote it by
y=X-x
. We denote the set of subcubes of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
by
\mathrm{S}(n)
and the set of all collections of subcubes of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
by
\mathrm{A}(n)=2^{\mathrm{S}(n)}
.

Boolean networks

A
Boolean network
(or simply, network) of dimension
n
is a mapping
f:\mathbb{B}^{n}\to\mathbb{B}^{n}
. We denote the set
of networks of dimension
n
as

.
For any

, we refer to the subcube

as the

of

with respect to

.
Any network

can be viewed as

where

is given by

for

. For any

In particular,

. We note the distinction between the update

The lattice of Boolean networks

The networks in
\mathrm{F}(n)
have a natural partial order in terms of their transitions. For any two graphs
\Gamma_{1}=(V,E_{1})
,
\Gamma_{2}=(V,E_{2})
on the same vertex

, we write
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\Gamma_{2}
as a shorthand for
E_{1}\subseteq E_{2}
.

Consider the relation

on

given by the four equivalent conditions:

•

\Delta(x,f(x))\subseteq\Delta(x,g(x))
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
;

•

[x,f(x)]\subseteq[x,g(x)]
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
;

•

\mathtt{GA}(f)\subseteq\mathtt{GA}(g)
;

•

\mathtt{A}(f)\subseteq\mathtt{A}(g)
.

The

partial order induces a lattice
\mathcal{F}_{n}=(\mathrm{F}(n),\sqcup,\sqcap,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
(a Boolean algebra isomorphic to
2^{E}
, where
E=\{(u,v):u,v\in\mathbb{B}^{n},d_{\mathrm{H}}(u,v)=1\}
is the set of arcs of the hypercube) with

\displaystyle\mathtt{GA}(f\sqcup g)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{GA}(f)\cup\mathtt{GA}(g)

\displaystyle\mathtt{GA}(f\sqcap g)

\displaystyle=\mathtt{GA}(f)\cap\mathtt{GA}(g)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}\quad(\text{the identity network: }f(x)=x)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\neg\quad(\text{the negation network: }f(x)=\neg x).

We note that the ordering behaves well when considering updates of subsets:
f^{(S)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S\cup T)}
for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
and
f\sqsubseteq g\implies f^{(S)}\sqsubseteq g^{(S)}
.

x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Firstly,
T_{f}(x)\subseteq T_{g}(x)
because
f\sqsubseteq g
. Conversely, for any
y\in T_{f}(x)
, we have
T_{f}(y)=[y,g(y)]\subseteq T_{f}(x)
, hence
g(y)\in T_{f}(x)
. Therefore
T_{f}(x)
is a trapspace of
g
containing
y
, thus
T_{g}(x)\subseteq T_{f}(x)
.
∎

We can now give an algebraic characterisation of
{f}^{\mathrm{T}}
.

Corollary 3.3
.

For all
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
we have

{f}^{\mathrm{T}}=\bigsqcap\{g\in{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}(n):f\sqsubseteq g\}.

Proof.

Immediately follows from
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
applied to
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

3.2
Trapping networks

We say a network is
trapping
if its general asynchronous graph is transitive. Denote the set of all trapping networks in
\mathrm{F}(n)
by
{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}(n)
. We now provide a list of equivalent definitions of trapping networks.

Many proofs of our results will be broken down into smaller proofs of the form “Property
A

\implies
Property
B
”. For all such proofs, we omit the introductory sentence: “Let
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
satisfy
A
.”

Theorem 3.4

(Alternate definitions of trapping networks)
.

For all
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
, the following are equivalent:

1.

f
is trapping, i.e.
\mathtt{GA}(f)
is transitive,

2.

[y,f(y)]\subseteq[x,f(x)]
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
y\in[x,f(x)]
,

3.

[x,f(x)]=T_{f}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
,

4.

f={f}^{\mathrm{T}}
,

5.

f={g}^{\mathrm{T}}
for some
g\in\mathrm{F}(n)
,

6.

\mathtt{T}(f)=\mathtt{GA}(f)
.

7.

f^{(S,T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S\cup T)}
for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
.

Proof.

\ref{item:g_trapping}\iff\ref{item:delta(y,g(y))}
. By definition.

\ref{item:delta(y,g(y))}\implies\ref{item:trapping_interval=Tf}
. If
f(y)\in[x,f(x)]
for all
y\in[x,f(x)]
, then
[x,f(x)]
is a trapspace. Since
T_{f}(x)
is the smallest trapspace that contains
x
, and
[x,f(x)]\subseteq T_{f}(x)
, we obtain
[x,f(x)]=T_{f}(x)
.

\ref{item:trapping_interval=Tf}\implies\ref{item:delta(y,g(y))}
. If
[x,f(x)]
is a trapspace, then
f(y)\in[x,f(x)]
for all
y\in[x,f(x)]
.

\ref{item:trapping_interval=Tf}\iff\ref{item:g=gt}
. By definition.

\ref{item:g=gt}\implies\ref{item:g=ft}
. Trivial.

\ref{item:g=ft}\implies\ref{item:g=gt}
. Follows directly from (
3
) in Proposition
3.2
.

\ref{item:g=gt}\implies\ref{item:TG(g)=GA(g)}
. If
f={f}^{\mathrm{T}}
, then
\mathtt{GA}(f)=\mathtt{GA}({f}^{\mathrm{T}})=\mathtt{T}(f)
.

\ref{item:TG(g)=GA(g)}\implies\ref{item:g_trapping}
. If
\mathtt{GA}(f)=\mathtt{T}(f)
, then
\mathtt{GA}(f)
is indeed transitive.

\ref{item:trapping_interval=Tf}\implies\ref{item:trapping_ST}
. Suppose
x\to z
in
\mathtt{GA}(f^{(S,T)})
. Denoting
s=f^{(S)}(x)
and
t=f^{(T)}(s)
, we have
s\in[x,f(x)]=T_{f}(x)
,
t\in T_{f}(s)\subseteq T_{f}(x)
, and
z\in[x,t]\subseteq T_{f}(x)=N^{out}(\mathtt{GA}(f);x)
. Since
\Delta(x,z)\subseteq\Delta(x,t)\subseteq S\cup T
, we obtain
z\in N^{out}(\mathtt{GA}(f^{(S\cup T)});x)
. Thus,
f^{(S,T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S\cup T)}
.

\ref{item:trapping_ST}\implies\ref{item:g_trapping}
. Let
x\to y\to z
in
\mathtt{GA}(f)
, so that
y=f^{(S)}(x)
and
z=f^{(T)}(y)=f^{(S,T)}(x)
for some
S,T\subseteq[n]
. We obtain
x\to z
in
\mathtt{GA}(f^{(S,T)})\subseteq\mathtt{GA}(f^{(S\cup T)})\subseteq\mathtt{GA}(f)
. Therefore,
\mathtt{GA}(f)
is transitive.
∎

Theorem
3.4
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

For all
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
,

\mathtt{T}(f)=\mathtt{GA}({f}^{\mathrm{T}})=\mathtt{T}({f}^{\mathrm{T}}).

We can also classify the trapping graphs as the transitive general asynchronous graphs.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Then
\Gamma=\mathtt{T}(f)
for some
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
if and only if
\Gamma
is reflexive transitive and all out-neighbourhoods are subcubes.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
,
\Gamma
is reflexive transitive and all out-neighbourhoods are subcubes if and only if it is a transitive general asynchronous graph, i.e.
\Gamma=\mathtt{GA}(g)
for some trapping network
g
. By Corollary
3.5
, this is equivalent to
\Gamma=\mathtt{T}(f)
for some network
f
.
∎

Trapping graphs form a rich class of graphs. For instance, any
X\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{n}
can appear as an initial strong component of some trapping graph (namely, for
f(x)=\neg x
if

and

otherwise). Note, however, that if

has two distinct strong components

with

, then
[T]\subset[S]
.

4
Collections of (principal) trapspaces

4.1
Boolean networks with the same collection of (principal) trapsapces

We begin this section with a characterisation of networks that have the same collection of trapspaces. In particular, Theorem
4.1
below shows that trapping networks are a canonical form for networks when studying their trapspaces. Recall that the collection of all trapspaces of
f
is denoted by
\mathcal{T}(f)
, while the collection of all principal trapspaces of
f
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(f)
.

Theorem 4.1

(Trapspace equivalent networks)
.

Let
f,g\in\mathrm{F}(n)
. The following are equivalent:

1.

f
and
g
have the same collection of principal trapspaces, i.e.

\mathcal{P}(f)=\mathcal{P}(g)
;

2.

f
and
g
have the same collection of trapspaces, i.e.

\mathcal{T}(f)=\mathcal{T}(g)
;

3.

f
and
g
have the same principal trapspaces pointwise, i.e.

T_{f}(x)=T_{g}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
;

4.

f
and
g
have the same trapping graph, i.e.

\mathtt{T}(f)=\mathtt{T}(g)
;

5.

f
and
g
have the same trapping closure, i.e.

{f}^{\mathrm{T}}={g}^{\mathrm{T}}
.

Proof.

\ref{item:T(f)=T(g)}\implies\ref{item:Tf(x)=Tg(x)}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. On the one hand, since
T_{f}(x)
and
T_{g}(x)
are trapspaces of
f
containing
x
, we have
T_{f}(x)\subseteq T_{g}(x)
. We similarly obtain
T_{g}(x)\subseteq T_{f}(x)
, and hence
T_{f}(x)=T_{g}(x)
.

\ref{item:Tf(x)=Tg(x)}\implies\ref{item:tau(f)=tau(g)}
. We have

A\in\mathcal{T}(f)\iff A=\bigcup\{T_{f}(x):x\in A\}\iff A=\bigcup\{T_{g}(x):x\in A\}\iff A\in\mathcal{T}(g).

\ref{item:tau(f)=tau(g)}\implies\ref{item:T(f)=T(g)}
. Trivial.

\ref{item:Tf(x)=Tg(x)}\iff\ref{item:TG(f)=TG(g)}\iff\ref{item:fT=gT}
. Immediate from the definitions of
\mathtt{T}(f)
and
{f}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For any network
f
,
\mathcal{P}(f)=\mathcal{P}({f}^{\mathrm{T}})
and
\mathcal{T}(f)=\mathcal{T}({f}^{\mathrm{T}})
.

4.2
Classification of collections of (principal) trapspaces

Intuition for the classification

In this section, we shall classify the collections of trapspaces and the collections of principal trapspaces of networks. We shall moreover illustrate a three-way equivalence amongst collections of principal trapspaces, collections of trapspaces, and trapping closures. This equivalence is similar to the situation for pre-orders on sets.

A
pre-order
on a set
\Omega
is a reflexive transitive binary relation on
\Omega
. If
R
is a pre-order on
\Omega
, then any set of the form
S^{\downarrow}=\{y\in\Omega:\exists s\in S,(s,y)\in R\}
for some
S\subseteq\Omega
is an
ideal
of
R
; a
principal ideal
of
R
is any set of the form
x^{\downarrow}=\{y\in\Omega,(x,y)\in R\}
for some
x\in\Omega
. Since
R=\bigcup_{x\in\Omega,y\in x^{\downarrow}}(x,y)
, we see that
R
can be reconstructed from its collection of principal ideals; the same can be said for its collection of ideals. It is well known that a collection of subsets of
\Omega
is the collection of ideals of a pre-order if and only if it is closed under arbitrary unions and intersections; similarly one can classify the collections of principal ideals of pre-orders.
Therefore, for the set
\Omega
, there is a three-way equivalence between a pre-order
R
on
\Omega
, iits collection of ideals, and its collection of principal ideals.

In this paper, we are interested in
\Omega=\mathbb{B}^{n}
, but we do not consider any possible (principal) ideal. Let
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
be a network. Then the reachability relation
R
given by
R=\{(x,y):x\to_{\mathtt{GA}(f)}^{*}y\}
is a pre-order on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. A subcube is an ideal of
R
if and only if it is a trapspace of
f
; it is a principal ideal of
R
if and only if it is a principal trapspace of
f
. The relation
R^{\prime}
given by
y\in T_{f}(x)
is also a pre-order, described by the trapping graph (
(x,y)\in R^{\prime}
if and only if
(x,y)
is an edge of
\mathtt{T}(f)
). This is the smallest pre-order such that all its principal ideals are principal trapspaces of
f
.
Therefore, the main result is a three-way equivalence between a trapping network, its collection of trapspaces, and its collection of principal trapspaces.

The rest of this subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem
4.3
. We first prove item
1
, by characterising the principal collection of subcubes.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathrm{A}(n)
be a collection of subcubes of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. We say
\mathcal{Q}
is
pre-principal
if

\mathcal{Q}=\mu(\mathcal{Q})=\{\mathcal{Q}(x):x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\}.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{Q}
is pre-principal if for any configuration
x
, there exists a smallest subcube in
\mathcal{Q}
that contains
x
, and conversely for any subcube
A\in\mathcal{Q}
, there is a configuration
x
for which
A
is the smallest subcube that contains
x
.

Lemma 4.4
.

A collection
\mathcal{Q}
of subcubes of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is pre-principal if and only if the following hold:

1.

\bigcup\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{B}^{n}
;

2.

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{Q}
, there exists
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
such that
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=A\cap B
;

3.

for all
A\in\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
,
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=A
implies
A\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}
is pre-principal. We prove that it satisfies all three properties.

1.

We have
x\in\mathcal{Q}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, hence
\bigcup\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

2.

For all
A,B\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\bigcup\{\mathcal{Q}(x):x\in A\cap B\}=A\cap B
.

3.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
with
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=A\in\mathcal{Q}
while
A\notin\mathcal{C}
. Then for all
x\in A
, there exists
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{Q}(x)\subseteq C\subset A
. Therefore,
A\notin\{\mathcal{Q}(x):x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\}
, which contradicts the fact that
\mathcal{Q}
is pre-principal.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{Q}
satisfy all three properties. We first prove that
\mathcal{Q}(x)\in\mathcal{Q}
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and consider the collection

\mathcal{S}=\{A\in\mathcal{Q}:x\in A;\forall B\subset A,B\in\mathcal{Q},x\notin B\}

of minimal subcubes in
\mathcal{Q}
that contain
x
. By Property
1
,
|\mathcal{S}|\geq 1
. If
|\mathcal{S}|\geq 2
, let
A,B\in\mathcal{S}
, then there exists
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
such that
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=A\cap B
. As such, there exists
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
x\in C
while
C\subseteq A\cap B\subset A
, which contradicts the fact that
A\in\mathcal{S}
. Therefore,
|\mathcal{S}|=1
, hence
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{Q}(x)\}
.

We now prove that
A\in\{\mathcal{Q}(x):x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\}
for all
A\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
A\in\mathcal{Q}
, and suppose that
\mathcal{Q}(x)\subset A
for all
x\in A
. Then
\mathcal{C}:=\{\mathcal{Q}(x):x\in A\}\subset\mathcal{Q}
satisfies
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=A
while
A\notin\mathcal{C}
, which contradicts the third property.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

A collection of subcubes is principal if and only if it is pre-principal. For all
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathrm{A}^{\mathcal{P}}(n)
and all
g\in{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}(n)
, we have

\mathcal{P}(F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q}))=\mathcal{Q},\qquad F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{P}(g))=g.

Proof.

Firstly, we prove that
F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q})
is trapping. Placing ourselves in the graph
\Gamma=\mathtt{GA}(F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q}))
, if
y\in N^{out}(\Gamma;x)=\mathcal{Q}(x)
, then
N^{out}(\Gamma;y)=\mathcal{Q}(y)\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(x)=N^{out}(\Gamma;x)
, and hence
\Gamma
is transitive.

Secondly, we prove that
\mathcal{P}(g)
is pre-principal by verifying that it satisfies the three properties of Lemma
4.4
. First, since
x\in T_{g}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, we have
\bigcup\mathcal{P}(g)=\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Second, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}(g)
, the collection
\mathcal{C}=\{T_{g}(x):x\in A\cap B\}
satisfies
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(g)
and
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=A\cap B
. Third, if there exists
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(g)\setminus\{T_{g}(x)\}
such that
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=T_{g}(x)
, then there exists
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
x\in C\subset T_{g}(x)
and hence
T_{g}(x)\subseteq C\subset T_{g}(x)
, which is the desired contradiction.

Since any principal collection of subcubes is of the form
\mathcal{P}(g)
for some trapping network
g
, we have just shown that any principal collection of subcubes is pre-principal.

Thirdly, we prove that
\mathcal{P}(F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q}))=\mathcal{Q}
for any pre-principal collection
\mathcal{Q}
of subcubes of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Let
g=F(\mathcal{Q})
. Since
g
is trapping, we have for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}

T_{g}(x)=N^{out}(\mathtt{GA}(g);x)=\mathcal{Q}(x).

Hence
\mathcal{P}(g)=\{\mathcal{Q}(x):x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\}=\mathcal{Q}
since
\mathcal{Q}
is pre-principal.

We have just shown that any pre-principal collection of subcubes is principal. Together with the previous item, we have proved that a collection of subcubes is principal if and only if it is pre-principal.

Fourthly, we prove that
F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{P}(g))=g
for all
g\in{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}(n)
. Let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}(g)
. For all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, we have

T_{g}(x)=\bigcap\{A\in\mathcal{P}(g):x\in A\}=\mathcal{Q}(x)

hence
\mathcal{Q}(x)=[x,g(x)]
(since
g
is trapping). On the other hand, by definition
\mathcal{Q}(x)=[x,F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q})(x)]
, thus
g=F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q})
.
∎

We now prove item
2
, by characterising ideal collections of subcubes. We say that a collection
\mathcal{J}
of subcubes is
pre-ideal
if it satisfies the following three properties:

1.

\mathbb{B}^{n}\in\mathcal{J}
;

2.

\mathcal{J}
is closed under intersection, i.e. if
A,B\in\mathcal{J}
and
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
, then
A\cap B\in\mathcal{J}
;

3.

for any subcollection
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
, if
R=\bigcup\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{S}(n)
, then
R\in\mathcal{J}
.

Intuitively, a pre-ideal collection of subcubes is closed under arbitrary unions and intersections, so long as those unions and intersections are actual subcubes. We note that property
3
above is equivalent to
\mathcal{J}=\lambda(\mathcal{J})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

A collection of subcubes is ideal if and only if it is pre-ideal. For all
\mathcal{J}\in\mathrm{A}^{\mathcal{T}}(n)
and all
g\in{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}(n)
, we have

\mathcal{T}(F_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{J}))=\mathcal{J},\qquad F_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{T}(g))=g.

Proof.

The proof uses a similar structure to that of Theorem
4.5
.

Firstly,
F_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{J})
is trapping. (The proof is the same as its counterpart for
F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Q})
.)

Secondly, we prove that
\mathcal{T}(g)
is pre-ideal. Then
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is a trapspace of
g
so
\mathbb{B}^{n}\in\mathcal{J}
; if
A
and
B
are trapspaces with non-empty intersection, let
x\in A\cap B
, then
g(x)\subseteq A\cap B
, hence
A\cap B
is also a trapspace; and if
R=\bigcup\mathcal{R}
for some
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
, then for any
x\in R
, there exists
A\in\mathcal{J}
such that
x\in A
and hence
g(x)\in A\subseteq R
, thus
R
is also a trapspace.

Since any ideal collection of subcubes is of the form
\mathcal{T}(g)
for some trapping network
g
, we have just shown that any ideal collection of subcubes is pre-ideal.

Thirdly, we prove that
\mathcal{T}(F_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{J}))=\mathcal{J}
for any pre-ideal collection
\mathcal{J}
of subcubes of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Let
g=F_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{J})
so that
[x,g(x)]=\mathcal{J}(x)\in\mathcal{J}
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Suppose
A\in\mathcal{J}
, then for all
x\in A
,
g(x)\in\mathcal{J}(x)\subseteq A
, hence
A\in\mathcal{T}(g)
. Conversely, suppose

Item

5
Commutative networks

5.1
Review of commutative networks

Definitions

In
[
6
]
, Bridoux et al. consider commutative networks, where asynchronous updates can be performed in any order without altering the result. This subsection is devoted to a review of some of the results in
[
6
]
. Foremost, the authors of
[
6
]
are interested in possibly infinite networks, and hence distinguish between so-called locally and globally commutative networks. However, these two concepts coincide for the finite Boolean networks we study in this paper. As such, we say a network
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
is
commutative
if it satisfies the following three equivalent properties:

1.

for all
i,j\in[n]
,
f^{(i,j)}=f^{(j,i)}
;

2.

for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
,
f^{(S,T)}=f^{(T,S)}
;

3.

for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
with
S\cap T=\emptyset
,
f^{(S,T)}=f^{(S\cup T)}
.

Properties

Commutative networks have some strong structural properties. Intuitively, they all stem from the key property that any update can be “serialised”, i.e. for any
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, we have

f^{(S)}=f^{(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})}=f^{(s_{k})}\circ\dots\circ f^{(s_{1})}.

For instance, for any property
P
of Boolean networks, we say
f
is
locally
P
if
f^{(i)}
satisfies
P
for all
i\in[n]
and globally
P
if
f^{(S)}
satisfies
P
for all
S\subseteq[n]
.
As such, a network
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
is
bijective
if
f
is a bijection (i.e., a permutation of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
);
f
is
locally bijective
if
f^{(i)}
is bijective for all
i\in[n]
;
f
is
globally bijective
if
f^{(S)}
is a bijection for all
S\subseteq[n]
. If
f
is a locally bijective commutative network, then for all
S\subseteq[n]
,
f^{(S)}=f^{(s_{k})}\circ\dots\circ f^{(s_{1})}
is also bijective, i.e.
f
is globally bijective. Bridoux et al. go further, and show that the following are equivalent for a commutative network:

1.

f
is bijective;

2.

f
is locally bijective;

3.

f
is globally bijective.

Moreover, recall that
f
is
idempotent
if
f^{2}=f
. The following are also equivalent for a commutative network:

1.

f
is idempotent;

2.

f
is locally idempotent;

3.

f
is globally idempotent.

Commutative networks have heavily constrained dynamics. Any function
\phi:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}
has transient length at most
1
and period at most
2
, and hence
\phi^{3}=\phi
. We then call a network
f

dynamically local
if
f^{3}=f
. Clearly, for any
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
and any
i\in[n]
the update
f^{(i)}
is dynamically local. In fact, commutative networks are also dynamically local, i.e. that they have transient length at most
1
and period at most
2
. Moreover, a network
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
is
involutive
if
f
is an involution, i.e.
f^{2}=\mathrm{id}
. Any involutive network is bijective, and as seen above any bijective commutative network is involutive.

5.2
Commutative networks are trapping

This subsection is devoted to comparing commutative networks to trapping networks. Recall (Theorem
3.4
) that a network is trapping if and only if for any
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and any
y\in[x,f(x)]
,

[y,f(y)]\subseteq[x,f(x)].

Theorem 5.2

(Alternate definitions of commutative networks)
.

Any commutative network is trapping. More precisely, the following are equivalent for
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
:

1.

f
is commutative, i.e.
f^{(i,j)}=f^{(j,i)}
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

2.

[y,f(x)]\subseteq[y,f(y)]\subseteq[x,f(x)]
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and any
y\in[x,f(x)]
.

3.

f^{(S\Delta T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S,T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S\cup T)}
for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
.

Proof.

\ref{item:commutative_definition}\implies\ref{item:commutative_xy}
. Let
f
be a commutative network,
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
y\in[x,f(x)]
. Denote
y=f^{(S)}(x)
for some
S=\Delta(x,y)\subseteq\Delta(x,f(x))
. We first prove
[y,f(y)]\subseteq[x,f(x)]
. For all
i\notin\Delta(x,f(x))
, we have

f_{i}(y)=f_{i}(f^{(S)}(x))=f_{i}(x)=x_{i}=y_{i},

hence
i\notin\Delta(y,f(y))
. We now prove that
[y,f(x)]\subseteq[y,f(y)]
. For all
j\in\Delta(y,f(x))=\Delta(x,f(x))\setminus S
, we have

f_{j}(y)=f_{j}(f^{(S)}(x))=f_{j}(x)\neq x_{j}=y_{j},

hence
j\in\Delta(y,f(y))
.

\ref{item:commutative_xy}\implies\ref{item:commutative_definition}
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
f
is not commutative, i.e. there exist
i,j\in[n]
and
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
such that
f_{i}(f^{(j)}(x))\neq f_{i}(x)
. Denoting
y=f^{(j)}(x)
, we have
y\in[x,f(x)]
. If
i\in\Delta(x,f(x))
, then
\Delta(y,f(x))\not\subseteq\Delta(y,f(y))
; if
i\notin\Delta(x,f(x))
, then
\Delta(y,f(y))\not\subseteq\Delta(x,f(x))
. In either case, we obtain a contradiction.

\ref{item:commutative_definition}\implies\ref{item:commutative_ST}
. Since
f
is trapping, we have
f^{(S,T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S\cup T)}
. Moreover, by commutativity,

f^{(S,T)}=f^{(S\Delta T,S\cap T,S\cap T)},

and hence
f^{(S,T)}_{S\Delta T}=f_{S\Delta T}
, which implies
f^{(S\Delta T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S,T)}
.

\ref{item:commutative_ST}\implies\ref{item:commutative_definition}
. If
S\cap T=\emptyset
, we have
S\Delta T=S\cup T
and hence
f^{(S,T)}=f^{(S\cup T)}
. Therefore,
f
is commutative.
∎

5.3
Collections of principal trapspaces of commutative networks

We now classify the collections of principal trapspaces of commutative networks. Say a collection of subcubes
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{A}(n)
is
convex
if the following holds: for all
Q,R\in\mathcal{A}
and any
S
with
Q\subseteq S\subseteq R
,
S\in\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 5.3

(Commutative networks and convex collections of principal trapspaces)
.

A pre-principal collection of subcubes is the collection of principal trapspaces of a commutative network if and only if it is convex.

We begin the proof by a technical lemma. For any
f\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and any
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, denote the dimension of
T_{f}(x)
as
\delta_{x}=d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,f(x))
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
f
be a commutative network. For any
y\in[x,f(x)]
, we have

d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)\geq\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}\geq 0,

and
d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)=\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}
if and only if
f(y)=f(x)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
y\in[x,f(x)]
. Firstly, since
f(y)\in[x,f(x)]
, we immediately obtain
\delta_{x}\geq\delta_{y}
. Secondly, we have

\delta_{y}+d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)=d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(y))+d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)\geq d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(x))+d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)\geq d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,f(x))=\delta_{x},

where we used Theorem
5.2
to show
d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(y))\geq d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(x))
. Thirdly, by the above, we have
d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)=\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}
only if
d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(y))=d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(x))
. Since
f(x)\in[y,f(y)]
, this is equivalent to
f(x)=f(y)
. Finally, if
f(x)=f(y)
, then

\delta_{x}=d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,f(x))=d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)+d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(x))=d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)+d_{\mathrm{H}}(y,f(y))=d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)+\delta_{y}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Let
f
be a commutative network. Now, suppose that
T_{f}(y)\subseteq T_{f}(x)
and that
x
and
y
are nearest possible, i.e. if
T_{f}(x^{\prime})=T_{f}(x)
and
T_{f}(y^{\prime})\subseteq T_{f}(y)
, then
d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)\leq d_{\mathrm{H}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

Claim 1
.

With the conditions above,
f(x)=f(y)
.

Proof.

We first prove that
y\in[x,f(y)]
. If there exists

satisfies

and

, which contradicts our assumptions. Therefore,
\Delta(y,f(y))\cap\Delta(x,y)=\emptyset
. We obtain that for all

,

y_{j}\neq x_{j}\implies y_{j}=f(y)_{j}\neq x_{j},

which is equivalent to
\Delta(y,x)\subseteq\Delta(x,f(y))
. Similarly, we obtain
\Delta(y,f(y))\subseteq\Delta(x,f(y))
. Thus,
y\in[x,f(y)]
.

Now,
f(x)\in[y,f(y)]
because

is commutative, hence

[x,f(y)]=[x,y,f(y)]=[x,y,f(y),f(x)]=[x,f(x)],

thus
f(y)=f(x)
.
∎

Any subcube

satisfying
T_{f}(y)=[y,f(x)]\subseteq S\subseteq[x,f(x)]=T_{f}(x)
can be expressed as

for some

. Therefore, we only need to prove that

for all

. Since

, we obtain

f^{(\Delta(z,y))}(z)=f^{(\Delta(z,y))}(f^{(\Delta(x,z))}(x))=f^{(\Delta(x,y))}(x)=y.

Therefore,
y\in[z,f(z)]
. We now repeatedly use Lemma

. Since

, we have
d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)=\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}
. We obtain

d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y)=\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}=(\delta_{x}-\delta_{z})+(\delta_{z}-\delta_{y})\leq d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,z)+d_{\mathrm{H}}(z,y)=d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,y),

thus
d_{\mathrm{H}}(x,z)=\delta_{x}-\delta_{z}
, and hence

, and we are done.

Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a convex pre-principal collection of subcubes and let
f=F_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{A})
; recall that
f
is trapping. We only need to prove that
f(x)\in[y,f(y)]
for all

7.3
Globally idempotent networks

In this subsection, we give alternate definitions for globally idempotent networks.

Theorem 7.4

(Alternate definitions of globally idempotent networks)
.

Globally idempotent networks are trapping. More precisely, the following are equivalent for
f\in\mathrm{F}(n)
:

1.

f
is globally idempotent, i.e.
f^{(S,S)}=f^{(S)}
for all
S\subseteq[n]
.

2.

[y,f(y)]\subseteq[y,f(x)]
for all
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and any
y\in[x,f(x)]
.

3.

f^{(S,T)}\sqsupseteq f^{(S\cap T)}
for all
S,T\subseteq[n]
.

Proof.

\ref{item:globally_idempotent_definition}\implies\ref{item:globally_idempotent_xy}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
y\in[x,f(x)]
. For any
i\in\Delta(y,f(y))
, we have
y_{i}\neq f_{i}(y)=f_{i}(x)
, hence
i\in\Delta(y,f(x))
.

\ref{item:globally_idempotent_xy}\implies\ref{item:globally_idempotent_definition}
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exist
S\subseteq[n]
,
i\in S
, and
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
such that
f_{i}(f^{(S)}(x))\neq f_{i}(x)
. Since
f^{(S)}(x)=f^{(S\cap\Delta(x,f(x)))}(x)
, we can assume
S\subseteq\Delta(x,f(x))
. Denoting
y=f^{(S)}(x)
, we have
y\in[x,f(x)]
,
y_{i}=f_{i}(x)=\neg f_{i}(y)
. Thus,
i\in\Delta(y,f(y))\subseteq\Delta(y,f(x))
, which is the desired contradiction.

\ref{item:globally_idempotent_definition}\implies\ref{item:globally_idempotent_ST}
.

f
is trapping, hence
f^{(S,T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S\cup T)}
. Moreover,

f^{(S,T)}=f^{(S,T\setminus S)},

and hence
f^{(S,T)}_{S\cap T}=f_{S\cap T}
, which implies
f^{(S\cap T)}\sqsubseteq f^{(S,T)}
.

\ref{item:globally_idempotent_ST}\implies\ref{item:globally_idempotent_definition}
. We have
f^{(S)}=f^{(S,S)}
for all
S\subseteq[n]
, i.e.
f
is globally idempotent.

8
Conclusion

Summary of results

This paper tied together the topics of trapspaces of Boolean networks and of commutative networks.
We have introduced trapping networks, that generalise commutative network and are a normal form for the study of trapspaces of networks.
We have also classified the collections of trapspaces and of principal trapspaces of networks.
We have then focused on two particular classes of commutative networks, namely Marseille (bijective commutative) and Lille (idempotent commutative) networks.
Those two classes are very well structured (for instance, one may extract over twenty different definitions of Lille networks in the paper) and highlight the broader structure of commutative and trapping networks at large.

Future work

We identify three main avenues for potential future work. First, one may want to develop the theory of trapping networks. In particular, one may classify networks of a particular class (e.g. linear, monotone, increasing, or with particular interaction graphs) that are trapping. Second, we have classified the collections of principal trapspaces as the so-called pre-principal collections of subcubes, which have a “simple” definition (i.e.
\mathcal{Q}=\mu(\mathcal{Q})
). However, we the computational complexity of deciding whether a collection of subcubes is pre-principal remains unknown. The same holds for collections of trapspaces, which are the pre-ideal collections of subcubes. Third, trapping networks are one way of generalising commutative networks; Table
1
naturally suggests other ways of doing so. It would be interesting to see how different generalisations of commutativity behave, both in terms of their dynamical properties and whether classifications such as in Figures
3
and
5
can be derived.
