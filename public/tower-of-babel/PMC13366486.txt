Self-Growing and Mechanically Tunable Living Materials with Paenibacillus mucilaginosus Extracellular Polymeric Substances
Authors: Yifan Cui, Stéphane Bernhard, Mathias Steinacher, Nika Petelinsek, Lorenzo Minervini, Stefan Mommer, Mark W. Tibbitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineered living materials (ELMs) typically embed functional cells in nonliving matrices, limiting growth, remodeling, and long-term adaptation at the material level. Here, we introduce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) produced by Paenibacillus mucilaginosus as a living carrier matrix for ELMs. P. mucilaginosus EPS possesses suitable rheological behavior, such as shear thinning and elastic recovery, required for extrusion-based fabrication. Its mechanical properties can be further tuned through dynamic covalent cross-linking via boronate ester formation or physical reinforcement with Laponite or xanthan gum. With boronate ester cross-linking (18 mM), the storage modulus increased from 100 to 800 Pa. The inclusion of 0.5 w/v % Laponite further increased the modulus to 1300 Pa. Uniquely, a living matrix comprised ofP. mucilaginosus EPS provides a substrate for material growth. As a proof of concept, we manufactured photosynthetic living materials by embedding a photosynthetic cyanobacterium strain,Synechococcus sp. PCC 7002, in the EPS matrix and printed into defined architectures. Both P. mucilaginosus and cyanobacterium PCC 7002 remained viable and grew over 30 days. Matrix outgrowth and microbial colonization enabled the expansion of the living constructs within the printed structure. This work establishes P. mucilaginosus EPS as a candidate living scaffold for self-growing and mechanically tunable ELMs.

Introduction

A defining advantage of engineered living materials (ELMs) is that they endow otherwise nonliving and static materials with active, smart behaviors, such as sensing, actuation, and self-repair. These properties position ELMs as promising platforms for applications ranging from environmental remediation to biosensing and biomanufacturing. Current ELMs primarily embed functional microorganisms within nonliving carrier matrices that provide mechanical support, physical containment, and a suitable living environment. While this current strategy ensures cell viability and enables the desired biological output, the carrier matrix is typically static and does not grow, remodel, or adapt over time and thus limits their lifetime. Advancing ELMs toward sustained long-term performance and broader utility will require dynamic matrices that can adapt and grow together with the co-cultured functional cellular constituents. One route to such dynamic matrices is to harness cells as in situ factories that continuously produce and remodel the matrix. At the same time, using cells as factories would allow the synthesis of complex materials from relatively simple and abundant feedstocks such as monosaccharides.

These factors motivate a shift in ELM design away from nonliving carrier scaffolds and toward cell-produced living matrices that contribute directly to material formation and growth. Several strategies were employed to achieve this. Genetic engineering integrated self-assembling proteins to express fibers with hydrogel-like properties in the extracellular space. For example, curli nanofibers in Escherichia coli , or engineered TasA matrices in Bacillus subtilis were genetically functionalized with peptide domains to directly form macroscopic living matrices. As a result, the microbes fabricated their own carrier matrix with tunable properties. De novo-designed proteins have also been engineered in C. crescentus to self-assemble into extracellular matrices, expanding this toolkit beyond natural fiber systems. However, these approaches rely on genetic engineering of the producing organism and are typically limited in throughput, making it difficult to scale material production under mild conditions. Fungal hyphae, as an alternative strategy, form interconnected networks that act as a biological binder, consolidating substrates into cohesive composites and enabling recovery through renewed outgrowth. Yet, mycelium-based living materials typically focus on the fungi as structural and metabolic drivers. The use of mycelium networks as the carrier matrix to co-culture other functional species remains underdeveloped in the context of ELMs, mainly due to differences in growth speed and potential biological competition.

Bacteria-derived extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) serve as promising candidates for self-growing and self-healing carrier matrices. Bacterial EPS naturally forms hydrated, biofilm-like architectures that can potentially be used as the carrier matrix. , In practice, however, bacteria-derived polymers are often harvested and purified before use and are therefore implemented as abiotic materials. A prominent example of living bacterial EPS is bacterial cellulose produced by many strains of Komagataeibacter. These cellulose nanofibers composed of linked glucose units have been leveraged as living matrices to host yeast cells, forming a co-culture with programmable sensing and signaling functions. However, a bacterial cellulose-based matrix is produced primarily as a pellicle at the air–liquid interface in a static culture, which limits the design freedom of the material. , Geometric control is important for many ELM applications as the spatial patterning of functional cells and materials can determine gas and nutrient transport, mechanical performance, and the distribution of functional activity within the construct. To this end, bacterial cellulose has been assembled into patterned constructs through spheroid-based building blocks. Printing-based strategies have further extended geometric freedom by embedding cellulose-producing bacteria within hydrogel matrices. However, these approaches rely on pregrown units or static carriers rather than using the bacterial EPS itself as a directly shapeable living matrix. Therefore, there is a lack of compatible living matrices to host co-cultures that can be shaped into complex geometries, while still retaining the ability to grow and remodel.

Here, we identify EPS produced by Paenibacillus mucilaginosus as a suitable living carrier matrix for self-growing ELMs. P. mucilaginosus is a nonpathogenic rhizobacterium soil bacterium known for secreting large quantities of EPS under mild aerobic culture conditions. The EPS exhibit shear thinning and elastic recovery, enabling extrusion-based processing. We further show that the EPS can be readily modified through dynamic covalent cross-linking or physical reinforcement to tailor the mechanical properties of the matrix material, rendering P. mucilaginosus EPS a versatile ELM scaffold. We demonstrate that the living nature of the matrix not only supports the growth of embedded cyanobacteria but also adaptively grows to maintain structural function and integrity.

Bacterial Cultivation

P. mucilaginosus (DSM 24461) was purchased from Leibniz Institute DSMZ GmbH (Germany). Liquid culture of P. mucilaginosus was obtained by growing overnight in modified DSMZ medium 1307 (standard culture medium provided by the Leibniz Institute DSMZ GmbH, Table S1) on an orbital shaker with a constant shaking speed of 150 rpm at 30 °C. P. mucilaginosus agar was prepared according to the protocol provided by the DSMZ institute (Table S2). 1 mL of overnight liquid culture was inoculated on the DSMZ agar plate and allowed to grow for 5 days before harvesting the produced EPS.

Cyanobacterium Synechococcus sp. PCC 7002 (Pasteur Institute, France) was cultured in BG11–ASNIII medium (Tables S3, S4) and was harvested from the exponential growth phase. The liquid culture was maintained in Erlenmeyer flasks with the same shaking speed under full spectrum white light (180 μmol photons m–2 s–1) with a 12 h day (on)/night (off) cycle. Cyanobacteria growth was measured with a UV–visible light spectrophotometer (Lambda 35, PerkinElmer). Optical density at 730 nm was measured.

Induced Sporulation and Germination of P. mucilaginosus

1 mL of P. mucilaginosus liquid culture was spun down at 4300 rcf for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 10 mL sporulation medium (Table S5). The culture was grown at 30 °C at 200 rpm. After 24 h, sporulation was induced by heating the culture at 80 °C for 15 min. The resulting culture was then spun down and inoculated in germination medium (Table S6) and was grown overnight at 30 °C at 200 rpm.

Alcian Blue Staining of P. mucilaginosus EPS

P. mucilaginosus EPS was stained by using 1% Alcian blue in 3% acetic acid. Alcian blue was directly added to P. mucilaginosus liquid culture and stained for 2 h under room temperature. The stained EPS was pelleted by centrifugation at 4300 rcf for 10 min and was gently washed with Milli-Q water 3 times to remove unbound dye. Stained EPS was inspected using an optical microscope (Panthera Classic, Motic) equipped with Plan UC 100×/1.25 oil objectives and a camera (Moticam S, Motic). Imaged were saved with the Motic Images Plus 3.090 software.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

P. mucilaginosus EPS was harvested by scraping the agar surface with a sterile polypropylene spatula and was lyophilized by using a Lyovapor L-300 lyophilizer (BUCHI Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland) operated at 0.200 mbar, with the ice condenser maintained at approximately −101 °C. Lyophilized samples were used for scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Prior to imaging, samples were sputter-coated with a 10 nm Pt/Pd (80/20) layer using a metal sputter coater (CCU-010, Safematic, Zizers, Switzerland). SEM images were acquired with a secondary electron detector on a JEOL JSM-7100F microscope at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a working distance of 10–20 mm.

Measurement of Water Content in P. mucilaginosus EPS

Agar-grown P. mucilaginosus EPS was harvested and transferred into a preweighed 50 mL centrifuge tube. The tube was reweighed, and the difference in mass was recorded as the wet mass of the EPS (mwet). The open tube was then dried at room temperature for 48 h until constant mass and was reweighed. The mass relative to the empty tube was taken as the dry mass (mdry). Water content was calculated as (mwet–mdry)/mwet × 100%.

Boronate Ester Cross-Linking of P. mucilaginosus EPS

Agar-grown P. mucilaginosus EPS with or without Laponite was cross-linked with 3 mg/mL (18 mM) of benzene-1,4-diboronic acid in HEPES buffer (1 M, pH = 8). 10 mL of benzene-1,4-diboronic acid in HEPES buffer was added directly to the EPS with a pipette, and the EPS was fully immersed. The cross-linking was allowed to proceed for 2 h before benzene-1,4-diboronic acid solution was gently removed with a 1 mL pipette.

Rheology

Agar-grown P. mucilaginosus EPS was harvested by gently scraping the colony surface of agar plates using a sterile polypropylene spatula and characterized using a shear rheometer (MCR 502, Anton-Paar). Wet samples were loaded onto a Peltier plate with a 20 mm plate–plate probe and a constant 0.5 mm gap. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C. Reinforcement with Laponite and xanthan gum was measured under the same conditions. For cross-linking with benzene-1,4-diboronic acid, P. mucilaginosus EPS was fully immersed in 3 mg/mL (18 mM) benzene-1,4-diboronic acid in HEPES buffer (1 M, pH = 8) for 2 h before loading onto the rheometer. For competitive binding with fructose, the cross-linked EPS was fully immersed in 200 mM fructose in HEPES buffer (1 M, pH = 8) for 2 h.

Stress relaxation of the material was performed with the same 20 mm plate–plate probe and a 0.5 mm gap. Measurements were carried out at 25 °C with silicone oil around the measurement probe to prevent evaporation. A constant step strain of 1% was applied within the linear viscoelastic regime, and the time-dependent relaxation modulus G­(t) was recorded over 36,000s for day 0 and 10,000s for day 6. The relaxation modulus was modeled with a stretched-exponential (Kohlrausch–Williams–Watts, KWW) (eq )­ 1 G(t)=G0e−(tτR)β where G0 is the initial relaxation modulus, τ is the characteristic relaxation time, and β (0 < β ≤ 1) is the stretching exponent describing the breadth of the relaxation-time distribution. Nonlinear least-squares fitting was performed in OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, with the KWW expression implemented as a user-defined fitting function. Fits were evaluated over the entire decaying region of each relaxation curve, and goodness of fit was assessed using the adjusted R2. Reported uncertainties in G0, τ, and β correspond to the standard errors returned by the fit (Table S7).

Gel Permeation Chromatography

Molecular weights (Mn and Mw) and dispersities (D̵ = M w/M n) were determined by size exclusion chromatography (SEC). SEC analyses were carried out on an OMNISEC Resolve system, equipped with an OMNISEC Reveal triple detector (refractive index, light scattering and viscosity). Milli-Q water was used as an eluent and contained 0.1 M sodium nitrate and 0.02% (w/v) sodium azide, which was filtered through a 0.2 μm Nylon filter before use. One guard column (8 × 50 mm, AGuard, Viscotek) and two A6000 M general mixed gel columns (8 × 300 mm) were applied at a flow rate of 0.7 mL·min–1 at 25 °C. The incremental refractive index value was set to dn/dc = 0.132, as reported for PEO in water. For sample preparation, lyophilized P. mucilaginosus EPS were dissolved in the carrier phase at a concentration of 0.25–1 mg·mL–1 at ambient conditions. Each sample was injected at least twice, and the values for Mn, Mw, and D̵ were averaged accordingly. The determination of molecular weights was achieved through triple detection mode, and the results were exported using the OMNISEC Software (Version 11.32, Malvern Panalytical).

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was performed on a Microcal PEAQ-ITC (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK) using 1 M HEPES buffer as solvent (pH = 8). In a typical ITC experiment, the reference cell was filled with Milli-Q water, the sample cell was filled with boronic acid solution, and the injection syringe was loaded with the sugar or EPS containing solution in HEPES buffer (1 M, pH = 8). Then, 19 consecutive injections were carried out (1 × 0.4 mL, 18 × 2 mL), with an interval time of 150 s and the first injection being discarded. To determine the heat of dilution, the sugar or EPS was titrated to HEPES buffer, and the resulting data set was used for baseline subtraction. All raw data of ITC were integrated by NITPIC (v.2.1.0), , fitted in Sedphat (v. 15.2b), and plotted through GUSSI (v.2.1.4).

Filament Collapse Test

Filament collapse tests were performed to assess the shape fidelity of raw wet P. mucilaginosus EPS and EPS-based living inks. The living ink was loaded into a syringe and extruded using an extrusion-based printer through a 22G (Ø = 0.41 mm) nozzle onto a bridging substrate consisting of parallel supports separated by gaps of increasing width. A single continuous filament was deposited across each gap, and the midspan deflection under the filament’s own weight was recorded after printing.

Vial Inversion Test

Vial inversion tests were performed to qualitatively assess the gel-like behavior of raw wet P. mucilaginosus EPS. 1 mL of EPS was transferred into a glass vial using a positive displacement pipette and was allowed to rest for 10 min at room temperature. The vial was then inverted (180°) and held upside down for 3 h. Samples were classified as gel-like when no visible flow or meniscus movement was observed during inversion.

Geometry-Guided Surface Coating

For geometry-guided coating, a mesh scaffold was used as a porous substrate to retain small volumes of P. mucilaginosus liquid preculture within its openings via surface tension. The mesh was fully immersed in a P. mucilaginosus preculture once per day for 7 days (dip-coating). After incubation, the scaffold was rinsed gently to remove excess liquid and stained with Alcian blue; EPS deposition within the porous regions was assessed by comparing uncoated and coated meshes.

For the 3D architecture, the base unit of a body-centered cubic (BCC) lattice was immersed in P. mucilaginosus liquid preculture, allowing liquid to travel upward through the interconnected pore network by capillary forces and surface tension. The lattice was maintained under these conditions for 7 days, after which excess liquid was removed through the porous region. EPS deposition within the wetted volume was stained with Alcian blue.

Chlorophyll Extraction of Cyanobacteria PCC 7002 in Liquid Coculture

Chlorophyll extraction was used to quantify cyanobacteria PCC 7002 growth in the co-culture condition. 0.5 mL of liquid co-culture was centrifuged at 4300 rcf for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. 1 mL of cold methanol (4 °C) was added to the remaining pellet. The mixture was sonicated and was incubated at 4 °C for 20 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4300 rcf for 10 min. 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred into a 96 well plate and absorption was measured at 665 nm with a UV–vis plate reader (HIDEX Sense).

3D Printing of Photosynthetic Living Materials

P. mucilaginosus EPS was harvested by gently scraping the colony surface of agar plates using a sterile polypropylene spatula. Photosynthetic living materials were prepared by directly mixing agar-grown P. mucilaginosus EPS with an appropriate volume of a 3 wt % sterile Laponite stock solution in Milli-Q water to obtain a final Laponite concentration of 0.5 w/v %. An equal volume of PCC 7002 liquid cultures at OD730 nm = 0.3 was centrifuged at 4300 rcf for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting cell pellet was then thoroughly mixed into the Laponite-loaded EPS. The living ink was printed directly on a 1:3 ratio DSMZ 1307:BG11–ASNIII agar using a pneumatic-driven direct ink writing 3D printer (BioX, Cellink). A 22G nozzle (Ø = 0.41 mm) was used, and the printing was done under a pressure range of P = 10–25 Pa with a speed of 5–10 mm s–1. The printed samples were cross-linked in 3 mg mL–1 (18 mM) benzene-1,4-diboronic acid solution in HEPES buffer (1 M, pH = 8) for 2 h. The cross-linking solution was then discarded. The printed samples were incubated under the same conditions as liquid PCC 7002 culture without shaking.

Results

Characterization of P. mucilaginosus EPSs

As a first step in identifying growth conditions where P. mucilaginosus produces EPS, P. mucilaginosus was grown overnight in liquid preculture and then plated on DSMZ medium 1307 agar, a standard culture medium for P. mucilaginosus. After 7 days of incubation, P. mucilaginosus produced a thick layer of EPS that covered the agar surface as a transparent, gel-like film (Figure a). The EPS had a cohesive and elastic texture, readily stretching into long filaments when lifted with a spatula, suggesting the presence of long-chain polysaccharides or loose polymeric networks (Figure b). We stained the produced bacterial EPS with Alcian bluea dye that binds to acidic polysaccharidesto visualize its structure and morphology. The EPS contained elongated, thread-like features (Figure c), indicative of entangled polymer chains and fiber-like assemblies. Interestingly, EPS produced in liquid culture appeared as irregular, flocculated aggregates (Figure d). This difference likely arose from the distinct physical environments. On agar, bacteria attached to a solid surface and secreted EPS continuously, which led to the formation of a hydrated matrix that spread as a uniform, gel-like film. In liquid culture, bacteria experienced constant shear and remained in small clusters. As a result, EPS accumulated locally around bacterial aggregates and formed flocculated clumps rather than an extended film. ,

To further characterize the nature of these assemblies, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) revealed a weight-average molecular weight (M w) of 6.8 × 106 Da for agar-produced EPS (Figures e and S1a), indicative of high molecular weight polymer chains that are intrinsically prone to entanglement, offering a direct explanation for the observed filament-forming morphology. The presence of alternating mannose and glucose groups on the EPS chain also contributes to the formation of hydrogen bonds, which likely further stabilized the network. , In contrast, EPS produced in liquid culture had a weight-average molecular weight of 1.2 × 106 Da (Figure S1b), which was significantly lower than the agar-produced EPS. These findings indicate that the morphology and physical properties of the EPS can be tuned by adjusting the growth conditions.

A large variety of ELMs, such as coatings, scaffolds, and structural matrices, find applications under static or low-shear conditions. The ability of P. mucilaginosus to produce a continuous EPS network in these environments bears the advantage that the material can be used directly as a supporting matrix without the need for extensive purification or processing. To account for this, EPS used in subsequent experiments was produced under static conditions by spotting or streaking liquid preculture onto agar plates and harvesting the resulting surface-associated filamentous network.

Besides the physical growth conditions, we observed that EPS production adapted to changes in the surrounding chemical environment. Successive passages in nutrient-rich liquid preculture reduced EPS production and water content, suggesting that prolonged planktonic growth under low nutritional stress reduced the EPS secretion (Figures S2 and S3a). However, a tailored medium formulation (hereafter referred to as the EPS production medium, adapted from refs and, Tables S8 and S9) restored and promoted EPS growth. In the EPS production medium, P. mucilaginosus produced 2.4 ± 0.4 g of raw dry EPS per mL of liquid preculture overnight. In comparison, 0.56 ± 0.15 g dry EPS were produced over 7 days on standard DSMZ agar. Alternatively, induced sporulation and germination of P. mucilaginosus also restored EPS production (Figure S4). EPS produced after germination also had comparable mechanical properties to the ones produced on DSMZ agar or EPS production agar (Figure S5). Water content of EPS produced on EPS production agar first decreased from day 0 to day 3, possibly due to the initial biomass accumulation. After 4 days, water content stabilized at approximately 92% (Figure S3b). EPS produced on DSMZ agar had a consistent water content of around 97% (Figure S3c). Together, these results indicate that EPS production in P. mucilaginosus depends on physiological state and is tunable through environmental and nutritional cues.

To characterize the microstructure of the agar-produced EPS, we examined its morphology by SEM (Figure S6). SEM of lyophilized P. mucilaginosus EPS showed homogeneous cell distribution on the EPS surface (Figure f, with representative bacteria false-colored pink).

The mechanical properties of the EPS were investigated with shear rheology over time. Strain sweeps showed linear viscoelastic regions up to 10% strain, indicating predominantly elastic, solid-like behavior (Figure g). Beyond 10% strain, both the storage modulus (G′) and the loss modulus (G″) decreased, with G″ surpassing G′, suggesting that the polymer network was disrupted and the material started to yield. This transition reflected the viscoelastic nature of P. mucilaginosus EPS, maintaining structural integrity under moderate strain but yielding under large deformations. Frequency sweeps conducted within the linear viscoelastic region showed that G′ remained higher than G″ over the entire frequency range from 0.01 to 10 rad s–1, indicating predominantly elastic, solid-like behavior of the EPS (Figure h). This rheological behavior was reproducible across three independent batches of agar-grown EPS (Figure S7). The mechanical properties of the EPS increased over time (Figures g,h, S8), possibly due to continued EPS accumulation, decreased water content (Figure S3), or microbial alteration of the produced EPS. The EPS also demonstrated shear thinning behavior and rapid recovery of the viscoelastic moduli following deformation, which is essential for injectable applications (Figure S9a,b, Table S10). In addition, the EPS maintained stable viscoelastic properties over a temperature range of 12–40 °C (Figure S9c).

Together, these rheological characteristics suggested that P. mucilaginosus EPS formed a soft, viscoelastic matrix capable of recovering quickly after deformation. Rapid recovery after step strains suggested that the material exhibited elastic recovery, with the viscoelastic response reforming after large-amplitude shear (Figure S9b). Stability across the physiological temperature range (12–40 °C, Figure S9c) further ensured consistent performance under typical physiological or operational conditions. The measured rheological properties suggested that the EPS can be processed by extrusion-based printing (Figure S9d).

Bulk Properties and Geometry-Guided Coating with P. mucilaginosus EPS

Building on the rheological characterization, we next examined the printability and bulk shape-retention behavior of raw wet P. mucilaginosus EPS as a carrier matrix for ELMs. For fabrication into defined architectures, particularly by extrusion-based printing, the material should ideally be shear-thinning and recover elastically after deposition. In addition, the matrix should maintain structural integrity and shape fidelity over relevant time scales. We quantified shape fidelity using a filament collapse test, in which extruded filaments were bridged across gaps of increasing width and the resulting deflection under self-weight was measured. Directly harvested EPS supported 8 mm gaps with minimal sagging, while 16 mm spans showed approximately 2.5 mm deflection (Figure a). These results indicated that raw wet EPS exhibited sufficient shape fidelity to bridge moderate gaps (≤8 mm) with minimal collapse. A vial inversion test further indicated gel-like behavior, as the EPS did not flow upon inversion over time (Figures b and S10). Directly harvested P. mucilaginosus EPS was printed with direct ink writing into a freestanding cylindrical shape. The printed cylinder exhibited a slight taper, with the upper diameter reduced by 2.3 mm relative to the base, indicating largely self-supporting behavior with modest shape relaxation during or shortly after deposition (Figure c).

Beyond bulk shaping and printability, we next investigated whether the EPS could form continuous, adherent layers on solid substrates to enable surface-associated applications, such as living coatings. To do this, EPS was produced in situ by P. mucilaginosus cells, and the substrate was used to define the final EPS geometry. Liquid P. mucilaginosus preculture was deposited onto a wood substrate and supplied with the EPS production medium to promote EPS growth. After 7 days, a transparent layer formed on the surface of the wood surface. Upon tilting, the layer remained intact and did not flow, indicating the formation of a continuous, adherent EPS coating (Figure d, Supplementary Video S1).

As P. mucilaginosus liquid preculture readily formed surface coatings, we explored how surface geometry could be used to localize the liquid preculture and, in turn, spatially guide EPS deposition. One approach exploited surface tension to retain small volumes of liquid within confined features and thereby localize cells and nutrients in selected regions. A mesh scaffold was designed to both define the target deposition sites and hold the P. mucilaginosus-containing preculture within the openings. We used repeated dip-coating to replenish the trapped liquid and promote EPS accumulation inside the mesh pores. The scaffold was fully immersed in P. mucilaginosus preculture once per day for 7 days. By day 7, an EPS layer had formed throughout the porous regions, sealing most openings compared with the uncoated scaffold (Figure e). This demonstrated that we could use surface tension and surface geometry to direct EPS formation.

Beyond geometry-guided transport on porous 2D surfaces, inducing EPS formation within a defined 3D architecture is of interest for creating volumetric coatings. Inspired by the cellular fluidics concept of unit-cell-based lattices that enable deterministic, surface-tension-driven transport in open cellular materials, we fabricated a BCC lattice. Its interconnected strut network provided continuous capillary pathways that passively drew up liquid preculture via capillary forces and surface tension. Only the base unit of the lattice was immersed in P. mucilaginosus preculture, which was drawn upward through the interconnected lattice pores. After 7 days, EPS formed within the wetted volume of the lattice, yielding a coated internal architecture (Figure f). Together, these results indicated that surface tension, capillary-driven transport, and scaffold geometry can guide and localize P. mucilaginosus culture to direct EPS deposition, supporting the use of EPS as coating materials on planar and structured 3D substrates.

Mechanical Reinforcement of P. mucilaginosus EPS

While raw P. mucilaginosus EPS supported the printing and surface coating, its relatively low storage modulus limited the load-bearing capacity, restricting applications that require precise shape retention. To address the limited mechanical properties, we explored reinforcement strategies to increase matrix stiffness and improve shape fidelity while preserving viscoelastic behavior.

P. mucilaginosus EPS contains predominantly high molecular weight polysaccharides with alternating glucose and mannose groups. ,, Noticeably, the mannose group contains a cis-diol, which can form dynamic covalent bonds with boronic acids. Using this strategy, we could introduce secondary, dynamic covalent cross-links into the EPS network to reinforce its mechanical properties (Figure a). , To achieve this, we cross-linked the EPS with benzene-1,4-diboronic acid, to form boronate ester cross-links (V = 10 mL, c = 18 mM, in HEPES buffer at pH = 8, t = 2 h). This dynamic covalent reinforcement increased the storage modulus G′ from 100 to 800 Pa (Figure b). Frequency sweep in the linear viscoelastic region also showed an increase in both G′ and G″, indicating an overall reinforcement of the network (Figure c).

Consistent with this, ITC measurements indicated an apparent Ka = 92 M–1 (Figure d) and ΔH of −3.0 kcal mol–1 between the EPS and benzene-1,4-diboronic acid, in line with the reported boronic acid–diol complexation and indicative of an enthalpically favorable interaction. In comparison, benzene-1,4-diboronic acid bound pure mannose with a weaker apparent affinity (K a = 22 M–1; Figure S11). This difference is consistent with multivalent diol presentation along the EPS backbone and contributions from additional saccharide units (K a,glucose = 32 M–1; Figure S11).

To further confirm the formation of boronate esters in the presence of diboronic acid, we tested whether these cross-links were dynamically reversible with fructose. When fructose is present in an EPS-diboronic ester formulation, it should act as a competitive binder and exchange with the dynamic covalent bonds in the network (Figure S12). Indeed, the addition of fructose reduced G′ to ∼100 Pa, similar to the G′ of pristine EPS, supporting reversible boronate ester formation (Figure e). Together, rheology and ITC confirmed reversible diboronic acid binding to EPS and the associated network stiffening. Unlike permanent cross-links, dynamic covalent bonding enables the network to relax and remodel while retaining bulk integrity via bond exchange. These properties retain printability while supporting the growth and colony expansion of embedded cells within a mechanically reinforced matrix.

In addition to chemical cross-linking, we explored physical reinforcement strategies as a complementary approach to further tune the EPS mechanics. We tested xanthan gum and Laponite, two common rheology modifiers. Xanthan gum increased the material’s mechanics by forming an entangled polysaccharide network, whereas Laponite strengthened polymer matrices by forming particle-mediated physical cross-links. , While both additives increased the moduli of the EPS, Laponite showed a larger reinforcement (Figure S13). To maximize the reinforcement, we combined physical reinforcement with diboronic acid cross-linking to further strengthen the EPS network. Combining chemical and physical cross-linking using diboronic acid and 0.5 w/v % Laponite resulted in synergistic reinforcement, reaching ∼1300 Pa (Figure b).

Dynamic covalent cross-linking and physical reinforcement provided routes to reinforce P. mucilaginosus EPS and tune its mechanical properties. These approaches increased the network’s mechanics while maintaining a dynamic matrix that could potentially accommodate microbial growth and reorganization without being as confined as covalent networks with fixed pore size.

Self-Growing, Photosynthetic ELMs Based on P. mucilaginosus EPS

The high production rate of EPS at neutral pH renders P. mucilaginosus a promising candidate to provide a supporting matrix for other microbial species when grown in cocultures. We used cyanobacteria as a model system to demonstrate the utility of P. mucilaginosus EPS as a carrier matrix to manufacture self-growing ELMs. Carbon-capturing photosynthetic living materials have emerged as a novel approach to complement existing carbon capture and storage strategies. The application and effectiveness of photosynthetic ELMs depend on the associated surface area. However, existing designs typically employ nonliving materials, such as alginate or pluronics, as matrices. Following these approaches, the supporting matrices themselves are passive holders of the encapsulated photosynthetic species and remain static. In such static matrices, the available space is fixed. As cells grow and divide, they eventually fill the matrix and reach a maximum cell density, which limits the total biomass and functional capacity. As demonstrated, P. mucilaginosus EPS is bioderived with dynamic viscoelastic properties, offering a route toward a living carrier that can better sustain cell viability and mitigate space limitation in long-term cultivation. Therefore, we applied P. mucilaginosus-produced EPS as a living matrix for designing photosynthetic ELMs that can grow over time. For practical deployment, hydration retention is also a key constraint for maintaining cell viability, and the high water content of the EPS (97.7 ± 0.6%, Figure S3) should maintain a hydrated microenvironment and reduce desiccation stress for encapsulated cyanobacteria.

We co-cultured P. mucilaginosus and cyanobacterium Synechococcus sp. PCC 7002, a model species used in photosynthetic living materials due to its innate carbon capture and storage ability. To identify a common growth medium, PCC 7002 and P. mucilaginosus cells were grown in liquid culture composed of different ratios of DSMZ 1307 medium to standard cyanobacteria medium (BG11–ASNIII medium, Table S5), and cyanobacteria PCC 7002 growth was evaluated after 1 week. Chlorophyll extraction showed that growth was not affected at a DSMZ 1307:BG11–ASNIII ratio of 1:3 relative to the PCC 7002 monoculture (Figure a,b). This medium ratio was therefore used for subsequent co-culture experiments. The proliferation of PCC 7002 under these conditions indicates that it is compatible with the co-culture environment and can be incorporated into the EPS without compromising viability.

We fabricated a living ink by embedding cyanobacteria PCC 7002 in freshly harvested P. mucilaginosus EPS from the EPS production medium agar. 0.5 w/v % Laponite was added to the living ink to improve its printability and shape fidelity (Figures S14 and S15). Rheological characterization confirmed the printability of the bioink, exhibiting shear thinning and elastic recovery (Figure S16). A filament collapse test also indicated that the incorporation of cyanobacteria PCC 7002 cells did not impair the shape fidelity of P. mucilaginosus EPS with Laponite (Figure c). In addition, the rheological properties of the extruded P. mucilaginosus EPS were comparable to those of the as-grown material, indicating that the printing step did not substantially alter the matrix property (Figure S17). The living ink was then printed into a tree geometry. After printing, the structure was cross-linked with 10 mL of benzene-1,4-diboronic acid in HEPES buffer (3 mg mL–1; pH = 8) for 2 h to stabilize the structure, after which the cross-linking solution was discarded (Figures d and S15).

The structure was incubated on an agar plate with DSMZ 1307: BG11–ASNIII medium ratio of 1:3. From day 0 to day 3, P. mucilaginosus grew radially from the printed structure while cyanobacteria PCC 7002 pigmentation increased over time (Figure d), consistent with continued chlorophyll a biosynthesis by metabolically active cells (Figure S18). The rheological properties of the printed (day 0) and grown living materials (day 6 and day 10) showed an increase in moduli (Figure S19), possibly due to a combined effect of pH increase due to cyanobacteria metabolism, , the increase in moduli of P. mucilaginosus EPS over time, and the increase in biomass as fillers within the network. Stress-relaxation data fitted to a stretched-exponential (KWW) function indicated that the relaxation time dropped from τ = 12,378 ± 2030 s (day 0) to 6125 ± 90 s (day 6) and did not reach a plateau over the measured time scale (Figure S20). Over time, EPS growth closed the initial gap around the tree trunk (Figure d, indicated by yellow contour, Figure S21), and cyanobacteria PCC 7002 then colonized the newly formed matrix, fully filling the gap by day 7. Over 11 days, EPS growth produced a white rim around the original print, while cyanobacteria PCC 7002 growth deepened the green shade. The same growth pattern continued over 35 days, with both P. mucilaginosus and cyanobacteria PCC 7002 expanding into their surroundings. These observations showed that EPS-based ink supported sustained co-culture growth after printing and exhibited matrix growth and expansion, further supporting the use of P. mucilaginosus EPS as a living carrier matrix for self-growing ELM applications.

Discussion

Engineering carrier matrices that serve as active components enables the design of dynamic and adaptive living materials. In the context of ELMs, this transforms the design space, where cells no longer simply reside in a nonliving scaffold but take the role of building and maintaining the matrix over time. Dynamic matrices can extend the lifetime of living materials by enabling cells to introduce additional functions, such as self-healing. In the current design of ELMs, self-growing matrices that are printable, readily compatible with other functional cells, and capable of high production rates have not yet been broadly demonstrated. In this work, we explored P. mucilaginosus EPS as a living matrix that enables printing, supports bacterial viability, and enables construct growth. We fabricated EPS-based photosynthetic living materials by 3D printing to define the initial geometry, while the final shape and scale were governed by the EPS growth. Growth-driven expansion into the surrounding space effectively extended the functional reach of living materials. At present, the growth of living materials is shaped by the EPS formation of P. mucilaginosus. Cyanobacteria PCC 7002 proliferated within EPS and sequestered atmospheric CO2. The viability of both species was maintained by the diffusion of nutrients from the underlying agar, which limits the maximum height of the designs (Figures S22 and d). In addition, as microbial growth was inhomogeneous, the printed features were progressively lost over the culture period. Designing co-cultures with forced metabolic dependency could provide a strategy to enable more precise control over these growth dynamics. For example, cyanobacteria could be genetically engineered to produce sucrose to support P. mucilaginosus viability, which would also reduce the geometric limitations imposed by the agar. Looking forward, this growth-driven behavior may be applicable to living surface coatings, and one could envision seeding a surface with an initial cell mixture, after which the material gradually colonizes an extended area. Such an approach could potentially reduce the initial fabrication burden and the need for prolonged maintenance.

Equally important is the ability to tune the mechanics of the living carrier matrix for applications that require high shape fidelity, or to account for ideal matrix stiffnesses to host specific cell types. We reinforced the living matrix with rheological modifiers and a dynamic covalent cross-linker, both of which increased the storage modulus of the material. These strategies represent an initial set of tools, and several directions remain open for future work. Future approaches could include alternative cross-linking strategies or polymer network knowledge to further improve the printability, stability, and shape retention. Control over matrix stiffness could also be used as a signaling cue for embedded functional cells. Beyond mechanical performance, matrix stiffness has been reported to affect cellular proliferation and protein production in living materials. More broadly, combining the inherent responsiveness of living matrices with the mechanical tunability and structural control enabled by established polymer network design could add a programmable mechano-biological feedback layer.

Another advantage of using cell-produced matrices for living materials is that their material properties can be tuned in situ by manipulating the living cells. For example, we demonstrated that the medium composition and nutritional availability changed the EPS production behavior. This observation points to broader possibilities for future work. Culture conditions may influence not only the quantity of matrix deposited but also its composition, mechanical properties, and spatial arrangement. Building on this, living carrier matrices could be designed to respond to external environmental cues and adapt over time, rather than remaining compositionally and mechanically fixed after fabrication.

Beyond tuning matrix properties through cell manipulation, another important yet underexplored advantage in this work is the sporulation capacity of the species. Paenibacillus is a spore-forming genus, and sporulation offers a natural pause–resume mechanism. Under harsh environmental conditions, such as drought or nutrient limitation, sporulation can maintain cell viability by placing cells in a dormant state. In principle, this may enable Paenibacillus-based living materials to survive periods of stress and resume growth and matrix production when conditions become permissive. In turn, this feature could reduce refabrication requirements and prolong the functional lifetime of the material. The use of sporulation has clear precedent in related living-materials work. Bacillus subtilis spores have been printed into agarose to produce resilient living materials. The materials retained function after extreme stress and germinated in newly formed cracks. For P. mucilaginosus, sporulation could therefore be leveraged to resist harsh environments, while still preserving the capacity for on-demand regrowth and matrix renewal.

Overall, this work established a proof of concept for P. mucilaginosus EPS as a compelling framework for constructing a self-growing matrix, a printed scaffold whose composition and mechanics can be codesigned by cells, and whose long-term function could be extended further by sporulation-enabled robustness.

Conclusion

This work establishes P. mucilaginosus EPS as a self-growing living matrix to support embedded functional cells. Rheological modifiers and dynamic covalent cross-linking via boronate ester chemistry were leveraged to tune matrix properties. As a proof of concept, we used P. mucilaginosus EPS to encapsulate photosynthetic cyanobacteria PCC 7002 to design living materials for carbon sequestration. Embedded cyanobacteria remained viable and proliferated over 30 days, while the designed constructs also grew and expanded beyond the printed geometry. Together, these features position P. mucilaginosus EPS as a promising carrier matrix for self-growing and mechanically tunable living materials.
