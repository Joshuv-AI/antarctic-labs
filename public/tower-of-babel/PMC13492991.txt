The How and Why of Exercise Prescription. Patients' Experience of Prescribed Exercise in Musculoskeletal Physiotherapy
Authors: Samantha S Smith, Suzanne J Snodgrass, Nicholas Mullen, Peter G Osmotherly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleMusculoskeletal physiotherapy, including the exercise prescription element, should be patient‐centred with an active patient involved in the decision making. Currently, exercise prescription relies heavily on published literature and clinical expertise, however there is limited published research on the patient's experience with the exercise prescription process in a one‐on‐one outpatient setting.Aims and ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore patients' experiences of the exercise prescription process in musculoskeletal physiotherapy and to develop a grounded understanding of the elements that are important considerations to patients in a one‐on‐one rehabilitation setting.MethodsTwenty participants who had recently completed outpatient physiotherapy for a musculoskeletal condition took part in a semi‐structured interview. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, with recruitment continuing until theoretical saturation was reached. Data were analysed using published grounded theory methods.ResultsTwo main categories emerged describing the patient experience of exercise prescription, WHY and HOW, with individualisation of the program to the patient acting as the core concept. WHY encompassed education, feedback and collaboration, which built patients' understanding, trust and confidence in their exercise program and supported a therapeutic alliance. HOW encompassed exercise selection, prescription method, dosage parameters, progressive overload and prescription tools, reflecting how the program was practically developed and delivered. These elements were interdependent, with the WHY elements informing the HOW elements, all converging on individualisation as the core concept for an effective exercise prescription process.ConclusionPatients value an individualised, collaborative approach to exercise prescription that integrates education, ongoing feedback and prescription methods tailored to their goals and context. These findings offer physiotherapists patient‐informed insight to complement existing clinician focused literature to guide the exercise prescription process.Keywords: exercise therapy, musculoskeletal diseases, patient outcome assessment, physical therapists, therapeutic alliance

Introduction

Effective exercise prescription in musculoskeletal rehabilitation involves the integration of evidence‐based exercise principles and inter‐personal behavioural techniques to motivate and support patients to achieve their goals [1]. Whilst there is strong evidence for exercise in the management of many musculoskeletal conditions, there are limited evidence‐based guidelines to support the exercise prescription processes specifically in musculoskeletal conditions [2, 3, 4, 5].

The majority of existing published literature for the exercise prescription process is based on experimental research and expert clinical knowledge, experience and opinion, however there is limited input from the patient's perspective. It is recognised that musculoskeletal rehabilitation should be patient‐centred with an active patient involved in the decision making within a strong therapeutic alliance [6, 7]. The aim of this study was to explore patient experiences and to develop an understanding of the elements of the exercise prescription process that are important to the patient for their musculoskeletal rehabilitation in a one‐one‐one rehabilitation setting.

Methods

Design

This study employed a grounded theory research design. To ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting, the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) was implemented [8]. Ethics was approved by The University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee (approval no. H‐2024‐0093).

Participants, Recruitment and Sampling

Individuals were eligible to participate if they had completed active physiotherapy treatment for a musculoskeletal condition in an outpatient one‐one‐one setting within the past months and were prescribed an exercise program. Participants had to be over the age of 18 years, able to speak and understand English and have access to internet to complete an online interview. Individuals were excluded if they were undergoing active physiotherapy or if treatment had concluded longer than 1 month prior, in an inpatient or group setting or being treated for a non‐musculoskeletal condition. Participants were recruited from around Australia via advertising flyers located in physiotherapy clinics and advertising posts on one researcher's social media (Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn). Interested participants were screened, consented in writing and enrolled in an interview.

A heterogenous purposive sampling strategy was implemented with the aim to capture a broad and representative sample of participants across a range of demographics and musculoskeletal conditions. This was achieved by seeking participants from a range of ages, locations around Australia and musculoskeletal conditions. Participant characteristics were evaluated during the recruitment process and participants from less represented groups actively enrolled. As data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, participants were further recruited based on emerging themes and as categories developed. This iterative sampling continued until theoretical saturation was achieved. A total of 20 participants were included in this study.

Data Collection

Data was collected via semi‐structured interviews over a 10‐month period between August 2024 and June 2025. Interviews were performed by two researchers, one male, one female, both clinical physiotherapists in musculoskeletal physiotherapy, all researchers had experience with qualitative research.

An open‐ended interview outline was developed based on the researchers own clinical experience and understanding of the literature regarding elements of the exercise prescription process in musculoskeletal rehabilitation [2, 9, 10]. As data was collected, coded and analysed, adaptations to the initial interview outline occurred to explore other aspects of the participants experiences.

Participants completed a single interview, lasting approximately 30 min. All interviews were performed, recorded and automatically transcribed by the videoconferencing platform ZOOM (Version 7.0; Zoom Video Communications Inc., 2026). NVivo 12 (QSR International Pty, Melbourne, Australia) software and Microsoft Excel (Version 16.107.2, Microsoft Corporation, 2026) was used to assist with data storage and analysis. Participants were assigned pseudonyms for the purposes of confidentiality. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, with analysis informing emerging categories and further refinement until theoretical saturation was achieved.

Data Analysis

Each transcript was individually read and checked for accuracy. NVivo 12 (QSR International Pty) software was then used by one researcher for the initial open coding, developing initial subcategories and categories. Transcripts were read and coding reviewed by an additional researcher with experience in qualitative research to ensure accuracy and credibility. Following the open coding, further coding occurred where relationships between the categories formed, core categories identified and integrated together to formulate a theory. Categories, subcategories and their relationships were reviewed and agreed upon by the research team. This data analysis process followed the principles of Grounded theory based upon Strauss (1990) and Charmaz (1990) as outlined by previous authors [11, 12, 13]. Once the initial theory had been developed, further review of the literature was undertaken in an attempt to enhance theoretical sensitivity [14].

Credibility and Trustworthiness

As all researchers had prior experience in musculoskeletal physiotherapy, to minimise the risk of interpreting findings with prior expectations, each transcript was reviewed by one other research team member and feedback provided to ensure minimal clinical influence, with the aim to leave the interview open for participants to share their experience. Reflective journalling was undertaken after each interview to recognise and manage any biases present. To support authenticity and credibility, the research team also engaged in ongoing critical discussion throughout the analysis process, with coding and theme development reviewed collaboratively to challenge any assumptions and ensure the participants experience was the focus over clinical expectations and experience.

Analysis of the data brought together elements of the exercise prescription process from the patient's perspective under two main categories, WHY and HOW. A core concept central to the whole exercise prescription process was individualisation of the exercise prescription process to the patient. (16) “I'm lucky because I have a fitness background… But if I was someone who was coming in and was relatively new to the gym, I think it would have been a little bit unhelpful or confusing if I didn't have those demonstrations” (P5, 23 yrs, female, acute neck and shoulder pain) 2.2 Dosage parameters—Knowing what to do and how much to do, including intensity, repetitions, holds, sets, rest, frequency and duration.2.2.1 Participants valued the provision of dosage parameters being individualized to them, then to be given an opportunity to discuss and ask questions to clarify. (17) “I think if she had communicated all the detail about the intensity where to start in the gym, what weights to start with, giving me demonstrations in real time, I think that would have bettered my journey… I think she could have done less assuming, decided to go fully encompassed in terms of how much weight I should start with, the level of intensity, the level of pain and how often I should do it. And maybe I just wanted the gold star or something…” (P13, 30 yrs, male, acute non‐specific knee pain) 2.4.3 Some participants preferred a written handout over an app, however for some they were hard to use. The suitability was influenced by the complexity of the exercises and participant preferences. (36) “She takes time to draw and to write things out for me that I can take home and refer to… In terms of having all that information on an app, I think it would be challenging because the way she does it is she writes it all on one piece of A4 paper and it's just a snapshot… All the information is on one page…” (P9, 29 yrs, female, acute non‐specific knee pain) (37) “Are hard to follow and hard to understand, because I can't identify with a stick figure…” (P15, 46 yrs, male, shoulder rotator cuff injury) (38) “Gave me a printout of guidance on what exercises…reps, weights… for me, it was fine, the exercises were pretty simple. WHY: Understanding the Exercise Program and Related Elements

WHY relates to understanding from the patients' perspectives. This means comprehending their possible diagnosis and relative physiology to understand their pain or dysfunction, their prognosis and the purpose of their exercise program.

Education

Education included understanding the diagnosis and prognosis of the condition and purpose of the exercises. The majority of the participants could recall the purpose of their exercises, strength being a clear goal (n = 18, 90%). Education was not only motivating but also relieved some anxieties around their pain (Quote 1). It was important to participants that education was understandable to their level of health literacy and broken down into frequent moments rather than all in one consultation (Quotes 2, 3). One participant noted that the education method and style varied between physiotherapists when being treated for the same condition (Quote 4). Physiotherapists may use a model to describe the injury, anatomy and prognosis, for multiple participants this supported their understanding and confidence in the exercises (Quote 5). When inadequate education was provided, participants reported performing their own research to understand further and felt frustrated if they could not understand the purpose of their exercises and expected outcomes (Quote 6). In contrast, another participant with a background in human physiology reported minimal need for further education from their physiotherapist, reporting they could understand the purposes of their exercises (Quote 7), reiterating the importance of individualising the education amount, method and style to the patient.

Feedback and Collaboration

Feedback included both internal feedback developed within the patient and external feedback provided by the physiotherapist to support the patient's understanding of progressing or regressing their exercises and the provision of proof of progress. Changes to the exercise program were either directed by the physiotherapist or more autonomously by the patient once sufficient education was provided. For most participants, feedback involved performing the exercises, the physiotherapist observing, providing feedback and discussing collaboratively with the participant before adjusting technique or dosage parameters (Quotes 8, 9). Many participants reported that a collaborative approach improved their understanding of and confidence in their exercises (Quotes 8, 9).

An element of providing feedback included evidence of progress as well as adapting the program as the patient progressed through their rehabilitation. Participants reported experiencing improvements in their pain and function motivating and valuable, however a lack of progress was a demotivator for some participants (Quotes 10, 11).

HOW: How the Exercise Program Was Developed and Prescribed

The HOW elements included the selection of the exercises and dosage parameters, how these were then prescribed to the patient, progressed or regressed as required and the prescription tools used to support the home exercise program component.

The Exercise Prescription Method

Most participants reported a similar experience in how the exercises were prescribed to ensure accurate and confident technique. Eleven participants (55%) reported the physiotherapist first demonstrated their exercises, thirteen (65%) reported performing their exercises with the physiotherapist then being provided feedback. Participants reported their preference of including a combination of demonstration, observation and provision of feedback in the exercise prescription process (Quote 12, 13). Two participants reported not being provided with in‐person demonstrations of all their exercises and were given videos to follow through independently, however they expressed a preference to perform these exercises with the physiotherapist (Quote 14). Participants valued the use of their own words to enhance their understanding of the exercises (Quote 15).

Some participants noted that for more simple exercises, they felt a video demonstration sufficed and for more complex exercises, the combination of demonstration, observation and feedback was important.

Whilst multiple participants recognized their general exercise experience may have influenced the exercise prescription process, they still preferred to perform the exercises under supervision with their physiotherapist to confirm technique and dosage parameters (Quote 16).

Based on the variety of responses from participants it is evident that appropriateness of how exercises are prescribed should be individualised based on the exercise complexity, the patients exercise experience and knowledge.

Participants valued the provision of dosage parameters being individualized to them, then to be given an opportunity to discuss and ask questions to clarify. For one participant in particular, they noted that the lack of intensity prescription potentially hindered their self‐reported progress and confidence in their exercise program (Quote 17), whereas another participant noted that their physiotherapist went through each exercise, determining the appropriate intensity prescription (Quote 18).

To some participants, the dosage parameters were prescribed via an app, however they still would have preferred an opportunity to discuss their program further (Quote 19). The participants that felt they did not receive adequate education reported a lack of confidence and concern for exacerbating their musculoskeletal condition (Quote 20).

How the program fits within the participant's daily routine and the time they had available to perform the exercises were also important considerations, however were not always evident to be considered by the physiotherapist (Quotes 21, 22). All participants valued being provided adequate detail on the appropriate dosage parameters that were relevant to them and were realistic to complete in their time available.

Adapting the Program as Required, Progressive and Regressive Techniques

Adapting the program to the patient throughout their rehabilitation journey involved a varying balance between providing the patient with the autonomy and being guided by the physiotherapist directly. There was no one method reported by the participants.

Eight participants reported being provided specific guidelines on how to progress their exercises independently (Quote 23). Other participants reported more vague guidelines and autonomy over their progressions (Quotes 24, 25). One participant reported that they were not provided progressive overload techniques to implement independently, instead this was provided during each follow‐up appointment (Quote 26).

Three participants reported being provided no guidance on progressing their exercises, rather they independently implemented progressive overload or regressions to prevent worsening of pain and symptoms (Quotes 27, 28, 29).

The balance between autonomy and direct supervision may be influenced by the complexity of the exercises prescribed, the patient's exercise experience and the stage of the presenting musculoskeletal condition. However, it was clear that adjusting the program as the participants responded to their exercise program was important.

Prescription Tools for Home Exercise Programs

Nineteen participants, (95%) performed their exercise program from home, nine (45%) also performed their exercises in a commercial gym and four (20%) performed their exercises in the physiotherapy clinic under supervision. Some participants performed their exercises across multiple locations. This was influenced by their time available, the exercises prescribed, the equipment required and patient preferences. To some, home exercise programs were more convenient, for others, it was hard to be motivated when exercising from home (Quotes 30, 31). Participants that performed exercises in the gym generally were current gym‐attendees and incorporated their rehabilitation exercises into their usual gym routine. For more complex exercises, more irritable or acute conditions, especially the post‐surgical participants in their initial rehabilitation phases, exercises were performed in‐clinic under the physiotherapist's supervision. Having the opportunity to perform their exercises with the physiotherapist was motivating for many participants (Quotes 30, 31).

Of the participants that performed their exercises externally from the physiotherapy clinic, a combination of applications, videos and written handouts for prescription tools were used. Participants reported a variety of preferences influenced by their own perceived benefits and complexity of using applications versus paper handouts, the exercise program complexity, previous experiences and personal preference (Quotes 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39). Therefore, the suitability of each tool should be individualized to each patient. For example, one participant reported that the use of an application to log their exercises would be a burden (Quote 33). They would perform the exercises, but rather not log them into an application. Whereas some participants found the use of an application motivational and beneficial as reminder tools to perform their exercises (Quote 32, 35). Of the participants that were provided an application, all reported that for an application to be effective, the reminders, the videos and the education within the application itself should support an existing exercise program that was developed collaboratively with their physiotherapist and individualised to them (Quotes 40, 41).

Not all participants preferred an app for their exercise program, and some participants preferred a written handout over an app (Quotes 36). Multiple participants suggested the use of videos versus paper handouts could be influenced by the complexity of the exercises (Quotes 38, 39). Whilst the handouts and applications helped to remember their exercises as a prompt, all participants noted that neither tool took away the value of the physiotherapist going through the exercises with them and providing an opportunity to ask questions (Quotes 40, 41). Evidently, the appropriateness between using an application, videos or paper handouts is mixed and dependent on the individual patient.

Discussion

From exploring patient experiences with the exercise prescription process, the two concepts of WHY and HOW emerged that work together to encourage individualisation of the exercise program, following a patient centred care approach. The WHY elements function as a foundation for the patient to develop an understanding of their musculoskeletal condition and their exercises, further supporting trust and confidence in their exercise program and physiotherapist. Each factor contributes to the HOW elements, meaning how the exercise program is developed and prescribed, and interacting to develop effective exercise prescription that was individualised to and built in collaboration with the patient.

Education can be an effective tool to empower patients to have an active role in their rehabilitation [15]. Patients in multiple studies have reported value in understanding their rationale for treatment, their progress and prognosis [6, 7, 16, 17, 18, 19]. This education needs to be tailored to the patient's learning style, experience and health literacy, with explanations that are logical and meaningful to them [15, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Participants in our study report the value of their physiotherapist's ability to communicate complex components of their rehabilitation in understandable lay terms, matching their level of understanding, experience and learning styles [15, 19, 21].

Education was not only motivating, but also relieved participants anxieties around their condition, supporting their confidence in performing their exercises without risking further injury or worsening of symptoms. This is consistent with previous research in which patients attending physiotherapy reported that education alleviated their concerns and improved their confidence that exercise can improve their pain and function [22].

Feedback includes both internal feedback developed within the patient and external feedback provided by the physiotherapist to ensure safe and effective performance of the exercises [2]. Both feedback to their physiotherapist and from the physiotherapist was highly valued by participants in our study. Previously published research has also reported the value of this feedback loop between patients and physiotherapists, enabling patients to work collaboratively with their physiotherapist [15, 23, 24]. Feedback that included providing proof of progress was reportedly beneficial for participants in facilitating confidence in and adherence to their rehabilitation program [23, 25]. Allison et al (2023) termed this a ‘positive symptom loop’, where evidence of symptom or physical improvements from completing their exercises were strong motivators to continue their program [23].

The WHY elements helped to develop a stronger therapeutic alliance, enabling the physiotherapist to better individualise the program to the patient and then informing HOW the exercise program was developed and prescribed.

Frequency of appointments, time to travel and time within appointments are three common barriers to regular face‐to‐face physiotherapy sessions, hence home exercise programs are often more feasible for patients [23, 26]. Most participants were prescribed a home exercise program. To some participants the home exercise programs were more convenient, to others it was difficult to be motivated when exercising from home. There is a high rate of non‐adherence to home exercise programs, commonly due to patients perceived lack of time or effectiveness of the exercises and lower habitual physical activity [15, 27]. Patients in other studies have reported mixed preferences to exercise both at home due to the convenience and also under supervision of their physiotherapist which can be more motivating [7]. The selection between a home exercise program and a supervised exercise program is heavily influenced by the patient's personal preferences, time available, exercise experience and confidence in their own performance of the exercise program.

When prescribing the exercise program, it is recommended that all dosage parameters, intensity, repetitions, holds, sets, rest, frequency and duration should be included [10]. According to Smith et al., intensity was the least described dosage parameter amongst randomized control trials in their exercise interventions [10]. This is reflected in the experiences reported in this study. Participants valued the provision of specific dosage parameters that were individualized to them. Participants also highly valued the opportunity to discuss, ask questions and collaborate with their physiotherapist regarding the dosage parameters. If this was lacking, participants reported lacking confidence in performing their exercise program correctly. Inadequate provision of education and appropriate dosage parameters may lead to poorer adherence to the program, and providing patients the opportunity to be more active in their exercise program development may enhance adherence [28, 29].

Exercise programs are commonly prescribed via verbal communication, written instructions and/or the use of technology like mobile phone applications [15]. Participants reported a variety of preferences influenced by their own perceived benefits and complexity of using applications versus paper handouts, the exercise program complexity, previous experiences and personal preference. Therefore, the suitability of each tool should be individualized to each patient. In previous research, patients reported benefits from using an application by providing accountability, motivation and enhancing their relationship with their physiotherapist by feeling more connected between in‐person appointments [15, 23]. Of all participants that were provided an application, all reported that it should exist to support an exercise program that was developed collaboratively with their physiotherapist and individualised to them. Patients elsewhere have also reported that the use of an application cannot replace the role of the physiotherapist, but could function as an extension of their physiotherapist [15].

Some participants reported preferring a paper handout, although some found them hard to understand. Patient's elsewhere have reported limitations with following the correct technique when being provided a paper handout [15]. Ouegnin and Valdes reported that participants preferred a video over a combination of paper handout and video or paper handout alone [27]. Similar to our findings, some participants in Ouegnin and Valdes study preferred a paper handout for simple exercises as it was easier to access [27]. It is clear across the literature and in our study that whilst there is a preference for videos as a tool for home exercise program prescription, it is still important to individualize the modality based on the patients' preferences, exercise experience, health literacy and ability or willingness to use technology [15, 22, 26].

Limitations

In considering the limitations of this study, some methodological points should be acknowledged, particularly regarding reflexivity. In grounded theory, theoretical preconceptions should be minimal, however in practice this can be challenging [11, 14]. Especially when researchers are experienced in the area of study, potentially resulting in an informed Grounded Theory approach [30]. The research team that conducted the interviews comprised of two clinical physiotherapists with experience working in musculoskeletal physiotherapy. A further two expert physiotherapists were involved in the data analysis, both Professors in Musculoskeletal Physiotherapy. Whilst the researcher's experience and knowledge in the exercise prescription process helped inform the interview question development and data interpretation, it also required active reflection and ongoing critical discussion to minimise the risk of bias by interpreting findings with prior expectations rather than inductively as further data was collected.

Authenticity and credibility were supported through multiple rounds of data interpretation when coding, and the inclusion of verbatim quotes providing a range of perspectives. To improve transferability, we aimed to recruit a wide variety of participants with varying demographics, locations around Australia and with different presenting musculoskeletal conditions through purposive sampling. We only included participants who had attended physiotherapy within the past month as participants memory and interpretation of their experience may be affected by recall bias over time. The researchers acknowledge the possibility that participants recruited may have held more positive views of physiotherapy. No participant in the study had a current therapeutic relationship with any member of the research team. One researcher had a previous therapeutic relationship with three participants. This researcher did not perform those interviews.

Conclusion

Our study provides insight into the exercise prescription process from the patient's perspective and experiences. An overarching theme across exercise prescription literature is the need for the program and the prescription process to be individualised to the patient, their context, current physical abilities, co‐morbidities, goals, beliefs and expectations [6, 20, 21, 31, 32]. From our study, patients appreciate a collaborative and individualised approach to all elements of the exercise prescription process. The key elements of the exercise prescription process are not only important for effective exercise prescription from a clinician perspective but also are recognised as meaningful by the patient for understanding their exercises, building confidence in their program, their rehabilitation and their therapeutic alliance with the physiotherapist.
