Long-term effects of a bony mucoperiosteal flap on bone preservation in bony impacted upper third molar surgery: a prospective exploratory study
Authors: Wei Qi, Jing Qian, Di Le, Leihao Hu, Jiannan Li, Rong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis exploratory study evaluates the long-term effects of a maxillary bony mucoperiosteal flap (BMF) technique on bone preservation and periodontal condition following extraction of deeply impacted upper third molars (UTMs).MethodsIn this two-arm, prospective exploratory study with a parallel-group design, bony impacted UTMs were allocated to either a control group (tooth extraction with pericoronal bone removal) or an experimental group (UTMs removal using the BMF technique). Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) was performed preoperatively and at 10–12 months postoperatively. Long-term bone volume change (LBVC) in the maxillary tuberosity was evaluated using Mimics 21.0 and Geomagic software. Distal probing depth and clinical attachment loss of the adjacent upper second molars were also recorded.ResultsA total of 28 bony impacted UTMs from 24 patients (6 men and 18 women) were included. The mean age was 23.8 ± 3.8 years in the control group and 26.5 ± 5.6 years in the experimental group. Preoperative and postoperative tuberosity volumes were 1053.7 ± 385.9 mm3 and 609.8 ± 235.2mm3 in the control group, and 762.6 ± 280.2 mm3 and 649.3 ± 257.0 mm3 in the experimental group, respectively. The BMF technique was associated with a significantly lower LBVC compared with the conventional method. No significant changes in probing depth or clinical attachment loss were observed in either group.ConclusionGiven the exploratory nature of this study, our findings provide preliminary evidence that the BMF technique may achieve superior volumetric outcomes without compromising the periodontal health of adjacent teeth. However, due to the limited sample size, the observed degree of bone preservation may be an overestimation. Therefore, higher-level evidence from adequately powered, well-matched prospective studies is required to further substantiate the long-term benefits of this technique.Trial registrationThis research was registered in the Chinese Medical Research Registration and Filing System (Registration Number: ChiCTR2400083354, registration date: 2024/4/22).

Introduction

Most patients with bony impacted upper third molars (UTMs) tend to be asymptomatic; however, the number of patients requiring removal due to molar distalization during orthodontic treatment is increasing [1]. Mesially inclined UTMs have also been proven to be a significant risk factor for root resorption of the upper second molars (USMs) [2–6]. Compared with lower third molars, research on the surgical protocols for bony impacted UTMs has been limited, and most relevant studies have focused on bony removal strategies [7–9]. In general, the maxillary tuberosity could be used as a frequently used intraoral donor site for alveolar ridge defect reconstruction, whether through block bone or complex tissue transplantation [10–13]. Intraoperative bone removal may not only increase surgical trauma but also severely reduce the potential of the maxillary tuberosity as a tissue donor site.

We previously proposed a novel maxillary bony mucoperiosteal flap (BMF) technique for bony impacted UTMs surgery and compared its short-term effects on postoperative complications with those of the traditional bone removal strategy [14]. The results indicated that the technique effectively preserved local bone without significantly increasing postoperative reactions within the first 7 days after surgery. Despite the initial success, concerns remained due to the challenging nature of the surgery and the porous structure of the maxillary tuberosity; thus, further clarification was required regarding the long-term contribution of the preserved bone to volume stability to confirm the clinical utility of the technique. Building upon our earlier findings, this continued study was conducted to evaluate the 1-year outcomes of this innovative technique, specifically focusing on changes in maxillary tuberosity bone volume and the periodontal status of the adjacent USMs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted in the outpatient clinic of the Department of General Dentistry, Peking University School of Stomatology. Eligible participants were healthy individuals aged 18–35 years presenting with fully bony impacted UTMs (classified as Archer Type C or D) and scheduled for extraction to facilitate orthodontic molar distalization or restoration of adjacent second molars. All included patients had stable USMs without untreated apical or periodontal pathology and no systemic disease. Patients who smoked, were pregnant, had chronic periodontal disease, or were unable to complete follow-up were excluded.

As this surgical technique represents a novel approach and no prior data on postoperative maxillary tuberosity bone volume changes were available, a formal sample size calculation was not feasible. Based on previous studies on third molar surgery [15–17], we initially planned to enroll 30 patients, with allocation to the experimental and control groups at a 1:1 ratio (Fig. 1).

Randomization was performed by the study coordinator at the time of surgery using an automatically generated randomization list in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Allocation concealment was ensured by means of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after patient enrollment. According to the randomization sequence, the experimental group reached its target sample size of 15 sites earlier. As orthodontic treatment constituted the primary indication for extraction in most cases, and preliminary observations suggested superior short-term bone preservation with the BMF technique, recruitment to the control group was discontinued at 12 sites to prioritize patient benefit. In addition, one patient requested allocation to the experimental group to preserve bone tissue for orthodontic purposes; this protocol modification was formally approved by the Ethics Committee. 23 patients (with 27 randomized sides) and outcome assessors responsible for volumetric and periodontal evaluations were blinded to group allocation during measurement and data analysis. Although the operating surgeon was necessarily aware of group assignment to ensure appropriate surgical execution, they were not involved in outcome assessment.

This study was designed as an exploratory clinical investigation, rather than a large scale randomized controlled trial. Early termination of the control group, potential selection bias, and the relatively limited sample size represent inherent limitations of this study, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, the overall study design was rigorously developed and conducted in compliance with clinical research guidelines, and the present results may provide preliminary implications for clinical practice.

Surgical duration

Surgical duration was recorded for each upper third molar and defined as the time from the initial incision to completion of wound closure.

Postoperative complications

Preoperative maximal mouth opening was measured as the distance between the mesio-incisal angles of the upper and lower central incisors. Facial contour was assessed according to a previously described method [15]. All evaluations were performed on postoperative days 2 and 7. Changes in mouth opening and facial contour were recorded as trismus and swelling, respectively. Pain intensity was assessed using a 0–100 visual analogue scale (VAS). In addition, the occurrence of sinus perforation and postoperative infection was recorded.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by a single maxillofacial surgeon with 12 years of experience, as previously described [14]. In the experimental group, the buccal bone overlying the UTMs was preserved using the BMF technique [14]. Briefly, after elevation of the mesial mucosal flap to the distobuccal corner of the adjacent second molar, cortical osteotomy was performed using a piezoelectric surgical device (Surgic Smart, 170VA; Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guilin, China) at the distobuccal, distal, and distolingual aspects of the second molar, extending toward the impacted third molar. The maxillary tuberosity, together with the attached soft tissue, was then mobilized as a single unit and displaced distally following partial elevation of the buccal mucoperiosteal flap. The impacted UTMs were subsequently removed using a dental elevator through the created bone-splitting space, with tooth sectioning performed when necessary (Fig. 2). In our clinical protocol, the accuracy of bone repositioning was primarily determined by assessing the contour consistency of the maxillary tuberosity region relative to its preoperative state. Precise reposition was confirmed intraoperatively when no bony steps or depressions were palpable and the overlying soft tissue could be precisely aligned without tension. In the control group, the pericoronal bone was removed to reduce bony resistance, after which the UTMs were extracted using a dental elevator and forceps (Fig. 3). Tooth sectioning was performed when required. Patients were followed up on postoperative days 2 and 7 and at 12 months postoperatively. Nevertheless, all randomized patients (23 patients with 27 UTMs) remained unaware of their assigned surgical intervention throughout the study. All outcome assessors were fully blinded to group allocation and treatment details during radiological evaluation, bone volume assessment, and periodontal measurements. CBCT was performed preoperatively and at 12 months after extraction (voxel size: 0.3 mm). In selected cases where sinus perforation was suspected, an additional CBCT scan was obtained on postoperative day 2 to verify repositioning of the bone segment and assess maxillary sinus status. To comply with the ALARA principle and minimize radiation exposure, these scans were acquired using a low-dose protocol with a limited field of view (60 mm × 60 mm; 85 kV, 3 mA, 4.8 s), in accordance with established safety recommendations for maxillofacial imaging [18].

Maxillary tuberosity volume was measured preoperatively and at 10–12 months postoperatively (one patient in the experimental group was evaluated at 6 months due to pregnancy planning). CBCT data were imported into Mimics (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium), and an appropriate threshold was applied to delineate the cortical boundaries of the tuberosity. The region of interest was defined in three dimensions using the inferior border of the pterygomaxillary fissure (PMF) as a fixed reference point. In the sagittal plane, the measurement extended mesially to the distal cementoenamel junction (CEJ) of the adjacent second molar and inferiorly to the lower boundary of the tuberosity. In the axial plane, the buccopalatal extent encompassed the alveolar bone. Preoperative CBCT parameters were replicated for postoperative measurements in the same patient. Areas not included in the initial mask due to grayscale variation were manually refined, filled, and merged with the original mask (Fig. 4). A three-dimensional model of the tuberosity was then generated, smoothed, and exported as stereolithography (STL) data. Volume measurements were subsequently performed using Geomagic Studio (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA).

All volumetric measurements were performed three times under identical conditions by an experienced engineer, with supervision from a maxillofacial radiology specialist. Assessments were conducted in a fully blinded manner to minimize bias. The mean of the three measurements was used for analysis. Long-term bone volume change (%) was calculated as [(preoperative volume − postoperative volume) / preoperative volume] × 100.

Measurement of the distance between the PMF and the USMs

The distance from the PMF to the USMs, denoted as d-PU, was measured for all cases. First, the lowest margin of the PMF was identified, and the coronal plane was reoriented to align with this reference point. Subsequently, the perpendicular distance from the coronal plane to the CEJ of the USMs was measured, and this value was recorded as the d-PU (Fig. 5).

Periodontal parameter

By using a Michigan O-Ring round dental periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA), a periodontist with 12 years of experience measured the probing depth (PD) and attachment loss (AL) of the periodontal socket at the adjacent USMs’ distal buccal axis angle (PDb and Alb, respectively) and lingual axis angle (PDl and All, respectively). The periodontist was unaware of the groupings of both groups preoperatively and 12 months after surgery.

Statistical analysis

The differences in surgical duration, postoperative complications, bone volume, and postoperative changes in PD and AL between the two groups were analyzed using an independent two-sample t test.

The reliability of repeated bone volume measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement) and Cronbach’s α. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was reported for the ICC.

To account for baseline imbalances in d-PU and bone volume between groups, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted. In this model, group allocation was specified as the fixed factor, postoperative bone reduction rate as the dependent variable, and baseline d-PU and baseline bone volume as covariates. This approach allowed assessment of the independent effect of the BMF technique on long-term bone volume change (LBVC). Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test, and the ANCOVA model was re-examined accordingly.

Furthermore, a linear mixed-effects model was applied to compare outcomes between groups, with patient included as a random effect to account for clustering and potential correlation between bilateral teeth within the same individual. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 24.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

We included 28 teeth from 24 patients, of which 12 sites from 11 patients were allocated to the control group and 16 sites from 15 patients to the experimental group. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Except for one case of bone plate fracture (Fig. 5), all BMF flaps were successfully prepared and repositioned en bloc. Stable fixation of the flap was achieved following full-thickness mucoperiosteal suturing. Owing to the learning curve, surgical duration was longer in the experimental group than in the control group; however, no statistically significant differences were observed in postoperative VAS scores, swelling, or trismus (Table 1). In addition, no cases of postoperative infection or sinus perforation were observed in either group.

After more than 6 months of follow-up, complete cortication was observed in all cases in both groups. Preoperative and postoperative tuberosity volumes were 1053.7 ± 385.9 mm3 and 609.8 ± 235.2 mm3, respectively, in the control group, and 762.6 ± 280.2 mm3 and 649.3 ± 257.0 mm3, respectively, in the experimental group. One case in the experimental group, characterized by a discontinuous and thin buccal bone wall, exhibited markedly greater volume reduction than other cases (Fig. 5), possibly due to intraoperative fracture during BMF preparation; nevertheless, the bone segment remained attached and was preserved. Overall, a substantially greater reduction rate was observed in the control group than in the experimental group (41.5% ± 9.4% [95% CI: 36.3%–46.8%] vs. 16.1% ± 9.0% [95% CI: 12.1%–21.0%], P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Excellent intra-rater reliability was exhibited for repeated bone volume measurements, with an ICC of 0.925 (95% CI: 0.884–0.954, P < 0.001) and a Cronbach’s α of 0.974. No significant differences were observed among the three repeated measurements (P > 0.05).

ANCOVA was performed to adjust for baseline imbalances in preoperative bone volume and d-PU. After adjustment, a significant difference in volume reduction rate remained between the groups. The adjusted mean reduction rate was 42.80% (95% CI: 36.80%–48.80%) in the control group and 15.44% (95% CI: 10.06%–20.82%) in the experimental group, corresponding to a mean difference of 27.36% (95% CI: 19.30%–35.42%; P < 0.001). Levene’s test suggested homogeneity of variances (F = 0.051; df1 = 1; df2 = 24; P = 0.823). Neither baseline bone volume (P = 0.449) nor d-PU (P = 0.980) significantly influenced the outcome. The large effect size (partial η2 = 0.774) and acceptable statistical power further support the consistency of the observed inter-group difference.

To account for within-patient clustering, a linear mixed-effects model was applied, which suggested a significant difference in volume reduction rate between the experimental and control groups (F = 49.358; df = 1, 24; P < 0.001), indicating that the findings remained consistent after adjusting for non-independence of bilateral observations.

The preoperative PDb and PDl of the second molar were 3.1 ± 0.7 mm and 3.9 ± 0.9 mm, respectively, in the control group, and 3.1 ± 0.7 mm and 3.8 ± 0.8 mm, respectively, in the experimental group. No significant postoperative changes in PD were observed in either group (PDb: P = 0.387; PDl: P = 0.162). In addition, no AL was detected in either group (Table 2).

Discussion

In this exploratory study, we evaluated the long-term effects of BMF on bone preservation following extraction of bony impacted UTMs. Our findings provide preliminary evidence that BMF may be associated with reduced postoperative bone resorption in the maxillary tuberosity after one year, compared with conventional bone removal strategies, particularly in cases with intact bone elevation (Fig. 6). Extra caution was suggested when CBCT indicates an extremely thin or discontinuous cortical plate. However, the small sample size limited our ability to fully explore the relationship between impaction type and periodontal outcomes or bone volume, thereby restricting the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, following preliminary tendency of the advantages of BMF in terms of bone preservation and acceptable surgical morbidity, recruitment to the control group was curtailed to prioritize patient benefit. This resulted in baseline imbalances in bone volume and d-PU between the two groups, which may have led to an overestimation of the effect of BMF on bone preservation and represents a key limitation of the present study. Although ANCOVA analyses indicated a significant difference in bone volume change between the two techniques, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, based on these preliminary findings, future studies with adequately calculated sample sizes, improved baseline balance, and more rigorous randomized controlled designs are warranted to validate the potential advantages of BMF in bone preservation.

Compared with other intraoral donor sites for bone harvesting, the maxillary tuberosity is considered advantageous due to its relative ease of access and low complication rate [10–13]. Previous studies have not only demonstrated favorable outcomes in alveolar defect reconstruction but also reported superior bone vitality and faster regeneration compared with retromolar grafts. Accordingly, preservation of bony tissue during UTMs surgery is essential to maintain the potential of the tuberosity as a donor site. In the present study, changes in maxillary tuberosity bone volume were therefore selected as the primary outcome measure. From a clinical perspective, greater residual bone volume may translate into increased availability of autologous graft material for future reconstructive procedures. Several studies have evaluated tuberosity bone volume using CBCT. For example, Diarjani et al. reported that the available bone volume for transplantation in this region is approximately 84 mm3 [19]. This estimate differs substantially from our findings, which may be attributable to differences in study populations and measurement protocols. Specifically, previous studies predominantly included erupted UTMs, and the distal boundary of the maxillary tuberosity was not clearly defined. In contrast, our results suggest that, in cases of bony impacted UTMs, the maxillary tuberosity may possess greater potential as a source of graftable bone. Preservation of bone in this region may therefore enhance the availability of autologous material for intraoral reconstruction [11–13]. Nevertheless, further studies are required to more precisely characterize tuberosity bone volume in patients with fully bony impacted UTMs and to establish standardized measurement approaches.

CBCT plays a critical role in three-dimensional evaluation, and its data can be imported into third-party software platforms for advanced image processing. A variety of such programs have been developed for quantitative analysis in both medical and dental research [20–22]. In the present study, Mimics 21.0 was used for volumetric assessment. Given that the maxillary tuberosity predominantly consists of cancellous bone, an appropriate threshold must be selected to clearly delineate cortical boundaries while minimizing image noise [23–26]. The marrow cavity was subsequently filled to ensure accurate volume reconstruction. Because UTMs are often removed for orthodontic indications, distalization of the second molar may occur in some patients, rendering UTMs unsuitable as reference landmarks. In contrast, the PMF provides a stable and readily identifiable reference point in both CBCT images and Mimics software. In cases involving molar distalization, portions of dental tissue may enter the measurement field during postoperative evaluation. In such instances, tooth structures extending beyond the alveolar bone were excluded from the analysis. Notably, the use of a fixed region of interest defined by the PMF ensured consistency of volumetric measurements within the same patient. Furthermore, the excellent intra-rater reliability observed in this study suggested internal consistency of the volumetric assessment.

Morphological and dimensional changes begin immediately after tooth extraction and continue until the crestal cortical plates have fully bridged. Approximately two-thirds of ridge dimensional alterations occur within the first 3 months, with up to 50% reduction in ridge width and more pronounced alveolar bone loss in the molar region [27–33]. Araújo et al. reported that socket cortication can be observed as early as 10 weeks post-extraction [29]. However, substantial morphological and dimensional changes may persist for up to 12 months. Fok et al., using CBCT, observed alterations in bone density and volume following UTMs extraction, with no evidence of cortication at 3 months postoperatively [28]. Similarly, Lindhe et al. found that lamellar bone and bone marrow remained predominant in the posterior maxilla after 16 weeks of healing, with dense cortical bone present only in a limited number of cases [31]. Furthermore, Bertl et al. evaluated 250 post-extraction sockets using computed tomography and reported that nearly 80% were non-corticated or only partially corticated, whereas more than 80% exhibited complete cortication at 9–12 months postoperatively [33]. Given that the maxillary tuberosity consists primarily of cancellous bone with a relatively thin cortical layer, a follow-up period of approximately 12 months is necessary to ensure adequate cortication for reliable digital volumetric analysis.

It is also important to consider the significant baseline difference in bone volume between the two groups. Further CBCT analysis implied that d-PU could partially explain the above mentioned discrepancy. A shorter d-PU reflects a more distal position of the USMs, which may be associated with reduced anatomical dimensions and, consequently, a smaller tuberosity volume. However, as no standardized classification based on the mesiodistal distance between the USMs and PMF currently exists, this variable was not accounted for during patient recruitment. This likely contributed to the relatively larger baseline bone volumes observed in the control group.

Nevertheless, the observed baseline imbalance represents an important limitation of this study. This imbalance was partly attributable to early termination of recruitment in the control group and partly to the relative rarity of clinically high-positioned impacted teeth. In combination with the small sample size, these factors contributed to uneven baseline characteristics between groups.

The healing of periodontal tissues adjacent to second molars after third molar extraction has been a major concern in third molar surgery and has garnered widespread attention. Regrettably, much of the research has concentrated on the variability seen in lower third molars [34–37]. In their recent systematic review, Pang et al. reported that lower third molar extraction can lead to periodontal PD reduction and clinical attachment level changes. However, periodontal defects remain detectable ≥ 6 months postoperatively, and baseline PD is strongly correlated with the final PD [35]. Similarly, Coleman et al. found that the extraction of impacted UTMs tends to improve the PD of adjacent USMs. However, the authors included patients aged 14–24 years, with both partially and fully impacted UTMs [37]. Moreover, USMs might erupt incompletely, potentially resulting in pseudoperiodontal pockets, which are characterized by increased PD without corresponding AL. In a randomized controlled trial, Zhang et al. demonstrated that mandibular third molar extraction aggravates the AL of the adjacent tooth at postoperative 8th week, with gradual recovery observed over time [38]. Herein, no significant pre- to post-operative differences in periodontal parameters were observed, possibly due to following factors. Initially, patients had good baseline periodontal health with fully erupted USMs and normal PD and no AL. Secondly, the senior surgeon with stable operative techniques restricted bone removal as much as possible during osteotomy to mitigate adverse effects on adjacent USMs in the control group. Finally, limited sample size and potential selection bias may account for the findings. Therefore, further large-sample prospective randomized controlled trials are needed for verification.

In addition to probing depth, the width of keratinized gingiva represents an important parameter for evaluating postoperative healing. Furthermore, changes in bone volume may influence the thickness and quality of the overlying mucosa [39, 40]. Although soft tissues were preserved in both groups during surgery, this parameter was not assessed, which limits the comprehensiveness of the evaluation of the BMF technique. In addition, although outcome assessors were blinded, the operating surgeon was aware of group allocation, which may have introduced performance bias and potentially influenced postoperative bone volume outcomes. Future studies should aim to minimize such bias by implementing standardized surgical protocols and, where feasible, incorporating objective measures such as procedural video recording and independent review.

Finally, although operative duration was not a primary outcome of this study, it is closely associated with postoperative complications and wound healing [41]. In the present study, the variability in surgical time was largely attributable to the fundamental objective of this technique: preservation of bony tissue, particularly maintaining the integrity of bone segments and their continuity with the overlying periosteum during the procedure. Although operative time was longer in the initial cases for each impaction type, all cases except one achieved intact bone–periosteum integration. Accordingly, while operative duration may influence short-term postoperative outcomes, its impact on long-term bone volume changes is likely limited.

In conclusion, our findings provide preliminary evidence that the BMF technique may be associated with improved volumetric outcomes compared with conventional methods. However, given the exploratory nature and inherent limitations of this study, the magnitude of the observed effect may be overestimated. Therefore, large-scale, well-designed randomized controlled trials with standardized measurement protocols and clearly defined clinical endpoints are required to validate these findings. Such studies are essential to determine whether this technique offers a definitive advantage over conventional approaches for long-term bone preservation.
