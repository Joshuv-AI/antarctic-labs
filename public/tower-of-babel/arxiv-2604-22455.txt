On the Hybrid Nature of ABPMS Process Frames and its Implications on Automated Process Discovery Journal: Information Systems
Anti Alman, Izack Cohen, Avigdor Gal, Fabrizio Maria Maggi, Marco Montali

Abstract
A core component of any AI-Augmented Business Process Management System (ABPMS) is the process frame , which gives the system process-awareness and defines the boundaries in which the system must operate. Compared to traditional process models, the process frame should, in principle, provide a somewhat more permissive representation of the managed processes, such that the (semi) autonomous behavior of an ABPMS, referred to as framed autonomy , could emerge. At the same time, it is not limited to a single linguistic or symbolic formalism and may incorporate heterogeneous knowledge ranging from predefined procedures to commonsense rules and best practices. In this paper, we conceptualize the notion of an ABPMS process frame as a hybrid business process representation, consisting of semi-concurrently executed procedural and declarative process models. We rely on our earlier works to outline the execution semantics of this type of process frame, arguing in favor of adopting the open-world assumption of the declarative paradigm also for procedural process models. The latter leads to a constraint-like interpretation, where each procedural model is considered to constrain the activities within that model, without imposing explicit execution requirements nor limitations on activities that may be present in other models. This is analogous to existing declarative languages, such as Declare , where each constraint has a direct effect only on the specific activities being constrained. Given this similarity, we propose mapping subsets of discovered declarative constraints into equivalent semi-concurrently executed procedural fragments, thus laying the foundation for a corresponding process (frame) discovery approach.

Introduction

The research manifesto on AI-Augmented Business Process Management Systems (ABPMS) outlines a vision in which the traditional BPMS lifecycle is augmented with AI technologies, enabling the system to “reason about the current state of the process (or across several processes) to determine a course of action that improves the performance of the process”
[
34

process frame
, which provides the system with process awareness and defines the boundaries in which the system must operate to achieve its goals. The latter represents a fundamental shift from a system that supports the execution of business processes, potentially through automating some tasks, towards a system that exhibits a degree of autonomy over how the processes are executed, which decisions are taken therein, and what optimizations are implemented over time, with the end-goal of uplifting business process management systems to a level of autonomous decision-making comparable to human actors.

In this paper, we propose an approach for representing a business process (or a set of processes) within the process frame, as this was left open in
[
34

. We take inspiration from hybrid business process representations

[

In particular, we conceptualize the notion of an ABPMS process frame as a hybrid business process representation, consisting of semi-concurrently executed procedural and declarative models, moving beyond a single modeling formalism and supporting the incorporation of heterogeneous knowledge. In this context, we argue for adopting the open-world assumption of the declarative modeling paradigm also for procedural models, such that, each model of the process frame, declarative or procedural, would impose explicit execution requirements and/or limitations only on the activities within that model.

As in our earlier works, we rely on Petri nets

Instead, the boundaries that would apply to any human actor, in terms of process executions and modifications, should also apply to the autonomy of the ABPMS. This may include, for example, legal requirements (e.g., obtaining a permit before starting construction), internal operating procedures (e.g., mandatory review of some decisions), organizational strategy (e.g., maintaining production costs below some threshold), and other similar knowledge that is relevant for the process execution.

The question of how to express such knowledge, as a process frame, is left open in
[
34

Reusing existing process modeling languages, especially ones with precise execution semantics, is indeed a natural starting point, given the importance of process-aware execution in the ABPMS vision (cf.

However, this entails a conceptual shift from explicit modeling of complete process executions to defining the behavioral boundaries of an ABPMS based on heterogeneous sources of process knowledge, which, in turn, warrants a corresponding paradigm shift in how process modeling languages are used.

2.1
Conceptualization

Contrary to traditional business process models, especially procedural ones, a process frame is not required to provide a complete specification of an entire business process. Instead, the actual executed process should emerge, within the boundaries of the process frame, through the reasoning capabilities of the ABPMS itself. To support this, the notion of a process frame, as described in
[
34
]
, is broader than that of a traditional process model. In particular, a process frame:

(i)

is not limited to a single linguistic or symbolic formalism;

(ii)

may incorporate heterogeneous knowledge ranging from predefined procedures to commonsense rules and best practices; and

(iii)

should, in principle, provide a more permissive process representation, such that, the (semi) autonomous behavior of an ABPMS could emerge.

We propose reusing existing process modeling languages for the process frame, as also suggested in
[
34
]
, but with a layer of overarching interpretation in support of the aforementioned broader characteristics. In that direction, we take inspiration from hybrid business process representations
[
9
]
and the recent call for holistic business process management
[
11
]
, combining both with our earlier contributions on the enactment and monitoring of interdependent business processes
[
6
,
5
,
2
,
80
]
and with our proposal for developing and adopting the multi-model paradigm for business process management
[
8
,
51
]
.

Similar to
[
34
]
, we do not fix the concrete modeling languages at the conceptual level. However, a distinction is made between procedural and declarative specifications, following the corresponding process modeling paradigms, where procedural refers to definitions of concrete activity sequences, and declarative refers to definitions of temporal activity relations and cardinalities over complete execution sequences. For example, a procedural specification may require
A
,
B
,
C
to be executed only in that order, while a declarative specification may require every
A
to be eventually followed by
B
at some, otherwise unspecified, future point in the process execution.

The starting point of our conceptualization is the open-world assumption of declarative process models, i.e., every execution, that does not contradict the model, is considered to be allowed
[
61
]
. This alone introduces inherent permissiveness into the process frame, but it also forms the foundation for moving beyond a single modeling formalism and supporting the incorporation of heterogeneous knowledge. More specifically, it enables compositionality of individual process specifications, akin to the compositionality of constraints in the
Declare
language
[
4
]
, such that, the existence of one specification does not preclude the existence of another, but rather, serves as a further refinement of the overall allowed behavior. The general idea is to think of each individual specification as some logical component of the overall behavioral boundary of the ABPMS, wether that component is a process, a sub-process, a common procedure, a dependency between some activities, an orchestration of processes, sub-processes, or procedures, etc. These specifications will then interact through behavioral overlaps, which, in this paper, correspond to sub-sequences of activity executions being governed by multiple specifications.

The specification boundaries may overlap, in which case, the allowed behavior corresponds to the intersection of the allowed behaviors of the corresponding individual process specifications. Meanwhile, the union of specification boundaries corresponds to all behaviors that the process frame is aware of, i.e., there may be other, potentially relevant, behaviors that fall outside the scope of the current process frame. If the ABPMS can observe such behaviors, then it should be decided if these behaviors are allowed or forbidden, or the process frame should be expanded by modifying the individual specifications or by adding new ones, potentially as part of autonomous reframing that is bounded by a corresponding meta-frame

2.2
Formalization

We start with some preliminary notions for formalizing the process frame. Given a finite set
\Sigma
of activities, a trace
\rho
over
\Sigma
is a finite sequence of activities in
\Sigma
. To clearly distinguish the activities contained in a trace, we use the tuple notation, e.g., the activity sequence
a
,
b
,
c
is denoted by
\langle a,b,c\rangle
, and an empty trace is denoted by
\langle\rangle
. As customary, we denote the set of traces over
\Sigma
as
\Sigma^{*}
. Since our process frame contains multiple “local” specifications operating over overlapping, distinct sets of activities, we need to define what such specifications see of a “global” trace defined over them. We do so through the notion of
trace projection
. Given a trace
\rho\in\Sigma^{*}
and a subset
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma
of activities, the projection of
\rho
on
\Sigma^{\prime}
, written
\rho|_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
is the sequence obtained from
\rho
by removing all elements that are not in
\Sigma^{\prime}
.

In the most general sense, a
process specification
defined over a set
\Sigma
is nothing else than a subset of
\Sigma^{*}
, identifying which traces belong to (or conform with) the specification (or, equivalently, which traces the specification accepts). As the set of traces belonging to a process specification may be infinite, one uses a compact, finite description (such as, for example, an accepting Petri net, or a set of
Declare
constraints). We generically assume that, for such a compact description
P
, we can then characterize the language of
P
, written
\mathcal{L}(P)
, as the set of traces in
\Sigma^{*}
captured by
P
.

Definition 1
(Process specification)

A
process specification
is a pair
\langle\Sigma,P\rangle
, where
\Sigma
is a finite set of activities and
P
is a process description over
\Sigma
.
\langle\Sigma,P\rangle
accepts trace
\rho\in\Sigma^{*}
if
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(P)
.

We formalize a
process frame
as a combination of process specifications defined over overlapping sets of activities. Such specifications can be seen as
local
process specifications that
synchronize
on their common activities. This is substantiated as follows. During the execution, whenever an activity
t
is performed, local specifications that do not have
t
among their own activities will simply ignore it, while those that have it will all execute it. This implicitly requires that such specifications will all need to be in a state where
t
is enabled, and will update their internal state by executing
t
concurrently.

Two observations are in place. First, we are not dictating
how
such local specifications are internally structured, i.e., they may be specified using Petri nets (with or without silent transitions and repeated labels), or other temporal/dynamic formalisms.
Second,
global constraints
applied to all specifications can still be defined by introducing a specification that sees the whole set of activities of other specifications. The interplay across specifications can then be captured by requiring that, given a trace over the entire set of activities, every specification (including the one containing global constraints) accepts the projection of the trace on its own set of activities.

Definition 2
(Process frame)

A
process frame

\mathcal{S}
is a finite set of process specifications
\langle\Sigma_{1},P_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle\Sigma_{n},P_{n}\rangle
, called
specifications
. The
set of activities
of
\mathcal{S}
is
\mathcal{S}.\Sigma=\bigcup_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\Sigma_{i}
, and the
set of common activities
of
\mathcal{S}
is
\mathcal{S}.\Sigma^{c}=\bigcap_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\Sigma_{i}
.
\mathcal{S}
accepts trace
\rho\in(\mathcal{S}.\Sigma)^{*}
if, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have that specification
\langle\Sigma_{i},P_{i}\rangle
accepts
\rho|_{\Sigma_{i}}
(in the sense of Definition
1
).

Definition
2
leads to relatively simple semantics where a trace is accepted by the process frame iff each specification therein accepts the projection of that trace on the alphabet of that specification, which is equivalent to synchronous execution of each activity over all specifications containing that activity.

We conclude by pointing out that Definition
2
can be grounded using any languages that allow for reasoning in terms of accepted and non-accepted activity sequences, including Petri nets
[
58
,
77
]
and
Declare

[
62
]
, which are used throughout the rest of this paper as representative examples of procedural and declarative modeling paradigms, respectively. In addition, the level of compositionality can be approached from the
representational
perspective as a convenient way to control the complexity and the different behavioral requirements of a given ABPMS. In fact, the set of traces accepted by variants of the same process frame, obtained by gluing together or spiting the specifications therein, will remain identical, which is a direct and deliberate consequence of the way a process frame is defined.

2.4
Implications on Discovery

In our previous works
[
6
,
5
]
, we have explored the modeling, enactment, and monitoring of interdependent process specifications, similar to the ones in this paper. In addition, we have outlined an initial connection between these works and framed autonomy in
[
2
,
80
]
, but without the conceptualization nor the general formalization of the ABPMS process frame provided in this paper. Another missing component is that of automated process discovery. While the usual motivation, i.e., capturing the as-is behavior of a business process
[
35
]
, applies also for the process frame, as discussed in this paper, there are some important implications to consider.

First, process discovery approaches rely on historical execution data, which may contain noise, infrequent behaviors, systematic mistakes, etc. As a result, automated process discovery alone is unlikely to yield a complete and accurate process frame. Instead, it should be positioned as an approach for discovering an initial process frame, that is then adjusted according to the expected behavioral boundaries of an ABPMS. One can take multiple strategies here. For example, first discovering an overview of the underlying processes, and then manually adding missing details (i.e., rarely observed valid behaviors). Another option, following the open-world assumption, is to first discover a permissive representation, which allows all observed behavior, and then manually constraining the behaviors outside the expected behavioral boundaries of a given ABPMS. The process frame, as introduced in this paper, is especially suitable for the latter, as additional specifications can always be added to constrain the behaviors that would be otherwise allowed by the specifications already present in the process frame (cf.
Section

2.1
).

Second, the ABPMS manifesto explicitly highlights that an ABPMS may manage more than a single process
[
34
]
. However, the majority of existing process discovery approaches are developed with a focus on discovering a single isolated process. This includes contemporary approaches, such as Heuristics Miner
[
78
]
, Inductive Miner
[
49
]
, and Split Miner
[
10
]
, as well as, hybrid approaches, such as Fusion Miner
[
31
]
and the discovery of hierarchical models in
[
54
]
. Similar argument can also be made for object-centric approaches, e.g.,
[
73
,
71
,
75
,
47
]
, where the notion of a business process is blurred by instead focusing on activity executions over the relevant object types and instances, with no corresponding hybrid discovery approaches yet developed.
Furthermore, recall that each specification represents, in this paper, some logical component of the overall behavioral boundary of the ABPMS, and is, therefore, not necessarily tied to specific processes nor objects therein.

3
Procedural Behaviors Under the Open-World Assumption

To recap, we propose a representation of an ABPMS process frame, consisting of declarative and procedural process specifications, where procedural specifications adopt the open-world assumption, analogous to the declarative modeling paradigm, and the interactions between the specifications are determined based on synchronous execution of their overlapping activities, with the allowed behavior of each individual specification being respected also beyond these overlaps. That is, each specification progresses according to its internal structure, synchronously with other specifications, in case of activity overlaps, while ignoring activities outside that specification.

The above leads to a constraint-like interpretation of procedural specifications, where each specification imposes explicit requirements and/or limitations only on the activities in that specification. For example,

In general, binary

and, as a further refinement, we distinguish

and

using the temporal ordering given by the constraint template. Specifically, the outgoing cardinality refers to the number of times the follower activity can occur after the predecessor activity has occurred, while the incoming cardinality refers to how many times the predecessor can occur before the follower occurs.

For example,

Combining relative activity cardinalities with the temporal orderings of
Declare
constraints allows identifying
predecessor
and
follower
sets of activities where the execution cardinality of the process changes. A limitation here is that every activity in a predecessor group (e.g., activities before a loop) will have the same relative cardinality with all activities in the follower group (e.g., activities inside the loop body). The precise point where this cardinality changes, such as the entry to a loop structure, can then be pinpointed through
Not Chain Succession
constraints between the groups. More specifically,
Not Chain Succession
constraints will exist from all activities in the predecessor group to all activities in the follower group, except for those directly preceding and succeeding the point at which the cardinality changes.

3.2
Mapping Common Procedural Behaviors

The mapping of
Declare
constraints to procedural constructs in our previous workshop paper
[
3
]
is relatively straightforward in the sense that the number of involved constraints per each construct is relatively small. This allowed determining these mappings by creating corresponding
Declare
models by hand, and manually checking if the corresponding DFA representations match the desired procedural behaviors. The inclusion of repetitions and the resulting higher number of constraints to be considered makes the aforementioned approach less feasible.

In this paper, we take a more systematic approach inspired by the process rediscovery problem
[
72
]
. More specifically, we first create a Petri net of the procedural behavior being considered, then we generate an artificial event log based on the model, discover a
Declare
model from that event log, validate that the DFA of the discovered
Declare
model corresponds to the procedural behavior being considered, and, if so, then we determine the relevant
Declare
constraints that have to be discovered for this procedural behavior to be detected correctly. The artificial event log is created, using
[
7
]
, such that it includes at least one example of each distinct activity sequence that is allowed and no activity sequences that are not allowed by the given Petri net, with an upper bound on the number of loop iterations.

The
Declare
models are discovered using a specifically developed prototype tool
4
4

4

Publicly available at:
https://github.com/antialman/multi-model-miner
, which uses the Declare Miner
[
53
]
internally, but with adjustments for the additional constraint templates considered in this paper, including the discovery of
Not Chain Succession
(
A
,
A
) (cf.
Section

3.1.2
). All constraints are discovered using 100% support threshold, but without applying vacuity detection
[
46
]
. This means that a constraint is discovered between a pair of activities if there is at least one positive example and no counterexamples of that constraint. Pruning of redundant relations
[
52
]
is not applied, as it is not available for
Alternate Succession
,
Alternate Response
, and
Alternate Precedence
, nor for the ternary constraints considered in this paper.

We focus on the same procedural constructs as in
[
3
]
, namely: sequence flows, parallelism (AND), exclusive choice (XOR), and inclusive choice (OR). Each construct, as a whole, is considered with respect to four cardinalities: 1…1, referred to as
mandatory

This results in a total of 16 procedural variants, all of which are considered in the context of a larger repeatable process, i.e., where the entire control-flow structure is encompassed by an outer loop. Note that, all of these, except for the mandatory sequence flow, require modeling both the control-flow split and join, and that the number of involved
Declare
constraints can be substantial, especially given the lack of pruning. As a result, figures of the discovered declare models are not given.

In the following, we describe the results for each procedural construct, including the four cardinalities. For sequence flows, we give the Petri nets of all cardinalities explicitly (cf.

Related Works

The ABPMS concept was first introduced in the corresponding manifesto
[
34
]
, with its earliest version appearing online in early 2022.

5

As a result, ABPMS specific research is only starting emerge, with the adoption of large language models (LLMs) being a major focus, e.g., decision-making through conversational interactions
[
15
,
23
,
24
,
22
]
, conversational model redesign
[

]
, explainability
[
39
,
50
]
, and causal reasoning
[
40
]
. There are also broader approaches that inject process-awareness into LLMs more directly, such as the Business Process Large Language Model Framework (BPLLM Framework)
[
16
]
, ProcessLLM
[
19
]
, and Large Process Model (LPM)
[
44
]
.

In addition, multiple other visions, building on the ABPMS concept, have been put forward. For example,
[
25
]
identifies the emmergence of augmented process execution, referring to continuous and automated improvement and adaptation of business processes. In the same vein,
[
37
]
advocates for developing self-modifying autonomous business process systems,
[
1
]
outlines how an ABPMS can increase business process automation is a real-world business process,
[
20
]
proposes agentic business process management, while
[
57
]
draws a connection between the
Declare
language and ABPMS.

The above overview is, by no means, comprehensive. However, it illustrates that the use of process modeling languages and the discovery of process models, has, thus far, not been explored in light of the broader characteristics of the ABPMS process frame (cf.
Section

2
). Instead, existing works either use process models as-is, do not use process models at all, or try to embed process knowledge into LLMs directly. That is certainly a valid approach for supporting the development of specific ABPMS related techniques, but we argue that this will ultimately lead to various ad-hoc representations of process knowledge, each tailored to the specific needs of different techniques.

Our conceptualization of the process frame aims to avoid such ad-hoc representations by providing a language-agnostic generalization that allows existing modeling languages to be combined in a fairly non-restrictive manner. It shares the most similarities with the recently defined notion of Hybrid Business Process Representations (HBPRs)
[
9
]
, which are also highlighted in the ABPMS manifesto
[
34
]
. Most HBPRs actually predate the term itself, with one of the earliest examples being “pockets of flexibility”
[
63
,
64
]
, that allows workflows to contain “pockets” where concrete executions are flexibly decided at runtime according to the requirements of that pocket. Interestingly, the original iteration of
Declare

[
62
]
also supports hierarchical composition of declarative constraints and procedural YAWL workflows
[
70
]
, making it another early example of an HBPR.

Hierarchical compositions of declarative and procedural models are also explored in multiple other works. For example,
[
66
]
presents formal semantics based on Petri nets and
Declare
, where any Petri net transition may require the completion of a
Declare
sub-model, and any activity of a
Declare
model may require the completion of a Petri net sub-model. A corresponding process discovery approach, on the basis of Petri nets and
Declare
, is developed in
[
54
]
. This semantics is general in the sense that other languages, such as BPMN
[
59
]
for procedural models and DCR Graphs
[
33
,
43
]
for declarative models, could also be integrated by following the same overarching principles. This generality is leveraged, for example, in
[
65
]
to develop a process discovery approach which combines
Declare
with process trees
[
48
]
instead of relying on Petri nets.

Although hierarchy provides a clear separation of declarative and procedural behaviors, it also assumes that these behaviors do not overlap in other ways, i.e., a sub-part of a process can only be declarative or procedural, but not a mix of both. An alternative is to allow (partial) intertwining of procedural and declarative process models by applying both on the same process execution (semi-)concurrently. This approach is taken in
[
30
]
, where declarative constraints are applied on Petri net transitions, such that the firing of any transition is interpreted as an occurrence of the corresponding activity within the context of all constraints. Discovery of such an intertwined HBPR is tackled in
[
31
]
, while conformance checking is targeted in
[
76
]
. We consider these works to be the closest to our conceptualization of the process frame, however, they place strong emphasis on single business processes in isolation.

There are also some similarities to the discovery of
local processes

[
69
]
, where the goal is to discover common (and possibly concurrent) sub-behaviors within a single process. Conceptually, this could be reformulated such that the sub-behaviors to be discovered are instead interpreted as parallel processes for which the process identifier is missing. Nonetheless,
[
69
]
is not developed with our setting in mind and, as shown in
[
18
]
, typically produces very small models (often containing only 2–3 activities).

In relation to local processes, we highlight region-based discovery techniques, which use region theory to discover characteristic regions that match the behavior observed in the log. These regions, formulated as Petri nets, represent partial views of the behavior captured in the log and are integrated into a complete process model
[
21
,
67
]
. While our approach shares certain conceptual similarities with region-based discovery, it relies on
Declare
discovery and hybrid semantics, offering the flexibility to represent any relation between (or within) processes declaratively, especially when such relations are difficult to encode in Petri nets. There are also conceptual parallels with multi-agent systems (MAS), where larger systems are modeled through decentralized decision units, referred to as agents. Although MAS primarily focuses on the behavioral rules of agents rather than on business processes per se, there is some research exploring the integration of MAS with process discovery techniques, e.g., the work in
[
13
]
.

A relatively recent research area, that entails a somewhat similar concurrent use of multiple models, is object-centric process mining
[
74
]
. It aims to replace the traditional case-centric view of business processes, where activities are executed within the context of process instances, with an object-centric view, where each activity is executed in the context of one or more instances of (possibly different) types of objects. It that way, it becomes natural to represent a business process as a set of interdependent models, each corresponding to a specific type of object. This is especially apparent in object-centric Petri nets
[
71
]
, OC-DCR
[
26
]
, and even the object-centric use of event knowledge graphs
[
38
]
, where activities and control-flow related to different objects can be relatively easily distinguished. However, as stated in
Section

2.4
, our conceptualization is not tied to specific objects, but rather positions each specification as a representation of some logical component of the overall behavioral boundary of the ABPMS. Furthermore, the work presented in this paper is more in line with traditional case-centric view of business processes, in the sense that it assumes only a single overarching case identifier (as opposed to identifiers of multiple different objects) that allows for a meaningful sequential grouping of activity executions.

Finally, we highlight the work in
[
32
]
, which provides a comprehensive lexicon of Petri net constructs that accurately capture the meanings of individual
Declare
constraints. However, most of these Petri net constructs involve the use of weighted, reset, and inhibitor arcs, which are not necessary for the procedural behaviors considered in this paper. Furthermore, this paper does not attempt to transform arbitrary
Declare
constraints, but rather, focuses on identifying a fixed set of common procedural behaviors based on their manifestation within the
Declare
language.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we presented a conceptualization of the ABPMS process frame as a set of semi-concurrently executed procedural and declarative process specifications, where each specification represents some logical component of the overall ABPMS behavioral boundary, and specifications interact through partial activity overlaps. In addition, we provided a corresponding language-agnostic formalization, based on trace projections, and explored the automated discovery of such a process frame. For the latter, we outlined the rationale for using various eventually-follows relations, instead of directly-follows relations, also for discovering the procedural specifications under the open-world assumption. In particular, we focused on 16 common procedural constructs, from sequence flows to inclusive choices, and demonstrated that 14 of these can be identified from automatically discovered
Declare
constraints, even in the presence of repetitions in the overall control flow, which was a significant limitation of our earlier workshop paper
[
3
]
.

In addition, the aim of our earlier workshop paper
[
3
]
was somewhat different than the aim of this paper. More specifically, it focused on first constructing a maximal Petri net, representing all procedural behaviors observed in a given event log. This Petri net would then be decomposed into individual procedural fragments, each representing a distinct underlying process. Meanwhile, the refined notion of a process frame, as given in this paper, allows for numerous different process frames which are behaviorally equivalent. In fact, the decision to replace some (discovered)
Declare
constraints with equivalent Petri nets, as per the mappings described in this paper, will not change the allowed behavior, but is only a representational change, as discussed in
Section

2.2
.

However, replacing some
Declare
constraints with equivalent Petri nets will make procedural behaviors more explicit, while at the same time simplifying the declarative model. To illustrate, a mandatory sequence flow of 4 activities results manifests itself as 6
Alternate Succession
constraints, while mandatory parallelism of two branches (both consisting of two sequential activities) manifests itself as 11
Alternate Succession
constraints. Replacing such constraints with equivalent procedural models (effectively introducing semi-concurrent “pockets of rigidity” into the process frame) allows reducing the size of the declarative model, and, perhaps even more importantly, allows the corresponding behaviors to be expressed in a modeling language that is more suitable for these behaviors.

The work in this paper allows for numerous future research directions. Focus can be placed on lifting some of the limitations of this paper by, for example, considering additional procedural constructs, removing the implicit use of directly-follows relations (in the form of
Not Chain Successions
), incorporating other types of eventually-follows relations, and considering interactions that go beyond the control flow perspective. In addition, this paper involves only Petri nets and
Declare
, while there are numerous other languages that could be explicitly considered, both from the representational and from the automated discovery perspectives.

But probably the most impactful and interesting research direction will be developing a corresponding complete process discovery approach. In particular, process frame, as presented in this paper, allows for multiple behaviorally equivalent representations, where the same behavioral aspects can be expressed not only using different modeling languages, but also using different modeling paradigms. From the process discovery perspective, it means that a single “ground truth” process frame is unlikely to exist for the majority of potential discovery inputs. Instead, factors, such as the ratio of declarative and procedural specifications, the size of individual specifications, the extent of specification overlaps, etc. become relevant, especially in cases where the process frame will also be analyzed (and possibly modified) by human-agents. Consequently, automated discovery task should incorporate an element of optimization with respect to (a combination of) the aforementioned factors, which could tackled, for example, by adopting genetic process discovery algorithms
[
28
]
.
