Integrative chemotaxonomic and micromorphological insights into Peucedanum (Apiaceae)
Authors: Seung Mok Ryu, Kyeonghee Kim, Yujin Jeon, Hyo seon Kim, Young Hye Seo, Sungyu Yang, Goya Choi, Jun Lee, Jun-Ho Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMembers of the genus Peucedanum (Apiaceae) are used as vegetables or medicinal materials but exhibit high morphological and chemical diversity, complicating their taxonomy. Here, we applied ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–quadrupole/time-of-flight mass spectrometry and field emission scanning electron microscopy to 24 Korean Peucedanum accessions from eight species, including five endemics. Multivariate analysis separated the genus into three distinct chemotaxonomic groups, supporting recent reclassification trends. P. japonicum, the source of Peucedani Japonici Radix, contained three unique pyranocoumarin derivatives and dense wax deposits, serving as chemotaxonomic biomarkers and distinguishing Sinopeucedanum from Peucedanum. Sillaphyton podagraria (formerly P. insolens) featured two flavonoid glucoside derivatives and hypostomatic, densely striated-surface leaves. Six species contained coumarin glucoside derivatives, supporting segregation of Peucedanum sensu lato to Kitagawia. These findings refine species boundaries and provide new perspectives for future taxonomic studies, while identifying robust chemical markers applicable to food authentication, quality control, and functional food development involving Peucedani Japonici Radix.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58111-y.Keywords: Metabolomics, Liquid chromatography–quadrupole/time-of-flight mass spectrometry, Chemotaxonomy, Pyranocoumarin, Stomata, Scanning electron microscopySubject terms: Biochemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

The accurate identification and classification of medicinal plants are essential to ensure the safety, efficacy, and quality of herbal medicines1–3. Plant taxonomy is increasingly supported by integrative approaches that combine both morphological and chemical data, which have been widely applied for plant identification4–7. The family Apiaceae Lindl. (Umbelliferae) comprises over 3,820 species in approximately 466 genera, including economically important culinary herbs, vegetables, and medicinal plants8. Peucedanum L., a large genus within the subfamily Apioideae Seem., tribe Selineae Spreng, is characterized by dorsally compressed mericarps with filiform dorsal ribs and narrowly winged marginal ribs9. With over 120 species distributed across Europe, Asia, Africa, and North America, this genus exhibits marked morphological heterogeneity, polymorphism, and polyphyly, prompting researchers to propose its reclassification into smaller, more natural groups10–17. Despite their pharmacological and economic significance, comprehensive genus-level taxonomic studies integrating multiple datasets remain scarce. This taxonomic ambiguity directly affects the authentication and quality control of herbal medicines and functional foods, highlighting the urgent need for integrative approaches relevant to food safety and nutraceutical development.

Chemotaxonomy, which classifies plants based on species-specific secondary metabolite profiles, has proven valuable for resolving taxonomic ambiguities, particularly in morphologically similar or polyphyletic groups18–20. Similarly, leaf micromorphological traits provide reliable diagnostic features for species delimitation and phylogenetic inference within the family Apiaceae21–24. However, in Peucedanum, systematic and chemotaxonomic investigations, particularly those integrating chemical and micromorphological evidence, remain limited.

Among the major Peucedanum species, P. japonicum Thunb. is widely utilized in East Asia as a traditional herbal medicine, vegetable, and functional food25. In particular, its roots, officially recognized in the Korean Herbal Pharmacopoeia (KHP) as “Peucedani Japonici Radix”, have traditionally been prescribed for the treatment of colds, fever, coughs, and inflammatory conditions26. Phytochemical analyses have revealed diverse secondary metabolites, including coumarins, flavonoids, phenolic acids, and chromones, which are linked to antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, and vascular-protective activities27–31. Recently, Iwasaki et al.32 developed an LC-qNMR method for the accurate quantification of khellactone esters in P. japonicum roots, contributing to improved quality control of Peucedanum-derived materials.

Recent studies have highlighted that the aerial parts, particularly the stems and leaves, possess similar pharmacological properties, contain higher concentrations of bioactive compounds, and exhibit stronger antioxidant activity than the roots31,33–36. These findings suggest that the edible aerial parts could serve as alternative or complementary medicinal resources with industrial potential, contributing to sustainable utilization and reducing harvesting pressure on roots.

This study aimed to evaluate the taxonomic relationships among Korean Peucedanum species, including five endemic taxa, using an integrated analytical approach. Eight species (24 accessions) collected from natural populations in South Korea were comprehensively analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS) for chemical profiling, combined with field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) for micromorphological examination.

In particular, this study focused on identifying chemotaxonomic relationships and leaf cuticular micromorphological characteristics in the context of the recently adopted classification of Peucedanum species. Furthermore, this analysis sought to identify potential biomarkers for Peucedani Japonici Radix, derived from P. japonicum, the officially recognized source according to the KHP. Moreover, we discussed the applicability of chemotaxonomy from a systematic perspective and the integration of additional evidence to support the recent classification of Peucedanum. Finally, we highlighted the applicability of identified biomarkers for food authentication, quality control, and the development of health-promoting functional foods, thereby bridging taxonomic findings with industrial relevance.

Preparation of crude extracts

Dried shoots, including stems and leaves (100 g each), of Peucedanum species were ground into powder and subjected to three 1-h microwave-assisted extractions with 1 L of 70% ethanol. Extracts were concentrated in vacuo at 35 °C and subsequently freeze-dried using a lyophilizer. The resulting powders were dissolved in 100% methanol to obtain a final concentration of 1 mg/mL, and the solutions were filtered through a syringe filter (0.2 μm) prior to UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis. All extracts were analyzed under identical extraction and analytical conditions at a standardized concentration (1 mg/mL) to enable comparative metabolite profiling among taxa.

UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis

UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system coupled with an Agilent 6546 Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with a jet-stream electrospray ionization source (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). An Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (i.d., 2.1 mm × 100 mm; particle size, 1.7 μm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was used for chromatographic separation, and 1 µL of each sample was injected into a gradient system at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The starting eluent was 5% B at 0 min, and the proportion of B increased linearly to 100% over 7 min. This 100% concentration was maintained for 1.5 min (until 8.5 min), then returned to the initial concentration of 5% at 8.6 min and held for 1.4 min to re-equilibrate the column. The column and sample managers were maintained at 35 °C and 15 °C, respectively.

The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode under total ion chromatogram conditions. The optimized mass conditions were as follows: drying gas temperature, 250 °C; drying gas flow, 10 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 35 psi; sheath gas temperature, 350 °C; sheath gas flow, 11 L/min; capillary voltage, + 4500 V; nozzle voltage, + 1000 V; fragmentor voltage, 120 V; skimmer voltage, 65 V. The spectra were recorded in the m/z range of 100–1700 at a rate of 10 spectra/s and a time of 100 ms/spectrum (846 transients per spectrum) for MS. The precursor isolation width was 4 m/z, with up to five precursors per cycle fragmented (absolute threshold, 15000 counts; relative threshold, 0.015%), and precursor exclusion after three spectra with a 0.2-min release time. Agilent HP-0921 (m/z = 922.009798) was used as a reference to maintain high mass accuracy. Data were acquired and processed using MassHunter software (version 10.0; Agilent). For MS/MS analysis, selected ion MS/MS mode was applied at collision energies of 20.0, 35.0, and 50.0 eV), using the same parameters as in the MS method.

Quality control analysis

A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots of all sample extracts and was used to evaluate the analytical stability and reproducibility of the UHPLC-Q-TOF MS system. The QC sample was injected repeatedly throughout the analytical sequence, including prior to sample analysis for system conditioning, after every ten sample injections, and at the end of the run. The stability of the analytical platform was assessed based on the clustering pattern of QC samples in the PCA score plots and the coefficient of variation (CV) values of detected features37.

Micromorphological analysis

FAA-fixed leaf samples were immersed in liquid CO2 for critical point drying (SPI-13200JE-AB; SPI Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA) and subsequently mounted on aluminum stubs using a double-sided adhesive conductive carbon disk (05073-BA; SPI Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA). All samples were gold-coated using an ion-sputtering device (208 h; Cressington Scientific Instruments Ltd., Watford, UK). FE-SEM was performed using a Zeiss CrossBeam 550 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany).

Data processing and statistical analysis

The raw data were preprocessed using MassHunter Profinder software, version 10.0.3 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), with the “Batch Molecular Feature Extraction” algorithm. The molecular feature extraction algorithm considered all ions exceeding 10,000 counts with a charge state equal to one. Isotope grouping was based on a common organic molecule model. The resulting feature files for each sample were created as “.cef” files containing a list of molecular features (MFs) with retention time (RT), accurate mass, and abundance, as determined by deconvolution of the total ion current chromatogram. These files were subsequently exported into MassHunter Mass Profiler Professional (MPP) software version 15.1 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and data alignment was performed using an RT window of 0.1% ± 0.05 min and a mass window of 5.0 ppm ± 1 mDa. Stepwise reduction of the number of MFs was performed based on the frequency of occurrence, abundance of respective MFs among classes (variety), and the results of a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). ANOVA was conducted, and significant differences were defined at p < 0.05 using Tukey’s test. Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) with heat maps were performed using MPP software.

Selection and identification of biomarker compounds (1–7) in Peucedanum species

Seven marker peaks (1–7) were selected from the UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS profiles of Peucedanum species based on differential peak patterns among species or chemotaxonomic groups. To identify the compounds corresponding to these marker peaks, MS-based dereplication was performed using the in-house KIOM compound library. Marker compounds 4–7 were identified by comparing their retention times, accurate masses, molecular formulas, isotope patterns, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns with those in the in-house library and previously reported data. In contrast, marker peaks 1–3 could not be assigned by the library-based MS analysis; therefore, they were targeted for isolation from P. japonicum.

Isolation of biomarker compounds (1–3) from P. japonicum

Targeted isolation of marker compounds 1–3 from P. japonicum was performed using MPLC-based chromatographic separation. The 70% EtOH extract (30.0 g) was fractionated by MPLC using Diaion HP20 cartridges (Mitsubishi Chemical, Tokyo, Japan; 400 g, H2O–MeOH, 100:0 to 0:100 at 18 CV, 40 mL/min), producing seven fractions (Frs 1–7). Fraction 7 (0.7 g) was purified by MPLC using Sfär C18 cartridges (120 g, MeOH: distilled water, 20:80–70:30, 20 mL/min), yielding compounds 1 (17.0 mg), 2 (25.5 mg), and 3 (66.7 mg).

Results

Gross morphology of Korean Peucedanum

P. japonicum Thunb. [≡ Sinopeucedanum japonicum (Thunb.) C.K. Liu & X.J. He], the original source species of Peucedani Japonici Radix, shares characteristics such as compound umbels, white petals, and typically a thick taproot with other congeneric species. However, P. japonicum is distinguished from other taxa within the genus by its 1-ternate basal and cauline leaves, which are coriaceous, glaucous on both surfaces, and possess obsolete calyx teeth (Fig. 1). Five Peucedanum species, including Sillaphyton podagraria (H. Boissieu) Pimenov formerly P. podagraria H. Boissieu (= P. insolens Kitag.), are endemic to South Korea. These endemic species occupy diverse habitats and exhibit various morphologies (Fig. 2). They are strictly distributed on the Korean Peninsula; P. chujaense K. Kim, S.-H. Oh, C.-S. Kim & C.-W. Park [≡ Kitagawia chujaensis (K. Kim, S.-H. Oh, C.-S. Kim & C.-W. Park) K. Kim & J.-H. Song] occurs on the southern islands, P. hakuunense Nakai [≡ K. hakuunensis (Nakai) K. Kim & J.-H. Song], P. miroense K. Kim, H.-J. Suh & J.-H. Song [≡ K. miroensis (K. Kim, H.-J. Suh & J.-H. Song) K. Kim & J.-H. Song], and S. podagraria are found in mountainous regions, and P. tongkangense K. Kim, H.-J. Suh & J.-H. Song [≡ K. tongkangensis (K. Kim, H.-J. Suh & J.-H. Song) K. Kim & J.-H. Song] grows on the riverside. P. japonicum, P. hakuunense, and S. podagraria have 1- or 2-ternately compound leaves, whereas the remaining species exhibit 1- to 3-pinnately compound leaves. Additionally, P. hakuunense, P. miroense, and P. tongkangense have linear ultimate leaf segments (Supplementary Information S5).

Quality control-based validation

To evaluate the analytical stability and reproducibility of the UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS system, pooled QC samples were repeatedly analyzed throughout the analytical sequence37. The QC samples showed tight clustering in the PCA score plots, indicating stable analytical performance during the UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis (Supplementary Information S15). In addition, the majority of detected features exhibited acceptable coefficient of variation (CV) values, supporting the reliability and reproducibility of the acquired metabolomics dataset for subsequent multivariate analyses. Specifically, 803 out of 975 detected features (82.36%) showed CV values below 30%, indicating satisfactory analytical stability and reproducibility of the UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS system.

Multivariate analyses of Peucedanum species

Twenty-four extracts of Peucedanum species were analyzed in the positive ionization mode using an optimized and validated UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS method (Supplementary Information S6). For multivariate analysis aimed at discriminating among the eight different Peucedanum species, chromatographic data, including retention time (tR), mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity values, were processed into a data matrix using MassHunter software.

The positive ionization mode dataset comprised 861 MFs, which were subsequently subjected to PCA and HCA. As shown in Fig. 3a, the two-dimensional PCA results clearly revealed separation among the eight groups, with score plots representing PC1 (47.1%) and PC2 (12.1%). Similarly, the three-dimensional PCA results demonstrated distinct group separation (Fig. 3b). In addition, the HCA dendrogram based on the selected metabolites indicated clear discrimination among the eight groups (Fig. 4).

Additionally, Sillaphyton podagraria (≡ P. podagraria = P. insolens) (H), which was reclassified based on recent taxonomic studies38, showed clear chemical differences from the other groups, further supporting its current taxonomic placement.

Compounds 1, 2, and 3 were identified as the pyranocoumarin derivatives (3′S,4′S)-3′-O-acetyl-4′-O-senecioylkhellactone (1), (3′S,4′S)-3′-O-acetyl-4′-O-(2-methylbutyroyl)khellactone (2), and (3′S,4′S)-disenecioyloxy-3′,4′-dihydroseselin (3) through comparison of their spectroscopic data with literature values (Fig. 5)39,40. This result confirmed that the three pyranocoumarin derivatives (compounds 1–3) were unique metabolites qualitatively present only in P. japonicum but absent in its closely related species (Table 2; Fig. 5).

Group C, comprising S. podagraria (syn. P. podagraria), is characterized by two flavonoid glucosides: rutin (6) and 6′′-O-malonylisoquercitrin (7) (Table 2; Fig. 5). These flavonoids clearly differentiate group C from the coumarin-rich groups A and B.

Collectively, the occurrence of coumarin glucoside derivatives in group A (six species), the presence of pyranocoumarin derivatives in group B (P. japonicum), and the identification of flavonoid glucoside derivatives in group C (S. podagraria) reveal the chemotaxonomic value of these biomarkers in distinguishing closely related species, and their potential use as reliable markers for authentication and quality control of medicinal and edible Peucedanum resources (see Supplementary Information Figure S14 for the chromatographic positions of the annotated marker compounds).

Leaf shapes, epidermal cells, and stomata micromorphology in combination with an HCA chemotaxonomical dendrogram

The first clade of the chemotaxonomical dendrogram consisted of four endemic species that shared similar leaf microstructures, including amphistomatic, anisocytic, and/or anomocytic stomata, as well as glabrous surfaces (Fig. 6A–H). Moreover, all three endemic species except P. chujaense had linear ultimate segments. Two varieties of P. terebinthaceum in the second clade shared similar leaf microstructures with species in the first clade, including amphistomatic, glabrous leaves and the absence of epicuticular wax. However, these taxa were distinguished from the first clade species by their staurocytic stomata on the abaxial surface (Fig. 6I–L). The second clade also comprised P. japonicum and Sillaphyton podagraria, which shared nonlinear ultimate segments with 1- or 2-ternately compound cauline leaves but had different leaf microstructures from the other species. P. japonicum shared amphistomatic features with other Peucedanum species, but it was the only species with epicuticular wax with dense scales and platelets (Fig. 6M–N). Moreover, S. podagraria was distinguished from all studied taxa by its hypostomatic leaves with sinuous anticlinal cell walls and densely striated fine relief on both surfaces (Fig. 6O–P).

Discussion

In this study, an integrative analysis of plant aerial parts, combining gross morphology, leaf micromorphology (FE-SEM), and phytochemical profiling (UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS), was conducted for eight Peucedanum taxa (including four Korean endemics: P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, P. miroense, and P. tongkangense) and Sillaphyton podagraria (≡ P. podagraria = P. insolens), which was recently transferred to another genus. These metabolite profiles, together with leaf cuticular characteristics, helped clarify species relationships within Korean Peucedanum.

Chemotaxonomy classifies medicinal plants by their chemical characteristics, providing molecular insights into species diversity and evolutionary relationships to complement traditional taxonomy6,41,42. Chemical characteristics have been used as chemotaxonomic markers to delimit plants at various taxonomic levels: species, genus, and family43–46. Peucedanum sensu lato exhibits considerable morphological and chemical diversity47,48. A recent trend in classification has led to the genus Peucedanum being redefined from a broad concept to a narrower one, based on morphological and molecular characteristics15–17,49–52. The current findings not only align with this reclassification trend but also provide new chemical and micromorphological evidence to refine species boundaries within Korean Peucedanum.

Two distinct clades were identified based on the HCA chemotaxonomic dendrogram. The first clade included all endemic Korean species with linear ultimate leaflet segments, except P. chujaense. However, P. terebinthaceum vars. terebinthaceum and deltoideum, belonging to the second clade, shared various chemical compounds, leaf micromorphological characteristics, and shapes with those of the first clade species. Lei et al.53 suggested that subclade I should be recognized as the genus Kitagawia Pimenov [K. baicalensis (Redowsky ex Willd.) Pimenov, P. baicalense (Redowsky ex Willd.) W.D.J. Koch, K. komarovii Pimenov, K. stepposa (Y.H. Huang) Pimenov, P. stepposum Y.H. Huang, and K. terebinthacea (Fisch. ex Trevir.) Pimenov]. Moreover, P. hakuunense and P. chujaense, which clustered together, likely belong to Kitagawia. Our mericarp and plastome analyses indicate that all Korean endemic Peucedanum species, except for P. japonicum, have been nomenclaturally recombined into the genus Kitagawia54. Thus, coumarin glucoside derivatives may serve as important supporting data and potential biomarkers for recognizing the genus Kitagawia, providing strong evidence of synapomorphic characteristics delimiting the genus. All Korean Kitagawia species were confirmed to contain coumarin glucoside derivatives; thus, further research is needed to determine whether other Kitagawia species contain coumarin glucosides such as apiosylskimmin (4) and apterin (5).

One of the species within the second clade, P. japonicum, was distinct from other Peucedanum species due to wax deposition on leaf surfaces and the presence of biomarkers such as (3′S,4′S)-3′-O-acetyl-4′-O-senecioylkhellactone (1), (3′S,4′S)-3′-O-acetyl-4′-O-(2-methylbutyroyl)khellactone (2), and (3′S,4′S)-disenecioyloxy-3′,4′-dihydroseselin (3). A recent classification of P. japonicum placed it in the newly established genus Sinopeucedanum based on morphology and plastid-based phylogenies55. Further research is required to determine whether these chemical compounds are consistently present and whether these biomarkers represent synapomorphy in Sinopeucedanum. The distinct metabolite profile of P. japonicum may partially reflect ecological adaptation to its coastal habitat, including exposure to salt stress and maritime environmental conditions. Likewise, habitat-specific environmental conditions may contribute to the characteristic clustering patterns observed among related taxa. Moreover, according to their classification, P. praeruptorum Dunn, the original species of Peucedani Radix, was reclassified as Sinopeucedanum praeruptorum (Dunn) C.K. Liu & X.J. He55. Thus, a comparative study including P. praeruptorum should be conducted to determine whether these three are newly identified markers distinguishing P. japonicum or the genus Sinopeucedanum.

This study demonstrates that the three pyranocoumarin derivatives (1–3) uniquely detected in P. japonicum serve as robust qualitative biomarkers for Peucedani Japonici Radix. Coumarins are a class of benzopyrone derivatives characterized by a fused benzene and α-pyrone ring system (1,2-benzopyrone). Pyranocoumarins are a subclass of coumarins in which a pyran ring is fused to the coumarin nucleus, typically at the C-6 and C-7 (linear) or C-7 and C-8 (angular) positions56. Naturally occurring pyranocoumarins are abundant in the families Apiaceae and Rutaceae57. Pyranocoumarins have been reported to exhibit a wide range of bioactivities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, antitumor, and enzyme inhibitory effects58. However, biological studies on the pyranocoumarin derivatives (1–3) identified in this study remain limited39,40, and further investigations are required to clarify their potential pharmacological relevance to the medicinal use of P. japonicum. Their structural diversity and taxonomic specificity make them valuable chemotaxonomic markers for differentiating closely related taxa, particularly within morphologically complex genera. Furthermore, these chemotaxonomic relationships may facilitate the preliminary screening of novel medicinal or edible plant resources by predicting characteristic metabolite patterns among related taxa. Further studies are warranted to elucidate their pharmacological potential and practical roles in food authentication, herbal medicine quality assurance, and functional food development.

Sillaphyton podagraria, formerly P. podagraria (homotypic, nomenclatural synonym) and P. insolens (heterotypic, taxonomic synonym), is now recognized as a monotypic, Korean endemic genus38,59,60. In addition to morphological and carpological traits, its nrDNA ITS sequences have been reported to differ significantly, placing S. podagraria in the Arcuatopeterus clade rather than in the tribe Selineae38. Our leaf cuticular data further support this classification. In addition, metabolite analysis revealed flavonoid glucosides, including rutin (6) and 6′′-O-malonylisoquercitrin (7), which were not detected in other groups and were specifically identified in S. podagraria. These compounds are expected to serve as distinctive biomarkers for S. podagraria; however, further studies are required to determine whether they are characteristic of the Arcuatopeterus clade.

The natural classification of medicinal plants, based on taxonomy and systematics, has proven to be a useful predictive tool for identifying plants with potential value using a targeted approach61. Species belonging to the genus Kitagawia, including P. terebinthaceum, have been suggested to possess potential medicinal value based on the present and previous studies62. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate species with high potential economic value, including endemic species, as candidates for new drugs using a targeted approach and more detailed research.

Conclusion

Overall, this study provides integrative chemical and micromorphological evidence that refines species boundaries within Korean Peucedanum, supports recent taxonomic revisions, and identifies pyranocoumarin biomarkers in P. japonicum for potential pharmacological and functional food applications. In addition, FE-SEM combined with UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS, as applied here for taxonomic purposes, proved advantageous for plant identification as well as for medicinal and food material authentication. These findings highlight the dual significance of taxonomy and chemotaxonomy: resolving systematic ambiguities and ensuring the safety, efficacy, and industrial utilization of Peucedanum resources.
