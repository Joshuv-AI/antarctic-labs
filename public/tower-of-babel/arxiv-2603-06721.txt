Preserver problems on Toeplitz matrices
Rayhan Ahmed, Vladimir Bolotnikov, William Hoyle, Chi-Kwong Li

Abstract
We study linear preserver problems on the linear space of n × n n\times n Toeplitz matrices over the real field or the complex field. In particular, characterizations are given for linear preservers of rank one matrices and linear preservers of the determinant. We also present related results and questions on other structured matrices.



In memory of Leiba Rodman.

AMS Classification. 15A86, 15B05.

Keywords. Linear preserver, Toeplitz matrix, rank one matrix, confluent Vandermonde matrix.

1
Introduction

Linear preserver problems form a classical and active area of matrix theory and
operator algebras. They concern the characterization of linear maps on spaces of
matrices (or operators) that leave invariant certain properties, functions, sets
or relations
[
8
,
9
]
.
The subject originated with Frobenius
[
3
]
who determined
the precise form of linear preservers of the determinant on
M_{n}
, the set of
n\times n
complex matrices.
It was shown that
such a map has the form
A\mapsto MAN
or
A\mapsto MA^{\top}N
with

\det(MN)=1
.
The result was extended to matrices of arbitrary fields by
Dieudonné in
[
2
]
.
Marcus and Moyls in
[
10
,
11
]
determined the structure
of linear preservers of rank and the set of rank one matrices. The preservers also have
the form
A\mapsto MAN
or
A\mapsto MA^{\top}N
for some nonsingular matrices
M
and
N
.
It turns out that many preservers can be shown to be rank one preservers so that
the result of Marcus and Moyls applies. The preserving properties can then be used
to deduce additional information on the matrices
M
and
N
.

In this paper, we study linear preserver problems in the linear space of
n\times n

Toeplitz matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}
, where
\mathbb{F}
is the complex field
\mathbb{C}
or
the real field
\mathbb{R}
. Recall that
a
Toeplitz matrix
(or diagonal-constant matrix) is a matrix in which each descending
diagonal from left to right is constant. Formally,
a matrix
A\in M_{n}
is Toeplitz if its

(i,j)
entry

A_{i,j}
depends only on the index difference, i.e.,
A_{i,j}=a_{i-j}
for
a_{1-n},\dots,a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}
.
Hence,

A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\dots&a_{-(n-1)}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{-1}&\dots&a_{-(n-2)}\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}&\dots&a_{-(n-3)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n-1}&a_{n-2}&a_{n-3}&\dots&a_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Toeplitz matrices are useful in many topics including signal processing, numerical
computation and operator theory; for example, see
[
4
,
13
,
12
]
and their references.
The study of preservers on Toeplitz matrices reveals new rigidity phenomena arising from the interplay between
linearity, structure, and the preserved quantity.

In this paper, we characterize
linear maps on Toeplitz matrices preserving the determinant function
and the rank function; see Section 4.
These are established after we obtain the basic
result on linear preservers of rank one (Toeplitz) matrices in Section 3.
The proof depends on some basic properties of Toeplitz matrices and rank one
Toeplitz matrices established in Section 2. In Section 4, we present additional
preserver results for Toeplitz matrices.
We also discuss some related results, and further questions.

2
Preliminary results

Let
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
be the linear space of
n\times n
Toeplitz matrices on
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
{\bf e}_{k}
denote the
k
-th column of the identity matrix

I_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf e}_{1}&\ldots&{\bf e}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
and
let
Z_{n}
be the lower shift matrix defined as

Z_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf e}_{2}&\ldots&{\bf e}_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&&\cr 1&0&&\cr&\ddots&\ddots&\cr&&1&0\cr\end{bmatrix}.

It is clear that
\dim{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}=2n-1
and

{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{Z_{n}^{n-1},\;Z_{n}^{n-2},\;\ldots,\;Z_{n},\;I_{n},\;Z_{n}^{\top},\,\ldots,\,(Z^{\top}_{n})^{n-2},\,(Z^{\top}_{n})^{n-1}\right\}

(2.1)

is a basis for
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
. We have the following characterization of rank-one Toeplitz matrices.

Proposition 2.1
.

A matrix
A\in{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
has rank one if and only if
there are
\mu,\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}
with
\mu\neq 0
such that

A
equals

A_{1}=\mu{\bf e}_{1}{\bf e}_{n}^{\top}\quad\mbox{or}\quad A_{2}=\mu\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\xi^{n-1}\vskip-2.84526pt\\
\vdots\\
\xi\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&\xi&\ldots&\xi^{n-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right].

(2.2)

Proof.

The “if” part is obvious. For the “only if” part, take a Toeplitz rank-one matrix in the form

A={\bf b}^{\top}{\bf c},\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;{\bf b}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}&\ldots&b_{n}\end{bmatrix}\neq 0,\quad{\bf c}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&\ldots&c_{n}\end{bmatrix}\neq 0.

(2.3)

If
c_{1}=0
, let
c_{k}
be the leftmost nonzero entry in
{\bf c}
. Then the
k-1
leftmost columns in
A
are zeros. Then the
Toeplitz structure of
A
guarantees that
b_{i}c_{k}=0
for
i=2,\ldots,n
,
and hence,
b_{i}=0
for
i\geq 2
. Thus,
{\bf b}=b_{1}{\bf e}_{1}^{\top}
.
Subsequently, only the top row in
A
is non-zero, which is possible (again by the Toeplitz structure of
A
) only if
k=n
, i.e., if

c_{n}
is the only non-zero entry in
{\bf c}
. Thus,
{\bf c}=c_{n}{\bf e}_{n}^{\top}
which together with
(
2.3
), leads us to
A=A_{1}
with
\mu=b_{1}c_{n}
.

If
c_{1}\neq 0
, we let
\xi:=\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}
.
Since
A
is a Toeplitz matrix, we conclude from equalities
a_{j,1}=a_{j+1,2}

that
b_{j}=b_{j+1}\xi
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. Hence,

{\bf b}=b_{n}\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{n-1}&\xi^{n-2}&\ldots\xi&1\end{bmatrix}.

Since
{\bf b}\neq 0
, we have
b_{n}\neq 0
. Since the diagonal entries in
A

are equal, we have

\xi^{n-1}c_{1}=\xi^{n-2}c_{2}=\ldots=\xi c_{n-1}=c_{n},

which implies
{\bf c}=c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1&\xi&\ldots\xi^{n-2}&\xi^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}
. Substituting
{\bf b}
and

{\bf c}
as above into (
2.3
) we conclude that
A=A_{2}
with
\mu=b_{n}c_{1}
.
∎

It is easy to see that with respect to the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
defined in
(
2.1
), the coordinate vectors of rank one Toeplitz matrices in
{\mathbb{F}}^{2n-1}
has the form

\mu[0,\dots,0,1]^{\top}\quad\hbox{ or }\quad\mu[1,\xi,\dots,\dots,\xi^{2n-2}]^{\top}

(2.4)

for some
\mu,\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}
with
\mu\neq 0
. Note also that

[0,\dots,0,1]=\lim_{\xi\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\xi^{2n-2}}[1,\dots,\xi^{2n-2}].

So, the first vector in (
2.4
) can be viewed as the limit of a vector of the second type.
In the following discussion, we use the notation

h(\infty)=[0,\dots,0,1]^{\top}\quad\hbox{ and}\quad h(\xi)=[1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{2n-1}]^{\top}\ \hbox{ with }\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}.

(2.5)

Furthermore, we let
\mathcal{R}
to denote the set of all non-zero multiples of vectors (
2.5
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mu h(\xi):\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\},\ \xi\in{\mathbb{F}}\cup\{\infty\}\}

(2.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{[0,\dots,0,\mu]^{\top}:\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\}\}\cup\{\mu[1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{n-1}]^{\top}:\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\},\ \xi\in{\mathbb{F}}\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{R}
can be equivalently characterized as

\mathcal{R}=\left\{h=\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}&\ldots&h_{2n-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\neq 0:\;h_{j}^{2}=h_{j-1}h_{j+1}\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;j=2,\ldots,2n-2\right\}.

(2.7)

We have the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}=\{{\mathbf{h}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{h}}_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.
Then
{\mathcal{S}}
is linearly dependent if and only if two of the vectors
{\mathcal{S}}
are multiples
of each other. Consequently,
{\mathcal{S}}
is a basis for
{\mathbb{F}}^{2n-1}
if and only if

k=2n-1
and no two vectors in
{\mathcal{S}}
are multiples of each other.

Proof.

For the first assertion, the sufficiency is clear. For the necessity, we
prove the contrapositive and assume that no two vectors in
{\mathcal{S}}
are multiples of each other.
By normalization, we may assume that the
k
vectors are of the form
h(\xi)
.
Let

R=\begin{bmatrix}h(\xi_{1})&\ldots&h(\xi_{k})\end{bmatrix}

so that no two columns are identical.

If
h(\infty)\not\in{\mathcal{S}}
,
then
{\mathcal{S}}
consists of the columns

h(\xi_{j})
(
j=1,\ldots,k)
.
The matrix
R_{1}
obtained by removing the last
n-k
rows from
R

is a square Vandermonde matrix with determinant
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})\neq 0
.
Hence,
R_{1}
has rank
k
, and so does

R
. Thus,
{\mathcal{S}}
is linearly independent.

Suppose
h(\infty)
appears in
{\mathcal{S}}
, we may assume that it is
{\mathbf{h}}_{k}
.
Let
R_{1}
be obtained by removing the rows with indices
k,\dots,n-1
.
Then the last column of
R_{1}

equals
[0,\dots,0,1]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{k}
and the leading
(k-1)\times(k-1)
submatrix
R_{2}

is again a Vandermonde matrix with determinant
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k-1}(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})\neq 0
.
Since the last column of
R_{1}
is
[0,\dots,0,1]^{\top}
, we see that
\det(R_{1})=\det(R_{2})
.
Hence,
R_{1}
has rank
k
, and so does

R
. Thus,
{\mathcal{S}}
is linearly independent.
The last assertion is clear.
∎

3
Rank one preservers

In this section, we determine the structure of linear maps
T:{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\to{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
sending rank one
matrices to rank one matrices. Recall that

{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{Z_{n}^{n-1},\;Z_{n}^{n-2},\;\ldots,\;Z_{n},\;I_{n},\;Z_{n}^{\top},\,\ldots,\,(Z^{\top}_{n})^{n-2},\,(Z^{\top}_{n})^{n-1}\right\}

and

{\mathcal{R}}=\{[0,\dots,0,\mu]^{\top}:\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\}\}\cup\{\mu[1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{n-1}]^{\top}:\mu\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\},\ \xi\in{\mathbb{F}}\}.

Let
L=\left[T\right]_{{\mathcal{B}}}\in M_{2n-1}
be the matrix of a linear map
T:{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\to{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
with respect to the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
.
By Proposition
2.2
,
T
preserves rank one matrices if and only if
Lh\in\mathcal{R}
for all
h\in\mathcal{R}
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we will write
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
to denote the latter invariance property. Also, if
h\in{\mathbb{F}}^{2n-1}
, we will not distinguish
L(h)
and
Lh
.
Furthermore,
we will use notation
F_{n}
and
D_{n}(r)
for the anti-diagonal permutation matrix and the diagonal matrix given by

F_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\ldots&0&1\\
\vdots&\mathinner{\mkern 1.0mu\raise 1.0pt\vbox{\kern 7.0pt\hbox{.}}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 4.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 7.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 1.0mu}&\mathinner{\mkern 1.0mu\raise 1.0pt\vbox{\kern 7.0pt\hbox{.}}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 4.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 7.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 1.0mu}&0\\
0&1&\mathinner{\mkern 1.0mu\raise 1.0pt\vbox{\kern 7.0pt\hbox{.}}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 4.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 7.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 1.0mu}&\vdots\\
1&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix},\quad D_{n}(r)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&r&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\ldots&0&r^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.1)

Lemma 3.1
.

The set of
L\in M_{2n-1}

that satisfy
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}

is closed under multiplication and contains
F_{2n-1}
,
rI_{2n-1}

and
D_{2n-1}(r)
for any nonzero
r\in{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proof.

The first statement is clear.

If
L=F_{2n-1}
, then for any nonzero
\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}
,

L(h(\xi))=\mu h(y)
with
\mu=\xi^{2n-1}
and
y=1/\xi
.
Moreover,
L(h(\infty))=h(0)
and
L(h(0))=h(\infty)
.

Suppose
r\in{\mathbb{F}}
is nonzero. If
L=rI_{2n-1}
, then
L({\mathcal{R}})={\mathcal{R}}
.
If
L=D_{2n-1}(r)
,
then for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}

L(h(\xi))=h(y)
with
y=r\xi
.
In addition,
L(h(\infty))=r^{2n-1}h(\infty)
.
In all cases, we see that
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.
∎

Note that it is essential to assume that
r\in{\mathbb{F}}

is nonzero in the above lemma.
If
r=0
, then
L(h(\infty))=0\notin{\mathcal{R}}
for
L=rI_{2n-1}
or
L=D_{2n-1}(r)
.

We will identify more matrices
L\in M_{2n-1}
satisfying
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.
For
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}
, let

V_{n}(\alpha):=\begin{bmatrix}1&\alpha&\alpha^{2}&\ldots&\alpha^{n-1}\\
0&1&2\alpha&\ldots&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n-1\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\alpha^{n-2}\\
0&0&1&\ldots&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n-1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\alpha^{n-3}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0&\ldots&1\end{bmatrix},

(3.2)

which is known as the confluent Vandermonde matrix associated
with
\alpha
. The study of confluent Vandermonde matrices is
connected to many different areas;
for example, see
[
1
,
7
]
and its references.

Denote by

\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}=\alpha I_{n}+Z_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&&&\cr 1&\alpha&&\cr&\ddots&\ddots&\cr&&1&\alpha\cr\end{bmatrix}

(3.3)

the lower triangular Jordan block
with
\alpha
on the main diagonal. Then we can verify that the

j
-th column of
V_{n}(\alpha)
is equal to
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j-1}{\bf e}_{1}
, i.e.,

V_{n}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf e}_{1}&\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}{\bf e}_{1}&\ldots&\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{n-1}{\bf e}_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.4)

The next statement presents a characteristic property of
V_{n}(\alpha)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}
. The matrix (
3.2
) is the unique matrix that satisfies the identity

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}V_{n}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}1&x+\alpha&\ldots&(x+\alpha)^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;x.

(3.5)

In particular, the matrix

L=V_{2n-1}(\alpha)^{\top}\in M_{2n-1}
satisfies
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.

Proof.

By (
3.4
), the equality (
3.5
) is equivalent to equalities

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j}{\bf e}_{1}=(x+\alpha)^{j}\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=0,\ldots,n-1,

(3.6)

which are immediate by (
3.2
) and the Binomial Theorem. On the other hand, equalities (
3.6
) uniquely determine
the entries in
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j}{\bf e}_{1}
as the coefficients of
x^{k}
in the expansion of
(x+\alpha)^{j}
.

Clearly, if
L:=V_{2n-1}(\alpha)^{\top}
, then

L(h(\infty))=h(\infty)
, and for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}
,

L[1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{2n-2}]^{\top}
has the form

[1,y,\dots,y^{2n-2}]^{\top}\in{\mathcal{R}}
with
y=\xi+\alpha
.
So,
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.
∎

Next, we introduce another type of matrices in
M_{2n-1}
which preserve

{\mathcal{R}}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}=\alpha I_{n}+Z_{n}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\beta}=\beta I_{n}+Z_{n}
with
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}
.
Then

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\ell}{\bf e}_{1}=(x+\beta)^{k}(x+\alpha)^{\ell},\quad\mbox{whenever}\;\;k+\ell<n.

(3.7)

Let

W_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-1}{\bf e}_{1}&\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-2}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}{\bf e}_{1}&\ldots&\mathcal{J}_{\beta}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{n-2}{\bf e}_{1}&\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{n-1}{\bf e}_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.8)

Then
W_{n}(\alpha,\beta)

is the unique matrix satisfying the identity

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}W(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}(x+\beta)^{n-1}&(x+\beta)^{n-2}(x+\alpha)&\ldots&(x+\alpha)^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}

(3.9)

for all
x
. We have
\det W(\alpha,\beta)=(\beta-\alpha)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}
.
Furthermore, if
\alpha\neq\beta
, then
L=W_{2n-1}(\alpha,\beta)^{\top}
is invertible
and satisfies
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.

Proof.

We derive (
3.7
) from (
3.6
).
Note that
\mathcal{J}_{\beta}=(\beta-\alpha)I_{n}+\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}
and hence,

\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}k\\
j\end{smallmatrix}\right)(\beta-\alpha)^{k-j}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j}.

Making use of the latter relation and (
3.6
), we get

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\ell}{\bf e}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}k\\
j\end{smallmatrix}\right)(\beta-\alpha)^{k-j}\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j+\ell}{\bf e}_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}k\\
j\end{smallmatrix}\right)(\beta-\alpha)^{k-j}(x+\alpha)^{j+\ell}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x+\alpha)^{\ell}\left(x+\alpha+\beta-\alpha\right)^{k}=(x+\beta)^{k}(x+\alpha)^{\ell}.

Thus, (
3.7
) holds.

The equality (
3.9
) is equivalent to equalities

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-j}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j-1}{\bf e}_{1}=(x+\beta)^{n-j}(x+\alpha)^{j-1}\quad\mbox{for}\;\;j=1,\ldots,n,

(3.10)

which hold true by (
3.7
) and uniquely determine
the entries of the columns
\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-j}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{j-1}{\bf e}_{1}
.

It is clear from (
3.2
) that
\det V_{n}(\alpha)=1
.
To evaluate the determinant of
W(\alpha,\beta)
, we first observe from (
3.8
) that

W(0,\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-1}{\bf e}_{1}&Z_{n}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-2}{\bf e}_{1}&\ldots&Z_{n}^{n-2}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}{\bf e}_{1}&Z_{n}^{n-1}{\bf e}_{1}\end{bmatrix}

is a lower triangular matrix with diagonal entries

\left[W(0,\beta)\right]_{jj}={\bf e}_{j}^{\top}Z^{j-1}_{n}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-j}{\bf e}_{1}={\bf e}_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}^{n-j}{\bf e}_{1}=\beta^{n-j},\quad j=1,\ldots n,

and therefore,
\det W(0,\beta)=\beta^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}
. By (
3.9
), we have

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}W(0,\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}(x+\beta)^{n-1}&(x+\beta)^{n-2}x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Replacing
x
and
\beta
in the latter identity by respectively,
x+\alpha
and
\beta-\alpha
, gives

\begin{bmatrix}1&x+\alpha&\ldots&(x+\alpha)^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}W(0,\beta-\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}(x+\beta)^{n-1}&(x+\beta)^{n-2}(x+\alpha)&\ldots&(x+\alpha)^{n-1}\end{bmatrix},

which being combined with (
3.5
) and (
3.9
)leads us to

\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}V_{n}(\alpha)W(0,\beta-\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}1&x&\ldots&x^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}W(\alpha,\beta).

By the uniqueness property in (a),

V_{n}(\alpha)W(0,\beta-\alpha)=W(\alpha,\beta)
and hence,

\det W(\alpha,\beta)=\det V_{n}(\alpha)\cdot\det W(0,\beta-\alpha)=\det W(0,\beta-\alpha)=(\beta-\alpha)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}},

which completes the proof.

Finally, let
L=W_{2n-1}(\alpha,\beta)^{\top}
.
If
\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{-\beta\}
,
then
L(h(\xi))=\mu h(y)
with
\mu=(x+\beta)^{2n-1}
and
y=(x+\alpha)/(x+\beta))
.
Moreover,
L(h(-\beta))=(x+\alpha)^{n+1}h(\infty)

and
L(h(\infty))=[1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{2n-1}]=h(\alpha)
.
Thus,
L=W_{2n-1}(\alpha,\beta)^{\top}
preserves
{\mathcal{R}}
.
∎

The next lemma shows that
L\in M_{2n-1}
satisfying
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
is either invertible
or a rank one matrix in the real case. Once it is proved, we can present the main theorem
giving the complete description of matrices in
M_{2n-1}
preserving
{\mathcal{R}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
L\in M_{2n-1}
satisfy
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.
Then either

(a)

L
is a invertible, or

(b)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
and
L=h[c_{1},\dots,c_{2n-1}]

for some
h\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{2n-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

c_{1}+c_{2}x+\dots+c_{2n-1}x^{2n-2}\neq 0\hbox{ for any }x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

If
L
is invertible, then (a) holds. Assume that
L
is singular.
Let us choose distinct
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}
for
k=(2n-2)^{2}+1
, introduce the vectors

y_{j}=Lh(\xi_{j})\in\mathcal{R}\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

(3.11)

and define an equivalence relation on the set (
3.11
):
y_{i}\sim y_{j}
if and only if
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
are
multiples of each other
. Since
L
is singular, any collection of
2n-1
vectors from (
3.11
) is linearly dependent.
By Lemma
2.2
, any collection of
2n-1
vectors from (
3.11
) contains two equivalent
vectors, i.e., the two vectors that are multiples of each other. Therefore, there are at most
2n-2
equivalence classes, and one of them must contain at least
2n-1
elements,
say,
y_{1},\dots,y_{2n-1}
. Then the matrix
Y=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}&\ldots&y_{2n-1}\end{bmatrix}
has rank one. Since

Y=\begin{bmatrix}Lh(\xi_{1})&\ldots&Lh(\xi_{2n-1})\end{bmatrix}=L\begin{bmatrix}h(\xi_{1})&\ldots&h(\xi_{2n-1})\end{bmatrix}

where
\begin{bmatrix}h(\xi_{1})&\ldots&h(\xi_{2n-1})\end{bmatrix}
is an invertible Vandermonde matrix, we conclude
that
{\rm rank}\,L=1
. It remains to show that the latter cannot happen if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
and may occur if

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. To this end, we write
L
of rank one in the form

L={\bf b}{\bf p},\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;{\bf b}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}b_{1}\vskip-2.84526pt\\
\vdots\\
b_{2n-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\neq 0,\quad{\bf p}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}&\ldots&p_{2n-2}\end{bmatrix}\neq 0.

(3.12)

Since the vectors
Lh(0)={\bf b}p_{0}
and
Lh(\infty)={\bf b}p_{2n-2}
belong to
\mathcal{R}
and in particular are nonzero,
we have
p_{0}\neq 0
,
p_{2n-2}\neq 0
, and
{\bf b}\in\mathcal{R}
. Upon rescaling
{\bf p}
by a nonzero
\mu
(if necessary)
we conclude that either

L=h(\infty){\bf p}\quad\mbox{or}\quad L=h(\zeta){\bf p}\quad\mbox{for some}\;\;\zeta\in\mathbb{F}.

(3.13)

Besides,
Lh(\xi)\in\mathcal{R}
and hence
Lh(\xi)\neq 0
for each
\xi\in\mathbb{F}
, so that

{\bf p}h(\xi)=p_{0}+p_{1}\xi+\ldots+p_{2n-2}\xi^{2n-2}:=p(\xi)\neq 0\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;\xi\in\mathbb{F},

(3.14)

where
p(x)
is a polynomial of degree
\deg p=2n-2
. If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, we get a contradiction, since
any complex polynomial of positive degree has a root in
\mathbb{C}
(we exclude the trivial case
n=1
).
If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, we do have rank-one matrices
L
of the form (
3.13
) with
{\bf p}
subject to condition (
3.14
).
∎

We are now ready to present the characterization of

L\in M_{2n-1}
satisfying
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is defined as in
(
2.6
). We will use the notation

D_{2n-1}(r),V_{2n-1}(\alpha),W_{2n-1}(\alpha,\beta)
defined in (

),
(

) and (

).

Theorem 3.5
.

A matrix
L\in M_{2n-1}
satisfies
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}

if and only if there are
\gamma,r,\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}

with
\gamma r\neq 0
and
\alpha\neq\beta
such that one of the following holds.

(a)

L^{\top}
has the form:

(i)

\gamma V_{2n-1}(\alpha)D_{2n-1}(r)
,
(ii)

\gamma V_{2n-1}(\alpha)F_{2n-1}D_{2n-1}(r)
,
or

(iii)

\gamma W_{2n-1}(\alpha,\beta)D_{2n-1}(r)
.

(b)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
and
L=h[c_{1},\dots,c_{2n-2}]

for an
h\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{2n-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

c_{1}+c_{2}x+\dots+c_{2n-1}x^{2n-2}\neq 0\hbox{ for any }x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

If (a) holds, then
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
by the previous lemma.
If (b) holds, then clearly
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.

We focus on the necessity. By Lemma
3.4
, if
L
is singular, then (b) holds.
So, we focus on the case when
L
is invertible.
With
L=\left[p_{ij}\right]_{i=1,\ldots,2n-1}^{j=0,\ldots,2n-2}
we associate the polynomials

P_{j}(x)=p_{j0}+p_{j1}x+\cdots+p_{j,2n-2}x^{2n-2}\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=1,\dots,2n-1.

(3.15)

Then

Lh(x)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}P_{1}(x)\\
P_{2}(x)\vskip-2.84526pt\\
\vdots\\
P_{2n-1}(x)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;x\in\mathbb{F}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;h(x)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
x\vskip-2.84526pt\\
\vdots\\
x^{2n-2}\end{smallmatrix}\right].

(3.16)

It is clear that
P_{j}(x)
completely determines the
j
-th row of
L
.
We will show that one of the following three cases holds.

\displaystyle{\rm(a)}\;P_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma r^{j-1}(x+\alpha)^{j-1},

\displaystyle{\rm(b)}\;P_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma r^{j-1}(x+\alpha)^{2n-j-1},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(j=1,\ldots,2n-1)

(3.17)

\displaystyle{\rm(c)}\;P_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma r^{j-1}(x+\alpha)^{j-1}(x+\beta)^{2n-j-1},

for some
r,\gamma,\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}
such that
r,\gamma\neq 0
and
\alpha\neq\beta
.
Then
L
will be in the form in (a).

Suppose
L\in M_{2n-1}
is invertible and
L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
.
Since a nonzero vector
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{2n-1})^{\top}
belongs to
{\mathcal{R}}
if and only if

h_{j-1}h_{j+1}=h_{j}^{2}
for
j=2,\dots,2n-2
(see (
2.7
)) we have

P_{j-1}(x)P_{j+1}(x)=P_{j}(x)^{2}\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;j=2,\dots,2n-2.

(3.18)

Since
L
is invertible,
P_{j}\not\equiv 0
and we let
\gamma_{j}\neq 0
to denote its leading coefficient.
Then
\widetilde{P}_{j}(x)=P_{j}(x)/\gamma_{j}
is a monic polynomial, and we have by (
3.18
),

\gamma_{j}\gamma_{j+2}=\gamma_{j+1}^{2}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\widetilde{P}_{j-1}(x)\widetilde{P}_{j+1}(x)=\widetilde{P}_{j}(x)^{2}\quad\mbox{for}\;\;j=2,\dots,2n-2.

By the first relation, the nonzero sequence
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2n-1}
is geometric and hence,

\gamma_{j}=\gamma r^{j-1}\quad\mbox{for}\;\;j=1,\ldots.2n-1,

where
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
and
r=\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1}
. Next, we turn to
\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{2n-1}
.
Since
L
is invertible, there exists
k
such that
\deg P_{k}=\deg\widetilde{P}_{k}>0
.
Then
\widetilde{P}_{k}
has a (complex) root
x=-\alpha
. Let us factor the polynomials
\widetilde{P}_{j}
as

\widetilde{P}_{j}(x)=(x+\alpha)^{\ell_{j}}Q_{j}(x),\quad\ell_{j}\geq 0,\;\;j=1,\ldots,2n-1,

(3.19)

where
Q_{j}
is a monic polynomial such that
Q_{j}(-\alpha)\neq 0
. By our assumption,
\ell_{k}>0
. Also,

\ell_{j}\leq 2n-2
since
\deg\widetilde{P}_{j}\leq 2n-2
for all
j
. Substituting the latter
factorizations into (
3.18
), we get

(x+\alpha)^{\ell_{j-1}+\ell_{j+1}}Q_{j-1}(x)Q_{j+1}(x)=(x+\alpha)^{2\ell_{j}}Q_{j}(x)^{2},

and subsequently,

\ell_{j-1}+\ell_{j+1}=2\ell_{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q_{j-1}(x)Q_{j+1}(x)=Q_{j}(x)^{2}\quad\mbox{for}\;\;2=1,\ldots.2n-2.

(3.20)

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\ell}=\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{2n-1}\}
is a nonnegative integer arithmetic sequence bounded above by
2n-2

and with
\ell_{k}\geq 1
.
If
\ell_{j}=\ell_{k}
for all
j
, then
P_{j}(-\alpha)=0
for all
j
and hence
Lh(-\alpha)=0
, which contradicts the invertibility
of
L
. If the sequence
\boldsymbol{\ell}
increases or decreases, then it contains
2n-1
distinct integers bounded by zero and

2n-2
. Hence, either
\ell_{j}=j-1
or
\ell_{j}=2n-j-1
for all
j=1,\ldots,2n-1
. In view of (
3.19
), we conclude that either

\widetilde{P}_{j}(x)=(x+\alpha)^{j-1}Q_{j}(x)\quad\mbox{or}\quad\widetilde{P}_{j}(x)=(x+\alpha)^{2n-j-1}Q_{j}(x)

(3.21)

for all
j=1,\ldots,2n-1
. In the first case, we have
Q_{2n-1}\equiv 1
, since
Q_{2n-1}
is monic and

\deg\widetilde{P}_{2n-1}
cannot exceed
2n-2
. For the same reason
\deg Q_{2n-2}\leq 1
and hence either

Q_{2n-2}\equiv 1
or
Q_{2n-2}(x)=x+\beta
for some
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
. Then we recursively get from (
3.20
) that either

Q_{j}\equiv 1\quad\mbox{or}\quad Q_{j}(x)=(x+\beta)^{2n-j-1}\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;j=1,\ldots,2n-1.

Combining the latter formulas with the first formula in (
3.21
) and recalling that
P_{j}(x)=\gamma r^{j-1}\widetilde{P}_{j}(x)

we get cases (a) and (c) in(
3.17
). In case
\widetilde{P}_{j}
is given by the second formula in (
3.21
), we use the same
degree argument to conclude that
either

Q_{j}\equiv 1\quad\mbox{or}\quad Q_{j}(x)=(x+\beta)^{j-1}\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;j=1,\ldots,2n-1.

Combining the latter with the second formula in (
3.21
) we come to polynomials
P_{j}
as in part (b) in (
3.17
) or
as in part (c) with
\alpha
and
\beta
switched. ∎

4.1
Rank preservers and determinant preservers

One may consider linear maps
T
on
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
such that

, where
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
is the set of
rank one matrices in
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
.
In such a case, we say that
T

strongly preserves
of

\hat{\mathcal{R}}
.
Since
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
contains a spanning set of
{\mathbb{F}}^{2n-1}
,
such a map will map a spanning set
onto a spanning set of
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
, and must be invertible.
Hence,
T
is invertible and preserves
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
.
So,
T
has the form as in Theorem
3.6
(a).
We may consider other preserving conditions that lead to the

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
T:{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
with

[T]_{\mathcal{B}}=L\in M_{2n-1}
. The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

L
satisfies Theorem
3.5
(a)
, i.e.,

T
satisfies Theorem
3.6
(a)
.

(2)

L
is invertible and
L^{-1}({\mathcal{R}})
, i.e.,

T
is invertible and
T^{-1}
maps the set of rank one matrices in
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
into
itself.

(3)

L({\mathcal{R}})={\mathcal{R}}
, i.e.,

T
maps the set of rank one matrices
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
onto itself.

Proof.

Suppose (1) holds. Then
L
is invertible and

L({\mathcal{R}})\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
by Theorem
3.5
.
For any distinct elements

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, we have

L[h(\xi_{1})\cdots h(\xi_{k})]=[y_{1}\cdots y_{k}]
.
If
i\neq j
, then
y_{i}
and

cannot be multiples of each other.
Note that at most one

is a multiple of

. If it happens, we may
replace
\xi_{\ell}
by a different value, and assume that

for

Then

Suppose
k-1\geq 4n-2
.
We may now assume that the rows of

correspond
to polynomials

such that

P_{j}(x)P_{j+2}(x)=P_{j}(x)^{2}\quad\mbox{for}\;\;x=\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{4n-2}\in{\mathbb{F}}\;\;\mbox{with}\;\;\zeta_{i}\neq\zeta_{j}\;\;\mbox{ whenever }i\neq j.

Using the arguments in the proof of Theorem

, we see that

has the form in (

), and

Thus, condition (2) holds.

Now, suppose condition (2) holds.

and

and apply

We see that

. Thus,

∎

One may consider linear preservers on
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
preserving the rank of matrices,

and

, we have the following.

.

Theorem 4.3
.

A linear map
T:{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\rightarrow{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
satisfies
\det(T(A))=\det(A)
for all
A\in{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}

if and only if
T
has the form as in Theorem
3.6
(a)

and
1=\det(\gamma F_{n}D_{n}(r)N^{\top}F_{n}D_{n}(r))
, equivalently,

\gamma^{n}r^{n(n-1)}=1

in case

.

Proof.

Assume that
T:{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\rightarrow{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
is linear and preserves
the determinant.
We establish three assertions to prove the necessity.

Claim 1.

T
is bijective.

If not, there is
A\in{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
such that
T(A)=0
. Then we
can find a singular

such that

is invertible. But then

,
which is a contradiction.

Claim 2.

T
preserves rank one matrices in
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
.

Note
that a matrix

is rank one if and only if there are invertible matrices

such that

.
Thus,

\det(xA+B)=\det(R)\det(x{\mathbf{e}}_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{1}^{\top}+R^{-1}BS^{-1})\det(S)=ax+b_{B}
for all

. Since
T
is bijective and
preserves determinant,

\det(xT(A)+T(B))=ax+b
for all matrices

. So,

has rank one.

Claim 3
The mapping
T
has the form as in Theorem
3.6
(a) with

1=\det(\gamma F_{n}D_{n}(r)N^{\top}F_{n}D_{n}(r))
, or equivalently,

and

.

By Claim 2,

preserves rank one matrices so that

has the form in

(a). Moreover, for any

,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(T(A))=\det(\gamma F_{n}D_{n}(r)N^{\top}F_{n})\det(A)\det(ND_{n}(r))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(A)\det(\gamma I_{n})\det(F_{n})^{2}\det(D_{n}(r)^{2})\det(N^{\top}N)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(A)\gamma^{n}r^{n(n-1)}\det(N^{\top}N).

The converse is clear.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose

T:{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\rightarrow M_{n}

and

are polynomials in

with coefficients
associated with the columns of

.

Proof.
The sufficiency is clear. We prove the necessity.

Suppose
L

T
with respect to the basis
{\mathcal{B}}
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as in (
2.1
)
and the standard basis for
M_{n}
.
Then
L\in\mathbb{M}_{n^{2},(2n-1)}
, the set of
n^{2}\times(2n-1)
matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}
.
Consider rank one matrix in
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
of the form

X=[x^{n-1},\dots,1]^{\top}[1,x,\dots,x^{n-1}]
. The vector of
X
with respect to
{\mathcal{B}}

has the form
[1,x,\dots,x^{2n-1}]^{\top}
, and

L(X)=[P_{1}(x),\dots,P_{n^{2}}(x)]^{\top}

for some polynomials
P_{1}(x),\dots,P_{n^{2}}(x)
, each has degree at most
2n-2
.
It follows that

T(X)=A_{0}+A_{1}x+\cdots+A_{2n-2}x^{2n-2},

for some matrices
A_{0},\dots,A_{2n-1}
.
Since
T(X)
is always rank one, it follows that
T(X)=u(x)v(x)^{\top}
for some

u(x)=[u_{1}(x),\dots,u_{n}(x)]^{\top}
and
v(x)=[v_{1}(x),\dots,v_{n}(x)]^{\top}
;
see
[
5
]
. The result follows.
∎

Note that the map
T
in the theorem is injective if and only if the matrix of transformation

L
has rank
2n-1
. The map
T
preserves determinant if and only if
\det(MN)=1
.

4.3
Maps on rectangular Toeplitz matrices

Let
2\leq m\leq n
, and let
{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
be the set of

m\times n
Toeplitz matrices, that is, matrices of the form

A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\dots&\dots&\dots&a_{-(n-1)}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{-1}&\dots&\dots&\dots&a_{-(n-2)}\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}&\dots&\dots&\dots&a_{-(n-3)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{m-1}&a_{m-2}&a_{m-3}&\dots&a_{0}&\dots&a_{n-m}\end{bmatrix}.

Then
{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
has dimension
m+n-1
with a basis consisting of
matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{m-1}\cr\vdots\cr\xi\cr 1\cr\end{bmatrix}[1\ \xi\ \dots\xi^{n-1}],\qquad\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}.

Linear maps
T:{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}\to{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
may be studied by considering

L=[T]_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
. The proofs in the previous sections works.
One may also consider maps from
{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
to
M_{m,n}
, the set of
m\times n
matrices.
For example, Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
become:

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
T:{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
be a linear map with
2\leq m\leq n
.
Suppose
L\in M_{m+n-1}
and
[T]_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
. Then the set of rank one matrices
in
{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
has coordinate vectors in the set

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}=\{[1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{m+n-2}]^{\top}:\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}\cup\{\infty\}\}.

The linear function
T

maps the set of rank one matrices in
{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
into itself,
i.e.,
L(\tilde{\mathcal{R}})\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
if and only if one of the following conditions
holds.

(a)

There are nonzero
\gamma,r\in{\mathbb{F}}
such that
T
has the form

A\mapsto\gamma F_{m}D_{m}(r)N_{1}^{\top}F_{m}AN_{2}D_{n}(r),

where for
k=m,n
, the matrices
D_{k}(r)
and
F_{k}
are defined in (
3.1
) and

(N_{1},N_{2})
equals to one of the following:

(1)

(V_{m}(\alpha)^{\top},V_{n}(\alpha)),

(2)

(F_{m}V_{m}(\alpha)^{\top},V_{n}(\alpha)F_{n}),

(3)

(W_{m}(\alpha,\beta)^{T},W_{n}(\alpha,\beta)).

Equivalently, for
\ell=m+n-1
,
L^{\top}
equals to

(i)

\gamma V_{\ell}(\alpha)D_{\ell}(r)
,
(ii)

\gamma V_{\ell}(\alpha)F_{\ell}D_{\ell}(r)
,
or

(iii)

\gamma W_{\ell}(\alpha,\beta)D_{\ell}(r)
.

(b)

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}

and
T
has the form
A\mapsto f(A)B
for some rank one matrix
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{n}

and a linear functional
f
on
{\mathcal{T}}_{m,n}
such that for

X_{\xi}=[\xi^{m-1},\dots,\xi,1]^{\top}[1,\xi,\dots,\xi^{n-1}]

with
\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}
,

[L(X_{\xi})]_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{m+n-1}c_{j}\xi^{j}\neq 0
for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
where
c_{0},\dots,c_{m+n-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}
.

4.4
Hankel matrices

The results on linear preservers on Toeplitz matrices are easily translated to Hankel matrices, matrices in which each ascending skew-diagonal from left to right is constant; that is,
matrices of the form
A=[a_{i+j}]
for some
a_{2},\dots,a_{2n}
.
Evidently,
A
is Hankel if and only if
F_{n}A
is Toeplitz.
Thus,
T:{\mathcal{H}}_{n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is a linear map if and only if

\tilde{T}
defined by
A\mapsto F_{n}T(F_{n}A)
is a linear map on
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
.
In particular,
T
is a rank one preserves
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
if and only
\tilde{T}
is a rank one preserves
on
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
;
T
is a determinant preserver on
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
if and only
if
\tilde{T}
is a determinant preserver on
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
. So,
we can translate the preserver results on
{{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
in the previous sections
to results on
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
.
For instance, we have the following.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
n>2
and

\tilde{T}:{{\mathcal{H}}}_{n}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
is a linear map.
Then
\tilde{T}
sends rank one matrices to rank one matrices
if and only if
\tilde{T}
has the form

A\mapsto\gamma N^{\top}AN,

where
\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}
is nonzero and
N

has one of the following forms:

{\rm(1)}\;N=V_{n}(\alpha)D_{n}(r),\quad{\rm(2)}\;N=V_{n}(\alpha)F_{n}D_{n}(r),\quad{\rm(3)}\;N=W_{n}(\alpha,\beta)D_{n}(r),

where
D_{n}(r)
and
F_{n}
are defined in (
3.1
).

The map
\tilde{T}

preserves the determinant if and only it has the above form with
\det(\gamma N^{\top}N)=1
, equivalently,

\gamma^{n}r^{(n(n-1)}=1
and

(\alpha-\beta)^{n(n-1)}=1
in case
N=W(\alpha,\beta)
.

4.5
Concluding remarks

A close examination of the proofs of Lemma
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
reveals that they go through over any field with at least

(2n-1)^{2}+1
elements. It would be interesting to know how these results look within smaller finite fields. It is also curious to consider
rank-preserver questions for matrices over a semiring or a
division ring, in particular, over the skew field
\mathbb{H}
of quaternions; e.g., see
[
6
]

It can be shown that rank-one Toeplitz matrices in this setting still are either of the form

A_{1}=\mu{\bf e}_{1}{\bf e}_{n}^{\top}\quad\mbox{or}\quad A_{2}=\mu\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\xi^{n-1}&\ldots&\xi&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{\top}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&\xi&\ldots&\xi^{n-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\lambda

for nonzero scalars
\mu,\lambda
. However, the polynomial approach in the proof of Theorem
3.5
requires significant adjustments
(if this is at all possible).

Finally, similar questions can be considered on other structured matrices;
e.g., see
[
14
,
15
]
.
