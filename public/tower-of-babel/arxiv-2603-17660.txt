1.
Introduction

A classical invariant of a topological space is the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category. For a (reasonable) space
X
, it is defined as the minimal number
d
such that there exists an open cover of
X
consisting of
d
open subsets each of which is contractible in
X
. An equivalent definition states that the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of a path connected space
X
is the (unreduced) Schwarz genus of the path-space fibration
p:PX\rightarrow X
, where
PX=\{\omega\in X^{I}\mid\omega(0)=x_{0}\}
is the space of paths in
X
starting at a base point
x_{0}\in X
, and
p(\omega)=\omega(1)
. We will denote by
\operatorname{cat}(X)
the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of
X
.

If we take the whole space of paths
X^{I}
and the fibration
\pi:X^{I}\rightarrow X\times X
given by
\pi(\omega)=(\omega(0),\omega(1))
, the Schwarz genus of this fibration is another invariant, called topological complexity of
X
, denoted by
\operatorname{TC}(X)
, and introduced by Farber in
[
6
]
.

One of the most common approaches for studying these invariants is the cohomological method, which provides lower bounds for them in terms of cup product. A lower bound for
\operatorname{cat}(X)
is the
R
-cup-length of
X
(for any commutative ring
R
), denoted by
\operatorname{cl}_{R}(X)
, and a lower bound for
\operatorname{TC}(X)
is the
R
-zero-divisor cup-length of
X
, denoted by
\operatorname{zcl}_{R}(X)
:

(1.1)

\operatorname{cl}_{R}(X)<\operatorname{cat}(X),

(1.2)

\operatorname{zcl}_{R}(X)<\operatorname{TC}(X).

The Grassmann manifold
\widetilde{G}_{n,k}
of oriented
k
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(the so-called oriented Grassmannian) is a closed manifold of dimension
k(n-k)
. When it comes to the invariants
\operatorname{cat}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
and
\operatorname{TC}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
, the case
k=1
is well established, since
\widetilde{G}_{n,1}
is just the sphere
S^{n-1}
, and so
\operatorname{cat}(\widetilde{G}_{n,1})=2
, while
\operatorname{TC}(\widetilde{G}_{n,1})=2
if
n
is even, and
\operatorname{TC}(\widetilde{G}_{n,1})=3
if
n
is odd (by
[
6
, Theorem 8]
). The case
k=2
is also uninteresting, because
\widetilde{G}_{n,2}
is a closed, simply connected, symplectic manifold (the symplectic structure comes from the identification of
\widetilde{G}_{n,2}
with the complex quadric
Q_{n-2}=\{[z_{0},\ldots,z_{n-1}]\in\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n-1}\mid z_{0}^{2}+\cdots+z_{n-1}^{2}=0\}
), and consequently, both Lusternik–Schnirelmann category and topological complexity are known:
\operatorname{cat}(\widetilde{G}_{n,2})=n-1
,
\operatorname{TC}(\widetilde{G}_{n,2})=2n-3
(see e.g.
[
9
, Proposition 2.3]
).

For
k\geqslant 3
these invariants are not known. However, there has been some development recently in calculating cohomological lower bounds from (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), mostly with modulo
2
coefficients (i.e., with coefficients in the two-element field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
). In the case
k=3
, the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-cup-length of
\widetilde{G}_{n,3}
was studied and computed for different values of
n
in a number of papers (e.g.
[
7
,
8
]
) and in
[
3
]
it was computed for all
n\geqslant 6
(it suffices to study
\widetilde{G}_{n,k}
for
n\geqslant 2k
, because
\widetilde{G}_{n,n-k}\cong\widetilde{G}_{n,k}
). The
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-zero-divisor cup-length of
\widetilde{G}_{n,3}
was studied in
[
4
]
, where
\operatorname{zcl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\widetilde{G}_{n,3})
is either exactly determined or placed between bounds that differ by
1
.

In the case
k=4
, some lower and upper bounds for
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
were established in
[
10
, Proposition 6.11]
, while the exact value of
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
for
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t}\}
(
t\geqslant 4
) was calculated in a recent paper by Rusin
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
.

In this paper we extend Rusin’s results to the case
n=2^{t}+1
(
t\geqslant 3
) by computing
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
in Theorem
4.1
, and in Corollary
5.7
we establish lower bounds for
\operatorname{zcl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
, which, along with the inequality (
1.2
), provide lower bounds for topological complexity of these manifolds. As another topic of the paper, which is intimately connected with cup-length and zero-divisor cup-length, we investigate the heights of the Stiefel–Whitney classes of the tautological vector bundle over
\widetilde{G}_{n,4}
, and determine them completely for
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
(Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
).

The main tool in our calculations are Gröbner bases for the ideals closely related to the mod
2
cohomology of
\widetilde{G}_{n,4}
. These bases for the cases
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t}\}
were obtained by Rusin in
[
11
]
. The corresponding ideal for
n=2^{t}+1
is actually the same as the one for
n=2^{t}
, so we have a Gröbner basis for each of the cases we consider.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give a necessary background for our calculations. We first define and discuss the main notions that we consider in the paper—the height of a cohomology class, the cup-length and the zero-divisor cup-length; then we give a brief review of the theory of Gröbner bases over a field; and finally we collect some basic facts concerning the mod
2
cohomology of oriented Grassmannians
\widetilde{G}_{n,k}
. Sections
3
,
4
and
5
are devoted to proving our main results, regarding (respectively) heights of Stiefel–Whitney classes,
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-cup-length and
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-zero-divisor cup-length.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Cup-length, zero-divisor cup-length, and height of an algebra element

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity, and
A=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{\infty}A^{i}
a graded
R
-algebra with identity
1\in A^{0}
. If an element
a\in A
belongs to the submodule
A^{i}
, then we say that
a
is homogeneous of degree
i
, and we write
|a|=i
.

If
a\in A
is homogeneous of positive degree, we define the
height
of
a
, denoted by
\operatorname{ht}(a)
, as the supremum of the set
\{m\mid a^{m}\neq 0\}
.

The
cup-length
of the algebra
A
, denoted by
\operatorname{cl}(A)
, is defined as the supremum of the set of all natural numbers
m
such that there exist homogeneous elements
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\in A
of positive degree with
a_{1}\cdot a_{2}\cdots a_{m}\neq 0
. Less formally,
\operatorname{cl}(A)
is the length of the longest nontrivial product of positive-degree elements in
A
.

It is obvious that for every
a\in A
(which is homogeneous of positive degree) one has
\operatorname{ht}(a)\leqslant\operatorname{cl}(A)
.

The tensor product
A\otimes A=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{\infty}\big(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}A^{i}\otimes A^{r-i}\big)
becomes a graded
R
-algebra as well, with the product defined on simple tensors (of homogeneous elements
a,b,c,d\in A
) by

(a\otimes b)\cdot(c\otimes d)=(-1)^{|b|\cdot|c|}(a\cdot c)\otimes(b\cdot d).

Moreover, if algebra
A
is commutative (in the graded sense—meaning that
b\cdot c=(-1)^{|b|\cdot|c|}c\cdot b
for any two homogeneous elements
b,c\in A
), then its product map
A\otimes A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\longrightarrow}}A
is an algebra morphism. In that case, the kernel of the map
A\otimes A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\longrightarrow}}A
is an ideal in
A\otimes A
, and its elements are called
zero-divisors
. For instance, if
a\in A
is any element, then

z(a)=1\otimes a-a\otimes 1

is a zero-divisor. Note that if
a
is homogeneous, then so is
z(a)
, and
|z(a)|=|a|
.

The
zero-divisor cup-length
of
A
, denoted by
\operatorname{zcl}(A)
, is the supremum of the set of all natural numbers
m
such that there are homogeneous zero-divisors
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m}\in\ker\big(A\otimes A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\longrightarrow}}A\big)
of positive degree with the property
z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\cdots z_{m}\neq 0
in
A\otimes A
. So, one can say that
\operatorname{zcl}(A)
is the cup-length of
\ker\big(A\otimes A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\longrightarrow}}A\big)
, if this kernel is considered as a graded algebra (although without identity).

Now, if
X
is a topological space, then for any commutative ring (with identity)
R
, the cup product of cohomology classes turns
H^{*}(X;R)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{\infty}H^{i}(X;R)
into a graded commutative
R
-algebra, and we define
R
-cup-length
and
R
-zero-divisor cup-length
of
X
as expected:

\operatorname{cl}_{R}(X):=\operatorname{cl}\big(H^{*}(X;R)\big)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\operatorname{zcl}_{R}(X):=\operatorname{zcl}\big(H^{*}(X;R)\big).

2.2.
Gröbner bases

The main purpose of Gröbner bases is deciding whether a given polynomial belongs to a given ideal (in a polynomial ring), i.e., whether the coset of that polynomial is zero in the corresponding quotient ring.
Let us recall some basics concerning Gröbner bases over a field. For a thorough introduction into the theory one can read
[
1
, Chapter 5]
. In order to simplify our discussion, we will present this review for the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
only, since that is the only case we will be working with in this paper.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
be the polynomial ring, where
\overline{X}
stands for the (finite) set of variables. Denote by
M
the set of all monomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
. Let us fix a
monomial order

\preceq
, that is, a well ordering on
M
with the property that for all
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\in M
,
m_{1}\preceq m_{2}
implies
m_{1}m_{3}\preceq m_{2}m_{3}
.

For a polynomial
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
, where
m_{i}\in M
are distinct monomials, define
M(f)=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}\}
(it is understood that
M(0)=\emptyset
). The
leading monomial
of a nonzero polynomial
f
is
\operatorname{LM}(f)\coloneqq\max M(f)
, where the maximum is taken with respect to the ordering
\preceq
.

Now let
F\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
be a finite set of nonzero polynomials, and denote by
\operatorname{LM}(F)=\{\operatorname{LM}(f)\mid f\in F\}\subseteq M
the set of leading monomials of the polynomials in
F
.

Definition 2.1
.

For two polynomials
p,q\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
we say that
p

reduces to
q
modulo
F
, and write
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\longrightarrow}}q
, if either
p=q=0
or there exists a finite sequence of polynomials
p=p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}=q
with the following property: for every
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,s-1\}
there is a polynomial
f_{j}\in F
such that
\operatorname{LM}(f_{j})\mid\operatorname{LM}(p_{j})
, say
\operatorname{LM}(p_{j})=m\cdot\operatorname{LM}(f_{j})
for some
m\in M
, and

p_{j+1}=p_{j}-m\cdot f_{j}.

If
I
is the ideal in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
generated by
F
, note that
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\longrightarrow}}q
implies
p-q\in I
. Therefore, if
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
, then
p\in I
. However, the opposite implication does not hold in general. A Gröbner basis for an ideal is its generating set for which this opposite implication does hold.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
F\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
be a finite set of nonzero polynomials, and
I
the ideal in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
generated by
F
. We say that
F
is a
Gröbner basis
for
I
with respect to
\preceq
, if every
p\in I
reduces to zero modulo
F
.

If
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\longrightarrow}}\overline{p}
, and if
\overline{p}
cannot be further reduced modulo
F
, that is, if either
\overline{p}=0
or
\operatorname{LM}(\overline{p})
is not divisible by any of the monomials from
\operatorname{LM}(F)
, we say that
\overline{p}
is
a normal form of
p
modulo
F
. It is a theorem that if
F
is a Gröbner basis (for the ideal generated by
F
), then every polynomial has a unique normal form, so we can speak of
the
normal form of a polynomial modulo
F
. So, we have the following theorem, which demonstrates the above mentioned main purpose of Gröbner bases.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
F
be a Gröbner basis for the ideal
I
(with respect to a given monomial order). For an arbitrary polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
denote by
\overline{p}
its normal form modulo
F
. Then the following equivalence holds:

p\in I\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\overline{p}=0.

An obvious consequence of this theorem is that if a nonzero polynomial
p
cannot be reduced modulo
F
(i.e., if
\overline{p}=p
), then
p\notin I
.

Note also that if
\overline{p}
is the normal form of
p
modulo
F
(so that
\overline{p}
cannot be reduced modulo
F
), then uniqueness of normal forms implies that none of the monomials from
M(\overline{p})
is divisible by any of the monomials from
\operatorname{LM}(F)
. Since
p-\overline{p}\in I
, this means that the cosets of monomials not divisible by any of the elements from
\operatorname{LM}(F)
generate the quotient vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]/I
. Additionally, the uniqueness of normal forms implies linear independence of these cosets. Therefore, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
F
is a Gröbner basis for the ideal
I\trianglelefteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]
(with respect to a given monomial order), then the set

\Big\{m+I\,\,\Big|\,\,m\in M,\,\,(\forall f\in F)\,\operatorname{LM}(f)\nmid m\Big\}

is a vector space basis for
\mathbb{F}_{2}[\overline{X}]/I
.

2.3.
Background on the cohomology algebra
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})

We will be working with
\mathbb{F}_{2}
coefficients exclusively, so in the rest of the paper we will write
H^{*}(X)
for
H^{*}(X;\mathbb{F}_{2})
,
\operatorname{cl}(X)
for
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(X)
, and
\operatorname{zcl}(X)
for
\operatorname{zcl}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(X)
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
and
n\geqslant 2k
be integers, and let
\widetilde{\gamma}_{n,k}
be the (
k
-dimensional) canonical vector bundle over the oriented Grassmann manifold
\widetilde{G}_{n,k}
. Since this manifold is simply connected, the first Stiefel–Whitney class
w_{1}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{n,k})\in H^{1}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
is trivial. We will denote by
\widetilde{w}_{i}
the Stiefel–Whitney classes
w_{i}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{n,k})\in H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant k
. Let
W_{n,k}
be the subalgebra of
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
generated by these classes
\widetilde{w}_{i}
,
2\leqslant i\leqslant k
. It is well known (see e.g.
[
5
, p. 197]
) that
W_{n,k}
is actually the image of the map
p^{*}:H^{*}(G_{n,k})\rightarrow H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
induced by the universal covering
p:\widetilde{G}_{n,k}\rightarrow G_{n,k}
(where
G_{n,k}
is the Grassmann manifold of
unoriented

k
-subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p
”forgets” the orientation), and that one has an isomorphism of graded algebras

(2.1)

W_{n,k}\cong\frac{\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},\dots,w_{k}]}{I_{n,k}},

where
I_{n,k}
is the ideal generated by the polynomials
g_{n-k+1}^{(k)},\dots,g_{n}^{(k)}
, which are defined by the relation

(2.2)

(1+w_{2}+\cdots+w_{k})(g_{0}^{(k)}+g_{1}^{(k)}+g_{2}^{(k)}+\cdots)=1.

Via the isomorphism (
2.1
) the Stiefel–Whitney class
\widetilde{w}_{i}\in W_{n,k}
maps to the coset of the variable
w_{i}
,
2\leqslant i\leqslant k
(it is understood that the degree of
w_{i}
is
i
). Therefore, for an arbitrary polynomial
f=f(w_{2},\ldots,w_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}]
we have the equivalence

f(w_{2},\ldots,w_{k})\in I_{n,k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(\widetilde{w}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{w}_{k})=0\,\mbox{ in }\,W_{n,k}\subset H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k}),

which will be used throughout the paper.

The following two relations are straightforward from (
2.2
):

(2.3)

g_{r}^{(k)}=\sum_{2a_{2}+\cdots+ka_{k}=r}\binom{a_{2}+\cdots+a_{k}}{a_{2}}\cdots\binom{a_{k-1}+a_{k}}{a_{k-1}}\,w_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots w_{k}^{a_{k}},\quad r\geqslant 0

(the sum is taken over all
(k-1)
-tuples
(a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})
of nonnegative integers such that
2a_{2}+\cdots+ka_{k}=r
);

(2.4)

g_{r}^{(k)}=w_{2}g_{r-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+w_{k}g_{r-k}^{(k)},\quad r\geqslant k.

Additionally, if we define
g_{r}^{(k)}=0
for each
r\in\{-1,-2,\ldots,-k+1\}
, it is easy to check that the last equation holds for all integers
r\geqslant 1
. Furthermore, using induction on
s
, we get the following generalized version of relation (
2.4
) (see e.g.
[
2
, (2.8)]
):

(2.5)

g_{r}^{(k)}\!=w_{2}^{2^{s}}g_{r-2\cdot 2^{s}}^{(k)}+\cdots+w_{k}^{2^{s}}g_{r-k2^{s}}^{(k)}=\!\sum_{j=2}^{k}w_{j}^{2^{s}}g_{r-j2^{s}}^{(k)},\,\,\,r\geqslant 1+k(2^{s}-1),\,s\geqslant 0.

Another consequence of (
2.4
) is the fact that not only
g_{n-k+1}^{(k)},\dots,g_{n}^{(k)}
but all polynomials
g_{r}^{(k)}
with
r\geqslant n-k+1
, belong to
I_{n,k}
:

(2.6)

g_{r}^{(k)}\in I_{n,k}\quad\mbox{ for all }r\geqslant n-k+1.

In particular, the polynomials that generate
I_{n+1,k}=(g_{n-k+2}^{(k)},\dots,g_{n+1}^{(k)})
belong to
I_{n,k}
, and therefore, the sequence of ideals
I_{n,k}
decreases with
n
:

(2.7)

I_{n,k}\supseteq I_{n+1,k}\quad\mbox{ for all }k\geqslant 2\mbox{ and all }n\geqslant 2k.

A useful fact concerning the polynomials
g_{r}^{(k)}
is proved in
[
8
, Lemma 2.3(ii)]
: for
k\in\{3,4\}
and all
t\geqslant 2

(2.8)

g_{2^{t}-3}^{(k)}=0.

This equation, along with (
2.4
), implies

g_{2^{t}+1}^{(4)}=w_{2}g_{2^{t}-1}^{(4)}+w_{3}g_{2^{t}-2}^{(4)}
,
and consequently

(2.9)

I_{2^{t}+1,4}=\big(g_{2^{t}-2}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}+1}^{(4)}\big)=\big(g_{2^{t}-2}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}}^{(4)}\big)=I_{2^{t},4}.

From (
2.1
) we now obtain the isomorphism of graded algebras

(2.10)

W_{2^{t},4}\cong W_{2^{t}+1,4},

via which the Stiefel–Whitney class
\widetilde{w}_{i}\in H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t},4})
corresponds to the Stiefel–Whitney class
\widetilde{w}_{i}\in H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
,
i=2,3,4
.

Similarly, using (
2.4
) and (
2.8
) one can check that

(2.11)

I_{2^{t},3}=\big(g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)},g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}\big)=\big(g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)}\big)=I_{2^{t}-1,3}

(cf.
[
3
, Remark 3.16]
).

Another useful fact was proved in
[
2
, Lemma 4.6]
: the variable
w_{4}
does not appear in
g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}}^{(4)}
for any
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,t-1\}
. On the other hand, it is obvious from (
2.3
) that the polynomial
g_{r}^{(3)}
is the reduction of
g_{r}^{(4)}
modulo
w_{4}
. We conclude

(2.12)

g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}}^{(4)}=g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}}^{(3)}\quad\mbox{ for all }i\in\{0,1,\ldots,t-1\}.

In general, the ideal
I_{n,k}\trianglelefteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}]\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},\ldots,w_{k},w_{k+1}]
need not be a subset of
I_{n,k+1}\trianglelefteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},\ldots,w_{k},w_{k+1}]
. Nevertheless, this does hold for
k=3
and
n
a power of two. More precisely, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
t\geqslant 3
one has
I_{2^{t},3}=I_{2^{t},4}\cap\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
.

Proof.

We already know (see (
2.9
) and (
2.11
)) that

I_{2^{t},3}=\big(g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)}\big)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]\mbox{ and }I_{2^{t},4}=\big(g_{2^{t}-2}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(4)},g_{2^{t}}^{(4)}\big)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}].

We certainly have
I_{2^{t},3}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
, and so, in order to establish the containment
I_{2^{t},3}\subseteq I_{2^{t},4}\cap\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
, it suffices to check that
g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)},g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)}\in I_{2^{t},4}
(since
I_{2^{t},4}
is an ideal in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
). According to (
2.12
), we have
g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)}=g_{2^{t}-2}^{(4)}\in I_{2^{t},4}
and
g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)}=g_{2^{t}-1}^{(4)}\in I_{2^{t},4}
.

For the reverse containment, let
f\in I_{2^{t},4}
be a polynomial which does not contain the variable
w_{4}
(i.e.,
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
). Write
f
in the form

f=p_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}^{(4)}+p_{1}g_{2^{t}-1}^{(4)}+p_{0}g_{2^{t}}^{(4)},

for some
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
. If we reduce this equation modulo
w_{4}
(this reduction
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]/(w_{4})=\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
is a ring epimorphism), using the fact
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
and (
2.11
) we obtain

f=\overline{p}_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)}+\overline{p}_{1}g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)}+\overline{p}_{0}g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}\in I_{2^{t},3}

(
\overline{p}_{0},\overline{p}_{1},\overline{p}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
are reductions of
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}
respectively).
∎

From now on we will be working mostly with the case
k=4
, and for that reason we will abbreviate
g_{r}^{(4)}
to
g_{r}
.

A Gröbner basis for the ideal
I_{2^{t},3}=I_{2^{t}-1,3}
, with respect to the lexicographic order in which
w_{2}>w_{3}
, was detected in
[
7
]
. It is the set

F=\big\{g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}}\mid 0\leqslant i\leqslant t-1\big\}=\big\{g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}}^{(3)}\mid 0\leqslant i\leqslant t-1\big\}

(see (
2.12
)), and the set of the leading monomials is

(2.13)

\operatorname{LM}(F)=\big\{w_{2}^{2^{t-1}-2^{i}}w_{3}^{2^{i}-1}\mid 0\leqslant i\leqslant t-1\big\}

(see
[
5
, Proposition 3.4]
).
Gröbner bases
F_{n}
for the ideals
I_{n,4}
in the cases
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
(
t\geqslant 4
), with respect to the lexicographic order in which
w_{4}>w_{2}>w_{3}
, were presented in
[
11
, Lemma 3.4]
, and they are as follows:

F_{2^{t}}=F_{2^{t}+1}=F\cup\{g_{2^{t}}\},\quad F_{2^{t}-1}=F\cup\{g_{2^{t}-4}\},\quad F_{2^{t}-2}=F\cup\{g_{2^{t}-4},g_{2^{t}-5}\}.

In fact, the claim that
F_{2^{t}}
is a Gröbner basis for
I_{2^{t},4}=I_{2^{t}+1,4}
is easily checked to be true for
t=3
as well. The sets of the leading monomials are:

\displaystyle\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\big\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}}\big\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}-1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\big\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}\big\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}-2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\big\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1},w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}\big\}.

We now present two lemmas which, along with Lemma
2.5
, establish some crucial connections between the ideal
I_{2^{t},3}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
on one side, and the ideals
I_{2^{t},4}
and
I_{2^{t}-1,4}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
on the other.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
p_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
,
0\leqslant j\leqslant 2^{t-2}-1
,
t\geqslant 3
, then

\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}p_{j}w_{4}^{j}\in I_{2^{t},4}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_{j}\in I_{2^{t},3}\mbox{ for all }j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-1\}.

Proof.

Suppose
\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}p_{j}w_{4}^{j}\in I_{2^{t},4}
. For
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-1\}
let
\overline{p}_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
be the normal form of
p_{j}
modulo
F
(which is a Gröbner basis for
I_{2^{t},3}
). Then
p_{j}\in I_{2^{t},3}
if and only if
\overline{p}_{j}=0
(Theorem
2.3
), and since
F\subset I_{2^{t},4}
we have
\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}\overline{p}_{j}w_{4}^{j}\in I_{2^{t},4}
. But this sum is a polynomial in which no monomial is divisible by any of the leading monomials
\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}})=\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}}\}
from Gröbner basis
F_{2^{t}}
of
I_{2^{t},4}
. This is possible only if
\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}\overline{p}_{j}w_{4}^{j}=0
, i.e.,
\overline{p}_{j}=0
for all
j
.

Conversely, if
p_{j}\in I_{2^{t},3}
for all
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-1\}
, then by Lemma
2.5
,
p_{j}\in I_{2^{t},4}
for all
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-1\}
, and so
\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}p_{j}w_{4}^{j}\in I_{2^{t},4}
.
∎

When it comes to the ideal
I_{2^{t}-1,4}
, using its Gröbner basis
F_{2^{t}-1}
(whose set of leading monomials is
\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}\}
) and the fact
I_{2^{t},4}\subseteq I_{2^{t}-1,4}
, one can easily adjust the proof of Lemma
2.6
to get a proof of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
p_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]
,
0\leqslant j\leqslant 2^{t-2}-2
,
t\geqslant 4
, then

\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t-2}-2}p_{j}w_{4}^{j}\in I_{2^{t}-1,4}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_{j}\in I_{2^{t},3}\mbox{ for all }j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-2\}.

3.
Heights of the Stiefel–Whitney classes

In this section we determine heights of the Stiefel–Whitney classes
\widetilde{w}_{2},\widetilde{w}_{3}
and
\widetilde{w}_{4}
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
for
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
,
t\geqslant 4
. In
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
we have

(1)

\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{2})=2^{t}-4
,

(2)

\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{3})=2^{t-1}-2
.

Proof.

We know that
\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}=0
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
if and only if
w_{2}^{b}w_{3}^{c}\in I_{n,4}
. Since
I_{2^{t}+1,4}=I_{2^{t},4}
(see (
2.9
)), from
Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
(applied to
p_{0}=w_{2}^{b}w_{3}^{c}
and
p_{j}=0
for
j>0
) we conclude that for
n\in\{2^{t}-1,2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
,
w_{2}^{b}w_{3}^{c}\in I_{n,4}
is equivalent to
w_{2}^{b}w_{3}^{c}\in I_{2^{t},3}
. Therefore, the heights of
\widetilde{w}_{2}
and
\widetilde{w}_{3}
in these cases are equal to the heights of
\widetilde{w}_{2}
and
\widetilde{w}_{3}
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t},3})
.
These heights are calculated in
[
3
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.3]
, and they are as stated.

For the remaining case
n=2^{t}-2
we have
w_{2}^{2^{t}-3},w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-1}\in I_{2^{t}-1,4}\subseteq I_{2^{t}-2,4}
(see (
2.7
)), and it remains to check that
w_{2}^{2^{t}-4},w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. We prove this using the Gröbner basis
F_{2^{t}-2}
for
I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. No monomial from
\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}-2})=\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\big\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1},w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}\big\}
divides
w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}
, i.e.,
w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}
cannot be reduced modulo
F_{2^{t}-2}
, and so
w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
.

From
[
3
, (4.3)]
we have
w_{2}^{2^{t}-4}+w_{2}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}\in I_{2^{t}-1,3}=I_{2^{t},3}
, and we know that
I_{2^{t},3}\subseteq I_{2^{t},4}\subseteq I_{2^{t}-2,4}
(Lemma
2.5
and (
2.7
)). So
w_{2}^{2^{t}-4}+w_{2}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
, which means that it is enough to prove
w_{2}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. It is shown in
[
3
, p. 277]
that no monomial from
\operatorname{LM}(F)
divides
w_{2}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}
, and obviously, none of
w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}
and
w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}
divides it either. Since
F_{2^{t}-2}
is a Gröbner basis, this implies
w_{2}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
, and consequently
w_{2}^{2^{t}-4}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

This theorem (and its proof) is valid for
t=3
and
n\in\{2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
, i.e., for
n\in\{8,9\}
, as well.

We are left to determine the height of
\widetilde{w}_{4}
for
n
as in the previous theorem. Let us first formulate the assertion.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
t\geqslant 4
and
j\in\{0,1,2\}
, in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}-j,4})
one has

\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{4})=2^{t-1}-1-j.

Also, for
t\geqslant 3
, in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
one has
\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{4})=2^{t-1}-1
.

The rest of this section is devoted to proving Theorem
3.3
. We will separate the proof into three parts:
j=0
,
j=1
and
j=2
. These parts are established in Propositions
3.5
,
3.6
and
3.8
respectively. Due to (
2.10
), the height of
\widetilde{w}_{4}
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
is the same as in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t},4})
, and so the part
j=0
(Proposition
3.5
) includes this case as well.

Let us first recall an equation from
[
11
, Lemma 3.2]
: for
t\geqslant 3

(3.1)

g_{2^{t}-4}=\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}.

Note, however, that this equation holds for
t=2
as well (in that case (
3.1
) reduces to
1=1
).

Another fact that will be used in the upcoming computations was proved in
[
2
, (4.7)]
:

(3.2)

w_{3}g_{r}^{2}=g_{2r+3}\quad\mbox{ for all }r\geqslant-3.

The next lemma expresses
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}
in terms of polynomials
g_{r}
, and we will use it for the proof of Theorem
3.3
for both
j=0
and
j=1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all integers
t\geqslant 3
one has

w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}=g_{2^{t+1}-4}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-4}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-4}.

Proof.

We use equation (
3.1
) to calculate:

\displaystyle g_{2^{t+1}-4}

\displaystyle+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-4}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t+1-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}\!\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}

\displaystyle+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{t-3}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t-1-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t+1-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(w_{2}^{2^{t-1-i}}g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(w_{3}^{2^{t-1-i}}g_{2^{t-1-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\bigg(g_{2^{t+1-i}-4}^{(3)}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1-i}}g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1-i}}g_{2^{t-1-i}-4}^{(3)}\bigg)^{2^{i}}

The sums in the third and the fifth row are equal, because the summand for
i=t-2
vanishes (since
g_{-2}^{(3)}=0
). Finally, by (
2.5
) (applied to
k=3
,
r=2^{t+1-i}-4
and
s=t-1-i
) the expression in the last brackets is zero, and we are done.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
t\geqslant 3
and
n\in\{2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
. Then in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
we have

\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{4})=2^{t-1}-1.

Proof.

We know that
I_{2^{t},4}=I_{2^{t}+1,4}
(see (
2.9
)), and so it is enough to prove that
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}}\in I_{2^{t},4}
and
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}\notin I_{2^{t},4}
.

The equation (
2.5
) for
k=4
,
r=2^{t+1}
and
s=t-1
is

(3.3)

g_{2^{t+1}}=w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}}+w_{4}^{2^{t-1}}.

Recall that
g_{r}\in I_{n,4}
for all
r\geqslant n-3
(see (
2.6
)), and therefore

g_{2^{t+1}},g_{2^{t}}\in I_{2^{t},4}
, while
w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}\in I_{2^{t},4}
since
\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{3})=2^{t-1}-2
(Theorem
3.1
). Equation (
3.3
) now implies
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}}\in I_{2^{t},4}
.

Using again the fact
w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}\in I_{2^{t},4}
, as well as
g_{2^{t+1}-4}\in I_{2^{t},4}
, from Lemma
3.4
we conclude that
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}\notin I_{2^{t},4}
if and only if
w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-4}\notin I_{2^{t},4}
. Since
\operatorname{LM}(g_{2^{t}-4})=w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}
, by Lemma
2.6
it suffices to prove
w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}\notin I_{2^{t},3}
, which is true because
\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{2})=2^{t}-4\geqslant 2^{t-1}
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t},3})
(see
[
3
, Theorem 1.2]
).
∎

So, we have verified Theorem
3.3
for
j=0
(including the case
n=2^{t}+1
). We now move on to the case
j=1
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
t\geqslant 4
. For
\widetilde{w}_{4}\in H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}-1,4})
we have

\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{4})=2^{t-1}-2.

Proof.

We need to check that
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}\in I_{2^{t}-1,4}
and
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}\notin I_{2^{t}-1,4}
. The first assertion follows from Lemma
3.4
, since
g_{2^{t+1}-4},g_{2^{t}-4},w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}\in I_{2^{t}-1,4}
(by (
2.6
) and Theorem
3.1
).

For the second one, we use (
3.1
):

\big(g_{2^{t}-4}\big)^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i+1}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i+1}}=w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-3}w_{4}^{2^{i+1}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i+1}}.

Using the fact
g_{2^{t}-4}\in I_{2^{t}-1,4}
again, we conclude that the claim
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}\notin I_{2^{t}-1,4}
is equivalent to the assertion that the last sum does not belong to
I_{2^{t}-1,4}
. By Lemma
2.7
it suffices to find
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,t-3\}
with the property
\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i+1}}\notin I_{2^{t},3}
. For
i=t-3
this polynomial is
\big(g_{4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{t-2}}=w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}
(since
g_{4}^{(3)}=w_{2}^{2}
), and the fact
w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}\notin I_{2^{t},3}
was established in the proof of the previous proposition.
∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
3.3
we are left to prove it for
j=2
. In that case Theorem
3.3
comes down to
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
and
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-3}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. In the previous proof, in order to show
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}\in I_{2^{t}-1,4}
, we used a convenient representation of
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}
, given in Lemma
3.4
. It would be helpful to obtain something similar for
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
t\geqslant 4
. In
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
we have the following identity:

w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}=\alpha_{t}g_{2^{t}-2}+(g_{2^{t}-4})^{2}+\beta_{t}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-1}g_{2^{t-1}-5},

for some polynomials
\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
.

Proof.

If we multiply the equation from Lemma
3.4
by
w_{3}
, and if we apply (
2.4
) (for
k=4
and
r=2^{t+1}-1,2^{t}-1,2^{t-1}-1
), we obtain

\displaystyle w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}=

\displaystyle\,w_{3}g_{2^{t+1}-4}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}w_{3}g_{2^{t}-4}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}w_{3}g_{2^{t-1}-4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,w_{4}g_{2^{t+1}-5}+g_{2^{t+1}-1}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}(w_{4}g_{2^{t}-5}+g_{2^{t}-1})

\displaystyle+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}(w_{4}g_{2^{t-1}-5}+g_{2^{t-1}-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,w_{4}\big(g_{2^{t+1}-5}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-5}\big)

\displaystyle+g_{2^{t+1}-1}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-1}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-1}.

According to (
2.12
),

g_{2^{t+1}-1}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-1}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-1}=g_{2^{t+1}-1}^{(3)}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-1}^{(3)}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-1}^{(3)},

and this vanishes by (
2.5
) (applied to
k=3
,
r=2^{t+1}-1
and
s=t-1
). Therefore

w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-1}=w_{4}\big(g_{2^{t+1}-5}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-5}\big),

and canceling out
w_{4}
we get

(3.4)

w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}=g_{2^{t+1}-5}+w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-5}.

Now, to obtain the desired equation, we wish to cancel out
w_{3}
, and for that purpose we write each of the summands on the right-hand side as a convenient multiple of
w_{3}
.
By (
3.2
),
g_{2^{t+1}-5}=w_{3}(g_{2^{t}-4})^{2}
and
g_{2^{t}-5}=w_{3}(g_{2^{t-1}-4})^{2}
. The monomial
w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}
is the leading monomial of
g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}
, and all other monomials of
g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}
are divisible by
w_{3}
(this is straightforward from (
2.3
)). Therefore,
w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}+g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}=w_{3}\beta_{t}
for some polynomial
\beta_{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
. On the other hand,
g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}=w_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}^{(3)}=w_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}
((
2.4
), (
2.8
) and (
2.12
)). Therefore

w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t}-5}=\big(w_{2}^{2^{t-1}}+g_{2^{t}}^{(3)}+w_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}\big)g_{2^{t}-5}=w_{3}\beta_{t}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}g_{2^{t}-5}.

By collecting all these facts, from (
3.4
) we obtain:

w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}=w_{3}(g_{2^{t}-4})^{2}+w_{3}\beta_{t}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{2}g_{2^{t}-2}w_{3}(g_{2^{t-1}-4})^{2}+w_{3}^{2^{t-1}}g_{2^{t-1}-5}.

Finally, canceling out
w_{3}
and defining
\alpha_{t}:=w_{2}(g_{2^{t-1}-4})^{2}
finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
t\geqslant 4
. For
\widetilde{w}_{4}\in H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}-2,4})
we have

\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{4})=2^{t-1}-3.

Proof.

Since
g_{r}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
for all
r\geqslant 2^{t}-5
(by (
2.6
)) and
w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-1}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
(because
\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{3})=2^{t-1}-2
, by Theorem
3.1
), Lemma
3.7
proves that
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-2}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. We are left to check that
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-3}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
.

By (
3.1
) we have

\displaystyle g_{2^{t}-4}(g_{2^{t-1}-4})^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{t-3}w_{4}^{2^{i+1}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-1-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{t-2}w_{4}^{2^{i}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\bigg(w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}+g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}+\sum_{i=1}^{t-3}w_{4}^{2^{i}-1}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\cdot\bigg(w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}+\sum_{i=1}^{t-3}w_{4}^{2^{i}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}\bigg)

If
S
denotes the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{t-3}w_{4}^{2^{i}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-i}-4}^{(3)}\big)^{2^{i}}
, then

\displaystyle g_{2^{t}-4}(g_{2^{t-1}-4})^{2}=

\displaystyle\,\big(w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}+g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}+w_{4}S\big)\big(w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}+S\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-3}+w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}+g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}S+w_{4}S^{2}.

Let
p=w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}+g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}S+w_{4}S^{2}
, so that
g_{2^{t}-4}(g_{2^{t-1}-4})^{2}=w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-3}+p
. Since
g_{2^{t}-4}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
, one has
w_{4}^{2^{t-1}-3}\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
if and only if
p\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. The exponent of
w_{4}
in a monomial from
g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}S+w_{4}S^{2}
is at most
2^{t-2}-3
, and so

\operatorname{LM}(p)=w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}\cdot\operatorname{LM}\big(g_{2^{t}-4}^{(3)}\big)=w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}w_{2}^{2^{t-1}-2}.

However, for the Gröbner basis
F_{2^{t}-2}
of
I_{2^{t}-2,4}
we know that

\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}-2})=\big\{w_{2}^{2^{t-1}-2^{i}}w_{3}^{2^{i}-1}\mid 0\leqslant i\leqslant t-1\big\}\cup\big\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1},w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}\big\}

(see (
2.13
)ff), so
\operatorname{LM}(p)
is not divisible by any of the leading monomials from
F_{2^{t}-2}
, i.e.,
p
cannot be reduced modulo
F_{2^{t}-2}
. We conclude
p\notin I_{2^{t}-2,4}
, and the proof is complete.
∎

4.
Cup-length of
\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4}

The main result of
[
11
]
states that for all integers
t\geqslant 4
and
j\in\{0,1,2\}
one has

\operatorname{cl}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}-j,4})=2^{t}+2^{t-2}-4-j.

(It is not hard to see that the proof of the result for
j=0
actually goes through for
t=3
as well, so that
\operatorname{cl}(\widetilde{G}_{8,4})=6
.)

We are going to extend this result and prove that
\operatorname{cl}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})=2^{t}+2^{t-2}-4
for all
t\geqslant 3
. Actually, a part of the proof is already contained in
[
11
]
. Namely, by definition all elements of
W_{2^{t},4}
are polynomials in
\widetilde{w}_{2}
,
\widetilde{w}_{3}
and
\widetilde{w}_{4}
, and so
\operatorname{cl}(W_{2^{t},4})
is reached by some monomial
\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}
. Therefore, Lemmas 4.1 and 4.4 from
[
11
]
imply
\operatorname{cl}(W_{2^{t},4})=2^{t}+2^{t-2}-5
, and since

W_{2^{t}+1,4}\cong W_{2^{t},4}
(see (
2.10
)), we have

(4.1)

\operatorname{cl}(W_{2^{t}+1,4})=2^{t}+2^{t-2}-5.

In transition from the subalgebra
W_{2^{t}+1,4}
to the algebra
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
, a useful result is obtained in
[
10
, Theorem 6.6]
. In the case we are interested in (by the notation used in
[
10
]
, it is the case
n+4=2^{t}+1
) it states that the so-called characteristic rank of the vector bundle
\widetilde{\gamma}_{2^{t}+1,4}
is equal to
2^{t}-1
. The characteristic rank of
\widetilde{\gamma}_{2^{t}+1,4}
is defined as the maximal integer
q
such that
H^{j}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})\subseteq W_{2^{t}+1,4}
for all
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,q\}
. We conclude that for a homogeneous class
y\in H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
the following implication holds

(4.2)

y\notin W_{2^{t}+1,4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|y|\geqslant 2^{t}.

We are now ready to prove the announced result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
t\geqslant 3
. Then

\operatorname{cl}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})=2^{t}+2^{t-2}-4.

Proof.

We know that
\operatorname{ht}(\widetilde{w}_{2})=2^{t}-4
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t},3})
(see
[
3
, Theorem 1.2]
), which means that
w_{2}^{2^{t}-4}\notin I_{2^{t},3}
. By Lemma
2.6
,
w_{2}^{2^{t}-4}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}\notin I_{2^{t},4}=I_{2^{t}+1,4}
, that is,
\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t}-4}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}\neq 0
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
. The degree of this class is
2^{t+1}-8+2^{t}-4=3\cdot 2^{t}-12
, while the dimension of the manifold
\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4}
is
4\cdot 2^{t}-12
. By Poincaré duality, there is a class
y\in H^{2^{t}}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})
with
\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t}-4}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1}y\neq 0
. We conclude that
\operatorname{cl}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-2}-4
.

We are left to prove
\operatorname{cl}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})\leqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-2}-4
. Suppose to the contrary that there exists a nonzero product
x_{1}\cdots x_{s}y_{1}\cdots y_{m}
of classes of positive degree such that
s+m\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-2}-3
, where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\in W_{2^{t}+1,4}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})\setminus W_{2^{t}+1,4}
. Since
\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4}
is simply connected,
|x_{i}|\geqslant 2
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, and according to (
4.2
),
|y_{i}|\geqslant 2^{t}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Thus for the total degree
d
of the class
x_{1}\cdots x_{s}y_{1}\cdots y_{m}
we have

d\geqslant 2s+2^{t}m\geqslant 2(2^{t}+2^{t-2}-3-m)+2^{t}m=(2^{t}-2)m+2\cdot 2^{t}+2^{t-1}-6.

By (
4.1
),
s\leqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-2}-5
, and so
s+m\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-2}-3
implies
m\geqslant 2
. We conclude

d\geqslant 2\cdot(2^{t}-2)+2\cdot 2^{t}+2^{t-1}-6=4\cdot 2^{t}+2^{t-1}-10>4\cdot 2^{t}-12=\dim(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4}),

and consequently
x_{1}\cdots x_{s}y_{1}\cdots y_{m}=0
. This contradiction finishes the proof.
∎

From (
1.1
) we now get a lower bound for the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of
\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4}
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For all integers
t\geqslant 3
one has

\operatorname{cat}(\widetilde{G}_{2^{t}+1,4})\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-2}-3.

5.
Zero-divisor cup-length of
\widetilde{G}_{n,k}

This section is devoted to finding lower bounds for topological complexity of Grassmannians
\widetilde{G}_{n,4}
by studying zero-divisor cup-length of
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
and its subalgebra
W_{n,4}
.

Let us first note that for any
k\geqslant 2
the sequence
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k})
increases with
n
:

(5.1)

\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k})\leqslant\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n+1,k}).

This was proved in
[
4
, Lemma 3.3]
for
k=3
, but virtually the same proof goes through for arbitrary
k
(the essence of the proof is the fact
I_{n,k}\supseteq I_{n+1,k}
).

We now establish a connection between
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k})
and
\operatorname{zcl}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
. Note first that, since we are working over a field,
W_{n,k}\subset H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
implies
W_{n,k}\otimes W_{n,k}\subset H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
. This means that
every zero-divisor in
W_{n,k}\otimes W_{n,k}
is a zero-divisor in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
as well (see the following diagram).

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{n,k}\otimes W_{n,k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{n,k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot}

A consequence of this remark is the inequality
\operatorname{zcl}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\geqslant\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k})
. However, we can prove more than that.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
n\geqslant 2k\geqslant 4
. Then

\operatorname{zcl}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\geqslant 1+\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k}).

Proof.

Let
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{s}\in W_{n,k}\otimes W_{n,k}
be homogeneous zero-divisors of positive degree such that
z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{s}\neq 0
and
s=\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k})
, i.e.,
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,k})
is reached by the product
z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{s}
, which we denote by
z
. Let
m
be the degree of
z
(i.e., the sum of degrees of
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{s}
). So
z
is an element of degree
m
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
, which means that

z\in\bigoplus_{i=0}^{m}H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{m-i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k}).

Let us write
z
in the form
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\alpha_{i}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{m-i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
. The dimension of the manifold
\widetilde{G}_{n,k}
is
k(n-k)
, and so
H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})=0
for
i>k(n-k)
. Furthermore, note that
\alpha_{i}
in fact belongs to
\big(H^{i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\cap W_{n,k}\big)\otimes\big(H^{m-i}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\cap W_{n,k}\big)
(because
z\in W_{n,k}\otimes W_{n,k}
), and it was proved in
[
8
, p. 1171]
that
H^{k(n-k)}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\cap W_{n,k}=0
. Therefore, the following implication holds:

(5.2)

i\geqslant k(n-k)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha_{i}=0.

Since
z\neq 0
there exists
j\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
such that
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
((
5.2
) implies
j<k(n-k)
).

Next, let
\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{r}\}
be a vector space basis for
H^{j}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
. We can now write
\alpha_{j}
in the form
\sum_{l=1}^{r}b_{l}\otimes v_{l}
, for some
v_{l}\in H^{m-j}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
. Since
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
, there exists
l_{0}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
with the property
b_{l_{0}}\otimes v_{l_{0}}\neq 0
. If we define a map
\varphi:H^{j}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\rightarrow H^{k(n-k)}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
on the basis elements by

\varphi(b_{l})=\begin{cases}c,&l=l_{0}\\
0,&l\neq l_{0}\end{cases},

where
c\in H^{k(n-k)}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
is the generator, then Poincaré duality gives us a class
a\in H^{k(n-k)-j}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
such that
\varphi
is the multiplication with
a
. So for
1\leqslant l\leqslant r
, we have
ab_{l}\neq 0
if and only if
l=l_{0}
.

We now claim that
z(a)\cdot z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{s}=(1\otimes a+a\otimes 1)\cdot z\neq 0
in
H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
, which will prove the proposition. The degree of this element is
k(n-k)-j+m
, and we have

(1\otimes a+a\otimes 1)\cdot z=(1\otimes a+a\otimes 1)\sum_{i=0}^{m}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(1\otimes a)\alpha_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m}(a\otimes 1)\alpha_{i}.

To verify that this is nonzero it will be enough to prove that the summand in
H^{k(n-k)}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})\otimes H^{m-j}(\widetilde{G}_{n,k})
is nonzero. However, the said summand is equal to
(a\otimes 1)\alpha_{j}
(if
m\geqslant k(n-k)
, then this summand contains
(1\otimes a)\alpha_{k(n-k)}
as well, but
\alpha_{k(n-k)}=0
due to (
5.2
)), and we have

(a\otimes 1)\alpha_{j}=(a\otimes 1)\sum_{l=1}^{r}b_{l}\otimes v_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}ab_{l}\otimes v_{l}=ab_{l_{0}}\otimes v_{l_{0}}\neq 0

(since we are working over a field, the fact
ab_{l_{0}}\otimes v_{l_{0}}\neq 0
follows from
ab_{l_{0}}\neq 0
and
v_{l_{0}}\neq 0
).
∎

Our aim now is to find a lower bound for
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{2^{t}-2,4})
, which will also be a lower bound for
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,4})
for all
n\geqslant 2^{t}-2
(by (
5.1
)). Together with Proposition
5.1
and (
1.2
), this will produce a lower bound for
\operatorname{TC}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})
. For that purpose we now make some preparatory remarks.

We will need an algebra morphism
\psi:W_{2^{t}-1,3}\rightarrow W_{2^{t}-2,4}
(for
t\geqslant 4
) that takes Stiefel–Whitney classes to corresponding Stiefel–Whitney classes, i.e., such that
\psi(\widetilde{w}_{2})=\widetilde{w}_{2}
and
\psi(\widetilde{w}_{3})=\widetilde{w}_{3}
. Note that if such a morphism exists, then it is unique, because
W_{2^{t}-1,3}
is generated by
\widetilde{w}_{2}
and
\widetilde{w}_{3}
. Here is how it can be constructed.
The kernel of the composition of the inclusion
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
with the natural projection
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]/I_{2^{t}-2,4}
is
I_{2^{t}-2,4}\cap\mathbb{F}[w_{2},w_{3}]
. By (
2.11
), Lemma
2.5
and (
2.7
),
I_{2^{t}-1,3}=I_{2^{t},3}=I_{2^{t},4}\cap\mathbb{F}[w_{2},w_{3}]\subseteq I_{2^{t}-2,4}\cap\mathbb{F}[w_{2},w_{3}]
, and so this composition induces the algebra morphism
\Psi
presented in the following diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3}]/I_{2^{t}-1,3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]/I_{2^{t}-2,4}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi}

Having in mind the isomorphism (
2.1
) we obtain the morphism
\psi:W_{2^{t}-1,3}\rightarrow W_{2^{t}-2,4}
with the desired property.

Now let
B
be the vector space basis for
W_{2^{t}-1,3}
obtained from the Gröbner basis
F
for
I_{2^{t}-1,3}=I_{2^{t},3}
using Theorem
2.4
and the isomorphism (
2.1
). So
B
consists of the cohomology classes
\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}\in W_{2^{t}-1,3}
such that the corresponding monomial
w_{2}^{b}w_{3}^{c}
is not divisible by any of the monomials from
\operatorname{LM}(F)
. Let
B_{2^{t}-2}
be the vector space basis for
W_{2^{t}-2,4}
obtained in the same way from the Gröbner basis
F_{2^{t}-2}
for
I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. Since
\operatorname{LM}(F_{2^{t}-2})=\operatorname{LM}(F)\cup\big\{w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1},w_{3}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}\big\}
and
\psi(\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c})=\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}
, we see that for any
\sigma\in B
and any
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-3\}
one has

(5.3)

\psi(\sigma)\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\in B_{2^{t}-2}.

Moreover, for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in B
and
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{t-2}-3\}
,

(5.4)

(\sigma_{1},d_{1})\neq(\sigma_{2},d_{2})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\psi(\sigma_{1})\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d_{1}}\neq\psi(\sigma_{2})\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d_{2}}.

In the next lemma we prove the crucial fact for obtaining the announced lower bound for
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{2^{t}-2,4})
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
t\geqslant 5
be an integer and
z\in W_{2^{t}-1,3}\otimes W_{2^{t}-1,3}
an arbitrary element. If
z\neq 0
in
W_{2^{t}-1,3}\otimes W_{2^{t}-1,3}
, then

(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)\cdot\sum_{d=2}^{2^{t-2}-3}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}\neq 0\quad\mbox{ in }W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}.

Proof.

Since
B
is a basis for
W_{2^{t}-1,3}
, the set
\{\sigma\otimes\tau\mid\sigma,\tau\in B\}
is a basis for
W_{2^{t}-1,3}\otimes W_{2^{t}-1,3}
. Similarly, simple tensors of elements from
B_{2^{t}-2}
constitute a basis for
W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
. Write
z
as a nonempty sum
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}\otimes\tau_{i}
of distinct basis elements. Then

\displaystyle(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)\cdot\sum_{d=2}^{2^{t-2}-3}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\psi(\sigma_{i})\otimes\psi(\tau_{i})\cdot\sum_{d=2}^{2^{t-2}-3}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{d=2}^{2^{t-2}-3}\psi(\sigma_{i})\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\psi(\tau_{i})\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d},

and according to (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), this is a nonempty sum of distinct basis elements in
W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
.
∎

Let us now indicate the idea of constructing a ”long” nontrivial product of zero-divisors in
W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
(i.e., obtaining a lower bound for
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{2^{t}-2,4})
). We will take
z\in W_{2^{t}-1,3}\otimes W_{2^{t}-1,3}
to be a product of zero-divisors that realizes
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{2^{t}-1,3})
(known from
[
4
]
), then we will note that
(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)
is the product of corresponding zero-divisors in
W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
, multiply it with
z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{2^{t-2}-1}
, and prove that the obtained product is nonzero. The zero-divisor
z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{2^{t-2}-1}
is equal to the sum
\sum_{d=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}
, and after applying Lemma
5.2
, to prove the claim it will suffice to verify that for
d\in\{0,1,2^{t-2}-2,2^{t-2}-1\}
one has
(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)\cdot(\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d})=0
. The identities from the following lemma will play the crucial role in that verification.

Lemma 5.3
.

For all integers
t\geqslant 4
the following equalities hold in
W_{2^{t}-2,4}
:

(a)

\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t-2}}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}=0
,

(b)

\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=0
,

(c)

\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=0
.

Proof.

(a) We want to prove that
w_{2}^{2^{t-2}}w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
. By
[
3
, Proposition 2.2(c)]
and (
2.6
),
w_{2}^{2^{t-2}}w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}=g_{2^{t}+2^{t-2}-3}^{(3)}\in I_{2^{t},3}
, and Lemma
2.5
, along with (
2.7
), implies
w_{2}^{2^{t-2}}w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}\in I_{2^{t},4}\subseteq I_{2^{t}-2,4}
.

(b) Since
g_{r}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
for all
r\geqslant 2^{t}-5
, the identity
\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=0
(i.e.,
w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}\in I_{2^{t}-2,4}
) is a consequence of the following relation in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
:

(5.5)

w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-2}g_{2^{t}-5}+\sum_{i=2}^{t-2}w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-2^{i}}w_{4}^{2^{i-1}-2}g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}}.

This relation actually holds for
t=3
as well (as usual, the empty sum is understood to be zero), and we are going to prove it by induction on
t\geqslant 3
.

For
t=3
, (
5.5
) simplifies to
w_{3}=g_{3}
, which is obviously true (see (
2.3
)).

Now let
t\geqslant 4
, and assume that the claim holds for
t-1
, that is

w_{3}^{2^{t-3}-1}w_{4}^{2^{t-3}-2}=w_{3}^{2^{t-3}-2}g_{2^{t-1}-5}+\sum_{i=2}^{t-3}w_{3}^{2^{t-3}-2^{i}}w_{4}^{2^{i-1}-2}g_{2^{t-1}-3+2^{i}}.

Squaring both sides and multiplying them by
w_{3}w_{4}^{2}
yields

w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-3}w_{4}^{2}\big(g_{2^{t-1}-5}\big)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{t-3}w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-2^{i+1}+1}w_{4}^{2^{i}-2}\big(g_{2^{t-1}-3+2^{i}}\big)^{2}.

By (
3.2
),
w_{3}(g_{2^{t-1}-5})^{2}=g_{2^{t}-7}
and
w_{3}(g_{2^{t-1}-3+2^{i}})^{2}=g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i+1}}
, so after applying this, and shifting the summation index, we get

(5.6)

w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-1}w_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-4}w_{4}^{2}g_{2^{t}-7}+\sum_{i=3}^{t-2}w_{3}^{2^{t-2}-2^{i}}w_{4}^{2^{i-1}-2}g_{2^{t}-3+2^{i}.}

Finally, by equation (
2.5
) for
k=4
,
r=2^{t}+1
and
s=1
, we have

g_{2^{t}+1}=w_{2}^{2}g_{2^{t}-3}+w_{3}^{2}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{4}^{2}g_{2^{t}-7}=w_{3}^{2}g_{2^{t}-5}+w_{4}^{2}g_{2^{t}-7}.

Therefore, we can substitute
w_{3}^{2}g_{2^{t}-5}+g_{2^{t}+1}
for
w_{4}^{2}g_{2^{t}-7}
in (
5.6
), and after doing so, we get (
5.5
). This completes the induction step.

(c) It was proved in
[
4
, Corollary 3.8]
that

w_{2}^{2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{t-3}w_{2}^{2^{i}+2^{i-1}}w_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2^{i}}\in I_{2^{t}+2^{t-2}+2,3}.

On the other hand, we know that
I_{2^{t}+2^{t-2}+2,3}\subseteq I_{2^{t},3}\subseteq I_{2^{t},4}\subseteq I_{2^{t}-2,4}
(by (
2.7
) and Lemma
2.5
). Therefore, in
W_{2^{t}-2,4}
we have
\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{t-3}\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{i}+2^{i-1}}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2^{i}}
, which means that

\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=\sum_{i=1}^{t-3}\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{i}+2^{i-1}}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-1}-2^{i}}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-2}=0,

by part (b), since
2^{t-1}-2^{i}\geqslant 2^{t-1}-2^{t-3}>2^{t-2}-1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,t-3\}
.
∎

We are finally ready to establish lower bounds for
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,4})
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For all integers
t\geqslant 5
and
n\geqslant 2^{t}-2
one has

\operatorname{zcl}(W_{n,4})\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}-5.

Proof.

We have already outlined the idea of the proof prior to Lemma
5.3
. First of all, according to (
5.1
), it is enough to prove
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{2^{t}-2,4})\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}-5
. This inequality will follow from the fact
z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{2^{t}-1}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{2^{t-1}-3}z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{2^{t-2}-1}\neq 0
in
W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
, which we now prove.

Let
z:=z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{2^{t}-1}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{2^{t-1}-3}\in W_{2^{t}-1,3}\otimes W_{2^{t}-1,3}
. It was proved in
[
4
, Proposition 3.9 and Lemma 3.4]
that
z\neq 0
. We have constructed the algebra morphism
\psi:W_{2^{t}-1,3}\rightarrow W_{2^{t}-2,4}
with the properties
\psi(\widetilde{w}_{2})=\widetilde{w}_{2}
and
\psi(\widetilde{w}_{3})=\widetilde{w}_{3}
. Therefore,

(\psi\otimes\psi)\big(z(\widetilde{w}_{2})\big)=(\psi\otimes\psi)\big(1\otimes\widetilde{w}_{2}+\widetilde{w}_{2}\otimes 1\big)=1\otimes\widetilde{w}_{2}+\widetilde{w}_{2}\otimes 1=z(\widetilde{w}_{2}),

and similarly,
(\psi\otimes\psi)\big(z(\widetilde{w}_{3})\big)=z(\widetilde{w}_{3})
. The tensor product
\psi\otimes\psi:W_{2^{t}-1,3}\otimes W_{2^{t}-1,3}\rightarrow W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
is also an algebra morphism, and so

(5.7)

(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)=z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{2^{t}-1}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{2^{t-1}-3}.

On the other, by binomial formula the zero-divisor
z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{2^{t-2}-1}
is equal to

(1\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}+\widetilde{w}_{4}\otimes 1)^{2^{t-2}-1}=\sum_{d=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}\binom{2^{t-2}-1}{d}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}=\sum_{d=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}

(since
\binom{2^{t-2}-1}{d}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
if
0\leqslant d\leqslant 2^{t-2}-1
), and therefore,

z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{2^{t}-1}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{2^{t-1}-3}z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{2^{t-2}-1}=(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)\cdot\sum_{d=0}^{2^{t-2}-1}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}.

Since
z\neq 0
, by Lemma
5.2
we know that

(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)\cdot\sum_{d=2}^{2^{t-2}-3}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}\neq 0,

and so it is now sufficient to verify that for
d\in\{0,1,2^{t-2}-2,2^{t-2}-1\}
the element
x_{d}:=(\psi\otimes\psi)(z)\cdot(\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d})\in W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
vanishes.

Using (
5.7
) we calculate:

\displaystyle x_{d}

\displaystyle=z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{2^{t}-1}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{2^{t-1}-3}\big(\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{2^{t}-1}\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t}-1-b}\cdot\sum_{c=0}^{2^{t-1}-3}\binom{2^{t-1}-3}{c}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-1}-3-c}\cdot\big(\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{2^{t}-1}\sum_{c=0}^{2^{t-1}-3}\binom{2^{t-1}-3}{c}\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t}-1-b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-1}-3-c}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}.

For
0\leqslant d\leqslant 1
let us show that in fact every summand of
x_{d}
is equal to zero. Pick an arbitrary summand
s_{d}(b,c)=\widetilde{w}_{2}^{b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{c}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{d}\otimes\widetilde{w}_{2}^{2^{t}-1-b}\widetilde{w}_{3}^{2^{t-1}-3-c}\widetilde{w}_{4}^{2^{t-2}-1-d}
. If
b\geqslant 2^{t-2}
and
c\geqslant 2^{t-2}-1
, then the first coordinate of
s_{d}(b,c)
is zero by Lemma
5.3
(a). Otherwise, if either
b\leqslant 2^{t-2}-1
or
c\leqslant 2^{t-2}-2
, then the second coordinate vanishes by parts (b) and (c) of the same lemma. We conclude
x_{d}=0
.

It is not hard to check that
x_{2^{t-2}-1-d}=T(x_{d})
, where
T:W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}\rightarrow W_{2^{t}-2,4}\otimes W_{2^{t}-2,4}
interchanges the coordinates (it is defined on simple tensors by
T(\sigma\otimes\tau)=\tau\otimes\sigma
). Therefore,
x_{d}=0
holds for
2^{t-2}-2\leqslant d\leqslant 2^{t-2}-1
as well. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

We believe that the inequality from the previous proposition is an equality for
n\in\{2^{t}-2,2^{t}-1,2^{t},2^{t}+1\}
. Computer calculations confirmed our assumption for
5\leqslant t\leqslant 20
.

Remark 5.6
.

The proposition does not hold for
t=4
. It is readily seen from its proof that the zero-divisor
z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{2^{t}-1}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{2^{t-1}-3}z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{2^{t-2}-1}=z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{15}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{5}z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{3}
vanishes in
W_{14,4}\otimes W_{14,4}
. Furthermore, it can be proved that
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{14,4})=21
.

However,
z(\widetilde{w}_{2})^{15}z(\widetilde{w}_{3})^{5}z(\widetilde{w}_{4})^{3}\neq 0
in
W_{15,4}\otimes W_{15,4}
, so Proposition
5.4
does hold for
t=4
and
n\geqslant 15
. Moreover,
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{15,4})=\operatorname{zcl}(W_{16,4})=\operatorname{zcl}(W_{17,4})=23
.

Let us also remark that one can show that
\operatorname{zcl}(W_{8,4})=\operatorname{zcl}(W_{9,4})=8
.

We have checked all these listed results by using computer software SageMath
[
12
]
.

5.1
we immediately get the following corollary.

Corollary 5.7

For all integers
t\geqslant 5
and
n\geqslant 2^{t}-2
one has

\operatorname{zcl}(\widetilde{G}_{n,4})\geqslant 2^{t}+2^{t-1}+2^{t-2}-4.

1.2

This theorem covers the cases
