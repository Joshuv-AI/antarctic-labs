Hormone-metabolism crosstalk in plants: an integrated signaling network coordinating growth and stress adaptation
Authors: Ghulam Murtaza, Zeeshan Ahmed, Sajid Ullah, Mohammed S Alotaibi, Sajad Ali, Rashid Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant growth, stress adaptation, and productivity depend on the continuous exchange of information between hormone signaling and metabolic networks. Although major phytohormones have been widely studied through their biosynthesis, receptors, and downstream transcriptional modules, these pathways are still often presented as linear systems. This view does not fully explain how plants integrate carbon status, nutrient availability, redox balance, cellular energy, and developmental signals under changing environments. In this review, we present plant hormone signaling and metabolism as a reciprocal regulatory network in which hormones reshape metabolic flux, while metabolites feedback to control hormone biosynthesis, transport, perception, degradation, and signal output. We synthesized evidence across salicylic acid, gibberellins, auxin, abscisic acid, strigolactones, ethylene, jasmonates, cytokinins, brassinosteroids, and melatonin, showing that sugars, amino acids, organic acids, lipid derivatives, sulfur metabolites, and redox signals act as shared regulatory nodes. This integrated perspective reveals that core metabolic signals such as sucrose, trehalose-6-phosphate, 2-oxoglutarate, citrate, malate, reactive oxygen species, glutathione, ascorbate, cysteine, and tryptophan coordinate multiple hormone pathways across tissues, developmental stages, and stress contexts. We further highlight emerging tools, including hormone atlases, live biosensors, spatial omics, single-cell omics, isotope tracing, genome editing, and computational network modeling, as essential approaches for moving from descriptive pathway maps to predictive systems biology. By defining hormone metabolism crosstalk as a central principle in plant biochemistry and physiology, this review provides a conceptual framework for identifying engineering targets that can improve crop resilience, nutrient use efficiency, and growth stress balance under future agricultural conditions.Keywords: hormone signaling, physiology, biochemistry, botany, pathways

Introduction

Plants constantly adjust their growth, development, and defense strategies in response to changing internal and external conditions. 1 , 2 This adjustment depends on close coordination between hormone signaling and metabolic regulation. 3 , 4 Plant hormones such as salicylic acid, gibberellins, auxin, abscisic acid, strigolactones, ethylene, jasmonates, cytokinins, brassinosteroids, and melatonin regulate almost every major physiological process, including seed germination, root and shoot architecture, flowering, fruit development, senescence, immunity, and abiotic stress tolerance. 5 , 6 Traditionally, these hormones have been studied as separate signaling pathways with defined biosynthetic routes, receptors, transcriptional regulators, and downstream responses. However, recent evidence has shown that hormone signaling rarely acts alone. Instead, each hormonal pathway is closely connected with cellular metabolism, and this connection shapes how plants allocate carbon, nitrogen, energy, and defense resources. 7 , 8

Metabolism provides the biochemical foundation for hormone action. Primary metabolites such as sugars, amino acids, organic acids, lipids, and redox molecules act as substrates, energy sources, and regulatory signals. 9 , 10 Secondary metabolites such as flavonoids, glucosinolates, phenolics, terpenoids, and alkaloids further connect hormone responses with defense, stress adaptation, and developmental control. 11 , 12 At the same time, hormones directly reshape metabolic flux by regulating enzymes, transporters, transcription factors, kinases, and protein stability. 13 , 14 This creates a reciprocal control system in which hormones regulate metabolism, while metabolites feedback to regulate hormone biosynthesis, perception, signal transduction, transport, and degradation. Such reciprocal regulation is now emerging as a central principle in plant biochemistry and physiology. 15

A growing body of research supports this concept across different hormone classes. Salicylic acid signaling interacts with redox metabolism, ascorbate, sugars, vitamin B6, monoterpenoids, and N-hydroxypipecolic acid during plant immunity and systemic resistance. Gibberellin pathways are connected with sucrose transport, 2-oxoglutarate availability, tricarboxylic acid cycle activity, the TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN signaling, and source-sink regulation. Auxin biosynthesis and signaling depend strongly on tryptophan metabolism, sugar availability, glycolysis, and PIN-FORMED-mediated transport. 16 Abscisic acid coordinates drought and seed maturation responses through organic acids, amino acids, cysteine, sulfate, proline, SnRK kinases, and energy signaling. Strigolactones integrate nutrient status, sucrose, trehalose 6-phosphate, citrate, glucosinolates, and shoot branching regulation. Ethylene links methionine metabolism, sucrose feedback, glucose sensing, reactive oxygen species, flavonoids, and hypoxia responses. 17 , 18 Jasmonates connect lipid metabolism with tryptophan, serine, glucosinolates, starch, sucrose, phosphate, potassium, and light signaling. Cytokinins interact with sulfur metabolism, glutathione, trehalose 6-phosphate, and hydrogen sulfide. Brassinosteroids coordinate sterol signaling with glucose, TOR, ascorbate, hydrogen peroxide, flavonoids, glucosinolates, cysteine, and glutathione. Melatonin further expands this network by linking tryptophan-derived metabolism with calcium signaling, reactive oxygen species, nitrogen metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, antioxidant defense, and hormonal crosstalk. 19 , 20

Despite these advances, the current knowledge remains fragmented. Most studies still describe individual hormone pathways or isolated metabolic responses. This limits our ability to explain how plants integrate multiple hormonal and metabolic signals at the same time. Linear pathway models are useful for describing core mechanisms, but they cannot fully explain dynamic feedback, tissue specificity, organelle-level regulation, environmental plasticity, or growth defense tradeoffs. Plants operate through networks rather than isolated chains of reactions. Therefore, the field needs a stronger systems-level framework that can connect hormone atlases, biosensors, spatial omics, single-cell omics, isotope tracing, genetic perturbation, and computational modeling. This review addresses this gap by synthesizing current knowledge on reciprocal regulation between plant hormone signaling and metabolic networks. It brings together evidence from major hormone pathways and examines how each hormone controls metabolism and how metabolites, in turn, regulate hormone signaling. The review also highlights emerging tools that can help move the field from descriptive pathway biology towards predictive network biology. This perspective is important because understanding hormone metabolism networks can reveal new strategies for improving crop productivity, stress resilience, nutrient use efficiency, and developmental stability under changing environments.

The major objectives of this review are to provide an integrated overview of hormone biosynthesis and signaling routes in relation to plant metabolism, to explain how primary and secondary metabolites regulate hormone responses across different physiological contexts, to identify common metabolic signals that coordinate multiple hormone pathways, to evaluate emerging technologies for mapping hormone metabolite interactions with spatial and temporal resolution, and to define future directions for predictive modeling and crop engineering. This review is guided by four major questions. First, how can bidirectional hormone metabolism networks be quantitatively modeled to move from linear pathways toward predictive systems-level frameworks? Second, what are the core metabolic signals that coordinate multiple hormone pathways, and how does this feedback vary across cell types, tissues, developmental stages, and stress conditions? Third, which combinations of hormone atlases, real-time biosensors, spatial omics, single-cell omics, and isotope tracing are most effective for identifying causal hormone metabolite interactions? Fourth, can integrated network models identify engineering targets that improve growth stress balance, nutrient use efficiency, and crop resilience without causing major yield penalties? By addressing these questions, this review presents hormone metabolism crosstalk as a unifying framework for plant biochemistry and physiology. It emphasizes that the next stage of hormone research should not only define individual receptors, enzymes, or transcription factors but also explain how these components operate within metabolic networks. Such a framework can help transform our understanding of plant adaptation and support the design of crops with improved productivity and stability in stressful environments.

Salicylic acid status

Mechanistic insights into salicylic acid production and signal transduction: overview

Salicylic acid was first identified about two hundred years ago and has since been recognized as a phytohormone essential for plant defense. 21 Salicylic acid is crucial in pathogen-mediated immunity, effector-associated immunity, and systemically developed immunity. Salicylic acid biosynthesis primarily transpires through the isochorismate pathway, which involves AVRPPHB SUSCEPTIBLE-3 and ISOCHORISMATE SYNTHASE, also referred to as GH3. This process promotes spontaneous degradation of ISC-9 to salicylic acid (Bhar et al., 2026, Figure 1). The two reactions are linked via the MATE transporter to improve disease sensitivity, which facilitates the transfer of the salicylic acid precursor iso-chorismate from the chloroplast to the cytoplasm, thus permitting the synthesis of salicylic acid. 22 , 23 Salicylic acid signaling is essential in the plant immune response. 24 Likewise, adequate accumulation and recognition of salicylic acid in crops are crucial for effector-triggered immunity. Transgenic plants harboring the microbe salicylate hydroxylase-based gene NahG, which is responsible for the degradation of salicylic acid, exhibited significantly worse crop immunity. 25 , 26 Systemic acquired resistance perception transpires via the NPR1 receptor in conjunction with the respective paralogs NPR3 and NPR4. Impaired salicylic acid sensing in the absence-of-function npr1 and npr4 mutants significantly diminishes the activation of defense-associated genes, resulting in markedly enhanced proliferation of bacterial strains. 27 , 28

Signaling mechanism and production of salicylic acid, as well as its interactions with aerobic and basal metabolism. The metabolic pathway of salicylic acid, depicted in this picture, begins with chorismate and ends with ISOCHORISMATE SYNTHASE. The immediate precursor of salicylic acid, isochorismate-9-glutamate, which spontaneously decays to salicylic acid, is formed when AVRPPHB SUSCEPTIBLE 3 converts isochorismate to isochorismate-9-glutamate. Salicylic acid perception and signaling are regulated downstream by NPR3 and NPR4, with mediation by the NONEXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED receptor (NPR1). The enzymatic activity of salicylate hydroxylase (NahG) can cause salicylic acid breakdown. Systemic acquired resistance is aided by N-hydroxypipecolic acid. The manufacture or modification of important metabolites like sugars, vitamin B6, and monoterpenoids, as well as redox regulation through ROS and sugar signaling through TOR, are all impacted by salicylic acid signaling.

Impact of salicylic acid on metabolic processes

Historically regarded as a signaling molecule in crop defense, current research has demonstrated that salicylic acid also exerts direct impacts on metabolic processes. Recent research on tobacco, a significant crop in the industrial aspect, showed that salicylic acid application led to higher concentrations of soluble carbohydrates, ascorbate, and soluble proteins 29 (Figure 1). A further enlightening investigation in Arabidopsis showed that transcriptome alterations in response to oxidative damage encompassed genes producing certain glucosyltransferases implicated in the glycosylation of salicylic acid. 30 , 31 Furthermore, salicylic acid induces cell proliferation in the quiescent center of Arabidopsis by enhancing the buildup of ROS and downregulating the expression of WUSCHEL-associated HOMEOBOX 5, PLETHORA2, and PLETHORA. 32 Salicylic acid has been demonstrated to orchestrate the onset of senescence in Populus tremula, with its concentration adjusted in response to fluctuations in B6 homeostasis. 33 (Figure 1) Recent research has indicated that the methyl esterase family transforms methyl salicylate into salicylic acid in ripened tomato fruits, enhancing their palatability. 34 Methyl salicylate has recently been identified as a significant airborne defense chemical in crops. 35 , 36

Metabolic reprogramming of salicylic acid signaling networks

Salicylic acid has long been acknowledged as crucial for plant defense through systemic acquired resistance. Initial research indicated that inhibiting salicylic acid accumulation in transgenic Arabidopsis and tobacco expressing NahG led to impaired systemic acquired resistance, implying that salicylic acid is a crucial signaling molecule in systemic acquired resistance. 37 , 38 Subsequent research validated this discovery by utilizing salicylic acid- insufficient mutants. Notwithstanding its significance for systemic acquired resistance, salicylic acid does not seem to function as a long-range signal for systemic acquired resistance, as demonstrated by grafting tests in tobacco. 39 , 40 N-hydroxypipecolic acid is now known as a prominent option for systemic acquired resistance mobile signal. N-hydroxypipecolic acid levels significantly rise after pathogen exposure, and Arabidopsis mutants lacking N-hydroxypipecolic acid production cannot initiate systemic acquired resistance responses. 41 In summary, N-hydroxypipecolic acid operates upstream of salicylic acid to systematically enhance immune reactions, serving as a long-range signal in systemic acquired resistance, 42 (Figure 1).

Recent research suggests that the metabolic state can influence the production and perception of salicylic acid. Previously examined how ascorbate production suppresses NPR1-salicylic acid-mediated signaling and the critical function of DEHYDROASCORBATE REDUCTASES in initiating the salicylic acid pathway during oxidative stress environments. 43 , 44 Concurrently, redox-stimulated metabolites such as tryptophan-mediated glucosinolates and hydrogen peroxide have been demonstrated to influence salicylic acid stability and perception. 45 , 46 In conclusion, in conjunction with prior research by Banoriya et al. 25 these interplays emphasize that the ascorbate metabolic pathway bidirectionally modulates salicylic acid perception and biosynthesis, highlighting the association of the primary metabolic pathway with salicylic acid signaling (Figure 1).

Emerging frontiers in tools and technologies

Recent advancements in biosensor technology and analytical chemistry are providing new insights into salicylic acid biology. Methyl esterases identification that transform methyl salicylate into salicylic acid in ripened fruits 47 underscores potential applications for microbial engineering aimed at enhancing flavor or resistance qualities. Isotopic labelling, in conjunction with metabolomics analysis, has commenced elucidating the flow dynamics of salicylic acid precursors and products. Genetic methods, such as the NahG transgenic line, are essential for analyzing salicylic acid-deficient phenotypes; however, newer tools, including N-hydroxypipecolic acid-pathway mutants, provide greater specificity in clarifying systemic acquired resistance signaling.

Gibberellins profile

Molecular framework of gibberellin synthesis and signaling overview

Gibberellins, phytohormones belonging to the tetracyclic diterpenoid class, are critical regulators of crop growth, overseeing key functions that include seed germination, leaf expansion, stem elongation, pollen maturity, and flower formation, seeds, and fruits. 48 , 49 Gibberellins play a key role in regulating plant growth, development, and stress adaptation. In rice, Gibberellins have been reported to enhance heterosis during the seedling stage by promoting vigorous early growth, improving biomass accumulation, and supporting developmental performance in hybrid plants. 50 , 51 Beyond growth regulation, Gibberellins also contribute to plant responses under challenging environments. It interacts with several hormonal and molecular pathways that control defense, growth adjustment, and stress recovery. Previous studies have shown that Gibberellin signaling influences plant tolerance to both biotic and abiotic stresses by regulating gene expression, protein turnover, and adaptive physiological responses. 52 , 53 Thus, Gibberellin acts as an important growth regulator and stress response modulator in plants. Furthermore, Gibberellin was essential in the ecological revolution, leading to the extensive cultivation of cereals and serving as the natural determinant between dwarf and tall coconut trees. Thus, the modulation of Gibberellin concentrations is a commonly employed agricultural tactic to enhance crop output. 54 Gibberellin biosynthesis comprises a three-stage mechanism: (a) entkaurene biosynthesis from GGDP in plastids; (b) entkaurene conversion to GA12 by CYP enzymes in the endoplasmic reticulum; and (c) GA12 cytosolic transformation into various Gibberellins via 2-ODDs (Figure 2). Gibberellin signaling pathway commences when bioactive Gibberellin attaches to the GIBBERELLIN GID1 receptor, which was initially recognized as a Gibberellin receptor in plants. 55 , 56 Upon Gibberellin binding, GID1 experiences a conformational alteration that enhances its contact with DELLA-based proteins, which serve as principal repressors of Gibberellin signaling (Figure 2). This connection initiates the binding and eventual proteasomal destruction of DELLA-based proteins, thus alleviating their inhibitory influence on Gibberellin-regulated downstream gene transcription.

Gibberellin biosynthesis and signaling pathway, along with its interaction with secondary and primary metabolism.

Gibberellin-driven metabolic reprogramming

Although gibberellin signaling pathways and biosynthesis are thoroughly characterized, our comprehension of the interaction between gibberellin signaling and metabolic processes is still limited. The interaction between gibberellin signaling and major metabolic enzymes of the TCA cycle was examined by Hirano et al. 57 . Gibberellin suppresses mitochondrial PDH activity via upregulating PDH KINASE-based gene, hence reducing the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Figure 2). Three recent investigations elucidated the impact of gibberellic acid on the metabolic processes of plants (Figure 2). In the initial study, Takehara and Ueguchi-Tanaka 58 investigated the biochemical pathways of fruit set by network evaluation of integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and enzymatic activity data. The findings indicated fruit set entails central C metabolism activation, characterized by elevated hexose phosphates, hexoses, and metabolite concentrations, encompassing derivatives and intermediates of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, glycolysis, and amino acids and organic acids. Furthermore, fruit set necessitates, at a minimum in part, fructokinase activity, which may extract fructose from the vacuole, thereby supplying the subsequent pathways. These data collectively suggest that Gibberellin cascades augment sink abilities via upregulating the functions of major metabolic enzymes at post-transcriptional and transcriptional stages. This results in enhanced sucrose absorption and C transfer to generate the biomass components and energy crucial for accelerated ovary development during the onset of fruit set. 59 , 60 The additional two studies demonstrated that reciprocal grafting can reinstate Gibberellin phenotypes through primary metabolite translocation, 61 , 62 and the resulting metabolic alterations between genotypes, either abundant or lacking in Gibberellin, indicate the involvement of such hormones in initial fruit formation. 63 Another domain of metabolic chemistry in which Gibberellin function has been recognized is pigment metabolic activity in red amaranth and citrus species, where it collaborates with the fluorescent signaling protein ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (Figure 2) to coordinate both pigmentation and internode elongation. 64 Likewise, Gibberellin has been demonstrated to elevate cellulose synthesis levels in plants, aligning with its function in promoting stem elongation, and similarly regulating lignin synthesis to influence seed rupture. 65 , 66 Recent findings indicate that energy receptor TOR is induced by Gibberellin treatment (Figure 2), whereas TOR-silenced crops exhibit reduced susceptibility to Gibberellin, 67 suggesting that Gibberellin signaling necessitates normal TOR function.

Metabolites as dynamic modulators of gibberellin signaling

Findings from compelling research on Arabidopsis indicated that certain SWEET family proteins could transport both Gibberellin and sucrose; however, only translocation of sucrose reinstated male fertility in sweet13;14 mutants of Arabidopsis. This implies that Gibberellin supplementation ameliorates the phenotype through an indirect mechanism that enhances sucrose availability. 68 Recently published research on apple demonstrated that its C storage substance, sorbitol, regulates growth tactics through Gibberellin signaling, 69 indicating the feedback loop where carbohydrate levels modulate hormonal regulation. A recent study on tomatoes indicated that the transport of sucrose through SUT4 has a role in flowering regulation by influencing the expression of Gibberellin biosynthesis genes 70 (Figure 2). These data indicate that sugar accessibility and distribution actively influence Gibberellin hormonal outputs, potentially incorporating source-sink phenomena into growth modulation. Furthermore, 2-oxoglutarate, a crucial intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, serves as a medium for Gibberellin synthetic enzyme 2-ODDs, which facilitate the entry of oxygen into the substrate. Numerous investigations have shown that mitochondrial 2-ODDs and 2-OG enhance Gibberellin metabolism. These results, which are along with those mentioned in the preceding subsection, provide a reciprocal regulatory framework between Gibberellin and primary metabolism via sugar signaling, transport, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Figure 2).

Emerging frontiers in tools and concepts

Recent technical advancements are yielding high-quality insights into genetic algorithm dynamics. Growing data indicate that a specific threshold of Gibberellin buildup is essential for crop growth. 71 Genetically encoding biosensor systems, like GIBBERELLIN PERCEPTION SENSOR1, provide real-time, in vitro single-cell tracking of bioactive Gibberellin concentrations via FRET-mediated conformational alterations in the GID1-DELLA regions. 72 Recent pertinent reports detail the advancement of 2nd-generation Gibberellin biosensor GPS2, indicating that Gibberellin functions as a beneficial mediator of nodule formation in legume species, 73 whereas GA is diminished in the hypocotyl during the transition to phototropic growth. 74 The combined application of DELLA-associated fluorescence sensors has facilitated the spatial tracking of Gibberellin function and concentrations in apical meristem. 75 In the coming years, it appears probable that integrating these detectors with metabolomics research will significantly improve our comprehension of the interplay between metabolism and Gibberellin with regard to model crops.

Auxin profile

Auxin biosynthesis and signaling networks in plant regulation overview

The endogenous auxins, such as phenylacetic acid, indole-3-acetic acid, 4-chloro-indole-3-acetic acid, and indole-3-butyric acid, are present in both bound and free forms. 76 The article will concentrate exclusively on indole-3-acetic acid. Indole-3-acetic acid is produced from the amino acid tryptophan through many intermediary mechanisms, with flavin monooxygenase YUCCA serving as a crucial enzyme in the auxin synthesis route driven by indole-3-pyruvic acid 77 (Figure 3). Auxin is detected via TRANSPORT INHIBITOR RESPONSE1/AUXIN SIGNALING F-BOX nuclear receptor molecule family members, which recruit Auxin/Indole-3-Acetic Acid repressors and later stimulate ARF transcription agents 78 to modulate the expression of auxin-reactive genes (Figure 3). Recently published research on ARFs' evolutionary history identified distinct ARF emergence and delineated the principal target molecules for their functional variation, while also offering a comprehensive evolutionary trajectory that elucidates auxin signaling pathway emergence. 79 Auxin is transported directionally by PIN-FORMED transmembrane-based proteins, which serve as auxin efflux transporters. 80

Signaling and the auxin production pathway, as well as its interactions with metabolism.

Auxin-driven metabolic reprogramming

Auxin signaling initiates transcriptional pathways and modulates fundamental metabolism to facilitate cell morphogenesis and expansion. Auxin is crucial for pollen maturity in cereal crops, as it synchronously elevates the gene expression required for the transport of hexoses and sucrose into glycolysis while also augmenting pyruvate concentrations and tricarboxylic acid metabolite levels. 81 Additionally, the pollen-associated enzyme HvYUCCA4 synthesizes bioactive auxin, reinforcing the idea that grains of pollen independently generate auxin to facilitate maturation phenomena, including starch buildup. These results suggest that auxin can alter central C metabolic pathways to facilitate plant cell proliferation, implying a direct pathway for auxin's role in promoting differentiation and growth. 82

Metabolites as dynamic modulators of auxin responses

We looked at many findings indicating that sugar signaling influences auxin perception, signaling, and transport via bZIP transcription factors and TOR–PIN modulation, therefore regulating initial root growth (Ahchouch et al., 2026, Figure 3). Moreover, hexokinase reduction in crops results in auxin hyposensitivity, while sucrose accessibility is associated with auxin biosynthesis and release in pea buds. 83 Auxin is a phytohormone that modulates the TOR system (Figure 3), which governs carbon and nitrogen metabolism, encompassing functions connected to the plant cell wall. 84 Furthermore, the activity of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, facilitated by PYRUVATE DECARBOXYLASE 1, regulates the amount of PIN proteins. 85 Collectively, these investigations demonstrate a multimodal regulation system linking the transport of auxin and signaling with core metabolism via the TOR signaling pathway and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Figure 3).

Transformative tools for plant hormone and metabolic research

Modern methodological advancements in molecular and synthetic tools have enhanced our ability to analyze auxin metabolic integration, perception, and distribution. Synthetic biology methodologies, such as pathway restoration in microbes and the synthesis of asymmetric auxin sensor pairs, provide innovative strategies for manipulating auxin signaling aspects. Genetically expressed FRET-linked auxin biosensors and fluorescent auxin analogs 86 provide in vivo visualization of auxin activity, mobility, and compartmentalization at the subcellular level. Collectively, these instruments establish a comprehensive framework for connecting auxin signaling with metabolic control, yielding breakthroughs into exactly how auxin orchestrates energy equilibrium, resource distribution, and stress response in crops.

Abscisic acid status

An introduction to abscisic acid signaling and its biosynthesis

An isoprenoid is the classification that abscisic acid falls under as a metabolite. 87 It is believed that the process of abscisic acid synthesis begins with C40 carotenoid conversion via ZEAXANTHIN EPOXIDASE, which is generated by ABA1. 88 The enzyme known as NCED is responsible for zeaxanthin conversion into cis-xanthophylls, and these are subsequently broken down into C-15 xanthoxin molecules. In the subsequent stage, xanthoxin is transformed into abscisic acid through a two-step reaction that is catalyzed via a short-chain-based alcohol dehydrogenase, which is equivalent to ABA2 in plants, and an ABSCISIC ALDEHYDE OXIDASE with its Mo cofactor, which are ABA3 and AAO3 in plants. According to Ruiz-Sola et al. 89 the production of zeaxanthin occurs by β-carotene hydroxylation, which is facilitated by CYP97A3 in thale cress. Figure 4 illustrates this process. An alternative abscisic acid synthetic mechanism devoid of zeaxanthin, derived from β-carotene, has been suggested recently. 90 C15, among carotenoid degradation products, was demonstrated to influence gene expression and seed germination akin to abscisic acid through exogenous usage, especially in the ABA1 mutant and wild type; however, not in the ABA3 and ABA2 mutants. Furthermore, newly synthesized abscisic acid following the administration of isotope-tagged β-apo-11-carotenoids was identified in both the ABA1 mutant and wild type; the quantity of labeled abscisic acid was markedly diminished in the ABA2 mutant. Considering that these endogenous quantities were assessed, β-apo-11-carotenoids were suggested as alternate xanthoxin precursors (Figure 4). The fundamental elements of abscisic acid sensing and signaling were discerned in Arabidopsis. 91 The cytosolic sensor proteins, identified as RCAR, PYR1, and PYL, recognize an abscisic acid molecule and establish a link with coreceptors, specifically PP2Cs of family A. The formation of a combination of PP2Cs inactivation leads to subclass III SnRK2s activation. Consequently, their substrates, including membrane proteins and transcription factors, are stimulated or inhibited through phosphorylation (Figure 4). Recent models elucidating the activation of SnRK2s via upstream kinases in response to water deficit have been developed. 92 Nevertheless, we shall not elaborate on this due to spatial limitations.

Abscisic acid production and signaling pathways and their interaction with metabolic processes.

Abscisic acid driven metabolic reprogramming

Similar to other phytohormone substances, abscisic acid participates in numerous processes in the plant growth cycle. Its function in seed development has been extensively researched. The impact of abscisic acid deficit on cellular activities was recently examined in Arabidopsis NCED mutants. 93 Extensive data acquired through metabolite profiling, proteome, and transcriptome assessment showed that abscisic acid enhances carbon reserve accumulation and desiccation resistance while inhibiting photosynthetic processes, the cell cycle, C reserve mobilization, and oxidative activities. Conversely, abscisic acid deposits in cells due to water stress, with NCED3 significantly contributing to de novo production of abscisic acid under such environments. 94 Metabolite profiling has indicated that many main metabolites exhibited differential buildup in NCED3 mutants, with certain abnormalities, such as proline buildup (Figure 4), correlating with transcriptional alterations in genes related to metabolism. 95 These investigations elucidated the role of abscisic acid in regulating stress responses and seed maturation.

However, mutant and transgenic lines of abscisic acid core signaling components reveal growth abnormalities to a certain degree. For example, Arabidopsis that overexpressed RCAR genes demonstrated elevated leaf temperatures are correlated with stomatal phenotype responses, whereas the proliferation of various lines, especially those overexpressing PYL12 and PYL4, was minimally or insignificantly impacted. 96 To elucidate the fundamental molecular process, multiomics investigations encompassing metabolite profiling, proteomics traits, and phospho-proteomics results were conducted on RCAR10 and RCAR6 lines. 97 Notably, such RCAR lines demonstrated a persistent reduction in succinate concentrations throughout the diurnal cycle, possibly linked to modified concentrations of other tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, including malate and citrate (Figure 4). Numerous potential enzymes modulated by abscisic acid signaling have been identified among phosphorylated sites and DEPs; however, additional research is required to elucidate the relationships among the modified proteins, metabolites, and phosphorylation sites.

In addition, studies using available Arabidopsis resources for clade A PP2Cs have strengthened the link between abscisic acid signaling and primary metabolism. Comparative metabolite profiling showed that several organic acids and amino acids, including malate and succinate, accumulated in opposite patterns in abscisic acid-insensitive and abscisic acid-hypersensitive mutants. 98 These contrasting metabolic profiles indicate that PP2Cs do more than regulate canonical abscisic acid responses. They also help shape metabolic balance, especially pathways connected with carbon flow, organic acid turnover, and amino acid accumulation. Moreover, reanalysis of transcriptome data from PP2C triple mutants showed that many genes associated with metabolism and transport changed their expression, in addition to typical abscisic acid- and stress-responsive genes. 99 This suggests that abscisic acid signaling affects a broader transcriptional program that connects stress perception with metabolic adjustment. A similar metabolic connection has been observed downstream of subclass III SnRK2 kinases. The triple mutant lacking all three subclass III SnRK2s, which shows strong abscisic acid insensitivity, displayed widespread changes in primary metabolite composition. 100 Isotope labeling experiments further showed that the tricarboxylic acid cycle was altered in the snrk2 triple mutant, indicating that abscisic acid signaling through SnRK2s can influence central respiratory metabolism. Transcriptome analysis also identified several possible downstream targets, including a cytosolic fumarase that participates in the reversible conversion between malate and fumarate (Figure 5). Together, these findings show that abscisic acid signaling regulates part of the primary metabolic network through its core signaling components, including SnRK2s and PP2Cs, and links hormonal control with metabolic adaptation under stress.

Strigolactone signaling and biosynthesis pathways, along with their interactions with primary/secondary metabolism.

Metabolic reprogramming of abscisic acid signaling networks

Recent research has elucidated the relationship between the abscisic acid synthesis route and the metabolism of amino acids, specifically tryptophan and cysteine metabolism. 101 Cysteine is believed to have a role in abscisic acid synthesis, as ABA3 functions as a Mo cofactor sulfurase that utilizes cysteine as sulfur donor. 102 Cysteine and precursor sulfate notably enhanced abscisic acid production, presumably through the activation of NCED3 in stomatal cells 103 (Figure 4). Abscisic acid is a phytohormone intricately linked to sugar, as demonstrated by the identification of many abscisic acid synthesis and signaling elements using glucose-independent mutant screening. 104 Furthermore, metabolite profiling experiments have indicated that abscisic acid signaling is intricately engaged in primary metabolic activity. The SnRK1 kinases, crucial for carbon and energy signaling, were demonstrated to be adversely regulated via PP2Cs. 105 , 106 Furthermore, TOR kinase, stimulated by nutrient accessibility, was demonstrated to associate with SnRK2s and RCARs through phosphorylation. 107 Multiomics investigations indicated that the SnRK2 and SnRK1 kinases, unlike TOR, were differently phosphorylated owing to the overexpression of RCAR. 108 However, owing to the inherent difficulty in identifying transient alterations in proteomics research, our phospho-proteomic investigation failed to yield definitive data about the involvement of TOR kinase in abscisic acid signaling. Recent studies have proposed a concept wherein SnRK1 translocation from the nucleus to the cytosol under stress reduces TOR function, whereas SnRK2 and SnRK1 in the nucleus are suppressed by PP2Cs in non-stress conditions 109 (Figure 4). These data indicate a reciprocal modulation between sugar signaling and the abscisic acid signaling pathway: the RCAR-PP2C complex regulates TOR kinases and SnRK1, whereas TOR influences abscisic acid signaling through SnRK2s and RCARs.

Emerging frontiers in tools and notions

The sites of phytohormone biosynthesis and activity are essential for an in-depth analysis of their actions. Spatial details are accessible by FRET-derived sensor systems, which were created by analyzing structural data on the RCAR-PP2C abscisic acid receptor complex. 110 The most recent iteration of these types of sensors can detect abscisic acid buildup trends at the cellular resolution. 111 Conversely, an alternative FRET-dependent sensor detects SnRK2 kinase activity. 112 Despite the restriction of such genetically programmed sensors to animals amenable to the development of mutant lines, these methods are enhancing our understanding of temporal and spatial dynamics of abscisic acid at both cellular and tissue scales.

Strigolactone profiling

Strigolactone signaling and biosynthesis overview

Strigolactones were the latest identified among the widely recognized phytohormones 113 ; yet, their production and signaling pathways are already comparatively well elucidated. 114 Strigolactones originate from carotenoid substrates through a predominantly linear process; yet, approximately 30 active strigolactones have been identified, but the physiological significance of this variety remains to be determined. 115 Canonical strigolactones possess an ABC ring component connected to a D ring, but noncanonical strigolactones diverge from this configuration yet continue to attach to the strigolactone receptor and initiate the subsequent signaling cascades it governs 116 (El‐Beltagi et al., 2026). Strigolactones influence their action by the selective degradation of certain SMXL proteins. Such proteins are believed to be associated with several activities, such as hypocotyl elongation, shoot branching regulation, and immediate transcriptional suppression. 117 The molecular aspects of strigolactone-associated breakdown of SMXL-based proteins have been well elucidated. The mechanism is facilitated by the assembly of a multifaceted structure consisting of strigolactone receptor D14 and D3/MAX2. Upon strigolactone engagement, the structure of D14 alters, enabling its contact with D3/MAX2, a component of Skp1-Cullin-F-box complex (Figure 5). This complex subsequently attracts D53/SMXL7, which undergoes ubiquitination, leading to its destruction via 26S proteasome, 118 hence activating unidentified downstream transcriptional factors that govern strigolactone-associated genes transcription. SMXL-based proteins, specifically BRC1, which encodes a transcriptional factor that suppresses branching. 119

Metabolites as regulatory nodes in strigolactone signaling

Although Strigolactones were the latest identified phytohormones, 120 substantial knowledge exists regarding their impact on metabolism. 121 The first focus of these impacts was on the incorporation of P and N signals to regulate root and shoot development, with the production of strigolactones being suppressed by both N and K (Figure 5). Conversely, a deficit in such nutrients results in significant elevations in strigolactone levels, facilitating mycorrhizal associations and nodule development, hence augmenting crops' nitrate and phosphate uptake rates. 122 The adverse effect of phosphate and nitrate deprivation on branched shoots in nonmycorrhizal Arabidopsis is mitigated in strigolactone-deprived mutants. 123 The molecular pathways by which phosphate and nitrate limit strigolactone production are largely elucidated, with PSR transcript factors from the Myb family identified as mediators of the phosphate response. 124 These transcriptional determinants may also function through the NSP2 gene, which encodes a transcription regulator including a GRAS domain. 125 , 126

Concurrently, research has demonstrated that sugar accessibility can modulate strigolactone activity. In this context, plant branching is significantly affected rather than root development. 127 Plant branching is a dynamic characteristic that is significantly regulated via hormonal signaling, particularly by strigolactones, which suppress the expression of this property. 127 , 128 Likewise, the long-duration carbon transport component sucrose is essential in modulating axillary bud expansion. 129 Strigolactones induce these impacts by promoting the degradation of D53/SMXL7, a mechanism facilitated by the MAX2 protein and strigolactone receptor D14. 130 Administering sucrose to cereal crops mitigated this degradation, since sucrose notably suppressed the expression of both D14 and D53, suggesting that the process is not transcriptional 131 (Figure 5). Conversely, the presence of sucrose mitigates the reaction to strigolactones by suppressing D3/MAX2 transcription across many species. 132 The antagonistic effect of sucrose on strigolactone-induced suppression of the tillering phase was nullified after overexpression of D3. 133 , 134

Impact of strigolactone on metabolic processes

Comparative investigations of mutants impaired just in strigolactone signaling with those impacted by both strigolactone and karrikin signaling indicated that the latter exhibited more pronounced abnormalities. 135 The results obtained suggest that karrikin signaling has activities beyond butanolide signaling 136 (Figure 5). Affinity filtration studies corroborate this theory by revealing the interaction of multiple proteins with MAX2, notably PAPP5, which is believed to dephosphorylate MAX2 to regulate seedling initiation, possibly through a sugar-associated mechanism 137 (Figure 5​​​​​). Karrikin signaling has been associated with glucose signaling to initiate Arabidopsis shoot branching via HEXOKINASE1; however, investigations utilizing glucose analogs in rose and pea have indicated that MAX2 functions independently of HXK1. 138 , 139

A significant metabolic connection to strigolactone was identified in a recent work demonstrating that controlling SnRK1 connects with SMXL7 140 (Figure 5). Furthermore, the sugar signal trehalose 6-phosphate has been demonstrated to enhance branching. Trehalose 6-phosphate is a sucrose-related signal with hormone-type properties that is essential for metabolic regulation through the management of sucrose distribution and utilisation. 141 , 142 Moreover, trehalose 6-phosphate suppresses SnRK1 function in Arabidopsis, but mutations in SnRK1 ameliorated growth deficiency in trehalose 6-phosphate-insufficient mutants. 143 Consequently, substantial evidence exists for signaling crosstalk between metabolites and elements of strigolactone signaling, despite the absence of precise mechanisms. A recent work indicated that glucosinolate production was elevated in strigolactone mutants, and conversely, treatment of glucosinolate mutants with strigolactone reinstated their branching morphology. 144

Recent findings indicate that strigolactone signaling is closely connected with central carbon metabolism, particularly through the tricarboxylic acid cycle. This interaction appears to involve trehalose 6-phosphate as an important metabolic signal. In Arabidopsis rosettes, trehalose 6-phosphate stimulates phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase activity, which increases the anaplerotic entry of photosynthetically fixed carbon into the tricarboxylic acid cycle. This enhanced carbon flow supports the accumulation of key organic acids, including malate and fumarate in the cytosol and citrate in the mitochondria. 145 These metabolites may function not only as respiratory intermediates but also as regulatory signals that connect the cellular carbon status with hormone-dependent growth responses. DICARBOXYLATE CARRIER 2 likely contributes to this metabolic exchange by mediating malate import into mitochondria and citrate export from mitochondria. 146 , 147 The exported citrate may then influence strigolactone signaling by modulating the activity of MORE AXILLARY GROWTH 2, also known as MAX2, which serves as a central component of the SL response pathway. Together, these observations suggest that trehalose 6-phosphate-driven carbon flux and mitochondrial organic acid transport provide a metabolic route through which the tricarboxylic acid cycle can regulate strigolactone signaling.

Innovative tools and ideas

Recent advancements in strigolactone research have been propelled by several investigations. Luo 148 significantly enhanced our comprehension of strigolactone receptor complex response and uncovered intriguing insights into N-dependent control of strigolactone signaling and the tillering phase. An especially compelling discovery is that strigolactone cleavage orchestrates several developmental events, and the resultant hydrolytic metabolites may serve as signaling compounds in the rhizosphere. 80 , 149 Additional investigation into the extensive metabolic ramifications of this breakage will be essential for clarifying its physiological significance.

Ethylene status

Ethylene signaling and biosynthesis pathways overview

Ethylene is the gaseous phytohormone renowned for its functions in senescence, fruit ripening and stress reaction. 150 Ethylene is produced from Met to SAM by the Met cycle, which maintains ethylene synthesis by regenerating SAM into Met (Figure 6). The biosynthesis of ethylene proceeds with the transformation of S-adenosylmethionine into ACC via ACC synthase, followed by its transformation en route to ethylene through ACC oxidase. 151 , 152 ACC may act as a signaling molecule, directly affecting various crop growth processes without ethylene. 109 Ethylene perception transpires via the ETR1 family receptor, and these receptors, which are subject to ethylene binding, alleviate the suppression of CTR1 on EIN2-based protein. This stimulates EIN2 and subsequent transcript factors, including EIN3 and EIL1, which govern ethylene-responsive gene expression. 153

Ethylene production and signaling pathways and their interaction with metabolic processes.

Ethylene impacts on metabolic processes and metabolite-driven modulation of ethylene signaling

Ethylene signaling is intricately connected to carbohydrate metabolism and signaling. Previous reviews have highlighted studies that illustrate ethylene's role in counteracting glucose signaling during the plant cycle. 154 GLUCOSE INSENSITIVE 1 suppresses the ethylene signaling pathway cascade of EIN3; although ethylene stabilizes EIN3, and HXK-induced glucose signaling promotes EIN3 breakdown (Figure 6). Recent investigations have illuminated the intricate relationship between glucose metabolism and ethylene signaling in seedling growth. A significant instance is research that revealed a sucrose-induced feedback loop regulating ethylene production to promote etiolated seedlings' greening. 155 In seeds growing in darkness, sucrose buildup stimulates ACS7 expression, which encodes a crucial enzyme in ethylene production (Figure 6). Ethylene stimulates EIN3, then inhibits the function of SUC2, resulting in less sucrose exportation from seed cotyledons and increased sucrose buildup within such tissues. 155 This buildup sustains ethylene synthesis, creating an advantageous feedback loop (Figure 6). In response to light, increased ethylene concentrations and functional EIN3 suppress phyA function (Figure 6), the photoreceptor that normally inhibits de-etiolation. 156 Such inhibition facilitates the greening mechanism, allowing seedlings to shift efficiently from darkness to light environments. These results demonstrate that in darkness, the sucrose feedback circuit facilitates sucrose buildup in seed cotyledons, hence assuring continuous ethylene synthesis. This mechanism promotes de-etiolation and aids seedling growth from the soil. The present study elucidates the complex interaction between sugar signaling and ethylene mechanisms, illustrating that sucrose functions as a signaling molecule that influences hormone networks to govern essential developmental events in crops.

An adversarial relationship between glucose and ethylene signaling modulates the self-renewal ability of stem stomatal tissue. 157 CTR1 receptors were discovered using a genetic screening aimed at identifying cell polarity modulators, demonstrating that ethylene adversely impacts stomatal polarity via suppressing stem cellular division. 158 Recent study evidence has revealed a biochemical mechanism via which sugar levels in leaves affect stomatal maturation in freshly formed leaves, emphasizing the intricate interaction between ethylene and sugar signaling pathways. 159 In healthy leaves, glucose is detected via HXK1, then reduces EIN3 stability (Figure 6). A decrease in EIN3 concentrations results in elevated SUC2 expression, hence augmenting sucrose transfer from matured to emerging leaves. Increased sucrose concentrations in these emerging leaves stimulate SnRK1, a vital controller of energy balance, which subsequently enhances SPCH expression, which is responsible for encoding a principal modulator of stomatal expansion. This pattern delineates a systemic signaling route wherein the glucose level of mature foliage governs stomatal development in fresh leaves via a module (HXK1-EIN3-SUC2-SnRK1-SPCH; Figure 6). The present study highlights the complex interaction between ethylene and sugar signaling pathways, illustrating how plants assimilate metabolic signals from mature cells to regulate developmental mechanisms in developing organs.

Ethylene interacts with other metabolic regulators and phytohormones to influence plant adaptability. Under hypoxic conditions, ethylene regulates essential metabolic and physiological responses. The aforementioned phytohormone primes crops for oxygen-depleted stress by reducing nitric oxide, hence augmenting survival strategies before hypoxia. 160 Moreover, ethylene promotes metabolic acclimatization by predicting hypoxia, initiating anaerobic cellular processes, and modulating reactive oxygen species metabolism to alleviate oxidative stress after reoxygenation. 161 In Arabidopsis species roots, ethylene enhances hypoxia resistance by causing growth halting and diminishing excessive reactive oxygen species buildup, hence improving plant stress resistance. 162 Moreover, ethylene was demonstrated to enhance flavonol deposition in the roots of Arabidopsis. 163 Ethylene promotes the formation of flavonols in stomatal cells via EIN2 (Figure 6), and such flavonols function as antioxidants to eliminate reactive oxygen species, thus preventing ABA-associated stomatal closure. In summary, ethylene counteracts ABA's influence on stomatal behaviour by adjusting flavonol and reactive oxygen species concentrations to regulate responses to abiotic stress. 161

Jasmonates profile

Jasmonate signaling and biosynthesis pathways: overview

Oxylipins constitute a group of oxygenated fatty acids encompassing several bioactive chemicals, including green leaf-derived volatiles and jasmonates. Jasmonic acid, jasmonoyl-isoleucine, methyl-JA, and other associated metabolites are commonly known as jasmonates. Jasmonates are synthesized in three cellular regions: chloroplasts, the cytosol, and peroxisomes. Galactolipids within the chloroplast, including digalactosyldiacylglycerol and monogalactosyldiacylglycerol, are hydrolyzed by lipases, including phospholipase A-Iγ1, PLIP3, and PLASTID LIPASE, in vegetative tissues. 164 The liberated fatty acids (C16:3 and C18:3) are subsequently oxidized and transformed into dnOPDA and cis-OPDA, respectively. The processes occurring in chloroplasts are facilitated by allene oxide cyclase and AOS (Figure 7). The biosynthesized cis-OPDA in cellular chloroplasts is released by a protein located in the outermost membrane called JASSY 165 and subsequently integrated into the cellular peroxisome; however, the mechanism of importation has yet to be clarified. 166 Thereafter, cis-OPDA is transformed into jasmonate via β-oxidation and OPRs. In the case of Arabidopsis, OPR3 is pivotal in jasmonate production (Figure 7).

Jasmonic acid production and signaling pathway, along with incorporation into light-signal and metabolic networks.

In alignment with the developing concept that jasmonoyl-isoleucine is a non-bioactive regulator in non-vascular plants, as elaborated further, jasmonoyl-amino acid synthetases, which combine jasmonate with jasmonoyl-isoleucine, seem to have developed in tracheophytes. 167 A model for jasmonate sensing and signaling has been developed in Arabidopsis plants. 168 Without jasmonates, transcriptional repressors identified as JAZ proteins engage with and inhibit the functions of transcription elements, including MYC2. Upon the accumulation of bioactive jasmonates, such as jasmonoyl-isoleucine, within cells, a molecule is recognized via a complex involving COI1 and JAZ, leading to the destruction of the JASMONATE ZIM DOMAIN. A contemporary model, together with other related aspects, is illustrated in another source 169 (Figure 7).

Jasmonate impacts on metabolism

Tryptophan is an aromatic amino acid that functions as a precursor for numerous specific metabolites. Genes associated with tryptophan metabolic activity and specific metabolism are controlled by MYC2-dependent jasmonate signaling in response to pathogens and wounds. 170 Furthermore, jasmonate signaling has recently been demonstrated to participate in growth via modulating tryptophan metabolic activity. 171 Arabidopsis contains 13 JAZ genes; a decuple JAZ-based mutant showed reduced vulnerability to pathogens and insects, along with a diminished growth rate. Additionally, metabolite profiling indicated that tryptophan and its product, glucosinolates build up in the JAZ-based mutant, whereas sucrose and starch levels diminished. Other findings, especially transcriptome data, suggested that the impaired development of the JAZ-based mutant was partially attributable to carbon deprivation. 172 In a subsequent investigation, Phy-B mutation was demonstrated to inhibit growth attributes, albeit not the defense mechanisms of the decuple JAZ-based mutant, indicating that its impaired growth was linked to the tryptophan metabolic process. 173 Genes associated with the tryptophan metabolic process through serine, indole, and chorismate were activated in the JAZ-Phy-B suppressor and JIAZ decuple mutants, correlating with their elevated serine and tryptophan levels (Figure 7). In conjunction with a clinical experiment utilizing a hazardous tryptophan analog, our findings demonstrated that tryptophan production was induced in decuple-JAZ mutants irrespective of Phy-B mutation.

Jasmonate signaling regulation mediated by metabolites

Numerous studies have investigated the correlation between other and jasmonate signaling pathways related to metabolism and energy. An analysis including a TOR blocker in Arabidopsis and cotton indicated an interaction between TOR and jasmonate signaling. 174 The TOR blocker treatment enhanced jasmonate production and signaling in cotton, while mutants deficient in jasmonate synthesis and signaling displayed either heightened or lower susceptibility to the blocker in Arabidopsis plants (Figure 7). Contrary to this, a mutant of Arabidopsis lacking the TOR system element regulatory-related proteins of TOR-1B displayed widespread alterations in metabolite and phytohormone levels. 84 The plastid glycolipid concentrations, specifically DGDG and MGDG, were elevated in the mutation, whereas the quantity of jasmonoyl-isoleucine, cis-OPDA, and Jasmonate diminished. However, the molecular determinants connecting jasmonate and TOR signaling have yet to be elucidated. The relationship between jasmonate signaling and central metabolism was confirmed by research on nutrient deficit reactions. In response to potassium deprivation, various synthetic genes, especially AOS, were activated, resulting in increased oxylipins, such as jasmonic acid, in Arabidopsis plants. 175 Furthermore, phosphate deprivation induced synthesis of jasmonic acid and jasmonic acid-isoleucine, along with their signaling pathways in the roots and leaves of Arabidopsis. 175 MYB-CC-type transcriptional factors that respond to phosphate deprivation, such as PHOSPHATE STARVATION RESPONSE-1, have recently been demonstrated to bind to JAZ suppressors and work cooperatively alongside MYC2 to activate jasmonate-regulated gene activity (Figure 7). The results presented here collectively indicate that jasmonate signaling may function as a warning in response to conditions of energy and nutritional scarcity.

Innovative technologies and ideas

Arabidopsis-OPR3 is primarily responsible for OPDA reduction. 176 Rather than dnOPDA, cis-OPDA, originating from cis-OPDA in the peroxisome or carried from the cellular chloroplast, is transformed into 4,5-ddh-JA, which then decreases to jasmonic acid via OPR2. 177 OPR3 demonstrated a wider substrate affinity than earlier identified enzymes and is implicated in the redox equilibrium related to glutathione and ascorbate. 177 Significantly, dnOPDA-based elements and dnOPDA-associated compounds, unlike jasmonoyl-isoleucine, exhibit biological activity in non-vascular plants, 178 whereas OPR genes demonstrate a high degree of conservation in streptophytes relative to different genes implicated in jasmonate synthesis. 179 It is possible that vascular plants evolved to use OPRs for jasmonate synthesis.

Cytokinins value

Cytokinins signaling and biosynthesis pathways: overview

Cytokinins are adenine-based phytohormones crucial for cellular division, nutrient mobilization, and shoot development. 180 Cytokinins are synthesized from isoprenoids and purines through two primary routes. 181 The de novo synthetic pathway entails the lipidation of free adenine nucleotides via adenosine isopentenyltransferase, which facilitates isopentenyl group transfer from DMAPP to AMP, culminating in tZRMP formation (Figure 8). The tRNA breakdown process concurrently entails tRNA isopentenylation via tRNA-IPT, leading to the synthesis of cZRMP. 182

Cytokinin biosynthesis, signaling pathways, and their interaction with metabolic processes.

Impact of cytokinins on metabolic processes and metabolite-driven modulation of cytokinin signaling

Cytokinin engages with various metabolic processes, including those involving sugar and sulfur metabolism. The interaction between cytokinin and sugar signaling, particularly with the trehalose-signaling pathway. 67 Exogenous administration of cytokinin elevates T6P transcription levels and suppresses TTP gene expression, leading to a buildup of T6P (Figure 8). Recent research identified novel pathways of interaction between cytokinin signaling and sulfur balance, as well as sulfur-rich metabolites. 183 The administration of exogenous cytokinin in Arabidopsis elicited a transcription response akin to sulfur deprivation, which was correlated with a lower concentration of glutathione and sulfate. This reaction transpired independent of the principal regulator of sulfur starvation signaling, SULPHUR LIMITATION-1, and of GGCT 2;1, a significant enzyme implicated in GSH catabolism; its gene level was markedly elevated by cytokinin. Conversely, plants deficient in the cytokinin receptor AHK3 or exhibiting reduced cytokinin production demonstrated increased GSH concentrations. The results presented underscore a novel governing function of cytokinin signaling in sulfur metabolism (Figure 8), indicating cytokinin's critical involvement in sulfur uptake, redistribution, assimilation, and crops' adaptations to environmental stress. 184 Recent research revealed the complex interaction between cytokinins and sulfur metabolism, demonstrating that hydrogen sulfide antagonizes cytokinin signaling through the cytokinin oxidase 2 persulfidating enzyme. 185 This post-translational alteration augments cytokinin oxidase 2 activity, resulting in heightened cytokinin degradation (Figure 8). The subsequent reduction in cytokinin levels modifies the root system structure, thereby fostering responses that are advantageous to crop in sulfur-insufficient environments. These outcomes elucidate an emerging bidirectional interaction mechanism wherein H2S influences cytokinin signaling, while cytokinin signaling regulates sulfur metabolism, emphasizing the intricate interplay between sulfur homeostasis and cytokinin-induced developmental pathways. 186

Innovative resources and tools

Recent advancements have revolutionized the examination of cytokinin perception, metabolic integration, and transport. Fluorescently labeled cytokinins, generated by Antoniadi et al. (2015), provide in vivo visualization of cytokinin absorption and receptor localization, uncovering several perception spots at the endoplasmic reticulum and plasma membrane. Complementary geographical research reported by Guo et al. (2026) shows that localized cytokinin metabolism creates hormonal microenvironments that regulate developmental and nutritional responses. Collectively, these instruments establish a framework to integrate real-time images with metabolic assessments, presenting novel opportunities to analyze the interaction between cytokinin signaling and sulfur assimilation pathways, encompassing sulfate transport, redox homeostasis, and cysteine production during development and stress adaptation.

Brassinosteroids profile

Brassinosteroid signaling and biosynthesis pathways: overview

Brassinosteroids belong to the polyhydroxylated steroid-based hormone class that governs plant growth. 187 Brassinosteroids were initially found in the 1970s research and are currently recognized for their essential functions in vascular differentiation, cell division, and cell elongation. 73 Brassinosteroid synthesis is a meticulously regulated mechanism that entails campesterol conversion, a sterol-derived precursor, into functional brassinosteroids, including brassinolide and castasterone, via several enzymatic stages facilitated via CYP 188 (Figure 9). Brassinosteroid signaling commences with the interaction of active BRs with the leucine-containing repeat receptor-based kinase brassinosteroid-INSENSITIVE-1 situated at the plasma membrane. 189 According to brassinosteroid cognition, BRI1 interacts with its underlying coreceptor BAK1, initiating transphosphorylation processes that activate the receptor complex. 190 Brassinosteroid signaling disables the kinase BIN2, which acts as the downstream negative modulator of this route, which typically phosphorylates and inhibits downstream transcriptional factors. Brassinosteroid signaling inactivates BIN2, thus liberating the downstream transcriptional factors BZR1 and BES1/BZR2, 191 thereby facilitating their nuclear relocation (Figure 9). Upon entering the nucleus, functional types of BZR1 and BES1 modulate brassinosteroid-specific genes pertinent to growth, stress responses, and development.

Brassinosteroid biosynthesis and signaling pathways and their interaction with metabolism.

Brassinosteroid impacts on metabolism

The interplay between brassinosteroid and glucose signaling, together with antioxidant ascorbate metabolism, has been investigated previously. 192 BRI1 exhibits epistasis over HXK1, and carbohydrate signaling through TOR kinase modulates the levels of BZR transcriptional factors: the glucose-TOR channel stimulates BZR1, whereas sugar deprivation results in TOR inactivity and subsequent degradation of BZR1 (Figure 9). Several recent investigations have illustrated the interaction between secondary metabolism and brassinosteroids. The initial study established the role of the SbBRI1 receptor in water stress resistance through phenylpropanoid metabolic pathways in cereal crops. 193 DNA alignment and purification sequencing showed that SbBES1 interacts with a homologous G-box domain, therefore modulating brassinosteroid equilibrium in a way akin to its Thaliana ortholog AtBES1. During typical circumstances, SbBES1 governs lignin production; yet, during water stress, diminished SbBES1 expression redirects metabolism towards the flavonoid route (Figure 9). Metabolic adaptation increases photosynthesis capacity and photoprotection, therefore augmenting drought tolerance in cereal crops. 194 Another investigation revealed that brassinosteroids inhibit glucosinolate synthesis while promoting sulfur metabolites synthesis, thus redistributing sulfur transport from secondary metabolism to primary metabolic pathways. 195 This control is governed by the brassinosteroid signaling transcriptional factor BZR-1, which inhibits genes involved in the glucosinolate cascade and stimulates the essential genes APR1 and APR2, which are responsible for primary sulfur uptake. In brief, brassinosteroids regulate sulfur flux by inhibiting the synthesis of secondary sulfur metabolites while promoting the formation of primary sulfur metabolites (Figure 9), thus facilitating plant performance.

Regulation of brassinosteroid signaling mediated by metabolites

Recent research identified a mechanism connecting reactive oxygen species metabolism and brassinosteroid to adenosine metabolism. Upstream genetic screens for tolerance to the BIN2 antagonist bikinin revealed mutations in the AMPD gene, resulting in lower susceptibility to both brassinosteroid and bikinin. 196 Subsequent investigation demonstrated that AMPD regulates BIN2 function via influencing its oligomerization in an H2O2-associated manner (Figure 9). Exogenous H2O2 promoted BIN2 oligomer formation, reduced kinase function, and increased susceptibility to bikinin. Inhibition of AMPD function diminished the cytosolic levels of reactive oxygen species and the quantity of BIN2 oligomers, resulting in lower susceptibility to bikinin and brassinosteroids. Furthermore, BIN2 was identified as an AMPD phosphorylator, indicating a feedback mechanism that regulates BIN2 kinase function to modulate plant growth 197 (Figure 9). These findings demonstrate that reactive oxygen species metabolism directly affects brassinosteroid pathway function, linking the crop's metabolic condition to hormonal control. Nazir et al. 198 identified a redox-induced mechanism in which H2O2, a metabolic waste, directly influences brassinosteroid transcriptional activity by oxidizing BZR1. Brassinosteroid therapy specifically elevates intracellular hydrogen peroxide concentrations, resulting in the oxidation of BZR1 at invariant Cys-63, thereby augmenting its bonds with key regulators, including ARF6 and PIF4, and facilitating the encoding of brassinosteroid-associated genes. Redox modulation is bidirectional; TRXh5 decreases oxidized BZR1, functioning as an input regulator that connects oxidative damage to brassinosteroid signaling (Figure 9). This approach establishes a direct connection between metabolic levels of reactive oxygen species and brassinosteroid signal intensity and BZR1's role as a redox-responsive transcriptional center that amalgamates metabolic signaling with hormone-mediated growth regulation. 198 Collectively, these studies highlight that although direct quantitative instances of input between brassinosteroid signaling and metabolism are scarce, there is increasing research support for bidirectional modulation between brassinosteroid signaling and metabolic systems, especially through redox and reactive oxygen species metabolism, as well as secondary biochemical phenylpropanoid routes.

Innovative instruments and tools

Recent advancements in brassinosteroid studies have broadened the experimental resources for analyzing hormone kinetics and signal transduction. Fluorescently labeled brassinosteroids, including Alexa Fluor-castasterone, facilitate live-cell visualization of brassinosteroid reception and BRI1-induced endocytosis, providing spatiotemporal insights into ligand‒receptor kinetics in Arabidopsis. Additionally, redox-dependent governing nodes have been identified, such as the hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidation process of BZR1 and BRI1-signaling kinases de-S-acylation, indicating the potential for the incorporation of reactive oxygen species manipulation or redox biosensors to investigate the interplay between brassinosteroid signaling and metabolism. Collectively, these instruments may offer a mechanistic comprehension of brassinosteroid functions in both developmental processes and metabolic control.

Melatonin profile

Molecular framework of melatonin biosynthesis and signal transduction

Melatonin, also known as N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine, is an indoleamine that occurs widely in plants and regulates growth, development, redox balance, and stress adaptation. In plants, melatonin biosynthesis mainly begins from tryptophan. Tryptophan is converted into tryptamine by tryptophan decarboxylase, and tryptamine is then hydroxylated into serotonin through tryptamine 5-hydroxylase. Serotonin is further converted into N-acetylserotonin by serotonin N-acetyltransferase, followed by methylation through N-acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase or caffeic acid O-methyltransferase to produce melatonin. 199 Alternative routes may also operate depending on the tissue type, developmental stage, and stress intensity. These routes include conversion through 5-hydroxytryptophan, indicating that plant melatonin synthesis is flexible and strongly linked with the cellular metabolic status. 200

Melatonin signaling in plants remains an active research area. The receptor candidate CAND2, also known as PMTR1, has been linked with melatonin perception in Arabidopsis, where it mediates stomatal closure through hydrogen peroxide and calcium signaling. Under osmotic stress, CAND2/PMTR1 contributes to melatonin-induced stress tolerance by improving reactive oxygen species scavenging capacity. 201 Melatonin also activates downstream signaling components, including calcium-dependent signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase cascades, antioxidant enzymes, and stress-responsive transcription factors. Through these pathways, melatonin connects environmental perception with redox regulation, hormone interaction, and metabolic adjustment. Recent studies have also suggested that melatonin communicates with the auxin, abscisic acid, salicylic acid, ethylene, jasmonate, and gibberellin pathways, making it a central regulator of plant growth and stress defense. 202

Melatonin-driven metabolic reprogramming

Melatonin has strong effects on primary and secondary metabolism. In primary metabolism, melatonin improves photosynthetic efficiency by protecting chloroplast structure, increasing chlorophyll accumulation, stabilizing electron transport, and reducing oxidative injury under stress conditions. It also supports carbohydrate metabolism by increasing soluble sugars, sucrose, and starch accumulation, which improves the energy supply and osmotic balance during stress recovery. In strawberry under aluminum stress, melatonin enhanced photosynthetic pigment levels, improved carbohydrate and nitrogen metabolism, and increased antioxidant enzyme activity, showing its ability to coordinate growth and stress tolerance through metabolic regulation. 47 Similar findings in different crops suggest that melatonin promotes carbon assimilation, nitrogen use, and energy balance under drought, salinity, heavy metal toxicity, and low light stress. Melatonin also regulates nitrogen metabolism. It can increase nitrate reductase activity, amino acid accumulation, protein content, and nitrogen assimilation efficiency. These effects are important because nitrogen metabolism supplies amino acids for protein synthesis, osmoprotectant production, and defense-related metabolites. Melatonin further enhances mitochondrial respiration and maintains the cellular energy balance, which helps plants sustain growth under stress. 203 In secondary metabolism, melatonin stimulates the accumulation of flavonoids, phenolics, anthocyanins, proline, glutathione, ascorbate, and phytochelatins. These metabolites contribute to antioxidant defense, membrane stability, metal detoxification, and protection against reactive oxygen species. Therefore, melatonin does not act only as a signaling molecule. It also functions as a metabolic coordinator that adjusts carbon flow, nitrogen assimilation, antioxidant metabolism, and specialized metabolite production.

Metabolites as dynamic regulators of melatonin signaling

Plant metabolites also regulate melatonin biosynthesis and signaling. Tryptophan and serotonin are the most direct metabolic precursors of melatonin, so changes in aromatic amino acid metabolism can influence melatonin accumulation. Under stress, enhanced tryptophan and serotonin availability may increase melatonin biosynthesis through the activity of tryptophan decarboxylase, tryptamine-5-hydroxylase, serotonin N-acetyltransferase, and acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase. 204 Sugars also influence melatonin metabolism. Sucrose and glucose can modify plant energy status and regulate genes involved in melatonin synthesis. This places melatonin within the broader carbon signaling network, which also includes the TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN and SNF1 RELATED PROTEIN KINASE1 pathways (Figure 10).

Overview of melatonin biosynthesis, signaling, metabolic functions, and metabolite mediated regulation in plants. Melatonin is synthesized from tryptophan through sequential conversion to tryptamine, serotonin, and N-acetylserotonin. It signaling involves putative receptor mediated activation of calcium influx, reactive oxygen species signaling, MAPK cascades, and transcriptional reprogramming, which together regulate plant growth, development, and stress tolerance. Melatonin also modulates primary and secondary metabolism, including photosynthesis, carbohydrate and nitrogen metabolism, antioxidant defense, membrane stability, and the accumulation of phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins. In addition, melatonin signaling is shaped by metabolic inputs such as sugars, redox metabolites, reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, hydrogen sulfide, and polyamines, highlighting its central role in integrating metabolic status with hormonal crosstalk and stress adaptation.

Redox metabolites provide another layer of control. Ascorbate, glutathione, hydrogen peroxide, nitric oxide, hydrogen sulfide, and reactive oxygen species interact with melatonin signaling during stress responses. Melatonin can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen species, but these molecules can also act upstream as signals that influence melatonin synthesis and downstream defense activation. 205 Gasotransmitters such as nitric oxide and hydrogen sulfide may act together with melatonin to regulate stomatal movement, antioxidant enzyme activity, protein modification, and stress tolerance. Polyamines, especially spermidine and spermine, also interact with melatonin through shared roles in redox control, membrane protection, and stress signaling. These findings indicate that melatonin signaling is not isolated. It is controlled by a metabolite network that links amino acid metabolism, sugar status, redox balance, and hormone crosstalk.

Emerging frontiers in melatonin tools and technologies

Recent advances in analytical chemistry, biosensing, and multiomics are improving the study of melatonin biology in plants. Accurate melatonin detection remains challenging because endogenous melatonin is often present at very low concentrations, and plant extracts contain many interfering compounds. Liquid chromatography combined with mass spectrometry has become one of the most reliable approaches for melatonin quantification because it offers high sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility. 206 Ultra-performance liquid chromatography, high-resolution Orbitrap mass spectrometry, targeted metabolomics, and isotope-based methods are now helping researchers measure melatonin and its intermediates with greater accuracy.

Genetic and molecular tools are also expanding this field. Mutants and overexpression lines for tryptophan decarboxylase, tryptamine-5-hydroxylase, serotonin N-acetyltransferase, acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase, CAND2/PMTR1, and related signaling components can clarify the contribution of each pathway step. CRISPR Cas9 can support functional validation of melatonin biosynthetic and signaling genes in model plants and crops. Transcriptomics, proteomics, phosphoproteomics, and metabolomics can identify how melatonin reshapes hormone signaling, antioxidant defense, and metabolic networks under stress. Future studies should combine genetically encoded sensors, spatial metabolomics, isotope tracing, and genome editing to reveal how melatonin moves across tissues, where it accumulates, and how it controls metabolism at a cellular resolution.

Final comments and prospective outlooks

Plant hormones and metabolism form an integrated regulatory system that allows plants to coordinate growth, development, defense, and adaptation to environmental stress. This review highlights that hormone signaling cannot be understood as a linear pathway alone. Instead, each hormone pathway is connected with primary and secondary metabolism through sugar status, redox balance, amino acid pools, organic acid turnover, nutrient availability, and energy-sensing modules. Salicylic acid, gibberellins, auxin, abscisic acid, strigolactones, ethylene, jasmonates, cytokinins, brassinosteroids, and melatonin all show strong connections with metabolic networks. These connections influence hormone biosynthesis, hormone perception, signal transduction, and downstream transcriptional responses.

A central message of this review is that metabolites do not act only as products of hormone action. They also function as regulators of hormone signaling. Sugars such as sucrose, glucose, and trehalose-6-phosphate influence gibberellin, auxin, strigolactone, cytokinin, ethylene, and abscisic acid responses. Organic acids such as citrate, malate, fumarate, succinate, and 2-oxoglutarate link the tricarboxylic acid cycle with hormone biosynthesis and signaling. Redox metabolites, including ascorbate, glutathione, hydrogen peroxide, reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, hydrogen sulfide, and polyamines, shape stress signaling and hormone sensitivity. Amino acids such as tryptophan, cysteine, serine, and related metabolites connect hormone production with defense, growth, and redox homeostasis. These findings support a reciprocal model in which hormones regulate metabolism, while metabolites also tune hormone signaling intensity, duration, and tissue specificity.

The reviewed evidence also shows that this hormone metabolism interaction operates at several biological levels. At the cellular level, metabolic status affects receptor activity, kinase cascades, transcription factors, protein stability, and post-translational modifications. At the tissue level, hormone and metabolite gradients help control source and sink relations, root and shoot architecture, stomatal behavior, reproductive development, and stress acclimation. At the whole plant level, long-distance movement of sugars, organic acids, amino acids, mobile defense metabolites, and hormone derivatives coordinates local perception with systemic responses. Therefore, future research should move beyond isolated hormone pathways and study hormone metabolism networks as dynamic regulatory systems.

Despite major progress, several important questions remain unresolved. First, the direction of causality between hormone signaling and metabolic change is often difficult to define. In many cases, metabolite accumulation appears after hormone treatment, but it is not clear whether these metabolites act as signals, substrates, buffers, or stress byproducts. Second, many studies rely on external hormone application or single mutant analysis, which may not reflect endogenous signaling under natural conditions. Third, most evidence comes from Arabidopsis, while crop-specific regulation remains less explored. Fourth, tissue-specific and organelle-specific hormone metabolism interactions remain poorly understood. Hormone and metabolite pools may differ among chloroplasts, mitochondria, peroxisomes, the cytosol, the endoplasmic reticulum, nuclei, and the apoplast. These compartments may create local signaling hubs that remain hidden in whole tissue analyses.

Future studies should prioritize spatial and temporal resolution. Genetically encoded biosensors, FRET-based reporters, fluorescent hormone analogs, live imaging, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial metabolomics, isotope tracing, and high-resolution mass spectrometry can reveal where and when hormone metabolism interactions occur. These approaches can help distinguish rapid signaling events from later metabolic responses. Stable isotope labeling will be especially useful for tracking metabolic flux through glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, amino acid metabolism, sulfur metabolism, lipid metabolism, and hormone biosynthetic routes. Combining isotope tracing with mutants, biosensors, and phosphoproteomics can clarify whether a metabolite directly regulates a signaling component or indirectly affects hormone responses through energy balance.

Genome editing will also play a key role in future research. CRISPR Cas9 and related technologies can validate genes involved in hormone biosynthesis, transport, perception, degradation, and metabolite-dependent regulation. However, single gene editing may not be enough because many hormone and metabolic pathways show redundancy. Future work should use multiplex editing, inducible systems, tissue-specific promoters, and precise allele engineering to dissect gene function without causing severe developmental defects. These tools will help test how specific receptors, transporters, kinases, transcription factors, enzymes, and metabolic carriers regulate hormone metabolism cross-talk in different tissues and stress environments.

Another future direction is the integration of multiomics data into predictive network models. Transcriptomics, proteomics, phosphoproteomics, metabolomics, ionomics, lipidomics, and phenomics can provide complementary views of hormone metabolism regulation. However, these datasets need better computational integration. Network biology, machine learning, and dynamic modeling can identify regulatory nodes that control both hormone signaling and metabolic flux. Such models should include environmental inputs such as drought, salinity, waterlogging, nutrient limitation, heat, cold, pathogen pressure, and light quality. This will help predict how plants allocate resources between growth and defense under complex field conditions.

Crop translation should become a major goal of this research area. Hormone metabolism networks have direct relevance for yield, stress tolerance, nutrient use efficiency, fruit quality, flowering, seed development, root architecture, and postharvest performance. Manipulating one hormone pathway may improve one trait but reduce another because metabolism links growth, defense, and resource allocation. Therefore, future crop improvement strategies should target network nodes that provide balanced outcomes. For example, sugar signaling modules, redox regulators, amino acid metabolism, sulfur metabolism, tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, and hormone transport systems may offer promising targets for improving stress resilience without strong growth penalties.

Melatonin adds an important emerging dimension to this framework. Its biosynthesis from tryptophan, its interaction with calcium signaling, reactive oxygen species, MAPK cascades, and hormone networks, and its strong impact on photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, antioxidant defense, and secondary metabolism suggest that melatonin functions as both a metabolic regulator and a stress signal. Future studies should clarify its receptor-dependent and receptor-independent mechanisms, its subcellular distribution, its movement between tissues, and its interaction with classical hormone pathways. More crop-based studies are also needed to evaluate whether melatonin-related pathways can improve stress tolerance, yield stability, and quality traits under field conditions.

Overall, the emerging picture is that plant hormones and metabolites form a reciprocal regulatory network rather than separate signaling and metabolic modules. Hormones guide metabolic reprogramming, while metabolites determine hormone output by influencing biosynthesis, transport, perception, degradation, and downstream signaling. Understanding this reciprocal control will improve basic knowledge of plant biochemistry and physiology and provide new opportunities for crop improvement. The next phase of research should integrate precise molecular tools, spatial technologies, isotope-based flux analysis, and crop-level validation. Such an approach will allow researchers to move from descriptive associations toward mechanistic models that explain how plants coordinate growth, metabolism, and stress adaptation in changing environments.

Implications for plant science and agriculture

The reciprocal relationship between plant hormone signaling and metabolism has major implications for plant science research. It shifts the field from viewing hormones as isolated signaling molecules toward understanding them as parts of integrated metabolic networks. This view is important because plant growth, defense, stress tolerance, nutrient uptake, and yield formation depend on how hormones interact with carbon metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, sulfur metabolism, redox balance, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and energy sensing pathways. By studying these interactions together, researchers can better explain how plants coordinate local cellular responses with whole plant adaptation.

This framework also provides a stronger basis for identifying regulatory hubs. Metabolites such as sucrose, glucose, trehalose-6-phosphate, citrate, malate, succinate, 2-oxoglutarate, tryptophan, cysteine, glutathione, ascorbate, reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, and hydrogen sulfide not only support metabolism. They also influence hormone biosynthesis, transport, perception, degradation, and signal output. These metabolites may serve as key control points where multiple hormone pathways converge. Identifying such nodes can help researchers understand why plants show different responses across cell types, tissues, developmental stages, and stress conditions. For agriculture, hormone metabolism networks offer practical routes for improving crop performance. Many current crop improvement strategies target single genes or single hormone pathways, but these approaches may create tradeoffs, such as improved stress tolerance with reduced growth or yield. A network-based approach can help identify targets that improve stress resilience while maintaining productivity. For example, manipulating sugar signaling, redox balance, amino acid metabolism, sulfur assimilation, organic acid turnover, or hormone transport may improve drought tolerance, salinity tolerance, nutrient use efficiency, pathogen resistance, flowering, fruit quality, and yield stability.

This concept is especially relevant under climate change. Crops face combined stresses such as heat, drought, salinity, flooding, nutrient limitation, and pathogen pressure. These stresses rarely act alone in the field. Hormone metabolism crosstalk provides the regulatory flexibility that plants need to balance growth and survival under such complex conditions. Therefore, understanding this crosstalk can support the development of crop varieties that respond more efficiently to changing environments. Emerging technologies will accelerate this progress. Hormone biosensors, spatial metabolomics, single-cell omics, isotope tracing, genome editing, and predictive network modeling can help move plant science from descriptive pathway studies toward causal and quantitative understanding. These tools can reveal where hormones and metabolites interact, how fast these interactions occur, and which regulatory nodes control major traits. In the long term, integrating these technologies with crop breeding and biotechnology can help design plants with better resource use, stronger stress tolerance, stable yield, and improved nutritional quality. Overall, this review highlights hormone metabolism crosstalk as a central principle in plant biology and a promising foundation for future agricultural innovation. It provides a framework for linking molecular mechanisms with crop-level outcomes and shows how plant science can move toward predictive, systems-based strategies for improving crop resilience and productivity.

Conclusion

Plant hormone signaling and metabolism are inseparable components of an integrated regulatory system that enables plants to coordinate growth, development, and environmental adaptation. Rather than functioning as independent linear pathways, hormone networks operate through continuous bidirectional interactions with primary and secondary metabolism, allowing plants to dynamically balance resource allocation, energy status, redox homeostasis, and stress responses. The evidence synthesized in this review demonstrates that metabolites such as sugars, amino acids, organic acids, sulfur-containing compounds, lipid derivatives, and reactive oxygen species are not merely metabolic intermediates but also act as regulatory signals that influence hormone biosynthesis, transport, perception, signaling, and turnover. Likewise, phytohormones actively remodel metabolic pathways to optimize cellular function across developmental stages and fluctuating environmental conditions.

Recognizing hormone-metabolism crosstalk as a central organizing key shifts plant biology from isolated pathway analysis toward systems-level understanding. This perspective explains how diverse hormonal pathways converge through common metabolic hubs to generate coordinated physiological responses while also revealing why manipulation of single genes or hormones often produces context-dependent outcomes. Emerging technologies, including spatial and single-cell omics, genetically encoded biosensors, stable isotope tracing, genome editing, and computational network modeling, are now providing unprecedented opportunities to quantify these interactions with high spatial and temporal resolution and to construct predictive models of plant regulatory networks.

Future research should focus on identifying conserved metabolic signaling hubs, resolving tissue- and cell-specific regulatory circuits, and integrating multi-omics datasets across developmental and environmental contexts. Such efforts will facilitate the rational engineering of hormone-metabolism networks rather than individual signaling components, enabling more precise improvement of crop resilience, nutrient use efficiency, productivity, and climate adaptation. Ultimately, embracing the reciprocal relationship between hormone signaling and metabolism provides a comprehensive conceptual framework for advancing plant systems biology and developing sustainable strategies to secure agricultural productivity under increasingly challenging environmental conditions.
