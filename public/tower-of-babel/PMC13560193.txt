Tertiary lymphoid structures in breast cancer: Mechanistic insights, subtype-specific patterns, and therapeutic implications
Authors: Ying Liang, Kongming Wu, Lihui Pan, Miaomiao Jia, Wenrong Zhang, Xiaojun Zhang, Jinnan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer exhibits substantial molecular and immunological heterogeneity, with tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) emerging as key modulators of local antitumor immunity. TLSs display subtype-specific prevalence and maturity, with mature TLSs enriched in triple-negative (TNBC) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+) tumors, correlating with improved prognosis, response to neoadjuvant therapy, and sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas hormone receptor–positive (HR+) tumors often lack organized TLSs and show limited immunogenicity. TLS formation is orchestrated by cytokine–chemokine networks, lymphoid tissue organizer/inducer interactions, and vascular remodeling via high endothelial venules, yet the regulatory mechanisms and functional heterogeneity across subtypes remain incompletely understood. Detection strategies range from histopathology and multiplex immunohistochemistry to spatial transcriptomics and radiomics, but standardization, reproducibility, and clinical scalability remain major hurdles. Emerging interventions—including chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, chemokine delivery, and combinatorial strategies—can induce or mature TLSs, offering potential therapeutic leverage, yet safety, phenotypic fidelity, and context-dependent efficacy require careful evaluation. Collectively, TLSs represent a dynamic, therapeutically targetable component of the tumor microenvironment; however, translating TLS biology into precision immuno-oncology demands mechanistic insight, standardized assessment, and integration with spatial multi-omic frameworks to inform subtype-specific, clinically actionable strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04467-4.

Background

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in women and a leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1]. Contemporary global surveillance shows incidence rising in many countries, while mortality declines are largely confined to high Human Development Index (HDI) settings, revealing persistent inequities in early detection and access to effective treatment; projections indicate a substantial expansion of the burden by 2050, with disproportionate growth in lower-HDI regions. These trends highlight an imperative to expand early diagnosis and treatment access in low- and medium-HDI countries, thereby reducing inequities and monitoring progress toward cancer control goals [2]. Breast cancer is a biologically heterogeneous malignancy encompassing subtypes such as hormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), each exhibiting distinct molecular profiles and clinical behaviors [3]. In this review, breast cancer subtypes are primarily described using clinically defined categories, namely HR+, HER2+, and TNBC. Molecular terms such as basal-like are retained only when they were used in the original cited studies. HR+ tumors are typically hormone-driven and associated with relatively favorable prognosis, whereas TNBC exhibits higher aggressiveness, increased risk of recurrence, and limited targeted therapies [4, 5]. HER2 + tumors, while targetable with HER2-directed therapies, often present intermediate immunogenicity. Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has expanded treatment options for certain subtypes. TNBC and some HER2 + tumors often have higher mutational burdens and greater tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), which may contribute to enhanced immunotherapy responsiveness. In contrast, HR+ tumors generally exhibit lower immunogenicity and reduced responsiveness to ICIs [6, 7]. Recently, tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs)—ectopic lymphoid aggregates within tumors—have gained attention for their potential role in shaping local immune responses. TLS presence is associated with immune-active microenvironments and may serve as a biomarker for predicting response to immunotherapy, although it remains unclear whether TLSs are drivers of antitumor immunity or markers of pre-existing immune activity [8]. Understanding the formation, composition, and context-dependent significance of TLSs is therefore important for optimizing immunotherapeutic strategies in breast cancer.

TLSs are ectopic lymphoid organs that form in non-lymphoid tissues in response to chronic inflammation, autoimmune diseases, and tumors. These structures exhibit a level of organization similar to secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs), with spatially segregated B cell follicles, T cell zones, and high endothelial venules (HEVs) [9, 10]. TLSs are dynamic and progress through distinct developmental stages, ranging from early, loosely organized aggregates to fully structured formations containing germinal centers (GCs) [11, 12]. In the initial stage, TLSs consist of small, perivascular clusters of B and T cells, lacking the distinct compartments and specialized cells, such as follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) and HEVs, that characterize more mature TLSs. As TLSs mature, they display a clearer segregation of B and T cell areas and may begin to include FDC networks and early HEV formation, supporting antigen presentation and T-B cell interactions. In the most advanced stage, TLSs are highly organized, with T cell-rich zones adjacent to B cell-rich areas and fully developed GCs containing proliferating B cells, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, and plasmablasts [10, 12]. Clinically, mature TLSs are associated with enhanced immune responses and improved clinical outcomes. TLSs containing GCs have been linked to prolonged survival and increased efficacy of ICIs in patients with melanoma [13], lung cancer[14], colorectal cancer[15], and breast cancer [16]. These findings support the potential of mature TLSs as biomarkers for predicting treatment responses and as targets for therapeutic strategies.

In breast cancer specifically, TLSs are often observed in tumors with high TIL density, HER2 expression, and Ki-67, and are less common in HR+ tumors [16]. Notably, in TNBC, mature TLSs correlate with improved outcomes following neoadjuvant therapy (P < 0.05) [17], whereas in HER2 + disease, high TLS presence is associated with better therapeutic response; low-TLS tumors may benefit from alternative or dual-targeted strategies [18].

This review situates TLSs within the heterogeneous molecular landscape of breast cancer, outlines their staged architecture, and synthesizes evidence linking TLS presence and maturity to prognosis and therapy response, particularly in immunologically “hot” subtypes such as TNBC and HER2+. We further summarize current and emerging methods for TLS detection, from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) to multiplex assays, spatial transcriptomics, and radiomics, and discuss how standardized scoring could enable TLS-guided patient stratification. Finally, we highlight strategies under investigation to induce or mature TLSs and their potential to synergize with checkpoint blockade, providing a framework for integrating TLS biology into precision breast cancer care.

Literature search

A structured literature search was conducted to improve the transparency and reproducibility of this narrative review. Comprehensive searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. The search period covered articles published from January 2015 to February 2026, and the literature search was updated before manuscript resubmission.The core search strategy combined terms related to breast cancer and tertiary lymphoid structures, including (“breast cancer” OR “breast neoplasms” OR “triple-negative breast cancer” OR “HER2-positive breast cancer” OR “hormone receptor-positive breast cancer”) AND (“tertiary lymphoid structures” OR “TLS” OR “ectopic lymphoid structures”). Additional terms were used to capture relevant biological, clinical, and translational aspects, including “tumor microenvironment,” “tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes,” “B cells,” “germinal center,” “high endothelial venules,” “spatial transcriptomics,” “single-cell RNA sequencing,” “immunotherapy,” “immune checkpoint inhibitors,” “neoadjuvant therapy,” and “chemotherapy.”

After removal of duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed according to predefined eligibility criteria. Studies were included if they: (i) were original research articles or authoritative review papers; (ii) investigated the biological mechanisms, maturation, cellular composition, spatial distribution, prognostic significance, predictive value, detection methods, or therapeutic relevance of TLSs in breast cancer; (iii) were published in peer-reviewed journals; and (iv) were written in English. Studies focusing on other tumor types were considered only when they provided mechanistic or therapeutic insights directly relevant to TLS formation, maturation, or therapeutic modulation. Exclusion criteria included: (i) case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, or commentaries without sufficient original or interpretive data; (ii) studies with unclear methodology or insufficient relevance to TLS biology; and (iii) duplicate publications reporting the same data.

Because this article is a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis was performed. Instead, the selected literature was synthesized thematically according to TLS architecture and maturation, detection approaches, breast cancer subtype-specific patterns, prognostic and predictive value, translational challenges, therapy-induced TLS remodeling, and TLS-directed therapeutic strategies.

TLS architecture and identification in breast tumors

Cellular and molecular basis of TLS neogenesis

TLS formation is primarily orchestrated by interactions between lymphoid tissue organizer (LTo) cells and lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells, regulated by cytokine and chemokine signaling as well as multiple downstream pathways. LTo cells, which include stromal cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, FDCs and fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs), provide a structural scaffold, whereas hematopoietic precursors including B cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) and T helper 17 (Th17) cells serve as LTi cells [19].

Cytokine–chemokine and stromal–vascular networks

Tumor-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) can activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling in the tumor microenvironment (TME), inducing lymphoid chemokines such as CXCL13, CCL19, and CCL21 [20, 21]. These chemokines recruit LTi cells, naive lymphocytes, and dendritic cells to the developing TLS niche, where membrane-bound lymphotoxin α1β2 (LTα1β2) engages lymphotoxin β receptor (LTβR) on stromal cells, upregulating vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), thereby promoting lymphocyte aggregation [22, 23]. LTi–stromal interactions also induce HEVs via LTβR signaling, which are both structural components and markers of TLS activity [24]. In a retrospective cohort of 146 invasive breast cancers, high HEV density was associated with lower relapse risk and longer metastasis-free, disease-free, and overall survival (OS) [25]. This suggests that HEVs are not only structural components but may also indicate functional TLS activity. Nascent TLSs organize into B cell follicles and T cell zones under the guidance of chemokines CXCL13 and CCL19/CCL21 through their receptors CXCR5 and CCR7 [26], with Tfh cells orchestrating B cell maturation and germinal center (GC) formation [27]. During TLS maturation, tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells (DCs) present antigens to naive CD4⁺ T cells, promoting differentiation into Tfh cells. Together with myeloid-derived cytokines (e.g., B-cell activating factor (BAFF) and IL-21), this process is associated with B cell activation, GC formation, somatic hypermutation, and the generation of plasma and memory B cells, which may contribute to antitumor immunity [28–30]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and B-cell receptor (BCR)-lineage analyses in TNBC show clonally expanded, class-switched, hypermutated, and memory-biased intratumoral B cells, which correlate with improved patient survival [31]. CCL19⁺ DCs have been suggested to function in an LTi-like capacity, potentially promoting TLS formation and enhancing response to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade in TNBC [32]. Importantly, TLS prevalence is not always correlated with programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression or bulk TIL density, suggesting that TLS-mediated immune activity can, in some contexts, operate independently of PD-1/PD-L1 signaling [8].

Together, these cellular and stromal components form a spatially organized immune network within breast cancer TLSs, shaped by cytokine–chemokine axes and downstream signaling pathways.

STAT3 pathway activated by cytokines (IL-6/IL-7/IL-21) promotes Th17 differentiation during TLS formation and controls FDC development [33]. When dysregulated, STAT3 can promote expression of immunosuppressive factors, including IL-10 and TGF-β, impairing dendritic cell maturation and T-cell function and potentially facilitating immune evasion [34]. Aberrant STAT3 signaling promotes breast tumor progression through the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway [35], supporting tumor growth and metastasis by suppressing immune responses and regulating genes like PD-L1 [36]. NF-κB signaling maintains chronic inflammation and drives tumor growth and metastasis, shaping a tumor-supportive microenvironment; it also cooperates with STAT3 and IL-6 to amplify breast tumor cell migration and metastatic spread [37]. Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibits DC migration into the TME [38], while Wnt3a signaling promotes estrogen independence in TNBC by suppressing aromatase expression, supporting tumor growth.

Overall, current evidence suggests that TLS development in breast cancer is not a linear or uniform process, but rather a coordinated sequence of immune, stromal, and vascular events linking innate inflammatory signals with adaptive antitumor immunity. Initial inflammatory cues and lymphoid chemokines recruit immune cells into the tumor microenvironment, where activated fibroblasts, endothelial cells, dendritic cells, Tfh cells, and B cells cooperate to support lymphoid aggregation, compartmentalization, and eventual germinal center formation. However, the direction and functional quality of this process are strongly influenced by tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental factors, including molecular subtype, mutational burden, metabolic status, and baseline immune infiltration. These contextual variables may determine whether TLSs evolve into organized, immunologically active structures or remain immature, poorly organized, and potentially suppressive.

Despite increasing mechanistic insight, several important uncertainties remain. The cellular populations that initiate TLS formation in established breast tumors have not been fully defined, particularly those that functionally substitute for classical LTi and LTo cells. It also remains unclear which molecular programs determine productive TLS maturation versus dysfunctional lymphoid aggregation across TNBC, HER2+, and HR+ breast cancers. Moreover, although LTβR signaling, CXCL13–CXCR5, CCL19/CCL21–CCR7, STAT3, NF-κB, and stromal–vascular remodeling have been implicated in TLS neogenesis, much of this evidence comes from preclinical systems or tumor types other than breast cancer. Therefore, the clinical relevance, therapeutic manipulability, and safety of these pathways in patients with breast cancer require further prospective validation before TLS-directed interventions can be rationally implemented.

TLS maturation and functional states

Across multiple cancer types, TLS maturity has emerged as an important prognostic factor. Mature TLSs, defined by well-organized B and T cell zones and the presence of GCs, are generally associated with stronger antigen-specific immune activity and improved responsiveness to immunotherapies, including checkpoint blockade and CAR-T cells. In contrast, immature or non-classical TLSs, which appear as disorganized immune cell clusters, may fail to support effective antitumor immunity or reflect persistent chronic inflammation. Cancer-associated TLSs are often staged by morphology and expression of the FDC marker CD21 and the GC marker CD23 into three maturation tiers: early TLS (E-TLS, CD21 − CD23−), primary follicle-like TLS (PFL-TLS, CD21 + CD23−), and secondary follicle-like TLS (SFL-TLS, CD21 + CD23+). Methodological challenges in TLS maturity assessment

Nevertheless, the clinical translation of TLS maturation as a reproducible biomarker and therapeutic target remains challenging. Current histopathological classification systems, which commonly divide TLSs into early, intermediate, and mature stages, are still largely descriptive and lack unified quantitative thresholds. This creates variability in TLS scoring across studies and limits direct comparison between cohorts. Although the presence of germinal centers is often regarded as a hallmark of mature TLSs, the biological and prognostic significance of specific GC features—including follicle size, proliferative activity reflected by Ki-67 or BCL6 expression, and the extent of plasma cell differentiation—has not been systematically standardized. In addition, most current assessments rely on static tissue sections, which provide only a single spatial and temporal snapshot and cannot fully capture TLS evolution during tumor progression or after treatment. Therefore, longitudinal approaches, including imaging-based methods, circulating immune or chemokine biomarkers, and repeated spatial profiling, may be required to monitor TLS dynamics more accurately. Resolving these methodological gaps will be essential before TLS maturity can be reliably incorporated into precision immuno-oncology.

Approaches for TLS detection and characterization

Accurate and standardized identification of TLSs is essential for evaluating their biological and clinical relevance in breast cancer. However, TLS assessment remains methodologically heterogeneous because studies differ in histological criteria, marker panels, scoring thresholds, maturity definitions, and whether spatial localization or cellular composition is incorporated. These inconsistencies limit cross-study comparability and complicate the development of clinically reproducible TLS-based biomarkers. Accordingly, current detection strategies can be broadly categorized into clinically deployable methods, near-term translational tools, and exploratory research platforms, as summarized below.

Clinically deployable histopathological methods

Clinically deployable methods include H&E staining, which allows evaluation of TLS morphology, including germinal center (GC) formation and high endothelial venule (HEV)-like structures [39]. H&E is simple and cost-effective, but its phenotypic resolution is limited, and manual annotation is labor-intensive and subject to interobserver variability. IHC and immunofluorescence (IF) extend these capabilities by detecting multiple markers, such as CD3, CD20, CXCL13, and CXCL21, enabling characterization of B and T cell zones and the spatial organization of TLSs [40]. Detailed characterization often requires multiple tissue sections, which may constrain throughput. Helmink et al. demonstrated co-localization of CD20⁺ B cells with CD21⁺ follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, FOXP3⁺ regulatory T cells, and MECA79⁺ HEVs within GCs [41], while Stowman et al. mapped TLS architecture with a six-marker panel, revealing central B cell zones surrounded by vascular and immune cell subsets [42].

Multiplex and AI-assisted translational approaches

Multiplex IHC (mIHC) platforms, such as Opal™-TSA, further enhance simultaneous detection of up to seven markers—including CD4, CD8, CD19, CD21, DC-LAMP, and PNAd—allowing assessment of TLS maturity and spatial interactions [43]. Translational approaches bridge research and near-term clinical application. Deep learning–based segmentation allows automatic quantification of TLS areas from H&E or mIHC slides. Chen et al. developed a TLS ratio, defined as the segmented TLS area over the total tissue area, which correlated strongly with B cell abundance and CXCL13 expression. Incorporation of TLS ratio into prognostic models alongside tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) generally improved OS prediction compared with TNM staging alone [39]. Similarly, Solis et al. applied a five-tier TLS scoring system to 989 evaluable breast cancers, showing TLS enrichment in HER2⁺ and TNBC subtypes, strong association with higher TILs, and stepwise B-/T-cell activation on gene expression. The HER2DX 14-gene IgG signature tracked TLS biology, independently predicting higher TLS categories, including TLS 2b (AUC = 0.83; OR = 1.62; IgG-high vs. TLS 2b OR = 2.41) [44]. Although the HER2DX IgG signature, TLS classification, and TIL abundance are interrelated, they are not interchangeable biomarkers. Therefore, TLS positivity should not be interpreted as an entirely independent prognostic variable unless analyses account for bulk TIL abundance or demonstrate TLS-specific predictive value beyond a broader immune-inflamed phenotype.

Spatial multi-omics, radiomics, and exploratory platforms

Experimental and exploratory methods provide high-resolution molecular and spatial insight. Spatial transcriptomics and scRNA-seq define TLS-associated gene-expression signatures, map cell-type composition, and characterize interactions among B cells, CD8⁺ T cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts. Andersson et al. applied spatial transcriptomics in HER2 + breast tumors to identify TLS-like structures and type I interferon programs [45], while Fan et al. delineated 18 cell types within TLS-associated regions, revealing macrophage reprogramming and lymphocyte recruitment via CXCL12–CXCR4 [46]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based radiomics has been explored for noninvasive TLS evaluation in 242 localized breast cancers, with radiomics and nomogram models distinguishing TLS-positive from TLS-negative tumors (AUC 0.766–0.820) and associating with disease-free and distant metastasis-free survival, though not 3-year OS [47]. In TNBC, an XGBoost model trained on DCE-MRI features generated a radiomics–TLS score (rTLS) predicting histologic TLS status and correlating with disease-free survival (DFS) [48]. Advanced 3D imaging techniques, such as wildDISCO in preclinical mouse models, allow visualization of CD23⁺ B-cell clusters across primary and metastatic sites, demonstrating TLS-like aggregates throughout intact tissue [49]. Although not yet applied clinically, such 3D mapping exemplifies future directions for nondestructive TLS visualization.

Standardization of TLS markers and scoring

Despite these advances, TLS detection lacks standardized definitions, and marker selection, TLS classification, and maturity grading vary across studies, limiting comparability. Cost, technical complexity, and field coverage further constrain broader adoption of multiplex assays, spatial transcriptomics, AI-based segmentation, radiomics, and 3D imaging. Nevertheless, the combination of histology, multi-omic assays, and AI-driven analysis offers a powerful framework to map TLS comprehensively and may ultimately guide strategies to induce or mature TLSs for improved breast cancer immunotherapy.

Cell-level specific biomarkers are core tools for histological identification and maturity grading of TLSs, and their selection directly affects the accuracy of TLS detection and the comparability between studies. Prevalence and distribution of TLSs across breast cancer subtypes

TLSs are unevenly distributed across breast cancer subtypes, reflecting the interplay between tumor-intrinsic features and the immune microenvironment. Their prevalence and maturity vary significantly depending on the molecular and histological characteristics of the tumor. Immunologically “hot” tumors, including TNBC and HER2 + cancers, often harbor more mature TLSs, which are associated with improved response to therapy and favorable prognosis. In contrast, HR+ tumors, which often exhibit an immunologically “cold” microenvironment, typically show lower TLS density and reduced maturation.

This section reviews the current evidence on TLS distribution across breast cancer subtypes, highlighting their potential prognostic relevance, functional heterogeneity, and implications for emerging immunotherapeutic strategies.

TNBC

TLSs are enriched in TNBC, with estimates suggesting presence in approximately 83.7% of cases [8]. Integrating clinical outcomes, TLSs have been generally associated with improved response to neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) and relapse-free survival. In a retrospective cohort of 397 chemotherapy-naive early TNBC tumors, TLSs were common (88%) and primarily peritumoral, correlating with basal-like, TIL-rich inflammatory patterns, increased lymphoid and myeloid cell densities, and PD-1/PD-L1 and TIGIT/PVR expression. Peritumoral TLSs sometimes outperformed TIL density as a prognostic indicator [56]. Notably, this study directly compared TLS-related parameters with conventional TIL assessment. Although high TIL levels were associated with longer relapse-free survival in univariate analysis, peritumoral TLS abundance, rather than the classical TIL variable, was retained in the multivariate model, suggesting that spatial lymphoid organization may provide prognostic information beyond bulk lymphocyte abundance. In neoadjuvant TNBC cohorts, TLS heterogeneity and maturity have been associated with improved NAT response and prognosis, with TLS-high tumors showing more active B-cell and cytotoxic T-cell immune features (P < 0.05) [17]. In parallel, radiomics-based machine learning models have been developed to predict histological TLS status and treatment response in TNBC, supporting the potential of non-invasive TLS assessment for personalized treatment planning [48].

Mechanistic studies have elucidated factors limiting TLS formation in TNBC. Calado et al. showed that tumor-derived lactate suppresses B-cell infiltration and TLS formation by disrupting mitochondrial metabolism and impairing chemotaxis. Importantly, targeting lactate metabolism in combination with chemokine delivery and CD40 stimulation restored B-cell trafficking and induced TLS formation, suggesting a metabolic reprogramming strategy to convert immune-cold tumors into immunologically active environments [57].

Further analyses of molecular heterogeneity have refined TLS characterization. Shen et al. analyzed 240 stage I–III TNBC tumors using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and consensus clustering, identifying three biology-anchored subtypes: C1 (hypoxia/angiogenesis/glycolysis-high with NOX1–CD73 upregulation, worst DFS), C2 (WNT/NOTCH-enriched), and C3 (IFN-γ–inflamed with immune infiltration consistent with mature TLS). An eight-gene recurrence-risk model (ABCD2, ACSM3, ADCYAP1, ADM, CXCL13, GLO1, PIGR, RRP8) was built and externally validated, stratifying 3-year recurrence risk with AUCs of 0.874, 0.844, and 0.761, emphasizing the prognostic relevance of TLS-linked immune signatures [58].

High-resolution spatial mapping has further enhanced understanding of TLS architecture and tumor heterogeneity. Sheridan et al. created a 3D spatial transcriptomic map from serial sections of a single TNBC core-needle biopsy, identifying stromal plasma infiltrates and TLSs more reliably than conventional 2D slices, illustrating both intratumoral heterogeneity and methodological strengths and limitations of core-biopsy profiling [59].

Collectively, these studies indicate that TLS presence and maturity in TNBC are generally associated with enhanced immune responsiveness and improved NAT outcomes, though observed associations may be potentially influenced by TIL abundance and tumor-intrinsic metabolic factors. Advances in machine learning, spatial transcriptomics, and immunometabolic interventions are reshaping our understanding of TLS biology in TNBC, with strong implications for personalized immunotherapy.

HER2 + breast cancer

TLSs are increasingly recognized as important immune features in HER2 + breast cancer, contributing to anti-tumor immunity and potentially influencing treatment responsiveness. Early clinical evidence from Lee et al. demonstrated that TLS presence, together with TIL densities, was generally associated with improved DFS in HER2 + patients receiving adjuvant trastuzumab. Because TLS presence was closely linked to abundant TILs and peritumoral lymphocytic infiltration in this cohort, these findings are best interpreted as evidence of an immune-enriched HER2 + tumor context rather than definitive proof that TLSs independently drive improved trastuzumab-associated outcomes [60]. Follow-up analyses by the same group identified TLS-associated molecules, including HMGB1 and HMGN1, which correlated with TIL density in HER2 + tumors, suggesting these factors may facilitate TLS formation [61]. Mechanistically, Lee et al. also showed that Myxovirus Resistance A (MxA), an interferon-stimulated gene, positively correlated with TLS and TIL presence, supporting a link between innate immune activation and TLS development [62]. Kim et al. further implicated endoplasmic reticulum stress via the PERK-eIF2α pathway in TLS-associated immune activation, expanding understanding of molecular pathways shaping TLS function in HER2 + breast cancer [63].

Recent multi-omics and spatial transcriptomic studies have provided deeper insights into TLS architecture and functional coordination. Andersson et al. used spatial deconvolution to map TLSs in HER2 + tumors and delineated their interactions with CD8 + T cells and B cells, highlighting coordinated immune activity within the TME [45]. Dieleman et al. reported that germinal centers quantified in tumor-positive sentinel lymph nodes (SLNpos) may serve as indicators of humoral anti-tumor immune responses, emphasizing the need to clarify underlying mechanisms and standardize prognostic assessment of SLN GCs [64]. Li et al. applied predictive modeling to I-SPY2 trial data, showing that high TLS expression was generally associated with superior NAT response, whereas low TLS expression identified patients who might benefit from dual-targeted therapy [18]. Spatial analysis by Sanfeliu et al. further revealed a correlation between TLS presence and the extension of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) in HER2 + lesions, suggesting that TLSs may arise early in tumorigenesis and reflect ongoing immune surveillance even at pre-invasive stages [65].

In summary, converging evidence from clinicopathologic, mechanistic, and spatial-omics studies indicates that TLSs in HER2 + breast cancer are generally associated with favorable immune responsiveness and treatment outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of standardized TLS assessment and support prospective TLS-guided therapeutic strategies in HER2 + breast cancer.

HR+ breast cancer

HR+ breast cancers generally exhibit lower TLS density and maturity, reflecting a relatively immunologically “cold” microenvironment. These tumors typically have low proliferation rates, minimal genomic instability, and fewer tumor-associated neoantigens, limiting recruitment and organization of immune cells into structured TLS. Immunosuppressive transcriptional regulators, such as FOXP1, frequently overexpressed in ER+ tumors, repress lymphoid-organizing chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL13, CCL20) and effector cytokines (IL-2, IL-21, IFN-γ, GZMB), while promoting immunosuppressive mediators such as TGF-β and IL-10, further inhibiting lymphocyte infiltration and TLS formation [66]. Notably, a subset of HR+/HER2 − tumors in very young patients (≤ 35 years) can display heightened immune activity, although TLS formation in these cases remains limited [67]. As a result, luminal tumors often lack the immunological signals required to attract and organize immune cells into structured TLS.

By comparison with TNBC and HER2 + tumors, HR+ tumors rarely develop mature TLS, and observed associations between TLS and prognosis or treatment response are generally weaker. This background highlights the subtype-specific variation in TLS prevalence and underscores the importance of considering immune contexture when evaluating TLS as a prognostic or predictive biomarker. Clinical significance and translational considerations of TLSs in breast cancer

Prognostic and predictive value of TLSs

TLSs in breast cancer range from poorly organized to highly structured forms, with their immune impact depending on maturation. Immature TLSs are disordered and may favor immunosuppressive cells, limiting anti-tumor T cell activity. Mature TLSs have distinct B and T cell compartments and active germinal centers, promoting tumor-specific T cell responses and antibody production. These antibodies, along with secreted cytokines and chemokines, help attract additional immune cells and enhance local and systemic anti-tumor immunity [68, 69].

TLSs have emerged as clinically meaningful biomarkers in breast cancer, particularly in TNBC, with relevance for both prognosis and treatment selection. In TNBC treated with NAT, Wang et al. demonstrated that post-treatment remodeling of the tumor microenvironment includes changes in TLSs; the presence of mature TLSs after NAT is generally associated with improved therapeutic outcomes, supporting post-NAT immune profiling to guide personalized care [17].

TLS-linked molecular biomarkers

Li et al. integrated over 6,000 breast-cancer transcriptomes with network- and machine learning–based analyses to identify LGALS2 as a TLS-linked marker. Single-cell and spatial analyses localized LGALS2 to TLS-associated dendritic-cell activation, and higher expression marked a “hot” tumor microenvironment with improved survival, validated by H&E/IHC and in vivo studies, supporting LGALS2 for TLS-directed immunotherapy and patient stratification [70]. In basal-like, node-negative TNBC, high primary-tumor CTLA4 expression (above the 63rd percentile) consistently identifies a very low-risk subgroup with excellent long-term outcomes, validated in Oslo1, SCAN-B, and METABRIC cohorts, correlating with TIL and TLS presence and suggesting potential de-escalation of adjuvant chemotherapy [71].

Spatial multi-omics for clinical translation

Despite the growing recognition of TLSs as prognostic and predictive biomarkers in breast cancer, their clinical utility hinges on the integration of spatial multiomics into routine diagnostic and therapeutic workflows. Stylianakis et al. provide a timely synthesis of the technological, operational, and translational considerations required to bridge experimental spatial biology and clinical practice [72]. Current spatial multiomics technologies—including high-resolution imaging platforms (MERFISH, seqFISH+), array-based spatial transcriptomics (Visium, GeoMx), multiplexed proteomics (IMC, CODEX, PhenoCycler), spatial metabolomics (MALDI-MSI, DESI-MSI), and epigenomic assays (ATAC-seq, CUT&Tag)—enable increasingly detailed characterization of TLS architecture, immune-stromal interactions, and spatially heterogeneous tumor niches. Spatial multiomics is also being operationalized through automated image registration, machine learning-driven cell segmentation, and multi-omic layer integration tools, such as Tangram, MaxFuse, SANTO, SpatialQC, and STdGCN. These pipelines may support more accurate mapping of TLSs and immune-stromal interactions, providing spatially resolved readouts with potential clinical interpretability.

Remaining barriers to clinical implementation

However, several barriers must be addressed before spatial TLS profiling can be routinely implemented in clinical practice. Key limitations include tissue compatibility, particularly FFPE versus fresh-frozen requirements, scalability, cost, workflow complexity, and standardization across laboratories. In addition, multi-modal data integration requires robust computational frameworks, quality control procedures, and metadata management to ensure reproducibility, cross-platform consistency, and compliance with regulatory expectations. To move spatial TLS profiling toward clinical implementation, spatial assays need to be aligned with routine pathology through simplified FFPE-compatible panels, standardized pre-analytic procedures, and clinically interpretable readouts. Such efforts may enable integration into neoadjuvant monitoring, surgical planning, post-treatment risk stratification, and immunotherapy selection.

Spatial multiomics may provide actionable insights for TLS-informed treatment strategies by combining morphological TLS assessment with spatial transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and epigenomic data. This integrated framework could help identify immune-hot and immune-cold regions, residual disease niches, and therapy-resistant clones. Importantly, clinical translation will require multi-center validation, harmonized metadata standards, interoperable data formats, and regulatory acceptance before spatial TLS profiling can move from experimental discovery toward actionable diagnostics and precision treatment planning in breast cancer.

Limitations and controversies in TLS research

TLSs in breast cancer are recognized as key mediators of anti-tumor immunity; however, their functional roles are heterogeneous and strongly influenced by maturation stage. Immature TLSs, lacking organized GCs, often form microenvironments enriched in immunosuppressive populations such as Tregs and M2 macrophages, along with elevated PD-L1 and IL-10 expression, which may suppress effector T cells and promote tumor progression [73, 74]. By contrast, mature TLSs with defined B cell and T cell zones and active GCs serve as hubs for adaptive immunity, facilitating CD8⁺ T cell activation and B cell-mediated antibody production [75, 76]. This dichotomy underscores the need to assess TLSs beyond mere presence, incorporating architecture, cellular composition, spatial location, and density to improve prognostic accuracy [77, 78]. However, the absence of harmonized TLS definitions and maturity-grading criteria remains a major source of inconsistency across studies. Consequently, reported associations between TLSs and clinical outcomes may vary depending on whether studies define TLSs by H&E morphology, B/T-cell aggregation, FDC networks, HEVs, germinal centers, or transcriptomic signatures. This methodological variability, together with subtype heterogeneity, retrospective study design, and limited prospective validation, complicates the interpretation of TLSs as prognostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets [79, 80]. Accordingly, while TLSs have therapeutic potential, careful evaluation of their functional state is essential to avoid overestimating their beneficial effects [81].

However, converting this functional model of TLS maturation into a clinically actionable framework remains difficult. A major unresolved issue is that the molecular programs determining whether an immature TLS progresses into an effective antitumor immune hub or instead persists as a dysfunctional, immunosuppressive aggregate remain insufficiently defined. This uncertainty is particularly important in breast cancer, where TLS biology may differ substantially across TNBC, HER2+, and HR+ tumors. In addition, much of the evidence linking TLS maturity with prognosis or therapeutic response is derived from retrospective analyses, often involving heterogeneous treatment regimens, variable scoring systems, and limited control for confounding immune features such as bulk TIL density. Large prospective studies using harmonized TLS definitions and uniformly treated cohorts are therefore required to determine whether TLS maturity has independent predictive value beyond established clinicopathological and immune biomarkers. Finally, although therapeutic induction or maturation of TLSs is an attractive strategy, most current approaches remain preclinical or exploratory, and it is still unclear whether they can generate stable, spatially organized, functionally productive, and safe TLSs in patients. Resolving these issues will be essential before TLSs can move from descriptive biomarkers to reliable components of precision cancer immunotherapy.

Therapy-induced TLS formation and maturation in breast cancer

The predictive value of TLSs extends to chemotherapy and immunotherapy responses. Bertucci et al. validated a TLS signature as an independent predictor of pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) [82], while Wang et al. showed that high TLS signatures are generally associated with improved immunotherapy response, suppressed cell-proliferation pathways, lower TP53 mutation rates, and increased infiltration by B cells, CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, and M1 macrophages [83]. Using single RNA molecule–resolution profiling of longitudinal biopsies from TLS-enriched TNBC, Mebane et al. introduced a computational framework to align TLS–tumor spatial trajectories and showed that high baseline T-cell infiltration predicted tumor eradication with pembrolizumab, whereas low malignant cell–T-cell interaction at baseline progresses through CXCL9⁺ macrophage influx after pembrolizumab and subsequent infiltration of T cells with CXCL9-associated programs before tumor-cell clearance after radiotherapy [84]. In a retrospective cohort of elderly HR+/HER2 − advanced breast cancer, adding low-dose oral cyclophosphamide to endocrine therapy produced a modest increase in clinical control rate (CCR) and objective response rate (ORR) and significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) (13 vs. 11 months; P = 0.03), with benefit linked to decreased circulating CD4 + CD25+Foxp3 + Tregs (P < 0.001) and to the presence of TLSs, where higher pretreatment TLS resident Treg density also predicted better efficacy, supporting TLS-informed immunomodulatory endocrine strategies [85].

In the therapeutic setting, TLS-related findings require particularly cautious interpretation. An increase in TLS abundance after treatment does not necessarily indicate de novo TLS induction, functional maturation, or a direct causal contribution to therapeutic response. Instead, it may reflect selective preservation of pre-existing immune-active regions, broader treatment-induced inflammation, changes in tumor cellularity, or shifts in the visibility of lymphoid aggregates after tumor regression. Moreover, different therapies may exert opposing effects on TLS biology: some may enhance antigen release, chemokine production, HEV formation, and lymphoid organization, whereas others may disrupt lymphocyte viability, stromal integrity, or germinal-center-like activity. Therefore, therapy-associated TLS remodeling should be assessed not only by abundance, but also by maturation state, spatial localization, cellular composition, and functional readouts such as B-cell activation, T-cell clonality, and effector programs.

TLSs are dynamically modulated by therapeutic interventions, influencing both antitumor immunity and treatment outcomes. Chemotherapy can induce de novo TLS formation, remodel existing structures, or, in some contexts, impair TLS function through lymphocyte depletion or stromal damage. Notably, cisplatin-based neoadjuvant therapy in hepatoblastoma [86] and gemcitabine plus cisplatin in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [87] increased intratumoral TLSs and promoted effector T cell differentiation, while carboplatin–paclitaxel in ovarian cancer enhanced TLS maturation and expanded T cell factor 1-positive (TCF1⁺) PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, reflecting a renewable antitumor T cell pool [88]. Mechanistically, chemotherapy-induced DNA damage, immunogenic cell death, and local inflammatory cues enhance antigen presentation and vascular–stromal remodeling, supporting TLS establishment. Clinically, TLS-related gene signatures predict neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in HER2-negative breast cancer, and circulating chemokines such as CXCL13 may serve as minimally invasive markers of TLS activity.

Radiotherapy can transiently disrupt TLSs, but appropriate dosing and fractionation often promote remodeling and maturation, particularly when combined with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING)-mediated type I interferon signaling and high endothelial venule (HEV) formation facilitate TLS induction, whereas excessively high single-fraction doses may limit these effects [89]. TLS density and maturity under different neoadjuvant regimens correlate with immune infiltration and clinical outcomes.

ICB further shapes TLSs, both as a predictive biomarker and through functional remodeling. Mature TLSs with organized HEVs support recruitment and differentiation of PD-1⁺ TCF1⁺ progenitor CD8⁺ T cells [90], and TLS spatial distribution particularly intratumoral localization modulates response [91]. ICB also alters regulatory circuits within TLSs, including Tfr/Treg populations, highlighting the context-dependent nature of TLS-mediated immunity [92].

Collectively, these findings highlight TLSs as dynamic, treatment-responsive structures that integrate chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy signals, offering predictive value for patient outcomes and potential mechanistic targets for therapeutic modulation. It is important to note, however, that direct evidence for TLS induction by specific therapies in breast cancer remains limited, and the clinical impact of manipulating TLSs in TLS-low tumors remains an area of active investigation.

TLS-directed interventions: translational and preclinical insights

TLS–directed strategies in breast cancer are moving from correlative biomarkers toward actionable targets. In HER2-negative disease, a tumor TLS gene signature tracks with higher pathologic complete response (pCR) rates to NACT and signals potential benefit from immunotherapy, supporting the possibility that TLSs may reflect functionally relevant immune organization rather than merely passive immune infiltration [82]. In early TNBC, peritumoral TLS abundance is frequent and, importantly, outperforms conventional TIL scoring for relapse-free survival, supporting TLS-anchored risk stratification on routine H&E/HES Sect. [93]. Beyond abundance alone, vascular programs that enable TLS biology matter: an intratumoral HEV transcriptional signature in breast cancer aligns with TLS presence, dense T/B-cell infiltration, and improved survival, highlighting HEV/TLS axes as therapeutic levers [94]. Because response rates to ICIs remain modest in TNBC, combination strategies that amplify intratumoral immunity and TLS formation are being explored. In an immunocompetent TNBC mouse model, combining anti–PD-1 with the CXCR4 inverse agonist balixafortide curtailed tumor growth and improved survival versus monotherapy, increased intratumoral TLSs, reduced myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)-mediated suppression, and shifted T cells from exhaustion to effector states by single-cell RNA-seq, supporting CXCR4 blockade as a strategy to potentiate checkpoint therapy [95]. TLSs also carry prognostic weight in metastatic settings: in breast-cancer brain metastases, higher TLS density associates with longer PFS and OS and yields nomograms with strong discrimination [96]. Radiomics- and AI-based approaches are also emerging as non-invasive tools for predicting TLS presence and neoadjuvant treatment response in multicenter TNBC cohorts, thereby supporting TLS-based patient selection and response monitoring [48]. Collectively, these data support a pragmatic development path in breast cancer: standardize TLS/HEV assessment, enrich trials by TLS signatures, pair ICIs with modalities that foster vascular/lymphoid organization, and use TLS-aware imaging/pathomic tools for patient selection and monitoring (Fig. 2).

Preclinical studies propose several strategies to increase intratumoral lymphoid organization. Vascular and gene-delivery approaches that drive lymphoid organogenesis, such as LIGHT pathway activation (e.g., vascular-targeted or adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated LIGHT delivery), have shown in preclinical systems the capacity to normalize aberrant vessels, induce HEVs, seed TLS-like aggregates, and enrich stem-like TCF1⁺ CD8⁺ precursors that sustain memory and checkpoint responsiveness [27, 97]. Clinical translation of these strategies will require careful consideration of delivery methods, vector safety, and off-target effects. Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) offers a clinically accessible priming strategy that can increase TLS quantity/maturity and synergize with PD-1 blockade in lung cancer models and small patient series [98]. However, fractionation, dosing, and patient-specific safety remain critical challenges for clinical implementation. Oncolytic virotherapy likewise appears to amplify TLS programs, for example, oncolytic herpes simplex virus (oHSV) upregulating CXCL10 and recruiting CXCR3⁺ T cells, with enhanced anti-PD-1 activity in preclinical work [99]. Translation to breast cancer will require evaluation of viral tropism, immune-related adverse events, and safety considerations. Chemokine-centric cell/gene approaches are also clinically tractable; intratumoral CCL21-engineered DCs in non–small cell lung cancer exemplify targeted chemoattraction that can reorganize the microenvironment toward TLS-like immunity [100]. Challenges for clinical application include intratumoral delivery, systemic effects, and heterogeneity of patient tumors. Although these interventional strategies are promising, translation to breast cancer will require careful attention to delivery feasibility, off-tumor effects, and vector/radiation safety.

Despite the therapeutic appeal of inducing or remodeling TLSs, translating these preclinical strategies into safe and effective breast cancer treatments remains a substantial challenge. One major concern is safety: artificially promoting lymphoid neogenesis may provoke excessive inflammation, autoimmune-like reactions, or ectopic immune activation beyond the tumor site. Therapeutic benefit is also likely to be highly context-dependent, shaped by breast cancer subtype, baseline immune infiltration, tumor metabolic state, prior treatment exposure, and the pre-existing stromal–vascular architecture. At present, there are no well-validated biomarkers that can reliably identify which patients are most likely to benefit from TLS-inducing interventions, particularly among TLS-low or immune-cold tumors. Moreover, most current evidence is derived from preclinical models or small exploratory studies, whereas large prospective trials using TLS induction, maturation, spatial organization, and functional immune activity as predefined endpoints are still lacking. Thus, before TLS-directed therapy can be incorporated into clinical breast cancer management, future studies must establish not only whether TLSs can be induced, but also whether the induced structures are stable, spatially organized, immunologically productive, and clinically safe.

Conclusion

TLSs are increasingly recognized as dynamic and spatially organized immune niches that contribute to the heterogeneity of antitumor immunity in breast cancer. Current evidence suggests that mature TLSs, characterized by organized B- and T-cell compartments, germinal center formation, follicular dendritic cell networks, and high endothelial venules, are generally associated with favorable prognosis and improved responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. These associations appear particularly relevant in immunologically active subtypes such as TNBC and HER2 + breast cancer, whereas HR+ tumors more often show lower TLS density and reduced lymphoid organization. However, TLSs should not be interpreted as uniformly beneficial structures. Their biological and clinical significance depends strongly on maturation state, spatial localization, cellular composition, and the broader immune contexture of the tumor microenvironment.

Despite substantial progress, several limitations continue to restrict the clinical translation of TLS biology. First, there is still no universally accepted definition or scoring system for TLS presence, maturity, or functional state, making cross-study comparisons difficult. Second, many reported associations between TLSs and clinical outcomes are derived from retrospective cohorts and may be confounded by bulk TIL abundance, tumor subtype, treatment regimen, or pre-existing immune activation. Third, although therapy-induced TLS formation and maturation represent an attractive concept, direct evidence in breast cancer remains limited, and it is still unclear whether treatment-associated increases in TLS abundance reflect de novo lymphoid neogenesis, remodeling of pre-existing immune structures, or broader inflammatory changes after therapy. These uncertainties highlight the need for prospective studies that incorporate standardized TLS assessment, longitudinal sampling, functional immune readouts, and clearly defined clinical endpoints.

Future research should move beyond binary assessment of TLS presence and focus on the functional quality of TLSs. Integration of histopathology, multiplex imaging, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell profiling, radiomics, and AI-assisted pathology may enable more precise characterization of TLS architecture and immune activity. However, clinical implementation will require scalable, reproducible, and cost-effective workflows that are compatible with routine pathology and validated across institutions. In parallel, TLS-directed therapeutic strategies must be evaluated carefully to determine whether induced lymphoid structures are stable, spatially organized, immunologically productive, and clinically safe. Overall, TLSs represent a promising but still incompletely defined biomarker and therapeutic target in breast cancer. Their successful translation into precision immuno-oncology will depend on harmonized definitions, mechanistic validation, subtype-specific interpretation, and prospective clinical evidence.
