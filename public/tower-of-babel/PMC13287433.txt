Self-Management Intervention for Adults with Epilepsy: A Systematic Review
Authors: Seung Ae Kim, Dong Wook Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to systematically review randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating self-management interventions for adult patients with epilepsy, examine their characteristics and effects, and suggest suitable intervention strategies for clinical application. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in five databases: CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science. RCTs published between January 2019 and October 2024 were included. Eight eligible studies were selected and analyzed in terms of intervention content, duration, frequency, delivery method, and outcome measures, including quality of life, self-management ability, and self-efficacy. Among the eight included studies, statistically significant improvements were reported in quality of life in three studies (37.5%), self-management ability in three studies (37.5%), and self-efficacy in two studies (25.0%). Interventions conducted over 6 months with biweekly or weekly sessions tended to show more favorable outcomes. Face-to-face education and telephone-based support were commonly used and were associated with positive results. Digital interventions, such as mobile applications, showed limited effectiveness when used alone but tended to be more effective when interactive features were included. These findings suggest that a hybrid approach combining initial face-to-face education with ongoing telephone or digital support may be appropriate for adults with epilepsy. A 6-month intervention period with regular biweekly or weekly sessions may be optimal. For digital tools, interactive components should be integrated to sustain engagement and enhance outcomes.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic brain disorder affecting approximately 50 million people worldwide and is reported as the third most common neurological disease in South Korea, only after stroke and dementia.1,2 Patients with epilepsy suffer from not only seizures but also a decline in quality of life due to psychological issues such as anxiety and depression, as well as social stigma.3 In addition, it has been reported that the suicide rate and the mortality rate due to accidents in patients with epilepsy are more than twice as high as those of the general population, emphasizing the necessity for systematic support for self-management in patients with epilepsy.4

Self-management interventions for patients with epilepsy consist of a variety of components, including education on disease and seizure management, medication adherence management, seizure coping training, lifestyle modification, problem-solving skills, and strengthening communication.5 These interventions are delivered via in-person education, group-based approaches, digital interventions, and other methods.6–8 Their usefulness is evaluated through various outcomes such as improvement in self-management skills, self-efficacy, quality of life, seizure frequency, and healthcare utilization.5 Following the coronavirus disease-2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, digital interventions have drawn attention for their ability to enhance healthcare accessibility and effectively deliver interventions to populations who have poor access to medical services.9

There are several studies analyzing the effectiveness of self-management interventions for patients with epilepsy. A meta-analysis by Li et al.10 on adult patients with epilepsy suggested that self-management could significantly improve quality of life, life satisfaction, and self-management performance compared to control groups. Lewinski et al.11 identified factors that promote the adoption of self-management interventions, such as relevance, individualization, intervention components, technological considerations, facilitator competency, and support across socio-ecological levels. These studies also identified barriers including intervention content inappropriateness, lack of individualization, issues within intervention components, technological barriers, insufficient facilitator preparedness, and system constraints. However, there is still a lack of comprehensive literature reviews that analyze intervention types and their effects focusing on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in adult patients with epilepsy. Therefore, we performed a systematic review to analyze self-management intervention methods for adult patients with epilepsy.

Methods

Study design

This study is a systematic literature review analyzing RCTs on the characteristics and effects of self-management interventions aimed at improving self-management abilities in adult patients with epilepsy.

Research questions

To select articles for analysis, the core questions for the systematic review were formed using the participants, intervention, comparison, outcomes, and study design framework, described as follows. The study participants were adult patients with epilepsy. The intervention was any intervention conducted to enhance self-management. The comparison group consisted of individuals who did not receive self-management-related interventions or who received only general inpatient or outpatient care. The outcomes were quality of life, self-management ability, and self-efficacy measured after implementing self-management interventions in adult patients with epilepsy. For study design, RCTs were included to provide objective evidence of the effectiveness of interventions.

Literature search strategy

For the literature search, the databases selected were CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science. To reflect recent research trends and technological advances in the field of self-management interventions for epilepsy, the search period was limited to the last 5 years, from January 1, 2019, to October 10, 2024. The main keywords used were ‘Epilepsy’, ‘Self-management’, and ‘Self-care’, with the study design restricted to RCTs. A total of 109 articles were identified; after removing 25 duplicates using a reference management program, 84 articles underwent the first screening. After reviewing titles and abstracts, studies were excluded if they were not RCTs (28 articles), targeted diseases other than epilepsy (13 articles), or focused on populations other than adults (12 articles). Pilot RCTs, which primarily explore feasibility or acceptability and have small sample sizes that limit evidence for effectiveness, were also excluded. Of the remaining 31 articles, 10 that had no accessible full text, one that was not in English, and 12 that were deemed difficult to apply to the domestic context were excluded, resulting in a final selection of eight articles for analysis (Table 1, Fig. 1). Studies considered difficult to apply generally included those conducted in low-income countries, with ethnic minorities in specific countries, with residents of rural or medically underserved areas, or those conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic with limited implementation. As these studies were considered markedly different from domestic healthcare infrastructure, resources, and patient characteristics, they were excluded from this analysis.

Results

Characteristics of studies

A total of eight studies were included (Table 1, Fig. 1). Among them, three studies (37.5%) were conducted in China,12–14 and other three studies (37.5%) were conducted in the United States.15–17 One study each (12.5%) was conducted in Taiwan and Germany.18,19 Regarding the sample sizes of the experimental groups: one study (12.5%) had between 30 and 40 participants;17 four studies (50.0%) had between 40 and 50 participants;12,15,16,19 two studies (25.0%) had between 50 and 100 participants;14,18 and one study (12.5%) had more than 100 participants.13

Outcome measurement

As for the outcome measurement used in the study results, six studies (75.0%)12,14,15,17–19 assessed quality of life. Four studies (50.0%)13–15,17 used self-management ability as an outcome measurement, while three studies (37.5%)14,15,19 used self-efficacy. Other outcome measurement included seizure frequency in three studies (37.5%);12,13,19 epilepsy knowledge in two studies (25.0%);18,19 depression in two studies (25.0%);16,17 medication adherence in two studies (25.0%);12,18 and accidental injury in two studies (25.0%).12,16 Subjective cognition was assessed in one study (12.5%),17 seizure severity in one study (12.5%),19 and psychological function, social function, and suicide risk were assessed together in one study (12.5%).14

Intervention

For the control group interventions, two studies (25.0%)13,14 provided usual inpatient or outpatient care, while three studies (37.5%)16,17,19 designated a waitlist group to receive the intervention after the study ended. Two studies (25.0%)12,18 provided monthly counseling sessions, and one study (12.5%)15 did not implement any follow-up or general intervention. The duration of interventions ranged from a minimum of 8 weeks to a maximum of 1 year, with 6 months being the most common duration, as reported by three studies (37.5%).13,14,16

Regarding intervention frequency, two studies (25.0%)13,19 did not specify the frequency, allowing participants to proceed during their usual life. For example, Michaelis et al.19 designed smartphone application-based sessions to last about 45 minutes on average, allowing users to freely participate according to their needs. Similarly, Si et al.13 allowed participants to autonomously utilize various educational materials and recording tools provided in the smartphone application, without defining a specific frequency. On the other hand, several studies specified intervention frequency. Two studies (25.0%)12,18 had interventions once per month, and three studies (37.5%)14–16 delivered weekly interventions. Three studies (37.5%)14,16,18 offered interventions twice a month or every 2 weeks. One study (12.5%)17 conducted eight sessions over 8 weeks to 12 weeks, though the specific frequency was not detailed.

As for methods of delivering interventions, face-to-face interventions were used in four studies (50.0%),12,15,17,18 and telephone calls were also used in four studies (50.0%).12,15,16,18 Two studies (25.0%)14,18 employed computer-based online interventions, three studies (37.5%)14–16 utilized group education methods, one study (12.5%)17 used a virtual format enabling real-time interaction, and two studies (25.0%)13,19 used smartphone apps.

Intervention effects

The effects of interventions varied depending on the variable measured. Significant improvements were observed in major outcome measurement such as quality of life, self-management ability, and self-efficacy in the intervention groups, but some variables showed limited improvements. Three studies (37.5%)12,14,17 reported significant increases in quality of life in the intervention group. Another three studies (37.5%)13–15 observed a significant improvement in self-management ability. For self-efficacy, two studies (25.0%)14,15 found a significant increase in the intervention group. Two studies (25.0%)12,18 reported significant improvements in medication adherence, and two studies (25.0%)12,13 reported significant reductions in seizure frequency. Improvements in epilepsy knowledge, 18 accidental injury,12 and depression16 were each reported in one study (12.5%), respectively. One study (12.5%)17 observed significant improvement in subjective cognition, and another (12.5%)14 reported significant improvement in psychological function, social function, and suicide risk in the intervention group. Conversely, one study (12.5%)19 showed no significant differences from the control group in any outcome measurement.

Conclusion

Self-management can be defined by which individuals actively take care of their own health and disease management, learning the skills and strategies necessary to improve their health status and quality of life. This is particularly important for people with chronic conditions, as they must take responsibility for their daily health management.20 Epilepsy, a chronic neurological disease, includes medication management, sleep management, stress management, safety management, and lifestyle management as components of self-management.21 Such self-management can be implemented through various methods, including face-to-face education, use of digital technology, and approaches that provide communication and emotional support among patients.22

This study performed a systematic literature review to evaluate the efficacy of self-management interventions for adult patients with epilepsy. By analyzing eight international RCT studies, it aimed to propose directions for self-management intervention methods. The results showed that the intervention groups displayed significant improvements in various outcome measurements such as quality of life, self-management ability, self-efficacy, medication adherence, and seizure frequency, with improvements in quality of life and self-management ability being particularly pronounced.

The intervention periods ranged from a minimum of 8 weeks to a maximum of 1 year, with 6 months being the most common duration, adopted by three studies, all of which showed significant effects on outcome measures. The frequency of interventions, which influences participant engagement and outcomes, was every 2 weeks or weekly in six studies, both demonstrating significant improvements in outcomes. In addition, both face-to-face approaches and telephone consultations were used as methods of intervention delivery in four studies, confirming these as the most frequently used delivery methods. Face-to-face methods were effective in strengthening self-management abilities and showed significant improvements in quality of life, self-efficacy, and medication adherence. Telephone consultations facilitated continuous interaction and psychological support, maintaining engagement and inducing positive changes such as reduced depression and improved medication adherence.

Elderly patients with epilepsy may have additional characteristics such as difficulty in mobility, low access to digital devices, communication problem, and short attention time. Therefore, these factors should be considered for elderly patients with epilepsy during the self-management intervention. From our study, a strategy can be proposed to provide disease-related information through initial face-to-face contact and then promote self-management intervention participation through ongoing interaction and psychological support via telephone consultations. This face-to-face and telephone hybrid approach may be considered a suitable intervention strategy for elderly patients with epilepsy.

One study that did not show significant changes in outcome measures used only a smartphone application as the self-management intervention method for patients with epilepsy.19 This study commented the low participation rate in the application as a limitation, and the application alone did not provide real-time communication with healthcare providers or interactions with other patients through online forums. Conversely, another smartphone application-based self-management study,13 which showed significant improvements in seizure frequency and self-management ability, provided opportunities for interaction to increase participation. Consequently, to maximize the effect of smartphone application interventions, it is important to provide opportunities for active engagement beyond simply supplying information.

In conclusion, effective self-management interventions for adult patients with epilepsy appear to be those lasting 6 months, conducted regularly every 2 weeks or weekly. For the method of intervention delivery, it is recommended to build basic disease management abilities through face-to-face methods during the first 1 or 2 months, followed by a hybrid approach combining telephone calls and digital technology to offer ongoing interaction and psychological support. This intervention design can be expected to significantly improve a variety of outcomes, such as quality of life, self-management ability, self-efficacy, and reduction in seizure frequency. Finally, the following recommendations can be made. First, for adult patients with epilepsy, a self-management intervention period of 6 months with regular implementation every 2 weeks or weekly, as well as a hybrid approach combining initial face-to-face education with subsequent telephone contact and digital technology, is effective. Second, when using smartphone application-based interventions, it is necessary to design them to provide real-time interactions with healthcare professionals and networking among peers to increase participation rates.
