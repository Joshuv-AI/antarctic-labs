Synthesis, structure, fluorescence properties and catalytic application of uranyl complexes supported by salen ligands
Authors: S Nakul, Varad Mahesh Phadke, I Ayisha, Jomon Mathew, Sanjeev P Maradur, Ajeetkumar Patil, Naveen V Kulkarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA series of three uranyl complexes were synthesised using salen ligands with different backbones. The synthesised ligands and complexes were duly characterised using various spectroscopic techniques which includes IR, UV–Vis and 1H NMR. The molecular structure of the complexes C1 and C2 was determined with the aid of Single Crystal X-ray analysis, both the complexes showed distorted pentagonal bi-pyramidal geometry and the core parameters are duly evaluated. The emission property of the uranyl complexes was analysed and exhibited prominent luminescence properties with well-defined sharp peaks at 450 nm, showing an emission in blue region and their lifetimes and comparative quantum yields were calculated. TDDFT calculation was done to understand the electronic absorption and emission mechanism. The synthesised uranyl complexes and bare uranyl acetate were utilised as a catalyst for the deoxydehydration reaction of the 1,2 hexanediol. The bare uranyl acetate showed best catalytic efficiency in the conversion of 1,2 hexanediol to hexene compared to the Schiff base derived complexes. Different reaction parameters were tested with the bare uranyl acetate as a catalyst and showed the best conversion at a temperature of 140 °C, with a catalyst to substrate loading of 1:30 and a catalyst to reducing agent loading of 1:1.2.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57706-9.Keywords: Uranyl complexes, Salen ligands, Luminescence properties, Deoxydehydration catalysisSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry

Introduction

The utilization of depleted uranium, a radioactive waste product, as a functional material has gained significant attention in recent years and is expected to contribute to future advancements in research1–3. With recent progress in the chemistry of actinides and actinyl compounds, researchers continue to explore their chemical, physical, and electronic properties, thereby opening pathways to novel applications and innovative ideas4. Uranium complexes exhibit a wide range of applications, including catalysis, photocatalysis, dye adsorption, magnetism, and optical materials, among others5. While numerous studies have been conducted on the optical properties of transition-metal complexes, reports focusing on uranium-based compounds remain relatively limited. Notably, uranyl complexes display broad luminescence in the 400–600 nm region, arising from electron transfer between oxygen and uranium 5f. orbitals. Recent research on uranyl compounds has also enhanced their catalytic profile, encompassing their application in C–H activation6–8 and diverse oxidation reactions1,5,9.

Schiff-base and their related compounds have major significance due to their structural versatility facile synthesis, binding ability with different metal ions and stabilise them across different oxidation state10,11. In recent researches Schiff base ligands are coordinated with f-block elements and these have attained considerable interest owing to their highly tailorable electronic properties which can be attained by fine tuning of ligands12–16. Till this date, many uranyl complexes supported by Schiff base and their derivatives are reported11,12,17–19. In this work we have synthesised three uranyl complexes (C1-C3) supported by Schiff base ligands containing different backbones, namely 2,2’-((1E,1’E)-(1,2-phenylenebis(azaneylylidene))bis(methaneylylidene))diphenol (L1), 2,2’-((1E,1’E)-(cyclohexane-1,2-diylbis(azaneylylidene))bis(methaneylylidene))diphenol (L2) and 2,2’-((1E,1’E)-(ethane-1,2-diylbis(azaneylylidene))bis(methaneylylidene))diphenol (L3) yielding a seven coordinated uranyl complexes. The emission properties of the synthesised complexes were investigated and compared with other synthesised complexes through fluorescence studies, lifetime analysis and quantum yield measurements along with TDDFT studies of the complexes. The complexes were duly characterised with spectroscopic techniques and structure of C1 and C2 was confirmed by SCXRD analysis. TG analysis was carried out to understand the thermal stability of the complexes. These complexes and bare uranyl acetate was tested as catalysts for deoxydehydration of 1,2 hexanediol and the reaction condition was optimised to get the higher yield. To best of our knowledge this is the first uranyl-based catalysts used for deoxydehydration of 1,2 octanediol.

All chemical reagents such as uranyl acetate (98%), 1,2 hexanediol (98%), triphenyl phosphine (98%), salicylaldehyde (99%), o-phenylenediamine (99.5%), 1,2 diamino cyclohexane (98%), ethylenediamine (99%) and chlorobenzene (99%) were purchased from commercial sources (Sigma Aldrich, LOBA Chemie and Avra ) and used without further purification. Chlorobenzene and 1,2 hexanediol were dried over 4 Å molecular sieves for three days before use. Experimental

Synthesis of Schiff bases ligands

General protocol used for the synthesis of Salen ligands (L1-L3) is described here 20–22. To a solution of salicylaldehyde (20.4 mmol) dissolved in methanol stirred at room temperature, a diamine (10 mmol) (i.e., o-phenylenediamine, 1,2 diamino cyclohexane or ethylenediamine) dissolved in methanol was added along with a drop of acetic acid. The mixture was stirred for 3 h to obtain a yellow/orange coloured precipitate. The solid product obtained was filtered and washed with methanol and dried under air.

Synthesis of uranyl complexes

2 mmol of the ligands L1, L2 and L3 is dissolved in 10 mL methanol and stirred. To the solution 2 mmol of UA (UO2(CH3COO)2·2H2O) dissolved in 10 mL methanol is added and stirred for 1 h. The precipitate obtained was washed with ethanol and dried under light at room temperature. The molecular structures of the uranyl complexes C1-C3 are shown in Fig. 1.

Single crystal X-ray diffraction studies

Single crystal X-Ray Diffraction analysis of the complexes (C1 and C2) was carried out in Bruker D8 Quest. For the analysis, SADABS multi-scan absorption correction technique was used, and data integration was done by using SAINT. Bruker SHELXTL package was used to solve and refine the structure23,24. Non-hydrogen atoms were represented anisotropically and hydrogen atoms were treated isotropically using a riding model (HFIX 43) at predefined places. The images and data provided in this article were produced by the WinGX suit25 and Mercury26.

TDDFT studies

Geometry optimizations and electronic structure calculations were performed using density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TDDFT) as implemented in Gaussian 0927. The uranium center was modelled using the Stuttgart-Dresden relativistic pseudopotential28–30 while the 6–311 + G(d,p) basis set was employed for all lighter atoms. The long-range corrected hybrid functional wB97X31 was used throughout, and the solvent effects (acetonitrile) were included implicitly using the polarizable continuum model32 wB97X hybrid functional set was employed throughout in the study due to its reliable performance in describing charge transfer excitation and excited state properties in coordination complexes. All TDDFT absorption spectra calculations were performed using the optimized ground-state geometries, while emission transitions were calculated from the optimized geometry of the first singlet excited state (S₁) facilitating a more accurate depiction of relaxation ahead of emission. Cartesian coordinates of the DFT optimised structures are given in SI.

Catalytic deoxydehydration reactions

A typical catalytic reaction was performed in a 25 mL Teflon-lined stainless hydrothermal autoclave charged with diol substrate (3 mmol), catalyst (0.09 mmol), reductant (PPh3 or Na2SO3) (3.6 mmol) and 10 mL of solvent, chlorobenzene (stored under molecular sieves 4 Å). The sealed autoclave was kept in a pre-heated hot air oven, and the reaction was carried out at 150 ⁰C for the appropriate reaction time under autogenous pressure. After the required reaction time, the reaction was cooled to room temperature. An aliquot of the reaction mixture was used for GC analysis, and the yield of the products was determined.

Result and discussion

IR and UV–Vis spectroscopic studies

Infrared spectra of both the ligands (L1-L3) and complexes (C1-C3) were recorded using KBr pellets in the range of 4000 – 400 cm-1. The medium to strong band observed in the range of 3000 to 3400 cm-1 is due to the NH/OH stretching. The strong peaks observed around 1600 to 1680 cm-1 are due to the presence of C = N stretching. A weak to medium peaks observed in the range of 2900 to 3000 cm-1 is due to the presence of aromatic and aliphatic C-H stretching33. In the IR spectrum of complexes, a strong peak observed in the range of 900 to 950 cm-1 is due to the presence of U = O stretching1. A significant shift in the peaks was observed for the C = N stretching in comparison with the ligand showing the participation of the imine nitrogen in metal coordination. A significant shift in both transmittance and wavenumber shift in the peaks was observed in the range of 500–1500 cm-1 due to metalation33.

The UV–Vis spectrum of the ligands and complexes was carried out using acetonitrile (10–4 M) as the solvent. The ligands have exhibited absorbance peaks in the range of 200 to 400 nm, which corresponds to the * transition of the aromatic ring and the * transition of the azomethine group. In case of the complexes slight shift in the wavelength is observed and absorbance peak around the range 400 to 500 nm is due to the presence of ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT)20. IR and UV–Vis spectra of the complexes are given in SI.

NMR studies

The synthesised ligands and their respective complexes are duly characterised by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectra of ligands (L1-L3) were recorded using CDCl3 as the solvent and the respective complexes (C1-C3) were recorded in DMSO-d6. The values of chemical shift are referenced to the solvent residue signals of CDCl3 or DMSO–d6 in relation to the standard SiMe4 and compared to the literature34,35. In the case of ligands (L1-L3) the singlet peaks detected in the ranges of δ 13.06–13.46 ppm and δ 8.38–8.67 ppm are attributed to the -OH and -N = CH- groups, respectively. In the case of the corresponding complexes (C1-C3), the absence of -OH peaks and significant shift in the chemical shift values of the imine proton indicates the effective chelation of the ligand around the metal centre36. The aliphatic and aromatic peaks are observed in the expected region. The coordinated methanol (MeOH) peaks were observed as a quartet and doublet in the corresponding δ values. The NMR spectra of the complexes are provided in SI.

TGA studies

The thermogravimetric studies of all three complexes (C1-C3) were carried out in a temperature range from 25 to 800 ⁰C, the decomposition of the complexes showed characteristic pathways based on the nature of the ligands. The complexes (C1-C3) show initial decomposition at a temperature range of 100–190 ⁰C, which can be attributed to the coordinated water molecule or solvents. Then at the second stage of decomposition, observed at a temperature range of 380 to 620 ⁰C which corresponds to the loss of organic moiety with a mass loss observed as 46.6% (C1), 49.6% (C2) and 44.1% (C3), the final decomposition step is the formation of stable species, UO3 which is observed in the thermal studies1,37. TG curves of the complexes are provided in SI.

Structural characterisation

The solid-state molecular structure of the complexes C1 (Fig. 2a) and C2 (Fig. 2b) was analysed using single-crystal XRD. Quality crystals were obtained using the slow-evaporation method from the respective solutions. Complex C1 was found to have a tetragonal crystal system with a space group observed as P41, while C2 contains a monoclinic crystal system with a space group of C 2/c. Both the complexes exhibit a distorted pentagonal bipyramidal geometry with the average U-N and U-OL bond lengths of 2.546(7) and 2.288(7) and 2.569(4) and 2.258(3) Å, respectively38. The N-U-N bite angle formed between two binding atoms is found to be 62.2(3) and 63.66° for C1 and C2 respectively. The oxo, O = U = O angle in both complexes, is measured as 178.3(3) and 178.06°, indicating a slight deviation from the ideal linear angle of 180°, hence implying a minimal distortion in the structure. In the case of C1 in Fig. 2a, a methanol molecule is found to coordinate to the uranium metal centre, which also establishes an intermolecular hydrogen bonding with the phenolate oxygen present in the salen ligand. In the case of C2 in Fig. 2b, a DMF molecule was found to coordinate with the metal centre through its carbonyl oxygen, taking up the seventh coordination site. DFT and TDDFT studies

Time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations were carried out on complexes C1–C3 to elucidate the electronic origin of their photophysical behaviour44–46. For the absorption spectra, vertical excitation energies were calculated at the optimized ground-state (S₀) geometries, and the simulated spectra were constructed from the calculated excitation energies and oscillator strengths using Gaussian broadening. Overall, the computed spectra reproduce the experimental profiles of all three complexes well, capturing both the intense high-energy absorptions and the lower-energy features.

For C1, the frontier molecular orbitals associated with the low-energy transitions are shown in Fig. 4. The HOMO and HOMO–1 are largely ligand-centered π orbitals with minor uranium 5f. admixture, while the LUMO and LUMO + 1 are predominantly ligand-centered π* orbitals containing moderate uranium 5f contributions. The higher virtual orbitals (LUMO + 2 and LUMO + 3) possess more pronounced uranium 5f character, whereas LUMO + 4 and LUMO + 5 exhibit mixed uranium 5f and ligand π* character. Intense absorptions near 239 nm and 243 nm are primarily attributed to intra-ligand π → π* transitions involving HOMO/HOMO–1 → LUMO/LUMO + 1 excitations, with additional LMCT contributions arising from mixing with uranium-centered virtual orbitals. The band at 293 nm originates from a combination of intra-ligand (π → π*) and LMCT transitions involving excitations into higher-energy uranium-containing virtual orbitals, while the 324 nm absorption displays enhanced LMCT character due to increased participation of higher virtual orbitals with substantial uranium 5f. contributions.

The frontier molecular orbitals of complex C1.

In complexes C2 and C3, the HOMO and HOMO–1 are likewise predominantly ligand-centered π orbitals; however, the low-lying virtual orbitals (LUMO–LUMO + 3) exhibit substantial uranium 5f character with comparatively smaller ligand π* contributions (Figs. 5 and 6, 8). For C2, the intense absorption bands in the 240–270 nm region arise predominantly from LMCT transitions involving HOMO/HOMO–1 → LUMO/LUMO + 1/LUMO + 2 excitations, while additional weaker π → π* contributions originate from excitations into the higher-lying ligand-centered virtual orbitals (LUMO + 4/LUMO + 5). The lower-energy features in the 280–350 nm region are dominated by LMCT transitions with moderate oscillator strengths, giving rise to the weak shoulder observed experimentally. In C3, the intense absorption bands in the 240–280 nm region likewise arise predominantly from LMCT transitions involving HOMO/HOMO–1 → LUMO/LUMO + 1/LUMO + 2 excitations, while weaker π → π* contributions involve higher-lying ligand-centered virtual orbitals (LUMO + 4/LUMO + 5). The bands in the 300–350 nm region exhibit enhanced LMCT character relative to C2, resulting in broader and slightly red-shifted absorptions.

Since strong fluorescence is observed experimentally only for C1, the emission properties were investigated computationally exclusively for this complex (Fig. 7). To model fluorescence, the lowest singlet excited state (S₁) geometry was optimized, and vertical emission energies were computed from this relaxed excited-state structure back to the ground state. These calculated emission energies correspond to the S₁ → S₀ radiative transition and are therefore directly comparable to the experimental fluorescence maxima. The first excited state (381.7 nm, f = 0.0384) involves a HOMO → LUMO transition, with a minor contribution from HOMO–1 → LUMO + 1. In the emission spectrum, the strongest calculated transition appears at 427.0 nm (2.90 eV) with an oscillator strength of 0.1525, corresponding mainly to a HOMO → LUMO excitation, along with lesser involvement of HOMO–1. The HOMO is localized on the ligand π-system, whereas the LUMO exhibits predominantly ligand π* character with moderate uranium 5f. contributions, supporting the assignment of this emission as a mixed π → π*/LMCT transition with significant ligand-centered character. The excellent agreement with the experimental fluorescence maximum near 450 nm further validates this interpretation. Additionally, the TDDFT emission spectrum predicts a series of weaker transitions between 357.8 and 396.3 nm, which correspond well to the minor experimental emission band around 390 nm. These transitions involve excitations from HOMO and HOMO–1 to a set of higher-energy virtual orbitals containing uranium 5f and ligand π* character. While they also display LMCT features, these excitations exhibit more mixed and delocalized character and occur at slightly higher energies.

Simulated (a) absorption and (b) emission spectrum of complex C1.

The comparatively stronger fluorescence observed for C1 relative to C2 and C3 can be attributed to the greater ligand-centered π → π* contribution in its low-lying excited states and the comparatively smaller uranium 5f. participation. The more extended π-conjugated ligand framework in C1 further promotes delocalized ligand-centered excited states, whereas the comparatively less extended conjugation in C2 and C3 enhances the contribution of uranium 5f. orbitals to the low-lying excited states. The stronger LMCT character and enhanced uranium 5f. involvement in C2 and C3 additionally promote more efficient nonradiative relaxation pathways through increased spin–orbit coupling, thereby leading to reduced fluorescence intensity.

Catalytic deoxydehydration reactions

Furthermore, the synthesized complexes (C1–C3) were evaluated for their catalytic performance in the deoxydehydration (DODH) reaction of 1,2-hexanediol (Scheme 1). In the initial screening (Table 2, Entry 3) complex C3 showed better catalytic activity than the other two, yielding 66% of 1-hexene without any detectable by-products. Interestingly, the bare uranyl acetate, under the same reaction conditions, produced an even higher yield of 1-hexene (84%) (Table 2, Entry 4) with excellent selectivity. This finding suggests that the reaction can efficiently proceed without the support of ligands, and that the coordinated ligands might actually slow down or inhibit the process, likely due to steric hindrance or reduced accessibility of the active uranyl centre. The observed variation in the catalytic efficiency of complexes with bare uranyl acetate can be due to steric hindrance imposed by the ligand framework. Considering the three ligand backbones, the rigidity is high in the case of orthophenylene diamine and 1,2 diaminocyclohexane, whereas in ethylenediamine it is most flexible in comparison with other ligands, which makes it easier for the substrate–catalyst interaction and results in a better yield. In comparison with bare uranyl acetate, higher catalytic activity can be due to its highly accessible and labile coordination, which facilitates faster binding compared to sterically constrained ligand stabilised complexes. Given that the catalytic activity of uranyl salts in deoxydehydration (DODH) reactions has not been previously reported, we further pursued a systematic investigation employing uranyl acetate as the catalyst. The study was conducted under varied reaction conditions to evaluate its catalytic efficiency, including the influence of temperature, reaction duration, nature of the reducing agent, and choice of solvent (Table 3)..

When the catalytic reactions were performed under different time intervals of 4, 6, 12 and 24 h (Table 3). The highest catalytic activity was attained at a reaction time of 12 h (Table 2, Entry 4), while increasing the reaction time to 24 h led to decrease in the yield of 1-hexene (76%), perhaps due to the formation of by-products (Table 3, Entry 3). The reactions conducted in shorter time periods, i.e., 4 and 6 h (Table 2, Entry 1 and 2) provided lower yields of 7% and 21% of 1-hexene, respectively, indicating that 12 h is the optimum time for the current catalytic reaction. Since deoxydehydration is an endergonic process that involves removing hydroxyl groups and forming a C = C bond, it generally requires significant thermal energy to proceed. Therefore, using a reductant and maintaining a high reaction temperature (i.e., 140 °C) are is unavoidable to overcome both kinetic and thermodynamic barriers. Indeed, when the reaction was conducted at a lower temperature (90 °C), a significant decrease in the 1-hexene formation (38%) was observed (Table 2, Entry 4). However, when the reaction temperature was increased to 180 °C, the yield of 1-hexene was limited to 60%, likely due to the formation of unidentified side products (Table 3, Entry 3). Hence, a reaction temperature of 140 °C was found to provide the highest yield, establishing it as the optimum reaction temperature.

It is clear that a sacrificial reducing agent is essential for this catalytic reaction. In previous studies with Mo, V, and Re-based catalysts, triphenylphosphine has been successfully employed, yielding olefins efficiently from diols. For this reason, it was chosen as the reducing agent in the present work47,48 It is evident that triphenylphosphine is compatible with uranyl acetate also and produces a high yield of 84% of 1-hexene (Table 2, Entry 4). When sodium sulfite was used as an alternative reducing agent under similar conditions, the yield of 1-hexene dropped to 51% (Table 3, Entry 4). Given that both uranyl acetate and sodium sulfite are highly soluble in water, the deoxydehydration of 1,2-hexanediol was performed using water as the solvent. Gas chromatography (GC) analysis indicated that about 30% of the starting diol remained unreacted, suggesting up to 70% conversion to 1-hexene at a lower reaction temperature of 120 °C. However, isolating 1-hexene from the aqueous phase proved challenging due to its volatility and poor phase separation. In line with the earlier report20, the current catalytic reactions were carried out with a catalyst-to- substrate ratio of 1:30, however, when the ratio was increased to 1:50, a decrease in the catalytic activity was observed, yielding only 15% of 1-hexene. Indicating that, 1:30; is the optimum ratio of catalyst to substrate in this system. Because of their similar structures, the uranyl-based catalyst is expected to follow reaction pathways similar to those reported for well-known di-oxo molybdenum complexes20,21,49. The TON and TOF values of the uranyl acetate were found to be 26.25 and 2.19 h-1 respectively. The GC data are given in the SI.

The study reports, the use of uranyl acetate as a catalyst for the deoxydehydration of 1,2-hexanediol; it is expected that uranyl complexes undergo a catalytic cycle similar to di-oxo molybdenum complexes21,49. The proposed mechanism undergoes two catalytic pathways as shown in Fig. 8. Where the pathway one, triphenylphosphine (PPh3) reduces the catalyst’s dioxo metal centre in the first phase, creating a metal oxo centre with a lower oxidation state with the removal of water from the coordinated metal centre. The reduced metal centre reacts with 1,2 hexanediol forming a metal diolate and elimination of water molecule. The alkene molecule is produced and the dioxo-catalyst is regenerated in the next phase, which is the deoxydehydration process. In pathway two, the catalyst interacts with the 1,2 hexanediol, results in the elimination of two water molecule and also the formation of metal diolate. In the next phase triphenylphosphine (PPh3) reduces, the metal centre to a lower oxidation state. Then the deoxydehydration takes place resulting in the formation of the product (1-hexene) and the catalyst is regenerated.

Conclusion

In summary, the present work investigates the synthesis, spectroscopic characterisation and catalytic and photophysical properties of newly developed salen-supported uranyl complexes C1-C3. The molecular structures of complexes C1 and C2 were unambiguously established through spectroscopic and analytical characterization. All uranyl complexes exhibited distinct luminescence with sharp emission bands centered at 450 nm, corresponding to the blue region. Time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations were carried out for complex C1 to gain insight into the observed optical behaviour. Catalytic studies revealed that all the uranyl complexes exhibit varying activity in the deoxydehydration reaction of 1,2-hexanediol to 1-hexene. Among the complexes, C3, shows moderate activity while C1 shows lower activity and C2 shows no activity under the studied condition. Notably, the catalytic performance of bare uranyl acetate was found to be superior to that of the salen-supported complexes for the deoxydehydration reaction of 1,2 hexanediol. This can be due to the less steric nature of the uranyl acetate compared to the steric natured metal complex which hinders the substrate (1,2 hexanediol) catalyst interaction. The TON for the catalytic reaction was found to be 26.25 with a TOF of 2.19 h-1.
