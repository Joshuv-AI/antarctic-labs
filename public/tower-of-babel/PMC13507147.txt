Compensatory coupling between leak and HCN conductances defines a low dimensional solution manifold in GPe neuron subtypes
Authors: Matheus Phellipe Brasil de Sousa, Gabriel Moreno Cunha, Gabrielle Emily Boaventura Tavares, Gilberto Corso, Karina Possa Abrahao, Gustavo Zampier dos Santos Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe external segment of the globus pallidus contains distinct neuronal subtypes, primarily classified as prototypical and arkypallidal neurons, which exhibit different anatomical, electrophysiological, and functional properties. This cellular heterogeneity plays a central role in shaping basal ganglia activity under both physiological and pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease. Alterations in intrinsic membrane conductances, particularly leak and hyperpolarization activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) currents, have been implicated in abnormal neuronal excitability and dysfunctional circuit dynamics. In this study, we used a Hodgkin–Huxley-like computational model to investigate how variations in leak () and HCN () conductances shape the intrinsic dynamics of these pacemaker neuronal subtypes. By systematically exploring the parameter space, we characterized key electrophysiological features, including firing rate, sag ratio, and trough potential, and constrained these responses using experimental benchmarks. We then introduced an intersection-based methodology to identify subsets of conductance values that simultaneously satisfy multiple physiological constraints. Within this constrained space, we uncovered a robust inverse relationship between and , defining a low-dimensional solution manifold that captures coordinated interactions between these conductances. This manifold preserves physiological excitability through balanced adjustments in ion channel properties, consistent with experimental observations and the principle of ion channel degeneracy. Importantly, deviations from this regime provide a mechanistic framework to understand how imbalances in and may drive to abnormal excitability and pathological dynamics in basal ganglia circuits. Overall, this work establishes a quantitative link between intrinsic conductance regulation and neuronal function, offering a framework to investigate how disruptions in these mechanisms may contribute to neurodegenerative conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56455-z.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Neuroscience

Introduction

The external segment of the globus pallidus (GPe) plays a central role in basal ganglia circuits, contributing to motor control and the modulation of cortical activity through subcortical loops1–4. Importantly, dysfunctions within basal ganglia circuits, particularly those involving the GPe, have been strongly associated with neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, where abnormal neuronal excitability and pathological oscillations emerge5,6. Once considered a homogeneous structure, the GPe is now recognized as a heterogeneous nucleus composed of distinct neuronal subtypes with divergent molecular profiles, projection targets, and intrinsic electrophysiological properties7–9. Two main subtypes have been characterized: prototypic neurons, often identified by parvalbumin (PV) expression, and arkypallidal neurons, marked by genes such as Npas1, FoxP2, or pre-proenkephalin7,8. These populations exhibit contrasting physiological signatures: prototypic neurons typically present high spontaneous firing rates, low sag ratios, and shallow hyperpolarization-induced troughs, whereas arkypallidal neurons display lower firing rates, more pronounced sag responses, and deeper trough potentials9,10.

Although these subtype-specific differences are well documented, the underlying biophysical mechanisms responsible for shaping these electrophysiological signatures remain incompletely understood. In particular, the role of two key ionic conductances, leak () and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channel conductance (), has not been fully characterized in the context of subtype identity. Notably, both conductances are directly involved in regulating subthreshold membrane dynamics, and their imbalance has been associated with altered neuronal excitability and the emergence of abnormal rhythmic activity in basal ganglia circuits. HCN channels are known to modulate sag responses, subthreshold oscillations, and pacemaking properties11–13, while leak conductance contributes to membrane stability and determines the depth of hyperpolarization-induced troughs14. Given their complementary roles, the balance between and may define a critical axis of intrinsic regulation, whose disruption could contribute to pathological states such as Parkinsonian network dysfunction. To understand how these conductances interact to shape the functional identity of GPe neuronal subtypes, it is crucial to utilize computational modeling approaches that complement experimental findings.

Several computational models of the GPe have been developed13,15,16. However, most of these models do not explicitly incorporate the neuronal heterogeneity evidenced in experimental studies8,10. In general, these approaches rely on networks composed of identical neurons and focus primarily on reproducing average firing rates. As a result, key subthreshold electrophysiological properties, such as sag dynamics, sag ratio, and trough potential, are often underexplored. Furthermore, the intrinsic variability observed within neuronal subtypes is rarely incorporated, limiting the ability of such models to capture biologically realistic dynamics. Therefore, there is a need for computational frameworks that account for both subtype-specific properties and intrinsic variability at the single-neuron level. Such an approach enables the identification of electrophysiological markers that distinguish neuronal subtypes. In addition, it allows for a systematic exploration of how variability in intrinsic conductances shapes neuronal function within physiologically plausible regimes.

A central concept explored in this study is the notion of a solution manifold. From the perspective of dynamical systems theory, a manifold can be interpreted as a low-dimensional geometric structure embedded in a higher-dimensional space, which is locally Euclidean (i.e., locally flat), but may exhibit a complex global organization17,18. Intuitively, rather than assuming that a single set of ion channel conductances uniquely determines neuronal behavior, we consider that multiple combinations of conductances can produce equivalent electrophysiological outputs. This idea is closely related to the well-established principle of ion channel degeneracy, whereby similar activity patterns emerge from distinct underlying biophysical configurations19–21. In this context, the solution manifold corresponds to the subset of conductance combinations that simultaneously satisfy experimentally constrained electrophysiological properties, such as firing rate, sag ratio, and trough potential. Importantly, this manifold is not imposed a priori, but emerges from the intersection of multiple physiological constraints within the parameter space. As a result, it defines a structured, low-dimensional region embedded in a higher-dimensional conductance space, capturing the compensatory relationships that preserve neuronal function despite underlying variability. This framework provides a bridge between experimental observations and computational modeling, offering a principled way to interpret variability as an organized and biologically meaningful feature of neuronal systems.

By varying and across a defined two-dimensional parameter space, we computed the firing rate, sag ratio, and trough potential for each conductance pair. These results were systematically compared to ex vivo electrophysiological recordings obtained from mouse GPe neurons10. This comparative analysis enabled the identification of specific regions in parameter space that reproduce experimentally observed neuronal behaviors. In particular, electrophysiological features such as firing rate, sag ratio, and trough potential were used as constraints to define biologically plausible regimes. Consistent with the concept introduced above, the intersection of these constraints defines a restricted region of conductance combinations, which we interpret as a low-dimensional manifold of physiological solutions embedded within a broader parameter space.

Our simulations further revealed a robust inverse correlation between sag ratio and trough potential, consistent with experimental observations. This emergent relationship supports the biological plausibility of the model and suggests an underlying compensatory mechanism between intrinsic conductances. Altogether, these findings indicate that coordinated variations in a reduced set of conductances can account for subtype-specific electrophysiological behavior. Finally, this framework provides a mechanistic basis to investigate how disruptions in the balance between and may contribute to dysfunctional basal ganglia dynamics in pathological conditions such as Parkinson’s disease and drug addiction22–24.

Methods

Experimental ex-vivo data

GPe neurons data were obtained from recordings performed for a prior publication10. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism Animal Care and Use Committee. For electrophysiological slice preparation experiments, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated prior to rapid brain extraction. For immunohistochemistry procedures, animals were anesthetized with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) before transcardial perfusion. Male C57BL/6J mice were used to identify PV-expressing neurons via post hoc immunostaining following whole-cell patch clamp electrophysiological recordings and 1% Neurobiotin filling procedures. Additionally, male and female FoxP2-Cre mice were used to target FoxP2-expressing neurons, after an AAV9.CAG.Flex.tdTomato.WPRE virus injection into the GPe 3–4 weeks prior to slice preparation and recordings.

Briefly, brain slices (250–300 μm) containing the GPe were prepared in ice-cold, oxygenated cutting artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM): 194 sucrose, 30 NaCl, 4.5 KCl, 26 NaHCO, 1.2 NaHPO, 10 D-glucose, and 1 MgCl, using a vibratome and incubated for 30–40 minutes at 32°C in recovery aCSF composed of (in mM): 124 NaCl, 4.5 KCl, 26 NaHCO, 1.2 NaHPO, 10 D-glucose, 1 MgCl, and 2 CaCl. After recovery, slices were maintained at room temperature until recording. Electrophysiological recordings were performed in whole-cell current clamp mode at 30–32°C. Micropipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (2–4 MΩ) and filled with the following pipette solution (in mM): 140 potassium gluconate, 10 Hepes, 0.1 CaCl2, 2 MgCl2, 1 EGTA, 2 ATP-Mg, 0.2 GTP-Na, pH 7.25 (290–295 mOsm). Neurons were visualized using an upright microscope with a LUMPlanFL N 40×/0.80 W objective.

Data was acquired using a Multiclamp 700A amplifier and a Digidata 1322A digitizer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Whole-cell current-clamp recordings (I = 0) were performed for five minutes, and the spontaneous firing rate (Hz) was quantified from the final minute of each trace. To assess intrinsic membrane properties, hyperpolarizing current steps of −200, −150, and −100 pA (1 s duration each) were applied, but only the −200 pA step was used to calculate the sag ratio and trough potential (mV). These electrophysiological parameters, measured in PV- and FoxP2-expressing neurons, served as empirical constraints for the computational model. Data were analyzed using pClamp 10.3 software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Simulated biophysical parameters for arkipallidal and prototypical neurons

The electrophysiological properties chosen for the two neuronal subtypes of the GPe—arkypallidal (Ark) and prototypic (Proto)—were characterized based on available experimental data (ex vivo) from the literature8,10,12,24. According to the experimental data: (i) the firing rate of Ark neurons shows a distribution with a first quartile (Q1) of 8 Hz, median (Q2) of 10 Hz, and third quartile (Q3) of 18 Hz. Proto neurons, on the other hand, fire at higher frequencies, ranging from 35 Hz (Q1) to 80 Hz (Q3), with a median of 53 Hz. The sag ratio index,defined as the ratio of the steady-state voltage deflection to the peak (minimum) voltage deflection, also differentiates the subtypes: Ark neurons present higher values, between 1.28 (Q1) and 1.46 (Q3), with a median of 1.35, while Proto neurons show sag ratios between 1.05 and 1.13, with a median of 1.10. Additionally, the minimum potential (trough potential) of Ark neurons exhibits more hyperpolarized values, ranging from − 140 mV to − 175 mV, with a median of − 150 mV. Proto neurons, however, concentrate between − 85 mV and − 102 mV, with a median of − 95 mV.

In the simulations (in silico), conductance densities of selected ion channels were systematically varied to assess the impact of heterogeneity on the membrane properties of GPe neurons. These parameters were defined based on previous computational models and electrophysiological data13,15,16.

NaF, Kv3, CaH, SK channels were simulated with fixed conductances for both neuronal GPe neuron subtypes. Manifold of physiological solutions: functional coupling between conductances

In this work, we use the term solution manifold to describe the subset of parameter space in which the electrophysiological properties of GPe neurons emerge as consistent and physiologically plausible solutions.

In the mathematical literature, a solution manifold denotes a continuous set of solutions that satisfy a system of constraints, typically forming a lower-dimensional structure embedded in a higher-dimensional parameter space. In conductance-based neuronal models, this concept arises from the well-established principle of ion channel degeneracy, whereby similar electrophysiological outputs can be produced by multiple distinct combinations of underlying conductances19,20.

In this context, the solution manifold represents a structured subset of conductance pairs that jointly reproduce experimentally constrained electrophysiological features, such as trough potential and sag ratio. This framework moves beyond isolated parameter tuning and instead captures the intrinsic interdependence between ionic conductances, whereby variations in one channel can be compensated by adjustments in another while preserving physiological function, an expected consequence of degeneracy in neuronal systems8. Sampling of the solution manifold for comparison with experimental data

Once the solution manifold is defined as a constrained subspace of physiologically plausible conductance combinations, we describe how this manifold was sampled to generate simulated datasets for comparison with experimental observations. To enable quantitative comparison with experimental data, we generated simulated datasets by sampling discrete combinations of conductance pairs along the solution manifold. For each neuronal subtype, we sampled multiple points along the manifold to capture the intrinsic variability permitted within the constrained parameter space. This ensemble of simulations was then compared with the empirically observed variability.

Individual membrane potential traces correspond to representative realizations of the model (i.e., single simulations) selected along the solution manifold, whereas the boxplots (see Fig. 6) are constructed from multiple independent simulations, following the same statistical framework applied to the biological data. Importantly, simulated data points do not correspond to individual neurons, but rather to distinct parameter configurations that reproduce the same set of electrophysiological constraints. This sampling strategy is consistent with the principle of ion channel degeneracy. Accordingly, the variability observed in the simulated datasets reflects structured variability within a constrained solution space, rather than random dispersion.

Statistical analysis

To quantify differences in electrophysiological properties between neuronal subtypes in, both computational simulations and ex-vivo experiments, we used the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U test)31, which does not assume data normality or a specific data distribution. This test evaluates whether two groups differ significantly by comparing the ranks of the observed values. Statistical significance was denoted by asterisks based on p values p: p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.05 (*); and p \ge 0.05 was considered not significant (ns). Analyses were performed using the ranksum function in MATLAB.

To assess the relationship between sag ratio and trough potential across neuronal subtypes, we performed both linear regression and Pearson correlation analyses on simulated and experimental datasets. For the simulated neurons, data points were extracted along the diagonal of the intersection region in the parameter space—defined as the set of conductance pairs that simultaneously satisfy empirical constraints for firing rate, sag ratio, and trough potential. These points correspond to physiologically plausible configurations derived from the Proto and Ark models.

For the experimental data, we used ex vivo whole-cell recordings from genetically identified PV and FoxP2 neurons, from which sag ratio and trough potential were extracted using standardized protocols under a − 200pA hyperpolarizing current steps. In both datasets, each neuronal subtype was independently fitted using a simple linear model: . Regression was performed using least-squares minimization, and the residual norm was computed to assess goodness of fit. In addition, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was calculated to quantify the strength and direction of the linear association between the two variables. These analyses enabled the comparison of slope, intercept, residuals, and correlation strength across neuronal subtypes, providing a quantitative basis for interpreting the observed co-variation as a structured, subtype-specific phenomenon.

Discussion

The GPe is a key structure within the basal ganglia, involved in diverse motor and cognitive functions. The discovery of cellular heterogeneity in the GPe, particularly the identification of two major neuronal subtypes, arkypallidal (Ark) and prototypical (Proto) neurons, has prompted more detailed investigations into the biophysical mechanisms underlying their distinct electrophysiological profiles2,3,7.

We constructed a detailed Hodgkin-Huxley-like computational model to simulate the intrinsic dynamics of Ark and Proto neurons. Model parameters were constrained using experimentally observed electrophysiological features, rather than selected as unique optimal values, reflecting the non-uniqueness of conductance-based solutions. The modeling focused on three key electrophysiological properties: spontaneous firing rate, trough potential, and sag ratio, all measured under current-clamp protocols and compared to ex vivo empirical data reported by Abrahao and Lovinger10. By constraining the model through empirical benchmarks, we restricted the parameter space to physiologically plausible regions, without assuming parameter uniqueness, thereby explicitly accounting for the inherent indeterminacy of conductance-based models.

Although traditional models often isolate the influence of individual ionic currents, emerging perspectives emphasize the importance of integrative conductance configurations21,32. Despite recent advances, there is still no consensus in the literature regarding which ion channels are primarily responsible for modulating specific electrophysiological features such as the sag ratio, trough potential, and firing rate. This lack of agreement stems from several factors, including variations in experimental protocols, species and strain differences, heterogeneous expression of channel isoforms (e.g., different HCN subtypes), interactions between ionic currents, and differing assumptions in computational modeling frameworks13,33.

Correlation between leak and HCN ion channels

Experimental and computational modeling studies indicate that the sag is an emergent electrophysiological property resulting from the activation of HCN channels in the neuronal membrane, which generate a depolarizing current during hyperpolarizing stimuli12,33,34. In contrast, the leak conductance () plays a fundamental role in determining the resting membrane potential and the depth of hyperpolarization in response to negative current injections, thereby modulating the basal excitability of the neuron13,14.

Consistent with previous biophysical findings10, our simulations confirmed the complementary roles of leak and HCN channels in shaping sag dynamics and rebound depolarization. More specifically, the interplay between HCN kinetics and background conductance contributed to reproducing the distinct response latencies and amplitudes observed in Ark and Proto neurons, although alternative conductance configurations may also produce similar outputs. The agreement between simulations and experimental recordings supports the physiological plausibility of the model, although it does not imply parameter uniqueness. Not only were central tendencies (e.g., median sag ratio and trough potential) replicated, but the direction and strength of inter-variable correlations were consistent across conditions. This convergence suggests that the model captures key organizational constraints of GPe excitability within the explored parameter subspace.

Our results revealed a restricted admissible region in the space for Proto neurons, where experimental firing rates, sag ratios, and trough potentials intersect. This constrained subset space defines a physiologically meaningful regime. Within this restricted region, linear regression revealed an inverse relationship between and , which should be interpreted as a local approximation rather than a universal law. We interpret this observation within the framework of solution manifolds, where compensatory relationships between conductances emerge as a consequence of degeneracy: increases in one conductance can be offset by decreases in another while preserving electrophysiological function.

In Ark neurons, simulations also revealed a correlated compensation between leak and fast HCN conductances, but within a distinct subdomain compared to Proto neurons. The Ark solution manifold is shifted toward lower and higher values, consistent with their increased sag responses. These findings are consistent with prior transcriptomic studies suggesting differential expression of HCN2/HCN4 across subtypes12.

To quantitatively assess this relationship, we performed linear regression analyses on the valid conductance pairs (Fig.5). For Proto neurons, we found a significant inverse relationship between and (slope = -1.025 ± 0.455, , ), indicating a relatively well-constrained compensatory interaction between these conductances.

In contrast, arkypallidal neurons also exhibited a statistically significant inverse relationship (slope = -0.075 ± 0.060, , p = 0.0386), but with substantially greater dispersion around the fitted line. To further evaluate the robustness of these relationships, we performed a bootstrap analysis (1000 resamples). For prototypical neurons, the 95% confidence interval of the slope remained strictly negative ([− 1.4663, − 0.6175]), confirming a stable and robust inverse relationship between and .

In contrast, for arkypallidal neurons, the bootstrap interval was broader and included values close to zero ([− 0.1418, 0.0216]), although the mean slope remained negative. This result indicates a weaker and more variable compensatory trend, consistent with a less constrained conductance relationship in this neuronal subtype. This suggests that, while a compensatory trend is present in both subtypes, Ark neurons occupy a broader region of the solution space, consistent with weaker constraints along the explored projection.

Furthermore, beyond its geometric interpretation, this inverse relationship between leak and HCN conductances likely reflects a homeostatic rule that helps GPe neurons preserve their intrinsic firing properties and maintain desynchronized network activity, even in the presence of variations in ionic channel gene expression, levels of neuromodulation, or metabolic state19,35. From a functional perspective, such compensatory interactions may contribute to the stability of input–output properties, although multiple conductances are likely involved in this regulation.

This type of compensation naturally fits within the broader framework of ion channel degeneracy, according to which distinct combinations of biophysical parameters can support equivalent electrophysiological behaviors36. In this context, our results suggest that GPe neurons operate along a restricted yet robust region of the conductance parameter space, which preserves consistent functional behavior within the basal ganglia circuitry despite underlying biological heterogeneity. Importantly, focusing on the leak–HCN relationship does not imply that other conductances play a negligible role in shaping neuronal stability.

Although the present work highlights the leak–HCN relationship as a key axis of intrinsic homeostasis, it is plausible that other channels, including different Kv subtypes, Ca currents, and other potassium currents as the BK channels conductances, which strongly shape spike repolarization and fast afterhyperpolarization, contribute additional dimensions to this compensatory manifold13. These channels likely contribute additional dimensions to a higher-dimensional compensatory structure, reinforcing the idea that neuronal stability emerges from coordinated adjustments across multiple conductances.

Intersection subspace and solution manifolds: biological and computational implications

The identification of a solution manifold within the electrophysiological parameter space represents an important conceptual advance in the modeling of GPe subtypes. Rather than considering ionic conductances in isolation, the manifold emphasizes the interdependent nature of biophysical parameters: variations in can be systematically compensated by reciprocal changes in , yielding stable and physiologically consistent neuronal responses. This observation is consistent with the idea that neuronal identity is not defined by single conductance values, but by a continuum of combinations that collectively preserve function37,38. This perspective aligns with the concept of degeneracy, where different sets of biophysical parameters can produce equivalent dynamics, conferring robustness and flexibility to the nervous system39,40.

From a mathematical standpoint, the solution manifold can be interpreted as a low-dimensional projection of a higher-dimensional conductance space41,42.

This approach provides a natural explanation for the electrophysiological variability observed in experimental recordings, since neurons of the same subtype may occupy different points along the manifold while maintaining comparable output features20,43.

Furthermore, this framework suggests that pathological states, such as those observed in Parkinson’s disease, may be associated with shifts or distortions of this manifold, although this hypothesis requires direct experimental validation.

In this sense, the concept of a solution manifold strengthens the methodological link between simulation and experiment, while also offering a testable hypothesis of how intrinsic conductance variability contributes both to resilience and to vulnerability in neuronal dynamics, across scales from membranes to behavior44.

Across both simulated and empirical datasets, we identified a subtype-specific correlation between sag ratio and trough potential, consistent with coordinated regulation of depolarizing (HCN-mediated) and stabilizing (leak) currents.

From a functional standpoint, this trade-off supports a form of dynamic compensation, whereby changes in excitability are constrained along an axis of preserved responsiveness.

The identification of a solution manifold in the (, ) parameter space suggests a biologically meaningful axis of compensatory regulation.

Such manifolds align with the concept of conductance degeneracy20,38, where different ionic configurations produce similar electrophysiological outputs. Our findings reinforce that GPe neurons likely exploit this degeneracy to preserve functionality in the face of biological variability or pathological insult.

More broadly, the modeling framework presented here illustrates how physiologically grounded parameter spaces can be used to reveal compensatory structures that would be difficult to infer from data alone. As such, the approach provides not only mechanistic insight but also a flexible platform for hypothesis testing, pharmacological prediction, and further extensions to network-level models.

Conclusion

We presented a biophysically grounded Hodgkin-Huxley model to investigate how the interaction between leak conductance () and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels () modulates the intrinsic properties of GPe neuronal subtypes. Our simulations reproduced key physiological features observed in ex vivo recordings, including firing rate, sag ratio, and trough potential for both prototypic (PV-expressing) and arkypallidal (FoxP2-expressing) neurons, within a constrained region of the parameter space.

A central methodological contribution was the definition of an intersection region in the conductance space, which revealed a low-dimensional projection of a broader manifold of physiological solutions. This structure captures a compensatory relationship between leak and HCN currents within the explored subspace, consistent with the principles of degeneracy and robustness in neural systems. Furthermore, we identified a subtype-specific correlation between the sag ratio and trough potential, observed both in silico and ex vivo, highlighting a coordinated balance between depolarizing and stabilizing membrane mechanisms.

These results provide a theoretical framework linking ion channel co-regulation to subtype-specific electrophysiological properties within the GPe, without assuming unique parameter configurations. Although our model focused on PV and FoxP2 subtypes, the framework can be extended to other GPe neuronal populations, such as those expressing NPas1 and Lhx6. Together, these subtypes, characterized in detail by Abrahao and Lovinger10, suggest a broader landscape of GPe diversity that may be governed by similar conductance-based organizing principles, although this generalization requires further investigation.

From a translational perspective, this framework provides a principled approach to investigate how imbalances in the – axis may contribute to pathological dynamics, particularly in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease45,46. Disruptions to this compensatory regime may destabilize neuronal excitability and facilitate aberrant oscillatory activity in basal ganglia circuits, although direct causal links remain to be experimentally validated.

Future extensions of this model may incorporate synaptic input variability, network coupling, and plasticity to assess how the manifold behaves under realistic activity regimes and disease conditions, including additional mechanisms such as synaptic interactions and extracellular effects (e.g., ephaptic coupling), which may modulate pathological beta-band oscillations47–50.

Taken together, our results support the role of structured compensatory mechanisms in shaping the electrophysiological diversity of GPe neurons and suggest that conductance manifolds may provide a useful framework for interpreting both physiological variability and its disruption in disease.
