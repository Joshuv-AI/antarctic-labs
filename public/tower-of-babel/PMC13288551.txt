Impact of technological acceptance on application-based engagement among type 2 diabetes patients: local insights and implications for developing countries
Authors: Afeefa Tahira, Mohamad Haniki Nik Mohamed, Mohamed Hassan Elnaem, Furqan Khurshid Hashmi, Nadeem Irfan Bukhari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAcceptability and sustained engagement of mobile health (mHealth) applications in resource-limited settings for chronic disease management are suboptimal. Research on mHealth applications overlooks contextual factors affecting the translation of their acceptance to long-term usage.MethodsThis study examined contextual factors using questionnaire developed by Technological Acceptance Models (TAM) and Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT), to study the influence of technology acceptance (TA) on Sustained App-based Engagement and Use (ABEU) among Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) patients. Data were collected through purposive sampling from 397 T2DM patients across public and private hospitals in four provinces of Pakistan. Exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and structural equation modelling validated hypothesised relationships.ResultsModel depicted a strong, positive effect of TA on ABEU (β = 0.65, p < 0.001), indicating acceptance led to sustained use of mHealth. Based on β ≥ 0.50 for acceptance and engagement, Social image boost, Confidence boost, Better health through app use, and Doctors’ recommendations were important cultural factors. Satisfaction, Better sugar levels, Confidence in App use, Search and download ability, and Easy access to reliable information were critical contextual factors. Better health facilities, Time-saving, and Internet speed and connectivity were key infrastructural factors. Improved health facilities, faster internet speeds, and reliable connectivity were boosted through users’ confidence and enhanced self-image, encouraging them to engage, supported by increased satisfaction and perceived health benefits.ConclusionTA significantly impacted ABEU through contextual, cultural and infrastructural factors in mHealth adoption, underscoring the need for actionable insights to improve mHealth adoption and retention.KEYWORDS: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, mHealth, technological adoption, App engagement, chronic disease management

Introduction

Mobile health (mHealth) applications (Apps) have emerged as significant personal health management tools (Fan & Zhao, 2022), offering, not limited to the following: disease monitoring and management, on demand access to medical guidance, remote consultations, access to health education (Grundy, 2022; Krishna Kotha, 2023), improved patient-physician relationship, access to information of diseases, treatment, dose, side effects, life style modification and alerts for patients or caregivers, and for physicians (Gu et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2022). Thus, the use of mHealth Apps enables patients to play an active role in their personal care and have more engagement, resulting in better compliance, making the healthcare system more efficient (Ahmad & Bukhari, 2024; Wang et al., 2014). A long-term App engagement is essential for the long-term health improvements and meaningful health outcomes in chronic conditions (Mustafa et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2014).

Literature cites several theories- and models- based, as well as independent factors, for the translation of technological acceptance (TA) of mHealth Apps into meaningful adoption (Birkmeyer et al., 2021; Rahimi et al., 2018; Rosli et al., 2022). The following theories and models dominate the literature on measuring App adoption and engagement: technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), TAM's extension, TAM2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al., 2003), UTAUT expansion, UTAUT2 (Venkatesh et al., 2012), expectation confirmation theory (ECT) (Bhattacherjee, 2001), theory of planned behaviour (TPB) (Ajzen, 1985) and self-determination theory (SDT) (Deci & Ryan, 2012). The independent categories of factors influencing acceptance and adherence to mHealth Apps are usability, contextual determinants, encompassing misaligned expectations, and organisational and human capacity variables (Kruse et al., 2019). The constructs or latent variables indirectly measure the above through multiple indicators (Hair et al., 2009). TA and ABEU are the reflective first-order constructs, in line with the above theoretical frameworks, which define acceptance and engagement as latent dispositions expressed in observable perceptions or behaviours (Bollen, 1989).

The factors driving Apps acceptance and engagement, being multidimensional constructs, are based on socio- and techno- centric approaches encompassing behavioural, emotional, and cognitive investment, thus are critical for their effective use and informing their design and relevant policy (Liu et al., 2017). Thus, the design and development of mHealth Apps requires understanding of acceptance and engagement (Ikwunne et al., 2021) and consideration of the preferences and aspirations of individual service users and the cultures of communities (Peiris et al., 2014). Exploring the acceptance-engagement interplay in the local context is more influential for engagement and essential to designing user-friendly, accessible, and localised mHealth Apps (Khalid et al., 2024). The above signifies the local studies on the translation of App acceptance into meaningful engagement, considering the sociocultural TA aspects (Nadal et al., 2020).

Pakistan presents a unique context for studying mHealth interventions in diabetes care due to high global ranking of country for diabetes prevalence (Bhatti, 2025), being resource-limited where internet infrastructure, digital literacy, and cultural health-seeking behaviours are barriers to mHealth App adoption (Jamil, 2021), having a tri-burdened healthcare system with high prevalence of infectious diseases, rapidly growing non-communicable diseases, disintegrated healthcare system with urban-rural area disparities (Raheem et al., 2024), and mHealth usage, being at the elementary stages (Mahdi et al., 2022). The traditional healthcare system in Pakistan faces substantial challenges; thus, mHealth Apps hold particular significance (Ali, 2024). The use of effective mHealth solutions could help fill the critical gaps in care delivery created by the above challenges (Jones-Esan et al., 2024). For instance, a particularly tailored mHealth App for long-term use could alleviate the burden on the healthcare system (Sahin et al., 2019) by several ways: transcending physical limitations and bringing chronic disease management tools directly to patients’ access (Takenga et al., 2014), empowering them to monitor conditions, adhere to self-care practices, and timely access to health information (Dike et al., 2025), and improving accessibility to better health services through education, reminder generation, and remote clinician contact (Kitsiou et al., 2017).

Pakistan is attempting to integrate mHealth Apps into its healthcare system for diabetes management (Ashraf et al., 2024). Interventions through SMS and phone calls have shown improved adherence and self-care behaviour in patients (Kamal et al., 2019; M. Shahid et al., 2015). The use of mHealth for glucose monitoring, adherence and medication reminders improves self-care (Siddiqui et al., 2015), and a promise in lowering the HbA1c levels and improving knowledge and practice in diabetes management (Hashmi & Khan, 2018; Shahid et al., 2015). All the above make the technological acceptance and engagement a research interest topic to manage diabetes effectively (Khalid et al., 2024).

The previous studies: (A) mostly focused collectively on TA and engagement (Khalid et al., 2024), (B) not demonstrating the long-term engagement and its consequences, (C) explored limited contextual-specific indicators (Kazi et al., 2025), (D) focused on a collective mHealth concept, not solely on Apps, (E) were mostly regional-specific, (F) carried on samples from small areas, and (G) were limited in scope (Azam et al., 2023), were generally on chronic diseases (Iftikhar et al., 2019) and not specifically on T2DM (Soomro & Memon, 2025). Added to the above, the theoretical frameworks for acceptance and engagement given in the preceding text do not adequately apprehend the cultural, educational, infrastructural, and behavioural realities of mHealth use in developing countries (Jacobs, 2021; Lee et al., 2025; Moreno-Ligero et al., 2023). Relative to acceptance, the sustained engagement got less global attention (Wang et al., 2014). The duality of the strength and directionality of acceptance and engagement – a conceptual interplay between TA and ABEU (Mustafa et al., 2022) has scarcely been studied, even globally (Chib et al., 2015) or statistically proven in T2DM patients (Khalid et al., 2024); thus, these domains are required to be explored. Hence, the present study aimed to identify the major locally-relevant contextual, cultural and infrastructural factors affecting mHealth acceptance and sustained use behaviour, and the causal relationship between TA and ABEU in T2DM patients using mHealth Apps. The study employed a parsimonious modelling approach (without the use of the whole models) to theorise and measure TA and ABEU with the expectation to provide evidence-based recommendations for developers, healthcare professionals, and policy makers to enhance the adoption and long-term engagement of the mHealth App.

Methodology

Conceptual model

The basis of this study was the concept that the adoption intention led to actual use and caused sustained engagement with mHealth technologies (Peng et al., 2016), which might be relevant for this local study due to the varied nature of contextual, cultural, and infrastructural barriers and facilitators. The study is hypothesised as follows: H0 (null hypothesis): TA had no significant positive effect on ABEU (or otherwise, H1). TA reflects users’ perception of an App's usefulness, image enhancement, confidence, and technical support, while ABEU encompasses indicators of App engagement, satisfaction, peer recommendation, behaviour, and health improvement via App use (Figure 1). TA and ABEU were conceptualised as a unidimensional construct affected by the above factors, reflecting T2DM patients’ overall readiness for adoption and engagement of mHealth Apps. The responses from respondents were statistically analysed using descriptive statistics and structural equation modeling (SEM) to assess the hypothesis of the study through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Human Ethics Committee of the University of the Punjab, Lahore, Pakistan (document reference: ACE/PUCP/1107, dated November 28, 2022) prior to data collection. The study adhered to ethical standards for research involving human participants.

Research design and participants

The current study used a three-stage analytical approach: EFA to identify latent constructs, CFA to validate the measurement model, and SEM to test causal relationships between latent factors. A quantitative, cross-sectional survey design based on a theoretical model was followed to examine the relationship between TA and ABEU among patients with T2DM in Pakistan. T2DM patients from urban and rural areas, aged >20 years, diagnosed with diabetes for more than 1 year, smartphone users for more than 2 years, already using a free mHealth App for at least one month to manage their diabetes, were recruited from public and private hospitals of the provincial capitals of the Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan and Sindh, respectively, Lahore, Peshawar, Quetta and Karachi. The patients were approached through trained pharmacy students in their 4th professional year and above at public and private institutes in their respective cities. The first author trained the above pharmacy students online to ensure consistent data collection. A precise set of inclusion and exclusion criteria for the selection of study participants was formulated to ensure congruence between the sample and the target population. The participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure representation from diverse socioeconomic and geographic backgrounds. The above sampling method is widely used in health technology and behavioural studies, particularly when the research focuses on specialised populations, such as T2DM patients. The study required respondents with direct experience of using smartphones, the free mHealth apps, and experience in managing diabetes with such Apps. Therefore, purposive sampling was considered more feasible and methodologically appropriate for reaching and capturing the targeted behavioural construct from the intended population.

Instrument development

A questionnaire was designed, using well-established theoretical models, including TAM (Davis, 1989), TAM2, UTAUT (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), and ECT (Bhattacherjee, 2001), as well as model-independent contextual, cultural and infrastructural factors affecting the acceptance and engagement of an mHealth App in T2DM. The purpose of the questionnaire was to find the respondents' perceptions about the above variables, followed by their use for testing the hypothesis through SEM, i.e. initial acceptance influenced the sustained use of mHealth Apps. The questionnaire was divided into two main sections: the first comprising questions on the socio-demographics of the T2DM patients, including province, age, gender, and education level. The second section included items related to TA, engagement, and use. The responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). To ensure the validity and reliability of the questionnaire, a multi-step process was followed. The questionnaire was developed based on a theoretical background, and a panel of healthcare researchers and digital health experts reviewed the initial items for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. The questionnaire was translated into the local language, Urdu and assessed by an Urdu language expert. The questionnaire was evaluated through pilot study on a small group of T2DM patients (N = 20) to ensure the appropriateness of the questionnaire; however, these data were not made the part of the final results, as the input from the findings of the pilot study required modifications and yielded a questionnaire that improved its overall flow, refined different items, and wordings to reflect participants perspective better. TA and continuance usage were explored in the present study through 16 variables, 6 for TA and 10 for ABEU, as shown in Table 1.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using Cochran's Formula (Cochran, 1977; Kotrlik & Higgins, 2001) as below to ascertain the necessary sample size, confidence level was 95%, 5% margin of error, and an esimated 50% response distribution considering the population of the T2DM patients ‘infinite’.

N = Sample size, Z = Standard normal value (1.96 for 95% confidence), P = Estimated proportion of the attribute present in the population (0.5%), e = Margin of error (0.05)

With the addition of 10% of the resultant N, i.e. 38 were added to 384 to compensate for non-response and missing data, in line with the literature (Israel, 1992), the total sample size calculated was 422.

Data collection and consent to participate

Data were collected between April and September 2025 through a multimethod recruitment strategy, including online surveys and in-person outreach, as well as both online and field-based surveys. The questionnaire proforma stated the understandable details of the study in the local language, i.e. Urdu, comprising a statement, ‘confidentiality will be ensured throughout the study’. Further, informed consent was obtained from all the participants by prominently mentioning the consent statement as ‘the filled and returned soft and hard copies of the questionnaires, respectively, would be considered as participants’ consent to take part in the survey’. The relevant details of the study were shared with the patients meeting the inclusion criteria. The survey was administered, and data were gathered by pharmacy students through face-to-face interaction or online via email or WhatsApp, as required, from the T2DM patients to record their responses.

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to measure the frequency and percentages of respondents’ gender, age, education level, and provinces from which the participants belonged, employing SPSS V27.

Latent factor exploration through exploratory factor analysis

EFA was conducted using SPSS V27 to examine the underlying structure of the observed variables and determine the associated factors. Principal component analysis (PCA) with varimax rotation was employed to explore the factor structure and loadings of the variables on factors. Variables with loadings of ≥ 0.60 were retained for further modelling (MacCallum et al., 1999). Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measured the sample adequacy, where a value ≥ 0.80 for the factors was considered appropriate (Braeken & van Assen, 2017; Kaiser, 1960). Bartlett's test of sphericity examined the suitability of the data for factor analysis, and P < 0.05 was considered significant. The total variance of factors with a value greater than 60% was considered acceptable (Hair et al., 2019), while the number of factors to retain was determined through Eigenvalues and an associated scree plot. The factors with Eigenvalues ≥ 1 shown in the scree plot were retained for further interpretation (Finch, 2025). Finally, a measurement model was derived.

Measurement model assessment via confirmatory factor analysis

CFA was conducted using AMOS 26 to validate the accurate representation of the latent factors by the corresponding observed variables of the measurement model. The following criteria were taken into account to confirm construct validity: (A) variable loadings λ, which showed the relationship between variables and the latent factor, and (B) composite reliability, which determined how reliable and internally consistent the variables measuring the same latent factor were. It also showed how well the variables that were supposed to measure a latent construct actually shared common variance with that of the latent construct, and (C) average variance extracted (AVE). The internal consistency of the model, as assessed by composite reliability, helped to test the overall goodness of fit of the measurement model before proceeding to SEM. Convergent validity is the extent to which indicators that are intended to measure the same construct are highly correlated with each other. The values of factor loadings λ ≥ 0.50, composite reliability ≥ 0.70, and AVE ≥ 0.5 were acceptable for construct reliability (Cheung et al., 2024; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The discriminant validity, the degree to which a construct was distinct from other constructs, was confirmed through the Fornell-Larcker criterion, where the square root of AVE for each construct should be greater than its correlation with any other construct (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The variables with high factor loadings were removed, and error terms were covaried to improve the model's fitness. This systematic validation approach assured that the questionnaire was both statistically sound and contextually relevant for studying mHealth App acceptance and engagement in the target population.

Hypothesis assessment via structural equation modelling

CFA allowed for theoretical richness while maintaining clarity in the measurement and structural modelling process. SEM was performed in AMOS V27 to test the hypothesised relationships between TA and ABEU (TACA → ABEU), using the standardised regression coefficient (β) to measure the degree of change in the dependent variable (in standard deviations) when the independent variable increased by one standard deviation (Figure 1). The following goodness of fit indices criteria were considered: β > 0.50, good fit indices (GFI) ≥ 0.90, adjusted good fit indices (AGFI) ≥ 0.80, Chi-square/df CMIN/DF ≤ 5.0, the comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90 (Hu & Bentler, 1999), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) ≥ 0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08–0.10 and RMSEA close fit test PCLOSE > 0.05 (Hair et al., 2019). RMSEA assess the degree of approximation error in the model and shows how far the model is from perfectly matching the real data. PCLOSE tests whether the model achieves a close fit in the population and exhibits whether the model is accurate enough to trust (Hair et al., 2009; Kline, 2023).

Bootstrapping, a resampling technique, was performed with 2,000 samples to evaluate the accuracy, stability, and robustness of the model estimates, path coefficients, indirect effects (such as mediation), and standard errors. The threshold for stability in bootstrapping requires 20,000 to 50,000 resamples, a 95% confidence interval (CI) with no zero value in the CI, and a stable β was used to consider the effect statistically significant. Multivariate normality was assessed through skewness + 2, kurtosis = 7, and Mahalanobis distance; cases exceeding the critical Chi-square threshold at p < 0.001 were considered potential outliers prior to further analysis. Discriminant validity was confirmed using Fornell-Larcker criteria (AVE > inter-construct correlation R2) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

The initial CFA model (Figure 4) yielded the following values: Chi Square χ2/df = 7.345, GFI = 0.812, AGFI = 0.751, CFI = 0.805, and TLI = 0.772, with RMSEA = 0.127. The variables with low factor loading, like overcome App usage problems (OVCM), family & friend recommendation (FARM), and medical information (MEDI) (Figure 4), were removed and the error terms (e6 & e8, e15 & e16) were covariate to improve the model fit values (Figure 5), in line with literaure (Brown, 2015). After removing three variables model was run with 13 factors, which showed factor loadings > 0.65 and Chi Square χ2/df = 3.530, GFI = 0.919, AGFI = 0.880, RMSEA = 0.085, CFI = 0.929 and TLI = 0.910 (Table 5, Figure 5) resulting in a final good fit CFA model. To evaluate the model fit, a combination of absolute, incremental, and parsimony-adjusted indices was also employed. Composite reliability values for both TA and ABEU were 0.88 and 0.90, being above 0.70, a criterion for the model to meet the internal consistency. The square root of AVE also supported discriminant validity for each latent construct, TA and ABEU, which were 0.59 and 0.61, respectively. The correlation between the latent constructs (TA ↔ ABEU) (Figure 4) was 0.47, showing that the square root values of AVE (0.59 and 0.61) were greater than its correlation with other constructs (0.47) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) (Table 5).

Hypothesis testing: structural model assessment of the hypothesis

Bootstrap statistics for hypothesis stability

The path coefficient between TA and ABEU was robust, with a standardised estimate of 0.65, a 90% bias-corrected CI ranging from 0.583 to 0.802, and a bias value of -0.001. Moreover, the bias value of –0.001 further indicated that the estimate was both precise and stable. Multivariate kurtosis = 41.26, indicating acceptable non-normality due to large sample size. TA explained approximately 72% of the variance (R2 = 0.72) in ABEU. Further, a strong positive relationship was also supported between TA and ABEU (β = 0.65) by SEM's findings. For the ABEU construct, SEM exhibited higher representation for Better healthcare facilities (BHFH) (1.00), Internet speed and connectivity (INSP) (0.93) and Time saving (TIME) (0.88). Discussion

The study aimed to investigate the effect of TA on ABEU in Pakistan. A theoretically grounded, well-structured questionnaire, comprising variables listed in Table 1, was used to gather responses from T2DM patients. The internal consistency of the questionnaire, as tested through Cronbach's alpha, and total reliability statistics were appropriate, as reflected by the lack of a significant impact of a single item on the overall reliability (Izah et al., 2023). From the total distributed questionnaires, the valid responses gathered surpassed the minimum sample size requirement and supported the credible results (Israel, 1992). The majority of participants belonged to Punjab Province overall, predominantly male, middle-aged population with tertiary education. which provided an important insight into the domestic sociocultural and healthcare context. A predominance of Punjab-based participants highlights regional disparities in mHealth adoption in Pakistan as compared to Balochistan and Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa, where digital infrastructure and healthcare facilities were not up to the requirements and aligned with the literature in which the education and geographic location determined TA (Alam et al., 2020; Scott Kruse et al., 2018).

The gender distribution indicated a balanced sample but a male dominance, reflecting cultural factors where males have greater access to smartphones compared to females due to socioeconomic norms (Ajina et al., 2023). The middle-aged population showed a high prevalence of T2DM (Aamir et al., 2020); thus, mHealth interventions were being largely used by this age group. The agreement of a large population to continue mHealth App usage after getting better health with App use was in line with a previous domestic study on mHealth (Siddiqui et al., 2015). The high representation of tertiary education of respondents suggested a digitally literate sample, resulting in Confidence in App usage (CNUS) and an Easy source of medical information (ESRC).

The majority of respondents agreed that overall Better Health (BTHL) after App use, a cultural factor was important for their sustained App use, showing health benefit expectancy was a major determinant of sustained adoption. According to the literature, perceived usefulness and improved disease management positively influence the continued use of diabetes-related mHealth Apps (Zhang et al., 2019). In contrast, Family and friend recommendation (FARM), Doctors recommendation (DRRC), Confidence boost (CONF) and Better image (BTIM) showed comparatively lower importance. The above suggests that respondents prioritised practical health benefits over social factors for mHealth acceptance and continuation, as reported earlier (Brew-Sam et al., 2020).

Among contextual factors, respondents showed relatively high agreement towards Easy and reliable access to medical information (ESRC), reflecting the importance of accessibility and reliability of health-related information for mHealth App appearance. According to the literature, information quality and accessibility positively influence engagement with digital health technologies (Kitsiou et al., 2017). Local diabetic patients identified Time Saving (TIME) as the most important infrastructural factor influencing mHealth App acceptance and continued usage, in line with the literature (Sidorov et al., 2002). Locally, the healthcare access is limited, particularly in rural areas (Abbas & Talib, 2024); thus, Apps could be used to manage diabetes by saving time and ensuring regular glucose monitoring, causing Better sugar level (BTSL) and resulting in Better overall health (BTHL).

To assess the hypothesis of the study through EFA and CFA, the respondents’ responses were statistically analysed through SEM, associated with bootstrapping. EFA supported the validity of a two-latent (TA and ABEU) factor structure, through sample adequacy provided by the KMO threshold and meeting the EFA parameters’ acceptance criteria, i.e. significance, good convergent validity, correlation matrix, and sufficient intercorrelations between items (Shrestha, 2021). Variables with loadings above the threshold were retained for use in the CFA statistics. The initial CFA model supported TA and ABEU as latent constructs, reflected by 6 and 10 observed variables, respectively, for the former and latter (Table 1 and Figure 4). The TA and ABEU factors, cumulatively, explained total variance that exceeded the commonly accepted threshold (Hair et al., 2009). Thus, the above latent constructs were theoretically grounded, justifying the next step of CFA for an improved model because with all 16 variables under study, the model fitted poorly (Figure 4). However, the variables bearing small loadings were decrementally removed unless a good-fitting model was achieved (Figure 6), shown by incremental and absolute fit indices within the stipulated thresholds (Byrne, 2013; Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hair et al., 2009). The factors removed were Overcome the mHealth App use problem (OVCM), Family & friend recommendation (FARM), and Medical information (MEDI). RMSEA value, though, was marginal yet within the acceptable bounds. Thus, the improved CFA model supported a good convergent validity of the latent factors (Brown & Hedges, 1994; MacCallum et al., 1996), suggesting a positive reciprocal relationship between TA and ABEU, where the former explained approximately 72% of the variance in the latter. Furthermore, EFA and CFA analysis, after reduction of variables, empirically supported that the 5 (instead of 6) and 8 (rather than 10) variables loaded on TA and ABEU, respectively, indicated a diverse perception about these two latent constructs.

The prerequisites of SEM for hypothesis testing, such as high internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity, supporting the accurate representation of the corresponding latent factors, through observed variables were met. Nevertheless, skewness and kurtosis in mHealth App use Satisfaction (SATF), mHealth App use on Doctor recommendation (DRRC), Internet speed & connectivity (INSP), and Better sugar level by mHealth App use (BTSL) violated the assumption of multivariate normality. Such deviations could distort the maximum likelihood estimation, thus triggered the use of bootstrap. Bootstrapping helped mitigate the issue by generating an empirical sample distribution through repeated random sampling, allowing an accurate CI range (without including zero) and bias assessment. It enhanced the robustness, reliability and replicability and validated the SEM model's structural paths, for factor representation (through loading) and the hypothesised TA-ABEU relationship aligning with best practices (Hair et al., 2019; Preacher & Hayes, 2008).

SEM revealed that Better personal image (BTIM), a cultural factor and Better health facilities (BHFH), infrastructural factors had the highest loading, respectively, on TA and ABEU latent constructs, suggesting them as the most influential for acceptance and continued use of mHealth App in local T2DM patients. The above implied that for the patients who adopt technology were perceived to be modern (image enhancement), socially-identified, health-conscious and getting better healthcare facilities, which remarkably impacted shaping health-related behaviour. This behaviour encourages the acceptance of mHealth tools, aligning with TAM2 construct of subjective norm and image (Bashir et al., 2022), while long-term mHealth use is primarily system-driven as SEM revealed through high loading of Better health facilities (BHFH) and Internet speed (INSP) related factor on ABEU. Other strong loadings, including Confidence boost (CONF), an important cultural factor, suggested that building patient confidence through the use of an mHealth App was a remarkable contributor in TA, as reported (Zhang et al., 2017). While Confidence in using the App (CNUS) was an important contextual factor highlighted as a cognitive pathway, suggesting that empowered and competent patients in using technology exhibited long-term engagement and more confidence in managing health through technology (Liu et al., 2022). Internet Speed and connectivity (INSP), an important infrastructural factor, was the strongest indicator of App engagement, reflecting the importance of the infrastructure quality and access to healthcare resources. Therefore, robust digital infrastructure, aligning with the UTAUT, is essential (Fayez et al., 2025) in Pakistan because, as reported, the connectivity issues hinder satisfaction and long-term engagement in local patients (Aamir et al., 2018).

In this study, descriptive statistics helped capture the respondents’ perceived importance of factors for App adoption and engagement, EFA and CFA identified the latent structural determinants, and SEM explored the hypothesised TA-ABEU relationship. Nevertheless, certain factors received higher weightage by the respondents, while lower in SEM or vice versa. This divergence between findings of descriptive and SEM statistics is common. It occurs due to strong reflection of the underlying latent construct, sharing of substantial variance with other construct indicators by items and distinction between self-reported importance and the latent behavioural detriments (Hair et al., 2009). Accordingly, a perception-behaviour gap was reflected for sustained App usage in this study; patients emphasised health improvement and time efficacy (functional determinants), while SEM revealed institutional and socio-cultural facilitation as a driver. TAM2 and UTAUT could explain the above discrepancy – perceived usefulness alone (a TAM construct) is insufficient to persuade sustained engagement without system-level support (UTAUT facilitating conditions) and social identity mechanism (TAM2 Image boost), as cited (Tao et al., 2020; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). This indicated the patients’ awareness of functional benefits of mHealth technologies, but system-level facilitation support and socially embedded technological intervention more strongly governed the long term behavioral engagement, implying TA was a multi-layered adoption mechanism.

In congruence with CFA, SEM showed a substantial practical impact of TA on ABEU (β = 0.65), where a rise in initial technological acceptance followed the enhanced long-term use, supporting acceptance of the alternative hypothesis (H1 = TA had a significant positive effect on ABEU). Literature supports and theoretically explains the above, as TA is a critical driver of ABEU (Browne et al., 1993) and as explained by TAM and ECT, App adoption mediates the transition from intention to actual use behaviour to continued use when users accept a technology on experiencing the expected benefits from mHealth technology (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Davis, 1989). This finding also addressed the gap of overlooking the post-adoption engagement in literature (Marangunić & Granić, 2015). Therefore, for persistent App use, initial acceptance challenges should be overcome and focused on fostering TA and engagement. The acceptance and engagement of mHealth App were influenced by contextual, cultural and infrastructural factors, in line with the literature (Liu et al., 2017). Therefore, the above variables should be considered for App design, being itself a determinant for App acceptance and engagement (Ikwunne et al., 2021) for effective medication adherence and self-care behaviour in T2DM patients. These local findings could be extrapolated to the other developing countries, like Pakistan.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

The study integrated EFA, CFA, and SEM in a unified empirical framework in a systematic, replicable, and rigorous way, a paucity of researched area. EFA helped uncover the latent structure in the questionnaire, which had not yet been established in T2DM patients in Pakistan. CFA validated the factor loadings and fit indices, revealing that these were context-sensitive, theoretically relevant, and statistically rigorous. SEM allowed the exploration of the relationship among latent variables to measure the relationship between acceptance and engagement.

Limitations

This study has following several limitations that must be acknowledged, which could provide a basis for future research directions:

Purposive sampling design

Purposive sampling is based on an intentional selection of participants who meet specific expertise or criteria. It, though, effectively gathers qualitative data, yet has certain limitations including subjective bias, limited likelihood of generalisability, and risk for narrow selection of participants with missed characteristics (Tajik et al., 2025). In this study, using the reported practices, the subjective bias was mitigated using clear inclusion criteria with ensured objectivity (Campbell et al., 2020). The lack of generalisability was ameliorated with the contextual descriptive data (Tisdell et al., 2025). The narrow focus was addressed using the strict inclusion criteria, which enabled the selection of information-intensive participants (Palinkas et al., 2015).

Cross-sectional study

The cross-sectional studies have several flaws. Causality and temporality, due to data collected at a single point in time, make scientific proof of the relationship between independent and dependent variable difficult (Kline, 2023). However, advanced statistical approaches used in this study significantly improve measurement quality (Beran & Violato, 2010). Response biases in cross-sectionally gathered data are handled by EFA and CFA, as they enhance measurement quality by assessing the reliability and validity of scales (Mishra, 2016). CFA and SEM, by utilising the latent variables through surveyed items, allowed the separation of true score variance from measurement error within the measurement model (Brown & Moore, 2012). CFA, EFA, and SEM improved construct validity and reduced measurement error, but cannot remove confounding from unmeasured variables. Method variance can only be assessed when explicitly modelled in CFA. Confounding bias due to unmeasured factors was partially fixed by AFA, CFA and SEM. CFA estimated the method variance (Hair et al., 2009). A cross-sectional studies are widely used for measuring the population behaviour at a particular time point. They are considered the first step in the scientific process and reveal patterns (correlation) in data signalling for future longitudinal studies, which are the true remedy for the drawbacks associated with cross-sectional studies (Levin, 2006). It offers fast results, high practicality, and low cost. The statistical approaches used in this study partially handled the drawbacks of this study, being cross-selctional. Further, use of the large, nationally representative samples exceeding the actual sample size for such studies, has made descriptive findings acceptably accurate for generalisability (Chomeya et al., 2024).

Reliance on self-reported data

This study relied on self-reported data, which might introduce response biases. Appropriately calculated sample size and incorporating objective mHealth App usage metrics or behavioural data could minimise the above biases and enhanced the accuracy of engagement measurements (Chomeya et al., 2024).

Studying the isolated effects of TAM, TAM2, UTAUT and ECT models

While this study integrates variable selection guided by literature, TAM, TAM2, UTAUT, and ECT to explore the dual phases of adoption and continued use, future studies could examine the models in isolation to compare their individual impact on T2DM patients in Pakistan. This could provide insights into whether the integrated approach offers a significant advantage in understanding sustained engagement with mHealth Apps.

Conclusion

Technological acceptance was found to play a pivotal role in influencing App-based engagement and use among patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). By integrating the technology acceptance models and theories, the study highlighted that both the acceptance factors (i.e. Better self-image (BTIM), Confidence (CONF), enhanced the perceived ease of use, and post-acceptance determinants (e.g. Satisfaction (STAF), confirmation of expectations through Better health (BTHL), Doctor recommendation (DRRC), Better sugar level (BTSL), Better overall health (BTHL), Time saving (Time) were critical in sustaining the engagement with mHealth Apps. Thus, for improving digital readiness and long-term engagement with digital health tools in resource-constrained settings, users need image-boosting features, increased user confidence, and addressing infrastructure barriers. Policymakers and healthcare providers should focus on the above aspects to optimise the adoption of mHealth Apps and ensure effective chronic disease management. The findings of the study are expected to contribute to a better understanding of design for mHealth Apps and their implementation to meet the needs of patients in low-resource environments, and to offer a framework for future digital health interventions.
