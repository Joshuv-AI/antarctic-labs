Reference Interval Establishment for Four Coagulation Parameters Using Indirect Methods in a High‐Altitude Chinese Population: A Retrospective Real‐World Analysis
Authors: Banjiu Zhaxi, Danni Mu, Shengfang Ye, Chaochao Ma, Zhen Da, Yichen Ma, Yumeng Gao, Shensong Li, Yixicuomu, Zhaxiyangzong, Jinrong Pang, Mingjun Zhan, Ling Qiu, Qu Dan, Xinqi Cheng, Zhijuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study utilized two indirect methods to establish reference intervals (RIs) for four coagulation parameters for the adult population in Tibet, a high‐altitude region in Western China.MethodsCoagulation Data, Including Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (APTT), prothrombin Time (PT), thrombin Time (TT), and Fibrinogen (FIB) Obtained From the People's Hospital of Tibet Autonomous Region Between 2019 and 2023, Were Subjected to Box‐Cox Transformation for Normalization and Tukey Method for Outlier Removal. the RIs Were Calculated Using Hoffmann and refineR Methods, Respectively.ResultsA total of 5086, 5282, 5386, and 5377 test results for APTT, PT, TT, and FIB were included. The Hoffmann and refineR method yielded the following total RIs: APTT 30.54–46.32 and 30.14–44.30 s; PT 10.54–14.51 and 10.32–13.89 s; TT 14.92–19.46 and 14.88–18.93 s; FIB 2.05–4.68 and 2.02–4.40 g/L, respectively. Gender‐partitioned RIs were calculated for all four parameters, while age‐partitioned RIs were specifically calculated for PT and FIB based on bias ratio values. The lower limits of RIs estimated by the two methods remained consistent. The established RIs for APTT and FIB were significantly higher than those utilized in the plain area and those provided by the manufacturer, whereas the established RIs of TT were significantly lower.ConclusionThe RIs for coagulation parameters in the population living on the Tibetan Plateau exhibited significant divergence from those utilized in the plain area. Specific RIs for high‐altitude regions should be established to accurately evaluate the presence of coagulation disorders.Keywords: coagulation, hypoxia, indirect method, reference interval, Tibet

Introduction

Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), and fibrinogen (FIB) are routinely used coagulation parameters reflecting different components of the coagulation cascade. These tests play an essential role in preoperative assessment, anticoagulant therapy monitoring, and the evaluation of coagulation disorders. The appropriate interpretation of these parameters relies heavily on the use of accurate reference intervals (RIs).

The Tibet Plateau is distinguished by its extremely high altitude (averaging over 4000 m) and chronic hypoxic conditions, which may impact physiological adaptations affecting the coagulation system. Previous research has indicated that increased altitude leads to a procoagulatory state during both acute and chronic exposure to hypoxia [1, 2]. Additionally, Ding et al. have developed a predictive model for APTT reference values, demonstrating a significant positive correlation with regional altitude in healthy elderly individuals [3]. Therefore, when assessing coagulation disorders using coagulation parameters in individuals residing at high altitudes, the effects of altitude should be taken into account.

The application of appropriate reference intervals (RIs) is essential for precise diagnosis and clinical decision‐making. General RIs may not be suitable for assessing the health status of certain populations, such as the elderly [4], pregnant women, and Tibetan individuals. Therefore, it is imperative to establish specific RIs for high‐altitude populations to assist accurate medical decision‐making.

Methods of RIs establishment can be divided into two categories: direct sampling methods and indirect methods [5, 6]. The conventional direct sampling method is often limited by high cost, time consumption, and difficulties in defining truly healthy individuals, particularly in special populations. In contrast, indirect methods based on real‐world laboratory data provide a practical alternative for RI estimation and have been increasingly applied in laboratory medicine. This study aims to utilize real‐world data from a clinical laboratory to investigate the factors influencing four coagulation parameters in plateau residents and establish RIs in Tibet using the Hoffmann [7] and refineR [8] methods, respectively. Then, we compared the results with manufacturer‐provided RIs and previously reported data from low‐altitude populations.

Material and Methods

Study Population

Health examination data of coagulation parameters including APTT, PT, TT, and FIB were obtained from the electronic health records at the People's Hospital of Tibet Autonomous Region (located at an altitude of 3650 m) for the period spanning January 2019 to December 2023. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) repeated test results of the same individuals; (2) age < 18 years; (3) incomplete information in the data (e.g., lack of gender information). Due to the retrospective and de‐identified nature of the laboratory data, disease‐specific exclusion (e.g., polycythemia) was not performed.

Ethics Statement

This retrospective real‐world study was approved by the Ethics Committee of People's Hospital of Tibet Autonomous Region (ethical approval document number: ME‐TBHP‐22‐KJ‐040).

Sample Collection

Fasting venous blood (2 mL) was collected in the morning and added to an anticoagulant tube containing sodium citrate (KANG JIAN Medical, Jiangsu, China). The samples were then centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 15 min. Plasma samples were collected at room temperature within 2 h for the determination of APTT, PT, TT, and FIB.

Laboratory Assays and Quality Control

The Stago STA‐R Evolution automatic hemostasis testing analyzer (Diagnostica Stago, Asnières sur Seine, France) and its corresponding analytical reagents were utilized. Parameter setting, calibration, and detection procedures were conducted in strict adherence to standard operating procedures outlined in the instruction manual. Daily internal quality control tests were performed on the instrument as per laboratory requirements to ensure comprehensive testing following quality assurance measures.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), SPSS 27.0 software (IBM, New York, United States), and R programming language (V.4.2.1). The Box‐Cox method was employed to improve the normality of the data prior to using the Tukey method for outlier identification [9, 10]. The Effect of Gender and Age on Four Coagulation Parameters

The distribution of coagulation parameters categorized by gender and age is illustrated in Figure 2. FIB levels were observed to increase with age. Females exhibited lower TT levels but higher FIB levels compared to males. Quantile regression analysis revealed significant gender‐related effects on all four coagulation parameters (p < 0.001, Table 2). Additionally, age was found to have significant effects on PT and FIB levels, with PT decreasing and FIB increasing with age. It is worth noting that the PT levels in individuals aged ≥ 60 showed no difference from the reference group, possibly due to the small sample size of elderly individuals in this study (97 individuals). Taking these findings into consideration, gender‐specific RIs for APTT and TT were established, as well as gender and age‐specific RIs for PT and FIB. Subsequently, decisions regarding partitioning based on BR values of these specific RIs were made.

Additionally, for PT, the RI of the female group should be further separately calculated by age, with a threshold of 40. For FIB, both male and female groups should be stratified by age. Subsequently, we compared the RIs established in our study with those provided by the manufacturer and previous articles. Our findings demonstrate significant differences in RIs for the four parameters among high‐altitude residents compared to those living in plain areas, with Tibetan individuals exhibiting higher levels of APTT and FIB and lower levels of PT and TT. Of note, RIs calculated in our study had more satisfactory consistency with those determined using the direct sampling method (sample size = 1298) [12], especially when using the refineR method. These findings suggested that indirect methods had potential for clinical implementation within the context of Tibet.

Discussion

To investigate the physiological distribution of coagulation parameters in Tibetans, two indirect algorithms (Hoffmann and refineR) were utilized to establish total, gender‐ and/or age‐partitioned RIs. Gender affected all four parameters, and age influenced PT and FIB. Based on BR values, RIs for APTT and TT were further partitioned by gender, and for PT and FIB by both gender and age. Our study revealed that the LLs and ULs of APTT, as well as the ULs of FIB, were significantly higher compared to those provided by the manufacturer; conversely, the ULs of TT were significantly lower. Therefore, appropriate RIs for Tibetans should be established to avoid inaccurate diagnoses.

Long‐term exposure to high altitude is associated with multiple physiological adaptations in individuals, including increased erythrocyte mass and hemoglobin concentration [14, 15, 16]. Previous studies have reported that hypoxic conditions may influence coagulation‐related parameters [1]. Excessive compensatory proliferation of red blood cells (RBC) can lead to pathological changes such as high‐altitude erythrocytosis [17]. Shi et al. reported that levels of FIB, APTT, and TT were higher in patients with high‐altitude erythrocytosis compared to controls, and comprehensive therapy was shown to promote recovery of coagulation function [18]. These alterations might reflect physiological or adaptive responses; therefore, differences in coagulation parameter levels observed in high‐altitude populations highlight the necessity of establishing region‐specific RIs to ensure appropriate clinical interpretation.

A previous study analyzing RIs for coagulation parameters in apparently healthy Tibetan adults employed a direct sampling method and included 1298 participants with consideration of laboratory and imaging indicators. This study revealed age‐related differences in FIB levels and gender‐related differences in APTT, PT, TT, and FIB levels. The calculated RIs for Tibetan adults using the direct sampling method are presented in Table 3 demonstrating significant discrepancies from those provided by the manufacturer. However, the direct sampling method itself has limitations, such as the requirement of a tedious, costly, and time‐consuming process to recruit a sufficient number of healthy individuals and the difficulty in clearly defining their health. Therefore, we applied indirect methods that utilize real‐world data to establish RIs based on a large sample size, which are typically regarded as cost‐effective and practical [5]. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that RIs calculated using Hofmann, Bhattacharya, and refneR methods for triiodo‐thyronine and thyroxine closely match standard RIs which are based on reference individuals selected following strict inclusion and exclusion criteria [11]. Therefore, specific RIs for coagulation parameters in the Tibetan population were established using physical examination data from the laboratory information system.

The RIs established in our study appear to differ from those provided by the manufacturer and those for non‐high‐altitude populations [13]. The LL and UL of total RIs for APTT, as well as the UL of total RIs for FIB calculated by two methods, were significantly higher than those from the manufacturer and from non‐high‐altitude areas. Meanwhile, the UL of total RIs for TT calculated by two methods was significantly lower than those from the manufacturer. The increased RI for APTT may be attributed to a chronic hypercoagulable state due to hypoxia, potentially inducing slow consumption of coagulation factors and damage to vascular endothelial cells, thereby continuously impeding normal regulation of coagulation function [19]. Gupta et al. also reported an increased level of APTT in individuals living at high altitudes [20]. Notably, conflicting findings have been reported in studies examining APTT levels following short exposure to high altitudes [1, 21]. The RI for PT closely aligns with that of low‐altitude regions. PT reflects the function of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, requiring the addition of excessive tissue thromboplastin and calcium ions to plasma to convert prothrombin to thrombin. It was demonstrated that PT levels had no difference in healthy individuals upon arrival at high altitude, after 3 months, and after 8 months of residence [21]. However, Jiang et al. observed an increase in PT levels at low altitude compared to those at high altitude (13.83 ± 1.14 vs. 12.86 ± 0.79) [20]. The study also observed an increase in the ULs of RI for FIB. FIB, which is converted to fibrin during blood coagulation, plays a crucial role in the processes of coagulation and hemostasis. The elevated levels of FIB indicate a hypercoagulable state and have been reported to be higher in low altitude healthy volunteers compared to healthy high‐altitude subjects [20]. Furthermore, FIB levels were found to increase significantly with prolonged stay at high altitude environments [21, 22]. TT is the time taken for fibrin to condense into a firm clot in the action of thrombosis, and decreased TT indicates a hypercoagulation state. Our study found that the RI for TT was significantly lower, possibly due to increased FIB levels in high‐altitude population. In summary, RIs calculated using indirect methods were consistent with those obtained through the direct sampling method, thereby supporting the reliability of our results.

Our findings indicated that levels of APTT, PT, and TT were significantly higher in males than in females, while FIB levels were higher in the female group, consistent with previous study [12]. Li et al. [23] demonstrated that gender had impacts on levels of TT and FIB but not on levels of APTT and PT. Abdullah et al. [24] reported that PT and INR levels of males were significantly higher than those of females, while APTT levels were significantly lower in men. Gender influence on coagulation may be associated with multiple factors, including menstruation, smoking habits, and alcohol intake. The discrepancy among studies can be attributed to differences in selected populations and measurement platforms. Additionally, our results showed that PT levels firstly decreased with age and then increased in people aged > 60, while FIB levels increased with age. This is consistent with previous studies [12, 23]. The increase in FIB with age is well supported by numerous studies [25, 26]. As an acute phase protein, FIB is linked to infection and inflammation. Moreover, an elevated level of FIB is considered a risk factor of the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, which may partly account for its age‐related increase [27].

The BR values (Table 4) were utilized for the objective comparison of RIs obtained by two indirect methods. The results indicated that the LLs of total and partitioned RIs for the four parameters calculated by Hoffmann were generally consistent with those by refineR. However, there were generally inconsistencies in the ULs calculated by the two methods. This discrepancy may be attributed to the large variation of the data at the right end of the right‐skewed distributions. Additionally, data‐mining algorithms combined with simplified data pre‐processing methods had superior performance in calculating LLs when handling data with near‐Gaussian or right‐skewed distributions [11]. These findings suggest that data distribution characteristics have an impact on algorithm consistency and accuracy, which aligns with previous research [11]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the RIs for APTT, TT, and FIB calculated using data from Tibetan residents significantly differ from those derived from non‐high‐altitude populations. Hence, it is imperative to establish RIs for laboratory coagulation indicators in a particular geographical area to ensure optimal clinical decision‐making.

This study has the advantage in establishing specific RIs for coagulation parameters using indirect methods for high‐altitude populations, which exhibit apparent differences in the coagulation system. Additionally, two algorithms (Hoffmann and refineR) were employed respectively to establish RIs, and the results of the two algorithms were objectively compared. However, there are also limitations in our study. Firstly, the sample size of the female group was significantly smaller than that of the male group, leading to wider confidence intervals especially for LLs and ULs of RIs for female subgroups. Secondly, the age distribution was uneven, which is consistent with the characteristics of health examination populations in high‐altitude ethnic minority regions. RIs for elderly individuals (age > 60) were not specifically established in this study due to a small sample size for elderly individuals undergoing health examinations in our cohort, precluding their use in calculating RIs using indirect methods. Thirdly, real‐world laboratory data is inherently limited by the absence of comprehensive clinical diagnosis and follow‐up information, which prevents precise exclusion of specific conditions. As a result, RIs were derived from mixed distributions and relied on statistical algorithms to identify the healthy population. Fourthly, the data utilized originated from a single laboratory and detection system; therefore, further research is needed to determine their applicability to other laboratories.

Conclusion

Using real‐world big data, total and gender/age‐specific RIs for APTT, PT, TT, and FIB were established for the Tibetan population in high‐altitude areas of China through two indirect algorithms. The RIs for the four coagulation parameters calculated in our study exhibited significant disparities from those provided by the manufacturer. Consequently, we advocate for developing region‐specific RIs for high‐altitude areas like Tibet to ensure accurate assessment of coagulation disorders.
