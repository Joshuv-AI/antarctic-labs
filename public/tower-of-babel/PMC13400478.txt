Efficacy and Mechanisms of Combined Central-Peripheral Magnetic Stimulation on Prefrontal-Sensorimotor Cortex Connectivity in Post-Stroke Dysphagia after Supratentorial Stroke: A Randomized, Single-Blind, Parallel-Controlled Trial
Authors: Zirui Luo, Zhiying Zhu, Yujue Wang, Na Su, Yajing Xue, Wanqi Zhang, Wei Liao, Yaoyao You, Lin Ling, Mei Li, Junjie Liang, Zulin Dou, Haian Mao, Nenggui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCombined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation shows promise for post-stroke dysphagia (PSD), but its neural mechanisms remain unclear. We evaluated the its efficacy and cortical mechanisms in PSD patients.MethodsIn this single-blind, parallel-controlled trial, 44 PSD patients were randomized to four groups: active-rTMS + active-rPMS, active-rTMS + sham-rPMS, sham-rTMS + active-rPMS, or sham-rTMS + sham-rPMS. Clinical scales, such as functional oral intake scale (FOIS) and Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS), and Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study (VFSS) were accessed before, after intervention. Cortical activation during rest and swallowing task was measured by functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS).ResultsSignificant interaction effects (time × intervention) were found for FOIS (Wald χ2 = 39.816, p < 0.001) and PAS scores (Wald χ2 = 45.433, p < 0.001), with the active-rTMS + active-rPMS group showing the most pronounced therapeutic gains. Concurrently, this group displayed significant improvements of laryngeal vestibular closure duration (LCD, MD = –3.598, 95% CI -4.177 to –3.019, adjusted p < 0.001) and superior hyoid excursion (SHE, MD = –3.708, 95% CI -4.213 to –3.18, adjusted p < 0.001). Neurophysiologically, increased functional connectivity (FC) within the ipsilesional prefrontal-sensorimotor network was observed and the changes of FC in ipsilesional M1 and S1 is significantly correlated with the change magnitude of LCD (rs = 0.78, p = 0.004) and SHE (rs = 0.67, P = 0.023).ConclusionsThe combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation robustly improved swallowing function, temporal and kinematic sequence of swallowing, and sensori-motor connectivity in PSD patients, representing a promising neuromodulatory therapy for PSD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12975-026-01482-1.Keywords: Post-stroke dysphagia, Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, Repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation, Combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation, Neuromodulation

Introduction

Post-stroke dysphagia (PSD) is a common sensorimotor dysfunction with an incidence rate ranging from 30 to 65% [1, 2]. It predisposes patients to a spectrum of complications, including aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, depression, and anxiety. These sequelae significantly impair quality of life, extend hospital stays, escalate medical expenditures, and increase mortality, thereby establishing the improvement of swallowing function as a critical priority in post-stroke rehabilitation [3, 4].

To harness neuroplasticity for PSD recovery, noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques are widely used [5], with the primary challenge being the identification of optimal targets and parameters. The pharyngeal M1 is the most common target, chosen for its dual role as a swallowing motor hub [6], and a critical site for recovery-related plasticity [7, 8]. Accordingly, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting M1 has seen growing application in PSD treatment [9, 10]. Despite this, improvements from M1-targeted rTMS are frequently limited, likely stemming from an insufficient induction of the comprehensive neuroplasticity needed for full recovery [11].

With advancements in NIBS, the “Central-Peripheral-Central” closed-loop model has gained traction as a promising framework to enhance recovery [12]. In line with this concept, the combination of rTMS with repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) has emerged as a promising intervention for improving swallowing function, with preliminary evidence suggesting potential additional benefits over single modality stimulation [13]. Beyond dysphagia, the combination of central and peripheral neuromodulation has been increasingly investigated in post stroke motor rehabilitation. For upper limb recovery, a meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reported that rTMS combined with rPMS produced larger improvements in Fugl Meyer Assessment scores compared with rPMS alone (rPMS: Hedge’s g = 0.703, rPMS + rTMS: Hedge’s g = 0.892) [14]. Similarly, a recent RCT found that compared with rTMS alone, rTMS combined with rPMS significantly improved lower limb motor function, walking ability, and balance in patients with chronic stroke [15]. These findings support the “central-peripheral-central” closed loop model [12], which posits that simultaneous or sequential activation of central motor pathways and peripheral afferent inputs produces synergistic neuroplastic effects.

rPMS may improve motor function in stroke patients by modulating the fronto-parietal network [16], yet its functional benefits in PSD are undetermined. Additionally, a standardized rPMS coil for the suprahyoid region is undefined, and supporting clinical evidence is sparse [17, 18]. To address these gaps, this single-blinded, randomized, parallel-controlled trial examined the efficacy of a 2-week long combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation protocol: 10-Hz rTMS over ipsilesional pharyngeal M1 and 10-Hz rPMS to the suprahyoid region, on swallowing function in PSD. We further assessed its impact on temporal and kinematic sequences of swallowing and cortical reorganization.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

This was a prospective, randomized, single-blinded, sham-controlled, single-center clinical trial. Participants with PSD were randomly allocated to one of four parallel groups using computer-generated permuted block randomization with block size of 4: 1) active rTMS + active rPMS (a-rTMS + a-rPMS), 2) active rTMS + sham rPMS (a-rTMS + s-rPMS), 3) sham rTMS + active rPMS (s-rTMS + a-rPMS), or 4) sham rTMS + sham rPMS (s-rTMS + s-rPMS). Each participant completed three laboratory visits. At the baseline (T0) and 1 day post-treatment (T1) visits, participants underwent comprehensive assessments including the Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS), the Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) with Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study (VFSS), and a functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) session. Swallowing function was further assessed using the FOIS at the 30-day follow-up (T2) (Fig. 1).

Experimental flowchart

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (GYWY-L2025-179) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500113782), Registration Date: 2025–12-03. It was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the CONSORT guidelines for randomized trials. All participants provided written informed consent following a full disclosure of the study's aims, procedures, potential benefits, and risks.

Participants

A total of 76 patients with PSD were initially screened for eligibility at the Rehabilitation Department of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University. Participants aged 30–90 years were enrolled if they met the following criteria: (1) first-ever unilateral subcortical stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic) confirmed by cranial CT or MRI; and (2) dysphagia, defined as a Kubota Water Swallowing Test score ≥ grade 3. Dysphagia was initially screened using the Kubota Water Swallowing Test (KWST; score ≥ 3). All screened patients subsequently underwent videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) to confirm the diagnosis. Only those with VFSS evidence of penetration (PAS ≥ 3) or aspiration (PAS ≥ 6) were enrolled. Exclusion criteria includes (1) comorbid neurological disorders (intracranial tumors, CNS infections, Parkinson's disease, etc.); (2) structural abnormalities of the oropharynx or esophagus; (3) prior oropharyngeal/neck surgery or dysphagia of other etiologies; (4) history of epilepsy; (5) metal implants in the body; (6)psychiatric disorders or severe claustrophobia; (7) cognitive impairment or aphasia; and (8) inability to cooperate or refusal to provide informed consent.

Sample Size

Sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1, Universität Düsseldorf, Germany). The effect size was derived from a meta-analysis of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) for post-stroke dysphagia, which reported a standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d) of approximately 0.65 for active versus sham stimulation and a RCT study [19, 20]. For our four-group, two-time-point repeated-measures design focusing on the group-by-time interaction, the appropriate effect size metric is Cohen’s f. Based on the relationship Cohen’s d = 2f for two-group comparisons, the original d = 0.65 corresponds to Cohen’s f = 0.325.

Using the “ANOVA: Repeated measures, within-between interaction” module in G*Power, with the following input parameters: number of groups = 4, number of measurements = 2, effect size f = 0.325, α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.95, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5 (conservative estimate), and nonsphericity correction ε = 1, the calculation yielded a total sample size of 48 participants (12 per group).

Interventions

A cooled figure-of-eight coil (model no. 020 YIRUIDE) was used to perform rTMS, positioned on the scalp over the ipsilesional hemisphere with the handle pointing anteriorly and the coil center located 6 cm lateral and 3 cm anterior to the Cz point according to the international 10–20 EEG system [21]. rPMS was conducted using a custom-designed coil of 2.5 cm diameter, with the handle pointing cranially and centered over the suprahyoid region. For rTMS, stimulation was delivered at 90%–120% of the resting motor threshold (RMT) of the unaffected thenar muscles. For rPMS, intensity was first set at 30% of the motor threshold required to elicit a visible twitch in the lesioned-side forearm extensor muscles, and then adjusted according to individual tolerance. Both modalities used a 10-Hz frequency, comprising 60 trains of 20 pulses each, with a 15-s inter-train interval. These parameters comply with international safety guidelines and have been used in prior PSD studies [6, 13, 17, 18, 22]. For sham conditions, the active coil was tilted 90 degrees away from the target (M1 for rTMS; suprahyoid region for rPMS), ensuring no effective stimulation while preserving auditory cues [23]. The treatment regimen consisted of 6 daily sessions per week, administered over 2 consecutive weeks. Each session began with rTMS, followed by rPMS and concluded with 30 min of conventional swallowing rehabilitation (Fig. 2). The sequence of rTMS followed by rPMS was chosen to prime central motor pathways prior to peripheral afferent stimulation, potentially enhancing Hebbian-type sensorimotor integration [24]. Conventional swallowing rehabilitation was provided last to translate neurophysiological gains into functional performance [25].

Clinical Scales for Swallowing Function

Swallowing function was comprehensively assessed using validated scales. FOIS was employed at T0, T1, and T2 to evaluate the overall oral intake capability. Swallowing safety and physiology, evaluated using the PAS combined with VFSS, the diagnostic gold standard, were measured at T0 and T1. The primary outcomes were the FOIS and PAS scores. All patient experiences and adverse events were recorded throughout the study.

Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study and Parameter Analysis

During the VFSS, participants remained in an upright seated position to ensure fluoroscopic visualization from the oral cavity to the C6 vertebra. They were presented with a series of calibrated boluses, prepared by mixing a contrast agent with a thickener to achieve four standardized consistencies: thin liquid (Food 1), nectar-thick (Food 2), honey-thick (Food 3), and pudding-thick (Food 4). The examination began with a 3 mL bolus of Food 2. The volume was then progressively increased to 5 mL and 10 mL based on the patient's tolerance and swallowing performance. A swallowing therapist was present throughout to monitor safety; the procedure was immediately paused or terminated upon observation of significant aspiration, with suctioning performed to clear the airway if necessary.

Temporal sequences of swallowing and kinematic parameters were analyzed offline by a specialized expert using high-resolution video recordings of 3 mL nectar-thick (Food 2) bolus swallows. The temporal sequences of swallowing include the following: 1) the onsets of hyoid excursion, 2) onset of laryngeal closure, 3) onset of upper esophageal sphincter (UES) opening, 4) pharyngeal constriction (PC) duration, 5) laryngeal vestibular closure duration (LCD), 6) UES opening duration and 7) hyoid excursion duration. The first three parameters were calculated based on the onset time point of PC and the last four parameters were illustrated in Fig. 3.

The kinematic parameters comprise UES opening diameter, the magnitude of superior hyoid excursion (SHE) and anterior hyoid excursion (AHE). Then the image was rotated until the reference line is perpendicular to the horizontal plane to correct for head tilt. The C4 anteroinferior corner at these two states were also marked as (C4X1, C4Y1) and (C4X2, C4Y2) [26]. All VFSS quantitative analysis were performed by a single analyst (Y. Wang), who has over 10 years of experience and was a co-author of a previously published reliability study validating the same VFSS measurement protocol [27]. In that study, intra rater ICCs for most temporal and kinematic parameters ranged from 0.75 to 0.98, indicating good to excellent reliability. Hyoid displacement parameters showed moderate intra rater reliability (ICC 0.54–0.57), which has since been improved by standardized image rotation and higher resolution digital acquisition. To minimize bias, the analyst was blinded to group allocation and time point, and video files were randomly reordered. Although a new reliability assessment was not performed for this study, the established track record of the analyst and the standardized protocol provide strong assurance of measurement consistency.

fNIRS Acquisition and Analysis

The participants were asked to sit in a 30-degree reclined position or upright with back support for 10 min. Then, they performed as many dry swallows as possible within twenty seconds, followed by a twenty-second rest. This cycle was repeated three times [28]. A multichannel system (NirSmart, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) was used to continuously monitor concentration changes of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) and total hemoglobin (HbT) in the cerebral cortex. The apparatus comprised dual-wavelength near-infrared light sources (730 nm and 850 nm light-emitting diodes, LEDs) and avalanche photodiodes (APDs) as detection elements. A configuration of 12 source optodes and 10 detector optodes generated 24 measurement channels (Fig. 5). Source-detector separation averaged 3.0 cm (range: 2.7–3.3 cm) across all channels, with hemodynamic data sampled at 11 Hz. Channel coordinates and corresponding brain region calibrations are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

fNIRS channel arrangements. Abbreviations: DLPFC: dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, FPA: frontopolar area, S1: primary somatosensory cortex, M1: primary motor cortex, PMC&SMA: premotor and supplementary motor area, Red cross illustrates the rTMS stimulation point in the ipsilesional hemisphere

The raw fNIRS data was preprocessed by using NirSpark (V1.8.9, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). In order to compare between groups, the channels in the right hemisphere from right side stroke participants were flipped to the left side before preprocessing. The raw data was preprocessed with following steps. First, based on visual inspection, channels contaminated by excessive noise, including motion artifacts, abrupt signal changes, or poor coupling, were flagged and removed. Participants for whom more than half of the channels were rejected were consequently excluded from the analysis. Any 20-s block with visible coughing, choking, or absence of swallowing movements was excluded from subsequent GLM analysis. Second, included raw datasets were detrended, artifact removed, converted to optical density, band-pass filtered (0.01–0.2 Hz), and converted to HbO concentration. The hemodynamic response was then modeled per trial from −2 s to 40 s (baseline: −2–0 s). Consistent with our 3-block paradigm (20 s task/20 s rest), the HbO signals from all three task blocks were time-locked, superimposed, and averaged to produce a representative block-averaged response for subsequent analysis. The general linear model (GLM) was applied to analyze preprocessed HbO time-series data for each channel. The resulting β-values, reflecting channel-specific cortical activation levels, were used as estimators to predict the hemodynamic response function (HRF) of HbO signals, representing peak HRF magnitude. Hemodynamic time courses of HbO concentration were extracted from predefined network modules (ROIs). Pairwise Pearson correlations between these time courses across channels were calculated and subsequently subjected to Fisher’s z-transformation. The transformed correlation coefficients served as the measure of FC between channels.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests were used to assess the data distribution and variance homogeneity. Values are reported as mean ± SD or median (quartiles) (as appropriate). To evaluate the effects of group, time, and their interaction on FOIS and PAS scores, a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was employed. For significant interaction effects, simple main effects were examined: (1) comparisons among the four groups at each time point, and (2) comparisons among the three time points within each group. All pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to control the type I error rate.

A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted with Group (4 levels) as the between-subjects factor and Time (2 levels: pre- and post-intervention) as the within-subjects factor for the VFSS-derived parameters. Levene’s test indicated heterogeneity of variances across groups at certain time points (p < 0.05), while Shapiro–Wilk test of normality and Mauchly’s test of sphericity was satisfied. Given that ANOVA is generally robust to violations of homogeneity of variance when group sizes are balanced, and sphericity was met, we proceeded with the repeated measures ANOVA and applied the Greenhouse–Geisser correction. To further validate the ANOVA findings, we performed Kruskal–Wallis tests as non-parametric sensitivity analysis. p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Sample Characteristics and Clinical Scales Before Treatments

A total of 76 PSD patients were accessed for eligibility and forty-four of them were enrolled and randomly assigned to the four groups. Participants in the a-rTMS + a-rPMS (n = 11), a-rTMS + s-rPMS (n = 10), s-rTMS + a-rPMS (n = 11) and s-rTMS + s-PMS (n = 11) completed the entire intervention. One participant in a-rTMS + s-rPMS group reported unbearable pain in the right frontal neck area and discontinued after 6 sessions of treatments and was excluded from analysis (Fig. 1). The demographic and clinical variables did not differ significantly among the four groups at T0 (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Clinical Outcome Changes Within and Across Groups

The GEE analysis revealed a significant group-by-time interaction effect on FOIS (Wald χ2 = 39.816, df = 6, p < 0.001) and PAS scores (Wald χ2 = 45.433, df = 3, p < 0.001), indicating differential changes over time among the four groups. Simple main effects analysis with Bonferroni correction showed that at baseline (T0), there were no group differences for FOIS (Wald χ2 = 0.768, df = 3, p = 0.857) and PAS scores (Wald χ2 = 0.492, df = 3, p = 0.921), confirming successful randomization. At post-intervention (T1), significant group differences emerged for FOIS (Wald χ2 = 21.174, df = 3, p < 0.001) and PAS scores (Wald χ2 = 54.44, df = 3, p < 0.001). At follow-up (T2), significant group differences emerged for FOIS (Wald χ2 = 74.146, df = 3, p < 0.001).

Pairwise comparisons revealed that a-rTMS + a-rPMS group had significantly higher FOIS scores than s-rTMS + s-rPMS at T1 (mean difference = 1.09, 95% CI 0.39 to 1.79, adjusted p < 0.001) and at T2 (mean difference = 1.45, 95% CI 1.0 to 1.91, adjusted p < 0.001). Within-group comparisons showed that all four groups experienced a significant increase of FOIS from T0 to T1 (a-rTMS + a-rPMS group: mean difference = −1.73, 95% CI [−2.35, −1.1], a-rTMS + s-rPMS group: MD = −1.55, 95% CI [−2.02, −1.07]; s-rTMS + a-rPMS group: MD = −1.27, 95% CI [−1.72, −0.83]; s-rTMS + s-rPMS group: MD = 0.64, 95% CI [−1.1, −017]; all p < 0.001, adjusted with Bonferroni correction) and from T0 to T2 (a-rTMS + a-rPMS group: MD = −3.09, 95% CI [−3.57, 2.61]; a-rTMS + s-rPMS group: MD = −2.55, 95% CI [−3.11, −1.98]; s-rTMS + a-rPMS group: MD = −2.18, 95% CI [−2.6, −1.77]; s-rTMS + s-rPMS group:MD = −1.64, 95% CI [−1.98, −1.29], all p < 0.001, adjusted with Bonferroni correction) (Fig. 6A).

Analysis of FOIS and PAS scores across different time points. (A) all the four groups demonstrate improvements in the FOIS over time at T1 and T2, compared with T0. (B) all the four groups showed decreases in the PAS over time at T1, compared with T0

Pairwise comparisons at T1 revealed that a-rTMS + a-rPMS group had significantly lower PAS scores than s-rTMS + a-rPMS (MD = –0.91, 95% CI –1.44 to –0.38, adjusted p < 0.001) and s-rTMS + s-rPMS group (MD = –1.55, 95% CI –2.11 to –0.99, adjusted p < 0.001). Within-group comparisons showed that all four groups experienced a significant decline of PAS from T0 to T1 (a-rTMS + a-rPMS group:MD = 2.09, 95% CI [1.79, 2.39]; a-rTMS + s-rPMS group: MD = 1.55, 95% CI [1.16, 1.93]; s-rTMS + a-rPMS group: MD = 1.27, 95% CI [0.83, 1.72]; s-rTMS + s-rPMS group: MD = 0.73, 95% CI [0.46, 0.99], all p < 0.001, adjusted with Bonferroni correction) (Fig. 6B).

Analysis of VFSS-Derived Swallowing Parameters

The a-rTMS + a-rPMS group demonstrated statistically significant increases in both LCD (MD = –3.598, 95% CI −4.177 to –3.019, adjusted p < 0.001) and SHE amplitude (MD = –3.708, 95% CI −4.213 to –3.18, adjusted p < 0.001) at T1 compared to T0. While the a-rTMS + s-rPMS group revealed only significant change in the SHE (MD = –3.567, 95% CI −4.165 to –2.909, adjusted p < 0.001) and the s-rTMS + a-rPMS group showed significant change in the LCD (MD = –3.598, 95% CI −4.177 to –3.019, adjusted p < 0.001). In the s-rTMS + s-rPMS group, who received only conventional swallowing training, there is only trend to improve in the VFSS derived parameters without significances (Fig. 7). Kruskal‑Wallis tests on SHE confirmed the same pattern of significant differences from ANOVA tests (Supplementary Table S3 and S4).

Cortical Activity Analysis

Before treatment, four groups demonstrated no significant differences in the activation of the region of interests during the repetitive swallowing task (Fig. 8A-D). However, after treatments, the four groups demonstrated stronger neural activation in most of the ROIs in the left hemisphere, except for the s-rTMS + s-rPMS group, which demonstrated decreased activation in all the left ROIs (Fig. 8). Especially, in the combined stimulation group, the ipsilesional primary somatosensory cortex (LS1) shifted from task-related deactivation at baseline (β = –0.0544) to robust activation post-intervention (β = 0.0112) (Table 2).

Complications and Adverse Effects

Overall, all patients tolerated the active treatments, and only one of them reported intolerant pain in the right frontal neck area and dropped out the experiment. Thirteen patients reported temporary muscle twitching in the forehead area that was relieved immediately after the session (a-rTMS + a-rPMS, n = 6; a-rTMS + s-rPMS, n = 2; s-rTMS + a-rPMS, n = 5; s-rTMS + s-rPMS, n = 0). No other common adverse effects, such as headache, tinnitus, dizziness, or seizures were reported.

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical efficacy and cortical dynamic responses of 10 Hz rTMS applied to pharyngeal M1 in the affected hemisphere combined with 10 Hz rPMS targeting the suprahyoid region in patients with PSD. A total of 44 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to four groups: a-rTMS + a-rPMS, a-rTMS + s-rPMS, s-rTMS + a-rPMS and s-rTMS + s-rPMS. All four groups showed improvements in FOIS scores and reductions in PAS scores. However, the combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation group demonstrated biggest improvements. Additionally, the combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation significant improved magnitude of SHE and LCD. FC between prefrontal and sensorimotor areas significantly increased in the combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation group, and furthermore the FC change of LM1-LS1 was correlated with ΔLCD and ΔSHE. Finally, no serious adverse effect, except for one case reporting intolerable neck pain, was reported, indicating good safety and efficacy of magnetic stimulation when applied with strict adherence to therapeutic indications.

Clinical Effects of Different Magnetic Stimulation Protocols

In clinical practice, magnetic stimulation is often used to address motor dysfunction in the paralyzed limbs of stroke patients, with combined stimulation showing positive effects [29, 30]. However, systematic comparisons of different magnetic stimulation protocols for swallowing dysfunction in PSD patients remain scarce. A randomized controlled trial by Wang et al. compared the efficacy of combined stimulation, central stimulation, peripheral stimulation, and conventional therapy on dysphagia recovery [13]. They found that the combined stimulation group showed the most significant changes in FOIS and PAS, which is consistent with our study, followed by the rPMS group, then the rTMS group, and finally the conventional therapy group. However, the rTMS group showed greater improvement than the rPMS group in our study. This discrepancy may be related to differences in rTMS parameters between the two studies. In our study, rTMS was applied to pharyngeal M1 of lesional hemisphere, whereas in Wang et al.'s study, the rTMS was the pharyngeal M1 of either the affected or unaffected hemisphere depending on the MEP. Previous research suggests that high-frequency rTMS applied to the unaffected M1 promotes neural reorganization in the affected hemisphere, while stimulation of the affected M1 modulates excitability in both hemispheres. Therefore, high-frequency rTMS to the affected M1 may promote swallowing recovery through stronger neural reorganization effects. Furthermore, our fNIRS results showed changes in bilateral motor cortex activation post-intervention in both the combined and rTMS groups, further supporting our interpretation of the clinical outcomes. Our study not only compared the clinical efficacy of different magnetic stimulation protocols but also explored the underlying mechanisms in depth. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to systematically investigate the neural plasticity mechanisms underlying the effects of combined central and peripheral magnetic stimulation on swallowing function in patients with subacute and chronic supratentorial stroke-induced PSD.

The Impact of Magnetic Stimulation on Swallowing Physiology

VFSS is gold standard for dysphagia and analytical parameters derived from VFSS are sensitive indicators that reflect the physiology of swallow [31]. We found that hyoid elevated higher after rTMS protocol. These results were supported by the findings reported by Khedr et al.’s study [32]. They found that 10 sessions of high-frequency rTMS over bilateral motor cortex significantly reduced the time to maximal hyoid elevation during solid swallows in Parkinson’s disease patients with dysphagia, suggesting that rTMS can accelerate hyoid movement and improve deglutition function. The enhanced movement of hyoid bone could facilitate laryngeal elevation and make UES more prepared for the coming bolus. rTMS probably focuses on the "top-down" central drive to the peripheral suprahyoid muscles, thereby improving hyoid elevation and reducing pharyngeal residue [33].Alternatively, the prolongation of LCD observed with rPMS may reflect a central compensatory response, as peripheral stimulation is known to enhance cortical excitability and facilitate sensorimotor integration of swallowing [22, 34]. However, given the modest sample size per group (10–11) and the presence of variance heterogeneity for SHE and LCD, these differential findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Replication in larger cohorts is needed to confirm these distinct effects.

Synergistic Effects of Combined Magnetic Stimulation and the "Central-Peripheral" Closed-Loop Hypothesis

Previous studies have focused mainly on the clinical effects of single or combined magnetic stimulation on swallowing dysfunction. The application of rTMS to M1 and/or rPMS to superior hyoid region have been shown to improve PSD, as confirmed by multiple randomized controlled trials and meta reviews [14, 35, 36]. Animal studies have demonstrated that 10 Hz rTMS increases c-Fos protein expression in the primary motor and sensory cortices of rats [37]. Our study suggested that 10 Hz rTMS increased the activity of sensorimotor cortices and further confirming that the rTMS-induced improvement in swallowing function in PSD is partially mediated by M1 and S1. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that the activation of nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of PSD mice is regulated by M1 and that enhancing the M1–NTS neural circuits improves swallowing function in PSD mice [38, 39]. Our results showed that SHE improved in the rTMS and combined magnetic group. We speculate that the 10 Hz rTMS effect is mediated through direct or indirect top-down M1–NTS neural circuits.

In addition, previous researches have demonstrated that 10 Hz electrical stimulation of the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) induced c-Fos expression in multiple NTS subnuclei and evokes complete primary peristalsis [40]. Moreover, a direct excitatory neural circuit from the NTS to the ventral posteromedial thalamic nucleus (VPM) and then to S1 mediated effect of electroacupuncture of CV-23 on the improvement of swallowing function in PSD mice [41]. We applied 10 Hz rPMS to target the CV-23 and surrounding superior hyoid area and found the LCD improved in the rPMS and combined magnetic group. We speculate that the 10 Hz rPMS effect is mediated through direct or indirect bottom-up NTS-VPM-S1 neural pathway. Furthermore, the combined central and peripheral magnetic stimulation not only significantly increased SHE and LCD, but also the connectivity of M1 and S1. The current results from our and previous studies provide overall supports to the "Central-Peripheral" closed-loop hypothesis. Future studies with fMRI to detect the activity of these pathways may needed to provide direct experimental evidence to support the hypothesis [5, 42].

Neural Plasticity Induced by Combined Stimulation: Associations Between fNIRS FC Changes and Swallowing Kinematics

This study found that the enhancement of LM1-LS1 functional connectivity (FC) was significantly correlated with improvements in both laryngeal closure duration (LCD) and superior hyoid excursion (SHE) in the combined stimulation group. These findings are supported by previous studies, which reported that sensorimotor integration is essential for the precise timing and coordination of swallowing [43, 44]. For example, Wei et al. investigated the relationship between corticobulbar excitability and swallowing kinematics using TMS-induced MEPs and found that the increase in submental MEP amplitude evoked by ipsilateral cortical stimulation was positively correlated with the improvement in hyoid displacement after modified balloon dilatation therapy [45]. This finding suggested that enhanced excitability of the corticobulbar projection is associated with better hyoid elevation, supporting the clinical relevance of motor cortex modulation in swallowing rehabilitation.

During swallowing, a complex motor act highly dependent on sensory feedback regulation, S1 receives sensory information from the oropharynx, while M1 initiates and coordinates the sequential contraction of swallowing muscles [43, 44]. MRI studies have shown significant S1 activation during swallowing [43, 46], which is notably reduced in PSD patients [47]. Animal research by Dai et al. further demonstrated that sensory information generated by peripheral stimulation is transmitted via the solitary nucleus and ventral posteromedial thalamic nucleus to S1 [41]. S1 then relays sensory feedback to the supplementary motor area (SMA), coordinating the swallowing motor planning process [48]. The observed associations between FC enhancement and kinematic improvements were most likely due to the enhanced sensorimotor integration efficiency elicited by combined central-peripheral stimulation, although causality cannot be directly inferred from these correlational data. Future studies employing causal connectivity methods—such as dynamic causal modeling of fMRI data or combined TMS-EEG with perturbation-based approaches—are needed to test whether the observed FC changes directly mediate the behavioral improvements.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and patients exhibited heterogeneity in lesion location and size. Although baseline clinical scores were comparable, the known influence of stroke lesion location (e.g., insula, primary sensorimotor cortex) on dysphagia patterns and recovery potential may have confounded the analysis of brain activation patterns [2, 49]. Future studies with larger, more homogeneous lesion groups are needed to validate these findings. Second, this study focused on subacute to chronic patients, a period when neural plasticity may have plateaued [50]. Applying the combined protocol during the early "golden window" of stroke rehabilitation might yield better outcomes [51], a hypothesis requiring future investigation. Third, this study only explored the "central-first, peripheral-second" sequence and did not compare the effects of different application orders or simultaneous stimulation. Although the standardized scalp measurement for rTMS targeting has been used in our study and was applied consistently across groups, it does not account for individual neuroanatomical variability or post-stroke reorganization. Future studies should consider MRI-guided neuronavigation or TMS-MEP mapping to enhance targeting precision. Finally, we flipped the fNIRS channels from right-sided stroke patients to the left side to enable pooled group analysis, however, it may mask genuine hemispheric differences in swallowing control. Swallowing exhibits some degree of hemispheric lateralization, with inconsistent laterality across individuals and shifts between swallowing preparation and execution [52], future larger-scale studies with balanced lesion sides should analyze left- and right-sided patients separately to fully characterize side-dependent mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, combined central-peripheral magnetic stimulation was associated with improved swallowing function in patients with subacute and chronic PSD. This modulatory effect might be related to the strengthening of sensorimotor connectivity.
