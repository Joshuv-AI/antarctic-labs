Biomass to bio-CNG from sorghum varieties for biogas production, energy recovery, and emission mitigation
Authors: Hemant Gurawalia, Naveen Yadav, Muskaan Jain, Radhika Gandu, Rivka Cahan, Bharath Gandu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) has emerged as a promising lignocellulosic substrate for anaerobic digestion; however, its biogas production potential and energy recovery efficiency are strongly influenced by varietal characteristics and operating temperature. In this study, eleven sorghum varieties were evaluated for their biogas production potential, biodigestibility, and theoretical energy recovery efficiency under mesophilic (37 ℃) and thermophilic (55 ℃) conditions. The experiments were conducted in 250 mL batch-scale anaerobic digestors operated for 60 days. A clear difference in biogas yield was observed, with consistently higher yields in thermophilic reactors. Among the evaluated varieties, Combined Sorghum Variety-48 (CSV-48), Combined Sorghum Variety-49 Sweet sorghum (CSV-49SS), yellow sorghum, and white sorghum showed superior biogas productivity and higher total solids, volatile solids, and chemical oxygen demand removal efficiencies. Assuming 50% methane content, Bio-Compressed Natural Gas (Bio-CNG) potential ranged from 36.06 to 83.27 Nm3 t− 1 FM under thermophilic conditions. Corresponding electricity and heat generation ranged from 146.3 to 337.9 kWh t− 1 FM. Sorghum-derived Bio-CNG could replace approximately 33.9–78.3 L of diesel or 37.9–87.4 L of petrol per tonne of fresh biomass, reducing CO2 emissions by 90.9–209.8 kg CO2-eq. t− 1 FM on diesel basis and 87.5–201.1 kg CO2-eq. t− 1 FM on a petrol basis. These findings highlight that identifying high-performing sorghum varieties with favourable lignocellulosic composition and a balanced C/N ratio can improve process stability, substrate biodegradability, and energy recovery efficiency. Using the identified high-yielding varieties for industrial purposes instead of random varieties can reduce operational variability in sorghum-based biogas systems.

Introduction

Increasing global energy demand and raising environmental pollution have intensified the search for sustainable and renewable energy solutions1. Within this context, biogas production via anaerobic digestion (AD) of organic biomass has emerged as a promising clean energy source2. Biogas consists mainly of methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) and can be used directly for cooking and electricity generation, or after purification, as compressed biogas (CBG) or Bio-Compressed natural gas (CNG) for vehicular fuel3. In India, biogas production has traditionally focused on small-scale “gobar gas” plants that leverage cattle dung in rural areas. Also, national initiatives like Deenbandhu models for household digesters and large-scale programs such as SATAT (Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation) and Galvanizing Organic Bio-Agro Resources Dhan (GOBARdhan) have significantly increased the investment and installation of commercial CBG systems, aligning with national targets to reduce dependence on liquified petroleum gas (LPG) and reduce greenhouse emissions4,5.

However, in the Indian context, a critical challenge is to identify a reliable, high-yielding biomass feedstock for biogas production. Agriculture accounts for more than 60% of India’s land use, generating approximately 620 million tonnes (MT) of crop residues annually, including straw, husk, stalks, and bagasse from wheat, rice, sorghum, sugarcane, etc.6. Efficient utilization of these residues not only adds economic value to farming systems, but also addresses issues of rising burning and environmental pollution7,8. Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) has a rich history in Indian agriculture and can stand out as a potential bio-energy crop. This crop is usually cultivated for forage, grain and for some industrial applications, especially in semi-arid areas. Sorghum is known for its ability to tolerate drought, its high biomass, and its impressive adaptability across different agro-climatic zones9. Presently, farmers are also adopting dual-purpose sorghum varieties to support food security and renewable energy production for sustainable development10.

Sorghum, as a C4 plant, reflects its high photosynthetic efficiency and drought tolerance, making it suitable for large-scale biomass production in India11. However, its effectiveness for biogas production depends on the biochemical and structural characteristics, such as lignin content, soluble carbohydrates, and fibre composition, which vary among the sorghum varieties. A high lignin content hinders microbial access to cellulose and hemicellulose, thereby reducing substrate biodegradability and methane yield12.

Globally conducted studies have reported significant differences in biomethane potential (BMP) among different sorghum varieties. According to the previous literature reviews, BMP values range from ~ 175 to 338 N mL CH4/g of Volatile solids added, depending on the sorghum variety and cultivation conditions provided13–16. Certain varieties, such as Rona1 can yield up to 338 m3/t methane under optimal conditions, highlighting the importance of suitable variety selection for maximizing biogas production16. Wannsek et al., 201715 reported substantial variation in methane productivity among five sorghum varieties, where the variety SOR 4 achieved methane yields up to 6500 m3 ha-1, while fast-ripening cultivars such as SOR 2 were considered more suitable for biogas systems under temperate climate conditions. Similarly, Windpassinger et al., 201517 demonstrated that early-maturing silage type sorghum hybrids possessing higher panicle and grain proportions achieved enhanced methane yield compared with conventional biomass type sorghum varieties, emphasizing the role of varietal architecture and maturity in determining energy density for biogas generation. Glab et al., 201918 further observed considerable differences in methane and ethanol recovery between sweet sorghum hybrids, where conversion of sorghum biomass generated 76.6–179.5 GJ ha-1 through methane production depending on hybrid type and biomass management practices, with the hybrid Rona 1 exhibiting superior methane energy efficiency under digestate fertilization. Likewise, Theuretzbacher et al., 201319 demonstrated that different Sorghum bicolor varieties varied significantly in combined ethanol and biogas production potential, where sweet sorghum variety SG1 and grain sorghum variety Chopper achieved total energy outputs of approximately 150 and 156 GJ ha-1, respectively, which were comparable or superior to maize under similar climatic conditions. The study further revealed that methane and ethanol contributions differed considerably among varieties: SG1 derived nearly 70% of its energy from methane, while the Chopper variety generated approximately 54% of its total energy from ethanol, highlighting the influence of substrate composition and varietal characteristics on integrated bioenergy recovery systems. In addition, life-cycle assessment studies demonstrated that sorghum-based biofuel systems can substantially reduce greenhouse gas emissions compared to fossil fuels20. Although at the global level sorghum has been widely studied as bioenergy crop, but in India majority of research on sorghum as a substrate for anaerobic digestion (AD) have largely emphasized the role of pretreatment strategies in enhancing biodegradability and biogas production. Also, some studies focus on using sorghum stover as animal fodder and assess its digestibility using in vitro rumen fermentation technology, whereas systematic investigations into the influence of varietal differences remain limited. For example, studies on brown midrib (bmr) sorghum have shown improved degradability, higher volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentrations, and enhanced biogas production due to its lower lignin content and more accessible hemicellulose compared to sweet and conventional sorghum varieties21. Such findings provide useful insights, and similar systematic evaluations under AD conditions are still lacking for Indian sorghum varieties. Anaerobic digestion systems are predominantly operated under mesophilic (35–39 ℃) or thermophilic (50–55 ℃) conditions because the temperature is one of the most critical operational parameters governing microbial activity, substrate degradation kinetics, methane generation, and overall process stability. Mesophilic digestion at approximately 37 ℃ is widely adopted due to its stable microbial community structure, lower heating requirements, and greater resistance to inhibitory compounds, thereby ensuring reliable, long-term reactor performance. Whereas, thermophilic digestion at around 55 ℃ promotes faster hydrolysis and organic matter degradation, enhanced pathogen destruction, shorter hydraulic retention time, and increased methane productivity due to accelerated microbial metabolism. Previous investigations have reported that thermophilic anaerobic digestion generally exhibits higher bioconversion efficiency and methane yield than mesophilic systems, although it may also show comparatively lower microbial diversity and greater sensitivity to operational disturbances22,23. Furthermore, studies evaluating temperature transitions in anaerobic digestion demonstrated that both mesophilic and thermophilic conditions represent the standard operational ranges used for optimizing methane production, process efficiency, and microbial adaptation24–26. Therefore, in the present study, reactors were operated under both mesophilic (37 ℃) and thermophilic (55 ℃) conditions to systematically evaluate temperature-dependent differences in biodegradability, biogas production potential, energy recovery efficiency, and process stability among different sorghum varieties.

In the present study a systematic approach was adopted to compare the biogas production potential of multiple sorghum varieties commonly cultivated in India. All the tested sorghum varieties were evaluated under optimal, identical, and controlled laboratory conditions to identify the variety with the highest biogas production potential. Compositional analysis, such as lignocellulosic structure and C/N ratio, and biodigestibility indicators, such as total solids (TS), volatile solids (VS), chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal efficiency, and temperature-dependent performance (mesophilic & thermophilic), are the main factors used to identify high-performing sorghum varieties. The findings are also expected to contribute to optimising substrate selection for bioenergy applications and to support the development of a biogas program in India, aligning with national initiatives such as the SATAT (Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation) and GOBARdhan schemes, as well as broader renewable energy targets.

Methodology

Substrate

11 sorghum varieties including Traditional/Local varieties - White Sorghum (WS), Yellow Sorghum (YS), Raichur Sorghum (RS), and Maldandi Sorghum (MS), Sorghum parental variety (SPV-2884) and Combined sorghum varieties (CSV-47 F, CSV-48, CSV-49SS, CSV-52SS, CSV-58SS, CSV-59SS) were collected from across Indian agricultural fields for the assessment of comparative biogas production. The study utilized residues obtained from different sorghum crop varieties rather than whole sorghum crops. All collected sorghum crop residues were uniformly ground using a 2000 W mixer grinder (Bullet Master Jaguar 2000) and stored in the refrigerator at 4 ℃ until the experiments began.

Inoculum

Digestate from the previous batch of thermophilic and mesophilic operational reactors was collected, mixed, and sieved through a 0.85 mm sieve to separate the solid digestate from the liquid. The separated slurry was stored in the refrigerator at 4 ℃ for 1 month until the experiment began. Fresh cattle dung was collected from Bilaspur Kalan, Haryana, India. Before starting the experiment, the inoculum was prepared by mixing cattle dung with the liquid slurry collected from previous reactors, then diluting with water to obtain a constant TS of 1.06%. The VS obtained was 41.10% of TS, and the pH was 7.71.

Laboratory scale reactor setup for biogas production

Two water bath assemblies were created using acrylic sheet containers and connected to separate heating elements to maintain two different temperature conditions: mesophilic (37 °C) and thermophilic (55 °C). 250 ml Borosilicate glass bottles were used as anaerobic digestors. Caps of all the AD reactors were fitted with silicon tubes, with the other end connecting to a measuring cylinder with a water displacement column. Collected biogas from the reactor headspace flows through these silicon tubes, displacing an equal volume of water from the measuring cylinders into the biogas produced in the ADs. This water displacement method can quantify the amount of biogas produced.

Experimental design and reactor operation

The batch experiments were performed in triplicate using 250 mL AD with a working volume of 225 mL as anaerobic reactors under both mesophilic and thermophilic conditions, as shown in Fig. 1. To each reactor, 75 mL of inoculum and 150 mL of substrate corresponding to 5% TS were added, resulting in an inoculum-to-substrate (TS) ratio (ISR) of 1:2. The working volume of each reactor was adjusted to the desired level by adding distilled water. After feeding the reactor with substrate and maintaining the optimum volume, the reactors was purged with 99% pure nitrogen (N2) for 2 min and closed tightly to create an anaerobic condition. Subsequently, the reactors were placed in the thermostat-controlled water bath.

Analytical techniques

Determination of total solid (TS), volatile solid (VS) and fixed solid (FS)

TS represent the amount of solids present in the substrate after the evaporation of moisture. TS was measured by oven drying the sample at 105 ℃ until a constant weight was achieved (Eq. 1). Whereas VS represent the biodegradable organic matter present in the substrate. VS was determined by combusting the dried material in a muffle furnace at 550 ℃ until a constant weight was achieved (Eq. 2). FS represents the non-combustible mineral fraction and is determined as the residue remaining after ignition of the dried sample at 550 ℃ (Eq. 3), according to the APHA Standard Method27. The FS values reported represent the ash fraction of the substrates.

Determination of lignin, cellulose and holocellulose

Lignin is the most recalcitrant heteropolymer in the plant cell wall, covering cellulose and hemicellulose and thereby hindering fermentation28. Quantification of lignin, Cellulose, and holocellulose was done by the acid-base reflux method as described by Wise et al., 194629.

Hemicellulose% = Holocellulose% - Cellulose%.

Physio-chemical analysis

Physicochemical parameters, including oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), salinity, conductivity, resistivity, and total dissolved solids (TDS), were analysed using a multiparameter analyser (Hanna HI 98194) at regular intervals from the reactor contents to assess process performance and operational stability. Volatile fatty acids (VFAs) were analysed by the titration method30, and alkalinity was determined according to the APHA Standard Method31. pH was maintained with 1 N NaOH and 1 N HCl solutions and analysed with a pH meter (Hanna HI2210). COD was determined by the dichromate method (APHA 2012) using a COD digester and analysed by a Multiparameter photometer (Hanna Multiparameter Photometer, HI83399). The Carbon, nitrogen, hydrogen and sulphur content of the substrates were analysed with a CHNS analyser (Varico Micro Cube Elementar, Germany).

Result and discussion

Physio-chemical characteristics of substrate and inoculum

Physicochemical characteristics of 11 sorghum varieties used in the present study were determined using the APHA standard method and summarised in Table 1. The TS content of the used sorghum varieties ranges from 15.56% in CSV-59SS to 59.40% in CSV-48, indicating substantial variability in the dry matter available for anaerobic digestion. High TS values, such as those observed in CSV-48 and CSV-47 F (> 55%), suggest a greater proportion of structural and fibrous material, while lower TS content in CSV-59SS and MS may reflect higher moisture levels. The VS content among the sorghum samples ranges from 86.93% in white sorghum to 96.93% in CSV-58SS. High VS content in the substrates indicates a greater amount of biodegradable organic matter. The VS/TS ratio, which reflects the organic fraction relative to the total solids, varied from 0.87 in WS to 0.97 in CSV-58SS. A higher VS/TS ratio indicates a larger fraction of biodegradable organic matter compared to inert material32. Considerable variation was observed in the TS and VS, consistent with earlier reports highlighting heterogeneity among sorghum varieties13. In addition to the bulk characteristics, the lignocellulosic composition of the sorghum varieties exhibited notable variation. Lignin content ranged from 22.57% in CSV-47 F to 27.82% in RS, representing differences in recalcitrant biomass fraction. Higher lignin content observed in RS and MS is associated with reduced biodegradability and slower hydrolysis during anaerobic digestion, whereas lower lignin content was observed in CSV-47 F, CSV-48 and WS, suggesting improved digestibility. Cellulose content varied between 38.38% in white sorghum to 45.63% in CSV-48, while hemicellulose ranged from 27.42% in CSV-52SS to 30.27% in RS. Varieties with higher cellulose and hemicellulose fractions and lower lignin content are more favourable substrates for anaerobic digestion12. The initial pH of all reactors after addition of substrate and inoculum ranged from 6.81 to 7.59, which lies near the optimal pH range (6.4–7.4); therefore, no initial pH adjustment was performed at the start of the reactors33.

C/N ratio

The carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio is a key parameter that influences the efficiency of anaerobic digestion (AD) by determining the nutritional balance for microbial communities, particularly methanogens. D.J. Hills,197934 reported that the ideal C/N ratio should be between 20 and 30, as it enhances microbial activity by providing sufficient carbon and nitrogen for biomass synthesis and avoiding ammonia inhibition or nutrient limitations. In our present study, the carbon content of the tested sorghum varieties ranged from 38.7% to 45.5% (Table 2). The highest carbon content was found in CSV-58SS (45.5%), while the lowest was found in MS (38.7%). Whereas the nitrogen content ranged from 0.6% to 1.2%. The highest nitrogen content was found in WS and RS (1.2%), while the lowest was found in CSV-52SS (0.6%). This resulted in the highest C/N ratio in CSV-52SS (74.91) and the lowest in WS (33.71), as shown in Table 2. These observations align with earlier studies that emphasised the influence of C/N ratio on methane yield and digestion kinetics in lignocellulosic biomass13. The addition of fresh cattle dung as inoculum (ISR 1:2) with lignocellulosic substrates helps maintain the C/N ratio and the alkalinity of the reactors, thereby enhancing biogas yield32,35. Substrates with higher C/N ratios provide more carbon for biogas production but create a nitrogen deficit, leading to slower degradation and lower pH in bioreactors36. At the same time, substrates with lower C/N ratios can lead to ammonia accumulation and raise the pH above 8.5, which inhibits the growth of methanogens37.

Biogas production potential

Figure 2 represents the cumulative biogas production from different sorghum varieties. The biogas production potential of eleven Sorghum bicolor varieties was evaluated under mesophilic (37 °C) and thermophilic (55 °C) anaerobic digestion conditions for 60 days to elucidate the influence of substrate characteristics and operational temperature on biogas productivity14,38. A significant difference in cumulative biogas yield was observed among the tested sorghum varieties, indicating the strong dependence of anaerobic digestion on feedstock composition and the digestion conditions used. Under mesophilic conditions, the cumulative biogas yield varied from 70.21 to 121.44 m3 t− 1 FM. Highest biogas yield was obtained from CSV-48 (125.44 m3 t− 1 FM), followed by YS (121.85 m3 t− 1 FM) and WS (119.63 m3 t− 1 FM). Moderate biogas production was observed in CSV-49SS and CSV-47 F. The lowest biogas production was observed in CSV-52SS and MS, indicating limited biodegradability in mesophilic conditions.

On the other hand, in thermophilic digestion, a significant enhancement in biogas production was observed among all sorghum varieties, and the cumulative biogas yield varied from 72.13 to 166.54 m3 t− 1 FM. Here, the highest biogas producer was CSV-48 (166.54 m3 t− 1 FM), followed by CSV-49SS (141.62 m3 t− 1 FM), YS (139.90 m3 t− 1 FM), and WS (132.79 m3 t− 1 FM). This indicates that varieties excel in biogas production potential at higher temperatures. Approximately 15–35% higher biogas yield was obtained during thermophilic digestion compared to the mesophilic one, due to accelerated hydrolysis, increased microbial activity and better breakdown of complex organic material at thermophilic temperature39. The difference in biogas productivity among sorghum varieties is associated with their biochemical composition, such as volatile solids content, lignocellulosic structure, and C/N ratio. These factors affect microbial accessibility and the kinetics of methane formation. Varieties such as CSV-48, CSV-49SS, YS, and WS, characterized by favourable organic matter composition and moderate C/N ratio, consistently exhibited higher biogas yields under both digestion regimes. In the present study, a sorghum variety with a moderately balanced C/N ratio (~ 30–45), coupled with optimized digestion temperature, and higher organic matter reduction capability, is found critical for maximizing biogas production potential and improving the feasibility of sorghum-based anaerobic digestion systems14–16,38,40.

Biodigestibility and organic matter reduction

Removal efficiencies of (a). total solids (TS), (b). volatile solids (VS), and (c). chemical oxygen demand (COD) during anaerobic digestion of different Sorghum bicolor varieties under mesophilic (37 ℃) and thermophilic (55 ℃) conditions. Error bars represent standard deviation (n = 3).

The biodigestibility of different sorghum varieties was evaluated based on TS reduction, VS reduction, and COD removal efficiency, as shown in Fig. 3. Across all substrates, organic matter degradation was observed, with mesophilic reactors showing TS reductions ranging from 75.41 to 90.36%. In contrast, in thermophilic conditions, TS reductions ranged from 81.64 to 94.33%, confirming the positive impact of elevated temperature on hydrolytic efficiency41. A similar trend was observed for VS reduction, which ranged from 78.22 to 91.20% in mesophilic reactors and further improved to 83.97–94.83% during thermophilic digestion, indicating that thermophilic conditions are more optimal for the bacterial culture during anaerobic digestion of biodegradable organic matter39. Among the sorghum varieties, CSV-48, YS, WS, and CSV-49SS consistently exhibit higher TS and VS removal efficiency, which corresponds to their superior biogas productivity, especially under thermophilic conditions. COD removal efficiency also shows a somewhat similar trend, with 38.95–79.65% under mesophilic conditions and 40.67–83.27% under thermophilic conditions, with CSV-48 and YS demonstrating the highest COD removal and biogas yield. Del Borghi et al.,199942 also reported a similar trend of increased COD removal efficiency during anaerobic digestion of municipal solid waste and sewage sludge. Whereas CSV-59SS and MS showed comparatively lower COD removal and biogas production despite moderate reductions in TS and VS, this may be due to their high C/N ratios, which led methanogens to satisfy their nitrogen demand early, reducing carbon conversion efficiency and lowering biogas production43,44. The consistently higher TS, VS, and COD removal under thermophilic conditions led to 5–9% greater organic matter degradation and enhanced biogas productivity compared to mesophilic digestion. These findings are in agreement with previous studies reporting that thermophilic digestion accelerates hydrolysis and improves the degradation of lignocellulosic biomass, leading to higher methane yields38,45.

Process stability based on ORP, VFAs and TDS profile

Process stability during anaerobic digestion was evaluated by integrating ORP, VFA, and TDS profiles under mesophilic and thermophilic conditions, as represented in Fig. 4. These parameters, overall, reflect redox status, the metabolic balance between acidogenesis and methanogenesis, and the extent of solubilization of complex organic matter46.

Startup phase (1st day): establishment of anaerobic conditions

During the initial stage of digestion, ORP values of all sorghum varieties ranged between − 78.7 mV in CSV-47 F to −144.9 mV in CSV-48. The ORP value during the startup phase was within the optimal range (−100 mV to −300 mV) for most varieties, except CSV-47 F and CSV-58SS, which showed ORP values above the optimal range, indicating a transition from facultative to obligate anaerobic metabolism47. At this stage, hydrolytic and fermentative bacteria begin to dominate, while methanogenic microorganisms start to adapt48. The initial VFA concentration ranged from 1066.53 mg/L in WS to 1920.39 mg/L in CSV-58SS, whereas the TDS ranged from 2118 mg/L (SPV2884) to 4784 mg/L (WS). Mildly reducing ORP values and the absence of excess VFA accumulation indicate a stable reactor startup without any sign of acidification or redox imbalance49.

Mid-digestion phase: peak solubilization and methanogenesis

By the 30th day, all the reactors exhibited a pronounced shift in ORP towards strongly reducing conditions, indicative of a methanogenic environment49. ORP values range from − 290.7 mV in CSV-49SS to −372.5 mV in RS under mesophilic and − 319.5 mV in MS to −368.9 mV in SPV-2884 under thermophilic conditions, which fall near the reported optimum value of −335.63 mV during the methanogenesis phase by Vongvichiankul et al., 201750. Whereas according to Duangmanee, 2009 and Hungate, 1969,51,52 ORP value lower than − 230 mV is optimum for methane-reducing microbes, and an ORP value above − 280 mV is inhibitory for sulphur-reducing microbes. The reduction in ORP value in the present study also coincided with the peak VFA accumulation, representing the peak phase of acidogenesis and methane formation during the middle of anaerobic digestion. Also, the VFA concentration ranged from 690 mg/L in SPV-2884 to 2775 mg/L in CSV-52SS. In thermophilic digestion, VFA accumulation was even higher than in mesophilic reactors, ranging from a minimum of 1070 mg/L to a maximum of 3009 mg/L in yellow sorghum. VFA accumulation in both thermophilic and mesophilic reactors was lower than the threshold level of inhibition (< 3000 mg/L)53. Similarly, TDS concentration also increased during this phase, from 3301 mg/L in CSV-47 F to 5120 mg/L in CSV-58SS in mesophilic conditions. In contrast, under thermophilic conditions, TDS values ranged from 3578 mg/L in MS to 6948 mg/L in CSV-48, indicating greater solubilization54. Highly negative ORP, elevated VFAs, and increased TDS confirm intensified microbial digestion and electron transfer reactions during the middle of anaerobic fermentation, a characteristic widely reported for the efficient acidogenic and methanogenic phases55.

Late digestion phase: late methanogenic phase

By the 60th day of digestion, all the reactors exhibited a clear transition towards stabilisation, as reflected by reduced conditions, VFAs depletion and stabilised dissolved solids49. ORP values during this phase ranged from − 144.2 mV in CSV-48 to −283.4 mV in MS in mesophilic conditions and − 46.1 mV in RS to −253.2 mV in CSV-58SS in thermophilic conditions. The stability in redox potential was also supported by a decline in VFA concentrations compared with the mid-digestion stage50. Under mesophilic digestion, total VFAs concentration ranged from a minimum value of 690.52 mg/L in SPV-2884 to a maximum value of 2279.55 mg/L in yellow sorghum. Whereas in thermophilic reactors, overall VFAs were higher than in mesophilic reactors, ranging from a minimum of 720.75 mg/L in CSV-52SS to a maximum of 2300.11 mg/L in CSV-58SS. Similarly, TDS values showed a slight decline, indicating the reduced solubilization rates and enhanced conversion of dissolved organic matter into biogas. During the late digestion phase, TDS values ranged from 2552 mg/L in CSV-47 F to 4602 mg/L in RS in mesophilic reactors, whereas in thermophilic reactors, TDS values ranged from 3090 mg/L in CSV-52SS to 5304 mg/L in CSV-49SS. High biogas-producing varieties, CSV-48, CSV-49SS, YS, and WS, exhibit lower residual VFA concentrations under thermophilic and mesophilic conditions, along with stable negative ORP values, indicating efficient syntrophic oxidation of fermentation intermediates and robust methane production56. On the other hand, lower biogas-producing varieties retained higher residual VFAs. It exhibited lower negative ORP values, suggesting incomplete VFA conversion and reduced methanogenic efficiency, a trend commonly reported for lignocellulosic feedstocks during late-stage digestion13,57,58.

Theoretical energy recovery and transport fuel substitution potential

Theoretical energy recovery potential of the biogas produced from the different sorghum varieties was estimated on the basis of Bio-CNG, electricity, and heat generation. Additionally, the potential for transport fuel substitution and the potential for CO2 emission reduction was also assessed through the established conversion frameworks reported in the literature13,32,59–61. Based on experimentally obtained biogas yields, the Bio-CNG potential was estimated by assuming a minimum methane content of 50%, a conservative value commonly reported for sorghum substrates during anaerobic digestion62. An energy content of 9.66 kWh Nm− 3 CH4 was applied, based on previously reported values for sweet sorghum-based biomethane systems32.

As represented in Table 3, Bio-CNG production in Mesophilic conditions ranged from 35.11 to 62.72 Nm3 t− 1 FM. In thermophilic conditions, a clear enhancement in Bio-CNG production was observed, with yields increasing to 36.06–83.27 Nm3 t− 1 FM. Also, the theoretical electricity generation potential ranged from 142.56 to 254.72 kWh t− 1 FM under mesophilic conditions and increased significantly to 146.33–337.85 kWh t− 1 FM under thermophilic conditions, assuming a combined heat and power (CHP) electrical efficiency of 42%33. A similar trend was also observed for heat generation. Under mesophilic conditions, the estimated values ranged from 142.56 to 254.72 kWh t-1 FM, whereas under thermophilic conditions, the values increased to 146.33–337.85 kWh t− 1 FM, assuming a similar CHP heating efficiency of 42% (Table 4). This improvement indicates enhanced methane conversion efficiency at elevated temperature. Among the evaluated sorghum varieties, CSV-48, YS, WS, and CSV-49SS consistently demonstrated the highest theoretical Bio-CNG, electricity, and heat generation potentials. These findings align with their superior biogas yields and higher organic matter degradation efficiencies observed during anaerobic digestion. Transport fuel substitution potential of sorghum-derived Bio-CNG was calculated by converting the biomethane yields into diesel and petrol equivalents using the standard conversion factors (1 Nm3 Bio-CNG ≈ 0.94 L diesel ≈ 1.05 L petrol). Under thermophilic conditions, the estimated Bio-CNG can replace approximately 33.93–78.27 L diesel t-1 FM or 37.86–87.44 L petrol t-1 FM, depending on the sorghum variety. This substitution of transport fuel can lead to a substantial reduction in greenhouse gas emissions. According to the standard emission factors of 2.68 kg CO2-eq. L− 1 diesel and 2.31 kg CO2 L− 1 petrol, the Bio-CNG yield can reduce maximum emissions by approximately 90.94–209.77 kg CO2 eq. t− 1 FM. Maximum fossil fuel displacement and CO2 mitigation potential can be demonstrated by varieties with the highest biogas productivity, such as CSV-48 and YS, under thermophilic conditions. Thermophilic anaerobic digestion offers a clear advantage in maximizing the energy output and environmental benefits.

Conclusion

This study comparatively evaluates the biogas production potential, biodigestibility, and energy recovery efficiency of different Sorghum bicolor varieties under mesophilic and thermophilic anaerobic digestion. The results clearly represent significant variability in the characteristics of sorghum varieties which leads to the variability in terms of biogas yield, organic matter degradation, and theoretical energy recovery. Among the evaluated sorghum varieties, CSV-48, CSV-49SS, YS, and WS demonstrated superior performance, with higher biogas yields due to greater removal efficiencies of TS, VS, COD, lower lignin content and a high amount of cellulose, consequently leading to enhanced energy recovery potential. Thermophilic digestion enhanced biogas productivity through key factors, including accelerated hydrolysis, improved microbial metabolic activity, enhanced degradation of lignocellulosic compounds, greater organic matter reduction efficiency, and improved COD removal efficiency. These factors collectively contributed to approximately 15–35% higher biogas yields and improved energy recovery efficiency under thermophilic conditions compared to mesophilic digestion. Considering the methane content of 50%, the energy assessment indicates that sorghum-derived biogas could theoretical yield 36.06–83.27 Nm3 t− 1 FM of Bio-CNG, corresponding to 146.3–337.9 kWh t− 1 FM of electricity, along with a comparable amount of thermal energy under thermophilic conditions. Also, the estimated transport fuel substitution potential ranged from 33.9 to 78.3 L diesel equivalents or 37.9 to 87.4 L petrol equivalents per tonne of fresh biomass. This could result in a reduction of up to ~ 210 kg CO2-eq. in CO2 emissions t− 1 FM. The study highlights the importance of selecting suitable high biogas-yielding sorghum varieties and appropriate operating temperatures for anaerobic digestion to maximize energy recovery, improve process efficiency, enhance environmental sustainability, and support Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The findings also suggest that industrial variability in sorghum-based biogas systems can be reduced by selecting specific high-performing sorghum varieties, thereby ensuring more consistent biogas yields and stable plant operation. In contrast, the use of random or unsuitable varieties may lead to variations in productivity and affect the long-term sustainability and performance of biogas plants.
