Subintimal bailout recanalization for acute stent-jailed side branch occlusion: a single-center retrospective case series with QFR assessment
Authors: Ruo-fei Jia, Ze-ning Jin, Jun-ping Tian, Shuai Meng, Xing-sheng Yang, Yong-zeng Chen, Long-fei Wang, Jing Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute side branch (SB) occlusion after main vessel (MV) stenting remains a challenging complication of bifurcation PCI, particularly when conventional true-lumen rewiring attempts fail. In such scenarios, operators face limited standardized bailout options, and guidance on decision-making is scarce.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed a highly selected single-center case series of patients with acute stent-jailed SB occlusion refractory to conventional rewiring who underwent chronic total occlusion (CTO)-derived subintimal bailout recanalization. A predefined technical framework was applied. Angiographic results were assessed using Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction(TIMI) flow, and angiography-derived quantitative flow ratio (QFR) was used as physiological confirmation of reperfusion.ResultsAmong 9,832 PCI procedures performed between 2019 and 2023, 15 consecutive patients met the inclusion criteria. Subintimal SB recanalization was achieved in all cases, resulting in restoration of TIMI 3 flow. Postprocedural SB QFR was successfully obtained in all patients, with a mean value of 0.91 ± 0.04; all exceeded the predefined ischemic threshold (QFR ≥ 0.80). No procedure-related perforation, tamponade, or device entrapment was observed. Limited follow-up angiography and QFR data, available in two patients, demonstrated stable or improved functional values.ConclusionsIn a highly selected case series managed at an experienced center, a structured subintimal bailout framework appears feasible for the treatment of acute stent-jailed SB occlusion when conventional rewiring fails. Integration of QFR provides objective physiological confirmation of SB reperfusion and may assist in procedural decision-making. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in larger, prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06115-x.Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Subintimal recanalization, Side branch occlusion, Quantitative flow ratio

Introduction

Side branch (SB) occlusion remains a clinically relevant complication during PCI for coronary bifurcation lesions, with reported incidences ranging from approximately 7% to 19% and higher when complex lesion morphologies are present [1, 2]. Importantly, SB loss is not merely an angiographic finding—it has been correlated with increased periprocedural myocardial injury and worse long-term clinical outcomes, including elevated one-year mortality following drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation [3].

Multiple angiographic and anatomical predictors for SB occlusion have been identified, including plaque burden at the SB ostium, ostial stenosis severity, bifurcation angle geometry, and carina shift [4, 5]. Although preventive techniques (e.g., jailed wire, jailed balloon) have been proposed, none eliminate the risk entirely, and they themselves can carry risks such as stent deformation or device entrapment [6–8].

When SB occlusion occurs and conventional rewiring through the stent struts fails, operators face limited, non-standardized bailout options. Morita et al. recently reported a small series applying Subintimal Tracking and Re-entry (STAR) for stent-jailed SB occlusions, demonstrating procedural feasibility but without functional (physiological) endpoints [9]. STAR, Limited Antegrade Subintimal Tracking (LAST), and related dissection/re-entry methods are well-known in CTO-PCI [10–13], yet their role in acute, stent-jailed SB occlusion has not been systematically studied.

Quantitative flow ratio (QFR), a noninvasive physiology assessment derived from angiography, has emerged as a validated surrogate for FFR in both main and side branches [14–16]. Integrating QFR allows objective functional evaluation of SB patency post-recanalization, beyond angiographic TIMI flow assessment.

In this study, we present a retrospective cohort of 15 cases of acute SB occlusion after MV stenting, all refractory to standard wiring. We describe a strategy-oriented, subintimal algorithm (LAST-first, STAR-for-rescue, Antegrade Dissection and Re-entry[ADR] as tertiary) and evaluate outcomes using TIMI flow and QFR. Our aim is to provide preliminary evidence to guide operators facing this high-risk scenario.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center, retrospective study was conducted using a prospectively maintained PCI database. From January 2019 to December 2023, 9,832 PCI procedures were performed. We screened all procedures for acute SB occlusion after MV stenting. Acute SB occlusion was defined as immediate post-stent SB TIMI flow 0 or 1. Patients were included if:

Only clinically relevant side branches considered to supply meaningful myocardial territory were included in the study. In general, operators considered bailout recanalization when the SB reference diameter was visually estimated to be ≥ 2.0 mm and when persistent SB occlusion was considered potentially clinically or hemodynamically significant. Very small side branches without anticipated clinical impact were typically managed conservatively and were not included in this exploratory cohort.

Importantly, no cases involved acute left circumflex artery (LCX) occlusion during left main bifurcation intervention. All included lesions were non-left-main bifurcation lesions.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval number: JS2025-027-55). QFR analyses were performed independently (blinded) by two experienced analysts.

Endpoint definitions

Primary endpoint

Successful SB recanalization, defined as restoration of TIMI grade 3 flow and SB QFR ≥ 0.80.

QFR assessment

QFR analysis was performed offline using commercially available software (Pulse Medical Imaging Technology, Shanghai, China). Angiographic acquisition for QFR analysis required two projections separated by at least 25° with minimal vessel overlap and adequate contrast opacification, according to standard acquisition recommendations. QFR measurements were independently performed by two experienced analysts blinded to procedural details, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Because the present study involved acute SB recanalization using subintimal techniques, QFR values were interpreted as exploratory functional estimates rather than definitive physiological confirmation. QFR computation was performed along the angiographically reconstructed vessel lumen after restoration of TIMI 3 flow. Potential influences of acute microvascular dysfunction, transient vasospasm, and procedural flow perturbation were acknowledged when interpreting QFR measurements in this setting.

Conventional side branch rewiring attempts

Prior to adoption of a subintimal bailout strategy, all cases underwent systematic conventional attempts to re-enter the side branch (SB) true lumen through the main vessel stent struts. These attempts were performed using standard workhorse or intermediate-support coronary guidewires (e.g., BMW Universal II, Runthrough NS, Sion Blue) advanced via the main vessel guiding catheter, with careful manipulation to avoid stent deformation or strut distortion.

When initial wiring with workhorse guidewires was unsuccessful, escalation to polymer-jacketed or tapered-tip guidewires commonly used for complex bifurcation or chronic total occlusion (CTO) wiring (e.g., Fielder XT, Fielder XTA) was performed, supported by systematic use of microcatheters (such as Finecross or Corsair) to improve torque control and facilitate wire exchange. Parallel wire techniques were attempted at the operator’s discretion when angiographic anatomy suggested a possible alternative true-lumen entry path.

Conventional rewiring was considered to have failed when repeated true-lumen-oriented attempts, despite guidewire escalation and microcatheter support, were unable to achieve distal SB true-lumen access or resulted in repeated subintimal passage without distal re-entry. Only after these predefined criteria were met did operators proceed to an intentional subintimal recanalization strategy as part of the study algorithm.

Conventional rewiring failure was not defined by a strict numeric threshold such as procedure duration, fluoroscopy time, or number of guidewires used. Instead, the transition from conventional rewiring to a subintimal bailout strategy was based on a structured operator-guided assessment integrating several procedural factors, including repeated inability to access the distal SB true lumen despite guidewire escalation and microcatheter support, repeated passage into the same subintimal plane, increasing procedural complexity or risk of vessel injury, and clinical urgency such as hemodynamic instability or ongoing ischemia.

Most included cases involved acute occlusion of non-protected side branches without persistent jailed-wire or jailed-balloon access after main vessel stenting. In cases where proximal optimization technique (POT) or conventional side-branch reopening maneuvers were considered feasible, these strategies were attempted prior to escalation to subintimal bailout recanalization.

Procedural strategy using subintimal techniques

In all cases, the recanalization of the acutely occluded SB was approached using a stepwise technique derived from CTO intervention principles, aiming to facilitate re-entry into the true lumen after subintimal tracking. The strategy involved three key phases: intentional subintimal entry, controlled re-entry, and escalation if necessary.

Step 1: Subintimal access using polymer-jacketed guidewires

Initial attempts to access the SB involved the use of a polymer-jacketed guidewire, typically the Fielder XTA (Asahi Intecc), with the goal of intentionally entering the subintimal space through the stent struts at the ostium of the jailed SB. A microcatheter (e.g., Corsair or Finecross) was systematically advanced to provide support and facilitate guidewire manipulation or exchange.

Step 2: Controlled re-entry using LAST technique

Once subintimal entry was confirmed, re-entry into the distal true lumen was attempted using the LAST technique. This involved exchanging the wire for a more flexible or tapered wire (e.g., Pilot 200 or another Fielder XTA) to gently probe and re-enter the distal SB lumen. Contrast injection via the microcatheter was used to confirm successful re-entry.

Step 3: Escalation strategy: STAR or ADR

If LAST attempts failed, escalation strategies were applied based on the clinical scenario. In hemodynamically stable patients, a modified ADR technique using a Stingray balloon was considered. This required pre-dilation of the subintimal SB entry through the MV stent struts to allow passage of the balloon into the SB subintimal space. In contrast, in cases involving large-caliber SBs or hemodynamic instability (e.g., cardiogenic shock), the STAR technique was employed to rapidly re-establish flow, prioritizing rapid flow restoration despite a potentially longer subintimal course.

In all cases, final angiographic and functional results were assessed using TIMI flow and QFR measurements. A schematic decision flowchart (Fig. 1) was developed to standardize decision-making.

Data collection

Baseline characteristics (age, sex, risk factors), angiographic details (target vessel, Medina classification, SB reference diameter), procedural variables (wire types, techniques, adjunctive devices), and outcomes (TIMI flow, QFR) were recorded. TIMI flow grade was assessed by operators and confirmed by a blinded reviewer.

Safety assessment

Procedural and in-hospital safety outcomes were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records and procedural reports. Recorded adverse events included coronary perforation, tamponade, device entrapment, no-reflow, distal embolization, hemodynamic instability, and in-hospital major adverse cardiac events (MACE). Periprocedural myocardial injury was assessed based on postprocedural cardiac troponin I elevation recorded during hospitalization.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patients

Among 9,832 PCI procedures performed during the study period, 732 patients developed acute SB occlusion after MV stenting. Conventional rewiring successfully restored SB flow in 623 patients. In 109 patients, conventional rewiring failed, among whom 48 underwent attempted subintimal bailout recanalization. After exclusion of 33 patients because of incomplete angiographic or QFR data, 15 consecutive patients were included in the final analysis.The most common MV was the left anterior descending (LAD) artery with diagonal branch occlusion. Functional results by QFR

QFR was successfully obtained in all 15 patients. Mean postprocedural SB QFR was 0.91 ± 0.04, and all patients achieved QFR ≥ 0.80, meeting the functional success criterion. Fig. 2 illustrates individual QFR values. Follow-up angiography and QFR assessment were available in only two patients at 40 and 60 days, respectively. Both cases demonstrated preserved TIMI 3 flow and numerically higher QFR values at follow-up (0.85→0.93 and 0.90→0.95). Given the extremely limited follow-up sample size, these observations should be considered anecdotal and hypothesis-generating only.

Procedural safety outcomes

Elevated postprocedural cardiac troponin I levels were observed in 10 of 15 patients (66.7%), consistent with periprocedural myocardial injury in the setting of acute SB occlusion and complex bifurcation intervention. No angiographically evident no-reflow, major distal embolization, or severe hemodynamic deterioration requiring mechanical circulatory support was observed during the procedures. No acute stent deformation requiring additional intervention was documented. In-hospital death, emergency repeat revascularization, or cardiogenic shock was not recorded among the included patients.

Discussion

Rationale for a strategy-oriented use of subintimal techniques

Acute SB occlusion that persists after conventional rewiring is a rare but challenging complication, particularly because it often arises in patients whose bifurcation anatomy is already complex—marked by heavy plaque burden, small bifurcation angles, or carina shift [4, 5]. Historically, operators have relied on ad-hoc escalation—first attempting softer polymer-jacketed wires, then escalating to stiffer wires, and finally, if all else failed, accepting SB loss. Such an empiric approach risks prolonged fluoroscopy time, excess contrast, and potentially irreversible SB ischemia.

Our data support a pre-specified, hierarchy-based algorithm in which the operator commits early to an intentional subintimal path once a true-lumen approach has demonstrably failed. The algorithm has three conceptual pillars:

Predictable entry: A polymer-jacketed wire such as Fielder XTA predictably dives subintimally through stent struts, creating an immediate channel for a microcatheter.

Controlled re-entry: LAST uses low-tip-load, tapered wires (e.g., Pilot 200) to search for micro-fenestrations between intima and media, limiting the longitudinal extent of dissection.

Graduated escalation: Operators escalate only when LAST fails and the clinical scenario demands faster flow restoration, thereby minimizing unnecessary use of aggressive STAR dissections.

By prospectively defining these transition points, we observed 100% angiographic success without perforation or significant “subintimal hematoma” propagation—complications that frequently plague untargeted dissection/re-entry attempts [10, 11].In this highly selected cohort, angiographic restoration of SB flow was observed in all included cases. However, the absence of failed or conservatively managed comparator cases limits interpretation regarding procedural reproducibility or generalizability.

Role of QFR in acute side branch bailout

A key concern when applying subintimal techniques to a stent-jailed SB is whether angiographic restoration of flow truly reflects adequate physiological reperfusion. In this acute setting, the primary procedural objective is timely recovery of TIMI 3 flow; however, angiography alone cannot reliably determine whether the newly created lumen—often partially subintimal—is hemodynamically sufficient or whether further intervention may be necessary.

In this context, the value of QFR should not be interpreted as a trigger for additional SB stenting or as a determinant of long-term prognostic management. Rather, QFR serves as an objective, wire-free physiological confirmation that angiographic success is functionally acceptable at the end of a complex bailout procedure. Importantly, a QFR value ≥ 0.80 provides operators with reassurance to terminate the procedure without further SB manipulation, thereby avoiding additional contrast use, radiation exposure, or mechanical trauma to a freshly recanalized branch.

It is acknowledged that SB ischemia, even if present, is often non-prognostic and typically managed conservatively in the absence of symptoms. Accordingly, in our practice, a reduced SB QFR would not mandate acute SB stenting if TIMI 3 flow is preserved. Instead, QFR functions as a procedural safety and decision-confirmation tool, rather than a driver of escalation. This distinction is critical to understanding its role in the proposed framework.

Although two patients demonstrated numerically improved follow-up QFR values, the extremely limited availability of follow-up angiographic and physiological assessment precludes any meaningful conclusions regarding long-term vessel remodeling, patency durability, or sustained functional benefit.

The interpretation of QFR in acutely recanalized SBs following subintimal intervention should be approached cautiously. Unlike stable epicardial stenosis assessment, acute procedural factors such as transient vasospasm, altered flow dynamics, endothelial injury, or microvascular dysfunction may influence angiography-derived physiological estimates. Therefore, in the present study, QFR was not intended as definitive physiological validation, but rather as an adjunctive tool providing functional reassurance following angiographic restoration of SB flow.

Positioning LAST, STAR, and ADR within a pragmatic treatment cascade

The debate over which subintimal technique should be “first-line” echoes similar discussions in CTO PCI. Our findings reaffirm several CTO insights:

By mapping these techniques onto a single algorithm (Fig. 1), we provide operators with a decision tool that balances the competing priorities of speed, safety, and long-term vessel integrity.

Comparative perspective and clinical implications

Previous studies of SB occlusion have largely focused on predictors rather than solutions; reports of bailout techniques are anecdotal and seldom include physiology. Morita et al. [9] first introduced STAR for jailed SBs but lacked QFR or FFR follow-up, leaving the functional adequacy in question. The proposed bailout framework may be most applicable to acute occlusion of non-protected side branches, where conventional rescue options such as jailed-balloon reopening or balloon delivery alongside a jailed wire are unavailable or unsuccessful. Hemodynamic deterioration during complex bifurcation PCI may not necessarily reflect the anatomical territory of the occluded side branch alone, but can also be influenced by ventricular arrhythmias, impaired cardiopulmonary reserve, and baseline comorbid conditions.Our cohort, though modest, is the first to combine (i) a predefined technique hierarchy, (ii) 100% procedural success, and (iii) systematic QFR confirmation. Clinically, this translates to several actionable points:

Early commitment to subintimal strategy avoids futility in repeated true-lumen rewiring attempts.

Functional confirmation by QFR can justify ending the procedure without additional SB dilation or stenting, reducing contrast and radiation.

Algorithmic escalation ensures that more aggressive STAR dissections are used sparingly, confining risk to situations where benefit is greatest.

Limitations

The retrospective, single-center design inherently limits external validity. Operator proficiency with CTO techniques may not be generalizable. The sample size (n = 15) precluded statistical comparisons between techniques. Follow-up angiography was available in only two patients; thus long-term patency and restenosis rates remain uncertain. Finally, ADR was not utilized, so its role in jailed SBs is speculative within this study framework.

The relatively frequent occurrence of postprocedural troponin elevation likely reflects the ischemic burden associated with acute SB occlusion and prolonged complex bifurcation manipulation, rather than being attributable solely to the subintimal recanalization technique itself.

Systematic angiographic and physiological follow-up was unavailable for most patients, substantially limiting interpretation of long-term vessel patency or functional durability.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter registries should test this algorithm against conventional rewiring-only strategies, incorporating QFR/FFR endpoints and clinical outcomes (PMI, target lesion failure). Refinements such as low-profile re-entry devices or dedicated microcatheters may expand ADR feasibility in stent-jailed SBs. Intravascular imaging could further elucidate the natural history of subintimal channels and guide optimal antiplatelet regimens in these unique dissections.

Conclusion

In selected patients treated at an experienced center, a structured CTO-derived subintimal bailout approach appeared technically feasible for acute stent-jailed side branch occlusion after failed conventional rewiring. TIMI 3 flow restoration was achieved with acceptable postprocedural QFR values in this highly selected cohort. These preliminary findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger prospective studies with systematic clinical and angiographic follow-up.
