𝖮 ⁡ ( N ) \OO(N) 𝖡𝖢𝖥𝖳 \mathsf{BCFT} : new data from conformal partial wave expansions
Jozef Csipes, and Petr Vaško

Abstract
The 𝖮 ⁡ ( N ) \OO(N) 𝖡𝖢𝖥𝖳 \mathsf{BCFT} is analyzed in the large N N expansion in a generic bulk dimension 2 < d + 1 < 4 2<d+1<4 . Focus is on boundary conditions corresponding to the ordinary transition, however techniques used can be generalized to special or extraordinary transitions. We study the system of bulk 2 2 -pt functions ⟨ ϕ ​ ϕ ⟩ \langle\phi\phi\rangle , ⟨ ϕ 2 ​ ϕ 2 ⟩ \langle\phi^{2}\phi^{2}\rangle and ⟨ σ ​ σ ⟩ \langle\sigma\sigma\rangle , where σ \sigma is the Hubbard–Stratonovich field. They are expanded in bulk/boundary conformal partial waves. The coefficient functions of this expansion – the spectral functions – encode 𝖡𝖢𝖥𝖳 \mathsf{BCFT} data and determine bulk/boundary conformal block expansions. We prove conjectures about boundary spectral functions in Dujava et al. 26 and reproduce the bulk expansion of ⟨ ϕ ​ ϕ ⟩ \langle\phi\phi\rangle in Giombi et al. 32 . The bulk expansion of ⟨ σ ​ σ ⟩ \langle\sigma\sigma\rangle is new and allows to extract the leading large N N expression for an unknown OPE coefficient C σ ​ σ ​ σ 3 C_{\sigma\sigma\sigma^{3}} (the computation outputs as a byproduct also a mixing coefficient, which matches an existing result by Derkachov et al. 15 , providing further support for the main result). Besides these derived data infinitely many constraints are produced, though due to operator mixing growing in complexity as degeneracy increases, they cannot be disentangled without further input.

1.2
Conventions

Before closing the introduction, let us compile here our conventions (which can be safely skipped, except (
1
), by expert readers familiar with standard
\mathsf{BCFT}
notation and different large
N
counting schemes), in order to facilitate the reading of this paper.

Coordinates and cross ratios.

•

A bulk point has coordinate
x=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{x},x_{\perp}}}\right)
, where
x_{\perp}
is the distance from the bulk point to the boundary in flat half plane.

•

In a
\mathsf{BCFT}
there is a single real-valued cross ratio for bulk-bulk
2-
pt functions, whose definition is not unique. We will work with the following two definitions

\displaystyle\xi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{1},x^{2}}}\right)=\mathinner{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{1}-x^{2}}}\right)^{2}\over\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2x^{1}_{\perp}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2x^{2}_{\perp}}}\right)}\in[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\rho=\mathinner{\xi\over 1+\xi}\in[0,1]\,.

(1)

It is worth recalling two important limits:
(i)
the bulk/OPE limit, when the two points collide in the bulk away from the boundary corresponds to
\xi\to 0
or
\rho\to 0
;
(ii)
the boundary/BOE limit when either of the points approaches the boundary while separated in the bulk corresponds to
\xi\to\infty
or
\rho\to 1
.

Bulk/boundary objects.

•

Bulk/boundary conformal blocks, partial waves, spectral functions are denoted by the superscript
B/\partial
:
\mathsf{G}^{B/\partial},\;\mathsf{CPW}^{B/\partial},\;\mathsf{Spec}^{B/\partial}
.

•

Bulk/boundary operators or scaling dimensions are denoted as:
\mathsf{O}/\widehat{\mathsf{O}}
,
\Delta/\widehat{\Delta}
.

Correlators.

•

Mostly, we consider correlators in
\mathsf{BCFT}_{d+1}
and by the Weyl mapping it is convenient to define them on the hyperbolic space
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
. So the symbol
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bullet}\rangle
defines a correlator on
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
.

•

When explicitly needed, we define correlators on flat half plane
\mathrm{FHP}\coloneq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bullet}\rangle_{\mathrm{FHP}}
. They are related to those on the hyperbolic space by standard Weyl factors to be defined below.

•

Occasionally, we write correlators on the flat plane without boundary
\mathrm{FP}\coloneq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
as
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bullet}\rangle_{\mathrm{FP}}
.

•

All correlators are understood as the complete ones, including disconnected parts. However, since we study the ordinary transition,
1
-pt functions of the
\phi
-field vanish. Thus, the only case where this distinction matters concerns the
\sigma
-field,
\sigma=\langle\sigma\rangle\widehat{\mathds{1}}+\delta\sigma
, such that
\langle\delta\sigma\rangle=0
and consequently
\langle\sigma\sigma\rangle\coloneq\langle\sigma\rangle^{2}+\langle\delta\sigma\delta\sigma\rangle
.

Large
N
counting.

•

We employ normalization of the Hubbard–Stratonovich field
\sigma
, so that the interaction vertex
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi^{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.75,0.75}\bullet}}}}\right)^{2}
scales as
\sim N^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}
.

•

When we need to show an explicit order of a quantity in the large
N
expansion, we do so by a superscript enclosed in parenthesis. For instance, with the above normalization, some important leading order observables scale as: OPE coefficient
C_{\phi\phi\sigma}\sim\langle\phi\phi\delta\sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{FP}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-1/2}}\right)}\sim N^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}
,
1
-pt function
\langle\sigma\rangle^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/2}}\right)}\sim N^{\tfrac{1}{2}}
, connected correlator
\langle\delta\sigma\delta\sigma\rangle^{(0)}\sim N^{0}
or
\langle\phi\phi\rangle^{(0)}\sim N^{0}
.

2.1
The
\OO(N)
model

Let us start by recalling certain aspects of the large
N
limit of the
\mathsf{O}(N)
model which will be fundamental to our work.
We are interested in its interacting fixed point (the Wilson-Fisher fixed point) in the presence of a boundary.
More thorough treatment of this model on flat space can be found in specialized reviews
52
;
29
, or for the case of
\mathsf{BCFT}

32
.
Recently, the behavior of this model on
\mathsf{AdS}
space has also been examined in
11
;
26
.

The
\mathsf{O}(N)
vector model describes
N
interacting scalar fields with the Euclidean bulk action

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[\phi^{i}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathinner{1\over 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial\phi^{i}}}\right)^{2}+\mathinner{1\over 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m^{2}+\xi R}}\right)\phi^{i}\phi_{i}+\mathinner{\lambda\over 4N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi^{i}\phi_{i}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]\,,

(2)

where we have included the potential coupling to the curvature
\mathinner{1\over 2}\xi R\phi^{2}
which is important if the underlying manifold
\mathcal{M}
is not flat.
Here,
R
is the Ricci scalar.

If, however, the underlying manifold
\mathcal{M}
is flat, the theory has a Gaussian fixed point in the IR, which corresponds to the free massless theory (
m^{2}=\lambda=0
).
In dimensions
2<d+1<4
the mass and the
\phi^{4}
interaction term are relevant and drive the RG flow to the interacting Wilson-Fisher fixed point (with a specific scheme-dependent value of
m^{2}/\lambda
).
To better understand the large
N
behavior of this theory, one can introduce the auxiliary Hubbard-Stratonovich field
\sigma
and consider the action

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{HS}}[\phi^{i},\sigma]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathinner{1\over 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial\phi^{i}}}\right)^{2}+\mathinner{1\over 2}(m^{2}+\xi R)\phi^{i}\phi_{i}-\mathinner{1\over 4\lambda}\sigma^{2}+\mathinner{1\over 2\sqrt{N}}\sigma\phi^{i}\phi_{i}}}\right]\,,

(3)

which is equivalent to (
2
) as can be seen by the equation of motion for
\sigma
which just sets
\sigma=\mathinner{\lambda\over\sqrt{N}}\phi^{2}
.
Here, the critical theory can be obtained by performing the limits
m^{2}\to 0
,
\lambda\to\infty
, and
\xi\to\mathinner{1\over 4}\mathinner{d-1\over d}
, resulting in the simplified action

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{HS}}[\phi^{i},\sigma]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathinner{1\over 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial\phi^{i}}}\right)^{2}+\mathinner{d-1\over 8d}R\phi^{i}\phi_{i}+\mathinner{1\over 2\sqrt{N}}\sigma\phi^{i}\phi_{i}}}\right]\,.

(4)

The benefit being that this action is quadratic which allows us to integrate out the
\phi^{i}
resulting in an effective action for
\sigma
.
The partition function for this effective action becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\int\mathcal{D}\sigma\,\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\mathinner{N\over 2}\Tr\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\nabla^{2}+\mathinner{d-1\over 8d}R+\mathinner{\sigma\over\sqrt{N}}}}\right)}}\right]\,.

(5)

For our purposes, the space manifold
\mathcal{M}
will be a half-space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{\geq 0}
with a flat boundary at
x^{d+1}\equiv x_{\perp}=0
.
Considering the action (
4
) on it leads to a
\mathsf{BCFT}
which has several available conformal boundary conditions that are connected by boundary RG flows (see
(
32
, Fig. 2)
for a useful summary).
Starting with the
special transition
in the UV, one can add a relevant boundary interaction term
c\hat{\phi}^{2}
(with positive
c
) which drives the RG flow to the
ordinary transition
while adding the same operator with a negative
c
leads to an RG flow to the
extraordinary transition
.
On the other hand, if one starts in the ordinary transition, a deformation by the operator
h\cdot\hat{\phi}
(where
h
is a vector of constants) leads again to the extraordinary transition.
Our interest will be in the ordinary transition which can intuitively be understood—if one considers the model as a lattice of spins—as a transition connecting a phase where both bulk and boundary are ordered with a phase where they are both disordered.

A different perspective on the boundary critical theory is provided by
\mathsf{AdS}/\mathsf{CFT}
techniques, from which it is well known that one can map the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{\geq 0}
onto a hyperboloid (the Euclidean Anti-de Sitter space)
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
by a Weyl transformation.
This map is particularly clear if one considers the metric for
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
in the Poincar´e coordinates,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\mathsf{H}_{d+1}}=\mathinner{1\over x_{\perp}^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{d}x_{\perp}^{2}+\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{2}}}\right)\,,\quad x_{\perp}\geq 0\,,

(6)

which is clearly Weyl equivalent to the flat metric on a half-space.
We always consider the hyperboloid to have a unit radius.
This provides a correspondence between
\mathsf{BCFT}
s and
\mathsf{CFT}
s on the hyperboloid, meaning that the correlators in
\mathsf{BCFT}
can be translated into correlators on
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
by simply performing a Weyl transformation.
The Ricci scalar on
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
is a constant
R=-d(d+1)
which can readily be substituted into (
5
) and then one can employ the saddle point approximation to find the large
N
description of the theory.
We can split the operator
\sigma
as

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=\langle\sigma\rangle+\delta\sigma(x)

(7)

where
\langle\sigma\rangle
is a constant when considered on the hyperbolic space (the saddle point) and
\langle\delta\sigma\rangle=0
.
Plugging this back into the Hubbard-Stratonovich action (
4

\langle\sigma\rangle
only modifies the effective mass of the
\phi

5

N
gives the connected part of effective propagator for the
\sigma
field

\displaystyle\langle\delta\sigma\delta\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=-4B^{-1}(x,y)

(8)

where
B(x,y)=\mathinner{\langle\phi^{2}(x)\phi^{2}(y)\rangle\over N}=\mathinner{2\over N}\langle\phi^{i}\phi^{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\rangle
is the bubble function and by
B^{-1}
we mean its inverse with respect to convolutions on
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
.

In the large
N

\langle\sigma\rangle
that extremizes the effective action for
\sigma

32

\displaystyle\langle\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma\rangle^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/2}}\right)}+\mathcal{O}(1)=\sqrt{N}\mathinner{(d-1)(d-3)\over 4}+\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

(9)

The saddle point of
\sigma

\phi

\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
with this mass. This can be translated by standard

dictionary to correspond to scaling dimension
\widehat{\Delta}_{\phi}=d-1

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{i}\phi^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\phi^{i}\phi^{j}\rangle^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{N}}}\right)=\delta^{ij}\mathcal{N}_{\phi}\rho^{-\mathinner{d-1\over 2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\rho}}\right)^{d-1}+\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{N}}}\right)\,.

Here we have used the

The presence of the boundary breaks the conformal group of the theory to a subgroup of transformations that stabilize the boundary.
In effect, bulk operators gain a non-zero expectation value whose form is entirely fixed by symmetries except for the overall normalization,

\displaystyle\langle\mathsf{O}(x)\rangle_{\text{FHP}}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{b_{\mathsf{O}\widehat{\mathds{1}}}\over x_{\perp}^{\Delta_{\mathsf{O}}}}\,.

(14)

The coefficient

2.3
Spectral representations

In order to extract the
\mathsf{BCFT}
data from 2-pt functions, it is imperative to have a reliable technique for finding the conformal block decomposition of these functions.
One such technique is provided by the spectral representations
36
which first decompose the 2-pt functions into a complete basis of the so-called conformal partial waves (CPWs) and then by the deformation of a contour one obtains a sum of conformal blocks.

Boundary channel.

For the boundary channel one defines the CPWs as a combination of a boundary CB and a shadow CB

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{CPW}_{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)&=\mathinner{1\over 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}_{\widehat{\Delta}}\mathsf{G}^{\partial}_{\widehat{\Delta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\Delta}\to d-\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)}}\right]\\
&=\mathop{{}_{2}\bm{\mathsf{F}}_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\+matrix[cells={c},rowsep=0.5pt,colsep=2pt]\widehat{\Delta},\,d-\widehat{\Delta}{\\
}\mathinner{d+1\over 2}{\\
}{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,0.4}\mathinner{}\middle|\mathinner{}}-\mathinner{\rho\over 1-\rho}\mspace{2mu}}}\right]\,,\end{split}

(20)

where we defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{2\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathinner{d+1\over 2}}}\right)\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-2\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)\over\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathinner{d+1\over 2}-\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)}\,.

(21)

If the scaling dimension
\widehat{\Delta}
is chosen to lie on the principal series,
\widehat{\Delta}\in\mathinner{d\over 2}+i\mathbb{R}
, then the CPWs form a complete orthogonal basis of eigenfunctions of the conformal Casimir operator.
This allows to decompose an arbitrary 2-pt function on
\mathsf{H}_{d+1}
—which is just a function of the cross-ratio
\rho
—into CPWs as

\displaystyle\langle\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathinner{1\over 2\pi i}\int_{\mathinner{d\over 2}+i\mathbb{R}}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}\widehat{\Delta}\over\mathcal{N}^{\partial}_{\widehat{\Delta}}}\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)\mathsf{CPW}_{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\text{where }\mathcal{N}_{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}=\mathinner{\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}\rvert^{2}\over 2}\,.

(22)

The CPWs are analogous to the
“harmonic functions”
that appear in the
\mathsf{AdS}/\mathsf{CFT}
literature
62
;
12
;
61
;
11
.
The spectral representation can also be viewed as a special case of the Jacobi transform
36
;
38
.
The orthogonality of the CPWs allows the spectral representation to be inverted and the
\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)
to be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\widehat{\Delta}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathinner{\rho^{\mathinner{d-1\over 2}}\over\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\rho}}\right)^{d+1}}\langle\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)\,\mathsf{CPW}_{\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)\,.

(23)

This is the Euclidean inversion formula for the boundary channel.
Once we have the decomposition into CPWs, it is easy to find the decomposition into conformal blocks by closing the integration contour in (
22
) which results in

\displaystyle\langle\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widehat{\Delta}_{n}\in\{\text{poles}\}}-2\Res_{\widehat{\Delta}=\widehat{\Delta}_{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathinner{\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{\partial}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\Delta}}}\right)\over\mathcal{Q}_{d-\widehat{\Delta}}^{\partial}}\mathsf{G}^{\partial}_{\widehat{\Delta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}}\right].

(24)

Bulk channel.

A very similar decomposition exists for the bulk channel as well. Here one defines the bulk CPW as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{CPW}_{\Delta}^{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)&=\mathinner{1\over 2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{Q}^{B}_{\Delta}\mathsf{G}^{B}_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta\to d+1-\Delta}}\right)}}\right]\\
&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathinner{1-\rho\over\rho}}}\right)^{\mathinner{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}\over 2}}\mathop{{}_{2}\bm{\mathsf{F}}_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\+matrix[cells={c},rowsep=0.5pt,colsep=2pt]\mathinner{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}\over 2}+\mathinner{\Delta\over 2},\,\mathinner{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}\over 2}+\mathinner{d+1-\Delta\over 2}{\\
}1+\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}{\\
}{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,0.4}\mathinner{}\middle|\mathinner{}}\mathinner{\rho-1\over\rho}\mspace{2mu}}}\right]\end{split}

(25)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta}^{B}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{2\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}}}\right)\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathinner{d+1\over 2}-\Delta}}\right)\over\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathinner{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}\over 2}+\mathinner{d+1-\Delta\over 2}}}\right)\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathinner{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}\over 2}+1-\mathinner{\Delta\over 2}}}\right)}\,.

(26)

Notice that the above formulas simplify substantially if the external scaling dimensions
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
are the same, which will be the most important scenario for our purposes.
The corresponding spectral representation and inversion formula is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathinner{1\over 2\pi i}\int_{\mathinner{d+1\over 2}+i\mathbb{R}}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}\Delta\over 2\mathcal{N}_{\Delta}^{B}}\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)\,\mathsf{CPW}^{B}_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathinner{1\over\rho^{\mathinner{d+3\over 2}}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)\,\mathsf{CPW}_{\Delta}^{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)\,.

(28)

Here we again defined
\mathcal{N}_{\Delta}^{B}=\mathinner{\lvert\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta}^{B}\rvert\over 2}
.
The integration contour runs over the bulk principal series
\Delta\in\mathinner{d+1\over 2}+i\mathbb{R}
.
Notice that we work with the factorized 2-pt function
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}
which relates to
\langle\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}\rangle
as in (
16
).
To decompose this function into CBs, one can again close the contour in (
27
) and reduce the integral to a sum of residues,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Delta_{n}\in\{\text{poles}\}}-\Res_{\Delta=\Delta_{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathinner{\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)\over\mathcal{Q}^{B}_{d+1-\Delta}}\mathsf{G}^{B}_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho}}\right)}}\right]\,.

(29)

Notice that if the
\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{B}
function has only simple poles to the right of the principal series, then the sum above will only contain conformal blocks multiplied by a factor of
\Res_{\Delta=\Delta_{n}}\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)/\mathcal{Q}_{d+1-\Delta}^{B}
.
But if
\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathsf{O}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}}^{B}
contains higher order poles, then (
29
) will contain derivatives of CBs as well. This subtlety will play an important role in our extraction of BCFT data in
Section

5
.

6
Outlook

In this paper we studied the
\OO(N)
model in the large
N
approximation at the critical point, with boundary conditions chosen to correspond to the
ordinary
universality class. To get a firm handle on
\mathsf{BCFT}
data, as summed up in
Section

1.1
, techniques of bulk/boundary conformal partial wave expansions were used.

There are a couple of future directions worth investigating, ranked by their (anticipated) difficulty:

1.

For simplicity, we focused entirely on the ordinary transition. But all methods used are general and should be extendable to the special and extraordinary/normal transition without major difficulties. Minor technical complications might be encountered, however the expectation is that the extension to the other two boundary universality classes should be fairly straightforward.

2.

In order to get additional constraints for
\mathsf{BCFT}
data, one could try to run the logic of conformal perturbation theory in reverse (typically, knowledge about the critical point is assumed and used to extend it to its vicinity). While working in the hyperbolic Weyl frame, the non-critical theory is still a theory in
\mathsf{EAdS}
. In particular, it supports a well defined (even though non-local)
\mathsf{CFT}
at its boundary. Scaling dimensions of certain primary operators (double twist families) in this boundary
\mathsf{CFT}
were already obtained in
11
;
26
(see also
27
for a detailed introduction to this topic) and their OPE coefficients can in principle be computed as well. Linking this known data to the critical theory (the
\mathsf{BCFT}
that was the subject of this paper) via conformal perturbation theory provides an additional handle on
\mathsf{BCFT}
data of interest.

3.

One could try to extend the computation of the simplest
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\phi\phi}}\right\rangle
correlator to the next large
N
order (that is
1/N
in our conventions). Partial results were published a long time ago in a series of papers
54
;
56
;
55
;
57
;
58
. The
2
-pt function was computed in position space and expressed implicitly via an integral representation with the single cross ratio as the upper bound of the integral. Reconstructing BOE of
\phi
translates to a computation of asymptotic expansion of this integral. In contrast to the leading large
N
order, where only a single boundary primary
\widehat{\phi}
contributes, at the subleading order there is an infinite tower. To our knowledge, only the leading term in the asymptotic expansion was derived, allowing to fix the scaling dimension of
\widehat{\phi}
. Results for the rest of the tower don’t exist.

We propose that instead of attempting the calculation in position space it might be simpler to go after the associated boundary spectral function (that anyhow packages BOE data more efficiently than a position space representation). As we saw in
Appendix

B
, it is highly constraint by basic principles. Thus one might hope for an efficient mixed analytic/bootstrap approach. It remains to be seen if it turns out as a viable option.
