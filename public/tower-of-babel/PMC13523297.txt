Impact of lipid-based nutritional supplementation on cardio metabolic risk factors in underweight pre-eclamptic primigravidas: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hafsa Zafar, Murad A Mubaraki, Mashal Zafar, Nabila Sher, Rubina Nazli, Kalsoom Tariq, Hashim Khan, Ijaz Ahmad, Fozia Fozia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-eclampsia is a major cause of maternal and fetal complications in Pakistan, particularly among primigravidas, where poor nutritional status further increases risk. Emerging evidence suggests that underweight pre-eclamptic women may have distinct cardio metabolic adaptations, higher susceptibility to adverse pregnancy outcomes, and increased long-term cardiovascular risk. Lipid-based nutritional supplementation provides essential energy and micronutrients and may help improve maternal metabolic status and pregnancy outcomes in pre-eclamptic women.ObjectiveTo determine the effect of lipid-based micronutrient supplementation on lipid profile, Insulin resistance and blood pressure in underweight pre-eclamptic primigravidas at delivery, six months and twelve months postnatal.MethodsAn open label randomized controlled trial was undertaken to explore the effects of supplementation on the cardio metabolic risk factors in the tertiary care hospitals of KPK, Pakistan. A total of sixty primigravidas were randomized equally into two groups; Group A: control group and Group B; supplement group. At delivery, 26 and 28 participants in Groups A and B, respectively and at 6 and 12 months, 23 and 25 participants were analyzed. In addition to their prescribed antenatal treatment of preeclampsia and IFA (60 mg iron and 400ug FA) daily, the Group B also received one 75gm of lipid-based nutrition supplement (LNS) sachet (MAAMTA) daily from their first antenatal visit after enrollment till one year postnatally. The IFA was stopped 6 weeks after delivery.ResultsThere was no discernable difference among the baseline cardio-metabolic parameters like insulin, glucose & HOMA-IR of two groups at delivery. Also, the difference in BP (systolic) was non-significant while BP (diastolic) was decreased in group B (p = 0.025). Similarly, at delivery the significant declined were observed in triglyceride (TGs) levels with LNS supplementation (p = 0.007), Total cholesterol (p = 0.01), LDL (p = 0.02) and VLDL (p = 0.005). At 6 months follow-up systolic (p = 0.002) and diastolic blood pressure (p = 0.013) were decreased significantly in group B & also a significant decline in TGs (p = 0.05), cholesterol (p < 0.001), LDL (p < 0.001) and VLDL levels (p = 0.04) observed in group B. A significant decrease was seen in cholesterol levels (p < 0.001) and LDL levels (p < 0.001) in group B at 12 months postnatal.ConclusionThis study concludes that long term LNS supplementation can cause a decrease in cholesterol, TGs, LDL and VLDL in underweight pre-eclamptic primigravidas and thus may contribute to a more favorable cardio metabolic risk profile.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: ISRCTN 15485068. Introduction

Preeclampsia is one of the hypertensive conditions associated with pregnancy [1] as stated by the “National High Blood Pressure Education Program Working Group”. It is described as “the presence of oedema, hypertension, and proteinuria in a previously non-proteinuric and normotensive pregnant woman after 20 weeks of gestation" [2]. Preeclampsia(PE) is the most frequent pregnancy complication worldwide, accounting for 10% to 15% of maternal deaths [3]. It is reported 7.4–11.3% in India [4] and 14.4% in Bangladesh [5] and 12.4% of pregnancies are complicated by PE in Pakistan [6].

Preeclampsia (PE) has a complex and not fully understood pathophysiology but is closely linked with adverse cardio-metabolic changes. It is characterized by dyslipidemia, including elevated triacylglycerol, total cholesterol, LDL, VLDL, and reduced HDL, along with abnormal apolipoproteins and insulin resistance [7]. These alterations contribute to endothelial dysfunction, promoting atherosclerosis and increasing the long-term risk of diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and renal disorders [8, 9]. Additionally, PE is associated with increased body weight and impaired glucose metabolism, further indicating its role in future metabolic risk [10].

Women with pre-eclamptic pregnancies have been known to re-hospitalize for cardiovascular disorders such as diabetes, hypertension, atherosclerosis, and stroke within the first year following delivery [11]. Intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), Small for gestational age (SGA), and a substantial perinatal mortality rate are among the additional complications experienced by babies of pre-eclamptic moms [12]. Pre-eclamptic individuals may have a higher risk of hypertension in their later years [13].

During the last trimester of pregnancy, adipose tissue experiences an increase in lipolytic activity and a reduction in lipoprotein lipase, leading to an increase in lipid levels [14]. As gestational age grows throughout pregnancy, there is a considerable increase in the levels of TG, phospholipids (PL), TC, lipoproteins including Apo lipoproteins in the plasma [15]. The metabolic alterations that occur during pregnancy are caused by the hormones catecholamine and insulin. Insulin increases the activity of lipolytic enzymes to regulate triglyceride absorption and catecholamine uptake [16]. The primary cause of endothelial cell damage in preeclampsia is the free fatty acid (FFA). In preeclampsia, the FFA to albumin ratio rises [17]. Nitric oxide synthase activity in the endothelium is hampered by elevated FFAs, which lowers nitric oxide levels [18]. The two most prevalent lipid abnormalities in PE are elevated triglycerides and increased FFAs. Apart from elevated triglyceride, fasting insulin, and urate levels, there is a low mean serum HDL level [19]. In preeclampsia, metabolic symptoms include low HDL, hypertriglyceridemia, hyper insulinemia, and hyper uricemia are the hallmark of the “insulin resistance syndrome” [20]. The byproducts of hyperlipidemia, elevated triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein, cling to the arterial wall and pierce the artery intima, frequently resulting in early atherosclerosis [20]. During pregnancy, the human placental lactogen multiplies approximately thirty times, causing the pancreas to secrete insulin [21]. Insulin resistance may result from the dyslipidemia and noticeably higher insulin levels in pre-eclampsia [22].

It is well known that a healthy diet prior to conception has a significant impact on the fetus’s growth, development, and gene expression. It could be the cause of adult cardiovascular abnormalities, acute and chronic diseases of the early prenatal stage, and metabolic health issues [23]. The fetus’s future health greatly depends on its intrauterine life. The fetus’s morphological and physiological growth is dependent on the mother consuming an ideal diet that includes all essential elements, such as vitamins and minerals [24].

PE is a complicated syndrome that is either directly or indirectly associated with nutritional insufficiency and micronutrient imbalance. Additional variables such as stress and physical state are secondary causes of preeclampsia [25]. Undernourishment in impoverished nations can result in PE and related problems. Inadequate food consumption prenatally and postnatally may put the health of the mother and the fetus at danger [26]. Deficits in trace elements including as magnesium, zinc, calcium, copper, and selenium have been implicated in numerous studies as causes of PE, early births, stillbirths, and low fetal body weight [27, 28].

When various trace elements are supplemented, preeclampsia development is slowed down [29]. Zinc and selenium, when combined with other minerals, have a meaningful effect on lowering PE and its complications clinically. Supplementation with multiple micronutrient serves a helpful function in prevention of PE and its therapy [30]. Pregnancy and supplements: the effects are well-established. It has been demonstrated that taking the right supplements throughout pregnancy can stop a baby from developing defects [31]. The WHO strongly advises using nutritional supplements during pregnancy and stresses the importance of supplementation during childbearing age [32].

The WHO introduced Maamta, a lipid-based nutrition supplement (LNS), in November 2016 with a focus on pregnant and nursing women (LNS-PLW). The United Nations International Multiple Micronutrient Preparation (UNIMMAP) served as the model for the nutrient content of the LNS-PLW [33].

Maamta (LNS-PLW), a product of the World Food Program, was introduced to the public sector in Pakistan to help in preventing & treating under nutrition during pregnancy & breastfeeding. It is available as a75 mg sachet which is ready-to-use & doesn’t need to be mixed, cooked, or diluted; it may be eaten right out of the bag. Additionally, one Maamta (LNS-PLW) sachet provides approximately 400 kcal. LNS-PLW is a simple, ready-to-use medication that treats under nutrition, pregnancy malnutrition, and women of childbearing age [34].

Pakistan is a developing nation, and due to social, traditional, and cultural norms, women in the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa avoid going to antenatal clinics. In Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province’s tertiary care facilities, the number of young women with complex pregnancies—such as preeclampsia—who are hospitalized for cardio metabolic diseases after a few years of postpartum is rising. The current study was conducted to examine the long term impact of LNS in pregnancies complicated by hypertension and to lessen the burden of maternal morbidity on the nation, as earlier studies have demonstrated the favorable effects of these supplements on normal pregnancies.

Hypothesis; The use of LNS by underweight pre-eclamptic primigravidas may improve their blood pressure, fasting blood sugar, lipid profile and insulin sensitivity.

Material & methods

It was a randomized controlled parallel- group trial with 1:1 allocation ratio. The framework was superiority trial, that carried out in the obstetrics and gynecology departments of Matta Civil Hospital Swat, Hayatabad Medical Complex Peshawar, Lady Reading Hospital Peshawar. The laboratory analytical work has been conducted by Khyber Girls Medical College (KGMC), Khyber Medical University Peshawar (KMU) and Rehman Research Institute (RMI) Peshawar, Pakistan. Approval from the Advanced Study and Research Board of Khyber Medical University Peshawar [(ASRB Approval No: DIR/KMU-AS&RB/EN/000527)]; and the institutional ethical committee of KMU (DIR/KMU-EB/EN/000314); and permission from the corresponding tertiary care hospitals was taken. This trial has been registered under ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: ISRCTN 15,485,068. on April 21, 2018: https://doi.org/10.1186/ISRCTN15485068.

A pilot phase was initiated in February 2018 to assess feasibility. The trial was subsequently registered on April 21, 2018 (ISRCTN15485068). Therefore, trial registration is considered retrospective with respect to the initial pilot phase. However, the study protocol, eligibility criteria, and outcome measures remained unchanged following registration. Between February 2018 and December 2019, 2,387 expectant mothers underwent screenings at the tertiary care prenatal of above-mentioned hospitals. Of the 2,387 expectant mothers, 463 were determined to be pre-eclamptic. The records of the participants were checked for calculation of their initial BMI at first visit. Primigravidas having preeclampsia having normal BMI (n = 113) and higher BMI based on gestational age (n = 77) were not included in this study. According to study criteria, only 64 pre-eclamptic primigravida with low BMIs for their gestational ages were eligible; four of the participants declined to take part. At the end of the trial 60 underweight primigravidas having preeclampsia were recruited (Fig. 1). Due to different reason shown in Fig. 1, there were six dropouts each at delivery and six months postnatal visits. There were no dropouts at 12 months because the study implemented intensive retention strategies, including scheduled follow-up alignment with routine immunization visits, repeated reminder calls, and home visits where necessary. Additionally, the study population was geographically stable, which minimized loss to follow-up.

Inclusion criteria

Preeclamptic primigravida from their record file whose BMI at the first antenatal appointment was less than the minimum required for the gestational age were selected. The participants did not smoke, ranged in age from 15 to 35, had a gestational age of > 20 weeks, had signs and symptoms of PE, and were not following any specific diet.

Exclusion criteria

Those who were obese or had a normal body mass index at their initial prenatal appointment, or who were taking any form of dietary supplementation other than folic acid and iron were not recruited. Participants in the trial who had a food allergy were also disqualified.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi (https://www.openepi.com). The calculation was based on the primary outcome variable, cardio metabolic biomarker change (e.g., fasting glucose and lipid profile parameters). Since no direct effect size was available for the target population, the expected difference and variability were approximated from a similar metabolic intervention study by Fatima et al., which reported significant changes in cardiometabolic biomarkers following nutritional exposure [35]. Assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and a power of 80%, the required sample size was estimated to be 60 participants.

Data collection tool

A pre-designed questionnaire was used to gather detailed medical history information about each participant, as well as information about their age, marital status, age at conception, family and personal history of diabetes, hypertension, and kidney disease, as well as their education, income, and living situation. Additionally, a history of preeclampsia/PIH in their family as well as the signs and symptoms of PE, including headaches, hematuria, reduced urinary output/anuria, rapid weight gain, nausea, vomiting, visual complications, cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, hepatic and hemostatic complications was collected. Moreover, prior to the study trial, participants’ anthropometric and socio demographic data were collected.

Randomization

Was done by principal investigator using online research randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/). The allocation sequence was generated in advance by an independent researcher not involved in participant recruitment. Group assignments were concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after participant enrollment. This ensured that the investigator enrolling participants remained unaware of upcoming allocations, thereby minimizing selection bias despite the open-label design. This simple randomization was used to divide the participants into two groups. It was an open label trial. Neither the participants nor investigators were blinded to group allocation after assignment to the interventions. The groups were.

Group A, which consisted of underweight preeclamptic primigravida on recommended IFA dose (60 mg iron, 400 ug folic acid) and conventional PE treatment; and Group B, which consisted of underweight preeclamptic primigravida on recommended IFA dose, conventional PE treatment, and one sachet of LNS daily. As directed by their gynecologist, participants in both groups received their usual course of PE and IFA (60 mg iron and 400 ug FA) every day. A 75 gm sachet of Maamta, a multiple micronutrient nutrition dietary supplement, was consumed daily by the LNS-PLW participants up until delivery and for a year after birth. The primary investigator verified the participants’ compliance by gathering the empty sachets on a weekly basis. Any remaining contents in the sachets were also computed to obtain an estimate of supplement usage. Because of their collaboration, the compliance was found to be good throughout the study. Aside from treatment of PE, dietary guidance was given to both groups.

Sample collection

The principal investigator monitored the subjects in both cohorts until delivery. During their hospital stay, the first follow-up blood sample was obtained within 24 h of delivery using aseptic procedure in a gel tube. Following centrifugation, the samples were stored at -80 °C for further examination. Utilizing a commercially available Merck kit, the lipid profile was determined using the colorimetry approach on the Beer-Lambert law enzymatic based assay on the Micro Lab 300 Merck semi-automated clinical chemistry analyzer. The Merck kit’s control sera were used to check the results for accuracy and precision. The detection method that was employed in the analysis of Insulin, Apo lipoprotein A, B, and Lpa was CM1A (Chemiluminescent Micropartic1e assay). The colorimetric approach was used to evaluate blood sugar levels after fasting. The insulin resistance (IR) was measured using the homeostasis model assessment, or HOMAI IR. The following formula is used by the HOMA IR score to determine insulin resistance [36].

HOMA IR = Fasting glucose (nmol/L) X Fasting insulin (uU/L) /22.5.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed by using SPSS version 20(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For normality of the data, Shapiro Wilk normality test was used. The mean and standard deviations were calculated, mean values of both groups were compared using independent sample t-test. The primary analysis was conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants. Missing data were handled using appropriate imputation methods. A secondary per-protocol analysis was also performed including only participants with complete follow-up data.

Results

The goal of this study was to assess the long-term effects of supplements on the cardio metabolic risk variables with controls by tracking the individuals from their first prenatal visit to one year postnatally. For primary & secondary outcomes, a total of 60 participants were included in the analyses, with 30 in each group. Outcome data for 26 in group A & 28 participants in group B was available at delivery and 23 and 25 participants in group A &B respectively at six months and a year postnatally. No adverse events attributable to LNS supplementation were observed in either group during the study period.

Following their study enrollment, their initial biochemical indicators were examined. There were no discernible variations found in the levels of proteinuria, glucose, insulin, and cholesterol (HDL, LDL, TGs, and VLDL). Likewise, there was no significant difference between the two groups’ Lpa, Apo-A, and Apo-B levels. In both groups, insulin resistance was established. When the HOMAIR of each participant was calculated at baseline, no discernible difference was found among the two groups. Both the A and B groups had their baseline blood pressure monitored. At baseline, there were no meaningful differences between groups across cardiometabolic parameters. Systolic blood pressure was comparable between Group A and Group B (mean difference = 1.00 mmHg; 95% CI: −3.06 to 5.06; Cohen’s d = 0.13), indicating a negligible effect size. Similarly, diastolic blood pressure (MD = 0.17 mmHg; 95% CI: −2.27 to 2.61; d = 0.03) and fasting glucose (MD = − 3.07 mg/dl; 95% CI: −6.29 to 0.15; d = 0.50) showed small to moderate but non-significant effects (Table 1).

Discussion

The potential benefits of lipid-based dietary supplements on metabolic, cardiovascular health, and nutritional improvement in a variety of populations have been investigated [37]. There has been little research on how LNS specifically affects the cardio metabolic risk factors in preeclampsia, although studies have indicated that LNS can improve lipid profiles and lower the risk of cardiovascular illnesses [38]. The presence of fatty acids(FAs), including omega-3 and omega-6 FAs, in LNS may have positive impacts on blood pressure and cardiovascular health by lowering inflammation and enhancing endothelial function [39]. Because they reduce inflammation and improve endothelial function, essential FAs like omega-3 and omega-6 FAs found in LNS may impart positively on blood pressure and cardiovascular health.

At the time of enrollment, the baseline levels of the cardiac & metabolic markers (TC, TGs, LDL, HDL, VLDL, Apo-A & B, Lpa, glucose and insulin) were examined. Additionally, the HOMAIR was computed, both groups displayed IR with no discernible variation. Participants in the study had varied baseline lipid profiles in both groups. There was no statistical difference in the groups’ higher serum triglyceride levels. Since every member of our study group had a preeclampsia diagnosis, our findings are in agreement with those of prior studies that found elevated TG levels in women with PE [40, 41]. Pre-eclamptic women may have more triglycerides, which are likely accumulated in the spiral arteries’ vascular walls and leads to the development of endothelial dysfunction [42].

Participants in this study had low baseline HDL values. Insulin resistance and hypo-estrogenemia are the causes of the low HDL levels in preeclampsia [43]. The results of earlier research, which showed lower HDL levels in pre-eclamptic women compared to normal pregnant women, are consistent with the low HDL levels found in this study [43, 44].

In the current investigation, both groups’ baseline elevated cholesterol levels were noted. Numerous research investigations on pre-eclamptic women reported similar elevated cholesterol levels [40, 41]. The current study’s pre-eclamptic females had greater LDL levels. This observation aligned with findings from other studies [40, 44]. The levels of VLDL were likewise higher in the current investigation. The levels of VLDL and LDL increased during pregnancy, reaching twice the pre-pregnancy levels. Following delivery, these levels revert to normal [45]. This can be because to elevated triglyceride levels, which lead to more of them entering VLDL. Triglycerides are carried into the bloodstream by lipoproteins such as HDL, LDL, and VLDL, which transport these endogenous lipids through plasma [46]. Elevated VLDL levels in PE worsen endothelial damage, which can result in pregnancy-related toxemia [47]. Numerous studies demonstrate elevated blood lipid levels during pregnancy, especially in preeclampsia [48]. In hypertensive pregnant women, particularly pre-eclamptic ones, a randomized prospective trial on normotensive and hypertensive women found high levels of TC, LDL, TGs, VLDL, & low levels of HDL [49]. Several investigations offered similar reports, which supported our findings even more [40].

At baseline analysis, it was discovered that the levels of Lipoprotein a, Apolipoprotein-A and B were elevated in the current study. Similarly, higher levels of Apo-A, Apo-B, and Lpa were reported in various investigations on pre-eclamptic women; these results are consistent with them [50]. High lipoprotein levels are significantly correlated with blood pressure and proteinuria, making them useful as screening indicators for PE risk [51].

Pre eclampsia is characterized by changes in metabolism, including dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, hyperlipidemia, and regulation of inflammatory markers [52]. The structural elements of lipoproteins are the Apo lipoproteins [50]. Pre-eclamptic women are more likely to experience cardiovascular problems in the future [53]. Additionally, we examined the insulin and glucose levels while fasting. No difference was seen among the groups’ baseline glucose levels, which were both normal. Although there was no variance between the two groups, the fasting insulin levels were found to be increased in both. Numerous investigations on pre-eclamptic women have revealed hyper insulinemia [22]. As each participant of each group was pre-eclamptic in the third trimester, insulin resistance was seen in both groups with no discernible differences between them. Insulin resistance was examined in a study as a possible factor for the etiology of PE [53]. The evidence showed association of preeclampsia with insulin resistance [54].

The pre-eclamptic women’s BP (systolic and diastolic) when measured at recruitment was elevated taken. Several studies have indicated that pre-eclamptic women had elevated BP [55, 56]. When pre-eclamptic women’s urine samples were examined for proteinuria during recruitment, the dipstick method revealed 1.9 ± 0.7. Our study population’s sudden weight increase, newly diagnosed hypertension, and proteinuria are all consistent with previous research findings [57, 58]. The most frequent cause of high BP and proteinuria in preeclampsia is aberrant lipoprotein levels that damage the endothelium [8].

When the blood pressures of the two groups were evaluated in the current study following births, the diastolic BP significantly improved with LNS supplementation (p = 0.027). Contrary to our findings, a study on Ghanaian women found that daily LNS supplementation had no effect on maternal BP [59] because of their normotensive participants.

The study groups’ lipid profiles and other biochemical characteristics were contrasted. There was no discernible variation in the levels of glucose, insulin, HOMAIR, HDL, Apo A & B, and Lpa. In contrast, the group B showed a significant drop in TC, TGs, LDL & VLDL levels. Contrary to our findings, a randomized controlled trial conducted in Ghana reported that daily lipid-based nutrient supplementation during pregnancy had no significant effect on maternal blood pressure [60]. Similarly, evidence from LNS supplementation trials has shown minimal or no effect on HDL cholesterol, although significant reductions in LDL concentrations have been observed [61].

In the current study, group B BP (diastolic) considerably dropped at delivery, but their BP (systolic) did not change. The lack of difference in BP(systolic and diastolic) between LNS and IFA consumers in a research on underweight pregnant women may have been caused by the normotensive nature of the participants [62].

According to a study, women with a history of preeclampsia had higher levels of TGs, cholesterol, free fatty acids, and LDL and lower levels of HDL [37]. In the current investigation, we found that group B had significantly lower levels of TGs, cholesterol, LDL, and VLDL. A study including non-obese women found that taking a peanut-based supplement during pregnancy reduced triglycerides and cholesterol levels while maintaining an unchanged HDL concentration [63]. Nonetheless, a different study conducted in Ghana found that the lipid profile of normotensive undernourished women was unaffected by LNS supplementation [64].

Six months postnatal, the two groups’ BP (systolic and diastolic) was equated. BP(Systolic and diastolic) was found to be significantly lower in the group B. Pregnant women from Ghana were the participants of studies that found no difference in BP between the LNS and IFA groups; these women were undernourished and normotensive [64, 65].

At the 12-month postnatal follow-up, group B’s maternal cholesterol, LDL, and glucose levels were significantly lower in the current study. However, when comparing the groups’ BP(systolic and diastolic) at 12 months postnatal, there was no discernible change. These results are consistent with a small number of research examining the impact on BP of LNS and IFA users [64, 65] but their study population were underweight normotensive women.

Conclusion

Long-term LNS supplementation in underweight preeclamptic primigravidas was associated with significant reductions in triglyceride, total cholesterol, LDL, and VLDL levels over a 12-month postpartum follow-up period. These findings suggest that LNS supplementation may contribute to a more favorable cardio metabolic risk profile in this population.

Recommendation

Larger, multicenter trials with clinical outcome endpoints are warranted to determine whether these biochemical changes translate into reduced cardio metabolic disease incidence.
