K+-free mica-assisted epitaxy of Bi-based chalcogenide and oxychalcogenide single-crystals
Authors: Tiange Zhao, Xiu Liu, Xun Ge, Shikun Duan, Yuzhuo Bai, Yiye Yu, Hengrui Fu, Hangyu Xu, Li Gao, Jianbin Xu, Martyniuk Piotr, Lingbo Zhang, Zhen Wang, Weida Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A material family that intrinsically hosts topological, high-mobility semiconducting and ferroelectric properties offers a platform for exploring new physical phenomena and advancing next-generation electronics and optoelectronics. Bismuth-based chalcogenides and oxychalcogenides are especially promising in this context due to their compositionally tunable functionalities. However, their development is hindered by the lack of a general, scalable synthesis method, as existing approaches are material-specific and rely on costly, lattice-matched substrates. Here, we report a synergistic epitaxy strategy that pairs elemental precursors with an atomically engineered K+-free mica surface, enabling universal growth of nine millimeter-sized bismuth-based chalcogenide and oxychalcogenide single crystals, including topological insulators, high-mobility semiconductors, and ferroelectrics/dielectrics. High-quality materials enable high-performance transistors and broadband photodetectors with high responsivities. Furthermore, we demonstrate their integration into large-scale, uniform arrays and showcase their imaging capabilities across visible to infrared bands. This work establishes a scalable and material-agnostic synthesis framework, unlocking the systematic exploration and device integration of this multifunctional material family.

Introduction

An emerging material family, including topological, high-mobility semiconducting, and ferroelectric properties that intrinsically coexist, is key for next-generation electronics and optoelectronics and offers a unique platform to explore novel physical phenomena. Within the rich landscape of two-dimensional materials, bismuth (Bi)-based chalcogenides and oxychalcogenides (e.g., Bi2X3, Bi2O2X, and Bi2XO5, X = Se, Te) stand apart by intrinsically combining multiple functionalities within a single, chemically related system1–7. Unlike graphene (zero bandgap)8–10, hexagonal boron nitride (electronically inert)11,12, or transition metal dichalcogenides (whose semiconducting, topological, and ferroelectric properties are scattered across different polymorphs requiring phase engineering)13,14, this family unifies high mobility, robust ferroelectricity, and topological surface states through the distinctive chemistry of bismuth. Examples include high-mobility Bi2O2Se (refs. 2,15), fatigue-resistant ferroelectric Bi2SeO5 (ref. 3), and topological Bi2Se3 with superconducting surface states1. This convergence of disparate properties within one material family establishes Bi-based chalcogenides as a monolithic platform for next-generation integrated electronics and optoelectronics.

Scalable synthesis of high-quality materials is essential for realizing their advanced functional applications. Despite considerable progress in producing individual Bi-based compounds, current synthesis methods are constrained by two principal shortcomings. Firstly, a universal synthetic framework is absent. Preparation remains reliant on compound-specific protocols. For instance, Bi2Se3 is typically grown from its stoichiometric precursor16, whereas Bi2O2Se requires reactive combinations such as Bi2Se3 + Bi2O3 (ref. 15), Se + Bi2O3 (ref. 17), Bi2Se3 + O2 (ref. 15), or Bi2O2Se (ref. 18), and Bi2SeO5 is derived from the oxidation of Bi2O2Se (refs. 19–21). Secondly, scalable epitaxy generally depends on specific and lattice-matched single-crystal substrates. In recent years, Peng’s group has reported landmark breakthroughs in this direction, first achieving wafer-scale epitaxy of Bi2O2Se on lattice-matched SrTiO3 (refs. 3,22,23), and subsequently extending this success to inexpensive sapphire21. Yet, each of these substrates is optimally suited for only one or two compounds from this family. Consequently, developing a universal synthesis platform that accommodates multiple distinct phases on a single substrate remains an urgent challenge.

To address these challenges, we developed a synthesis strategy centered on the kinetic control of chemical vapor deposition (CVD), targeting the two sequential processes that govern thin‑film growth: mass transport of precursors and surface kinetic reactions24–26. First, we engineered the substrate interface to promote favorable surface kinetics. We selected the common layered fluorophlogopite mica as the substrate27,28. Through pre-treatment, we removed the randomly distributed K+ ions on freshly cleaved surface24,29,30, thereby creating a truly atomically smooth template that enhances adatom mobility and facilitates large-domain growth31. Subsequently, we combined the elemental precursors with controlled oxygen to achieve a tunable and stable vapor-phase supply. This integrated approach, which simultaneously addresses precursor kinetics and substrate engineering, enables the general synthesis of high-quality, large-area crystals.

Using this method, we successfully synthesize nine types of (sub)millimeter-sized Bi-based chalcogenides and oxychalcogenides, including topological insulators (Bi2Se3, Bi2Te3, Bi2SexTe3-x), high-mobility semiconductors (Bi2O2Se, Bi2O2Te, Bi2O2SexTe1-x), a polar semiconductor (Bi3O2.5Se2), and ferroelectric/dielectric materials (Bi2SeO5, Bi2TeO5), in which Bi2SeO5 was derived through subsequent in-situ oxidation of Bi2O2Se. The high crystallinity and phase purity of the synthesized materials are confirmed through comprehensive spectroscopic, microscopic, and elemental analysis. Furthermore, we constructed high-performance field effect transistors and broadband photodetectors to showcase their electric and optoelectronic potential. Based on the large-size, high-quality single crystals, we further fabricated an 8 × 8 photodetector array, which demonstrates good photodetection and imaging capabilities. This work establishes a versatile and scalable synthesis route, providing a critical material foundation for the integration of bismuth-based compounds into advanced optoelectronic systems.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of Bi-based crystals

The growth of high-quality, large-area Bi-based crystals begins with understanding the substrate surface. Fluorophlogopite mica, a common layered substrate, is known for its atomically flat appearance after cleavage. However, freshly cleaved mica does not present a “truly” atomically smooth interface; instead, K+ ions, weakly bound between adjacent layers27,32,33, remain randomly distributed, creating short-range ordered corrugations28 (Supplementary Fig. 1). These K+ ions act as diffusion barriers (schematics in Fig. 1a, b), limiting the migration distance of adatoms and consequently restricting crystal domain size. Diffusion barrier calculations using DFT confirm that the K+-free mica surface exhibits a substantially lower energy barrier for adatom migration (Fig. 1c, d for calculated energy profiles; Supplementary Fig. 2 for the corresponding atomistic diffusion paths). To overcome this limitation, we removed surface K+ ions by a mild pre-annealing step (see Methods for more details). This simple step produces an atomically smooth, chemically uniform surface that promotes enhanced adatom diffusion and enables the growth of large-area crystals (Fig. 1b). Atomic force microscope (AFM) measurements confirm that the annealed surface remains atomically smooth, with an RMS roughness of 144.4 pm, comparable to that of pristine mica (140.6 pm, Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, we employed angle-resolved X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (ARXPS) at emission angles of 0° (bulk-sensitive) and 70° (surface-sensitive) to compare the surface chemistry of freshly cleaved and annealed mica. The results confirm that the annealing step removes K+ nearly completely from the topmost surface, thereby creating the atomically smooth template essential for large-domain epitaxy (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1). With an optimized substrate in hand, we turned to precursor design. To achieve both thermodynamic uniformity and kinetic control, we employed elemental bismuth and selenium as precursors34. Using this approach, we systematically investigated the temperature-dependent morphological evolution of three representative compounds: the topological insulator Bi2Se3, the polar semiconductor Bi3O2.5Se2, and the high-mobility semiconductor Bi2O2Se (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). In all cases, crystal size initially increases and then decreases with rising temperature, while thickness gradually grows. This trend can be understood by considering the three key kinetic processes that govern lateral growth: edge adsorption of precursors, surface diffusion on the growing crystal facets, and surface diffusion on the substrate. At low temperatures, edge adsorption dominates while surface diffusion is limited, resulting in small and thin crystals. As the temperature increases, both surface diffusion on the crystal facets and on the substrate are thermally activated, enhancing the supply of adatoms to the growth front and promoting lateral expansion. Consequently, both crystal size and thickness increase. When the temperature exceeds the optimal value, adatom desorption becomes significant and competes with surface diffusion, progressively reducing the effective adatom concentration available for incorporation. This leads to a decrease in lateral crystal size while the thickness continues to increase monotonically owing to the sustained vertical growth component. The optimal temperature thus represents the kinetic balance point between surface-diffusion-enhanced lateral growth and desorption-limited adatom supply35,36. At an optimized temperature, these processes reach an optimal synergy, yielding maximum lateral sizes. Under these circumstances, we successfully synthesized 1 mm Bi2Se3, 1.1 mm Bi3O2.5Se2, and 1.2 mm Bi2O2Se (Fig. 1e–g).

The phase purity and crystallographic uniformity of the as-grown crystals were confirmed by multiple complementary techniques. Optical band gaps extracted from spectroscopic measurements agree well with reported values15,37,38 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Raman spectroscopy characterization across ten different regions of each mm-scale crystal revealed peak position variations of less than 1.3 cm−1 for Bi2Se3, 1.1 cm−1 for Bi3O2.5Se2, and 0.4 cm−1 for Bi2O2Se, indicating high spatial homogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 9). XPS quantification yields atomic ratios consistent with the nominal stoichiometries for all three representative compounds (Supplementary Figs. 10–12). Together, these results confirm the high crystalline quality and good uniformity of the mm-scale Bi-based single crystals grown by our method.

To demonstrate the synthetic controllability of our method, we investigated the product distribution as a function of both growth temperature and oxygen concentration (Supplementary Fig. 13). Holding temperature constant, varying the O2 concentration in the CVD atmosphere allowed us to selectively tune the predominant product phase, shifting from Bi2Se3 to Bi3O2.5Se2, then to planar Bi2O2Se, with vertically oriented Bi2O2Se becoming dominant at 0.2% O2. This is consistent with the pattern reported in previous reports34,39–41. Notably, these three compounds are each synthesized directly under independent O2 concentration regimes, rather than through sequential oxidation of a single precursor. Conversely, at a fixed O2 concentration, the lateral crystal size exhibited a non-monotonic dependence on temperature, initially increasing before decreasing, consistent with an optimized kinetic window (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). Figure 1h shows the controllable synthesis design of Bi-based compounds with different band gaps via temperature and O2 concentration regulation. The use of H2/Ar (schematically represented as the negative region on the horizontal axis in Fig. 1h) provides a reducing atmosphere that enables nucleation of phase-pure chalcogenides without competing oxide formation.

The versatility of our approach extends beyond these three representatives. Unlike established two-dimensional families such as molybdenum-sulfur (Mo–S)13,14, graphene (Gra.)8, boron-nitrogen (B–N)11,12 and indium-selenium (In–Se)42 systems, the bismuth‑oxygen‑selenium (Bi–O–Se) family uniquely integrates multiple functionalities, including semiconducting (Semi.), ferroelectric (FE), dielectric (Dielec.), topological (Topo.), and metallic (Met.) behaviours (Fig. 1i). Moreover, the nine distinct bismuth‑based chalcogenide and oxychalcogenide single crystals synthesized in this work achieve lateral dimensions significantly larger than those previously reported, representing a substantial advance in domain size control (Fig. 1j, Supplementary Figs. 30–35, and Supplementary Table 2)18,19,37,43–48. The lateral dimensions reported here are compared at comparable thickness ranges (see Supplementary Table 2 for detailed data), confirming that the size enhancement originates from the K+-free surface engineering rather than from differences in crystal thickness. Therefore, the novelty of this work lies not in using elemental precursors per se, but in the synergistic integration of a universal precursor design with an atomically engineered substrate surface. This combination—elemental Bi and chalcogen sources, plus K+-free mica—enables, the selective synthesis of nine distinct Bi-based phases spanning topological insulators, high-mobility semiconductors, and ferroelectrics on a single, readily available substrate platform. We note that wafer-scale epitaxy of Bi2O2Se on sapphire represents an important advance in this field21. The application has so far been centered on specific compounds within the bismuth oxychalcogenide family. The present method builds on these advances, yielding all nine phases on a single K+-free mica substrate, demonstrating a materials generality that, to our knowledge, has not been achieved previously. Together, the combination of broad functional diversity and large single‑crystal domains provides a robust material foundation for next‑generation integrated electronics and optoelectronics.

The critical role of substrate engineering was further validated by comparative growth experiments. Across five independent trials, crystals grown on K+-free mica consistently exhibited lateral sizes approximately an order of magnitude larger than those grown on pristine mica (Fig. 2 a, b and Supplementary Fig. 14). To directly probe the origin of this enhancement, we examined the atomic-scale interface between epitaxially grown Bi-based single crystals and the mica substrate, with and without K+ removal (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 15). Cross-sectional high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images reveal that K+ ions remain present at the interface on pristine mica (Fig. 2c), where the spacing between the top K+ layer of mica and the bottom Bi atomic layer of the crystal is only ~0.16 nm, which is notably smaller than the radius of a K atom (0.243 nm) and close to that of a Bi atom (0.143 nm)49. This close proximity likely constrains adatom diffusion and limits lateral growth. In contrast, on K+-depleted mica, no K+ ions are observed at the interface (Fig. 2d), and the separation widens to ~0.35 nm, which is larger than the radii of Bi (0.143 nm), Al (0.118 nm), Si (0.111 nm), and O (0.048 nm) atoms49. It is plausible that on pristine mica the epitaxial layer conforms to the heterogeneous K+ distribution, with locally varying interlayer spacing. Removal of K+ eliminates this inhomogeneity, providing a uniform growth template. In Fig. 2c, distinct bright spots corresponding to K+ ions are clearly visible at the interface (highlighted by red dotted circles), while the white dotted circles mark the aluminosilicate background. In striking contrast, only the aluminosilicate lattice is observed at the interface in Fig. 2d, with no detectable K+ bright contrast. The red dotted lines in Figs. 2c and 2d indicate the expected positions of the K+ ion layer: whereas a clear K signal is present along this line in Fig. 2c, it is entirely absent in Fig. 2d. The intensity line profiles taken from the yellow dashed boxes in Fig. 2c, d further corroborate this observation, showing a pronounced K signal at the interface in Fig. 2c and a flat baseline in Fig. 2d. Together, these results confirm that K+ ions have been effectively removed from the annealed mica surface. The expanded interface provides greater freedom for precursor migration and facilitates lateral expansion. Corresponding atomic models are shown in Fig. 2f, g. Electronic transport and broadband detection

To evaluate the fundamental electrical and optoelectronic performance of the synthesized bismuth-based single crystals, we fabricated field-effect transistors (FETs) based on these materials. The output and transfer characteristic curves are presented in Supplementary Figs. 16–18. For Bi2Se3 FETs, the output source-drain current (Ids) shows a linear dependence on the bias voltage (Vds), indicating satisfactory contacts (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Notably, the field-effect mobility increases with flake thickness. This trend can be attributed to the reduced relative contribution of topological surface states to the total conductance in thinner samples, where surface scattering and disorder may dominate the transport50. In thicker samples, higher-quality bulk conduction becomes more significant. Bi2O2Se FETs also exhibit good contact characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 17). As the channel thickness increases, we observe a concomitant rise in mobility but a gradual decrease in the current on/off ratio. This inverse relationship is typical for layered semiconductors: enhanced bulk conduction in thicker channels improves mobility, while the diminished gate-field coupling leads to poorer electrostatic control, resulting in higher off-state current and a reduced on/off ratio51. Owing to the high crystal quality, a 3-nm Bi2O2Se FET demonstrates good electrical performance, with a mobility of 121 cm2 V−1 s−1 and an on/off ratio of 106. Interestingly, a pronounced electrical hysteresis is observed in output and transfer curves of Bi3O2.5Se2 FETs (Supplementary Fig. 18). This hysteresis, with a window reaching 26.25 V (comparable to that of α-In2Se3)52, likely originates from charge trapping at the interface or within the gate dielectric, possibly related to the polar semiconductor nature of Bi3O2.5Se2 arising from internal charge transfer between its sublattices37,53.

Building on their favorable electronic properties and tunable bandgaps, these materials are compelling for broadband photodetection. Devices fabricated from the crystals achieve ultrabroadband operation from the visible to terahertz (THz) regimes (Fig. 4a). Leveraging the Dirac fermions in its topological surface states54,55, Bi2Se3 exhibits strong THz detection capabilities (Fig. 4b–d). The device generates photocurrents on the order of hundreds of nanoamperes in the 0.02–0.04 THz and 0.08-0.1 THz bands, with a fast response time of 7.4/7.6 μs. It also maintains a strong photoresponse across the visible to short-wave infrared spectrum (520 nm–2 μm). Integrated arrays and imaging applications

To demonstrate the potential of the synthesized bismuth-based single crystals for integrated optoelectronics, we fabricated large-scale, ordered arrays. Figure 5a presents a uniform, periodic array of isolated two-dimensional Bi2O2Se squares. An enlarged view of a 4 × 4 sub-array confirms good morphological uniformity (Fig. 5b), which is further corroborated by homogeneous Raman mapping and surface morphology, indicating high crystallographic consistency across the entire array (Fig. 5c). Similarly, we fabricated highly ordered arrays of triangular Bi2Se3 flakes and hexagonal Bi3O2.5Se2 flakes, both of which exhibit good crystallinity and spatial uniformity (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 28). Capitalizing on this high-quality material platform, we constructed a functional 8 × 8 Bi2O2Se photodetector array (Fig. 5h; see “Methods” and Supplementary Fig. 29 for fabrication details). The array demonstrates good device-to-device uniformity, as evidenced by the tightly clustered dark currents of all 64 pixels (Fig. 5i). Under 1550 nm illumination, the photocurrent response across the array is high consistent, highlighting its reliability for imaging applications (Fig. 5j). Finally, we leveraged the high-performance single-pixel detectors to demonstrate broadband imaging capabilities. High-fidelity image reconstruction was achieved across the visible, near-infrared, and short-wave infrared (communication band) spectral ranges (Fig. 5k–m). The clear delineation of the target pattern in all bands confirms the promising imaging potential of our Bi-based photodetectors. Universality of the synthesis method for Bi-based single crystals

The designed synthesis strategy exhibits broad applicability across a spectrum of Bi-based chalcogenides and oxychalcogenides (e.g., Bi2X3, Bi2O2X, and Bi2XO5, X = Se, Te). By simply substituting the Se source with Te or using a mixture of both, we successfully synthesized large-area, high-quality topological insulators Bi2Te3 and the ternary alloy Bi2SexTe3-x (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). Atomic-resolution STEM analysis confirms the ordered arrangement of Se and Te atoms within the alloyed crystal lattice (Supplementary Fig. 31b, c). Extending this control, the introduction of precise oxygen flow during growth enables the synthesis of high-mobility semiconductors Bi2O2Te and Bi2O2SexTe1-x, as well as the ferroelectric material Bi2TeO5. The high crystallinity and phase purity of all obtained compounds are consistently verified by their sharp Raman spectra and uniform elemental distribution in EDS mapping. Notably, for the ferroelectric/dielectric Bi2SeO5, we developed an in-situ oxidation protocol. When the process of synthesizing Bi2O2Se is cooled down to 350°C, increasing the O2 flow rate directly converts the precursor into large, high-quality Bi2SeO5 single crystals. These results collectively demonstrate the versatility and generalizability of our kinetic-controlled synthesis approach for fabricating a diverse library of bismuth-based single crystals.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a universal chemical vapor deposition strategy for the synthesis of nine distinct millimeter-scale Bi-based single crystals, encompassing topological insulators (Bi2Se3, Bi2Te3, Bi2SexTe3-x), high-mobility semiconductors (Bi2O2Se, Bi2O2Te, Bi2O2SexTe1-x), a polar semiconductor (Bi3O2.5Se2), ferroelectric/dielectric materials (Bi2SeO5, Bi2TeO5), in which Bi2SeO5 was derived through subsequent in-situ oxidation of Bi2O2Se. The success of this approach hinges on two key design principles: using elemental precursors for precise stoichiometric control, and engineering an atomically smooth substrate surface to minimize diffusion barriers. Comprehensive characterization confirms the high crystallinity, phase purity, and good uniformity of all synthesized materials. Field-effect transistors and photodetectors fabricated from these crystals exhibit good electrical and broadband photoresponse properties. The successful demonstration of large-scale, uniform arrays and their imaging capabilities underscores the strong potential of these materials for integrated optoelectronics. This work provides a versatile and scalable materials platform, paving the way for the exploration and application of Bi-based compounds in next-generation electronic and photonic devices.

Methods

Synthesis

Synthesis recipe

The 2D compounds were synthesized in a quartz tube (25 mm diameter, 1000 mm length). Freshly exfoliated fluorophlogopite mica (KMg3(AlSi3O10)F2) substrates (10 × 10 mm) were first annealed in air at 200 °C for 10 min, and then positioned at the heating center of the tube furnace. Alumina boats containing Bi and Se (or Te, and mixed Se/Te) were placed upstream of the tube furnace, maintained at temperatures of ~270–300 °C for Bi and 230–250 °C for Se, or 450–500 °C for Te or Se/Te, respectively. Prior to growth, the tube was purged with high-purity Ar at a flow rate of 500 sccm for 10 min to eliminate residual oxygen and moisture. The furnace was heated at a ramping rate of 30 °C min−1, and all reactions were carried out under atmospheric pressure. The growth duration for Bi-based compounds was 5-40 min. After growth, the furnace was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. Detailed process parameters are shown in Supplementary Table 4.

Bi2Se3

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and 250 mg Se) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. Bi pellets were chosen over powder to provide a stable, moderate evaporation flux throughout the growth duration, minimizing flux transients that could lead to non-uniform nucleation67,68. The growth temperature was set to 450–650 °C, with the Bi and Se source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 230–250 °C, respectively. A 5% H2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 50 sccm to provide a reducing atmosphere that suppresses premature oxidation of precursors and promotes the formation of phase-pure chalcogenides.

Bi2Te3

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and 250 mg Te) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–650 °C, with the Bi and Se source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 450–500 °C, respectively. A 5% H2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 50 sccm.

Bi2SexTe3-x

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and a mixed powder of 100 mg of Se and 200 mg of Te) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–650 °C, with the Bi and Se source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 450–500 °C, respectively. A 5% H2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 50 sccm.

Bi3O2.5Se2

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and 250 mg Se) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–700 °C, with the Bi and Se source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 230–250 °C, respectively. A 0.01–0.02% O2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 100 sccm.

Bi2O2Se

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and 250 mg Se) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–700 °C, with the Bi and Se source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 230–250 °C, respectively. A 0.05–0.2% O2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 100 sccm.

Bi2O2Te

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and 250 mg Te) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–700 °C, with the Bi and Te source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 450–500 °C, respectively. A 0.05–0.2% O2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 100 sccm.

Bi2O2SexTe1-x

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and a mixed powder of 100 mg of Se and 100 mg of Te) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–700 °C, with the Bi and Se source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 450–500 °C, respectively. A 0.05–0.2% O2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 100 sccm.

Bi2TeO5

The precursors (3 Bi pellets and 250 mg Te) were placed in alumina boats in the upstream region of the tube, while the mica substrate was positioned at the furnace center. The growth temperature was set to 450–700 °C, with the Bi and Te source zones maintained at 270–300 °C and 450–500 °C, respectively. A 0.5% O2/Ar mixture was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 100 sccm.

Bi2SeO5

Bi2SeO5 was obtained through a post-growth transformation of the as-grown Bi2O2Se. After the Bi2O2Se growth process, the furnace temperature was decreased to 350 °C and maintained for 30–40 min under 0.5% O2/Ar at a flow rate of 100 sccm. Subsequently, the furnace was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature, yielding Bi2SeO5.

Materials characterization

The morphology of the synthesized samples was examined by optical microscopy (Olympus BX51). Raman spectroscopy was conducted on a Renishaw inVia Qontor system using a 633 nm excitation laser. The surface morphology was measured by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Oxford MFP-3D). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on a ULVAC-PHI PHI5000 VersaprobeIII system to determine the chemical states. Atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) imaging was carried out using a spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (Thermo Scientific Themis Z).

Detectors fabrication and measurements

Photodetector fabrication

Photodetectors based on bismuth-based single crystals were fabricated on 285 nm SiO2/Si substrates. The grown Bi-based single crystals were transferred from mica onto SiO2/Si substrates using a polystyrene (PS)-assisted transfer method. First, a 5 wt% PS solution in toluene was prepared. The mica substrate with as-grown crystals was then spin-coated with the PS solution at 2000 rpm for 30 s and baked at 90 °C for 10 min. The PS/crystal/mica stack was subsequently immersed in deionized water at 40 °C for 5 min, allowing the PS/crystal film to detach and float on the water surface. The floating film was then collected onto a SiO2/Si substrate. Finally, the sample was baked at 90 °C for 10 min, and the PS layer was removed by dissolving in toluene at 40 °C. Electrode patterns were then defined by electron-beam lithography, followed by thermal evaporation of Cr/Au (15/45 nm) contacts. The devices were completed after a standard lift-off process.

Phototransistor array fabrication

An 8 × 8 photodetector array was fabricated directly on a fluorophlogopite mica substrate. A Bi2O2Se film was first patterned into an array via laser direct writing and subsequent inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etching. Electrode patterns were then written by laser lithography, and Cr/Au (15/45 nm) contacts were deposited by thermal evaporation. A ~ 100 nm Al2O3 insulating layer was grown by magnetron sputtering, followed by the fabrication of second electrodes to complete the array.

Measurements

The optoelectronic performance was characterized by measuring the photocurrent under laser illumination using a Keithley 2636B semiconductor parameter analyzer. The temporal response (rise/fall time) of the photodetectors was recorded with a low-noise current preamplifier (Stanford Research Systems SR570) connected to a mixed-domain oscilloscope (Tektronix MDO3014). The noise spectrum was analyzed using a Keysight N9010B EXA signal analyzer in conjunction with the SR570 preamplifier. For measurements in the terahertz and microwave regimes, the incident radiation from a VDI 175 T source and an Agilent E8257D signal generator, respectively, was modulated at 1 kHz using a square-wave signal.

DFT calculation

First-principles calculations were performed using the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional69 as realized in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)70,71. The electron-ion interactions were described by the projector augmented wave (PAW)72 method. To ensure accuracy, a plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV was employed. Both atomic positions and lattice parameters for mica were fully relaxed until the total energy and interatomic forces converged to below 10−5 eV and 0.01 eV/Å, respectively. The first Brillouin zone of mica was sampled using a k-mesh density of 2π × 0.04 Å−1 for structural relaxations. To investigate the adsorption behavior of Bi and Se atoms on the mica surface, the DFT-D3 corrections73 were incorporated to account for van der Waals interactions. A vacuum layer of 15 Å was applied along the z axis to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images. In addition, the diffusion barriers of Bi and Se atoms on the mica surface were determined using the climbing nudged elastic band (NEB) method74.
