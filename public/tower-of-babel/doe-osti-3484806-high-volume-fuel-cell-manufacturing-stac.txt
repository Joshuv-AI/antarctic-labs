 Summary of Research Results:

 Plug Power Inc (Plug) contracted with DOE on DE EE0011343 to demonstrate a production
 pathway to meet the projected system cost of $80/kW for 100,000 heavy duty systems per year.
 To meet these objectives, the team proposed to manufacture a high-performance membrane
 electrode assembly (MEA) that meets the program goals of 2.5 kW/gPGM power at 1.07
 A cm-2 and 0.7 V after 25,000 hours of operation. NLR carried out research toward minimizing
 scrap and waste produced by an MEA coating line by modeling coating processes. NLR also
 developed processes to accelerate and automate MEA quality inspection.

 2

 Task 1: Coating Process Simulation and Development

 The objective was The Contractor and the Participant will develop models to evaluate ink flow
 based on alternative ink formulations. They will investigate how to increase coating speed
 through process simulations and associated experimental efforts for both gas diffusion electrodes
 (GDEs) and catalyst coated membranes (CCMs). Models will be based on physics-based finite
 element modeling to simulate R2R coating flows on desired line speeds, fluid properties, liquid
 film thicknesses, and environmental room controls. Small scale experimental studies will be
 conducted on both parties prototyping lines to validate model robustness. These studies will be
 used to predict stable process conditions for R2R membrane electrode assembly (MEA)
 manufacturing. Larger scale demonstrations will be carried out at Plug and compared to the
 results of the small-scale experiments. Simulations and experiments will be used to understand
 process sensitivities to variations in process conditions and fluid properties to enable the
 development of a robust coating process. The studies will provide guidance for quality assurance
 specifications for Plug’s manufacturing processes. Additional studies will be conducted to
 investigate the impacts of ink formulation, coating process, and drying process on catalyst layer
 roughness and uniformity and resulting influence on MEA performance. Ink formulation studies
 will also explore the incorporation of novel materials (ionomer and/or catalysts) with the
 objective to develop formulations suitable for R2R processing and performance high-
 performance catalyst layers.

 Task 1 Results:

 NLR utilized computational fluid dynamic (CFD) simulations to investigate the impact of
 catalyst ink rheology and coating process conditions on the uniformity of coated catalyst layer
 films. The objective was to determine process conditions capable of producing uniform and
 defect-free coatings at the above-mentioned cost and production volume targets.

 Rheology Modeling

 The steady shear rheology for five different catalyst ink formulations prepared and measured by
 Plug were modeled by NLR for use in coating simulations. The experimental flow curves and
 corresponding model fits are shown in Figure 1. The Type 3 fluid can largely be described as
 Newtonian, where the shear viscosity 𝜂 is constant with shear rate 𝛾̇ . The remaining flow curves
 were fitted with the Carreau-Yasuda model1 for shear thinning fluids,

 Numerical simulations of the flow inside die channels

 This study investigated how different ink rheologies affect flow in the slot die channel, where a
 uniform velocity profile indicates uniform crossweb flow. To accomplish this, single-phase CFD
 simulations were performed. This part of the study involved simulating flow in the slot die
 channel alone, without modeling ink deposition on the substrate. Three channel thicknesses—75
 μm, 125 μm, and 250 μm—were tested. Single-phase flow simulations were performed using the
 commercial finite-volume CFD solver ANSYS Fluent 2023 R1. Steady-state simulations
 employed a pressure-based, segregated solver on 104 cores of the Kestrel HPC system. CFD
 results showed a parabolic velocity-magnitude profile along the exit channel height for all ink
 rheologies in both the 75- and 125-μm channel-height cases. A slightly different velocity profile
 was observed for the 250-μm case, with the profiles becoming flatter at the channel center. The
 viscosity profiles also exhibited a sharp peak near the channel centerline, due to low strain rates
 at the center. Figure 2 displays the average velocity profiles across the web width for all the
 simulated inks. The results show minimal variation among Type-2, 3, and 4 fluids compared to
 Type-1 and 5 fluids. Velocity variations tend to increase with growing channel thickness.
 Nonetheless, it should be noted that the magnitude of these variations is on the order of 1e-4 and
 1e-3 m/s for the 75 and 125-μm channels, respectively. High-frequency oscillations are also
 observed at 250-μm thickness, which could indicate (although further studies would be needed to
 confirm this) instabilities at higher channel widths.

 Numerical simulations of the coating bead

 Building on previous results of uniform flow profiles, two-dimensional multiphase simulations
 were conducted to analyze coating-bead formation and potential flow instabilities on the moving
 substrate. A 125-μm slot die channel and a 125-μm die-web gap were selected for the initial
 study using the Type-4 fluid. A total of 18 simulations were performed to study the effect of
 various flow parameters such as surface tension (0.025 N/m and 0.035 N/m), contact angles (20°,
    40°, and 60°), upstream pressure (-250 Pa and -500 Pa), and web speeds (0.3 m/s, 0.1 m/s, and
 0.05 m/s). The Volume of Fluid (VoF) model in ANSYS Fluent 2023R1 was used to simulate the
 multiphase flow. Implicit time stepping with larger time steps was employed to simulate
 unsteady flow features. The die channel exit velocity profile from the previously mentioned die-
 only simulations was used to specify the inflow boundary condition at the die exit. Unsteady
 CFD simulations were performed using 104 computing cores on the Kestrel HPC machine at
 NLR. NLR was not able to validate these modeling results using its coating equipment or
 through coating experiments at Plug due to the project’s cancellation.

 Uniformity of coated electrode

 Fig. 3. Representative XRF map showing higher Pt loadings on the edges of the coating than in
 the center.

 Plug coated the Type 4 ink formulation using roll-to-roll coating. NLR assessed coating
 uniformity using X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF) and scanning electron microscopy
 (SEM). Using XRF, Pt loading maps were generated across the entire cross-web direction at
 three different regions in the machine direction of the sample. Generally, the XRF maps showed
 that the Pt loadings tend to increase towards the edges (in the cross-web direction) of the coating,
 but the loadings exhibited a relatively small range of variation of less than 10% of the average
 loading (Figure 3). Next, SEM was used to validate catalyst layer thickness in the cross-web
 direction (Table 1). Back-scattered electron-mode (BSE) SEM was required to distinguish the Pt-
 laden catalyst layer from the polymer-laden microporous layer. Cross sections were obtained
 from the center and edges of the coating. BSE SEM micrographs showed that the catalyst layer
 thickness was consistent in the cross-web direction, and measurements from each section were
 largely within error of each other. Thus, XRF and SEM measurements show that the coated
 catalyst layer was overall uniform.

 Ink Formulation with Novel Materials

 NLR explored collaborative opportunities with pH Matter and UCLA to benchmark their
 proprietary materials and assess their suitability for a roll-to-roll coating process. Using catalyst
 samples received from pH Matter, NLR conducted initial investigations that validated processing
 parameters. These preliminary results demonstrated the feasibility of electrode fabrication at the
 roll-to-roll scale using bench-scale methods.

 Discussions with UCLA focused on approaches for baselining and evaluating the compatibility
 of their material systems with targeted coating speeds and electrode uniformity specifications.
 These technical exchanges provided valuable insights into materials integration strategies for
 scalable electrode architectures.

 Additional work to further develop these catalyst ink formulations was not conducted due to the
 project’s cancellation. Similarly, NLR did not investigate any novel ionomers because of the
 project’s cancellation.

 Task 2: On-web Defect Detection

 On-web inspection, or defect detection of the material while still on the roll or web, before it is
 manufactured into a discrete part, is a critical component of the MEA manufacturing process to
 ensure high final yield of the product while minimizing the commitment of high-dollar raw
 materials to a faulty product. The Contractor and the Participant will collaborate to test and
 validate high-volume, in-line characterization techniques available either in the laboratory or on
 the market, with the goal of adopting them to advance the Participant’s MEA components.
 Methods may include high-resolution optical imaging, spectroscopy, hyperspectral imaging, and
 thermography with various excitation sources tailored to specific defect detection requirements.
 The quality-control teams from the Contractor and the Participant will collaborate to evaluate
 methods for inspection of electrode and substrate layers to detect non-uniformities and to inspect
 topography of surface interfacing a membrane. The comparison and assessment of various
 quality control methods will be performed through analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Gauge
 Repeatability and Reproducibility (Gauge R&R) study. Depending on the need, the Contractor
 and the Participant may also work to determine the impact of defects, to improve measurement
 rates to match desired production cycle time, to develop 100% area mapping capability when
 only single point measurement is currently available, and on quality monitoring of MEA
 cassettes and stacks. It is expected that implementation of such methods as Six Sigma, big data,
 artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) methodologies will lead to statistically
 significant improvements and a deep understanding of the Participant’s fabrication process.

 Task 2 Results:

 Task 2 targets high-yield, high-throughput inspection of cell materials prior to MEA assembly by
 validating and integrating on-web methods that detect surface/topography defects and thickness
 variation, and by establishing measurement system capability to support a target Overall
 Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) of 95%. Work focused on method validation (off-line and in-
 line), quantitative characterization of defect and topography features, speed–resolution tradeoffs,
 and hardware design to stabilize in-line metrology.

 Quality monitoring of catalyst layer sheet

 NLR assessed the surface condition and topography of a Plug Power–fabricated catalyst layer
 sheet using three complementary approaches: optical reflectance imaging, structured-light
 profilometry, and high‑magnification optical microscopy. The 8 in × 13 in specimen was first
 scanned in reflectance mode at 1200 dpi with an Epson 12000XL, acquiring images at both 0°
 and 180° orientations without automatic filters, image enhancement, or color management.
 Post‑processing in ImageJ improved visualization of surface texture (see Figure 4).

 Fig. 4. Reflectance imaging of the catalyst layer at 0° and 180° orientations (1200 dpi); the
 observed white streak aligns with a scanner artifact rather than an electrode defect. Post-
 processed ImageJ enhancements improve visibility of surface texture.

 For quantitative topography, the catalyst layer was mapped by the Keyence VR‑6200
 structured‑light profilometer at 50× resolution. Because of the sample size, the sheet was divided
 into four quadrants with four scans per quadrant (for a total of 16 runs), and each run took
 approximately 30 minutes. A surface shape correction was applied to remove the natural
 curvature of the free‑standing specimen. In the first half of the sheet (runs 1–8), the highest point
 reached 21 µm above the reference plane (Figure 5, top). Runs 9–12 exhibited a characteristic
 trench spanning the field, with the highest point recorded at 24 µm (Figure 5, middle). The
 region about 10 mm from the coating edge (runs 13–16) showed the most pronounced long‑range

 morphology: a ridge adjacent to a trench, with relief up to 43 µm between the ridge peak and the
 trench bottom (Figure 5, bottom). Cross‑sectional profiles through this area indicated height
 differences of 29–43 µm and trench widths of 11.5–16.6 mm, confirming a large‑scale feature
 near the coating edge that is more prominent than elsewhere on the sheet.

 Fig. 5. After surface shape correction, runs 1–8 (top) show peak height of 21 µm; runs 9–12
 (middle) reveal a characteristic trench with a 24-µm peak; runs 13–16 (bottom) near the coating

 High‑magnification optical microscopy with a Keyence VHX‑7000 at 1000× was performed to
 validate the profilometry findings locally. A 1 mm × 1 mm region near the coating edge
 (profilometry run 16) was mapped and stitched to extract precise height profiles across the ridge–
 trench feature. A representative measurement (see Figure 6) showed 40.8 µm between the trench
 bottom and the ridge peak. Across multiple profiles, ridge height above the reference plane
 ranged from 19 to 26 µm, while ridge‑to‑trench relief ranged from 39 to 41 µm. These values
 agree well with the structured‑light profilometry, corroborating both the presence and magnitude
 of the long‑range ridge–trench morphology identified near the coating edge.

 Fig. 6. High-magnification optical microscopy (1000×) near the coating edge confirms ridge–
 trench morphology. Measured reliefs of approximately 40.8 µm (example) and ranges of 39–41 µm
 are consistent with profilometry; ridge heights above the reference plane are 19–26 µm.

 Together, these experiments provide a robust, cross‑validated baseline of the Plug‑fabricated
 catalyst layer’s surface condition. Reflectance imaging confirmed the absence of obvious
 intrinsic defects while highlighting and dismissing a scanner artifact. Structured‑light
 profilometry mapped the global topography and revealed modest curvature and a pronounced
 ridge–trench feature localized near the coating edge. High‑magnification microscopy
 independently verified the relief amplitudes at the microscale. The observed topographical
 variations—up to approximately 43 µm relief with trench widths of approximately 12–17 mm—
 may influence downstream coating uniformity, interfacial contact, or fluid transport, and they
 offer actionable metrics for fabrication optimization and quality assurance.

 Quality Monitoring of Microporous Surface for Rolls of GDL Materials

 The report documents a comprehensive set of achievements toward reliable gas diffusion layer
 (GDL) surface inspection spanning off-line imaging, large-area metrology, in-line compatible
 sensing, and enabling web-line hardware. The team began by visually inspecting long GDL rolls
 with a high‑powered LED to locate protrusions, then excised representative regions for detailed
 analysis. A professional flatbed scanner (Epson 12000XL) was used in reflectance mode at 1200
 dpi to quickly catalog surface features without automatic filters or color management.
 Post‑processing in ImageJ enhanced contrast and brightness to aid defect visualization, enabling
 side‑by‑side comparison of sections from two rolls and clear localization of protrusions for
 follow‑on metrology (Figure 7).

 10

 Fig. 7. Characterization of selected defects identified on the surface of GDL from two different
 rolls.

 High‑magnification optical microscopy with a Keyence VHX‑7000 at 1000× resolved the local
 morphology and topography of the protrusions, distinguishing large features with micro‑lumps
 from smoother features of comparable height. The microscopy established accurate local height
 and shape benchmarks, including a representative protrusion approximately 40-µm tall with two
 micro‑lumps and corresponding line profiles drawn through the highest points (Figure 4), as well
 as examples without micro‑lumps that exhibit simpler geometry. To bridge from microscale
 detail to mesoscale coverage, the team deployed structured‑light profilometry (Keyence
 VR‑6200) to map areas on the order of 100 cm² at 25× resolution in about 10 minutes per
 sample, applying a surface shape correction to remove natural curvature, then using height
 thresholding for automatic protrusion detection. This workflow quantified defects identified by
 scanning, with example heights of 38 µm and 124 µm for two prominent protrusions.
 Comparative analysis indicated that the first roll primarily exhibited protrusions under 40 µm,
 whereas the second had more frequent and larger features, with the largest reaching 124 µm
 (Figure 8, Figure 9).

 Fig. 8. Detail morphology and shape analysis for a large size protrusion with two micro-lumps on
 a GDL. The line profile at the bottom passes through two highest points on the protrusion surface.

 11

 Fig. 9. Detail size and height determination based on large area profilometry for two protrusion
 defects.

 Recognizing the need for in‑line compatibility, laser triangulation profilometers were evaluated
 for their ability to capture protrusion height at web speeds. Ten pre‑characterized samples were
 scanned with Keyence KJ‑X8060 and KJ‑X8200 probes at 19.2 m/min and 2.4 m/min, using
 different interpolation settings to balance motion‑ and cross‑web resolution. Analysis of exported
 height fields showed that GDL features with micro‑lumps or stray fibers exhibited larger speed‑
 and probe‑dependent variability, while smoother, laterally larger protrusions were measured
 more consistently; higher XY resolution tended to yield height values about 10% higher for those
 smoother defects (Figure 10 and 11). To broaden the sensor comparison, LMI Gocator probes
 were tested on three additional specimens.

 Fig. 10. 2D and 3D representation of the height field for a 6-µm high protrusion on GDL sample.

 12

 Fig. 11. Protrusion height measurements, Z, for 10 samples as a function of probe type and
 scanning speed.

 Because coating‑gap management depends on both local protrusions and bulk thickness
 variability, the team implemented chromatic confocal sensing to measure top and bottom surface
 distances and derive thickness on moving web. For a 3-ft GDL with MPL, measured at 1 kHz
 and 5 fpm, the average thickness was 197 µm with a standard deviation of 12 µm and a large 90
 µm min‑to‑max range, implying that a 65-µm protrusion located on a locally thick region could
 contact a 120-µm slot‑die gap if that gap is set to the average thickness. The confocal results
 aligned with nominal data (205 µm at 1 MPa) and micrometer checks (approximately 217–226
 µm depending on sample and method), validating the approach and guiding gap‑setting strategy.
 To address latent structural damage that may not be visually obvious, ultrasonic transmission
 mapping at 200 kHz with a 13-mm spot and 1-mm step was applied to GDL samples with
 controlled bends and creases. The measurements were sensitive to most permanent bends and
 creases, including some without clear visual signatures, demonstrating a promising pathway for
 non‑destructive detection of hidden defects before coating as shown in Figure 12.

 Fig. 12. Ultrasonic response of GDL specimen with and without folding defects.

 13

 Finally, to enable high‑fidelity on‑web profilometry, the team designed and fabricated a
 precision anvil roller with tight concentricity and a low roughness surface to provide a stable
 reference plane for height measurements, and engineered alignment‑adjustable stands for
 dedicated unwind/rewind units to remove and reintroduce PET interleaf without contaminating
 the MPL layer. Together, these capabilities establish a validated, multi‑scale inspection toolkit
 and the web‑handling infrastructure needed to translate accurate off‑line measurements into
 robust in‑line monitoring and process control.

 M12 deliverable - Report on In-Line Methods Including TEA Assessment.

 The Task 2 Results detailed in the preceding section serve as the final report for the M12
 deliverable, documenting all work completed prior to the project's cancellation. Those results
 cover the extensive investigation of various inspection methods—including optical profilometry,
 high-magnification microscopy, laser triangulation, chromatic confocal measurements, and
 ultrasonic testing—to characterize defects, topography, and thickness variation in GDL and
 catalyst layer materials.

 The original work plan included subsequent studies to further test and validate high-volume, in-
 line characterization techniques. These planned activities included: (a) assessing quality-control
 methods through ANOVA and Gage R&R studies; (b) determining the impact of defects; (c)
 increasing measurement rates to match the desired production cycle time; (d) developing 100%
 area mapping capability when only single-point measurement is currently available; (e)
 monitoring the quality of MEA cassettes and stacks; and (f) establishing the allowable number of
 defects to achieve an OEE of 95% for on-web defect detection.

 These subsequent studies were not conducted because the project was canceled.
