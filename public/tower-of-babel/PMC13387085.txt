A clear view at altitude: layer-specific control of the choroidal circulation
Authors: Jui-Lin Fan, Philip R. K. Turnbull
Published: 2026

The ocular choroid is one of the most highly perfused vascular beds in the body, responsible for supplying the metabolically demanding outer retina, in addition to contributing to thermoregulation and broader ocular homeostasis (Fig. 1A) (1). Dysregulation of choroidal perfusion has been implicated in a range of retinal disorders, but blood flow requires careful regulation (2). As the choroid sits within a rigid scleral shell, changes in choroidal volume can shift the photoreceptor layer away from the ideal focal plane, impacting visual quality (Fig. 1B) (1). Therefore, blood flow to the choroid must be delicately regulated to meet the high metabolic demands of the retina, without impairing retinal function. Both environmental hypoxia and spaceflight can strain this regulation, due to choroidal expansion from hypoxia-induced vasodilation and microgravity-induced headward fluid shift (3, 4). Yet the current literature on choroidal vascular adaptation to these environmental stresses is sparse, largely due to difficulties in assessing the choroidal circulation in vivo.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a noninvasive imaging modality that provides high-resolution cross-sectional images with micrometer resolution, using near-infrared light. In this journal, Safarzadeh et al. (5) used OCT to quantify choroidal vascular perfusion density (VPD) changes within the superficial (choriocapillaris and Sattler’s layer), deep (Haller’s layer), and total choroidal layers in 16 healthy individuals at baseline (1,100 m) and during acute (day 2) and sustained (day 9) exposure to 3,800 m, enabling assessment following partial acclimatization. They observed distinct, layer-specific responses to hypoxia within the choroid. Specifically, VPD in the superficial choroidal decreased with acute hypoxia, whereas the deep and total choroidal VPD increased with acute exposure and remained elevated at day 9, with the overall increase driven predominantly by changes within the deep choroid. In addition, they found that the deep choroidal VPD was closely associated with changes in the partial pressure of arterial oxygen, whereas superficial choroidal VPD more closely tracked the partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide. These findings suggest that the choroid does not respond uniformly to hypoxic stress and that deeper vascular layers may contribute disproportionately to the overall adaptive signal detected by functional-OCT (f-OCT).

An important caveat is that VPD is an image-derived index of vascular occupancy from which blood volume changes are inferred, rather than a direct measure of volumetric blood flow or oxygen delivery. The principal advance of the study is therefore not that it resolves the physiology of choroidal oxygen regulation, but that it demonstrates a reproducible approach for detecting layer-specific vascular changes in humans during environmental stress. These reciprocal changes in deep and superficial choroidal VPD raise the possibility that different choroidal layers contribute differently to outer retinal support under hypoxic conditions, but the physiological and clinical implications remain to be established.

Previous work by the same group has shown that f-OCT-derived VPD is sensitive to choroidal changes during hypoxic and hypercapnic challenges (6). The current study by Safarzadeh et al. (5) extends this by showing that f-OCT can detect VPD changes within superficial and deep choroidal layers during both acute and prolonged exposure to altitude. This sits alongside other research and clinical approaches to assessing choroidal circulation. Alongside Doppler- and speckle-based methods, OCT angiography offers high-resolution, depth-resolved imaging of retinal and choroidal vasculature, but its interpretation remains vulnerable to segmentation errors and artefacts from projection, motion, and vascular shadowing (7). In contrast, arterial spin labeling MRI provides quantitative and repeatable estimates of chorioretinal perfusion but lacks the spatial resolution required to segment choroidal sublayers and is substantially less accessible (8). In this context, the potential value of f-OCT is not that it replaces these modalities, but that it may offer a practical means of probing layer-specific vascular responsiveness to physiological challenges. In addition, f-OCT may allow the assessment of vascular function with less confounding from the structural changes seen in chronic retinal diseases. Whether f-OCT will provide clinically useful information beyond structural OCT or alternative methods for assessing choroidal vascular dysfunction, for example, in early disease detection, risk stratification, or monitoring progression, remains to be determined.

In summary, Safarzadeh et al. (5) used a novel imaging approach to provide new insights into choroidal vascular adaptation to acute and prolonged exposure to 3,800 m. Using f-OCT, they showed that superficial and deep choroidal layers exhibit distinct changes in vascular perfusion density, with differential associations with arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide tensions. These findings support the use of f-OCT to study choroidal vascular changes during environmental stress and suggest the method may also prove useful in other settings, such as microgravity. More broadly, because choroidal vascular dysfunction is implicated in ischemic retinal diseases, this work highlights the potential utility of f-OCT as a tool for investigating choroidal vascular dysfunction, although its translational relevance will require further validation.
