Abstract
We study the long-only minimum variance (LOMV) portfolio under a one-factor covariance model with asset betas of arbitrary sign. We provide an explicit solution in terms of the set of active (positive weight) assets, and provide an explicit and computable characterization of the active set. As a corollary we resolve an open question of 9 concerning the extension to mixed-sign betas. In the high-dimensional regime p → ∞ p\to\infty where the betas are drawn from a distribution with cdf F F , we prove that the proportion of active assets (the active ratio) in the LOMV portfolio converges in almost all cases to F ⁡ ( β ∗ ) F(\beta^{*}) , where β ∗ ≥ 0 \beta^{*}\geq 0 is the root of an explicit integral equation determined by F F . 1  Introduction

Interest in equity portfolios optimized to have the lowest possible variance goes back at least to
[
8
]
. The optimal such portfolio depends on the covariances between pairs of assets, and on the particular constraints of interest.

If there are
p
assets available for investment, we denote by
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the
p
-dimensional vector of asset weights defining the portfolio. The global minimum variance (GMV) portfolio
w^{LS}
denotes the (unique) long-short portfolio solving the simplest minimum variance problem

\begin{split}&\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}w^{\top}\Sigma w\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&w^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{p}=1,\end{split}

(1)

where
\Sigma
denotes the positive definite covariance matrix of asset returns;
\mathbf{1}_{p}
denotes the vector of dimension
p
whose every entry is 1;
and
w^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{p}=1
is the full investment condition setting the sum of the weights equal to 1.
For the long-short portfolio, some of the weights may be negative.

Our focus is the long-only minimum variance (LOMV) problem

\begin{split}&\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}w^{\top}\Sigma w\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&w^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{p}=1\\
&w_{i}\geq 0\text{ }\text{for all $i=1,2,...,p$.}\end{split}

(2)

The long-only constraints
w_{i}\geq 0
are often required for real investment portfolios due to the complications and costs of short positions. Since the objective function is convex, and the constraint set

\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:w^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{p}=1,w\geq 0\}
is non-empty and convex, there is always a unique LOMV solution, denoted
w^{L}
.

The solution
{w^{L}}
of (
2
) can be contrasted with the solution
w^{LS}
of the long-short problem.
It is well-known that the portfolio
w^{LS}
solving problem (
1
) is given by the
simple formula

w^{LS}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{p}}{\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{p}}.

(3)

The long-only problem (
2
) is less straightforward.

As we show in this article, the main difficulty in problem (
2
) is determining which are the active (positive weight) assets in the optimal portfolio, or, equivalently, which are the assets for which the long-only constraints are binding.
Let

P=\{1,2,\dots,p-1,p\}\text{ and }K=\{i\in P:w^{L}_{i}>0\}.

(4)

P
is the set of (indices of) all
p
available assets, and
K
identifies the active assets in the long-only optimal portfolio. Let
k\leq p
denote the number of elements of
K
. Once we have determined
K
, Proposition
1
below solves the problem explicitly, by expressing
the active long-only holdings as equal to the long-short minimum risk portfolio holdings corresponding to the reduced set
K
of assets.
This is intuitively reasonable
because the long-only constraints are not binding on the positive holdings of
w^{L}
.

This leaves the problem of determining
K
, the set of active assets of
w^{L}
, which is normally much smaller than the set of positive-weight assets in the long-short portfolio
w^{LS}
(e.g. see Figure
1
and Theorems
2
and
3
). In this article we analyze that problem when the covariance matrix comes from a 1-factor (“single index”) model, in which the covariance matrix takes the form

\Sigma=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\Delta,

(5)

where
\sigma^{2}>0
is the variance of the single factor return,
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a
p
-vector of exposures of the asset returns to the factor, and
\Delta
is a diagonal matrix of variances
\delta_{i}^{2}>0,i\in P
, of asset specific returns uncorrelated with the common factor return.

Figure 1
:

assets under the single-factor model (

\beta_{i}

There are several reasons for our focus on the 1-factor model case. It has been a long-accepted base case of study ever since
11
. It remains a workhorse in practice because the dominant eigenvalue of most equity covariance matrices captures a large fraction of the total variance, and so, as with the capital asset pricing model, it serves as a useful initial approximation to real markets.
The special tractability of the 1-factor model is useful in identifying phenomena present in more complicated models, in which the relationships between portfolio weights, risk, and their dependence on parameters are much less transparent. Understanding of the behavior of more descriptive multi-factor market models must begin with the clearest possible picture in the 1-factor case. This is the context for the present article.

The contributions of this article in this general 1-factor setting may be summarized as follows:

•

We provide a new explicit and easily computable solution (Theorem
1
) for the LOMV problem in terms of the parameters
\sigma^{2},\beta_{i},\delta_{i}
, along with a rigorous proof that applies to a general 1-factor model without requiring that the betas are all positive. This answers an open question stated in
[
9
]
concerning the extension to mixed-sign betas. Proposition
1
describes a general reduction of the problem to identifying the active set
K
, and then Theorem

determines
the active set by means of a threshold on the ordered

•

We provide a new rigorous analysis of the asymptotic proportion

of active assets (the

) in the LOMV portfolio in terms of the asymptotic distribution

of the betas (Theorem

).
To our knowledge, this problem was first addressed in
[
2
]
. We show that in almost all cases,

\lim_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p=F(\beta^{*}),

where

is the root of an explicit integral equation determined by

show that the asymptotic active ratio

There is a substantial literature on the LOMV portfolio in general and in the one-factor setting, e.g.
[

Since negative market factor loadings are rare empirically, Theorems

Our work was initially inspired by

2  Main Results

2.1  Determining the LOMV portfolio

We first state a general proposition describing the LOMV portfolio for any positive definite covariance matrix, without regard to whether it comes from a factor model.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of returns for a universe of
p
assets is an arbitrary
p\times p
symmetric positive definite matrix. Denote by
w^{L}
the solution of problem (
2
) and let
K
denote the set of active assets in
w^{L}
:

K=\{i\leq p:w^{L}_{i}>0\},

(6)

and
k=|K|\leq p
, the number of active assets.
Let
\Sigma^{K,0}
be the modified matrix obtained from
\Sigma
by setting to zero the rows and columns not belonging to
K
.

Then

w^{L}=\frac{(\Sigma^{K,0})^{+}\mathbf{1}_{p}}{\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}(\Sigma^{K,0})^{+}\mathbf{1}_{p}}

(7)

where
+
denotes the Moore-Penrose inverse.
1
1

1

For a symmetric matrix
S
with diagonalization
S=UDU^{\top}
for orthogonal
U
and diagonal
D
, the Moore-Penrose inverse is defined by
S^{+}=UD^{+}U^{\top}
, where the diagonal matrix
D^{+}
is obtained by replacing each nonzero element by its inverse, and leaving the zero entries unchanged. When
S
is positive definite, this coincides with the usual matrix inverse.

Equivalently, if we denote by
w^{K}
the
k
-vector obtained by deleting all the zero entries of the
p
-vector
w^{L}
, and
\Sigma^{K}
denotes the
k\times k
matrix obtained from
\Sigma
by deleting the rows and columns not belonging to
K
, then

w^{K}=\frac{(\Sigma^{K})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}}{\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}(\Sigma^{K})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}}.

(8)

This is the unique solution of the
k
-dimensional long-short problem (
1
) with

\Sigma
replaced by
\Sigma^{K}
, and
w^{L}
can be recovered from
w^{K}
by adding back zero entries for the deleted assets.

Proposition
1
allows us to determine the long-only minimum risk portfolio as soon as we know the set
K
of active assets. It remains to determine
K
, and for the single-factor case this can be accomplished by an explicit method described next in Theorem
1
.

We henceforth restrict to the case of a single factor covariance matrix
\Sigma
of the form

\Sigma=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\Delta,

(9)

as discussed above.

We permit the entries
\beta_{i}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to be positive, negative, or zero, but not all zero, which represents the general setting of a 1-factor model of returns.
Since the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
may be replaced by
-\boldsymbol{\beta}
without changing
\Sigma
, without loss of generality we choose the sign so that

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\delta_{i}^{2}}\geq 0,

(10)

as would be the case, for example, if all the betas were positive.

In addition, by re-ordering the assets if necessary, for convenience we further assume without loss of generality that the betas are arranged in increasing order:

\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\beta_{p}.

(11)

With these assumptions, our first main result is

Theorem 1
.

(
Explicit Solution to the long-only minimum variance problem under a general 1-factor model
)
Suppose the covariance matrix
\Sigma
has the 1-factor form
(
9
).

1.

Let
R_{1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}
, and, for
2\leq i\leq p
, let

R_{i}={\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}(\beta_{j}-\beta_{i})}={\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}(\beta_{j}-\beta_{i})}.

Then there exists
s\leq p
such that the initially positive sequence
\{R_{i}\}
is monotonically increasing until
i=s
, then monotonically decreasing for
i>s
. That is,

0<R_{1}\leq R_{2}\leq\cdots\leq R_{s}\geq R_{s+1}\geq\cdots\geq R_{p}.

(12)

In particular, if

\ell=\max\big\{i:R_{i}>0\big\},

(13)

then
i\leq\ell
if and only if
R_{i}>0
, and
the
sequence
\{R_{i}\}
crosses zero at most once.

2.

Let
w^{L}
denote the LOMV solution of problem (
2
),
K=\{i\in P:w^{L}_{i}>0\}
denote the set of active assets, and let
k=|K|
denote the number of active assets.

Then

k=\ell\text{ and }K=\{1,2,\dots,\ell\}.

(14)

Let
\Sigma^{K}
be the
k\times k
submatrix of
\Sigma
consisting of the first
k
rows and columns,
and let

w^{K}=\frac{(\Sigma^{K})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}}{\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}(\Sigma^{K})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}},

(15)

the long-short fully invested minimum variance solution for the first
k
assets.

Then, by Proposition
1
, the solution
w^{L}=(w_{1}^{L},\dots,w_{p}^{L})^{\top}
of (
2
) is given by

\begin{split}&w^{L}_{i}=w^{K}_{i}\text{ for $i=1,...,k$}\\
&w^{L}_{i}=0\text{ for }i=k+1,\dots,p.\\
\end{split}

(16)

We note that in any case
k>0
, and
k=p
if the long-short fully invested minimum variance portfolio happens to be long-only already. Otherwise,
k<p
, and
k
has the property

R_{k}>0\text{ and }R_{k+1}=R_{k}+(\beta_{k}-\beta_{k+1})\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}\leq 0.

(17)

This means that the threshold index
k
and the solution

are not influenced by the values of
\delta_{j}
for
j>k
, nor by the values of
\beta_{j}
for
\beta_{j}>\beta_{k+1}
.
In particular, this implies

Corollary 1
.

With the notation and assumptions as in the previous theorem, suppose we add
q>0
additional assets to the market, resulting in an enlarged set of
p+q
total assets. As long as the betas of the added assets are greater than or equal to
\beta_{k+1}
, the active set
K
and the weights of the active assets in

are unaffected.

9
has a related discussion.

The closed form solution (
15
) depends on first determining
k
from (
14
). From the monotonicity properties of
\{R_{i}\}
, this may be quickly accomplished, for example, by the bisection method in
O(\log p)
steps.

A solution of problem (
2
) in semi-explicit form was described for the positive beta case in
[
5
]
. They give the following condition:

w_{i}>0\text{ if and only if }\beta_{i}<\tau,

(18)

where
\tau
is the solution of the equation

\tau=\frac{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{\beta_{j}<\tau}\frac{\beta^{2}_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}}{\sum_{\beta_{j}<\tau}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}}.

(19)

The active set described this way is a consequence of the following corollary of the proof of Theorem
1
:

Corollary 2
.

Either
K=P
and the long-short solution
w^{LS}
is already long-only (
w^{LS}=w^{L}
), or else
K\neq P
,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}>0
, and

w_{i}>0\text{ if and only if }\beta_{i}<\frac{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}}.

(20)

This formulation requires the convention (
10
), as a simple argument shows:
In the absence of any hypotheses about the signs of the betas, replacing
\boldsymbol{\beta}
by
-\boldsymbol{\beta}
leaves the covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and hence the solution
w
, unchanged. But in that case the long positions of

would correspond to betas above a threshold, not below it, contradicting the threshold condition (
18
).
The need for assumption (
10
) is easily overlooked because negative betas tend to be rare empirically.

2.2  High dimensional asymptotics of the LOMV portfolio

We have an interest in the proportion
k/p
of active (non-zero weight) assets in the LOMV portfolio in a universe of
p
assets, for large
p
. A natural approach to this problem is to study the asymptotic limit, if it exists.

We consider a sequence of LOMV problems, one for each dimension
p
, with
the following modeling assumptions:

•

The entries
\beta_{i}
of the
p
-vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are drawn iid
from a distribution with finite mean and cdf
F
. We denote by
\beta
a random variable with this distribution.

•

The diagonal entries
\delta_{i}^{2}=\Delta_{ii}>0
are drawn
iid from an independent distribution with
\E[1/\delta^{2}]<\infty
.

•

The distribution
F
satisfies:

1.

\E[\beta]\geq 0
(non-negative mean), and

2.

\E\!\left[\beta^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\beta\leq 0\}}\right]<\infty

(finite negative-tail second moment).

The non-negative mean assumption is without loss of generality, since the
covariance matrix is unchanged if
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is replaced by
-\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
The negative-tail second moment condition is mild: it holds whenever the
left tail of
F
is sub-Gaussian, sub-exponential, or has any polynomial
decay faster than
|x|^{-3}
. It is satisfied by finite variance
\E[\beta^{2}]<\infty
but does not require it; distributions with a heavy right tail (such as Pareto with index

\alpha\in(1,2)
) are permitted. This allows the theorem to cover cases where the cross-sectional betas are leptokurtic in the right tail.

The ideas here can also be adapted to the case where the betas and deltas are correlated, which we omit.

Adapting the notation of previous sections,
let
k_{p}\leq p
denote the number of active assets in the LOMV solution in dimension
p
, so that the remaining
p-k_{p}
weights are zero. We call the relative number
k_{p}/p
of active assets in the LOMV portfolio the
active ratio
.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic active ratio)
.

With the notation and assumptions above, define

G(y)\;=\;\int_{-\infty}^{y}\bigl(x^{2}-yx\bigr)\,dF(x),\qquad\beta^{*}\;=\;\sup\bigl\{x\geq 0:G(x)=0\bigr\}.

(21)

G
is continuous and concave on
[0,\infty)
.
Then, almost surely:

1.

(Negative betas, positive mean.)

If
P(\beta<0)>0
and
\E[\beta]>0
, then
G
has a unique
zero
\beta^{*}>0
, and

\liminf_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p\;=\;F(\beta^{*-})\;>\;0,\qquad\limsup_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p\;=\;F(\beta^{*}).

(22)

In particular,
\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p
exists
if and only if
F
is continuous at
\beta^{*}
, in which case

\lim_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p\;=\;F(\beta^{*})\;>\;0.

(23)

2.

(Negative betas, zero mean.)

If
P(\beta<0)>0
and
\E[\beta]=0
, then
G(y)>0
for all

y\geq 0
, and

\lim_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p\;=\;1.

3.

(Non-negative betas.)

If
P(\beta<0)=0
, then

\beta^{*}=\inf\{x\geq 0:F(x)>0\}
is the left endpoint of
the support of
F
, and

\lim_{p\to\infty}k_{p}/p\;=\;F(\beta^{*})\;=\;P(\beta=\beta^{*})\quad\text{a.s.}

In particular, the limit is zero whenever
F
has no atom
at
\beta^{*}
, and equals the atom mass otherwise.

Remark 1
.

In Cases 2 and 3, the limit
\lim k_{p}/p
always exists. In
Case 1, the limit exists if and only if
F
is continuous
at
\beta^{*}
; non-convergence requires
\beta^{*}
(determined
implicitly by
G(\beta^{*})=0
) to be an atom of
F
. An example of this is given in section
2.3
, but it is rare. In particular, if
F
is continuous, as is typical with model distributions, the limit in Case 1 exists. See also the Remark following the proof.

Theorem
2
tells us that the active ratio, when the universe of available assets is large, is determined by the distribution of the asset betas. In particular, a key factor is the proportion
F(0^{-})=P(\beta<0)
of negative betas.

In the non-negative beta case
P(\beta<0)=0
treated by
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
, in the realistic case where
F
has no atoms, Theorem
2
tells us to expect that the active ratio asymptotically vanishes as
p\to\infty
. Even when
P(\beta<0)
is positive but small, the result of the next theorem helps to explain the finding in
[
3
]
that the long-short minimum variance portfolio is very unlikely to be long-only already.

In practice one may find the proportion
P(\beta\leq 0)=F(0)
is small but positive, and any negative betas tend to be small in magnitude. The next theorem shows, under reasonable assumptions, that the limiting active ratio
F(\beta^{*})
is small if the proportion
F(0)
of non-positive betas is small.
More specifically,
F(\beta^{*})=O(F(0)^{1/3})
, with the constant depending on distributional bounds.

Theorem 3

(Continuity of the limiting active ratio at zero)
.

Let
(F_{n})
be a sequence of probability distributions, and let
X
denote
a random variable with distribution
F_{n}
. Assume:

1.

Vanishing mass on
(-\infty,0]
:

0<F_{n}(0)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

2.

Uniformly positive mean:

\mu_{n}:=\E_{F_{n}}[X]\geq\mu
for some fixed constant
\mu>0
.

3.

Uniform second moment, conditional negative tail second moment, and concentration bounds:

there exist constants
C,K,M<\infty
such that for every
n
:

•

\E_{F_{n}}[X^{2}]\leq C
,

•

\E_{F_{n}}\!\bigl[X^{2}\bigm|X\leq 0\bigr]\leq K
, and

•

F_{n}(x+t)-F_{n}(x)\leq Mt
for all
x\geq 0
and
t>0
.

Define

G_{n}(y)\;=\;\int_{-\infty}^{y}(x^{2}-yx)\,dF_{n}(x),

and let
y_{n}^{*}
be the (unique) zero of
G_{n}
, i.e.
G_{n}(y_{n}^{*})=0
. Then

F_{n}(y_{n}^{*})\;=\;O\!\bigl(F_{n}(0)^{1/3}\bigr)\;\to\;0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

More explicitly,

F_{n}(y_{n}^{*})\;\leq\;F_{n}(0)+\Theta^{1/3}F_{n}(0)^{1/3}

where

\Theta=27\,({K}+C\sqrt{K}/\mu)M^{2}.

Remark 2
.

The uniform moment bounds will be satisfied by any realistic family of empirical distributions, such as those with uniformly bounded support.

Remark 3
.

The concentration function condition
F_{n}(x+t)-F_{n}(x)\leq Mt
for
x\geq 0
,

t>0
is a mild continuity hypothesis driving the cube root rate of convergence. Any
absolutely continuous family on
(0,\infty)
with uniformly bounded density
satisfies this condition.

It allows
F_{n}
to have atoms and singular-continuous parts on

(-\infty,0]
, and even a singular-continuous component on
(0,\infty)

provided the CDF does not increase faster than slope
M
there. In
particular, it covers distributions
F_{n}
with an atom at zero (representing a population in which a
non-negligible fraction of assets have exactly zero market beta), as long as the mass at zero decays to zero with
n
. In case the mass at zero converges to a positive value
m>0
but
F_{n}(0^{-})\to 0
, similar arguments and Theorem
2
give the bound

\lim k_{p}/p\leq m+\Theta^{1/3}m^{1/3}.

Remark 4
.

The concentration function condition may be further weakened to the assumption that the family
\{F_{n}\}
is equicontinuous on
(0,\infty)
. The conclusion remains that
F_{n}(y^{*}_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, but the rate of convergence depends on the modulus of continuity of the family
\{F_{n}\}
. The proof of this statement is similar to the proof of Theorem
3
, but we omit it here.

Remark 5
.

The implication of Theorems
2
and
3
is that when the number
p
of assets is large, the active ratio is small if non-positive betas are rare and small in magnitude, and shrinks further as they become more rare, becoming asymptotically zero when all betas are positive.

The bound of Theorem
3
is not sharp, but serves to establish the continuity claim. As an example, if the betas across
p\gg 1
assets are normally distributed with mean 1 and standard deviation 0.4, calculation
gives
P(\beta<0)\approx 0.62\%
, or about one asset out of every 160 with a negative beta. We can compute
\beta^{*}\approx 0.288
and
F(\beta^{*})\approx 3.8\%
active assets. By comparison, computation of the asymptotic upper bound above gives a value of about 15% active assets in the LOMV portfolio. If the standard deviation is reduced to 0.25,
P(\beta<0)
is lowered to about
0.0032\%
,
\beta^{*}\approx 0.12
, and
F(\beta^{*})\approx 0.021\%
. The
active ratio theoretical upper bound is about 1%. As expected, knowledge of the specific distribution allows for a much sharper estimate of the asymptotic active ratio than our general bound.

(24)

Then
\E[\beta]=-0.05+0.15+0.60+2.50=3.20>0

Computing

on each interval between successive atoms:

•

•

1
and Theorem
1

We begin with the proof of the Proposition. The solution

of our constrained optimization problem (
2
) satisfies the well-known Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions

2

2

See, for example,
[
4

or
[

]
.

w

{\lambda}

\nu
(the Lagrange multipliers) as follows:

\displaystyle 2\Sigma w-{\lambda}+\nu\mathbf{1}_{p}=\mathbf{0}_{p}

(25)

\displaystyle w^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{p}=1

(26)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}w_{i}=0\text{ }i=1,2,...,p

(27)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\geq 0,w_{i}\geq 0\text{ }i=1,2,...,p,

(28)

where
\mathbf{0}_{p}
denotes the
p

These KKT conditions include
2p+1
equations (
25
) – (
27
) in the
2p+1
unknowns

w_{1},\dots,w_{p},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p},\nu,

along
with
2p
inequality constraints (
28

Define the (necessarily non-empty) set

K=\{i\leq p:w_{i}>0\}.

(29)

Let
k>0
denote the cardinality of

x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

the
k

for all

Likewise, for any

Since
\Sigma

\Sigma^{K}
is invertible.

w^{\top}\Sigma w=(w^{K})^{\top}\Sigma^{K}w^{K}.

(30)

Since

and therefore

(33)

or, equivalently,

completing the proof of Proposition
1
.

Part 1 of Theorem
1
follows solely from the monotonicity of the sequence
\{\beta_{i}\}
.
For convenience, define

C_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\delta_{j}^{2}}.

(35)

It is easy to verify, for all

, that

R_{i+1}-R_{i}=(-\beta_{i+1}+\beta_{i})C_{i}.

(36)

Since

is always non-positive, this means

C_{i}\geq 0\implies R_{i+1}\leq R_{i}\text{ and }C_{i}\leq 0\implies R_{i+1}\geq R_{i}.

(37)

If

for all

, then

satisfies the conclusion of the theorem.

Otherwise, let

.
If

, then

by definition of

, so

. Since
\beta_{s}>0

, and we have

. This establishes the conclusion of part 1.

We proceed to Part 2.
Our goal now is to determine

of the problem.

Let
k=|K|
and define

(38)

We will complete the proof by establishing

Let
\beta^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and the
k\times k

be determined from

as before.
For ease of notation write

.
