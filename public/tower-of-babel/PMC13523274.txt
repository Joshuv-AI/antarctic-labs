Nutritional status and quality of life of older persons on Social Assistance Grant for Empowerment (SAGE) in Kampala City, Central Uganda
Authors: Daniel Hendry Ruma, Joy Francesca Tumusiime, Faith Muyonga Namayengo, Norman Paul Shapiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation ageing is a growing public health concern. Despite the Ugandan Government’s efforts, like the Social Assistance Grant for Empowerment (SAGE) programme, malnutrition and poor Quality of Life (QoL) are still older persons’ challenges. This study assessed the nutritional status and QoL of older persons on the SAGE programme in Kampala City, Central Uganda.MethodologyA cross-sectional study with a quantitative research approach was conducted. Data was collected in 2025 among 159 persons aged 80 years and above using a 24-hour recall, the 26-item World Health Organisation BREF, and anthropometric assessments. Data analysis involved descriptive statistics, chi-square, analysis of variance, and regression. Statistical significance was read at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean (SD) meal frequency was 1.95 ± 0.81, dietary diversity score (DDS) was 6.69 ± 2.77 in the past 24 h, and Body Mass Index (BMI) was 22.8 ± 4.5 kg/m². Intake of meat (16.7%), fruits (14.6%) and eggs (10.2%) was low. Underweight, overweight, and obesity prevalence were 18.9%, 22.6%, and 8.2%, respectively. Underweight odds were lower in males (AOR = 0.36, CI: 0.14–0.92, p = 0.032) and persons who consumed ≥ 3 meals (AOR = 0.04, CI: 0.01–0.34, p = 0.003). The odds of overweight/obesity were higher among males (AOR = 2.01, CI: 1.36–2.69, p = 0.041). The mean (SD) overall QoL score was 52.27 ± 15.75, with 23.3% of respondents having a good QoL. Males had higher odds of a poor QoL (AOR = 2.45, CI: 1.53–3.04, p = 0.021), while having secondary or higher education (AOR = 0.25, CI: 0.11–0.57, p < 0.001) and other income sources (AOR = 0.32, CI: 0.28–1.38, p = 0.027) protected against a poor QoL.ConclusionThe study highlights a double burden of malnutrition and poor QoL linked to sex, socioeconomic and dietary factors among older persons on the SAGE programme. This points to challenges emanating from gender and policy disparities among older persons in Uganda.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28226-y.Keywords: Kampala, Nutritional status, Older persons, Quality of life, SAGE

Introduction

Population ageing is becoming a serious public health and social development concern [1]. According to the United Nations [2], older persons are people aged over 60 years. Older persons face several physical and financial challenges as well as social vulnerabilities, especially in developing countries. Increasing age is frequently associated with lower income, limited food access, chronic illnesses, and physical functional decline, all of which have a detrimental impact on dietary consumption, nutritional status, and overall quality of life (QoL) [3]. Social assistance initiatives, such as Uganda’s Social Assistance Grants for Empowerment (SAGE), aim to protect older people against these risks by enhancing their income security and well-being [4].

Global estimates indicated that 703 million people were aged 65 or above in 2019, and the number is expected to increase by 42.3% by the year 2050 [5]. However, ageing comes with vulnerability, poor QoL and high risks of morbidities and mortalities among older persons [6]. Moreover, malnutrition is another challenge affecting the well-being of older persons in Africa, with a scoping review indicating that undernutrition ranges between 2.9% and 41%, overweight ranges between 8.1% and 54.1%, and obesity ranges between 2.7% and 44.7% [7]. The QoL of older persons has been associated with sex, disease and access to social support [3, 8, 9].

Uganda registered a 54% increase in the population of older persons from 993,357 in 2014 to 1,532,750 people in 2024 [10], and yet older persons continue to face several challenges, notably loneliness, disease and lack of social and financial support [11]. It has been reported that a large proportion of older persons in Uganda had poor QoL scores in physical, social, psychological and environmental domains [1]. Other studies in rural Uganda indicated that 66% of older persons in Dokolo [12] and 6.0% in Kween districts [13] were malnourished. This situation complicates the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3, which focuses on health and wellbeing at all ages and SDG 10, which focuses on reducing inequalities and ensuring no one is left behind. As such, several interventions have been implemented worldwide to promote the health and well-being of older persons.

In Uganda, the SAGE support programme comprises the disbursement of a monthly stipend of 25,000 Ugandan shillings (approximately 7 United States Dollars) to persons aged 80 and above [14]. Previous studies have revealed that the programme has improved the lives and well-being of older persons. Byaruhanga and Debesay [15] reported that, among other factors, the programme contributed to food security, and another study reported that the programme improved dietary intake and contributed to a positive effect on the weight for age z-scores in children living with older persons [16].

Despite previous studies reporting the impact of the SAGE programme among older persons, several gaps are identified. For instance, no standard metrics have been used to assess the QoL of older persons in the programme in Uganda. Whereas some studies have questioned the implications of the programme [1], studies that reported the impact of the programme on food security have not used standard dietary assessment or food insecurity indicators or QoL assessment metrics to explore these factors [14, 15]. Moreover, there is a dearth of studies assessing the nutritional status and QoL of older persons on the SAGE programme in Uganda.

Therefore, it is against this background that our study assessed nutritional status and QoL alongside their determinants in persons aged 80 years and above receiving social assistance in Nakawa, Kawempe and Rubaga divisions. This study will build on existing literature through data collected from an urban setting. At the District level in Kampala, the SAGE programme is implemented by different officers headed by the Chief Administrative Officer (CAO), who oversees all Government programmes. The District Community Development Officer (DCDO) is in charge of all operations supported by other officers, including parish chiefs and the District Sage Support Team (DSST), who, alongside village chairpersons, arrange and attend the village council meetings for verification purposes. The funds are disbursed by the Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development (MGLSD) through a largely direct-to-beneficiary quarterly electronic payment system.

Methodology

Study design and setting

The study design was cross-sectional, with a quantitative research approach employed to assess the nutritional status and QoL of older persons. Data collection commenced on 20th September 2025 and ended on 20th October 2025. The study was conducted in Kampala City, which is the capital of Uganda. Kampala city comprises five divisions. Specifically, the study targeted older persons on the SAGE programme in three (Kawempe, Nakawa and Rubaga) of the five divisions of Kampala.

Study population

The study population comprised older persons in Uganda aged 80 years and above who were on the SAGE programme in the Kawempe, Nakawa and Rubaga divisions of Kampala City, Uganda.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion in the study if they were aged 80 years or above and were registered and active on the SAGE programme, resided in Kawempe, Nakawa, or Rubaga division for at least 6 months, mentally competent and physically fit to respond to all the study questions. On the other hand, older persons who were ill, experiencing bodily pain and for whatever reasons declined to participate in the study were excluded.

Sampling size determination

Cochran’s formula for determining a proportion [17] was used to determine the study’s sample size, taking into account a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and a 5% (population of elders) in Uganda [10]. The sample size was first estimated at 73 participants based on the initial calculation. It was then increased to 146 by applying a design effect of 2 to account for clustering in the sampling strategy. To further accommodate an anticipated 10% non-response rate, the sample size was 163 participants. Ultimately, 159 participants were successfully recruited for the study.

Sampling procedure

Three SAGE distribution points from each of the Kawempe, Nakawa and Rubaga divisions were randomly selected. The community development officers in charge of the SAGE programme were identified to help in identifying and listing older persons on the programme at the different distribution points in the divisions. The lists provided indicated that registered older persons on the programme were 380 in Kawempe, 393 in Nakawa and 498 in Rubaga. From the generated lists, using simple random sampling, 53 older persons were randomly selected from each of the selected 3 divisions and recruited for the interviews and anthropometric assessments.

Data collection methods and tools

Interviews: These involved asking probing and guiding questions about different factors; sociodemographic information like sex, marital status, education levels and economic factors like income source; SAGE-related factors like the use of the funds received; and QoL and dietary data, among others. A semi-structured questionnaire designed with different sections on the relevant factors was used to collect data.

Nutritional status assessment

(a) Dietary assessment: Given that we assessed people in an age group vulnerable to challenges with memorising events, a 24-hour recall was used for dietary assessment. This involved asking the respondents and, in some cases, their caregivers to mention all of the foods and beverages that the respondents consumed in the past 24 hours from the time of the interview. In addition, we also asked for the main sources of the foods and beverages consumed.

QoL assessment

Respondents were asked questions regarding the general QoL, physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships and environmental domains. The WHOQoL-BREF was used to assess the QoL. This is a 26-item, self-report, shortened version of the WHOQoL-100 item assessment questionnaire used to measure subjective QoL. The questionnaire comprises two general questions regarding the respondent’s perceptions of their QoL, satisfaction in life and health condition, as well as four domains comprising 24 questions over the past two weeks [22]. The four domains include (i) physical health comprising 7 items- mobility, daily activities, functional capacity, energy, pain and sleep; (ii) psychological health comprising 6 items -self-image, negative thoughts, positive attitudes, self-esteem, mentality, learning ability, memory concentration, religion and mental status; (iii) social relationships comprising 3 item -personal relationships, social support and sex life; and (iv) environmental health comprising 8 items- financial resources, safety, health and social services, living environment, opportunities to acquire resources, safety, health and social services, living physical environment, opportunities to acquire skills and knowledge, recreation, general environment and transportation. A score of 1 to 5 is assigned on a response scale stipulated as a five-point ordinal scale for each item.

Standardisation of tools and materials

Before the commencement of data collection, the tools were checked and pretested to establish their suitability for use. Batteries for the weighing scales were checked for expiry dates, and the functionality of the weighing scales was established. Weighing scales were standardised by the use of objects of known weights after every ten assessments. The non-stretchable tapes were checked for calibration and to identify faults such as warping. To ensure validity, the semi-structured questionnaires, 24-hour recall and the WHOQoL-BREF were pretested on a sample of 10 older persons on the SAGE programme who were not included in the study population. Challenges with the tools were identified and adjustments made before collecting data.

Data quality assurance

Following data collection, the questionnaires were reviewed daily for missing information and errors with data entry. This review was done by the Principal Investigator of the study and the data collection supervisors. Upon identifying errors, research assistants were tasked with verifying the information and, in some cases, sent back to the field to collect missing information. As such, only complete questionnaires were entered into Microsoft Excel. The entered information was then coded, where numbers representing the variables were used. The data was transferred into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27, where relevant labels were created for the different variables.

Data analysis

Analysis of dietary data

The total number of meals eaten was summed and categorised according to the report meal frequency. After obtaining the details of all of the foods and beverages consumed, we categorised the foods and beverages into twelve food groups namely (i) eggs, (ii) meat and meat products, (iii) fruits, (iv) milk and milk products, (v) fish and fish products, (vi) cereals, (vii) sugars and confectioneries, (viii) vegetables, (ix) oils and fats, (x) pulses, legumes and nuts, (xi) condiments, spices and non-alcoholic beverages and (xii) roots and tubers [23, 24]. A score of 1 was awarded if a food was eaten in the household, and 0 if the food was not eaten. The scores were summed, with 0 being the lowest and 12 being the highest. Total scores were categorised to report household dietary diversity score (DDS) based on standard cut-offs, with < 4.5 food groups considered low, 4.5 to 6.0 food groups considered medium, and > 6.0 food groups considered high DDS, which have been used in similar contexts [25].

Analysis of anthropometric data

The height and weight data were used to compute the body mass index (BMI) using the standard BMI calculation formula [26]. The BMI was categorised based on the WHO standard cut-offs [27] for underweight, overweight and obesity.

Analysis of QoL

This involved finding the mean score of items within a domain. Scores from each domain were transformed to a 0 to 100 scale [28]. Cut-off points of the raw scores have been suggested by Silva et al. [29]. Therefore, a score of ≤ 45 was considered poor QoL, 46 to 64 was considered moderate QoL and ≥ 65 was considered high QoL as used in previous studies [3, 30]. Higher scores indicate a higher QoL [22]. The overall score was categorised to report the QoL as poor or high.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square analysis was used to analyse the relationship between categorical variables, such as sociodemographic factors, with the established QoL categories and the nutritional status. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were run to analyse for mean differences between categorical and continuous variables, such as sociodemographic factors and SAGE characteristics, with the absolute QoL scores. Binary logistic regressions with crude odds ratio and adjusted odds ratio (COR and AOR) and confidence intervals (CIs) were run to identify unique predictors of a poor QoL and malnutrition among the respondents. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Study results

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

As shown in Table 1, the study assessed 159 older persons on the SAGE programme. The mean (SD) age was 88.45 (± 5.06) years, with most of the respondents aged 91 or more years, 42.1%. The sample comprised slightly more males 50.3% than females, 49.7%. Nearly half, 47.2% of the respondents were widowed, while 22.6% were divorced or separated, and 20.1% were married. In terms of educational attainment, 36.5% had no formal education, 32.1% had completed primary level, and only 8.2% had attained higher education. Most participants 64.8% lived with family members, whereas 25.2% lived alone and 10.1% resided with caregivers. The SAGE grant served as the primary source of income for more than half of the participants, 56%, and the funds were used to support grandchildren, 28.3%, pay household utilities, 26.4%, and address healthcare needs, 25.2%. The main challenges experienced by the older persons included scarcity of clean water 23.9%, lack of electricity 23.3% and others 25.2%.

Predictors of the QoL of older persons

As shown in Table 8, the respondent’s sex, level of education attained, and the household’s main income source were significant predictors of the QoL. Males were twice as likely, AOR = 2.45, CI: 1.53–3.04, p = 0.021, to have a poor QoL than females. Protective factors against a poor QoL were having secondary or higher education levels AOR = 0.25, CI: 0.11–0.57, p < 0.001 and having other sources of income besides the SAGE AOR = 0.32, CI: 0.28–1.38, p = 0.027.

Discussion of findings

Nutritional status of older persons in Kampala City

The current study revealed that the assessed older persons mostly ate cereals, roots and tubers, pulses and nuts, which reflects the diet in most Ugandan communities [31]. The intake of other nutritious foods, including fruits and leafy vegetables and animal source foods, especially beef, dairy, eggs and fish, was generally low. Their low intake might be attributed to the fact that such foods are expensive, perceived adverse health consequences linked to their intake and expectations of future economic conditions [32]. Moreover, other factors that might have led to the low intake of such foods include access, duration of preparation and lack of support to prepare the foods [33].

The majority of the assessed older persons ate two or fewer meals. Similarly, a previous study revealed that on average, two meals were consumed in older persons’ households in Uganda [4], which is below the recommended minimum of three meals daily. This is indicative of insufficient dietary intake among older persons and failure to meet the required food intake as reported by Motadi and colleagues [34]. The intake of fewer meals could be attributed to physical challenges such as ageing-induced anorexia [35], challenges with obtaining and preparing meals [36], as well as individuals’ choice to have fewer meals [37].

The DDS in most of the assessed older persons were below the recommended thresholds. The proportion of the assessed older persons with a high DDS in the current study is higher than that (35.6%) reported among older persons in rural Zambia [38]. The large proportion of older persons with a low DDS might be explained by the fact that the study assessed older persons in an urban setting with limited facilities to support activities like the establishment of backyard gardens, which could potentially enhance their dietary intake. The low DDS might also be attributed to the tendency among older persons to rely on the widely available staples in Uganda [31] and other challenges, like decreasing tastes for certain foods [36]. Moreover, the majority of the assessed older persons did not utilise the funds to buy food, which might also have contributed to the low DDS.

Overall, the majority of the assessed older persons had a normal nutritional status, close to one-fifth (18.9%) were underweight and close to one-third (30.8%) were overweight (22.6%) or obese (8.2%). The combined prevalence of malnutrition (underweight and overweight/obesity) in the current study is comparable to the high prevalence (53.9%) reported in a study that used a different methodology to assess the prevalence of malnutrition among older persons in Northern Uganda [33]. The high prevalence of malnutrition among the older persons in this study might be attributed to several factors, such as geriatric conditions, insufficient care and poverty, which compromise intake.

The prevalence of underweight revealed in our study is higher than the national average for men and women in Uganda [39]. The high prevalence of underweight in the present study underscores the fact that many older persons are disadvantaged in terms of socioeconomic and livelihood status. The prevalence of underweight in our study is comparable to that reported among older persons in Africa, 21% [40] and in Ethiopia, 20.6% [41], but less than the 30.4% reported among older persons in rural Zambia [38]. The likelihood of being underweight was lower among men than among women. This might be explained by gender inequalities in favour of men in many communities. Similarly, the likelihood of being underweight was lower among older persons who ate three or more meals. Having more meals nourishes the body with excessive nutrients stored up, thus protecting the body from sarcopenia. Being underweight was more likely in older persons who did not obtain food through market purchase, implying that they did not have the funds to buy food and probably had insufficient intake.

The prevalence of overweight and obesity in the current study is lower than that reported in studies that assessed older persons in South Africa [34] and in Nigeria [42]. This discrepancy might be linked to low-income levels and rampant poverty among older persons in Uganda, which contributes to insufficient intake. In the current study, the odds of being overweight were twice as high among older persons from households with 5 or fewer members. Having smaller household sizes reduces competition for scarce household resources, including food, thus safeguarding household members from inadequate intake and its associated undesirable consequences [43]. Moreover, the odds of overweight and obesity were three times higher among older persons from households that did not depend on the stipend as the main source of funds to meet household needs. Previous studies have revealed that the stipend is small and covers a very small proportion of older persons’ household expenses [44].

QoL of older persons in Kampala City

Overall, our study revealed that the environmental domain had the highest mean scores, while the psychological domain had the lowest score. This contradicts findings reported in a previous study that revealed that the physical domain had the highest scores [30]. This discrepancy might be attributed to the variations in respondent socioeconomic status and the small sample size used in our study. In addition, the study assessed older persons living in an urban Kampala city with relatively better conditions [45].

The proportion of the assessed older persons with a poor QoL in the physical, psychological, social and environmental domains in the current study is high and validates findings reported in a study that assessed older persons in Wakiso district, neighbouring our study area [1]. This puts the lives of older persons in jeopardy. The proportion of older persons who had a good QoL in our study is lower than that (31.2%) reported in a study that assessed older persons in Nigeria [42].

Our findings revealed that males were twice as likely to have a poor QoL as females. Contrarily, Boah et al. [46] found that the Qol was higher among males than females in a study that assessed older adults in Rwanda. Lodhi et al. [47] found that being female was a strong predictor of poorer QoL. This discrepancy in findings might be attributed to methodological differences in the studies. In addition, the higher likelihood of males having a poor QoL might be explained by the fact that men tend to be overworked in most communities as they work for their families, which causes them to easily break down physically and emotionally [48].

Our study found that having secondary or higher education levels was protective against a poor QoL. Similarly, Magakwe et al. [49] reported that higher educational attainment reduces the odds of depression and anxiety among African populations, which negatively affect the QoL. Higher education qualifies individuals for employment and other income-generating ventures, which are key determinants of the nature of one’s later years.

Our study revealed that having other sources of income besides the SAGE funds was protective against a poor QoL. In line with this finding, previous studies have revealed that lower income was a significant predictor of QoL [47, 50]. The amount of money disbursed in the Ugandan SAGE programme benefits the recipients but is insufficient to meet the continuous needs faced by the older persons.

Our study found that the QoL scores were significantly higher in all of the four domains among respondents who ate 3 or more meals. Having more meals can reflect a higher or good QoL. This emphasises the role of sufficient nutrition in the quality of human life. Moreover, a previous study revealed that meal timing shifts with older age through delayed eating times of breakfast and dinner and a shorter eating time window [51].

Methodological strengths and limitations

The study had some limitations. A large majority of the respondents had representatives (such as relatives and grandchildren) who collected the funds on their behalf, which led to a very low turnout at the pay points. Cultural adaptation was not considered. The cross-sectional design suffers from seasonality bias and does not allow to analyse cause and effect. Finally, the BMI computed from weight and height that the study used to reflect older persons’ nutritional status does not account for age-related changes in body composition, especially loss of muscle mass and the increase in fat mass. Nonetheless, the study provides important insights into the dietary intake of older persons through a method that involves a shorter 24-hour recall period, suitable for individuals who might have cognitive decline and memory loss. In addition, the findings of the study provide some information regarding the potential contribution of the SAGE programme to the nutritional status and the QoL of older persons in the programme in Uganda.

Conclusion and recommendations

Overall, our study revealed insufficient intake of animal source foods, eggs and fish, as well as fruits and vegetables in most of the assessed older persons on the SAGE programme. The meal frequency was also insufficient, with most of the older persons having two or fewer meals from fewer than six food groups. This might predispose older persons to sarcopenia and other undesirable conditions.

Malnutrition was a main challenge among older persons on the SAGE programme, especially underweight and overweight, which would exacerbate the development of non-communicable diseases and their associated adverse consequences, which also disrupts the well-being of older persons. Important predictors of undernutrition were being male, depending on relatives and the meal frequency, whereas not depending on the stipend for household expenses and having other income sources were important predictors of overweight and obesity.

The QoL of older persons on the SAGE programme was generally poor, given that the proportion of older persons with a poor QoL was high for the physical, psychological, environmental and social domains. Significantly lower QoL scores were observed among older persons in Kawempe division, those from large-sized households, with lower education levels and those who ate fewer meals. Older persons with higher education and those who did not depend on the SAGE for their household expenditure seemed to be protected from a poor QoL.

Given that older persons who did not depend on the stipend to meet their household expenses had higher odds of being overweight and lower odds of having a poor QoL, this might suggest that the stipend has a minimal contribution towards the nutritional well-being and health of the older persons. As such, the government should consider increasing the amount disbursed for the programme to have a reasonable contribution to the well-being of older persons. Given that the meal frequency seemed to have a pronounced influence on both the nutritional status and QoL, interventions that promote dietary intake among older persons should be prioritised while extending equitable support based on sociodemographic discrepancies. In addition, future research should consider comparative study designs among older persons who are on the SAGE programme and those who are not to appropriately explore the contribution of the programme to the QoL, nutritional status and other factors.
