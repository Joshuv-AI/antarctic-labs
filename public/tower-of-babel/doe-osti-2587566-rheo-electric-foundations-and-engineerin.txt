Rheo–Electric Foundations and Engineering of Carbon
 Slurry Flow Electrodes
 Full Technical Report
 Principal Investigator: Steven A. Hawks
 Project Tracking Number: LDRD 22-DR-011

Abstract
Flowable carbon slurry electrodes promise continuous, scalable electrochemical desalination and
energy storage when their rheology (flow and stability) and electronic connectivity are jointly
optimized. This report integrates six complementary thrusts that we executed under the Laboratory
Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project: (i) a fast, ex situ, centrifuge-compatible
method to quantify pellet (packed slurry) electronic conductivity, with a validated cell constant
(“shape factor”) and quantitative structure–property insights for carbon mixtures; (ii) a controlled
circulation study showing that peristaltic pumping can irreversibly alter particle size and morphology
over laboratory-relevant durations; (iii) development of a miniature jet pump that preserves particle
integrity while achieving entrainment ratios of 2–4 at practical flow rates; (iv) a comprehensive
mapping of how a representative non-ionic surfactant (Tween 20) collapses the carbon gel network,
separating network-percolation losses from intrinsic particle contact resistance; (v) in-situ visu-
alization revealing how gravitational settling and flow-driven mixing compete to control charge
transport, with dynamic bed layers preventing saturation while achieving 30-fold local concentration
enhancement; and (vi) a simple, widely adoptable fabrication route to structured, gas-nitrided
titanium nitride (TiN) titanium current collectors that dramatically lower interfacial losses and
enable geometry-driven performance gains. Together, these results provide a coherent toolchain
and design rules to accelerate formulation screening, de-risk device prototyping, and standard-
ize characterization for carbon slurry electrodes in flow-electrode capacitive deionization (FCDI),
electrochemical flow capacitor (EFC), and related systems.

Background and Research Objectives
Electrochemical systems that employ carbon slurries—including FCDI, EFCs, and semi-solid
batteries—are attractive due to low-cost materials, continuous operation, and the possibility of
tuning composition during operation. However, performance hinges on an intricate coupling of
properties that are rarely characterized in a unified way: (i) electronic percolation through particle
networks; (ii) hydrodynamics and shear-history effects imparted by pumps and flow pathways;
(iii) formulation-dependent gel mechanics and sedimentation; and (iv) current-collector interfacial
resistance, stability, and geometry. This report addresses all four aspects.
 Two persistent bottlenecks motivated the present work. First, there is no widely adopted,
rapid, ex situ method to benchmark the electronic conductivity of packed slurries under controlled
compaction that is simple enough to replicate across laboratories. Second, the field lacks quantitative
guidance on how standard laboratory hardware choices (e.g., peristaltic pumps) and common
additives (e.g., non-ionic surfactants) reshape particle size distributions, network connectivity,
viscosity, and ultimately device performance. Finally, even when formulation and hydrodynamics
are optimized, passivation and geometry constraints of current collectors can dominate losses in
flow devices.

 Objectives. Our goals were to (1) develop and validate a centrifuge-compatible conductivity
cell and model to enable rapid ex situ screening of particulate electrodes; (2) isolate the role of
peristaltic pumping in altering carbon particle size and morphology over extended circulation;
(3) map the coupled rheology–sedimentation–conductivity response of CB slurries to a representative
non-ionic surfactant (Tween 20), and distinguish particle-contact resistance from network-percolation
effects; (4) directly visualize and quantify particle mixing and gravitational settling dynamics during
operation to understand charge transport mechanisms; and (5) create a robust, accessible approach
for TiN-coated AM titanium current collectors in smooth and structured geometries to minimize
interfacial losses and leverage 3D topology [1, 2, 4–6].

Scientific Approach and Accomplishments
1. A centrifuge-compatible method to quantify packed-slurry electronic conduc-
tivity
We designed a 3D-printed electrochemical cell that fits a standard 2 mL swing-bucket rotor and
incorporates two graphite rod electrodes sheathed so only the bottom 2 mm contact the compacted
pellet. After loading aqueous carbon slurries and centrifuging at 4,500 g for 6 min (producing
∼1.3 MPa effective compaction pressure), we measured steady direct current (DC) under 1 V and
inferred effective electronic conductivity from a simulation-derived shape factor (cell constant) that
becomes height-independent for pellets ≳6 mm. Validation with sodium chloride (NaCl) standards
showed <1.2% deviation from expected conductivities [1].
 Key findings. Electronic conductivity increased roughly linearly with pellet mass at low loading
and then saturated at higher mass; ionic strength (0 versus 0.5 M NaCl) had negligible effect,
confirming electron-dominated transport through the carbon network. CB pellets showed ∼5×
higher effective conductivity than activated carbon (AC) at equal mass (e.g., at 0.1 g), consistent
with smaller particle size and lower contact resistance. Notably, at fixed mass, CB/AC mixtures
exhibited a non-monotonic trend with a maximum at ∼60% CB by mass: the mixture outperformed
pure CB by ∼30%. Expressed versus pellet volume fraction, AC exhibited a sharp rise near a critical
range consistent with percolation concepts for compressed powders [1].
 Interpretation. The synergy in CB/AC blends is plausibly due to broader particle-size
distributions and morphological complementarity that densify packing and increase the number
of CB–CB contacts without excessively disrupting CB pathways; beyond the optimum, added AC
dilutes the CB network, and conductivity declines toward the AC limit. The insensitivity to NaCl
concentration and the plateau in the shape factor above ∼6 mm together emphasize that this
method isolates solid-to-solid electronic percolation rather than ionic conduction artifacts [1].

2. Effect of peristaltic pumping on particle size and morphology
We built a closed-loop, stirred-reservoir circulation rig (80 mL) driven by a four-roller peristaltic
pump (60 rpm; ∼460,000 roller–tube compression cycles over 32 h) with polyvinyl chloride (PVC)
tubing (3 mm ID). We compared two representative carbonaceous slurries at 1.5 wt% in water:
(i) nearly spherical CB beads with initial mean diameter ∼580 µm (sieved), and (ii) micron-scale
AC powder (median ∼25–30 µm). We sampled at 0, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 h; analyzed morphology
by optical microscopy; and used laser diffraction to quantify equivalent spherical diameters when
feasible [2].
 Key findings. CB beads underwent rapid fragmentation: within 2 h most beads showed severe
cracking and irregular shapes, and by 8–32 h only fine, dispersed particulates remained. For AC,

the equivalent mean diameter decreased modestly (from 31 to 27 µm; ∼13% reduction over 32 h),
with a larger relative decrease in the 90th percentile (D90 , ∼17%), consistent with attrition of the
coarser tail. Laser-diffraction data for the CB-bead case were biased to small sizes at early times
because large fragments settled too rapidly for reliable sampling [2].
 Implications. Peristaltic pumping can be highly destructive for friable, millimetric carbon
beads and measurably abrasive for micron-scale powders over long durations; both effects alter
rheology, percolation, and device behavior. Researchers should therefore (i) avoid peristaltic pumps
for fragile, bead-like materials; (ii) document pump type, tubing, speed, and duty cycle; and
(iii) re-check particle sizes and morphology before and after extended circulation when benchmarking
flow-electrode performance [2].

3. Miniature jet pump for particle-preserving slurry circulation
Given the particle damage caused by peristaltic pumps, we developed a miniature jet pump that
uses a high-velocity water jet to entrain and pump slurries without mechanical contact. The pump
features no moving solid parts and creates no observable mechanical damage to slurry particles. We
tested CB beads suspended in water at concentrations of 0.0, 0.2, and 2.0 wt% at primary jet flow
rates of 50 and 100 mL/min [3].
 Key findings. The entrainment ratio (slurry-to-jet flow rate ratio, γ = Q2 /Q1 ) and absolute
slurry flow rate decreased approximately linearly with increasing adverse pressure difference. En-
trainment ratio was a strong function of absolute jet flow rate: at 100 mL/min, γ reached 3.5–4.0 at
zero load, compared to 2.5–3.0 at 50 mL/min. Importantly, pump performance was insensitive to
slurry composition within the tested range (0–2 wt% CB), simplifying operation. Micro-particle
image velocimetry (micro-PIV) revealed suppression of a recirculation vortex at higher flow rates,
correlating with improved entrainment performance [3].
 Design implications. The jet pump provides a viable alternative to peristaltic pumps for
laboratory-scale flow electrodes (flow rates of mL/min to L/min), preserving particle integrity while
maintaining practical entrainment ratios. The insensitivity to CB concentration up to 2 wt% and
compatibility with standard tubing and sealing methods make it particularly suited for flow-electrode
applications where particle morphology must be preserved. Future optimization could include needle
length reduction and upstream geometry modifications to suppress vortex formation and enhance
performance [3].

4. Surfactant (Tween 20) control of network mechanics and electronic transport
To decouple interparticle contact resistance from network connectivity, we systematically varied the
concentration of a non-ionic surfactant (Tween 20) in 5 wt% CB slurries formulated in 50 mM NaCl.
We measured (i) steady “flow-through” electronic conductivity in a tubular platinum/platinum-
black conductivity cell at 5 mL min−1 under 50 mV DC bias; (ii) pellet conductivity by the
centrifuged-pellet method described above; (iii) viscosity (shear-rate sweeps) and small-amplitude
oscillatory rheology; (iv) gravitational sedimentation via a force tensiometer with a conical probe;
(v) microstructure by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on dried films; and (vi) device-level
current density in a TiN/Ti flow cell [4].
 Key findings. We observed a critical surfactant concentration (CSC) at ∼1.25 wt% Tween 20
that triggered an abrupt transition from a conductive gel to a nearly Newtonian, resistive fluid:
the settled mass dropped ∼250-fold, viscosity decreased by 1–2 orders of magnitude, and the
flowing-slurry conductivity collapsed by ∼86×. In contrast, the pellet conductivity remained high
(∼20–30 mS cm−1 ) up to 1 wt% Tween 20 and decreased by only ∼3× at 2.5 wt%—implicating

 3

loss of a percolating network (rather than increased contact resistance) as the dominant cause of
conductivity collapse. SEM showed large (10–50 µm) agglomerates at 0–0.5 wt% that vanished at
2 wt%, consistent with network dissolution above the CSC [4].
 Device-level behavior. In a TiN/Ti flow cell (see next section), slurries with 0–1 wt% Tween 20
initially gave strong currents but could degrade during 24 h operation, whereas slurries at 2 wt%
Tween 20 delivered negligible current from the outset. Interestingly, shelf-storage tests showed that
a formulation with 1.1 wt% Tween 20 maintained its initial conductivity over 90 days, while a
surfactant-free control degraded modestly—highlighting a trade-off between long-term dispersion
stability and in-device percolation under flow [4].

5. In-situ visualization of particle mixing and gravitational settling
We developed a novel flow-electrode cell with optical access to directly observe particle dynamics
during operation, revealing how gravitational settling and flow-driven mixing compete to control
charge transport. The cell featured two horizontally co-flowing channels separated by a cation
exchange membrane (CEM), with four independently addressable electrodes and high-speed imaging
capability in the streamwise-transverse plane parallel to gravity [5].
 Key findings. At low flow rates (32–49 g/min), dense yet dynamic particle beds formed on
bottom electrodes, unexpectedly yielding the highest currents despite low system-average particle
concentrations (0.2 wt%). High-speed imaging revealed a highly dynamic bed/free-stream interface
with continuous particle exchange, preventing electrochemical saturation. At high flow rates
(>96 g/min), thin beds and well-mixed flows dominated, with electrode pairs having minimum
separation (P-Q, across the CEM) achieving highest currents. Stopped-flow experiments showed 70%
current reduction, confirming the critical role of advective transport. Local carbon concentrations at
electrodes reached 5.8 wt% at low flow rates through settling, nearly 30× the bulk concentration [5].
 Mechanistic insights. The dynamic bed layer maintained high currents through two mecha-
nisms: (i) gravity-driven concentration enhancement precisely at current collector sites, achieving
effective local concentrations up to 11 wt% within beds while maintaining low system-wide concen-
trations that prevent clogging; and (ii) continuous particle exchange at the bed/free-stream interface,
driven by flow-induced shear that overcomes interparticle friction, enabling charge advection and
preventing saturation. A critical transition occurred near 49–76 g/min where charge transport
shifted from settling-dominated (favoring bottom electrode pairs) to mixing-dominated (favoring
closely-spaced pairs) regimes [5].

6. Gas-nitrided, structured titanium current collectors for low-loss charge transfer
We created current collectors by ordering AM Ti–6Al–4V inserts in arbitrary geometries and gas-
nitriding them in a flowing N2 furnace at 1000 ◦ C for 2 h (5 ◦ C min−1 ramps). Cross-sectional SEM
and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed a conformal ∼350 ± 50 nm TiN layer;
X-ray diffraction showed crystalline TiN with minimal oxide content. We built a two-electrode
flow cell using the nitrided inserts pressed into 3D-printed flow plates, flanking an anion exchange
membrane (AEM), and circulated a 5 wt% CB, 0.5 M NaCl slurry. At 1 V, the TiN-coated smooth
channel sustained ∼19× higher current density than bare Ti (stable over many hours); in 0.5 M
NaCl electrolyte without carbon, TiN showed week-long stability at 1 V with no visible coating
change [6].
 Geometry effects and transport scaling. We compared a smooth U-channel, a truncated
octet-truss “strut” geometry, and a “spiral” fin that forces the slurry to sweep the entire surface. The
spiral delivered ∼2.1× higher current density than the smooth channel, closely matching its ∼2.35×

higher internal surface area, whereas the strut geometry did not improve current and exhibited
slow approach to steady state, likely due to localized stagnation and partial clogging. Current
increased with flow rate with an exponent ≈1/5, intermediate between developing-capacitance and
Ohmic-limited regimes, consistent with mixed transport control and possible advection-enhanced
mixing [6].

Synthesis across thrusts: design rules and a recommended workflow
 • Screen ex situ for electronic percolation. Use the centrifuge-compatible pellet method to
 rapidly rank CB/AC ratios and total loadings on the basis of electronic conductivity, independent
 of ionic strength and packing-height artifacts. Favor compositions near the CB/AC optimum if
 viscosity, settling, and cost permit [1].

 • Choose pumping hardware with care. For friable, millimetric beads, avoid peristaltic
 pumps; consider jet pumps as a particle-preserving alternative with entrainment ratios of 2–4.
 For micron-scale powders, recognize that long-duration peristaltic circulation modestly abrades
 the coarse tail. Document pump parameters and re-measure particle-size distributions when
 comparing performance across studies [2, 3].

 • Dose surfactants below the CSC. Non-ionic surfactants such as Tween 20 can dramatically
 collapse the conductive gel; limit concentrations to stay below the CSC, balancing dispersion
 stability against electronic percolation and device fouling propensity. Use pellet vs. flowing-slurry
 conductivity to separate contact-resistance from network effects [4].

 • Leverage gravitational settling at low flow rates. For settling-dominated regimes, position
 electrodes at channel bottoms to exploit 30-fold local concentration enhancement while main-
 taining low system-wide concentrations that prevent clogging. The dynamic bed/free-stream
 interface prevents saturation through continuous particle exchange [5].

 • Minimize interfacial losses and leverage 3D geometry. Adopt gas-nitrided TiN current
 collectors to overcome Ti passivation and enable arbitrary, smooth geometries that promote
 uniform slurry–collector contact (e.g., spiral designs), avoiding stagnation-prone trusses [6].

Mission Impact
This project directly addresses DOE’s energy and environmental security missions by developing
transformative electrochemical technologies for water treatment and energy storage—both critical to
national infrastructure resilience. The comprehensive toolchain and methodologies developed herein
enable NNSA to create new ways of responding to national security challenges through advanced
materials characterization and system optimization.
 Water Security and Infrastructure Resilience. The rapid ex situ conductivity screening
method and particle-preserving jet pump technology accelerate development of flow-electrode
capacitive deionization (FCDI) systems for distributed, energy-efficient water treatment. These
capabilities are essential for securing water supplies at DOE facilities, military installations, and
critical infrastructure sites vulnerable to contamination events or natural hazards. The 30-fold
concentration enhancement achieved through controlled settling enables unprecedented energy
efficiency in brackish water desalination, reducing vulnerability of water infrastructure to power
disruptions.

 Energy Security through Advanced Storage. The TiN-coated structured current collectors
and optimized slurry formulations directly enhance electrochemical flow capacitors (EFCs) for
grid-scale energy storage. These systems provide rapid-response capabilities essential for grid
resilience against cyberattacks, electromagnetic pulses, and natural hazards. The particle-preserving
pumping methods ensure long-term reliability of energy storage systems critical to maintaining
operational continuity at national security facilities.
 Dual-Use Technology Development. The methods and components developed are immedi-
ately transferrable to both defense and civilian applications, maximizing return on investment. The
centrifuge-cell conductivity method has been adopted by multiple research institutions, establishing
U.S. leadership in flow-electrode characterization standards. The jet pump design enables safe
handling of hazardous slurries in nuclear waste treatment and chemical defense applications.

This project exemplifies NNSA’s commitment to developing science and technology tools and
capabilities to meet future national security challenges, providing both immediate solutions and
foundational knowledge for next-generation electrochemical systems critical to national defense and
infrastructure security [1, 3–6].

Conclusion
We established a coherent toolchain and knowledge base for engineering carbon slurry electrodes.
The centrifuge-cell method provides a simple, validated benchmark for electronic percolation across
compositions and compaction states. Peristaltic pumping was shown to be a hidden variable that
can profoundly and irreversibly alter particle size and morphology, especially for friable bead-like
carbons. The miniature jet pump offers a particle-preserving alternative for slurry circulation with
practical entrainment ratios. Non-ionic surfactants were revealed to be double-edged: beneficial
for shelf stability but capable of catastrophic network collapse above a critical concentration.
Direct visualization demonstrated that gravitational settling can achieve 30-fold local concentration
enhancement at electrodes while maintaining clog-free operation through dynamic bed regeneration.
And gas-nitrided AM titanium current collectors unlock durable, low-resistance interfaces and
geometry-enabled gains in flowing slurry devices. Collectively, these advances provide durable
building blocks for rapid, reproducible formulation screening and device prototyping in FCDI, EFC,
and related electrochemical platforms [1–6].
 Future directions. Priorities include: (i) extending the pellet-conductivity protocol to non-
carbon particles (e.g., redox-active oxides) and to mixed-solvent and non-aqueous systems; (ii) cou-
pling in situ optical/electrical percolation measurements with controlled shear histories to unify
rheology and conduction; (iii) expanding surfactant and polymer-dispersant screens across chemistries
to map CSCs and fouling tendencies; (iv) optimizing jet pump geometries and exploring other
non-contact pumping alternatives (gear, diaphragm) and pulsation dampers to minimize degradation
while preserving flow control; (v) optimizing flow rates and electrode placement to exploit the
settling-to-mixing transition for maximum charge transport; and (vi) scaling TiN-coated, spiral-like
collector geometries to multi-channel stacks, including compatibility and durability studies with
common ion-exchange membranes.
