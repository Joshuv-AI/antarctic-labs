Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of a mutant reveals the potential mechanism of endosperm content increase in sweet-waxy double recessive maize
Authors: Zhihong Wang, Baohong Lu, Rushan Du, Lei Hou, Zhiyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe shrunken kernel phenotype in double recessive sweet-waxy maize (sh2sh2wxwx), caused by impaired starch biosynthesis, severely limits its commercial value. Methods: To explore mechanisms for overcoming this limitation, we characterized a novel mutant, M4-6, derived from EMS mutagenesis, which exhibits significantly increased kernel weight. An integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis was performed on 20-day-after-pollination endosperms of M4-6 and its wild-type (WT) counterpart. Results: The mutant displayed a 41.4% increase in dry grain weight, accompanied by elevated levels of soluble sugars (43.4%), sucrose (21.2%), and a marked enhancement in ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) content (85.3%) and activity (45.2%). Transcriptome analysis revealed 2,055 differentially expressed genes, predominantly enriched in carbon metabolism, amino acid degradation (e.g., valine, leucine, and isoleucine), and transport (e.g., ABC transporters) pathways. Metabolomic profiling identified 68 differential metabolites, indicating enhanced turnover of carbon compounds, with depletion of intermediates like succinic acid and serine alongside accumulation of metabolites such as β-alanine and tartaric acid. Integrated analysis suggested that the mutant orchestrates a metabolic shift characterized by a state of heightened carbon flux and interconversion, driven by augmented AGPase activity and transcriptional reprogramming of starch and sucrose metabolism. Conclusions: Our study reveals that the M4-6 mutant achieves improved kernel filling through a multifaceted reprogramming of central metabolism, providing a theoretical basis and candidate pathways for potentially overcoming the post-harvest shriveling defect and improving kernel quality of sweet-waxy maize.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09231-0.

Introduction

The double recessive sweet-waxy (sh2sh2wxwx) maize, characterized by simultaneous loss-of-function mutations in the key starch synthesis genes Sh2 (encoding the large subunit of ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, AGPase) and Wx (encoding granule-bound starch synthase, GBSS), exhibits unique breeding advantages [1, 2]. Utilizing this germplasm as a parent, the derived “sweet-waxy” hybrid corn produces a single ear with both sweet and waxy kernels, a phenotype that effectively meets the diverse demands of consumers [3, 4]. However, this exceptional market quality is accompanied by a critical postharvest physiological defect: the sh2 mutation-impaired accumulation of endosperm dry matter causes the sweet kernels to rapidly dehydrate and shrivel after harvesting, which significantly shortens shelf life and restricts the application of processing techniques such as vacuum packaging [5]. Therefore, elucidating the limiting factors for dry matter accumulation in the double recessive sweet-waxy endosperm and exploring the underlying mechanisms for breaking this limitation are of critical importance for the genetic improvement of this crop.

In maize, the accumulation of endosperm dry matter centers on starch biosynthesis. The rate-limiting step of starch biosynthesis in the endosperm is catalyzed by ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase), which converts glucose-1-phosphate (G-1-P) and ATP to ADP-glucose (ADP-Glc), the activated glucosyl donor for starch synthesis [6, 7]. In the classical model, the sh2 mutation drastically reduces AGPase activity, creating a bottleneck at the entry point of the starch synthesis pathway. This leads to an accumulation of upstream metabolites, such as sucrose and hexose phosphates, and a severe reduction in starch deposition, resulting in the characteristic shrunken kernel phenotype [8–10]. Although studies indicate that the sh2 mutant retains residual AGPase activity [11], sufficient for synthesizing trace amounts of branched starch [12, 13], this residual activity is far from sufficient to support the kernel plumpness and typical waxy texture required for market quality.

Despite these inherent limitations, recent studies suggest potential strategies to enhance endosperm filling even within the sh2sh2wxwx genetic background. Allosteric control (e.g., 3-PGA/Pi), thermal stability, subunit composition, and post-translational regulation (e.g., phosphorylation) have the potential to enhance AGPase activity and abundance, thereby increasing the content of waxy starch in the genetic background of sweet and waxy double recessive traits [14–18]. In addition, AGPase-independent starch synthesis pathways also exist in the endosperm, and their enhancement could contribute to greater kernel plumpness. The identification and characterization of novel genetic variants that modulate these regulatory mechanisms or alternative pathways therefore offer a promising strategy to overcome the dry matter accumulation bottleneck.

In this study, we isolated an EMS-induced mutant, designated M4-6, from a sweet-waxy double recessive inbred line. This mutant exhibits a significant increase in kernel weight, indicating a potential breakthrough in endosperm filling capacity within this constrained genetic background. We hypothesize that the improved phenotype of M4-6 results from enhanced AGPase functionality, a reprogramming of carbon metabolism, or the activation of alternative starch synthesis pathways, collectively overcoming the bottleneck imposed by the sh2 mutation. To test this hypothesis, we employed an integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic approach to compare the mutant with its wild-type counterpart. This study aims to systematically decipher the molecular and metabolic basis underlying the enhanced endosperm filling in M4-6, thereby providing valuable insights and candidate targets for the genetic improvement of sweet-waxy double recessive maize.

Methods

Plant materials and experimental design

The wild-type (WT) control used in this study was LJ/TY32, a sweet-waxy double recessive maize inbred line (genotype: sh2sh2wxwx) independently developed and maintained by the Maize Research Institute, Shanxi Agricultural University. This line was constructed by crossing two parental inbred lines from our institute’s germplasm bank: the waxy maize inbred line ‘LJ’ (carrying the waxy (wx) mutation) and the sweet corn inbred line ‘TY32’ (carrying the shrunken-2 (sh2) mutation). The F1 hybrid was then self-pollinated for over ten generations to fix the double recessive genotype and achieve homozygosity. The M4-6 mutant was generated by ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis of the WT (LJ/TY32) seeds and systematically screened for increased kernel weight over four consecutive generations (Fig. 1A).

All materials were cultivated during the summer of 2023 at the experimental station in Xiaotan Village, Xinzhou City, Shanxi Province. WT and M4-6 were planted in adjacent plots, with 30 plants each, at a spacing of 50 cm between rows and 40 cm between plants. Plants with uniform growth were selected and self-pollinated on the same day. At 20 days after pollination (DAP), five uniformly developed ears from both WT and M4-6 were randomly selected, and endosperm tissues from the middle section of the ears were collected. The samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent use.

For biochemical parameter measurements, three biological replicates were used, each consisting of 10 endosperms pooled from a single ear. For transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, three independent biological replicates were also used, each consisting of endosperm tissue from a single ear. Specifically, three ears were randomly chosen from the five harvested ears per genotype for RNA-seq and metabolomic analyses, with each selected ear serving as one biological replicate (n = 3). Thus, each biological replicate represents an independent ear, ensuring that biological variation among individual ears is captured.

Determination of biochemical parameters

The contents of sucrose, soluble sugar, and amylopectin were determined using commercial assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, measurements were performed by the resorcinol colorimetric method (Cat. No. BC2465), anthrone colorimetric method (Cat. No. BC0030), and dual-wavelength spectrophotometry (Cat. No. BC4275), respectively. The AGPase content and AGPase activity were assessed using an assay kit (Cat. No. ml20341) obtained from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

RNA Extraction and Quality Control: Total RNA was extracted from endosperm samples using the TAB-PBiozol reagent. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.

Library Construction and Sequencing: Poly(A)-tailed mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The enriched mRNA was fragmented and reverse-transcribed into first- and second-strand cDNA with random primers. The purified double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, adenylation, and adapter ligation, followed by PCR amplification to construct the final cDNA libraries. Libraries that passed quality control were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a PE150 strategy.

Bioinformatic Analysis: Raw sequencing data were processed using the bioinformatic pipeline provided by the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net). Clean data were obtained by filtering raw reads and were subsequently aligned to the designated reference genome (GCF_902167145.1, accessed October 16, 2024) to generate mapped data. Downstream analyses included library quality assessment, structural-level analysis, differential expression analysis, gene functional annotation, and functional enrichment.

qRT-PCR Validation: To validate the RNA-seq results, ten DEGs were selected for qRT-PCR. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (TUREscript, Aidlab, China). qPCR was performed using 2×SYBR Green PreMix (DF, China) on a FQD-96 C real-time PCR system (Bioer Technology, China). The cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, with a melt curve analysis. The 18 S rRNA gene was used as the internal reference, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S5.

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolite Extraction: Fifty milligrams of freeze-dried endosperm powder was homogenized with 1 mL of a pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2:2:1, v/v/v). The mixture was vortexed and subsequently subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min at 4 °C. Following incubation at -20 °C for 10 min, the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and dried using a vacuum centrifugal concentrator. Prior to mass spectrometric analysis, the dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 µL of an acetonitrile/water solution (acetonitrile: water = 1:1, v/v), followed by centrifugation at 14,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min. The final supernatant was injected for analysis.

Chromatographic Separation: Metabolite extracts were separated using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system. To achieve broad coverage of metabolites with diverse polarities, a complementary strategy employing both HILIC and C18 reversed-phase chromatography was applied to each sample to maximize metabolite detection. For HILIC-based separation, the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and an injection volume of 2 µL. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 10% acetonitrile in water containing 2 mM ammonium formate and (B) acetonitrile with 0.4% formic acid. For C18-based separation, the column temperature was set at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and an injection volume of 2 µL. The mobile phase comprised (A) water with 5 mM ammonium acetate and (B) 99.5% acetonitrile. Gradient elution programs were used for both methods.

Mass Spectrometric Detection: Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using an AB 6500 + QTRAP mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The ESI source parameters were set as follows: source temperature, 580 °C; ion source gas 1 (GS1), 45; ion source gas 2 (GS2), 60; curtain gas (CUR), 35; and ion spray voltage (IS), + 4500 V or -4500 V for positive and negative ionization modes, respectively. Data acquisition was conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode.

Data Processing and Bioinformatics Analysis: Peak integration for MRM transitions was performed using MultiQuant software. The ratio of the peak area of each metabolite to that of the internal standard was calculated, and metabolite concentrations were determined based on their respective standard curves. Subsequently, differential metabolites were screened, followed by bioinformatic analysis of these identified metabolites.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). For biochemical parameters (grain weight, sugar content, AGPase content and activity), significant differences between the mutant and wild-type were determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. For transcriptomic and metabolomic data, DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log₂FC| > 2 and FDR < 0.01, while differential metabolites were screened using OPLS-DA with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05.Results.

Comparison of grain weight and major endosperm components between mutant and wild type

The mutant line M4-6 displayed significant differences in key agronomic and physiological parameters relative to the wild-type (WT; Fig. 1B-H). The dry grain weight and fresh grain weight of the mutant were significantly higher, with increases of 41.4% and 30.9%, respectively.

Analysis of endosperm composition revealed that the mutant accumulated 43.4% more soluble sugar and 21.2% more sucrose than the wild-type. While the inter-group difference in amylopectin content was not statistically significant, the measured value in the mutant was 19.2% higher.

Additionally, the content of ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) was 85.3% greater, and its enzymatic activity was 45.2% higher in the mutant. These results demonstrated that the M4-6 mutant has a clear advantage in grain weight accumulation and endosperm carbon metabolism. Notably, there was no significant difference in grain number per ear and grain weight per ear between the mutant and wild-type (Supplementary Table S1).

Transcriptome analysis of endosperm in mutant and wild type

Transcriptome sequencing and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Following the removal of low-quality reads, a total of 45.52 Gb of clean data was obtained. The single-base sequencing error rate was below 0.01% across all samples, with over 92.93% of bases achieving a Q30 quality score. The guanine-cytosine (GC) content ranged from 51.36% to 53.16%. The alignment rate of reads to the reference genome was between 86.60% and 89.49%. These metrics collectively confirmed the high quality of the transcriptome data and its suitability for subsequent analyses. Comparative analysis identified 2,055 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the mutant compared to the wild-type, consisting of 1,014 up-regulated and 1,041 down-regulated genes (Figure S1C).

Verification of DEGs by qRT-PCR

To independently validate the reliability of our RNA-seq results, we selected ten key differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising six upregulated and four downregulated genes, for qPCR analysis. The fold-change trends in gene expression (Log2 Fold Change) measured by qPCR were highly consistent with those obtained from RNA-seq. Linear regression analysis revealed a strong correlation between the two methods (R² = 0.7956, Figure S1D), confirming that the RNA-seq data accurately reflected the transcriptomic differences between the sample groups.

Functional classification of DEGs via KEGG pathway analysis

According to the KEGG-based classification, the majority of DEGs were mapped to the Metabolism category. Within this superclass, prominent subpathways included Carbon metabolism, Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Starch and sucrose metabolism, Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and Glutathione metabolism, all of which constituted substantial proportions. Notably, several key pathways across other major functional domains also displayed significant DEG enrichment: Environmental Information Processing (e.g., Plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway–plant); Genetic Information Processing (e.g., DNA replication, Homologous recombination); and Organismal Systems (specifically, Plant–pathogen interaction). Nevertheless, the aggregate contribution of these non-metabolic categories was markedly lower than that of Metabolism (Fig. 2A).

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs via KEGG pathways

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that up-regulated DEGs were significantly enriched in multiple metabolic pathways. The top 20 most significantly enriched pathways, ranked by q-value, are shown in (Fig. 2B). Among these, the most significantly enriched pathways in up-regulated DEGs were primarily related to carbon metabolism and transport, including ABC transporters (ko02010), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (ko00630), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010), beta-alanine metabolism (ko00410), carbon metabolism (ko01200), fatty acid degradation (ko00071), pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (ko00770), valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation (ko00280), and fructose and mannose metabolism (ko00051).

Several of these pathways are directly relevant to the M4-6 phenotype. The enrichment of ABC transporters (ko02010) and carbon metabolism pathways (ko01200, ko00010, ko00051) aligns with the observed increases in soluble sugar (43.4%) and sucrose (21.2%) accumulation, as these pathways are involved in sugar transport and central carbon catabolism. The enrichment of amino acid degradation pathways (ko00280, ko00410) and cofactor biosynthesis (ko00770) suggests enhanced carbon flux through catabolic routes, which may provide carbon skeletons and energy for endosperm filling. In addition, the enrichment of glutathione metabolism (ko00480) may reflect cellular stress responses associated with elevated sugar levels in the mutant endosperm.

Analysis of the sh2 gene in mutant and wild-type lines

Based on the reference sequence (NM_001127632.1, associated with normal kernel phenotype), we reconstructed the full-length sh2 transcript sequences using the RNA-seq data for both the mutant and wild-type lines (Figure S3). Sequence alignment revealed a high degree of conservation within the coding region of the sh2 gene between the two lines. Notably, compared to the reference, both sequences shared an identical single-nucleotide variation in the region encoding the protein (P55241.1), which provides a molecular explanation for their common sh2 mutant phenotype (shrunken kernels). However, neither the mutant nor the wild-type sequence contained large deletions relative to the reference, indicating that the core domains of the AGPase large subunit, such as substrate-binding or catalytic sites, remained largely intact. This may account for the partial AGPase activity retained in both lines. The relative integrity of the wild-type sh2 nucleotide sequence also provided a structural basis and technical feasibility for partially restoring its function through EMS mutagenesis.

Although the overall gene expression level of AGPase in the mutant showed no significant upregulation compared to the wild type, and preliminary sequence alignment did not identify other definitive nucleotide differences within the sh2 coding sequence, further transcriptomic depth analysis revealed significantly higher transcript abundance of the sh2 gene in the mutant compared to the wild type at specific regions (e.g., nucleotide positions 288–369, and 1266–1368; Supplementary Table S2). IGV visualization revealed region‑specific differences in strand‑specific splicing signals between the mutant and wild‑type, including loss of a wild‑type‑specific junction (Figure S4A), enhanced bidirectional junction usage and coverage (Figure S4B), and strand‑preference inversion with a mutant‑specific blue arc (Figure S4C‑D). The increased AGPase protein level and enzymatic activity in the mutant may be associated with these observed changes. However, rMATS analysis detected no statistically significant alternative splicing events in sh2, including exon skipping (SE), intron retention (RI), or alternative 5′/3′ splice sites (FDR > 0.05 for all event types).

Metabolome analysis of endosperm in mutant and wild type

Global metabolite profiling

To profile the metabolic changes associated with the enhanced grain filling phenotype, targeted metabolomic analysis was performed on endosperm tissues dissected from 20-day-after-pollination (DAP) kernels of the M4-6 mutant and wild-type (WT) plants. A total of 337 metabolites were identified in both the mutant and wild-type samples (Table S3). Four major classes—carboxylic acids and derivatives, fatty acyls, organooxygen compounds, and purine nucleotides—collectively accounted for over 50% of all detected metabolites. Principal component analysis (PCA) performed on this dataset revealed a clear separation between the mutant and wild-type samples, with principal component 1 (PC1) and principal component 2 (PC2) explaining 36.9% and 24.9% of the total variance, respectively (Figure S2C).

Differential metabolite identification

Differential metabolites between the mutant and wild-type were identified based on a threshold of p < 0.05, yielding a total of 68 compounds (Figure S2A). Among these, 15 metabolites were upregulated and 53 were downregulated in the mutant. A complete list of all differential metabolites is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

The most prominent metabolic changes in the M4-6 mutant were observed in the following categories:

Amino acid metabolism: Intermediates of branched-chain amino acid degradation (3-methyl-2-oxobutanoic acid, methylmalonic acid, ketoleucine, and 3-methyl-2-oxovaleric acid) were significantly depleted, while β-alanine accumulated. This pattern indicates enhanced carbon flux through valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation pathways.

Carbohydrate metabolism: The mutant accumulated higher levels of melibiose, galactitol, and sorbitol, while stachyose was significantly decreased. These changes suggest a shift in oligosaccharide profiles that may contribute to osmotic regulation.

TCA cycle and organic acids: Succinic acid and serine were decreased, whereas tartaric acid accumulated, reflecting enhanced metabolic turnover of carbon compounds.

Polyamine metabolism: Putrescine and spermidine accumulated in the mutant, indicating enhanced polyamine synthesis, which may function in stress adaptation and cellular homeostasis.

Nucleotide and vitamin metabolism: Pantothenic acid (VB5) was significantly depleted, consistent with the enrichment of the pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis pathway observed in the transcriptomic analysis ("Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs via KEGG pathways" section).

Several other metabolites (including D-Ribose, Raffinose, Glucose 1-phosphate, Cellobiose, Glucose 6-phosphate, Galactose 1-phosphate, Mannose 6-phosphate, and Fructose 6-phosphate) showed elevated mean abundance in the mutant, but these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to high variability across biological replicates. No significant differences were observed for sucrose, maltotriose, or D-fructose between the mutant and wild-type at this developmental stage.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis

To systematically decipher the molecular basis of enhanced kernel filling in the M4-6 mutant, an integrated KEGG-based analysis was performed on the transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets. This analysis identified a coordinated reprogramming of central carbon metabolism. Among the significantly enriched pathways, five—Valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation (ko00280), β-Alanine metabolism (ko00410), Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (ko00770), ABC transporters (ko02010), and Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (ko00630)—were co-enriched in both transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets (Figs. 2B and 3C). Additionally, two pathways central to carbohydrate metabolism—Carbon metabolism (ko01200) and Starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500)—were also significantly enriched. The integrated patterns of change were summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Enhanced interconversion among carbon-containing compounds

The integrated data revealed a significant enhancement in metabolic turnover and conversion between different classes of carbon compounds. This was evidenced by the co-enrichment and inverse expression patterns in key catabolic pathways.

In the Valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation pathway (ko00280), eight genes were significantly upregulated in the mutant, while the downstream carbon-containing intermediates—3-Methyl-2-oxobutanoic acid, Methylmalonic acid, Ketoleucine, and 3-Methyl-2-oxovaleric acid—were significantly depleted (Supplementary Table S3). Similarly, in the Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism pathway (ko00630), thirteen genes were upregulated and three were downregulated. Notably, formate dehydrogenase 2 (LOC103627631) a key enzyme in formate catabolism, was among the most significantly upregulated genes (log₂FC = 2.94, FDR = 1.28e-65). Metabolically, this pathway showed a significant reduction in Serine and Succinic acid, alongside an accumulation of Tartaric acid. This consistent pattern indicated an intensified metabolic flux where carbon skeletons are rapidly processed and redirected.

A similar trend was observed in β-Alanine metabolism (ko00410) and Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (ko00770), where gene upregulation (7 and 10 genes, respectively) coincided with increased abundance of Beta-Alanine and depletion of 3-Methyl-2-oxobutanoic acid and Pantothenic acid (VB5) (Supplementary Table S3), further supporting active carbon channeling into specific biosynthetic routes.

Within the broader Carbon metabolism pathway (ko01200), 24 genes were upregulated and 11 were downregulated in the mutant (Supplementary Table S4). The coordinated upregulation of genes involved in glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and organic acid metabolism further supported the notion of enhanced carbon flux through central metabolic routes.

Transcriptional reprogramming of starch and sucrose metabolism

Consistent with the physiological phenotype (Fig. 1C), the Starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (ko00500) exhibited distinct transcriptional reprogramming. Ten genes were upregulated, while fourteen genes were downregulated in the mutant (Supplementary Table S4). Among the upregulated genes, ATP-dependent 6-phosphofructokinase 5 (LOC100277929), a key glycolytic enzyme, showed a 1.89-fold increase (log₂FC = 1.89, FDR = 1.30e-26), suggesting enhanced carbon flux through glycolysis. Metabolomic analysis further revealed that the mutant accumulated higher levels of melibiose, galactitol, and sorbitol, while stachyose was significantly decreased (Supplementary Table S3).

Together, these integrated analyses revealed that the M4-6 mutant exhibited a metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced carbon flux through catabolic pathways and transcriptional activation of starch and sucrose metabolism,.

Discussion

This study employed an integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and biochemical phenotyping approach to systematically elucidate the metabolic reprogramming mechanism in the endosperm of a double‑recessive sweet‑waxy maize mutant during the critical grain‑filling stage at 20 days after pollination. The observed increases in kernel weight and sugar content were driven by an active carbon‑management strategy. This strategy accelerates central carbon metabolism to utilize incoming sucrose, channeling it into starch and energy production, while diverting excess carbon toward osmotically compatible small molecules. Such coordinated regulation likely alleviates feedback inhibition on sustained sucrose import imposed by the high‑sugar environment, ultimately leading to a synchronized enhancement of sink strength and dry matter accumulation.

A state of heightened metabolic flux and carbon redirection

A defining feature of the M4-6 mutant is a globally enhanced carbon flux through several interconnected catabolic pathways, including valine/leucine/isoleucine degradation (ko00280) and glyoxylate/dicarboxylate metabolism (ko00630). The inverse relationship between upregulated genes and depleted downstream intermediates ("Enhanced interconversion among carbon-containing compounds" section) is a diagnostic signature of a substrate‑limited, high‑turnover state, where carbon skeletons are rapidly withdrawn from these pathways rather than allowed to accumulate [19, 20].

Why would the mutant accelerate such catabolic routes? One compelling explanation is that it actively redirects carbon toward soluble sugars and compatible solutes. The metabolomic data ("Differential metabolite identification" section) show elevated levels of sucrose, melibiose, galactitol and sorbitol, while stachyose decreases. In cereal endosperm, sucrose serves both as a carbon source and as a signalling molecule; its accumulation can feed back to inhibit its own import [21]. By channeling surplus carbon into alternative osmotically active compounds, the mutant likely alleviates this auto‑inhibition, thereby maintaining a sustained sucrose influx that fuels both starch synthesis and energy production. This interpretation is consistent with the observation that plants often convert excess sucrose into osmotically less active sugars such as trehalose or redirect it into other soluble sugars to fine-tune sugar signaling and avoid osmotic stress [22–24].

Thus, the enhanced carbon flux observed in the M4-6 mutant is not merely a metabolic curiosity — it directly supports the increased sink strength that underlies the 41.4% higher grain weight. The diversion of carbon toward polyamines (putrescine, spermidine) and stress‑associated glutathione metabolism further suggests that the mutant has also engaged adaptive mechanisms to cope with the elevated sugar environment.

It is noteworthy that the biochemically determined sucrose content showed a 21.2% increase in the mutant (Fig. 1E), while the targeted metabolomic analysis did not detect a statistically significant difference in sucrose levels. This discrepancy arises from fundamental differences between the two analytical methods. The resorcinol colorimetric assay (Cat. No. BC2465) measures total fructose-based soluble sugars after an alkaline pre-treatment that destroys reducing sugars and subsequent acid hydrolysis of sucrose, providing a bulk measurement of sucrose content. In contrast, the targeted LC-MS/MS method quantifies specific sucrose molecular ions, which can be subject to matrix effects, ion suppression, and differences in extraction efficiency for labile primary metabolites. Moreover, biochemical measurements used larger sample sizes (n = 3, each replicate pooling 10 endosperms from one ear), while metabolomic analysis used individual endosperms (n = 3), making the latter more susceptible to biological variation. Therefore, the biochemically determined value is likely a more accurate reflection of the bulk sucrose level in the endosperm, and the 21.2% increase is considered reliable.

Enhanced AGPase activity and starch metabolism reprogramming

The enhanced AGPase content (85.3%) and activity (45.2%) observed in our mutant are consistent with occasional reports of increased seed mass in AGPase-related mutants [25]. As described in("Analysis of the sh2 gene in mutant and wild-type lines" section), the observed increases could potentially be associated with elevated transcription of specific regions of the sh2 gene, even though overall AGPase gene expression was not significantly upregulated and no statistically significant alternative splicing events were detected by rMATS. Additionally, the enhancements might also result from post‑translational allosteric regulation of AGPase by cellular metabolites such as 3‑PGA and Pi [26, 27]. This regulatory mode aligns with our metabolomic data showing a general upward trend of soluble sugars and starch precursors, suggesting that the enhanced biosynthetic machinery efficiently consumes available precursors, generating a potent “pull” force on central carbon metabolism.

An alternative possibility is that EMS mutagenesis may have targeted sugar transporter proteins [28, 29], a hypothesis supported by the significant upregulation of ABC transporters (ko02010) in our transcriptome data. Such a modification could enhance sugar transport efficiency, compensating for the carbon flow blockage caused by the sh2 mutation.

Collectively, the establishment of this augmented enzymatic sink is further corroborated by the transcriptional reprogramming of the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (ko00500) described in ("Transcriptional reprogramming of starch and sucrose metabolism" section), including the upregulation of ATP-dependent 6-phosphofructokinase 5 (LOC100277929). This transcriptional reprogramming indicates a fine‑tuning of resource allocation that preferentially directs metabolic flux toward starch synthesis.

Conclusions and future perspectives

In conclusion, the M4-6 mutant illustrates a case of “systemic compensation”—where a network of metabolic adaptations collectively mitigates the severe bottleneck imposed by a single-gene defect (sh2). Based on the integrated multi-omics data, we propose a hypothetical model that suggests a link between the causal mutation and key gene expression changes, altered metabolite profiles, enriched KEGG pathways, and ultimately the enhanced kernel filling phenotype (Fig. 4). These findings suggest a theoretical strategy to counteract the post-harvest kernel shriveling defect of sweet-waxy maize, and provide a conceptual framework with candidate pathways for future breeding efforts to improve kernel plumpness and shelf life.

A major limitation of this study is that the causal mutation in M4-6 has not yet been identified. Preliminary sequence analysis has excluded coding-region mutations in Sh2 and other key starch synthesis genes, suggesting that the mutation likely resides in a cis-regulatory element or a trans-acting factor. Another limitation is the static, single-time-point design of the multi-omics analysis. Future work must dynamically resolve the metabolic trajectory to determine if the observed reprogramming initiates or sustains improved filling. Most importantly, identifying the causal mutation in M4-6 is the critical next step. This discovery could reveal a master regulator of sink strength and provide a direct genetic tool for breeding. Functional validation of key candidate pathways (e.g., polyamine biosynthesis, specific ABC transporters) and direct measurement of carbon import rates via isotopic tracing will be essential to transform these correlative insights into causal understanding and applicable breeding strategies for improving yield and quality in sweet-waxy and related crops.
