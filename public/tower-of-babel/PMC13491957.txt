Sustaining values-driven innovation in primary healthcare: a protocol for identifying implementation strategies for long-term impact (longitudinal participatory study)
Authors: Nicky Van Broeck, Birgitte Schoenmakers, Pauline Boeckxstaens, Patricia De Vriendt, Dominique Van de Velde, Sibyl Anthierens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResponding to system pressures, fragmentation, and growing project fatigue in primary care, this study examines how values-driven innovations (VDIs) can be successfully embedded and sustained by (1) defining what constitutes a values-driven innovation, (2) identifying strategies and mechanisms that support long-term implementation, and (3) co-developing evidence-based sustainability indicators with stakeholders. By integrating four complementary theoretical frameworks and examining the full lifecycle from conceptualization to long-term sustainment, it generates actionable insights to strengthen enduring, values-based transformation in primary care.Methods and analysisThis study uses a longitudinal, theory-informed, multi-stakeholder design. Over three years, we follow selected VDIs from conceptualization through long-term sustainment, using multiple data sources and repeated timepoints to capture dynamic implementation processes. An integrated conceptual framework: combining realist evaluation, the Dynamic Sustainability Framework, Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, and Normalization Process Theory, guides all phases. The three-phase design includes: (1) defining VDI and mapping the innovation landscape; (2) conducting longitudinal case studies using document analysis, serial interviews, and questionnaires; and (3) co-creating an actionable sustainability model and monitoring indicators with stakeholders. Continuous reflexive practice, triangulation, and participatory engagement ensure rigor and relevance.DiscussionDespite the analytical complexity of using multiple frameworks, this integration provides a comprehensive understanding of sustainability and contextual adaptation in primary care.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Antwerp (reference number 2025–7124).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-01003-z.Keywords: Primary care, Implementation science, Sustainment, Valuesdriven innovation

Contributions to the literature• Advances sustainability science by examining the critical but understudied transition period between initial implementation and long-term normalization, revealing how innovations evolve and adapt over time rather than treating sustainability as a binary outcome.• Develops an innovative integrated framework that synthesizes realist, process-oriented, and contextual theories (DSF, CFIR, NPT, realist evaluation) to capture both mechanisms of change and dynamic adaptation processes across the full innovation lifecycle.• Co-produces practical tools with stakeholders to operationalize sustainability monitoring in real-world primary care settings, addressing the implementation-to-practice gap through participatory indicator development.

Introduction

The transformation challenge in primary care

Healthcare systems worldwide face mounting pressure to transform primary care delivery in response to aging populations, rising chronic disease prevalence, and increasing multimorbidity [1, 2]. Primary care, which the WHO describes as “a key process in the health system that supports first-contact, accessible, continued, comprehensive and coordinated patient-focused care”, sits at the heart of this transformation challenge [3]. The question is no longer whether primary care must change, but how to achieve changes that endure beyond pilot phases and genuinely improve care delivery.

In Belgium, while people with chronic conditions report experiencing good quality primary care that is focused on their needs, with results comparable to the OECD Patient-Reported Indicators Surveys (PaRIS) average, significant systemic challenges persist [4]. The healthcare system continues to over-rely on hospitals for primary care services, creating inefficiencies [3]. Moreover, the primary care system in Flanders is under considerable pressure, characterized by fragmentation and poor coordination [4, 5]. Belgium’s low maturity level in integrated care illustrates the urgent need for systematic change and innovation [5].

The promise of values-driven Innovation

Primary care is fundamentally anchored in core values including first contact access, continuity, comprehensiveness, coordination, community engagement, patient-centeredness, and management of complexity [6, 7]. These values offer a promising foundation for addressing current healthcare challenges by moving beyond single-disease frameworks toward more holistic, integrated approaches [2, 8]. Values-driven innovations (VDI) (interventions specifically designed to uphold and strengthen these core primary care values on micro-, meso-, and macrolevel) represent a strategic approach to healthcare transformation [9, 10]. Rather than pursuing innovation for its own sake, VDI ensures that changes remain grounded in what matters most: the fundamental principles that make primary care effective.

The sustainability crisis: when good ideas don’t last

Despite recognition of the need for values-driven change in primary care, a critical gap exists between demonstrating that innovations work and ensuring they become sustainably embedded within healthcare systems once pilots conclude [11, 12]. Healthcare providers and stakeholders across micro-, meso-, and macro-levels in Flanders increasingly experience “project fatigue” [13–15]. This fatigue stems from the complexity of achieving sustainable change in contexts heavily influenced by local differences, and the frequent failure of innovations to persist beyond initial implementation phases, often due to discontinued funding [16]. Consequently, fostering a culture that embraces sustained change has become increasingly challenging [17, 18].

The sustainability challenge is particularly acute for values-driven innovations in primary care. While numerous pilot projects have successfully demonstrated VDI potential during implementation phases, ensuring long-term sustainability remains elusive. As Ilot et al. observe, the lack of sustained interventions represents “a waste of time, financial resources and leadership effort at a time of economic austerity” [19]. This sustainability gap not only undermines the potential benefits of successful innovations but also contributes to the project fatigue that impedes future transformation efforts. When stakeholders repeatedly invest energy in changes that ultimately fade, their capacity and willingness to engage in subsequent innovations diminishes, creating a vicious cycle that threatens primary care’s ability to evolve.

Context - the primary care academy’s innovation journey

The Daniel De Coninck Fund, managed by the King Baudouin Foundation, has been instrumental in supporting values-driven innovations in primary care, with particular attention to sustainable implementation. The Primary Care Academy (PCA), funded by this initiative, serves as a knowledge center comprising a consortium of researchers dedicated to primary healthcare innovation. Previous research within the PCA has examined the implementation of goal-oriented care in primary care settings, focusing primarily on development and early implementation phases [11, 20]. This foundational work has revealed important insights about how innovations take root, but questions remain about how to ensure these innovations achieve long-term sustainability.

Building on this foundation, the current study shifts focus from initial implementation to the complete lifecycle of values-driven innovations—from conceptualization through sustained embedding in everyday practice. This represents a critical evolution: understanding not just how innovations begin, but how they endure, adapt, and ultimately become the “new normal” in primary care delivery.

The knowledge gap: understanding sustainable implementation

Understanding how healthcare organizations, providers, policymakers, and community stakeholders can work together to sustain values-driven innovations represents a critical knowledge gap in implementation science. While substantial research examines implementation processes, far less attention has been paid to what happens after initial implementation, the sustainability phase where many innovations falter [21]. Moreover, existing sustainability research often treats it as a static endpoint to be achieved, rather than recognizing it as a dynamic, ongoing process of adaptation and alignment [22].

This study addresses these gaps through an integrated, longitudinal investigation guided by the following research question: How do organizations achieve the sustainable implementation of values-driven innovations to improve healthcare delivery in primary care?

Study objectives and research questions

To comprehensively address this central question, the study pursues four interconnected objectives, each building on the previous:

Objective 1: establish conceptual clarity

We seek to understand how stakeholders across the primary care system perceive and define values-driven innovations. Without shared understanding of what VDI means in practice, sustainability efforts lack coherent direction.

RQ1: How do stakeholders perceive and define values-driven innovations within primary care settings?

Objective 2: identify sustainability strategies

We aim to document the full range of strategies that healthcare organizations and stakeholders employ—both planned and improvised—to sustain VDIs over time. This includes understanding when and why strategies are adapted in response to changing contexts.

RQ2: What strategies do healthcare organizations and stakeholders employ to sustain values-driven innovations in primary care?

Objective 3: explain sustainability mechanisms

Moving beyond description to explanation, we seek to understand how sustainability strategies interact with contextual conditions to trigger mechanisms that support (or hinder) long-term embedding. This addresses the “how” and “why” questions essential for transferable learning.

RQ3: How do these sustainability strategies influence the long-term embedding of values-driven innovations in primary care?

Objective 4: develop monitoring frameworks

Finally, we aim to co-create with stakeholders a set of indicators that can help implementation teams and policymakers assess and support sustainability as an ongoing process.

RQ4: What process and outcome indicators do stakeholders identify as essential for monitoring and supporting the sustainability of values-driven innovations in primary care?

Distinctive contributions of this study

This study makes several distinctive contributions to implementation science and primary care practice:

Longitudinal focus on sustainability as process: while most implementation studies focus on initial adoption or short-term outcomes, we follow innovations across multiple years to capture sustainability as it unfolds. This longitudinal lens reveals how innovations, contexts, and people co-evolve over time [21].

Integration of complementary theoretical perspectives: rather than applying a single framework, we integrate four complementary theories—realist evaluation, Dynamic Sustainability Framework, Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, and Normalization Process Theory—to enable richer, more nuanced understanding of complex sustainability processes [22–26].

Emphasis on the pivot from implementation to sustainment: we pay particular attention to the critical transition phase where implementation intentions meet real-world practice, adaptations occur, and innovations either become routinized or fade away. This “pivot point” has received insufficient attention in existing research [20, 27].

Co-creation with diverse stakeholders: throughout the study, we engage stakeholders not as research subjects but as partners in knowledge creation. This participatory approach enhances both the relevance and usability of findings for those working to sustain innovations in practice [28].

Focus on values-driven change: by centering on innovations that strengthen core primary care values, we ensure the research addresses changes that matter for patient care and system effectiveness, not simply changes that are novel or technologically sophisticated.

Structure of this protocol

This protocol paper describes our integrated three-phase study design. We first present the conceptual framework integrating our four theoretical perspectives. We then describe each phase of the investigation, showing how they build cumulatively: phase 1 establishes conceptual and empirical foundations by defining VDI and mapping the innovation landscape; phase 2 conducts longitudinal investigation of selected cases from implementation through sustainment; phase 3 collaboratively synthesizes findings into actionable models and indicators. Throughout, we emphasize the iterative, participatory nature of the research process and the continuous refinement of understanding as the study unfolds.

Our ultimate aim is to generate knowledge that not only explains how sustainability happens but also equips primary care stakeholders with practical strategies for ensuring that values-driven innovations endure long enough to fulfill their transformative promise.

Methods/design

The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist is used for this protocol paper to demonstrate adherence to the SRQR reporting guidelines (see supplementary materials A) [29].

Study design

This study employs a longitudinal, theory-driven, multi-stakeholder investigation of how values-driven innovations (VDIs) achieve sustainable implementation in Flemish primary care. We have designed a qualitative study with an integrated three-phase approach where each phase builds cumulatively on the previous one, with continuous stakeholder engagement and theoretical refinement throughout the entire research. The study spans approximately three years and follows a cohort of VDI cases from conceptualization through long-term sustainment, using multiple data sources and timepoints to capture the dynamic nature of implementation in complex primary care healthcare systems.

Integrated conceptual framework

Given the multi-layered nature of implementation processes in complex contexts, this study is guided by a conceptual framework that integrates four complementary theoretical perspectives: the realist approach, the Dynamic Sustainability Framework (DSF), the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), and Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [22, 24–26]. These frameworks operate synergistically throughout all phases of the study rather than being applied separately to different components. The integration of the selected frameworks has the strength to address distinct but interconnected aspects of the sustainability process, from mechanistic understanding to contextual adaptation (see Fig. 1).

Integrated conceptual framework

The realist approach provides the foundational epistemological stance for understanding deeper processes at play in complex healthcare systems. Rather than focusing solely on outcomes, this perspective emphasizes uncovering the underlying mechanisms that explain how and why certain strategies work in specific contexts [22, 25]. We employ the SCMO heuristic tool (Strategy–Context–Mechanism–Outcome) as a consistent analytical lens across all phases, examining how particular strategies interact with local conditions and trigger responses that support or hinder sustainability [30].

The Dynamic Sustainability Framework (DSF) contributes a systems-level perspective by viewing sustainability as an ongoing process of adaptation rather than a static endpoint. DSF directs our attention to how innovations, practice settings, and ecological systems co-evolve over time, requiring continuous alignment across these levels [22, 31]. This dynamic view shapes our longitudinal design and informs our data collection at multiple timepoints.

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) complements DSF by providing a comprehensive and updated taxonomy of contextual determinants that influence implementation outcomes [26, 32]. While DSF emphasizes dynamic adaptation, CFIR ensures we maintain systematic attention to all relevant contextual factors across intervention, inner setting, and outer setting levels (see Table 1). The recent CFIR update and its explicit linkage to NPT strengthens our integrated framework [32].

Normalization Process Theory (NPT) focuses on the social processes through which new practices become embedded in everyday work. NPT’s four constructs; coherence (collective sensemaking), cognitive participation (engagement and commitment), collective action (enactment of practices), and reflexive monitoring (ongoing appraisal), provide a lens for understanding how sustainability emerges through the practical and relational work people do [24, 33]. Recently identified NPT-derived implementation strategies further guide our analysis of what actions support sustained change [34].

Together, these frameworks create an integrated analytical lens that operates throughout the study: the realist approach provides our logic of inquiry (SCMO), DSF and CFIR direct attention to multi-level contextual dynamics, and NPT illuminates the social mechanisms of embedding and routinization. This integration enables us to trace how intentional strategies interact with evolving contexts to trigger mechanisms that either support or impede the normalization of VDIs over time.

Research team and reflexivity

The study is conducted by an interdisciplinary team including occupational therapists, general practitioners, sociologists, and implementation scientists. This diversity of expertise provides ongoing researcher triangulation, strengthening validity through multiple perspectives on data collection, analysis, and interpretation [35]. Throughout the research process, we maintain detailed audit trails documenting analytical decisions, theoretical developments, and methodological adaptations. Preliminary findings are continuously discussed with participants and stakeholders through member checking, ensuring their active involvement in the interpretive process.

Phase 1: establishing conceptual and empirical foundations (Year 1)

Phase 1 simultaneously establishes what we are studying (conceptual clarity around VDIs) and where we are studying it (the landscape of primary care innovations in Flanders). These activities operate in parallel and inform each other iteratively.

Activity 1.1: continuous context scanning

Drawing on DSF’s emphasis on dynamic systems, we establish a permanent scanning process to capture the evolving Flemish primary care context [22]. Twice a year, an impact group and strategic council, representing end users, practice-level stakeholders, and ecological system actors, convene to identify emerging VDIs, contextual changes within implementation settings, and broader system shifts.

Cases are documented using a consistent framework describing three implementation components: context, intended change, and implementation strategies employed [20]. This creates a continuously updated map of the innovation landscape in Flemish primary care. Critically, this scanning process does not conclude after Phase 1; it continues throughout the study, providing real-time contextual knowledge that informs case selection, analysis, and interpretation in subsequent phases.

Activity 1.2: conceptualizing values-driven innovation

Concurrent with context scanning, we develop conceptual clarity around “values-driven innovation” through theoretical and practice-based approaches. Using Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method, we systematically identify the defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences of both “value” and “innovation” [36]. We then employ concept blending methodology to integrate these concepts into a coherent definition of VDI [37].

This theoretical work is complemented by participatory workshops where primary care stakeholders across multiple system levels co-construct shared understanding of what VDI means in practice. The theoretical and practice-based streams are synthesized into effectiveness principles using the GUIDE framework [38]. Through iterative cycles of individual interviews, focus groups and a Delphi study with coordinators of the Flemish care zones and policy makers at both practice and ecological system levels, these principles are refined and validated [31].

The resulting effectiveness principles serve multiple integrated functions: they provide criteria for purposive case selection in Phase 2, they establish the value-based lens through which we analyze implementation processes, and they anchor our assessment of whether innovations maintain fidelity to core primary care values during adaptation. This ensures our sampling and analysis are both methodologically sound and socially grounded, a critical consideration within realist and NPT frameworks that emphasize stakeholder meaning-making [25, 33].

Phase 2: longitudinal investigation of implementation and sustainment (Years 1–3)

Phase 2 represents the empirical core of the study, following selected VDI cases across time to understand the complete lifecycle from implementation intentions through sustained normalization. Rather than conducting separate retrospective and prospective studies, we employ an integrated temporal design that combines backward-looking analysis of how cases reached their current state with forward-looking tracking of ongoing evolution.

For each of the five selected cases, we recruit participants representing multiple roles and perspectives: frontline providers, organizational leaders, administrative staff, patients/community members, and ecological system actors (funders, policymakers, network coordinators). This multi-level sampling enables us to examine how sustainability is co-produced across the entire implementation system.

Integrated data collection: tracing implementation trajectories

For each case, we employ three complementary data collection methods that together create a comprehensive longitudinal dataset (See Fig. 3).

Timeline data collection phase 2

Retrospective Document Analysis provides historical perspective on implementation intentions and early adaptation. We systematically analyze funding applications, strategic plans, evaluation reports, meeting minutes, and internal communications. These documents reveal the intended implementation strategies, anticipated challenges, and early decision points that shaped each innovation’s trajectory. Document analysis continues throughout Phase 2 as new materials emerge.

Serial semi-structured interviews capture stakeholder perspectives at multiple timepoints. Initial interviews (Year 1–2) use retrospective probing to understand how planned strategies evolved into actual practices, what improvisational actions emerged in response to contextual realities, and what mechanisms supported or hindered early normalization. These interviews employ the FRAME-IS framework as a structured tool to document adaptations: who initiated changes, what was modified, at what level (intervention, setting, system), and why [40, 41].

Follow-up interviews occur every six months over two years (Year 2–3), exploring newly adopted strategies, shifts in contextual conditions, activated mechanisms, and perceived impacts on sustainability. Additional interviews may be initiated by participants whenever important or notable developments occur that merit an extra conversation. This serial approach allows us to track how stakeholder sensemaking and engagement evolve over time which is a key NPT consideration [24].

An Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) provides real-time capturing of contextual changes and strategic adaptations between interview timepoints. Participants complete a form when a substantive or unexpected change occurs in the organizational or governmental context (meso, macro) or in used strategies (event-based sampling), guided by open questions derived from FRAME-IS, DSF, CFIR, and NPT [42, 43]. These reflections capture: emerging contextual shifts, improvised adjustments in sustaining strategies, evolving collective understandings or commitments, and practical challenges in enactment. This approach preserves temporal sensitivity while remaining feasible in busy primary care settings, reducing reliance on retrospective reconstruction [42, 44]. Physical nudges (ex: A marker or notepad on the participants desk with the trigger and QR code that directs them instantly to the form), and online reminders will be provided as compliance strategies to minimize the response burden, as it is an intensive longitudinal method. A pilot questionnaire will be sent out, to finetune the questionnaire.

Together, these methods create a multi-layered temporal dataset: documents show initial intentions, retrospective interviews reveal how intentions translated into practice, the EMA capture qualitative real-time adaptation, and prospective interviews enable future reflection on documented experiences. This triangulation strengthens our ability to trace the complex, non-linear pathways through which VDIs become sustainably embedded.

Phase 3: co-creating actionable knowledge (Year 3)

Phase 3 translates empirical findings into actionable frameworks through intensive stakeholder collaboration. Rather than researchers independently developing models that are later “disseminated,” this phase embeds stakeholders as co-creators of knowledge from the outset. This participatory approach enhances relevance, usability, and ultimately the likelihood that findings will influence future implementation efforts [28].

Activity 3.1: collaborative model development

Drawing on the empirical foundations established in Phase 2, we facilitate collaborative development of a model articulating how VDIs achieve sustainable implementation in primary care. The model synthesizes key strategies, contextual influences, mechanisms, and stakeholder dynamics that support or hinder sustainability.

We employ world café methodology, involving multiple iterative discussion rounds with diverse stakeholders [47]. Participants include individuals involved in the Phase 2 case studies, enriched with additional stakeholders identified through ongoing context scanning (Phase 1, Activity 1.1). This ensures broad representation across micro (provider/patient), meso (organizational), and macro (policy/system) levels.

World café sessions are structured around provocative questions derived from Phase 2 findings. For example: “When implementation strategies had to be improvised, what made some adaptations sustainable while others failed?” or “How do you maintain coherence around innovation goals when contexts constantly shift?” Small rotating discussion groups generate insights that are progressively synthesized across rounds.

Between world café sessions, the research team conducts thematic analysis of session recordings and artifacts, identifying emerging patterns and tensions. These provisional insights are brought back to subsequent sessions for validation, refinement, and elaboration. This iterative process continues until theoretical saturation is reached, when additional sessions generate minimal new insights.

The resulting model maintains fidelity to our integrated theoretical framework: it reflects realist logic by articulating SCMO configurations, incorporates DSF’s dynamic contextualism by acknowledging ongoing adaptation, attends to CFIR’s comprehensive taxonomy of contextual determinants, and foregrounds NPT’s focus on collective sensemaking and embedding work [22, 24–26]. Importantly, the model is expressed in language accessible to diverse stakeholders while retaining theoretical precision.

Activity 3.2: validating sustainability outcomes

Concurrent with model development, we systematically assess how stakeholders perceive and evaluate sustainability outcomes. This addresses a critical gap: ensuring the model not only explains how sustainability happens but also resonates with lived experiences of what sustainability means.

Through structured interviews and reflective discussions with case study participants, we explore: What outcomes indicate to you that an innovation is sustainably embedded? How do you distinguish between superficial adoption and deep normalization? What unintended consequences of sustainment have emerged?

We complement these qualitative assessments with structured tools such as the Program Sustainability Assessment Tool (PSAT) or adapted self-report measures [48]. These instruments anchor perception data in validated frameworks while allowing quantitative comparison across cases. However, quantitative data serve as prompts for deeper qualitative exploration rather than definitive outcome measures.

Critically, outcome assessment findings feed directly back into model refinement. If stakeholders identify sustainability indicators not captured by our provisional model, we investigate why. If certain proposed strategies are viewed as impractical or misaligned with lived experience, we interrogate those disconnections. This ensures the final model is both conceptually grounded and empirically credible.

Data management and quality assurance

Throughout the study, rigorous data management procedures ensure integrity and traceability. All interviews are audio-recorded with participants’ informed consent and professionally transcribed verbatim. Transcripts are checked for accuracy and pseudonymized. Documents are catalogued systematically with metadata recording source, date, and relevance. EMA data is collected through secure digital platforms with regular prompting to maximize completion rates.

All data are stored in REDCap, a secure research data management system hosted at the University of Antwerp. Participant identifiers are maintained separately from research data, with unique ID codes linking consent forms to data. Only authorized research team members have access.

Quality assurance mechanisms include regular team debriefings to discuss emerging findings and analytical decisions; maintaining detailed audit trails documenting the evolution of interpretations; member checking to validate findings with participants; and peer review through presentations at research seminars and conferences.

Ethical considerations

Approval for this study has been obtained from the Committee for Medical Ethics (CME) of the Antwerp University Hospital/University of Antwerp (reference number 2025–7124). Amendments will be submitted as each phase develops and specific protocols are finalized.

All participants provide written informed consent before participating. Consent forms clearly explain: the longitudinal nature of the study, time commitments for different participation roles, how data will be used, measures to protect confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Given the longitudinal design, we implement ongoing consent procedures: participants are reminded of study purposes at each contact point and given opportunities to decline specific activities or withdraw entirely.

Confidentiality is protected through: pseudonymization of all transcripts and reports; secure data storage with restricted access; and careful attention to potentially identifying details when presenting case examples. In small communities like Flemish primary care, maintaining anonymity requires particular vigilance, which we address through composite case descriptions when necessary.

Direct access to data will be granted to authorized representatives from participating universities (University of Antwerp, Ghent University, KU Leuven, and Artevelde University of Applied Sciences) for monitoring and audit purposes to ensure regulatory compliance.

All data will be securely stored for 20 years after study completion in a post-project database at the University of Antwerp, consistent with institutional policies and research integrity standards.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and community members are engaged throughout the study as essential stakeholders. In Phase 1, patient representatives participate in conceptualizing VDI through workshops. In Phase 2, we explicitly recruit patients/community members affected by selected innovations as case study participants, ensuring their experiences of sustained care changes are captured. In Phase 3, patients join world café sessions as co-creators of the sustainability model.

This involvement is not tokenistic consultation but genuine partnership: patient perspectives shape what questions we ask, how we interpret findings, and what recommendations emerge. We provide accessible summaries of findings to all participants and will develop plain-language materials suitable for community dissemination.

Discussion

A longitudinal research investigating sustainability with a focus on the implementation processes is rare [21]. Exploring the strategies that can be employed in the implementation of VDI may contribute to addressing the current project fatigue experienced by primary care providers which often stems from repeated cycles of short-term pilots that fail to achieve sustainable impact [17, 49]. At the policy level, such efforts could potentially result in a better return on investment within the healthcare system. Furthermore, this research offers an important contribution to the existing body of literature regarding the necessary actions for the development and translation of innovations into practice [11]. Ensuring that values-driven innovations are sustainably implemented and normalized within primary care could also help alleviate the burden currently placed on secondary care services.

Possible strenghts and limitations

This study represents several methodological and theoretical strengths and limitations. At the base is an integration of four complementary theoretical frameworks: Realist Evaluation, the Dynamic Sustainability Framework (DSF), the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), and Normalization Process Theory (NPT). This provides a comprehensive lens for understanding how innovations are sustained in real-world healthcare settings [22, 24, 25, 32]. The complexity of integrating multiple theoretical frameworks, while offering comprehensive insights, can present challenges in data analysis and interpretation, potentially complicating the synthesis of findings. While combining multiple frameworks risks this theoretical complexity, the integration of the selected frameworks has the strength to address distinct but interconnected aspects of the sustainability process, from mechanistic understanding to contextual adaptation.

The co-creation of conceptual clarity around “values-driven innovation” with stakeholders in phase 1 enhances the relevance, coherence, and practical applicability of the findings and establishes a solid foundation for phase 2 and 3 [24, 28].

In phase 2, the longitudinal, multi-level design represents a methodological strength, which combines document analysis, serial interviews, EMA method, used to track adaptations at innovation-, organisation- and ecosystem level [22]. Longitudinal research with a focus on sustainable implementation is unique and, therefore, adds value to the existing research [21]. This setup allows the researchers to study how context and implementation develop in real time, instead of solely retrospectively. However, the longitudinal nature of the study, though methodologically valuable, introduces practical challenges including potential participant attrition over time, which could affect data continuity.

In the final phase, the focus shifts to eliciting stakeholders’ experiential knowledge to strengthen the content validity of the strategies used to sustain values-driven innovation [50]. World café sessions are organized around both anticipated implementation outcomes (sustainability) and observed outcomes in practice (sustainment), providing a more comprehensive understanding of what it means to sustain a values-driven innovation [45].

Despite some challenges, the study’s design and theoretical grounding position it to make a significant contribution to implementation science by deepening understanding of sustainment as a dynamic and co-produced process. The insights generated are expected to inform future implementation efforts by offering a grounded, context-sensitive model of long-term innovation sustainability.
