Challenges for implementation of “Enhanced Recovery After Surgery” (ERAS) protocols and artificial intelligence support tools in resource-limited countries
Authors: Abdullahi Hassan Elmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical patient safety remains a major challenge in resource-limited settings, where preventable harm may be increased by delayed assessment, limited monitoring, workforce shortages, weak documentation, and inconsistent use of standardized perioperative protocols. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery offers an evidence-based pathway for improving perioperative care, while artificial intelligence may support risk prediction, complication surveillance, audit feedback, and clinical decision-making. This correspondence argues that artificial intelligence and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery should be considered complementary patient safety strategies rather than separate innovations. In low-resource surgical systems, their combined use may strengthen perioperative reliability, improve early recognition of risk, and support continuous quality improvement. However, implementation must be gradual, locally adapted, ethically governed, and built on essential safety practices such as checklist adherence, multidisciplinary teamwork, staff training, and audit.

Patient safety in surgery depends on reliable systems before, during, and after an operation. In resource-limited settings, surgical teams often work under difficult conditions, including shortages of trained staff, limited monitoring capacity, weak referral systems, delayed recognition of complications, and inconsistent documentation. These challenges can increase the risk of preventable harm during the perioperative period and make practical, low-cost, and system-based safety strategies urgently needed [1–4]. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) has become an important approach for improving perioperative care. ERAS pathways bring together evidence-based practices such as preoperative counselling, nutritional optimization, rational fluid management, multimodal pain control, early mobilization, and structured postoperative follow-up. Importantly, ERAS recommendations have been adapted for elective abdominal and pelvic surgery in primary and secondary hospitals in low- and middle-income countries, showing that enhanced recovery principles can be applied beyond highly resourced surgical centres [1–6]. A recent meta-analysis also reported that ERAS guidelines are associated with improved surgical outcomes, including shorter hospital stay and reduced postoperative complications [2, 7].

Artificial intelligence (AI) may add another layer of support to perioperative safety. In surgical care, AI can assist with risk stratification, early identification of patients at higher risk of complications, prediction of delayed recovery, theatre planning, antibiotic stewardship, postoperative surveillance, and audit feedback. When combined with ERAS pathways, AI could help surgical teams identify which patients require closer monitoring, which elements of the ERAS pathway are being missed, and where delays or safety gaps are occurring. This combined approach is especially relevant in settings where clinical teams must make timely decisions with limited resources [4, 8]. By linking AI with ERAS, surgical safety can move beyond isolated interventions toward a more connected perioperative safety model. ERAS provides the structured clinical pathway, while AI may support timely decision-making, monitoring, prediction, and continuous feedback. This is not a call to replace clinicians with technology. Rather, it is a call to use simple, responsible, and locally appropriate digital tools to strengthen the work that surgical teams already do.4,7,8 However, AI should not be introduced before basic safety systems are strengthened. The World Health Organization Surgical Safety Checklist remains one of the most important tools for reducing avoidable surgical harm. Its use has been associated with reductions in morbidity and mortality across diverse global settings [8]. In Somalia, a closed-loop audit study of WHO surgical safety checklist adherence has shown the value of local audit, feedback, and quality improvement in strengthening surgical safety practice [4]. These lessons are important: AI will only improve patient safety if it is integrated into a culture of teamwork, communication, checklist use, and accountability [4].

There are also ethical and practical concerns. AI tools may not perform safely if they are trained mainly on data from high-income settings and then used in low-resource hospitals without local validation. Differences in patient populations, disease patterns, documentation quality, infrastructure, and available treatments may affect accuracy and usefulness. The World Health Organization has emphasized that AI in health must be guided by transparency, accountability, safety, equity, and governance [6]. Reviews of AI standards in medicine also show the importance of validation, reporting quality, and responsible implementation [4, 7]. For resource-limited surgical systems, the safest approach is gradual and practical. Hospitals should first strengthen ERAS basics, checklist adherence, documentation, infection prevention, postoperative monitoring, and multidisciplinary communication. AI-supported tools can then be introduced to support specific safety functions, such as risk assessment, complication alerts, audit dashboards, or follow-up reminders. These tools should be affordable, explainable, locally tested, and easy for frontline staff to use [1, 4, 6, 8].

Integrating AI with ERAS offers a timely opportunity to strengthen surgical patient safety in resource-limited settings. Its value lies not in advanced technology alone, but in supporting structured perioperative pathways, earlier recognition of risk, better audit feedback, and more reliable care. For Somalia and similar low-resource settings, the priority should be a locally adapted, ethically governed model that combines ERAS principles, checklist adherence, staff training, multidisciplinary teamwork, and simple AI-supported tools to reduce preventable surgical harm.
