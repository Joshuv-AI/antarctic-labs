 ABSTRACT
This report summarizes developmental activities to establish synthesis and purification
capabilities for candidate fluoride fuel salts for molten salt reactors. The primary technical
focus is centered on purification of a lithium- and beryllium-free carrier salt, NaF-KF, and an
oxygen-free synthesis method for UF4 that involves reduction from UF6, which is relevant for
the high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) fuel cycle. Regarding carrier salt purification,
thermodynamic calculations were performed in FactSage 8.3 to compare the traditional
hydrofluorination purification route to an alternative route utilizing NF3. Baseline oxygen and
hydrogen concentrations in unpurified NaF and KF were used to inform the analysis and
provide justification for implementation of the alternative NF3 route. Regarding UF4 synthesis,
a batch-scale reduction system utilizing CCl4 was downselected and designed. The route
satisfies the key criteria associated with technology readiness level and will limit the
propagation of trace oxide or oxyfluoride impurities. To provide real-time reaction kinetic data,
a laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) platform has been designed for integration to
monitor the effluent gas stream in the carrier salt purification system and the UF4 synthesis
system. Tracking the decline of certain volatile by-products will provide indication of reaction
completion. At the time of reporting, the NF3 system specifications are finalized, the reaction
vessel and system components for the UF4 synthesis system are fully specified and under
acquisition, and the LIBS system integration plans are finalized.

 Summary of Initial Fluoride Fuel Salt Purification and
 Synthesis
1. INTRODUCTION
 Molten salt reactors (MSRs) are a particularly unique Generation IV nuclear reactor class; in these
reactors, the primary coolant, and oftentimes the nuclear fuel itself, is a high-temperature liquid molten salt
mixture. In these liquid-fueled MSRs specifically, the fissile nuclear material (e.g., uranium, thorium,
plutonium) is dissolved in either a molten chloride or fluoride carrier salt. These reactors are attractive for
several reasons: (1) they operate at low operating pressures due to the exceptionally high boiling points of
most molten halide salts; (2) they are passively safe due to the thermal expansion of the molten salt during
accidental overheating conditions, such that the reactor automatically shuts itself down; (3) they operate at
significantly higher temperatures than Generation III reactors, leading to higher thermal efficiency; and (4)
they may be able to facilitate significantly higher burnup of fuel because the fuel is not limited by
degradation mechanisms associated with solid fuels.
 However, one of the general challenges that must be addressed with MSRs is establishing a domestic
capability to synthesize large quantities of highly pure fuel salts that are of the appropriate specifications
for commercial use. This is true for both chloride and fluoride salts, which is why the Advanced Fuels
Campaign (AFC) Fuel Salt Focus Area (FSFA) under the US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear
Energy (DOE-NE) has a defined technical focus area on the synthesis and purification pathways for fuel
salts from a variety of feedstock materials (Roper, et al., 2025; Dulovic, et al., 2025). This supply chain
challenge is especially great if a given MSR design constitutes the use of high-assay low-enriched uranium
(HALEU), as domestic capabilities to synthesize UF4 are more so in the pilot testing stages through the
HALEU Deconversion Services Program (U.S. Department of Energy, 2024). As such, the national
laboratory complex can serve a key role in research and development (R&D) efforts, which can be
conducted at the laboratory scale to support the broader, commercial-scale efforts for fuel salt synthesis,
such that legacy processes can be optimized and more novel methods can be explored.
 It is important to understand that at a high level, fuel salts comprise two components: (1) the carrier salt
(which is a fuel solvent) and (2) the fuel. For the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE), for example,
the composition of the fuel salt was 7LiF-BeF2-ZrF4-UF4 (65.0-29.1-5.0-0.9 mol %). However, for this fuel
salt, the fuel solvent, 7LiF-BeF2-ZrF4 (64.7-30.1-5.2 mol %), the depleted fuel concentrate, 7LiF-UF4 (73-
27 mol %), and the enriched fuel concentrate (chemically identical to the depleted fuel concentrate) were
prepared in separate batches (Shaffer, 1971). Based on this precedent, R&D efforts should therefore not be
limited to the synthesis of compounds such as UF4 or UCl3 through fuel-cycle-relevant routes but should
also include efforts to better understand, optimize, and strategize processes for carrier salt preparation and
fuel salt dissolution. Furthermore, with significantly improved molten salt characterization techniques that
exist today that did not exist in legacy developments of fuel salt synthesis processes, the research
community is poised to make more well-informed decisions on salt preparation processes that will improve
final fuel salt purity, adjust chemistry for enhanced performance in an MSR, and better understand
underlying mechanisms that dictate the efficacy of any given method being studied.
 There are several possible combinations of different carrier salts and fuels that could be used in MSRs
(McMurray, et al., 2021). These combinations differ in terms of their moderation capability, the fuel cycle
that they support, the neutron flux spectrum associated with them, their toxicity and potential for tritium
generation, and their necessitation for enriched material. One particular fuel salt of interest (especially for
R&D purposes) is eutectic NaF-KF-UF4 (FUNaK). This composition does not include any beryllium, which
is highly toxic and requires extreme engineering work controls to handle safely. It also does not contain
any lithium, so there is no need for sourcing isotopically enriched 7LiF, which can be challenging to enrich

 10

to levels required for MSR operations to limit tritium generation. The downside to FUNaK from a fuel
cycle standpoint is that its poor moderation capability would almost definitely necessitate the use of
HALEU to limit the core footprint to a reasonable size. Regardless, salt synthesis and purification of
FUNaK (and other non-Be-bearing candidate fuel salts) yield useful insights that would apply to Be-bearing
fuel salts as well.
 Based on the merits of FUNaK’s use in R&D and potentially in MSRs as well, Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) has conducted developmental efforts to synthesize UF4 using an oxygen-free route,
purify the target carrier salt (NaF-KF), and monitor these processes using laser-induced breakdown
spectroscopy (LIBS). Specifically, a CCl4 reduction system is currently under construction; this system will
reduce UF6 to UF4 by reaction with CCl4 gas. This process is particularly relevant when considering that
HALEU UF4 production may be entirely dependent on a deconversion facility (Redomond & Ashkeboussi,
2022). Regarding NaF-KF purification, a NF3 gas purification system (formerly a hydrofluorination system)
is under development to ensure that the fuel solvent may be as free as possible of O and H impurities before
introducing the fuel to the system. Finally, developmental efforts on an integrated LIBS system for both
processes will enable on-line monitoring of the reaction kinetics, which will enable an improved
understanding of how these salt synthesis and purification processes can be further optimized in terms of
reaction times, temperatures, flow rates, etc. The overall goal is that when these systems are fully
operational, they may better support the mission of AFC-FSFA and provide highly pure UF4-bearing salts
for further studies of this fuel type, such as irradiation experiments. It is important to note that both the
purification and UF4 synthesis systems described herein could be used to prepare a myriad of different fuel
salts, because the methodology is fuel salt agnostic.

2. CARRIER SALT PURIFICATION: NaF-KF
 Hydrofluorination (HF), which was employed to purify the salt for the MSRE (Shaffer, 1971), is the
most widely used technique to purify molten fluoride salts and is a well-established method for removing
impurities in fluoride salts. However, the use of HF presents handling and safety challenges as well as
challenges regarding the susceptibility of containment materials to corrosion. The use of NF3 as an
alternative purifying agent was investigated and shown to be viable (Scheele, Casella, & McNamara, 2017).
NF3 is considerably less dangerous to humans, it is stable at room temperatures, and has high fluorinating
strength at high temperatures. More recently at ORNL, a fluoride salt purification system was built by the
Corrosion Science and Technology Group in 2021 and was operated until 2023. The system was designed
to compare two fluorinating gases (HF and NF3), but up to now, only HF has been used to purify FLiNaK
(eutectic LiF-NaF-KF) and FLiBe (eutectic LiF-BeF2).
 Regarding fluoride salts of interest for thermal spectrum MSRs, FUNaK (eutectic NaF-KF-UF4) has
been identified as a candidate fuel for a fuel salt due to its lithium- and beryllium-free fluoride composition
(Fache, Voigt, Colle, Hald, & Beneš, 2024), as discussed in the previous section.
Its chemistry eliminates both the toxic hazards associated with beryllium and the need for enriched lithium-
7 to mitigate tritium production. Generally speaking, the synthesis of FUNaK requires prior purification
of NaF-KF, such that the addition of purified UF4 can be performed without risk of subsequent
pyrohydrolysis (contrarily, the fuel salt could be purified after the addition of UF4, but the radiological cost
for going this route would be higher).
 The melting temperature of eutectic NaF-KF (40-60 mol %) is around 717 °C, whereas it is around
454 °C for FLiNaK. Therefore, purifying NaF-KF requires significantly higher operational temperatures
than purifying FLiNaK. Thermal decomposition of NF3 initiates near 300 °C and peaks between 800 °C
and 1200 °C (MacFadden & Tschuikow-Roux, 1973; Schott, Blair, & Morgan, 1973; Dorko, Grimm,
Scheller, & Mueller, 1975; Evans & Tschuikow-Roux, 1976). Consequently, the reactivity of NF3 toward

 11

metallic structural materials and polymeric components is expected to increase significantly across
this temperature range. To minimize materials compatibility risks, the initial NF3 purification process will
be demonstrated initially on FLiNaK before it is attempted on NaF-KF.
 The following section describes thermodynamic calculations performed to compare the two
purification processes in terms of ease of purification, required gas chemistries, and reaction products (e.g.,
potential HF generation).

2.1 Purification System Specifications and Planned Operation with
 FLiNaK Thermodynamic Calculations
 A schematic and a photo of the purification system are shown in Figure 1 and Figure 2. The system is
equipped with several safety features such as gas detection (both inside and outside the hood) and automatic
safety shutdown.
 The system utilizes two ultra-high-purity (UHP) argon bottles and one nitrogen trifluoride bottle
that contains a mixture of 5 mol % NF3 in argon. A moisture trap and a Drierite purifier further clean the
UHP Ar gas. The purified Ar is then split into three dedicated lines: cover, sparge, and flush. The NF3 line
is connected to the Ar sparge line before the vessel, so the molten salt can either be sparged with Ar or the
NF3/Ar mixture. The Ar flush line is connected to the NF3 line just after the NF3 bottle to flush the system
in case of leak or at the end of the purification. The vessel has two influent lines for the sparge and the
cover gas, one port for a thermocouple, and one effluent line that is connected to a scrubber to neutralize
HF. The scrubber is composed of a series of four 2 L bottles. The first bottle is an anti-siphon empty bottle,
the second and third bottles contain about 1.5 L of a 3 M solution of sodium hydroxide, and the fourth
bottle contains deionized (DI) water. All four bottles are immersed in a slightly alkaline solution (0.0014 M
NaOH) with phenolphthalein to trap and visually detect HF leaks. The plastic tank is semitransparent, so
pink indicates a standard operation and clear indicates an acid leak.

 Figure 1. Schematic of the fluoride purification system at ORNL.

 FLiNaK (with a molar ratio of 46.5-11.5-42 mol %) will be weighed and mixed in a plastic bottle. The
salt mixture will be loaded in a high-purity boron nitride tapered crucible with 10 mm wall
thickness and a 3 kg salt capacity. The crucible will then be inserted in the C-276 reaction vessel. The
bottom of the vessel will be layered with graphite spacers to prevent cracking during thermal
cycling. The ConFlat flange with nickel gasket will fit on top of the vessel body and be tightened with 24
nickel-plated bolts to a specified torque (15 ft-lb). The vessel will be placed between two semicylindrical
ceramic heaters and connected to the gas lines.

 The operational steps are listed below:
 1. Flow Ar through the cover and sparge lines.
 2. Heat up vessel using two-step thermal ramp.
               a. 25–300 °C for 4–8 h
               b. 300–600 °C for 4–8 h
 3. Lower sparge line to near the bottom of the crucible.
 4. Switch sparge gas to NF3/Ar (150 mL/min). This step can take several weeks.
 5. Turn off NF3/Ar and flush the line with Ar for several hours.
 6. Raise sparge line.
 7. Cool down salt slowly.
 8. Disconnect and seal the vessel.
 9. Open vessel in a glove box to collect purified salt.

2.2 Salt Analysis with LECO
 An inert gas fusion method (using a LECO system) to measure oxygen and hydrogen in salt was
previously developed and published (Sulejmanovic, Kurley, Robb, & Raiman, 2021). In addition, an Eltra
O-N-H analyzer is planned to be installed in a glove box to measure O and H in any salts, including Be-
containing salts.
 LECO analysis was used to quantify oxygen and hydrogen concentrations in unpurified NaF and KF
(Table 1). The molar amounts of O and H are nearly equal in both salts, suggesting they originate from
hydroxide impurities. Per 5 kg of NaF-KF (40-60 mol %), KF contains about 3 mol of O and H, and NaF
contains about 0.08 mol of O and H. These values provide a baseline for comparison with the oxygen and
hydrogen levels that will be measured after heating and fluorination of the salt.

3. FUEL SALT SYNTHESIS: UF4
 There are a wide variety of methods available to produce uranium tetrafluoride, largely predicated on
assumed feedstocks (Figure 5). For the purposes of the synthesis system development at ORNL, the analysis
on appropriate methods is centered around three key factors:
 1. Appropriate chemistry
 2. High technology readiness level (TRL)
 3. Relevant throughput
As further discussed in this section, the utilization of the reduction route, specifically by use of CCl4, is an
attractive route for UF4 synthesis and is thus the primary focus of future experimental synthesis efforts at
ORNL to supply UF4 to dissolve into carrier salts such as NaF-KF.

 Figure 5. Process pathways for various uranium conversion operations.

3.1 Motivations for UF6 Reduction by CCl4

3.1.1 Appropriate Chemistry
 In brief, the chemistry must be satisfactory with respect to both the nature of the feedstock and the
specification of the product. The HALEU UF6 feedstock is of particular interest to the advanced reactor
ecosystem. A survey of the current HALEU supply ecosystem suggests that the “bottle-neck” is less so the
ability to achieve HALEU enrichments at scale and more so the ability to “de-convert” HALEU UF6 to
usable forms (U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), 2024). As such, per Figure 5, methods that lead straight
from UF6 to UF4 have the most “big-picture” relevance.

 17

 Regarding the nature of the outgoing product, there are constraints when considering the implications
that these impurities would have downstream during the intended use. There is therefore a desire to pursue
methods that minimize oxygen content or the propagation of trace oxides (U3O8, UO3, or UO2) or
oxyfluorides (UO2F2) in the UF4. These may occur due to incomplete reactions in any of the prior processing
steps. In Figure 5, following the rightward or diagonal arrows from UF6, each of those individual steps
incurs a slight penalty due to the unlikelihood of 100% conversion. These propagating impurities ultimately
affect the net conversion to UF4 and the ingrowth of oxide impurities.
The intention to pursue methods that minimize oxygen content or the propagation of trace oxides limits
the options to:
 • Reduction by HCl
 • Reduction by hydrogen
 • Reduction by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)

3.1.2 High Technology Readiness Level
 In the interest of standing up the UF4 production capability, one must consider methods that have been
extensively validated through large-scale use and have historical precedents. This would enable better use
of staff time and efficient use of funding while mitigating risk due to the need for extensive method
development to optimize process parameters.
 Additionally, when viewed from the perspective of research at ORNL, researchers are enabled to draw
on local and historical subject matter expertise and established precedents for work controls to ensure that
our safety posture is well maintained.
 The HCl method has not yet been extensively demonstrated at scale, and many unknowns remain.
Applying the limitation of TRL limits the options to:
 • Reduction by hydrogen
 • Reduction by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)

3.1.3 Relevant Throughput
 The final downselection criterion used to further screen applicable methods was whether the chosen
technology matched the material throughput required for AFC-FSFA testing purposes. The direct reduction
from UF6 to UF4 by hydrogen gas has been extensively performed at scale by the National Lead Company
(Smiley, Brater, & Nimmo, 1956). At the level of process engineering, two systems have been developed
that utilize this chemistry for the production of UF4. They differ primarily in the origin of the necessary
heat to be supplied.
 • Hot-wall reactor: heat supplied externally from the heated wall of the reactor
 • Cold-wall reactor: heat supplied internally by the reaction of hydrogen and fluorine
In either case, due to highly nonlinear engineering scaling factors, these systems are best suited to the
production of hundreds of kilograms to metric tons of U per day.

 18

Based on these factors, the CCl4 method presents the most attractive pathway for the direct deconversion
of UF6 to UF4.

3.1.4 Description of the Reaction
 The process of reducing UF6 to UF4 as patented by John Syme Nairn et al. proceeds by the following
overall reaction mechanism (Nairn, Collins, & Taylor, 1958):
                       𝑈𝐹6 (𝑔) + 𝐶𝐶𝑙4 (𝑙 𝑜𝑟 𝑔)→ 𝑈𝐹4 (𝑠) + 𝐶𝑙2 (𝑔) + 2𝐶𝐶𝑙3 𝐹 (𝑔)
 UF6 has been shown to be soluble in CCl4, and the reaction may proceed in both the liquid and the gas
phase. As such, Nairn et al. also patented a continuous method for the reduction, relying exclusively on the
gas phase reaction in a rotary kiln configuration that continuously withdraws the UF4 product.
 For ORNL’s purposes, a batch process was found to be more suitable and required less engineering. A
process flow diagram is shown in Figure 8. The general process is as follows:
 1.  The reaction vessel is evacuated and cooled by liquid nitrogen.
 2.  Heat the UF6 feed vessel and begin the transfer into the reaction vessel.
 3.  Add in the CCl4.
 4.  Seal the vessel and heat to ~150 ℃.
 5.  The reaction will be indicated by the increase of pressure from gaseous products. An
 accumulation of these by-products shifts the reaction equilibrium and inhibits further conversion.
 6. Cool the vessel to ambient conditions and vent the pressure and bleed off the reactants.
 7. Seal the vessel and repeat steps 4–6.
 8. Cycle until there is no appreciable buildup of pressure from the reactants.
 9. Distill the remaining CCl4 to the liquid hold tank.
 10. Collect the UF4 product.

 3.2 Synthesis System Specifications
3.2.1 Sizing and Throughput
 The reduction process scales to serve the conversion of 1 kg on a uranium basis or 1.47 kg on a UF6
basis. As such, the reaction vessel has been sized to support the equivalent volume of material. The system
process also contains a dump tank, schematically shown in Figure 8 and visible in Figure 9, which is for
emergency purposes only. The design also allows for access points in the off-gas handling systems:

 1. Headspace above the reaction
 2. After the condenser and before the chlorine gas scrubber
 3. After the scrubber

These points enable the analysis of volatile gaseous products and by-products and may be leveraged for
more scientific studies on the process. Instrumentation such as LIBS presents an attractive opportunity to
study the nature of gaseous or aerosol species throughout the off-gas system.
3.2.2 Materials of Construction and Product Impacts
 To further serve the needs of AFC-FSFA regarding the pedigree of materials that will be tested, we are
designing two separate reaction vessels. Due to known material compatibility constraints (another benefit
of the downselection of technologies based on TRL), the current plan is to utilize a nickel-lined vessel with
the option of stainless steel as an alternative. The aim is to determine the true impact of materials borne
from either vessel on the final UF4 samples bound for irradiation studies to be conducted under AFC-FSFA.
If the impacts are shown to be negligible (no appreciable amount of alloy impurities migrating into the
UF4), then this would remove a layer of complexity or supply chain constraints in industrial scale-up.

3.2.3 Product Analysis
 The products shall be analyzed for speciation and impurities by a variety of methods, ranging from
electron microscopy for particle size distribution to x-ray diffraction for impurity analysis. Additionally,
aging studies aimed at understanding the packaging and transportation of the UF4 and the ingrowth of
UO2F2 are planned.
 3.3 Progress on System Construction
 The material and energy balances as well as relevant reaction kinetics calculations for the feed, reaction
vessel, and off-gas handling systems have been performed. The flanged reaction vessel has been designed
and is currently out for fabrication. The condenser, accompanying chiller and scrubbing tower have been
purchased and are awaiting delivery. Installation is estimated to be completed at the end of the calendar
year.

 4. LIBS INTEGRATION
 4.1 Motivations for LIBS in Synthesis and Purification
 LIBS is performed by focusing a pulsed laser into a sample, where it either ablates a solid or ionizes a
liquid/gas to form a microplasma. The atoms within the vicinity of the focal point are excited and/or ionized
within the plasma. As the plasma cools, the atoms/ions return to their ground states through a cascading
photon release process. The wavelength of these emitted photons depends upon the differences in the high
and low energy levels of the transition, and their intensity is related to the branching ratio of a given
element’s electronic structure. As an element’s atomic concentration increases in the plasma volume, the
peak intensities increase as well. The emitted photons can be captured through optical lenses and routed to
a dispersive spectrometer using fiber optics. Ultimately, a broadband spectrum ranging typically from 190
to 1000 nm is collected, where peak locations provide elemental identification and their intensities provide
concentration information.
LIBS has been under development for molten salt applications at ORNL for the last several years, largely
due to its versatility in application to solids, liquids, and gases as well as its capability to detect nearly all
elements in the periodic table. As an optical spectroscopic method, it can be configured for in situ
measurements, and its measurement rate (~1 ms/spectrum) permits real-time monitoring. Much of the
previous ORNL LIBS work has been focused on gas/aerosol detection for off-gas systems, but many of the
lessons learned allow for the translation to monitoring salt synthesis and purification activities. The typical
LIBS setup for gas phase measurements is shown in Figure 10. Here, the process gas flows through an
optical cell with either a gas focusing nozzle or sheath gas system to facilitate the flow across a sampling
gap. The laser is focused downward into the optical cell into this sampling gap to form the microplasma.
As the plasma cools, the light is collected from a series of optics located around the laser focusing lens. The
light is then sent to a spectrometer for measurement.

 23

 Figure 10. Example configuration for a gas phase LIBS measurement.

 Because LIBS has a wide range of elemental sensitivity, it can detect elements of interest for the
synthesis and purification activities, such as H, C, N, O, and F. A calibration curve for H in an Ar bulk gas
from a previous study is shown in Figure 11a, along with an example application of LIBS-based H
monitoring during the drying and melting of FLiNaK salt doped with FeF3 in Figure 11b. Note that many
other elemental signals were tracked during this test, but H was selected for a focused discussion. By
correlating these time series data to the salt temperature, clear phenomena can be seen, such as the release
of absorbed moisture below 100 °C and several subsequent releases below 270 °C. This salt was doped
with FeF3 which was suspected to be hydrated to some level. The intermediate releases between 100 and
270 °C are indicative of chemical reactions occurring in the salt, such as hydroxide formation and
subsequent decomposition into Fe2O3. Ultimately, as the salt reaches the melting point at approximately
458 °C, nearly all H release has been completed. This measurement highlights the capabilities of LIBS to
monitor the effluent of salt vessels to understand their quality and ensure they perform as expected.

 4.2 Considerations for Application to NF3 Purification System
 Purification of the coolant salt is required to remove O and H impurities from the as-received stock
salts. Two purification processes are proposed: (1) a traditional HF process and (2) an alternative NF3
process. While the chemical reactions in these two processes differ, the application of LIBS is largely the
same. A gas LIBS system will be configured in-line between the reaction vessel and the scrubber banks.
For the NF3 system, the effluent signatures of N, F, H, and O in a bulk Ar gas are expected. LIBS would
provide valuable insight into the dominant purification mechanisms in the process based on the relative
loadings of these analytes. The purification process can take up to several hours depending on the impurity
content, the loading of NF3 in the sparge, and mixing efficiency in the reaction vessel. LIBS would provide
the operators with information on when the purification is complete based on when the H and O loadings
tail off, and through quantitative models like that shown in Figure 11, the removed impurities could be
quantified in situ.
 Due to the potential HF in the effluent, the gas measurement cell will be designed with a flow-through
design to minimize the residence time of the sample stream in the cell. The cell will use sapphire or CaF2
windows to ensure optical access is not degraded during the purification run. The main LIBS system has
been designed to fit inside the fume hood, whereas the spectrometer and operating computer will be located
on the benchtop outside the hood. Light will be routed to the spectrometer via optical fibers.
 4.3 Considerations for Application to UF4 Synthesis System
The planned UF4 synthesis method is a batch process using CCl4 to convert UF6 feedstock into UF4. The
by-products of this process are gaseous Cl2 and 2CCl3F, which will be vented at regular intervals. The
conversion is considered complete when there is no more formation of by-products based on pressure;
however, on-line concentration monitoring can facilitate additional insight into the reaction rates as well as
any unexpected impurities within the system. Here, a LIBS system like that shown in Figure 10 would be
integrated into the effluent lines of the reaction vessel. As the vessel is vented, the effluent would flow
through the LIBS for measurement prior to moving toward the appropriate getters. LIBS should be able to
detect C with sufficient precision to track the levels of CCl4 being generated during each reaction cycle. Cl
and F may also be detected by LIBS; however, halogens are subject to a larger detection limit in LIBS, and
it would be likely that these signals would be lost in the detector noise prior to C. Additionally, any H, O,
or N that would be unexpected in the system would easily be detected by the on-line LIBS. This would

 25

inform the operators of initial impurities in the UF6, of impurities in the stock gases used, or of potential
atmospheric leaks into the integrated system.
 5. CONCLUSION
The development of synthesis and purification systems for UF4-bearing candidate fuel salts at ORNL, as
reported herein, can be summarized as follows:
 1. For the purification of carrier salts, the conversion of an HF purification system to a NF3 system
 has been specified and technically justified through thermodynamic analysis
 2. To reduce UF6 to UF4, a CCl4 reduction system has been justified as a fuel-cycle-relevant, high-
 TRL path forward and has been fully designed to be installed at ORNL
 3. A versatile LIBS platform has been designed that is planned for integration into the NF3 purification
 system and UF4 synthesis system to enable effluent monitoring to enhance the understanding of
 reaction kinetics
When these systems are fully completed, ORNL will be in a position to perform synthesis and purification
studies that are relevant to HALEU deconversion processes, supply fluoride fuel salts to support irradiation
campaigns under AFC-FSFA, and gather in situ characterization data that may assist synthesis or
purification technique standardization.
