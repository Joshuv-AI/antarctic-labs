Sweet’s syndrome during anti-TNF therapy with successful rescue using a JAK inhibitor in refractory ulcerative colitis: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yanting Shi, Jingbin Ding, Wenjing Cheng, Jindan He, Yu Da, Jie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis report describes the diagnostic and therapeutic management of Sweet’s syndrome (SS) presenting as a painful forehead plaque in a patient with refractory ulcerative colitis (UC) during apparent endoscopic remission. It highlights the association between active intestinal inflammation and extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) and evaluates the efficacy of the Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor upadacitinib after anti-TNF therapy failure.MethodsA case study was conducted on a 60-year-old woman with refractory UC who presented with new-onset cutaneous lesions accompanied by fever. Diagnostic evaluation included skin biopsy histopathology, comprehensive microbiological cultures, serological testing, and sequential colonoscopies to assess UC activity.ResultsHistopathology revealed a dense neutrophilic infiltrate highly suggestive of SS. Infectious etiologies were ruled out. Repeat colonoscopy revealed active colitis in the setting of newly diagnosed SS. After infliximab failure, upadacitinib 45 mg/day led to pain resolution within one week and complete plaque healing by week 8, with mucosal healing and normalized inflammatory markers at follow−up. Maintenance with 30 mg/day sustained remission.ConclusionActive UC may drive severe neutrophilic EIMs such as SS, even when prior endoscopy suggests remission. Re-assessment of bowel disease activity is crucial in this context. Upadacitinib rapidly controlled both conditions in this anti−TNF−refractory case, supporting JAK inhibition for similar overlapping immune−mediated presentations.

Introduction

UC is a chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory disorder of the large intestine, characterized by mucosal inflammation confined to the colon and rectum (1). Up to 40% of UC patients develop EIMs, which can precede, coincide with, or occur independently of gastrointestinal disease activity (2). Cutaneous EIMs of UC include pyoderma gangrenosum, erythema nodosum, and SS (3). SS being a rare acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis defined by tender erythematous plaques, nodules, fever, leukocytosis, and dermal neutrophilic infiltrates on histopathology (4). SS associated with UC typically reflects underlying immune dysregulation, though its onset may not always align with active gastrointestinal inflammation. Management of UC-associated SS is challenging, as it requires balancing treatment of the underlying bowel disease and the cutaneous manifestations, which often involve immunomodulatory or biologic agents. We report the case of simultaneous mucosal and cutaneous remission after upadacitinib in a patient who failed optimized infliximab therapy.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old woman first presented in August 2020 with symptoms of severe UC, including frequent bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain, and fever. Colonoscopy confirmed severe active pancolitis. She initially responded to mesalazine but experienced relapses every 2–3 months. In February 2023, her symptoms worsened significantly. Colonoscopy revealed ulcerative colitis, chronic recurrent type, involving the left-sided colon, with moderate-to-severe activity and a Mayo endoscopic score of 2 (Figure 1A). Laboratory evaluation showed an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 44 mm/hr (reference 0–20) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) of 16.9 mg/L (reference 0–6). Prompting initiation of infliximab (300mg, 5mg/kg) therapy. Despite standard therapy she exhibited only partial and transient response, with ongoing symptoms and subtherapeutic drug levels. In May 2024, Symptoms had worsened again with progression of endoscopic inflammation. The terminal ileum was normal, but a colonic diverticulum was noted in the proximal ascending colon. At 45 cm from the anal verge, an inflammatory stricture was present, with diffuse inflammation extending proximally to 40 cm and distally to the rectum. The Mayo endoscopic subscore was 3 (Figure 1B), the serum infliximab level was 0.3 μg/mL and the anti-infliximab antibody level was <5 ng/mL, subsequently, the infliximab dose was escalated to 500 mg every 8 weeks, and she was started on oral prednisolone 45 mg daily for disease flare-up. A colonoscopy report in March 2025 revealed diffuse white reticular scarring, mild luminal rigidity and scattered small sessile hyperplastic polyps distal to 40 cm from the anal verge (Figure 1C).The findings were consistent with ulcerative colitis in the scar healing stage, with a Mayo endoscopic score of 0. In August 2025, the patient presented with rapidly progressing, painful erythematous papules, nodules, pustules, and subsequent ulceration on the forehead (Figure 1D) accompanied by low grade fever.

A pivotal skin biopsy revealed epidermal hyperplasia with mild spongiosis and dense neutrophilic and lymphohistiocytic infiltration extending through the dermis into the subcutaneous fat (Figures 2A, B), with significant papillary edema. Special stains Gram (Figure 2C), Grocott-Gomori methenamine silver stain (GMS) (Figure 2D), and Acid fast bacillistain (AFB) (Figure 2E) were negative. An extensive infectious workup was negative, including multiple pus and tissue cultures, blood cultures, and a T-SPOT (TB assay), effectively ruling out bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial pathogens. The lack of clinical improvement despite empirical antibiotics further supported a non-infectious cause. Laboratory investigations showed hs-CRP at 4.62 mg/L and ESR of 67 mm/hr. Autoimmune serology was notable for a positive perinuclear ANCA (pANCA) pattern with negative cytoplasmic ANCA (cANCA). Specific antibodies for PR3-ANCA and MPO-ANCA were negative. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) was positive at a low titer of 1:32. This pattern, while not indicative of classic ANCA-associated vasculitis, suggested an underlying autoimmune diathesis. Crucially, the systemic workup linked the skin lesion to her underlying IBD. Although her recent colonoscopy suggested scarring, the significantly elevated ESR prompted a repeat endoscopic evaluation. This follow-up colonoscopy demonstrated diffuse active inflammation starting 40 cm from the anal verge, characterized by erythema, friability, and erosions (Figure 2F). This revealed that the cicatricial phase was deceptive, and the patient actually had active refractory UC, serving as the trigger for her Sweet’s Syndrome. Given her refractory intestinal disease and new dermatological manifestation, a decision was made to discontinue infliximab. After providing informed consent regarding potential risks, she was started on monotherapy with upadacitinib 45 mg once daily.

Outcome and follow-up

Within one week the administration of upadacitinib 45 mg daily treatment bowel frequency decreased from 5–6 to 2 soft stools daily without visible blood, pain associated with the dermatitis resolved and the forehead lesion flattened. At week 8 the frontal plaque healed and formed a linear scar (Figure 3A). Colonoscopy at week 10 showed mucosal healing (Mayo 0) (Figure 3B). Laboratory parameters normalized hs-CRP 0.781 mg/L (Figure 3C), ESR decreased to 25 mm/L (Figure 3D), Following a dose reduction, the patient was maintained on upadacitinib 30 mg daily for a period of six months before undergoing assessment. No adverse events infection, cytopenia, thrombosis, or hepatic dysfunction have been observed to date.

Discussion and literature review

Sweet’s syndrome is an autoinflammatory neutrophilic dermatosis. Associated with a broad spectrum of underlying conditions, including infections, hematological malignancies, and autoimmune or inflammatory disorders, whose common pathophysiological basis involves the activation of specific pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including TNF-α, IL-1, IL-12/23, and IL-17, among others (5). However, its precise pathogenesis, pathophysiology, and definitive treatment strategies beyond corticosteroids remain incompletely understood. The clinical manifestations of the disease are diverse, presenting with skin lesions such as erythema, papules, nodules, plaques, and ulcers, often accompanied by pain or tenderness. Some patients may also experience systemic symptoms including fever, arthralgia, and myalgia (6).

The patient’s forehead lesion was ulcerated and accompanied by fever. Histopathologically characteristic of SS, showing dense neutrophilic infiltrate with papillary dermal edema and no evidence of vasculitis. This differs from pyoderma gangrenosum (PG), which clinically presents as a severely painful erythematous lesion that rapidly advances to a blistering or necrotic ulcer; the borders are frequently irregular and undermined, with a violaceous or erythematous rim. PG typically exhibits a neutrophilic infiltrate with suppurative folliculitis and often presents with prominent ulceration and undermined borders, histology may reveal a deeper, more destructive neutrophilic process (7, 8). SS can occur as an extraintestinal manifestation of UC. Controlling the underlying IBD activity forms the cornerstone for managing its associated cutaneous manifestations, such as SS and erythema nodosum (9). Conversely, medications used to treat IBD can themselves induce or exacerbate dermatological issues, which require careful differentiation and management (10). The association between anti-TNF agents and neutrophilic dermatoses is complex and bidirectional. On one hand, TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab and adalimumab have been successfully used to treat refractory cases of pyoderma gangrenosum and Sweet syndrome, which are prototypical neutrophilic dermatoses. On the other hand, paradoxical neutrophilic dermatoses have been reported in patients receiving anti-TNF therapy for inflammatory bowel disease or rheumatoid arthritis, suggesting that TNF blockade may, in rare cases, trigger rather than ameliorate these conditions (11–13). The underlying mechanism remains incompletely understood but may involve immune dysregulation, including altered cytokine networks (e.g., upregulation of interferon-α or interleukin-8) and enhanced neutrophil recruitment independent of TNF signaling. In this case, the temporal relationship between the UC flare and the onset of SS suggests that active intestinal inflammation was the likely driver. However, the possibility of infliximab-induced paradoxical neutrophilic dermatosis cannot be completely excluded. Notably, although the patient had received optimized infliximab therapy with dose escalation, colonoscopy had demonstrated scar healing. The subsequent development of SS prompted repeat endoscopic evaluation, which revealed active intestinal inflammation. This sequence makes disease-driven SS much more likely than a paradoxical drug reaction. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that causality cannot be definitively established from a single case.

Systemic corticosteroids are the first-line treatment for SS. The dosage, typically prednisone at 0.5–1 mg/kg/day, is often titrated to achieve effective control of fever. This regimen usually leads to rapid symptomatic improvement, with skin lesions gradually resolving within 4 to 6 weeks. Biologic agents targeting interleukin-1 (IL-1) or tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) have also been reported to be effective (14). Furthermore, managing SS in the context of refractory IBD presents a challenge. While anti-TNF agents are often effective for EIMs, they can paradoxically induce neutrophilic dermatoses in rare cases. Furthermore, this patient had already failed optimized infliximab therapy. Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi), including tofacitinib, upadacitinib, and filgotinib, offer an alternative therapeutic strategy for inflammatory diseases where anti-TNF agents are ineffective or contraindicated (15). By competitively inhibiting the ATP-binding domain of Janus kinases, these agents block the JAK-STAT signaling pathway downstream of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, and type I interferons) (16). This broad immunomodulatory effect reduces the activation, proliferation, and migration of neutrophils and other immune cells. Recent evidence suggests that JAK inhibitors may also be effective in treating neutrophilic dermatoses, including pyoderma gangrenosum and hidradenitis suppurativa (17, 18), potentially through direct suppression of STAT-dependent neutrophil activation and chemotaxis. The therapeutic efficacy of JAK inhibitors, including upadacitinib, in SS is primarily attributed to their ability to disrupt the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, which mediates the downstream effects of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines. In SS, elevated levels of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α drive the aberrant activation and chemotaxis of neutrophils, a hallmark of the disease. By selectively inhibiting JAK1, upadacitinib effectively blocks the IL-6/STAT3 axis, thereby reducing neutrophil hyperactivation and dermal infiltration. Furthermore, JAK inhibition modulates T-cell-driven inflammatory pathways, including the IL-23/IL-17 axis, which is particularly relevant in IBD-associated SS, where both intestinal and cutaneous inflammation may be concurrently ameliorated. As a highly selective JAK1 inhibitor, upadacitinib offers a favorable balance between efficacy and safety by specifically targeting the key pathogenic pathways while minimizing off-target effects associated with non-selective JAK inhibition.

In this case, SS developed during a phase of UC characterized by endoscopic scarring, challenging the traditional assumption. Data on JAKi for this specific presentation are lacking. In recent years JAK inhibitors have been approved for the treatment of a range of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs), including rheumatoid arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, giant cell arteritis, atopic dermatitis, alopecia areata, psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease (19–21). Existing reviews of JAKi for inflammatory and immune mediated skin diseases consolidate its efficacy, safety, and therapeutic role, offering a reference for treating cutaneous manifestations in patients with ulcerative colitis (22, 23). This case supports considering JAK inhibitors for UC patients with severe or refractory neutrophilic extraintestinal manifestations.

Conclusion

This report represents a novel, effective, and safe use of upadacitinib to treat refractory UC-associated SS and serves as a promising therapeutic option in neutrophilic dermatoses. Further studies are warranted to analyze the role of the JAK-STAT pathway in SS pathogenesis in the context of IBD and investigate the efficacy of upadacitinib across the spectrum of neutrophilic dermatoses. In conclusion, upadacitinib should be considered as one of the targeted immunotherapeutic options for neutrophilic dermatoses, particularly those secondary to inflammatory bowel disease.
