Hemodynamic stability should not re-establish etomidate as the default induction agent
Authors: Guofu Mao, Haizhen Lan
Published: 2026

We read with interest the systematic review and network meta-analysis by Zampieri et al. comparing induction agents for emergency tracheal intubation in critically ill adults [1]. Their synthesis of randomized evidence is valuable and clinically timely. The main finding was that etomidate and ketamine probably result in similar mortality, while ketamine probably increases cardiovascular collapse and may increase post-induction hypotension and peri-intubation vasopressor use [1]. We agree that this hemodynamic signal matters. However, it should not be interpreted as re-establishing etomidate as the default induction agent for heterogeneous critically ill adults.

The first concern is the hierarchy of outcomes. Cardiovascular collapse during intubation is a serious peri-procedural endpoint, but it is not equivalent to net clinical benefit. In the large RSI trial, ketamine did not significantly reduce in-hospital death by day 28 compared with etomidate, while cardiovascular collapse was more common with ketamine [2]. This supports careful hemodynamic preparation when ketamine is selected, but it does not prove that etomidate should be preferred for all patients. The mortality estimate in the meta-analysis also remained compatible with clinically relevant effects in either direction [1].

The second concern is the discordance between immediate hemodynamic endpoints and downstream outcomes. A target trial emulation using data from 18 Brazilian emergency departments reported higher 7-day and 28-day in-hospital mortality with etomidate than with ketamine, although early hemodynamic instability was more frequent after ketamine [3]. This observational analysis cannot override randomized evidence. Nevertheless, the opposite direction of the early and later signals is clinically informative. It cautions against treating short-term blood pressure behavior as a surrogate for overall benefit.

The third concern is patient phenotype. Etomidate is attractive when immediate circulatory tolerance is the dominant concern, but its adrenal effects remain relevant in sepsis, catecholamine-dependent shock and patients with limited adrenal reserve. Current Society of Critical Care Medicine guidance does not identify a universal preferred induction agent and suggests no clear difference between etomidate and other induction agents with respect to mortality, hypotension or vasopressor use [4]. Pooled comparisons may therefore obscure effect modification by sepsis, shock severity, pre-intubation vasopressor exposure, corticosteroid use, induction dose and post-intubation sedation.

The practical implication is not to replace one universal default with another. Etomidate remains reasonable when immediate hemodynamic tolerance is prioritized. Ketamine remains reasonable when analgesia, bronchodilation or avoidance of adrenal suppression is prioritized. For both drugs, outcome likely depends on the surrounding intubation bundle, including preoxygenation, vasopressor readiness, dose adjustment, neuromuscular blockade, first-pass success and prompt post-intubation analgosedation.

Zampieri et al. have clarified an important short-term safety signal for ketamine in the available randomized evidence [1]. The clinical message should remain narrower. Short-term hemodynamic stability is important, but it should not be used alone to define the default induction agent in critically ill adults. Future trials and evidence syntheses should prioritize phenotype-based treatment effects and report both immediate cardiovascular events and downstream patient-centered outcomes.
