Transforming duck egg liquid albumen by-products into sustainable technology for artificially cured salted egg production
Authors: Wen-Chang Chang, Chia-Hui Li, Ping-Hsiu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing use of salted egg yolks (SEY) from salted duck eggs (SEs) in the food industry has drawn attention to the byproduct, liquid albumin (LA). This study addressed the high cholesterol in salted duck egg yolks by developing a plant-based low-cholesterol alternative using mung bean paste (MBP) for texture and pumpkin paste (PP) for color. Next, the optimal SEY (MBP-PP) will be combined with varying amounts of LA to produce ready-to-eat artificially cured SEs. Results show that MBP-PP achieved the best visual appearance, with no significant differences in color, yellowness index (YI), or particle size compared with the control. The 30 g artificially cured SEY (37.5% MBP-PP) mixed with 50 g LA formulation received the highest overall acceptance (5.82). Moreover, the control had higher caloric and nutrient values, but the 30:50 group showed significantly lower cholesterol (LC; 5.83 and 575.81 mg/100 g; p < 0.05). An artificially cured LC-SE formulation was developed using MBP-PP as the SEY and LA as a co-blend, supporting the circular bioeconomy. However, its visual appearance and texture require further optimization for use in simulated egg products and industrial-scale production.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Engineering, Plant sciences

Introduction

Many consumers hold the stereotype that egg yolks’ (EY) high cholesterol content (about 200 mg/egg) is detrimental to health1. At the same time, leading them to discard EYs and their products while still consuming eggs in daily life, particularly hard-boiled eggs2. It is worth noting that other nutrients in EY, including the amounts of lipoproteins, vitamins (A, D, E, and B12), phospholipids, and lecithin, contribute to flavor, nutritional value, and superior emulsifying properties3,4. Tang et al.2 reported that incorporating β-cyclodextrin to eliminate cholesterol from EY effectively reduced off-flavors and bitterness, while preserving its essential amino acids, color, and consumer acceptance, despite a reduction in polyunsaturated fatty acid content. Furthermore, approaches such as supercritical carbon dioxide extraction, solvent extraction, cholesterol oxidase treatment, and chitosan adsorption each exhibit distinct characteristics and are suited to different application scopes1.

Meanwhile, the global SE market has expanded rapidly, with a reported compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of ~7.5% between 2026 and 20335, and a projected increase in market value from USD 0.5 billion in 2025 to nearly USD 4 billion by 20335,6. These processed duck egg products include preserved duck eggs (pidan, century eggs) and SEY7–10. Apart from overseas Chinese, the primary market also encompasses countries across Asia6,10,11. Despite its long history and large-scale production, the traditional SE curing process still falls short of meeting the rapidly growing demand for SEY products6,12,13. Nevertheless, SEY’s production capacity continues to expand daily, even amid challenges such as lengthy curing times and difficulties in maintaining strict hygiene standards12,13. It is undeniable that current processing results in substantial LA waste9,12,14, while SEs inherently pose health risks, including elevated cholesterol and sodium levels6,15. It has been confirmed that consistently high-salt diets have been closely correlated with a wide range of health issues, such as metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, stomach cancer, and increased risk of kidney disease15–17. Hence, concerns arise that SEs produced via traditional high-salt curing methods may pose health risks to humans6,9,15. These threats have also prompted research and development efforts to improve SE production processes and the LA’s circular bioeconomy. Approaches such as separating SEY from LA, minimizing salting concentrations18–21, or reprocessing LA (50% diluted) for pickled hog casings, followed by the production of Chinese-style sausages 6, have been reported to effectively address these limitations. Interestingly, Wang et al.14 also reported that a hybrid gel prepared from LA and SEY (ratio 1:1, w/w) enabled regulation of the gel’s structure and nutrient delivery, releasing more free calcium (with bioavailability reaching 14.17%), thereby enhancing calcium transport in food.

Previously, reports surfaced about fake eggs lacking nutritional value and health benefits, made using various chemicals, additives, and auxiliary agents (such as pigments, sodium alginate, benzoic acid, resins, and coagulants) that had appeared on the market, posing a chemical hazard to consumers22,23. As global demand for sustainable development, reducing greenhouse gas emissions, and low-carbon lifestyles continues to rise, consumer interest in plant-based foods is growing24–26. This trend is contributing significantly to the growth of products associated with the circular bioeconomy24. In recent years, a cascade of interconnected events has laid bare the fragility of the global egg supply chain. These include the COVID-19 pandemic, avian influenza outbreaks, rising feed costs, animal welfare concerns, growing vegetarian preferences, and persistent health concerns about eggs and their derivatives6,24–26. Taking all these factors into consideration has generated considerable interest in developing plant-based egg alternatives among both the scientific community and the general public25,26. Interestingly, the majority of the public has voiced a favorable outlook on plant-based egg alternatives26. Therefore, formula-designed simulated eggs are more economically viable than traditional eggs. Through food processing techniques that combine necessary ingredients with nutrients tailored to diverse communities (including gender, age, religion, etc.), they can deliver specific levels of sensory, nutritional, and functional properties26. These substantial advantages will yield significant and enduring benefits for the foreseeable future.

Moreover, this study aimed to use MBP’s unique gritty texture to simulate SEY’s sandiness while adhering to circular bioeconomy and cost-saving principles. Furthermore, the formulation also considered incorporating sweet potato paste (SPP) and pumpkin paste (PP) to match the orange-yellow hue of SEY to produce artificially cured LC SEY. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of LA was added to develop artificially cured SEs. This study anticipates effectively utilizing the byproduct LA from pickled SEs to address the prevailing oversupply issue, thereby mitigating environmental pollution and unlocking the potential to develop more diverse, novel simulated egg products.

Results

Appearance and color

This study employed starch from various plant-based sources to artificially cured SEY, aspiring to develop an LC alternative. Briefly, the artificially cured formulations employed MB flour (MBF) and MBP as primary materials, supplemented with tapioca starch (TS), SPP, pumpkin flour (PF), and PP. In terms of appearance, the MBF-PP and MBP-PP groups most closely resembled the control, despite lacking the oily sheen under light (Supplementary Fig. S1). It is undeniable that the surfaces of all MBF-based SEY groups exhibit a dry, hardened, and rough-textured structure, accompanied by numerous irregular cracks. In contrast, the MBP-SPP and MBP-PP groups may retain higher moisture content, forming smooth, uniform surfaces, though fewer minor fissures remain.

Regarding the L* values of artificially cured SEY across all groups in this study, the MBF-PP group most resembled the control group, with no significant difference (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, the other groups all exhibited significant differences (p < 0.05) compared to the control group. In addition, the a* values for each group were highest for MBF-PP, followed by MBP-PP, then the control group, with significant differences (p < 0.05) observed among all groups.

Regarding b* values, MBF-PP exhibited the highest value, followed by the control group. Between these two groups, no significant difference was observed; yet, compared to the other groups, a significant difference (p < 0.05) was noted. Additionally, regarding YI values, the MBF-PF, MBF-PP, MBP-PF, and MBP-PP groups all showed values lower than those of the control group, but no significant differences were observed among them. Common knowledge holds that SEY containing abundant β-carotene typically exhibits an orange-yellow hue.

Particle size distribution (D)

The particle size D of the formulations in this study, including the control group, showed slight variations due to the use of materials derived from different sources (Supplementary Fig. S2). Specifically, the D[4,3] values of the MBF-PP (79.82 μm) and MBP-TS (74.33 μm) groups were closest to that of the control (74.95 μm), while significant differences were observed among most other groups (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S3).

In terms of D[3,2], the values for MBF-PP and MBP-PP (82.18 and 86.89 μm) were nearest to the control group (77.70 μm) in all groups, while significant differences existed overall (p < 0.05).

Regarding the Span value, a smaller value reflects greater uniformity in particle size and a smoother D. This study revealed significant differences among all groups (p < 0.05), with the top three groups with the largest Span values being MBF-PF (4.56), MBP-TS (5.45), and MBP-PF (2.90) (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, the MBP-SPP group (1.50) was closest to the control group (1.57), with no significant difference between the two. Based on the results from the previous section, this study selected MBP (providing a sandy mouthfeel) and PP (providing an orange-yellow appearance) as ingredients to investigate the optimal blending ratio. Specifically, the goal was to develop a formulation with a mouthfeel profile comparable to that of commercially available SEY. Briefly, PP were used at 12.5, 25, 37.5, and 50%, while MBP was supplemented at different ratios to a total of 100% (w/w) to formulate artificially cured SEY (Supplementary Fig. S3). Subsequently, the following analyses were performed: As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, artificially cured SEY exhibits a color shift from yellow to orange-red. These changes increase the proportion of PP in the formulation, thereby corresponding to reducing MBP. Consequently, the MBP-PP 37.5 (62.5 g of MBP combined with 37.5 g of PP) and 50% (both MBP and PP weigh 50 g) formulations most closely match the visual appearance of commercially available SEY.

The L* values of all MBP-PP groups except the 12.50% group differed significantly from those of the control group (p < 0.05) (Table 1). The results showed that as the PP content in the formulation increased, the L* value decreased. Interestingly, the observed increase in PP was accompanied by a marked increase in the a* value. Notably, all groups except the 25% group showed statistically significant differences compared with the control group (p < 0.05). This phenomenon appeared to be attributable to the intrinsic color of PP, which enhances the redness of artificially cured SEY.

Moisture content and water activity (AW)

All formulations of artificially cured SEY had higher moisture content than the control sample, which had 22.56% (Fig. 1A). Notably, the moisture levels increased progressively with a higher proportion of PP in the formulation. There were significant differences (p < 0.05) between all groups except the 37.5 and 50% groups. In particular, the moisture content of artificially cured SEY was increased with the proportion of PP in the formulation. The results illustrate formulation-dependent differences in moisture retention and AW, which are relevant to texture and shelf-life considerations.

In terms of AW, values ranged from 0.91 to 0.95 across all groups (Fig. 1B). Although the differences in AW were statistically significant, the relatively narrow range (0.91–0.95) suggests that AW played a secondary role compared with moisture content in influencing textural and sensory attributes.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

This study’s results revealed that TPA values in each group showed significant differences, varying in degree, compared with those in the control group (p < 0.05) (Table 2). This phenomenon is still largely explained by the fundamental differences between commercial SEY and their plant-based ingredients. Specifically, the hardness of all groups decreased gradually with increasing PP incorporation in artificially cured SEY, and all groups showed significant differences compared with the control group (p < 0.05). In contrast, all groups tested exhibited a progressive increase in key textural properties, including adhesiveness, springiness, cohesiveness, and chewiness. Furthermore, gumminess initially decreased and then increased as the proportion of PP incorporated into the artificially cured SEY formulation increased.

Discussion

This study showed that the lower L* values observed in groups containing PF and PP can be attributed to water filling cellular spaces, which facilitates heat conduction and triggers carotenoid conversion27. While samples exhibit yellow-orange to red hues, this process simultaneously darkens and reduces brightness (L*)27. Concurrently, the temperature-induced Maillard reaction (MR) contributed to non-enzymatic browning27–29. Furthermore, it has been reported that the L* value of cooked MBP decreases progressively with increasing cooking time30. A study noted that dough containing 20% freeze-dried PF exhibited the most saturated color characteristics (increased a* and b* values), while its L* value decreased31. The same authors reported that these effects are primarily attributed to reddening and yellowing caused by carotenoids. These color-changing trends align with the findings reported by Huang et al.30 on cooked MBP.

Moreover, pumpkins were reported to undergo various thermal processing methods, including pureeing, steaming, boiling, and hot-air drying, which degrade carotenoids and induce structural modifications, such as cis-isomerization27,32. Ouyang et al.32 also reported that pumpkin pulp contains 25 types of carotenoids, where those with 5,6-epoxy groups exhibited better heat resistance than β-carotene, while xanthophylls demonstrated lower thermal stability than their diesters. Notably, the β-carotene content in these crops varies depending on factors such as cultivar, growing location, climate, harvest timing, and agronomic management practices31,33,34. Therefore, the coloration of the four groups above most closely resembles that of commercially available SEY. It is worth noting that during rearing, achieving this appealing EY color in poultry feed is facilitated by nutritional additives (such as Dunaliella salina microalgae, red using canthaxanthin, astaxanthin, capsanthin, capsorubin, or lycopene, while yellow uses zeaxanthin, lutein, violaxanthin, β-cryptoxanthin, etc.) designed to meet consumer expectations35–37. Furthermore, Chen et al. 38 also reported that feeding Hailan brown laying hens aged 48 weeks selenium methionine (0.75 mg/kg) for 42 consecutive days would yield eggs enriched in selenium. Hence, this also indicates that the nutritional profile or color of poultry eggs can be significantly improved by using scientifically formulated feed enriched with essential additives or nutrients. This approach, known as nutritional fortification, effectively aligns the final product with consumer expectations38. Overall, color parameter analysis indicated that among all artificially cured SEY formulations, the MBF-PP group exhibited the closest color match to the control, particularly in L*, b*, and ΔE values, whereas formulations containing PF generally showed lower L* values and more pronounced color differences.

Based on the above values, the calculated ΔE values indicated that, compared with the control group, differences among the MBF-PF, MBF-PP, and MBP-PP groups were minor, and significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed within each group. This also suggested that the appearance and color of these three groups were more similar to commercially available SEY. Since a ΔE value of at least 3 represents the color difference perceptible to most human observers27. Consequently, this demonstrates that the use of PP with abundant β-carotenoids in this study effectively achieves the natural orange-red hue of SEY based on the inherent color sources.

Gritty texture (sandiness) serves as a key indicator of SEY quality39. In traditional SE production processes, abundant granular matter forms during plasma gel (solid or semisolid, gel-like) formation, primarily contributing to the gritty texture characteristic of SEY20,40,41. Therefore, the particle D pattern can be used to analyze the gritty texture of SEY. Studies suggest that the average particle size of MBP ranges from 47 to 80 μm30, whereas adzuki bean paste typically measures 80–110 μm42. However, larger particles in bean paste yield a more satisfying grainy texture42. Therefore, the grainy texture arises primarily from the thermal denaturation of proteins during the cooking process, which encapsulates starch granules, forming these particles30,42. Rodríguez-Manrique et al.43 also reported that the homogenized pumpkin pulp-hawthorn yam modified starch suspension exhibited physicochemical stability, with relatively high D[2, 3] and D[3, 4] values. In addition, all groups except the MBF-TS group showed distinct double-peaked patterns in both D[10] and D[90] values (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Fig. S2), exhibiting significant differences (p < 0.05; except for the MBF-PP group). Notably, most formulations exhibited a bimodal particle-size D, characterized by distinct D[10] and D[90] peaks (Supplementary Table S3; Supplementary Fig. S2), consistent with the granular structure reported for traditional SEY of the Melese and Keyata28 report. However, the particle size was used in this study as an indirect physicochemical indicator of sandiness rather than a direct measure of sensory perception, providing insight into the granular characteristics relevant to gritty texture simulation. Moreover, heating-induced changes in the physicochemical and structural properties of MBP, along with the formation of a gritty texture30, impart the sandiness of artificially cured SEY particles. Consequently, this facilitates a more direct simulation of the mouthfeel characteristic of native SEY.

Mitiku and Bereka44 also reported that incorporating PF into bread formulas increased the finished product’s moisture content (from 11.07 to 13.19%), consistent with this study’s findings. However, the low moisture content of SEY (from commercially available SE) has been reported with two potential mechanisms: (1) Osmotic pressure differences between the interior and exterior of the SEY, driven by salt ion diffusion from the exterior to the interior (eggshell-LA-SEY) during pickling, promote water molecule efflux from the SEY, thereby reducing its moisture content17. (2) SEY’s protein folding exposed hydrophobic groups, thereby enhancing surface hydrophobicity17. Typically, higher moisture content results in a softer texture, a principle that also holds for traditionally cured SEs45. However, protein oxidation also accelerates softening in traditionally cured SEY, thereby intensifying mudding progression45–47. In other words, oxidative reactions also weaken hydrogen bonding between molecules by disrupting the SEY gel structure, causing water molecules within lipoproteins to switch from bound to free water48. These properties also affect the TPA of the samples, including decreased hardness and increased springiness. It has also been reported that traditionally cured SEY forms a muddy consistency internally, which is related to moisture content45. Specifically, the water content of food promotes the dissolution of reaction substrates, thus accelerating the MR within the food45. The phenomenon has also been attributed to the migration of water from the LA to the SEY during pickling and cooking, thereby increasing the SEY’s water content and elevating the AW within the SEY45.

These AW values were insufficient to inhibit the growth of foodborne microorganisms (the threshold must be below 0.65)6,49. Therefore, freezing is required to preserve such SEY and extend its shelf life49. Collectively, these findings suggest that the elevated moisture content and AW in artificially cured SEY are closely associated with textural softening, consistent with the observed changes in TPA parameters and expected to influence sensory mouthfeel.

Moreover, Gallardo et al.31 reported that replacing a portion of wheat flour with dehydrated or freeze-dried PF in pumpkin cookie recipes increases dough hardness while decreasing cohesiveness and springiness. According to the same authors, larger PF particles produce a less uniform dough with diminished water retention, leading to a denser, more compact crumb structure and greater firmness in the final baked product. However, these findings differ from those obtained with PP in this study. According to the above results, all TPA values, except hardness, were highest in the 50% group, yet still showed a considerable gap compared to the control group. These phenomena and differences stem from alterations in SEY’s protein secondary structure during commercial SEs pickling, specifically the transformation from α- and β- to T-turn structures, accompanied by weakened hydrogen bonds within the secondary structure40.

Furthermore, Zhang et al.17 also indicated that the increase in SEY hardness is due to the incorporation of sodium chloride, which modified electrostatic interactions in the system; namely, sodium ions neutralize the negatively charged proteins, thereby reducing electrostatic repulsion between them. As a result, intermolecular attractive forces become more dominant, which facilitates protein aggregation17. Alternatively, sodium chloride promotes protein aggregation in SE, while SEY dehydration also contributes to a more compact structure, thereby increasing hardness48. Li et al.48 noted that sodium chloride disrupts lipoprotein structures, resulting in reduced springiness of SEY, and this effect may be linked to SEY’s lower water content. Wang et al.20 indicated that the internal aggregation within the SEY three-dimensional network was uniform, and that gumminess properties resulted from the combination of hardness and cohesiveness; namely, the trend of change paralleled that of hardness, while springiness and chewiness demonstrated analogous patterns. Nevertheless, Yuanyuan Liu et al.39 reported that cooked EYs can develop a gritty texture due to the accumulation of EY spheres, thereby reducing the resistance experienced during chewing. The same authors noted that elevated salivary adsorption, combined with oral friction, may contribute to a sensation of choking. However, investigations into simulating the disintegration of these oral sensory and artificially cured SEY-particles-mediated motility and accumulation structures are currently a limitation of this study, and these areas represent key targets for future research.

Based on the above results, the artificially cured SEY formulation (37.5%) was subsequently blended with varying proportions of LA. The appearance of the artificially cured SEs in each group is shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. However, samples used for supply evaluation require reshaping and slicing into flat pieces to minimize the impact of panel members’ bias toward fragmented appearances on the final evaluation results. Interestingly, a computational fluid dynamics study of the LA solidification process revealed that solidification (cooked for 800–1200 s) occurs uniformly, progressing from the outer surface of the sphere (eggshell) toward the center50. The same authors also reported that heterogeneity in LA determines the cooking process, whereby even during solidification, a non-uniform geometric sequence persists, subsequently trapping liquid within regions of the solid. According to Liu et al.51, higher temperatures also promote protein interactions, resulting in a denser and less elastic network in both LA and chicken albumen. These mechanisms also explain the issue with the current heating method (cooking for 30 min in a multi-functional cooker), where overly intense heat results in poor shaping and affects appearance. Despite Liu et al.52 reporting that liquid EY, following pasteurization, undergoes further thermal denaturation of small aggregates through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions when heated at 90 °C for 30 min in an amino acid solution. Subsequently, it forms a dense, ordered gel network structure with satisfactory mechanical properties and water retention capacity52. To ensure optimal efficiency, we must either reduce the processing time appropriately or adopt an alternative heat-treatment method. Undeniably, current manufacturing processes also require supplementation with incorporating metal ions (Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, etc.), hydrogels, emulsifiers, proteases, and stabilizers to better simulate the intricate intermolecular structures of native SE and EYG21,53,54. It has been reported that K+, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺ enhanced the hydrophobic interactions between SEY and LA, promoting the transition of protein secondary structures from α-helix and β-turn to β-sheet55. Consequently, these ions facilitated the formation of a compact, regular network with smaller pore diameters. In parallel, the synergistic effect of UA and amino acids (arginine and lysine) reported in Zang et al.56 may also be explored to delay heat-induced protein aggregation in LA and enhance the gel-forming properties. Notably, the addition of xylitol has been reported to reduce the surface hydrophobicity of LA proteins while enhancing the thermal stability, structural integrity, and functional properties57. It also inhibits EY aggregation and minimizes the conversion of soluble components into insoluble forms during pasteurization, thereby improving its thermal stability57. However, employing a thermally-enzymatically co-modified LA approach is also worth considering, as it facilitates protein unfolding and exposure of enzymatic cleavage sites58. The increased surface hydrophobicity enables the formation of low-volume-density, uniform aggregates with enhanced thermal stability58.

Sensory differences among formulations mainly reflect variations in appearance and overall acceptability, which may be associated with underlying physicochemical characteristics. To avoid additional LA generation, Liu et al.12 and Wang et al.9 reported that the flavor (taste and odor) of SEY produced via composite molding methods differs substantially from that of traditional SEY. Notably, the 30:50 group demonstrated the highest overall acceptance score (5.82). It is noteworthy that the acceptability ratings across all participant groups exceeded the 5-point threshold, reflecting a consistently satisfactory performance28. Therefore, this higher overall acceptability is consistent with the moderate moisture content and balanced physicochemical properties of the 30:50 formulation, which likely contributed to a favorable mouthfeel without excessive softness or mudding. Overall, the sensory results support the physicochemical findings, indicating that formulations with appropriate moisture retention and structural balance tend to achieve higher consumer acceptance.

Interestingly, substituting pumpkin or legumes into compound dough has also been reported to reduce the carbohydrate content of the final product28. Conversely, incorporating legumes enhances the product’s protein content28. However, both the control group and the artificially cured SE produced using LA-blended artificially cured SE exhibit high sodium content. This phenomenon was attributed to the substantial use of salting in traditional SE curing processes9,13. It serves multiple purposes: enhancing flavor, inhibiting microbial growth, regulating SE moisture content, and effectively altering their texture (loose, sandy, and rich with oil), color, and flavor characteristics13,15,45,47. Unfortunately, the salt content of SEY produced by traditional curing typically exceeds 2 g, thereby increasing the burden on people’s daily salt intake15, while global average salt intake estimates range from 9.1 to 11.8 g per day16. However, in Asian countries, daily sodium chloride intake frequently exceeds 12 g59. In contrast, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that adults limit daily sodium intake to no more than 5 g15,16. Therefore, innovative processes have been reported to improve SE by reducing salt or minimizing curing time through either physical approaches (such as water-cycle, ultrasound-assisted (UA), vacuum decompression, magnetoelectric-assisted, high-pressure processing, or pulsed electric fields) or alternative salt-substituting substances (including arginine, flavonoids, polyphenols, polysaccharides [cellulose, carrageenan], CaCl2, ZnCl2, NH4Cl, KCl, MgCl2, citric acid, ferric citrate, Chinese wine (baijiu), rice wine, ascorbic acid, flavorings, etc.)9,15–17,19,41,60,61.

It is worth noting that the total caloric value of commercially available SE (182.06 kcal/100 g) exceeds that of artificially cured SE (77.67 kcal/100 g) by 2.34-fold. The findings of this study correspond with the results of the Zahir and Akhter26 report, namely that one serving of chicken eggs (70 kcal/100 g) contains twice the calories of one serving of plant-based eggs (35 kcal/100 g). In terms of cholesterol content, commercially available SE contained 575.81 mg/100 g, which was 98.8-fold higher than the artificially cured SE (5.83 mg/100 g) in this study. There was a significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.05). Zahir and Akhter26 also indicated that plant-based eggs produced through food processing technologies offer potential to minimize dietary cholesterol intake while enhancing the bioavailability and stability of other nutrients (such as omega-3 fatty acids, essential amino acids, minerals, vitamins, etc.). Notably, plant-based eggs remain cholesterol-free, whereas a single egg contains ~200 mg of cholesterol26. The use of artificially cured SEY in conjunction with LA for the production of artificially cured SEs has demonstrated noteworthy advances; however, there remains substantial potential for further improvement. It requires deeper investigation and trials to refine the production techniques, ingredient formulations, and sensory characteristics of such an innovative food product26. This study showed that the MBP (62.5 g)-PP (37.5 g) formulation delivered a satisfyingly grainy texture and orange-red-yellow coloration, effectively simulating the visual appearance and gritty texture of SEY. In addition, employing the above formulation (30 g of artificially cured SEY mixed with 50 g of LA) could yield a ready-to-eat artificially cured SE product with high consumer acceptance.

In conclusion, this study provides critical knowledge for practical innovations in LA utilization, to develop potentially viable artificially cured SEY formulations while simultaneously creating artificially cured SEs with overall quality comparable to conventional SEs. Most importantly, this technology can reduce consumer cholesterol intake, a critical benefit in today’s fast-food-dominated society, where excessive dietary fat consumption is prevalent. Despite efforts, challenges in formulation, processing technology, and nutritional requirements persist, underscoring the need for further investigation through both basic and applied research. In particular, the limitations of this study involve the laboratory-scale nature of the current results, necessitating further refinement of the auxiliary effects of other ingredients to achieve an appearance and texture more akin to SEs. In particular, subsequent optimization involves screening for optimal ratios of food additives, such as oils, flavorings, and antioxidants, and conducting shelf-life tests on the products to facilitate a smooth transition into the consumer market. Therefore, it is beneficial for the food-related industry to achieve environmental, economic, and social sustainability. We can realize this by promoting emission reduction and pollution control, thereby broadening the innovative application value of LA in the food processing industry.

Methods

Materials

Commercially available SEY and LA (containing 25% salt) were purchased from Yong Hao merchant (Tainan, Taiwan). MBF was purchased from Yuxing Industrial Co., Ltd. (Tainan, Taiwan). Shelled MB (Origin: Myanmar) were purchased from Xin Hong Grains Distributor (Taipei, Taiwan). Sweet potatoes (Tainong No. 57) and pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata) were purchased from Madou Farmers’ Association Fresh supermarket (Tainan, Taiwan). TS was purchased from Sunright Foods Industrial Co., Ltd. (New Taipei City, Taiwan). PF was purchased from Chia-Hui Spices Co., Ltd. (Taoyuan, Taiwan). All necessary chemical reagents were sourced directly from Sigma-Aldrich® (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and employed as received, without additional purification steps, except where explicitly indicated.

Raw material pretreatment

All paste-processing conditions were established according to the method described by Huang et al.30, with some modifications. Extract 300 g of SMB in 300 mL of distilled water (DW), soak at 25 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, place the mixture in a multi-functional cooker and steam for 30 min. Allow to cool to 25 °C before grinding into a paste. Transfer to a BPA-free, low-density polyethylene (LDPE) zipper bag (measuring 180 × 320 × 0.08 mm, GE-SEN Plastics Enterprise Co., Ltd., Taoyuan, Taiwan) and store refrigerated at 4 °C for later use. Specifically, all samples have preservation periods of no more than 2 weeks; in contrast, the artificially cured SE samples produced in this study exhibit a shelf life of 12 months (at room temperature) following sterilization. Peel the pumpkin and sweet potato, each weighing 300 g. Then steam them individually for 15 min using a multi-functional cooker (Tatung Co., Taipei, Taiwan). Then, allow them to cool to 25 °C before mashing them into a puree, respectively. Next, place the samples in a zipper bag (GE-SEN Plastics Enterprise Co.) and refrigerate them at 4 °C until needed.

Plant-based artificially cured SEY substitute ingredients and formulation

This study used eight formulations to prepare artificially cured SEY, employing the following preparation methods and codes, with commercially available SEY serving as the control group. This study employed eight formulations (Supplementary Table S1) of artificially cured SEY (each weighing 15 g) and molded them into shapes. Briefly, there are two main categories: one primarily uses (1) MBF, while the other primarily uses (2) MBP. More specifically, preparation methods and codes are as follows: (1) The MBF-TS group was prepared as follows: 35 g of MBF was added to 15 mL of DW, followed by 50 g of TS. The MBF-SPP group employed 50 g of MBF mixed with 50 g of SPP. The MBF-PF group consisted of 35 g MBF mixed with 15 mL DW, followed by the addition of 50 g PF. The MBF-PP group used a mixture of 50 g MBF and 50 g PP. (2) The MBP-TS group was prepared by mixing 50 g MBP with 15 mL DW, then supplementing with 35 g TS. The MBP-SPP group consisted of 50 g of MBP mixed with 50 g of SPP. The MBP-MBF group was prepared by adding 50 g of MBP to 15 mL of DW, with 35 g of MBF. The MBP-PP group was prepared as follows: 50 g of MBP mixed with 50 g of PP.

Formulation and ingredient options for artificially-cured SEY

Based on the grouping and screening from the previous section, this study identified MBP blended with PP as the optimal material for artificially-cured LC-SEY. Subsequently, the two materials were mixed and grouped according to the following ratios. Each sample was molded at 15 g for subsequent evaluation to identify the optimal formulation ratio. The 12.5% group consists of a mixture of 87.5 g of MBP and 12.5 g of PP (w/w); the 25.0% group combined 75.0 g of MBP with 25.0 g of PP (w/w); the 37.5% group combined 62.5 g of MBP with 37.5 g of PP (w/w); the 50.0% group combined equal portions of both 50.0 g MBP and PP (w/w).

Formulation and ingredient options for ready-to-eat artificially-cured SEs

The artificially-cured SEs were processed by combining the above-mentioned SEY with varying proportions of LA. Briefly, the formulation from the previous section (37.5%: MBP, 62.5 g, and PP, 37.5 g, w/w) was used to prepare artificially-cured SEY samples weighing 15 and 30 g. The samples were then divided into four groups based on the ratio of LA added, designated as follows: 15:50, 30:50, 15:100, and 15:150. Afterwards, transfer to heat-resistant aluminum (AL) foil bags [four-layer: polyethylene terephthalate (PET)/AL/nylon/high-temperature-resistant polypropylene, 240 × 160 × 0.11 mm, GE-SEN Plastics Enterprise Co., Ltd.], seal, and place in a multi-functional cooker (Tatung Co.) for 30 min of steaming.

Gritty texture analysis

The sample analysis followed the methodology described by Liu et al.40 with minor modifications. Specifically, 5 g of sample was suspended in 200 mL of DW (w/v), blended for 15 s, and then analyzed using a laser particle size analyzer (Partica LA-960V2, HORIBA, Ltd., Kyoto, Japan).

TPA

The TPA of the sample was determined according to the methodology of Lin et al.7 with minor modifications. Sliced the sample in half horizontally and performed testing using a texture analyzer (TA-XT2, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK) equipped with a P/36 R probe. The setting parameters were as follows: initial test speed: 5 mm/sec; test speed: 1 mm/sec; post-test speed: 5 mm/sec; compression ratio: 50%; recoverable time: 5 s; trigger point load: 5 g. Afterward, the samples’ hardness (Newton, N), adhesiveness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness (N) were measured.

Measurement of moisture content and AW

The moisture content and AW of the samples were determined according to the methods described by Cheng et al.64 and Huang et al.11, respectively. The moisture content of the sample was analyzed using a moisture analyzer. 5 g of the sample was weighed, spread evenly on an AL tray, heated at 105 °C, and the moisture content (%) was determined after heating; the value was observed and recorded. The sample’s AW was measured using an AW analyzer (AquaLab WA 99163, Meter Group, Pullman, USA).

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation conducted according to the method described in Lin et al.65, with slight modifications. The sensory evaluation of samples in this study employed an acceptance trial to assess consumer acceptance of artificially cured SE. The samples were randomly coded (526, 227, 144, and 742) and presented to a panel of 30 consumer-type panelists (15 male and 15 female participants aged 18–60, with no specialized training in food science) for evaluation of the appearance, color of SEY, aroma, flavor, saltiness, gritty texture, and overall acceptance of artificially-cured SE and SEY. After being informed of the protocol and agreeing to the participation terms, all participants provided signed informed consent, acknowledging the publication of anonymous data, voluntary, unpaid involvement, and free withdrawal without repercussions. During the evaluation, the room was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C in silence, with panelists isolated in partitioned seats and required to rinse their mouths thoroughly between samples to remove residual taste. Ratings are based on a 9-point hedonic scale ranging from 1 (“Very unacceptable”) to 9 (“Very acceptable”), with intermediate scores reflecting varying levels of acceptability and a neutral midpoint; namely, with higher scores reflecting greater consumer preference for the product.

Nutritional composition analysis

The nutritional composition analyses of the samples, including crude protein, crude fat, crude ash, carbohydrates, and sodium, were performed using the standard methods described in the Chinese National Standards66 5035-N6116, 5036-N6117, 5034-N6115, 12634-N6223, and 12638-N6227, respectively. The saturated- and trans-fat contents in the samples were determined according to the fatty acid testing methods for foods announced by the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration67. Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). An exception is the nutritional composition analysis in Table 3, which was conducted as a single measurement (n = 1).
