 Abstract
 Activation detectors developed at LLNL for measuring real-time neu-
 tron fluence from fusion sources are used in the broader fusion community.
 The recommended fluence operating range of this diagnostic is 5x102 –
 1x106 n/cm2. The upper limit on this fluence range is set by the dead
 time caused by data transfer between the detector and data acquisition
 computer. Delaying the start of counting is a possible strategy to operate
 these detectors in higher fluences.

1 Introduction
The Portable Adaptable Neutron Diagnostic for Advancing Fusion Energy Sci-
ence (PANDA-FES) system[1] uses commercially available Lanthanum Bromide
gamma spectrometers to measure neutron activation from a prompt burst of
2.45 MeV neutrons. These detectors are comprised of an yttrium cap, a lan-
thanum bromide crystal scintillator, a Hamamatsu R6231 PMT[2], and a OR-
TEC DigiBASE-E list mode data acquisition (DAQ)[3], shown in Figure 1. 89 Y
and 79 Br both get activated in high energy (MeV) neutron fluence through in-
elastic scattering reactions and de-excite with a characteristic gamma emission
of 909 keV and 208 keV, respectively. These gammas are detected with the lan-
thanum bromide scintillator and recorded as a function of time and energy using
the list mode DAQ. The calibration and operation of these detectors is described
in detail in a previous publication[1]. The fluence range of the calibration was
between 5x102 and 3x105 n/cm2 , as shown in Figure 2 These activation diagnos-
tics were deployed initially at lower neutron yield experimental platforms and
the lower limit of detection was characterized as 500 n/cm2 . The upper limit
of the fluence range was not initially characterized. This report will discuss the
factors that impact the upper limit of the neutron fluence, define the order of
magnitudes of reliable operation, and make recommendations for increasing the
upper limit of neutron fluence.

the maximum neutron fluence that this diagnostic can function in. If the fluence

2.1 Dead Time
Dead time in the DigiBASE-E is calculated using a Gedcke-Hale live-time clock,
which has uncertainty less than 5% up to 50 kcps count rate.[4] Settings in the
DAQ software can impact the dead time. The lower level discriminator (LLD),
upper level discriminator (ULD), amplifier rise time and flattop width can be
tuned to decrease dead time. Raising the LLD and decreasing the ULD will de-
crease the overall count rate of the system. Decreasing the rise time and flattop
width of the pulse in the amplifier as shown in Figure 4 will decrease the dead
time as well, although the peaking time (rise time + flattop width) is recom-
mended to be greater than 4 times the light collection time of the scintillator.[4]
For the LaBr3 scintillator, the light decay time constant is 16 ns[5], meaning the
peaking time should be greater than 0.064 µs. If the DAQ settings are tuned
to reduce count rate to below 50 kcps, then the dead time can be accurately
corrected for using the live time clock.
 The data transfer rate between the DigiBASE-E and the DAQ computer
that saves the data has been observed to be limited by the rate at which the
computer can pull data from the DigiBASE-E. The ORTEC DigiBASE-E maxi-
mum throughput is advertised as 196 kcps at a 532 kcps input rate.[6] That high
rate has not been realized by this team. When the period of the data trans-
fer between DigiBASE-E and computer is increased from the Windows default
250 ms to 6 ms, the observed saturation level increased from 8 kcps to 50 kcps.

2.2 Pulse Pileup
Pulse pileup is when two particles deposit energy in the scintillator within the
time response of the scintillator. This results in a single event recorded with the
summed energy deposited by both particles. The probability of pileup increases
when count rate is high. This effect would be observed as an upshift in the
gamma decay line energy during the high count rate, shown in Figure 5. The
maximum count rate for the scintillator is governed by the light decay time
within the scintillator. For LaBr3 this is 16 ns, which results in a theoretical
maximum count rate of 62.5 Mcps. This count rate is three orders of magnitude
greater than the count rate limits from the DAQ electronics of this system,
making significant gains in the max fluence theoretically possible with faster
electronics.

2.3 PMT Saturation
PMT non-linearity for a detector in pulse counting mode is dominated by space
charge effects, in which the last dynode has a high electron density and prevents

3 Conclusion
The PANDA-FES activation detector starts to show obvious signs of saturation
at fluences in the few-106 n/cm2 range. This is indicated by a deviation from
the expected exponential decay from activation, with lower number of counts
recorded when the count rate is too high for this detector, as shown in Figure 7.
With the diagnostic as is, the recommended operating range for measuring DD
neutron fluence is 5x102 – 1x106 n/cm2 . The limiting factor for this diagnostic
is the dead time, with both the data transfer rate and DAQ electronic dead time
having a maximum of 50 kcps.

4 Future Strategies for High Fluence
In order to operate in higher fluence environments, some changes to the DAQ,
analysis, and hardware may be considered. Changes to the DAQ and analysis
software are the cheapest and fastest method to consider. The simplest change
is to wait until the count rate has decreased below the detector saturation level

by changing the DAQ routine to open the counting window some time after
the radiation impulse, instead of at the same time. The analysis could also be

changed to start fitting the exponential decay after the count rate is below a
threshold. Because the activation signals are recorded on the order of seconds
and the scintillator and PMT operate on the time scale of 10−8 seconds, the
detector will recover from high count rates before the activation signal decays
away.
 Changes in detector geometry would require more investment, but can reduce
count rate and thus enable operation in higher fluence environments. Decreasing
the size of the scintillator crystal will decrease the count rate in the detector.
For example, the smallest commercially available ORTEC LaBr3 scintillator
is a 1”x1” cylinder, which will reduce the volume by a factor of 3 compared
with the current 1.5”x1.5” cylinder. A more risky method for increasing the
operating fluence range is to add shielding to reduce the fluence incident on the
scintillator. This would require design optimization to ensure that the gamma
background is not inadvertently increased due to neutron interactions in the
shielding material. If the attenuation through the added shielding material is
well known and corrected for, the source yield can be calculated. This will effect
the systematic uncertainty in the yield calculation, which currently includes the
effect of the aluminum EMI shielding tube and yttrium cap in the calibration
constant.
 Software and hardware modifications to the current activation detector are
worth considering in the case of high yields where the facility layout precludes
increasing the distance of the detector from the source.
