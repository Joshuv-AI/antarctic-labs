Towards Automated Community Notes Generation with Large Vision Language Models for Combating Contextual Deception
Jin Ma, Jingwen Yan, Mohammed Aldeen, Ethan Anderson, Taran Kavuru

Abstract
Community Notes have emerged as an effective crowd-sourced mechanism for combating online deception on social media platforms. However, its reliance on human contributors limits both the timeliness and scalability. In this work, we study the automated Community Notes generation method for image-based contextual deception , where an authentic image is paired with misleading context ( e.g. , time, entity, and event). Unlike prior work that primarily focuses on deception detection ( i.e. , judging whether a post is true or false in a binary manner), Community Notes-style systems need to generate concise and grounded notes that help users recover the missing or corrected context. This problem remains underexplored due to three reasons: (i) datasets that support the research are scarce; (ii) methods must handle the dynamic nature of contextual deception; (iii) evaluation is difficult because standard metrics do not capture whether notes actually improve user understanding. To address these gaps, we curate a real-world dataset, XCheck , comprising X posts with associated Community Notes and external contexts. We further propose the Automated Context-Corrective Note generation method, named ACCNote , which is a retrieval-augmented, multi-agent collaboration framework built on large vision-language models. Finally, we introduce a new evaluation metric, Context Helpfulness Score ( CHS ), that aligns with user study outcomes rather than relying on lexical overlap. Experiments on our XCheck dataset show that the proposed ACCNote improves both deception detection and note generation performance over baselines, and exceeds a commercial tool GPT5-mini. Together, our dataset, method, and metric advance practical automated generation of context-corrective notes toward more responsible online social networks.

Related Work

2.1
Contextual Deception Datasets

In the field of image-based contextual deception, several datasets synthesize deceptive examples from non-deceptive data. For instance, NewsCLIPpings
[
29
]
begins with genuine image-text pairs from news outlets and constructs context manipulation cases by semantically mismatching images and captions using CLIP
[
39
]
and SBERT
[
41
]
. Similarly, MMOOC
[
55
]
recombines verified multimodal news articles to produce synthetic mismatches. MMFakeBench
[
26
]
instead leverages generative models and AI tools to construct a mixed-source multimodal misinformation benchmark. While such synthesization enables large-scale data creation, it may not capture the full complexity of real-world deceptive content on social media platforms. By contrast, Fakeddit
[
33
]
and VERITE
[
35
]
collect real-world data from online websites,
e.g.
, Reddit and professional fact-checkers, but provide only veracity labels, without explanatory context.

Recent datasets begin to include explanations to support further research. AMMeBa
[
13
]
compiles a large-scale dataset of fact-checked claims with associated media through ClaimReview
[
44
,
16
]
. Fin-Fact
[
40
]
aggregates image-text items and explanations from sources such as PolitiFact
[
48
]
, Snopes
[
45
]
, and FactCheck
[
14
]
with a focus on finance. However, these datasets are primarily drawn from already fact-checked items, limiting coverage of in-the-wild deception.
Therefore,
XCheck
directly collects data from the X social media platform, covering data that has not been fact-checked yet (Section
3.2
). Our
XCheck
provides high-quality ground-truth explanations for developing and evaluating automated note generation methods.

2.2
Combating Contextual Deception

In order to improve the explainability of online deception detection, multiple works have proposed adding explanations beyond detection results. In the text domain, Wang
et al.

[
50
]
developed “competing wisdom” by organizing true and false evidence and prompting an LLM to produce dual justifications, yielding rationales alongside decisions. Ma
et al.

[
30
]
proposed LoCal, a multi-agent pipeline to produce structured and reviewable explanations, in which a decomposer breaks complex claims into sub-tasks, reasoning agents retrieve and answer with evidence, and evaluating agents enforce logical equivalence and counterfactual robustness. De
et al.

[
12
]
addressed rating latency in Community Notes by proposing Supernotes, which combines existing user-proposed notes and used a personalized “simulated jury” to select the candidate most likely to be rated helpful, but still depends on the availability of human-written notes. However, these methods operate primarily on the text domain, thus do not directly extend to image-based settings. For combating image-based contextual deception, Abdelnabi
et al.

[
2
]
retrieved textual and visual web evidence separately and assessed cross-modal consistency, while SNIFFER
[
38
]
checks both internal image-text consistency and alignment with external evidence using an LLM. However, these systems were evaluated mainly on synthetic data such as NewsCLIPpings
[
29
]
, restricting their applicability for real-world settings. In Section
6
, we show that on the collected
XCheck
, SNIFFER even underperforms foundation LVLM baselines. Motivated by these works, we introduce
ACCNote
, a retrieval-augmented, multi-agent framework to generate context-corrective notes for combating image-based contextual deception.

Dataset Collection and Analysis

The collected dataset
XCheck
is designed to support the systematic study of automated Community Notes generation. We define the threat model as below.

Threat model:
We consider an attacker who can publish posts on social platforms that present
image-based contextual deception
. A post is considered deceptive if it contains a non-synthetic and unaltered image, paired with an accompanying claim that makes a false or misleading assertion about the image’s meaning, time, location, entities, or broader context. The image may be a single photo, a photo with overlaid text (
e.g.
, meme), or a multi-photo collage. Claim can be conveyed, but not limited, in the post text or the text within the image.

3.1.3
Data Cleaning and Annotation

After data collection, we conducted a manual cleaning process. Five graduate annotators were trained using written guidelines and exemplars, and each reviewed a disjoint subset of the 3,574 initially collected deceptive candidates. They performed (i) a language check to remove non-English items, and (ii) a content check to verify that each item matches our definition of image-based contextual deception. Items failing either check were removed, leaving 1,577 deceptive candidates. Next, two experienced researchers independently re-verified the remaining items. Their agreement was 0.87 measured by Jaccard similarity score. We retained only items labeled as contextual deception by both researchers, resulting in a final English-only set of 1,088 posts. To construct a balanced dataset, we then randomly sampled 1,088 non-deceptive posts from VisualNews, yielding a final dataset of 2,176 items with a balanced class distribution (50% deceptive, 50% non-deceptive).

After cleaning, we annotated the dataset using a human+LLM collaboration pipeline. We first performed topical categorization with OpenAI GPT5
[
34
]
. GPT5 was used to propose candidate topics at first, which two researchers consolidated into a 14-topic taxonomy as shown in Table
1
(full taxonomy with subcategories is provided in Appendix
A
, Table
8
). Using each post’s text, image, and Community Note, GPT5 then assigned one or more topic labels to the data (prompts for this process are shown in Appendix
A
). Because multiple topics can co-occur in one data entry, we allow multi-label assignments. Then we use the same human+LLM workflow to annotate the fine-grained deceptive factors in deceptive data, indicating which factors were misleading in each post. The 10-factor taxonomy used in this process is shown in Table
2
. Finally, in a random audit of 100 items, two researchers observed high labeling accuracy at 98% and 97%, respectively; we therefore include these LLM-generated annotations in
XCheck
dataset.

3.2.2
Factor Analysis

Table
2
presents the deceptive factor taxonomy and the distribution of ten factors within the deceptive subset. The results show that deceptive posts frequently manipulate context factors that are not directly observable from the image itself. Specifically, factors such as event, time, entity, location, and attribution each occur in more than 40% of deceptive posts, suggesting that these factors are the primary targets of contextual deception. In contrast, factors such as object, action, impact scale, and quote source occur substantially less often. These factors typically correspond to visual elements that can be directly inferred from the image content and are therefore easier for users to verify. Overall, this distribution suggests that deceptive content preferentially exploits factors that require external context beyond the image, enabling misleading narratives without altering the visual evidence.

4
Proposed Method

Figure 7
:
System design of the proposed
ACCNote
framework.

Existing approaches cannot be directly applied to Community Notes generation because they struggle to meet the five criteria: credibility, veracity, clarity, relevance, and neutrality. To address this gap, we propose
ACCNote
, an automated context-corrective note generation framework that combines (i) retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to improve credibility and veracity by grounding the model in external evidence, and (ii) multi-agent collaboration to improve clarity, relevance, and neutrality by filtering noisy context, separating conflicting evidence, and refining the final note. In our setting, the retrieved external evidence corresponds to the context provided in
XCheck
, which is collected via Google reverse image search over the open Web.

4.1
System Overview

As shown in Figure
7
,
ACCNote
comprises a Data Organizer agent, a pool of Reasoner agents, and a Judge agent. Given a social media post and its external context, the Data Organizer agent first filters the external contexts and clusters them into different groups according to their stance with respect to the post. Then, the Reasoner agents perform candidate notes generation in parallel. Finally, the Judge agent selects the best one from all candidates based on the Community Notes criteria.

\mathbf{P}=(I,T,\tau)
consisting of an image
I
, tweet text
T
, and publication timestamp
\tau
, along with an external context set
\mathcal{C}=\{\epsilon_{i}\}^{m}_{i=1}
, where each context item
\epsilon_{i}=(u_{i},s_{i})
contains a source URL
u_{i}
and its summary
s_{i}
,
m
is the total number of external contexts. The purpose of
ACCNote
is to generate a context-corrective note
\mathbf{N}
:

\mathbf{N}\leftarrow\textsc{{\small{{{ACCNote}}}}}(\mathbf{P},\mathcal{C})

(1)

where
\mathbf{N}=(y,r,\mathcal{R})
,
y
is the class label (
e.g.
, deceptive vs. non-deceptive),
r
is a natural-language explanation, and
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{u_{i}\}^{m}_{i=1}
is the set of cited source URLs.

Agents Design

This section outlines the core design and responsibilities of each agent. The complete prompt templates for all agents are provided in Appendix

4.2.1
Data Organizer Agent

Given that raw results from reverse web search are often heterogeneous and noisy (
e.g.
, broken links, advertisements), we introduce a Data Organizer agent with two stages: filtering and clustering. These two stages can be represented as:

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\leftarrow\textsc{Filter}(\mathcal{C});\quad\{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\triangle},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\triangledown},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\square}\}\leftarrow\textsc{Cluster}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})

(2)

In the
filtering stage
, the agent reduces the retrieved external context set
\mathcal{C}
to a credible subset
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Specifically, the agent performs two binary filters in parallel. First, it predicts
usefulness
for each context item based on its summary, labeling it as
useful
or
useless
. A context is considered
useful
if it contains information related to the post, whereas
useless

4.2.2
Reasoner Agents

Once the Data Organizer partitions the retrieved evidence into stance-specific clusters, we employ Reasoner agents to analyze each cluster independently, thereby reducing interference from conflicting narratives. We instantiate a pool of Reasoner agents to generate candidate notes in parallel. Each agent takes the post
\mathbf{P}
and one evidence cluster
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{*}
as input (supporting, refuting, or irrelevant), and outputs a candidate note
\textbf{N}_{*}=(y_{*},r_{*},\mathcal{R}_{*})
. The procedure can be represented as:

\mathbf{N}_{*}\leftarrow\textsc{Reasoner}(\mathbf{P},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{*}),*\in\{\triangle,\triangledown,\square,\circ\}

(3)

where
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\circ}=\emptyset
is a placeholder for empty context, used for an optional Reasoner agent in case the Data Organizer agent does not return any cluster.

The generation pipeline for each Reasoner agent is summarized in Algorithm
1
. To improve note quality, we adopt a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting routine that encourages the agent to follow explicit intermediate reasoning steps before drafting the final note. The process begins with Step 1, where the agent extracts the post’s main claim
\kappa
by jointly analyzing the image, the accompanying text, and publication timestamp. In Step 2, the agent determines whether the claim is image-dependent. If the claim relies on the image, the agent verifies it against the image’s visual details and the factual context, and identifies potential contradictions. If the claim is not image-dependent, the agent evaluates it against the LVLM’s internal knowledge to detect inconsistencies. In Step 3, when external context is available, the agent further checks the claim against the retrieved evidence and again searches for contradictions. In Step 4, the agent assigns the post label based on whether any contradiction was identified in Step 2 or Step 3.
If any contradiction is found, the post is labeled as
deceptive
, since it indicates context manipulation; otherwise it is labeled as
non-deceptive
. Finally, in both cases, the agent distills a concise rationale from its CoT trace and attaches the source URLs that were used during the resoning process to the output note. This structured CoT procedure makes the decision process explicit and promotes faithfulness by tying the final note directly to the supporting evidence.

4.2.3
Judge Agent

Upon receiving the set of candidate notes generated by the parallel Reasoners, the Judge agent serves as the final decision to resolve the conflicting verdicts (
e.g.
, where one Reasoner argues the post is true and another argues it is false).
To do this, the Judge agent evaluates all candidates
\{\mathbf{N}_{\triangle},\mathbf{N}_{\triangledown},\mathbf{N}_{\square},\mathbf{N}_{\circ}\}
against five Community Notes criteria: credibility, clarity, relevance, veracity, and neutrality. Then it selects the candidate that best satisfies these criteria and outputs it as the final note, ensuring that the system’s response is both supported by evidence and appropriate for users.

\mathbf{N}\leftarrow\textsc{Judge}(\mathbf{P},\{\mathbf{N}_{\triangle},\mathbf{N}_{\triangledown},\mathbf{N}_{\square},\mathbf{N}_{\circ}\})

(4)

5
Metric Development

In order to evaluate the generated context-corrective notes, we need to measure how useful they actually are in helping users understand the deception, specifically against the five Community Notes criteria. Existing text generation metrics focus primarily on lexical overlap between generated text and reference text, and therefore do not directly measure the user-perceived helpfulness (Section
5.2
). To evaluate whether a generated note actually helps users understand a post, we first conduct a pilot user study to characterize how users assess notes based on the five key dimensions, establishing a ground truth for what makes a note helpful. Guided by these user study results, we then propose Context Helpfulness Score (CHS), a new metric that runs five specific checks and aligns with user expectations.

5.2
Context Helpfulness Score

Community Notes aim to help users understand posts and make informed decisions. However, standard text generation metrics primarily quantify lexical and structural overlap between the generated text and a reference text, and therefore do not directly measure whether a note satisfies the Community Notes criteria. 6.1
Detection Performance

As shown in the left panel of Table
7
, the end-to-end detector SNIFFER underperforms on the collected dataset
XCheck
, and its detection quality further decreases when web search is enabled. A plausible explanation is that SNIFFER is trained on synthetic data (an image paired with a caption from another image), which do not adequately reflect the diversity and subtlety of real-world contextual deception. GPT5-mini achieves very high recall (0.9292) but comparatively low precision (0.5824), indicating a tendency to over-predict the
Deceptive
class. Enabling its web search yields only modest improvements, for example, increasing F1 from 0.7160 to 0.7243 and accuracy from 0.6314 to 0.6613.

For open-source LVLM baselines, we observe two consistent patterns. First, scaling the model within the same family substantially improves performance. For instance, the closed-book Qwen2.5-VL F1 increases from 0.7228 (7B) to 0.7931 (32B), and NVILA improves from 0.7690 (8B) to 0.8576 (15B). Second, naively concatenating all retrieved context (naive RAG) typically degrades performance, particularly in terms of F1, with only two minor exceptions, LLaVA-NeXT@8B and InternVL3@8B. Among the evaluated LVLMs, LLaVA-NeXT performs the worst, with an F1 of 0.6229 (8B), largely due to its weak capability of instruction-following. In particular, it frequently violates the required output format that the response must begin with a deterministic class label
Deceptive
or

7
Limitations and Discussions

The design of
ACCNote
relies on tight coupling among multiple agents, which introduces a strong dependency on the full pipeline. Each component plays a structurally necessary role in producing the final note. For example, if the data organizer agent is removed, all retrieved external context collapses into a single cluster, which prevents multi-way stance-aware reasoning. If the reasoner agents are absent, the system cannot generate multiple candidates conditioned on different evidence partitions, and the downstream selection stage becomes ill-posed. This dependence limits the modularization of individual agents, and makes the overall system sensitive to failures in any single stage. A promising direction is to improve modularity by introducing more robust intermediate representations and explicit confidence signals such that the partial pipeline execution remains effective.

We observe that most LVLMs, including GPT5-mini, exhibit higher recall than precision, indicating a tendency to incur more false positives (FPs). Adding external evidence can reduce FPs, but it may also increase false negatives (FNs), reflecting a non-trivial precision-recall trade-off. In our experiment, enabling web search for GPT5-mini reduces FP from 725 to 616 (-109), while FN increases from 79 to 122 (+43). For NVILA@15B,
ACCNote
reduces FP from 208 to 135 (-73), with a smaller FN increase from 115 to 138 (+23). This motivates future work on better calibration with the goal of reducing FPs without disproportionately increasing FNs.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we address the gap in automated Community Notes generation for image-based contextual deception. We introduce
XCheck
, a real-world multimodal dataset of X posts paired with ground-truth Community Notes, together with retrieved external context. We further propose
ACCNote
, a retrieval-augmented multi-agent framework that enhances LVLMs to generate context-corrective notes. To evaluate whether generated notes are helpful to users, we develop a new metric
CHS
that aligns with pilot user study outcomes. Extensive experiments show that
ACCNote
consistently outperforms foundation LVLM baselines and naive RAG on both deception detection and note generation tasks on
XCheck
, and also exceeds the commercial baseline GPT5-mini. Taken together, our dataset, method, and evaluation metric establish a solid and promising foundation for practical and scalable automated note generation.

Ethical Considerations

This work studies automated Community Notes generation for contextual deception. Because the data originate from real-world social media content and the system may influence how users interpret posts, we consider ethics across data collection, annotation, user studies, and dataset release.

Dataset collection and privacy.

XCheck
is constructed from publicly accessible posts on X. We only use content that is available without accessing private accounts, private messages, or restricted groups. To reduce privacy risks, we minimize the amount of personally identifying information (PII) stored and presented. In particular, we avoid collecting or releasing user profile metadata beyond what is necessary for research purposes, and we do not attempt to infer sensitive attributes about individuals. When presenting examples in the paper, we avoid highlighting usernames or personal details unless they are essential for understanding the study.

Annotation procedure and annotator welfare.
Annotations are performed with written guidelines and exemplars to promote consistency and to reduce subjective or identity-based judgments. Because deceptive content can include disturbing or sensitive material, we adopt standard annotator welfare practices, including clear content warnings, the ability to opt out of specific items, and limiting exposure to potentially harmful content.

User study protections.
The user study is designed to evaluate note quality dimensions such as credibility, clarity, relevance, veracity, and neutrality. Participants are informed about the study purpose, the types of content they may encounter, and that their participation is voluntary. They can skip questions or withdraw at any time without penalty. We collect only the minimum information necessary for analysis, store responses securely, and report results only in aggregate form. Since study materials may include misleading claims, we include debriefing language that clarifies that some posts are deceptive and provides guidance to consult reliable sources when uncertain. The user study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Dataset release and responsible use.
To support reproducibility while respecting platform policies and copyright constraints, we release
XCheck
in a policy-compliant format that contains only tweet ID rather than redistributing raw media. Reconstructing full post content requires “hydration” via official X APIs or other compliant procedures, and any hydrated content remains subject to X policy terms and the original creators rights.

Attribution for visual materials.

All screenshots and photos reproduced in this paper are sourced from X, and we credit X as the platform of origin. Copyright and ownership of the original media remain with their respective rights holders. These materials are included solely for research illustration.

Open Science

We release all materials used in this study, including the code, dataset, and experimental results, at
https://github.com/MaJinWakeUp/ACCNote
.
