Combination therapy with a novel CD2-targeted costimulatory bispecific antibody overcomes limitations of CD3 T cell engager treatment for solid tumors
Authors: Welbeck Danquah, Mélanie Pichery, Thomas Eden, Aurelien Boyance, Lise Pasquet, Virginie Roure, Marion Mars, Alice Marchand, Ellen Gumz, Mylène Gador, Lou Valente, Marion Contini, Lukas Czernecki, Sanjith Shanmuganathan, Pauline Barron, Sophie Chabot, Markus Dangl, Gerhard Niederfellner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCD3 T cell engagers (TCEs) have transformed hematologic oncology, but dose-liming toxicity and the absence of adequate costimulation have limited TCE success in solid tumors. Consequently, to date, only one classical TCE developed for solid tumors – tarlatamab – has been granted a marketing approval. Here, we report a pioneer combination strategy using a novel CD2-targeted costimulatory bispecific antibody to overcome these limitations. Building on a unique non-blocking CD2 antibody, we developed a HER2×CD2 proof-of-concept bispecific that, combined with an EpCAM×CD3 TCE, provides tumor-dependent costimulation and enhances anti-tumor cytotoxicity mediated by the TCE. We show that HER2×CD2 can be dosed independently to restore optimal anti-tumor cytotoxicity of a sub-efficacious low dose of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, thus providing a route to avoid TCE-driven toxicity while maintaining efficacy. In a humanized xenograft model, co-treatment with HER2×CD2 achieved complete tumor remission in 8 of 9 mice at a TCE dose that otherwise mediated complete remission in only 1 of 9 mice. We show that HER2×CD2 compensates for the loss of CD58 expression by tumor cells – a well-documented tumor escape mechanism. Notably, unlike CD28-based costimulation, HER2×CD2 effectively also harnessed the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of CD28-negative CD8 T cells – a potent cytotoxic subset prevalent in elderly patients and dominant in solid tumors. Furthermore, HER2×CD2 induced markedly lower cytokine release than a HER2×CD28 bispecific while mediating comparable improvement in anti-tumor cytotoxicity. These findings establish our novel CD2-targeted costimulatory bispecific antibody approach as a promising and potentially safe way to expand and enhance TCE immunotherapy for solid tumors.

Introduction

In recent years, a class of bispecific antibodies known as T cell engagers (TCEs) has been successfully developed, leading to 11 US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency approvals − 9 for blood cancers and 2 for solid tumors.1 TCEs redirect T cells to attack tumor cells by simultaneously binding the CD3 receptor on T cells and a tumor-associated surface antigen. This mechanism delivers a strong, tumor-dependent artificial signal 1 to drive T cell-mediated anti-tumor cytotoxicity.

However, experience from chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy, another T cell-based immunotherapy, shows that optimal clinical outcomes require a costimulatory signal 2 that helps prevent T cell exhaustion and boosts formation of memory T cells.2 Accordingly, all approved CAR T cell therapies include signaling domains from either CD28 or 4–1BB to enable costimulation.3 To enhance TCE efficacy, Skokos et al. designed bispecific antibodies targeting CD28 and a tumor antigen to be used in combination with TCEs.4 Likewise, Claus et al. demonstrated that a construct consisting of a tumor-targeting moiety fused to 4–1BB ligand significantly boosts the anti-tumor activity of TCEs in preclinical models.5

CD2 is a costimulatory receptor that, unlike CD28 and 4–1BB, is widely expressed by T cells found infiltrating solid tumors.6 This profile makes CD2 an attractive and practical option for providing costimulation in the tumor microenvironment. However, so far, CD2-mediated costimulation for TCEs has only been achieved by fusing the extracellular domain (ECD) of CD58 – the natural ligand for CD2 – directly to TCE molecules, resulting in trispecific and other multispecific engager formats.6,7 To our knowledge, CD2-based costimulation by a separate bispecific antibody as an off-the-shelf combination agent for TCEs has never been reported. Indeed, scientists using the EVOLVE platform demonstrated that combination with a tumor-targeted CD2 bispecific based on the CD58 ECD resulted in reduction in the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of the TCE.6 This observation led the authors to conclude that incorporation of the CD3 and CD2 binding moieties into a single TCE molecule may be necessary to achieve a CD2 costimulatory effect. However, particularly for solid tumor therapy, providing the costimulatory signal via a separate agent is highly desirable. First, it prevents the adverse effects mediated by the CD3 TCE itself from becoming dose-limiting for CD2 costimulation. Furthermore, it enables the use of less tumor-specific antigen for costimulation without altering on-target off-tumor toxicity, as signal 2 alone does not elicit T cell activation. Importantly, the ability to dose the costimulatory agent separately and much higher than the TCE can enable recovery of the cell killing propensity of the TCE at toxicity-limited suboptimal TCE doses.

Here, we present the first proof-of-concept data of a novel CD2 bispecific that, as a separate combination agent to a TCE, enhances the anti-tumor activity of the TCE both in vitro and in vivo. We show that the combination with this CD2 bispecific can overcome two known tumor immune escape mechanisms under TCE treatment: loss of CD58 expression and downregulation of the tumor antigen for the CD3 TCE. Further, we demonstrate that the CD2 bispecific can harness the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of CD28-negative CD8+ T cells – a potent cytotoxic T cell subset that is highly prevalent in the T cell repertoire of elderly patients and the predominant T cell phenotype observed in solid tumors. Finally, we show that, as a combination agent, the CD2 bispecific triggers markedly less cytokine release than a CD28 bispecific at matched anti-tumor cytotoxicity levels, making it a potentially safer option for TCE costimulation in solid tumors.

Materials and methods

Immunization and hybridoma screening

NZBWF1 mice were immunized with human CD4+ effector memory T cells (TEM) that were subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting and collected from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of 13 healthy donors over the course of the immunization regimen.

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 1 million CD4+ TEM cells in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) per immunization on days 0, 7, 14, 28, and 31. Before (pre-immune serum) and after (immune serum) immunization, responder animals were determined by testing binding of mouse serum to CD4+ T cells by flow cytometry. Mice showing high titers were selected for hybridoma generation and screening by standard methods. Culture supernatants of hybridoma were screened for selective binding to memory T cells as compared to naïve T cells by flow cytometry. Hit hybridomas were subcloned to monoclonals by standard methods and sequenced, recombinantly expressed and tested for binding to a panel of immune cell antigens, including CD2. The CD2 binder was further characterized.

Recombinant antibody expression

Mouse hybridoma-derived CD2 antibody was recombinantly produced as chimeric human IgG4 S228P antibody using standard protocols. Briefly, the antibody was expressed by transient co-transfection of heavy-chain and light-chain constructs into Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells and was purified from the culture supernatants by Protein A affinity chromatography.

Bispecifics were expressed in the appended IgG-single-chain variable fragment (scFv) format. Briefly, scFv constructs designed in the VH-(G4S)3-VL format were fused to the C-terminus of the light chain via a flexible (G4S)3 linker. All scFv designs were disulfide-stabilized via H44C/L100C mutations.8 Bispecifics were recombinantly expressed in CHO cells and purified by a two-step process consisting of initial capture by Protein A chromatography and followed by a preparative size exclusion chromatography (SEC) step. All bispecifics were generated with the mutations L234A, L235E, G237A, A330S, P331S introduced into the Fc-domain to abrogate Fc-mediated effector functions.9 Protein integrity and homogeneity of purified antibodies were confirmed by analytical SEC and SDS-PAGE.

Cell lines

HCC1954 and BT474 Clone 5 breast cancer cell lines (ATCC) were both grown in RPMI 1640 (Gibco A1049101) supplemented with 10% of FCS (Fetal Calf Serum, Sigma F0392) with or without 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibo, 15,140–122).

HCC1954 mKate2+ line was generated by transduction of the parental line with Incucyte Nuclight red Lentivirus (Sartorius, 4476) and mKate2+ cells were sorted by FACS and cultivated in selection media containing 0.25 µg/ml of puromycin (Sigma P9620).

CD58-knock out (CD58-KO) mKate2+ HCC1954 cells were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Briefly, a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex consisting of Cas9 protein and a single guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting exon 3 of CD58 gene were directly delivered to HCC1954 mKate2+ cells by electroporation. A CD58-KO HCC1954 stable pool was then expanded after FACS-based cell sorting of CD58-negative cells.

HEK293-CD2 cells were generated by transfection of HEK293 cells with a cDNA expression construct encoding the full-length human or mouse CD2 open reading frame with C-terminal green fluorescent protein-fusion (Origene) to monitor transfection efficiency.

Human primary cell isolation

Human PBMCs were isolated from buffy coats, which were obtained from blood banks (French Blood Bank or German Red Cross) supplied by normal healthy donors who provided informed consent, using the density gradient media lymphoprep (StemCell) or lymphosep (Biowest).

CD3+ T cells were isolated by using the MACSxpress Buffy Coat Pan T Cell Isolation Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol.

CD4+ or CD8+ T cell subsets were isolated from PBMCs by immunomagnetic negative isolation using corresponding human CD4+ or CD8+ T cell isolation kit (Stem Cell or Miltenyi Biotec). To purify the CD28− CD8+ subset, total CD8+ T cells were incubated with 2 µg/ml of anti-CD28-PE (CD28.2 clone, BD), and cell suspensions were processed using a cell sorter (BD FACSAria Fusion). The CD28− subset was isolated based on negative CD28 staining. Total CD8 T cells (59−90% CD28+ cells) from the same donors were harvested as outlined above and used as control.

CD2-KO CD4+ T cells were generated with the CRISPR-Cas9 system as described above using a sgRNA targeting the CD2 gene.

Cell phenotyping by flow cytometry

HCC1954 mKate2+ tumor cell suspensions were stained with anti-HER2-Alexa eFluor 647 (24D2 clone, 10 µg/ml, Biolegend), anti-EpCAM-PeVio770 (REA764 clone, 0.5 µg/ml, Miltenyi), anti-CD58-PE (TS2/9 clone, 4 µg/ml, Biolegend) antibodies or their respective isotype controls recommended by the provider at the same concentration.

CD8+ T cells were stained with 0.5 µg/ml of anti-CD3-BV510 (OKT3 clone, Biolegend), 0.25 µg/ml anti-CD8-PE-Cy7 (SK1 clone, Biolegend), 2 µg/ml of anti-CD28-PE (CD28.2 clone, BD) or their respective isotype control recommended by the provider at the same concentration.

All stainings were performed in the presence of 1:100 Human TruStain FcX (Fc receptor blocking solution, Biolegend) and with 1:1000 eFluor 780 viability dye (Invitrogen) to gate out dead cells. Flow cytometry data acquisitions were performed on BD (Fortessa or Symphony) cytometers, and FlowJO_v10.10.0 was used for data analysis.

Target engagement of parental CD2 antibodies and bispecifics

HEK293 cells transiently transfected with either human or mouse CD2 as described above were harvested 2 d after transfection. A total of 4 × 104 cells were incubated for 60 min on ice with serially titrated chimeric CD2 consisting of mouse variable domain, human IgG4 S228P constant domain or isotype control antibodies (10-fold dilutions starting at 10 µg/ml). Binding was detected with a 1:200 dilution of a fluorochrome-labeled anti-human IgG secondary antibody (ab102427, Abcam) for 30 min on ice. The expression of the mouse CD2 receptor was verified by incubation with a 1:200 dilution of a rat anti-mouse CD2 antibody (RM2-5 clone, BioLegend) for 60 min on ice that was subsequently detected with a 1:200 dilution of an anti-rat IgG secondary antibody (ab102235, Abcam) for 30 min on ice.

To also confirm specific engagement of endogenously expressed CD2, binding to wildtype or CD2-KO CD4+ T cells was analyzed. Successful knockout of CD2 was demonstrated by flow cytometry through staining with a 1:200 dilution of an allophycocyanin (APC)-labeled anti-CD2 antibody (RPA-2.10 clone, BioLegend) for 30 min on ice. Wildtype (untreated), CD2-KO or mock-treated primary CD4+ T cells were incubated with 10 µg/ml of the chimeric CD2 Ab or an isotype control for 60 min on ice. Following a washing step, bound antibodies were detected by a 1:200 diluted DyLight 650-labeled anti-human IgG secondary (ab102427, Abcam) after 30-min incubation on ice.

Binding of bispecific antibodies to HER2-expressing breast cancer cell lines HCC1954 and BT474-clone 5 cells as well as primary human CD8+ T cells was analyzed by flow cytometry staining. Cells were incubated with serially diluted bispecific antibodies on ice for 60 min. Following a washing step, bound antibodies were detected by a 1:200 dilution of a DyLight 650-labeled anti-human IgG secondary antibody (ab102427, Abcam).

Data were collected by flow cytometry (iQue 3). MFI was analyzed with Forecyte and plotted with GraphPad Prism.

Surface plasmon resonance analysis

To determine the binding kinetic parameters of the antibodies, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analyses were performed on the Biacore T200 platform. A clinical-stage reference CD2 antibody siplizumab (PX-TA1051, ProteoGenix) and our novel non-blocking CD2 antibody were separately captured on an anti-human Fab CM5 chip surface in HBS-EP+ buffer at a flow rate of 10 µl per minute. Seven different concentrations of recombinant CD2 antigen (3-fold dilutions starting at 200 nM) were tested, with each run comprising a cycle of 3-min antigen association, 30-min dissociation and a regeneration phase of two 60 s runs with glycine buffer, pH 2.1. Experiments were conducted in HBS-EP+ buffer at a flow rate of 30 µl/min at 25°C. Sensorgram data were fitted to global 1:1 Langmuir model and dissociation rate constants (kd) and association rate constants (ka) determined. KD values were calculated from the ratio of the kd and ka values.

ELISA-based cross-reactivity assessment to cynomolgus CD2

Reactivity of the novel CD2 antibody to cynomolgus CD2 was tested by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Recombinant His6x-tagged proteins of the ECD of human and cynomolgus CD2 (Biozol, Eching) were diluted to 2 µg/ml in PBS and adsorbed onto 384-well microtiter plates. After blocking with a 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS solution, plates were incubated with 10 µg/ml chimeric CD2 antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Bound CD2 antibody was detected with a horse-radish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody (ab97175, Abcam). A commercially available positive control mouse anti-human CD2 antibody (clone RPA-2.10, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was detected with HRP-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (ab98771, Abcam). Bound detector antibodies were visualized by the addition of 3,3‘,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate, and the ensuing enzymatic reaction was subsequently quenched with addition of one molar sulfuric acid. Absorbances were measured at 450 nm wavelength and data plotted as bar graphs.

Cell-based studies of the impact of anti-CD2 antibodies on CD2-CD58 interaction

Respective wells of 4 × 104 purified primary human CD8+ T cells were treated with 6-step 1:5 serial titrations of respective antibodies: our novel CD2 Ab, siplizumab (ProteoGenix, batch# PX-TA1051) or an isotype control antibody (MOPC21 as human IgG4 chimeric antibody, produced in-house). The highest antibody concentration used was 125 nM, and the lowest was 0.04 nM. Each assay point was generated in technical duplicates. Following a 30-min incubation at room temperature, the samples were treated with 2 µg/ml biotinylated CD58-Fc (10068-CD-050, R&D Systems; biotinylation was performed in-house). After an additional incubation on ice for 60 min, the samples were washed once with a washing buffer consisting of PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cell-bound biotinylated CD58-Fc was detected by incubating samples with a 1:200 dilution of an APC-labeled streptavidin secondary reagent (BioLegend, #405207) for 60 min on ice. Following two washing steps, data were collected by flow cytometry (iQue, Satorious). Median intensity values of APC fluorescence (MFI) corresponding to the relative level of cell-bound CD58-Fc molecules were extracted and analyzed by GraphPad Prism. Data were represented as a 4-parameter non-linear regression of the mean from the technical duplicates (error bars show standard deviation).

Cell-based epitope binning of our novel CD2 binder against commercially available CD2 antibodies

Epitope binning assays were performed to investigate the impact of commercially available CD2 antibodies on the binding by our novel CD2 antibody or siplizumab to CD8+ T cells. Commercial antibodies tested were: Siplizumab (PX-TA1051, ProteoGenix), RPA-2.10 (14–0029-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific), LT2 (MA1-10131, Thermo Fisher Scientific), MEM-65 (MA1-19070, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TS1/8 (65192–1-Ig, Proteintech).

In a volume of 25 µl (PBS, 2% FBS), primary human CD8+ T cells endogenously expressing CD2 were incubated with or without 20 µg/ml of unlabeled anti-CD2 antibodies or an isotype control at a cell density of 1 × 106 cells/ml for 60 min at 4°C. Thereafter, 25 µl of 2 µg/ml of biotinylated version of the novel CD2 antibody or siplizumab was added (final concentration of 1 µg/ml of biotinylated antibody) and the assay incubated for an additional 60 min at 4°C. Following subsequent washing steps with PBS supplemented with 2% FBS, T cell-bound biotinylated antibodies were detected with a 1:200 dilution of an APC-labeled streptavidin reagent (BioLegend, Cat# 405, 207). Data were collected by flow cytometry (iQue, Sartorius), and MFIs were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and normalized to respective signal of binding in the presence of buffer only.

Conjugate formation assay

The ability of bispecifics to simultaneously bind to two separate antigens, one each on T cells and tumor cells, was investigated in a conjugate formation assay. To this end, CD8+ T cells were adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells per ml and incubated with 4 µM CellTrace™ Violet. An equal amount of tumor cells (HCC1954 or BT474 Clone 5) was separately incubated with 0.5 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester. After 20 min at 37°C, cells were washed with 5 times the volume of the cell culture medium and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. After an additional washing step with assay buffer (PBS with 2% FBS), cells were adjusted to a final concentration of 2 × 106 cells per ml.

Each 3 × 104 pre-labeled CD8+T cells were mixed with tumor cells in a 1:1 ratio, treated with 1:10 serially diluted concentrations (100 nM to 0.1 pM) of the HER2×CD2 bispecific or control bispecifics, centrifuged for 1 min at 300 g to trigger conjugate formation and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Thereafter, cells were fixed with a final concentration of 1.5% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to maintain conjugates. After washing, conjugate formation was determined by flow cytometry (iQue, Sartorius) as percentage of T cells detected to be positive for both cell tracers.

Tumor cell cytotoxicity assays by LDH release assay and flow cytometry

HCC1954 or BT-474 clone 5 breast cancer tumor target cells were seeded 1 d prior to the assay at densities of 1.5 × 104 (HCC1954) cells per well or 2.5 × 104 cells per well (BT‑474 clone 5) in 96‑well tissue culture plates. Cryopreserved T cells were thawed and incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2 to allow recovery.

On the following day, the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, was prepared as a 1:10 serial dilution series starting at 100 nM and spanning seven dilution steps. The HER2×CD2 bispecific or the non-targeting mesothelin (MSLN) or respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)-binding control bispecifics (HER2×MSLN and HER2×RSV, used interchangeably and collectively denoted HER2×NT here) produced in-house were diluted to achieve a final assay concentration of 10 nM. A total of 25 µl of each solitomab dilution and 25 µl of the bispecific antibodies were added to the corresponding wells with target cells, followed by a 60‑min incubation.

T cells were counted and adjusted to 1.2 × 106 cells/ml for assays using HCC1954 target cells and to 2 × 106 cells/ml for assays using BT‑474 clone 5 target cells. Subsequently, 50 µl of the prepared T‑cell suspension was added to each well. Co‑cultures were incubated for 48 h at 37°C.

For assay setups with suboptimal signal 1 conditions, co-cultures were treated with a low fixed concentration of 2 pM (in case of co-cultures HCC1954 tumor cells) or 5 pM (in case of co-cultures with BT474 clone 5 tumor cells) of EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, in the presence of 1:10 serially diluted concentrations (100 nM to 0.1 pM) of either the HER2×CD2 bispecific or the non-targeting control bispecifics (HER2×NT).

After a 2-d co-culture, anti-tumor cytotoxicity was determined by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay using the CyQUANT™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay (Thermo Scientific). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism.

Cytotoxicity was quantified and represented as a percentage of maximum non-spontaneous target cell release using the following formula: Cytotoxicity=Experimental value−spontaneous release by effector cells−spontaneous release by target cellsmaximun release by target cells−spontaneous release by target cells

Cytotoxicity was then normalized to responses measured at 10 nM of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, as 100% cytotoxicity, as this represented the condition at which maximum cytotoxicity by solitomab was routinely observed. Cytotoxicity by 10 nM solitomab corresponded to between ~25% (BT474) and ~30% (HCC1954) of cell death mediated by complete cell lysis of target cells through detergent treatment (1% Triton X-100, CyQUANT™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

T cell activation was measured by flow cytometry staining of CD25 expression levels on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the co-culture assays. To this end, cell suspensions were stained with 1:100 dilutions of fluorochrome-labeled antibodies: anti-CD45-BV421 (2D1 clone, Biolegend), anti-CD8a-PE (RPA-T8 clone, Biolegend), anti-CD4-PerCP/C5.5 (RPA-T4 clone, Biolegend) and anti-CD25-APC (BC96 clone, Biolegend). Data collected by flow cytometry (iQue3) were analyzed using Forecyte and GraphPad Prism.

Tumor cell cytotoxicity assessment by live-cell imaging

Direct cell killing of tumor cells using either PBMCs, total CD8+ or CD8+CD28− T cells from healthy donors was evaluated by live-cell imaging. HCC1954 breast cancer cells (EpCAM+ and HER2+) overexpressing the far-red fluorescent protein mKate2 were co-cultured at a 2:1 effector-to-target ratio with the respective immune cell population in effector cell medium (31870–025 Gibco, 1% pen/strep, 10% FBS, 1% Glutamax 35,050–038 Gibco). Co-cultures were treated with serially diluted EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, (MedChemExpress) in the presence of 10 or 50 nM fixed concentration of HER2×CD2 bispecific or either the HER2×RSV or HER2×MSLN non-targeting control bispecific. Cell growth of mKate2+ wildtype or CD58-KO HCC1954 cell line was tracked by imaging each well every 4 h over a 4-d culture period using the Incucyte S3 live cell imaging system. The software Incucyte S3 2022A (Essen Bioscience) was used to analyze the images and define a mask to identify mKate2+ fluorescent nuclei of target cells and to monitor total red object count per well at each time-point. Anti-tumor cytotoxicity was then determined by calculating area under curve values (AUC) of the mKate2+ HCC1954 cell growth curves over 4 d and with normalization to untreated control wells using the following formula: % Cytotoxicity = (1 – (AUC experimental value/AUC untreated control)) × 100.

Data were represented using a four-parameter non-linear regression based on the mean of technical replicates (error bars show standard deviation). Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.

Cytokine-release assay

Supernatants from co-cultures of PBMCs with HCC1954 mKate2+ (see Tumor cell cytotoxicity assessment by live-cell imaging section above) were collected at 72 h for interleukin (IL)-2 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) quantification and 96 h for interferon (IFN)-γ and IL-6 quantification respectively. For each timepoint, culture supernatants were evaluated using U-Plex human 96-well assay from Mesoscale Discovery (MSD, K15067M-2) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Plates were read immediately on MSD sector 600 imager (MSD). Data were analyzed using MSD Discovery Workbench v4 and represented using GraphPad Prism v10.

Mice

NOD.Cg-B2mem1Tac Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Sug/JicTac (B2m-NOG) mice of 8 weeks were obtained from Taconic Biosciences A/S (US). Mice were housed under specific opportunist pathogen-free conditions in accordance with Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care guidelines in groups of maximum 6 mice per cage in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) (184 L IVC from Tecniplast) on a bed of poplar wood granulate (select fine from Safe) with free access to food (Ro4 irradiated at 25 kg ray from Safe) and water (0.2 μm filtered water and sterilized) ad libitum. Species-appropriate environmental enrichment (2 nestlet/6 mice, 1 bed’R’nest, 1 aspen brick, 1 plexiglass tunnel and/or 1 cardboard igloo from Bioservices) was added to the cages. Animals were maintained under artificial lighting between 7:00 am and 7:00 pm (12 h) at a controlled ambient temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and at a relative humidity of 55 ± 10%. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the European Council Directive (2010/63/UE) and were approved by the French Animal Ethical Committee (APAFIS#47793-2024022716325549 v2).

Hormone supplementation in mice

Estrogen was supplied to the animals using subcutaneous (s.c.), sustained-release (60 d) pellets, containing 10.5 mg/pellet of 17β-Estradiol (0.5) + Progesterone (10.0) (IRA, USA). Pellets were implanted using forceps according to the IRA recommendations, and incision was closed with 3 M animal care Vetbond Tissue Adhesive (Phymep).

Human PBMC engraftment and tumor injection in mice

Human PBMCs were isolated from buffy coats obtained from blood banks of normal healthy donors (French Blood Bank) using StraightFrom Buffy coat PBMC kit (Miltenyi) and frozen at 30 × 106 cells/ml in CryoStor CS10 (Stemcell) at −80°C for 24 h, then moved to liquid nitrogen for storage.

BT474-clone 5 cells (10 × 106 cells/mouse) were combined with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio immediately prior to s.c. injection into the right flank of recipient animals in a total volume of 200 μL.

After 10 d when tumors were established, mice were randomized, using RandoMice software, based on tumor volume (100–150 mm3) to evenly distribute the 4 different donors of PBMC. PBMCs were thawed in a 37°C water bath, followed by one wash with RPMI containing 2% FBS, and one wash with PBS. 10 × 106 PBMCs were injected intravenously (i.v.) in a volume of 100 μL PBS per recipient mouse.

Mice treatments and monitoring

Animals were randomized, using RandoMice software, to six different treatment groups based on body weight, tumor volume, and donor of PBMC. Seven or nine animals per group were treated with daily intraperitoneal injections with: 5 or 10 µg/kg of the EpCAM×CD3 BiTE, solitomab (MedChem) alone; 10 mg/kg HER2×CD2 alone; or a combination of either solitomab at 5 µg/kg with 10 mg/kg HER2×CD2, or solitomab at 10 µg/kg with 10 mg/kg HER2×CD2. A vehicle control group was treated only with PBS. For the efficacy study, antibody treatment was given between day 14 and day 42.

Tumors were measured thrice weekly using digital calipers, and the tumor volume was calculated as: Volume (mm3) = 0.52 × (width)2 × (length). Animals were also monitored for body weight and clinical symptoms three times a week. The earliest sign of graft vs host disease (GvHD) was observed at 28 d post-tumor cells injection. Clinical signs observed were ruffled coat, hunched posture, lethargy, alopecia, xerosis, and weight loss. Mice presenting with severe GvHD clinical signs were humanely euthanized.

Results

Generation of a novel non-blocking CD2 binder

A novel CD2 antibody was generated by immunizing mice with human CD4+ effector memory T (TEM) cells from healthy donors and phenotypically screening the resulting hybridomas for antibodies that: 1) preferentially bound to TEM versus naïve T cells, 2) scored positive in a T cell costimulation assay using plate-immobilized antibodies, but 3) did not bind to a non-T cell line, HEK293 (data not shown). Among the hits that passed these criteria, we identified an antibody that binds CD2, henceforth designated CD2 Ab. CD2 Ab showed concentration-dependent binding to human CD2 overexpressed by transfected HEK293 cells (Figure 1A), but not mouse CD2 (Fig. S1A), by flow cytometry. By ELISA, CD2 Ab showed cross-reactive binding to human and cynomolgus CD2 (Fig. S1B). To evaluate binding specificity for endogenous CD2, we generated CD2-knockout and mock-treated samples of blood-derived human CD4+ T cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. CD2 Ab bound to either untreated or mock-treated human CD4+ T cells that endogenously express CD2 on the surface, but not when the CD2 gene was deleted (Figure 1B). Thereafter, we performed cell-based epitope binning studies investigating the impact of a set of commercially available unlabeled anti-CD2 antibodies on the binding by biotinylated versions of CD2 Ab and for comparison with the clinical-stage CD2 antibody, siplizumab. Using blood-derived human CD8+ T cells as the source of endogenous CD2 antigen, we found that unlabeled CD2 Ab competed with its biotinylated counterpart whereas all other CD2 antibodies showed no substantial impact on its binding. In contrast, binding by biotinylated siplizumab was blocked by excess of unlabeled siplizumab and clone MEM-65 (Figure 1C). SPR measurements using recombinant human CD2-ECD showed that CD2 Ab binds with a KD of 11 nM that is comparable to the 19.2 nM KD of siplizumab measured in the same experiments (Fig. S1C and Table S1). Next, we evaluated the impact of CD2 Ab on the interaction between CD2 and its ligand, CD58. Here too, we chose a cell-based assay format as it was previously reported that recombinant CD58, even as Fc-fusion protein, failed to show detectable binding to plate-bound CD2 whereas its binding to a T cell line was readily detected.11 Thus, we biotinylated recombinant human CD58-Fc and incubated primary human CD8+ T cells with serial titrations of the biotinylated reagent. After washing steps, cell-bound CD58-Fc was detected with an APC-labeled streptavidin secondary reagent and data collected by flow cytometry. We observed concentration-dependent binding by CD58-Fc to CD8+ T cells (data not shown). We selected a concentration corresponding to EC60 (2 µg/ml biotinylated CD58-Fc) to study the impact of CD2 Ab on CD58-Fc binding to CD8+ T cells. CD8+ T cells were incubated with 2 µg/ml biotinylated CD58-Fc in the presence of serial titrations of CD2 Ab, siplizumab, or an isotype control antibody. In line with reported data, siplizumab had no impact on the binding of CD58-Fc to CD8 T cells showing a comparable profile to the isotype control antibody (Figure 1D).11 Surprisingly, CD2 Ab mediated a concentration-dependent increase in the binding of recombinant CD58-Fc to human CD8+ T cells (Figure 1D). This impact resulted in a greater than two-fold increase in maximal binding by CD58-Fc to CD8+ T cells across experiments (Table S2). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a CD2 antibody that positively modulates the CD2-CD58 interaction axis in a cell-based assay. Together, these findings reveal that our proprietary CD2 Ab binds a unique functional epitope. Hence, using CD2 Ab, we generated a novel tumor-targeted CD2-engager bispecific and evaluated its ability – as a combination agent – to drive costimulation of the anti-tumor activity mediated by a CD3 TCE.

Generating a bispecific HER2×CD2 proof-of-concept molecule

To demonstrate the concept of CD2-mediated costimulation by targeting a tumor antigen separate from that of the corresponding TCE, we generated bispecific antibodies based on the anti-HER2 antibody trastuzumab and our proprietary CD2 Ab. We selected the 2 + 2 appended IgG format to circumvent potential chain mispairing. A previous study showed that a bispecific format in which a CD3-binding scFv moiety is fused to the C-terminus of the light chain of a tumor-targeting IgG via a flexible GS-linker provides superior in vivo efficacy compared to other commonly used formats.12 Hence, we generated a HER2×CD2 bispecific by fusing disulfide-stabilized scFv fragments of CD2 Ab to the C-termini of both trastuzumab light chains (Figure 2A). To avoid depletion of CD2-expressing immune effector cells, we rendered the Fc domain functionally inactive by introducing mutations L234A, L235E, G237A, A330S, and P331S.9 Negative control bispecific molecules in the same format were also generated based on scFv moieties derived from either the MSLN binder SS1 or the anti-RSV antibody palivizumab (used interchangeably and collectively denoted non-targeting, NT). Using a two-step purification process consisting of tandem Protein A and size exclusion chromatography, we purified the bispecifics from CHO cultures and achieved high monomerity and purity of intact bispecifics (Table S3). We confirmed that the HER2×CD2 bispecific binds to CD2 overexpressed in transfected HEK293 cells and to endogenously expressed CD2 on CD8+ T cells (Figures 2B, C). Both the HER2×CD2 bispecific and the non-targeting HER2×NT bispecific showed comparable binding to the HER2+ breast cancer cell line HCC1954 (Figure 2D). However, only HER2×CD2, but not the non-targeting control bispecific, induced robust stable conjugate formation between the tumor cell line and CD8+ T cells, presumably by simultaneously engaging both HER2 and CD2 antigens on the respective cells (Figure 2E). Combination with a HER2×CD2 bispecific enhances tumor killing and T cell activation triggered by an EpCAM×CD3 T cell engager

Next, we investigated the propensity of the HER2×CD2 bispecific to costimulate and enhance the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of a TCE when used as a combination agent. We cultured purified primary human T cells with HER2+ tumor cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 2:1. The co-cultures were treated with a fixed 10 nM dose of either the HER2×CD2 or non-targeting control HER2×NT bispecific in the presence of serial dilutions of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab (Figure 3A). As HER2+ target tumor cells, we used the human breast cancer cell lines HCC1954 (Figure 3B) and BT474 clone 5 (Figure 3C) (see Figure S2 for expression levels of HER2 and EpCAM on HCC1954 and BT474 clone 5 cells). After 2 d in culture, tumor cell killing was determined by an LDH release assay whereas T cell activation was monitored by flow cytometry as an increase in CD25 surface staining on T cells. Across experiments, the HER2×CD2 bispecific consistently enhanced tumor cell killing by ~10-fold and increased T cell activation (Figure 3 and Figure S8). This is the first demonstration of CD2 costimulation of the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of a TCE via combination with a separate CD2 bispecific targeting a different tumor antigen. Costimulation by HER2×CD2 recovers anti-tumor cytotoxicity at an ineffective dose of the EpCAM×CD3 T cell engager

Next, we investigated the impact of dose-titrating the HER2×CD2 bispecific at a low-fixed sub-optimal concentration of the EpCAM×CD3 bispecific to mimic conditions of low signal 1 activity by a TCE. Low ineffective TCE-mediated signal 1 may arise either by dose-limiting toxicity or loss of the tumor antigen of the TCE. The latter is an established mechanism by which tumor cells evade TCE and CAR-T therapies, often leading to treatment relapses.13 Hence, we treated co-cultures of T cells and either HCC1954 or BT474 clone 5 breast cancer cells with a hitherto established sub-optimal low dose of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, that mediates inefficient anti-tumor cytotoxicity (2 pM solitomab alone mediated ~18% cytotoxicity against HCC1954 targets cell and 5 pM solitomab alone mediated ~8% cytotoxicity against BT474 clone 5 target cells). We then titrated increasing concentrations of the HER2×CD2 bispecific or the HER2×NT control bispecific into the assay (Figure 4A). We observed that increasing CD2 costimulation mediated by the HER2×CD2 bispecific rescued T cell cytotoxicity as well as T cell activation, particularly in CD8+ T cells (Figure 4B-C and Figure S9). Comparable results were observed using either HCC1954 or BT474 clone 5 as tumor target cells. These data indicate that low suboptimal anti-tumor activity of a CD3 TCE can be rescued by strong costimulation via a separately dosed CD2 bispecific combination agent targeting a different surface antigen co-expressed by the cancer cells. In vivo combination treatment of the HER2×CD2 bispecific with an EpCAM×CD3 T cell engager unlocks anti-tumor efficacy

Next, using a xenograft mouse model, we evaluated whether costimulation with the HER2×CD2 bispecific can similarly enhance the anti-tumor activity of a TCE in an in vivo setting. Trastuzumab-resistant human BT474 clone 5 cells 14 were subcutaneously injected into B2m-NOG immunodeficient mice (Taconic). Once tumors were established (tumor volume of 100–150 mm3; 10 days post inoculation), mice were injected with human PBMCs to reconstitute a human T cell compartment. Four days after PBMC injection, mice were treated with daily injections of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE solitomab at 5 or 10 µg/kg or the HER2×CD2 bispecific at 10 mg/kg ***either alone or in combination (Fig. S5A,5 B). All treatments were well tolerated as determined by the absence of significant weight loss among the treatment groups (Fig. S3A).

Compared to the vehicle cohort, treatment with either the HER2×CD2 bispecific alone or the 5 µg/kg dose of solitomab alone did not affect tumor growth (Figure 5B). Notwithstanding, combination treatment of the HER2×CD2 bispecific and the 5 µg/kg dose of solitomab mediated meaningful tumor growth control, although the effect did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1792, Figure 5B). In contrast, treatment with solitomab alone at 10 µg/kg resulted in significant tumor growth control compared to the vehicle. Remarkably, when combined with the HER2×CD2 bispecific, the anti-tumor effect was further enhanced (p < 0.0003, Figure 5B). Indeed, while only 1 of 9 mice in the 10 µg/kg solitomab monotherapy cohort rejected the tumor, 8 of 9 mice in the combination group achieved complete tumor regression that persisted beyond the end of the treatment phase (p = 0.0098, Figure 5C and Figure S3B). Together, these data confirm that combination with a CD2 Ab-based bispecific targeting a separate tumor antigen can similarly unlock and enhance significant overall anti-tumor response by a TCE in vivo as observed in vitro. HER2×CD2 costimulation overcomes impairment of EpCAM×CD3 T cell engager response due to loss of CD58 expression on tumors cells

Having established that combination with our proprietary CD2 Ab-based HER2×CD2 bispecific can enhance the anti-tumor activity of a TCE both in vitro and in vivo, we investigated if this combination approach can also address reported shortcomings in the TCE field. Loss of CD58, the ligand for CD2, is a reported tumor immune escape mechanism under TCE treatment.15 Presumably, the lack of CD58 results in attenuation of the endogenous costimulation mediated by T cell-tumor cell CD2-CD58 interaction, resulting in reduced TCE activity and relapse. To investigate the importance of endogenous expression of CD58 by tumor cells for anti-tumor cytotoxicity of TCEs, we generated CD58-knock-out (CD58-KO) HCC1954 cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. We confirmed that the CD58-KO HCC1954 line retained the same expression levels of HER2 and EpCAM as the parental cell line (Figure 6A). Next, we used PBMCs as effector cells and co-cultured them with either wildtype or CD58-KO HCC1954 target cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 2:1. We monitored tumor cell killing in the presence of increasing concentrations of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE solitomab over a period of 4 d using the Incucyte system. Anti-tumor cytotoxicity mediated by solitomab was 2–6-fold less potent against CD58-KO compared to wildtype HCC1954 cells, confirming the importance of the CD2-CD58 interaction for the anti-tumor activity of the TCE (Figure 6B and Figure S4A). Notwithstanding, treatment with serial dilutions of solitomab in the presence of a fixed 50 nM concentration of the HER2×CD2 bispecific enhanced potency of solitomab-mediated cytotoxicity against the CD58-KO tumor cell line by 25–35-fold (Figure 6C and Figure S4B). These results demonstrate that the HER2×CD2 molecule is effective in the absence of endogenous CD58 expression by tumor target cells. Further, the data show that combination with the CD2 bispecific can more than compensate for the reduction in anti-tumor TCE activity caused by loss of CD58. Costimulation by HER2×CD2 harnesses the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells lacking CD28 expression

One hallmark of age-associated changes in the human immune system is the accumulation of a large proportion of CD8+ T cells that do not express the prototypic costimulatory receptor CD28.16 CD28− CD8+ T cells are absent in neonates, but make up the majority of circulating CD8+ T cells in the elderly.16 Compared to their CD28+ counterparts, CD28− CD8+ T cells show an enhanced cytotoxic profile that includes gain in expression of activating natural killer cell receptors, elevated expression of chemokine and cytokine receptors, and expression of larger amounts of effector proteins such as perforin and granzyme B.17 We investigated if tumor antigen-dependent CD2 costimulation by the HER2×CD2 bispecific could harness the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of CD28− CD8+ T cells comparably to CD28+ CD8+ T cells. To exclude the impact of endogenous CD2-CD58 signaling, we used CD58-KO HCC1954 cells as target cells and co-cultured them with effector cells comprising either total CD8+ T cells (59−90% CD28+) or FACS-sorted CD28− CD8+ (Fig. S5A) at an effector-to-target ratio of 2:1. We treated the co-cultures with serial dilutions of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, and a fixed 50 nM concentration of either the HER2×CD2 or a HER2×CD28 bispecific (in the same format using the CD28 antibody clone 9–310; Figure S7). Negative control samples were treated similarly with a non-targeting HER2×NT bispecific. Both the CD2 and the CD28 costimulatory bispecifics enhanced the TCE-mediated anti-tumor cytotoxicity by total CD8+ T cells to a comparable level (Figure 7A and Figure S5B). However, only the HER2×CD2 bispecific, but not the HER2×CD28 bispecific, enhanced anti-tumor cytotoxicity by CD28− CD8+ effector T cells. Comparable results were observed across experiments using CD8+ T cells from four different donors (Figure 7 and Figure S5B). These data show that CD2 costimulation is a promising approach to harness the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of CD28− CD8+ T cells. HER2×CD2 triggers substantially less cytokine release than HER2×CD28

Engineering TCEs to minimize cytokine release syndrome (CRS) toxicity while maintaining tumor killing efficacy is a focus area in TCE research.18 While the combination of a TCE with a costimulatory bispecific can enhance the anti-tumor cytotoxicity and improve the clinical outcome, it also poses the risk of amplifying CRS. Thus, we compared the cytokine release accompanying TCE costimulation by our HER2×CD2 bispecific with that from a HER×CD28 bispecific at comparable levels of tumor killing. For this, we treated co-cultures of PBMCs and HER2+ HCC1954 cells with serial titrations of the EpCAM×CD3 TCE, solitomab, and a fixed 10 nM concentration of the respective costimulatory bispecific. We observed consistent enhancement of TCE-mediated cytotoxicity by both HER2×CD2 and HER2×CD28 bispecifics with the CD28 bispecific being more potent (Fig. S6). Notwithstanding, combined with 2 nM solitomab, both CD2 and CD28 bispecifics elicited the same level of tumor cell killing. Consequently, we measured the cytokine release level at 2 nM solitomab to see if there is any difference in the cytokine release level elicited by combination with the respective costimulatory bispecific at a data point where they mediate the same tumor killing propensity (Figure 8). We measured TNF and IL-2 after 3 d of co-culture and IFN-γ and IL-6 on day 4. Combination with the CD2 bispecific had little impact on IL-6, TNF, and IL-2 release, mediating only up to 2-fold increase compared to the control across the four PBMC donors. In stark contrast, combination with the CD28 bispecific mediated up to a 200-fold increase in cytokine release. Combination with the CD2 bispecific showed the strongest impact on IFN-γ release, leading to up to a 9-fold increase using PBMCs from donor 4. Still, the CD28 bispecific drove massive IFN-γ increase, reaching up to 84-fold compared to the control bispecific. Together, these results show that costimulation via CD2 represents a promising means to enhance anti-tumor cytotoxicity of TCEs without exacerbating cytokine release, in stark contrast to CD28 costimulation. Discussion

In this study, we report the first example showing the use of a tumor-targeted costimulatory CD2 bispecific antibody to enhance the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of a TCE directed against a separate solid tumor antigen. While TCEs have had broad success in B cell malignancies, only one classical TCE drug (tarlatamab, DLL3×CD3) is approved for a solid tumor indication.19

A potential reason for their limited success in solid tumors is the absence of exogenous costimulation to counter the strong immunosuppressive microenvironment of solid tumors.20 Considering this, efforts are being made to either incorporate moieties targeting a costimulatory molecule into the designs of next-generation TCEs 21 or combine TCEs with costimulatory bispecifics.22 While the former strategy requires highly selective tumor antigens, the latter approach can accommodate less selective targets for the costimulatory bispecific molecule, as by itself the costimulatory bispecific should not elicit immune activation. In this study, we harness a bispecific CD2 antibody to provide costimulation enhancing the anti-tumor response of a TCE both in vitro and in vivo. Alternative costimulatory receptors more extensively explored for immunotherapy are CD28 and 4–1BB (particularly in CAR-T therapy). However, CD2 is the only costimulatory receptor that shows high expression by T cells found in solid tumors across different cancer indications.6, Furthermore, though TCEs can, in principle, recruit fresh CD28+ T cells from the periphery to the tumor site, cancer being a malignancy of aging presents a conundrum: the majority of cytotoxic CD8+ in the peripheral blood of the elderly do not express CD28.17 Leitner et al. showed that CD2 mediates the most effective costimulation for CD28- CD8+ T cells.23 In line with these findings, we show that a tumor-targeted CD2 costimulatory bispecific can equally harness the anti-tumor cytotoxicity of both CD28+ CD8+ and CD28− CD8+ T cells.

In the clinic, low signal 1 response by a TCE that is inadequate to drive anti-tumor cytotoxicity may arise either due to toxicity-driven dose limitation or downregulation of the tumor antigen of the TCE.24,25 We showed that in a setting of low inefficacious signal 1, costimulation with a CD2 bispecific largely restores anti-tumor cytotoxicity. Additionally, tumor loss of CD58, the ligand for CD2, leading to reduced endogenous costimulation and cell–cell adhesion, has emerged as a tumor immune escape mechanism in primary tumors and in settings of TCE or CAR-T cell treatment.15,24 Here, our CD2 bispecific showed robust enhancement of the TCE-mediated anti-tumor cytotoxicity against target cells that lacked CD58 expression. This demonstrates that tumor-targeted CD2 costimulation can counter immune escape mediated via CD58 loss by tumor cells. Altogether, our data show that combination treatment with a CD2 bispecific can counter key tumor resistance mechanisms to TCE therapy.

Moreover, costimulation via our CD2 bispecific elicited strikingly lower cytokine release than a CD28 bispecific at matched levels of anti-tumor cytotoxicity. This profile appears to be inherent to CD2 biology. In line therewith, Barret et al. reported that CAR constructs bearing endodomains of CD2 mediated lower production of IL-2 and IFN-γ while maintaining comparable T cell activation as their counterpart harboring 4–1BB endodomains.26 Hence, CD2 bispecifics present an efficacious yet potentially safer alternative to CD28 and 4–1BB bispecifics in harnessing the therapeutic promise of TCEs.

In the CD2-targeted T cell engager landscape, PIT565, a trispecific CD19×CD3×CD2 antibody is in Phase 1 clinical development 27 and EvolveImmune Therapeutics recently started first-in-human dosing for their ULBP2×CD3×CD2 TCE (NCT07217171). Further, QLSF Biotherapeutics has a suite of trispecific molecules in preclinical development that also target a respective tumor target and both CD3 and CD2. All the above CD2-targeting molecules engage CD2 via a binding moiety comprising a derivative of the extracellular domain of CD58, the ligand of CD2. While a single molecule that engages both CD3 and CD2 offers a less complex clinical development pathway compared to a combination approach, it comes with significant potential limitations. Specifically, it requires highly selective tumor antigens and is susceptible to resistance due to the downregulation of a single tumor target antigen. The recent publication on EvolveImmune’s platform hints at a potential necessity to incorporate both the CD3-binding and the CD2-binding domains into a single-agent molecule when using the CD58 ECD as the CD2-binding module.6 The novel CD2 bispecific reported here differentiates from the above-mentioned CD58-based molecules by binding to CD2 using a unique non-blocking antibody moiety and by providing costimulation via a separate tumor-targeted bispecific molecule.

In this study, we show, both in vitro and in vivo, that providing CD2 costimulation using a separate tumor-targeted bispecific enhances T cell engager-mediated anti-tumor cytotoxicity. Although our investigation was limited to bispecifics targeting HER2, these findings pave the way for future studies aimed at evaluating the potential of CD2 bispecifics targeting alternative tumor-associated antigens as combinations for TCEs particularly in solid tumors. Indeed, the molecular architecture and the epitope targeted by bispecifics have both been shown to dictate their activity. It has been suggested that bispecifics that target membrane proximal tumor antigen epitopes would generally result in better functionality.12,28–30 Thus, we predict that careful evaluation of tumor antigen epitopes, their binders, and additional bispecific formats may be required for generating additional CD2 costimulatory bispecifics to be used as combination agents for enhancing the activity of other TCEs. In summary, we show that CD2 Ab-based costimulatory CD2 bispecifics are a novel, effective, and potentially safer approach to enhance anti-tumor efficacy of TCE-based cancer immunotherapy in solid tumors.
