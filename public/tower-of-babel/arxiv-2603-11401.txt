Exceptional theta correspondences via Plancherel formulas for rank one symmetric spaces
Jan Frahm, Quentin Labriet

Abstract
We consider the minimal representation of (a finite cover of) the conformal group of a simple split Jordan algebra over ℝ \mathbb{R} or ℂ \mathbb{C} , whenever it exists. The conformal group contains a natural dual pair G × G ′ G\times G^{\prime} , where G G is essentially the automorphism group of the Jordan algebra and G ′ G^{\prime} is either PSL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \PSL(2,\mathbb{R}) , PGL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \PGL(2,\mathbb{R}) or PGL ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \PGL(2,\mathbb{C}) . The groups G G that arise in this way include the complex exceptional group of type F 4 F_{4} as well as its compact and split real form. We explicitly determine the direct integral decomposition of the minimal representation restricted to the corresponding cover of G × G ′ G\times G^{\prime} . This yields a one-to-one correspondence between certain representations of G G and (a finite cover of) G ′ G^{\prime} . The representations of G G that occur in this correspondence are in the support of the Plancherel measure for a rank one symmetric space for G G , and the proof makes use of the corresponding Plancherel formula.

Introduction

The classical theta correspondence is a one-to-one correspondence between certain representations of two groups
G
and
G^{\prime}
that form a dual pair inside a symplectic group. It is obtained by restricting the metaplectic representation of the metaplectic group, a double cover of the symplectic group, to the product of
G
and
G^{\prime}
(or rather their corresponding coverings). The groups
G
and
G^{\prime}
that can occur in this way are all classical groups.

In order to also obtain correspondences for exceptional groups, and also to enlarge the general framework of the theta correspondence, one can replace the metaplectic representation by a minimal representation of another simple Lie group and consider its restriction to dual pairs. This approach has received quite some attention during the past years, and many
exceptional theta correspondences
have been studied. Most results in the literature are for dual pairs where one member is compact (see e.g.
[
7
,
11
,
19
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
31
]
), and there are only few results, mostly partial, where both members are non-compact (see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
). Moreover, in contrast to the classical theta correspondence, which is a general statement for all dual pairs inside symplectic groups, most exceptional theta correspondence are studied case-by-case, i.e. for a specific dual pair inside a particular simple Lie group.

In this paper, we obtain novel exceptional theta correspondences for a family of dual pairs inside the conformal groups of simple Jordan algebras. The first member
G
of the dual pairs is essentially the automorphism group of the Jordan algebra, which is often non-compact. The second member
G^{\prime}
is either
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
,
\PGL(2,\mathbb{R})
or
\PGL(2,\mathbb{C})
. By using the
L^{2}
-model for the minimal representation of the conformal group, we relate the theta correspondence to the Plancherel formula for a rank one symmetric space for the automorphism group. Our correspondences can be seen as an archimedean analogue of some results by Savin
[
32
]
for
p
-adic groups.

Statement of the results.

Let

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}

Inside

Method of proof.

We use the
L^{2}
-model of
\Pi_{\textup{min}}
that was constructed by Vergne–Rossi
[
34
]
for
V
Euclidean and by Dvorsky–Sahi
[
1
]
and Kobayashi–Ørsted
[
17
]
for the other cases. In this model, the representation space is
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
, where
\mathcal{O}\subseteq V
is the subvariety of rank one elements (with an additional positivity condition if
V
is Euclidean). The restriction of
\Pi_{\textup{min}}
to
G
is the left regular representation of
G
on
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
. We show that
\mathcal{O}
contains an open dense subset of the form
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{O}_{G}
,
\mathbb{R}^{\times}\times\mathcal{O}_{G}
or
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{O}_{G}
for
V
real Euclidean, real non-Euclidean and complex, respectively (see Proposition
2.3
). This relates the decomposition of
\Pi_{\textup{min}}|_{G\times\widetilde{G^{\prime}}}
to the Plancherel formula for
\mathcal{O}_{G}
. To also keep track of the action of
G^{\prime}
, we use the Lie algebra representation which was studied in
[
9
]
. The key computation is Lemma
3.5
where the underlying Lie algebra representation of
G^{\prime}
on each
G
-isotypic component is determined. This essentially proves Theorem
A
.

To show Theorem
B
, we make the Plancherel formula for
\mathcal{O}_{G}
explicit using results from the literature (see e.g.
[
28
,
33
]
). In the case where
V
is real and non-Euclidean, a few additional arguments using
K
-types and intertwining operators are necessary to obtain the theta correspondence (see Remark
3.7
and Section
4.2
).

We remark that some parts of this paper could be significantly shortened using case-by-case computations instead of the framework of Jordan algebras. However, in this way, we also treat the exceptional groups along the same lines.

Relation to other work.

For
V
Euclidean, our result can mostly be reduced to the classical theta correspondence and is well-known (see e.g.
[
12
]
). We decided to include this case, because the proof is structurally identical to the other cases, the main difference being that
\mathcal{O}_{G}
is compact. All other correspondences seem to be new, except for the discrete spectrum in the case
\mathcal{G}=\SO(p+1,q+1)
which is a special case of
[
16
, Theorem C]
.

Similar results for the same algebraic groups, but over non-archimedean local fields have been obtained by Savin
[
32
]
. Instead of decomposing the unitary representation
\Pi_{\textup{min}}
, he considers quotients of the smooth vectors
\Pi_{\textup{min}}^{\infty}
, just as in the classical theta correspondence. It would be interesting to investigate these questions also in the archimedean case.

Our proof builds on the
stratified model
by the second author, which was applied in the context of holomorphic discrete series in our previous work
[
3
]
.

The conformal group and its Lie algebra

Let

\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
. In the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}

as a real Jordan algebra and note that as such it is also simple. If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}

to make the formulation uniform.

We write

for the dimension of

and
r=\mathrm{rk}_{\mathbb{F}}V
for its rank, and we assume

. For

denote multiplication by

\tau_{\mathbb{F}}:V\times V\to\mathbb{F},\hskip 9.24994pt(x,y)\mapsto\frac{r}{n}\tr(L(x)L(y))

is a Euclidean Jordan algebra.

Let

as constructed in

If

Lemma 1.4

If

It is easy to check that if we define

2
Dual pairs and orbits

We identify a dual pair of the form
G\times G^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
, where
G=\mathcal{G}\cap\Aut(V)
and
G^{\prime}
has Lie algebra
\sl(2,\mathbb{F})
, and study orbits of
G
in
\mathcal{O}
.

2.1
The dual pair

We first identify the dual pair on the Lie algebra level. The Lie algebra of
G=\mathcal{G}\cap\Aut(V)
is
\mathfrak{g}=\aut(V)
. We write
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
for the Lie algebra spanned over
\mathbb{F}
by

E=(e,0,0),\hskip 18.49988ptF=(0,0,e),\hskip 18.49988pt\mbox{and}\hskip 18.49988ptH=(0,2L(e),0).

By (
1.3
) these elements satisfy the standard commutator relations

[H,E]=2E,\hskip 18.49988pt[H,F]=-2F,\hskip 18.49988pt[E,F]=H,

so
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\simeq\sl(2,\mathbb{F})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})
is a dual pair in
\co(V)
.

Proof.

Let first
X=(u,T,v)
be in the centralizer of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\simeq\sl(2,\mathbb{F})
. By (
1.3
) we find
(u,0,-v)=[H,X]=0
, so
u=v=0
, and further
(Te,0,0)=[X,E]=0
, so
Te=0
and hence
T\in\aut(V)=\mathfrak{g}
by Lemma
1.1
.

Now let
X=(u,T,v)
be in the centralizer of
\aut(V)
, then by (
1.3
)

(Su,[S,T],-S^{\#}v)=[(0,S,0),X]=0\hskip 18.49988pt\mbox{for all }S\in\aut(V).

In particular,
Su=Sv=0
for all
S\in\aut(V)=\aut(V)^{\#}
. In the case where
V
is complex,
[
2
, Lemma VIII.5.1]
implies that
u,v\in\mathbb{C}e
. If
V
is real, then
V_{\mathbb{C}}
is simple and
\aut(V_{\mathbb{C}})=\aut(V)_{\mathbb{C}}
by
[
2
, Proposition VIII.1.1]
, so
u,v\in\mathbb{C}e\subseteq V_{\mathbb{C}}
by the same argument. But
V\cap\mathbb{C}e=\mathbb{R}e
, so
u,v\in\mathbb{R}e
in this case. It remains to show that
T\in\mathbb{F}L(e)
. Write
T=L(x)+D
with
x\in V
and
D\in\aut(V)
. Then

[S,T]=L(Sx)+[S,D]=0
for all
S\in\aut(V)
, which implies that
Sx=0
and
[S,D]=0
for all
S
. Since
\aut(V)
is semisimple, this implies
D=0
, and by using once more
[
2
, Lemma VIII.5.1]
we find
x\in\mathbb{F}e
.
∎

Now let
G^{\prime}
denote the subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
given by the fractional linear transformations of the form

z\mapsto(az+b)(cz+d)^{-1}\hskip 18.49988pt\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\begin{cases}\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})&\mbox{if $V$ is Euclidean,}\\
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})&\mbox{if $V$ is non-Euclidean or complex.}\end{cases}

Since
-\id_{V}\in\mathcal{G}
for
V
real and non-Euclidean, this disconnected group is indeed a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Clearly
G^{\prime}\simeq\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
resp.
\PGL(2,\mathbb{R})
resp.
\PGL(2,\mathbb{C})
for
V
Euclidean resp. non-Euclidean resp. complex. The Lie algebra of

is
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
.

To prove that
(G,G^{\prime})
is a dual pair in
\mathcal{G}
, we first consider the pair
(\Aut(V),\PGL(2,\mathbb{F}))
inside
\Co(V)
, where
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})
is defined as above, also for
V
Euclidean. For
r>2
, the automorphism group
\Aut(V)
has trivial center and
(\Aut(V),\PGL(2,\mathbb{F}))
turns out to be a dual pair inside
\Co(V)
. For
r=2
, the center
Z(\Aut(V))
of
\Aut(V)
has two elements, the non-trivial one being the linear map that maps the identity element
e
of
V
to itself and acts by
-1
on the orthogonal complement
e^{\perp}\subseteq V
. In this case, the centralizer of
\Aut(V)
is the product of
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})
and
Z(\Aut(V))
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
V
be a simple split Jordan algebra over
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
of rank
r
.

•

If
r>2
, then
(\Aut(V),\PGL(2,\mathbb{F}))
is a dual pair in
\Co(V)
and
(G,G^{\prime})
is a dual pair in
\mathcal{G}
.

•

If
r=2
, then
(\Aut(V),\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})Z(\Aut(V)))
is a dual pair in
\Co(V)
and
(G,G^{\prime}Z(G))
is a dual pair in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Since elements of
\Aut(V)
are multiplicative and fix the identity, it follows immediately that
\Aut(V)
commutes with all fractional linear transformations in
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})
. It remains to show that
\Aut(V)
and
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})\times Z(\Aut(V))
are mutual centralizers.

Let
g\in Z_{\Co(V)}(\PGL(2,\mathbb{F}))\subseteq Z_{\Co(V)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})
, then
\Ad(g)H=H
. Since
H
defines the three-grading (
1.2
), it follows that
g
is contained in the Levi subgroup of the corresponding maximal parabolic subgroup, i.e.
g\in\Str(V)
. Moreover,
\Ad(g)E=E
implies that

and hence

by Lemma
1.1
.

Now assume that
g\in Z_{\Co(V)}(\Aut(V))
. Since
\Aut(V)
commutes with
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})
, we must have
\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\subseteq Z_{\mathfrak{g}}(\aut(V))=\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.1
. So
\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
and hence
\Ad(g)|_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})\simeq\PGL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
. We can therefore choose
g^{\prime}\in\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})\subseteq\Co(V)
such that
\Ad(g)=\Ad(g^{\prime})
on
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
. It follows that
g^{-1}g^{\prime}\in Z_{\Co(V)}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})=\Aut(V)
. But since
g^{-1}g^{\prime}
also commutes with every element in
\Aut(V)
, it has to be contained in the center of
\Aut(V)
. If
r>2
, the center of
\Aut(V)
is trivial, so

. For
r=2
, the center of
\Aut(V)
also contains the non-trivial element which acts by
-1
on the orthogonal complement

of the identity

.

The same arguments apply to
(G,G^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{G}
, because they have the same Lie algebras as
(\Aut(V),\PGL(2,\mathbb{F}))
.
∎

2.2
Orbits of
G
in
\mathcal{O}

We now study orbits of

in
\mathcal{O}
. Note that the action of

commutes with dilations by
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
and
\mathcal{O}
is
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
-stable (resp.
\mathbb{R}_{+}
if

is Euclidean). Moreover, the map
\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{F},\ z\mapsto\tau_{\mathbb{F}}(z,e)
is constant on each
G
-orbit and has value

at

. This implies that
\mathcal{O}_{G}=G\cdot c
and its dilations by
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
(resp.

) are distinct

-orbits in
\mathcal{O}
. Their union turns out to be open and dense.

Proposition 2.3
.

(1)

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
V
is Euclidean, then
\mathbb{R}_{+}\mathcal{O}_{G}
is open dense in
\mathcal{O}
.

(2)

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
V
is non-Euclidean, then
\mathbb{R}^{\times}\mathcal{O}_{G}
is open dense in
\mathcal{O}
.

(3)

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, then
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\mathcal{O}_{G}
is open dense in
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Write
L=\Str(V)\cap\mathcal{G}
and
K_{L}=L^{\theta}
for its maximal compact subgroup. By
[
29
, Proposition 1.5.5]
the subgroup
Q_{1}=\{g\in L:gV(c,1)\subseteq V(c,1)\}
is parabolic in
L
with Langlands decomposition
Q_{1}=M_{1}N_{1}
, where
M_{1}=\{g\in L:gL(c)=L(c)g\}
and
N_{1}
fixes
c
. Moreover, the stabilizer
L_{c}
of
c
in
L
is given by
L_{c}=(M_{1}\cap L_{c})N_{1}\subseteq Q_{1}
and
M_{1}=(M_{1}\cap K_{L})A_{1}(M_{1}\cap L_{c})
with
A_{1}=\exp(\mathbb{R}L(c)+\mathbb{R}L(e-c))
. It follows that

Q_{1}\cdot c=(M_{1}\cap K_{L})A_{1}(M_{1}\cap L_{c})N_{1}\cdot c=(M_{1}\cap K_{L})A_{1}\cdot c=(M_{1}\cap K_{L})\cdot\mathbb{R}_{+}c.

Since
M_{1}\cap K_{L}
is compact and leaves

invariant, its orbit through
c
is
\{c\}
for
V
real and Euclidean,
\{\pm c\}
for
V
real and non-Euclidean, and
\{e^{i\theta}c:\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
for
V
complex. This implies
Q_{1}\cdot c=\mathbb{F}^{\times}c
, so
L/Q_{1}=\mathcal{O}/\mathbb{F}^{\times}
. To show the claim of the proposition it therefore suffices to show that the orbit of
G
through the base point of
L/Q_{1}
is open and dense.

This is equivalent to showing that the
Q_{1}
-orbit through the base point of the semisimple symmetric space

is open and dense. The subgroup
G\subseteq L
is open in the fixed point set of the involution
g\mapsto g^{-\#}
which commutes with the Cartan involution
g\mapsto\theta(g)=g^{-*}=\vartheta\circ g^{-\#}\circ\vartheta
. The intersection of both
(-1)
-eigenspaces in

is

which contains
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{R}L(c_{i})
as a maximal abelian subspace, where

is a Jordan frame in
V^{+}
(see
[
29
, Lemma 1.4.5]
). Note that this subspace is also maximal abelian in the structure algebra
\str(V^{+})
of the Euclidean Jordan subalgebra
V^{+}
. The corresponding root systems are both of type

(see
[
29
, Proposition 1.4.6]
), so they have the same Weyl group. Together with
[

this shows that

has a unique open (hence dense) orbit in

.
∎

In the next section we show that
\mathcal{O}_{G}
is a semisimple symmetric space, so it carries a unique (up to scalar multiples)

. We relate the restriction of the measure

to the open subset

Lemma 2.4
.

After appropriate normalization of the measures

, the following integral formula holds for all

The character by which the measure

transforms is a power of the absolute value of the determinant, hence it is trivial on

by (
1.1
) and the measure

is

-invariant. It follows that

for some function

∎

Let

denote the stabilizer of

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

It follows from the formula (

\exp(t\ad(c,0,-c))(0,\id,0)=(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sin(2t)c,\id+(\cos(2t)-1)L(c),-\tfrac{1}{2}\sin(2t)c),

. Moreover, a similar computation shows that

It is clear that

is of index two for

3.1

\PSL(2,\mathbb{F})
and
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})

We recall the representation theory of
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})
in order to fix notation (see e.g.
[

for details). For

and

\sigma_{m,\nu}
of
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\

Note that for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
the representation only depends on

modulo
2

as an element of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
in this case.

Fact 3.1
.

The infinite-dimensional irreducible unitary representations of
\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})
are:

•

\sigma_{m,\nu}
with
\nu\in i\mathbb{R}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. The only equivalences among them are

.

•

The complementary series
\sigma_{m,\nu}
with
\nu\in(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})\setminus\{0\}
and

for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
m=0
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, where

. The only equivalences among them are
\sigma_{m,\nu}\simeq\sigma_{-m,-\nu}
.

•

For
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
the discrete series of parameter
k\geq 2
even, which is the quotient of
\sigma_{m,\nu}
for
\nu=\frac{1-k}{2}
and arbitrary

by the kernel of the standard intertwining operator

All irreducible unitary representations are irreducible when restricted to a parabolic subgroup.

To exhibit these representations in the theta correspondence, we use explicit realizations. By restriction to the opposite unipotent radical, the representation
\sigma_{m,\nu}
can be realized on a space of smooth functions on
\mathbb{F}
with the action

\sigma_{m,\nu}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}f(z)=\left|\frac{(a+bz)^{2}}{ad-bc}\right|^{-\nu-\frac{\delta}{2}}_{-m}f\left(\frac{c+dz}{a+bz}\right),

where
\delta=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{F}
, i.e.
\delta=1
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
\delta=2
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. In particular, we find

d\sigma_{m,\nu}\begin{pmatrix}0&u\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\partial_{uz^{2}}+(2\nu+\delta)\operatorname{Re}(uz)+2mi\operatorname{Im}(uz).

In this picture, the Hilbert space for the unitary principal series is simply
L^{2}(\mathbb{F})
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
dz
on
\mathbb{F}
. For the invariant inner product on the complementary series and the discrete series, we need the standard intertwining operator
A_{m,\nu}:\sigma_{m,\nu}\to\sigma_{-m,-\nu}
which is in this picture given by

A_{m,\nu}f(w)=\int_{\mathbb{F}}|z|^{2\nu-\delta}_{2m}f(w+z)\,dz.

The invariant inner product on complementary series and discrete series is induced by

(f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{F}}(A_{m,\nu}f_{1})(z)\overline{f_{2}(z)}\,dz.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F})
the Euclidean Fourier transform given by

\mathcal{F}u(\zeta)=\int_{\mathbb{F}}e^{-i\operatorname{Re}(z\zeta)}f(z)\,dz,

where we write
\zeta=\xi+i\eta\neq 0

\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}(g)=\mathcal{F}\circ\sigma_{m,\nu}(g)\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1}
defines an equivalent representation on a subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F})
. In case
\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}
has a finite-dimensional subrepresentation, this subrepresentation consists of distributions supported at
\{0\}
(i.e. the Fourier transforms of polynomials). If we replace
\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}
by its quotient by this finite-dimensional subrepresentation, we can consider the representation space to be a subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}^{\times})
. The standard intertwining operator
\widehat{A}_{m,\nu}=\mathcal{F}\circ A_{m,\nu}\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1}:\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}\to\widehat{\sigma}_{-m,-\nu}
is up to scalar multiples given by

\widehat{A}_{m,\nu}f(\zeta)=|\zeta|_{-2m}^{-2\nu}f(\zeta),

so the Hilbert spaces for complementary series representations
\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}
(
\nu\in(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})

\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}
(
\nu=\frac{1-k}{2}
,
k\geq 2
even) are given by

.

It is easy to see the action of the parabolic subgroup

P^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}:ad\neq 0\right\}\subseteq\PGL(2,\mathbb{F})

in this picture:

\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
v&1\end{pmatrix}f(\zeta)=e^{-i\operatorname{Re}(v\zeta)}f(\zeta),\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\

To compute the action of the unipotent radical we note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\circ\frac{d}{dx}

\displaystyle=i\xi\circ\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\circ x

\displaystyle=i\frac{d}{d\xi}\circ\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\circ\frac{d}{dy}

\displaystyle=-i\eta\circ\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\circ y

\displaystyle=-i\frac{d}{d\eta}\circ\mathcal{F}.

Then a short computations shows that

0&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle i\,d\widehat{\sigma}_{m,\nu}\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

where we interpret

Moreover, using

we obtain for

\displaystyle i\,d\tau_{m,\nu}\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=2\xi\partial_{\xi}\partial_{\eta}-\eta(\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\partial_{\eta}^{2})+rd\partial_{\eta}+\frac{\left(\left(\frac{rd-2}{2}\right)^{2}-\nu^{2}-m^{2}\right)\eta-2im\nu\xi}{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}.

We define a map

again with

of

.

intertwines the representations

\displaystyle=T(f_{1}\otimes\tau_{m,\nu}(\exp(sF))f_{2})(tg\cdot c).

Now we consider the action of

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textup{min}}(\exp(sH))\circ T(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})(tg\cdot c)

\displaystyle=e^{-\delta\frac{rd}{2}s}T(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})(e^{-2s}tg\cdot c)=e^{-\delta\frac{rd}{2}s}f_{1}(g\cdot c)f_{2}(e^{-2s}t)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+8T\Big(d\ell(C)f_{1}\otimes\tfrac{\xi}{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}f_{2}-d\ell(D)f_{1}\otimes\tfrac{\eta}{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}f_{2}\Big),

4
The Plancherel formula for
\mathcal{O}_{G}

In this section we use results from the literature to make the Plancherel formula for
\mathcal{O}_{G}
explicit in all cases. As a consequence, the theta correspondence from Theorem
3.6
becomes completely explicit and is shown to be one-to-one.

4.1
V
Euclidean

If
V
is Euclidean,
G
is compact and the decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{G})=L^{2}(G/G_{c})
is discrete and given by the Cartan–Helgason Theorem. For this, recall from Lemma
2.6
that
\mathfrak{t}=\mathbb{R}T_{0}
is a maximal torus in
\mathfrak{g}^{-\sigma}
. The Cartan–Helgason Theorem
[
8
, Ch. V, Theorem 4.1]
asserts that
L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{G})
decomposes into the multiplicity-free direct sum over all irreducible representations of
G
of highest weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
such that

•

\frac{\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle}{\langle\mu,\mu\rangle}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for all
\mu\in\Sigma^{+}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}},\mathfrak{t}_{\mathbb{C}})
,

•

e^{\lambda}
is trivial on
\exp(\mathfrak{t})\cap G_{c}
.

In view of Proposition
2.8
(
2
), the first condition leads to
\lambda=k\beta
such that
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
if
d=1
and
k\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
if
d>1
. But
e^{tT_{0}}\in G_{c}
if and only if
t\in\pi\mathbb{Z}
(see Lemma
2.9
), so the second condition forces
k\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
in all cases. This implies

L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{G})\simeq\widehat{\bigoplus_{k\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}}\,\pi_{k\beta},

where
\pi_{k\beta}
is the irreducible representation of
G
with highest weight
k\beta
.

Since
\rho_{\mathfrak{t}}=(\frac{rd}{2}-1)\beta
(see Proposition
2.8
(
2
)), the eigenvalue
d\pi_{k\beta}(C)
of the Casimir element
C
on
\pi_{k\beta}
is given by

d\pi_{k\beta}(C)=B(k\beta+2\rho_{\mathfrak{t}},k\beta)=-\frac{1}{32}k(k+rd-2),

where we have used that under the identification
\mathfrak{t}\simeq\mathfrak{t}^{*}
in terms of the Killing form

, we have

.

Theorem
3.6
implies that
\theta(\pi_{k\beta})
is an irreducible unitary representation of a finite cover of
G^{\prime}=\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},|\xi|^{\frac{rd}{2}}d^{\times}\xi)
that agrees with
\tau^{\textup{hds}}_{k+\frac{rd}{2}}
on
P^{\prime}
and whose derived representation agrees on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
d\tau^{\textup{hds}}_{k+\frac{rd}{2}}
. In view of Theorem
3.2
this implies:

Corollary 4.1
.

\theta(\pi_{k\beta})=\tau^{\textup{hds}}_{k+\frac{rd}{2}}
is the holomorphic discrete series representation of
\widetilde{\PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
with parameter
k+\frac{rd}{2}
.

4.2

For the continuous part, recall from Lemma

the maximal torus

,

of
c
in

Lemma 4.2
.

M/M_{c}=\{1,m_{0}\}
, where
m_{0}\in M
is an element with

,
m_{0}c_{2}=c_{1}

Proof.

We claim that
M\cdot(c_{1}-c_{2})=\{\pm(c_{1}-c_{2})\}
and that

is the stabilizer of
c_{1}-c_{2}
in

.

To this end, we first observe that

must be finite. In fact, from Proposition
2.8
(
1
) we know that

\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{g}_{0,h_{0}}\oplus\bigoplus_{3\leq i<j\leq r}\mathfrak{g}_{ij},

where
\mathfrak{g}_{0,h_{0}}
denotes the stabilizer of
h_{0}
in
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{ij}=[L(c_{i}),L(V_{ij})]

\mathfrak{m}\cdot(c_{1}-c_{2})=\{0\}
. Since
M
has finitely many connected components, it follows that
M\cdot(c_{1}-c_{2})
is finite.

Next we argue that
M\cdot(c_{1}-c_{2})\subseteq\{\pm(c_{1}-c_{2})\}
. For this, we first observe (in a similar way to Lemma
2.9
) that the one parameter group
A=e^{\mathbb{R}H_{0}}
is acting on
V
by

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}(c_{1}-c_{2})

\displaystyle=\cosh(2t)(c_{1}-c_{2})-\frac{1}{4}\sinh(2t)h_{0},

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}(c_{1}+c_{2})

\displaystyle=c_{1}+c_{2},

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}h_{0}

\displaystyle=\cosh(2t)h_{0}-4\sinh(2t)(c_{1}-c_{2}),

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}x

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle(x\in V_{12},\tau(x,h_{0})=0),

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}y

\displaystyle=\cosh(t)y+\sinh(t)z

\displaystyle(y\in V_{1j},z=-\frac{1}{2}L(h_{0})y\in V_{2j}),

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}z

\displaystyle=\cosh(t)z+\sinh(t)y

\displaystyle(z\in V_{2j},y=\frac{1}{2}L(h_{0})z\in V_{1j}),

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}w

\displaystyle=w

\displaystyle(w\in\bigoplus_{3\leq i\leq j\leq r}V_{ij}).

So if
g\in M
, then

centralizes

and hence

A similar expression holds for

, so we conclude that
u=g(c_{1}-c_{2})
and

are solutions of the system

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}u

\displaystyle=\cosh(2t)u-\frac{1}{4}\sinh(2t)v,

(4.1)

\displaystyle e^{tH_{0}}v

\displaystyle=-4\sinh(2t)u+\cosh(2t)v.

(4.2)

In view of the action of

on
V

\tau(a_{1}(c_{1}-c_{2})+b_{1}h_{0},a_{2}(c_{1}-c_{2})+b_{2}h_{0})=2a_{1}a_{2}-32b_{1}b_{2},

is the stabilizer of

It remains to construct

Lemma 4.3
.

The discrete series parameters for

are
\lambda=k\beta
with

Proof.

By

such that

•

(fair range)

•

\frac{\langle\mu,\gamma\rangle}{\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all positive roots
\gamma
, where

, where

•

Since

(

Lemma 4.5

The restriction of
\Pi_{\textup{min}}
to

(

We first show the statement for

on the space of harmonic homogeneous polynomials on

of degree

\displaystyle V_{0}|_{K\times K^{\prime}}

Corollary 4.6
.

(a)

For
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda\in i\mathfrak{a}^{*}
we have
\theta(\pi_{\xi,\lambda})=\tau_{\xi,\nu}
, where
\nu=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(H_{0})\in i\mathbb{R}
, except for
V\simeq\mathbb{R}^{p,q}
with
p-q\equiv 2\pmod{4}
where
\theta(\pi_{\xi,\lambda})=\tau_{\xi+1,\nu}
.

(b)

For
\lambda=k\beta
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k>-(\frac{rd}{2}-1)
, we have
\theta(A_{\mathfrak{q}}(\lambda))=\tau_{k+\frac{rd}{2}}^{\textup{ds}}
.

Proof.

We first show (b). By Theorem
3.6
and Lemma
4.4
, the unitary representation
\theta(A_{\mathfrak{q}}(\lambda))
of
G^{\prime}
has an irreducible infinite-dimensional restriction to
P^{\prime}
, and its Lie algebra action on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\times})
is the same as the one of the discrete series representation
\tau^{\textup{ds}}_{k+\frac{rd}{2}}
. This implies that the irreducible unitary representations
\theta(A_{\mathfrak{q}}(\lambda))
and
\tau^{\textup{ds}}_{k+\frac{rd}{2}}
have the same infinitesimal character, so by the classification of the unitary dual of
\PGL(2,\mathbb{R})
, it follows that they are equivalent.

To show (a) we first observe that by the same argument as above,
\theta(\pi_{\xi,\lambda})
is isomorphic to
\tau_{\eta,\nu}
for some
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. We claim that
\eta=\xi
(resp.
\eta=\xi+1
in the case
V\simeq\mathbb{R}^{p,q}
with
p-q\equiv 2\pmod{4}
). Note that by Lemma
4.5
there is a
(K\times K^{\prime})
-representation
W
in
\Pi_{\textup{min}}|_{K\times K^{\prime}}
which is a
(K\times K^{\prime})
-type of
\pi_{\xi,\lambda}\boxtimes\tau_{\eta,\nu}
for
\eta=\xi
(resp.
\eta\neq\xi
), but not for
\eta\neq\xi
(resp.
\eta=\xi
). Since this
K
-type does not occur in the discrete spectrum (the discrete series for
G^{\prime}=\PGL(2,\mathbb{R})
does never contains
\mathbb{C}_{\triv}
or
\mathbb{C}_{\sgn}
as
K^{\prime}
-type), it has to occur for some part of the continuous spectrum. So for a subset of
\lambda\in i\mathfrak{a}^{*}
of positive measure, we must have
\eta=\xi
(resp.
\eta=\xi+1
). To show that this implies
\eta=\xi
(resp.
\eta=\xi+1
) for almost all
\lambda\in i\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, we use an analyticity argument.

By
[
4
]
, the projections to the representations in the direct integral are given in terms of a family of intertwining operators

L^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{\infty}\to\pi_{\xi,\lambda}\boxtimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\times},|\zeta|^{\frac{rd}{2}-1}\,d^{\times}\zeta),

and by
[

]
these intertwining operators can be written as (after possibly renormalizing them)

f\mapsto\left[(h,\xi)\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{O}_{G}}u_{\xi,\lambda}(x)f(\xi h^{-1}x)\,d\mu_{G}(x)\right],

(4.3)

where

is a family of spherical distributions on
\mathcal{O}_{G}
depending holomorphically on
\lambda\in i\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. Now let
W\subseteq L^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{\infty}
be the
(K\times K^{\prime})
-type from above, embedded into

-type in

(
