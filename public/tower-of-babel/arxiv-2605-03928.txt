Tree transducers of linear size-to-height increase
Luc Dartois, Lê Thành Dũng (Tito) Nguyễn, Charles Peyrat

Abstract
We investigate a natural generalization to trees of Hennie machines, a known automaton model for regular string functions. Tree-to-tree Hennie machines are tree-walking tree transducers with the ability to rewrite the node labels of their input tree, subject to a bounded visit restriction. Interestingly, they do not merely compute regular tree functions (i.e. 𝖬𝖲𝖮 \mathsf{MSO} transductions), but a larger class of functions with linear size-to-height increase (LSHI). We prove that this class sits between LSHI macro tree transducers (MTTs) and 𝖬𝖲𝖮 \mathsf{MSO} set interpretations. To argue for its robustness, we show that it is closed under precomposition (resp. postcomposition) by MTTs of linear size (resp. height) increase. As a consequence, it contains the entire composition hierarchy of MTTs of linear height increase; we also prove that this composition hierarchy is strict. Finally, we give an alternative characterization of this function class based on a λ \lambda -calculus with linear types. The key difference with similar characterizations of 𝖬𝖲𝖮 \mathsf{MSO} transductions is the use of additive tuples in the encoding of output trees. Our equivalence proof, using game semantics / geometry of interaction, is heavily inspired by an analogous result on higher-order recursion schemes.

Notations & preliminaries

A
ranked set
is a set
\Sigma
endowed with a function
\rank\colon\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
. A finite ranked set is called a
ranked alphabet
. We use the notations
\Sigma^{(k)}=\{\sigma\in\Sigma\mid\rank(\sigma)=k\}
and
\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}=\max(\rank(\Sigma))
.
We use
[k]
as a shorthand for the set
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

A
tree

t
over a ranked set
\Sigma
is defined by:

•

a nonempty prefix-closed domain
\Dom(t)\subset[\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}]^{*}
, whose elements are called the
nodes
of
t
;

•

a map
\lab_{t}\colon\Dom(t)\to\Sigma
, the
labeling
of
t
, such that

\forall u\in\Dom(t),\quad\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid ui\in\Dom(t)\}=[\rank(\lab_{t}(u))]

Thus, we also refer to
\rank(\lab_{t}(u))
as the
rank of the node
u
.

We write
\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
for the set of trees over
\Sigma
, and when
\Sigma\cap Y=\varnothing
,

\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}(Y)=\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma\cup Y}\quad\text{with}\ \rank(y)=0\;\forall y\in Y

In other words, the elements of
\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}(Y)
are like trees over
\Sigma
except that some leaves may have labels from
Y
.

The axioms entail that
\Dom(t)
contains the empty sequence
\varepsilon
; it is called the
root
of
t
. The
leaves
are the rank 0 nodes, or equivalently, the maximal nodes for the prefix ordering. This ordering is also called the
ancestor
relation on nodes, and its converse is the
descendant
relation.
For
u\in\Dom(t)\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
, we write
u{\uparrow}
for the
parent
of

, i.e. the unique node for which
\exists i\in[\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}]:u=(u{\uparrow})i
.

We write
\mathsf{D}_{k}=\{\uparrow\}\cup[k]
for the set of directions we can move in from a node of arity
k
in a tree, where
\uparrow
represents the upward direction towards the parent. According to the previous notations, if
u\neq\varepsilon
is a rank
k
node of a tree
t
and

, then

t

A

is the downward closure of a node

— its

\Dom(t)
with respect to the prefix order.
A branch, or

The

|t|
of a tree

is the cardinality of its domain.
Its

is the maximum size of its branches minus one.

For a node
s
in a tree

, the

t

s

\displaystyle\Dom(t{\restriction_{s}})

\displaystyle=\{u\in[\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}]^{*}\mid su\in\Dom(t)\}

\displaystyle\lab_{t{\restriction_{s}}}(u)

\displaystyle=\lab_{t}(su)

The

t^{\prime}
in

is obtained from

by replacing the subtree rooted at

t^{\prime}
— formally:

\displaystyle\Dom(t[s\leftarrow t^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=(\Dom(t)\setminus\{s\}\cdot[\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}]^{*})\cup\{s\}\cdot\Dom(r)

\displaystyle\lab_{t[s\leftarrow t^{\prime}]}(u)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lab_{t^{\prime}}(v)&\text{if}\ u=sv\ \text{for some}\ v\in\Dom(r)\\

In this paper, it is often (but not always) the case that in such a substitution,

For two families
\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_{i})

For

A running example

Let
\Sigma^{\mathtt{exa}}=\{a,b\}
with
\rank(a)=2
and
\rank(b)=0
. We define
\mathrm{pbt}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma^{\mathtt{exa}}}
(for

) by:

\mathrm{pbt}(0)=b\qquad\mathrm{pbt}(n+1)=a(\mathrm{pbt}(n),\mathrm{pbt}(n))\ \text{for}\ n\in\mathbb{N}

and our running example
\mathtt{exa}\colon\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma^{\mathtt{exa}}}\to\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma^{\mathtt{exa}}}
by

\mathtt{exa}(t)=\mathrm{pbt}(|\Dom(t)\cap 1^{*}2^{*}|)

In other words,
\height(\mathtt{exa}(t))
counts the number of nodes of
t
that are reached from the root by paths that cannot take a
“turn”
from going right to going left. This function has:

on inputs

\height(\mathtt{exa}(t))\leqslant|t|
;

the number of words in

of length at most

.

2.2
Classical tree automata

A
deterministic bottom-up tree automaton
over
\Sigma
consists of:

•

a finite set
Q

•

for each
\sigma\in\Sigma

\delta[\sigma]\colon Q^{\rank(\sigma)}\to Q
.

We may identify
\delta[\sigma]
with an element of

when
\rank(\sigma)=0
. As usual, this can be extended to
\delta[t]\in Q
for every tree

by structural induction:
\delta[\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})]=\delta[\sigma](\delta[t_{1}],\dots,\delta[t_{k}])
.

We shall be concerned with two extensions of deterministic bottom-up tree automata with additional data:

•

A
bottom-up recognizer
comes with a codomain
F
and a map

\varphi\colon Q\shortrightarrow F
. It defines a
recognizable function

t\mapsto\varphi(\delta[t])
from trees to
F
(whose range is always finite even when

is infinite). The languages whose indicator functions are recognizable (with
F=\{0,1\}
) form the well-known class of

.

•

A
bottom-up relabeler
comes with a rank-preserving map
\rho\colon\Sigma\times Q\to\Gamma
. It defines a shape-preserving tree-to-tree function, called a
bottom-up relabeling
: for
k=\rank(\sigma)
,

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket\colon\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}\qquad\text{(where $\mathcal{R}$ is the relabeler)}

\displaystyle\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})

\displaystyle\mapsto\rho(\sigma,\delta[\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})])(\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket(t_{1}),\dots,\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket(t_{k}))

2.3
Tree-generating machines

We recall from
[
46
, §3]
a lightweight formalism for describing various tree transducer models. A
tree-generating machine
with output alphabet
\Gamma
is made of:

•

a set
\mathsf{Conf}
of
configurations
;

•

an
initial configuration

C_{init}\in\mathsf{Conf}
;

•

a (partial)
computation-step function

\mathsf{Conf}\rightharpoonup\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(\mathsf{Conf})
.

\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(\mathsf{Conf})
should be seen as a set of global states of a computation:

•

the nodes with labels in
\Gamma
are part of the eventual output tree, which is being produced in a top-down fashion;

•

the leaves with labels in
\mathsf{Conf}
correspond to processes running in parallel, each in charge of producing one of the remaining subtrees of the output.

By taking rewrite rules that substitute a leaf by the image of its label by the computation-step function, we get a confluent rewriting system on
\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(\mathsf{Conf})
.
A
run
is a rewriting sequence starting from
C_{init}
. By confluence, there is at most one tree in
\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}
that can be reached by the runs (indeed, such trees are normal forms). If it exists, we call it the
output
of the machine.

To define the
semantics
\llbracket\mathcal{T}\rrbracket\colon\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}\rightharpoonup\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}
of a tree transducer
\mathcal{T}
, our methodology is to specify how to build a tree-generating machine from
\mathcal{T}
and a given input tree
t\in\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
, depending on the transducer model. The output of this tree-generating machine is then used as the definition of
\llbracket\mathcal{T}\rrbracket(t)
.

2.4
A branchwise semantics

It is often useful to reason on the trajectory of a single process producing one of the output branches, rather than the entire family of processes producing the output tree in parallel. This is why we propose an alternative presentation of the semantics of a tree-generating machine, which does not appear in
[
46
]
.

Let
\Gamma
be the ranked output alphabet.
We define the set of
branch words
of a tree over
\Gamma
by structural induction:

\displaystyle\brawo(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\}\quad\text{when}\ \rank(\gamma)=0

\displaystyle\brawo(\gamma(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}))

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i}(\gamma,i)\cdot\brawo(t_{i})\ \text{when}\ k\geqslant 1

Claim 5
.

For

,

•

•

Consequently,

\displaystyle=\{(\gamma,i)\mid\gamma\in\Gamma,\;i\in\{1,\dots,\rank(\gamma)\}\}

We therefore aim to characterize the output of a tree-generating machine (with the notations of the previous subsection) through its branch words.
To do so, we consider a labeled transition system over

.

Let

If the output of the tree-generating machine exists, then its set of branch

In general, we refer to a path starting from

3
Tree-to-tree Hennie machines

3.1
Unrestricted THMs: definition & semantics

Definition 8
.

An
unrestricted tree-to-tree Hennie machine
(
\mathsf{uTHM}
) is a tuple
\mathcal{H}=(Q,M,\top,\Sigma,\Gamma,q_{init},\delta)
where:

•

Q
is the finite set of
states
of
\mathcal{H}
;

•

M
is the finite set of
memory symbols
of
\mathcal{H}
;

•

\top\in M
is the initial memory value of
\mathcal{H}
;

•

\Sigma
(resp.
\Gamma
) is the ranked
input
(resp.
output
) alphabet;

•

q_{init}\in Q
is the initial state of
\mathcal{H}
;

•

\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\times M\rightharpoonup\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(Q\times M\times\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}})
is the (partial)
transition function
of
\mathcal{H}
, such that for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

\delta(Q\times\{\sigma\}\times M)\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(Q\times M\times\mathsf{D}_{\rank(\sigma)})

The semantics of this
\mathsf{uTHM}
has been informally sketched in the introduction. To describe it rigorously, we use the formalism of tree-generating machines. We start with its
configurations
over an input
t\in\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
: they are tuples
(u,q,\mu)
where

•

u
is a node of
t
, the current position;

•

q\in Q
is the current state;

•

\mu\colon\Dom(t)\to M
describes the current memory values.

For such a configuration and
(q^{\prime},m,d)\in Q\times M\times\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}}
, let

(u,q,\mu)\ogreaterthan(q^{\prime},m,d)=(ud,q^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\ \text{where}\ \begin{cases}\mu^{\prime}(u)=m\\
\mu^{\prime}(v)=\mu(v)\ \text{for}\ v\neq u\end{cases}

— the machine
“writes the symbol
m
in memory at the current position
u
before moving to the new position
ud
”
.
The result of this operation is
undefined
if
ud\notin\Dom(t)
.

We extend the definition of
\ogreaterthan
to
\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(Q\times M\times\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{R}_{\Sigma}})
by application to the leaves; inductively:
C\ogreaterthan\gamma(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})=\gamma(C\ogreaterthan t_{1},\;\dots,\;C\ogreaterthan t_{k})
.

Let
\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{H},t)
be the set of configurations of the
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}
on the input tree
t
. The
computation-step function
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{H},t)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{H},t))

\displaystyle(u,q,\mu)

\displaystyle\mapsto(u,q,\mu)\ogreaterthan\delta(q,\lab_{t}(u),\mu(u))

Finally, the
initial configuration
is
C_{init}(t)=(\varepsilon,q_{init},(v\mapsto\top))
.

Example 9
.

We give a
\mathsf{uTHM}
that computes
\mathtt{exa}
from §
2.1
.

•

There are 2 states,
q_{0}
(initial) and
q_{1}
.

•

There are 4 memory symbols
\top,\mathtt{L},\mathtt{R},\maltese
.

•

The transitions are (undefined in the 2 unspecified cases):

\displaystyle(q_{0},a,\top)

\displaystyle\mapsto a((q_{1},\mathtt{L},2),(q_{1},\mathtt{L},2))

\displaystyle(q_{0},b,\top)

\displaystyle\mapsto a(b,b)

\displaystyle(q_{1},a,\top)

\displaystyle\mapsto a((q_{1},\mathtt{R},2),(q_{1},\mathtt{R},2))

\displaystyle(q_{1},a,\mathtt{L})

\displaystyle\mapsto(q_{0},\maltese,1)

\displaystyle(q_{1},b,\top)

\displaystyle\mapsto a((q_{1},\maltese,\uparrow),(q_{1},\maltese,\uparrow))

\displaystyle(q_{1},a,\mathtt{R})

\displaystyle\mapsto(q_{1},\maltese,\uparrow)

On an input tree
t\in\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma^{\mathtt{exa}}}
, all branch-outputting runs ending in
b
traverse the same configurations in the same order; the only difference is in the labels (
(a,1)
vs.
(a,2)
). Such a run starts by writing
\mathtt{L}
to the root, and going down to the right child in state
q_{1}
. It then goes right, writing
\mathtt{R}
in memory on all encountered nodes, until it reaches a leaf. It then starts going up while writing
\maltese
on the visited nodes (including the leaf) until it reaches a node where the memory is
\mathtt{L}
. After having thus visited all nodes in
\Dom(t)\cap 2^{*}
, it then moves to the left child of the root, which has not yet been visited, and switches back to the initial state
q_{0}
. The computation then proceeds as it did before on the subtree rooted at the left child.

Note that the transitions only output a node
a
when the current memory is
\top
, which ensures that each input node is counted at most once when determining the output height.

3.2
The bounded-visit restriction

Remark 10
.

A
\mathsf{uTHM}
computing a string-to-boolean function (i.e.
\max(\rank(\Sigma))=1
and
\Gamma=\{\mathtt{true}:0,\;\mathtt{false}:0\}
) is the same thing as a deterministic
linear bounded automaton
. It is well known that this automaton model can recognize at least all context-free languages
[
40
, Theorem 3]
while its nondeterministic variant recognizes precisely the context-sensitive languages
[
40
, Theorem 1]
.

In order to stay within the realm of
“finite-state computability”
we shall therefore impose the bounded-visit restriction. The latter can be conveniently defined by using the branchwise semantics.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a
\mathsf{uTHM}
with input alphabet
\Sigma
.

The
number of visits
of a branch-outputting run of
\mathcal{H}
at a node
u
(resp. a set of nodes
S
) of the input tree is the number of configurations in the path whose current position is
u
(resp. is in
S
).

\mathcal{H}
is
bounded-visit
on an input language
L\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
whenever there is a uniform bound
N
such that, for any
t\in L
, any node
u
of
t
and any branch-outputting run on the input
t
, the number of visits at
u
is at most
N
.
A
tree-to-tree Hennie machine
(
\mathsf{THM}
) is a
\mathsf{uTHM}
that is bounded-visit on all inputs (
L=\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
).

\Cref

ex:thm is a
\mathsf{THM}
: it visits each node at most twice.

We can now establish linear size-to-height increase rigorously.

Proof of Claim
3
.

By definition of height & Claims
5
/
7
,

\displaystyle\height(\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket(t))

\displaystyle=\max\{|b|\mid\exists\gamma:b\gamma\in\brawo(\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket(t))\}

\displaystyle=\max\{|b_{1}\dots b_{\ell}|\mid\exists\gamma:C_{init}(t)\xrightarrow{b_{1}}\dots\xrightarrow{b_{\ell}}\gamma\}

The labeled transition system
\to
for
\mathcal{H}
has labels derived from the branch words of the range
\delta(Q\times\Sigma\times M)
of the transition function of
\mathcal{H}
(to see this, unfold the definition of the computation-step function). Since this range is finite,
|b_{i}|=O(1)
in the above description, so
|b_{1}\dots b_{\ell}|=O(\ell)
. Finally,
\ell=O(|t|)
because any branch-outputting run of a
\mathsf{THM}
visits each of the
|t|
nodes
O(1)
times — this is the bounded-visit restriction.
∎

Remark 12
.

Unfortunately, it is undecidable whether a
\mathsf{uTHM}
is a
\mathsf{THM}
, already in the string-to-boolean case
[
32
, Theorem 1 + Lemma 1]
. However, given an explicit bound, we have a decidability result (
\Cref
cor:reg-domain).

We also define the
number of downwards visits
of a branch-outputting run of a
\mathsf{uTHM}
at an input node
u
by counting the occurrences of transitions of the form
(u{\uparrow},\dots)\to(u,\dots)
(thus, if
u
is the root, this number is zero). The following property guarantees that
“bounded-downwards-visit”
is the same thing as bounded-visit.

Claim 13
.

The number of visits at a node is at most the sum of the numbers of downwards visits at this node and at its children.

Proof.

Along a branch-outputting run, the position of a
\mathsf{uTHM}
moves locally, therefore every upwards visit of
u
from
ui
must have been preceded by a downwards visit of
ui
from
u
.
∎

3.3
Weight-reducing
\mathsf{THM}
s & totality

The bounded-visit restriction is defined semantically. We present here a syntactic counterpart, taken from
[
32
]
for Hennie machine as acceptors of string languages.

Definition 14
.

A
\mathsf{uTHM}
with the set
M
of memory symbols and the transition function
\delta
is
weight-reducing
if there exists a partial order on
M
such that

•

the initial symbol
\top
is maximal;

•

for every

\delta(\dots,\dots,m)
.

For instance,
\Cref
ex:thm is weight-reducing (
\top>{\mathtt{L},\mathtt{R}}>\maltese
).
The following result may be compared with
[
32
, Lemma 2]
.

Proposition 15
.

Every weight-reducing
\mathsf{uTHM}
is a
\mathsf{THM}
.

Conversely, given a
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, one can compute a weight-reducing
\mathsf{THM}

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
such that
\llbracket\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\rrbracket
is a
total function
and:

•

for
t
in the domain of
\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket
s.t. all branch-outputting runs of
\mathcal{H}
visit at most
N
times each node,
\llbracket\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\rrbracket(t)=\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket(t)
;

•

on other inputs,
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
returns an output that contains some chosen default leaf symbol.

In particular, if
\mathcal{H}
is a
\mathsf{THM}
whose number of visits is uniformly bounded by
N
, then
\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket\subseteq\llbracket\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\rrbracket
.

Speaking of totality, the semantics of a
\mathsf{THM}
is naturally a
partial
function, but we focus on the case when the function happens to be total. This restriction is not very significant: as we explain below (
\Cref
cor:reg-domain), the domain of a partial
\mathsf{THM}
is effectively regular.

3.4
The narrow-visit restriction

Recall that a
chain
in a partially ordered set (poset) is a totally ordered subset, while an
antichain
is a subset of pairwise incomparable elements. When we say that a set of nodes in a tree is an antichain, we mean with respect to the ancestor ordering.

Definition 16
.

A
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}
is
narrow-visit
on an input language
L
whenever there is a uniform bound
N
such that, for any
t\in L
,
any antichain
S
of
t
and any branch-outputting run on the input
t
, the number of visits at
S
is at most
N
.

We rephrase the narrow-visit property by combining a basic observation with a classical duality theorem (which is closely related to other dualities in combinatorial optimization, cf.
[
25
]
).

Theorem 17

(Dilworth
[
14
]
)
.

A poset is included in the union of
N<\infty
chains iff all its antichains have cardinality at most
N
.

Claim 18
.

For a set of tree nodes, the following are equivalent:

•

it is a chain (for the ancestor ordering);

•

it is included in some branch of the tree.

Corollary 19
.

An
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}
is narrow-visit on an input language
L
if and only if the two following conditions hold jointly:

•

\mathcal{H}
is bounded-visit on
L
;

•

for every branch-outputting run whose input tree is in
L
, the set of visited nodes (i.e. those with a nonzero number of visits) is included in the union of
O(1)
input branches.

Corollary 20
.

If a
\mathsf{uTHM}
is narrow-visit on all inputs, then it is a
\mathsf{THM}
and it has linear height increase.

Proof.

As we have seen when proving Claim
3
, the output height is at most linear in the length of branch-outputting runs. On an input
t
, such a run visits
O(1)
branches, each of which has at most
\height(t)+1
nodes, and each node is visited
O(1)
times.
∎

3.5
Basic extensions

To conclude
\Cref
sec:hennie, we present three additional features that can be added to
\mathsf{uTHM}
s and
\mathsf{THM}
s. We show that these extensions are conservative, i.e. do not lead to an increase in expressive power.

Stay-instructions

The positions of two successive configurations of an
\mathsf{uTHM}
are always distinct neighbors. We extend the syntax of transitions to allow staying on the current position; this is often convenient in our constructions of
\mathsf{THM}
s. Stay-instructions are indicated by a new symbol
\circlearrowleft
, and relax the requirement on the transition function
\delta
to be

\delta(Q\times\{\sigma\}\times M)\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(Q\times M\times(\{\circlearrowright\}\cup\mathsf{D}_{\rank(\sigma)}))

The semantics is extended by taking
u{\circlearrowleft}=u
for any input node
u
.

Lemma 21
.

Every
\mathsf{uTHM}
with stay-instructions can be effectively translated to an equivalent
\mathsf{uTHM}
. This translation preserves the bounded-visit and narrow-visit restrictions on any input language.

Bottom-up initialization.

Our second conservativity result can be understood as a
“closure under precomposition”
property.

Lemma 22
.

Given a
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}
and a bottom-up relabeler
\mathcal{R}
(cf. §
2
), whose respective input and output alphabets match, one can effectively construct a
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
such that
\llbracket\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket\circ\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is bounded-visit on
\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket(L)
, then
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is bounded-visit on
L
.

The idea of the proof is to perform a post-order tree traversal that computes and records in memory, at each node, the state reached there by the deterministic bottom-up automaton contained in
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 23
.

A
\mathsf{uTHM}

with bottom-up initialization
is:

•

the data of a
\mathsf{uTHM}
— we reuse the notations of Def.
8
;

•

a deterministic bottom-up tree automaton
(Q^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
;

•

a map
\mathtt{init}\colon Q^{\prime}\to M
.

Its semantics is the same as for
\mathsf{uTHM}
except that for an input tree
t\in\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
, the initial memory value at node
u
is
\mathtt{init}(\delta^{\prime}[t{\restriction_{u}}])
.

We then define in the same way as
\Cref
def:bounded-visit the bounded-visit condition. A
\mathsf{THM}
with bottom-up initialization
is a
\mathsf{uTHM}
with bottom-up initialization that happens to be bounded-visit.

Proposition 24
.

Every
\mathsf{uTHM}
with bottom-up initialization can be effectively translated to an equivalent
\mathsf{uTHM}
.

The translation preserves the bounded-visit condition on any input language, but not the narrow-visit condition.

Proof.

For any
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}
with bottom-up initialization, let:

•

\mathcal{R}
be the bottom-up relabeler using
(Q^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
from the above definition and
\rho\colon(\sigma,q^{\prime})\mapsto(\sigma,\mathtt{init}(q^{\prime}))
;

•

\mathcal{H}
be a
\mathsf{uTHM}
simulating
\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}
while reading from the input letters the initial memory values when it encounters a node for the first time — thus, when
\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}
is bounded-visit on all inputs,
\mathcal{H}
is bounded-visit on the range of
\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket
.

Then
\llbracket\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket\circ\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket
.
Therefore, we can apply
\Cref
lem:bottom-up-rel.
∎

Regular lookaround.

Finally, we consider the extension of tree-to-tree Hennie machines with the ability to use regular information about the current configuration to decide the next transition.

Definition 25

(Encodings of configurations)
.

Consider a
\mathsf{uTHM}

\mathcal{H}
with the notations of
\Cref
def:uthm.

For
t\in\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma}
, we encode
(u,q,\mu)\in\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{H},t)
as the tree over
\Sigma\times M\times(Q\cup\{\mathtt{notHere}\})
, with the ranks inherited from
\Sigma
, whose domain is
\Dom(t)
and whose label at each node

(\lab_{t}(v),\;\mu(v),\;[q\ \text{if}\ v=u\text{, otherwise}\ \mathtt{notHere}])

Definition 26
.

A
\mathsf{uTHM}
with regular lookaround
(
\mathsf{uTHM}^{\mathsf{R}}
)
\mathcal{H}
from an input alphabet
\Sigma
to an output alphabet
\Gamma
consists of:

•

the data of a
\mathsf{uTHM}

without the transition function
as in
\Cref

(Q,M,\top,\Sigma,\Gamma,q_{init})
;

•

The configurations of the machine

(the transition function is not involved). The semantics is defined
analogously to ordinary
\mathsf{uTHM}

passes the entire configuration to the transition function — since it is recognizable, it only depends on a finite amount of information:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{H},t)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma}(\mathsf{Conf}(\mathcal{H},t))\qquad\qquad(\text{for}\ t\in\mathsf{T}_{\Sigma})

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\mapsto C\ogreaterthan\delta(\text{encoding of}\ C)

We combine bottom-up initialization with dynamic maintenance of local lookaround information to prove:

Theorem 27
.

Every
\mathsf{uTHM}^{\mathsf{R}}
can be effectively translated to an equivalent
\mathsf{uTHM}
. The translation preserves the bounded-visit condition on any input language.

Corollary 28
.

The classes of functions computed by
\mathsf{uTHM}
s and by
\mathsf{THM}
s are both closed by precomposition by
\mathsf{MSO}
relabelings
.

The aforementioned
\mathsf{MSO}

[
11
, Definition 7.20]
for a book reference.

Remark 29
.

For automata models such as e.g. tree-walking tree transducers and macro tree transducers, regular lookaround morally amounts to preprocessing by
\mathsf{MSO}
relabelings. But for
\mathsf{THM}
s, this is not the case because the information given by the regular lookaround depends not only on the input tree and current position, but also on the current memory values at each node.

Macro tree transducers

Notations specific to this section.

We reserve two special sets of symbols, a set

A tree in

For

\langle q,z\rangle
for

4.1
MTTs: definition and semantics

Definition 30
.

A (total deterministic)
\mathsf{MTT}

\mathcal{M}
from an input
alphabet
\Sigma

\Gamma
(both ranked) consists of:

•

a finite

set
Q

q_{init}\in Q^{((0))}
;

•

for each state
q\in Q
and each input letter

\mathsf{RHS}_{\mathcal{M}}(q,\sigma)\in\mathsf{T}_{\Gamma\cup\langle Q,X_{\rank(\sigma)}\rangle}(Y_{\rank(q)})

The latter is meant to be the right-hand side of a rewrite rule

\langle q,\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{\rank(\sigma)})\rangle(y_{1},\dots,y_{\rank(q)})\to\mathsf{RHS}_{\mathcal{M}}(q,\sigma)

Let us illustrate what this means before giving a formal definition:

Example 31
.

Here is an
\mathsf{MTT}
for the function
\mathtt{exa}
from §
2.1
.

\displaystyle\langle q_{0},a(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\to\langle q_{1},x_{2}\rangle(a(\langle q_{0},x_{1}\rangle,\langle q_{0},x_{1}\rangle))

\displaystyle\langle q_{0},b\rangle

\displaystyle\to a(b,b)\quad(\text{i.e.}\ \mathrm{pbt}(1)\ \text{as defined in \lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:running-example}})\qquad\phantom{.}

\displaystyle\langle q_{1},a(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle(y_{1})

\displaystyle\to\langle q_{1},x_{2}\rangle(a(y_{1},y_{1}))

\displaystyle\langle q_{1},b\rangle(y_{1})

\displaystyle\to a(y_{1},y_{1})

\begin{aligned} \text{We have}\ \langle q_{0},a(b,b)\rangle&\to\langle q_{1},b\rangle(a(\langle q_{0},b\rangle,\langle q_{0},b\rangle))&\to\langle q_{1},b\rangle(a(\mathrm{pbt}(1),\langle q_{0},b\rangle))\\
&\to\langle q_{1},b\rangle(\mathrm{pbt}(2))\to\mathrm{pbt}(3)=\mathtt{exa}(a(b,b)).\end{aligned}

In the literature, the semantics of
\mathsf{MTT}

\langle q,t\rangle
. Let us call such trees

The idea is to represent as a

Essentially, the composite

15

15

We store a representation of a

16

16

]
.

\mathsf{uTHM}

deterministically either : yields a finite sequence such that

\mathcal{H}
starts simulating
\mathcal{M}
at the root, applying its initial transition.
When
\mathcal{H}
reaches a node from its parent, it does so in a given state

\mathcal{M}

Together with the node label

\mathcal{M}
