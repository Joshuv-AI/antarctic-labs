Establishing weighted criteria for return to play after hamstring injury: a systematic review and methodological quality analysis
Authors: Juan Javier Bru-Ruiz, David Pérez-Cruzado, Jerónimo García-Romero, María Ruiz-Muñoz, José Carlos Canca-Sánchez, Manuel González-Sanchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHamstring injuries are the most prevalent in active individuals aged between 20 and 70 years, with a high recurrence rate of 57% during the first month. The lack of a clear consensus on the return to activity of these patients could lead to an increase in the incidence of relapses, as well as greater economic costs.MethodsA systematic review was carried out, following the PRISMA guidelines. The article search was conducted in the PubMed, Sportdiscuss, WOS and Scielo databases, using the terms “hamstring muscle” OR “biceps femoris” OR “semimembranosus” OR “semitendinosus” OR “hamstring tendon” OR “semitendinosus tendon” OR “semimembranosus tendon” OR “biceps femoris tendon” AND “return to sport” OR “return to play” OR “resumption of sporting activities” OR “return to sporting activities” OR “recreational activities resumption” OR “return to recreational activities”.Results22 studies were finally included in the present review. The main variables analyzed were the presence of pain, the performance of functional tests, the measurement of flexibility and range of motion, the strength developed and the use of imaging tests.ConclusionPain measurement has been shown to be the most common criterion for determining return to activity, and its combination with other complementary measurements such as strength, flexibility and the ability to perform sport-specific movements at maximum speed is recommended.

Introduction

Hamstring injury is the most prevalent type of injury in active people aged between 20 and 70 years [1]. In competitive sports, it is the main lesion in those sports where high-speed sprints are frequently performed, as well as jumps, kicks and explosive movements of the lower limb [2], together with changes of direction [3]. In football, it has an incidence of 4% to 30% of all injuries [4] and accounts for 25% of all sick leaves due to injury [5], with a recurrence rate of up to 68% [6] and an average absence time that varies between 14 and 20 days [7]. In addition, it is more frequent in men than in women [8], with an annual increase in incidence of 4% since 2001 in men´s elite football players [9]. It has also been associated in the literature with other sports, such as dancing [10] and athletics [11].

These injuries have a high recurrence rate, which can reach up to 63% two years after the injury, although 57% occur in the first month [12]. These relapses increase the rehabilitation period after the injury [13], which causes in the athlete an increase in frustration [14], decreased capacity to develop maximum strength [15], and reduced extensibility [16], resulting in an alteration of the structure and function of these muscles [17], together with a lower performance in sports practice [18]. Factors such as decreased muscle strength [19], inadequate rehabilitation [20], incomplete recovery [21] or a premature return to competition [22] constitute some of the risks of suffering a relapse after a muscle injury.

What criteria should be used to determine the return to play? In the scientific literature, there is no consensus that clearly answers this question [23]. To date, the criteria used for returning to activity have mainly been based on diagnostic imaging [24], in addition to a wide range of functional tests [25] and the absence of pain in specific tests for each sport [20]. Moreover, financial [26] and sports performance [18] reasons have gained relevance in elite sports, modifying the criteria for rehabilitation and return to activity [27], gaining ground over clinical decisions [28]. A consensus has not been established on the tests to predict recovery time [29], although factors that will influence them have been determined, such as the injury mechanism [30], the location of the injury [31], its extension seen in diagnostic imaging [32], and the degree of involvement of the proximal tendon [33]. Thus, not using adequate criteria for returning to physical activity could lead to new injuries, as well as greater economic costs.

The objective of this study is to analyze the scientific literature in order to establish defined criteria for clinical decision-making regarding the return to play following a hamstring muscle injury.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted under the PRISMA [34, 34] regulations and registered in OSF under registration number https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/GSVE6.

Search strategy

The bibliographic search was carried out 20th April 2026, using the PubMed, SportDiscuss, Web of Science (WoS) and SciELO databases. To carry out the article search, the PRESS strategy [35] was followed. Thus, the terms and strategies used for the search in all databases cited were: (“hamstring muscle” OR “biceps femoris” OR “semimembranosus” OR “semitendinosus” OR “hamstring tendon” OR “semitendinosus tendon” OR “semimembranosus tendon” OR “biceps femoris tendon”) AND (“return to sport” OR “return to play” OR “resumption of Sporting activities” OR “return to sporting activities” OR “recreational activities re-sumption” OR “return to recreational activities”). The MeSH terms mentioned above were included in all databases used in this review, following the structure described above. No filters were applied during the search, and all articles published up to the search date were considered. Grey literature, such as clinical trials, theses, and conference proceedings, was not included in this study.

Document selection

The inclusion criteria used are as follows: Studies dealing with patients who have suffered a hamstring injury, regardless of the grade of the injury and the level of physical activity performed. It must include at least one measurement variable for return to activity, regardless of whether it is compared to another variable. This integrative review incorporated a high-impact selection of scientific literature structured around three methodological pillars: randomized controlled trials to evaluate therapeutic interventions, which were appraised using the PEDro scale; observational longitudinal studies to establish prognostic indicators and biomechanical symmetry metrics, assessed via the STROBE checklist; and systematic reviews to synthesize international consensus, validated through the PRISMA checklist. Studies published in English, Spanish, French, Portuguese or Italian.

The exclusion criteria are patients who have undergone surgery. In addition, articles that are case reports or opinion pieces will also be excluded.

After carrying out the search, the obtained studies were classified independently and blindly by two researchers (D.P.C. and J.J.B.R.), using the Rayyan platform. This was conducted in two stages. Firstly, the titles and abstracts of the studies found were evaluated; then, the full text of said articles was evaluated. After this procedure, both researchers discussed those articles in which they presented different opinions. After the initial blinded screening, the inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s kappa) was 0.81 and after the open discussion between both initial researcher as 0.96. In those articles in which the researchers did not reach a common opinion, a third reviewer (M.G.S.) decided on their selection.

Internal validity measurement

With the aim of analyzing the internal validity of the different types of studies included in this review: PEDro internal validity scale was used with the clinical trials, STROBE internal validity scale was used with the observational articles and the PRISMA scale was used with the reviews.

Study variables

The objective variables analyzed were: flexibility and range of motion (degree of stretching and/or range of motion of the injured lower limb compared with the contralateral leg and/or at the pre-injury time), functional tests (performance of sport-specific tests and functional measurement tests of the lower limb), strength (measurement of eccentric, concentric and/or isometric strength values of the affected lower limb, compared with the contralateral leg and/or pre-injury values), imaging tests (determination of the injury characteristics using diagnostic imaging tests), inflammation (presence or absence of signs of inflammation at the time of injury as a predictive value for recovery time) and injury history (previous history of injuries in the hamstring muscles).

At the same time, subjective variables were also analyzed, which aim to evaluate the athlete’s perception towards their pathology from different points of view, such as pain (presence of pain at the time of the injury or in several hours after the injury, as well as when returning to activity), the ability to walk without pain (the time it takes the patient to walk without pain after the injury) and the athlete’s subjective perception when carrying out the functional tests proposed.

Data synthesis and evidence hierarchy modeling

To maximize transparency, standardization, and rigor in our reporting, data extraction and synthesis were conducted following strict predefined parameters. Acknowledging the inherent heterogeneity of the included study designs—spanning randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and systematic reviews—we implemented a standardized evaluation approach to ensure consistent comparison across all methodological frameworks.

To establish a clinical decision-making framework, the data extracted from the selected studies were synthesized into a hierarchical model of importance, represented by a multidimensional overlapping diagram. The weighting of each variable—including Pain, Strength, Flexibility and Range of Movement, Functional Tests, and Imaging Tests—was determined by triangulating three fundamental factors. This methodological approach ensured that the resulting model is not merely a qualitative summary, but a weighted representation of return-to-play criteria, where the size and positioning of each domain reflect its empirical reliability and functional relevance within the current scientific literature:

Level of scientific evidence

Variables were prioritized based on their position within the evidence pyramid. Greater specific weight was assigned to criteria validated by systematic reviews (appraised via the PRISMA checklist) and randomized controlled trials (appraised via the PEDro scale), while findings from observational studies (appraised via the STROBE checklist) provided supplementary prognostic value.

Methodological internal validity

The individual scores of the studies, as detailed in the quality assessment tables, directly influenced the centrality of each variable. For instance, parameters frequently validated in high-scoring trials, such as biomechanical symmetry and functional capacity, were categorized as primary pillars.

Clinical consensus and relapse prediction

Data regarding the predictive capacity of each variable for re-injury risk—such as specific strength deficits or the persistence of imaging findings—were used to define the degree of overlap between clinical recovery and functional discharge.

Results

Figure 1 defines the process carried out to obtain the total number of articles. After performing the search using the aforementioned keywords, a total of 2482 articles were obtained, 2424 of which remained after discarding duplicates. Subsequently, 2350 were eliminated after reading the title and abstract to obtain a total of 74 articles analyzed. Finally, after subjecting them to the inclusion criteria that were previously specified, 45 articles were selected, eventually obtaining a final sample of 26 studies (13 of them are considered as a “main articles” and 13 of them as considered as a “secondary articles”).

On the other hand, Table 1 also highlights clear areas of opportunity to standardize and improve future trials in this line of research. A dispersion is observed in the compliance with relevant parameters during the execution and statistical analysis phases, such as the blinding of therapists and assessors (items 6 and 7), or the detailed presentation of between-group comparisons and point measures and measures of variability (items 10 and 11), aspects that are not addressed uniformly. On the other hand, Table 2 also reflects certain areas of opportunity to standardize and enrich future publications in this field. A margin for improvement is identified in specific methodological details, such as the formal justification of the sample size (item 10), which is explicitly detailed in a minority of the works. Likewise, highly valuable aspects such as the reporting of bias control (item 9) or the discussion regarding the applicability and generalization of the results (item 21) show a lower frequency of compliance. A greater emphasis on these methodological elements in future research will contribute to consolidating the excellent descriptive base that this literature already possesses, increasing the robustness and overall rigor of the conclusions in this matter.

The analysis of pain measurement reflects an extremely high clinical value in the reviewed literature, highlighting a strong and enriching consensus in placing this variable as the central axis for decision-making regarding the return to sporting activity. It is especially positive to observe how the evidence not only aligns around the standard of ‘absence of pain’, but also fosters a mature and highly practical debate; innovative approaches question the dogma of ‘zero pain’ by proposing more realistic and functional tolerance thresholds, while other authors manage to use initial pain as an excellent predictor for recovery prognosis, expanding the tools available to professionals. To further consolidate this magnificent clinical foundation, a methodological aspect that would present an interesting margin for improvement in future research would be a greater specification of the measurement instruments used, since standardizing the use of validated scales—currently detailed in only a minority of the works—would help homogenize reporting and greatly facilitate the exact comparison of these valuable clinical criteria (Table 6).

The analysis of flexibility and range of motion measurement reveals a great effort in the literature to clinically objectify physical recovery, highlighting a valuable functional consensus by establishing biomechanical symmetry—whether by reaching pre-injury values or matching the flexibility of the healthy contralateral leg, often with well-defined deficit margins, such as the 10% proposed by Van der Horst et al.—as the main standard for authorizing the return to activity. It is very positive to observe the wealth of specific tests analyzed, such as the Active Knee Extension Test or the rigorous Askling-H Test, which provides professionals with excellent quantitative tools to evaluate tissue functionality and associate specific deficits with prognosis, as wonderfully quantified by the work of De Vos et al. by calculating a 13% risk of relapse for each degree of deficit. To further polish and consolidate this solid framework, an area with an interesting margin for improvement for future publications would be to reduce heterogeneity and always specify the measurement instrument, since a block of studies still omits this methodological detail; furthermore, although setting ‘pre-injury values’ as a goal is ideal in theory, in clinical reality, these prior records of the athlete are often unavailable, so prioritizing and standardizing protocols based on direct comparison with the uninjured limb could prove to be a much more pragmatic and reproducible criterion for day-to-day practice (Table 7).

The analysis of muscle strength measurement presented in Table 8 reflects an appropriate level of clinical objectification in the literature, consolidating this variable as a relevant element for guiding the return to sporting activity. It is positive to observe the diversity of instrumental tools employed—such as dynamometers, the Nordbord system, or the single-leg bridge test—and the definition of precise prognostic thresholds, such as the repetition bands proposed by Erickson et al., or the calculation by De Vos et al. associating a 4% increase in relapse risk per Newton of force lost. Likewise, the consensus of using symmetry with the healthy contralateral limb as a reference standard, typically accepting a maximum deficit of 10%, provides a functional criterion of great practical utility. To further consolidate this methodological framework, future publications should systematically detail the instrument and the exact angulation of the test, as a subgroup of studies in Table 8 currently omits this information; given that force production is directly dependent on the joint angle and contraction type, specifying these technical aspects would improve the standardization and clinical reproducibility of the findings in daily professional practice.

The analysis of imaging measurement using Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) presented in Table 9 highlights the significant diagnostic value of this tool while simultaneously fostering a relevant clinical debate regarding its predictive capacity for returning to activity. It is positive to observe how the evidence recognizes MRI as an essential instrument for characterizing “anatomical severity,” allowing for the precise identification of edema size, the specific muscles involved, and the involvement of the tendon, which provides an objective baseline for tissue healing. However, the data in Table 9 also introduces a valuable nuanced perspective by showing that structural imaging findings do not always correlate directly with functional readiness; for instance, as noted by Sanfilippo et al. and Otsuka et al., significant signs of injury or high-intensity signals can persist even when an athlete is clinically ready to compete. To further refine this methodological area, future research could focus on integrating these objective imaging findings with functional biomarkers, as this would help clarify the discrepancy between anatomical recovery and clinical discharge, ultimately leading to more personalized and safer return-to-sport protocols.

Discussion

The present systematic review aimed to analyze the tests to determine the return to activity after a hamstring injury. This wide range of variables described requires that a wide variety of articles be analyzed, in order to reach all the scientific literature published to date and thus be able to obtain conclusive results in this review. In the existing literature, it was observed that the variables of pain, flexibility and range of motion, functional tests and strength were the most studied and the most frequently used as criteria for returning to activity. At the same time, the variability in the existing tests and in the parameters applied to them hinders analyses and comparisons in this field of study.

General interpretation of results

The primary purpose of this systematic review was to establish defined criteria for clinical decision-making regarding the return to play (RTP) following a hamstring muscle injury. The findings suggest a paradigm shift: functional discharge has moved away from being a process based exclusively on biological timelines toward a multifactorial, hierarchical, and performance-based model, where biomechanical symmetry and dynamic functional capacity take precedence over isolated anatomical resolution. However, the interpretation of these findings must be contextualized within the significant heterogeneity of study types and the inconsistent methodological quality observed across the literature. By applying rigorous transparency and standardized reporting in our hierarchical model, we were able to filter and weight the criteria appropriately, ensuring that the most clinically reliable variables stand out despite the varying quality of the primary sources.

Our evidence synthesis is articulated through two complementary visual models. On one hand, Fig. 2 positions functional tests and flexibility/range of motion as the central pillars with the greatest specific weight. This hierarchy is based on the high internal validity of the analyzed studies (Tables 1 and 2, and 3); variables validated by high-quality systematic reviews (such as Van der Horst et al. in Table 3) and clinical trials with high scores on the PEDro scale (such as De Vos et al. and Jacobsen et al. in Table 1) possess greater centrality than the findings of observational studies. On the other hand, Fig. 3 represents the magnitude of these criteria according to their frequency of repetition in the literature. In this model, the variable “Walking without pain” stands out prominently as the most cited criterion, followed closely by “Functional Tests.” This recurrence underscores that, for the scientific community, basic pain-free functionality and the execution of specific sporting movements are the indispensable minimum requirements for initiating any advanced phase of RTP.

Regarding symmetry parameters, data from Tables 7 and 8 consolidate the need to restore physical capacity relative to the contralateral limb or pre-injury levels. Consensus points toward a maximum deficit threshold of 10% in flexibility and strength. The relevance of this data is critical: according to the calculations of De Vos et al. (Table 8), each Newton of force lost is associated with a 4% increase in the risk of relapse. However, Fig. 3 also introduces variables such as “Previous injury history,” whose repeated mention in the literature alerts clinicians to the need for a more conservative approach to exercise progression (Table 11) when the athlete has a history of injury, due to alterations in muscle architecture.

A central point of debate is the role of imaging tests. As observed in Fig. 3, the “Imaging test” occupies a peripheral and smaller position. Data extracted from Table 9 (Otsuka et al., Sanfilippo et al.) demonstrate a “diagnostic paradox”: the persistence of edema or high-intensity signals on MRI does not necessarily correlate with functional incapacity. Therefore, while initial diagnosis is key for staging anatomical severity, its use as a discharge criterion is questionable unless accompanied by a satisfactory functional battery (Table 10) that includes maximum speed sprints and changes of direction.

Finally, the integration of all information suggests that RTP success lies in a structured load progression (Table 11) that validates both tissue recovery and neuromuscular competence. Consistency between the analyzed methodological quality and the frequency of reported criteria allows for the conclusion that a safe discharge must prioritize the restoration of symmetrical strength, the absence of pain during walking, and, above all, the successful completion of functional tests that replicate the actual intensity of competition. This study provides an objective framework that allows clinicians to prioritize variables with the greatest scientific impact, optimizing athlete safety and reducing uncertainty in one of the injuries with the highest recurrence rates in professional sports.

Pain

The absence of pain in palpation has been established as a criterion to be taken into account for progression in the recovery process and to determine the return to activity [56, 59], having been defined as the most referenced criterion for return to activity [40, 55, 62]. In addition, it has demonstrated a sensitivity of 90% in the diagnosis of injury in the hamstring muscles [63]. Moreover, it has been determined that the presence of pain on palpation at the time of return to activity increases the risk of relapse by almost four times, compared to the absence of pain [36], thus it could be compatible with an incomplete recovery process [64].

On the other hand, the presence of pain on contraction, especially in resisted knee flexion with the hip and knee flexed, has been established as a criterion for returning to activity and related to the time of absence [63, 65]. Specifically, it was observed that there was an increase of 4.7 days between those athletes with pain on contraction and those who did not present it [65].

There are divergences regarding the definition of absence of pain, since, on the one hand, it has been determined that a pain less than or equal to 4 on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) is considered safe [51], favoring early exposure and increasing the stimulus to contraction [66]. On the other hand, other authors determine, as a criterion for returning to activity, the absence of pain upon contraction [67] and at maximum stretch [68].

These differences in the conception of the need for absence of pain as a criterion for returning to activity could be determined by the presence of pain in the athlete´s daily life [69]. A prevalence of hip pain of 97.2% was determined in hockey players and 81.9% of knee pain in soccer players [70]. The existence of good pain and bad pain has also been determined, with the latter being more frequent with the presence of fatigue and presenting worse adaptive responses in the athlete [71]. At the same time, and in relation to what was mentioned above, the largest number of muscle injuries occur when the muscle is fatigued [72–74].

It is worth highlighting the importance of the pain variable in predicting the time of return to activity, since the greater the intensity of the pain, the longer the time of absence [63, 75]. In this sense, Wangensteen et al. [65] specified that each point added to the VAS scale at the time of injury increases the time of return to activity by 1.6 days. Guilodo et al. [66] determined that a score higher than 6 on the VAS for more than 3 days requires more than 40 days to return to competitive activity. In this regard, although the absence of pain does not imply the absence of tissue involvement in imaging tests, the opposite case has been demonstrated in that the presence of pain has a strong relationship with the visualization of tissue damage, an aspect that is associated with an increase in the period of sick leave due to injury.

Flexibility and range of motion

A total of 13 articles included hamstring flexibility and range of motion as criteria to determine the return to activity. Some of these articles determined that failure to fully recover this parameter negatively influences the risk of a possible relapse [25, 27, 33, 36, 41, 44, 45, 47, 48, 52, 54, 59, 61]. In relation to this variable, two approaches can be found to determine full recovery. On the one hand, there are different studies in which the achievement of flexibility values similar to those before the injury is determined [36, 44, 45, 47, 52] and, on the other hand, obtaining values similar to those of the contralateral leg [25, 39, 41]. Despite these two well-defined approaches, there is no consensus on which of them to apply when returning to activity, although there are studies that established both criteria to determine complete recovery [27, 56, 61]. When this difference in flexibility between both legs is greater than 10%, it has been considered a risk factor for muscle injury [76].

This difference in determining complete recovery could be explained by the flexibility test selected, since the fact that a specific test for measuring flexibility in the hamstring muscles was not selected increased the relative error of the measurement, which may negatively influence the comparison between studies [77, 78]. Reliability has been determined in the existing literature in those tests that measure the flexibility of this muscle, both actively and passively, with the knee fully extended [79, 80] and with the hip flexed at 90° [79, 81]. The rest of the tests used must be analyzed in larger studies, where their sensitivity to differentiate between injury and non-injury values is determined, as well as their specificity to quantify the recovery process [39].

An increased risk of relapse has been shown when flexibility is incompletely recovered. In this regard, De Vos et al. [36] determined this risk with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.13 per degree deficit measured with the active knee extension test. When there is a sensation of instability during the active flexibility test, an increase of 1–2 weeks in the rehabilitation period is recommended [7, 39, 82]. On the other hand, it has been shown that a limitation in active knee flexibility [36] and hip abduction [83] increases the risk of muscle injury.

Strength

In the 13 articles that used the strength variable as a criterion for returning to activity, six different types of instruments were used. Despite this variability in the measurement instruments, it seems to be clear that the eccentric phase of the contraction of the hamstring muscles plays a more important role than the concentric phase in injuries of the hamstring muscle [17, 56, 84–87], with the largest number of muscular imbalances occurring in the eccentric phase [84]. In addition, it has been shown that an increase in eccentric strength decreases the risk of relapse [42, 85, 88]. When there is a decrease in eccentric strength, the capacity of the hamstring muscles to slow down the movement of the lower limb during the last phase of the running is reduced, which produces an increased risk of stretching and increases the risk of suffering an acute injury to said muscles [87, 88].

In relation to this last phase of the running, it has also been shown that a low ratio between hamstring muscles and quadriceps increases the risk of injury in this last phase of the running [87]. In professional American football players, it has been determined that a ratio of less than 0.50 increases the risk of injury in the hamstring muscles [89]; on the other hand, in professional soccer players, a ratio of 0.45–0.47 significantly increases the risk of relapse [84].

There is disagreement in whether determining the recovery of pre-injury values or comparing with the contralateral leg as a criterion for returning to activity. Thus, it has been shown that a difference in ipsilateral or bilateral strength would be a consequence after an injury of the hamstring muscles [90, 91]. It has been demonstrated that the presence of an imbalance in muscle strength between both limbs increases the risk of muscle injury [87, 92], establishing a limit of 8–15% [55, 84, 88, 93].

Functional test measurement

Although the use of functional tests, especially those involving sport-specific movements, has been considered the gold standard, the existing evidence is scarce [22]. One of the reasons that could explain this aspect is the lack of technical equipment in rehabilitation centers for their implementation, such as GPS systems and oxygen meters [62]. This fact increases the need to implement an algorithm that integrates the performance of sport-specific movements and neuromuscular control exercises as a criterion for return to activity [58, 94].

The parameter that was most widely studied, with respect to the performance of functional tests, is related to the ability to run at maximum speed [25, 44, 46, 52, 59], since it has a direct relationship with the ability to develop horizontal force and the power developed [95]. In that sense, the moment of return to activity has been determined when the difference in the “60-m sprint time” test with respect to pre-injury levels is less than 2% [44]. In addition, it has been established that those athletes who have to reach a running speed greater than 24 km/h are those who have a greater probability of muscle injury, which could lead to taking this value as the minimum value to be reached without pain in these subjects at the time of return to activity [96]. Thus, in order to avoid relapses, the results seem to indicate that adaptive work by the rehabilitation team is necessary to reduce physical stress when performing a sprint [97].

Another parameter studied is related to the execution of movements typical of the sport at maximum speed. In this parameter, not only the data obtained in the tests performed are important, but also the subjective opinion of the injured person and the technical staff [98].

Other measurements

Regarding imaging tests, some authors are in favor of their use as a criterion for returning to activity [9, 27, 38, 52, 60], with the distance between the proximal area of the injury and the ischial tuberosity being the most relevant measure [39]. However, other authors report that their use is not necessary, especially in mild or moderate injuries [44, 65, 99], and that this information can be obtained by performing other tests that are less expensive [6]. What has been demonstrated is that performing imaging tests at the beginning of the injury serves as a predictive value for recovery time [33, 40, 100]. In any case, hamstring injuries have been classified into different grades (grades I, II and III) depending on the amount of pain, disability and loss of movement, which has been considered as a reflection of the amount of muscle and tendon fibers affected (from grade I, with minimal damage, to grade III, with a complete rupture) [101].

Another important parameter to take into account is the history of previous injuries in the hamstring muscles, since it can be considered as an independent prognostic indicator [102]. Those athletes with a history of previous injuries in this musculature will have a greater predisposition to suffering future injuries, which increases the need for preventive work in these patients [63, 103]. Age has also been related to an increase in the incidence of muscle injury [104]. We can add that the combination of a history of previous injury together with a delay of more than one day in returning to walking without pain increases by 93% the chances of returning to activity in more than 3 weeks [102].

Clinical implications

It is widely established that the diagnosis of a hamstring injury is based on the patient’s previous injury history, a physical examination, and imaging tests [46]; however, there is still no consensus to determine the return to activity after this type of injury, which could generate controversy over which criteria to use, making it difficult to transfer this strategy to daily clinical practice [25]. This last aspect results in an excessively high relapse rate at present, which is coupled with a long rehabilitation period [87, 105]. It is important to highlight the importance of preventing relapses, since they are associated with a longer sick-leave period [5, 12, 20].

The absence of pain at the time of return to activity, as well as the recovery of the strength developed in said musculature together with the optimal performance of the functional tests, constitute the main criteria for a safe return to activity [23, 59]. The use of ultrasound imaging tests, better than the image obtained by magnetic resonance due to its difficult accessibility, serves as a support tool during the different phases of the rehabilitation period and not so much as a criterion for the return to activity [23].

According to the analyzed literature, preventive training of the hamstring muscles should focus on the eccentric phase [106, 107], which translates into an improvement in the work of said muscles during the sprint phase [3]. It is also important to achieve a correct activation of the hamstring muscles, in addition to reducing fatigue during muscle work and thus increasing their resistance [93, 108]. Finally, it is important to highlight the importance of flexibility work, since a correct length of the hamstring muscles will influence the correct development of muscle strength, in addition to providing an optimal angle in the knee, which favors the action of the muscles [109].

Strengths and weaknesses

The main strength of the present review is that this is the first study to bring together all the existing literature on return-to-activity criteria in lower limb injuries, providing the clinician with a broad overview of the existing battery of tests and the functionality of their use. This review provides the clinician with a wide range of variables to analyze, both objective and subjective, including the presence of the latest imaging technology, allowing them to choose the appropriate tool according to the resources available and those most convenient for their patient. Furthermore, the inclusion of a wide variety of studies, in addition to the breadth of languages analyzed, means that this work compiles all the existing literature to date.

However, this study has certain limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. For example, the lack of standardization when defining the return to activity in the literature makes it difficult to study, which is compounded by the lack and small number of quality studies. At the same time, although an attempt was made to cover practically all scientific articles published in the world, using the main databases and selecting documents in English, French, Portuguese, Italian and Spanish, there could be other potentially selectable studies that were not considered for the reasons mentioned above. In this sense, although the most important databases that collect the most relevant literature published in health sciences have been consulted, there could be others that could contain other studies that were not included in this review.

Furthermore, it is crucial to acknowledge the inherent heterogeneity in the study designs included in this review, which encompasses randomized controlled trials, observational longitudinal studies, and systematic reviews. This heterogeneity complicates the direct comparison of outcomes. Additionally, there is a notable inconsistency in the methodological quality across the analyzed literature. As demonstrated in our quality assessments, PEDro scores ranged from 3 to 6 out of 10, STROBE scores varied widely from 8 to 19 out of 22, and PRISMA scores showed significant dispersion from 2 to 17 out of 27. This variability underscores an urgent need for future research in this field to significantly improve overall methodology, transparency, and reporting standards. To establish highly reliable and universal return-to-play criteria, subsequent studies must prioritize the standardization of measurement instruments and explicitly detail functional testing protocols.

Conclusion

The systematic analysis of the current scientific literature demonstrates that clinical decision-making for the return to play following a hamstring muscle injury has shifted toward a multifactorial and criteria-based paradigm. A safe return is no longer determined by a single isolated marker or a rigid timeline, but rather by a synergistic combination of biological healing, clinical stability, and functional readiness. The evidence highlights the objective measurement of physical qualities as a primary pillar, establishing a clear consensus on the necessity of achieving biomechanical symmetry. Specifically, muscle strength and flexibility must be restored to match the uninjured limb, typically accepting a maximum deficit of 10% as a safety threshold to minimize the risk of relapse. While advanced diagnostic tools like Magnetic Resonance Imaging are essential for characterizing anatomical severity and tissue healing, findings suggest that imaging results must be integrated with functional performance, as structural signals of injury can often persist even when an athlete is clinically ready for competition.

The transition to high-level play is increasingly guided by dynamic, sport-specific criteria that bridge the gap between clinical rehabilitation and competitive demands. Successful discharge requires the completion of functional batteries that include maximum-speed sprints, acceleration-deceleration patterns, and sport-specific movements performed without pain or instability. This progression is underpinned by structured exercise protocols that prioritize a gradual increase in mechanical load and respect for biological healing times. Despite these advances, the literature reveals a need for greater standardization in how pain thresholds and specific measurement instruments are reported to ensure more reproducible decision-making. In conclusion, a successful return to sport following a hamstring injury requires a transition from time-based protocols to a rigorous, criteria-based progression that validates both objective physical metrics and the athlete’s ability to meet the high-intensity functional demands of their specific sport.
