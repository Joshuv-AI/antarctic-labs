Seasonal variation in ammonia among euthymic women with Bipolar I Disorder treated with valproic acid in Turkey: a prospective study
Authors: Ipek Özönder Ünal, Habib Erensoy, Tonguc Demir Berkol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundValproic acid (VPA)–induced hyperammonemia (VPA-IH) is a common metabolic complication in Bipolar I Disorder (BD-I). Although VPA creates a state of metabolic vulnerability, the influence of seasonal environmental stressors on ammonia homeostasis remains unexplored.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal study followed 112 euthymic female patients with BD-I receiving stable VPA therapy over one year. Plasma ammonia levels and mood symptoms (YMRS, HAM-D) were assessed across four seasons: winter (January), spring (April), summer (July), and autumn (October).ResultsRepeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of season on plasma ammonia levels (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.131). Ammonia concentrations peaked during summer (47.41 ± 8.48 µg/dL) and were significantly higher than winter (43.20 ± 8.20 µg/dL, p < 0.001), spring (44.18 ± 7.95 µg/dL, p < 0.001), and autumn (45.10 ± 6.99 µg/dL, p = 0.002). Autumn ammonia levels also remained modestly but significantly higher than winter (p = 0.006). Mood scores remained within the euthymic range across all seasons, with no significant seasonal variation. A modest but significant positive correlation between YMRS scores and ammonia levels was observed exclusively during summer (r = 0.245, p = 0.009).ConclusionsPlasma ammonia levels in VPA-treated patients exhibit a distinct seasonal pattern, peaking during summer despite sustained euthymia. These findings support a seasonally modulated “multi-hit” model of VPA-associated hyperammonemia and highlight the importance of season-informed metabolic monitoring and hydration strategies during warmer months.

Introduction

Valproic acid (VPA), a branched-chain fatty acid, remains a cornerstone of contemporary psychopharmacology and is widely prescribed as a first-line mood stabilizer for both the acute and maintenance treatment of Bipolar I Disorder [1]. Despite its well-established clinical efficacy, the therapeutic use of VPA is frequently complicated by significant metabolic adverse effects, most notably valproate-induced hyperammonemia (VPA-IH). Elevated ammonia levels have been reported in up to 50% of patients receiving VPA therapy [1], representing a clinically relevant complication that may occur even in the absence of hepatic dysfunction and at therapeutic serum VPA concentrations [2–3]. Although VPA-IH is often asymptomatic, it has been associated with a broad spectrum of neurotoxic manifestations, ranging from subtle cognitive slowing and lethargy to, in rare cases, severe and potentially life-threatening hyperammonemic encephalopathy [2, 4].

The pathophysiology of VPA-IH does not reflect simple hepatotoxicity but rather represents a complex, multisystem disruption of ammonia homeostasis [3]. VPA and its metabolites impair ammonia metabolism through several converging mechanisms. First, VPA directly and indirectly inhibits the hepatic urea cycle by reducing the activity of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I), the rate-limiting enzyme of the cycle [5]. This inhibition is further amplified by reduced synthesis of N-acetylglutamate (NAG), an essential allosteric activator of CPS-I [6]. Second, chronic VPA exposure is a well-recognized cause of systemic carnitine depletion, as VPA interferes with carnitine transport and increases renal excretion [7]. Carnitine deficiency shifts VPA metabolism away from mitochondrial β-oxidation toward alternative toxic pathways, thereby exacerbating urea cycle dysfunction [8]. Finally, VPA exerts extrahepatic effects by stimulating renal ammoniagenesis, further contributing to systemic ammonia accumulation [3].

A persistent clinical challenge is the well-documented dissociation between serum VPA levels, plasma ammonia concentrations, and the presence or severity of clinical symptoms [9]. This lack of correlation strongly suggests that VPA-IH should not be conceptualized as a simple dose-dependent pharmacological adverse effect. Instead, it supports a “multi-hit” model in which VPA induces a baseline state of metabolic vulnerability, rendering ammonia homeostasis highly susceptible to additional endogenous or environmental modifiers [9–11]. Identifying such modulatory factors is essential for improving risk stratification, monitoring strategies, and patient safety in routine clinical practice.

Seasonality represents a particularly powerful yet underexplored potential modulator in this context. Bipolar disorder is intrinsically linked to chronobiology, with a “seasonal pattern” formally recognized as a diagnostic specifier [12]. Numerous studies have demonstrated seasonal clustering of mood episodes, most consistently showing a peak in manic episodes during the spring months [13, 14]. Importantly, this seasonal rhythmicity extends beyond clinical symptomatology to underlying metabolic processes. In a recent targeted metabolomics study, Liu et al. (2023) identified significant seasonal alterations in arginine biosynthesis, D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism in patients with bipolar disorder during the spring [13]. These findings are particularly salient, as they implicate metabolic pathways directly involved in ammonia production and clearance, precisely the systems targeted and disrupted by VPA.

In addition to intrinsic metabolic rhythms, seasonal environmental factors provide plausible mechanistic pathways for ammonia variability. Ambient temperature, physical activity, and hydration status all exhibit marked seasonal variation and are known modulators of ammonia homeostasis [15, 16]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that heat stress significantly increases plasma and skeletal muscle ammonia accumulation [15, 16]. Physical activity, which generally increases during warmer months, is a major contributor to muscle-derived ammonia production [17]. Furthermore, the increased risk of dehydration during summer months can impair both hepatic and renal clearance mechanisms, further compromising ammonia detoxification in the context of a VPA-inhibited urea cycle [18].

The present study specifically focused on a female cohort, a deliberate and biologically informed design choice. Sex is a fundamental determinant of ammonia metabolism, with established sex-based differences in baseline renal ammonia handling that are at least partially mediated by sex hormones [19]. Moreover, the female endocrine milieu and its cyclical fluctuations may influence VPA pharmacokinetics and hepatic metabolism, creating a distinct physiological context that warrants targeted investigation [20].

To date, no study has systematically examined seasonal variation in ammonia levels among patients receiving VPA therapy. Given the multisystem metabolic vulnerability induced by VPA and the convergence of seasonally patterned physiological stressors; including heat exposure, physical activity, hydration status, and intrinsic metabolic rhythms; we hypothesized that plasma ammonia levels would exhibit significant seasonal variability. Accordingly, the present study aimed to conduct the first comprehensive evaluation of ammonia levels across all four seasons in a cohort of euthymic female patients with Bipolar I Disorder receiving stable VPA treatment.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective, longitudinal observational study was conducted between January 2 and October 31, 2024, at Bakirkoy Mazhar Osman Mental and Nervous Diseases Hospital Istanbul, Bakirkoy in Istanbul, Turkey. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Üsküdar University Ethics Committee (September 29, 2020; Decision No: 61351342/2020 − 452). All study procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study participants

A total of 230 patients with a registered diagnosis of Bipolar I Disorder (F31) were initially identified from the outpatient clinics of Bakirkoy Mazhar Osman Mental and Nervous Diseases Hospital and screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: (1) female sex; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) literacy, defined as the ability to read and write fluently in Turkish to ensure independent comprehension of the study protocols and the valid provision of written informed consent; (4) a confirmed DSM-5 diagnosis of Bipolar I Disorder; (5) stable VPA treatment for a minimum of one year with the requirement that this stable daily dose remained strictly unchanged throughout the entire 12-month prospective follow-up period; (6) confirmed euthymia at baseline, defined as a Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) score ≤ 5 and a 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) score ≤ 7; and (7) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) failure to meet the one-year clinical remission criterion; (2) presence of systemic conditions that could influence metabolic or inflammatory status, including diabetes mellitus, hepatic or renal insufficiency, hypertension, active infection, or acute or chronic immuno-inflammatory diseases; (3) obesity (body mass index [BMI] > 29.9 kg/m²) or underweight status (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²); (4) concurrent use of lithium, anti-inflammatory agents, or immunosuppressive medications; (5) conditions precluding valid informed consent, such as intellectual disability (IQ < 70), dementia, or other organic mental disorders; and (6) pregnancy or lactation.

Of the 230 patients screened, 97 were excluded during the eligibility assessment. The primary reasons for exclusion were failure to meet the one-year remission criterion (n = 35), concurrent lithium treatment (n = 23), presence of a confounding systemic illness or obesity (n = 26), refusal to participate (n = 8), and other exclusion criteria, including insufficient duration of VPA treatment or cognitive impairment (n = 5). Ultimately, 133 eligible participants were enrolled at baseline (T1; January). During the 12-month follow-up period, 21 participants (15.8%) discontinued the study due to relocation, loss to follow-up, or withdrawal of consent. The final analytic sample therefore consisted of 112 participants who completed all four seasonal assessments and were included in the repeated-measures analyses (Fig. 1).

Procedures and assessments

Participants were evaluated at four predefined time points corresponding to the four meteorological seasons: January (Winter), April (Spring), July (Summer), and October (Autumn). At each visit, participants underwent concurrent clinical assessments and laboratory evaluations.

Clinical and sociodemographic data collection

At baseline, participants completed a comprehensive Sociodemographic and Clinical Data Form developed by the research team. Sociodemographic variables included age, educational attainment, marital status, and employment status. Clinical variables encompassed age at illness onset, illness duration, total number of lifetime manic, hypomanic, mixed, and depressive episodes, history of psychiatric hospitalizations, suicide attempts, and prior treatment history, including electroconvulsive therapy. Lifestyle factors, including current and past tobacco use (quantified in pack-years), alcohol consumption, and substance use, were systematically recorded. Relevant baseline laboratory data (e.g., hemogram, fasting glucose, thyroid-stimulating hormone, lipid profile) were extracted from medical records.

Mood assessment

Mood status was assessed at each seasonal visit to ensure continued euthymia throughout the study period. Manic symptoms were evaluated using the 11-item clinician-rated Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS), employing the validated Turkish version developed by Karadağ et al. [21, 22]. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), utilizing the validated Turkish version by Akdemir et al. [23, 24].

Laboratory analysis

At each seasonal assessment, fasting venous blood samples were obtained following a minimum 8-hour fast. Given the well-recognized pre-analytical instability of ammonia, blood samples were collected into pre-chilled EDTA tubes, immediately placed on ice, and centrifuged within 30 min of collection [25]. Plasma ammonia concentrations were analyzed promptly following separation. Additional serum samples were processed for alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), urea, creatinine, and trough VPA concentrations using standardized automated laboratory methods.

Statistical analysis

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1 to estimate the required sample size for the primary outcome. Assuming a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with four time points (seasons), a conservative medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power of 90% (1 − β = 0.90), and a moderate correlation among repeated measures (r = 0.5), the minimum required sample size was calculated as n = 38. Accordingly, the final sample of 112 participants was considered more than sufficient to detect small-to-moderate seasonal effects.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for baseline demographic and clinical variables and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or frequency, as appropriate. Normality of continuous variables, including plasma ammonia, ALT, AST, and VPA levels at each time point, was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. As distributions approximated normality, parametric analyses were applied.

Seasonal differences in plasma ammonia levels were examined using a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA. The assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test; when violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds.

As an exploratory secondary analysis, associations between subclinical mood symptoms and laboratory parameters were examined. Given the restricted range and non-normal distribution of subthreshold mood scores (e.g., YMRS ≤ 5), Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to assess relationships between YMRS and HAM-D scores and plasma ammonia, VPA, ALT, and AST levels at each seasonal time point. To control for multiple testing inflation of Type I error across these exploratory evaluations, a Bonferroni correction was applied, adjusting the local alpha significance threshold accordingly. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 (or the adjusted Bonferroni threshold for exploratory correlations) was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 112 female patients with Bipolar I Disorder completed all four seasonal assessments and were included in the final analyses. The mean age of the sample was 31.70 ± 6.63 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 24.36 ± 2.95 kg/m². Participants were receiving stable valproic acid (VPA) treatment, with a mean daily dose of 1089.28 ± 338.67 mg that remained completely identical across all four sequential seasonal assessments. The mean serum trough VPA concentration was 72.29 ± 7.91 µg/mL. Liver enzyme levels remained within normal reference ranges throughout the study period (ALT: 26.16 ± 4.13 U/L; AST: 21.47 ± 4.02 U/L). Clinical stability and seasonal mood scores

All participants maintained clinical euthymia throughout the longitudinal follow-up. Repeated assessments demonstrated that YMRS and HAM-D scores remained consistently within the euthymic range across all four seasonal time points. Mean YMRS scores were 2.29 ± 0.92 in winter, 2.04 ± 0.82 in spring, 2.21 ± 0.99 in summer, and 2.49 ± 1.32 in autumn. Mean HAM-D scores were 4.01 ± 1.83 in winter, 3.93 ± 1.16 in spring, 4.01 ± 1.26 in summer, and 4.13 ± 1.47 in autumn. No statistically significant seasonal variation was observed for either YMRS or HAM-D scores (p > 0.05 for all comparisons), confirming sustained mood stability during the observation period (Table 1).

Seasonal variation in plasma ammonia levels

A one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance revealed a significant main effect of season on plasma ammonia concentrations (F = 16.75, df = 2.85, 316.11, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.131).

Plasma ammonia levels demonstrated a clear seasonal pattern, with the highest mean concentration observed during summer (47.41 ± 8.48 µg/dL), followed by autumn (45.10 ± 6.99 µg/dL), spring (44.18 ± 7.95 µg/dL), and winter (43.20 ± 8.20 µg/dL) (Table 2).

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation analyses examining the relationship between subclinical manic symptoms and plasma ammonia levels revealed a season-specific association. A modest but statistically significant positive correlation between YMRS scores and plasma ammonia concentrations was observed during summer (r = 0.245, p = 0.009), which retained its significance/statistical trend after Bonferroni adjustment for multiple exploratory comparisons. In contrast, no significant correlations were identified during winter (r = 0.063, p = 0.507), spring (r = 0.144, p = 0.129), or autumn (r = − 0.040, p = 0.675) (Table 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first longitudinal investigation to demonstrate significant seasonal fluctuations in plasma ammonia levels among euthymic female patients receiving stable valproic acid (VPA) therapy. Our findings provide empirical support for the proposed “multi-hit” model of valproate-induced hyperammonemia [9-11], indicating that VPA-related metabolic vulnerability is not static butmay be dynamically modulated by seasonal environmental and biological stressors. The primary observation, a pronounced peak in plasma ammonia concentrations during July (summer) compared with January (winter) (p < 0.001), suggests a potential link to seasonally patterned modifiers, highlighting ambient temperature and hydration status as highly plausible environmental pathways that warrant further, direct investigation in this clinically stable population.

The summer peak in plasma ammonia levels observed in this study is consistent with experimental and physiological evidence demonstrating that environmental heat stress increases systemic ammonia accumulation [15, 16]. Both laboratory and human studies have shown that hyperthermia promotes muscle-derived ammonia production while simultaneously impairing hepatic clearance mechanisms [15, 16]. In patients receiving VPA, an agent that already compromises ammonia detoxification through inhibition of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I) and depletion of systemic carnitine reserves, the additive burden of elevated ambient temperatures may overwhelm compensatory metabolic pathways [5, 7]. In parallel, the increased risk of subclinical dehydration during summer months is likely to further impair renal ammonia clearance, thereby amplifying circulating ammonia concentrations [18].

Notably, although ammonia levels began to rise during the spring, the most marked increase occurred with the transition into summer. This temporal pattern is clinically meaningful, as it follows the well-documented spring peak in manic episodes in bipolar disorder [13]. Emerging metabolomic evidence suggests that bipolar patients experience seasonal alterations in nitrogen, glutamine, and arginine metabolism during spring, potentially creating a “priming” effect that renders the urea cycle particularly vulnerable to subsequent physiological stressors [13]. Within this framework, summer heat exposure may act not as an isolated trigger but as a final stressor superimposed on an already destabilized metabolic system.

The statistically significant difference between autumn (October) and winter (January) ammonia levels (p = 0.006) further suggests that the metabolic consequences of summer exposure may persist beyond the peak season. This residual elevation may reflect delayed recovery of urea cycle efficiency or cumulative inhibition of N-acetylglutamate (NAG) synthesis secondary to chronic VPA exposure [6]. Importantly, the maintenance of euthymia across all seasons represents a key methodological strength, allowing the observed biochemical variability to be interpreted independently of acute mood episodes or overt psychomotor changes.

The statistically significant elevation of ammonia levels in autumn (October) compared to winter (January) (p = 0.006) further suggests a prolonged ‘metabolic lag’ in the recovery of urea cycle efficiency. While plasma ammonia concentrations began to decline following the summer zenith, they remained modestly elevated through October, failing to return to the baseline winter levels. This delayed normalization may reflect a residual metabolic burden on mitochondrial function. We propose that the cumulative inhibition of N-acetylglutamate synthesis and the gradual depletion of systemic carnitine reserves, both exacerbated by summer thermal stress, do not recover immediately upon the onset of cooler temperatures. This observed ‘slow recovery’ pattern indicates that the VPA-inhibited urea cycle undergoes a period of sustained metabolic fragility that persists well into the post-summer months, highlighting the need for continued clinical vigilance even after the peak environmental heat has subsided.

Beyond direct environmental stressors, the exclusive summer association between subclinical manic symptoms and plasma ammonia levels (r = 0.245, p = 0.009) points toward a complex interaction with the patients’ underlying chronobiology. Although all participants remained strictly within the predefined euthymic range, the extended summer photoperiod is a well-known driver of subclinical psychomotor activation, reduced sleep duration, and circadian instability in Bipolar I Disorder [13]. Within the “multi-hit” framework, we hypothesize that these subtle chronobiological mood shifts may catalyze a subthreshold increase in physical activity and muscle turnover, thereby enhancing muscle-derived ammoniagenesis and altering nitrogen balance. Consequently, the summer period may represent a unique window of heightened metabolic vulnerability during which external heat stress, potential dehydration, and endogenous chronobiological loads converge to maximize the functional burden on the already compromised, VPA-inhibited urea cycle.

The exclusive focus on a female cohort addresses an important gap in the literature, as sex-related differences in ammonia metabolism, particularly in renal handling are increasingly recognized and are thought to be modulated by sex hormones [19]. The relatively stable ammonia levels observed across seasons, remaining largely within the upper-normal range, reinforce the notion that VPA-induced hyperammonemia is often a subclinical and asymptomatic phenomenon in clinically stable patients. However, the distinct seasonal “hit” identified in this study suggests that even in the absence of clinical symptoms or laboratory abnormalities at baseline, ammonia levels may rise statistically significantly during summer months. It is critical to underscore that this observed variation represents a subclinical, physiological fluctuation, as the mean values remained within normal limits and the patients experienced no overt clinical symptoms or functional impairment.

From a clinical perspective, these findings do not warrant an immediate modification of standard clinical guidelines, but they provide an important signal that plasma ammonia concentrations may fluctuate across seasons. Rather than establishing direct clinical implications, these results highlight the necessity for larger, multicenter studies to determine the exact extent and potential causal factors driving this pattern. Seasonal risk stratification, increased vigilance during summer may represent future avenues for study, even in euthymic patients with previously stable liver function tests and therapeutic VPA levels. Future studies incorporating hydration status, objective physical activity measures, and circadian rhythm markers may further clarify the relative contribution of these interacting factors and help refine individualized monitoring and prevention strategies.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its prospective longitudinal design and adequate statistical power, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although the within-subject repeated-measures design across four seasons strengthens inference regarding seasonal variation, the study was conducted at a single geographic location. Regional climate characteristics, particularly ambient temperature and humidity, may limit the generalizability of our findings to other latitudes or climatic zones. Accordingly, multicenter studies across diverse geographic regions are warranted to validate the observed seasonal pattern.

Second, while we carefully controlled for major medical and psychiatric confounders and ensured sustained euthymia throughout the follow-up period, we did not directly assess hydration status, physical activity levels, dietary protein intake, or circadian rhythm parameters. These factors are well-established modulators of ammonia metabolism and may have contributed to inter-individual variability, particularly during summer months. Future studies incorporating objective measures such as bioimpedance-based hydration assessment, wearable activity monitoring, dietary records, and actigraphy would allow a more granular mechanistic understanding of seasonal ammonia fluctuations.

Third, the exclusive focus on female patients, while a deliberate strength in terms of biological homogeneity, limits extrapolation of the findings to male populations. Given established sex differences in renal ammonia handling and valproate pharmacokinetics [19], parallel investigations in male cohorts are needed to determine whether similar seasonal vulnerability patterns exist.

Fourth, although all participants were clinically euthymic, we did not control for the specific timing of the menstrual cycle. Fluctuations in sex hormones across the menstrual cycle may influence both mood-related parameters and valproate pharmacokinetics, potentially contributing to intra-individual variability in ammonia levels [20]. Future studies incorporating cycle phase or hormonal measurements would help clarify this interaction.

Fifth, ammonia levels were measured at discrete seasonal time points rather than continuously. While this approach reflects routine clinical practice and captures broad seasonal trends, it may not fully account for short-term fluctuations related to acute heat exposure, transient dehydration, or brief changes in physical activity. More frequent sampling or continuous monitoring strategies could better delineate temporal dynamics.

Sixth, a key methodological limitation of this prospective study is the management of missing data through a complete-case analysis approach. Out of the initially enrolled sample, 20 participants were dropped from the final analysis due to various reasons, including medication non-adherence, subsequent dosage titrations, or missed follow-up appointments. By restricting our final analysis strictly to patients with complete assessments across all four seasonal time points (n = 112), we cannot rule out the introduction of potential selection or attrition biases. To mitigate this concern, we conducted a baseline comparison analysis between completers and dropouts; no statistically significant differences were observed regarding age, sex distribution, baseline VPA dosage, or baseline ammonia levels (p > 0.05). Nonetheless, future multi-center studies utilizing imputation models are required to ensure greater statistical generalizability.

Finally, we measured total plasma ammonia rather than the un-ionized ammonia fraction (NH₃), which is considered the more neurotoxic form [26]. Although total ammonia and NH₃ are highly correlated in clinical settings, future studies incorporating direct NH₃ measurement or related biomarkers may further refine the biological interpretation of hyperammonemia risk.

Future research integrating environmental, behavioral, hormonal, and metabolomic data may facilitate the development of season-informed monitoring strategies for patients receiving valproate. Prospective interventional studies examining whether targeted preventive measures, such as hydration optimization, carnitine supplementation, or seasonal dose adjustments, can mitigate summer-related ammonia elevations may have important clinical implications. Ultimately, a more nuanced understanding of seasonal metabolic vulnerability may support personalized risk assessment and enhance the long-term safety of valproate therapy in bipolar disorder.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study have several important clinical implications for the long-term management of patients with Bipolar I Disorder receiving valproic acid (VPA) therapy. First, the demonstration of significant seasonal variation in plasma ammonia levels, despite sustained euthymia and therapeutic VPA concentrations, suggests that hyperammonemia risk is dynamic rather than static. Clinicians should therefore be aware that normal baseline ammonia levels do not preclude subsequent seasonal elevations, particularly during summer months.

Second, the identification of a summer peak in ammonia levels signals a prospective area for further research rather than justifying an immediate change in routine protocols. Periodic ammonia assessment during warmer months represents a plausible strategy that should be evaluated in future trials to determine its actual clinical utility and cost-effectiveness before routine implementation can be recommended, even in clinically stable patients without overt symptoms of hyperammonemia or hepatic dysfunction. Future exploratory research could specifically evaluate whether this seasonal monitoring strategy yields any tangible clinical value for subgroups with elevated metabolic risk, such as those on higher VPA doses, patients with long-term treatment exposure, prior episodes of VPA-induced hyperammonemia, or subtle increases in psychomotor activity.

Third, the observed association between subclinical manic symptoms and ammonia levels during summer underscores the importance of careful clinical monitoring beyond categorical mood episode thresholds. Even mild elevations in psychomotor activation or circadian disruption, common during seasonal transitions, may contribute to metabolic stress in vulnerable patients. Integrating routine mood symptom monitoring with laboratory surveillance may therefore improve early detection of metabolic risk.

Finally, these findings suggest that while preventative counseling (such as discussing hydration, heat exposure, and physical activity levels during summer) is a safe and intuitive supportive measure, its definitive efficacy in mitigating subclinical hyperammonemia remains unproven. In selected cases, consideration of adjunctive strategies, such as carnitine supplementation or closer laboratory follow-up, could be considered on a strictly individualized basis; however, we must heavily stress that well-designed, prospective interventional studies are fundamentally required to validate these approaches and establish formal, evidence-based recommendations.

Taken together, a seasonally tailored, proactive approach to monitoring and patient education may enhance the safety of VPA therapy and reduce the risk of subclinical or overt hyperammonemia, even in well-controlled patients with Bipolar I Disorder.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this prospective longitudinal study demonstrates that plasma ammonia levels exhibit significant seasonal variation in euthymic female patients with Bipolar I Disorder receiving stable valproic acid therapy. Despite sustained clinical stability and therapeutic valproate concentrations, ammonia levels were highest during the summer months, supporting the concept that valproate-induced metabolic vulnerability is dynamically modulated by seasonal environmental and biological factors.

These findings provide empirical support for a “multi-hit” model of valproate-associated hyperammonemia, in which pharmacological disruption of ammonia homeostasis may interact with seasonally patterned stressors such as ambient temperature, hydration status, physical activity, and subtle chronobiological mood fluctuations. The observed association between subclinical manic symptoms and ammonia levels during summer further underscores the relevance of metabolic-mood interactions, even in the absence of overt affective episodes.

From a clinical perspective, the results highlight that we should further investigate season-informed monitoring and preventive strategies in order to prevent the potentially preventable clinical or metabolic destabilization of our patients receiving long-term valproate therapy. Incorporating seasonal risk awareness into routine care may improve early detection of metabolic changes and enhance the long-term safety of valproate treatment in bipolar disorder. Consequently, future studies integrating environmental, behavioral, and metabolomic measures across diverse populations and climates are warranted to refine individualized monitoring approaches and to inform targeted interventions aimed at reducing seasonal metabolic risk.
