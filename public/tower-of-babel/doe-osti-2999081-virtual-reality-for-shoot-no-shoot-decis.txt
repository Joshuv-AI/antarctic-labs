EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Virtual reality (VR) can materially improve “shoot / no-shoot” (SNS) training by giving officers
realistic, repeatable practice making high-stakes decisions under pressure. Traditional tools—
live-fire ranges and video simulators—build basics, but they cannot adapt to each officer in real
time or fully mirror the complexity of the field. VR closes that gap by creating immersive
scenarios that are safer, more flexible, easier to scale across units, and able to capture objective
performance data.
SNS decisions are not just about marksmanship; they rely on perception, judgment, memory, and
the ability to hold fire when a threat is uncertain. Effective training therefore needs realism,
decision complexity, and branching outcomes that reflect the true consequences of choices.
These elements strengthen recognition of hostile intent while reducing false positives and
building the self-control required in ambiguous situations.
VR brings specific advantages: dynamic environments, full-body interaction, and the ability to
measure performance with precision—enabling targeted feedback and better transfer of learning
to the street. At the same time, responsible deployment must address scenario quality (credible
environments and behaviors), lawful decision models, and user wellbeing (appropriate stress
levels, comfort, and safety).
Sandia’s VIPER Lab is positioned to lead this work. The team combines human-performance
science, AI/ML, and VR/AR development with a deep equipment bench (e.g., omnidirectional
treadmill, eye-tracking, haptics, multiple HMDs). This ecosystem supports building and
validating next-generation SNS training that is immersive, measurable, and trustworthy.
Bottom line: Investment in VR-enabled SNS training that blends evidence-based design with
careful validation and legal safeguards is expected to pay off in safer, more consistent decision-
making and improved community trust, delivered through training that is practical to deploy at
scale.

1. INTRODUCTION
The ability to make accurate, timely, and appropriate shoot/no-shoot (SNS) decisions is a
cornerstone of modern law enforcement. These decisions often occur in high-stakes
environments characterized by uncertainty, time pressure, and stress. Mistakes can result in
tragic consequences for civilians, officers, and communities. Traditional SNS training methods,
including live-fire ranges, judgment simulators, and role-playing scenarios, have provided
foundational skill development, but questions remain regarding their ecological validity,
adaptability, and transferability to real-world contexts. With the rapid maturation of virtual
reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) technologies, a new frontier for immersive, scalable,
and data-rich SNS training has emerged. This literature review synthesizes the last 20 years of
empirical and conceptual research on SNS decision-making, with a specific focus on how VR
can enhance learning, decision accuracy, and public safety. VR provides additional advantages
that are difficult to achieve in real-world training scenarios, including unlimited variability
within simulated environments, increased opportunities for experience via easily repeatable
scenarios and improved enjoyment and motivation (Makransky et al., 2019a). The existing
technology space for VR deployment allows for expansion beyond the traditional visual
experience to the inclusion of auditory, haptic, kinesthetic, and olfactory simulation (Xie et al.,
2021). It includes suggestions for future development and research, along with technical
challenges in creating realistic SNS scenarios.

1.1. Foundations In Shoot/No-Shoot Training
SNS training aims to improve officers' ability to discriminate between threats and non-threats
and to act in accordance with legal and departmental standards. Historically, SNS training
evolved from live-fire drills and judgmental use-of-force simulators such as FATS (Firearms
Training System) and Multiple Interactive Learning Objectives (MILO), which use pre-recorded
video scenarios to assess and develop threat recognition skills (Lewinski et al., 2015).
MILO is a widely used interactive training system that presents officers with video-based
scenarios projected onto a large screen. Officers respond to situations using modified firearms
that interface with the system, allowing for real-time decision-making practice. The MILO
system tracks weapon aim, trigger pulls, and timing while presenting branching scenarios based
on officer actions. Unlike static target practice, MILO scenarios feature live actors in realistic
situations - domestic disputes, traffic stops, armed encounters - that can escalate or de-escalate
based on officer responses. However, MILO's limitations include predetermined branching paths,
limited environmental interaction, and inability to modify scenarios in real-time based on
individual performance patterns. MILO has recently been advanced to utilize VR, likely
improving upon some of its earlier limitations.
While effective in improving basic procedural competence, such systems are limited in their
interactivity, realism, and adaptability (Engel et al., 2020). Reality-based training (e.g., shoot
house) is expensive and operationally demanding. VR delivers a cost-effective, highly adaptable
alternative that can be implemented rapidly and scaled across units. Key characteristics of
effective SNS scenarios include:
 • Realism and Contextual Fidelity: Training must replicate environmental, emotional, and
 cognitive pressures officers face in the field (Grossman & Christensen, 2008).
 • Decision Complexity: Scenarios must present ambiguous stimuli requiring rapid
 interpretation (Nieuwenhuys & Oudejans, 2011).

 5

 • Branching Outcomes: Real-time consequences for officer decisions enhance engagement
 and support experiential learning (Blumberg et al., 2019).
Recent research emphasizes that SNS is not merely a motor skill but a perceptual-cognitive
process that integrates attention, working memory, emotion regulation, and inhibitory control
(Biggs et al., 2022). A perceptual-cognitive process integrates sensory input (what officers see,
hear, feel) with cognitive interpretation, memory retrieval, and decision-making. Rather than
treating shooting decisions as simple stimulus-response patterns, research shows SNS decisions
involve:
 • Pattern recognition: Quickly identifying threat-relevant cues (hand movements toward
 waistband, aggressive posturing, weapon-like objects) among environmental noise
 • Contextual interpretation: Understanding situational factors (lighting conditions, crowd
 dynamics, suspect behavior history) that influence decision making
 • Memory integration: Comparing current observations to stored experiences and training
 scenarios to predict likely outcomes
 • Inhibitory control: Suppressing the impulse to shoot when threat cues are ambiguous or
 when non-lethal alternatives remain viable
 • Working memory management: Maintaining awareness of multiple factors (backup
 officer locations, civilian positions, legal justification standards) while processing
 immediate threats
This process occurs in milliseconds but involves sophisticated neural networks spanning visual
cortex, prefrontal regions responsible for executive control, limbic areas processing emotional
significance, and motor regions preparing potential responses.

SNS performance encompasses two interrelated but distinct components:
  • Execution Training: Emphasizes speed, accuracy, and mechanics of firing or restraint

 • Decision Training: Focuses on perceptual judgment, emotional control, and situational
 analysis
Training programs that conflate the two may undermine learning. For example, frequent firing in
ambiguous scenarios may inadvertently train officers to favor force. To counter this:
 • Scenarios should include non-shootable threats, promoting inhibitory control

 • Use-of-force should incur virtual consequences (e.g., simulated legal review, civilian
 casualties)

1.2. Transfer of Training and Decision Accuracy
The ultimate goal of SNS training is transfer—the ability of officers to apply what they learned
in training to real-world encounters. Transfer is influenced by the similarity between the training
environment and the operational context, the retention of decision heuristics, and the integration
of affective and physiological states into memory traces (Schmidt & Bjork, 1992).

 6

Studies show that:
 • High-fidelity scenarios improve recognition of hostile intent and reduce false positives
       (Olma et al., 2024).
 • Repeated exposure to SNS dilemmas under stress increases inhibitory control and
 improves discrimination accuracy (Johnson et al., 2014).
 • Mixed-modality training (e.g., verbal, visual, kinesthetic) promotes better long-term
 retention (Macedonia, 2019).
However, traditional training environments often fail to replicate the cognitive load and affective
arousal present in actual use-of-force situations, thereby limiting transfer.
Cognitive load refers to the amount of mental effort and working memory resources required to
process information and make decisions. In law enforcement contexts, high cognitive load occurs
when officers must simultaneously process multiple information streams (visual threats, radio
communications, environmental hazards, legal considerations) while under time pressure. When
cognitive load exceeds capacity, decision quality deteriorates, tunnel vision occurs, and officers
may revert to over-learned responses rather than adaptive problem-solving.
Affective arousal describes the physiological and emotional activation state of an individual,
typically measured through heart rate, breathing, stress hormones, and subjective stress reports.
Moderate arousal enhances performance (the Yerkes-Dodson law), but excessive arousal impairs
fine motor skills, narrows attention, and disrupts complex reasoning. Real-world police
encounters often generate high affective arousal through life-threat perception, time pressure,
and high-stakes consequences. Training must authentically replicate this arousal to build stress
inoculation and maintain performance under pressure.

1.3. The Emergence of VR/AR in Use-of-Force Training
VR and AR technologies offer solutions to the limitations of conventional training by enabling
highly immersive, repeatable, and modifiable environments. Unlike video-based simulators, VR
allows for dynamic interactions, spatial exploration, and sensory engagement that closely mirror
field conditions (Kleygrewe et al., 2023).
Specific applications of VR in law enforcement/physical security training could include:
 • De-escalation and verbal tactics in simulated crisis encounters (Lavoie et al., 2023):
 Officers can practice communication techniques with AI-driven characters experiencing
 mental health crises, domestic disputes, or substance abuse situations. The VR
 environment allows for multiple attempts at the same scenario with varied outcomes
 based on verbal approach, tone, and body language.
 • Active shooter and high-threat drills with spatial audio and weapon tracking: Three-
 dimensional environments simulate school, office, or public spaces where officers must
 navigate complex layouts, coordinate with team members, and identify threats among
 civilians. Spatial audio provides directional gunfire cues and victim calls for help.
 • Tactical movement and cover utilization: VR environments allow officers to practice
 room clearing, pursuit scenarios, and tactical positioning without physical space
 limitations or safety concerns associated with live training.

 7

 • Multi-officer coordination exercises: Virtual environments enable synchronized training
 where multiple officers in different locations can participate in the same scenario,
 practicing communication and coordinated responses to complex situations.
A growing body of empirical work supports the utility of VR for shoot/no-shoot (SNS) and
physical security training, showing that immersive, interactive scenarios can sharpen perceptual
attention, improve decision processes under stress, and yield measurable performance gains. For
example, full-body immersive VR has been used with active officers to rehearse crisis-
communication and decision-making during mental-health encounters while capturing
psychophysiological and user-experience outcomes consistent with effective de-escalation
training (Muñoz et al., 2024). Controlled research on police firearms decisions further indicates
that training which explicitly tunes visual attention and tactical gaze—skills readily trained and
assessed in VR—improves decision quality and threat-response timing in shoot/no-shoot
contexts (Heusler & Sutter, 2022). Complementing these findings in the adjacent physical-
security domain, Jones et al. (2024) demonstrate that VR scenarios can be instrumented to
quantify human performance (e.g., alarm-resolution behavior) while maintaining presence and
engagement, underscoring VR’s value for high-stakes, time-pressured decision training. Users
report higher levels of presence, emotional engagement, and confidence in VR-based scenarios
(Slater & Sanchez-Vives, 2016). Leveraging these can allow for realistic LEO training scenarios
under stress (Giessing 2021).

1.4. The Role of Immersion in Learning and Performance
Immersion refers to the perceptual and psychological sense of "being there" within a simulated
environment. In the context of SNS training, immersion can:
 • Enhance episodic memory consolidation by engaging the hippocampus during
 emotionally salient scenarios (Dunsmoor et al., 2015): Episodic memory consolidation
 refers to the brain's process of converting short-term experiences into stable, long-term
 memories tied to specific contexts (time, place, emotional state). The hippocampus plays
 a crucial role in binding sensory details, emotional significance, and situational context
 into coherent memory traces. High-immersion VR scenarios engage multiple sensory
 systems simultaneously, creating rich episodic memories that officers can later recall
 when facing similar real-world situations. Unlike abstract classroom learning, immersive
 experiences create "experiential memories" that include not just factual knowledge but
 embodied understanding of how situations feel, sound, and unfold temporally.
 • Improve threat cue discrimination via embodied cognition (Wilson, 2002): Threat cue
 discrimination involves the ability to rapidly differentiate between actual threats and
 harmless stimuli that may superficially resemble threats (a cell phone vs. handgun,
 aggressive posturing vs. defensive positioning). Embodied cognition theory suggests that
 physical interaction with environments enhances this discrimination by engaging
 sensorimotor systems that process spatial relationships, object affordances, and
 movement patterns. VR training allows officers to practice threat recognition from
 multiple angles, lighting conditions, and movement contexts, building robust perceptual
 templates that generalize beyond specific scenarios.

 8

 • Amplify motivational salience, increasing effortful processing and recall (Makransky &
 Lilleholt, 2018): Motivational salience describes the psychological significance or
 importance that individuals assign to stimuli, which directly affects attention allocation
 and memory encoding. High-immersion environments naturally increase motivational
 salience because officers perceive virtual scenarios as consequential rather than abstract.
 When training feels "real," officers invest greater cognitive effort, maintain sustained
 attention, and process information more deeply. This enhanced engagement leads to
 stronger memory formation and better skill transfer compared to low-stakes, non-
 immersive training methods.
However, immersion must be calibrated to the training goal:
  • For procedural memory, lower immersion may suffice (e.g., rehearsing commands)

 • For adaptive decision-making, high immersion facilitates realism and behavioral transfer
 (Makransky et al., 2021)
Over-immersion can produce feelings of presence, but lead to less learning (Makransky et al.,
2019b). Thus, the target cognitive process should guide immersion level (Slater, 2009).

3. OPPORTUNITIES AND RESEARCH AGENDA
While the promise of VR SNS training is strong, empirical gaps remain:

 • Longitudinal studies on skill retention and decay post-training
 • Comparative trials between VR and live-action or video simulators
 • Effects of VR training on shooting behavior
 • Integration with departmental data systems for performance tracking
 • Use of generative AI to create adaptive scenarios on demand
 • Fusion of VR and generative AI to create dynamic, realistic interaction and character
 behavior using natural interactions
Emerging technologies like machine learning, biometric adaptive difficulty, and cloud-based
analytics offer new frontiers for scalable, validated, and just-in-time training.

Machine Learning Applications:
  • Predictive scenario generation: Algorithms analyze officer performance patterns to
 automatically create scenarios targeting specific weaknesses (e.g., hesitation in domestic
 violence calls, over-reaction to non-threatening movements)
 • Real-time performance assessment: Computer vision systems track gaze patterns, weapon
 handling, and movement efficiency to provide immediate feedback without human
 instructors
 • Behavioral pattern recognition: Systems identify when officers exhibit concerning
 decision patterns (racial bias, excessive force tendencies) and recommend targeted
 intervention training
 • Natural language processing: AI analyzes officer verbal responses during de-escalation
 scenarios, providing feedback on tone, word choice, and communication effectiveness
Biometric Adaptive Difficulty:
 • Heart rate variability monitoring: Systems automatically adjust scenario intensity based
 on officer stress levels, maintaining optimal arousal for learning without overwhelming
 trainees
 • Pupil dilation tracking: Cognitive load assessment through eye tracking enables real-time
 difficulty adjustment, ensuring scenarios remain challenging but manageable
 • EDA response integration: Emotional arousal monitoring helps calibrate scenario
 emotional content to build stress tolerance without causing training trauma
 • Performance-based progression: Biometric data combined with decision accuracy creates
 personalized learning paths that advance difficulty only when officers demonstrate
 physiological and performance readiness

 16

Cloud-Based Analytics:
 • Department-wide performance dashboards: Aggregate training data across entire
 departments to identify systemic training needs and measure program effectiveness
 • Longitudinal skill tracking: Cloud storage enables multi-year analysis of officer
 development, skill retention, and performance trends
 • Comparative benchmarking: Departments can compare their training outcomes against
 anonymized national databases to identify best practices and improvement opportunities
 • Real-time incident correlation: Post-incident analysis can compare officer field
 performance to training data, enabling continuous validation and refinement of training
 scenarios
 • Collaborative scenario libraries: Departments share effective training scenarios and
 lessons learned through secure cloud platforms, accelerating innovation and reducing
 development costs
These technologies enable truly personalized, adaptive training that evolves with individual
officers and departmental needs while maintaining rigorous performance standards and
accountability measures.

Best Practices for Scenario Design
Drawing from the reviewed literature, optimal SNS training scenarios should:
 • Present ambiguous stimuli requiring careful decision making

 • Include branching outcomes based on trainee actions
 • Vary environmental context, lighting, noise, and movement patterns
 • Include non-threatening civilians, ambiguous actors, and delayed threat emergence
 • Integrate stress-inducing elements (e.g., time pressure, audio distractions)
 • Provide after-action review with video, metrics, and feedback

3.2. Integrating Generative AI and VR for Enhanced Realism
The fusion of generative AI with VR offers a transformative opportunity to expand the
complexity, realism, and adaptability of shoot/no-shoot (SNS) training scenarios. Traditional
SNS simulations—whether conducted via live-action roleplay or rigid computer-based branching
logic—struggle to replicate the nuanced and unpredictable nature of real-world encounters.
Generative AI, particularly in the form of large language models (LLMs) and multi-modal
agents, can introduce dynamic dialogue, behavior variability, and reactive non-player characters
(NPCs) that more closely resemble authentic human interactions.
Recent advancements in LLM-driven avatars (e.g., PaLM-E, GPT-4o, Gemini) allow for real-
time natural language conversation within VR environments (Huang et al., 2023). When
embedded into law enforcement training modules, these AI-driven agents can simulate de-
escalation attempts, non-compliance, mental health crises, or aggressive escalation based on
trainee input, vocal tone, or body posture. This enables the development of branching, emergent
scenarios that unfold organically, rather than along pre-scripted paths. Moreover, generative AI
can vary scene details (e.g., NPC background, location layout, environmental context) across
training iterations, reducing memorization effects and improving transfer to real-world decision-
making.

 18

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) also contribute to realism by enhancing scene
photorealism, synthesizing new environments, or dynamically modifying lighting, weather, or
visual occlusions to challenge perception under variable conditions (Goodfellow et al., 2014).
Coupled with AI-driven motion capture and behavior modeling, these tools create immersive,
reactive environments where every decision is consequential.
Importantly, generative systems also allow for tailored training: scenarios can be procedurally
generated based on an individual officer's performance history, risk profile, or prior errors,
enabling adaptive practice that targets specific decision-making weaknesses. This kind of
precision feedback loop has been linked to faster skill acquisition and better long-term retention
in simulation-based learning (Ericsson, 2004; Salas et al., 2009).
In short, the integration of generative AI into VR SNS training enables more fluid, human-like
interaction, customizable difficulty levels, context variability, and narrative depth. These
advances not only mirror the complexity of real-world policing but also address long-standing
limitations of traditional training modalities by better engaging the cognitive, affective, and
perceptual systems involved in rapid decision-making.

4. TECHNICAL CHALLENGES IN BUILDING SNS SIMULATIONS
Despite the promise of VR- and AI-enabled SNS training, several technical hurdles must be
overcome to ensure that simulations achieve both ecological validity and training efficacy.

Multimodal Synchronization:
Effective simulation requires real-time coordination between multiple input/output streams—
speech, gesture recognition, eye tracking, biometric sensors (e.g., HRV, EDA), and weapon
tracking. Ensuring low-latency synchronization across these modalities, particularly within
complex VR engines (e.g., Unity, Unreal Engine), is non-trivial and prone to jitter,
misalignment, or false positives.

Realistic Human-AI Interaction:
While LLMs offer compelling dialogue capabilities, maintaining coherent character behavior,
emotional expression, and situational awareness remains a challenge in dynamic, embodied VR.
Generative agents often lack grounded spatial awareness, and translating textual outputs into
fluid avatar animations and voice synthesis that respond to player actions in real time is still an
active area of research.

Procedural Scene Generation:
To simulate variability and prevent overfitting to specific scenarios, scenes must be procedurally
generated with meaningful variation while maintaining fidelity to tactical and legal constraints.
Ensuring object permanence, correct cover placement, line-of-sight logic, and believable crowd
behaviors all require sophisticated rule-based and learning-based systems.

Ground Truth and Scenario Scoring:
Capturing accurate ground truth for after-action review (e.g., timing of weapon draw, gaze
fixation, intent to shoot) requires tightly integrated tracking systems and clear definition of
evaluation metrics. Differentiating between hesitation, correct inhibition, and unjustified delay is
particularly difficult without high-fidelity sensor fusion and precise temporal logging.

Psychological and Cognitive Load Calibration:
A well-designed SNS scenario must balance stress inoculation with psychological safety.
However, inducing authentic levels of cognitive load, perceptual threat, and moral ambiguity in
VR without causing training fatigue or traumatic stress is complex. System designers must
monitor physiological markers (e.g., EDA, pupil dilation) and dynamically modulate difficulty to
maintain the optimal training zone (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908; Hancock & Szalma, 2008).

Hardware Limitations and Ergonomics:
Maintaining frame rates above 90 FPS and minimizing motion blur or headset weight is critical
for presence and user comfort—especially in kinetic simulations involving movement, weapon
handling, or crouching. Limited field-of-view, resolution constraints, and hand-tracking
imprecision can all reduce realism and introduce training artifacts (Slater & Sanchez-Vives,
2016).

Legal and Ethical Constraints on AI-Generated Behavior:

 20

Ensuring that AI-driven NPCs follow lawful use-of-force models is essential for both legal
defensibility and public trust. This necessitates the integration of policy constraints and
boundaries into scenario design pipelines, possibly through rule-based overlays or human-in-the-
loop approaches (Binns et al., 2018).
Together, these challenges form suggestions for both technical development and scientific
validation. Addressing them will require collaboration between cognitive scientists, engineers,
tactical experts, and frontline officers, ensuring that future VR/AI-enhanced training
environments are not only immersive but also reliable, transferable, and trustworthy.

6. CONCLUSION
Virtual reality represents a paradigm shift in SNS training for law enforcement. Its ability to
deliver immersive, adaptable, and measurable learning experiences aligns with both the cognitive
science of decision-making and the practical needs of modern policing. By incorporating
evidence-based design principles, physiological monitoring, and adaptive feedback, VR systems
can support safer, more accurate, and accountable use-of-force decisions. Future research and
funding should prioritize validation and integration into real-world training ecosystems.
