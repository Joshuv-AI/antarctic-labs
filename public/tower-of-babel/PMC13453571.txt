Foretinib triggers immunogenic cell death and enhances efficacy of anti-CD47 immunotherapy in colorectal cancer
Authors: Lin Li, Nannan Zhu, Tao Zhu, Yuxuan Wang, Jieran Deng, Han Xie, Xiaoyun Guo, Qianqian Duan, Lei Chen, Jingshan Tong, Xiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The dual-target c-Met/VEGFR kinase inhibitor foretinib reshapes tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD).•We identified an on-target mechanism of foretinib in transcriptionally activating Drath Receptor 5 (DR5), a key component of the extrinsic apoptotic pathway.•The combination of foretinib with anti-CD47 antibody significantly inhibits tumor growth in colon cancer model.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-associated mortality [1]. As the most prevalent gastrointestinal cancer, CRC is associated with a dismal prognosis, primarily owing to distant metastatic dissemination [2]. Despite recent advances in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, the overall clinical outcomes for CRC patients remain suboptimal, underscoring an urgent demand for novel and more effective therapeutic strategies [3]. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that immune dysregulation plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of CRC [4]. Immune evasion is a well-established hallmark of malignant tumors, enabling cancer cells to elude host immune surveillance [4]. Tumor-derived immune tolerance in the tumor microenvironment (TME) largely accounts for the insufficient clinical efficacy of existing CRC therapies [5]. Approximately 85% of CRC cases are characterized by microsatellite-stable (MSS) phenotype, which is typified by diminished neoantigen presentation, impaired CD8+ T cell recognition, and sparse immune cell infiltration within tumor tissues [6]. Such immunosuppressive features are responsible for generally unsatisfactory response to immunotherapy in patients with MSS-CRC [7]. In this context, therapeutic strategies capable of reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME into an immunostimulatory environment hold great promise for improving the therapeutic efficacy of CRC treatment.

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a unique subtype of programmed cell death that triggers robust adaptive immune responses targeting tumor-associated antigens [8]. This immunogenic process relies on the coordinated surface exposure and extracellular secretion of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which collectively remodel the TME into an immunostimulatory state [8]. The core DAMP signals consist of surface-exposed calreticulin (CRT), which tags dying cancer cells for phagocytosis clearance; extracellular ATP, which recruits and activates antigen-presenting cells (APCs); and secreted HMGB1, which facilitates efficient antigen processing and presentation [8,9]. Together, these hallmark molecular cues synergistically initiate and sustain potent antitumor immune response.

Current evidence indicates that ICD can only be reliably elicited by specific therapeutic interventions [[10], [11], [12]]. Mechanistically, tumor cells undergoing ICD function as endogenous vaccines, which recruit immune cells to tumor lesions and facilitate antigen cross-presentation within lymphoid tissues [11]. This immune-priming cascade ultimately activates tumor-specific T cells. Consistent with this notion, preclinical studies have validated that immunization with ICD-stimulated tumor cells confers protective immunity against subsequent tumor rechallenges [12]. Clinical observations further corroborate these mechanistic findings. In breast and colorectal cancer patients receiving ICD-inducing chemotherapy, favorable clinical outcomes are strongly correlated with elevated intertumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration [13,14]. Conversely, impaired dendritic cells (DCs) function has been consistently recognized as a negative prognostic biomarker for therapeutic response [14]. Collectively, these findings highlight the great potential of ICD inducers as indispensable components of combination immunotherapy regimens, which have yielded promising efficacy in preclinical investigations.

Foretinib is a multi-target receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) inhibitor with validated antitumor efficacy against various malignancies [15]. It predominantly targets the oncogenic signaling pathways driven by VEGFR2 and c-MET [15]. While its preclinical antitumor activity has been well-established in multiple cancer models [[16], [17], [18]], its capacity to induce ICD in CRC remains unexplored. Herein, this study systematically investigated the ICD-inducing potential of foretinib in CRC cells. Our findings demonstrate that foretinib effectively triggers robust ICD, thereby eliciting prominent immunostimulatory effects that remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Importantly, these results indicate that foretinib, administered either as a single agent or in combination with anti-CD47 therapy, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC by eliciting ICD-mediated antitumor immunity.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and drug treatment

The human CRC cell lines HCT116 and HT29, as well as murine colon cancer cell line MC38 were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). p53-KO HCT116 cells were generated as previously described [19]. DR5-KO HCT116 cells were generated using Crispr-Cas9 genome editing system, with a specific guide RNA (gRNA) (5′-CCTACCGCCATGGAACAACG-3′) targeting DR5. All cells were cultured in high Glucose DMEM supplemented with Pyruvate, l-glutamine (Meilunbio), 10% defined FBS (Capricor-Scientific, FBS-12A), 100 units/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Beyotime). Cells were maintained in a 37°C incubator at 5% CO2. For foretinib treatment, cells were seeded into 12-well plates and treated with foretinib (MCE) at 20-30% confluence. Foretinib was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, VICMED).

p53-KO HCT116 cells generation

The p53-knockout HCT116 cell line was generated via the CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing system. Briefly, sgRNA targeting the p53 gene (5′-GTTGATTCCACACCCCCGCC-3′) was designed and inserted into a lentiviral CRISPR vector. For lentivirus packaging, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the recombinant sgRNA-Cas9 vector and packaging plasmids. After 48 h of transfection, the cell culture supernatant containing lentivirus was collected, centrifuged, and filtered to harvest purified viral particles.

Wild-type HCT116 cells were infected with the harvested lentivirus for 72 h, stably transfected cells were screened with puromycin at a final concentration of 2 μg/ml for 7 consecutive days to eliminate untransfected cells. For single-clone selection, surviving cells were digested into single-cell suspensions, counted, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of one cell per well by limiting dilution. Cells were cultured in complete medium for 10–14 days until single-cell clones were successfully formed. Positive p53-knockout clones were identified using Western blotting.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described [19], with antibodies against DR5 (15497-1-AP, Proteintech), p53 (21891-1-AP, Proteintech), cleaved caspase 3 (AF7022, Affinity) and β-actin (BS6007M, Bioworld).

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptotic cells were quantified by counting cells with condensed and fragmented nuclei following nuclear staining by Hoechst Staining Kit (C0003, Beyotime), with a minimum of 300 cells were analyzed per experimental condition. Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) staining was performed using Annexin V-FITC and PI Kit (APExBIO) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For colony formation assay, foretinib-treated WT and DR5-KO HCT116 cells were plated in 6-well plates at appropriate dilutions, and colonies were stained with crystal violet after 2 weeks of culture.

CCK-8 assay

HCT116 and HT29 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured overnight. Cells were treated with foretinib (1 μM, 2 μM, 5 μM) for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μl (10% in 100 μl) CCK-8 solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was supplemented to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Synergy H1, BioTek).

Crystal violet staining

HCT116 and HT29 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated overnight prior to treatment with foretinib at indicated concentrations for 24 h. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and rinsed three times with PBS post-fixation. The samples were stained with crystal violet (548-62-9, Kemiou) for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, and then photographed.

Flow cytometric analysis of CRT translocation

HCT116 or HT29 cells (2.5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 12-well plates and incubation at 37°C for 24 h prior to treatment with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h. Cells were then stained with rabbit anti-CRT primary antibody (1:1000, A1066, Abclonal) and goat anti-Rabbit IgG highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody (1:200, A32733, ThermoFisher). Flow cytometry was used to quantify cell surface CRT.

ATP assay

Intracellular and extracellular ATP were separately quantified with the APExBIO ATP Detection Kit per manufacturer’s instructions. Culture supernatants were collected and clarified by low-speed centrifugation for extracellular ATP testing, and the residual adherent cells were lysed to measure intracellular ATP. Raw ATP data were normalized to parallel viable cell counts.

HMGB1 assay

HCT116 or HT29 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h. Extracellular HMGB1 levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using an ELISA kit (ABclone Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR)

The indicated cells were treated with foretinib for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme) and synthesized to cDNAs with the PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (RR036A, Takara). qRT-PCR was performed with PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (AQ601-2, TransGen Biotech) on a Roche Light Cycler 480II system (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Relative mRNA levels were quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

HCT116 cells were seeded in 12-well plates overnight and treated with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (R401-01, Vazyme), and subsequently subjected to RNA sequencing analysis by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, Transcriptome sequencing of RNA samples was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Each group was set up with 2 biological replicates. Raw sequencing reads were filtered for quality control, and clean reads were obtained by removing adaptor contamination, low-quality bases and undetermined bases. Subsequently, clean reads were aligned to the reference genome, followed by gene expression quantification. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the thresholds of |log2(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for false discovery rate (FDR) correction. All differential expression analyses were implemented in R software. The R clusterProfiler package was applied to conduct Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

DC activation and phagocytosis

BM cells were harvested from the femurs of C57BL/6 mice and cultured in complete RPMI medium containing mouse recombinant GM-CSF (50 ng/ml) and IL-4 (10 ng/ml) (Peprotech) for 7 days to induce the differentiation of CD11c+ DCs. For the phagocytosis assay, MC38 cells were labeled with CFSE (MeilunBio) and pretreated with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h. Subsequently, foretinib-treated tumor cells were co-cultured with immature DCs at a fixed cell ratio of 1:1 for 4 h at 37°C and 5% CO2.

After co-culture, rigorous washing and enzymatic digestion were performed to eliminate non-specific cell adhesion and cell aggregation. Briefly, cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS (300 × g, 5 min per wash) to remove unbound free cells and residual extracellular CFSE. To further distinguish genuine intracellular phagocytosis from surface-attached MC38 cells, cell pellets were incubated with pre-warmed 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution for 5 min at 37°C to strip off membrane-adhered target cells. Digestion was immediately terminated by adding complete RPMI medium containing 10% FBS.

For flow cytometry analysis, single-cell suspensions were stained with PE anti-mouse CD11c antibody (117308, Biolegend), FITC anti-mouse I-A/I-E antibody (107605, Biolegend), APC anti-mouse CD80 antibody (65076, Proteintech) and FITC anti-mouse CD86 antibody (105005, Biolegend). DC-mediated phagocytosis of MC38 cells was quantitatively determined by detecting CD11c/CFSE double-positive populations via flow cytometry. Only intracellular CFSE signals within CD11c+ DCs after trypsin digestion were defined as true phagocytosis events, excluding signals derived from cell surface adhesion and cell aggregation. A standardized stepwise gating strategy was applied, and multiple control groups including blank DC control, single CFSE-labeled MC38 control, and non-digested adhesion control were included to validate the assay specificity.

In vivo experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Xuzhou Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (202402T001). Tumors were established in mice by subcutaneous inoculation with MC38 cell suspension (1 × 106 cells/mouse) into the flank region of 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (n = 6). Seven days after tumor formation, mice were random assigned to different groups and treated with 5 mg/kg foretinib every day by oral gavage for 2 weeks. Tumor growth was monitored using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated with the formula: ½ × length × width2.

For combination treatment, 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (n = 6) were injected with MC38 cells into the right flank. On day 7 post-tumor implantation, mice were random assigned to different groups and treated with foretinib (oral gavage; 5 mg/kg), anti-CD47 (BioXcell; 100 μg/dose i.p.), or their combination as indicated in Fig. 6a.

For the immunization study, MC38 cells were either subjected to three rounds of freeze-thawing on dry ice or treated with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h. The processed cells were subcutaneously injected into the left flank of C57BL/6 mice (n = 6). Seven days later, viable MC38 cells were inoculated into the right flank, and tumor growth was continuously monitored. All researchers responsible for animal treatment, sample collection and outcome evaluation were kept blinded to the group allocation until the completion of data analysis.

Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Tumors from mice were minced into small pieces using a razor blade, then transferred to 70 μm cell strainers (BD) to generate single-cell suspensions. For functional detection of T-cell intracellular cytokines, ex vivo restimulation and cytokine secretion blockade were performed prior to intracellular staining. Briefly, isolated single tumor cells were resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h for polyclonal restimulation. After restimulation and secretion blockade, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to surface marker staining. Cells were incubated with the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4°C for 30 min in the dark: FITC anti-mouse CD45 Recombinant Antibody (157608, Biolegend), APC anti-mouse CD3 Antibody (100236, BioLegend), PE anti-mouse CD4 Antibody (100408, BioLegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD8α Antibody (100722, BioLegend), PE anti-mouse CD11c Antibody (117308, BioLegend), APC anti-mouse CD80 Antibody (65076, Proteintech), FITC CD86 Antibody (105005, BioLegend), and FITC anti-mouse I-A/I-E Antibody (107605, BioLegend).

For intracellular cytokine staining, after surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using commercial fixation/permeabilization reagents for intracellular flow cytometry. Subsequently, intracellular staining was performed with APC/Cyanine7 anti-human/mouse Granzyme B Recombinant Antibody (372228, Biolegend) and FITC anti-mouse IFN-γ Antibody (505806, Biolegend) at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Finally, cell fluorescence data were acquired on a BD FACScanto II Flow Cytometer, and all flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA). One-way or two-way ANOVA was used to determine significance differences among multiple experimental groups. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). P-values were considered significant if P < 0.05.

Results

Foretinib induces immunogenic cell death in colorectal cancer cells

To evaluate the effects of forethinib on colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of foretinib, specifically 1 μM, 2 μM and 5 μM, for 24 h, followed by cell viability detection using the CCK-8 assay. The results showed that foretinib significantly suppressed the viability of HCT116 and HT29 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1a). To further verify these results, crystal violet staining was performed to evaluate foretinib-induced morphological changes and cell viability alterations. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in viable cell numbers after 24 h of foretinib treatment (Fig. 1b), which corroborated the dose-dependent cytotoxic observed in CCK-8 assays. To determine whether foretinib triggers ICD in CRC cells, we detected three core ICD hallmarks, including cell surface CRT exposure, extracellular HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion. Briefly, HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with foretinib, and surface CRT exposure was analyzed by flow cytometry. Foretinib treatment markedly increased the membrane exposure of CRT in HCT116, HT29 and MC38 cells (Fig. 1c and d, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Similarly, extracellular HMGB1 levels in the cell culture supernatant were significantly elevated after foretinib intervention (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1B). Moreover, foretinib substantially promoted ATP release in these cells relative to the untreated control group (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 1C-1F). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that foretinib effectively induces ICD in CRC cells.

Foretinib induces p53-dependent DR5 upregulation

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying foretinib-induced ICD, HCT116 cells were treated with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h and subjected to RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq). Transcriptomic profiling revealed profound changes in global gene expression, with 1905 upregulated and 2296 downregulated genes upon foretinib stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 2A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further identified robust activation of p53 signaling pathway and enrichment of apoptosis-related processes in foretinib-treated cells (Fig. 2a–c). Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that apoptotic signaling serves as a potent inducer of ICD [20,21]. We further validated the pro-apoptotic effect of foretinib via flow cytometry, confirming that foretinib induced apoptosis in HCT116 and HT29 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 2B). Enhanced caspase-3 activation and nuclear fragmentation were also observed in both cell lines, further verifying foretinib-mediated apoptosis (Fig. 2e and f, Supplementary Fig. 2C). Bioinformatic reanalysis of RNA-seq data revealed that Death Receptor 5 (DR5), a canonical downstream target of p53, was among the most significantly upregulated genes in apoptotic pathways (Fig. 2g). Consistently, both DR5 mRNA and protein expression were markedly upregulated in HCT116 and HT29 cells following foretinib treatment (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 2D and 2E). To clarify the regulatory role of p53 in foretinib-induced DR5 upregulation, p53-knockout (p53-KO) HCT116 cells were utilized for subsequent validation. Compared with wild-type (WT) HCT116 cells, p53 deficiency completely abrogated foretinib-induced DR5 upregulation at both mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 2F and G). Furthermore, we explored the essential role of DR5 in foretinib-induced cell death and ICD. Indeed, compared to WT cells, DR5-KO cells exhibited significantly attenuated apoptosis and improved clonogenic survival after foretinib exposure (Supplementary Fig. 2H–J). Consistent with these findings, foretinib-induced caspase-3 activation was also significantly suppressed in DR5-KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 2K). Importantly, DR5 deficiency significantly attenuated foretinib-induced CRT translocation, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion in HCT116 cells (Fig. 2i–l). These results indicate that foretinib elicits ICD through activating the p53/DR5-dependent cell death.

For this co-culture assay, MC38 cells were pretreated with foretinib and then co-culture with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) according to the schematic workflow (Fig. 3a). The results showed that BMDCs exhibited significantly enhanced phagocytic capacity against foretinib-pretreated MC38 cells compared with untreated control cells (Fig. 3b and c). In parallel, foretinib treatment markedly promoted DCs maturation, as characterized by the upregulated expression of the mature markers MHC II, CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 3d–f, Supplementary FigS. 3A–C, 4A). Foretinib triggers antitumor immune responses in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor immune effect of foretinib, we adopted a well-established prophylactic tumor vaccination model [22]. MC38 cells were pretreated with 5 μM foretinib for 24 h to generate ICD-based tumor vaccines, while cells subjected to three repeated freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen served as the control vaccine. The two groups of treated cells were subcutaneously injected into the left flank of immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice. Seven days post-vaccination, all mice were rechallenged with viable MC38 cells on the contralateral right flank (Fig. 4a). Notably, mice vaccinated with foretinib-pretreated tumor cells exhibited markedly prolonged tumor-free survival and decreased tumor incidence relative to the freeze-thaw control group (Fig. 4b–d). Foretinib synergizes with anti-CD47 to suppress tumor growth

CD47-targeted therapy has emerged as a promising and novel immunotherapeutic strategy for cancer treatment [23]. Accumulating studies have validated that cell-surface CRT exposure substantially potentiates the phagocytic antitumor effect induced by anti-CD47 antibodies [24]. Given our observation that foretinib robustly triggers CRT membrane translocation and ICD in CRC cells, we further explored whether foretinib could synergize with anti-CD47 immunotherapy to amplify antitumor immune responses (Fig. 6a). In vivo therapeutic experiments demonstrated that combined treatment with foretinib and anti-CD47 exerted superior tumor-suppressive efficacy compared with single-agent monotherapy (Fig. 6b–e). Notably, no obvious systemic toxicity was observed in the combination group, as indicated by stable body weight throughout the treatment period (Fig. 6f). Taken together, these results reveal that foretinib treatment enhances the antitumor efficacy of CD47 blockade via ICD-dependent mechanisms. Discussion

Exploring the development of novel drugs that selectively activate the ICD pathway presents a highly promising strategy for cancer therapy [14]. Recently, multiple antitumor agents with ICD-inducing capacity have been approved for tumor treatment [13,25], strongly demonstrating the tremendous translational potential of ICD-based cancer therapeutics. Foretinib has been well documented to exert potent antitumor efficacy against various types of tumors [16,17]. However, its ability to trigger ICD remains unreported to date. In the current study, we identified that foretinib effectively induces CRT exposure, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion in CRC cells. These foretinib-stimulated tumor cells release DAMPs that further facilitate the maturation and activation of DCs. Moreover, tumor vaccination assays verified that foretinib-induced ICD is capable of triggering long-term immune memory response. Consistent with in vitro findings, in vivo experiments result further confirmed that foretinib markedly suppresses tumor growth, promotes the maturation of CD11c+ DCs, and potentiates the infiltration and activation of T cells within the TME. Mechanistically, foretinib upregulates DR5 expression in a p53-dependent manner, which subsequently initiates tumor cell apoptosis and ICD activation. Collectively, our findings suggest that foretinib serves as a promising therapeutic candidate for CRC treatment by targeting and inducing ICD.

Apoptosis is primarily triggered through two distinct signaling cascades: the extrinsic pathway, driven by the activation of death receptors (DRs), and the intrinsic pathway, which is governed by Bcl-2 family proteins and mitochondrial dysfunction [26,27]. Both apoptotic cascades converge on caspase activation to execute programmed cell death [27]. As a critical cell surface death receptor, DR5 is activated upon binding to its cognate ligands, including tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) [28]. Additionally, DR5 expression can be transcriptionally upregulated by p53 under DNA damage or by C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) during endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [29]. Different forms of cell death elicit drastically distinct immunological consequences in the TME [30]. In our study, we identified that p53-dependent DR5 upregulation serves as a core molecular mechanism underlying the antitumor activity of foretinib in CRC cells. Mechanistic validation further confirmed that DR5 induction is indispensable for foretinib-induced CRC cell death. This finding aligns with the role of DR5 acts as a critical convergence point for multiple apoptotic pathway axes, encompassing p53-mediated DNA damage apoptosis, ER stress-induced apoptosis, and cytokine-driven apoptosis, all of which are crucial for antitumor immune responses.

Dendritic cell (DC) activation is well established as an essential step for antigen processing and the delivery of costimulatory signals required for T cell priming and activation [31]. In vivo observations from our study demonstrated that foretinib significantly potentiates the activation and functional capacity of both DCs and T cells. Given that mature and activated DCs are pivotal for diving effector T cell infiltration into tumors, we further investigated the maturation status and phagocytic activity of BMDCs following foretinib treatment. Our results demonstrated that foretinib effectively boosted BMDCs maturation and enhances their phagocytic capacity. Collectively, these findings suggest that foretinib facilitates DCs activation and function predominantly by triggering ICD in CRC.

Combination strategies based on immune checkpoint blockade and immunogenic cell death (ICD) inducers have been widely investigated in numerous ongoing clinical trials, which can broaden the therapeutic indications of conventional chemotherapeutics to benefit more patients and reduce clinical drug doses, thereby minimizing treatment-related adverse effects [32]. Accumulating clinical and preclinical evidence has validated the promising application of CD47-targeted immunotherapy in solid tumor treatment [33,34]. Mechanistically, ICD inducers upregulates pro-phagocytic signals on tumor cells, whereas anti-CD47 therapy efficiently blocks the tumor surface “don’t eat me” signal [35]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the combination of ICD induction and CD47 blockade has been proven to facilitate phagocytic clearance of tumor cells, potentiate systemic antitumor immunity and suppress tumor progression in previous studies [36,37]. Consistent with these established therapeutic synergies, the present study further verified that the combined treatment of foretinib and anti-CD47 antibody exerts superior antitumor efficacy by robustly activating dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells in TME. Mechanistically, foretinib acts as a potent ICD inducer in tumor cells, triggering robust plasma membrane translocation of CRT, a canonical “eat-me” signal of ICD. The extensive surface exposure of CRT drastically remodels the balance between pro-phagocytic and anti-phagocytic signals on tumor cell surfaces and reverses the immunosuppressive phenotype of tumors, ultimately sensitizing tumor cells to phagocytic elimination by macrophage and dendritic cell-mediated antigen presentation. Importantly, this ICD and CRT externalization-dependent regulatory mechanism constitutes the core synergistic principle of foretinib combined with anti-CD47 therapy. Anti-CD47 monotherapy achieves tumor immunotherapy efficacy primarily by neutralizing the dominant “don’t eat me” anti-phagocytic signal on tumor cells, while foretinib complements and amplifies this therapeutic effect via ICD-initiated CRT externalization to activate pro-phagocytic signaling. Collectively, foretinib remodels the pro/anti-phagocytic signal landscape of tumor cells through triggering ICD, which substantially enhances anti-CD47-mediated tumor phagocytosis and clearance, ultimately strengthening the overall antitumor immune response in the TME.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that foretinib induces ICD in CRC cells by triggering DR5-mediated cell death. Mechanistically, foretinib-elicited ICD further activates innate immune response, which serves as a key driver underlying the antitumor potency of foretinib. Additionally, the combination strategy of foretinib with CD47 blockade represents a novel and viable approach to boost the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.
