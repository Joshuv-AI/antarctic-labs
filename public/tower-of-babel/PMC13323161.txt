Left Atrial Appendage Electrical Isolation Reduces Residual Patency After Device Closure in Patients With Persistent Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Peng‐Cheng Yao, Mu Chen, Qun‐Shan Wang, Mei Yang, Rui Zhang, Peng‐Pai Zhang, Wei Li, Yan Zhao, Xiao‐Ming Lian, Qian Wang, Yu‐Dong Fei, Zhi‐Xing Wei, Xiao‐Min Yang, Xing‐Xing Cai, Jian Sun, Yi‐Gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial appendage (LAA) patency after catheter ablation and LAA closure (LAAC) is a key determinant of postprocedure stroke. The effect of LAA electrical isolation (LAAEI) on LAA patency after LAAC in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) remains unknown. Our objective was to explore the effect of LAAEI on LAA patency after catheter ablation and LAAC in patients with AF.MethodsPatients with persistent AF who underwent combined catheter ablation and LAAC were enrolled. LAA patency was detected by cardiac computed tomographic angiography at 3 months after the procedure.ResultsA total of 686 patients with persistent AF with 3‐month device surveillance by cardiac computed tomographic angiography were included initially. Propensity score matching analysis was conducted on the basis of clinical features, echocardiographic parameters and procedural characteristics, and 469 patients were matched, including 363 with non‐LAAEI and 106 with LAAEI. Patients in matched groups showed 53.7% complete closure (no patency). Of the 46.3% of cases with LAA patency, visible peridevice leak was seen in 72.8%, and 27.2% had patency without visible peridevice leak. Compared with the non‐LAAEI group, the LAAEI group had a lower rate of LAA patency (49.6% versus 34.9%; adjusted odds ratio, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.48–0.98]; P=0.040). LAAEI was protective against LAA patency with an odds ratio of 0.68, and the other predictor of contrast leak into the LAA was maximum diameter of LAA orifice (odds ratio, 1.04 [95% CI, 1.00–1.08]; P=0.046).ConclusionsLAAEI is an effective strategy for reducing LAA patency in patients with persistent AF undergoing combined catheter ablation and LAAC.RegistrationURL: clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT03788941.

Percutaneous catheter ablation is an established and effective rhythm control strategy for atrial fibrillation (AF). 1 The BELIEF (Effect of Empirical Left Atrial Appendage Isolation on Long‐Term Procedure Outcome in Patients With Persistent or Longstanding Persistent Atrial Fibrillation Undergoing Catheter Ablation) trial 2 demonstrated that adjunctive left atrial appendage (LAA) electrical isolation (LAAEI) significantly reduces atrial arrhythmia recurrence in patients with longstanding persistent AF. Subsequent propensity score matching (PSM) analysis further confirmed this finding, showing improved arrhythmia‐free survival in patients with persistent and long‐standing persistent AF undergoing LAAEI. 3 Mechanistically, LAAEI achieves therapeutic benefit through left atrial substrate modification by linear ablation and elimination of AF triggers in the LAA. 4 However, LAAEI may increase thromboembolic risk due to postprocedural LAA dysfunction. 5 The OPTION (Comparison of Anticoagulation With Left Atrial Appendage Closure After Atrial Fibrillation Ablation) trial 6 established that LAA closure (LAAC) demonstrates noninferiority to oral anticoagulation for stroke prevention, with previous studies confirming the safety and long‐term efficacy of combining catheter ablation with LAAC. 7 These collective findings suggest that combined LAAEI by catheter ablation with LAAC may offer dual therapeutic advantages, including enhanced stroke prevention and reduced atrial arrhythmia recurrence.

Previous studies have established that LAA patency, including those with visible peridevice leak (PDL) and without visible PDL (or termed as incomplete endothelialization in prior literature), post‐LAAC constitutes a significant risk factor for postprocedural stroke. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, the incidence of PDL following combined LAAEI with concomitant LAAC remains undetermined. Herein, we aimed to investigate and compare the incidence of residual patency, as assessed by cardiac computed tomographic angiography (CCTA) between patients undergoing combined LAAEI by catheter ablation with LAAC versus those receiving LAAC without LAAEI.

Methods

Study Population

Data are available from the corresponding authors upon reasonable request. From the prospective LAACablation (Left Atrial Appendage Closure in Combination With Catheter Ablation) registry (NCT03788941), which enrolled patients undergoing combined catheter ablation and LAAC in Xinhua Hospital, Shanghai, China, we identified 2292 patients with AF, including 1219 with persistent AF. Among these patients with persistent AF, 686 patients who completed 3‐month postprocedural device surveillance by CCTA were eligible for this study (Figure 1). The LAACablation registry is a physician‐initiated prospective observational study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No. XH‐18‐015) and complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was provided by all participants. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria of the LAACablation registry are listed in Table S1. The study group included patients receiving LAAEI; the control group consisted of patients undergoing standard ablation without attempted LAAEI.

Pulmonary Vein Isolation and LAAEI

The entire procedure was performed under light sedation. After excluding LAA thrombus, mapping and ablation were performed under the guidance of the CARTO (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) or Ensite (St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN) 3‐dimensional electroanatomic mapping systems, in addition to standard fluoroscopy. Pulmonary vein isolation was performed in all patients; ethanol infusion of the vein of Marshall and additional linear or complex fragmented atrial electrogram ablation were performed if indicated. LAAEI was conducted by combining a left atrial anterior line, a roof line, and a mitral isthmus line (Figure 2). 5 LAAEI was inferred if the surrounding myocardium inside the lines of block was isolated.

LAAC Procedure

The LAAC device was implanted following radiofrequency ablation during the same procedure. The occluders used included single‐seal Watchman 2.5/FLX (Boston Scientific Corporation, Natick, MA) and dual‐seal LACbes (PushMed, Shanghai, China), which are among the most frequently used occluders in China. Intraprocedural LAA angiography was performed to measure the maximum diameter of the LAA orifice for appropriate device sizing. The device would only be released when the position–anchor–size–seal principle (for Watchman device) or proper position–absolute anchor–separate seal–typical tire principle (for the LACbes device) was achieved, verified by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiogram or angiography. The procedural details were described in our previous report. 7 Unless contraindicated, oral anticoagulation was continued until postprocedural device surveillance.

Device Surveillance by CCTA at 3 Months After LAAC

Patients underwent device surveillance by CCTA (Somatom Definition; Siemens Medical Solutions, Forchheim, Germany) at 3 months after LAAC, with 100 mL of iodinated contrast media injected with a 50‐mL saline flush followed through the elbow vein at a rate of 5 mL/s. End‐systolic image acquisition was used for device surveillance independently from the heart rate to acquire the image data during the maximum distention of the left atrium and LAA. Imaging of the left atrium, LAA, and adjacent structures was obtained (Figure 3). The temporal resolution was 330 ms, with a detector collimation of 64×0.6 mm, a tube voltage of 120 kV, and a tube current of 380 mA.

Images were analyzed by 2 experienced physicians independently, who were blinded to patients' clinical characteristics, using Extended Brilliance Workspace version 4.5 (Philips Healthcare, Cleveland, OH). The measurement results were confirmed by both physicians. In cases of disagreement, a third physician reviewed the images to reach a final consensus, which was based on agreement from at least 2 evaluators. Detailed analytical methods have been reported in earlier studies. 10 , 11

Quantitative contrast evaluation of LAA after implantation was carried out by measuring the average Hounsfield units attenuation within the LAA distal to the occluder. According to the 3‐month contrast evaluation results, patients were divided into the complete closure (Figure 3A and 3B) and LAA patency groups, the latter of which was further divided into 2 forms, that is, the LAA patency with visible PDL (Figure 3C and 3D) and without visible PDL (Figure 3E and 3F) groups. Complete closure was defined as the average LAA attenuation <100 Hounsfield units, while LAA patency was >100 Hounsfield units. Continuous contrast enhancement could be observed from left atrium to the LAA alongside the device (LAA patency with visible PDL) and through the fabric of the device (LAA patency without visible PDL). 10 , 11 The device of the dual‐occlusive LACbes taken into account was defined by default as the device disc, which was aimed at closure. The width of the PDL was defined as the maximum diameter of the contrast gap adjacent to the device on a reconstructed plane parallel to the LAA orifice. Therefore, visible PDL was further categorized into 3 types based on the severity of PDL width, that is, mild PDL (>0 to ≤3 mm), moderate PDL (>3 to ≤5 mm), and severe PDL (>5 mm).

Statistical Analysis

The study population was divided into non‐LAAEI and LAAEI groups for comparative analysis. Baseline features included clinical features (age [years], sex, body mass index [kg/m2], hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, history of stroke or transient ischemic attack, heart failure), echocardiographic parameters (left atrial diameter [mm], left ventricular ejection fraction [%] and procedural characteristics [maximum diameter of LAA orifice measured by intraoperative LAA angiography, type of device [Watchman versus LACbes], size of the device [mm]). Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD and compared using Student's t tests, while categorical variables were presented as n (%) and analyzed with χ2 tests. To minimize selection bias, we performed PSM using a 1:4 nearest‐neighbor algorithm without replacement and a caliper width of 0.1. A standardized difference >10% represents a meaningful imbalance in a given variable between groups. Odds ratios (ORs) between the non‐LAAEI and LAAEI groups were calculated using a conditional logistic regression model. Additionally, univariate and multivariable analyses of predictors of residual patency were assessed using conditional logistic regression for clinical features, echocardiographic parameters, and procedural characteristics. The threshold for entry of independent variables into the multivariable model was set at 0.10. Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Software, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). PSM was conducted using the MatchIt package within R. A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From the LAACablation registry, we initially identified 686 patients with persistent AF who underwent 3‐month CCTA surveillance. PSM yielded 469 matched patients (mean age, 70.8±7.8 years; 260 men; CHA2DS2‐VASc score, 3.9±1.6; HAS‐BLED score, 1.4±0.8), including 363 with non‐LAAEI and 106 with LAAEI. After PSM, there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics (Table 1), and standardized differences were <10% for all variables, except sex (male sex, 57.6% versus 48.1%, standardized difference, 0.190).

Incidence of LAA Patency

In the PSM cohort, CCTA analysis revealed that 252 (53.7%) achieved complete closure, while 217 (46.3%) demonstrated LAA patency. Among those with LAA patency, 158 (72.8%) exhibited visible PDL, whereas 59 (27.2%) showed LAA patency without visible PDL. Further stratification of patients with visible PDL demonstrated a predominance of mild (n=86 [54.4%]) and moderate (n=65 [41.1%]) PDL, with only 7 (4.4%) cases showing severe PDL.

Logistic regression analysis demonstrated significantly lower rates of LAA patency in the LAAEI group compared with the non‐LAAEI group (34.9% versus 49.6%; OR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.48–0.98]; P=0.039). This protective effect was also pronounced for cases with visible PDL but had no statistical significance (25.5% versus 36.1%; OR, 0.71 [95% CI, 0.47–1.07]; P=0.099; Table 2). No significant intergroup difference was observed for LAA patency without visible PDL (9.4% versus 13.5%; OR, 0.70 [95% CI, 0.35–1.38]; P=0.302). After adjustment for the baseline sex imbalance, these findings remained robust, with significant differences in overall LAA patency (adjusted OR [aOR], 0.69 [95% CI, 0.48–0.98]; P=0.040), while those with or without visible PDL continued to show no statistical significance (aOR, 0.70 [95% CI, 0.46–1.06]; P=0.095; aOR, 0.70 [95% CI, 0.36–1.39]; P=0.312).

Additional comparative analyses were performed between the non‐LAAEI and LAAEI groups stratified by PDL severity (Table S2). The results demonstrated that the LAAEI procedure was significantly associated with a reduced incidence of mild PDL compared with the non‐LAAEI group (8.5% versus 21.2%; P=0.003). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups for either moderate PDL (16.0% versus 13.2%; P=0.461) or severe PDL (0.9% versus 1.7%; P=0.596).

Furthermore, we conducted device‐specific subgroup analyses. Baseline characteristics for each device group were presented separately in Table S3 (single‐seal Watchman) and Table S4 (dual‐seal LACbes). Among patients implanted with the Watchman device (Table S5), LAAEI did not significantly reduce the risk of LAA patency (OR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.54–1.31]; P=0.440), regardless of those with (OR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.49–1.37]; P=0.245) or without visible PDL (OR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.38–2.15]; P=0.824). In contrast, for patients with the LACbes device (Table S6), LAAEI showed significant benefits, reducing both overall LAA patency (aOR, 0.23 [95% CI, 0.07–0.74]; P=0.14), while showing no significant effect on LAA patency without visible PDL (aOR, 0.14 [95% CI, 0.01–1.80]; P=0.132). LAAEI reduced the risk of LAA patency with visible PDL, although with no statistical significance (aOR, 0.29 [95% CI, 0.07–1.10]; P=0.068).

Predictors of LAA Patency

Risk factors for LAA patency were explored. Multivariable regression analyses showed that a larger maximum diameter of the LAA orifice (OR, 1.04 [95% CI, 1.00–1.08]; P=0.046) was identified as an independent risk factor, while LAAEI (OR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.47–0.97]; P=0.034) was the sole protective factor (Table 3). A larger maximum diameter of LAA orifice (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.01–1.10]; P=0.018) remained significant for LAA patency with visible PDL, while LAAEI failed to predict it (OR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.44–1.02]; P=0.059; Table S7). Notably, no predictor was found in LAA patency without visible PDL (Table S8).

We further evaluated the predictors of both LAA patency and patency with visible PDL in the non‐LAAEI and LAAEI groups separately. In the non‐LAAEI group, older age was the only independent predictor of LAA patency (OR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.02–1.09]; P<0.001; Table S9) and patency with a visible PDL (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.01–1.08]; P=0.004; Table S10). Conversely, in the LAAEI group, logistic regression revealed that the maximum diameter of the LAA orifice, rather than the age, was the only risk factor for both LAA patency (OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.02–1.26]; P=0.017; Table S11) and patency with a visible PDL (OR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.02–1.27]; P=0.020; Table S12).

Further analyses were conducted to identify predictors according to PDL severity. The results showed that LAAEI independently predicted a reduced risk of mild PDL (OR, 0.38 [95% CI, 0.19–0.78]; P=0.008; Table S13), while no significant predictors were identified for a mild PDL in either non‐LAAEI (Table S14) or LAAEI groups (Table S15) when analyzed separately. For moderate‐to‐severe PDL (PDL >3 mm), multivariable analysis revealed that a larger maximum diameter of the LAA orifice (OR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.07–1.39]; P=0.002) was the only independent risk factor (Table S16).

Besides, univariate and multivariable analyses of predictors of residual patency were assessed separately in patients implanted with the Watchman or LACbes. For the Watchman cohort, no risk factor was predicted (Tables S17 and S19). In the LACbes cohort, results showed that LAAEI was a significant protective factor against LAA patency (OR, 0.28 [95% CI, 0.10–0.84]; P=0.023; Table S20), but no factor could predict patency with or without a visible PDL (Tables S21 and S22).

To assess the potential benefits of LAAEI, we reviewed 10 patients who underwent redo procedures ≈3 months after the operation (Table S23). LAAEI was inferred when LAA‐surrounding myocardium inside the lines of block demonstrated electrical isolation. Among them, 5 maintained persistent LAAEI at 3 months, with none demonstrating LAA patency. The remaining 5 patients showed recovered LAA conduction, of whom four had a visible PDL and 1 had complete closure despite electrical reconnection. These results suggested potential benefits of LAAEI.

Discussion

Results from this PSM cohort from the LAACablation registry showed that the overall incidence of LAA patency at 3‐month CCTA device surveillance was 46.3%, including 72.8% with a visible PDL and 27.2% without a visible PDL. Intraoperative LAAEI significantly reduced the occurrence of LAA patency. This protective effect was even evident for mild PDL, where LAAEI demonstrated a significant reduction. No significant intergroup differences were observed for moderate/severe PDL or patency without a visible PDL. In addition, LAAEI and a larger maximum diameter of the LAA orifice were independent predictors of LAA patency, while the maximum diameter of the LAA orifice could independently predict LAA patency with visible PDL. In the LAAEI group, the maximum diameter of the LAA orifice was the only independent risk factor of LAA patency; in the non‐LAAEI group, older age was independently associated with the occurrence of LAA patency. Also, device‐specific analyses revealed that the benefits of LAAEI were particularly evident when it came to the LACbes device. Our results thus highlighted the positive effect of LAAEI on concomitant device closure in terms of reducing or minimizing LAA patency.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest and first PSM study from a prospective registry database evaluating the impact of LAAEI on residual leaks after LAAC in the combined procedure. Previous research, such as the COMBINATION (Strategy Optimization for Combined Procedure of Left Atrial Appendage Closure and Catheter Ablation in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation) randomized clinical trial, 13 demonstrated that edema resolution from catheter ablation could result in increased residual leaks. On the contrary, our results revealed that LAAEI was able to reduce LAA patency, especially mild PDL, by >10%. One main reason was the contractile nature of the LAA, regarded as one of the mechanisms for PDL, which vanished post‐LAAEI. 14 Thus, LAAEI could provide a stable device–tissue contact during the critical early healing period. Besides, acute ablation‐induced edema might transiently reduce the apparent LAA orifice size, potentially leading to undersized device selection and finally resulting in residual patency. Taking this point into consideration, operators may intentionally choose an occluder with slightly larger devices when performing combined procedures. Therefore, these findings hinted that, in addition to reducing atrial arrhythmia recurrence as previously reported, 2 , 3 LAAEI could also help reduce or minimize a PDL in a combined ablation and LAAC procedure. It is suggested that patients undergoing combined LAAEI and LAAC were strongly more possible to discontinue oral anticoagulants after LAAC than those undergoing LAAC alone. Furthermore, analyses based on PDL severity indicated that the protective effect of LAAEI against PDL was most pronounced for mild PDL, suggesting that it was probably applicable within a certain scope. The reason for this may be that ablation‐induced edema could misguide some operators into choosing a relatively smaller size, while the effect of LAAEI could not compensate for the deviations. A previous study demonstrated that mild PDL was independently associated with ischemic stroke/transient ischemic attack/systemic embolism, and patients with a mild PDL had a poorer prognosis than those without PDL. 12 Thus, patients undergoing combined LAAEI and LAAC, due to a reduction of mild PDL, might account for the further reduced risk of thromboembolic events compared with those who did not undergo LAAEI.

Additionally, our results showed that the effect of LAAEI may differ between different types of occluders. Patients implanted with dual‐seal occluders, such as LACbes, may benefit more from the combined procedure. Our previous research demonstrated that incidences of adverse events increased with larger PDLs. 10 Thus, LAAEI may help improve the prognosis in patients undergoing a combined procedure. Further research is needed to explore methods for reducing LAA patency due to its complicated mechanisms.

This study also identified a larger maximum diameter of the LAA orifice as an independent risk factor for LAA patency and those with a visible PDL, consistent with previous reports. 10 , 11 , 15 , 16 , 17 These findings suggested that, in patients with a large LAA orifice diameter, device surveillance should be closely carried out. Also, we observed differential predictors between groups: In the non‐LAAEI group, the risk factor was older age, while a larger LAA orifice diameter independently predicted patency in the LAAEI group. This distinction suggested that LAAEI could be a strategy to reduce the occurrence of postprocedural LAA patency in older patients. Given that LAAEI could lead to acute edema of LAA‐adjacent structures, operators should consider selecting slightly larger occluders during combined procedures with additional LAAEI. 18

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study did not assess prognostic outcomes. Follow‐up studies are currently underway to evaluate long‐term safety and feasibility of the LAAEI in a 1‐stop procedure of standard ablation and LAAC. Second, the sample size was small. Although we found a lower rate of mild PDL after LAAEI and mild PDL was linked with a higher risk for ischemic stroke/transient ischemic attack/systemic embolism and poor outcome, these results need to be validated in future prospective trials with a larger patient cohort. Third, this study could not eliminate operators' subjective issue in the size choice on occluders after LAAEI. A randomized controlled trial based on an LAAC‐first approach may better help evaluate the effect of LAAEI. Fourth, the risk factors predicted by multivariable regression (except for LAAEI), such as old age and large LAA orifice diameter, were not modifiable. Close device surveillance remains the only way to mitigate adverse outcomes associated with LAA patency. The clinical significance of the established predictive model from this study also needs to be verified in future clinical studies. Fifth, dynamic device surveillance by CCTA is required to judge whether the impact of LAAEI on concomitant LAAC at 3 months after the procedure will last over time. Also, whether LAAEI would last and whether LAA function would be partially or completely restored remains unclear. Epicardial electrogram recordings from the occluded LAA's outer surface should be obtained to confirm LAAEI after LAAC. Further research based on redo procedures will help reinforce the conclusions. Finally, due to the widespread adoption of pulsed‐field ablation, additional studies must determine whether our findings can extend to this emerging technology.

Conclusions

LAAEI is effective in reducing or minimizing LAA patency detected by CCTA at 3 months after LAAC. In particular, the incidence of mild PDL, with width ≤3mm, could be significantly decreased. Patients with a larger LAA orifice require careful surveillance in case of residual patency. These findings therefore suggest that LAAEI could serve as a strategy to reduce the occurrence of postprocedural LAA patency after concomitant LAAC. In addition, operators should consider choosing a larger size of occluder to avoid postprocedural PDL after LAAEI.
