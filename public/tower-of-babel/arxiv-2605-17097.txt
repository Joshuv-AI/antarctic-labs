Gauss curvature solitons on invariant surfaces in the homogeneous space Sol
Rafael Belli, Rafael López

Abstract
. We classify invariant surfaces in the 3-dimensional solvable Lie group Sol 3 \text{Sol}_{3} that act as solitons for the Gauss curvature flow. We consider solitons associated with the canonical basis of Killing vector fields { F 1 , F 2 , F 3 } \{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\} , where F 1 F_{1} and F 2 F_{2} generate horizontal translations and F 3 F_{3} generates the scaling isometry. We establish rigidity results for F 3 F_{3} -invariant surfaces, proving that specific totally geodesic vertical planes are the only F 1 F_{1} - and F 2 F_{2} -solitons. For F 1 F_{1} -invariant surfaces, we establish the main geometric properties of F 2 F_{2} - and F 3 F_{3} -solitons in both the extrinsic and intrinsic Gauss curvature.

1.
Introduction and statement of the main results

The study of geometric flows has become a fundamental tool in modern Riemannian geometry,
providing deep insights into the classification and structure of manifolds. Among these, the Gauss curvature flow (GCF), where a surface evolves with a normal velocity proportional to its Gauss curvature
K
, has been
extensively studied since its introduction by Firey to model the shape of tumbling stones
[
4
]
. A smooth immersion
\psi:\Sigma\to\text{Sol}_{3}
evolves by the Gauss curvature flow if it satisfies
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=-KN
, where
K
is the (extrinsic or intrinsic) Gauss curvature and
N
is the unit normal vector field. Seminal papers on the GCF in Euclidean space include
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
13
]
.

A particularly interesting class of solutions are the solitons, which are surfaces whose shape remains fixed along the flow and evolve purely by the isometries of the ambient space. If we consider the isometries generated by a Killing vector field
X
, then solitons are characterized by the PDE

(1)

K=-\langle N,X\rangle.

In this paper, we study solitons of the GCF in the space
\text{Sol}_{3}
. The space
\text{Sol}_{3}
is one of the eight model geometries of Thurston, defined as a simply connected
3-dimensional solvable Lie group equipped with a left-invariant metric. Unlike spaces of constant sectional curvature,
\text{Sol}_{3}
possesses a more rigid isometry group of dimension 3, and its sectional curvatures are not constant.

The study of solitons is difficult in all its generality. In order to simplify this problem, it is natural to assume a certain type of invariance of the surface that allows the PDE (
1
) to be reduced to an ODE. In the case of
\text{Sol}_{3}
, an interesting family of surfaces consists of those that are invariant under a one-parameter group of isometries of
\text{Sol}_{3}
. Since the isometry group is of dimension
3
, essentially there are three types of invariant surfaces. Invariant surfaces with constant mean curvature or constant Gauss curvature have been studied in the literature: see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
14
]
.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the study of the GCF in
\text{Sol}_{3}
has not been considered yet. Regarding another geometric flow of great interest, the mean curvature flow, it is worth pointing out the remarkable paper by Pipoli, where he classified all invariant surfaces that are solitons under the mean curvature flow
[
10
]
. The present paper is an extension of this line of research to the GCF. Moreover, we will consider solitons where
K
is either the extrinsic or the intrinsic Gauss curvature of the surface.

In order to precisely state the results proved in this paper, we need to introduce the
\text{Sol}_{3}
space. Consider the model of
\text{Sol}_{3}
as the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
endowed with a group operation
defined by

(x,y,z)\ast(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=(x+e^{-z}x^{\prime},y+e^{z}y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}),

where
(x,y,z)
are Cartesian coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The metric that makes
\text{Sol}_{3}
a Riemannian manifold is the left-invariant metric given by

ds^{2}=e^{2z}dx^{2}+e^{-2z}dy^{2}+dz^{2}.

In the soliton equation (
1
), we can replace
X
with elements from a basis of Killing vector fields of
\text{Sol}_{3}
, which is formed by

F_{1}(x,y,z)=\partial_{x},\quad F_{2}=\partial_{y},\quad F_{3}(x,y,z)=-x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+\partial_{z}.

Definition 1.1
.

A surface
\Sigma
in
\text{Sol}_{3}
is called an
F_{k}
-soliton,
1\leq k\leq 3
, if
\Sigma
satisfies

(2)

K=-\langle N,F_{k}\rangle,

where
N
is the unit normal vector field of
\Sigma
and
K
is the extrinsic (
K_{ext}
) or intrinsic (
K_{int}
) Gauss curvature of
\Sigma
.

On the other hand, in
\text{Sol}_{3}
there are three types of surfaces invariant under a one-parameter group of isometries, corresponding to the isometries generated by the Killing vector fields
F_{k}
. According to each case, the invariant surfaces can be parametrized by

\displaystyle(s,t)\mapsto

\displaystyle(t,y(s),z(s)),

F_{1}
-invariant

\displaystyle(s,t)\mapsto

\displaystyle(x(s),t,z(s)),

F_{2}
-invariant

\displaystyle(s,t)\mapsto

\displaystyle(x(s)e^{-t},y(s)e^{t},t),

\displaystyle\mbox{$F_{3}$-invariant}.

The isometry of
\text{Sol}_{3}
defined by
(x,y,z)\mapsto(y,x,-z)
maps
F_{1}
-invariant surfaces into
F_{2}
-invariant surfaces and
vice versa
. In particular, we can restrict our study of invariant surface to only
F_{1}
and
F_{3}
-invariant surfaces.

A consequence of the definition of an invariant surface is that the vector field
F_{k}
is tangent to an
F_{k}
-invariant surface. Thus, the right-hand side of (
2
) vanishes identically. This proves that an
F_{k}
-invariant surface is an
F_{k}
-soliton if and only if it is extrinsically or intrinsically flat (Theorem
3.4
).

Although the study of solitons under the GCF in
\text{Sol}_{3}
is restricted to
F_{1}
- and
F_{3}
-invariant surfaces, each one has two types of solitons, and for each case, there are two types of Gauss curvature, namely, extrinsic and intrinsic. This yields a total of eight cases to study. However, for
F_{3}
-invariant surfaces, we have obtained a full classification in Section
4
. We have proved that the only extrinsic
F_{1}
-soliton (resp.
F_{2}
-soliton) is the vertical plane
y=0
(resp.
x=0
). Also, we prove that there are no intrinsic
F_{1}
-solitons nor
F_{2}
-solitons (Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
).

For
F_{1}
-invariant solitons, we have given a geometric description of the surfaces in Sections
5
and
6
, indicating their main properties. We have obtained an explicit parametrization of
F_{2}
-solitons in terms of an implicit equation (Theorems
5.4
and
5.7
). In the case of
F_{3}
-solitons, we demonstrate that horizontal and vertical planes
are the only trivial solutions, while any other generating curve inevitably reaches
a vertical tangent in finite arc-length, preventing its smooth extension: see Theorems
6.4
and
6.5
.

Before these results, we have deduced the parametrizations of invariant surfaces by calculating their Gauss curvature (Section
2
). In Section
3
, we derive the soliton equation (
2
).

2.
Invariant surfaces in
\text{Sol}_{3}
and its Gauss curvature

For the sake of completeness of the paper, in this section we recall the basic Riemannian geometry of
\text{Sol}_{3}
, emphasizing the space of Killing vector fields, and we deduce the invariant surfaces of
\text{Sol}_{3}
. Finally, we compute the Gauss curvature of these surfaces. These results are well-known in the literature. For example, invariant surfaces appear in
[
6
,
7
,
9
]
. The calculation of the Gauss curvature for
F_{1}
-invariant surfaces is done in
[
9
]
.

Let
\{e_{1}=\partial_{x},e_{2}=\partial_{y},e_{3}=\partial_{z}\}
be the canonical basis of vector fields of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
A global orthonormal frame for the Lie algebra of
\text{Sol}_{3}
consists of the left-invariant vector fields

E_{1}=e^{-z}e_{1},\quad E_{2}=e^{z}e_{2},\quad E_{3}=e_{3}.

Using Koszul’s formula, the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
of the metric
g
with respect to the orthonormal frame
\{E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}\}
is given by

(3)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\nabla_{E_{1}}E_{1}=-E_{3},&\nabla_{E_{1}}E_{2}=0,&\nabla_{E_{1}}E_{3}=E_{1}\\
\nabla_{E_{2}}E_{1}=0,&\nabla_{E_{2}}E_{2}=E_{3},&\nabla_{E_{2}}E_{3}=-E_{2}\\
\nabla_{E_{3}}E_{1}=0,&\nabla_{E_{3}}E_{2}=0,&\nabla_{E_{3}}E_{3}=0.\end{array}

The isometry group of
\text{Sol}_{3}
has dimension 3. The identity component is generated by the left translations

L_{(j,k,l)}:(x,y,z)\in\text{Sol}_{3}\rightarrow(j,k,l)\ast(x,y,z)\in\text{Sol}_{3}\text{,}

hence
\mbox{Iso}(\text{Sol}_{3})
is 3-dimensional. Given
p=(x,y,z)\in\text{Sol}_{3}
, to find a basis for this isometry group, let us consider a smooth curve
\alpha_{v}:(-\delta,\delta)\rightarrow\text{Sol}_{3}

given by
\alpha_{v}(t)=tv
. Since the map

\Phi_{p}:v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}(L_{\alpha_{v}(t)}p)\in\mathfrak{X}(\text{Sol}_{3})

is an isomorphism onto its image (the space of Killing vector fields), a
basis of Killing vector field is
\left\{F_{k}(p):=\Phi_{p}(e_{k})\colon 1\leq k\leq 3\right\}
. Note
that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha_{e_{1}}(t)}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(t,0,0)\ast(x,y,z)=(t+x,y,z)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha_{e_{2}}(t)}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(0,t,0)\ast(x,y,z)=(x,t+y,z)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha_{e_{2}}(t)}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(0,0,t)\ast(x,y,z)=(e^{-t}x,e^{t}y,t+z).

Differentiating and evaluating at
t=0
, a basis for all
Killing vector fields in
\text{Sol}_{3}
i given by

(4)

\begin{split}F_{1}(x,y,z)&=e_{1}=e^{z}E_{1},\\
F_{2}(x,y,z)&=e_{2}=e^{-z}E_{2}\\
F_{3}(x,y,z)&=-xe_{1}+ye_{2}+e_{3}=-xe^{z}E_{1}+ye^{-z}E_{2}+E_{3}.\end{split}

Definition 2.1
.

A surface
\Sigma
in
\text{Sol}_{3}
is said to be invariant under the action of a one-parameter group of isometries
\{\Phi_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
if
\Phi_{t}(\Sigma)=\Sigma
for all
t
. If this group is determined by the Killing vector field
F_{k}\in\mathfrak{X}(\text{Sol}_{3})
, we say that
\Sigma
is
F_{k}
-invariant.

In order to simplify the notation and present the results clearly, we will restrict our study to invariant surfaces for the basis
\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\}
of Killing vector fields. Furthermore, the isometry of
\text{Sol}_{3}
given by

(x,y,z)\mapsto(y,x,-z)

maps
F_{1}
-invariant surfaces to
F_{2}
-invariant surfaces and
vice versa
. Therefore, it is enough to consider
F_{k}
-invariant surfaces for
k=1,3
.

Parametrizations of
F_{k}
-invariant surfaces are the following (
[
7
,
9
]
):

(5)

\displaystyle\Psi(s,t)

\displaystyle=(t,y(s),z(s)),

\displaystyle k=1,

(6)

\displaystyle\Psi(s,t)

\displaystyle=(x(s)e^{-t},y(s)e^{t},t),

\displaystyle k=3.

where
x,y,z\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}
are smooth functions and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

We compute the Gauss curvature of both types of invariant surfaces. Although the calculations are known (
[
9
]
), we also provide the explicit expression for the unit normal vector field
N
, which is necessary in the soliton equation (
2
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
, parametrized by (
5
). Then
K_{ext}
and
K_{int}
of
\Sigma
are given by

\displaystyle K_{ext}

\displaystyle=-\frac{y^{\prime}e^{2z}(y^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}+2y^{\prime}z^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 3}e^{-2z})}{W^{4}},

\displaystyle K_{int}

\displaystyle=K_{ext}+\frac{y^{\prime 2}-z^{\prime 2}e^{2z}}{W^{2}},

where
W=\sqrt{y^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}e^{2z}}
, and the prime denotes differentiation with respect to the parameter
s
.

Proof.

We have
\Psi_{t}=\partial_{x}
and
\Psi_{s}=y^{\prime}\partial_{y}+z^{\prime}\partial_{z}
. The coefficients of the first fundamental form are

E=\langle\Psi_{t},\Psi_{t}\rangle=e^{2z}\quad F=\langle\Psi_{t},\Psi_{s}\rangle=0\quad G=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{s}\rangle=y^{\prime 2}e^{-2z}+z^{\prime 2}.

Let
W=\sqrt{EG-F^{2}}=\sqrt{y^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}e^{2z}}
.
The unit normal vector field
N
is

(7)

N=\frac{1}{W}\left(z^{\prime}e^{z}E_{2}-y^{\prime}E_{3}\right).

Next, we compute the coefficients of the second fundamental form
e,f,g
. We use the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
of
\text{Sol}_{3}
. Using (
3
), we have

\nabla_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{x}=-e^{2z}\partial_{z},\quad\nabla_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{y}=e^{-2z}\partial_{z},\quad\nabla_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{z}=\partial_{x},\quad\nabla_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{z}=-\partial_{y}

Taking the second derivatives of
\Psi
, we find

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{t}

\displaystyle=-e^{2z}\partial_{z}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{s}

\displaystyle=z^{\prime}\partial_{x}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{s}

\displaystyle=(y^{\prime\prime}-2y^{\prime}z^{\prime})\partial_{y}+\left(z^{\prime\prime}+y^{\prime 2}e^{-2z}\right)\partial_{z}.

Therefore

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{t},N\rangle=\frac{y^{\prime}e^{2z}}{W},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{s},N\rangle=0,

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{s},N\rangle=-\frac{1}{W}\left(y^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}+2y^{\prime}z^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 3}e^{-2z}\right).

The extrinsic Gauss curvature is given by
K_{ext}=\frac{eg-f^{2}}{EG-F^{2}}
. Since
f=0
, we have:

K_{ext}=-\frac{y^{\prime}e^{2z}\left(y^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}+2y^{\prime}z^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 3}e^{-2z}\right)}{W^{4}}.

To determine the intrinsic Gauss curvature
K_{int}
, we use the Gauss equation

K_{int}=K_{ext}+\bar{K}(\Pi),

where
\bar{K}(\Pi)
is the ambient sectional curvature of
\text{Sol}_{3}
evaluated on the tangent plane
\Pi
of
\Sigma
.
For a plane
\Pi
in
\text{Sol}_{3}
which is determined by its unit normal vector,
N=n_{1}E_{1}+n_{2}E_{2}+n_{3}E_{3}
, the sectional curvature
\bar{K}(\Pi)
is computed by the formula
\bar{K}(\Pi)=K_{23}n_{1}^{2}+K_{13}n_{2}^{2}+K_{12}n_{3}^{2}
,
where
K_{ij}
denotes the sectional curvature of the plane spanned by
\{E_{i},E_{j}\}
. Since,
K_{12}=1
,
K_{13}=-1
and
K_{23}=-1
, and from (
7
), we arrive at
\bar{K}(\Pi)=\frac{y^{\prime 2}-z^{\prime 2}e^{2z}}{W^{2}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be an

-invariant surface parametrized by (

3.
Solitons of Gauss curvature flow

Given an initial smooth surface
\psi\colon\Sigma\to\text{Sol}_{3}
, the Gauss curvature flow is a smooth map
\Psi:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times\Sigma\rightarrow\text{Sol}_{3}
that satisfies the following PDE

(11)

\left\{\begin{split}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}(t,p)&=-K(t,p),N(t,p)\\
\Psi(0,p)&=\psi(p).\end{split}\right.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\text{Sol}_{3})
be a Killing vector field and let
\{\Phi_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the one-parameter group of isometries associated with
X
, that is,
\Phi_{t}\colon\text{Sol}_{3}\to\text{Sol}_{3}
is an isometry such that
\Phi_{0}=\mbox{id}
and

(12)

\frac{\partial\Phi_{t}(p)}{\partial t}=X(\Phi_{t}(p)),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},p\in\Sigma.

Let us write

\Phi_{t}(p)=\Phi(t,p).

Definition 3.1
.

A family of immersions
\Psi:\Sigma\times[0,T)\to\text{Sol}_{3}
evolving by the GCF is said to be a soliton if there exists a one-parameter group of isometries
\{\Phi_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
of
\text{Sol}_{3}
generated by a Killing vector field
X
, such that the image
\Sigma_{t}=\Psi(\Sigma,t)
of
\Sigma
under the flow satisfies

\Sigma_{t}=\Phi_{t}(\Sigma_{0})

for all
t\in[0,T)
, where
\Sigma_{0}=\Psi(\Sigma,0)
is the initial surface.

To derive the stationary equation for such a soliton, we note that the condition
\Sigma_{t}=\Phi_{t}(\Sigma_{0})
implies that, up to a time-dependent reparametrization (diffeomorphism)
\sigma_{t}:\Sigma\to\Sigma
with
\sigma_{0}=id_{\Sigma}
, the flow can be written as

\Psi(t,p)=\Phi(t,\sigma_{t}(p)).

Differentiating this expression with respect to
t
and evaluating at
t=0
, we obtain by the chain rule

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}(0,p)&=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}(0,\sigma_{0}(p))+(d\Phi_{0})_{p}\left(d\psi\left(\left.\frac{\partial\sigma_{t}(p)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\right)\right)\\
&=X(p)+d\psi(V(p)),\end{split}

where we have used (
12
),
d\Phi_{0}=\mbox{id}
, and we have set
V(p)=\left.\frac{\partial\sigma_{t}(p)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}
.
Substituting into (
11
), we have

X(p)+d\psi_{p}(V_{p})=-K(p)N(p),

for all
p\in\Sigma
.
Multiplying by
N
and taking into account that
d\psi_{p}(V_{p})
is tangent to
\Sigma
, we arrive at the soliton equation (
2
). We have proved the following resut:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
and let
X
be a Killing vector field. Then
\Sigma
is a soliton with respect to
X
if and only if
\Sigma
satisfies

(13)

K=-\langle N,X\rangle.

In the following result, and for invariant surfaces, we obtain the expressions for
\langle N,X\rangle
for invariant surfaces, that is, the right-hand sides of (
13
). The proof is immediate from (
4
), (
7
) and (
9
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an

-invariant surface,

) or (

(1)

If

As a consequence of the fact that

Theorem 3.4
.

An

(16)

In the following two results, we investigate the case where the angle function

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
generated by a curve
\gamma
. Suppose that the angle function is a constant,

(1)

The surface
\Sigma
cannot be an extrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton.

(2)

If
\Sigma
is an intrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton, then

is a horizontal plane

.

Proof.

(1)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an extrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton.
Substituting
\theta=c
and
\theta^{\prime}=0
in (
17
), we get
\cos^{2}c=\sin c\,e^{-z(s)}
. In consequence,
\sin c\neq 0
. Since the right-hand side is constant,

must be constant, yielding

(2)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an intrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton. Substituting
\theta=c
in (

), we obtain
\sin^{2}c=\sin c\,e^{-z(s)}
.
If

is constant, but this gives a contradiction with the identity

.
Therefore, we must have

for some constant

. The condition

, which integrates to

∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
generated by a curve
\gamma
. Suppose the angle function satisfies
\theta^{\prime}(s)=c
for some non-zero constant

. Then
\Sigma
can neither be an extrinsic nor an intrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton.

Proof.

Assume
\theta^{\prime}(s)=c\neq 0
, which implies that
\theta(s)=cs+\theta_{0}
.

(1)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an extrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton.
Equation (
17
) becomes
c\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta=\sin\theta\,e^{-z}
.
Since
\theta(s)
is non-constant, we deduce that
\sin\theta\neq 0
almost everywhere. Differentiating both sides gives

c(-c\sin\theta-2\sin\theta\cos\theta)=c\cos\theta\,e^{-z}-\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-z}.

To eliminate the
e^{-z}
terms, we multiply (
17
) by
\sin\theta
and substitute
\sin\theta\,e^{-z}=c\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta
from the original equation,

-c^{2}\sin^{2}\theta-2c\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta=c\cos\theta(c\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta)-\sin^{2}\theta(c\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta).

This equation reduces to
-8c^{2}-8c\cos\theta-\cos(4\theta)+1=0
. This is a contradiction.

(2)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an intrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton. Equation (
18
) gives
c\cos\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=\sin\theta\,e^{-z}
.
Differentiating both sides yields

c(-c\sin\theta+2\sin\theta\cos\theta)=c\cos\theta\,e^{-z}-\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{-z}.

Multiplying by
\sin\theta
and substituting
\sin\theta\,e^{-z}=c\cos\theta+\sin^{2}\theta
gives

-c^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+2c\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta=c\cos\theta(c\cos\theta+\sin^{2}\theta)-\sin^{2}\theta(c\cos\theta+\sin^{2}\theta),

or equivalently,

-8c^{2}+4c\cos\theta-4c\cos(3\theta)-4\cos(2\theta)+\cos(4\theta)+3=0.

This is a contradiction.

∎

We now study the general case for the angle function
\theta(s)
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(y(s),z(s))
be a generating curve of an
F_{1}
-invariant extrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton. Then
\gamma
is a global graph on the
y
-axis, and the function

is given by the implicit integral

\int\frac{e^{-z}}{1+W\left(Ae^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2z}}\right)}dz=s+C,

where
A,C
are constants and
W
is the Lambert function. The coordinate
y(s)
is determined, up to a constant, by

y(s)=\int\pm e^{z(s)}\sqrt{1-z^{\prime}(s)^{2}}\,ds.

Proof.

Suppose that
\gamma^{\prime}
is vertical at some point
s
. Then
\cos\theta(s)=0
and (
17
) gives a contradiction. This proves that
\gamma
is a graph on the
y
-axis. On the other hand,
z^{\prime\prime}=\theta^{\prime}\cos\theta
and
\cos^{2}\theta=1-z^{\prime 2}
. Thus equation (
17
) becomes

z^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime 2}-z^{\prime}e^{-z}+1=0.

Since the independent parameter
s
does not appear explicitly in (

), we can reduce its order by treating

as the independent variable by introducing the function
v(z)=z^{\prime}(s)
, with
v=v(z)
. Using the chain rule, the second derivative of

with respect to

can be rewritten as

(21)

vv^{\prime}-v^{2}-ve^{-z}+1=0,\quad\text{with }v\neq 0.

By applying the change of variable

, the ODE is

hh^{\prime}e^{2z}=h-1.

Solving by separation of variables, we arrive at

h+\log(h-1)=-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2z}+C_{1}.

We now use the Lambert

function, which is defined as the inverse of
x\mapsto xe^{x}
. Thus we need to write the above equation as

h-1+\log(h-1)=-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2z}+C_{1}-1,

or equivalently,

(h-1)e^{h-1}=A\exp(-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2z}),\quad A=e^{C_{1}-1}.

Thus

h(z)=1+W\left(Ae^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2z}}\right).

Since

, then

z^{\prime}=e^{z}\left[1+W\left(A\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2z}\right)\right)\right]

This gives (
19
) by separation of variables.

∎

We now describe the geometric properties of the generating curves of
F_{1}
-invariant intrinsic
F_{2}
-solitons. We restrict this study to the case where the generating curve has a point with a horizontal tangent vector.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant extrinsic
F_{2}
-soliton generated by a curve
\gamma
which is a solution of (

)–(

) with initial conditions

,

and

. Then

has the following geometric properties:

(1)

The curve
\gamma
cannot be a horizontal line.

(2)

The point

is a strict global maximum for the height function
z(s)
.

(3)

For
s>0
,
\gamma
ends at a finite arc-length and a finite height with vertical velocity (

(4)

For

,
\gamma
descends infinitely (

Proof.

As established in (

), if

, then
v=v(s)
satisfies the second-order equation
v^{\prime}=v^{2}+ve^{-z}-1
. Let us introduce the function
w(s)=e^{-z(s)}>0
and define a planar autonomous dynamical system by

(22)

\begin{cases}v^{\prime}=v^{2}+vw-1,\\
w^{\prime}=-vw.\end{cases}

The asymptotic behavior as

corresponds to the limit

. The equilibrium points of the system are
P_{1}=(1,0)
and
P_{2}=(-1,0)
. Since

and these points represent generating curves that are vertical lines. By computing the Jacobian matrix associated with (

and

are saddle points.

Denote by
\eta(s)=(v(s),w(s))
the trajectory associated with a solution
(y(s),z(s),\theta(s))
of (
8
)–(
17

(v,w)\in\Theta:=[-1,1]\times(0,\infty)
. Note that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
belong to the boundary of
\Theta
.

(1)

If
\gamma
is a horizontal line, then
v=0
and thus
v^{\prime}=0
. However, the first equation of (
22
) yields a contradiction. The result is also a consequence of Theorem
5.2
.

(2)

At
s=0
, we have
z^{\prime}(0)=0
and by (

. Thus

is a strict local maximum. Since

for any critical point, no more local maximum can exist by the Rolle’s theorem, making
s=0
the strict global maximum.

(3)

For
s>0
,
v(s)
initially becomes negative because
v^{\prime}(0)=-1
. As long as
v\in(-1,0)
and
w>0
, the first equation of (
22

v^{\prime}=(v^{2}-1)+vw<0
. Furthermore, since
w^{\prime}=-vw>0

is strictly increasing, making

even more negative. Consequently,

strictly decreases without asymptotic limits in

. Since
v(s)\geq-1
, the trajectory
\eta
must reach the boundary

of

. Evaluating the vector field at this boundary gives

. Since
\eta
is strictly transverse to

, it reaches this state in finite time

at some finite value

. Geometrically, this implies

cannot be smoothly extended further attaining a height

. Moreover, since

becomes vertical.

(4)

For

, since

and

must be strictly positive. Since

\lim_{s\to-\infty}vw=1

with respect to

Integrating from

\begin{split}\log|f(0)-1|-\log|f(s)-1|&=\int_{s}^{0}w(\tau)\,d\tau\geq w(0)\int_{s}^{0}e^{-\tau}\,d\tau\\

Since

Finally, from the claim,

\frac{dz}{dy}=\frac{z^{\prime}(s)}{y^{\prime}(s)}=\frac{v(s)}{\frac{1}{w(s)}\sqrt{1-v(s)^{2}}}=\frac{v(s)w(s)}{\sqrt{1-v(s)^{2}}}\to 1,\quad\mbox{as $s\to-\infty$}.

∎

Remark 5.6
.

The previous theorem describes the family of generating curves that possess a point with a horizontal tangent (
v=0
). We may use an argument by contradiction to prove that any trajectory must contain points with a horizontal tangent. The reasoning would be the following. Suppose there exists a trajectory
\eta(s)=(v(s),w(s))
such that
v\not=0
for all
s
. Without loss of generality, suppose
v>0
. By the second equation of (
22
),
w
is monotonic decreasing, and because
w>0
, there is some
w_{\infty}\geq 0
such that
w\to w_{\infty}
and
w^{\prime}\to 0
. If
w_{\infty}>0
,
the first equation of (
22
) implies that
v\to 0
. Now, the first equation of (
22
)
yields
v^{\prime}\sim-1
at the limit. Thus, necessarily,
\eta
must cross the line
v=0
, obtaining a contradiction. Thus, we
have proved that
w_{\infty}=0
. However, we can neither deduce that
v\to 0
nor establish
the monotonicity of
v
using the first equation of (
22
). This is a situation that
really can occur. This trajectory corresponds to the stable manifolds of the saddle points

P_{1}=(1,0)
or
P_{2}=(-1,0)
. For the generating curve, a branch of it strictly ascends
without ever attaining a global maximum. Moreover, its tangent vector tends to be vertical
because
v\to 1
(
\theta\to\pi/2
). An analogous strictly decreasing solution exists associated with
P_{2}
.

Now we do a similar treatment for intrinsic
F_{2}
-solitons.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
. Then
\Sigma
is an intrinsic

-soliton if and only if the height function

satisfies

(23)

z^{\prime\prime}+z^{\prime 2}-z^{\prime}e^{-z}=0.

Moreover, either
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane or
z(s)
is implicitly determined by

(24)

s=e^{-C}\operatorname{Ei}(z+C)+A,

where
\operatorname{Ei}(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{u}\frac{e^{t}}{t}dt
is the exponential integral function and

is a constant. In the latter case, a branch of

is asymptotic to a horizontal line at finite height

and the height of the other branch goes to

Proof.

Using that

, equation (
23
) is an immediate consequence of (

). It is known by Table

that the horizontal planes are intrinsic
F_{2}
-solitons.

We prove that if
\gamma
has a point, say

where
z^{\prime}(0)=0
, then
\gamma
is a horizontal line, hence
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane. Without loss of generality, assume
\theta(0)=0
. In such a case, the triple of functions
(e^{z_{0}}+y_{0},z_{0},0)
is a solution of the system with the case initial conditions, and by uniqueness,
\gamma(s)=(0,e^{z_{0}}+y_{0},z_{0})
. This proves that
\gamma
is a horizontal line.

From the above argument, if
\Sigma
is an
F_{1}
-invariant surface which is also an intrinsic
F_{1}
-soliton and if, in addition,
\Sigma
is not a horizontal plane, then the coordinate

of its generating curve
\gamma
satisfies

for all

. Let

. By the chain rule,

, so equation (

) transforms into

v\frac{dv}{dz}+v^{2}-ve^{-z}=0.

Since

, dividing by

yields the first-order linear ODE

\frac{dv}{dz}+v=e^{-z}.

Multiplying by the integrating factor

, the equation becomes

. Integrating with respect to

, we find

(25)

v(z)e^{z}=z+C,

where

is a constant. Thus,

. Separating variables and integrating yields

(26)

\int ds=\int\frac{e^{z}}{z+C}dz.

The change of variable

leads directly to

s=e^{-C}\int\frac{e^{u}}{u}du=e^{-C}\operatorname{Ei}(z+C)+A,

where
A\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant of integration. This proves (

).

We now examine the geometric properties of
\gamma
. For this, we use the properties of the function
\operatorname{Ei}(x)

[
1
]
. Since the integrand in the right-hand side of (
26
) is not integrable as
z\to+\infty
, the arc length
s(z)
diverges to
+\infty
. For large positive values of the argument, the asymptotic expansion of the exponential integral is
\operatorname{Ei}(x)\sim\frac{e^{x}}{x}
. Therefore, we have
z\to+\infty
as
s(z)\to+\infty
. Furthermore, the derivative of
z
is
z^{\prime}=(z+C)e^{-z}
, which tends to
0
as
z\to\infty
. This means that its slope becomes nearly horizontal at infinite height.

On the other hand, the condition
|z^{\prime}|=|\sin\theta|\leq 1
must hold. Equation (
25
) implies that
z
must satisfy
(z+C)e^{-z}\leq 1
for all
z
in the domain of the solution. Since the function
z\mapsto(z+C)e^{-z}
attains its maximum value
e^{C-1}
at
z=1-C
, a global solution exists for all
z>-C
if and only if
e^{C-1}\leq 1
, which leads to
C\leq 1
. Under this condition, the logarithmic divergence of the integral
\int\frac{e^{z}}{z+C}dz
as
z\to-C^{+}
proves that the generating curve is asymptotic to the horizontal line
z=-C
as
s\to-\infty
. See Fig.
1
, right.

We first obtain the equations of extrinsic and intrinsic
F_{3}
-solitons in terms of the angle
\theta
of the generating curve.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
generated by a curve parametrized by arc-length according to (
8
). Suppose that
\Sigma
is an
F_{3}
-soliton.

(1)

If
\Sigma
is an extrinsic soliton, then

(27)

\theta^{\prime}\cos\theta=y\sin\theta\,e^{-z}-\cos\theta-\cos^{2}\theta.

(2)

If
\Sigma
is an intrinsic soliton, then

(28)

\theta^{\prime}\cos\theta=y\sin\theta\,e^{-z}-\cos\theta-\sin^{2}\theta.

From now on, we will assume that the generating curves of
F_{1}
-invariant surfaces are parametrized according to (
8
).

In the following, we study the case where the function
\theta^{\prime}
is constant. First, we assume
\theta(s)=c
(Theorem
6.2
) and then
\theta(s)=cs
(Theorem
6.3
).

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
generated by a curve
\gamma(s)
. Suppose that
\theta(s)=c
.

(1)

If
\Sigma
is an extrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton, then
\Sigma
is either a horizontal plane
z=z_{0}
or the vertical plane
y=0
.

(2)

The surface
\Sigma
cannot be an intrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton.

Proof.

(1)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an extrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton. If
\theta=c
, then (
27
) gives

\cos^{2}c=y(s)\sin c\,e^{-z(s)}-\cos c.

We analyze two cases based on
\sin c
.

(a)

If
\sin c=0
, then
c=0
or
c=\pi
. The equation reduces to
\cos^{2}c=-\cos c
. Since
\cos c\in\{1,-1\}
, the only valid solution is
\cos c=-1
, which implies
c=\pi
. Consequently,
z^{\prime}(s)=\sin\pi=0
, so
z(s)=z_{0}
for some constant
z_{0}
. The condition
y^{\prime}(s)=e^{z_{0}}\cos 0
integrates to
y(s)=-se^{z_{0}}+y_{0}
. This generates a horizontal plane
z=z_{0}
.

(b)

If
\sin c\neq 0
, we can isolate
y(s)
:

y(s)e^{-z(s)}=\frac{\cos c-\cos^{2}c}{\sin c}\equiv C,

where
C
is a constant. Thus,
y(s)=C\,e^{z(s)}
. Differentiating with respect to
s
gives
y^{\prime}(s)=Cz^{\prime}(s)e^{z(s)}=C\sin c\,e^{z(s)}
. However, from the arc-length parametrization, we know
y^{\prime}(s)=\cos c\,e^{z(s)}
. Equating both expressions yields
\cos c=C\sin c
. Substituting the value of
C
into this relation gives, after simplification,
\cos^{2}c=0
.
Therefore,
c=\pm\pi/2
and
\sin c=\pm 1
. Since
\cos c=0
, we have
C=0
, which implies
y(s)=0
. This generates the vertical plane
y=0
.

(2)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an intrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton. If
\theta=c
, then (
28
) becomes

\sin^{2}c=y(s)\sin c\,e^{-z(s)}-\cos c.

Again, we analyze two cases:

(a)

If
\sin c=0
, then
\cos c=\pm 1
. The equation becomes
0=\pm 1
, which is a contradiction.

(b)

If
\sin c\neq 0
, then

y(s)e^{-z(s)}=\frac{\cos c(1+\sin^{2}c)}{\sin c}\equiv C,

for some constant
C
. Thus,
y(s)=C\,e^{z(s)}
. Differentiating yields
y^{\prime}(s)=C\sin c\,e^{z(s)}
. Equating this to
y^{\prime}(s)=\cos c\,e^{z(s)}
gives
\cos c=C\sin c
. Substituting
C
back into this relation yields
\sin^{2}c=0
,
which contradicts our assumption that
\sin c\neq 0
. Thus, no such surface exists.

∎

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
generated by a curve
\gamma
. Suppose the angle function satisfies
\theta^{\prime}(s)=c
for some non-zero constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\Sigma
can be neither an extrinsic nor an intrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton.

Proof.

Assume
\theta^{\prime}(s)=c\neq 0
, which implies that
\theta(s)=cs+\theta_{0}
is non-constant.

(1)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an extrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton.
Substituting
\theta^{\prime}=c
in (
27
) yields

ye^{-z}\sin\theta=(c+1)\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta.

Differentiating this equation with respect to
s
and simplifying gives

\cos\theta\sin^{2}\theta+\left((c+1)\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta\right)(c\cos\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)=-c(c+1)\sin^{2}\theta-2c\cos\theta\sin^{2}\theta,

or equivalently,
8c^{2}+8c\cos\theta+8c+\cos(4\theta)-1=0
. This yields a contradiction.

(2)

Suppose
\Sigma
is an intrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton.
Substituting
\theta^{\prime}=c
in (
28
) yields

ye^{-z}\sin\theta=(c+1)\cos\theta+\sin^{2}\theta.

Differentiating this with respect to
s
gives

-\cos^{4}\theta+2c\cos^{3}\theta+2\cos^{2}\theta-2c\cos\theta+(c^{2}+c-1)=0.

This can be written as

8c^{2}-4c\cos\theta+4c\cos(3\theta)+8c+4\cos(2\theta)-\cos(4\theta)-3=0,

obtaining a contradiction.

∎

We now consider the general case for the function
\theta(s)
. A key difference from the
F_{2}
-soliton case
is that the function
y
appears explicitly in (
27
) and (
28
), in contrast to
Eqs. (

) and (

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\gamma
be the generating curve of an
F_{1}
-invariant extrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton.

(1)

Horizontal planes
z=z_{0}
are extrinsic
F_{3}
-solitons, which correspond to
\theta(s)=\pi
in (
27
).

(2)

The plane
y=0
is the unique solution generated by a vertical line. Any other generating curve is a graph over the
y
-axis.

Proof.

(1)

This follows directly from Theorem
6.2
. Note that, viewing a horizontal line as a solution of (
8
)-(
27
), then
\theta(s)=\pi
.

(2)

We know that the plane
y=0
is an extrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton by Theorem
6.2
. Suppose that
\gamma
is a generating curve such that at
s=0
,
\gamma^{\prime}(0)
is a vertical vector. Then the triple of functions
(y(s),z(s),\theta(s))
is a solution of (
8
)-(
27
) with initial conditions
y(0)=y_{0}
,
z(0)=z_{0}
and
\theta(0)=\pi/2
or
\theta(0)=-\pi/2
. Then (
27
) at
s=0
implies
y_{0}e^{-z_{0}}=0
, hence
y_{0}=0
. Now, it is immediate that the triple of functions
(0,\pm s+z_{0},\pm\frac{\pi}{2})
is a solution of (
8
)-(
27
) with the same initial conditions. By uniqueness,
\gamma(s)=(0,0,\pm s+z_{0})
, proving that
\gamma
is the vertical line
y=0
.

As a consequence, any other solution must be a graph over the
y
-axis, proving the result.

∎

The variety of generating curves depends on the initial conditions for the system (
8
)-(
27
). This can be, in part, simplified by defining the following dynamical system and studying its phase portrait. Let
u(s)=y(s)e^{-z(s)}
.
By differentiating
u
, we find
u^{\prime}=y^{\prime}e^{-z}-yz^{\prime}e^{-z}=\cos\theta-u\sin\theta
. Similarly, (
27
) can be written in terms of
u
as
-\theta^{\prime}\cos\theta=-u\sin\theta+\cos\theta+\cos^{2}\theta
. We have constructed the dynamical system

(29)

\begin{cases}u^{\prime}=\cos\theta-u\sin\theta\\
\theta^{\prime}=u\tan\theta-1-\cos\theta,\quad\theta\neq\pm\pi/2.\end{cases}

To analyze the behavior at the singular lines
\theta=\pm\pi/2
, we introduce the time reparametrization
d\tau=\frac{ds}{\cos\theta}
to desingularize the vector field, obtaining the smooth system:

(30)

\begin{cases}\frac{du}{d\tau}=\cos^{2}\theta-u\sin\theta\cos\theta\\
\frac{d\theta}{d\tau}=u\sin\theta-\cos\theta-\cos^{2}\theta.\end{cases}

Setting the derivatives to zero yields the equilibria
P_{1}=(0,\pi/2)
and
P_{2}=(0,-\pi/2)
, as well as their
2\pi
-multiplies in the second coordinate. These points correspond strictly to the vertical plane
y=0
(Theorem
6.2
). In Fig.
2
, we depict the phase plane, indicating the vertical line
u=0
, and the horizontal lines
\theta=\pm\pi/2
and
\theta=\pm\pi
.

The Jacobian matrix of (
30
) is

J(u,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\cos\theta&-2\sin\theta\cos\theta-u(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)\\
\sin\theta&u\cos\theta+\sin\theta+2\cos\theta\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}.

Evaluating at
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, we obtain:

J(P_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad J(P_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues are
\{1,0\}
for
P_{1}
and
\{-1,0\}
for
P_{2}
. In particular,
P_{1}
has an unstable manifold for the eigenvalue
1
and
P_{2}
a stable manifold for the eigenvalue
-1
.

Recall that
\cos\theta\not=0
, and thus, although the trajectories cross the horizontal line
\theta=\pm\pi/2
, the generating curve cannot be extended further. For example, if
u=0
, which is equivalent to considering
y_{0}=0
, and if
\theta(0)\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
, the trajectory in the phase plane crosses the lines
\theta=\pm\pi/2
for some values of
u
. This implies that the generating curve is defined in some bounded interval of the
y
-line (because
u
is finite) and, at the endpoints, the curve
\gamma
has vertical tangent vectors. See Fig.
2
, left.

).

However, there are other trajectories that cross
\theta=\pi/2
but not
\theta=-\pi/2
, with the other branch of the trajectory being asymptotic to the line
\theta=\pi
. This implies that the solution ends vertically at a finite parameter
s
for one branch, but the other branch goes to
y\to\infty
. Also, other trajectories remain in the strip
\pi/2<\theta<\pi
, which implies that
\gamma
is a graph over the entire
y
-axis, being asymptotic to horizontal line at infinity. See Fig.

.

We now consider intrinsic
F_{3}
-solitons.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
F_{1}
-invariant surface in
\text{Sol}_{3}
. Then
\Sigma
is an intrinsic
F_{3}
-soliton if and only if the angle
\theta(s)
and the scaled horizontal coordinate
u(s)=y(s)e^{-z(s)}
, satisfy the following autonomous planar system:

(31)

\begin{cases}u^{\prime}=\cos\theta-u\sin\theta\\
\theta^{\prime}=u\tan\theta-1-\sin\theta\tan\theta,\quad\text{with }\theta\neq\pm\pi/2.\end{cases}

Furthermore:

(1)

The surface
\Sigma
cannot be a horizontal plane.

(2)

There are generating curves that reach a vertical tangency in finite arc length
s
. At these points, the solution cannot be extended.

(3)

There exist solutions that are entire graphs over the
y
-axis.

Proof.

The derivation of (
31
) is analogous to that of the previous theorem. We consider (
31
). Since it is singular at
\theta=\pm\pi/2
, we apply the reparametrization
d\tau=\frac{ds}{\cos\theta}
to obtain a smooth, globally defined vector field

(32)

\begin{cases}\frac{du}{d\tau}=\cos^{2}\theta-u\sin\theta\cos\theta\\
\frac{d\theta}{d\tau}=u\sin\theta-\cos\theta-\sin^{2}\theta\end{cases}

To find the equilibria, we set the derivatives to zero, finding that the equilibrium points are
Q_{1}=(1,\pi/2)
and
Q_{2}=(-1,-\pi/2)
. See Fig.
4
.

Figure 4.
The phase portrait of (
32
), indicating the horizontal lines
\theta=\pm\frac{\pi}{2},0,\pi
and
\frac{3\pi}{2}
. Note that there are trajectories that remain in the strip
\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\frac{3\pi}{2}
. However, no such trajectories remain in the strip
-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}
.

To determine the stability, we compute the Jacobian matrix of (
32
):

J(u,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\cos\theta&-2\sin\theta\cos\theta-u(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)\\
\sin\theta&u\cos\theta+\sin\theta-2\sin\theta\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}

Evaluating at
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, we obtain

J(Q_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad J(Q_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Both points are saddle points because their eigenvalues are real numbers with opposite signs.

Unlike the extrinsic case, the equilibrium points
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
do not correspond to any exact geometric solution. Indeed, a constant solution at
Q_{1}
would require
\theta=\pi/2
(hence
y^{\prime}=0
and
z^{\prime}=1
) and simultaneously
u=1
(hence
y=e^{z}
). Differentiating the latter yields
y^{\prime}=e^{z}\neq 0
, which is a contradiction.

(1)

This is a consequence of Theorem
6.2
.

(2)

Consider initial conditions
\theta(0)=0
,
y(0)=0
. Then the corresponding trajectory in the phase plane crosses the lines
\theta=\pm\pi/2
. However, the solution
\gamma
cannot be extended past
\cos\theta=0
. Since this occurs at some finite values of
y
, then
\gamma
is defined on a a finite interval and, at the endpoints, the tangent vectors are vertical (Fig.
5
, left).

(3)

Trajectories that remain entirely within the strip
\pi/2<\theta<3\pi/2
for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
correspond to curves where
\cos\theta
remains strictly negative. For these trajectories, as
\tau\to\pm\infty
, the scaled coordinate
|u|
diverges and the angle
\theta
approaches
\pi
. Since
\cos\theta\to-1
in both limits, the relation
ds=\cos\theta\,d\tau
implies that the integral for the arc length diverges in both directions. Thus, the generating curve is defined for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and, since
y^{\prime}(s)=e^{z}\cos\theta
never vanishes and does not change sign, the solution constitutes an entire graph over the
y
-axis. See Fig.
5
, center.

∎

Unlike the extrinsic case, the generating curve may not be a graph over the
y
-axis. For example, if at some point the tangent vector is vertical, this implies
\theta=\pm\pi/2
. Then (
28
) implies
ye^{-z}=\pm 1
at that point. See Fig.
5
, right. In the phase plane of (
32
) (Fig.
4
), the corresponding trajectory comes from
u=\infty
towards the equilibrium point
Q_{1}
and then comes back to
u\to\infty
remaining the trajectory in a horizontal strip.
