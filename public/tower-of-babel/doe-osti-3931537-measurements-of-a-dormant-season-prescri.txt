 Executive Summary
The United States Forest Service at the Savannah River Site (SRS) routinely burns vegetation to manage timber
compartments around SRS and reduce the risk of wildfires. This provides a unique opportunity to study the
meteorological parameters surrounding a prescribed burn. Meteorological measurements were conducted around a
prescribed burn at SRS on February 24, 2026. The burn took place at the Forested Advanced Tracing in the Environment
research site (FATE) that includes an instrumented meteorological research tower. Additional measurements were made
at additional locations around the fire region using portable meteorological stations and drone flights from two different
drones. Measurements included wind speed, wind direction, temperature, relative humidity, pressure, soil temperature,
soil moisture, precipitation, soil heat flux, H2O and CO2 concentrations. Prior to the burn, LiDAR measurements were
performed in the growing and dormant season to characterize the vegetation. Additional drone imagery provided
thermal tracking of the burn. This report provides an overview of these measurements. The data will provide important
input and validation information for the QUIC-Fire model and other fire spread modeling efforts.

1.0 Introduction
Fire spread modeling is important for simulating the impacts of fuel structure on local winds and fire
behavior. Results from these models are significant for improving planning, evaluating, and designing
burn plans by including complex ignition patterns and coupling fire-atmospheric interactions [1].
However, validating these models requires representative burn data. The United States Forest Service
Savannah River office (USFS-SR) at the Savannah River Site (SRS) routinely burns vegetation to manage
timber compartments around SRS during both dormant and active growing seasons. Dormant season
burns are performed to reduce the risk of wildfires by reducing fuel loading; to reduce the litter layer for
local wildlife; and to remove undergrowth to allow increased sunlight penetration for growth of native
species [2]. Growing season burns have shown slower regrowth which can aid in longer term
management objectives for pine forests by reducing hardwood competition [3].
The Forested Advanced Tracing in the Environment research site (FATE) is located in a pine forest at SRS.
At this site the Atmospheric Technologies Group (ATG) at the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL)
maintains the Flux Tower, a 30 m research tower outfitted with multiple levels of meteorological
instruments. This site and tower provide a unique opportunity to study the meteorological parameters
surrounding a prescribed burn. The Flux Tower has instrumentation mounted at 2 meters (m) and 30 m
above the ground. Measurements from the tower include wind speed, wind direction, temperature,
relative humidity (RH), and carbon dioxide/water vapor (CO2/H2O) concentration; associated heat,
moisture, and carbon fluxes can be estimated from these measurements. Ground-level measurements
at the FATE site include soil moisture, soil temperature, soil heat flux, surface pressure and precipitation.
Additionally, SRNL has multiple measurement capabilities including portable meteorological stations,
which measure wind speed, wind direction, temperature, RH, pressure, and precipitation, and UAS
measurement capabilities for describing atmospheric response to prescribed burns. Since data collected
around prescribed burns at SRS is representative of wildfire conditions, it is important to use the data to
validate wildfire spread models.
In this study, measurements from the meteorological tower (Flux Tower), three portable stations, and
drone flights were used to characterize the atmospheric conditions surrounding a prescribed burn. One
portable meteorological station was planned to be placed upwind of the prescribed burn, and the other
two portable stations were located in expected downwind locations. Meteorological measurements
were compared to see the differences in wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and RH prior to a
prescribed burn and after a prescribed burn. These measurements can subsequently be used as inputs
to the QUIC-Fire model or for model validation. The QUIC-Fire model uses 3-D fuel inputs, similar to the
computational fluid dynamics model FIRETEC, to simulate the effects of fuel structure on local winds and
fire behavior [1]. This model improves understanding of complex ignition patterns and fire-atmospheric
feedbacks to aid in comparing, evaluating, and designing burn plans.

3.0 Pre-Burn SRS Field Measurements
LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) data were collected to create 3D (three-dimensional) point clouds
of burn areas adjacent to the Flux Tower. 3D point clouds provide spatial representations of vegetation
structure that can be used to improve accuracy of the fire spread models through fuel identification.
SRNL uses a YellowScan Mapper+ LiDAR, which includes a 905 nm laser sensor that captures more than
240,000 pulses per second (Figure 3-1).

2025 and during the dormant season in February 2026. An example of the LiDAR data is shown in Figure

below the main canopy. During post-processing, points were additionally classified into two categories:

4.0 Burn SRS Field Measurements
In addition to measurements from meteorological stations/tower, a drone was used to collect
meteorological data at an altitude of 50 m above ground level (AGL). Figure 2-1 shows the locations for
the three 3 m meteorological stations (MET1-MET3), the multi-level meteorological tower (Flux Tower),
and the drone takeoff/landing pad (Drone Control).
4.1 Portable Meteorological Stations
Three portable meteorological stations were placed around the prescribed burn. One station was
planned to be placed upwind of the prescribed burn (MET3) and the other two stations were placed at
expected downwind locations (MET1 and MET2). These meteorological stations were placed at these
locations to characterize meteorological parameters around the prescribed burn. Meteorological
stations consisted of a tripod 3 m tall outfitted with a Campbell Scientific METSENS-600 instrument. This
instrument makes 5-minute average measurements of temperature, RH, wind speed, wind direction,
pressure, and precipitation. The METSENS-600 measures more frequently than 5-minutes and uses
these measurements to calculate standard deviations. The measurements are collected using a
Campbell Scientific Datalogger (CR-1000). Figure 4-1 shows the portable meteorological station MET2 as
installed at the FATE site.

Figure 4-1. Portable meteorological station (MET2). The METSENS 600 instrument is mounted at 3 m.

4.2 Meteorological Tower
The prescribed burn took place near a stationary meteorological tower, the Flux Tower (shown in Figure
4-2) [4]. The Flux Tower is located at SRS near the northeast site boundary surrounded by forest. The
Flux Tower is 29 m tall with multiple levels of instrumentation. An RM Young 81000 is located at 30 m
and measures the three orthogonal wind components. A CSAT-3 and CSAT-3b are located at 28 m and 2
m, respectively, which also measure the three orthogonal wind components and virtual temperature.
The Flux Tower was constructed to aid in measurements of H2O/CO2 flux in a forested location.
Currently the trees in this forest are approximately 50-year old pine trees with an average height of 25
m [4]. Measurements taken from instruments located at 30 m and 28 m represent the atmosphere
above a forest. Data collected at other heights on the Flux Tower characterize the vertical profile in a
forest. At 30 m above ground, measurements include wind speed, wind direction, RH, and temperature.
At 2 meters, measurements include wind speed, wind direction, temperature, RH, and H2O/CO2 flux at 2
m and at 28 m measurements include wind speed, wind direction, and H2O/CO2 flux above the ground.
H2O/CO2 and wind data are collected at 10 Hz high-resolution, and temperature/RH is sampled at 1
second. All data is averaged into 15-minute periods. Gound measurements are also taken near the
tower as part of the complete FATE research site. These include precipitation, soil temperature, soil
moisture, and soil heat flux.

4.3 Drone Flights
A Freefly AltaX drone was equipped with an iMet-XQ2 sensor which measures temperature, RH, and
pressure at 1 Hz frequency (Figure 4-3). In addition to the iMet-XQ2, the Alta-X records data from
onboard peripherals and sensors including Global Positioning System (GPS) location, acceleration,
attitude, motor speeds, and wind estimations. The Alta-X wind data is estimated using drone controller
correction (i.e., how much the drone is correcting its path to stay at the desired location) and
approximate reference area of the drone’s side profile. This data has not been validated for accuracy
and will not be presented in this report but is available. A pending project aims to validate Alta-X wind.

5.0 Results and Discussion
Rain occurred several days before the burn during February 20-22 (Figure 5-1, top). After the first rain
event, the daytime increase in the near surface soil temperature (2 cm and 5 cm) showed a peak
(February 20) but after the water penetrates deeper in the soil, the fluctuations and gradient in the soil
temperature become limited due to elevated soil moisture limiting the sensible heat flux through latent
heating (February 21-22). As the soil begins to dry out on February 23rd the soil temperature begins to
fluctuate especially over nighttime which is shown by the variation in soil heat flux seen after February
23rd (Figure 5-1, bottom). The soil temperature measurements at each level have a similar trend until
February 23rd. After February 23rd there is a larger difference in the soil temperature at 10 cm compared
to measurements at 2 and 5 cm. The sensible heat flux at the surface has greater influence on the soil
temperatures at 2 and 5 cm than at 10 cm (Figure 5-1, middle). The 10 cm soil is somewhat insulated
from the impacts of atmospheric temperatures overnight.

 8

Figure 5-1. Surface measurements at the FATE site. Top) precipitation ~70 m east of the tower; Center) soil
temperatures from 2 cm deep at ~4.5 m north (N) of the tower, 5 and 10 cm deep at ~13 m south (S) of the
tower; Bottom) soil heat flux from 8 cm deep at ~4.5 m N of the tower (positive is downward into the soil).

Figure 5-2 shows the soil temperature at varying depths extending to 100 cm and the soil moisture
percentage down to 75 cm. Similar to Figure 5-1, the soil temperature is more consistent during the first
few days with precipitation and then the temperature begins to vary more as the soil starts drying out.
The soil closest to the surface has more variation as it is impacted more by the changes in atmospheric
temperature especially at nighttime. At 5 cm the soil moisture is the highest and most variable. As the
depths increase the soil moisture is more consistent across multiple days. There is generally less soil
moisture at greater depths with the exception of 75 cm which has a similar soil moisture to 30 cm. After
the precipitation, the overall drying pattern set up conditions requisite for the prescribed burn.

Figure 5-2. Vertical profiles of soil moisture and soil temperature at the Flux Tower.

Atmospheric conditions were compared before, during, and after the burn. The prescribed burn lasted
from approximately 11:19 – 12:49 (local time). Figure 5-3 to Figure 5-5 show the temperature, RH, wind
speed, and wind direction measured during the burn as well as an hour before and after the burn (10:00
- 14:00 local time). All three figures show a similar trend in temperature, wind speed, and wind
direction. An increase in temperature is seen during the time of the burn at the Flux Tower. The portable
meteorological stations show a gradual increase in temperature during the day as expected but not a
large increase in temperature during the burn. This was because they were sufficiently outside the burn
area and below the rising thermal plumes. However, MET1 shows a small decrease in temperature
shortly after 12:00 (local time) and stays slightly lower than before 12:00 (local time) until around 13:00
(local time). The wind speed and wind direction are both variable across the time of the burn at all three
portable stations but there is an increase in the wind speed beginning around 11:15 (local time). There is
a difference seen in the RH between the portable stations. MET2 and MET3 track similarly to the Flux
Tower RH at 2 m and 30 m above the ground before and after the burn. During the burn the RH
decreased at MET2, MET3, and the Flux Tower (sharpest RH drop at the Flux Tower). The RH at MET1
was always higher than the RH at the Flux Tower (2 m and 30 m heights). The RH gradually decreased
during the day, as expected. Since the MET1 station was located closest to the prescribed burn, the
slight drop in temperature is likely from cooler air with higher humidity pulled across MET1 as the return
flow from rising warm over the burn. Indeed, at the 11:45 time (data averaged from 11:45 to 12:00),
Figure 5-6 indicates winds from MET1 flowing into the burn ahead of the temperature drop. The
increase in RH could be from evapotranspiration of vegetation outside the burn area that is not being
consumed by the fire [5]. Additionally, burning organic material can release latent heat and water vapor
which could be circulating above and back down. Figure 5-7 shows a vertical conceptualization of the
general flow during the burn. The Flux Tower at 2 m and MET1 show the low-level winds into the burn.
The Flux Tower at 30 m shows the upper-level winds and the general flow above the canopy.

Vertical profiles of temperature and RH were measured using the drone (Alta-X) at “Drone Control” (see
Figure 4-1). Figure 5-8 shows the vertical profile data before and after the prescribed burn. The
temperature before the burn was consistent with a gradual decrease in temperature as the height
increased. The temperature post-burn shows a slight increase in temperature near the surface and then
begins to gradually decrease with height. Since the post-burn flight was several hours later, the
temperatures compared to the pre-burn temperatures were warmer from diurnal heating. The overall
trends in the RH profiles pre-burn and post-burn are different. The pre-burn RH is lowest at the surface,
as the drone increases in height the RH gradually increases until 40 m and then begins to decrease. Post-
burn RH is low at the surface and begins to decrease until 20 m. Then the RH increases to approximately
30 m before decreasing until 40 m. The RH increases in both soundings at the canopy level (20-30 m).
From 40 m to 100 m the RH slowly increases.

Figure 5-10 shows the 30-meter vertical velocity, w-component of the wind, measured at a 10 Hz
frequency at the Flux Tower and surface pressure at the bottom of the tower. The increase in vertical
velocity slightly before 12:30 shows the time the prescribed burn was near the tower. The strong

6.0 Conclusions
Measurements of a prescribed dormant season burn were performed around the FATE research site
which includes the ATG Flux Tower. These environmental measurements included three-dimensional
winds, temperature, RH, H2O, and CO2 at multiple levels on the tower; two-dimensional winds,
temperature, RH and pressure on three mobile meteorological stations; soil temperature, soil moisture,
soil heat flux, precipitation and surface pressure near the tower; and temperature, RH and pressure on
an Alta-X drone flying above the burn. Additional measurements include LiDAR point cloud
measurements that were taken in the growing season and dormant season before the burn to map the
vegetation; and visual and thermal imagery from Skydio X2 flown during the burn to view the
progression of the fire line.
Soil measurements indicated multi-day drying that occurred in the days leading up to the burn after
previous precipitation. Shortly after the onset of the burn, the meteorological station nearest the burn
experienced low-level winds into the burn area suggesting a low-level return flow as the heated air rose
over the area. Three-dimensional wind data will improve model simulations for near fire motions which
improve the simulation of movement and fire dynamics. Measurements of temperature from the Alta-X
flights showed the progression of the burn in time. With the burn near the tower, large vertical motions
were clearly evident with increases in H2O and CO2 coinciding with the burn. The heating from the burn
created an exaggerated atmospheric temperature lapse rate, as measured by the Flux Tower and Alta-X
sensors, which alters the atmospheric thermodynamic response when compared to an atmosphere not
impacted by the burn. This report presents an overview of the available measurements taken during the
burn.

 17

The measurements taken during this study can be used to validate modeling results from the QUIC-Fire
model. Common output files from QUIC-Fire include wind field (3D wind velocity components), fuel
state (surface fuel density, moisture, height), and atmospheric/thermodynamic (temperature, pressure,
and humidity). The Flux Tower, portable meteorological towers, and drone measurements can be used
to validate the outputs in the wind field and atmospheric/thermodynamic files. Specific features of
interest could be the ability of the model to resolve the low-level return flow of cool air into the burn,
the exaggerated lapse rates, or the pressure perturbation associated with the highly variable vertical
velocities above the burn. LiDAR measurements can be used to validate the fuel state files or developed
into fuel loading input. This will provide insight into how representative the QUIC-Fire model outputs are
compared to actual measurements conducted around a prescribed burn.
