Differential Analysis of Factors Associated with Vaginal Lesions in Women of Different Age Groups
Authors: Yi-Fen Li, Xiao-Hui Yang, Wen-Wei Li, Xiao-Mao Li, Yue-Bo Yang, Min-Juan Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate differences in factors associated with vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (presence/absence and grade) such as HPV, TCT results, and concurrent lesions across age groups, supporting precise prevention and screening strategies.MethodsA retrospective study included 212 patients undergoing colposcopy and vaginal biopsy (2020–2024), grouped as <50 years (n=88) and ≥50 years (n=124). Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test were used to compare categorical variables between groups and to analyze associations between VaIN presence and clinical factors within each age group. Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate the correlation between VaIN grade (ordinal) and ordinal clinical factors (eg, TCT result grade, grade of previous/concurrent cervical or vulvar lesions).ResultsAge <50 years was significantly associated with VaIN occurrence (P<0.05). In the ≥50 years group, TCT results more often indicated high-grade lesions (P<0.05). Within the <50 years group, VaIN presence/grade showed no significant association with HPV, TCT, concurrent cervical/vulvar lesions, or lesion history. In the group aged ≥50 years, the presence or absence of VaIN was not associated with any of the factors. However, the grade of VaIN lesions was significantly positively correlated with the grade of previous/concurrent cervical or vulvar lesions (Spearman ρ = 0.208, P = 0.020), and showed a trend toward a positive correlation with TCT result severity (Spearman ρ = 0.154, P = 0.088).ConclusionThe correlation between VaIN grade and prior lower genital tract lesions reverses with age, being negative in younger women and positive in older women. These findings underscore the necessity for age-stratified screening and management strategies for VaIN. In clinical practice, for women aged ≥50 years, greater emphasis should be placed on TCT screening results and the history of previous cervical/vulvar lesions.Keywords: vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia, age, human papillomavirus, thinprep liquid-based cytology test

Introduction

Vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VaIN) is a precursor lesion of vaginal invasive carcinoma, confined to the epithelial layer and not penetrating the basement membrane. Both the 2012 Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) project and the 2020 WHO classification have adopted a two-tiered terminology: low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL, encompassing VaIN1) and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL, encompassing VaIN2-3).1 Although VaIN accounts for only 0.4% of lower genital tract intraepithelial neoplasia,2,3 it is one of the most frequently encountered non-cervical lower genital tract lesions in colposcopy practice, with over 90% of cases being associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, and it carries a certain risk of malignant transformation, posing a serious threat to women’s health.4–6 The global annual incidence of VaIN is approximately 0.2–2 per 100,000, accounting for 0.4% of lower genital tract intraepithelial neoplasia.2,3 Data from large tertiary hospitals in China indicate that the detection rate of VaIN in colposcopic biopsies reaches 11% and is increasing year by year.7 Due to the lack of specific symptoms (with only a minority presenting as vaginal bleeding or abnormal discharge), VaIN is easily missed, and the risk of developing the disease is increased, especially in populations with a history of cervical lesion treatment.8–10

In recent years, with increased attention to female reproductive system health, early screening and intervention for vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia have become research hotspots. The epidemiological characteristics, risk factors, and progression patterns of VaIN remain incompletely understood. Age, as a significant biological factor, may influence the body’s response to HPV infection and the progression of lesions. Differences in physiological status, immune function, and sexual activity patterns among women of different age groups may lead to variations in the associations related to vaginal lesions. Our previous research also found that in patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, those aged ≥50 years should be evaluated for biopsy based on TCT results.11 However, large-scale epidemiological studies have shown that persistent high-risk HPV infection is the primary etiology of VaIN, and a history of cervical lesions is a key risk factor.12,13 This study aims to clarify the clinical characteristics, HPV infection status, cytological results, and other related factors of VaIN patients across different age groups, in order to provide a basis for developing targeted screening and management strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No.: ZDSFYLL-II2024-288-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Female patients who underwent colposcopy and vaginal biopsy at the Gynecology Department of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024, based on clinical indications (eg, HPV positivity, abnormal TCT results, abnormal vaginal bleeding); 2) Availability of complete clinical, HPV, TCT, and pathological data. Exclusion criteria were: 1) Patients with severe systemic diseases; 2) Incomplete data; 3) Patients with a history of total hysterectomy. Patients with a history of total hysterectomy were excluded because the absence of the cervix alters the anatomical continuity of the lower genital tract, and the risk factors and natural history of VaIN may differ between women with and without prior hysterectomy. Including these patients could introduce heterogeneity into the study population.4) Inability to obtain informed consent. A flowchart of the study population selection is presented in Figure 1.

Study Methods

Data Collection

Information was collected by reviewing electronic medical records and test reports. Age groups were defined as <50 years and ≥50 years.6,11 HPV genotyping was performed using a PCR-based reverse hybridization assay (Yaneng Bio, Shenzhen, China), which detects 17 high-risk HPV types (including 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82) and 6 low-risk types (6, 11, 42, 43, 44, 81). HPV infection status was categorized as positive or negative. Positive cases were further stratified by HPV type: ① HPV 16/18; ② Other high-risk (HR) HPV (non-16/18 HR-HPV); ③ Mixed HPV infection (defined as concurrent infection with two or more high-risk HPV types, including HPV 16/18, or concurrent infection with high-risk and low-risk HPV types). TCT results were classified according to the 2014 Bethesda System (TBS) as: ① Negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM); ② Atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US); ③ Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL); ④ Atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H) or High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL). For statistical analysis, these were coded as 0–4. Concurrent and Past Medical History: The presence of concurrent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) or vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) and their respective grades (none, grade 1, grades 2–3) were recorded. Past lesions were defined as diagnoses made more than one year prior to the current examination. The composite variable “grade of previous/concurrent lesions” was defined as the highest histological grade among any prior or concurrent CIN or VIN lesions, categorized as: 0 = none, 1 = grade 1, 2 = grades 2–3. Colposcopy and biopsy procedure: After applying 5% acetic acid and Lugol’s iodine solution to the vaginal wall, colposcopy was performed using a standard colposcope. Directed biopsies were taken from the most suspicious areas of the vagina, including areas with acetowhite epithelium, punctation, mosaicism, or iodine-negative staining. The number of biopsies ranged from 1 to 4, depending on the extent and number of visible lesions. All biopsy specimens were sent for histopathological examination. Vaginal Lesion Status: Using pathological biopsy as the gold standard, the presence/absence of VaIN and its grade (no VaIN, VaIN1, VaIN2-3) were documented. Patients with incomplete data were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were made using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup comparisons made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman rank correlation was used to analyze correlations between ordinal variables. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Post-hoc power analysis was performed based on the observed VaIN detection rates (85.2% in the <50 years group and 69.4% in the ≥50 years group) and the actual group sizes (n1=88, n2=124), using a two-sided chi-square test for two independent proportions with α=0.05. The achieved power was 0.83, indicating that the sample size was sufficient to detect the observed difference in VaIN detection rates.

Results

Baseline Demographic Characteristics and Total Screening Scores of the 212 Patients

A total of 212 patients were included, with an age range of 20–76 years and a mean age of 48.4 ± 13.7 years. They were divided into a <50 years group (n=88) and a ≥50 years group (n=124). Among patients diagnosed with VaIN after colposcopy-directed biopsy, the VaIN detection rate in the <50 years group (85.2%, 75/88) was significantly higher than that in the ≥50 years group (69.4%, 86/124) (P=0.008). When comparing VaIN-positive (n=161) and VaIN-negative (n=51) patients, the mean age was significantly lower in the VaIN-positive group (47.57 ± 13.86 vs. 52.63 ± 11.77 years, P=0.012). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding HPV infection rates, HPV subtype distribution, TCT results, concurrent cervical or vulvar lesions, or history of previous lesions (all P > 0.05). Analysis of Factors Associated with VaIN within Each Age Group

Within the <50 years group, the presence or absence of VaIN showed no significant association with HPV infection status, TCT results, concurrent CIN/VIN lesions, or history of previous lesions (P > 0.05). Similarly, within the ≥50 years group, the presence or absence of VaIN was not significantly associated with the aforementioned factors (P > 0.05).

However, Spearman rank correlation analysis revealed distinct patterns of association between VaIN grade and clinical factors across the two age groups. In the <50 years group, VaIN grade was negatively correlated with the grade/concurrent cervical or vulvar lesions (Spearman ρ = −0.215, P = 0.045). In contrast, in the ≥50 years group, VaIN grade was positively correlated with the grade of previous/concurrent cervical or vulvar lesions (Spearman ρ = 0.208, P = 0.020). Furthermore, in the ≥50 years group, VaIN grade was significantly negatively correlated with the grade of concurrent cervical lesions (Spearman ρ = −0.347, P < 0.001), and showed a borderline positive trend with TCT result severity (Spearman ρ = 0.154, P = 0.088). No significant correlations were observed for other factors in either group. See Table 3.

Discussion

The average age of onset for VaIN is 50 ± 10 years,6,12–14 with no significant difference in the mean age between women with vaginal LSIL and HSIL.15 The risk of developing VaIN in postmenopausal women is reportedly 2.09 times that of premenopausal women,16 a phenomenon often attributed to reduced estrogen secretion leading to vaginal epithelial atrophy, thinning of the mucosal layer, and diminished self-cleaning capacity. However, in our study of 212 patients undergoing colposcopy, the detection rate of VaIN was significantly lower in patients aged ≥50 years compared to the younger group. This apparent contradiction with previous literature likely stems from differences in study design. While earlier reports focused on incidence or prevalence in general populations, our study is a cross-sectional investigation within a specific clinical context (a colposcopy clinic). Consequently, our results are heavily influenced by referral patterns and screening practices. Young women, due to higher HPV infection rates and greater screening participation, constitute the majority of colposcopy attendees, thus exhibiting a higher proportion of detected lesions. In contrast, older women, particularly those who have withdrawn from routine screening, may be underrepresented in our cohort.

In the analysis of risk factors, we found no significant association between the occurrence of VaIN and common factors such as HPV infection, abnormal TCT, or concurrent cervical/vulvar lesions in the <50 years group. This may be related to the stronger immune function and higher rate of spontaneous lesion regression in younger women, or it could be due to the limited sample size failing to detect weak associations.

More notably, Spearman rank correlation analysis revealed distinct age-specific patterns concerning VaIN grade. In the <50 years group, VaIN grade was negatively correlated with the grade of previous/concurrent cervical or vulvar lesions (Spearman ρ = −0.215, P = 0.045). In striking contrast, in the ≥50 years group, VaIN grade was significantly positively correlated with the grade of previous/comorbid cervical or vulvar lesions (Spearman ρ = 0.208, P = 0.020).

Previous studies have considered the cervix and vagina to be anatomically continuous, with both covered by squamous epithelium. High-risk HPV positivity and persistent multiple HPV infections can lead to cervical lesions. After infecting the cervix, the virus can simultaneously involve the vaginal wall, thereby inducing the occurrence of VaIN.4,17,18 Moreover, studies have shown that women who have undergone treatment for CIN2⁺ are at a higher risk of developing vaginal cancer.10,19 Therefore, in the ≥50 years group, previous CIN or VIN may have altered the local tissue microenvironment, rendering the vaginal mucosa more susceptible to pathogenic influences, ultimately leading to concordant high-grade vaginal lesions.

Furthermore, in the ≥50 years group, VaIN grade showed a borderline positive trend with TCT result severity (Spearman ρ = 0.154, P = 0.088). Although not statistically significant, this trend aligns with the principle of TCT testing,20,21 which assesses the presence and degree of lesions by observing cellular morphological changes. Clinicians should remain attentive to TCT results, particularly in older women, as they may provide valuable clues regarding the presence and severity of VaIN.22 In clinical practice, for women aged 50 and above, a comprehensive evaluation integrating TCT results and history of previous cervical/vulvar lesions is recommended to facilitate early detection of vaginal abnormalities.

Interestingly, the grade of vaginal lesions was not associated with HPV infection within any age group, which differs from some previous studies.4–6,16,23 Possible reasons include the relatively uniform distribution of HPV infection across age groups in our sample, or the relatively small sample size, which may have been insufficient to adequately demonstrate the association between HPV infection and vaginal lesion grade. However, numerous studies indicate that HPV infection remains a significant risk factor for the development of vaginal lesions,4–6,16,23 and its association might be more evident in broader populations or different study designs.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size may be relatively small, potentially preventing the full manifestation of associations between certain factors and VaIN, especially for factors with weaker associations. Second, as a retrospective study, information bias may exist, such as incomplete or inaccurate historical records for some patients. Third, patients with a history of total hysterectomy were excluded from this study (n=33). Since hysterectomy is commonly performed for carcinoma in situ or invasive carcinoma, this exclusion may have removed a population at particularly high risk for VaIN, as also reported by Gallio et al14 Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to women with prior hysterectomy may be limited. Furthermore, the results of this study primarily reflect the age-specific detection characteristics within the colposcopy-indicated population, rather than the true age distribution of VaIN in the general population. Fourth, although HPV infection status and genotypes were comprehensively assessed, HPV vaccination status was not routinely recorded in the medical records and was therefore unavailable for analysis. This may limit the interpretation of HPV type distribution, particularly given the increasing uptake of HPV vaccination in younger cohorts. Fifth, other factors not considered in this study, such as lifestyle and genetic factors, may influence the development and progression of VaIN.

Conclusion

The detection rate of VaIN is paradoxically higher in younger women undergoing colposcopy, likely reflecting screening biases. Crucially, the correlation between VaIN grade and prior lower genital tract lesions reverses with age, being negative in younger women and positive in older women. These findings underscore the necessity for age-stratified screening and management strategies for VaIN. Future research should expand the sample size, conduct prospective studies, and employ designs based on community populations or large-scale screening cohorts to more accurately delineate the age-related risk of VaIN and its underlying mechanisms.
