Management of Recurrent Linear IgA Dermatosis of Childhood in the Absence of Histopathology and Immunofluorescence: A Case Report from a Low-Resource Setting in South-Western Uganda
Authors: Gerald Olwit, Arthur Jonathan Nek, Hans Chinomso Chris-Uchendu, Sam Tumwijukye, Gorrette Nansimbe, Francis Munyaneza, Leo Odongo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLinear IgA Dermatosis of Childhood (LAD of childhood) is a rare autoimmune sub-epidermal blistering disorder classically confirmed by direct immunofluorescence demonstrating linear IgA deposition along the basement membrane zone. However, in resource-limited settings, access to immunopathological diagnostics is often restricted, posing significant challenges to timely diagnosis and management.Case PresentationWe report the case of a 12-year-old girl from rural South-Western Uganda with a history of recurrent vesiculobullous eruptions beginning at 2 years of age, with subsequent episodes at 7 years and a current severe presentation at 12 years. The disease demonstrated a progressive increase in severity with each recurrence. On examination, she had multiple large, tense bullae arranged in annular and polycyclic patterns over the trunk, limbs, thighs, and groin, with a characteristic “string-of-pearls” configuration and no mucosal involvement. Due to unavailability and unaffordability of histopathology and immunofluorescence studies, a clinical diagnosis of LAD of childhood was made based on history, morphology, distribution, recurrence pattern, and exclusion of close differentials. The patient was treated with dapsone, systemic corticosteroids, antibiotic prophylaxis, and local wound care. Within one week of initiating therapy, there was marked clinical improvement, with cessation of new blister formation, resolution of existing bullae, and progressive re-epithelialization.ConclusionThis case underscores the critical role of clinical acumen in diagnosing LAD of childhood in resource-constrained settings where gold-standard investigations are inaccessible. It highlights that a thorough history, careful physical examination, recognition of characteristic lesion patterns, and therapeutic response to dapsone can provide sufficient diagnostic confidence. Importantly, lack of advanced diagnostic tools should not delay initiation of appropriate treatment. This report also emphasizes the need to strengthen diagnostic capacity in low-resource settings while reinforcing the value of clinical judgment in managing rare dermatological conditions.

Introduction

Linear IgA Dermatosis is an acquired rare autoimmune blistering disorder of the skin, defined by the linear deposition of immunoglobulin A (IgA) along the basement membrane zone.1–5 The disease can occur in both pediatric and adult populations, demonstrating a bimodal age distribution, with an estimated incidence ranging from 0.2 to 2.3 cases per million individuals annually.4,6 When the condition occurs in children, it is commonly termed LAD of Childhood, a term that reflects its characteristic clinical presentation with tense, arc-shaped blisters that may arrange in a distinctive “string of pearls” pattern, typically arising on an erythematous or urticarial base.1,6 While LAD of childhood is typically self-limiting, chronic relapsing disease may occur in minority of patients.3,4,6

In affected pediatric patients, the condition most often arises during the preschool and early school-age period, usually between 3 and 10 years of age, with lesions primarily involving the anogenital region and lower abdomen, while the trunk and extensor aspects of the limbs are also commonly involved.2,6–9 The exact cause of the disease remains unknown.10 However, a notable feature is that its onset is often preceded by identifiable triggering factors, most commonly exposure to certain medications such as vancomycin, beta-lactam antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and anticonvulsants.9–12 Less commonly, the disease has been associated with infections, autoimmune diseases, vaccinations, and malignancies.10

Although LAD of childhood is primarily a clinical entity, its definitive diagnosis relies on immunopathological confirmation.5,9,13 Direct immunofluorescence (DIF), demonstrating a continuous linear deposition of IgA along the basement membrane zone, remains the gold standard diagnostic modality, while histopathology typically reveals subepidermal blister formation with a predominantly neutrophilic infiltrate.9,11,14,15 However, in many low-resource and rural healthcare settings, including much of sub-Saharan Africa, access to DIF and dermatopathology services is extremely limited due to infrastructural constraints, financial barriers, and lack of specialized laboratory capacity.

In the absence of confirmatory immunopathological testing, the clinical diagnosis of LAD of childhood becomes particularly challenging due to its overlap with several other vesiculobullous disorders of childhood.4,5 Conditions such as bullous impetigo, dermatitis herpetiformis, bullous pemphigoid, epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, and Stevens-Johnson syndrome may present with similar blistering eruptions, yet differ significantly in etiology, prognosis, and management.5

We report the case of recurrent LAD of childhood with progressively increasing disease severity over a ten-year period in a rural low-resource setting. The case highlights the diagnostic challenges encountered in the absence of immunopathological investigations and underscores the value of careful clinical evaluation and prompt therapeutic intervention.

Case Presentation

A 12-year-old girl presented to the emergency department of Kabale Regional Referral Hospital in South-Western Uganda with a history of recurrent blistering skin eruptions of progressively increasing severity. The initial episode occurred at the age of 2 years and 4 months, followed by a second episode at 7 years, and the current episode at 12 years, which was the most severe. The earlier episodes were relatively mild, self-limiting, and managed at home with over-the-counter medications, including paracetamol and amoxicillin, with eventual resolution and no prior need for hospitalization. In contrast, the current episode was extensive and debilitating, prompting hospital presentation. There was no clearly identifiable precipitating factor for any of the episodes. Although drugs are well-recognized triggers of this condition, we were unable to ascertain a definitive drug history due to limitations in medical record systems and recall challenges from the caregiver, a common issue in our setting. The patient reported no known drug allergies; however, this likely reflects poor documentation and limited prior evaluation. Notably, she had a history suggestive of food-related hypersensitivity, developing urticaria, pruritus, and flushing following meat consumption. She had no history of chronic medication use and denied symptoms suggestive of mucosal involvement, including odynophagia, dysuria, or ocular lesions.

On general examination, the patient appeared acutely ill and in pain but was fully conscious and oriented. She had no pallor, jaundice, or peripheral edema. Dermatological examination revealed multiple large, tense vesicles and bullae arranged in annular and polycyclic patterns, predominantly distributed over the trunk, upper and lower limbs, thighs, and groin. The lesions exhibited a characteristic circular configuration consistent with a “string-of-pearls” appearance. Several bullae were intact while others had ruptured, leaving behind erosions (Figure 1). The patient was initiated on oral dapsone at a dose of 50 mg once daily for one month, alongside adjunctive therapies including oral cefixime 200 mg once daily for one month to prevent secondary bacterial infection, oral prednisolone 20 mg once daily for one month to control inflammation, oral omeprazole 20 mg once daily for one month to offer gastric protection and Calcium and Vitamin D supplements to protect against osteoporotic effects of long-term use of systemic steroid, and intravenous paracetamol 1 g three times daily for three days for analgesia. Local wound care was performed using potassium permanganate solution applied twice daily for two weeks, primarily to disinfect the lesions, reduce microbial burden, and promote healing.

Ideally, screening for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency should have been performed before initiating dapsone therapy because of the increased risk of dapsone-induced hemolytic anemia in G6PD-deficient individuals. Monitoring for dapsone-induced methemoglobinemia was also considered given the known association between dapsone use and oxidative hemoglobin changes. However, these investigations were unavailable within our rural healthcare setting, and referral testing was financially inaccessible for the patient’s family. Despite these limitations, the patient underwent close clinical monitoring throughout treatment and did not develop features suggestive of hemolytic anemia or methemoglobinemia, including pallor, jaundice, dark-colored urine, fatigue, tachycardia, dyspnea, cyanosis, hypoxia, headache, dizziness, or worsening generalized weakness.

Within one week of treatment initiation, there was marked clinical improvement with cessation of new blister formation, flattening of existing bullae, progressive re-epithelialization, and healing with residual post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (Figure 2). At discharge, the patient and her parents expressed concern about returning to school due to fear of stigma and social isolation, as they believed her peers might consider her condition contagious. We counseled both the patient and her caregivers, emphasizing that LAD of childhood is a non-infectious autoimmune condition and poses no risk of transmission to others. The parents were advised to communicate this clearly to school authorities to facilitate a supportive reintegration into the school environment.

Discussion

LAD of Childhood demonstrates considerable clinical heterogeneity, with disease severity ranging from mild self-limiting eruptions to chronic relapsing forms associated with significant morbidity.6,10 Although many cases respond favorably to dapsone therapy and undergo spontaneous remission, persistent or recurrent disease has been increasingly described in the literature.4,5 Persistent or recurrent antigenic stimulation has been proposed as a potential mechanism contributing to chronic or relapsing disease patterns.5,6 Repeated immune activation may promote amplification of IgA-mediated inflammatory responses at the basement membrane zone, resulting in more extensive blister formation during subsequent episodes.5

The clinical presentation in this patient closely mirrors the classical features described for LAD of childhood in the literature, which typically manifests as tense vesicles and bullae arranged in annular or polycyclic patterns, often described as a “string-of-pearls” configuration, predominantly affecting the trunk, lower abdomen, perineal region, and extensor surfaces.8–11,14 Previous studies have reported mucosal involvement in a variable proportion of pediatric cases, particularly affecting the oral and ocular surfaces;9–11,13 however, its absence in our patient is not uncommon and has been documented in several case series.3,12 Additionally, the chronic relapsing course seen in this case aligns with existing reports that describe LAD of childhood as a condition with intermittent exacerbations and remissions over time.3,5,6,8 Nevertheless, the progressive increase in severity with each recurrence observed in our patient is less frequently emphasized in the literature, where many cases tend to demonstrate either stable or fluctuating disease activity rather than a clearly escalating pattern. This distinction further underscores the uniqueness of our case while remaining broadly consistent with the recognized clinical spectrum of the disease.

The management of this patient was centered on dapsone, which is widely regarded as the first-line therapy for LAD of childhood due to its rapid anti-inflammatory effects mediated through inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and function.1,7,15 Consistent with existing literature,1,4,5 our patient exhibited a prompt and marked clinical response within one week of initiation. Adjunctive systemic corticosteroids were administered to provide additional anti-inflammatory control during the acute phase of severe disease, a strategy that has been described in more extensive or refractory presentations.5,6,15 This multimodal approach aligns with supportive care strategies reported in the literature, particularly in settings where delayed presentation and extensive disease burden are common.11,15

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for LAD of childhood in children presenting with recurrent vesiculobullous eruptions, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to confirmatory immunopathological investigations may be unavailable. Early clinical recognition and prompt initiation of appropriate treatment may substantially reduce disease progression and morbidity. Nevertheless, the absence of histopathology, immunofluorescence, microbiological cultures, G6PD screening, and methemoglobin testing represent important limitations of this report. Consequently, a small degree of diagnostic uncertainty remains despite the supportive clinical features and therapeutic response. Despite these constraints, the favorable therapeutic response and characteristic clinical features strongly supported the working diagnosis and underscore the continued importance of careful clinical evaluation in resource-constrained dermatological practice.

Conclusion

This case of LAD of childhood demonstrates that a confident and effective diagnosis can be achieved in the absence of histopathology and immunofluorescence through careful clinical evaluation, exclusion of key differentials, and recognition of a characteristic therapeutic response to dapsone. The patient’s recurrent disease with progressively increasing severity further expands the clinical spectrum described in the literature. In resource-limited settings such as Uganda, where access to gold-standard diagnostic tools and specialist dermatological care is often constrained, reliance on strong clinical acumen remains essential. This report underscores that lack of advanced diagnostics should not delay initiation of appropriate management and highlights the need to strengthen both diagnostic capacity and clinical training. Greater awareness of this condition may reduce underdiagnosis and improve outcomes for affected children in similar settings.
