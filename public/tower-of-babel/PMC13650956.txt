Substituent-Controlled
Double Orthometalation and
Anticancer Activity in Half-Sandwich Ir(III) Complexes Bearing C∧N Ligands

Mario Sánchez-Migallón, Carlos Gonzalo-Navarro, Ana María Rodríguez, Cristina Bermejo-Casadesús, Blanca R Manzano, Anna Massaguer, Gema Durá

Abstract

Platinum-based chemotherapeutic
agents remain among the
most widely
used anticancer drugs; however, they show two main limitations: intrinsic
or acquired resistance and severe side effects. To address these limitations,
the design of alternative metal-based complexes with tunable properties
is of growing interest. In this work, we report the rational design
and synthesis of five novel half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes incorporating
benzo­[h]­imidazoquinoline ligands with an aryl ring.
Two strategic structural modifications were introduced into the ligand:
(i) alkylation of the imidazole nitrogen (H or 4-ethylmorpholine)
and (ii) variation of the aryl substituent (OMe or CN), which not
only induced the formation of a dinuclear complex but also modulated
key physicochemical properties, including lipophilicity, cytotoxicity,
or stability. The new complexes exhibited cytotoxicity toward different
cancer cell lines, with IC50 values in the low micromolar
range, which are within the same order of magnitude as cisplatin,
though operating through a distinct mechanism. Notably, the complexes
induced mitochondrial dysfunction, even when morpholine-containing
ligands, typically associated with lysosomal targeting, were employed.
Additional studies revealed interactions of the complexes with cellular
molecules such as NADH and glutathione (GSH), disrupting redox homeostasis
and increasing ROS (reactive oxygen species) levels. Furthermore,
the complexes exhibited moderate binding affinity for bovine serum
albumin (BSA), with molecular docking analyses suggesting a specific
interaction near tryptophan residue 134. These Ir­(III) complexes are
promising candidates for further development as anticancer agents
with distinct mechanisms of action capable of overcoming drug resistance.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of
the most prevalent and complex diseases affecting
humanity, with high mortality rates and increasing global incidence, and chemotherapy continues to play a central
role in its treatment. Platinum-based drugs, such as cisplatin, carboplatin,
and oxaliplatin, have long dominated the chemotherapeutic landscape
due to their potent cytotoxicity and broad-spectrum efficacy. However, their clinical application is limited
by severe systemic toxicity, poor selectivity toward cancer tissues,
and the emergence of drug resistance.
−

These limitations have prompted increasing interest in nonplatinum
metallodrugs. Within this broader field,
Ir­(III)-based anticancer compounds are being actively investigated
owing to their rich coordination chemistry, favorable photophysical
properties, and tunable ligand environments,
−

 although none has yet reached clinical use. These
systems can operate through alternative mechanisms of action, including
redox activity, organelle-specific accumulation, and mitochondrial
dysfunction.
−

In the field of Ir­(III) complexes, several
structural families
have been explored, including tris­(chelate) biscyclometalated and
half-sandwich families, among others. The latter, when they have the
formula [Cp*Ir­(L∧X)­Z]0/+, have shown
anticancer activity. Sadler and co-workers first reported that half-sandwich
Ir­(III) complexes surpass cisplatin in potency, and that replacing N∧N chelating ligand
for its C∧N analogue significantly enhances cytotoxicity.

Ligand screening and structural modifications
of the complexes
have proven to be effective strategies for modulating biological activity,
solubility, and subcellular localization. In this context, Paira et al. have extensively explored Cp*Ir systems
bearing π-extended 1H-imidazo­[4,5-f]­[1,10]­phenanthroline ligands, including mononuclear Cp*Ir­(III) complexes
designed to promote DNA intercalation, mitochondrial accumulation,
or activity under hypoxic environments,
,
 including
bimetallic systems capable of inducing oxidative stress and apoptosis.
,
 Zhe Liu et al. developed cationic/neutral half-sandwich Ir­(III)
imidazole-phenanthroline or imidazole-phenanthrene complexes, which exhibited suitable fluorescence, and a
nonenergy-dependent cellular uptake, lysosomal accumulation, and intracellular
NADH oxidation, leading to increased ROS levels.

Despite these
advances, half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes bearing
benzo­[h]­imidazoquinoline ligands remain unexplored.
This is particularly noteworthy given the exceptional expected stability
of the orthometalated systems, the biological relevance of the imidazole-containing
scaffolds,
,
 and the tunability of the benzo­[h]­imidazoquinoline frameworks for modulating electronic
structure and reactivity.

Organelle-specific localization, particularly
mitochondrial targeting,
is of great interest in cancer treatment due to the central role of
mitochondria in cellular metabolism and apoptosis.
,
 Disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) by Ir­(III)
complexes can trigger apoptosis,
−

 while modulation of
cellular redox homeostasis represents a complementary mechanism. Key
cofactors such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and its reduced form (NADH) and nicotinamide
adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) are essential in metabolic
and antioxidant pathways, and their perturbation can compromise cellular
metabolism and weaken antioxidant defenses, sensitize cancer cells
to oxidative stress, and enhance the efficacy of redox-active metallodrugs.
,,

Beyond the intracellular
mechanisms of action, the systemic transport
and delivery of therapeutic agents are critical factors in their efficacy.
In this regard, interaction with serum proteins is relevant for understanding
the pharmacokinetic behavior of metallodrugs.

Herein, we report
the design and synthesis of five novel half-sandwich
Ir­(III) complexes incorporating benzo­[h]­imidazoquinoline
as the C∧N ligand, including one dinuclear species
(). Structural
modifications include (i) alkylation of the imidazole nitrogen (R1 = H or 4-ethylmorpholine) to promote lysosomal targeting,
and (ii) variation of the aryl substituent (R2 = OMe or
CN) to modulate electronic density. All complexes exhibited moderate
cytotoxicity against different cell lines and a mechanism of action
distinct from cisplatin, involving reactions with NADH and GSH, intracellular
ROS generation, and disruption of redox homeostasis. Binding studies
and computational molecular docking analysis further revealed interactions
with BSA, specifically involving the residue tryptophan 134.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

The
four HC∧N proligands prepared contained R1 = H or 4-ethylmorpholine and R2 = OMe or CN. They are
reported in this paper for the first time and were synthesized by
adapting a literature procedure ().
,
 The two possible orientations
of the morpholine fragment led to the formation of two isomers in
case of H-L3 (65:35 ratio) and H-L4 (64:36
ratio) ligands.

Five new cyclometalated half-sandwich
Ir­(III)
complexes were synthesized,
four of the general formula [Cp*Ir­(C∧N)­Z] and one
bimetallic compound of formula [(Cp*IrCl)2
L4] (C∧N = 2-phenyl-3H-benzo­[h]­imidazo­[4,5-f]­quinoline derivatives,
Z = chloride or trifluoroacetate) (, Chart S1). The
synthesis of the iridium complexes was attempted by a reported procedure, by reaction of the HC∧N proligand
with 0.5 equiv of the precursor dimer [Cp*IrCl­(μ-Cl)]2 in the presence of a silver salt (Ag­(OCOCF3)) and a base
(Na2CO3) in DCE (). However, only complexes C1 and C2 could be obtained as chlorido complexes following
this method. In the case of complex C4, a larger amount
of silver salt was required, likely due to chloride abstraction and
subsequent coordination of the trifluoroacetate anion.

For complex C3, complete ligand transformation was
not achieved, and another half-sandwich Ir­(III) complex was formed
as side product. The formation of the latter could be suppressed by
shortening the reaction time and lowering the temperature, resulting
in the isolation of C3 as the chlorido complex. However,
these conditions also reduced the overall conversion. To identify
the nature of the side product, the reaction was conducted using 1.0
equiv of [Cp*IrCl­(μ-Cl)]2 at room temperature for
a longer time, obtaining the bisorthometalated Ir­(III) complex C5 with the ligand acting as ditopic. An analogous species
was detected as an impurity during the synthesis of C1 (Figure S1), but this compound could
not be obtained in pure form using similar conditions to those of
synthesis of C5. Efforts to generate the corresponding
biscyclometalated Ir­(III) complex related to C4 were
unsuccessful, as no evidence of a second orthometalation was observed.
The absence of double orthometalation with proligand H-L4 points to a definite role of the activating (OMe) or deactivating
(CN) groups of the aryl substituents on the reaction of the C–H
group with the electrophilic iridium fragment. Besides, in the case
of OMe, the contribution of the resonance form of would increase the donor ability
of the imidazolyl nitrogen. The difficulty in obtaining a pure bisorthometalated
product with H-L1 reflects an influence of the 4-ethylmorpholine
fragment, increasing the basicity of the imidazolyl nitrogen.

The complexes were obtained in moderate yields
(31–58%)
as pale yellow or orange solids and were fully characterized, along
with the ligands, by elemental analysis, 1H, 13C­{1H} and 19F­{1H} (for C4) NMR spectroscopy, including 1H–1H
gCOSY, 1H–13C gHSQC and 1H–13C gHMBC (Figures S2–S37) and mass spectrometry (Figures S38–S46). In the case of C5, the 13C­{1H} spectrum could not be obtained. The 1H spectra were
fully assigned in most cases, by means of 1H–1H COSY experiments and considering the different coupling
constants expected in the pyridyl ring. It was also possible to assign some of the 13C­{1H} NMR resonances through 1H–13C gHSQC
and 1H–13C gHMBC experiments. In addition,
the solid structure of complexes C1, C3, C4 and C5 was determined by single crystal X-ray
diffraction.

Complexes C1-C5 exhibited
good solubility
in polar solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and chlorinated solvents
as dichloromethane (DCM) or dichloroethane (DCE). All complexes were
poorly soluble in aqueous media but dissolved upon addition of a small
amount of DMSO. Thus, nontoxic amounts of DMSO were used in the biological
experiments to assist dissolution.

The analysis of the 1H NMR spectra allowed us to corroborate
the cyclometalation. For all the complexes, the absence of the signal
corresponding to H7 and the shift of the rest of the protons
confirm the cyclometalation through the benzo­[h]­quinoline
fragment (see ). In the case of C3 and C4, two isomers
were detected, which likely correspond to different orientations of
the morpholine fragment with respect to the benzo­[h]­quinoline unit. For complex C5, the marked modification
of the typical pattern of two doublets (each integrating for two protons)
of the aryl ring observed for the other complexes, together with the
appearance of several peaks in two distinct Cp* regions (one similar
to the other complexes and another at higher field), is consistent
with the formation of a biscyclometalated species via a second orthometalation
at the 2-phenylimidazole unit. In this case, in addition to the two
possible orientations of the morpholine fragment, the presence of
two stereogenic iridium centers gives rise to two pairs of enantiomers
(RR/SS and RS/SR), corresponding to two possible diastereomers. Therefore,
a total of four isomers can exist. In the Cp* region, three peaks
are observed for each type of Cp*, indicating the presence of at least
three of these isomers in solution.

At this point, it is worth emphasizing that, as
far as we are aware,
complex C5 constitutes the first reported case of double
orthometalation in complexes bearing benzo­[h]­imidazo­[4,5-f]­quinoline ligands. A complex notably different from C5, containing two “Cp*IrCl” fragments, was
obtained with a phenanthroline derived ligand coordinated through
two N∧N units. A mono-orthometalation
involving imidazo and aryl rings has been described with an imidazole-phenanthrene
derived ligand. These types of dinuclear
species may be of interest across various fields. Thus, numerous studies
have reported enhanced catalytic efficiency in bimetallic species
when compared with mononuclear analogues.
,
 Furthermore, dinuclear complexes frequently display noticeable improvements
in selected photophysical parameters over their mononuclear counterparts.
−

Solid State Characterization

The
molecular and crystal structures of complexes C1, C3, C4 and C5 were determined by
X-ray diffraction. Details of the crystallization procedures and space
groups are provided in the Supporting information. Selected bond distances and angles are gathered in , the crystallographic data
are provided in Table S1, and the corresponding
ORTEP diagrams are shown in .

The unit cells of the complexes
contain both enantiomers,
RIr and SIr (C1, C3 and C4) or SIr,RIr and RIr,SIr (C5) arising from the stereogenic
nature of
the metal center. All the complexes presented a pseudo-octahedral
half-sandwich geometry in which the Cp* rings adopt a η5-coordination mode and occupy three coordination positions,
the C∧N ligands present a bidentate-chelate coordination
mode (κ2-C,N) and the Z ligands
are coordinated in a monodentate mode, completing the coordination
positions. In complexes C3 and C4, the morpholine
unit is positioned on the same side as the benzene ring of the C∧N ligand, whereas in C5, it lies on the
same side as the pyridine ring. In addition, in C5, the
two Cp* rings are orientated opposite to each other with respect to
the plane defined by the L3 ligand.

In the solid
structure of the complexes, the C∧N ligands retain
planarity across the 3H-benzo­[h]­imidazo­[4,5-f]­quinoline moiety, which
is illustrated by red planes in Figures S47–S50. In C1, the methoxyphenyl ring is slightly deviated
(22.4°) regarding to this plane, while larger torsion angles
are observed in C3 (38.3°) and C4 (39.9°),
which can be attributed to steric hindrance involving the 4-ethylmorpholine
group. In complex C5, the torsion is smaller (22.0°),
as a consequence of the lateral orthometalation of the L3 ligand, which occurs between the 3H-benzo­[h]­imidazo­[4,5-f]­quinoline and the methoxyphenyl
moieties.

Concerning noncovalent interactions, π–π
interactions
involving the C∧N ligands were observed only in
complex C1, leading to the formation of box-like dimers
further stabilized by C–H···π interactions
(Figure S51). Similar box-like dimeric
arrangements stabilized by π–π interactions have
been previously reported for other half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes.
,
 The reason for the lack of this type of interactions in the solid
structures of complexes C3-C5 must lie in
the loss of coplanarity of the 4-substituted phenyl ring, the presence
of the 4-ethylmorpholine group and, in the case of C5, the presence of the second “Cp*Ir” fragment. For
these complexes, only weak C–H···π interactions
and hydrogen bonds were found (Figures S52–S54).

Stability in Solution

In many anticancer
transition metal complexes, hydrolysis of the M–Cl bond serves
as a critical activation step. The resulting aqua complex (M–OH2) exhibits enhanced reactivity compared to its chlorido analogue,
enabling interactions with biological targets such as DNA and proteins
that underlie its therapeutic activity.
,
 The solvolysis
process of the new five Ir­(III) complexes was studied by 1H and 19F NMR spectroscopy in DMSO-d
6:D2O (8:2) (Figures S55–S60 and Table S2) and in DMSO-d
6 (Figures S61–S66 and Table S3) at room
temperature.

The four chlorido complexes (C1, C2, C3 and C5) underwent solvolysis.
The disappearance of the chloride derivatives was relatively fast
for C1, C2 and C3 and moderate
for C5. The reaction profiles were fitted to a pseudo-first
order kinetic model (), only as a descriptive tool to compare the apparent solvolysis
behavior under identical experimental conditions. The resulting parameters
are summarized in . In DMSO-d
6:D2O (8:2), the t
1/2 values estimated from the fitted profiles
were in the range of 2–4 h for C1, C2 and C3 and 8 h for C5. The NMR monitoring
also indicated that solvolysis was favored by increasing the water
content of the solvent mixture. In DMSO-d
6, containing only trace amounts of water, the reaction proceeded
much more slowly than in DMSO-d
6:D2O (8:2) (see Figure S67), as reflected
by the corresponding solvolysis profiles and the apparent half-life
times listed in . On the contrary, complex C4, with a trifluoroacetate
ligand, exhibited robust stability under these conditions. No evidence
for the formation of a solvent adduct was observed, even when the
solution was heated at 90 °C for 1 h in DMSO-d
6:D2O (8:2) (Figures S68–S69).

The hydrolysis of complex C1 was evaluated
by UV–vis
spectroscopy in aqueous solution (4% DMSO). However, the equilibrium
was reached before the acquisition of the first spectrum and no changes
were observed after 24 h (Figure S70),
indicating that the hydrolysis process leading to the aqua adduct C1w occurs almost immediately when the water fraction is close
to 100%.

The reversibility of the solvolysis process was evaluated
by 1H NMR upon addition of 150 equiv of n-Bu4NCl to a solution of C3w in DMSO-d
6:D2O (8:2) after 48 h, mimicking
the high
concentration of chlorides typically present in biological environment. No evidence of regeneration of the chlorido
complex was observed at room temperature (Figure S71), indicating that solvolysis is irreversible under these
conditions.

Additional NMR experiments were performed in the
presence of increasing
chloride concentrations (23 mM, representative of cytoplasmic conditions;
104 mM, representative of blood plasma chloride levels; and up to
100 equiv) for complex C4 in DMSO-d
6:D2O (8:2) in order to evaluate the stability of
the trifluoroacetate ligand toward chloride attack. However, no significant
spectral changes were observed in any case, even after thermal treatment
(Figure S72). Therefore, the trifluoroacetate
ligand is expected to remain coordinated in aqueous media.

Taken
together, these results suggest that complexes C1–C3 and C5 are able to generate the corresponding cationic
aqua species under aqueous conditions, whereas C4 remains
comparatively stable. The aqua species (C1w, C2w, C3w, and C5w), are likely to contribute
to the speciation profile relevant to biological media. The generation
of these aqua adducts are expected to enhance both solubility and
cellular uptake. Consequently, any observed cytotoxic activity is
attributed to the aqua complexes rather than the chlorido precursors
of these complexes.

pK
a Determination

The reactivity of the aqua adducts
is strongly influenced by the
pK
a of the Ir–OH2 bond.
Previous studies have demonstrated that the biological activity of
the classical half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes comes from the aqua
adduct Ir–OH2 rather than the chlorido complexes.
,
 Furthermore, the presence of morpholine units in the C∧N ligands can modulate their behavior in solution, as the N atom
is capable of undergoing protonation/deprotonation equilibria, which
may, in turn, influence their biological properties. The pK
a of complexes C1w, C2w and C3w was determined
by UV–vis spectroscopy. Changes in absorbance at different
wavelengths were monitored as a function of pH (Figures S73–75).

The titration curves were analyzed
using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation to determine the pK
a values. Complexes C1w (pK
a1 = 7.04 and pK
a2 = 9.55) and C2w (pK
a1 =
7.00 and pK
a2 = 8.94) exhibited two equilibria
in the same pH ranges. In contrast, C3w exhibited two
peaks at pK
a1 = 5.55 and pK
a2 = 7.14. The common feature observed for all complexes
is a deprotonation process occurring around pK
a ≈ 7, which can be attributed to the deprotonation
of the coordinated water molecule to the Ir­(III) atom. This assignment
is consistent with previous reports on half-sandwich Ir­(III) aqua
complexes exhibiting similar proton dissociation behavior.
,

In addition to the deprotonation of the Ir–OH2 moiety, other pK
a values, corresponding
to different protonation–deprotonation equilibria occurring
at different fragments of the molecules, were obtained. In C1w and C2w, the pK
a ≈
9 value is assigned to the deprotonation of the NH group on the imidazole
ring of the C∧N ligand (). This value is consistent with those reported
for other N∧N-type ligands bearing an analogous
NH group.
,

Conversely, the pK
a value of 5.55 observed
for C3w is attributed to the protonation of the nitrogen
atom in the morpholine moiety. This finding is consistent with those
reported for other compounds containing a morpholine unit.
,
 Therefore, the nitrogen atom of the morpholine group in C3w would be expected to remain predominantly unprotonated under physiological
conditions (pH = 7.4), but could become protonated within the acidic
environment of lysosomes.

Photophysical Properties

The UV–vis
spectra of complexes C1w-C3w, C4 and C5w and the proligands H-L1-H-L4 were recorded in aqueous solution (4% DMSO, pH 7.0) at
25 °C ( and S76). provides a summary of all collected data.
The UV–vis absorption spectra of the complexes exhibited very
intense bands in the UV region and less intense bands in the vis region
up to 450 nm. The strong bands observed at λ ≤ 300 nm
can be attributed to the spin-allowed ligand-centered (LC) π–π*
transitions combined with metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT) transitions,
while the bands of lower energy may be due to MLCT transitions, as
it has been reported for other similar half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes
with C∧N ligands.
,
 In the case
of C5w, the absorption spectrum displays additional bands,
which are attributed to an increased number of MLCT transitions arising
from the second Cp*Ir fragment, in comparison with C3w.

The photoluminescence emission spectra of the compounds
() and the
proligands
(Figure S76) in aqueous solution (4% DMSO,
pH 7.0) were recorded at 5.0 × 10–5 M at 25
°C. Overall, the emission intensity of the complexes was markedly
weak, and vibronically structured bands could be observed in some
cases. The emission band is centered at around the 425–450
nm range, closely resembling the emission profiles of the corresponding
proligands. This similarity suggests that the emission originates
predominantly from the C∧N ligand. Additionally,
complex C5w exhibited the longest wavelength emission
at 450 nm. Nevertheless, since the emission is notably weak across
all complexes, it was not possible to determine their emission quantum
yields.

Given the presence of the morpholine moiety, the effect
of the
pH on the emission properties of complex C3w was monitored.
Previous studies have reported enhanced emission intensity under acidic
conditions for morpholine derivatives.
,
 Accordingly,
the emission behavior of C3w was monitored across a pH
range of 2 to 11. The results revealed that the emission intensity
remained largely invariant throughout the range, indicating minimal
sensitivity to proton concentration under the employed conditions.
A slight spectral variation was observed at pH values above 6, which
could be attributed to the deprotonation of the fragment Ir–OH2 (Figure S77).

Lipophilicity

The lipophilicity of
complexes C1w-C3w, C4 and C5w was assessed by determining their log P values, using the “shake-flask” method, to examine
a possible correlation with their biological activity. The results
are shown in . Focusing on complexes bearing R2 = OMe, a notable decrease
in lipophilicity was observed upon the introduction of the morpholine
moiety into the C∧N ligand (as shown by the log P values of C1w and C3w). This
reduction could be attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between
the heteroatoms in the rings and water molecules. Complex C5w, which features two Cp*IrCl fragments capable of undergoing hydrolysis
to generate a dicationic species, exhibited the highest hydrophilicity
in the series. Complexes with R2 = CN (C2w and C4) were more hydrophilic and interestingly, the
presence of the morpholine group had a negligible effect on lipophilicity,
suggesting that the electron-withdrawing nature of the CN substituent
may counterbalance the hydrophilic contribution of the morpholine
unit.

Oxidation of NADH

Based on the central
role of NAD+ and NADPH in cellular metabolism and redox
homeostasis, the catalytic oxidation of NADH by complexes C1w-C3w, C4 and C5w was studied
over 24 h by UV–vis spectroscopy. The evolution of the reaction
was monitored by the decrease of the characteristic absorption band
of NADH at 340 nm (Figure S78). Overall,
all complexes demonstrated moderate catalytic activity toward the
oxidation of NADH. Among them, complexes C2w and C4 exhibited the highest catalytic activities, as evidenced
by their turnover numbers (TONs) and turnover frequencies (TOFs),
presented in and Table S4. These values are comparable
to those reported for some cyclometalated half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes
under similar experimental conditions (Table S5), although they remain lower than those of the most active systems
described in the literature.
,,

The catalytic mechanism for NADH oxidation by half-sandwich
Ir­(III)
complexes has been reported to proceed via the formation of transient
iridium hydride (Ir–H) intermediates, which can transfer a
hydride equivalent to molecular oxygen, thereby generating H2O2.
,
 To examine whether a similar
pathway operates in the presented Ir­(III) systems, the catalytic oxidation
of NADH was investigated by 1H NMR spectroscopy. A solution
containing complex C2w (2 mM) and NADH (5 equiv) in DMSO-d
6:H2O (1:1) was monitored over time.
As shown in Figure S79, the characteristic
resonances of NADH at 8.43 and 8.11 ppm progressively decreased, accompanied
by the concomitant appearance of signals attributable to NAD+ at 8.37 and 8.09 ppm. However, in the present study, neither Ir–H
species nor H2O2 could be detected under the
experimental conditions.

GSH Depletion

Considering the pivotal
role of NADPH and reduced GSH in the regulation of intracellular oxidative
stress,
,
 and bearing in mind the demonstrated ability
of the presented Ir­(III) complexes to oxidize NADH (employed here
as a structural and functional analogue of NADPH) their ability to
deplete GSH levels was subsequently investigated. The experiments
were performed using Ellman’s reagent (5,5′-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic
acid), DTNB), a classical probe used for quantifying free thiol groups.
In this assay, the colorless DTNB reacts with thiols to produce 2-nitro-5-thiobenzoate
(TNB), which exhibits a characteristic yellow color that can be easily
monitored by UV–vis spectroscopy.

First, a calibration
curve was established using increasing concentrations of GSH (Figures S80–S81). Subsequently, solutions
of the Ir­(III) complexes (10 μM) and GSH (50 μM) in Tris-HCl
buffer (pH 7.2) were incubated for 4 h at room temperature, and the
UV–vis spectra were recorded after the addition of DTNB. The
residual GSH concentration was quantified from the calibration curve,
showing that approximately 20 μM of GSH was consumed in the
presence of each complex (Table S6). These
results indicate that all five Ir­(III) complexes can deplete GSH in
physiologically relevant conditions.

Taken together with the
observed NADH oxidation, these findings
suggest that these Ir­(III) complexes are capable of disrupting the
biochemical regeneration pathways of two key antioxidant species,
NADH/NADPH and GSH, both of which play essential roles in neutralizing
ROS within cancer cells. This dual interference is anticipated to
compromise the cellular antioxidant defense system of cancer cells,
thereby enhancing their susceptibility to oxidative stress.

Interaction with BSA

In the rational
design of effective anticancer agents, the investigation of their
interactions with serum proteins is essential for evaluating their
potential therapeutic application. In this context, human serum albumin
(HSA) is often considered since it is the main protein in the blood
of mammals and is involved in the transport and distribution of a
wide range of endogenous and exogenous molecules. To perform this study, bovine serum albumin (BSA) was selected
as a model of albumin protein, as it enables the assessment of this
interaction by spectroscopic methods.

The interaction between
the half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes bearing the R2 = OMe
substituent (C1w, C3w and C5w) and BSA was examined by fluorescence emission spectroscopy. The
intrinsic fluorescence of BSA, centered at 340 nm (λexc = 285 nm), was recorded upon incremental addition of the complexes
(0–60 μM). A progressive, concentration-dependent quenching
of the BSA emission was observed, consistent with interactions involving
the protein’s emissive tryptophan residues.
,
 As shown in Figure S82, the stepwise
decrease in fluorescence intensity reflects a moderate binding affinity
of the complexes for BSA.

To gain further insight into the quenching
mechanism, the data
were evaluated using the Stern–Volmer eq (, Experimental Section and Figure S83). At high concentrations of the complexes,
the Stern–Volmer plots deviated from linearity, indicating
that the model cannot fully describe the system. Such deviation excludes
a purely dynamic quenching process and points instead to a more complex
mechanism. As a result, the Lineweaver–Burk eq (, Experimental Section), commonly applied to static quenching, was therefore used. Analysis
with this model yielded a linear correlation (Figure S84), confirming the predominance of such a mechanism.
The static quenching constants (K
LB) derived
from this approach are summarized in and lie in the range of 8.68 × 104 to 1.11 × 105.

Finally, the Scatchard
eq (, Experimental
Section) was
applied to further
evaluate the binding parameters (Figure S85 and ). The
calculated binding constants (K
b) for
the interaction of the complexes with BSA fall within the range of
1.97 × 105 to 2.74 × 106 M–1. These values are comparable to those reported in the literature
for other Ir­(III) complexes and suggest a moderate interaction with
the protein.
−

 In all cases, the binding site number (n) was close
to 1.0.

Molecular Docking

The emission quenching
of BSA upon increasing concentrations of the complexes provides experimental
confirmation of their interaction. However, to gain further insight
into the most favorable binding sites and the nature of these interactions,
molecular docking studies were performed.

BSA features three
main ligand-binding regions: Sudlow site I, Sudlow site II and site
III. Interestingly, the two tryptophan residues responsible for the
proteins’ intrinsic fluorescence are located within these regions:
TRP134 resides on subdomain IA at the hydrophilic surface of site
III, whereas TRP213 is buried within the hydrophobic cavity of subdomain
IIA (Sudlow site I). Based on these features,
these sites were anticipated to serve as the primary docking regions
for the complexes. Therefore, the preferred binding site was identified
by comparing the predicted binding energies obtained for each TRP
region and by evaluating the consistency of the binding modes across
the poses generated in the docking runs, with the lowest-energy representative
conformation selected for further analysis (Table S7).

Once the calculations were completed, site III was
identified as
the preferential binding site ( and S86). Complex C1w fits neatly within the cavity, whereas the steric hindrance
from the morpholine moiety in C3w and the second Ir­(III)
orthometalation in C5w push them toward the outer region
of the site, which also coincides with the protein surface. The reliability
of the method to produce meaningful binding poses was supported by
a redocking test of the cocrystallized triethylene glycol (TEG) molecule
within the BSA structure (RMSD = 2.56, Figure S87). A summary of the interactions between the complexes and
the protein residues is presented in Table S8. In general, all complexes form hydrogen bonds with nearby polar
residues, particularly glutamic acid (GLU), via their aqua ligands
or the O atoms of the anisole or morpholine units. Moreover, these
GLU residues play a key role in establishing electrostatic attractions
with the positively charged Ir­(III) centers, as their negatively charged
carboxylate groups interact favorably with the metal cores. However,
notable differences arise among the complexes when considering the
interactions between their planar biquem units and the residues of
site III. The similar orientation of C3w and C5w leads to a combination of electrostatic π–anion, π–cation,
and hydrophobic π–alkyl interactions with their local
environment. In contrast, complex C1w is stabilized within
the site III cavity mainly through hydrophobic contacts, including
π–σ, π–π T-shaped, π–alkyl,
and pure alkyl interactions, in which the contribution of the anisole
unit is also significant. Detailed 3D and 2D representations of the
interactions are shown in Figure S86.

Given the predicted absence of direct interactions
with TRP134,
the observed experimental fluorescence quenching is likely mediated
by a distance-dependent, through-space mechanism. This behavior is
characteristic of Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET),
a nonradiative energy transfer process that depends strongly on the
distance between the fluorescent donor (TRP134) and the acceptor (the
complex).

Cytotoxic
Activity

The cytotoxic
activity of the complexes was determined in human lung carcinoma (A549),
pancreatic carcinoma (BxPC-3), and cervical carcinoma (HeLa) cell
lines, as well as in nonmalignant lung fibroblasts (MRC-5), using
the standard MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium
bromide) assay. As summarized in , all five Ir­(III) complexes displayed cytotoxicity
across all tested cell lines after 48 h of treatment, with IC50 values in the low micromolar range. Comparable IC50 values were obtained for malignant and nonmalignant cells, indicating
a lack of intrinsic cytotoxic selectivity toward cancer cells, a behavior
comparable to that of cisplatin. Notably, the IC50 values
determined for these complexes were also in the same range as those
of the cyclometalated Ir­(III) complex [Cp*Ir­(ppy)­Cl] (ppy = 2-phenylpyridine),
reported by Sadler, which is structurally and mechanistically more
closely related to the new Ir­(III) complexes.

Overall, no significant differences
in cytotoxicity
were observed
among the complexes, suggesting that neither morpholine functionalization
nor the presence of the trifluoroacetate ligand has a major impact
on the cytotoxic response under the conditions tested.

Prompted
by previous reports describing remarkable light-induced
anticancer activity of half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes,
,−

 the biological response of the new Ir­(III) complexes
under light irradiation was next assessed using A549 cells. Blue light
irradiation (460 nm, 1 h, 24.1 J cm–2) was selected
based on the UV–vis absorption spectra of the compounds. Under
these conditions only a slight increase in cytotoxicity, of approximately
2-fold, was obtained for methoxy-containing complexes C1w and C3w, as shown in Table S9.

Intracellular ROS Generation

Intracellular
ROS levels were analyzed using the dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate
(H2DCFDA) probe, a nonfluorescent molecule that is oxidized
by intracellular ROS to yield the green fluorescent dichlorofluorescein
(DCF). A549 cells were treated with the complexes at an equimolar
concentration of 25 μM for 4 h, and the intracellular fluorescence
intensity was measured by flow cytometry. As shown in , treatment with all complexes
resulted in a significant increase of fluorescence intensity compared
to untreated control cells, indicating elevated intracellular ROS
levels, with increases of up to 3.4-fold. These results support the
hypothesis that the complexes disrupt the redox balance inside cancer
cells, weakening the antioxidant defense system and leading intracellular
ROS levels elevation, which may contribute to their cytotoxic mechanism.

Organelle
Damage

Next, the specific
organelle damage induced by the complexes was evaluated. Mitochondria
and lysosomes are among the major intracellular targets of Ir­(III)
complexes.
−

 In accordance, the effects of the compounds on these organelles
were investigated using confocal fluorescence microscopy and specific
probes. Mitochondria were first examined since cationic Ir­(III) compounds
tend to accumulate preferentially in this organelle due to the negative
potential across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The integrity of
mitochondria was assessed after 24 h of treatment with the complexes
at their IC50 values using MitoTracker Red CMXRos, a fluorescent
red dye that selectively accumulates in healthy mitochondria. As illustrated in , control cells displayed intense red fluorescence,
indicative of functional mitochondria. Similar staining was observed
in cells treated with cisplatin, consistent with its established mechanism
of action, which targets nuclear DNA rather than mitochondria. In
contrast, treatment with the Ir­(III) complexes markedly reduced the
red fluorescent staining, revealing that they significantly disrupt
mitochondrial integrity.

Similarly, lysosomal integrity was assessed using
Acridine Orange
(AO), a cationic fluorescent dye whose emission depends on the environment.
In acidic compartments as lysosomes, it accumulates at high concentrations,
emitting a red fluorescent signal. In contrast, when AO is distributed
in the cytosol or nucleus, where the pH is neutral, it intercalates
into DNA and RNA and emits green fluorescence. Thus, the loss of red
signal indicates lysosomal damage and membrane permeabilization. Microscopy
experiments showed a similar punctate red fluorescence staining in
both control and all treated cells (), indicating that lysosomes remained unaffected
upon the treatment with the Ir­(III) complexes. Consequently, lysosomes
do not appear to be the primary targets of these compounds.

Overall, these results indicate that the new Ir­(III) complexes
localize to mitochondria regardless of the presence of the morpholine
moiety. This behavior stands in contrast to earlier Ir based systems
incorporating morpholine units,
,,,
 even those bearing analogous
N∧N ligands that have been reported as long-lived
probes for lysosomal tracking, suggesting
that the organelle specificity in the systems presented in this work
is primarily determined by the Cp*Ir fragment. The observed mitochondrial-targeted
mode of action exhibited by the new Ir­(III) complexes is clearly distinct
from that of conventional chemotherapeutic drugs, such as cisplatin.
It should be noted that the mitochondrial damage appears to result
from a direct interaction between the complexes and the mitochondria
rather than from secondary apoptotic processes associated with apoptosis,
since no mitochondrial damage was observed following cisplatin treatment,
which induces cell apoptosis. Thus, this different mode of action
may be particularly advantageous for the treatment of cancer cells
resistant to conventional DNA-targeting drugs.

Conclusions

In this study, four benzo­[h]­imidazoquinoline derivatives
and five new half-sandwich Ir­(III) complexes incorporating such ligands
were successfully synthesized and characterized. Different substituents
were incorporated into the ligands to fine-tune the structural and
physicochemical properties of the resulting complexes. The substituents
on the aryl ring modulated key chemical parameters such as reactivity,
ligand basicity, pK
a and hydrolysis kinetics
at the Z-position reflecting the importance of the push–pull
of electronic density on the 2-carbon of the imidazole ring. The presence
of a morpholine substituent on the imidazole ring, and particularly
a methoxy group on the aryl ring, promoted double orthometalation,
yielding the complex with the highest hydrophilicity and the greatest
stability among the chloride derivatives. The observation of this
unprecedented double orthometalation in this class of ligands opens
the way for synthesizing other types of bimetallic complexes, with
the possibility of enhancing the second C–H activation by placing
an OMe substituent in the para position of the group
to be activated. Furthermore, the incorporation of two emissive units,
such as biscyclometalated Ir­(III) fragments, in this asymmetric ligand,
could yield complexes with intriguing photophysical properties.

All five Ir­(III) complexes displayed cytotoxicity against three
different cancer cell lines with IC50 values in the low
micromolar range. However, despite the inclusion of morpholine, none
of the complexes accumulated in lysosomes, indicating that the Cp*Ir­(III)
fragment predominantly governs organelle targeting. The complexes
disrupted mitochondrial function, suggesting a mechanism of action
distinct from that of cisplatin. These findings highlight their potential
as alternative agents for the treatment of tumors exhibiting cisplatin
resistance. Furthermore, the complexes displayed reactivity with biomolecules
such as NADH and GSH, thereby impairing the cellular antioxidant defense
system and sensitizing tumor cells to chemotherapy. The observed increase
in intracellular ROS levels supports this mechanism. In addition,
molecular docking studies revealed moderate interactions between the
complexes and serum albumin, particularly involving the tryptophan-134
residue. Such moderate binding prevents the complexes from being sequestered
by serum proteins, thereby maintaining their availability for biological
activity. Overall, these findings indicate that these Ir­(III) complexes
are promising candidates for further development as anticancer agents
with distinct mechanisms of action capable of overcoming drug resistance.

All synthetic
manipulations were performed under an inert, oxygen-free, dry nitrogen
atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques. All metal complexes

aluminum foil throughout each step of synthesis, isolation and characterization.
Solvents were dried and distilled under nitrogen atmosphere before
use. Flash chromatography was carried out using neutral alumina (Acros,
Brockmann I, 60A, 40–300 μM). Elemental analyses were

APCI+ MS (mass spectrometry) data were recorded on a Thermo MAT95XP
mass spectrometer using DCM or DMF:DCM (1:9) as the sample solvent.
UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded on a Secomam Uvikon

with a 75 W xenon short arc lamp and a model 814PTM detection system.
Multinuclear NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) spectra were recorded
at 298 K on a Varian Unity Inova 400 or on a Varian Inova 500 spectrometer.
Chemical shift values (δ) are reported in ppm (parts per million)

Depletion of GSH

A calibration
curve to quantify free l-glutathione (GSH) was performed
by using the Ellman’s reagent (5,5-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic
acid), DTNB). For this purpose, a solution of DTNB (60 μM) in
Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.2) was monitored by UV–vis spectroscopy
upon addition of increasing concentrations of GSH (0–60 μM).
The absorbance at 412 nm, corresponding to the formation of TNB, was
used to obtain the calibration curve. Subsequently, solutions of the
Ir­(III) complexes (10 μM) and GSH (50 μM) were kept in
the dark for 4 h. After the indicated times, DTNB (60 μM) was
added to the solutions, and the UV–vis spectra were recorded.
The absorbance at 412 nm was used to determine the concentration of
free GSH remaining in solution. The amount of depleted GSH was calculated
by subtracting the concentration of free GSH from the initial GSH
concentration.

Molecular Docking

The binding
modes of complexes C1w, C3w and C5w with BSA were investigated through molecular docking calculations
using Autodock 4.2. The geometries of
the complexes were optimized in Gaussian 09 employing the DFT method with the B3LYP functional.
,
 The 6–31G­(d,p) basis set was used for C, N, and H atoms,
while Ir atoms were described using the SDD effective core potential
(ECP) basis set. The effects of water as solvent were taken into account
through the self-consistent reaction field (SCRF) approach employing
the SMD solvation model. Atomic charges
of the optimized geometries were obtained using the electrostatic
potential fitted (CHELPG) method at the
same level of theory. The CHELPG scheme was selected because it provides
a reliable representation of the molecular electrostatic potential
around transition metal complexes while remaining numerically stable
for systems including ECP. The resulting
charges were incorporated into the docking input file of the complexes
to achieve an accurate description of the electrostatic interactions
between the complexes and the protein. The crystal structure of BSA
(PDB ID 4F5S) was obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics
Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB). The AutoDockTools
1.5.7 program, provided by MGLTools software suite, was used to prepare the BSA structure for the docking studies.
All crystallographic water molecules were removed, polar hydrogens
were added, and nonpolar hydrogens were merged. Finally, Gasteiger
charges were assigned. Two independent docking simulations were performed
for each complex to evaluate which binding site (around TRP134 or
TRP213) was energetically preferred. The grid size was set to 80 ×
80 × 80, with a spacing of 0.375 Å. The grid center xyz-coordinates were 19.639, 35.691, and 90.174 for TRP134
and −4.896, 23.661, and 106.964 for TRP213. Docking calculations
were carried out with Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA). The maximum
number of energy evaluations was set to 25,000,000 and for each of
the 10 independent runs, a maximum number of 27,000 generations was
performed on a population size of 150 individuals. The lowest-energy
docked conformation, according to the Autodock predicted binding energy,
was selected as the most favorable binding mode of each complex. To
assess the reliability of the docking protocol, a redocking study
was performed using the cocrystallized triethylene glycol (TEG) molecule
present in the BSA structure. The ligand was extracted from the crystal
structure and prepared following the same protocol used for the studied
complexes, including the assignment of CHELPG charges while preserving
the experimental geometry. Docking calculations were carried out under
the same conditions described above. The agreement between the docked
and crystallographic poses, evaluated over the heavy atoms of the
ligand, yielded an RMSD value of 2.56 Å, consistent with a reasonable
reproduction of the experimental binding mode for a flexible ligand. The analysis of the interactions between the
complexes and the protein was carried out using Discovery Studio 2025
software.

Effect of Cell Viability

Cells
were seeded in 96-well plates at densities of 3000 cells per well
for A549 and HeLa cells and 5000 cells per well for BxPC-3 cells and
MRC-5 fibroblats, and allowed to attach for 24 h. Complexes were dissolved
in DMSO to a concentration of 5 mM, which was then diluted with sterile
water to obtain a 1 mM stock solution. Subsequently, the stock solutions
were diluted in culture medium to prepare working solutions ranging
from 100 to 1 μM. The maxim percentage of DMSO in the working
solutions was 2%. Nontreated cells were used as negative control.
Cisplatin (1 mg/mL; Accord Healthcare) was used as a positive control.
Cells were treated with the different solutions of the compounds for
a total of 48 h. For photoactivation experiments, cells were incubated
with the compounds for 4 h, to allow cellular internalization, followed
by 1 h irradiation with blue light (460 nm) using a light-emitting
diode (LED) system (LuxLight), providing a total light dose of 24.1
J cm–2. Following the treatment, cells were washed
with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and the cell viability was assessed
using an MTT assay. Briefly, fresh culture medium containing 10% MTT
(0.5 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well. Following a 2 h
incubation at 37 °C, the medium was removed, and the precipitates
were dissolved in DMSO. The absorbance of each well was measured at
570 nm in a Multiscan Plate Reader (Synergy 4, Biotek, Winooski, USA)
and the concentration that reduces cell viability by 50% (IC50) was calculated with the Gen5 Data Analysis Software (BioTeck).
In photoactivation experiments, the phototoxic indexes (PIs) of the
complexes were established by dividing the IC50 value obtained
in dark (IC50,dark) by the value obtained in photoactivation
conditions (IC50,light). All experiments were performed
at least three times with triplicates per each condition.

Intracellular ROS Generation

A549
cells were seeded on 12-well plates at a density of 100,000 cells/well.
24h later, cells were treated with the complexes at 25 μM for
4h. In parallel, cells were incubated with culture medium alone as
a negative control, or treated with 20 μM FeCl2 in
the presence of 1.75% H2O2 for ROS generation,
as positive control. All treatments were carried out in the dark at
37 °C. After washing with PBS, intracellular ROS were detected
using the dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA)
probe (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were immediately collected by trypsinization,
and the intracellular fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry
using a Novocyte flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies) equipped with
the NovoExpress software. For each treatment, the fluorescence intensity
of 10,000 cells was analyzed.

Microscopy
Experiments

For confocal
microscopy experiments, A549 cells were seeded onto glass-bottomed
8 well chamber slides (Ibidi) at a density of 50,000 cells per well
and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were then treated with the
complexes at their respective IC50 for 24 h at 37 °C
and washed with PBS before staining. For mitochondria visualization,
cells were incubated with the fluorescent dye MitoTracker Red CMXRos
(Molecular Probes) at 200 nM in serum free culture medium for 15 min
at 37 °C. Cell nuclei were counterstained in blue with Hoechst
33342 (Invitrogen) diluted 1:4000 in DMEM without phenol red. To analyze
lysosomal damage, cells were incubated with AO at 5 μM at 37
°C for 15 min. After washing with PBS, cell images were immediately
obtained by a Nikon A1R confocal microscope and images were analyzed
using the NIS-Elements AR (Nikon, Japan) and the Fiji/ImageJ softwares.
