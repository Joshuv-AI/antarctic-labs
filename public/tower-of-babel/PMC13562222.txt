Non-invasive evaluation of the respiratory system’s static compliance during assisted ventilation at various pressure support levels
Authors: Hai Zhang, Yueyang Yuan, Yuqing Chen, Qing Chang, Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous breathing activity during pressure support ventilation (PSV) is not beneficial for the assessment of static compliance (Cst). We designed a new scheme for determining Cst during PSV and assessed its accuracy using a lung model. A Hamilton C3 ventilator was connected to an ASL5000 lung simulator that simulated different lung mechanics [system’s static compliance (Cst), 30 or 60 mL/cmH2O; airway resistance (Raw), 5, 10, 15, or 20 cmH2O/(L·s)]. PSV and volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) underwent activation with tidal volume (VT) values of 5, 7, and 10 ml/kg. Respiratory mechanics were obtained and corrected with virtual extrapolation by applying RCexp and relevant equations. The repeated change in pressure support (PS) level was set at ± 1 cmH2O. Cst−PSV was determined from volume fluctuations resulting from PS level changes. The classic Cst measurement was obtained with the end-inspiration approach during volume-controlled ventilation (Cst−VCV). Comparing the value of Cst−VCV, similar values for Cst−PSV were obtained at low PS level. By changing the PS level, the estimated Cst−PSV resulted in a relatively high error of about 10–15%.The novel scheme provided reliable estimated results for Cst during pressure support ventilation. Despite its limited accuracy affected by the spontaneous effort participation, this scheme may help in non-invasive, uninterrupted monitoring of Cst.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10877-026-01455-7.

Background

Static compliance (Cst) is a crucial physiological parameter for assessing the total respiratory system’s elastic features. Monitoring of Cst alteration is beneficial in analyzing the states and pathophysiological characteristics of diseases, estimating the driving pressure, and optimizing the applied positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) [1–3]. During volume-controlled ventilation (VCV), Cst may be calculated by using the end-inspiration occlusion method, as tidal volume (VT)/(plateau airway pressure (Pplat) – PEEP) [4].

Saving spontaneous breathing during mechanical ventilation improves lung gas exchange, blood circulation, and tissue oxygen supply, which jointly shorten weaning time [5, 6]. Therefore, timely maintenance of spontaneous breathing with continual adaptation of ventilatory support based on individual needs is highly recommended. Nevertheless, lung mechanics and breathing effort are always variable in a patient during partial-assisted ventilation. Some assisted ventilatory modes have been designed for enhancing inspiratory effort in patients, preventing diaphragm atrophy/dysfunction, and restoring the natural variability of respiration-promoting weaning [7–9].

Pressure support ventilation (PSV), a widely employed assisted ventilatory mode, is patient-directed, with limited pressure and flow cycled. PSV is also frequently selected for weaning from mechanical and non-invasive mask ventilation [10]. The inspiratory effort is essential during partial-assisted ventilation such as PSV, but the appropriate setting for the assistance level may be hard to achieve as the patient’s condition may change frequently [11]. Since the assessment of respiratory mechanics by the occlusion method requires interrupting spontaneous breathing. Recently, some dynamic analysis techniques such as least squares fit (LSF), expiratory time constant (RCexp), and the utilization of pulses for resistance determination have been examined to various degrees to noninvasively estimate single indexes during active breathing and ventilatory support [12–17]. Navajas et al. evaluated an approach termed the “Delta-inst” method that assesses respiratory mechanics by changing inspiratory pressure for only one respiratory cycle. Cst and airway resistance (Raw) values can be calculated during dynamic assisted ventilation using the assumption that respiratory muscle activity does not markedly change in the current cycle versus the previous one. However, a significant variability for Raw measurement (30%) with the Delta-inst method was found in COPD patients, as this index was affected by the severity of airflow obstruction [18, 19]. Pan developed another new method for Cst estimation in the PCV mode. By calculating an extra inspiratory VT with a virtual prolongation of inspiratory flow, the value of Cst was similar to the result yielded by the end-inspiratory occlusion technique in the VCV mode [20].

Based on these previous schemes, we developed and validated a procedure to estimate the Cst value without the interference of spontaneous breathing. By calculating an extra virtual VT with RCexp and PS level variations, a quasi-static condition was obtained, and the elastic mechanical properties might be estimated. In this work, we aimed to assess Cst calculation in PSV mode for accuracy.

Methods

Lung models

This study employed the ASL 5000 Breathing Simulator (IngMar Medical, USA), set to a single-compartment as proposed by our team and other investigators [12, 21]. The used respiratory mechanics parameters simulated an adult individual [70 kg predicted body weight (PBW)] in the semi-recumbent position (inclined at 45°). Several clinical situations were constructed as follows [22–24]: Cst was set at 30 or 60 mL/cmH2O, Raw at 5, 10, 15, or 20 cmH2O/(L·s), and inspiratory (Rinsp) and expiratory (Rexp) resistances of the same value. The spontaneous breathing rate and the inspiratory time were set at 15 breaths per minute (bpm) and 1.6 s, respectively, which were 30 bpm and 0.8 s in the lung profile with Cst=30 mL/cmH2O and Raw=5, 10 cmH2O/(L·s). Inspired oxygen fraction (FIO2) was kept at 0.21 for all measurements (Fig. 1).

Ventilator settings

The current bench study utilized a dry circuit. The passive condition with breathing frequency and Pmus of zero was simulated at first. A Hamilton C3 ventilator (Hamilton Medical, Switzerland) was connected to the lung simulator. Simulator calibration was performed using end-inspiratory occlusion maneuvers in the VCV mode with continuous flow. Meanwhile, the ventilator was also calibrated and configured in the VCV mode. PEEP and breathing rate were 5 cmH2O and 10 breaths/min, respectively. Then, active breathing was simulated to achieve an inspiratory effort (Pmus) value of −5 cmH2O. A semi-sinusoidal inspiratory waveform was employed (rise or release time, 50%; inspiratory hold time, 0%). The ventilator’s indexes were set based on respiratory mechanics profiles to achieve output tidal volume (VT) values of 5, 7, and 10 ml/kg PBW. A low inspiratory rise time (0.1 s) was selected for overshooting prevention, and the inspiration discontinuation criterion was set at 25% of peak inspiratory flow in PSV mode (Fig. 1).

Calculation of static compliance during VCV

During the measuring period of VCV, the passive condition with zero inspiratory effort was activated. The estimation of Cst during VCV (Cst−VCV) was performed with constant inspiratory flow (≤ 30 L/min) and end-inspiration pause ≥ 0.3 s.

Calculation of static compliance during PSV

During PSV, two compliance indices were calculated. Static compliance during PSV (Cst−PSV) was defined as the ratio between the change in RCexp-corrected tidal volume (ΔVT−PSV, where VT−PSV = VT + RCexp × EIF) and the corresponding change in pressure support (ΔPS), assuming linear elastance and unchanged respiratory muscle activity between consecutive breaths. Respiratory system compliance by the two-point method (Crs−2P) was defined as VT divided by driving pressure (EIP − PEEP) and was obtained without correction for residual end-inspiratory flow or virtual volume extrapolation.

During PSV, total pressure represents the sum of pressure applied by the ventilator to the airway (Paw) and that produced by respiratory muscles (Pmus). The respiratory system’s elastic features are calculated using VT and compliance (Cst−PSV), while lung resistance is determined by flow and Raw. Therefore, the motion equation is:

During assisted ventilation, a manual increase or decrease of PS level by 1 cmH2O was performed following every 5th breath, and immediate switching to the previous value was made for the next breath. Assuming elastic linearity of the respiratory system and muscular activity are unchanged, the differences of Paw, VT, and flow could be expressed as ΔPaw + ΔPmus = ΔVT/Cst + ΔFlow×Raw, and ΔPEEP = 0 as PEEP remains constant. The value of Cst−PSV was determined as tidal volume change (ΔVT−PSV) divided by inspiratory Paw change resulting from pressure support variation (ΔPS).

The expiratory time constant (RCexp) almost entirely depends on a given patient (passive expiration) and not on ventilator settings, so that the pressure decreases instantly to PEEP, and is determined as VT/flow ratio at 75% of expiratory VT [24, 25]. The quasi-static VT during PSV (VT−PSV) was calculated with the outputted VT plus an extra virtual VT with the product of RCexp and end-inspiratory flow (EIF):

And the equation of motion during PSV can be simplified as:

The two-point respiratory system compliance (Crs−2P) was derived as VT/driving pressure (DP), with DP calculated as end-inspiration EIP − end-expiration PEEP [12, 26]:

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Linear mixed-effects models were employed for the primary statistical analysis to account for the within-group correlation introduced by repeated measurements under different conditions of static compliance (Cst), airway resistance (Raw), and tidal volume (VT). The fixed effects included the calculation method (Crs−2P, Cst−PSV, Cst−VCV), compliance level (30 or 60 mL/cmH₂O), airway resistance level (5, 10, 15, or 20 cmH₂O/(L·s)), and tidal volume (5, 7, or 10 mL/kg). The respiratory sample (lung model) was included as a random effect. For post-hoc multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied. Furthermore, Bland–Altman analysis and linear regression were performed to evaluate the agreement and correlation between the measurements obtained from the Cst−PSV method (VT correction during PSV) and the Cst−VCV method (end-inspiration occlusion during VCV). All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 software, and a P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of Crs-2P, Cst-PSV, and Cst-VCV in lung models with different combinations of Cst and Raw, at a fixed tidal volume (7 ml/kg)

Method differences strongly depended upon lung compliance and airway resistance levels. The highly significant three-way interaction (P < 0.001) indicated that the pattern of method differences varied completely across different combinations of Cst and Raw. The differences among methods were highly dependent upon lung compliance and airway resistance conditions. When lung compliance was 30 ml/cmH2O, no significant differences were existed at Raw = 5, 10, or 15 cmH2O/(L·s), and when Raw was increased to 20 cmH2O/(L·s), the values of Cst−PSV were lower than Crs−2P (P = 0.0064). When lung compliance was increased to 60 ml/cmH2O, Crs−2P values were significantly higher than the values of Cst−VCV and Cst−PSV at Raw = 5–10 cmH2O/(L·s) (both P < 0.0001), and Crs−2P values were lower than the Cst−VCV and Cst−PSV at Raw = 15, 20cmH2O/(L·s) (P = 0.0317 and P < 0.0001) (Table 1, Supplementary Tables S1-S2, and Fig. 2).

Comparisons of Crs−2P to Cst−PSV in PSV mode with different values of VT (Cst=60 mL/cmH2O)

When lung compliance was 60 mL/cmH2O, significant difference between the measurements of Crs−2P and Cst−PSV was found (method main effect P < 0.001),, and this difference was significantly moderated by tidal volume and airway resistance (three-way interaction: P = 0.014) (Supplementary Table S4). When the resistance increased (≥ 15 cmH2O/L/s), or the tidal volume increased (10 mL/kg), the difference between the Crs−2P and Cst−PSV was no longer statistically significant (Table 2, Supplementary Table S3, and Figs. 3 and 4).

Comparisons of Crs-2P to Cst-PSV in PSV mode with different values of VT (Cst=30 mL/cmH2O)

There were no significant differences between Crs−2P and Cst PSV measurements when the lung compliance was 30 mL/cmH2O (method main effect P = 0.839), and the main effects of tidal volume and airway resistance were not significant. However, the interaction between resistance and method was extremely significant (P < 0.001), indicating that the differences between the two methods changed with alterations in airway resistance; Meanwhile, the third-order interaction between tidal volume, resistance, and method was also significant (P = 0.001). Furthermore, there were significant differences under specific conditions, including at lower resistance (5 cmH2O/L/s) and lower tidal volume (5 mL/kg), as well as at medium resistance (10 cmH2O/L/s) and high tidal volume (10 mL/kg). Under most conditions (10 among the 12 combinations of conditions), there were no significant differences. The results suggest that under low lung compliance (Cst = 30), the two methods have clinical equivalence in most conditions (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S4).

Bland-Altman and linear regression analysis of the difference between Cst-PSV and Cst-VCV

In all lung profiles with different Cst settings (30 and 60 mL/cmH2O), the differences in Cst values between the extra VT correction and end-inspiration occlusion methods were − 3.80 ± 5.38 mL/cmH2O and 1.96 ± 3.68 mL/cmH2O, respectively, and the weighted correlation coefficients for Cst and VT were 0.348 and 0.036, respectively (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this bench study, continuous, non-interrupted estimation of static respiratory system compliance (Cst) during PSV by applying small breath-by-breath changes in inspiratory pressure yielded Cst values that closely matched those obtained with VCV using end-inspiratory occlusion, particularly at VT values of 7–10 mL/kg. In contrast, under non-severe obstructive conditions and at low VT (5 mL/kg), Crs−2P derived from the two-point method markedly overestimated compliance at small tidal volumes and low airway resistance, and diminished as VT or Raw increased, consistent with a significant interaction among tidal volume, resistance, and method. Cst−PSV estimated with new-designed RCexp-based scheme showed conditional differences at 5 mL/kg and remained comparable to Cst−VCV in both normal and obstructive lung profiles at VT values of 7–10 mL/kg, suggesting a clinically acceptable range of accuracy for quasi-static monitoring in assisted modes.

During assisted ventilation, end-inspiratory occlusion with constant inspiratory flow remains the reference method for assessing static respiratory mechanics, but it requires passive conditions and is difficult to use during PSV because patient effort (Pmus) distorts airway pressure and volume signals. Esophageal manometry is the classic approach to quantify Pmus but is invasive, technically demanding, and prone to artifacts from cardiac and gastrointestinal activity, which must be carefully filtered to yield reliable Peso estimates. Foti et al. showed that end-inspiratory occlusion during PSV can be repurposed to estimate inspiratory muscle activity and titrate pressure support in patients with acute lung injury, but this method still depends on accurate detection of muscle pressure and interruption of the breath cycle. Alternative techniques using rapid expiratory pause and adding the corrected Pmus to the driving pressure have also been proposed to estimate respiratory mechanics during assisted ventilation, but they remain intermittent and sensitive to patient–ventilator interaction [27, 28]. Under non-invasive ventilation, these limitations are exacerbated: Pmus is usually unknown without an esophageal catheter, breath-by-breath variability is high, and leaks around the mask make a stable end-inspiratory pause practically unachievable.

To overcome these constraints, several groups have proposed estimating Cst by exploiting small cycle-to-cycle changes in airway pressure during assisted ventilation. Cortis et al. and Navajas et al. described methods in which inspiratory pressure is transiently increased or decreased for a single breath, assuming that the pattern and magnitude of Pmus remain similar to those in the preceding cycle, so that differences in driving pressure and volume can be used to infer compliance [29, 30]. Building on this concept, we applied 1 cmH2O step changes in PS, assumed a stable Pmus profile over consecutive breaths in the lung model, and computed Cst−PSV with several modified equations. Under these controlled conditions, our Cst−PSV estimates closely approximated Cst−VCV obtained with the occlusion method at VT values of 7–10 mL/kg, indicating that small, well-controlled pressure perturbations can provide robust compliance information without interrupting spontaneous breathing [31, 32].

A central challenge for Cst estimation in PSV is the absence of a true zero-flow plateau at the end of inspiration. PSV typically cycles from inspiration to expiration when inspiratory flow decays to a fixed percentage (commonly 25%) of peak flow, and the termination criterion is often adjusted to patient-specific mechanics and comfort [33]. As a result, Crs−2P is prone to systematic under- or overestimation, driven by both the choice of cycling threshold and the presence of Pmus, which alters the effective driving pressure across the respiratory system [34]. In non-severe obstructive model, Method differences were highly dependent on both compliance and resistance. While Crs‑2P tended to show conditional differences in compliance in high-compliance/low-resistance settings, its values became lower than Cst‑VCV when airway resistance increased. In contrast, under lower compliance lung profiles, all three methods yielded similar estimates except at high resistance, where Cst‑PSV was slightly lower than Crs‑2P. During PSV, expiration is essentially passive, and in a one-compartment system with single resistance and compliance, expiratory flow and volume show an approximately linear relationship on a semilogarithmic scale, which supports the use of RCexp as a surrogate of global respiratory mechanics. In our study, RCexp was derived from the last 75% of the expiratory VT, consistent with prior work showing that time constants calculated from this portion of the flow-volume curve closely track interrupter-derived time constants and the time needed for complete expiration in mechanically ventilated patients [31, 32].

The RC-based virtual volume correction in our scheme (VT−virtual = RCexp × EIF) is grounded in the assumption that, for a given breath, the product of resistance and compliance governing passive emptying remains sufficiently constant over the late expiratory phase, such that the residual expiratory flow at cycling (EIF) reflects a proportion of volume that would have been delivered had inspiration been prolonged to zero flow. Under these conditions, extrapolating VT by RCexp × EIF approximates the quasi-static volume associated with the end-inspiratory pressure, thereby reducing the bias introduced by premature cycling and incomplete filling during PSV. However, this approach implicitly assumes that inspiratory and expiratory resistances are similar and that expiratory flow is not limited; when expiratory resistance exceeds inspiratory resistance (Rexp > Rinsp) or when expiratory flow limitation develops, RCexp may overestimate the “missing” volume, leading to systematic overestimation of Cst. Our findings in obstructive settings at higher VT values suggest that RCexp-based correction remains reasonably accurate within a moderate range of airflow limitation, but the method should be applied cautiously in patients with marked heterogeneity of time constants or pronounced expiratory flow limitation, where expiratory emptying deviates from a simple mono-exponential pattern [31, 35, 36].

Al-Rawas et al. previously proposed an RCexp-based method to derive Cst and total airway resistance during PSV by combining measured VT with a virtual inspiratory volume term based on peak inspiratory flow and RCexp, while explicitly accounting for Pmus estimated from equation-of-motion models. In their lung-model study, Crs could be predicted with negligible bias and narrow limits of agreement when Pmus was low, and airflow obstruction was not extreme, but the accuracy deteriorated as patient effort and flow limitation increased. Conceptually, our approach differs in two key aspects: first, we used single-breath step changes in airway pressure to minimize the impact of cycle-to-cycle variation in Pmus, effectively treating Pmus as constant over the two breaths used for Cst estimation; second, we defined the quasi-static inspiratory volume as the sum of the monitored VT and a correction term based on EIF and RCexp, rather than on peak inspiratory flow. These choices are intended to reduce sensitivity to early inspiratory dynamics and to emphasize the late expiratory phase, where passive emptying dominated by the lung-chest wall system may be better captured by RCexp. In our lung model, this strategy yielded Cst−PSV values that were systematically closer to Cst−VCV than those obtained with the two-point method, suggesting that the combination of small PS steps and RCexp-based virtual volume extrapolation can partially compensate for the absence of an end-inspiratory pause [35, 37]. At lower compliance lung models, both Crs‑2P and Cst‑PSV produced statistically similar estimates across most resistance-volume combinations, supporting their practical equivalence except in extreme conditions. In addition, Agreement analysis confirmed acceptable bias and variability between Cst‑PSV and Cst‑VCV (mean difference within ± 4 mL/cmH₂O), with minimal dependence on tidal volume.

Several methodological and physiological limitations merit consideration. First, we used a single, standardized active lung simulator with a one-compartment linear model (Cst = 30 and 60 mL/cmH2O, Raw = 5–20 cmH2O/(L·s)), whereas in vivo, compliance and resistance are nonlinear and volume- and flow-dependent; compliance typically decreases at higher lung volumes, and resistance may vary with both volume and flow, so that a single RCexp cannot fully describe the entire breath. Second, spontaneous breathing was modeled using only one preset Pmus waveform, limiting our ability to explore how different shapes, magnitudes, or timing of inspiratory effort influence the accuracy of Cst−PSV and Crs−2P estimates. Third, our method assumes that inspiratory and expiratory time constants are comparable, an assumption that may be violated in diseases with pronounced small-airway involvement and dynamic airway closure, such as COPD and asthma, where Rexp often exceeds Rinsp and RCexp varies substantially with lung condition. Arnal et al. and others have demonstrated that RCexp differs significantly across normal, COPD, and ARDS lungs, and that inspiratory and expiratory resistances are frequently asymmetric, particularly in the presence of expiratory flow limitation. Volta et al. further showed that expiratory flow limitation can markedly impair the accuracy of resistance and compliance measurements, underscoring that RCexp-based corrections may become unreliable when flow is limited over a substantial portion of expiration. Finally, our findings derive from a bench model and have not yet been validated in patients. Important clinical variables, including heterogeneity of regional mechanics, variable respiratory drive, asynchronous patient–ventilator interaction, and leaks in non-invasive settings, may all affect the stability of Pmus, the reliability of RCexp, and the robustness of the proposed virtual volume extrapolation. Future clinical studies should therefore: (1) systematically test the method across a spectrum of respiratory system mechanics (including COPD and ARDS), (2) quantify its performance at different levels of inspiratory effort and degrees of flow limitation, and (3) compare its accuracy against reference measurements based on esophageal manometry or low-flow pressure–volume maneuvers. Such work will be essential to determine whether RCexp-based virtual volume correction can be safely adopted for routine bedside estimation of Cst in partially assisted modes and to define the boundaries within which the underlying assumptions, particularly regarding symmetry of resistance and absence of flow limitation, remain valid.

Conclusions

In PSV, inspiratory flow decays exponentially, and cycling occurs before flow reaches zero, making classical Cst estimation based on an end-inspiratory plateau difficult and prone to error in the presence of Pmus. We propose an RCexp-based scheme that combines small step changes in PS level with virtual extrapolation of VT using EIF and RCexp, thereby approximating the volume that would have been delivered under quasi-static conditions without interrupting spontaneous breathing. In our lung-model experiments, this approach showed good agreement with Cst values obtained during VCV with the classic end-inspiratory occlusion maneuver, especially at VT values of 7–10 mL/kg, while outperforming the traditional two-point method under non-severe obstructive conditions. Enhancing the accuracy of compliance estimation in PSV likely requires both optimization of inspiratory cycling (to minimize residual EIF) and appropriate RCexp-based correction for incomplete filling; however, the influence of asymmetric resistance, expiratory flow limitation, and variable Pmus must be acknowledged, and algorithm performance should be carefully evaluated before clinical implementation. Further patient studies are warranted to assess whether this strategy can be generalized to other assisted ventilatory modes and to establish its role in monitoring and titrating ventilatory support at the bedside.
