Maternal postpartum depression risk differentially affects infant development across sociocultural groups
Authors: Noa Gueron-Sela, Yonatan Bilu, Guy Amit, Vered Bar, Yair Sadaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal postpartum depression (PPD) is a significant public health concern with well-documented implications for children’s developmental outcomes. Despite growing evidence of these effects, limited attention has been given to potential sociocultural differences in how PPD influences child development. To address this gap, the present study utilized a nationally representative dataset to examine whether the associations between maternal PPD risk and developmental delays in children vary across two major sociocultural groups in Israel: The Jewish and Muslim-Arab populations.MethodsData from a population-based birth cohort in Israel (N = 401,942) were collected in approximately 700 in national maternal child health clinics. PPD risk was estimated using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) and developmental assessments were conducted by trained public health nurses. Participants included all children born between January 2014 and September 2020, who were followed at the maternal child health clinics from birth to age 2 years. Three different types of indicators for developmental delay were considered, based on recorded intervention, nurse’s assessment, and milestone achievement scales.ResultsPPD risk was associated with an increased risk of developmental delay in both the first and second years of life, according to all three types of developmental delay indicators. These estimated effects of PPD risk were significantly lower among Muslim-Arabs than among Jews for all three indication types, both during the first year of life, and in the second.ConclusionsThe findings support the cultural mitigation hypothesis, indicating that sociocultural factors can potentially attenuate the negative impact of maternal postpartum depression risk on child developmental outcomes. These results underscore the importance of recognizing and leveraging communal and familial resources as protective factors in the postpartum period, particularly within culturally distinct populations. Tailoring interventions to align with sociocultural strengths may enhance support for mothers and infants and mitigate long-term developmental risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05041-z.

Background

Maternal postpartum depression (PPD) is a significant public health concern due to its high prevalence and negative implications for maternal health, mother-child relationships, and child developmental outcomes [1]. PPD affects approximately 14% of mothers worldwide (range: 5–26%) and varies by country, cultural background, and economic status [2]. There is ample evidence that PPD is associated with adverse child outcomes in multiple domains [1, 3, 4]. However, knowledge is scarce on potential differences in these associations between sociocultural groups. Children’s rearing environments are situated within the sociocultural context in which families reside, and as such, PPD may affect children from minority groups in a different manner [5]. To address this gap in the literature, we used a national dataset to examine whether the associations between maternal PPD risk and children’s developmental delay differ between two distinct sociocultural groups residing in Israel, namely Jewish and Muslim-Arab mother-child dyads.

Numerous studies have explored the adverse effects of PPD on various aspects of maternal well-being and infant development [6–8]. One area of particular concern is its potential impact on child developmental delay. Research has shown that maternal PPD is associated with an increased likelihood of child developmental delay during the early years of life. Children born to mothers experiencing PPD symptoms may exhibit delays in cognitive, language, motor, and socio-emotional development [1, 3, 4]. The mechanisms underlying this association remain complex and multifaceted, involving both biological and psychosocial factors.

The cultural mitigation hypothesis posits that sociocultural factors can influence and moderate the impact of certain psychological states on individuals within a specific cultural context [9]. According to this hypothesis, cultural norms, values, and practices can act as protective factors, mitigating the adverse effects of psychological challenges or risk factors. In the context of maternal PPD, the cultural mitigation hypothesis suggests that certain cultural practices, beliefs, and social support structures may buffer the negative consequences of PPD on both the mother and the child [9]. These cultural factors could include traditional postpartum rituals, extended family support, or community involvement in childcare. Indeed, previous research suggests that social support may reduce both the likelihood of maternal PPD and its impact on parenting and child outcomes. Mothers embedded in stronger family and community networks often report lower levels of psychological distress and greater parenting efficacy [10, 11]. Regarding children’s developmental outcomes, Astor et al. [9] found that maternal PPD was negatively associated with joint attention abilities in Swedish infants but not in Bhutanese infants. Similarly, research among Latino families suggests that cultural values emphasizing familism and strong family support networks may buffer some of the adverse consequences of maternal depression for children [5]. Together, these findings suggest that the developmental implications of maternal depression may vary across sociocultural contexts.

Israel is a multi-ethnic society, made up of a majority Jewish population (74%) and an Arab minority (21%) of whom approximately 80% are Muslim [12]. Most of the Arab society is religiously, culturally, and geographically distinct from the Jewish majority. While the Jewish population is oriented towards individualism, the Arab society is considered traditional and collectivistic [13]. In collectivistic cultures, family interdependence, mutual obligation, and community cohesion are emphasized, and childrearing responsibilities are often shared among multiple family members. In contrast, Jewish Israeli society has been described as relatively more individualistic, with greater emphasis on autonomy and the nuclear family [13]. These differences may be particularly relevant during the postpartum period, when support from extended family members and the broader community can play an important role in maternal adjustment and infant care.

At the same time, the Arab population in Israel experiences substantial socioeconomic disadvantage relative to the Jewish majority, including lower levels of education, income, and employment, as well as higher fertility rates [12, 14]. Such structural inequalities have been linked to increased exposure to chronic stress and reduced access to health and social resources [15–17]. Consistent with these disparities, previous studies have found that Arab women in Israel are at elevated risk for postpartum depression compared to Jewish women [18–20]. Thus, the Arab-Muslim population represents an important context in which potentially competing influences may operate: greater vulnerability to maternal psychological distress due to socioeconomic disadvantage, alongside family- and community-based resources that may buffer its impact on child development.

The current study

Although it has been established that PPD increases the risk for adverse maternal and child outcomes, including developmental delay [1, 3, 4], the role of sociocultural factors in this process is not well understood. Cross-cultural research suggests that the impact of maternal PPD on child development differs among cultures [9]. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including differences in family structure, caregiving arrangements, social support, and community involvement. However, empirical evidence regarding the specific mechanisms underlying such variation remains limited. Accordingly, the current study examined whether associations between maternal PPD risk and infant developmental delay differ between Jewish and Muslim-Arab populations in Israel [9, 21]. To that aim, we used data from a national dataset that includes information on maternal PPD risk and assessments of infants’ developmental milestones conducted at maternal child health clinics at various time points between 1-to-24 months postpartum. We expected the association between maternal PPD risk and infant developmental delay to be weaker among Muslim-Arab families than among Jewish families.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the Soroka Medical Center institutional review board and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. An exemption of informed consent was granted by the ethics committee at Soroka Medical Center because the data were anonymous.

Setting and data

Developmental surveillance (ages birth to 6 years) in Israel is performed routinely according to national standards by trained public health nurses in approximately 1,000 maternal child health clinics (MCHC). About 70% of the data collected is documented in a single common database. The developmental assessments include 59 milestones across four domains: personal-social, language, fine motor, and gross motor [22]. Parents are instructed to visit the MCHC after hospital discharge and then at ages 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months. At each visit, a predefined group of age-related milestones is evaluated, according to the expected development at that age. Developmental assessments are conducted by trained public health nurses according to standardized national developmental surveillance guidelines. Children may also be assessed on milestones of the previous age step, in cases of a missed visit, or previously failed them. The child’s ability to attain each milestone is typically reported as observed in the clinic, though it may be documented according to parent’s report.

Study cohort

The current study included all children born between July 1st, 2014, and July 1st, 2019, who were followed at the MCHCs and had at least one developmental evaluation recorded between ages 1–24 months. Data was available until October 17th, 2021. As in [22], pre-term children (gestational age < 37 weeks), and those with extreme birth and growth measures (birth weight < 2.5 kg, any weight measurement < 3%, any head circumference measurement < 3% or > 97%) were excluded. The rationale for this is that such characteristics are confounders that impact both PPD and child development. Children whose mother’s Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [23] score was missing were excluded as well. The analyzed cohort included 401,942 children (Fig. 1). The MCHC data included the ethnicity of the mother, with the two largest ethnic groups being Jews (70%), and Muslim Arabs (23%).

Postpartum Depression (PPD) risk

During their first MCHC visit after giving birth, typically one month postpartum, mothers were screened for PPD using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS; [24]). The questionnaire was self-administered and completed in the mother’s primary language using either the validated Hebrew [25] or Arabic [26] version of the EPDS. The overall score, as well as the score for the last question (pertaining to self-harm) are recorded in the child’s electronic health record. For the purpose of the current study, mothers were considered to be at risk for PPD if their overall EPDS score was 10 or higher, or they have answered the last question with anything other than “Never” [4].

Infant developmental delay

Three types of indication for developmental delay were used:

Tracked - Children were considered as delayed according to whether or not they are under developmental tracking, as recorded in any of their MCHC visits. That is, whether they are undergoing remedial therapy, such as occupational therapy or speech therapy. Statistical analysis

Odds ratios of developmental delay for children whose mothers reported risk for PPD (compared to those whose mothers did not) were computed using a logistic regression model, adjusting for maternal demographics and obstetrics covariates. Maternal demographics were encoded as the following binary covariates: region of birth (Israel or other), employment status (employed or other), marital status (married or other) and education level (academic or other). Obstetrics covariates included gestational age, mother’s age at childbirth and birth weight (continuous covariates); child’s biological sex and firstborn (binary covariates) and birth type - spontaneous, instrumental, or cesarean (encoded as two binary covariates). Binary covariates for which data was missing were set to 0. Analysis was done using python (version 3.9) and the scikit-learn package (version 1.0.2). Results

The initial cohort of 331,849 children was composed of two subpopulations (Table 1) – Muslim Arabs (89,996 children) and Jews (241, 853 children). Risk for PPD was more prevalent among Muslim Arab mothers compared to Jewish mothers (8.1% vs. 2.7%). Importantly, risk for PPD was also more prevalent among mothers of children excluded from the study − 6.5% among mothers of preterm children; 5.1% among mothers of children with extreme birth or growth measures. Muslim Arab mothers were also more frequently born in Israel (99.5% vs. 89.1%), and less frequently employed (24.2% vs. 44.8%).

Outcomes of suspected developmental delay also differed between subpopulations (Table 2). Muslim Arab children were less frequently under developmental tracking than Jewish children − 1.7% (95% CI 1.6%-1.8%) vs. 3.5% (95% CI 3.4%-3.6%; p < 0.001) and were less frequently considered delayed according to THIS scale − 22.6% (95% CI 22.3%-22.9%) vs. 25.7% (95% CI 25.6%-25.9%; p < 0.001). However, they were more frequently categorized as “age inappropriate” by the MCHC nurse’s summary − 13% (95% CI 12.8%-13.2%) vs. 9.7% (95% CI 9.6%-9.8%; p < 0.001).

These estimated effects of PPD risk on child development were lower among Muslim Arabs than among Jews for all three indication types, both during the first year of life, and in the second. Interaction analysis suggested that this result is statistically significant in all cases (see supplemental). When comparing children of ultra-Orthodox families to those of non-ultra-Orthodox families, the odds ratios tended to be lower among ultra-Orthodox, though less distinctly. Odds ratio for the “Tracked” indication was lower among ultra-Orthodox than non-ultra-Orthodox, during both the first and second years (OR 0.95 vs. OR 1.48 and OR 0.86 vs. OR 1.47, respectively). For the “age inappropriate” indication the odds ratios was lower during the first year (OR 1.1 vs. OR 1.17), but similar in the second (OR 1.35), while for the THIS indication it was similar during the first year (OR 1.28 vs. OR 1.27) but lower in the second (OR 1.12 vs. OR 1.39). Interaction analysis suggested that these differences are not statistically significant, and sensitivity analysis gave more mixed results (see supplemental).

When considering developmental delay outcomes in specific domains, odds ratios of maternal PPD risk were consistently lower among Muslim Arabs relative to Jews. Comparing ultra-Orthodox to non-ultra-Orthodox led to mixed results, with odds-ratios usually lower among the former - the two exceptions being fine-motor milestones during the second year of life, and social milestones during the first year of life. Again, interaction analysis suggested that these differences are statistically significant in the first case, and mostly non-significant in the second case (see supplemental).

Discussion

In this population-based longitudinal birth cohort study, we found that the impact of PPD risk on infant development delay varied by sociocultural group. Specifically, Jewish infants exposed to maternal PPD risk were more likely to exhibit developmental delays in social, language, fine and gross motor domains compared to Muslim-Arab infants during the first two years of life. Although the observed effect sizes were generally modest at the individual level, they may nevertheless have important public health implications. Given the high prevalence of maternal PPD and the large number of children potentially exposed to it, even relatively small increases in developmental risk may translate into a substantial number of affected children at the population level.

Previous research has consistently shown that maternal PPD symptoms are related to a plethora of adverse child outcomes [1, 3]. Findings from the current study extend this literature and offer insight into sociocultural factors that can mitigate the effects of PPD. Consistent with the cultural mitigation hypothesis [6], PPD risk was a stronger risk factor for infant developmental delay among the Jewish population compared to the Muslim-Arab population. A possible mechanism underlying these observed differences may be the distinctive social structure of these cultural groups. In cultures considered traditional and collectivistic (i.e., Muslim-Arabs), support from the extended family and community are a deeply embedded norm. As a result, poor maternal mental health may be less determinantal to infant development owing to children’s frequent exposure to alternative adult caretakers [6]. Support for this notion comes from previous research showing that familial allomaternal (non-maternal) care is associated with improved developmental outcomes in both human and animal samples [17, 18]. However, because these factors were not directly measured in the current study, they should be considered hypothetical explanations. Alternative explanations, including differences in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare utilization, family structure, developmental surveillance practices, and reporting patterns, may also contribute to the observed findings.

Importantly, consistent with previous research [5, 10, 11], we found that Muslim-Arab mothers were at higher risk for PPD than Jewish mothers. Whereas the Arab-Muslim culture seemed to protect infants from the harmful effects of PPD, this cultural context conferred an increased risk for poor maternal mental health. It is possible that paradoxically, close relationships with the extended family and community may intensify feelings of shame and incompetence in women experiencing emotional difficulties in the postpartum period, perpetuating PPD symptoms [19]. Additional factors associated with minority statuses, such as low income and education level, inequalities in health, and chronic stress, can also explain the high rates of PPD risk in this population [5].

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, our measurement of PPD risk was based on a self-report screening questionnaire. Although the EPDS has been extensively used to assess depression in the perinatal period, diagnostic interviews could detect aspects of depression that are not disclosed in self-report measures and further validate our findings. Importantly, the prevalence of PPD risk (EPDS scores ≥ 10) in our sample was relatively low compared to previous studies conducted in Israel [5, 11]. This may suggest underreporting of PPD symptoms, perhaps due to situational demands. In the context of the infants’ developmental assessment, mothers may be reluctant to expose emotional distress that may (wrongfully) portray them as incompetent mothers. The under-reporting of PPD symptoms may be particularly substantial in ultra-Orthodox mothers [14]. In addition, since EPDS scores are higher among mothers of preterm children, and of those with extreme birth and growth measures, their exclusion is part of the reason for the lower prevalence in our cohort. Second, data were limited to information that could be obtained from the MCHC database. Direct diagnosis of developmental delay was not recorded, leading us to consider three different proxy indicators for it. Relatedly, the ‘age-inappropriate’ outcome was based on a clinical summary recorded by the attending nurse and may therefore be more susceptible to subjective judgment than the milestone-based THIS indicators. Although assessments are conducted according to national guidelines, potential differences in assessment practices across groups cannot be entirely ruled out. Moreover, we could not directly measure aspects that can explain the observed differences in the effect of PPD risk between groups. Therefore, our interpretation of the findings is currently theoretical and requires further substantiation in future research. Future research employing qualitative and mixed-methods approaches may be particularly valuable for elucidating the family, caregiving, and community processes that underlie the observed differences between sociocultural groups.

Conclusions

Although the mechanisms underlying the observed group differences remain unclear, the findings have potentially important implications for maternal and child health policy. Current approaches to addressing postpartum depression often focus primarily on identifying and treating maternal symptoms. However, our findings suggest that the broader caregiving environment may also play a role in shaping developmental outcomes among children exposed to maternal PPD. If future research confirms that family and community support systems help buffer children from the adverse consequences of maternal depression, interventions could be expanded beyond the mother–infant dyad to include partners, extended family members, and community-based support networks. Such an approach may complement existing screening and treatment efforts and contribute to reducing developmental risks among children exposed to maternal PPD, particularly in populations facing elevated rates of maternal psychological distress.
