Oral frailty and its influencing factors in elderly patients with multimorbidity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xingxin Wang, Lin Xu, Yun Wang, Meifang Zhou, Xin Zhou, Yun Guo, Kaiyue Zhi, Yun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to investigate the prevalence and influencing factors of oral frailty among elderly patients with multimorbidity.DesignA cross-sectional study.MethodsTwo hundred and thirty-five elderly people with multimorbidity were enrolled in this cross-sectional study in the the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University from March 2024 to December 2024. A series of questionnaires were conducted: the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8), the Family Assessment Device (APGAR), the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS 2002), the Barthel Index, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), the Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), and the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT). Independent t test, chi-square tests, Mann-Whitney U test, binary logistic regression, spearman correlation coeffcients, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis were used to analyze the data.ResultsIn this study, the prevalence of oral frailty among elderly patients with multimorbidity was 78.3%. The number of missing teeth, the number of readmission within one year, comorbidity degree, frailty, and dry mouth were the influencing factors of oral frailty (P < 0.05). The evaluation of the prediction results showed that the frailty (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.727, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.656–0.798), the number of missing teeth (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.694, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.615–0.773), the dry mouth (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.652, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.565–0.739), the number of readmission within one year (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.649, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.565–0.732), and the comorbidity degree (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.617, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.525–0.710) had a higher predictive value for OF.ConclusionThe prevalence of oral frailty among elderly patients with multimorbidity was high. The elderly who have a higher frequency of readmission within one year, missing more teeth, severe comorbidity degree, severe frailty degree, and dry mouth symptoms were more prone to oral frailty. Clinical practitioners should screen and intervene for this group. They should actively provide oral health knowledge, formulate personalized management plans, and work to prevent and control oral frailty, thereby improving the patients’ overall quality of life.Keywords: Oral frailty, Elderly patients, Multimorbidity, Cross-sectional survey, Influencing factors

Introduction

As the world rapidly ages, the phenomenon of multimorbidity in the older population has become increasingly prominent. Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of two or more chronic diseases whose pathophysiological changes are often degenerative and irreversible, leading not only to functional decline and impaired quality of life but also significantly increasing the risk of disability [1]. A recent meta-analysis indicated that the global prevalence of multimorbidity among individuals aged 65 and above is 46.0%, with subgroup analysis showing a prevalence of 40% among older adults in China [2]. These individuals often suffer from multiple chronic conditions alongside complex medication regimens, representing one of the subgroups within the older population at the highest risk of functional decline.

Oral health has long been neglected in comprehensive geriatric assessment. In recent years, the concept of oral frailty has been introduced, defined as an age-related decline in oral and maxillofacial functions, including a reduction in tooth number, diminished masticatory ability, swallowing difficulties, and oral dryness. This decline can ultimately lead to malnutrition and accelerate the progression of physical frailty [3]. Hihara et al. [4] suggest that oral frailty is an early indicator of physical frailty and an independent precursor to the risk of physical frailty, cognitive impairment, and even mortality in older adults. Like a “sentinel,” it signals an impending decline in overall health. Currently, the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8) has been widely used in community-dwelling older populations [5], with reported prevalence rates ranging from 22.5% to 49.4%. It is closely associated with factors such as age, educational level, nutritional status, and social determinants [6–8]. Furthermore, each 1-point increase in the OFI-8 score elevates the risk of oral frailty by 1.3 times and the risk of disability by 1.1 times [9]. However, while community populations reflect oral functional changes during the natural aging process, the multimorbid population is subject to the complex impacts of multiple chronic diseases and their treatments, which exert a significant accelerating effect on oral frailty. First, the increase in the number of medications associated with multimorbidity can reduce salivary secretion and flow, inducing xerostomia, thereby impairing oral health and function [10]. Second, impaired self-care ability and reduced physical and psychological reserves due to disease burden can easily lead to inadequate oral hygiene, promoting bacterial proliferation and consequent functional impairment [11]. Third, recurrent acute exacerbations of various chronic conditions mean patients’ focus is concentrated on disease control, leaving little time or energy for oral care, resulting in the “silent accumulation” of oral problems.

However, current research on oral frailty in older patients with multimorbidity remains insufficient to determine its prevalence in this population, its association with the degree of multimorbidity, and the interaction between oral frailty and various dimensions of physical frailty. Therefore, this study aims to: (1) assess the prevalence of oral frailty using the OFI-8 scale in older patients with multimorbidity and compare it with data from community populations; (2) systematically investigate the predictors of oral frailty in this population; and (3) analyze the interplay between oral frailty and frailty-related dimensions. The findings will inform targeted screening and intervention strategies.

Patients and methods

Participants

Patients were recruited at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between March 2024 and December 2024. Participants met the inclusion criteria below: (1) age≥60 years old, (2) having at least two chronic diseases, (3) no language communication barriers, (4) willingness to participate in the study and provide written informed consent. Subjects who met any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) people with severe cognitive impairment or mental illness, and unable to cooperate, Based on information from the patients’ electronic medical record system, patients were excluded if their medical records explicitly documented a diagnosis of ‘dementia’, ‘Alzheimer’s disease’, ‘vascular dementia’, or other neurological disorders leading to severe cognitive impairment. (2) failed to complete the questionnaire. This cross-sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (LK2024052).

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated using PASS 15.0 software based on the confidence interval for a single mean method. Based on prior pilot study results, the standard deviation was set at 2.182 points, with a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error (half-width of the confidence interval) of ± 0.6 points. Considering a 10% dropout rate, the required sample size was calculated to be 229 participants. In this study, a total of 250 questionnaires were distributed, and 235 valid questionnaires were recovered, yielding a valid response rate of 94%.

Research instruments

Demographics, disease, and oral health characterization by a self-designed questionnaire

The demographic data of the elderly patients include age, gender, marital status, educational level, average monthly household income, and residence. The disease-related data include the number of comorbidities, polypharmacy, and the number of readmission within one year. The oral health-related data include dry mouth, the number of missing teeth, the number of decayed teeth, whether they enjoy sweet food/night snacks, whether they have a history of oral treatment, and whether they have the habit of gargling after eating. In addition, the following questionnaires were also conducted in this survey.

Oral frailty

This study used the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8) developed by Tanaka et al. to assess the patients’ levels of OF [9]. The scale consists of eight items, comprising five dimensions: Masticatory ability, Swallowing ability, False teeth, Social participation, and Oral health-related behaviors. The total score ranges from 0 to 11 points, with a score of ≥ 4 indicating the occurrence of OF. A lower score reflects better oral health. The Cronbach’s alpha for the Chinese version of OFI-8 was 0.949 [12].

Family functioning

This study used the Family Assessment Device (APGAR) to assess patients’ subjective satisfaction with family functioning [13]. The APGAR comprises 5 items, covering five dimensions: family adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and intimacy. The total score ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating better family functioning. The Cronbach’s alpha for the Chinese version of APGAR was 0.84 [14].

Frailty

The Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI) was used to measure the frailty of older people, which was developed by Steverink in the Netherlands [15]. The scale consists of four items: Physical, Cognition, Society, and Psychology-related behaviors. It was a 15-item screening tool. The total score ranges from 0 to 11 points, with a score of ≥ 3 indicating the occurrence of physical frailty. A higher GFI total score indicated a higher level of frailty [16]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the Chinese version of GFI was 0.87 [17].

Oral Health

The lips, tongue, gum tissue, saliva, natural teeth, dentures, oral hygiene, and dental pain are among the eight components that make up the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT). The scores for each item are 0 (normal), 1 (abnormal), or 2 (severely aberrant). A higher total score indicates poorer oral health. The Cronbach’s alpha for the Chinese version of OHAT was 0.71 [18].

Nutritional risk

This study used the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002(NRS 2002) to assess patients’ nutritional risk [19]. The scale mainly includes three parts: nutritional status damage score, disease severity, and age to assess the nutritional risk. The total score is 0–7 points, and a score of ≥ 3 points indicates the presence of nutritional risk. The higher the score, the higher the nutritional risk.

Self-care ability

The Activities of Daily Living of the patients in the present study were assessed on the day of admission to the hospital by the Barthel Index, which includes feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, bowel control, bladder control, toileting, chair transfer, ambulation, and stair climbing [20]. Each item was divided into different grades of 15 (10), 5, and 0, depending on how much help they needed to complete the relevant items. Zero points meant patients could not do the relevant item independently and were completely reliant on help from others, while 15 (10) indicated that they did not need any help from others. Patients who needed a little or more help to complete the activities would be given scores somewhere between 0 and 15 (10). Then, the score of each item was added to get the final score, which had a range of 0-100. One hundred meant patients could take care of themselves without any help, while 0 meant they could not live without help from others. Finally, patients were given different scores to show their ability to perform daily activities.

Charlson comorbidity index

The Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) was used to assess the severity of multimorbidity. It includes 19 kinds of diseases. Each disease is given different scores of 1, 2, 3, and 6 points. The corresponding scores of each disease are recorded. At the same time, it needs to be corrected according to age. 1 point is calculated for the age of 50–59, and 1 point is added every 10 years thereafter. The sum is the patient’s CCI score. The higher the score, the heavier the degree of multimorbidity. In this study, the CCI score of 1–4 points is rated as medium/high risk, and ≥ 5 points is rated as extremely high risk [21].

Data analysis

SPSS 26.0 was conducted to analyze data. KolmogorovSmirnov test was used to evaluate normal distribution. For normally distributed continuous data, statistical descriptions were presented as “mean±standard deviation”, and intergroup differences were compared using the independent samples t-test; for skewed continuous data, statistical descriptions were given as median and interquartile range, and intergroup differences were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were described using frequency and percentage, and intergroup differences were compared using the chi-square tests. Spearman correlation coeffcients were used to analyze the oral frailty and physical, cognitive, social, and psychological domains in multimorbid elderly patients with OF. Additionally, binary logistic regression was employed to identify the factors influencing OF in patients with elderly patients with multimorbidity. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The predictive performance of the five independent factors from the regression model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 235 elderly patients with multimorbidity were involved in the study. As shown in Table 1, more than half of the participants were male (58.3%), with an average age of 72.86 ± 8.13 years. Most of them were married (86.8%), while 54.9% had an education level of primary school or below, and only 6.8% had completed college or attained higher education. Most of the elderly patients in this group lived with their families (86.8%), and only 13.2% lived alone. The median number of missing teeth in elderly patients with multimorbidity was 7, and the median number of decayed teeth were 2. Among the participants, the median score of GFI was 5, and the median score of CCI was 7; all of them exhibited a multimorbidity level of at least moderate, with an notably 79.1% of patients falling into the extremely high-risk category. Furthermore, 63% of elderly patients experienced dry mouth symptoms, while 43.8% had the habit of eating midnight snacks and sweet foods (see Table 1).

Discussion

This investigation revealed a high incidence of OF (78.3%) among elderly multimorbidity patients, which is notably greater than the 69% prevalence noted in a general elderly community setting [22]. This discrepancy was likely attributable to differences in the study populations. Community-dwelling older adults are relatively healthy and able to live independently. However, in our study population, patients had at least two chronic diseases. Acute exacerbations of these chronic conditions resulted in a higher inflammatory burden and greater functional decline [23]. These factors may synergistically impair oral function, leading to a higher prevalence of oral frailty. Based on the ROC curve analysis, the predictive strength of five significant influencing factors for OF followed the descending order: frailty > the number of missing teeth > dry mouth > number of readmission within one year > comorbidity degree. A study of community-dwelling older adults using ROC analysis confirmed the role of physical frailty in oral frailty but failed to establish an effect of multimorbidity [24]. In addition, in this study, elderly patients with multimorbidity had impaired eating or masticatory ability and poor oral health-related behaviors. All might interact with the oral frailty condition. A systematic review has also suggested that frailty and poor oral health had a positive association, particularly regarding missing teeth and impaired oral function [25]. In elderly individuals, particularly those with multimorbidity, age-related degenerative oral changes are compounded by increased disease burden and reduced self-care capacity. This leads to a high prevalence of poor oral health behaviors, which subsequently diminish interest in oral care, cause eating difficulties, lead to dietary imbalances, and accelerate the progression of oral frailty [26].

Our study found that dry mouth is a significant predictor of oral frailty in elderly patients with multimorbidity. It should be noted that although the OFI-8 already includes a dry mouth item, the dry mouth symptom used as an independent variable in this study differs in connotation from that item. The independent variable assessed eating‑context dry mouth (“In the past six months, have you felt dry mouth that makes it difficult to swallow dry foods, e.g., biscuits, bread?”), whereas the OFI-8 dry mouth item asks about general dry mouth (“Do you often feel a dry mouth?”) without specific reference to eating. Furthermore, the dry mouth item in the OFI-8 carries only limited weight (maximum 1 point) in the total score and is merely one component of the multidimensional score, not an equal representation of the total. Therefore, including eating-related dry mouth as an independent predictor does not create a substantial circularity problem. The occurrence of dry mouth can be attributed to direct disruption of the oral environment. With aging, salivary gland atrophy or functional decline in older adults leads to reduced salivary secretion, causing dry mouth [27]. This decrease in salivary flow further impairs oral self-cleaning, lubrication, and antimicrobial defense capabilities, thereby increasing the risk of dental caries, periodontal disease, and oral discomfort — all of which constitute core components in the development of oral frailty [26]. Furthermore, this survey revealed that the median number of missing teeth in the oral frailty group of elderly adults with multimorbidity was 8, significantly more than the 2 teeth in the non-oral frailty group. Functionally, this disparity primarily manifests as masticatory difficulty, with the number of missing teeth serving as a significant influencing factor for oral frailty in this population. Compared to older adults with a normal dentition, those with a critically limited number of teeth experience persistent chewing difficulties, even when wearing well-fitted dentures. This leads to restricted food choices, with a marked tendency towards highly processed foods, resulting in insufficient intake of dietary fiber, trace elements, and other essential nutrients, and ultimately suboptimal nutrient intake [28]. This nutritional compromise has consequences that extend far beyond dietary adequacy - it directly contributes to the acceleration of systemic physical decline. Inadequate protein and micronutrient intake is a well-established risk factor for sarcopenia, as it impairs muscle protein synthesis and accelerates age-related muscle loss [29]. Our study also found an association between nutritional risk and oral frailty.

The core connection between physical and oral frailty lies in shared muscle degeneration and functional disuse, which is exacerbated by a vicious cycle of reduced nutritional intake. Previous study has shown that frailty was frequently associated with conditions such as sarcopenia and skeletal muscle dysfunction. This encompasses the functional decline of the orofacial musculature, resulting in reduced masticatory force and impaired swallowing function [30]. As a result, they often avoid hard-to-chew foods such as vegetables, fruits, and meat, which generally contain rich nutrients [31]. A survey conducted among community - dwelling older adults in China showed that people with physical frailty are 3 times more likely to develop oral frailty than robust people [22]. However, frailty is multidimensional. Cognitive decline compromises oral self-care, dental attendance, and dietary habits, accelerating tooth loss and oral dysfunction [32, 33]. Social vulnerability - exacerbated by hospitalization - reduces oral stimulation through conversation and shared meals, limits access to dental services, and promotes inadequate nutrition [34]. These dimensions interact synergistically. Frailty is often associated with muscle weakness and decreased physical activity, which can affect the muscles involved in chewing and swallowing [35]. Additionally, frail individuals may have difficulty maintaining proper oral hygiene and accessing dental care, leading to oral health issues that can contribute to oral frailty. In turn, oral frailty can lead to malnutrition and deteriorate physical frailty [36].

The results of this study indicate that elderly adults with multimorbidity are more susceptible to oral frailty, a finding consistent with multiple previous studies [9, 37]. The state of multimorbidity in older adults is often accompanied by immunosenescence and chronic low-grade inflammation, which directly attack periodontal tissues and masticatory muscles, accelerating oral function decline and increasing the risk of oral frailty [38]. Furthermore, patients with multimorbidity are typically on multiple medications, which reduce salivary secretion and flow rate, significantly impairing the oral self-cleaning capacity. This leads to the proliferation of oral bacteria, forming a vicious cycle of “reduced saliva-bacterial proliferation-further reduction in saliva.” [39]. Although polypharmacy did not emerge as a significant independent predictor in the regression model in this study, this may be related to the unique national context in China that cannot be overlooked. In China, due to the uneven distribution of healthcare resources, insufficient awareness among older adults regarding long-term management of chronic diseases, and economic constraints, medication adherence among elderly patients with multimorbidity is generally low [40]. This results in a discrepancy between the actual number of medications taken and the reality of their multiple chronic conditions, and our analysis lacked stratification based on the number of medications. Our study identified that a higher frequency of hospital admissions in one year was independently associated with a greater severity of oral frailty among older adults with multimorbidity. Hospitalization itself can be a catalyst for oral frailty. Patients, especially the frail elderly, may experience rapid sarcopenia, including the disuse atrophy of masticatory muscles due to reduced food intake or liquid-only diets [11]. This direct loss of masseter and temporalis muscle strength and coordination can precipitate chewing and swallowing difficulties, core components of oral frailty [41]. Although observational studies on oral frailty, including our study, have been increasingly accumulating, intervention-based evidence remains scarce. Only a few studies have explored the reversibility of individual components of oral frailty in community-dwelling older adults, such as through oral function training [42]. However, interventions targeting elderly patients with multimorbidity - the potentially most vulnerable population that may benefit the most from early intervention - have not yet been reported. Therefore, the hospital setting should be recognized as a critical intervention point for breaking this cycle. Systematic screening for oral frailty upon admission and at discharge, coupled with integrated oral care protocols during hospitalization, should be part of comprehensive geriatric care.

This study has several limitations. First, this study had a cross-sectional design, which allows us to establish associations but not to determine causality. Second, the key outcome measures in this study were based on patient-reported outcomes rather than objective measures. Third, this study did not conduct analyses stratified by specific multimorbidity types, thus precluding a detailed analysis of how individual diseases contribute to oral frailty. Furthermore, selection bias may exist in this study. As all participants in this study were recruited from inpatient wards or outpatient clinics, this sample represents a population with higher illness severity requiring active disease management. Consequently, the prevalence of oral frailty in this study is substantially higher than that in community-dwelling older adults. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to community populations. Future studies should aim to enlarge the cohort, refine multimorbidity categorization, and integrate objective instrumental measurements to facilitate a more comprehensive and precise evaluation of oral frailty.

Policy and clinical implications

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of oral frailty among elderly patients with multimorbidity and identifies its significant associations with the degree of multimorbidity, frailty status, and frequency of hospital readmissions. Given the frequent transitions between hospital and community, we call for integrating oral health screening, using simple tools like the OFI-8 to identify at-risk patients and trigger timely referrals, into geriatric assessments or community-based frailty programs, which would ensure that older adults reap the benefits.

Conclusion

In summary, this study found that the incidence of OF among elderly patients with multimorbidity was 78.3%. Several factors, such as the number of missing teeth, the number of readmission within one year, comorbidity degree, frailty, and dry mouth, significantly influence the severity of OF. Therefore, the risk of oral frailty among older patients with multimorbidity is alarmingly high. Clinical practitioners should therefore prioritize screening and intervention for this group. They should actively provide oral health knowledge, formulate personalized management plans, and work to prevent and control oral frailty, thereby improving the patients’ overall quality of life.
