DPP-4 inhibitory and insulin-signaling-modulating potential of Allomyrina dichotoma larval protein hydrolysate
Authors: Jae Hee Han, Joong-Hyuck Auh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the DPP-4 inhibitory and insulin signaling modulating activities of Allomyrina dichotoma (rhinoceros beetle) larval protein hydrolysate. Following enzymatic hydrolysis, three fractions were obtained by HPLC fractionation. Among them, fraction 3 (F3) exhibited the strongest dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitory activity (IC50: 0.72 mg/mL). In an insulin-resistant HepG2 cell model, F3 significantly increased glucose uptake and enhanced Akt phosphorylation at Thr308, along with upregulation of GLUT4 expression, indicating improved insulin signaling responses. Furthermore, LC–MS/MS analysis identified 22 compounds enriched in hydrophobic and aromatic amino acids within F3. Molecular docking analysis predicted that these compounds interact with key DPP-4 active-site residues, including Glu205/Glu206, Tyr547, and Tyr662. Additionally, quantitative analysis revealed substantial levels of Trp, Val-Leu, and Met-Leu, which have previously been reported as DPP-4 inhibitory compounds. Collectively, these findings suggest that A. dichotoma larval hydrolysate contains bioactive peptides with DPP-4-inhibitory and insulin-signaling-modulating properties, supporting its potential for glucose homeostasis.Keywords: Allomyrina dichotoma larvae, Bioactive peptide, DPP-4 inhibition, Insulin resistance, LC–MS/MS analysis

Introduction

Global population growth and increasing pressure on natural resources have raised significant concerns regarding the sustainability of conventional livestock production as a primary protein source. Edible insects have garnered increasing attention as a promising alternative, offering nutrient profiles comparable to those of traditional livestock while generating substantially lower greenhouse gas emissions (Lisboa et al., 2024). In Korea, Allomyrina dichotoma larvae were approved as a novel food ingredient and classified as a general food ingredient in 2016 by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. These larvae contain more than 50% protein on a dry matter basis, highlighting their potential as a viable alternative protein source (Ghosh et al., 2017). Enzymatic hydrolysis has been proposed as a strategy to further enhance the functional properties and digestibility of such protein fractions (Yi et al., 2016). Consistent with this approach, protein fractions and peptides derived from A. dichotoma larvae have demonstrated various bioactive properties, including antineoplastic and anti-obesity effects (Kim et al., 2016).

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from insulin resistance. Insulin resistance impairs glucose uptake in hepatic, muscle, and adipose tissues, leading to systemic glucose dysregulation. The liver plays a role in maintaining systemic glucose homeostasis through insulin receptor signaling, which activates the PI3K/Akt pathway via phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate proteins (Galicia-Garcia et al., 2020). Accordingly, HepG2 cells are widely used as an in vitro model to investigate hepatic insulin resistance, glucose uptake, and the effects of potential antidiabetic agents due to their metabolic similarities to human hepatocytes (Lammi et al., 2015; Meng et al., 2025). In these models, hepatic glucose uptake and homeostasis are regulated by glucose transporters, including both GLUT2 and the insulin-responsive GLUT4 (Kim and Auh, 2025; Miao et al., 2023). In particular, the downregulation of GLUT4 expression is recognized as a characteristic indicator of metabolic dysfunction in HepG2 cells under insulin-resistant conditions induced by high glucose or fatty acids (Cho and Shaw, 2023; Miao et al., 2023). Previous studies have also shown that restoring the Akt/GLUT4 signaling pathway effectively improves glucose consumption and utilization in these insulin-resistant models (Kim and Auh, 2025; Wang et al., 2020). Activated Akt promotes the translocation of GLUT4 to the plasma membrane, thereby facilitating cellular glucose uptake (Li et al., 2023).

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) degrades incretin hormones such as glucagon-like peptide-1, thereby reducing their insulinotropic activity (Liu et al., 2019). Although pharmaceutical DPP-4 inhibitors are widely prescribed, they may cause adverse effects such as gastrointestinal discomfort and headache (Huang et al., 2020). Therefore, food-derived bioactive peptides have been investigated as natural DPP-4 modulators, offering a promising alternative strategy (Jao et al., 2015). Protein hydrolysates derived from edible insects have also been reported to exhibit DPP-4 inhibitory activity, including those from Antheraea assamensis and Gryllodes sigillatus (Ma et al., 2023).

Although A. dichotoma larvae exhibit diverse bioactive properties, evidence supporting the antidiabetic effects of its protein hydrolysate on glucose metabolism and insulin resistance remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the antidiabetic potential of A. dichotoma larval hydrolysate (ADLH) through in vitro assessment of DPP-4 inhibitory activity and modulation of the insulin signaling pathway, combined with LC–MS/MS identification of bioactive compounds. Additionally, in silico molecular docking was performed to characterize the binding interactions between selected compounds and DPP-4, complementing the evaluation of its DPP-4 inhibitory potential.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dried A. dichotoma larvae fed fermented sawdust were obtained from Insect Vision (Yangju, Korea) and stored at − 50 °C until use. n-Hexane (EP grade) was purchased from Honeywell (Charlotte, NC, USA), and Alcalase (2.4 L) was obtained from Novozymes (Bagsvaerd, Denmark). High-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) were supplied by Biowest (Nuaille, France). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), HEPES, trypsin–EDTA (0.05%), and penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). MTT was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Preparation of ADLH

ADLH was prepared according to a previously reported method with slight modifications (Kang et al., 2023). Ground larval powder was defatted with n-hexane at a 1:20 (w/v) ratio for 36 h, with solvent replacement every 12 h, and subsequently dried for 8 h. The defatted powder (12.5 g) was dispersed in 200 mL of distilled water (DW) and sonicated using a Sonics® Vibra-Cell™ VCX 750 (Sonics & Materials Inc., Newtown, CT, USA) at 20 kHz for 5 min at 75% amplitude (3 s pulses with 1 s intervals, three cycles on ice). The resulting extract was passed through a 1 mm stainless-steel sieve and freeze-dried for 72 h. The crude extract (500 mg) was reconstituted in 20 mL of DW, and Alcalase (72 mU/g protein) was added for enzymatic hydrolysis at 55 °C for 4 h. After hydrolysis, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 40 min. The supernatant was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper (GE Healthcare, UK), lyophilized for 72 h, and stored at − 50 °C until further analysis.

Protein degradation patterns of ADLH

Protein degradation was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Samples containing 50 µg of protein were mixed with 4 × Laemmli sample buffer at a 3:1 (v/v) ratio and heated at 97 °C for 5 min. The denatured samples were loaded onto 5% stacking and 15% separating polyacrylamide gels and electrophoresed at 80 V for 150 min. After electrophoresis, gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for 30 min and destained for 4 h. Protein bands were visualized using a Molecular Imager Gel Doc™ XR + (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Fractionation of ADLH by preparative liquid chromatography

ADLH was fractionated using preparative reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) (LC-Forte/R, YMC, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a C18 column (Triart C18, 250 × 15 mm, 10 µm; YMC). The mobile phases consisted of DW (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–5 min, 0% B; 5–30 min, 0–30% B; 30–32 min, 30% B; and 32–35 min, 30–100% B. The flow rate was set at 10 mL/min, and detection was performed at 210, 254, and 285 nm. The collected fractions were concentrated using a rotary vacuum evaporator (Eyela, Tokyo, Japan) at 36 °C, freeze-dried, and stored at − 50 °C until further use.

DPP-4 inhibitory activity assay

DPP-4 activity was measured using a fluorescence-based DPP-4 inhibitor screening kit (ab133081; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) in half-volume 96-well white plates. Each reaction mixture contained 30 µL of assay buffer, 10 µL of diluted DPP-4 enzyme, and 10 µL of ADLH samples or sitagliptin (positive control). The reaction was initiated by adding 50 µL of fluorogenic substrate (Gly-Pro-AMC), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Cell culture and insulin resistance model

HepG2 cells (HB-8065) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% HEPES, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To establish an insulin-resistant model, cells at approximately 70%–80% confluence were exposed to serum-free high-glucose DMEM (4.5 g/L) supplemented with 1 µM insulin for 24 h (Choi and Auh, 2025). The insulin-resistant HepG2 cells were subsequently treated with 400 µg/mL of ADLH fractions for 24 h, followed by stimulation with 100 nM insulin for 20 min prior to analysis.

Cell viability assay

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using an MTT assay. Insulin-resistant HepG2 cells (1.0 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with ADLH fractions at concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL for 20 h. After treatment, 20 µL of MTT reagent (1 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The medium was then removed, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 200 µL of DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M2, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Glucose uptake assay

Cellular glucose uptake was quantified using a luminescence-based Glucose Uptake-Glo™ Assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Following treatment with samples, cells were incubated with 50 µL of 1 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then sequentially treated with 25 µL of stop buffer, 25 µL of neutralization buffer, and 100 µL of detection reagent to measure intracellular 2-DG6P levels. After 1 h of incubation, luminescence was recorded using a GloMax® Discover microplate luminometer (Promega).

Western blot analysis

HepG2 cells (8 × 105 cells/dish) were lysed in RIPA (radioimmunoprecipitation) buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor, sonicated for 30 s, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein samples (20 µg) were separated by 8% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked for 90 min in 5% BSA prepared in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST). Membranes were incubated for 24 h at 4 °C with primary antibodies (β-actin from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; Akt, phospho-Akt Thr308, and GLUT4 from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using electrochemiluminescence (ECL) solution (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and imaged with an Amersham ImageQuant 800 (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageQuant TL software (version 10.2; Cytiva), with phospho-Akt (Thr308) normalized to total Akt and GLUT4 normalized to β-actin.

LC–MS/MS analysis

ADLH fractions were analyzed using UHPLC-HESI-Orbitrap MS/MS. The analysis was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive™ Orbitrap MS/MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), equipped with a Hypersil GOLD C18 column (1.9 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 36 °C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–3 min, 5% B; 3–6 min, 5–12% B; 6–10 min, 12–30% B; 10–15 min, 30–100% B; and 15–18 min, 100% B. A 5 µL sample was injected at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Full MS scans were acquired in positive ionization mode.

Data processing and statistical analysis

LC–MS/MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with the following parameters: retention time (RT) window of 0–15 min; minimum peak intensity of 1 × 105 ions; and alignment mass tolerance of 5 ppm. Metabolite identification was based on accurate m/z values and MS/MS fragmentation patterns, with all assignments restricted to mass errors within ± 5 ppm. Metabolite identification was performed using public spectral databases, including MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), FOODB (https://foodb.ca/), MassBank (www.massbank.jp), and HMDB (https://www.hmdb.ca/). Identification levels were assigned according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) criteria. Level 1 identification was achieved for Trp, Val-Leu, and Met-Leu through validation with authentic standards obtained from Biostem (Suwon, South Korea). Level 2 identification was assigned to the remaining metabolites listed in Table 1 based on spectral library matching. Heatmaps for visual comparison of sample differences were generated using Compound Discoverer 3.3.

All experiments were conducted independently in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 12.0 software (Statistical Packages for Social Science; IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago, IL, USA). Differences among groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05). For metabolite profiling, multivariate statistical analyses were conducted using SIMCA 18.0 (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden) with Pareto scaling. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed to assess sample clustering and differences in metabolite profiles among groups. Variables were selected based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values ≥ 1.5 and p < 0.05.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of human DPP-4 complexed with sitagliptin (PDB ID: 1X70) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. The structure was prepared by removing water molecules and co-crystallized ligands using the UCSF ChimeraX software. Three-dimensional structures of the identified bioactive compounds were generated using RDKit, an open-source cheminformatics software, and energy-minimized using the MMFF94 force field. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and Gasteiger charges were assigned using AutoDockTools. Molecular docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina (Gonzatti et al., 2023). The grid box was centered at x = 41.26, y = 51.05, z = 35.56 with dimensions of 26 × 26 × 26 Å. Protein–ligand interactions were analyzed and visualized using Discovery Studio Visualizer.

Metabolite quantification

DPP-4 inhibitory compounds were quantified using parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) mode under the same conditions described for the LC–MS/MS analysis. The purity of the target compounds (Trp m/z 188.0705, Val-Leu m/z 231.1702, and Met-Leu m/z 263.1423) was confirmed to be greater than 95% by HPLC analysis.

Results and discussion

Extraction yields and protein hydrolysis of ADLH

The yield of ADLH was calculated on a dry weight basis. Defatting of dried A. dichotoma larvae produced 66.52 ± 2.41% defatted powder. Ultrasonic-assisted extraction yielded 35.81 ± 3.08% crude protein extract. Following enzymatic hydrolysis with Alcalase, 76.00 ± 2.00% hydrolysate was recovered, resulting in an overall yield of 18.12 ± 1.94%. SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 1A) demonstrated that high-molecular-weight proteins (10–40 kDa) were extensively degraded into peptides below 10 kDa, confirming effective proteolysis. This pattern of extensive proteolysis into low-molecular-weight fragments is consistent with the broad endopeptidase specificity of Alcalase toward various amino acid residues, which has been reported across diverse protein substrates (Cui et al., 2021).

Preparation and fractionation of ADLH. A SDS-PAGE analysis illustrating protein degradation patterns during Alcalase hydrolysis. B Preparative HPLC chromatogram showing separation of ADLH into three fractions (F1, F2, F3), monitored at 210, 254, and 285 nm

Fractionation of ADLH by preparative liquid chromatography

ADLH was separated into three fractions (F1, F2, and F3) by preparative HPLC, to separate peptide components with differing physicochemical properties and evaluate their individual contributions to bioactivity. The corresponding UV chromatogram is presented in Fig. 1B. Three fractions were collected according to retention time and peak characteristics: F1 (2.0–9.0 min) was colorless, F2 (9.0–17.5 min) appeared pale yellow, and F3 (17.5–32.0 min) exhibited a deeper yellow coloration.

DPP-4 inhibitory activity of ADLH fractions

Assessment of DPP-4 inhibitory activity revealed that F3 exhibited the strongest inhibition (IC50 = 0.72 ± 0.03 mg/mL), followed by F2 (0.81 ± 0.07 mg/mL), crude ADLH (0.90 ± 0.06 mg/mL), and F1 (0.98 ± 0.03 mg/mL) (Fig. 2A). Sitagliptin, a well-known DPP-4 inhibitor used as the positive control, showed an IC50 of 0.011 ± 0.001 μg/mL.

These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating the DPP-4 inhibitory activity of insect protein hydrolysates. Reported IC50 values include Alphitobius diaperinus (0.63 mg/mL), Gryllodes sigillatus (0.27 mg/mL), and Antheraea assamensis (5.06–5.22 mg/mL) (Lacroix et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2023). The IC50 values of ADLH fractions are comparable to those reported for other insect protein hydrolysates. Notably, the superior activity of F3 indicates that chromatographic fractionation effectively enriched peptides with DPP-4 inhibitory activity. This finding supports that ADLH may serve as a source of bioactive compounds warranting further investigation for glucose metabolism.

Cytotoxicity assessment of ADLH fractions

The cytotoxicity of ADLH and its fractions was evaluated using an MTT assay in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells. Cell viability below 80% was considered indicative of cytotoxicity. All samples maintained cell viability above 80% across the tested concentrations, indicating no significant cytotoxic effects in HepG2 cells. Based on these results, the concentration range of 100–400 μg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments.

Glucose uptake enhancement by ADLH fractions

While DPP-4 inhibition primarily acts through incretin-mediated insulin secretion, improvement of hepatic insulin signaling represents a complementary mechanism contributing to glucose homeostasis. Accordingly, the effects of ADLH fractions on glucose uptake and insulin signaling were evaluated in an insulin-resistant HepG2 cell model. Insulin resistance in HepG2 cells was confirmed by a significant reduction in glucose uptake under high-glucose and hyperinsulinemic conditions compared with the low-glucose control, consistent with previously reported induction conditions (Choi and Auh, 2025).

As shown in Fig. 2B, F3 (1.25 ± 0.01) produced the most pronounced increase in glucose uptake compared with the insulin-resistant control group. This corresponded to approximately a 25% enhancement relative to the insulin-resistant baseline and exceeded the response observed in the insulin-stimulated group (1.04 ± 0.02). F2 (1.16 ± 0.01) also significantly enhanced glucose uptake, whereas F1 (1.07 ± 0.03) and crude ADLH (1.12 ± 0.01) showed less pronounced effects than F2 and F3. These findings suggest that the peptides enriched in F3 may contribute to improved glucose uptake and insulin signaling responses under insulin-resistant conditions.

Comparable effects have been reported for other food protein-derived peptides; soy glycinin-derived peptides such as IAVPGEVA, IAVPTGVA, and LPYP significantly increased glucose uptake in insulin-resistant models (Lammi et al., 2015). Similarly, walnut-derived peptides have been shown to improve glucose uptake and GLUT4 translocation in high glucose-induced insulin-resistant HepG2 cells (Wang et al., 2020). These previous findings align with the results of the present study and further support that F3 effectively restores glucose uptake capacity in insulin-resistant hepatocytes.

Activation of insulin signaling pathway by ADLH fractions

To evaluate the effects of ADLH fractions on insulin signaling, western blot analysis was performed to examine the PI3K/Akt pathway, a central regulator of insulin-mediated cellular responses. Phosphorylation of Akt at Thr308 is a critical step in this pathway, initiating the downstream cascade that mediates insulin's metabolic effects (Boucher et al., 2014). As shown in Fig. 2C and 2D, treatment with ADLH samples enhanced Akt phosphorylation at Thr308 compared with the insulin-stimulated group (insulin: 1.23 ± 0.11; crude: 1.48 ± 0.16; F1: 1.37 ± 0.14; F2: 1.87 ± 0.05; F3: 2.15 ± 0.18). In insulin-resistant HepG2 cells, phospho-Akt (Thr308) expression was moderately increased in the insulin-stimulated group relative to the insulin-resistant control, indicating partial retention of insulin responsiveness. Notably, F3 induced the greatest increase in phospho-Akt (Thr308) among all tested samples. These findings indicate that ADLH fractions, particularly F3, enhance PI3K/Akt pathway activation and may therefore contribute to the improvement of insulin resistance in HepG2 cells.

In parallel, GLUT4 expression was evaluated as a downstream target of Akt activation and an insulin-dependent glucose transporter essential for cellular glucose uptake (Fig. 2C and 2E). Under insulin-resistant conditions, GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane is impaired, thereby restricting glucose entry into cells (van Gerwen et al., 2023). GLUT4 expression levels were as follows (insulin: 1.47 ± 0.09; crude: 1.85 ± 0.21; F1: 1.61 ± 0.23; F2: 2.92 ± 0.19; F3: 3.34 ± 0.08). Fractions F1–F3 increased GLUT4 expression relative to the insulin-stimulated group, with F3 exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation. The coordinated enhancement of phospho-Akt (Thr308) and GLUT4 expression suggests that F3 contains bioactive peptides capable of restoring insulin signaling in insulin-resistant hepatocytes.

Metabolomic profiling and multivariate statistical analysis

To investigate the compositional basis of the DPP-4 inhibitory activity and signaling modulation observed in F3, metabolomic profiling of the ADLH fractions was conducted using UHPLC-Orbitrap MS/MS data, followed by multivariate statistical analyses to differentiate their compositional characteristics. Heatmap analysis of the 765 detected metabolites revealed distinct abundance patterns among the ADLH fractions (Fig. 3A). The PCA score plot (Fig. 3B) showed that principal component 1 (PC1) and principal component 2 (PC2) accounted for 55.7% and 39.2% of the total variance, respectively. Notably, F3 was clearly separated from F1 and F2 along PC1, indicating substantial compositional differences. These results suggest that the three fractions differ in their peptide compositions, with F3 displaying the most distinctive metabolomic profile. Further discrimination by OPLS-DA identified 22 characteristic compounds enriched in F3, with VIP values > 1.5 and p(corr) < 0.05 (Fig. 3C and 3D).

Identification of bioactive peptides and amino acids

Metabolite identification revealed that F3 was particularly enriched in aromatic and hydrophobic aliphatic dipeptides and amino acids. Tryptophan (m/z 188.0704) was identified by characteristic MS/MS fragment ions at m/z 146.0596, 118.0650, and 144.0805. The fragment at m/z 146 arises from the loss of CH2CO (42 Da) from protonated tryptophan, followed by CO loss (28 Da) to generate m/z 118, which is characteristic of indole ring fragmentation (Zhang et al., 2019). Leu/Ile-Tyr (m/z 295.1650) was identified by fragment ions at m/z 86.0968, 136.0756, and 165.0545, where the immonium ion at m/z 136 is characteristic of tyrosine and essential for identifying Tyr-containing peptides (Dorner et al., 2021). Phenylalanine containing compounds, such as Val-Phe (m/z 265.1545), was characterized by the phenylalanine immonium ion at m/z 120 (Dorner et al., 2021) (Fig. 4).

Two-dimensional binding interaction diagrams of bioactive compounds with DPP-4. A Sitagliptin (reference inhibitor). B Val-Leu. C Met-Leu. D Tryptophan (Trp). Green dashed lines indicate hydrogen bonds, and pink lines represent hydrophobic interactions (π–π stacking, π–alkyl, and π–σ interactions)

Among the identified aliphatic dipeptides, Leu/Ile-Leu/Ile (m/z 245.1854) was identified based on key fragment ions at m/z 86.0967 and m/z 69.0704. The fragment at m/z 86 corresponds to the characteristic immonium ion of leucine/isoleucine residues (Maibom-Thomsen et al., 2018). Although the ion at m/z 69 has been reported as a diagnostic fragment for isoleucine, its formation has also been reported for leucine under sufficient collision energy, precluding definitive sequence assignment from MS/MS data alone (Zhang et al., 2019). Val-Leu (m/z 231.1702) was validated using an authentic standard and characterized by MS/MS fragment ions at m/z 72.0812, 55.0548, and 86.0967, consistent with established fragmentation patterns for branched-chain dipeptides (Zhang et al., 2019). Met-Leu (m/z 263.1423) was also validated using an authentic standard and showed characteristic fragment ions at m/z 104.0529 (methionine immonium ion), 61.0112, and 56.0500, consistent with established fragmentation patterns for methionine-containing peptides (Zhang et al., 2019).

Basic residue containing peptides identified in F3 included arginine and lysine containing compounds. Arginine containing peptides, including Leu/Ile-Arg (m/z 288.2024), were identified based on fragmentation patterns featuring a diagnostic arginine fragment ion at m/z 70 (Galligan et al., 2017). Lys-Leu/Ile (m/z 260.1967) was identified based on fragment ions at m/z 84.0812 and 86.0968, consistent with characteristic lysine and leucine/isoleucine residue fragmentation patterns (Maibom-Thomsen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019).

Collectively, F3 was enriched in aromatic, aliphatic, and basic residue containing compounds, many of which were dipeptides. The predominance of hydrophobic and aromatic residues supports a strong interaction potential within the hydrophobic pockets of DPP-4 (Lacroix and Li-Chan, 2016). Dipeptides and tripeptides are generally more resistant to gastrointestinal digestion than larger peptides, which may favor their bioavailability (Mora-Melgem et al., 2023). Furthermore, food-derived DPP-4 inhibitory peptides may exert inhibitory activity locally within the proximal small intestine rather than relying exclusively on systemic absorption (Liu et al., 2019) (Table 2).

Molecular docking analysis of DPP-4 inhibition

Molecular docking was conducted to predict the binding interactions between the free amino acids and dipeptides identified in F3 and human DPP-4. The calculated docking scores ranged from − 5.05 to − 7.71 kcal/mol, indicating generally stable binding affinities (Mora-Melgem et al., 2023). These values are consistent with previously reported AutoDock Vina scores for amino acids (− 3.4 to − 6.2 kcal/mol) and dipeptides (− 5.4 to − 8.1 kcal/mol) interacting with DPP-4, and fall within the range previously reported for small ligands interacting with DPP-4 (Nongonierma et al., 2013).

Sitagliptin, a reference pharmaceutical inhibitor, exhibited a strong binding energy of − 8.33 kcal/mol, consistent with the binding energy reported for sitagliptin in a mechanistic interaction study of the sitagliptin–DPP-4 complex (Gonzatti et al., 2023).

Among the tested compounds, aromatic dipeptides demonstrated the most favorable binding energies. Tyr- containing dipeptides, Leu-Tyr (− 7.71 kcal/mol) and Ile-Tyr (− 7.60 kcal/mol) formed hydrogen bonds with Tyr547 and Tyr662, along with hydrophobic interactions across both the S1 subsite (Trp659, Tyr631, Tyr666, Val656) and the S2 subsite (Phe357). Similarly, Phe-containing dipeptides such as Val-Phe (− 7.07 kcal/mol) and Thr-Phe (− 6.82 kcal/mol) showed notable binding energies, forming hydrogen bonds with Tyr547 and Tyr662 and hydrophobic contacts within S2 subsite (Phe357). These shared interaction features are consistent with the established role of Tyr547 and Tyr662, and Phe357 in aromatic ligand recognition by DPP-4 (Arulmozhiraja et al., 2016; Pan et al., 2022). Tryptophan (− 7.28 kcal/mol), also exhibited favorable binding through hydrogen bonds with His740 and Tyr662, with its indole ring positioned within the S2 hydrophobic region, consistent with the S2 engagement reported for N-terminal tryptophan (Lan et al., 2015).

Aliphatic dipeptides, including Val-Leu (− 5.86 kcal/mol) and Met-Leu (− 5.57 kcal/mol) showed moderate binding affinities. Val-Leu formed hydrogen bonds with Tyr547 and Tyr662, with its aliphatic side chain accommodated within the S1 hydrophobic pocket (Trp659, Phe357, Val656, Tyr631, Tyr666). Met-Leu showed similar interaction profile, with additional hydrogen bonding involving His740. Nevertheless, its slightly lower affinity compared to Val-Leu may reflect suboptimal accommodation of the methionine side chain within the S1 pocket, consistent with reports that exclusion volumes in this region restrict the access of bulkier residues (Nongonierma et al., 2014). These results align with previous findings that aliphatic side chains contribute to DPP-4 inhibitory activity through favorable accommodation within the hydrophobic S1 subsite (Araki et al., 2020).

Dipeptides containing basic residues, including Arg and Lys, were characterized by interactions with the Glu205/Glu206 glutamate dyad in the S2 subsite. Among these, Ile-Arg exhibited the most favorable binding energy (− 7.23 kcal/mol), with predicted hydrogen bonds at Tyr547 and Tyr662 and electrostatic interactions with both Glu205 and Glu206. Leu-Arg showed a comparable binding energy (− 6.80 kcal/mol), forming hydrogen bonds with Glu205 and Glu206, Tyr547, and Tyr662, as well as additional contacts with Ser209 and Val207, alongside hydrophobic interactions across the S1 subsite. Lys-containing dipeptides, Lys-Leu (− 6.37 kcal/mol) and Lys-Ile (− 6.50 kcal/mol), also engaged Glu205 through hydrogen bonding and Glu206 through electrostatic interaction, in addition to contacts with Tyr547 and Tyr662. The involvement of Glu205 and Glu206 is consistent with previous reports identifying this glutamate dyad as a key recognition site for positively charged ligands in the S2 subsite (Arulmozhiraja et al., 2016).

Overall, the docking results suggest that the amino acids and dipeptides identified in fraction F3 interact with multiple residues located within the S1 and S2 subsites of DPP-4. Key residues, including Tyr547, Tyr662, Glu205, Glu206, and Phe357, were consistently involved in stabilizing ligand binding through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts. These docking results provide a computational basis for rationalizing the observed DPP-4 inhibitory activity, and are consistent with the enrichment of hydrophobic and aromatic compounds identified in F3 by metabolomic analysis.

Quantification of key bioactive compounds

Previous studies have reported that the dipeptides Val-Leu and Met-Leu exhibit DPP-4 inhibitory activities, with IC50 values of 74 ± 10 μM and 91 ± 4 μM, respectively (Lan et al., 2015). In addition, Trp has been shown to possess comparatively weaker inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value of 4280.40 ± 48.03 μM (Nongonierma et al., 2013). Based on these reported potencies, Val-Leu, Met-Leu, and Trp were selected for quantitative determination in F3.

Among the quantified compounds, Trp exhibited the highest concentration in F3 (8488.04 ± 44.38 μg/g), followed by Val-Leu (5100.63 ± 36.26 μg/g) and Met-Leu (756.85 ± 2.96 μg/g). Although Trp demonstrates relatively weak DPP-4 inhibitory potency compared to the dipeptides, its substantial abundance suggests that it may still contribute to the overall inhibitory activity of F3. Notably, the combination of relatively high concentration and strong inhibitory potency of Val-Leu indicates that this dipeptide is likely a major contributor to the DPP-4 inhibitory capacity of the fraction.
