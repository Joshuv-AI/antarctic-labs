Effects of intra-articular injection of dimethyl itaconate combined with systemic vancomycin on periprosthetic joint infection in rats
Authors: Tianyu Dai, Chi Ma, Fan Zhang, Yinxian Wen, Liaobin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThis investigation aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of intra-articular dimethyl itaconate (DI) combined with systemic vancomycin (Van) for the treatment of periprosthetic joint infections (PJI) induced by Staphylococcus aureus.MethodsThe fifty rats were divided into five groups: Control (Con), PJI, PJI + DI, PJI + Van, and PJI + Van + DI. After three weeks, systemic and local inflammation, osteolysis, bone histomorphometry, bacterial load, and biofilm formation were analyzed. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was used to evaluate osteoclast inhibition by DI and vancomycin, and Alizarin Red staining was used to assess its effect on osteogenic differentiation.ResultsIn vivo, the combination therapy demonstrated superior mitigation of bone loss and intra-articular synovial inflammation compared to Van monotherapy. Furthermore, the co-administration of DI and vancomycin effectively facilitated bacterial and biofilm clearance within the joint, further alleviating the dysregulation between bone resorption and remodelling. Compared to the DI and vancomycin monotherapy group, the Van + DI group showed reduced bacterial counts in implants, bone tissue, and soft-tissue. However, intra-articular DI alone was insufficient for complete eradication of S. aureus-induced PJI. Safety assessments indicated that neither DI monotherapy nor its combination with vancomycin elicited hepatic or renal toxicity in rats. In vitro, it was validated that DI exerts a promotive effect on osteoblast function while concurrently inhibiting osteoclast formation.ConclusionIntra-articular injection of DI combined with systemic vancomycin appears to be a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for PJI in our current rat model.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(6):739–751.

Introduction

The incidence of complications has risen in proportion to the increasing global volume of joint arthroplasties. Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) represents a severe postoperative complication.1Staphylococcus aureus is a predominant pathogen in PJI, responsible for approximately 40% of cases.2 This bacterium possesses extensive genetic diversity and multiple virulence factors, which serve a pivotal function in the initiation, persistence, and recurrence of PJI.3,4 A major challenge in managing PJI is biofilm formation, which reduces bacteria susceptibility to antibiotics by 10 to 1,000 fold, markedly enhancing the likelihood of antimicrobial resistance.5,6 Furthermore, S. aureus and its biofilm-related components have been implicated in bone resorption by stimulating osteoclast activity.7-10 Additionally, S. aureus contributes to prosthesis loosening by impairing osteogenic function and inducing excessive osteoclast activity, ultimately leading to periprosthetic osteolysis.11 Addressing the effective eradication of biofilm-embedded bacteria and managing bone defects from biofilm-induced infections remains a significant clinical challenge.

Itaconic acid, a metabolite of the tricarboxylic acid cycle in eukaryotic cells, along with its derivatives, has been demonstrated to exert significant anti-inflammatory effects within cellular environments.12,13 Preclinical investigations have indicated that itaconic acid exhibits notable anti-inflammatory properties in various disease models, including acute liver failure, atherosclerosis, osteoarthritis, gout, and pulmonary fibrosis.14-16 Recent research has identified dimethyl itaconate (DI) as an effective agent in diminishing bacterial pathogenicity, encompassing virulence, motility, and biofilm formation.17 Vancomycin (Van) remains one of the important antibiotics for PJI management.18 While high-dose intravenous administration effectively controls Gram-positive PJI, it is also associated with hepatorenal toxicity and other systemic complications. The combination therapy has been recognized for its ability to penetrate or disrupt biofilms, effectively counteracting bacterial resistance while allowing for a reduction in antibiotic dosage to minimize adverse reactions.19 Consequently, this study sought to evaluate the efficacy and tolerability of joint-specific DI delivery combined with systemic vancomycin administration when treating PJI in a rat model of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA)-induced PJI, which has been previously established by Dai et al.20 This study strives to establish experimental evidence regarding the ability of DI to resist infection and bone loss in managing endoprosthetic infections caused by MRSA, and to propose potential therapeutic strategies for orthopaedic implant-related infections.

Methods

Bacterial preparation

The MRSA ATCC43300 strain (Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, China) was used. Monoclonal colonies were selected from Lysogeny agar plates and transferred into Lysogeny broth (LB) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) medium, followed by incubation at 37°C in a constant-temperature shaker for 14 hours at 6,000 RPM.Using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), a 1:100 dilution of the overnight culture was created, which was then further cultivated for an additional four hours at 37°C under continuous shaking at 220 RPM to achieve passivation. Following centrifugation (6,000 rpm, two minutes, 10°C), the supernatant was removed, and the bacterial sediment was reconstituted in PBS. The bacterial suspension was subsequently calibrated to reach 2 × 10⁷ colony-forming units (CFU)/ml, based on optical density (OD) measurements at 600 nm, and subsequently stored at 4°C for future use.

Animal and surgical modelling

The rats were maintained in strictly regulated environmental conditions in individually ventilated cages, with a room temperature of 18°C to 22°C, relative humidity ranging from 40% to 60%, and a 12-hour light-dark cycle. Approval for all animal experiments was sanctioned by the Experimental Animal Management and Utilization Committee of Wuhan University, and all experimental procedures were executed per the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals set forth by the China Animal Welfare Committee.21 We have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and have included the ARRIVE checklist in the Supplementary Material.

Surgical procedures were performed according to a previous rat PJI model.20 In this study, orthopaedic titanium screws were selected to replicate clinical knee arthroplasty and prosthesis implantation within the femoral marrow. All surgical interventions were carried out in a sterile environment, adhering to aseptic techniques and utilizing sterilized instruments and materials. To begin the experiment, each rat was anaesthetised using 2% isoflurane (RWD Life Science, China). The anaesthesia process, dosage and drug administration were approved by the Animal Experiment Center of Wuhan University. Starting the surgical procedure, a 2 cm longitudinal midline incision was made over the right knee to expose the joint. The femoral canal was then drilled to allow insertion of the prosthesis (1.4 mm diameter, 8 mm length). The patella was reduced, and the joint capsule was closed with 4-0 suture (Servicebio, China). In the experimental group, rats received an intra-articular injection of 50 μl of 2 × 10⁷ CFU/ml S.aureus, while the control group received an injection of 50 μl of 0.9% physiological saline as a control. The 50 rats were randomly assigned to five groups: the control group (Con) (non-infected group following femoral prosthesis implantation), the PJI group (infected group after femoral prosthesis implantation), the PJI + DI group (100 mg/kg DI locally injected in the PJI model), the PJI + Van group (44 mg/kg Van administered via intraperitoneal injection), and the PJI + Van + DI group (combination treatment with Van and DI). The systemic dosage of Van based on body weight is derived from standard therapeutic antibiotic dosages for human orthopaedic infections, and corresponds to the dosage used in rat models in previous studies.22,23 The selection of the DI dose for intra-articular injection was based on previous relevant in vivo studies.24-26 Considering the modelling cycle and dosing schedule of this study, we selected a high dose of 100 mg/kg as the injection concentration.

Mirco-CT and data analytics

To evaluate bone resorption, osseointegration, and the infectious bone response in osteomyelitis, imaging of the operated bone was conducted perpendicular to its long axis using the SkyScan 1276 system (Bruker, Germany). The acquired scans were subsequently reconstructed with Mimics Viewer (Materialise, Belgium) and analyzed for periprosthetic bone parameters. 3D-reconstructed images were further processed using CTAn v1.15.4 software (Bruker) for quantitative assessment. A 2 mm area surrounding the prosthesis was identified as the region of interest (ROI), and the bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) were measured.

Scanning electron microscope

The prosthesis was meticulously removed from the bone tissue, and following fixation, it underwent sequential dehydration by immersion in a graded ethanol series with concentrations of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%. Subsequently, the specimens were subjected to drying utilizing a critical point dryer (Quorum, UK), coated by MC1000 ion sputter coating machine (Hitachi, Japan), and ultimately examined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi).

Haematoxylin and eosin staining

Intact joint tissues obtained from rats were immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for fixation, maintained for 48 to 72 hours, and subsequently embedded in paraffin for histological evaluation through haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The tissues were then sectioned, dehydrated, and further embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded specimens were sliced into 5 μm serial sections, stained with haematoxylin for five minutes, and rinsed with water. Following this, the sections were immersed in an 1% ammonia solution and washed again. Subsequently, staining with 1% eosin dye was performed, followed by additional rinsing with water. For each sample, ten distinct fields of view were selected, and all images were acquired using a Nikon NS Elements BR optical microscope (Nikon, Japan). Histological scoring was performed using the modified Petty’s score and synovitis score.27,28

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase staining of bone tissue

The staining protocol was followed as previously published in the literature.29 Paraffin sections of bone tissue were sequentially processed through dewaxing and dehydration, followed by three rinses with distilled water. Sections were circled with a histochemistry pen and placed in a humid chamber, then incubated in distilled water at 37°C for two hours. After incubation, the distilled water was decanted, and the tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) working solution was added dropwise to cover the tissue. The sections were reacted in the dark at 37°C for two hours, then stained with haematoxylin for one minute and counterstained with 1% ammonia solution. Positive TRAP staining was identified by the apperance of burgundy cytoplasm and pale blue nuclei in osteoclasts.30

Immunofluorescence staining of bone tissue

Paraffin-embedded sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-based buffer in a microwave oven for three minutes. After circling the tissue sections with a hydrophobic barrier pen, they were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Roche, USA) for 30 minutes at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight with primary diluted antibody anti-osteocalcin (OCN) (1:200), anti-runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) (1:200), and anti-receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) (1:200) diluted with primary antibody diluent (Servicebio) at 4°C. Following primary antibody incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody for one hour at room temperature, protected from light. After three additional PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Servicebio) for ten minutes. Finally, following a final series of PBS washes, an antifluorescence quencher was added, and the plates were sealed.

CFU assay

The study protocol was conducted in accordance with our previously published study.31 Briefly, the bone and soft tissues obtained from the operated knee of the rats were homogenized with a tissue grinder. At the same time, the prosthesis was immersed in PBS containing 0.3% Tween 20, sonicated for 15 minutes, and vortexed for one minute.32,33 The LB plates were then inoculated using 100 μL from each sample and incubated overnight at 37°C to measure the amount of sample bacteria through colony counting.

Serum biomarker analysis

Serum inflammatory markers, including interleukin 6 (IL-6) and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (α1-AGP), along with biochemical indicators of liver and kidney function such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and creatinine (Cr), were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits procured from ELK Biotechnology (China) and Wuhan Elabscience (China).

In vitro antibacterial experiment

To evaluate the impact of DI on S. aureus proliferation, S. aureus suspensions were co-cultured with varying concentrations of DI in vitro. Specifically, DI was introduced into the bacterial suspension at final concentrations of 2.5, 5, and 10 mg/ml. The cultures were incubated at 37°C with agitation at 220 RPM, and bacterial growth inhibition was assessed by plotting the OD 600 values of the suspension at designated timepoints.34

Osteoclast differentiation of Ralph and William’s 264.7 cells

Ralph and William’s (RAW) 264.7 cells were cultured in a complete medium. For the induction of osteoclast differentiation, the cells underwent cultivation in a complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) comprising 100 ng/ml RANKL and 100 ng/ml macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). The culture medium was refreshed every two days. TRAP staining was executed to verify osteoclast formation and activity.

Isolation of BMSCs and osteogenic differentiation

Primary bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were procured from the lower limb bones of four-week-old rats and cultured in alpha minimum essential medium (α-MEM), comprised of 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. Once BMSCs reached approximately 80% to 90% confluency, they were passaged and seeded into six-well plates. Subsequently, osteogenic differentiation was induced using a specialized medium containing 10 nM dexamethasone (Merkcl, Germany), 50 μg/ml ascorbic acid (Aladdin Biochemical Technology, China), and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Aladdin Biochemical Technology). During the differentiation process, peptidoglycan (PGN) or DI was either introduced or omitted to evaluate the effect of DI on the osteogenic potential of BMSCs. The extent of osteogenic differentiation was determined through Alizarin Red S staining, a recognized indicator of osteogenic activity.

CCK-8 assay

The cell concentration was regulated to 1 × 10⁴/mL and seeded into 96-well plates at 200 μL per well, followed by incubation for 24 hours. Subsequently, BMSCs or RAW264.7 cells (in combination with DI) were exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for an additional 24 hours. Afterward, cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) (Servicebio) (10 μL per well) was introduced, and the cells underwent incubation at 37°C for four hours. The absorbance (A) at 450 nm was then recorded utilizing an automatic microplate reader.

TRAP staining

The RAW264.7 cells underwent fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, and membrane permeabilization was achieved using 0.1% Triton. Following PBS washing, a tartrate-containing buffer and chromogenic substrate were introduced. The samples were then maintained at 37°C for one hour, followed by PBS washing, haematoxylin counterstaining for two minutes, and an additional rinse with Double-Distilled Water (ddH₂O). TRAP enzymatic activity was visualized per the TRAP staining kit (Merck, Germany). Imaging was conducted using a NIS Elements BR light microscope (Nikon, Japan), and quantification analyses were performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Alizarin Red staining

The BMSCs underwent fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes, succeeded by three washes with PBS. Subsequently, Alizarin Red solution (Merck) was applied for staining, and images were captured using a microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

The extraction of total RNA from BMSCs and RAW264.7 cells was performed employing TRIzol solution (Takara, Japan). Reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA was executed utilizing the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara). Subsequently, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was carried out with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) per the supplier’s protocols.35 Gene expression analysis was performed using a real-time PCR system, and relative gene expression levels were determined with glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the reference gene.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, USA). The results are presented as Mean (standard error of the mean (SEM)). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by a post hoc test for multiple comparisons, was employed for statistical evaluation of all experimental data. For comparisons between the two groups, an independent-samples t-test was performed. When the assumption of variance homogeneity was not satisfied, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. p < 0.05 indicates that the difference was set as statistically significant.

Results

General condition of the rats

Rats with PJI exhibited a reduction in body weight and an elevation in localized skin temperture. Throughout the period spanning from postoperative day seven to 21, all treatment groups demonstrated weight gain, accompanied by localized temperature changes in the knee joint to varying extents. However, no statistically significant disparity was identified when comparing the Van group to the Van + DI group (Figures 1a and 1b, p = 0.850 and 0.385, F-test). Serum levels of the inflammatory markers IL-6 and α1-AGP were markedly elevated in the PJI group, but decreased to varying degrees in all treatment groups. Notably, the reduction in expression was more pronounced in the Van + DI group relative to the Van group and DI group (Figures 1c and 1d, p < 0.001, F-test).

Intra-articular inflammation

To evaluate the effect of Van + DI combination therapy on local joint inflammation, we performed H&E staining. H&E staining of synovial tissue within the joint cavity revealed extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in the PJI group, accompanied by pathological hyperplasia and an irregular arrangement of synovial cells. This condition was alleviated to varying degrees across the treatment groups, with a significant reduction in synovitis scores, the most pronounced improvement being observed in the Van + DI group (Figures 2a and 2c, p < 0.001, F-test). Through femoral pathology examination, osteomyelitis characteristics, including severe bone necrosis, intramedullary abscess formation, and leucocyte infiltration, were identified in the PJI group. Van + DI combination therapy led to a substantial decline in the pathological score of PJI rats. The difference in Petty’s score for the Van + DI group was statistically significant compared with the Van group (Figures 2b and 2d, p < 0.001, F-test). Additionally, by quantifying the number of neutrophils in fixed areas, we found that the Van + DI group exhibited a more significant decrease in neutrophil count compared to the Van group and DI group (Figure 2e, p < 0.001, F-test).

Bone mass around the articular prosthesis

To further investigate whether Van + DI combination therapy facilitates the restoration of bone defects and the remodelling of bone tissue, changes in bone volume around the prosthesis were assessed using micro-CT. Massive osteolysis, and disruption of trabecular bone structures, was observed in the PJI group (Figure 3a). The bone loss induced by PJI was notably mitigated following treatment with either DI or Van monotherapy. Furthermore, BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th demonstrated significant increases in the Van + DI group in comparison to the Van group (Figures 3b to 3d, p < 0.001, F-test). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining showed that Van + DI promoted higher expression of osteogenic markers (osteocalcin (OCN), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2)) than Van group (Figures 4a, 4b, 4e, 4f, p < 0.001, F-test). TRAP staining and RANKL IF results indicated that DI effectively mitigates bone loss induced by PJI. Van + DI demonstrated stronger inhibition of osteoclast formation compared to Van alone (Figures 4c, 4d, 4g, 4h, p < 0.001, F-test).

Biofilm formation and bacterial colonization of joint prosthesis

In vitro, it was observed that DI exhibited the ability to effectively inhibit the proliferation of S. aureus in a concentration-dependent manner (Supplementary Figure S1). In vivo, scanning electron microscopy revealed that the surface of the prosthesis in the PJI group was extensively covered with reticular fibrin protein structures, with numerous cocci adhering to form a dense biofilm (Figure 5a). Furthermore, DI and Van demonstrated efficacy in markedly reducing the adhesion of S. aureus on the prosthesis, bone tissue, and soft-tissue, leading to a notable decline in CFUs. When compared to the Van group, the Van + DI group exhibited a substantial reduction in biofilm formation and a marked decrease in bacterial CFUs (Figures 5b to 5d, p < 0.001, F-test).

Effect of DI on bone remodelling function

To explore the mechanism of DI in bone defects and bone repair, we induced osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vitro and assessed the effects of DI on the process. Based on CCK-8 results, DI was found to exert no inhibitory effect on BMSC proliferation when the concentration remained within 10 μM. However, at a concentration of 50 μM, an increase in BMSC proliferation was observed (Figure 6a, p < 0.001, F-test). Moreover, osteogenic mineralization capacity was diminished following PGN treatment (Figure 6c), accompanied by a statistically significant downregulation of osteogenic differentiation marker genes, including ALP, RUNX2, collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), and OCN (p < 0.001, F-test). Nevertheless, DI supplementation successfully reversed the PGN-induced decline in osteogenic differentiation capacity (p < 0.001, F-test). Additionally, CCK-8 results indicated that DI at a concentration of 50 μM exhibited no significant effect on RAW264.7 cell proliferation, whereas exposure to 250 μM resulted in a substantial inhibition of cell proliferation (Figure 6b, p = 0.019). Furthermore, PGN stimulation was observed to enhance the osteoclastic differentiation of RAW264.7 cells and upregulate the expression of osteoclastic marker genes, including NFATc1 and Ctsk (Figure 7a and 7b, p = 0.039 and 0.007, F-test). However, DI effectively suppressed the differentiation of RAW264.7 cells while downregulating the expression of NFATc1 and Ctsk (p < 0.001, p = 0.012, F-test).

Safety assessment of Van + DI combination therapy

The potential liver and kidney injuries associated with Van + DI application in the treatment of PJI were further examined. H&E staining analysis revealed no pathological abnormalities, including tissue degeneration, necrosis, or microstructural alterations, in the liver and kidney tissues of the rats across all groups (Figures 8a and 8b). Additionally, serological testing indicated that there were no statistically significant differences in ALT and Cr levels between the treatment groups and the control group (Figure 8c and 8d, p = 0.439 and 0.796, F-test).

Discussion

The diagnosis and management of PJI remain major clinical challenges, with biofilm formation being a key factor in disease progression due to its role in shielding bacteria from host defenses and antibiotics.5,36 Once established on bone and surrounding tissues, biofilms become difficult to reverse, and can lead to numerous postoperative complications.37 Additionally, biofilms contribute to bone loss around the prosthesis by disrupting bone metabolism, accelerating bone matrix degradation, and inhibiting bone remodelling.38-40 Postoperative implant infections may also affect periprosthetic bone volume, elevating the risk of periprosthetic fractures and increasing the complexity of revision surgery, ultimately influencing surgical outcomes. Given these concerns, identifying therapeutic agents that are capable of simultaneously preventing bone loss while effectively eliminating and controlling biofilms has emerged as a critical research focal point in the management of implant infections. In this study, we first observed that intra-articular administration of DI combined with systemic vancomycin effectively inhibited MRSA biofilm formation and mitigated periprosthetic bone resorption. These findings suggest that the combination of antibiotics with anti-inflammatory or anti-osteoporotic agents may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for PJI treatment.

Most existing studies have indicated that DI exhibits antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory properties.41-43 DI could inhibit the tricarboxylic acid cycle, thereby reducing bacterial viability and pathogenicity while exerting other antimicrobial effects.17 Additionally, DI has been reported to possess in vivo antimicrobial activity, effectively inhibiting bacterial proliferation in lung tissue infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis.44 Based on this evidence, we hypothesized and subsequently confirmed that DI inhibits biofilm formation in PJI. In vitro, based on bacterial growth inhibition data, further intervention was conducted using a combination of DI and vancomycin in PJI rats. Scanning electron microscopy was used to analyze S. aureus biofilm formation on the implant surface, revealing characteristic adhesive reticulation and adherent coccoid structures covering the prosthesis. Additionally, colony plate counting was used to assess bacterial adherence, demonstrating that intra-articular injection of vancomycin in conjunction with DI effectively reduced bacterial colonization on the prosthesis surface. These results suggest that DI disrupts biofilm structure, thereby enhancing bacterial exposure to vancomycin, although complete eradication of the biofilm was not achieved. Furthermore, systemic administration of vancomycin in combination with intra-articular DI injection exhibited superior efficacy in reducing bacterial load in both bone and articular soft tissues compared to vancomycin monotherapy.

Strategies aimed at restoring the balance between bone formation and resorption in the context of PJI remain a subject of considerable interest. Imaging assessments indicated that DI effectively alleviated periprosthetic bone loss associated with PJI, with the Van + DI group exhibiting increased bone mass parameters, including BT/TV, TB.Th, and TB.N, in comparison with vancomycin monotherapy. Furthermore, in vitro experiments revealed that the expression of Nfatc1 and Ctsk decreased to varying extents following DI administration during osteoclast differentiation. Similarly, during BMSC osteogenic differentiation, a concentration-dependent upregulation of osteogenic-specific genes, including ALP, RUNX2, COL1A1, and OCN, was observed in response to DI treatment. Based on these findings, it is hypothesized that DI may contribute to restoring the functional balance between osteoclasts and osteoblasts under PJI-induced inflammatory conditions while simultaneously exerting antibacterial effects. These in vitro and in vivo findings provide reliable data and a theoretical basis for the application of DI in PJI treatment. It is worth noting that DI demonstrates significant potential in preclinical studies of PJI, but further clinical trials are required to validate its efficacy.

In clinical settings, debridement or revision surgery combined with antibiotics is commonly used as a treatment strategy.45-48 However, because this study aimed to evaluate changes in the bone volume surrounding the prosthesis, debridement and prosthesis removal procedures were not included in the treatment protocol. This limitation may explain why complete eradication of pathogenic bacteria and biofilm was not achieved in this study. Furthermore, this study used a limited number of animal samples, necessitating additional research to validate the efficacy of this combined treatment strategy. The observational focus of this study was the acute phase of PJI; its reliability in chronic and haematogenous PJI infections requires further confirmation. Translating knowledge derived from animal studies to human applications remains a critical challenge in medical research. This translational gap often impedes the successful conversion of promising preclinical findings into clinical practice.

In conclusion, the results indicated that the combination of intra-articular DI injection with systemic vancomycin in preclinical studies represents a safe and effective treatment strategy for MRSA-associated PJI. DI appears to serve a dual function by exerting both antibacterial effects and contributing to bone preservation, potentially offering a novel therapeutic option for implant infection treatment.
