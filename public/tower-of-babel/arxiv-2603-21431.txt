Cocycles and positive functionals in higher cohomology
Antonio López Neumann, Piotr W. Nowak

Abstract
We establish and explore the correspondence between positive functionals and cocycles in higher unitary cohomology. We generalize the classical cocycle version of the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction to higher degrees and apply it to characterize vanishing of higher unitary cohomology as an extension property for positive functionals. We also prove that under mild conditions the algebraic spectral gap for the one sided Laplacian characterizes cohomological vanishing instead of reducedness of unitary cohomology.



Introduction

Property
(T)
is a central notion in analytic group theory with many applications to other areas of mathematics, including operator algebras, combinatorics, ergodic theory or dynamics.
For a group
\Gamma
generated by a finite symmetric generating set
S
, the Laplacian

\Delta=1-\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}s,

viewed as an element of the real group ring
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
, plays a central role in studying property
(T)
.
It is easy to see that the group
\Gamma
has property
(T)
if and only if for every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
, zero is an isolated point in the spectrum of the Laplacian
\pi(\Delta)

[
5
, 5.4.5]
.

In
[
22
]
, Ozawa showed a remarkable fact, that for groups with property
(T)
this spectral gap is in fact witnessed on the level of the group ring
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
, via the equation

\Delta^{2}-\lambda\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{*}\xi_{i},

for some
\lambda>0
and some finite collection of
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma
.
The above characterization enables using computer
assistance in proving property
(T)
. This approach allowed later to prove property
(T)
for
\mathrm{Aut}(F_{n})
for
n\geq 5

[
18
,
16
]
as well as n=4
[
21
]
.

In recent years there has been increasing interest in generalizing property
(T)
-type phenomena to higher cohomology, see e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
.
Property
(T)
has a couple of equivalent cohomological descriptions, one in terms of the first cohomology
H^{1}(\Gamma,\pi)
being reduced (i.e. the image of the codifferential being closed) for every unitary representation

\pi
of
\Gamma
, and the other in terms of vanishing of
H^{1}(\Gamma,\pi)
, again for every unitary representation
\pi
. The equivalence between these two conditions is known as the Delorme-Guichardet theorem (see
[
5
, 2.12.4]
).
This allows for two different routes of generalizing property
(T)
to higher cohomology, one via reducedness, and another one via vanishing, see
[
1
, Section 5.1]
and
[
3
]
.
As it turns out, in general these conditions are no longer equivalent for higher cohomology.
Indeed, for a lattice
\Gamma
in
\mathrm{PGL}_{n+1}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
the cohomology spaces
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
are always Hausdorff but not necessarily zero
[
2
, Proposition 19]
.

In
[
2
]
a characterization of vanishing of unitary cohomology in higher degrees was proved in terms of an algebraic spectral gap of the cohomological Laplacian
\Delta_{n}=d_{n}^{*}d_{n}+d_{n-1}d_{n-1}^{*}
, where
d_{n}
is the appropriate group codifferential.
This characterization in particular allowed again for the use of computer-assisted methods for proving vanishing of cohomology with unitary coefficients.
This approach was implemented in
[
17
]
.

For the parallel question of whether unitary cohomology is always reduced, there is an analytic characterization in terms of the spectral gap of the one-sided Laplacian
\Delta_{n}^{+}=d_{n}^{*}d_{n}
, see
[
2
]
.
However, its algebraic counterpart, the condition that
\Delta_{n}^{+}
has algebraic spectral gap, remained only a sufficient condition for the cohomology being reduced.
The question whether reducedness of unitary cohomology can be characterized in terms of positivity in the matrices over the group algebra served as starting point of this work.
In particular, it was expected that the analytic characterization
of reducedness from
[
2
]
has an algebraic counterpart.
The following theorem gives the unexpected result that this is not the case and the algebraic version of the characterization from
[
2
]
is sufficiently strong to imply vanishing of cohomology.

Theorem A
.

(Theorem
5.2
)
Let
\Gamma
be a group of type
F
and
n\geq(\mathrm{cdim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\Gamma)+1)/2
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
for every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
.

(2)

There exists
\lambda>0
and a finite collection
M_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
such that

(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}=\sum M_{i}^{*}M_{i}.

The above result gives a complete higher dimensional generalization of Ozawa’s characterization of property
(T)
. See Theorem
5.2
for a version with more general hypotheses.

In order to prove the above, new techniques were necessary and this had led us to exploring the connection between positive functionals, their extensions, and cocycles in group cohomology. As a result, we establish a higher-dimensional version of the celebrated Gelfand-Naimark-Segal-construction. For a group
\Gamma
, the classical cocycle GNS construction provides
a correspondence between positive definite functionals on the augmentation ideal on one side, and 1-cocycles of unitary representations on the other. The correspondence is expressed via the formula

\phi(1-\gamma)=||z(\gamma)||^{2},

where
\gamma\in\Gamma
,
\pi
is a unitary representation,
z
an associated 1-cocycle and
\phi
is a positive functional.

In order to generalize the above cocycle version of the GNS construction we consider a certain chain complex on algebras of matrices
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
, induced by the group cochain complex with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
.
This matricial chain complex is not acyclic in general and it is a natural question when does its homology vanish.
For us, matricial cycles and boundaries serve as a natural environment for generalizing the GNS construction.
Our higher GNS construction, formulated in Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
, gives a correspondence between
n
-cocycles
z
in the cohomology
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
, where
\pi
is a unitary representation, and functionals defined by the matrix coefficient
\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{Bmatrix}\phi\text{ positive functional on}\\
\text{ matricial boundaries}\end{Bmatrix}\par\par}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{Bmatrix}z\in Z^{n}(\Gamma,\pi),\\
\pi\text{ unitary $\Gamma$-representation}\end{Bmatrix}}

An important technical element, formulated as Theorem
3.3
, is a description of matricial cycles as sums of images of one-sided differentials that we call
split-conjugation spaces
(see Definition
3.2
). For this we use a homological telescoping argument together with acyclicity
of the underlying group chain complex.

As one of the consequences we prove a new characterization of vanishing of higher unitary cohomology via an extension property for positive functionals defined on the matricial coboundaries.

Theorem B
.

(Theorem
4.5
) Let
\Gamma
be a group of type
F
and
n\geq(\mathrm{cdim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\Gamma)+1)/2
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
for every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
.

(2)

Every positive hermitian functional
\phi:\operatorname{im}D_{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
can be extended to a positive hermitian functional
\overline{\phi}:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
.

Above,
D_{n-1}
is the appropriate matricial differential operator and its image is the space of matricial boundaries.

We also apply our methods to discuss generalizations of Shalom’s property
H_{T}

to higher degrees. Namely, we obtain characterizations of a higher version of property
H_{T}
in the spirit of
[
23
, Corollary 12]
. See Theorems
6.3
and
6.4
.

We first collect some definitions and notations on
*
-algebras. We refer to
[
2
]
,
[

]
,
[

]

]

A
*
-algebra

A
is an algebra over
\mathbb{R}
equipped with an involution
x\mapsto x^{*}
that is
\mathbb{R}
-linear and such that
(ab)^{*}=b^{*}a^{*}
for
a,b\in A
. For a subspace
V\subseteq A
we define the cone of
sums of hermitian squares
by

\Sigma^{2}V:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{*}a_{i}\colon a_{i}\in V,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Given a
*
-algebra
A
and a subspace
V\subseteq A
, we denote by
V^{h}=\{a\in V:a^{*}=a\}
the subspace of
self-adjoint
elements of
V
. We have
\Sigma^{2}V\subset V^{h}
. An
order unit

\delta\in\Sigma^{2}A
is an element such that for every
a\in A^{h}
there exists
R>0
such that
a+R\delta\in\Sigma^{2}A
.

We say that a
*
-algebra is
Archimedean
if it is unital (with unit denoted by
1
),
-1\notin\Sigma^{2}A
and
1
is an order unit of
A
.

Given two
*
-algebras (resp. unital
*
-algebras)
A
and
B
, a map
f:A\to B
is a
*
-homomorphism
(resp. a
unital
*
-homomorphism
) if it is an algebra homomorphism and
f(a^{*})=f(a)^{*}
for
a\in A
(resp. satisfying furthermore
f(1_{A})=1_{B}
).

Given a
*
-algebra
A
, we say that a linear functional
\varphi:A\to\mathbb{R}
is
hermitian
if
\varphi(a)=\varphi(a^{*})
for every
a\in A
. Such a functional is characterized by its restriction to
A^{h}
. We say that a linear functional
\varphi:A\to\mathbb{R}
is
positive
if

\varphi(\Sigma^{2}A)\geq 0.

Our
*
-algebras will be endowed with the so-called
algebraic topology
as in
[
22
]
, defined as follows.
Let
V
be a real vector space and
C\subset V
a convex subset. We say that
c\in C
is an
algebraic interior point
if for every
v\in V
, there exists
t\in(0,1]
such that
(1-t)c+tv\in C
. We denote by
\mathrm{int}(C)
the set of all interior points of
C
. We define a locally convex topology on
V
by defining open sets to be all convex subsets
C
such that
C=\mathrm{int}(C)
. In this topology all linear functionals are continuous and all vector subspaces are closed.

If we endow a
*
-algebra
A
(or
A^{h}
) with this topology and
\delta
is an order unit such that
-\delta\notin\Sigma^{2}A
, we have

\overline{\Sigma^{2}A}=\{a\in A^{h}\colon\forall\varepsilon>0,a+\varepsilon\delta\in\Sigma^{2}A\}

and if
W
is a real vector subspace of
A^{h}
, then

\overline{\Sigma^{2}A+W}=\{a\in A^{h},\forall\varepsilon>0,a+\varepsilon\delta\in\Sigma^{2}A+W\}.

We now turn to group algebras.
Recall that given a countable discrete group
\Gamma
, the group algebra
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
is the vector space over
\mathbb{R}
of finite linear combinations
\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}a_{\gamma}\gamma
of elements of
\Gamma
, with the product given by the linear extension of the group product. It is a unital
*
-algebra when endowed with the involution
\sum a_{\gamma}\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma\mapsto\sum a_{\gamma}\gamma^{-1}
and unit
e_{\Gamma}
. The subspace

I(\Gamma):=\left\{\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}a_{\gamma}\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma\colon\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}a_{\gamma}=0\right\}

is an ideal, called the
augmentation ideal
of
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
.
One can show that
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
is an Archimedean
*
-algebra
[
10
, Example 3]
and hence for
k\geq 1
the algebra
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)

of matrices with coefficients in the group algebra is also an Archimedean
*
-algebra
[
2
, Corollary 13]
, with
*
-involution given by taking the transpose and applying the
*
-involution of
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
on each entry.

A
*
-representation

(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a unital
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
with values in the
*
-algebra of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We say that the
*
-representation
\pi:\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is
bounded
if the image
\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))
lies in the subspace of bounded operators
\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

The following Proposition says that
*
-representations of
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
are always induced by unitary representations of the underlying group
\Gamma
.

Proposition 1.1
.

[
2
, Proposition 12]

Let
k\geq 1
and
\rho:\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{K})
be a bounded
*
-representation of
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
. Then there exists a unitary representation
\pi:\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and a Hilbert space isometric isomorphism
\theta:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that for
a=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
we have

\rho(a)=\theta^{-1}\circ\pi(a_{ij})\circ\theta.

As a corollary, the classical Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction for positive functionals on matrices over the group algebra can be understood via matrix coefficients for unitary representations of the underlying group.

Proposition 1.2

(GNS construction)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
k\geq 1
.
Let
\psi:\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive hermitian linear functional. Then there exist a unitary representation
\pi:\Gamma\to\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and
c\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that for
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
we have

\psi(a)=\langle\pi(a)c,c\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.

The vector
c
is cyclic for the corresponding
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
-representation, that is, the space
\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))c
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
.

(Uniqueness) Let
(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
be another unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
c^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}
be a vector such that
\psi(a)=\langle\pi^{\prime}(a)c^{\prime},c^{\prime}\rangle_{(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}}
for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
and such that the space
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))c^{\prime}
is dense in
(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}
. Then there exists a unitary isomorphism
U:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
intertwining
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
as unitary
\Gamma
-representations and mapping entries of
c
to entries of
c^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The classical GNS construction
[
24
, Theorem 4.38]
applied to the unital
*
-algebra
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
says that there exist a bounded
*
-representation
\rho:\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathrm{B}(\mathcal{K})
of
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
and a cyclic vector
\xi\in\mathcal{K}
such that

\psi(a)=\langle\rho(a)\xi,\xi\rangle.

Applying Proposition
1.1
to
\rho
yields a unitary representation
\pi:\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and a Hilbert space isometric isomorphism
\theta:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that for
a=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
we have
\rho(a)=\theta^{-1}\circ(\pi(a_{ij}))\circ\theta
. By setting
c:=\theta(\xi)
we obtain

\psi(a)=\langle\rho(a)\xi,\xi\rangle=\langle\theta^{-1}\circ\pi(a)\circ\theta(\xi),\xi\rangle=\langle\pi(a)c,c\rangle.

Since
\theta
is an isometric isomorphism and
\xi\in\mathcal{K}
is a cyclic vector, we see that
c
is a cyclic vector in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
for the action of
\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))
.

To prove uniqueness, let
(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
c^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}
a vector such that
\psi(a)=\langle\pi^{\prime}(a)c^{\prime},c^{\prime}\rangle_{(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}}
and such that
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))c^{\prime}
is dense in
(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}
. Write
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})^{T}
and
c^{\prime}=(c_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,c_{k}^{\prime})^{T}
.
The orbit
\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))c
can be described using coordinates as

\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))c=\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)c_{j}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)c_{j}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)c_{j}\right)^{k}.

Hence, if the space
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)c_{j}
was not dense in
\mathcal{H}
, then
\pi(\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))c
would not be dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
.
Our density assumptions imply that the subspaces

\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)c_{j},\quad\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)c_{j}^{\prime},

are dense in
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
respectively. Consider the maps

\displaystyle\Phi:(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{k}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}:(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{k}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\xi_{j})c_{j}

\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\xi_{j})c_{j}^{\prime}

For any
1\leq i\leq k
, we consider the matrix

a_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\xi_{1}&\ldots&\xi_{k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix}

where
\xi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma
for
1\leq j\leq k
. Since we have
\langle\pi(a)c,c\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}=\langle\pi^{\prime}(a)c^{\prime},c^{\prime}\rangle_{(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k}}
for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
, replacing
a
by
a_{i}
yields

\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\xi_{j})c_{j},c_{i}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\xi_{j})c_{j}^{\prime},c_{i}^{\prime}\right\rangle.

From this, using linearity and adjoints, we can show that for
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma
, where
1\leq i\leq k
, we also have

\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\xi_{j})c_{j},\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi(\eta_{i})c_{i}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\xi_{j})c_{j}^{\prime},\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\eta_{i})c_{i}^{\prime}\right\rangle,

which means that

\left\langle\Phi(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}),\Phi(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})\right\rangle=\left\langle\Phi^{\prime}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}),\Phi^{\prime}(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})\right\rangle.

This implies that
\ker\Phi=\ker\Phi^{\prime}
, so the map
\Phi^{\prime}:(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{k}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
factors through
\Phi:(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{k}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}
via a linear map
U:\mathcal{H}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
such that
\Phi^{\prime}=U\circ\Phi
. Since
\Phi
is surjective onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, the previous equality implies that
U:\mathcal{H}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
is unitary, and onto
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
as
\Phi^{\prime}
is surjective. By density of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
respectively, the map
U
can be extended to a surjective unitary linear operator
U:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.

By choosing vectors
(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)\in(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{k}
, the relation
\Phi^{\prime}=U\circ\Phi
implies that
Uc_{i}=c_{i}^{\prime}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. The map
U
intertwines
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, since for every
\xi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma
we have

U\left(\pi(\gamma)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\xi_{j})c_{j}\right)=U\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi(\gamma\xi_{j})c_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\gamma\xi_{j})c_{j}^{\prime}=\pi(\gamma)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi^{\prime}(\xi_{j})c_{j}^{\prime},

so
U
intertwines
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and hence on
\mathcal{H}
, by density.

2
Group cohomology and finiteness properties

We refer to standard references for elementary definitions of cohomological nature such as group cohomology or geometric dimension
[
8
,
7
]
.

We say that a group
\Gamma
is of
type
F_{d}
for some
d\geq 0
if
\Gamma
admits a free action by simplicial automorphisms on some locally finite contractible simplicial complex
X
, such that for every
n\leq d
the induced action of
\Gamma
on the set of
n
-simplices
X^{(n)}
of
X
has a finite number of
\Gamma
-orbits
k_{n}
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group of type
F_{d+1}
for some
d\geq 0
and
X
be a simplicial complex as above.
The space of
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
-valued
\Gamma
-equivariant
n
-cochains is denoted by

C^{n}(X,\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{\Gamma}:=\{c:X^{(n)}\to\mathbb{R}\Gamma,c(\gamma\sigma)=\gamma c(\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma,\gamma\in\Gamma,\sigma\in X^{(n)}\}.

Notice that by choosing representatives for the
\Gamma
-orbits in
X^{(n)}
, we can identify
C^{n}(X,\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{\Gamma}
and
(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{k_{n}}
. By tensoring with the identity, the standard simplicial coboundary operator on
X
extends naturally to an
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
-equivariant map

d_{n}:C^{n}(X,\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{\Gamma}\to C^{n+1}(X,\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{\Gamma}.

Hence we may view the operator
d_{n}
as a matrix
d_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n+1}\times k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
.
The adjoint matrix
d_{n}^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}\times k_{n+1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
of
d_{n}
is just the transpose of the matrix
d_{n}
after applying the
*
-involution of
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
on each entry. The adjoint
d_{n}^{*}
can be obtained by the same method from the simplicial boundary operator on
X
.

The above setting is assumed for the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the chain complex
d^{*}_{n}\square
given by left multiplication by the matrices
d^{*}_{n}
,

0\xleftarrow{}\mathbb{M}_{1\times j}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{0}\square}\mathbb{M}_{k_{1}\times j}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{1}\square}\mathbb{M}_{k_{2}\times j}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{}\ldots

is acyclic in degrees
>0
. Similarly, for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
the chain complex
\square d_{n}
of right multiplication by the matrices
d_{n}
,

0\xleftarrow{}\mathbb{M}_{j\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{\square d_{0}}\mathbb{M}_{j\times k_{1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{\square d_{1}}\mathbb{M}_{j\times k_{2}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{}\ldots

is acyclic in degrees
>0
.

Proof.

We first prove the result for
j=1
. Notice that
\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\simeq C_{n}(X,\mathbb{R})
, the space of simplicial cochains, and that the operator
d_{n}^{*}
corresponds to the usual simplicial boundary operator
\partial_{n}
. This identification is given explicitly by numbering
n
-simplices in a fundamental domain of
X
for the
\Gamma
-action by
\tau_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k_{n+1}
and associating the chain
c=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma,1\leq i\leq k_{n}}v_{i}(\gamma)\gamma.\tau_{i}
to the vector
v\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
. This means that the chain complex given by

0\xleftarrow{}\mathbb{R}\Gamma\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{0}}\mathbb{M}_{k_{1}\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{1}}\mathbb{M}_{k_{2}\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\xleftarrow{}\ldots

has the same homology as the complex given by
C_{*}(X,\mathbb{R})
, and hence is acyclic. This proves the first statement for
j=1
.

Now let
j\geq 2,n\geq 1
and
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}\times j}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
such that
d_{n-1}^{*}a=0
. We write
a=(a_{i})_{1\leq i\leq j}
as a horizontal vector, where each
a_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
corresponds to the
i
-th column of
a
. Note that when
d_{n-1}^{*}
acts on the left of
a
, it acts separately on each column
a_{i}
. Hence the condition
d_{n-1}^{*}a^{*}=0
is equivalent to
d_{n-1}^{*}a_{i}=0
for every
1\leq i\leq j
. There exist
b_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n+1}\times 1}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
such that
a_{i}=d_{n}^{*}b_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq j
. Hence the matrix
b=(b_{i})_{1\leq i\leq j}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n+1}\times j}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
satisfies
a=d_{n}^{*}b
and the assertion is proved.

The statement about the complex
\square d_{n}
given by right multiplication by
d_{n}
follows, as it suffices to apply the involution
*
and use the acyclicity for
d_{n}^{*}\square
.
∎

Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a unitary
\Gamma
-representation on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. We define
C^{n}(X,\mathcal{H})^{\Gamma}
to be the set of
\Gamma
-equivariant maps

. As before, choosing representatives for the
\Gamma
-orbits in

allows us to identify
C^{n}(X,\mathcal{H})^{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{H}^{k_{n}}
.
Under this identification, we can see the coboundary operator
C^{n}(X,\mathcal{H})^{\Gamma}\to C^{n+1}(X,\mathcal{H})^{\Gamma}
as the matrix
\pi(d_{n})\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n+1}\times k_{n}}(B(\mathcal{H}))
, where one applies the representation
\pi
entry-wise to the the matrix
d_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n+1}\times k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
defined earlier.
The matrices

compute group cohomology, that is, for every

we have:

\displaystyle H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)

\displaystyle=\ker\pi(d_{n})/\mathrm{im}\,\pi(d_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)

\displaystyle=\ker\pi(d_{n})/\overline{\mathrm{im}\,\pi(d_{n-1})}

(see for instance
[

, Proposition 1]
).
An important tool for computing unitary cohomology is simplicial Hodge theory, that is, the fact that we can compute it via harmonic cochains. Let

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{+}:=d_{n}^{*}d_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\text{ for }n\geq 0,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{-}:=d_{n-1}d_{n-1}^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\text{ for }n\geq 1,

\displaystyle\;\Delta_{0}^{-}=0\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=\Delta_{n}^{+}+\Delta_{n}^{-}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\text{ for }n\geq 0.

Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
n\geq 0
. We have continuous isomorphisms:

\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)\mathrm{\simeq}\ker\pi(\Delta_{n}),

(1)

see e.g.
[
2
, Proposition 16 (1)]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

be of type

for some

The inclusion

follows from Lemma

(1)

.
We will verify that for a finite collection of

implies

for some

.
By Lemma
1.3

(2)
,
the equality

is equivalent to

for all

. By Lemma

, there exist

In our further investigation it will be important to describe the kernels of the maps

in

Definition 3.2
.

The

-th split conjugation space

is defined by

\mathcal{SC}_{i}:=\left\{a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{i}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma):a=d_{i}^{*}x+yd_{i},x\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{i+1}\times k_{i}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma),y\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{i}\times k_{i+1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\right\}.

Notice that

Indeed, for

where

. For the inclusion
\mathcal{SC}_{i}\subseteq\ker D_{i-1}
, let
a=d_{i}^{*}x+yd_{i}
for some

and

. We have

d_{i-1}^{*}ad_{i-1}=d_{i-1}^{*}(d_{i}^{*}x+yd_{i})d_{i-1}=(d_{i-1}^{*}d_{i}^{*})xd_{i-1}+d_{i-1}^{*}y(d_{i}d_{i-1})=0.

The following Theorem gives a criterion to ensure that the last inclusion is an equality.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group of type
F
and geometric dimension
\leq n
. Then

\ker D_{i-1}=\mathcal{SC}_{i},

for

.

Proof.

Suppose

and let

; that is,
d_{i-1}^{*}ad_{i-1}=0
. By Lemma

, there exists

such that

ad_{i-1}=d^{*}_{i}a_{1}.

Hence

d^{*}_{i}a_{1}d_{i-2}=ad_{i-1}d_{i-2}=0.

We can iterate this argument and construct a sequence of matrices
(a_{j})
, where
a_{0}=a
and
a_{j}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{i+j}\times k_{i-j}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
is such that

a_{j}d_{i-j-1}=d_{i+j}^{*}a_{j+1},

provided

.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}\Gamma^{k_{i-2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}\Gamma^{k_{i-1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}\Gamma^{k_{i}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}\Gamma^{k_{i+1}}}

4
The higher dimensional GNS construction

The Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction is a fundamental tool in representation theory
and cohomology with unitary coefficients. One classical application of this construction, is that it gives a correspondence between 1-cocycles of unitary representations and conditionally negative functions (that we view as positive functionals on the augmentation ideal). We first recall the statement.

Proposition 4.1

(Classical cocycle GNS)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group.

(1)

For every hermitian positive functional
\phi:I(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and a 1-cocycle
z\in Z^{1}(\Gamma,\pi)
such that

\phi(1-\gamma)=||z(\gamma)||^{2}.

(2)

For every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and every 1-cocycle

z\in Z^{1}(\Gamma,\pi)
there exists a hermitian positive functional

\phi:I(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\phi(1-\gamma)=||z(\gamma)||^{2}.

We remark that these two constructions are inverses one of another. We refer to
[
5
, 2.10.2]
and
[
23
, Section 6]
for details.
The main result of this section is a generalization of the above fundamental relation between positive functionals and
cocycles with unitary coefficients, to higher degrees.
Our next proposition is an intermediate form of the higher GNS construction.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group of type
F_{n+1}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
The following conditions hold:

(1)

For every positive hermitian functional

\psi:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}

which vanishes on
\mathrm{im}\,D_{n}
there exists
a unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
and a cyclic cocycle
z\in Z^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
such that

\psi(a)=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle,

for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
.

(2)

For every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma

and every cocycle
z\in Z^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
, the positive hermitian functional
\psi:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\psi(a)=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle,

for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
, vanishes on
\mathcal{SC}_{n}
.

Proof.

(1)
Let
\psi:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive hermitian functional. By the GNS construction given in Proposition
1.2
there exists a unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and a cyclic cochain
z\in C^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
such that for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
we have:

\psi(a)=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle.

Evaluating
\psi
on the element
\Delta_{n}^{+}=d_{n}^{*}d_{n}\in\mathrm{im}\,D_{n}
, we obtain:

0=\psi(\Delta_{n}^{+})=\langle\pi(\Delta_{n}^{+})z,z\rangle=||\pi(d_{n})z||^{2}.

This shows that
z
is indeed a cocycle.

(2)
Let
z\in\ker\pi(d_{n})
and
a\in\mathcal{SC}_{n}
. There exist rectangular matrices

such that

a=d^{*}_{n}x+yd_{n}
. Therefore

\psi(a)=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle=\left\langle\pi(x)z,\pi(d_{n})z\right\rangle+\left\langle\pi(y)\pi(d_{n})z,z\right\rangle=0.

∎

Consider the following diagram.

Remark 3
.

Note that for

, Theorem

gives the classical Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction of Theorem

. Indeed, when restricting to hermitian matrices
we have

where

denotes the self-adjoint elements in the augmentation ideal of
\mathbb{R}\Gamma
, which is generated by the set
\{2-\gamma-\gamma^{-1}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
(for the non-hermitian case, compare with
[

).

Note that
\mathrm{im}\,(D_{0}|_{\mathbb{M}_{k_{1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{h}})\subseteq I(\Gamma)^{h}
is evident. To see the converse inclusion, fix a finite symmetric generating set

of
\Gamma
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
fix a decomposition

\gamma=s_{1}\cdots s_{n}

of
\gamma
as a word in
S
. For
s\in S
let

\gamma(s)=\{i,s_{i}=s\}

and for

define

a_{t}=\sum_{i\in\gamma(t)}s_{1}\cdots s_{i-1}.

Then the horizontal vector

a=(a_{t})_{t\in S}
satisfies

1-\gamma=ad_{0}

and consequently

(1-\gamma)^{*}(1-\gamma)=d_{0}^{*}a^{*}ad_{0}.

If
\phi:I(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive hermitian functional, let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be the unitary
\Gamma
-representation and

be the cocycle given by Proposition

(1)
, satisfying
\phi(d_{0}^{*}ad_{0})=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle
. Then

\displaystyle 2\phi(1-\gamma)

\displaystyle=\phi(2-\gamma-\gamma^{-1})=\phi(d_{0}^{*}a^{*}ad_{0})

\displaystyle=\langle\pi(a^{*}a)z,z\rangle=\|\pi(a)z\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{i\in\gamma(s)}\pi(s_{1}\ldots s_{i-1})z(s)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(s_{1}\ldots s_{i-1})z(s_{i})\right\|^{2}=\left\|z(\gamma)\right\|^{2}.

We now turn to give a characterization of vanishing of higher unitary cohomology in terms of
an extension property for positive functionals.
The first observation is that functionals induced by coboundaries can be extended to the whole matrix algebra.

,
such that
\phi(d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1})=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle
,
assume that
z=d_{n-1}c
for some
c\in C^{n-1}(\Gamma,\pi)
.
Let
\overline{\phi}:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\overline{\phi}(m):=\langle\pi(m)c,c\rangle

for
m\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
.
For
m=d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1}
we have

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}(d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\langle\pi(ad_{n-1})c,\pi(d_{n-1})c\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle

\displaystyle=\phi(d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1}).

∎

It is unclear whether the converse of Proposition
4.4
holds individually for each functional. Nevertheless, the following Theorem shows that if one assumes the conclusion of Proposition
4.4
for all positive hermitian functionals
\phi:\mathrm{im}\,D_{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, then all their associated cocycles are coboundaries.
This gives a characterization of vanishing of unitary cohomology as a property of extension of functionals.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group of type
F_{n+1}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{SC}_{n}=\ker D_{n-1}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
for every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
;

(2)

Every positive hermitian functional
\phi:\operatorname{im}D_{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
can be extended to a positive hermitian functional
\overline{\phi}:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The implication
(1)\implies(2)
is a direct consequence of Proposition
4.4

(1)
. We will now show the converse.

Suppose that condition
(2)
holds, let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
z\in Z^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
be a harmonic cocycle. Using Theorem
4.3

(2)
, there is a positive hermitian functional
\phi:\operatorname{im}D_{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\phi(d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1})=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle

for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
. By hypothesis, this functional can be extended to a positive hermitian functional
\overline{\phi}:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
. Proposition
1.2
yields a unitary representation
(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
of
\Gamma
and a cochain
c\in(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{k_{n-1}}
such that

\overline{\phi}(a)=\langle\pi^{\prime}(a)c,c\rangle

for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
. We have

0=\langle\pi(\Delta_{n})z,z\rangle=\phi((\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2})=\langle\pi^{\prime}(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}c,c\rangle=||\pi^{\prime}(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})c||^{2}.

Hence
\pi^{\prime}(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})c=0
and

||z||^{2}=\phi(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})=\langle\pi^{\prime}(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})c,c\rangle=0.

Thus we have shown that
\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})=0
for every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
. Using the argument in
[
3
, Theorem 3.7]
, we obtain that
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
for every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
.
∎

5
Reducedness of cohomology and sums of squares

In this section we will consider algebraic conditions,
involving sums of squares,
for the cohomology with unitary coefficients
being reduced or for it to vanish.
The following result, proved in
[
2
]
characterizes when cohomology is reduced for every unitary representation (here we state this differently, the equivalence follows from Positivstellensatz
[
24
, Theorem 10.20]
, and a standard direct sum argument for the uniformity of
\lambda
).

Theorem 5.1
.

[
2
, Proposition 16 3.]

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group of type
F_{n+1}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

For every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
, the cohomology spaces
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
are Hausdorff.

(2)

There exists
\lambda>0
such that

(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)}.

In view of the above characterization of
cohomology with unitary coefficients being reduced,
it is a natural question if the property

(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)

is equivalent to the one given in the previous result.
As it surprisingly turns out, when the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
holds, this property characterizes cohomological vanishing, which is strictly stronger than reducedness of cohomology for
n\geq 2
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group of type
F_{n+1}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and suppose that
\ker D_{n-1}=\mathcal{SC}_{n}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

For every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
, we have
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
;

(2)

There exists
\lambda>0
such that

\Delta_{n}-\lambda I\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma);

(3)

There exists
\lambda>0
such that

(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Proof.

The equivalence between the conditions
(1)
and
(2)
is
[
2
, Main Theorem]
combined with
[
3
, 3.11]
. We will now show that these two conditions are equivalent to
(3)
.

The implication
(2)\implies(3)
follows from Lemma
1.3
and the equality

(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}=d_{n-1}^{*}(\Delta_{n}-\lambda I)d_{n-1}=D_{n-1}(\Delta_{n}-\lambda I).

We will now show
(3)\implies(1)
.
Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
z\in\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})
be a harmonic cochain. In particular,
z
is a cocycle and Theorem
4.3

(2)
(which we can use since we are assuming
\ker D_{n-1}=\mathcal{SC}_{n}
) implies that there exists a positive functional
\phi:\operatorname{im}D_{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\phi(d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1})=\langle\pi(a)z,z\rangle,

for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
. Since by assumption
(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
and
\phi
is positive, we have

\phi\left((\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\right)\geq 0.

On the other hand, harmonicity of
z
gives

\phi\left((\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\right)=\langle\pi(\Delta_{n}-\lambda I)z,z\rangle=-\lambda||z||^{2}.

These two conditions force
z=0
.
It follows that

\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})=0.

However, by Theorem
5.1
the assumed condition
(3)
implies that cohomology of
\Gamma
with any unitary coefficients is reduced. Hence

H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0.

∎

Remark 4
.

For
n=1
the main idea behind the proof of Theorem
5.2
was outlined in
[
23
, p. 2554]
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a cocompact lattice in
\mathrm{SL}_{r+1}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
with
r\geq 2
and
p
a prime (or more generally in any simple algebraic group of rank
r
defined over a non-Archimedean local field). There exists a constant
\lambda>0
such that

\Delta_{r-1}-\lambda I\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{r-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma),\qquad(\Delta_{r-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{r-1}^{+}\in\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{r-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)}.

On the other hand, for any
\lambda>0
we have:

(\Delta_{r-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{r-1}^{+}\notin\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{r-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Proof.

The results of Garland
[
12
]
and Casselman
[
9
]
(see
[
14
]
for a shorter proof) show that for any cocompact lattice
\Gamma
in a simple algebraic group
G
of rank
r
defined over a non-Archimedean local field we have

H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0

for every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
and
1\leq n\leq r-1
.
At the same time there exist unitary representations

\rho
such that

H^{r}(\Gamma,\rho)\neq 0.

Bader and Sauer showed that the condition
H^{n-1}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
for every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
, implies that
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
is Hausdorff for every unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma

[
3
, 3.11]
.

The first two equations in our statement follow from Theorem
5.1
and
[
2
, Main Theorem]
as they are the group algebraic translations of the corresponding vanishing and reducedness results.

The lattice
\Gamma
has geometric dimension
r\geq 2
. Theorem
3.3
says that
\ker D_{n-1}=\mathcal{SC}_{n}
for
n>r/2
(so in particular for
n=r
) and hence Theorem
5.2
says that
H^{r}(\Gamma,\rho)\neq 0
implies that for any
\lambda>0
we have

(\Delta_{r-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{r-1}^{+}\notin\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{r-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

∎

6
Higher Property
H_{T}

We now define property
H_{T}^{n}
and give two characterizations of it in terms of equations in the group algebra (though we need closures to formulate them). The first one holds in general for all groups of type
F_{n+1}
, while the second one requires the conclusion of Theorem
4.3
.

The following definition appeared implicitly in the work of Shalom
[
25
]
and explicitly in
[
13
, 2.1.1]
. The following discussion is relevant in the broader context of locally compact groups, for consistency here we restrict to discrete groups.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. We say that
\Gamma
has
property
H_{T}^{n}
if for every unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
without
\Gamma
-invariant vectors, we have
\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=0
.

Property
H_{T}^{1}
corresponds to the more classical property
H_{T}
defined by Shalom
[
25
, p. 125]
. For finitely generated groups, property
(T)
is equivalent to having property
H_{T}
and finite abelianization. Other examples of groups satisfying property
H_{T}
are often amenable, such as finitely generated nilpotent groups and wreath products
F\wr\mathbb{Z}
, where
F
is finite
[
25
, 1.14]
.

In a similar fashion, higher versions of property
(T)
imply the corresponding higher versions of property
H_{T}
. Namely, properties
(T_{n})
and
[T_{n}]

[
3
]
, imply property
H_{T}^{k}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Finitely generated nilpotent groups satisfy property
H_{T}^{n}
for every
n\geq 1

[
6
, 10.5]

[
15
, p. 243]
. A discrete group of type
FP_{n+1}
has Property
[T_{n}]
defined by Bader-Sauer if and only if it has Property
H_{T}^{k}
and satisfies
H^{k}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})=0
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

We come back to the usual setting:
\Gamma
denotes a group of type
F_{n}
for some
n\geq 1
and we use notation from previous sections. For any
k\geq 0
we define

\eta_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{0},\ldots,\Delta_{0})\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

The following lemma explains the relation between the matrix
\eta_{k}
and the existence of invariant vectors.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
c\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
. We have:

\langle\pi(\eta_{k})c,c\rangle=0
if and only if
c\in(\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)})^{k}
.

Proof.

By writing
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})^{T}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
we see that

\langle\pi(\eta_{k})c,c\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\pi(\Delta_{0})c_{i},c_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}||\pi(d_{0})c_{i}||^{2}\geq 0,

and these are equal to
0
if and only if
c_{i}\in\ker\pi(d_{0})
for every
i=1,\ldots,k
. If
S
denotes the finite generating set of
\Gamma
we used to define
d_{0}
, we have
\ker\pi(d_{0})=\mathcal{H}^{\pi(S)}=\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)}
, hence
\langle\pi(\eta_{k})c,c\rangle=0
if and only if
c_{i}\in\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)}
for every
i=1,\ldots,k
.
∎

We obtain the following characterization of Property
H_{T}^{n}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
. A discrete group
\Gamma
of type
F_{n+1}
has property
H_{T}^{n}
if and only if

-\eta_{k_{n}}\in\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)+\mathbb{R}\Delta_{n}},

or equivalently, if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
R>0
such that

R\Delta_{n}-\eta_{k_{n}}+\varepsilon I\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Proof.

Suppose first that
-\eta_{k_{n}}\in\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)+\mathbb{R}\Delta_{n}}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
R>0
such that

-\eta_{k_{n}}+\varepsilon I_{k_{n}}+R\Delta_{n}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
without
\Gamma
-invariant vectors and let
c\in\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})
. For every
\varepsilon>0
we have

\langle\pi(-\eta_{k_{n}}+\varepsilon I_{k_{n}})c,c\rangle\geq 0.

Hence
\langle\pi(\eta_{k_{n}})c,c\rangle\leq 0
. Since
\eta_{k_{n}}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
, Lemma
6.2
implies that
c\in(\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)})^{k}
. The hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)}=0
implies that
c=0
. Equation
1
shows that
\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})=\{0\}
.

Conversely suppose that
-\eta_{k_{n}}\notin\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)+\mathbb{R}\Delta_{n}}
. Then applying the Hahn-Banach theorem to the convex subset
\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)+\mathbb{R}\Delta_{n}}
in
\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)^{h}
implies that there exists a hermitian functional
\psi:\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\psi(-\eta_{k_{n}})<0\text{ and }\psi(\overline{\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)+\mathbb{R}\Delta_{n}})\geq 0.

Since
\psi(\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))\geq 0
, Proposition
1.2
gives a unitary representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
of
\Gamma
and a vector
c\in\mathcal{H}^{k_{n}}
such that
\psi(a)=\langle\pi(a)c,c\rangle
for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
.

Since
\psi(\eta_{k_{n}})>0
, Lemma
6.2
implies that
c\notin(\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)})^{k_{n}}
. This means that if
P:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}
denotes the
\Gamma
-equivariant orthogonal projection onto the quotient
\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\mathcal{H}/\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)}
, then in the quotient representation
(\pi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0})
of
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
(which has no
\Gamma
-invariant vectors) we have
c_{0}:=P(c)\neq 0
in
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{k_{n}}
.

Since
\psi(\mathbb{R}\Delta_{n})\geq 0
, we necessarily have
\psi(\Delta_{n})=0
as
\psi
is linear. However, this means that

0=\psi(\Delta_{n})=\langle\pi(\Delta_{n})c,c\rangle=||\pi(d_{n-1}^{*})c||^{2}+||\pi(d_{n})c||^{2},

and
\pi(d_{n-1}^{*})c=\pi(d_{n})c=0
and so
\pi(\Delta_{n})c=0
. Hence
\pi_{0}(\Delta_{n})c_{0}=P(\pi(\Delta_{n})c)=0
.

This shows that
(\pi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0})
is a unitary representation of
\Gamma
without
\Gamma
-invariant vectors for which
\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi_{0})=\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})\neq\{0\}
by Equation
1
.
∎

We now turn to another characterization of property
H_{T}^{n}
, which is precisely the higher-dimensional analogue of Ozawa’s characterization of property
H_{T}

[
23
, Corollary 12]
. We set

\square_{n}:=d_{n}^{*}\eta_{k_{n+1}}d_{n}=d_{n}^{*}\mathrm{diag}(\Delta_{0},\ldots,\Delta_{0})d_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group of type
F_{n+1}
for some
n\geq 1
, satisfying
\mathcal{SC}_{n}=\ker D_{n-1}
. Then
\Gamma
has property
H_{T}^{n}
if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
R>0
such that

R(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\square_{n-1}+\varepsilon\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Proof.

If the group
\Gamma
has property
H_{T}^{n}
, then, by Theorem
6.3
,
for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
R>0
such that we have

R\Delta_{n}-\eta_{k_{n}}+\varepsilon I\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

applying the map
D_{n-1}
to this equation gives

R(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\square_{n-1}+\varepsilon\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in D_{n-1}(\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma))\subseteq\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Conversely, suppose that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
R>0
such that

R(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\square_{n-1}+\varepsilon\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma).

Let
\pi
be a unitary representation without
\Gamma
-invariant vectors and
b\in\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})
be a harmonic cocycle. Theorem
4.3

(2)
gives a hermitian functional
\phi:\operatorname{im}D_{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for every
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
we have

\phi(d_{n-1}^{*}ad_{n-1})=\langle\pi(a)b,b\rangle.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
R>0
so that
R(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\square_{n-1}+\varepsilon\Delta_{n-1}^{+}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
. Then

\phi(-\square_{n-1}+\varepsilon\Delta_{n-1}^{+})=\langle\pi(R\Delta_{n}-\eta_{k_{n}}+\varepsilon I)b,b\rangle\geq 0.

Since
\square_{n-1}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k_{n-1}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
, we have
0\leq\phi(\square_{n-1})\leq\varepsilon\phi(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})
. Since this holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\langle\pi(\eta_{k_{n}})b,b\rangle=\phi(\square_{n-1})=0
. By Lemma
6.2
, this means that
b\in(\mathcal{H}^{\pi(\Gamma)})^{k_{n}}
. Since
\pi
has no invariant vectors, we have
b=0
. By Equation
1
we have
\overline{H}^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)=\ker\pi(\Delta_{n})=0
, hence
\Gamma
has property
H_{T}^{n}
.

∎

7
Remarks and questions

7.1
Matricial homology and ideals

In the above considerations it is natural to ask when do we have

\ker D_{n-1}=\operatorname{im}D_{n}
.
This property is equivalent to the fact that
\Delta_{n}^{+}=d_{n}^{*}d_{n}

is an order unit in
\ker D_{n-1}
.
It was observed by P. Mizerka and the second author that in
such a case
\ker D_{n-1}
is an ideal in
\mathbb{M}_{k_{n}}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
, that
plays a similar role as the augmentation ideal.
However, there are simple examples where we observe that this is not the case.

Example 7.1
.

Let
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\langle s,t\,|\,st=ts\rangle
acting on its simply-connected 2-dimensional Cayley complex
X
. The number of
\Gamma
-orbits on each dimension are
k_{0}=1,k_{1}=2
and
k_{2}=1
. The codifferentials can be taken to be

d_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1-s\\
1-t\end{pmatrix},\quad d_{1}=(1-t,s-1)

(where the computation for
d_{1}
was obtained using its characterization as Fox derivatives of relations by generators, as in
[
17
]
).
If
a=\begin{pmatrix}a_{ss}&a_{st}\\
a_{ts}&a_{tt}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
, we let
D_{0}(a)=d_{0}^{*}ad_{0}
.
Then

\ker D_{0}\neq\mathrm{im}\,D_{1}.

Indeed, the condition
a\in\ker D_{0}
means

\displaystyle 0=d_{0}^{*}ad_{0}

\displaystyle=(1-s^{-1})a_{ss}(1-s)+(1-s^{-1})a_{st}(1-t)

\displaystyle+(1-t^{-1})a_{ts}(1-s)+(1-t^{-1})a_{tt}(1-t).

On the other hand for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}\Gamma
we have

\displaystyle D_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{*}\xi d_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1-t^{-1}\\
s^{-1}-1\end{pmatrix}\xi\begin{pmatrix}1-t&s^{-1}-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(1-t^{-1})\xi(1-t)&(1-t^{-1})\xi(s-1)\\
(s^{-1}-1)\xi(1-t)&(s^{-1}-1)\xi(s-1)\end{pmatrix}.

Hence if we choose a matrix
a\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
of the form

a=\begin{pmatrix}(1-t^{-1})\xi(1-t)&0\\
0&(s^{-1}-1)\xi(1-s)\end{pmatrix},

we obtain a matrix
a
lying in
\ker D_{0}
but outside of
\mathrm{im}\,D_{1}
.

In view of this example, the natural question to ask is the following.

Question 1
.

Does there exist an infinite group
\Gamma
of type
F_{n+1}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\ker D_{n-1}=\mathrm{im}\,D_{n}
?

7.2
Algebraic characterizations of reducedness

Theorem
5.2
implies that elements of the form
(\Delta_{n-1}^{+})^{2}-\lambda\Delta_{n-1}^{+}
do not provide an algebraic characterization of reducedness of cohomology.
Nevertheless, reducedness could still be characterized by a similar condition involving other elements in the group algebra.

Question 2
.

Does there exist
a\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
(or a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
) such that the property that
H^{n}(\Gamma,\pi)
is Hausdorff for every unitary representation is equivalent to
a\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
? (or that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{n}\in\Sigma^{2}\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathbb{R}\Gamma)
?)

7.3
Split conjugation spaces and matricial homology

We expect the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
to hold in greater generality.

Question 3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group of type
F_{n+1}
. Do we have
\ker D_{n-1}=\mathcal{SC}_{n}
in all dimensions?

The condition
\ker D_{n-1}=\mathcal{SC}_{n}
is our main tool to prove Theorem
4.3
. Even if its conclusion always holds for
n=1
, we do not know whether
\ker D_{0}=\mathcal{SC}_{1}
holds for finitely presented groups.
