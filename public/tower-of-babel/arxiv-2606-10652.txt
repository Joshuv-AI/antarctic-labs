A sporadic strongly regular graph with parameters ( 120 , 56 , 28 , 24 ) (120,56,28,24) from a primitive action of the symmetric group on 7 7 elements
Sam Adriaensen, Robert F. Bailey, Jan De Beule, Morgan Rodgers

Abstract
There are up to isomorphism exactly three strongly regular graphs with parameters ( 120 , 56 , 28 , 24 ) (120,56,28,24) whose automorphism group acts primitively on the vertices. Two of these graphs belong to classical families: one is the non-orthogonality graph on anisotropic points of the hyperbolic quadric 𝒬 + ​ ( 7 , 2 ) \mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2) , and the other one belongs to the Johnson scheme. The third one is not well understood. In this paper, we give a description of this graph in terms of ovoids and spreads of 𝒬 + ​ ( 7 , 2 ) \mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2) , or equivalently in terms of overlarge sets of Steiner systems with parameters ( 3 , 4 , 8 ) (3,4,8) .



1
Introduction

A graph
\Gamma
is called
strongly regular
with parameters
(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
if it has
n
vertices, is
k
-regular, and given two distinct vertices, the number of common neighbours is given by
\lambda
if they are adjacent, and
\mu
if they are not adjacent.
We say that
\Gamma
is an
\SRG(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
.
A strongly regular graph is said to be
primitive
if both it and its complement (which is necessarily also strongly regular) are connected, and
imprimitive
otherwise. It is well-known that the only imprimitive SRGs are complete multipartite graphs and their complements. We note that a primitive SRG has diameter 2; SRGs are essentially the diameter-2 case of
distance-regular graphs
(see
[
BCN89
]
). For further background on strongly regular graphs, we refer the reader to the recent monograph of Brouwer and Van Maldeghem
[
BVM22
]
.

A permutation group
G
acting on a set
V
is
transitive
if it has only one orbit on
V
. A transitive action is said to be
primitive
if the only equivalence relations on
V
preserved by
G
are the trivial or universal relations (i.e. where the equivalence classes are singletons or the whole of
V
, respectively), and is said to be
imprimitive
otherwise. For further background information on permutation groups, see
[
Cam99
]
.
We note that if
G\leqslant\Aut(\Gamma)
for some strongly regular graph
\Gamma
and
G
acts primitively on the vertex set
V(\Gamma)
, then
\Gamma
is primitive (in the strongly regular graph sense), but the converse is not true in general: it is possible for a SRG
\Gamma
to be primitive but for
\Aut(\Gamma)
to be imprimitive (in the permutation group sense).

In this paper, we consider strongly regular graphs
\Gamma
where a subgroup
G\leqslant\Aut(\Gamma)
of automorphisms of
\Gamma
acts primitively on
V(\Gamma)
. Then
G
naturally acts on the set
V(\Gamma)^{2}
of ordered pairs of vertices, and the orbits of this action are the
orbitals
of the action of
G
of
V(\Gamma)
.
The number of orbitals is called the
rank
of the action of
G
on
V(\Gamma)
.
Equivalently, given any vertex
x\in V(\Gamma)
, the rank equals the number of orbits of the action of the stabiliser
G_{x}
of
G
acting on
V(\Gamma)
.
If
\Gamma
is neither complete nor empty, the rank of
G
has to be at least 3.
Graphs with a rank 3 automorphism group are necessarily strongly regular, and these have all been classified: see
[
BVM22
]
for details.

More specifically, we are interested in
\SRG(120,56,28,24)
’s. There are many known constructions of such graphs, including one-off examples, members of infinite families, or prolific constructions yielding multiple non-isomorphic graphs with the same parameters. For instance, there is an imprimitive vertex-transitive example obtained by Brouwer, Ivanov, and Klin
[
BIK89
]
, the non-collinearity graphs of partial geometries with parameters
(7,8,4)
(such as in
[
Coh81
]
; see e.g.
[
BVM22
, §8.6]
for background), and the prolific constructions of Wallis
[
Wal71a
]
and of Goethals and Seidel
[
GS70
]
.
In addition, one can obtain extra graphs through switching, see e.g. Schmidt
[
Sch24
]
, who used switching to obtain several pairs of
\SRG(120,56,28,24)
’s (or rather their complements) that are only isomorphic in the quantum sense, but not in the classical sense.

However, there are only three
\SRG(120,56,28,24)
’s with a primitive automorphism group. This was verified by the second author and his students using GAP
[
GAP25
]
and GRAPE
[
Soi23
]
using the following approach, as part of a larger analysis. GAP contains libraries of all primitive groups on up to
4095
points, obtained by the efforts of several authors (see
[
CQRD11
,
DM88
,
RD05
,
RDU03
]
). Also, the GRAPE package contains a function (which utilises methods developed by Praeger and Soicher
[
PS97
]
),
VertexTransitiveDRGs
, which for a given transitive permutation group
G
determines the distance-regular (and thus strongly regular) graphs
\Gamma
with
G\leqslant\Aut(\Gamma)
, in terms of which combinations of orbitals of
G
yield the edge set of each possible
\Gamma
. By applying this function to each primitive group from the library on 120 points, and eliminating duplicates, it was determined that only three such examples with parameters
(120,56,28,24)
exist. (This can also be seen in
[
BVM22
, Table 11.9]
: while this table considers
\Aut(\Gamma)
with rank at most
10
, from the primitive group library we know that the largest rank of a primitive group on
120
points is
8
.)
Two of them are classical and well understood:

(1)

The graph
\overline{\NO^{+}}(8,2)
, defined on the set of anisotropic points of
\PG(7,2)
with respect to a hyperbolic quadratic form
f
.
Two vertices are adjacent if they are not orthogonal for the alternating bilinear form associated to
f
.
The full automorphism group is
\PGO^{+}(8,2)
, with rank 3.

(2)

The distance 1-or-3 graph of the Johnson graph
\Johnson(10,3)
, where the vertices are the 3-element subsets of
\{1,\dots,10\}
, and where two vertices are adjacent if they share either 2 or 0 elements.
The full automorphism group is the symmetric group
\Sym_{10}
in its natural action on subsets, and the rank of this action is 4.

The third graph, which we denote by
\Gamma^{*}
, is listed in
[
BVM22
, Table 11.9]
, but no more information is presented.
Its automorphism group
G^{*}
is isomorphic to the symmetric group
\Sym_{7}
, and the stabiliser of a vertex has orbits of sizes
1,7,14,14,21,21,42
.
The purpose of this paper is to give a description of this graph.

The action of
G^{*}
on
V(\Gamma^{*})
is generously transitive and hence yields a
6
-class association scheme.

\Gamma^{*}
can then be obtained as a fusion of certain relations in this scheme.
Interestingly, the graph
\overline{\NO^{+}}(8,2)
can also be obtained as a fusion in the same scheme.

We present two descriptions of the association scheme.
One which emphasises how
\Sym_{7}
arises as automorphism group, and one which emphasises how
\overline{\NO^{+}}(8,2)
can be obtained as a fusion of the scheme:

(1)

In the first description, the vertices of the scheme are certain overlarge sets of Steiner systems
\OS(4,8)
.
This is a partition
\mathcal{S}
of
\binom{A}{4}
, where
A
is a 9-element set, into 9 classes
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{a}\,\,||\,\,a\in A\right\}
such that each
\mathcal{B}_{a}
constitutes a Steiner
(3,4,8)
-system on
A\setminus\{a\}
.
We choose a two-element set
B\subset A
, which allows each
\mathcal{S}
to be split into
\mathcal{S}_{B}=\left\{\mathcal{B}_{a}\,\,||\,\,a\in B\right\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\overline{B}}=\left\{\mathcal{B}_{a}\,\,||\,\,a\in A\setminus B\right\}
.
The relation containing a pair
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
is then determined by the intersections between
(\mathcal{S}_{B},\mathcal{S}_{\overline{B}})
and
(\mathcal{T}_{B},\mathcal{T}_{\overline{B}})
.
The action of
G^{*}
on the elements of
A\setminus B
is then given by the natural action of
\Sym(A\setminus B)\cong\Sym_{7}
.

(2)

By the work of Cameron and Praeger
[
CP91
]
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between
\OS(4,8)
’s and spreads of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
.
Using the principle of triality, we can translate the association scheme as being defined on a set of ovoids of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
.
We find a one-to-one correspondence between these ovoids and the anisotropic points of
\PG(7,2)
.
Hence, we find a description of the association scheme as being defined on the set of vertices of
\overline{\NO^{+}}(8,2)
, which explains why
\overline{\NO^{+}}(8,2)
arises as a fusion in this scheme.

We will also check that
\Gamma^{*}
cannot be obtained from the constructions of Wallis
[
Wal71a
]
or Goethals–Seidel
[
GS70
]
, or from a partial geometry.

A large part of our analysis will be done by the aid of the software GAP
[
GAP25
]
, and the GAP packages GRAPE
[
Soi23
]
and FinInG
[
BBC+23
]
.
Results obtained through computation will be labelled by “GAP”. All GAP code has been collected in one file and is available online via a github archive: see
[
ABDBR
]
.

2
Preliminaries

We denote the projective space arising from an
(n+1)
-dimensional vector space
V
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
q
elements by
\PG(n,q)
.
Given a vector
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
, we denote by
(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n})
the projective point
\left\langle(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\right\rangle
in
\PG(n,q)
.

We will give an overview of results on quadrics in finite projective spaces and association schemes.
The reader can consult
[
BVM22
]
as a reference.

2.1
Quadrics

A
quadric
in
\PG(n,q)
is the zero locus
\mathcal{Q}
of a homogeneous quadratic form
f(X)
, where
X
denotes a vector of variables.
Related to
f
is the symmetric bilinear form
B(X,Y)=f(X+Y)-f(X)-f(Y)
.
A point is called
singular
if its coordinate vectors
x
satisfy the property that
Y\mapsto B(x,Y)
is the zero function.
The quadric
\mathcal{Q}
is called
non-singular
if none of the points on
\mathcal{Q}
are singular.
The set of singular points of
\mathcal{Q}
forms a subspace
\rho
of
\PG(n,q)
, and
\mathcal{Q}
is a cone with vertex
\rho
, and as base a non-singular quadric.

If there are no singular points, then we can use the bilinear form
B
to take orthogonal complements.
We map a subspace
U
of
V
to

U^{\perp}=\left\{v\in V\,\,||\,\,(\forall u\in U)(B(u,v)=0)\right\}.

This induces an involution on the subspaces of
\PG(n,q)
that reverses inclusion, and maps
k
-spaces to
(n-k-1)
-spaces.
We call
\perp
the
polarity
associated to
\mathcal{Q}
.
Given two subspaces
\pi
and
\rho
, we write
\pi\perp\rho
if
\pi\subseteq\rho^{\perp}
or equivalently
\rho\subseteq\pi^{\perp}
.
A subspace
\pi
is called
totally isotropic
if it is completely contained in the quadric, in which case
\pi\perp\pi
.
A point is called
isotropic
or
anisotropic
, according to whether or not it is on the quadric.
If
P,Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, then the line
PQ
is totally isotropic if and only if
P\perp Q
.
In that case, we say that
P
and
Q
are
collinear
points of
\mathcal{Q}
.
If
q
is even (and
n
is odd), then
B
is alternating, and every point is orthogonal to itself according to
\perp
, regardless of whether it is on the quadric.
In that case, for any two points
P
and
Q
, we have
P\perp Q
if and only if the line
PQ
is either totally isotropic, or intersects the quadric in a unique point.

Suppose that
d
is the maximum projective dimension of the totally isotropic subspaces of
\mathcal{Q}
.
Then the totally isotropic
d
-spaces are called
generators
, and the
rank
of
\mathcal{Q}
is
d+1
.
The quadrics over finite fields have been classified.
Up to isomorphism, in
\PG(n,q)
there are
2
non-singular quadrics if
n
is odd, namely the elliptic quadric
\mathcal{Q}^{-}(n,q)
of rank
\frac{n-1}{2}
and the hyperbolic quadric of rank
\frac{n+1}{2}
, and a unique non-singular quadric if
n
is even, namely the parabolic quadric
\mathcal{Q}(n,q)
of rank
\frac{n}{2}
.
If
\mathcal{Q}
is a non-singular quadric, then none of the anisotropic points are singular, unless
\mathcal{Q}
is parabolic and
q
is even, in which case there is a unique singular point called the
nucleus
of
\mathcal{Q}(n,q)
.

Given a quadratic form
f
on the vector space
V\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
defining the quadric
\mathcal{Q}^{\varepsilon}(n,q)
, with
\varepsilon
either
-
,
+
, or the empty character, we denote by
\GO^{\varepsilon}(n+1,q)
the group of invertible linear operators
\varphi
on
V
such that
f\circ\varphi=\lambda f
for some non-zero scalar
\lambda
.
We denote by
\PGO^{\varepsilon}(n+1,q)
the group of collineations in
\PGL(n,q)
arising from the action of
\GO^{\varepsilon}(n+1,q)
on
V
.

A set of pairwise non-collinear points is called a
partial ovoid
of
\mathcal{Q}
.
A partial ovoid contains at most one point of each generator.
If it contains a point of each generator, it is called an
ovoid
.
A set of pairwise disjoint generators is called a
partial spread
of
\mathcal{Q}
.
Every point of
\mathcal{Q}
is on at most one generator of a partial spread.
If every isotropic point is on a generator of the partial spread, i.e. if the partial spread partitions the isotropic points, we call it a
spread
.

2.2
Systems of generators and the triality

The hyperbolic quadric
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(n,q)
has several remarkable properties.
It generators can be split into two systems: generators
\pi
and
\rho
belong to the same system if and only if
\dim\pi\cap\rho\equiv\frac{n-1}{2}\pmod{2}
.
We note that
\PGO^{+}(n+1,q)
acts transitively on the generators of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(n,q)
, and the systems form blocks of imprimitivity.

In the case
n=7
, there is a special map associated to
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(n,q)
called the
triality
map.
Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
be the sets of points and lines respectively on
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,q)
, and
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}
the systems of generators.
Then the triality map
\tau
is a permutation of order 3 on
\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{G}_{1}\cup\mathcal{G}_{2}
that maps
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{G}_{1}
,
\mathcal{G}_{1}
to
\mathcal{G}_{2}
,
\mathcal{G}_{2}
to
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{L}
to itself, and respects incidence.
Note that
\tau
permutes ovoids, spreads of generators in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, and spreads of generators in
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
Ovoids and spreads of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,q)
have size
q^{3}+1
.

2.3
Association schemes

Consider a finite set
\mathcal{X}
, and a relation
R\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{2}
on
\mathcal{X}
.
The
adjacency matrix
of
R
is defined as the matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
with
A(x,y)=1
if
(x,y)\in R
, and
A(x,y)=0
otherwise.
Now consider a set
\{R_{0},\dots,R_{d}\}
of relations on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
A_{i}
denote the adjacency matrix of
R_{i}
.
Then we call
(R_{0},\dots,R_{d})
, or equivalently
\mathcal{A}=(A_{0},\dots,A_{d})
, a (
d
-class)
symmetric association scheme
if

(1)

A_{0}=I
,

(2)

A_{0}+\ldots+A_{d}
is the all-one matrix, which we denote by
J
,

(3)

all matrices in
\mathcal{A}
are symmetric,

(4)

the vector subspace
\mathbb{R}[\mathcal{A}]
of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
generated by
\mathcal{A}
is a commutative algebra for the ordinary matrix product.

If
\mathcal{A}=(A_{0},\dots,A_{d})
is a symmetric association scheme, then
A_{0},\dots,A_{d}
can be diagonalised simultaneously.

\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
admits an orthogonal decomposition
W_{0}\oplus\dots\oplus W_{d}
such that every eigenspace of every
A_{i}
is the sum of some of the
W_{j}
spaces.
Moreover,
W_{0}
is spanned by the all-one vector.
The decomposition is unique up to permutation of
W_{1},\dots,W_{d}
.
The
(d+1)\times(d+1)
-matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
where
\boldsymbol{P}(j,i)
is the eigenvalue of
A_{i}
on the space
W_{j}
is called the
matrix of eigenvalues
of
\mathcal{A}
.

The action of a group
G
on
\mathcal{X}
is called
generously transitive
if for each
x
and
y
in
X
, there exists some
g\in G
with
x^{g}=y
and
y^{g}=x
.
Equivalently,
G
acts transitively, and its orbitals are symmetric.
In that case, the orbitals of
G
yield a asymmetric
d
-class association scheme, where
d+1
is the rank of
G
acting on
\mathcal{X}
.

If
(R_{0},\dots,R_{d})
is an association scheme with eigenvalue matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
, and we take a union of relations
R=R_{i_{1}}\cup\dots\cup R_{i_{t}}
, then
A=A_{i_{1}}+\ldots+A_{i_{t}}
is the adjacency matrix of the graph
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{X}
with adjacency relation
R
.
Its eigenvalues are given by
\boldsymbol{P}(e_{i_{1}}+\ldots+e_{i_{t}})
, where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
th
standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
Since every relation of an association scheme must be regular,
\Gamma
is a regular graph.
It is well-known that a regular graph is strongly regular if and only if it has at most 3 distinct eigenvalues.
Thus,
\Gamma
is strongly regular if and only if
\boldsymbol{P}(e_{i_{1}}+\ldots+e_{i_{t}})
has at most 3 distinct entries.
If these entries are
k>\theta_{1}\geqslant\theta_{2}
, then the strongly regular graph has parameters
\SRG(|\mathcal{X}|,k,\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{1}\theta_{2}+k,\theta_{1}\theta_{2}+k)
.

3
Overlarge sets of Steiner systems and spreads of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)

3.1
Overlarge sets of Steiner systems

Suppose that
A
is a set of
v
points.
Denote by
\binom{A}{k}
the set of subsets of
A
of size
k
.
Consider a set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\binom{A}{k}
, and call the elements of
\mathcal{B}

blocks
.
Then
\mathcal{B}
is called a
t
-
(v,k,\lambda)
design
if any
T\in\binom{A}{t}
is contained in exactly
\lambda
blocks.
If
\lambda=1
, the design is also called a
Steiner system
with parameters
(t,k,v)
.

Now suppose that
A
has size
v+1
, and suppose that we can partition the sets of
\binom{A}{k}
into
v+1
classes
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{a}\,\,||\,\,a\in A\right\}
, such that each
\mathcal{B}_{a}
is a
(k-1)
-
(v,k,1)
design on
A\setminus\{a\}
.
We call
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{a}\,\,||\,\,a\in A\right\}
an
overlarge set of Steiner systems
, and denote it as an
\OS(k,v)
.

There are up to isomorphism two non-isomorphic
\OS(4,8)
.
This was first proven by Breach and Street
[
BS88
]
, and reproven by
Cameron and Praeger
[
CP91
]
in a particularly elegant way.
Let us recall their results.

(1)

Take
A
to be the set of points of
\PG(1,8)
.
Define

\mathcal{B}_{(1:0)}=\left\{\{(x_{1}:1),\dots,(x_{4}:1)\}\,\,||\,\,\{x_{1},\dots,x_{4}\}\in\binom{\mathbb{F}_{8}}{4},\,x_{1}+\ldots+x_{4}=0\}\right\}.

For each point
P
of
\PG(1,8)
, let
\mathcal{B}_{P}
be the image of
\mathcal{B}_{(1:0)}
of a collineation
\varphi\in\PGamL(2,8)
that maps
(1:0)
to
P
.
The set
\mathcal{B}_{P}
is independent of the choice of
\varphi
.
Then
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{P}\,\,||\,\,P\in A\right\}
is an
\OS(4,8)
on
A
.
Its automorphism group is
\PGamL(2,8)
.

(2)

Take
A=\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
. Define

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\left\{\{\pm v,\pm w\}\,\,||\,\,v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}\text{ linearly independent}\right\}\cup\left\{\{v,w,w+v,w-v\}\,\,||\,\,v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}=1\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{v}
be image of
\mathcal{B}_{0}
under the translation
w\mapsto w+v
for each
v\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
.
Then
\left\{\mathcal{B}_{v}\,\,||\,\,v\in\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}\right\}
is an
\OS(4,8)
.
Its automorphism group is
\ASL(2,3)
.

There are
|\Sym_{9}:\PGamL(2,8)|=240

\OS(4,8)
’s of type (1), and
|\Sym_{9}:\ASL(2,3)|=1680
of type (2).
Since both the
\PGamL(2,8)
and
\ASL(2,3)
seen as subgroups of
\Sym_{9}
are subgroups of the alternating group
\Alt_{9}
, there are two
\Alt_{9}
orbits of size
120
on the
\OS(4,8)
’s of type (1), and two
\Alt_{9}
orbits of size
840
on the
\OS(4,8)
’s of type (2).
Moreover, both
\PGamL(2,8)
and
\ASL(2,3)
act 2-transitively.
Hence, if
G
is the setwise stabiliser in
\Alt_{9}
of a 2-element subset, then
G
has the same orbits as
\Alt_{9}
on the
\OS(4,8)
’s.

3.2
Spreads of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)

Following Cameron and Praeger
[
CP91
]
, we can represent
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
using the following vector space
V
and quadratic form
f
:

\displaystyle V=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{9})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{9}\,\,||\,\,x_{1}+\ldots+x_{9}=0\right\}\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}^{8},

\displaystyle f(X)=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant 9}X_{i}X_{j}.

(1)

The
weight
of a vector is defined as the number of coordinate positions with a non-zero entry.
If
x\in V
has weight
n
, then over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
,

f(x)=\binom{n}{2}=(n-1)\frac{n}{2}=\frac{n}{2}.

Thus,
\left\langle x\right\rangle
is an isotropic point if and only if
x
has weight 4 or 8.
Note that the points of weight 8 form an ovoid.

Let
A=\{1,\dots,9\}
.
We can identify each vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{9}
with the set
\left\{a\in A\,\,||\,\,x_{a}=1\right\}
.
Cameron and Praeger
[
CP91
]
observed that a set
\mathcal{B}\subset\binom{A}{4}
constitutes a
3
-
(8,4,1)
design on
A\setminus\{a\}
if and only if
\mathcal{B}\cup\{A\setminus\{a\}\}
are the points of a generator in
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
.
This in particular yields a one-to-one correspondence between spreads of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
and

If we represent
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
using the vector space and quadratic form from (

Clearly, the group
\Sym_{9}\leqslant\PGO^{+}(8,2)\leqslant\PGL(8,2)

The ovoid is a frame and since
\PGL(8,2)

\Sym_{9}

\mathcal{O}
in
\PGO^{+}(8,2)

Moreover,
\Alt_{9}
is the setwise stabiliser of

that also stabilises the systems of generators.

All ovoids of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
are isomorphic, see e.g.
[
CP91

.
Thus, we can give a coordinate-free definition of the group
G^{*}
from the previous section, by letting it be the setwise stabiliser of any ovoid of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
that stabilises the systems of generators.
Using the principle of triality, we can translate the results of the previous section to the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a spread of
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
, and let
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Let
G^{*}
be the setwise stabiliser of
\{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\}
and
\mathcal{S}\setminus\{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\}

\PGO^{+}(8,2)
.
Then
G^{*}
has two orbits of sizes
120
and
840

\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)

Let
\mathcal{Y}
denote the orbit of size
120

The action of
G^{*}
on
\mathcal{Y}
is generously transitive of rank
7
.
Moreover, distinct ovoids in
\mathcal{Y}
share at most one point.

This means that any ovoid
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{Y}
is uniquely determined by the points
\mathcal{O}\cap\Sigma_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}\cap\Sigma_{2}
.
On the other hand, there are 15 choices for a point

, and 8 choices for a point
P_{2}\in\Sigma_{2}\setminus P_{1}^{\perp}
, which yields a total of
15\cdot 8=120=|\mathcal{Y}|
pairs of non-collinear points.
This gives a one-to-one correspondence.
On the other hand, since the vector space underlying
\PG(7,2)
is a direct sum of the subspaces corresponding to
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, given a point
P=\left\langle x\right\rangle\notin\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}

as a linear combination of a vector in
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
seen as vector subspaces.
This means that there is a unique line
\ell_{P}
through
P
that intersects both
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
.
Note that given two points

and

contains one extra point
P_{3}
, and this point belongs to
\mathcal{Q}^{+}(7,2)
if and only if

.
Therefore, there is a one-to-one correspondence between anisotropic points and pairs of non-collinear points in
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
.

To summarise, for each anisotropic point

, there is a unique line

through

that intersects
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, and a unique ovoid
