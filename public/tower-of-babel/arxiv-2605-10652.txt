Cavity-Induced Excitonic Insulation and Non-Fermi-Liquid Behavior in Dirac Materials
Yuxuan Guo, Yuto Ashida

Abstract
We investigate two-dimensional Dirac fermions embedded in a deep-subwavelength cavity formed by high-impedance metasurfaces. We point out that, unlike conventional metallic boundaries, these metasurfaces support quasielectrostatic transverse-magnetic modes that mediate a long-range interaction between two-dimensional electrons. Combining static electronic screening with a Dyson-Schwinger analysis, we show that this engineered interaction can qualitatively alter the ground-state properties of Dirac materials. For a fermion flavor number N f N_{f} below a critical value N c = 16 / π N_{c}=16/\pi , the interaction drives an excitonic insulating phase through an infinite-order quantum phase transition and spontaneously generates a mass gap. At N f > N c N_{f}>N_{c} , the system remains gapless but enters a non-Fermi-liquid critical regime where the quasiparticle residue is singularly suppressed to zero, and the Dirac cone exhibits a nonanalytic dispersion relation. Furthermore, under a perpendicular magnetic field, the cavity fluctuations dynamically lift the zeroth Landau level degeneracy across all N f N_{f} . These results identify high-impedance metasurface cavities as promising platforms for engineering correlated Dirac matter.

High-impedance-surface cavity-mediated interaction
.-

We consider a planar cavity formed by two identical HISs located at
z=\pm d/2

The cavity is translationally invariant in the in-plane coordinate

H_{y}

In the frequency range of interest, the metasurface period

The HIS behaves as a reactive surface with an LC-type impedance, which we parametrize as

Z_{s}(\omega)=\left(\mathrm{i}\omega C_{s}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega L_{s}}\right)^{-1}

Gapless critical phase for

When

Interaction-induced splitting of the zeroth Landau level
.-

Finally, we consider the Dirac system subject to a strong perpendicular magnetic field, which reorganizes the low-energy spectrum into discrete Landau levels. The low-energy Hamiltonian with
(-1)^{a}=1
is

\hat{H}_{B,+}=v_{F}\begin{pmatrix}0&\hat{\Pi}_{x}-\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}\\
\hat{\Pi}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{\hat{\Pi}}=\bm{\hat{p}}+e\bm{\hat{A}}
is the kinetic momentum; similarly, the Hamiltonian with
(-1)^{a}=-1
is given by
\hat{H}_{B,-}=\hat{H}_{B,+}^{T}

Introducing the ladder operators

\hat{a}=\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{\Pi}_{x}-\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}),\quad\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{\Pi}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}),

with
\ell_{B}=\sqrt{\hbar/eB}
, one obtains the corresponding Landau level spectrum

E_{n,\pm}=\mathrm{sgn}(n)\sqrt{|n|}\omega_{B}
, where
\omega_{B}=\,v_{F}\sqrt{2}/\ell_{B}.

The ZLL lying exactly at zero energy is macroscopically degenerate and polarizes onto a single sublattice component for a fixed chirality, making it particularly susceptible to interaction effects.

We consider how the cavity-induced interaction can lift this degeneracy and alter the low-energy physics. The low-energy dynamics is dominated by the ZLL, whose Green’s function factorizes into a gauge-dependent Schwinger phase and a translationally invariant part

\widetilde{G}_{0,\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf q})=2\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{-\mathrm{i}\omega}\,\mathcal{P}_{\pm},

where
\mathcal{P}_{\pm}=\frac{1\mp\sigma_{z}}{2}
projects onto the sublattice component supporting the ZLL.
The corresponding real-space Dyson-Schwinger equation for the interaction-dressed ZLL propagator is

\Delta(0)\approx 60~\mathrm{meV}

The key experimental requirement is therefore not extreme geometric or spectral scales, but sufficiently high impedance and weakly dissipative mirrors over the relevant infrared or THz window. Other candidate platforms include split-ring-resonator arrays
121
;
102
;
103
, superconducting high-impedance surfaces
22
, hBN-based phonon-polaritonic structures
19
;
33
. A complementary route may also be offered by artificial graphene platforms interfaced with microwave resonators
117
;
114
;
112
, where both cavity coupling and Dirac bands can be controlled in a tunable way.

Conclusions
.-

We have shown that a high-impedance metasurface cavity can qualitatively reshape the low-energy physics of Dirac fermions by engineering the vacuum-mediated electron interaction. The effective interaction drives an excitonic insulating phase for
N_{f}<N_{c}
and induces a non-Fermi-liquid phase for
N_{f}>N_{c}
, and lifts the degeneracy of the zeroth Landau level under magnetic fields. From an experimental perspective, these predictions can be tested by the associated depletion or redistribution of low-energy spectral weight; for instance, one may use scanning tunneling spectroscopy to observe suppressed zero-bias conductance
75
;
125
or measure reduced low-frequency spectral weight via infrared or THz conductivity
77
;
15
. Under a magnetic field, one can use Landau-level spectroscopy together with suppressed longitudinal magnetotransport near charge neutrality
87
;
123
. More broadly, our work highlights engineered electromagnetic vacuum in metasurface cavities as a new control knob for quantum matter.

Derivation of the cavity-mode dispersion and normalization factor

In this section, we derive the dispersion relation of the even TM mode in the high-impedance-surface (HIS) cavity and determine the normalization factor
\mathcal{N}_{\bf q}
used in the scalar-potential quantization. To this end, we consider two identical high-impedance metasurfaces located at
z=\pm d/2
, separated by a dielectric spacer of relative permittivity
\varepsilon_{d}
.
The cavity is translationally invariant in the in-plane coordinate
{\bm{r}}=(x,y)
.
For a TM mode with in-plane momentum
{\bf q}=q\hat{x}
, the fields can be written as

{\bf E}({\bm{r}},z,t)=\bigl(E_{x}(z),0,E_{z}(z)\bigr)^{\rm T}e^{-\mathrm{i}{\bf q}\cdot{\bm{r}}+\mathrm{i}\omega t},\qquad{\bf H}({\bm{r}},z,t)=\bigl(0,H_{y}(z),0\bigr)^{\rm T}e^{-\mathrm{i}{\bf q}\cdot{\bm{r}}+\mathrm{i}\omega t}.

(S1)

Inside the dielectric spacer, Maxwell’s equations give

(\partial_{z}^{2}-\kappa_{d}^{2})H_{y}(z)=0,\qquad\kappa_{d}^{2}=q^{2}-\varepsilon_{d}\omega^{2}/c^{2},

(S2)

together with

E_{x}(z)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}}\,\partial_{z}H_{y}(z),\qquad E_{z}(z)=-\frac{q}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}}\,H_{y}(z).

(S3)

We focus on the even TM branch, for which
H_{y}(z)
is odd under
z\to-z
and
E_{x}(z)
is even.
A convenient choice is therefore

H_{y}(z)=A_{\bf q}\sinh(\kappa_{d}z),

(S4)

which gives

E_{x}(z)=\frac{\mathrm{i}A_{\bf q}\kappa_{d}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}}\cosh(\kappa_{d}z).

(S5)

At the upper boundary
z=d/2
, the effective impedance boundary condition reads

E_{x}(d/2)=Z_{s}(\omega)\,H_{y}(d/2).

(S6)

Similarly at
z=-d/2
, we have
E_{x}(-d/2)=-Z_{s}(\omega)\,H_{y}(-d/2),
since the orientation of the metasurface is different.
Substituting Eqs. (
S4
) and (
S5
) into Eq. (
S6
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{i}\kappa_{d}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}}\cosh\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}d}{2}\right)=Z_{s}(\omega)\,\sinh\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}d}{2}\right),

(S7)

or equivalently

\coth\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{d}d}{2}\right)=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}\,Z_{s}(\omega)}{\mathrm{i}\kappa_{d}},

(S8)

which is the dispersion relation quoted in the main text.

We now specialize to the deep-subwavelength regime

q\gg\omega/c,\qquad qd\ll 1,

(S9)

for which
\kappa_{d}\simeq q
and

\coth\!\left(\frac{qd}{2}\right)\simeq\frac{2}{qd}.

(S10)

Assuming an LC surface impedance

Z_{s}(\omega)=\left(\mathrm{i}\omega C_{s}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega L_{s}}\right)^{-1},

(S11)

Eq. (
S8
) reduces to

\frac{2}{qd}\simeq\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}}{\mathrm{i}q}\left(\mathrm{i}\omega C_{s}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega L_{s}}\right)^{-1}.

(S12)

After rearranging, this gives

\omega_{\bf q}^{2}\simeq\omega_{p}^{2}=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{1+\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d\,L_{s}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}},\qquad\omega_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{s}C_{s}}},

(S13)

showing that the even TM mode becomes nearly dispersionless in the low-energy regime.

Because the mode is quasielectrostatic, it is convenient to introduce a scalar potential
\phi
such that
{\bf E}\simeq-\nabla\phi
.
For the even TM mode, the potential profile is proportional to
\cosh(qz)
, and we can write

\hat{\phi}({\bm{r}},z)=\sum_{\bf q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\bf q}}}\cosh(qz)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}{\bf q}\cdot{\bm{r}}}\left(\hat{a}_{\bf q}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-{\bf q}}\right).

(S14)

The corresponding electric field is

\hat{E}_{x}=-\partial_{x}\hat{\phi},\qquad\hat{E}_{z}=-\partial_{z}\hat{\phi}.

(S15)

In the narrow-gap limit
qd\ll 1
, one has

\frac{|E_{z}|}{|E_{x}|}\sim q|z|\ll 1,

(S16)

so the mode is dominated by its in-plane electric field on the plane near the cavity center.

To fix the normalization, we evaluate the total energy stored in one mode. A complete
calculation should involve summing the time-averaged energies of both the
capacitance and the inductance; however, for the convenience of our
calculation, the normalization is determined by calculating the energy when
the capacitance reaches its maximum energy storage, which should be the same as the total energy in electric field and magnetic field.
There are two contributions: the electric-field energy in the dielectric slab and the reactive capacitive energy stored on the two HIS boundaries.
To leading order in
qd
, the field energy is

U_{\rm diel}=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}}{2}\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}\mathrm{d}z\int_{S}\mathrm{d}^{2}r\,|\nabla\phi|^{2}\simeq\frac{Sq^{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{\bf q}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}{2}\right)\left(\hat{a}_{\bf q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bf q}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(S17)

where
S
is the in-plane quantization area and terms like
\hat{a}_{q}\hat{a}_{-q}
are canceled by the magnetic part in the inductance.
The reactive energy stored on the two identical high-impedance boundaries is

U_{\rm surf}=\frac{Sq^{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{\bf q}}\,C_{s}\left(\hat{a}_{\bf q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bf q}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(S18)

Adding the two contributions gives

U_{\bf q}=\frac{Sq^{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{\bf q}}\left(C_{s}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}{2}\right)\left(\hat{a}_{\bf q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bf q}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(S19)

Imposing canonical quantization,

U_{\bf q}=\hbar\omega_{\bf q}\left(\hat{a}_{\bf q}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bf q}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(S20)

we obtain

\mathcal{N}_{\bf q}=\frac{Sq^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{p}}\left(C_{s}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}{2}\right).

(S21)

Substituting Eq. (
S21
) into Eq. (
S14
), one finally arrives at

\hat{\phi}({\bm{r}},z)=\sum_{\bf q}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{p}}{Sq^{2}\left(C_{s}+\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}{2}\right)}}\,\cosh(qz)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}{\bf q}\cdot{\bm{r}}}\left(\hat{a}_{\bf q}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-{\bf q}}\right),

(S22)

which is the form used in the main text.

II
Derivation of the excitonic gap equation and mass scale

II.1
Frequency integration with the retarded kernel and the cutoff structure

We now analyze how the full retarded cavity kernel determines the natural energy and momentum window of the excitonic gap equation before the static reduction used in the main text.
The key point is that the finite cavity mode frequency
\omega_{p}
does not merely set an overall interaction scale, but also restricts the internal fermionic energies that can participate efficiently in pairing.

The dressed Green’s function satisfies

G^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=G_{0}^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})-\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k}),

(S23)

with the free propagator

\displaystyle G_{0}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega+v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\!\cdot\!{\bf k}}{\omega^{2}+v_{F}^{2}k^{2}}.

(S24)

In the symmetry-broken phase, we project the self-energy onto the mass channel and approximate

\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})\simeq M(k)\sigma_{z},

(S25)

where
\sigma_{z}
acts on the sublattice space and corresponds to
\gamma_{0}
in the covariant notation.
The Dyson-Schwinger equation then takes the form

\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=-e^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,D_{\phi}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\,G(\mathrm{i}\omega+\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf k}+{\bf q}).

(S26)

Keeping the full frequency dependence of the cavity-mediated interaction, the Dyson-Schwinger equation in the mass channel takes the form

M(p)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{2\omega_{p}|g_{q}|^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+{\omega}_{p}^{2}}\,\frac{M(k)}{\Omega^{2}+v_{F}^{2}k^{2}+M^{2}(k)},

(S27)

where

q\equiv|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|,\qquad E_{k}\equiv\sqrt{v_{F}^{2}k^{2}+M^{2}(k)}.

(S28)

The gap equation reduces to

M(p)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,M(k)\,I(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k}),

(S29)

with

I(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{2\omega_{p}|g_{q}|^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+{\omega}_{p}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+E_{k}^{2}}.

(S30)

Using the standard identity

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\frac{1}{(\Omega^{2}+a^{2})(\Omega^{2}+b^{2})}=\frac{1}{2ab(a+b)},\qquad a,b>0,

(S31)

we obtain

I(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})=|g_{q}|^{2}\frac{1}{E_{k}}\frac{1}{(E_{k}+{\omega}_{p})}.

(S32)

Therefore the retarded gap equation becomes

M(p)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,|g_{q}|^{2}\frac{M(k)}{E_{k}\left(E_{k}+{\omega}_{p}\right)}.

(S33)

Equation (
S33
) makes two facts explicit:
first, dynamical screening enters through
{\omega}_{p}
; second, retardation produces the additional suppression factor
(E_{k}+{\omega}_{p})^{-1}
.

In the low-energy regime relevant to the gap-opening instability, the dominant contribution comes from fermionic states with

E_{k}\ll\omega_{p}.

(S34)

In this regime one may approximate

\frac{1}{E_{k}+{\omega}_{p}}\simeq\frac{1}{{\omega}_{p}},

(S35)

where the second relation uses the weak dispersion of the cavity mode in the quasielectrostatic regime.
Equation (
S33
) then reduces to

M(p)\simeq\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{|g_{q}|^{2}}{{\omega}_{q}}\frac{M(k)}{E_{k}}.

(S36)

Using the static interaction in the form

\mathcal{V}(q,0)=\frac{2|g_{q}|^{2}}{{\omega}_{p}},

(S37)

we obtain the static gap equation

M(p)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}(q,0)\,\frac{M(k)}{2E_{k}},

(S38)

which is the equation used in the main text.

Equation (
S33
) also makes clear why the finite cavity resonance provides a natural ultraviolet energy cutoff.
For
E_{k}\gg{\omega}_{p}
, the retarded factor behaves as

\frac{1}{E_{k}(E_{k}+{\omega}_{q})}\simeq\frac{1}{E_{k}^{2}},\qquad E_{k}\gg{\omega}_{p},

(S39)

so the kernel is parametrically suppressed compared with the low-energy form
1/(E_{k}\widetilde{\omega}_{q})
.
Physically, once the internal fermionic energy exceeds the cavity resonance, the interaction is strongly reduced by retardation: the bosonic mode can no longer mediate pairing efficiently at such high frequencies.
Therefore the relevant internal energy window is effectively restricted to

E_{k}\lesssim\omega_{p},

(S40)

which identifies
\omega_{p}
as the natural ultraviolet cutoff of the low-energy gap equation.

The same reasoning determines the momentum window relevant to the mass self-energy
\Sigma_{0}
.
In the pairing kernel, the fermionic energy entering Eq. (
S33
) is
E_{k}\simeq v_{F}k
as long as
v_{F}k\gg M(k)
.
The retardation-induced suppression therefore becomes important once

v_{F}k\sim\omega_{p}.

(S41)

Accordingly, the ultraviolet momentum scale of the low-energy gap equation is

k_{\rm UV}\sim\frac{\omega_{p}}{v_{F}}.

(S42)

Beyond this scale, the fermionic states are too energetic to couple efficiently through the nearly flat cavity mode, and the static reduction leading to Eq. (
S38
) is no longer justified.

There is also a lower momentum boundary associated with the validity of the quasielectrostatic cavity mode itself.
The cavity interaction used throughout the main text relies on the deep-subwavelength condition

q\gg\omega/c.

(S43)

Since the dominant retarded pairing processes are restricted to frequencies of order
\omega\lesssim\omega_{p}
, the lower edge of the momentum window is set parametrically by

q_{\rm IR}\sim\frac{\omega_{p}}{c}.

(S44)

Equivalently, for the momentum integration in the gap equation one may regard

k_{\rm IR}\sim\frac{\omega_{p}}{c}

(S45)

as the infrared cutoff associated with the breakdown of the quasistatic plasmonic description at too small momentum transfer.
This cutoff is not generated by the fermionic dynamics itself, but by the range of validity of the effective cavity kernel.

Taken together, Eqs. (
S42
) and (
S45
) show that the retarded HIS-mediated interaction is effective only within the momentum window

\frac{\omega_{p}}{c}\;\lesssim\;k\;\lesssim\;\frac{\omega_{p}}{v_{F}}.

(S46)

Within this window the interaction may be treated as quasistatic, whereas outside it either retardation suppresses pairing at large
k
or the quasielectrostatic cavity-mode approximation breaks down at small
k
.

II.2
Vacuum Polarization of 2+1D Dirac Fermions at Zero Magnetic Field

In the absence of a magnetic field, the one-loop vacuum polarization tensor for
N_{f}
flavors of Dirac fermions is given by the standard Feynman rule:

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(p)=-\mathrm{i}N_{f}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\text{Tr}\left[\gamma^{\mu}S(k+p)\gamma^{\nu}S(k)\right],

(S47)

where the free fermion propagator is
S(k)=(\not{k}+m)/(k^{2}-m^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)
.

Evaluating the trace of the
2\times 2
Dirac matrices in 2+1 dimensions,
\text{Tr}(\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu})=2\eta^{\mu\nu}
and
\text{Tr}(\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\beta})=2(\eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}-\eta^{\mu\nu}\eta^{\alpha\beta}+\eta^{\mu\beta}\eta^{\nu\alpha})
, we obtain:

\text{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu}(\not{k}+m)\gamma^{\nu}(\not{k}+m)]=2\left[(k+p)^{\mu}k^{\nu}+(k+p)^{\nu}k^{\mu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}(k\cdot(k+p)-m^{2})\right].

(S48)

To perform the momentum integration, we introduce the Feynman parameter
x\in[0,1]
and shift the loop momentum
l=k+xp
. Dropping terms linear in
l
due to symmetric integration, the tensor takes the form:

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(p)=-2\mathrm{i}N_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}l}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{2l^{\mu}l^{\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}l^{2}-2x(1-x)p^{\mu}p^{\nu}+\eta^{\mu\nu}(x(1-x)p^{2}+m^{2})}{[l^{2}-\Delta+\mathrm{i}\epsilon]^{2}},

(S49)

where
\Delta=m^{2}-x(1-x)q^{2}
. Using dimensional regularization to evaluate the integrals, the result naturally satisfies the Ward identity
q_{\mu}\Pi^{\mu\nu}=0
and can be decomposed as:

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(q)=(q^{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}-q^{\mu}q^{\nu})\Pi(q^{2}).

(S50)

The scalar polarization function
\Pi(p)
is found to be:

\Pi(p)=\frac{N_{f}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\frac{x(1-x)}{\sqrt{m^{2}-x(1-x)p^{2}}}.

(S51)

For massless Dirac fermions (
m=0
), which is relevant to, e.g., graphene, the static polarization function simplifies to

\Pi(p)=\frac{N_{f}}{16P},\;\;\;\;P=\sqrt{-p^{2}}.

(S52)

This
1/P
dependence is a hallmark of 2+1-dimensional massless Dirac fermions, leading to a strongly momentum-dependent dielectric screening.
In condensed matter physics, particularly when studying Coulomb screening and dielectric properties, the most relevant quantity is the
density-density response function
. In the field-theoretic language, the density operator is
\rho=\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\psi
, which means the density-density correlation is exactly given by the time-time component (
\mu=\nu=0
) of the polarization tensor:

\Pi^{00}(p)=(p^{2}\eta^{00}-p^{0}p^{0})\Pi(p).

(S53)

Using the Minkowski metric
\eta^{00}=1
and the four-momentum
p^{\mu}=(\omega,\mathbf{q})
, we have
p^{2}=\omega^{2}-{q}^{2}
. Substituting this into the 00-component yields:

\Pi^{00}(\omega,\mathbf{q})=(\omega^{2}-{q}^{2}-\omega^{2})\Pi(q^{2})=-{q}^{2}\frac{N_{f}}{16\sqrt{{q}^{2}-\omega^{2}}}.

(S54)

To evaluate the thermodynamic properties and Matsubara frequency responses, we perform a Wick rotation to Euclidean space by taking
\omega\to\mathrm{i}\omega
. The momentum squared becomes strictly negative. In the Euclidean signature, the retarded density-density response function
\Pi(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\omega)
is defined with an overall Wick rotation to the Minkowski
\Pi^{00}
, leading to:

\Pi(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=-\frac{N_{f}{q}^{2}}{16\sqrt{\omega^{2}+v_{F}^{2}{q}^{2}}}.

(S55)

We next derive the low-energy gap equation used in the main text and the resulting scaling form of the excitonic mass.
We work within the rainbow Dyson approximation and use the static RPA-screened interaction appropriate to the low-energy instability.
The basic logic is as follows: starting from the self-consistent Dyson-Schwinger equation, we first reduce the problem to a nonlinear integral equation for the momentum-dependent mass function. We then perform the angular integration and convert the result into a local differential equation, and finally solve the latter subject to infrared and ultraviolet matching conditions.

We thus replace the full propagator by the RPA-screened interaction

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf q})=\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf q})}{1-\Pi(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf q}){\mathcal{V}}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf q})}.

(S56)

For the low-energy cavity mode, the bare static interaction has the infrared form

{\mathcal{V}}_{0}({\bf q})\simeq-\frac{\alpha}{q^{2}},

(S57)

while the static polarization of massless Dirac fermions is

\Pi({\bf q},0)=-\frac{N_{f}q}{16v_{F}}.

(S58)

Substituting Eqs. (
S57
) and (
S58
) into Eq. (
S56
) gives

{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(0,{\bf q})=\frac{-\alpha/q^{2}}{1-\dfrac{N_{f}q}{16v_{F}}\dfrac{\alpha}{q^{2}}}

(S59)

In the infrared regime
q\ll\lambda_{\rm scr}
, this reduces to

{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(0,{\bf q})\simeq\frac{16v_{F}}{N_{f}}\frac{1}{q}.

(S60)

Thus screening softens the bare logarithmic interaction but preserves a singular long-range kernel and the UV cutoff is set arround
\Lambda_{\rm UV}=\alpha
, which is the origin of the nonperturbative instability.

In practice, the ultraviolet cutoff
k_{\text{UV}}
should be determined by
\min[\omega_{p}/v_{F},2\pi/d]
. While
1/d
represents a geometric constraint contingent upon the specific cavity architecture—which could be further increased via optimized fabrication—the scale
\alpha/v_{F}
constitutes the physical energy scale where the effective field theory remains valid. Consequently, we adopt
k_{UV}=\alpha/v_{F}
as the UV cutoff for our theory.

II.3
Detailed Derivation of the Gap Equation

In this section, we provide the full derivation of the self-consistent gap equation and the resulting scaling behavior. We start by projecting the rainbow-approximation Dyson-Schwinger equation onto the mass channel. And in the low frequency window we care about we can approximate the photon by static potential,

M(p)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\;\mathcal{V}(|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}|)\;\frac{M(k)}{\Omega^{2}+v_{F}^{2}k^{2}+M^{2}(k)}.

(S61)

After performing the internal frequency integration, we obtain the zero-frequency nonlinear integral equation for the mass function
M({\bf p})
:

M({p})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(0,|{\bf p}-{\bf k}|)\,\frac{M(k)}{2E_{k}},\qquad E_{k}=\sqrt{v_{F}^{2}k^{2}+M^{2}(k)}.

(S62)

Where we use the infrared limit of the screened potential
{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}
from Eq. (
S60
), the kernel is dominated by the cavity-induced
1/q
term, leading to

M({\bf p})\simeq\frac{16v_{F}}{N_{f}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{1}{|{\bf p}-{\bf k}|}\,\frac{M(k)}{2E_{k}}.

(S63)

After performing the internal frequency integration, we obtain the zero-frequency nonlinear integral equation for the mass function
M({\bf p})
:

M({\bf p})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(0,|{\bf p}-{\bf k}|)\,\frac{M(k)}{2E_{k}},\qquad E_{k}=\sqrt{v_{F}^{2}k^{2}+M^{2}(k)}.

(S64)

Using the infrared limit of the screened potential
{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}
from Eq. (
S60
), the kernel is dominated by the cavity-induced
1/q
term, leading to

M({\bf p})\simeq\frac{16v_{F}}{N_{f}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{1}{|{\bf p}-{\bf k}|}\,\frac{M(k)}{2E_{k}}.

(S65)

To evaluate the integration, we express the measure as
\mathrm{d}^{2}k=k\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}\theta
and expand the distance as
|{\bf p}-{\bf k}|=\sqrt{p^{2}+k^{2}-2pk\cos\theta}
, which gives

M(p)=\frac{8v_{F}}{N_{f}}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{k\,\mathrm{d}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{M(k)}{E_{k}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{p^{2}+k^{2}-2pk\cos\theta}}.

(S66)

To proceed analytically, we adopt the standard angle-averaging approximation, which captures both the UV and IR scalings correctly
8
:

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\frac{1}{|{\bf p}-{\bf k}|}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{\max(p,k)}.

(S67)

Substituting this into Eq. (
S66
), we reduce the two-dimensional integral to a one-dimensional form:

M(p)=\frac{4v_{F}}{\pi N_{f}}\left[\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{p}\mathrm{d}k\,\frac{k\,M(k)}{E_{k}}+\int_{p}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}k\,\frac{M(k)}{E_{k}}\right].

(S68)

By defining the auxiliary functions
F(k)\equiv\frac{k\,M(k)}{E_{k}}
and
G(k)\equiv\frac{M(k)}{E_{k}}
, the integral equation can be written compactly as

M(p)=\frac{4v_{F}}{\pi N_{f}}\left[\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{p}\mathrm{d}k\,F(k)+\int_{p}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}k\,G(k)\right].

(S69)

Next, we convert this integral equation into a local differential equation. Multiplying Eq. (
S69
) by
p
yields

p\,M(p)=\frac{4v_{F}}{\pi N_{f}}\left[\int_{0}^{p}\mathrm{d}k\,F(k)+p\int_{p}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}k\,G(k)\right].

(S70)

Differentiating once with respect to
p
using the Leibniz rule, we find

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}[pM(p)]=\frac{4v_{F}}{\pi N_{f}}\left[F(p)+\int_{p}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}k\,G(k)-pG(p)\right].

(S71)

Given that
F(p)=pG(p)
by definition, the boundary terms cancel, simplifying the expression to

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}[pM(p)]=\frac{4v_{F}}{\pi N_{f}}\int_{p}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}k\,G(k).

(S72)

Applying a second derivative and utilizing the identity
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}p^{2}}[pM(p)]=\frac{1}{p}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}\left(p^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}M(p)}{\mathrm{d}p}\right)
, we obtain the exact nonlinear local equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}\left(p^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}M(p)}{\mathrm{d}p}\right)=-\frac{4v_{F}}{\pi N_{f}}\,\frac{p\,M(p)}{\sqrt{v_{F}^{2}p^{2}+M^{2}(p)}}.

(S73)

In the scaling regime
M(0)\ll p\ll\alpha
, where
M(p)\ll v_{F}p
, the denominator can be linearized to
v_{F}p
. Equation (
S73
) then reduces to the Euler-type differential equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}\left(p^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}M(p)}{\mathrm{d}p}\right)+\frac{4}{\pi N_{f}}\,M(p)=0.

(S74)

Assuming a power-law solution
M(p)\sim p^{s}
, the characteristic equation and its roots are

\displaystyle s(s+1)+\frac{4}{\pi N_{f}}=0,

(S75)

\displaystyle s=-\frac{1}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{4}{\pi N_{f}}}.

(S76)

From this, we identify the critical flavor number
N_{c}=16/\pi
as the threshold where the roots become complex.

The existence of physically viable solutions is determined by the boundary conditions. The IR condition follows from the regularity of the mass function at the origin,
p^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}M(p)}{\mathrm{d}p}\Big|_{p=M(0)}=0
. The UV condition is obtained by evaluating Eq. (
S72
) at the cutoff
p=\alpha
:

\left[M(p)+p\frac{\mathrm{d}M(p)}{\mathrm{d}p}\right]_{p=\alpha}=0.

(S77)

For
N_{f}<N_{c}
, we define
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{N_{c}}{N_{f}}-1}
and express the general oscillatory solution as

M(p)=A\,p^{-1/2}\sin\!\left[\gamma\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{M(0)}\right)+\delta_{N_{f}}\right].

(S78)

Differentiating this solution to satisfy the IR condition, we find

p^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}p}=A\,p^{1/2}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sin\!\left(\gamma\ln\frac{p}{M(0)}+\delta_{N_{f}}\right)+\gamma\cos\!\left(\gamma\ln\frac{p}{M(0)}+\delta_{N_{f}}\right)\right].

(S79)

At
p=M(0)
, this yields the phase shift
\tan\delta_{N_{f}}=2\gamma
. Finally, substituting the solution into the UV boundary condition (
S77
) gives

\tan\!\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\alpha}{M(0)}+\delta_{N_{f}}\right)=-2\gamma.

(S80)

For the ground state, we match the argument to the first node,
\gamma\ln\frac{\alpha}{M(0)}+\delta_{N_{f}}\simeq\pi
. This leads to the final Miransky scaling for the mass gap:

M(0)\simeq\alpha\exp\!\left[-\frac{\pi-\delta_{N_{f}}}{\gamma}\right]\sim\alpha\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{N_{c}/N_{f}-1}}\right].

(S81)

This exponential behavior confirms that the excitonic insulation occurs via an infinite-order quantum phase transition at
N_{c}
.

III
Derivation of the Non-Fermi-Liquid Behavior

For fermion flavors
N_{f}>N_{c}
, the gap equation does not admit a non-trivial solution, meaning the ground state remains gapless (
M=0
). However, the strong, long-range cavity-mediated interaction fundamentally reconstructs the low-energy excitations. In this section, we derive the non-Fermi-liquid behavior by evaluating the one-loop self-energy. We will demonstrate that the highly singular nature of the effective interaction leads to an anomalous power-law scaling of the Fermi velocity and a complete suppression of the quasiparticle residue, signaling the breakdown of the standard Fermi liquid paradigm.

III.1
One-loop self-energy

In the gapless phase, the low-energy screened interaction is polarization dominated and takes the retarded form

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\simeq\frac{16}{N_{f}\tilde{Z}^{2}}\,\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}}{q^{2}},

(S82)

valid within the low-energy window
|\Omega|\lesssim\alpha
.
The one-loop self-energy is

\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\,G(\mathrm{i}\omega+\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf k}+{\bf q}).

(S83)

Substituting Eq. (
S23
) gives

\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\,\frac{\mathrm{i}(\omega+\Omega)+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!({\bf k}+{\bf q})}{(\omega+\Omega)^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}|{\bf k}+{\bf q}|^{2}}.

(S84)

We parameterize the self-energy as

\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=\tilde{Z}^{-1}[\mathrm{i}\omega\,\Sigma_{\omega}(\omega,k)+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!{\bf k}\,\Sigma_{k}(\omega,k)]

(S85)

With this convention, the dressed inverse Green’s function takes the form

G^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=\tilde{Z}^{-1}\left[-\mathrm{i}\omega(1+\Sigma_{\omega}(\omega,k))+\tilde{v}_{F}(1-\Sigma_{k}(\omega,k)\bigr)\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!{\bf k}\right].

(S86)

III.2
Derivation of
\Sigma_{\omega}

The temporal coefficient is extracted by differentiation with respect to the external frequency,

\Sigma_{\omega}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left[\frac{\partial\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf 0})}{\partial(\mathrm{i}\omega)}\right]_{\omega=0},

(S87)

where the trace is over the two-component pseudospin space.
Differentiating Eq. (
S84
) and setting
{\bf k}=0
gives

\Sigma_{\omega}=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}-\Omega^{2}}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{2}}.

(S88)

Substituting Eq. (
S82
), we obtain

\Sigma_{\omega}=-\frac{16}{N_{f}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}-\Omega^{2}}{q^{2}(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{3/2}}.

(S89)

Introducing
x=\Omega/(\tilde{v}_{F}q)
, so that
\mathrm{d}\Omega\mathrm{d}q=\tilde{v}_{F}q\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}q
, yields

\Sigma_{\omega}(\mu)=-\frac{8}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\int^{\alpha/\tilde{v}_{F}}_{\mu/\tilde{v}_{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{q}\int_{0}^{\alpha/(\tilde{v}_{F}q)}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{1-x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}}.

(S90)

For large upper cutoff
X=\alpha/(\tilde{v}_{F}q)\gg 1
,

\int_{0}^{X}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{1-x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}}=2-\operatorname{arcsinh}X+\mathcal{O}(X^{-1})\simeq-\ln X+2-\ln 2.

(S91)

The remaining momentum integral is therefore of the form
\int(\mathrm{d}q/q)\ln(\alpha/\tilde{v}_{F}q)
and generates a double logarithm,

\Sigma_{\omega}(\mu)\simeq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\,\ln^{2}\!\frac{\alpha}{\mu}-\frac{8(2-\ln 2)}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\ln{\frac{\alpha}{\mu}}.

(S92)

In the IR limit, where the running energy scale
\mu\ll\alpha
, the asymptotic expansion of the integral correctly captures the dependence of
\mu
and
\Sigma(\mu)
.

III.3
Derivation of
\Sigma_{k}

Starting from

\Sigma(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\,\frac{\mathrm{i}(\omega+\Omega)+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!({\bf k}+{\bf q})}{(\omega+\Omega)^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}|{\bf k}+{\bf q}|^{2}},

(S93)

we extract the spatial coefficient using

\Sigma_{k}(\mu)=\sum_{i=x,y}\frac{1}{4\tilde{v}_{F}}\text{Tr}\left[\sigma_{i}\frac{\partial\Sigma(0,{\bf k})}{\partial k_{i}}\right]_{|{\bf k}|=0}.

(S94)

At
\omega=0
, differentiation with respect to
k_{i}
gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!({\bf k}+{\bf q})}{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}|{\bf k}+{\bf q}|^{2}}=\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}\sigma_{i}}{D}-\frac{2\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}(k_{i}+q_{i})\bigl(\mathrm{i}\Omega+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!({\bf k}+{\bf q})\bigr)}{D^{2}},\qquad D=\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}|{\bf k}+{\bf q}|^{2}.

(S95)

Setting
{\bf k}=0
, we obtain

\left.\frac{\partial\Sigma(0,{\bf k})}{\partial k_{i}}\right|_{{\bf k}=0}=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\left[\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}\sigma_{i}}{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}-\frac{2\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q_{i}\bigl(\mathrm{i}\Omega+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!{\bf q}\bigr)}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{2}}\right].

(S96)

Substituting this into Eq. (
S94
) and using

\text{Tr}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})=2\delta_{ij},\qquad\text{Tr}(\sigma_{i})=0,

(S97)

one finds

\sum_{i}\text{Tr}\!\left[\sigma_{i}\left(\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}\sigma_{i}}{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}-\frac{2\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q_{i}(\mathrm{i}\Omega+\tilde{v}_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!{\bf q})}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{2}}\right)\right]=\frac{4\tilde{v}_{F}}{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}-\frac{4\tilde{v}_{F}^{3}q^{2}}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{2}}.

(S98)

Therefore

\Sigma_{k}=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\left[\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}-\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{2}}\right].

(S99)

Combining the two terms gives the compact form

\Sigma_{k}=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\frac{\Omega^{2}}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{2}}.

(S100)

Substituting the low-energy interaction

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\rm RPA}(\mathrm{i}\Omega,{\bf q})\simeq\frac{16}{N_{f}\tilde{v}_{F}}\,\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2}}}{q^{2}},

(S101)

we obtain

\Sigma_{k}=-\frac{16}{N_{f}\tilde{v}_{F}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{\Omega^{2}}{q^{2}(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{3/2}}.

(S102)

Introducing
x=\Omega/(\tilde{v}_{F}q)
, so that
\mathrm{d}\Omega=\tilde{v}_{F}q\,\mathrm{d}x
and
\mathrm{d}^{2}q=2\pi q\,\mathrm{d}q
, yields

\Sigma_{k}=-\frac{8}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\int_{\mu/\tilde{v}_{F}}^{\alpha/\tilde{v}_{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{q}\int_{0}^{\alpha/(\tilde{v}_{F}q)}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}}.

(S103)

To extract the leading divergence, we first evaluate the
x
integral,

J(X)\equiv\int_{0}^{X}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}}.

(S104)

Using

\frac{x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}-\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}},

(S105)

we obtain

J(X)=\operatorname{arcsinh}X-\frac{X}{\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}.

(S106)

For
X\gg 1
,

J(X)=\ln(2X)-1+\mathcal{O}(X^{-2})\simeq\ln X+\ln 2-1.

(S107)

Substituting
X=\alpha/(\tilde{v}_{F}q)
into Eq. (
S103
), the remaining momentum integral can be easily performed, leading to

\Sigma_{k}(\mu)\simeq-\frac{4}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\ln^{2}\!\frac{\alpha}{\mu}-\frac{8(\ln 2-1)}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\ln\frac{\alpha}{\mu}.

(S108)

III.4
Renormalization Group Equations of Fermi Velocity and Quasiparticle Residue

Before deriving the renormalization group (RG) equations, let us briefly comment on the running of the effective coupling constant
\alpha
. Since
\alpha
is not a fundamental UV cutoff but rather the scale at which the RPA-dominated low-energy effective field theory becomes valid, one might worry whether
\alpha
runs as the cutoff is tuned. For example, if one only considers the electron self-energy correction, the effective coupling would naively change as
\alpha\rightarrow\tilde{Z}^{2}\alpha
. However, this is precluded by the gauge invariance of the system. According to the Ward identity, the vertex corrections (where the interaction line connects two different fermion lines) must exactly cancel the corrections originating from the fermion self-energy (i.e.,
\tilde{Z}
). Therefore, the coupling
\alpha
remains a non-running constant when we treat the RPA corrections self-consistently.

With
\alpha
fixed, we turn to the dressed inverse propagator, which is parameterized as

G^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=\tilde{Z}^{-1}\left[-\mathrm{i}\omega\bigl[1+\Sigma_{\omega}(\omega,k)\bigr]+\tilde{v}_{F}\bigl[1-\Sigma_{k}(\omega,k)\bigr]\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!{\bf k}\right].

(S109)

From this expression, we can identify the running quasiparticle weight
\tilde{Z}
and the running Fermi velocity
\tilde{v}_{F}
at an energy scale
\mu
:

\tilde{Z}^{-1}(\mu)=\tilde{Z}^{-1}(\alpha)(1+\Sigma_{\omega}(\mu)),\qquad\tilde{v}_{F}(\mu)=\tilde{v}_{F}(\alpha)\,\frac{1-\Sigma_{k}(\mu)}{1+\Sigma_{\omega}(\mu)}.

(S110)

At this stage, we must address a subtle issue regarding the
\ln^{2}(\alpha/\mu)
double logarithmic divergences obtained in our one-loop calculations. In fact, this double logarithmic divergence originates from the singular behavior of the integrands in the limit
\Omega/q\to\infty
(or equivalently
q/\Omega\to 0
) is a IR divergence comes from the fact that Coulomb potential is unscreened at
q=0
. In this zero-momentum limit, the primary effect of the fluctuating cavity gauge field is merely to phase-rotate the fermion operator
111
. However, because such a global phase rotation constitutes a gauge redundancy, its associated singular divergences must not appear in any physical, gauge-invariant observables.

Indeed, the quasiparticle weight
\tilde{Z}
, which is closely related to
\Sigma_{\omega}
, is not a gauge-invariant quantity (since the fermion field
\hat{\Psi}
itself is not gauge invariant) and consequently suffers from this severe double logarithmic suppression. In contrast, the physical Fermi velocity
\tilde{v}_{F}
is a gauge-invariant observable. From Eq. (
S110
), the renormalized velocity is approximately
\tilde{v}_{F}(\mu)\approx v_{F}(1-\Sigma_{k}-\Sigma_{\omega})
. Crucially, the double logarithmic terms
\frac{4}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\ln^{2}(\alpha/\mu)
in
\Sigma_{\omega}
and
-\frac{4}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\ln^{2}(\alpha/\mu)
in
\Sigma_{k}
exactly cancel each other out in the sum. This exact cancellation leaves only a universal single logarithmic divergence, which not only provides a rigorous consistency check for our one-loop calculation but also correctly governs the linear RG flow of the Fermi velocity in the non-Fermi-liquid critical regime.
As a further consistency check, we can directly evaluate the leading divergences of
\Sigma_{k}
and
\Sigma_{\omega}
.

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}+\Sigma_{\omega}

\displaystyle=-\frac{16}{N_{f}}\int\frac{d\Omega d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}}{(\Omega^{2}+\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}q^{2})^{3/2}}=-\frac{8}{N_{f}\pi^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha}{\mu}\right).

(S111)

The result agrees with the sub-leading
\ln{\frac{\alpha}{\mu}}
divergence in Eqs. (
S108
) and (
S92
).

To formalize this RG flow, we differentiate Eq. (
S110
) with respect to the running scale
\mu
, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}^{-1}\tilde{v}_{F}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=\tilde{Z}^{-1}\tilde{v}_{F}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}

(S112)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}^{-1}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=\tilde{Z}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{\omega}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}

(S113)

The difference between Eq. (
S112
) and
\tilde{v}_{F}
times Eq. (
S113
) explicitly cancels the gauge-dependent double logarithms, yielding the leading-order RG equations:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln Z}{\mathrm{d}\ell}=-\frac{8\ell}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{f}^{2}}\right),\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\tilde{v}_{F}}{\mathrm{d}\ell}=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{N_{f}^{2}}\right),\qquad\ell=\ln\frac{\alpha}{\mu}.

(S114)

By integrating these RG equations, we obtain the scaling behaviors for the quasiparticle residue and the Fermi velocity:

Z_{\rm qp}(\mu)\sim\exp\left[\frac{4}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}\ln^{2}\frac{\mu}{\alpha}\right],\qquad\tilde{v}_{F}\sim\exp\!\left[\frac{8}{N_{f}\pi^{2}}\ln\!\frac{\alpha}{\mu}\right]

(S115)

Before proceeding, we briefly justify the instantaneous approximation used in our Dyson–Schwinger analysis of excitonic insulator in the previous section. The effective Fermi velocity
\tilde{v}_{F}
is significantly renormalized toward larger values in the infrared, bounded only by the dynamically generated excitonic gap and will not flow to infinity. Consequently, in the low-energy polarization function
\Pi(q,\omega)\sim-q^{2}/\sqrt{q^{2}+\omega^{2}/\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}}
, the frequency-dependent retardation term
\omega^{2}/\tilde{v}_{F}^{2}
becomes parametrically suppressed compared to the spatial momentum
q^{2}
. Since the interaction is overwhelmingly dominated by its spatial component, neglecting the explicit frequency dependence constitutes a physically consistent approximation for capturing the essential critical dynamics.

Finally, we briefly discuss the low-energy behavior of the single-particle Green’s function. By terminating the RG flow at the physical energy scale
\mu=\max[\omega,v_{F}k]
, we construct the full Green’s function:

\displaystyle G(\mathrm{i}\omega,{\bf k})=\frac{(\omega/\alpha)^{\frac{4\ln(\omega/\alpha)}{\pi^{2}N_{f}}}}{(-\mathrm{i}\omega+v_{F}(\omega/\alpha)^{-\frac{8}{N_{f}\pi^{2}}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\bf k})}

(S116)

Here, the nonanalytic structure of the elementary excitations is explicitly manifested. The fractional power-law dependence on the cutoff in the numerator arises directly from the
\ln^{2}(\alpha/\mu)
divergence of the quasiparticle weight. Since the electron operator itself is not gauge invariant, the specific coefficient of this anomalous dimension depends on the choice of gauge. Nevertheless, the non-Fermi liquid nature of the system—characterized by the vanishing of the quasiparticle pole and the singular velocity enhancement—remains a robust, physical consequence of the cavity-mediated interactions.

IV
Zeroth-Landau-Level Propagator and Polarization in a Magnetic Field

In this section, we summarize several technical ingredients used in the strong-field analysis of the main text. We first derive the free Green’s function projected onto the zeroth Landau level (ZLL) for a single Dirac valley, and then introduce the corresponding massive projected propagator employed in the Dyson–Schwinger analysis. Finally, we discuss the structure of the density-density polarization bubble in the ZLL and calculate the leading inter-Landau-level form factor, which governs the dynamical screening response.

IV.1
Single Particle Green’s function in the zeroth Landau level

We consider a single Dirac cone with chirality
(-1)^{a}=1
in a perpendicular magnetic field

\mathbf{B}=B\hat{z}
. The low-energy Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}_{B,+}=v_{F}\begin{pmatrix}0&\hat{\Pi}_{x}-\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}\\
\hat{\Pi}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}&0\end{pmatrix},

(S117)

where
\hat{\bm{\Pi}}=\hat{\bm{p}}+e\hat{\bm{A}}
is the kinetic momentum. For definiteness, we assume
eB>0
and work in the Landau gauge
\bm{A}=(0,Bx)
.

Introducing the standard ladder operators,

\hat{a}=\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{\Pi}_{x}-\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}),\qquad\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{\Pi}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\Pi}_{y}),

(S118)

with the magnetic length
\ell_{B}=1/\sqrt{eB}
, we have the commutation relation
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1
. The Hamiltonian takes the off-diagonal form

\hat{H}_{B}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,v_{F}}{\ell_{B}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\hat{a}\\
\hat{a}^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix},

(S119)

yielding the well-known Landau-level spectrum

E_{n}=\mathrm{sgn}(n)\,\frac{v_{F}\sqrt{2|n|}}{\ell_{B}}.

(S120)

The zeroth Landau level lies precisely at zero energy and, given
eB>0
, is fully polarized onto the lower sublattice. Its normalized eigenstates are expressed as

\Psi_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k_{y}y}}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\phi_{0}(x-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2})\end{pmatrix},

(S121)

where

\phi_{0}(x)=(\pi\ell_{B}^{2})^{-1/4}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}\right)

(S122)

is the ground-state wavefunction of a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator. The projector onto this ZLL subspace is

\mathcal{P}_{+}=\frac{1-\sigma_{z}}{2},

(S123)

for our chosen valley and magnetic-field orientation.

The noninteracting Matsubara Green’s function projected onto the ZLL is defined as

G_{0,\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega;\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{k_{y}}\frac{\Psi_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r})\Psi^{\dagger}_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{-\mathrm{i}\omega}.

(S124)

Its spatial dependence is determined by the kernel

I(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{k_{y}}\Psi_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r})\Psi^{\dagger}_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(S125)

Substituting the explicit ZLL wavefunctions, we have

\Psi_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r})\Psi^{\dagger}_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}}{L_{y}}\mathcal{P}_{+}\,\phi_{0}(x-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2})\phi_{0}(x^{\prime}-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2}).

(S126)

Taking the thermodynamic limit
\sum_{k_{y}}\to\dfrac{L_{y}}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}k_{y}
, the kernel becomes

I(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\mathcal{P}_{+}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}k_{y}}{2\pi}\,e^{\mathrm{i}k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}\phi_{0}(x-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2})\phi_{0}(x^{\prime}-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2}).

(S127)

Inserting the Gaussian form of
\phi_{0}
gives

I(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\mathcal{P}_{+}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\ell_{B}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}k_{y}}{2\pi}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2})^{2}+(x^{\prime}-k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2})^{2}}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}+\mathrm{i}k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})\right].

(S128)

By defining
X=k_{y}\ell_{B}^{2}
and utilizing the algebraic identity

(x-X)^{2}+(x^{\prime}-X)^{2}=2\left(X-\frac{x+x^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}{2},

(S129)

the integration can be performed by completing the square. This yields

I(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\mathcal{P}_{+}\,\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})^{2}}{4\ell_{B}^{2}}\right],

(S130)

where

\Phi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{(x+x^{\prime})(y-y^{\prime})}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}

(S131)

is the standard Schwinger phase in the Landau gauge. Substituting Eq. (
S130
) into Eq. (
S124
), we arrive at the full real-space Green’s function

G_{0,\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega;\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\widetilde{G}_{0,\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega;\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(S132)

where the translation-invariant part can be expressed as

\widetilde{G}_{0,\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega;\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{-\mathrm{i}\omega}\,\mathcal{P}_{+}\,\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4\ell_{B}^{2}}\right).

(S133)

Performing a Fourier transform on Eq. (
S133
), and adopting the convention used in the main text, we obtain the projected momentum-space Green’s function:

\widetilde{G}_{0,\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=2\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{-\mathrm{i}\omega}\,\mathcal{P}_{+}.

(S134)

To understand how dynamical mass generation modifies the spectrum, we consider a single gapped Dirac cone in a perpendicular magnetic field,

\tilde{H}_{B,+}=v_{F}\left(\sigma_{x}\hat{\Pi}_{x}+\sigma_{y}\hat{\Pi}_{y}\right)+m\sigma_{z},

(S135)

where
m
represents a dynamically generated Dirac mass. In terms of the ladder operators, this reads

\tilde{H}_{B,+}=\begin{pmatrix}m&\omega_{B}\hat{a}\\
\omega_{B}\hat{a}^{\dagger}&-m\end{pmatrix},\qquad\omega_{B}=\frac{\sqrt{2}v_{F}}{\ell_{B}}=v_{F}\sqrt{2eB}.

(S136)

For
n\geq 1
, the Landau-level energies are shifted to

E_{n,\lambda}=\lambda\epsilon_{n},\qquad\epsilon_{n}=\sqrt{m^{2}+n\omega_{B}^{2}},\qquad\lambda=\pm.

(S137)

The corresponding eigenstates mix the sublattice components:

\Psi_{n,\lambda,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{e^{ik_{y}y}}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\begin{pmatrix}u_{n,\lambda}\,\phi_{n-1}(x-x_{0})\\
v_{n,\lambda}\,\phi_{n}(x-x_{0})\end{pmatrix},\qquad x_{0}=-\ell_{B}^{2}k_{y},

(S138)

with coefficients

u_{n,\lambda}=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{n}+\lambda m}{2\epsilon_{n}}},\qquad v_{n,\lambda}=\lambda\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{n}-\lambda m}{2\epsilon_{n}}}.

(S139)

However, the zeroth Landau level (
n=0
) remains uniquely immune to this sublattice mixing. For
B>0
, it retains the form

\Psi_{0,k_{y}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{e^{ik_{y}y}}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\phi_{0}(x-x_{0})\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_{0}=-m.

(S140)

Thus, the ZLL remains fully polarized on a single sublattice. In the massless limit
m\to 0
,
E_{0}\to 0
, while the first excited level approaches the cyclotron scale:

\epsilon_{1}=\sqrt{m^{2}+\omega_{B}^{2}}\;\;\xrightarrow{m\to 0}\;\;\omega_{B}=v_{F}\sqrt{2eB}.

(S141)

For the interacting problem discussed in the main text, we seek a self-consistent symmetry-breaking solution where a mass gap
m(\mathbf{q})
is dynamically generated within the ZLL sector. The dressed projected propagator used in the strong-field Dyson–Schwinger equation is therefore formulated as

\widetilde{G}_{\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=2\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega+m(q)}{\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}\,\mathcal{P}_{+}.

(S142)

Since the propagator is rigorously projected onto the ZLL subspace, its internal matrix structure is completely described by
\mathcal{P}_{+}
. The opposite Dirac valley, characterized by chirality
(-1)^{a}=-1
, follows analogously.

IV.2
One-loop vacuum density-density polarization in the projected ZLL

To evaluate the one-loop density-density polarization bubble in a magnetic field, it is most convenient to compute it in real space, where the gauge-dependent Schwinger phases can be handled seamlessly. The bubble is given by

\Pi_{B}(x,x^{\prime})=-N_{f}\text{Tr}\left[G_{\text{ZLL}}(x,x^{\prime})G_{\text{ZLL}}(x^{\prime},x)\right].

(S143)

Since the Schwinger phases from the two propagators cancel exactly, the spatial part of the bubble is simply the square of the Gaussian envelope:

f(\Delta\mathbf{r})f(-\Delta\mathbf{r})=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}}\right)^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(\Delta\mathbf{r})^{2}}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}\right).

(S144)

Performing the two-dimensional Fourier transform with respect to the external momentum
\mathbf{q}
yields

\Pi_{\text{space}}(\mathbf{q})=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}(\Delta\mathbf{r})\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{r}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}}\right)^{2}e^{-\frac{(\Delta\mathbf{r})^{2}}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}=\frac{eB}{2\pi}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}.

(S145)

The prefactor
eB/(2\pi)
exactly reproduces the Landau-level degeneracy per unit area, consistent with physical expectations.

Taking the matrix trace
\text{Tr}[\mathcal{P}_{+}]=1
, the full polarization bubble factorizes in Euclidean frequency-momentum space as

\Pi(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\Omega)=-N_{f}\frac{eB}{2\pi}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\,\Pi_{B,\text{freq}}(\mathrm{i}\Omega),

(S146)

where the frequency-dependent part takes the form of an effective
0+1
-dimensional convolution:

\Pi_{B,\text{freq}}(\mathrm{i}\Omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{m^{2}(q)-\omega(\omega+\Omega)}{(\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q))\left[(\omega+\Omega)^{2}+m^{2}(q)\right]}.

(S147)

To determine the static Thomas–Fermi screening, we consider the limit
\Omega\to 0
, which simplifies Eq. (
S147
) to

\Pi_{B,\text{freq}}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{m^{2}(q)-\omega^{2}}{(\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q))^{2}}.

(S148)

By rewriting the numerator as
m^{2}-\omega^{2}=(m^{2}+\omega^{2})-2\omega^{2}
, we can partition the integral into two pieces:

\Pi_{B,\text{freq}}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{2\omega^{2}}{(\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q))^{2}}.

(S149)

The first integral gives

I_{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}=\frac{1}{2m(q)},

(S150)

while the second evaluates to the same value via integration by parts:

I_{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{2\omega^{2}}{(\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q))^{2}}=\frac{1}{2m(q)}.

(S151)

Consequently, we find

\Pi_{B,\text{freq}}(0)=I_{1}-I_{2}=0.

(S152)

The static density response vanishes identically within the projected ZLL. Physically, this reflects the incompressibility of the completely filled (or empty) states, dictating the absence of a regular intra-ZLL density response in the gapped projected theory.

Because the intra-ZLL channel is suppressed, for finite frequencies
\Omega\neq 0
, higher Landau levels must be included to capture the physical absorption. The leading nonvanishing dynamical contribution in the strong-field regime originates from inter-level transitions between the zeroth and first Landau levels (
n=0\leftrightarrow 1
). Let us now evaluate the corresponding form factor for massless Dirac fermions.

The density operator is given by
\rho(\mathbf{q})=e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
. At zero temperature, the density-density polarization function admits the standard spectral representation

\Pi_{B}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{a,b}\frac{f(E_{a})-f(E_{b})}{-\mathrm{i}\omega+E_{a}-E_{b}}\,\left|\langle a|\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{q})|b\rangle\right|^{2},

(S153)

where
(2\pi\ell_{B}^{2})^{-1}
is the density of states per Landau level, and the indices
a,b
run over all exact Landau-level quantum numbers including band indices.

In the Landau level basis, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\langle n^{\prime},k^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|n,k,\lambda\rangle\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n^{\prime},k^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{n,k,\lambda},

(S154)

and we only keep with the contribution of zeroth and first Landau levels, leading to

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{q})\sim\hat{\rho}_{0}(\mathbf{q})+\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\langle 0,k^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|1,k,\lambda\rangle\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0,k^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{1,k,\lambda}+{\rm H.c.},

(S155)

where
\hat{\rho}_{0}(\mathbf{q})
is the ZLL projecting density operator.
For a neutral system, the dominant low-energy transition providing the lowest excitation energy allowed by the density operator is the
n=0\leftrightarrow 1
channel. The essential matrix element required is

F_{01}(\mathbf{q})=\langle 0,k^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|1,k\rangle,

(S156)

where
\lambda
is dropped off for convenience, since it only gives an overall
\pm
factor.

The single-particle Landau-level wavefunctions in the Landau gauge take the form

\psi_{n,k}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{y}}}\,e^{iky}\,\varphi_{n}(x-x_{k}),\qquad x_{k}\equiv k\ell_{B}^{2},

(S157)

where
\varphi_{n}(\xi)
are harmonic-oscillator eigenfunctions. Specifically, we need the first two states:

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{\ell_{B}}}\,e^{-\xi^{2}/(2\ell_{B}^{2})},

(S158)

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,\frac{\xi}{\ell_{B}}\,\varphi_{0}(\xi).

(S159)

We define the transition integral explicitly as

\displaystyle F_{01}(\mathbf{q};k^{\prime},k)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle 0,k^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|1,k\rangle

\displaystyle=v_{1,\lambda}\int d^{2}r\;\psi^{*}_{0,k^{\prime}}(x,y)\,e^{i(q_{x}x+q_{y}y)}\,\psi_{1,k}(x,y).

(S160)

Integrating over the
y
coordinate yields the momentum conservation condition:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{y}}\int_{0}^{L_{y}}dy\;e^{i(k-k^{\prime}+q_{y})y}=\delta_{k^{\prime},\,k+q_{y}}.

(S161)

This allows us to write
F_{01}(\mathbf{q};k^{\prime},k)=\delta_{k^{\prime},\,k+q_{y}}\;I_{01}(q_{x};k)
, where the residual integration over
x
is

I_{01}(q_{x};k)=\int dx\;\varphi_{0}(x-x_{k^{\prime}})\,e^{iq_{x}x}\,\varphi_{1}(x-x_{k}),\qquad(k^{\prime}=k+q_{y}).

(S162)

Since the shift between the center coordinates is
x_{k^{\prime}}-x_{k}=(k^{\prime}-k)\ell_{B}^{2}=q_{y}\ell_{B}^{2}\equiv\delta
, we can evaluate this analytically. By shifting the integration variable to
u=x-x_{k}
(and defining
a\equiv x_{k}
), we get

\displaystyle I_{01}

\displaystyle=\int du\;\varphi_{0}(u-\delta)\,e^{iq_{x}(u+a)}\,\varphi_{1}(u)

\displaystyle=e^{iq_{x}a}\,\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\ell_{B}}\int du\;u\;\varphi_{0}(u-\delta)\varphi_{0}(u)\,e^{iq_{x}u}.

(S163)

The product of the Gaussian functions simplifies to

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(u-\delta)\varphi_{0}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\ell_{B}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(u-\delta)^{2}+u^{2}}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\ell_{B}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(u-\delta/2)^{2}}{\ell_{B}^{2}}\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4\ell_{B}^{2}}\right].

(S164)

Inserting this back, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{01}=e^{iq_{x}a}\,\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\ell_{B}}\,\frac{e^{-\delta^{2}/(4\ell_{B}^{2})}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\ell_{B}}\int du\;u\,e^{-(u-\delta/2)^{2}/\ell_{B}^{2}}\,e^{iq_{x}u}.

(S165)

Making a second shift
t=u-\delta/2
, the integral becomes

\int du\;u\,e^{-(u-\delta/2)^{2}/\ell_{B}^{2}}\,e^{iq_{x}u}=e^{iq_{x}\delta/2}\int dt\;(t+\delta/2)\,e^{-t^{2}/\ell_{B}^{2}}\,e^{iq_{x}t}.

(S166)

Using the standard Gaussian integrals,

\displaystyle\int dt\;e^{-t^{2}/\ell_{B}^{2}}e^{iq_{x}t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\,\ell_{B}\,e^{-q_{x}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/4},

(S167)

\displaystyle\int dt\;t\,e^{-t^{2}/\ell_{B}^{2}}e^{iq_{x}t}

\displaystyle=i\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\ell_{B}^{3}}{2}q_{x}\,e^{-q_{x}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/4},

(S168)

and assembling all terms, we find

\displaystyle I_{01}

\displaystyle=e^{iq_{x}(a+\delta/2)}\,e^{-\delta^{2}/(4\ell_{B}^{2})}\,e^{-q_{x}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/4}\;\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\ell_{B}}\left(\frac{\delta}{2}+i\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}q_{x}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{iq_{x}(x_{k}+x_{k^{\prime}})/2}\;\exp\!\left[-\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}(q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2})}{4}\right]\;\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\,(q_{y}+iq_{x}),

(S169)

where we have reinstated
\delta=q_{y}\ell_{B}^{2}
.

Combining these ingredients, we arrive at the exact matrix element:

\langle 0,k^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|1,k\rangle=v_{1,\lambda}\delta_{k^{\prime},\,k+q_{y}}\;e^{\,iq_{x}(x_{k}+x_{k^{\prime}})/2}\;\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}\,(q_{y}+iq_{x})\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}{4}\right)

(S170)

with
q^{2}=q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2}
. In most physical applications, only its modulus squared enters the calculation, in which case the phase factor smoothly drops out:

\left|\langle 0,k^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|1,k\rangle\right|^{2}=|v_{1,\lambda}|^{2}\delta_{k^{\prime},\,k+q_{y}}\;\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}q^{2}}{2}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}{2}\right).

(S171)

We then sum over
k_{y},k_{y}^{\prime}

\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k_{y},k_{y}^{\prime}}\left|F_{01}(\mathbf{q};k_{y}^{\prime},k_{y})\right|^{2}=\frac{|v_{1,\lambda}|^{2}}{4\pi}q^{2}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2},

(S172)

where we use
\sum_{k_{y}}1=\frac{S}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}}.
This form factor controls the leading inter-Landau-level contribution to the dynamical density response in the strong-field regime.

Substituting Eq. (
S170
) into Eq. (
S153
), the polarization function evaluates to

\displaystyle\Pi_{B}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{|v_{1,\lambda}|^{2}}{4\pi}q^{2}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\left[\frac{f(E_{0})-f(E_{1,\lambda})}{-\mathrm{i}\omega+E_{0}-E_{1,\lambda}}+\frac{f(E_{1,\lambda})-f(E_{0})}{-\mathrm{i}\omega+E_{1,\lambda}-E_{0}}\right].

(S173)

At zero temperature and exact charge neutrality, only appropriate interband transitions contribute. For the massless Dirac case,
E_{0}=0
and
E_{1,\lambda}=\lambda\omega_{B}
. The two terms combine into a standard resonant denominator:

\Pi_{B}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=\frac{N_{f}}{8\pi}q^{2}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega-\omega_{B}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega+\omega_{B}}\right].

(S174)

Combining these fractions yields the dynamical polarization:

\Pi_{B}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=-\frac{N_{f}}{8\pi}q^{2}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\frac{2\omega_{B}}{\omega^{2}+\omega_{B}^{2}}.

(S175)

We can now revisit the gap equation in the strong-field regime. Taking the bare interaction kernel to be

\mathcal{V}_{0}(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{\alpha}{q^{2}},

(S176)

and assuming that the magnetic field is sufficiently strong, the dominant screening effect can be described within the quasistatic approximation. In this regime, the dynamical polarization from the
n=0\leftrightarrow 1
transition reduces to its static limit,

\Pi_{B}(0,\mathbf{q})=-\frac{N_{f}}{4\pi\omega_{B}}q^{2}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}.

(S177)

The fully screened effective interaction is therefore given by

V_{\rm eff}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{q})-\Pi(0,\mathbf{q})}=\frac{1}{-q^{2}/\alpha+\alpha_{B}q^{2}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\,\frac{1}{-\alpha^{-1}+\alpha_{B}e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}}.

(S178)

In the long-wavelength limit
q\ell_{B}\ll 1
, the dressed interaction is given by

\mathcal{V}(q)=\frac{\alpha_{\text{eff}}}{q^{2}},

(S179)

where the effective coupling strength
\alpha_{\text{eff}}
satisfies

\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{-1}=\frac{N_{f}}{4\pi\omega_{B}}-\alpha^{-1}.

(S180)

The system exhibits a critical magnetic field that marks a transition from the
underscreening
to the
overscreening
regime.

Specifically, when
\omega_{B}\ll\alpha
, the screening effect dominates, and the effective interaction takes the form

\mathcal{V}(q)=\frac{4\pi\omega_{B}}{N_{f}}\frac{1}{q^{2}}e^{q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}.

(S181)

Note that the exponential factor
e^{q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}
does not lead to a physical divergence; in the Landau level basis, the correlation functions are naturally regularized by the form factor
e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}
, ensuring the stability of the theory.

In the opposite limit where
\omega_{B}\gg\alpha
, the interaction enters the overscreening regime and becomes purely attractive:

\mathcal{V}(q)=-\frac{\alpha}{q^{2}}.

(S182)

IV.3
Dyson–Schwinger equation and mass generation in the ZLL

In this section, we provide the detailed derivation of the gap equation in the strong magnetic field limit, as discussed in the main text. Starting from the projected Dyson–Schwinger equation,

\widetilde{G}_{\rm ZLL}^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega;\mathbf{r})=\widetilde{G}_{0,\rm ZLL}^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega;\mathbf{r})+\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})\,e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,\widetilde{G}_{\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega+\mathrm{i}\Omega;\mathbf{r}),

(S183)

we seek a self-consistent symmetry-breaking solution in which the cavity-mediated interactions dynamically generate a mass gap
m
entirely within the ZLL.

For the Dirac cone with chirality
(-1)^{a}=1
, we employ the massive projected propagator ansatz:

\widetilde{G}_{\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=2\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega+m(q)}{\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}\,\mathcal{P}_{+}.

(S184)

Similarly, for the opposite Dirac valley with chirality
(-1)^{a}=-1
, the corresponding ansatz takes the form:

\widetilde{G}_{\rm ZLL}(\mathrm{i}\omega,\mathbf{q})=2\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega-m(q)}{\omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}\,\mathcal{P}_{-}.

(S185)

Assuming the bare cavity-induced interaction kernel,

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})\simeq\frac{\alpha_{eff}}{q^{2}},

(S186)

one can project Eq. (
S183
) onto the ZLL mass channel. Since the full propagator is rigorously restricted to the ZLL subspace, its matrix structure is entirely encoded by the projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{\pm}
. Consequently, the Dyson–Schwinger equation simplifies into a scalar integral equation for the dynamically generated gap
m(q)
:

\frac{m(k)}{2}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})\,e^{-(q^{2}+2qk\cos\theta)\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{2m(q)}{\Omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}.

(S187)

When
k=0
, we have

m(0)=4\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\frac{m(q)}{\Omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}.

(S188)

A remarkable and defining feature of dynamical mass generation within the zeroth Landau level is the effective dimensional reduction from
2+1
to
0+1
dimensions. Specifically, the temporal and spatial loop integrations factorize completely. Performing the Matsubara frequency integral yields exactly:

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\ \Omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{m(q)}{\Omega^{2}+m^{2}(q)}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad(m>0).

(S189)

Because this integral is simply a constant, the mass function
m(q)
miraculously drops out of the remaining momentum integration. As a result, the gap equation determines a uniform mass scale and reduces to:

m(0)=2\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}.

(S190)

Substituting the cavity interaction Eq. (
S186
) and transitioning to polar coordinates (
\mathrm{d}^{2}q=2\pi q\,\mathrm{d}q
). The integrand exhibits a
1/q
behavior at small momenta, signaling a severe logarithmic infrared singularity. However, within the physical metasurface cavity platform, the idealized bare form
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})\simeq\alpha/q^{2}
derived from the quasistatic approximation is no longer valid at length scales exceeding the cavity wavelength. This divergence is naturally cut off at a characteristic infrared momentum scale
k_{p}\sim\omega_{p}/c
, where
\omega_{p}
is the effective cavity plasma frequency. By implementing this physical IR cutoff, the regularized gap equation becomes

m(0)=\frac{4\omega_{B}}{N_{f}}\int_{k_{p}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{q}\,e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}.

(S191)

where
q_{\rm UV}\sim 2\pi/d
, beyond which the electron cannot feel the existence of metasurfaces.

This integral can be evaluated analytically in terms of the exponential integral function. With the change of variable

u=\frac{q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}{2}\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{q}=\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{2u},

(S192)

Eq. (
S191
) transforms into

m(0)=\frac{2\omega_{B}}{N_{f}}\int_{k_{p}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u}}{u}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\omega_{B}}{2N_{f}}E_{1}\!\left({k_{p}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\right),

(S193)

where
E_{1}(x)
is the standard exponential integral,

E_{1}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u}}{u}\,\mathrm{d}u.

(S194)

In the strong-field regime where
k_{p}\ell_{B}\ll 1
, we can expand
E_{1}(x)
using its small-argument asymptotic form,

E_{1}(x)=-\gamma-\ln x+\mathcal{O}(x),

(S195)

where
\gamma\approx 0.5772
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Substituting this expansion gives

\displaystyle m(0)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2\omega_{B}}{N_{f}}\left[-\gamma-\ln\!\left({k_{p}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{B}}{N_{f}}\ln\!\left(\frac{\,\sqrt{2}e^{-\gamma/2}}{k_{p}\ell_{B}}\right).

(S196)

Finally, using the definition of the magnetic length
\ell_{B}=1/\sqrt{eB}
, we arrive at the expression for the dynamically generated magnetic mass:

m(0)\simeq\frac{2\omega_{B}}{N_{f}}\ln\!\left(\frac{2\text{e}^{-\gamma}eB}{\hbar k_{p}^{2}}\right),

(S197)

which is in precise agreement with the scaling behavior quoted in the main text. The approximation neglecting transitions to higher Landau levels is self-consistent provided that
m(0)\ll\omega_{B}
. Consequently, this result remains reliable in the large-
N_{f}
limit.

IV.4
Interlayer pairing in a magnetic field

In this section, we formulate the interlayer pairing instability for two neutral Dirac layers in a perpendicular magnetic field. We consider two graphene-like layers, labeled by
s=\uparrow,\downarrow
, and restrict ourselves to a single valley. Both layers are fixed at charge neutrality throughout this section. We work in the Landau-level basis and project onto the ZLL, where the single-particle kinetic energy is quenched and the low-energy physics is governed by the projected interaction.

We denote the ZLL annihilation operator in layer
s
by
c_{s,k}(\tau)
, where
k
is the momentum quantum number. In the projected ZLL subspace, we introduce the standard two-component Nambu spinor in imaginary time,

\hat{\Psi}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{\uparrow,k}\\
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\downarrow,-k}\end{pmatrix}.

(S198)

We use
\sigma_{i}
to denote Pauli matrices acting on the layer space
(\uparrow,\downarrow)
. In the absence of pairing, the inverse Green’s function in Matsubara space is

\mathcal{G}_{0}^{-1}(\mathrm{i}\omega;k)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\omega e^{k^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\,\sigma_{0},

(S199)

We now consider the interlayer Cooper channel. Since the two fermions are exchanged between the two layers, the pairing matrix in the layer space must be antisymmetric and is therefore proportional to
\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}
. By choosing a gauge where the pairing amplitude is purely real, interlayer pairing matrix in the ZLL basis is then

\bm{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta(k)\,\sigma_{1},

(S200)

. In the standard Nambu basis (
S198
), the BdG self-energy explicitly incorporates the Landau-level form factor and takes the form

\Sigma_{\rm BdG}(k)=\frac{e^{k^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}}{2}\Delta(k)\,\sigma_{1}

(S201)

The full inverse Green’s function is obtained from the Dyson equation

\mathcal{G}^{-1}(i\omega;k)=\mathcal{G}_{0}^{-1}(i\omega;k)-\Sigma_{\rm BdG}(k).

(S202)

For the uniform ansatz this gives

\mathcal{G}^{-1}(i\omega;k)=\frac{1}{2}\left[-i\omega\sigma_{0}+\Delta(k)\,\sigma_{1}\right]e^{k^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}.

(S203)

we obtain

\mathcal{G}(i\omega;k)=2{e^{-k^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}}\frac{i\omega\,\sigma_{0}+\Delta(k)\,\sigma_{1}}{\omega^{2}+\Delta(k)^{2}}.

(S204)

The anomalous block (upper right element) of the Green’s function is therefore

\mathcal{F}(i\omega;k)=2e^{-k^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\frac{\Delta(k)\,}{\omega^{2}+\Delta(k)^{2}}.

(S205)

We now include the cavity-mediated interlayer attraction. In momentum space, we take

\mathcal{V}(q)=-\frac{\alpha}{q^{2}},\qquad\alpha>0.

(S206)

And it contribute an additional
\sigma_{3}
when interacting with the vertex,The BCS channel Dyson-Schwingle equation gives

\frac{{\Delta}(0)}{2}=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}(q)\,\mathcal{F}(i\omega;q).

(S207)

Substituting Eq. (
S205
), we obtain

\Delta(0)=-4\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\left(-\frac{\alpha}{q^{2}}\right)\left(e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\frac{\Delta(q)\,}{\omega^{2}+\Delta(q)^{2}}\right).

(S208)

Since both sides are proportional to the same antisymmetric tensor
i\sigma_{2}
, this reduces to a scalar equation for
\Delta(q)
:

\Delta(0)=4\alpha\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{e^{-q^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}}{q^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta(q)}{\omega^{2}+\Delta(q)^{2}}.

(S209)

At zero temperature, the frequency integral is

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta(q)}{\omega^{2}+\Delta(q)^{2}}=\frac{\Delta(q)}{2|\Delta(q)|}.

(S210)

Hence, for a nontrivial solution
\Delta(q)>0
, the self-consistency equation gives

\Delta(0)=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{k_{p}^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}}\right).

(S211)

V
Detailed estimation of experimental parameters

Here we give further details about the numerical estimates underlying the discussion in the main text.
As a representative metasurface platform, we consider the graphene nanodisk arrays reported in Ref.
38
, where the disk diameter is
D\approx 60~\mathrm{nm}
and the edge-to-edge gap is
\delta\approx 30~\mathrm{nm}
.
The corresponding lattice period is therefore

a\approx D+\delta\approx 90~\mathrm{nm}.

(S212)

For the experimentally relevant doping range, the measured plasmonic resonance of the array lies near
\hbar\omega_{0}\approx 0.17
–
0.18~\mathrm{eV}
.
This corresponds to a free-space wavelength

\lambda_{0}=\frac{hc}{\hbar\omega_{0}}\approx 6.9\text{--}7.3~\mu\mathrm{m},

(S213)

so that

\frac{a}{\lambda_{0}}\approx\frac{90~\mathrm{nm}}{7~\mu\mathrm{m}}\approx 1.3\times 10^{-2}\ll 1.

(S214)

Thus the effective-medium description of the patterned graphene layer as a homogenized high-impedance surface is well justified.

To obtain a concrete order-of-magnitude estimate for the effective surface impedance, we take a representative unit-cell capacitance

C_{s}=5\times 10^{-18}~\mathrm{F}.

(S215)

Using
\hbar\omega_{0}\approx 0.18~\mathrm{eV}
, the corresponding angular frequency is

\omega_{0}=\frac{0.18\times 1.602\times 10^{-19}~\mathrm{J}}{1.055\times 10^{-34}~\mathrm{J\,s}}\approx 2.73\times 10^{14}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}.

(S216)

The effective inductance then follows from
\omega_{0}=(L_{s}C_{s})^{-1/2}
:

L_{s}=\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}C_{s}}\approx\frac{1}{(2.73\times 10^{14})^{2}\times 5\times 10^{-18}}\approx 2.7\times 10^{-12}~\mathrm{H}.

(S217)

Next we take the cavity spacing to be one tenth of the free-space wavelength,

d\sim\lambda_{0}/10\approx 0.7~\mu\mathrm{m},

(S218)

and use
\varepsilon_{d}=1
for a vacuum spacer.
The cavity mode energy is controlled by

\omega_{p}^{2}=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{1+\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}{2C_{s}}}.

(S219)

With
\varepsilon_{0}=8.85\times 10^{-12}~\mathrm{F/m}
and
d=0.7\times 10^{-6}~\mathrm{m}
, one finds

\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}{2C_{s}}=\frac{(8.85\times 10^{-12})(0.7\times 10^{-6})}{2\times 5\times 10^{-18}}\approx 0.62.

(S220)

Hence

\omega_{p}\approx\frac{\omega_{0}}{\sqrt{1.62}}\approx 0.79\,\omega_{0},

(S221)

which gives

\hbar\omega_{p}\approx 0.14~\mathrm{eV}.

(S222)

The interaction scale is

\alpha=\frac{4e^{2}}{2C_{s}+\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}.

(S223)

Substituting
e=1.602\times 10^{-19}~\mathrm{C}
,
C_{s}=5\times 10^{-18}~\mathrm{F}
, and
d=0.7\times 10^{-6}~\mathrm{m}
, we obtain

2C_{s}+\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d\approx 1.0\times 10^{-17}+6.2\times 10^{-18}=1.62\times 10^{-17}~\mathrm{F},

(S224)

and therefore

\alpha\approx\frac{4\times(1.602\times 10^{-19})^{2}}{1.62\times 10^{-17}}\approx 6.34\times 10^{-21}~\mathrm{J}\approx 3.96\times 10^{-2}~\mathrm{eV}.

(S225)

Here we give a representative estimate of the zero-field excitonic gap using the improved Miransky form

M(0)=\alpha\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\pi-\delta_{N_{f}}}{\sqrt{N_{c}/N_{f}-1}}\right],\qquad\delta_{N_{f}}=2\arctan\!\bigl(\sqrt{N_{c}/N_{f}-1}\bigr),

(S226)

with

\alpha=\frac{4e^{2}}{2C_{s}+\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{d}d}.

(S227)

For
N_{f}=2
, using
N_{c}=16/\pi\simeq 5.09
,
we find

\sqrt{N_{c}/N_{f}-1}=\sqrt{5.09/2-1}\simeq 1.24.

(S228)

This gives

\delta_{N_{f}}=2\arctan(1.24)\simeq 1.79.

(S229)

The exponential factor in Eq. (
S226
) then becomes

\exp\!\left[-\frac{2\pi-\delta_{N_{f}}}{\sqrt{N_{c}/N_{f}-1}}\right]\simeq\exp\!\left(-\frac{6.283-1.79}{1.24}\right)\simeq 2.7\times 10^{-2}.

(S230)

Substituting this together with the representative metasurface parameters adopted in the main text,

C_{s}=5\times 10^{-18}~\mathrm{F}
,
\varepsilon_{d}=1
, and

d\simeq 0.7~\mu\mathrm{m}
, we obtain

M(0)\simeq(3.96\times 10^{-2}~\mathrm{eV})(2.7\times 10^{-2})\simeq 1.1~\mathrm{meV}

(S231)

In temperature units, this corresponds to

\frac{M(0)}{k_{B}}\simeq\frac{1.1\times 10^{-3}~\mathrm{eV}}{8.617\times 10^{-5}~\mathrm{eV/K}}\simeq 12.8~\mathrm{K}.

(S232)

Thus, for the representative parameter set used here, the improved gap formula yields a zero-field excitonic gap of order

M(0)\sim 10^{-3}~\mathrm{eV}
for
N_{f}=2

10~\mathrm{K}
