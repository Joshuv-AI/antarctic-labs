Disentangling Similarity and Relatedness in Topic Models
Hanlin Xiao, Mauricio A Álvarez, Rainer Breitling

Abstract
The recent advancement of large language models has spurred a growing trend of integrating pre-trained language model (PLM) embeddings into topic models, fundamentally reshaping how topics capture semantic structure. Classical models such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) derive topics from word co-occurrence statistics, whereas PLM-augmented models anchor these statistics to pre-trained embedding spaces, imposing a prior that also favours clustering of semantically similar words. This structural difference can be captured by the psycholinguistic dimensions of thematic relatedness and taxonomic similarity of the topic words. To disentangle these dimensions in topic models, we construct a large synthetic benchmark of word pairs using LLM-based annotation to train a neural scoring function. We apply this scorer to a comprehensive evaluation across multiple corpora and topic model families, revealing that different model families capture distinct semantic structure in their topics. We further demonstrate that similarity and relatedness scores successfully predict downstream task performance depending on task requirements. This paper establishes similarity and relatedness as essential axes for topic model evaluation and provides a reliable pipeline for characterising these across model families and corpora. 1 1 1 Code and data will be made publicly available upon acceptance.

In this paper, we address this gap by training a two-dimensional neural scorer for word pair similarity and relatedness using a large-scale LLM-annotated synthetic dataset. Using this scorer, we conduct a comprehensive survey of topic model behavior across multiple corpora, revealing a consistent pattern: co-occurrence-based models incline toward relatedness, while PLM-augmented models incline toward similarity. This distinction proves to be not merely descriptive but predictive: in downstream task experiments, tasks whose nature favors relatedness are better served by relatedness-inclined models, and vice versa. Beyond these core results, we employ the proposed metric to further quantitatively analyze topics and corpora.

The main contributions of this paper include:

•

We introduce a novel two-dimensional evaluation framework that explicitly disentangles taxonomic similarity from thematic relatedness in topic models, addressing a fundamental gap in how the semantic structure of topics is evaluated.

•

We construct a large-scale synthetic benchmark of approximately 51K word pairs sampled from existing knowledge networks with LLM-imputed annotations, and train a neural scorer that reliably quantifies both semantic dimensions.

•

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of topic model families across multiple corpora, revealing that the similarity–relatedness distinction is not merely descriptive but predictive of downstream task performance, validating the explanatory power of disentangling these two semantic axes.

3.1
Similarity–Relatedness Scorer

3.1.1
Training Data Construction

Building a robust similarity–relatedness neural scorer requires high-quality training data that distinguishes these two overlapping yet distinct semantic dimensions. Historically, various word-pair datasets have been curated to evaluate similarity and relatedness
51
;
19
;
1
;
9
;
26
. However, existing benchmarks typically focus on a single dimension and suffer from systematic conflation of the two (Table
6
, Appendix
A.1
). Combining all existing manually annotated datasets yields only approximately 9,000 word pairs of noisy data, which is insufficient to fully exploit the capacity of a neural scoring model.

To address these limitations, we sampled from existing knowledge networks and employed an
LLM Imputation
approach to curate large-scale training data. By targeting specific word relations, including shared synsets and co-hyponyms in WordNet
41
, associative edges (
RelatedTo
,
UsedFor
,
PartOf
,
AtLocation
,
Causes
) in ConceptNet
53
, cosine-nearest neighbors in the BERT embedding space
16
, and controlled negatives from cross-domain and cross-POS pairings, we obtained candidate word pairs whose relationships span the full spectrum of similarity and relatedness. These candidate pairs were then annotated using DeepSeek-V3
37
. The prompt provides explicit scoring anchors, low-relatedness calibration examples, and critical distinction rules that delineate synonyms, complementary pairs, same-category pairs, and antonyms across both dimensions (see Appendix
B.3
for the full curation details).

Through this pipeline, we curated a dataset of 51,523 word pairs that offers balanced scoring across the similarity–relatedness spectrum and broad lexical diversity through its multi-source design. The dataset could be expanded to potentially improve the scorer further, as our scaling-law analysis shows that performance continues to improve at larger scales (Figure
6
, Appendix
B.4
); however, the current size already achieves a strong performance baseline.

3.1.2
Neural Network Architecture and Training

We train a neural network-based scoring function that takes a word pair as input and jointly predicts similarity and relatedness scores using the synthetic dataset.

For input representation, we use GloVe 840B 300-dimensional embeddings
45
. From each embedding pair
(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2})
, we compute four interaction features
[\,\mathbf{e}_{1}{+}\mathbf{e}_{2},\;\mathbf{e}_{1}{-}\mathbf{e}_{2},\;\mathbf{e}_{1}{\odot}\mathbf{e}_{2},\;|\mathbf{e}_{1}{-}\mathbf{e}_{2}|\,]

44
;
14
. These features are concatenated with an 8-dimensional WordNet-derived feature vector and passed through a multilayer perceptron that produces a 2-D output representing the predicted similarity and relatedness scores. To encourage the model to learn a meaningful separation between the two dimensions, we apply a small regularization term penalizing the squared gap between the output scores. Full architectural details and hyperparameter settings are reported in Appendix
B.4
.

3.2.3
Atlas Construction and Consistency Analysis

For each (model, corpus) pair, we run the topic model to generate
k
= 20 topics and retain the top 10 words from each run. To obtain robust estimates, each model is run 10 times on each corpus with different random seeds, yielding a substantial number of independent topic sets across the atlas.

For each topic produced in a given run, we compute the pairwise similarity and relatedness scores over the top five words using the trained scorer and average these to obtain a single 2D metric per topic. This procedure results in, for each model, up to 60 scored topic sets (six corpora times ten runs), providing sufficient data for reliable statistical analysis. To summarize a model’s position along the similarity–relatedness spectrum, we define the
shifted normalized gap
as follows: similarity and relatedness scores are first normalized to
[0,1]
within each corpus, and the gap (relatedness minus similarity) is then shifted so that the random-word baseline sits at zero. Positive values indicate a relatedness-leaning profile relative to random, while negative values indicate a similarity-leaning profile.

To verify that each model’s similarity–relatedness profile is consistent across corpora, we compute Kendall’s coefficient of concordance
W

3.3
Downstream Tasks

The atlas analysis assigns each topic model a position in the similarity–relatedness space. We expect this metric to serve as a predictor of downstream performance on tasks with different semantic requirements. To test this hypothesis, we evaluate on three downstream tasks, two favoring relatedness-inclined models and one favoring similarity-inclined models; the reasoning behind each task’s expected alignment is detailed below. Not all atlas models appear in every task due to implementation constraints; model coverage is noted per task.

3.3.1
Task A: Event
Monitoring (Reuters)

The first task evaluates topic models on event monitoring using the Reuters corpus
33
, a multi-label collection of 90 thematic categories where each document may be assigned to one or more of them. For each category in the training set, we embed all associated documents as topic vectors and compute a centroid to represent that category. At test time, each document is similarly embedded and ranked against all category centroids. Performance is measured by Hit@3 (whether at least one of the top-3 predicted categories matches a true label) and Recall@3, the fraction of true labels recovered by the top-3 predictions. Since event monitoring relies on recognizing which words co-occur to form coherent themes, it favors models that capture this co-occurrence structure; such models tend to produce topics with higher relatedness, a signal directly quantified by our metric.

3.3.2
Task B: Category Retrieval (Reuters)

The second task also uses the Reuters corpus but evaluates category retrieval via nearest-neighbor consistency. Each document is embedded as a topic vector, and for every document, we retrieve its 10 nearest neighbors in topic space, checking whether their category labels match the query document’s label. Performance is measured by the same-category rate (SCR), the fraction of the 10 nearest neighbors that share at least one category label with the query document. By the same reasoning as Task A, we expect relatedness-inclined models to perform better.

3.3.3
Task C: Synonym Pair Retrieval Consistency

The third task is designed to favor similarity-inclined models. We manually curate 50 strict synonym pairs (e.g.,
increase
/
rise
,
decline
/
fall
,
company
/
firm
); and, for each word in a pair, compute the conditional probability
P(w|d)
of word
w
given document
d
, marginalized over topics using the model’s native topic–word distribution. We then select the top-10 highest-scoring documents for each word and measure the overlap between the two ranked lists using Jaccard similarity. The underlying intuition is that synonyms, being semantically interchangeable, should induce similar document rankings under a model that captures semantic similarity.

Because this task does not require ground-truth labels, we evaluate it across all six corpora, using cross-corpus consistency as evidence of validity. Since the task requires access to the native topic–word distribution, which is difficult to extract uniformly across all architectures, we include only a representative subset of models spanning the major model families; per-corpus performance and model coverage are reported in Appendix
A.4
.

3.3.4
Regression Analysis

To test whether our similarity–relatedness metric predicts downstream performance, we conduct a series of single-factor linear regressions for each task, regressing task performance on each predictor individually: the model’s similarity score, relatedness score, the gap between them, as well as
C_{V}
coherence
50
and topic diversity—two widely adopted intrinsic metrics for evaluating topic model quality. For Task C, which is evaluated across multiple corpora, we introduce a corpus-level intercept to account for baseline variation while fitting a single shared slope across all corpora. We report statistical significance for each predictor.

To optimize the configuration of the neural scorer, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study evaluating key component choices: the static word embedding space, the input representation, the inclusion of WordNet features, and the network architecture. Because the scorer is designed to evaluate topic words generated from external corpora, its ability to generalize to entirely unseen vocabulary is critical. Further Analysis

5.1
Corpus Properties as Moderators

Across the atlas, we observe that document length, domain specificity, and vocabulary characteristics (Table
17
) systematically influence how models score on similarity and relatedness. Topics derived from the ACL corpus consistently exhibit elevated similarity yet comparatively low relatedness. The high similarity reflects a narrow technical vocabulary in which many core terms are near-synonymous (e.g.,
lexical
/
semantic
,
speech
/
language
), while the low relatedness arises from the corpus’s domain-specific vocabulary tail: topics frequently contain acronyms, proper names, and fragment-like tokens that compress relatedness scores.

Moreover, 20 Newsgroups yields the least consistent cross-model rankings, driven by two vocabulary-induced forces that pull similarity and relatedness in opposite directions. First, because it consists of informal discussions, 20 Newsgroups contains frequent unnormalized contractions and discourse tokens (e.g.,
dont
in 21.3% of documents and
doesnt
in 7.7%) that are essentially absent from the other corpora. These tokens form tight style clusters that our scorer can assign high similarity yet low relatedness (e.g.,
dont
/
doesnt
/
isnt
/
wouldnt
/
wasnt
), and co-occurrence models surface them far more often than embedding-based models: contraction-bearing topics comprise 26% of LDA topics but only 5% of BERT-KT topics on 20 Newsgroups. Second, outside this conversational layer, 20 Newsgroups topics are dominated by plain, everyday vocabulary that lacks the intricate, specialised expressions found in edited-text corpora; unlike ACL, where morphological families and near-synonyms abound (e.g.,
parse
/
parsing
/
parser
), 20 Newsgroups domains are discussed with common words that are thematically associated but rarely substitutable, so similarity stays depressed while PLM-augmented models push relatedness sharply upward. These competing forces make similarity, relatedness, and gap rankings on 20 Newsgroups markedly less stable than on the edited-text corpora. This further suggests that for noisy, informal corpora of this kind, PLM-augmented models may be preferable, as they inject stronger semantic priors that yield more meaningful topic clusters—a consideration particularly relevant for the design of VAE-based models.

5.2
BERTopic: Document-Level Embeddings

Despite being a PLM-augmented model, BERTopic consistently achieves relatedness scores comparable to co-occurrence-based models while exhibiting only moderate similarity, and performs strongly on the relatedness-favoring Tasks A and B, well above other PLM-augmented models. This is because BERTopic employs Sentence-BERT
47
embeddings primarily for
sentence-level document clustering
, not direct word-level topic construction. Topic words are then extracted via c-TF-IDF, which ranks terms by their frequency within a cluster relative to their frequency across all clusters. This criterion rewards terms that recur consistently throughout a cluster and are distinctive to it, but does not explicitly encourage near-synonymy among top-ranked words. Synonyms tend to substitute for one another rather than co-occur, splitting counts across alternative surface forms and thereby reducing each form’s individual TF score. As a result, BERTopic topics remain thematically coherent yet not synonym-rich, consistent with its high relatedness but moderate similarity profile.

5.3
BERT-KT’s Inflated Similarity and ECRTM’s Low Relatedness

On the
Reuters
corpus, BERT-KT and ECRTM yield divergent metric anomalies: the former exhibits spuriously high similarity scores, while the latter demonstrates exceptionally low relatedness. Investigating these outliers reveals contrasting failure modes regarding how each model utilizes the embedding space.
BERT-KT’s inflated similarity originates from
generic-topic artifacts
. The model tends to cluster high-frequency register words, such as reporting verbs (
told
,
said
) and temporal markers, into topics that appear coherent in embedding space but remain semantically vacuous. On average, 28% of the topics generated per run consist almost entirely of such lexical terms, which receive high scores from our neural scorer in terms of similarity. This artificially inflates BERT-KT’s overall performance despite the lack of thematic substance, highlighting a notable caveat when relying solely on embedding-based similarity.
Conversely, ECRTM’s deflated relatedness stems from
rare-token clustering
. The model’s embedding clustering regularizer, which enforces topic separation via optimal transport, inadvertently biases the model toward geometric outliers. To maximize inter-topic dispersion, ECRTM groups rare, isolated entities or surnames (e.g.,
baird
,
brusca
,
crosby
) that possess distinct embeddings but lack stable document-level co-occurrence. Consequently, approximately 63% of ECRTM’s top-word pairs exhibit zero co-occurrence on
Reuters
, compared to only 4% for LDA and 7% for BERTopic. This validates the diagnostic utility of the relatedness dimension, as it successfully exposes genuine co-occurrence failures that embedding-based metrics alone would overlook.

5.4
Divergence Between Coherence and Relatedness

Coherence and relatedness both quantify associations between topic words, yet they operationalize distinct forms of association. Coherence measures corpus-conditional co-occurrence, rewarding word pairs that frequently appear together within the training corpus. Relatedness, in contrast, reflects a semantic prior induced by general-language supervision, independent of the target corpus. The two metrics may therefore diverge in specialized domains. For instance, in ECRTM on ACL, a topic containing the words
patr, ewan, calder, unification, gazdar
attains high coherence because these terms are densely coupled within a specific theoretical and citation community centered on unification grammar and the PATR framework
22
. Yet the same topic receives a low relatedness score, since a scorer trained on broad semantic relations does not encode such domain-specific author- and framework-level associations. This gap serves as a quantitative signal of corpus specificity: coherence captures internal structural regularities, while relatedness captures domain-general lexical semantics.

6
Discussion

6.1
Limitations

Vocabulary Generalizability
The synthetic dataset is constructed from samples drawn from ConceptNet
53
, WordNet
41
, and the BERT vocabulary
16
, while the static embeddings rely on GloVe’s
45
capacity to generalize to unseen word pairs. Although these resources encode broad common-sense and lexical knowledge, they tend to overestimate associations among high-frequency, generic words and perform poorly on domain-specific terminology. For instance, highly specialized terms commonly encountered in venues such as ACL may not be adequately captured. Consequently, the neural scorer should be interpreted alongside complementary metrics, such as topic coherence, which can capture corpus-internal regularities that the neural approach alone may overlook. Moreover, obtaining domain-specific training data or fine-tuning the underlying embeddings on the target domain could substantially improve the scorer’s coverage of specialised vocabulary.

Human Annotation
Our evaluation relies mostly on automated metrics and LLM-based imputation. Human annotation of topic quality and curated evaluation datasets spanning diverse specialist domains remains scarce. Direct human judgments would provide a valuable check on the patterns identified here and help establish more reliable benchmarks for interpreting metric behavior.

Multilingual Support
Our findings are constrained by the English-centric nature of both the evaluation resources and the corpora examined. Extending this framework to multilingual settings would require comparable semantic resources across languages, which remain unevenly developed. However, recent advances in multilingual language models and text encoders
13
;
61
offer a promising foundation for such extensions in future work.

Model Coverage
The static embeddings and topic model architectures examined here represent only a subset of available approaches. Incorporating more recent embedding methods and topic models equipped with more capable encoders
62
;
12
would broaden the scope of the evaluation and may reveal additional performance patterns not captured by the current framework.

6.2
Applications

The proposed two-dimensional metric offers a diagnostic capability absent from conventional measures. By inspecting a model’s similarity–relatedness profile, practitioners can explicitly characterize the type of semantic structure being captured. For example, in VAE-based topic models augmented with pretrained embeddings, developers can track how increasing the weight of the embedding component shifts the balance toward taxonomic similarity, enabling principled architectural and hyperparameter decisions. The metric also complements existing benchmarking axes by exposing structural trade-offs that coherence or diversity alone cannot reveal. Moreover, the predictive relationship between the 2D metric and downstream performance suggests that the metric can guide practitioners in matching models to task requirements. For instance, retrieval-augmented generation pipelines
34
;
59
could leverage relatedness-inclined models for broad recall and similarity-inclined models for precise reranking.

When applied to a new corpus, the metric profile across multiple models could serve as a diagnostic of corpus-level semantic structure, revealing the specificity of its vocabulary. Such signals can inform preprocessing choices, including domain-specific stopword filtering or vocabulary normalization prior to topic modeling. Beyond topic model evaluation, the neural scorer functions as a general-purpose instrument for quantifying taxonomic similarity and thematic relatedness over arbitrary word sets, supporting tasks such as evaluating keyword extraction outputs
63
or assessing the semantic cohesion of word clusters.

7
Conclusion

This work introduces a two-dimensional evaluation framework that disentangles
taxonomic similarity
from
thematic relatedness
in topic model outputs. By training a neural scorer on a large-scale, synthetic LLM-annotated dataset, we quantify both dimensions across a comprehensive atlas of topic models and corpora, revealing that the similarity–relatedness profile is a stable, intrinsic property of each model family: co-occurrence-based models consistently incline toward relatedness, while PLM-augmented models incline toward similarity. Moreover, this distinction proves predictive of downstream performance, while established metrics such as coherence and diversity cannot replicate. These findings enhance topic model evaluation toward understanding what kind of semantic structure a model captures, opening avenues for task-aware model selection and principled tuning of embedding-augmented architectures.
