Dietary conflicts with standard clinical procedures for colonoscopy in Phenylketonuria: a case report
Authors: Martina Tosi, Anne Daly, Catherine Ashmore, Alex Pinto, Sharon Evans, Elvira Verduci, Suzanne Ford, Melanie Hill, Anne Grimsley, Anita MacDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColonoscopy is a diagnostic technique used for gastrointestinal tract pathologies that presents additional challenges when used in individuals with phenylketonuria (PKU). The dietary requirements for colonoscopy preparation conflict with a phenylalanine (Phe) restricted diet essential in classical PKU, and aspartame containing laxatives must also be avoided. This case study highlights the complexities of applying conventional colonoscopy protocols to patients with PKU and emphasis the importance of personalized preparation strategies that consider strict dietary requirements.Case presentationA 24-year-old White British woman with classical PKU, with a Phe tolerance of 250 mg/day (5 g/day natural protein) and a consistent maintenance of blood Phe < 360 µmol/L, required an urgent colonoscopy/gastroscopy for investigation of bowel cancer. In the 3 days preceding the procedure, for the first 48 h she followed a low fibre/residue diet (avoiding fruit/vegetables), followed by 24 h of clear fluids only, omitting the Phe-free amino acid supplements. On the evening before the procedure, the first dose of a bowel preparation solution (Plenvu®, containing Macrogol 3350 but aspartame-free) was taken. Immediately, she was lightheaded, had a headache with extreme fatigue. Three hours later, she took the second dose of Plenvu®, containing aspartame (0.88 g/493 mg Phe). Her headache became intense, she felt exhausted and was unaware of her surroundings. It was unclear if the symptoms were caused by side effects associated with the bowel preparation solution or probable high Phe levels following 3 days of a very low energy diet with cessation of protein substitute, together with a high Phe intake associated with the aspartame load.ConclusionAwareness of potential dietary conflicts with standard procedures, together with effective communication within and between healthcare teams, is essential to ensure patient-centered care. Developing practical guidelines and specific warnings could significantly support both patients and healthcare providers.Keywords: Phenylketonuria, Aspartame, Colonoscopy, Laxatives, Case report

Introduction

Phenylketonuria (PKU, OMIM 261600) is an inherited metabolic disorder (IMD) of amino acid metabolism, characterized by autosomal recessive inheritance, identified by newborn screening programs. It is caused by pathogenic variants in the gene encoding phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH), impairing enzyme function so that it cannot metabolize phenylalanine (Phe) to tyrosine [1]. To avoid neurological deficits, classical PKU is treated with a life-long Phe restricted diet. It requires avoidance of high protein foods, such as meat, eggs, fish, bread, unrestricted use of special low protein foods, fruits and some vegetables and a Phe-free protein substitute derived from Phe-free L-amino acids or low-Phe glycomacropeptide [2]. It necessitates careful navigation, and compromise in any life situation that may alter the ability to adhere to the usual dietary restrictions.

As adults with PKU age, the type and complexity of care changes associated with increasing co-existent and chronic disorders. They may develop symptoms of other disorders that require rapid diagnosis and involvement of hospital specialty teams who may have little knowledge of PKU. Diagnostic techniques for non-PKU conditions can complicate PKU management or conflict with a Phe restricted diet but this is rarely considered or reported in PKU.

A diagnostic technique used for gastrointestinal tract pathologies, particularly colorectal cancer that may be challenging to successfully implement in PKU is colonoscopy [3]. This is due to opposing dietary restrictions and aspartame containing laxatives that may be used in bowel preparation. The quality of a colonoscopy is entirely dependent on adequate bowel cleansing, which can affect diagnostic accuracy and the rate of malignancy detection [4]. Inadequate bowel preparation leads to decreased sensitivity to colonoscopy, increased procedural time, a higher risk of adverse events, and a greater likelihood of having to repeat the examination [4]. Prior to a colonoscopy, a low fibre/low residue diet or clear liquids are usually advised in the preceding days. The terms low fibre or low residue diet are commonly used interchangeably by researchers [3]. Foods high in fibre, such as fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, are avoided to achieve a low-fibre intake [5]. Coloured liquids are discouraged because they can obscure mucosal visualization. Water, black tea or coffee, or ice are considered the best clear liquid choices. Laxatives are used to clean the bowel pre-colonoscopy [3], but their choice should depend on the patient’s health status, tolerance, and hospital specific protocols.

This is the first reported case detailing the experience of an adult with PKU undergoing colonoscopy preparation, emphasizing the unique dietary and metabolic challenges encountered.

Case presentation

The case study is a 24-year-old White British woman with classical PKU, diagnosed by newborn screening (NBS). She has had excellent life-long blood Phe control, aiming to maintain blood Phe levels < 360 µmol/L. She was sapropterin unresponsive, tolerated 5 g/day natural protein (250 mg/day Phe) supplemented with 55 g/day protein equivalent (P.Eq.) from Phe-free L-amino acids (protein substitue) taken 3 times daily (PKU Cooler® – Vitaflo®). She had a body mass index of 19.3 kg/m2 at the time of procedure.

She reported a 12-month history of chronic gastrointestinal symptoms including abdominal pain and diarrhoea. Following detection of blood in the stools, she had a positive faecal immunochemical test and was referred for an urgent investigation (colonoscopy plus gastroscopy) for bowel cancer investigation. During the 72 h preceding the procedure, she adhered to the instructions provided by the hospital radiology department. Specifically, she followed a low fibre/residue diet for the first 48 h (avoiding all fruits and vegetables), transitioning to clear fluids only for the final 24 h before the procedure. She stopped her protein substitute for 48 h before the colonoscopy. Details of the dietary instructions are outlined in Table 1.

On day-1, she began the first 500 ml dose of the 1-L bowel preparation solution, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based bowel preparation agent NER1006 (Plenvu®; Norgine, Harefield, UK) [6]. Plenvu® is a powder given as an oral solution laxative, designed to cleanse the bowels prior to a colonoscopy. It contained polyethylene glycol, a polymer commonly referred to as Macrogol 3350 (3350 refers to the average molecular weight of the polyethylene glycol). It was administered in two separate doses, with the second dose provided in two separate sachets. The ingredients, with their amounts are listed in Table 2.

After consuming 90% of dose 1 at 6.00 p.m., the case study developed immediate lightheadedness, headache, extreme fatigue, and drowsiness. At 9.00 p.m., she took the second dose of Plenvu®. She only managed 70% of dose 2, which contained approximately 345 mg of Phe. She developed an intense headache, severe exhaustion, became disoriented, losing awareness of her surroundings, experienced an increased gag reflex and tremors. Emergency services were contacted. No significant bowel movements were observed, her bowel was not emptied, and the next day colonoscopy was unsuccessful. No blood Phe spots were taken during the procedure.

As an alternative procedure, the case study was offered computed tomography (CT) colonography. She was given CitraFleet, an aspartame-free sodium picosulfate bowel preparation medication, and was instructed to continue following a low fibre/residue diet for breakfast and midday meal the day before the procedure only. She consumed a rice-based breakfast cereal, almond milk, white gluten free bread and white rice. She maintained a high intake of fluids (water or sugary drinks) for the remainder of the day. Discussion

This case study of a young adult with classical PKU highlights the difficulty of achieving a successful colonoscopy outcome when standard procedures are applied without consideration of the medical needs of the disorder. This procedure was not well tolerated and led to distressing side effects, and probable but unrecorded poor metabolic control. It involved a higher level of dietary restriction over 3 days, some fasting and cessation of protein substitute. She was inadvertently given a high dose of Phe from her bowel cleansing laxative. This is a concern, as in the future, it is highly likely that colonoscopy will be increasingly used in adults with PKU as colorectal cancer is the third leading cause of cancer death for both men and women in the general population. In the UK, all adults aged 50–74 years are requested to undertake bowel cancer screening every 2 years and positive stool-based screening tests require follow-up with colonoscopy [7]. Inadequate colon preparation is not uncommon and is reported in up to 18–35% of patients undergoing colonoscopy [8].

Although there are disease specific bowel cleansing regimens for chronic diseases such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease or cardiovascular disease [4, 9–11], there are no agreed guidelines for PKU. Endoscopic centres may prescribe the same regimen, irrespective of patient characteristics. For any individual undergoing this procedure, it is important to take into consideration their current medical condition, treatment needs, patient medical history, medications, and, when available, the adequacy of bowel preparation reported from prior colonoscopies [12]. There are updated (2019) European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) Guidelines on bowel cleansing for colonoscopy [3] that have studied the colonoscopy preparation in detail, and their recommendations could have been adapted to better meet the needs of this patient with PKU.

This case study was advised to commence a low fibre/residue diet 72 h prior to the colonoscopy, even though the foods permitted were incompatible with the prescribed Phe-restricted diet. She was not given guidance on the maximum amount of fibre that could be consumed, although a low fibre diet is usually considered < 10 g/day [3]. The low fibre/residue diet reduced both her food and energy intake, leading to probable catabolism, and thereby impacting metabolic control. Current colonoscopy guidelines by the ESGE (2019) suggest a 1-day low fibre/residue diet prior to colonoscopy with moderate evidence [3], but there is no substantial data for extending the timing of diet beyond one day. A randomized, controlled trial showed that a bowel preparation protocol based on a 3-day low fibre/residue diet associated with a standard 4-L polyethylene glycol split-dose was not superior to a 1-day low fibre/residue diet with the same laxative bowel preparation (91.7 versus 94.7%) [13]. A similar study but using 2-L split-dose of polyethylene glycol plus ascorbic acid found similar results [14]. In addition, multi-day restrictive dietary instructions cause patient confusion and do not provide significant improvement in bowel preparation quality or procedure time [5]. Therefore, prescribing a low fibre/residue diet for more than one day appeared unnecessary and if our case study had followed such a regimen for 24 h this would have minimized any adverse impact on dietary intake and metabolic control and made the bowel preparation regimen more tolerable.

Traditionally, patients are instructed to ingest only clear liquids the day before the colonoscopy. This was problematic for our case study. She had already had 48 h following a discordant low energy diet but then stopped her Phe-free L-amino acid-based protein substitute which is critical for the metabolic control of her condition [1]. It is highly likely that this procedure would have led to deterioration in her blood Phe control, although no blood Phe levels were available. Three meta-analyses have found that when comparing a clear liquid diet with a low residue/fibre diet, that the latter was associated with better tolerability. No differences between groups were found in terms of adequate bowel preparation or adverse effects [15–17]. Therefore, it appears there was little benefit to our case study following a clear fluid diet one day before the colonoscopy procedure when 24 h of a low fibre diet with her Phe-free L-amino acid protein substitute should have had the same effect.

The case study was given a 1 L (split dose) polyethylene glycol (PEG) laxative (Plenvu®) for bowel cleaning. The second dose contained 880 mg aspartame (containing 493 mg Phe), even though this preparation is contraindicated in PKU. Although it was recognized that the PEG laxative contained aspartame, she was advised by her clinical team to continue with this medication as the procedure was being conducted as an emergency and it was unlikely to be problematic. Following the first aspartame-free dose she developed lightheadedness, headache, extreme fatigue and after the second aspartame containing dose became disorientated and unaware of her surroundings. It is unclear if it was the impact of the laxative or the high dose of Phe from aspartame that caused the symptoms. In a controlled study in adults with PKU, short-term elevations in blood Phe were associated with measurable neuroanatomical changes, including reduced cortical thickness and increased white‑matter volume. High blood Phe may also cause headaches, tiredness, high anxiety, and emotional lability [18, 19]. However, the prescribed laxative is also associated with a range of side effects, including dehydration, nausea, and vomiting, headache or migraine, fatigue, increased heart rate, palpitations, sleepiness, electrolyte imbalances, and generalized weakness, and all cleansing regimens may cause dehydration and potential electrolyte imbalances. Some widely used medications, including antibiotics, are sweetened with aspartame, however, the amount of phenylalanine is usually only 1–25 mg per dose, so it is unusual to identify a preparation with such a high concentration of Phe. The prescribed second dose of Plenvu® contained almost 2 times her daily phenylalanine allowance. Although the manufacturers of Plenvu® indicate that this preparation is unsuitable for PKU and it is contraindicated in the ESGE updated 2019 Guideline for PKU, there is low awareness amongst adult metabolic and non PKU medical specialists about the Phe content of this laxative. One and two litre PEG + ascorbate laxatives (containing aspartame) should be avoided in PKU. The bowel preparation procedure for this case study could have been individualized. This would have improved tolerability, enhanced treatment compatibility with a Phe-restrictive diet, lessened side-effects, and lowered the chances of inadequate colon preparation. For all patients with chronic disorders, an accurate characterization of procedural and patient variables would lead to an increased probability of better outcome. The following are recommended for PKU and bowel cleaning:

The multi-disciplinary teams (i.e. gastroenterology/surgical, radiology and IMD teams) should discuss each patient with PKU prior to the procedure to agree the protocol/procedures, including a plan for blood Phe monitoring.

Limitations

Unfortunately, for this case study, there was no information about blood Phe control as the patient did not collect blood spots before, during or immediately after the procedure. Moreover, we do not have details about the actual food or energy intake during the bowel preparation phase.

Conclusion

There is a pressing need for additional research and case studies on adults with PKU undergoing clinical procedures, to ensure these are compatible with PKU management, avoiding adverse outcomes and discomfort for patients. Developing practical guidelines and specific warnings could significantly support both patients and healthcare providers. Furthermore, effective communication among medical, surgical, nursing and dietetic teams is essential to ensure patient-centered care, in the paediatric and adult settings. Healthcare staff have a responsibility to prioritize the well-being of patients with IMDs, proposing effective, economical, and safe solutions, especially as patients face increased medical interventions in adulthood.
