Spherical-tensor description of the Jahn–Teller–Hubbard molecule and local electron–phonon entanglement
Koichiro Takahashi, Shuichiro Ebata, Naotaka Yoshinaga, Shintaro Hoshino

Abstract
We investigate the localized-electron character of the Mott-insulating phase in A 3 C 60 using a single-site multiorbital electron model coupled to anisotropic molecular vibrations (Jahn–Teller phonons). We apply the spherical-tensor formalism, a framework originally developed in nuclear physics, to analyze the electron–phonon-coupled ground-state multiplet. Focusing on multipole moments, we find that both the conventional electronic quadrupole moment and the lattice displacement associated with the molecular vibrations vanish, even though the degenerate ground-state multiplet implies the presence of quadrupolar degrees of freedom. By analyzing these degrees of freedom within the spherical-tensor framework, we introduce composite (two-body) quadrupole operators involving both electrons and phonons and study their parameter dependence numerically. Furthermore, using quasispin selection rules, we demonstrate that the composite quadrupole does not couple to either the conventional quadrupole or lattice-displacement operators, thereby distinguishing it fundamentally from standard quadrupolar degrees of freedom. In addition, we investigate the nature of the electron–phonon entanglement and characterize it from the viewpoint of angular momentum. Analysis of the entanglement spectrum reveals that the ground state consists of superpositions of multi-phonon states with angular momenta L ph = 2 L_{\rm ph}=2 and L ph = 3 L_{\rm ph}=3 , formed through coupling to three-electron states with L = 1 L=1 and L = 2 L=2 .



I
Introduction

The multiorbital nature of strongly correlated electron systems in solids and molecules generates rich physical phenomena.
Such systems range from inorganic materials with
d
- and
f
-electrons to organic conductors with molecular orbitals.
In this paper, we focus on the alkali-doped fulleride A
3
C
60
, where
the triply degenerate
t_{1u}
orbital for each fullerene molecule is occupied by three electrons.
This material exhibits high-temperature superconductivity of
T_{\mathrm{c}}\sim 40
K,
and becomes a Mott insulator as the intermolecular distance increases
Gunnarsson 1997
;
Capone et al. 2009
;
Takabayashi and Prassides 2016
;
Nomura et al. 2016
.

A striking feature of fulleride systems, compared with transition-metal-based multiorbital systems, is the nature of the Hund’s coupling: the ferromagnetic Hund’s coupling originating from the Coulomb interaction is small in fullerides due to the extended nature of the molecular orbital, and its sign is inverted by the coupling to molecular vibrations
to become antiferromagnetic Hund’s coupling
Fabrizio and Tosatti 1997
.
As a result, an unconventional type of orbital order is identified, in which the ordinary orbital order parameter is zero and the doublon orbital moment is regarded as a relevant order parameter
Hoshino and Werner 2017
;
Hoshino et al. 2019
.
This anomalous metallic state is interpreted as a spontaneous orbital selective Mott state, and provides a candidate scenario for the experimentally observed Jahn–Teller metallic phase
Zadik et al. 2015
.
The vanishing of the ordinary orbital moment persists in the ordered states of the Mott-insulating phase
Ishigaki et al. 2019
;
Iwazaki and Hoshino 2021
.

Although previous theoretical studies have revealed characteristic behaviors arising from antiferromagnetic Hund’s coupling in a purely electron model, such effective descriptions require justification through comparison with full electron–phonon models.
We refer to this electron–phonon coupled model as the “Jahn–Teller–Hubbard” model
Kaga et al. 2022
, which can be viewed as a multiorbital extension of the Holstein–Hubbard model
von der Linden et al. 1995
.
Phonons have been treated explicitly in earlier studies of superconductivity
Han et al. 2000
;
Han et al. 2003
;
Yamazaki and Kuramoto 2014
;
Nomura and Arita 2015
;
Kaga et al. 2022
;
Ishida et al. 2025
;
Okada et al. 2026a
;
Okada et al. 2026b
as well as in investigations of single C
60
ions
Auerbach et al. 1994
;
Manini et al. 1994
;
Gunnarsson 1995
;
Gunnarsson et al. 1995
;
Liu et al. 2018a
;
Matsuda et al. 2018
.
Across these works, either Cartesian-coordinate or spherical-tensor representations have been employed, with the choice varying from study to study, which complicates direct comparison.
It is therefore desirable to clarify the correspondence between these two representations.
Furthermore, in the context of the unconventional orbital orders discussed above
Hoshino and Werner 2017
;
Ishigaki et al. 2019
;
Werner et al. 2017
;
Iwazaki and Hoshino 2021
, it is essential to examine the phonon properties associated with distortions of the fullerene cage, as they play a crucial role in experimentally characterizing unconventional ordered states.

In this paper, we consider a localized-electron model for the Mott-insulator phase coupled to anisotropic (Jahn–Teller) phonons.
While intersite interactions lead to various ordered states, as demonstrated for the multiorbital Hubbard model with antiferromagnetic Hund’s coupling in Refs.
Ishigaki et al. 2019
;
Iwazaki and Hoshino 2021
, here we focus on the single-molecule limit (Sec.
II
) as a first step toward understanding electron–phonon–entangled Mott insulators.
Even in this simplified setting, the problem remains nontrivial because the Hilbert space in the molecular limit is infinite due to the phonon degrees of freedom.

To clarify the electron–phonon properties associated with unconventional orbital ordering, we introduce unconventional quadrupole tensors constructed from composite two-body operators of the form
c^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}cc
, rather than the standard one-body
c^{\dagger}c
operators (where
c
and
c^{\dagger}
denote electron annihilation and creation operators, respectively).
We further examine how these composite quadrupoles couple to the standard quadrupole moments and lattice distortions using symmetry-based selection rules.
In addition, we investigate the nature of the entanglement induced by the electron–phonon coupling.
By organizing the analysis from the viewpoint of angular momentum using the spherical-tensor formalism, the behavior of the reduced density matrix and the entanglement spectrum can be understood transparently.

To address this problem, we begin by discussing two types of representations: Cartesian tensors (Sec.
III
) and spherical tensors (Sec.
IV
).
The former is more intuitive and is naturally suited for applications in solid-state systems, whereas the latter, widely used in atomic and nuclear physics, provides a powerful and systematic framework for many-body calculations
Condon and Shortley 1935
;
Rose 1957
.
Since the
t_{1u}
system inherits an approximate spherical symmetry in the single-molecule limit, it is closely analogous to nuclear systems, in which fermions interact with bosons associated with collective excitations
Iachello and Arima 1987
;
Talmi 1993
.

A nucleus is an isolated quantum many-body system composed of nucleons in which the angular momentum is a good quantum number.
The spherical-tensor description provides a powerful
theoretical
framework to construct operators that preserve
rotational symmetry, enabling the derivation of selection rules and the evaluation of reduced matrix elements.
Historically, the coupling between collective and intrinsic nucleon motions has been studied since the early days of nuclear physics, beginning with investigations of nuclear fission phenomena
Bohr and Kalckar 1937
and the development of the Bohr–Mottelson collective model
Bohr and Mottelson 1953
.
This description was later systematized in an algebraic
framework by de Shalit
de Shalit 1961
, leading to particle–boson coupling schemes such as the interacting boson–fermion model (IBFM)
Arima and Iachello 1976a
, as well as modern shell-model (SM) approaches
Mayer 1949
;
Haxel et al. 1949

with explicit phonon degrees of freedom
Otsuka et al. 1978
;
Yoshinaga et al. 2024
.

\displaystyle\bar{\uparrow}=\downarrow

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c_{\gamma\sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{\sigma}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}},

\displaystyle D_{\gamma}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{2}\sum_{\eta=1,3,4,6,8}:Q_{\eta}^{2}:,

We refer to this system as a “Jahn–Teller–Hubbard molecule,” in analogy with the single-orbital Hubbard model in its atomic limit, often called the “Hubbard atom.”
In actual solids, intermolecular hopping is also present; however, in this paper we focus on the localized-electron limit of the model in the strong-interaction regime.

|by\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},b,\gamma=y,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\\[2.84526pt]
|bz\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},b,\gamma=z,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\\[2.84526pt]
\end{matrix}\ \ \right\}\ \ \ (L=1),

(24)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{matrix}|ax\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},a,\gamma=x,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\\[2.84526pt]
|ay\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},a,\gamma=y,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\\[2.84526pt]
|az\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},a,\gamma=z,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\\[2.84526pt]
|+\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},\tau=+,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\\[2.84526pt]
|-\rangle&=&|\tfrac{1}{2},\tau=-,\sigma=\uparrow\rangle\end{matrix}\ \ \right\}\ \ \ (L=2),

where we have introduced a short-hand notation as shown in the left-hand side.
As for phonons,
their basis functions are trivial: the eigenstates of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{p}}
are characterized by the boson numbers
n_{\eta}=a_{\eta}^{\dagger}a_{\eta}
for
\eta=1,3,4,6,8
.

III.2
Composite quadrupole

Figure 1:
Schematic picture of the
|bx\rangle=|\tfrac{1}{2},b,x,\uparrow\rangle
state given in Eq. (
20
). The right-hand side shows the mean occupation number for each orbital regardless of the spin, where the three orbitals are equally occupied in average.

We now discuss the degrees of freedom within the
|b\gamma\rangle
(
L=1
) subspace defined in Eq. (
24
), which becomes the ground state in the effectively antiferromagnetic Hund’s coupling
J\to J_{\rm eff}<0
[see the energies written below Eq. (
22
)].
Because this subspace is labeled by a single orbital index
\gamma=x,y,z
, it is expected to exhibit properties analogous to those of a
p
orbital.
However, the standard quadrupole operator is inactive
Anderson et al. 1992
;
Ceulemans 1994
;
Chancey and O’Brien 1997
;
Hoshino and Werner 2017
.
An intuitive explanation for the disappearance of the standard orbital moment in the
|b\gamma\rangle
subspace is schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Focusing on the
|bx\rangle
state, which exhibits anisotropy oriented along the
x
direction, the occupation number of each orbital is given by

\displaystyle\langle bx|\sum_{\sigma}c_{\gamma\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\gamma\sigma}|bx\rangle=1

(25)

for
\gamma=x,y,z
.
Thus, no imbalance in the single-particle orbital occupations occurs, even though the wave function
|bx\rangle
is anisotropic, as shown in Fig.
1
.
By contrast, the doublon occupation numbers clearly capture the orbital imbalance:

\displaystyle\langle bx|D_{x}^{\dagger}D_{x}|bx\rangle=0,

(26)

\displaystyle\langle bx|D_{y}^{\dagger}D_{y}|bx\rangle=\langle bx|D_{z}^{\dagger}D_{z}|bx\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}.

This indicates that doublon-based quadrupolar degrees of freedom are active in the
L=1
subspace
Iwazaki and Hoshino 2021
.
Although this picture is most transparent in the Cartesian representation, it is less apparent
in the spherical-tensor representation discussed in Sec.
IV
; consequently, the Cartesian formulation provides a more intuitive physical interpretation.

As discussed above, the doublon captures orbital-symmetry breaking, which motivates the construction of doublon-based quadrupolar degrees of freedom.
A naive generic form of an active quadrupole operator may be written as

\displaystyle Q_{\eta}^{\rm C\,(tentative)}=\sum_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}D_{\gamma}^{\dagger}\lambda^{\eta}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}D_{\gamma^{\prime}},

(27)

which is analogous to Eq. (
7
).
Here, the superscript “C” denotes the composite nature of the operator.
The operator
Q_{\eta}^{\rm C\,(tentative)}
indeed captures the orbital imbalance of the
L=1
state, and its
\eta=3,8
components were discussed previously without explicit inclusion of phonon degrees of freedom
Hoshino and Werner 2017
.
However, this naively defined operator does not transform properly as a spherical tensor.
For example, the norm of the matrix elements

\langle b\gamma|Q_{\eta}^{\rm C\,(tentative)}|b\gamma^{\prime}\rangle

depends on
\eta
, whereas that of a standard quadrupole operator is independent of
\eta
under spherical symmetry.

The above observations lead to two questions:
(i) What is the proper definition of a composite quadrupole operator that behaves as a spherical tensor in the
L=1
subspace?
(ii) How do the phonon degrees of freedom behave in relation to the electronic composite quadrupole?
The second question will be addressed in the next section (Sec.
IV
).
Here, we focus on the first question (i) and construct a composite quadrupole operator that satisfies the required tensorial properties.
Specifically, we define the desired quadrupole operator as

\displaystyle Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}=\sum_{\mu\nu}\lambda_{\mu\nu}^{\eta}:L_{\mu}L_{\nu}:,

(28)

where
\bm{L}
is defined in Eq. (
23
).
This form of composite operator is inspired by the following identity for the Gell-Mann matrices:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}^{\eta}=-\sum_{\mu\nu}\lambda^{\eta}_{\mu\nu}\hat{\ell}^{\mu}\hat{\ell}^{\nu}

(29)

for
\eta=1,3,4,6,8
.
Namely, the combination of angular-momentum matrices (
\hat{\ell}^{\mu}
) leads to the quadrupole (
\hat{\lambda}^{\eta}
).
The operator
\bm{L}
is constructed from a bilinear fermionic form,
c^{\dagger}c
, and therefore
Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}
is a two-body operator.
Nonzero matrix elements of
\bm{L}
in the
L=1
subspace imply that
Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}
is also finite in this subspace.
Indeed, by introducing the projection operator
P_{L}
onto the electronic
L
multiplet [see Eq. (
24
)], we obtain

\displaystyle P_{L=1}Q_{\eta}^{\rm C}P_{L=1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\left|\tfrac{1}{2},b,\gamma,\sigma\right\rangle\lambda^{\eta}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2},b,\gamma^{\prime},\sigma\right|,

(30)

which is the desired property as a spherical tensor, since it has the same structure as Eq. (
7
).

We can also introduce another form of two-body quadrupoles by using Eq. (
125
):

\displaystyle\overline{Q}^{\rm C}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\,\overline{Q_{8}Q_{1}}+\frac{3}{2}\,\overline{Q_{4}Q_{6}},

(31)

\displaystyle\overline{Q}^{\rm C}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(-\,\overline{Q_{8}Q_{4}}+\sqrt{3}\,\overline{Q_{3}Q_{4}})+\frac{3}{2}\,\overline{Q_{1}Q_{6}},

\displaystyle\overline{Q}^{\rm C}_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(-\,\overline{Q_{8}Q_{6}}-\sqrt{3}\,\overline{Q_{3}Q_{6}})+\frac{3}{2}\,\overline{Q_{1}Q_{4}},

\displaystyle\overline{Q}^{\rm C}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\,\overline{Q_{8}Q_{3}}+\frac{3}{4}(Q_{4}^{2}-Q_{6}^{2}),

\displaystyle\overline{Q}^{\rm C}_{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\quantity[ Q_1^2+Q_3^2- Q_8^2 - \frac 1 2 (Q_4^2 + Q_6^2) ],

where
\overline{AB}=(AB+BA)/2!
is the symmetrized product.
This expression yields

\displaystyle P_{L=1}\overline{Q}^{\rm C}_{\eta}P_{L=1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{7}{4}P_{L=1}Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}P_{L=1}.

(32)

Namely, there are two ways to define the composite (two-body) quadrupole operators [Eqs. (
28
,
31
)], and, once projected onto the
L=1
subspace, they reduce to the same expression up to a constant factor as in Eq. (
32
).

In this way, the electric quadrupole necessitates consideration of composite operators within the
L=1
multiplet, whereas the magnetic degrees of freedom, whose lowest-order contributions are angular momentum (magnetic dipole) represented by one-body operators, have nonzero matrix elements within this restricted Hilbert space.
Although composite quadrupoles involving phonons can be constructed in a manner similar to the formulation above, we instead employ the spherical tensor representation, since its compatibility with the quasispin formulation (see Sec.
IV
) is particularly convenient for interpreting the results.

We remark that, while the composite quadrupole considered here arises in the context of fulleride materials, composite physical quantities have also been studied in condensed matter physics as order parameters associated with symmetry breaking
Andreev and Grishchuk 1984
;
Emery and Kivelson 1992
;
Balatsky and Bonča 1993
;
Coleman et al. 1995
;
Nourafkan and Nafari 2008
;
Hoshino et al. 2011
;
Hoshino and Kuramoto 2014
;
Fernandes et al. 2019
;
Geilhufe 2024
;
Miki et al. 2026
;
Kuniyoshi et al. 2026
.
In nuclear physics, similar concepts have mainly been discussed in terms of intrinsic collective degrees of freedom and excitation modes
Teng et al. 2025
, due to the limited controllability of external fields.

IV
Spherical tensor representations

While the Cartesian representations used in the previous sections are intuitive, a systematic formulation is more naturally achieved using tensorial algebra based on the Wigner–Eckart theorem.
We first introduce the spherical basis states (Sec.
IV.1
),
which are followed by the formulation of operators (Secs.
IV.2
–
IV.5
).
We can construct the analytic solution for the case with one boson number as explained in Sec.
IV.6
.

IV.1
Relation between spherical basis and Cartesian basis

The eigenstate of
\mathscr{H}_{\rm ee}
is denoted as
|LM\rangle

(labeled by eigenvalues of
\bm{L}^{2}
and
L_{z}
). Namely, we write the eigenequation

\displaystyle\big(\mathscr{H}_{\rm ee}+2J\big)\ |LM\rangle=E_{F}(L)\ |LM\rangle,

(33)

where the eigenenergy
E_{F}(L)
is given by

\displaystyle E_{F}(L=0)

\displaystyle=-3J,

(34)

\displaystyle E_{F}(L=1)

\displaystyle=2J,

\displaystyle E_{F}(L=2)

\displaystyle=0.

Note that the subscript
F
emphasizes the energy of the fermionic systems, and we have shifted the energy by
2J
to make the analytic equation simple [cf. Eq. (
61
)].
The wave function
|LM\rangle
is related to the previously introduced Cartesian basis representation [see Eq. (
24
)] as

\displaystyle|1,\pm 1\rangle

\displaystyle=\mp\quantity( |bx\ra\pm\imu|by\ra)/\sqrt{2},

(35)

\displaystyle|1,0\rangle

\displaystyle=|bz\rangle,

for
L=1
, and

\displaystyle|2,\pm 2\rangle

\displaystyle=\quantity(\omega|+\ra- \omega^* |-\ra\mp\sqrt 2 |az\ra)/2,

(36)

\displaystyle|2,\pm 1\rangle

\displaystyle=\quantity( |ax\ra\mp\imu|ay\ra)/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle|2,0\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\quantity( \omega|+\ra+ \omega^* |-\ra)/\sqrt{2},

for
L=2
.
Note that we have focused on the total spin
S=1/2
sector which couples to Jahn-Teller phonons, and have not
considered the
S=3/2
state (
L=0
) whose wave function has been given in Eq. (
18
).

IV.2
Rank-1 electron tensors

Next we consider the tensorial representation of operators.
The
p
-fermion creation operators are defined by

\displaystyle p_{\pm 1,\sigma}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mp\quantity( c_{x\sg}^\dg\pm\imu c_{y\sg}^\dg)/\sqrt{2},

(37)

\displaystyle p_{0,\sigma}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=c_{z\sigma}^{\dagger},

which defines the unitary transformation
c^{\dagger}_{\gamma\sigma}\to p^{\dagger}_{m\sigma}
,
satisfying standard anticommutation relation.
Here, the label ‘
p
’ is used to emphasize the character of the angular momentum
\ell=1
(called
p
-fermion).
It is also worth noting that the
p
-fermions are regarded as a rank-1 spherical tensor:

\displaystyle=m\ p^{\dagger}_{m\sigma},

(38)

\displaystyle[L_{\pm},p^{\dagger}_{m\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2-m(m\pm 1)}\ p^{\dagger}_{m\pm 1,\sigma},

where
L_{\pm}=L_{x}\pm\mathrm{i}L_{y}
.
We provide the definition of generic spherical tensors in Appendix
C
.
As for the annihilation operator, since
p_{m\sigma}
itself does not behave as a spherical tensor, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{m\sigma}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1-m}(-1)^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma}p_{-m,\bar{\sigma}},

(39)

which satisfies the definitive relation of spherical tensor.
Here
\sigma={\frac{1}{2}}
for spin
\uparrow
and

\bar{\sigma}=-\sigma=-{\frac{1}{2}}
for spin
\downarrow
.

The ladder operators, which are also regarded as spherical tensors are defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\mp(L_{x}\pm\mathrm{i}L_{y})/\sqrt{2},

(40)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{0}

\displaystyle=L_{z}.

These are spherical tensors of rank 1:

\displaystyle=m\ \mathscr{L}_{m},

(41)

\displaystyle[L_{\pm},\mathscr{L}_{m}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2-m(m\pm 1)}\ \mathscr{L}_{m\pm 1}.

The matrix element is given by

\displaystyle\langle L^{\prime}M^{\prime}|\mathscr{L}_{m}|LM\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{LL^{\prime}}\sqrt{L(L+1)}\langle LM1m|LM^{\prime}\rangle,

(42)

where
\left\langle{Lm1m|LM^{\prime}}\right\rangle
are the Clebsch–Gordan (CG) coefficients and only the diagonal component with respect to
L
becomes nonzero.

IV.3
Rank-2 electron tensors

We define the spherical-basis rank-2 tensors by

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{\pm 2}

\displaystyle=(Q_{3}\pm\mathrm{i}Q_{1})/\sqrt{2},

(43)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\mp(Q_{4}\pm\mathrm{i}Q_{6})/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{0}

\displaystyle=Q_{8},

with which
\mathscr{Q}_{M}^{\dagger}=(-1)^{M}\mathscr{Q}_{-M}
is satisfied.
Equation (
43
) originates from the relation in Eq. (
129
).
The left-hand side of Eq. (
43
) is called ‘complex spherical tensor’, and the right-hand side is called ‘tesseral spherical tensor’.

The operator
\mathscr{Q}_{M}
satisfies the definitive relation of a spherical tensor:

\displaystyle=M\mathscr{Q}_{M},

(44)

\displaystyle[L_{\pm},\mathscr{Q}_{M}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{6-M(M\pm 1)}\ \mathscr{Q}_{M\pm 1}.

The matrix element of the electronic quadrupole is given by

\displaystyle\langle 1m^{\prime}|\mathscr{Q}_{M}|2m\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{10}\ \langle 2m2M|1m^{\prime}\rangle,

(45)

\displaystyle\langle 2m^{\prime}|\mathscr{Q}_{M}|1m\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{6}\ \langle 1m2M|2m^{\prime}\rangle,

and others are zero (see Eq. (
160
) for more details).
As discussed in Sec.
III.2
, the matrix elements of the standard quadrupole operator within the
L=1
subspace vanish, motivating consideration of the composite quadrupole.

IV.4
Phonon angular momentum and tensors

We define the following boson operators associated with angular momentum tensor:

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\pm 2}

\displaystyle=(a^{\dagger}_{3}\pm\mathrm{i}a^{\dagger}_{1})/\sqrt{2},

(46)

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\mp(a^{\dagger}_{4}\pm\mathrm{i}a^{\dagger}_{6})/\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{0}

\displaystyle=a_{8}^{\dagger}.

Here, the label ‘
d
’ is used to emphasize the character of the angular momentum
\ell=2
(called
d
-boson).
The
d
-bosons satisfy standard commutation relations and are regarded as a rank-2 spherical tensor:

\displaystyle=Md_{M}^{\dagger},

(47)

\displaystyle[L_{\pm}^{\rm ph},d_{M}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{6-M(M\pm 1)}\ d_{M\pm 1}^{\dagger},

where the angular momentum operator for
d
-bosons is given by
Iachello and Arima 1987

\displaystyle L^{\rm ph}_{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M}Md_{M}^{\dagger}d_{M},

(48)

\displaystyle L^{\rm ph}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M}\sqrt{6-M(M\pm 1)}\ d_{M\pm 1}^{\dagger}d_{M}.

The boson annihilation (creation) operator
d_{M}
(
d_{M}^{\dagger}
) is interpreted as removing (adding)
the magnetic quantum number
M
.
The
x
and
y
components are then also introduced
as
L^{\rm ph}_{x}=(L^{\rm ph}_{+}+L^{\rm ph}_{-})/2
and
L^{\rm ph}_{y}=(L^{\rm ph}_{+}-L^{\rm ph}_{-})/2\mathrm{i}
,
with which one can confirm that
\bm{L}^{\rm tot}=\bm{L}+\bm{L}^{\rm ph}
and
(\bm{L}^{\rm tot})^{2}
commute with the total Hamiltonian.

The electron–phonon coupling and the bare phonon Hamiltonian is then rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\rm ep}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{M}\quantity( \mathscr Q_M^\dg d_M^\dg+ \mathscr Q_M d_M ),

(49)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\sum_{M}d_{M}^{\dagger}d_{M},

(50)

where the constant term is neglected.
Since
d_{M}
annihilates a phonon carrying angular momentum projection
M
,
the tensor
\mathscr{Q}_{M}
may be viewed as an operator that carries (and thus adds) the corresponding angular momentum.
The total magnetic quantum number is thus conserved as a manifestation of the rotational symmetry, which imposes a constraint on the
Hilbert space relevant to electron–phonon coupling.

The annihilation-operator tensor is also defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{d}_{M}=(-1)^{M}d_{-M}.

(51)

Using this, we further introduce

\displaystyle x_{M}=d_{M}^{\dagger}+\tilde{d}_{M},

(52)

which corresponds to the spherical-tensor representation of the displacement operator
\phi_{\eta}
.
The electron–phonon coupling Hamiltonian now becomes

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\rm ep}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{M}\mathscr{Q}_{M}^{\dagger}x_{M}=g\sum_{M}\mathscr{Q}_{M}x_{M}^{\dagger}.

(53)

It follows from this expression that
x_{M}
acts as an operator that raises the phonon angular momentum by
M
, whereas
x_{M}^{\dagger}
lowers it by the same amount.

IV.5
Composite quadrupoles with spherical tensor

Whereas a careful definition of the composite quadrupole is required in Cartesian representation, as discussed in Sec.
III.2
, it can be introduced in a straightforward manner using spherical tensors.
We consider both the electron and phonon degrees of freedom and define
the following composite quadrupoles:

\displaystyle[\mathscr{Q}\otimes\mathscr{Q}]_{M}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M_{1}M_{2}}\langle 2M_{1}2M_{2}|2M\rangle\mathscr{Q}_{M_{1}}\mathscr{Q}_{M_{2}},

(54)

\displaystyle[x\otimes x]_{M}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M_{1}M_{2}}\langle 2M_{1}2M_{2}|2M\rangle,x_{M_{1}}x_{M_{2}},

(55)

\displaystyle[\mathscr{Q}\otimes x]_{M}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{M_{1}M_{2}}\langle 2M_{1}2M_{2}|2M\rangle(\mathscr{Q}_{M_{1}}x_{M_{2}}+x_{M_{1}}\mathscr{Q}_{M_{2}}).

(56)

Equation (
54
) is a spherical tensor version of the previously defined composite quadrupole in Eq. (
31
).
The matrix element of these operators are determined by the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients by the Wigner-Eckart theorem in Eq. (
132
):

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle 1m^{\prime}|\ [\mathscr{Q}\otimes\mathscr{Q}]^{(2)}_{M}\ |1m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=A_{QQ}\ \langle 1m2M|1m^{\prime}\rangle,

(57)

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle 1m^{\prime}|\ [x\otimes x]^{(2)}_{M}\ |1m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=A_{xx}\ \langle 1m2M|1m^{\prime}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle 1m^{\prime}|\ [\mathscr{Q}\otimes x]^{(2)}_{M}\ |1m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=A_{Qx}\ \langle 1m2M|1m^{\prime}\rangle,

where we have introduced the electron-phonon-coupled state
|L_{\rm tot},M\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle
with a total angular momentum for a general multiphonon configuration.
The coefficients
A
are the reduced matrix elements of the composite quadrupoles.
Note that the factor of
1/\sqrt{2\cdot 1+1}=1/\sqrt{3}
is absorbed into the definition of
A
.
We show the numerical results in Sec.
V.3
.

IV.6
Analytic solution for the restricted Hilbert space

We consider the restricted Hilbert space with boson number
n_{d}\leq 1
for
L_{\rm tot}=1
.
We have three categories of the states:

\displaystyle|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=|1m\rangle|0\rangle_{\rm ph},

(58)

\displaystyle|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}}\langle 2m_{1}1m_{2}|1m\rangle\ d_{m_{1}}^{\dagger}|1m_{2}\rangle|0\rangle_{\rm ph},

(59)

\displaystyle|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}}\langle 2m_{1}2m_{2}|1m\rangle\ d_{m_{1}}^{\dagger}|2m_{2}\rangle|0\rangle_{\rm ph},

(60)

for
m=0,\pm 1
, where the vacuum of phonons is defined by
d_{M}|0\rangle_{\rm ph}=0
. The spin is omitted for simplicity.
The state
|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{2}
does not hybridize with the other states through
\mathscr{H}_{\rm ep}
, and we therefore focus on
|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{1}
and
|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{3}
.
The origin of this selection rule is discussed in Sec.
VI
.
The matrix element of electron–phonon interaction Hamiltonian is given by

{}_{a}\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle m|\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{b}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2J&\sqrt{10}g\\
\sqrt{10}g&\omega_{0}\end{pmatrix},

(61)

where
a,b=1,3
.
The ground state energy at large
g
is then calculated as

\displaystyle E_{\rm gs}

\displaystyle=\frac{2J+\omega_{0}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-2J)^{2}+40g^{2}}.

(62)

The corresponding wave function is

\displaystyle|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{\rm gs}

\displaystyle=C_{1}|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{1}+C_{3}|m\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle_{3},

(63)

where

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{10}\,g}{\sqrt{(E_{\rm gs}-2J)^{2}+10g^{2}}},

(64)

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{\rm gs}-2J}{\sqrt{(E_{\rm gs}-2J)^{2}+10g^{2}}},

(65)

which satisfies the normalization
C_{1}^{2}+C_{3}^{2}=1
.

The straightforward calculation gives the reduced matrix elements for composite quadrupoles defined in Eq. (
57
) as

\displaystyle A_{QQ}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{35}\quantity( 1 - \frac{7}{10} C_3^2 ),

(66)

\displaystyle A_{xx}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{35}}{10}C_{3}^{2},

\displaystyle A_{Qx}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{14}\,C_{1}C_{3}.

The ability to explicitly evaluate these matrix elements is one of the advantages of the spherical-tensor formalism.

V
Numerical results

V.1
Calculation method

We perform numerical diagonalization of the total Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}
with a cutoff in the total phonon number
n_{\mathrm{c}}
.
We choose the basis for the three-electron states as the eigenstates of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ee}}
, listed in Eq. (
24
).
Below, we focus on the formulation with a Cartesian basis, but it can also be formulated
with spherical-tensor basis in a similar manner.
We choose the basis for the Jahn-Teller phonons as the eigenstates of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{p}}
. With these choices, we denote the electron-phonon basis as

\displaystyle|\alpha,n\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle=|\alpha\rangle\big|\{n\}\big\rangle_{\rm ph},

(67)

where
\alpha=1,2,\dots,8
labels the eigenstates of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ee}}
in Eq. (
24
) and
\{n\}=(n_{1},n_{3},n_{4},n_{6},n_{8})
labels the eigenstates of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{p}}
. We truncate the Hilbert space by imposing a total-phonon-number cutoff,

\displaystyle n_{d}\equiv\sum_{\eta}n_{\eta}\leq n_{\rm c}.

(68)

The matrix element of
\mathscr{H}

is expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle\alpha,n|\mathscr{H}|\beta,n^{\prime}\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle\alpha,n|\left(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ee}}+\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}\right)|\beta,n^{\prime}\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle.

(69)

The first and second terms in Eq. (
69
) are already diagonal in this basis. The matrix element of the first term is

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle\alpha,n|\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ee}}|\beta,n^{\prime}\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=E^{\mathrm{ee}}_{\alpha}\,\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\prod_{\eta}\delta_{n_{\eta},n^{\prime}_{\eta}},

(70)

where
E^{\mathrm{ee}}_{\alpha}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ee}}
,

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{ee}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=E_{F}(L)=\begin{cases}2J&(\alpha=1,2,3;\,L=1),\\
0&(\alpha=4,5,6,7,8;\,L=2).\end{cases}

(71)

The matrix element of the second term is

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle\alpha,n|\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{p}}|\beta,n^{\prime}\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\,n_{d}\,\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\prod_{\eta}\delta_{n_{\eta},n^{\prime}_{\eta}}.

(72)

Finally, we find that the matrix element of the third term,
due to the electron-phonon coupling
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}
, can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle\alpha,n|\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}|\beta,n^{\prime}\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=g\,\sum_{\eta}Q^{\eta}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\sqrt{n_{\eta}+1}\,\delta_{n_{\eta}+1,n^{\prime}_{\eta}}+\sqrt{n^{\prime}_{\eta}+1}\,\delta_{n_{\eta},n^{\prime}_{\eta}+1}\right),

This perspective is useful for recognizing the structure of the Hamiltonian, particularly for readers familiar with multicomponent tight-binding models commonly encountered in condensed matter theory.

We now discuss the possible relevance of the above results in the context of alkali-doped fullerides.
As shown in Refs.
Hoshino and Werner 2017
;
Iwazaki and Hoshino 2021
, the composite quadrupole order parameter
\langle Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}\rangle
emerges spontaneously at low temperatures in the strongly correlated regime, while the conventional quadrupolar order parameter
\langle Q_{\eta}\rangle
remains zero.
It creates an effective internal mean-field conjugate to
\langle Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\rm MF}

\displaystyle=-hQ_{\eta}^{\rm C},

(74)

which is expected to affect the other physical quantities.
In the linear response theory, the physical quantity
\mathscr{O}
, whose expectation value without
h
vanishes, is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{O}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathscr{O}}h+O(h^{2}),

(75)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathscr{O}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\tau\langle Q_{\eta}^{\rm C}(\tau)\mathscr{O}\rangle_{0},

(76)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature,
A(\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{\tau\mathscr{H}}A\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathscr{H}}
is the Heisenberg representation with imaginary time, and

\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm Tr\,}(\cdots)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\mathscr{H}+\mathscr{H}_{\rm MF})}}{{\rm Tr\,}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\mathscr{H}+\mathscr{H}_{\rm MF})}}

(77)

indicates the quantum-statistical average in the presence of the mean-field
h
.
Using the matrix representation, one obtains

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathscr{O}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{nm}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{n}}}{Z}\langle n|Q_{\eta}^{\rm C}|m\rangle\langle m|\mathscr{O}|n\rangle\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{\tau(E_{n}-E_{m})}\mathrm{d}\tau,

(78)

where
Z=\sum_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{n}}
with
\mathscr{H}|n\rangle=E_{n}|n\rangle
.
From this expression, we find that the physical quantity
\langle\mathscr{O}\rangle
is induced by the composite quadrupole order
Q^{\rm C}_{\eta}
only when the corresponding
m
-
n
matrix elements are simultaneously nonzero.
However, for the simple quadrupoles
Q_{\eta}
and
\phi_{\eta}
(or
\mathscr{Q}_{M}
and
x_{M}

in the spherical-tensor representation), no such matrix elements exist.
We therefore conclude that neither static electronic charge distortion nor lattice distortion is induced by the composite quadrupole order, even though they share the same spatial rotational symmetry.
This result follows from the selection rules inherent to the present electron–phonon coupled system, which are discussed in detail in Sec.
VI
.

Given the eigenenergies and corresponding wave functions, we analyze the entanglement properties that characterize Jahn–Teller phonon-coupled localized electrons. In our model, the Hilbert space factorizes into electronic and phononic sectors, as prescribed by the tensor-product structure of composite quantum systems, which naturally enables a characterization of their quantum correlations. Within the low-energy manifold, we therefore compute the reduced density matrix and the entanglement spectrum between the electronic and phononic degrees of freedom as a function of the coupling constant
g
. While entanglement properties in electron–phonon coupled systems have previously been investigated in one-dimensional Holstein models
Zhao et al. 2004
;
Stojanović and Vanević 2008
;
Zhang et al. 2009
, the present study instead focuses on a local multiorbital problem, with particular emphasis on the role of angular momentum.

The eigenstate of the electron-phonon-coupled system is labeled by the total angular momentum as

\displaystyle|L_{\rm tot},M\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\sum_{\{n\}}C_{\ell m}^{L_{\rm tot}M}\big(\{n\}\big)\ \big|\ell m\big\rangle\ \big|\{n\}\big\rangle_{\rm ph},

(79)

where
\{n\}=(n_{-2},n_{-1},n_{0},n_{1},n_{2})
.
Conservation of angular momentum conservation imposes the constraint
M=m+\sum_{m^{\prime}=-2}^{2}m^{\prime}n_{m^{\prime}}
.
In this subsection, we focus on the lowest
L_{\rm tot}=1
multiplet, which becomes the ground state at sufficiently large electron–phonon coupling, and omit the label
L_{\rm tot}
when no confusion arises.
We also restrict our attention to the spin sector
\sigma=\uparrow
and suppress this label in the following.

V.4.1
Reduced density matrix for electrons

When the states are classified solely by the total angular momentum, the electronic and phononic components are intrinsically entangled.
The use of reduced matrix elements, however, allows one to factor out the angular-momentum coupling and thereby access quantities specific to the electron or phonon sectors.

The reduced density matrix for the electronic degrees of freedom is defined by taking the partial trace over the phonon Hilbert space as

\displaystyle\rho_{{\rm el},M}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{\rm ph}\,|{1M}\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle{1M}|,

(80)

where
|L_{\rm tot}M\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle
is defined in Eq. (
79
).
Using this reduced density matrix, we introduce the entanglement entropy for the pure state as

\displaystyle S_{M}

\displaystyle=-{\rm Tr}_{\rm el}\,\rho_{{\rm el},M}\ln\rho_{{\rm el},M},

(81)

with
M=-1,0,+1
.
In the quantum Rabi models (see Table

;
Liu et al. 2017
;

Figure

We also evaluate the entanglement entropy for the
L_{\rm tot}=1

\displaystyle S_{M}

\displaystyle\simeq-C_{1}^{2}\ln C_{1}^{2}-C_{3}^{2}\ln C_{3}^{2}+C_{3}^{2}\tilde{S}_{M}.

(82)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{M}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m_{1}}A_{m_{1}}^{M}\ln A_{m_{1}}^{M},

\displaystyle A_{m_{1}}^{M}

\displaystyle=\langle 2,m_{1},2,M-m_{1}|1M\rangle^{2}.

(84)

The solid lines in Fig.

It is also of interest to examine the mixed state within the

subspace, which is relevant to effective quantum statistical properties at low temperatures.
We define the reduced density matrix for the electronic degrees of freedom as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm el}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{M=-1}^{1}\rho_{{\rm el},M}=\sum_{L=1}^{2}\lambda_{\rm el}^{(L)}\sum_{M=-L}^{L}\ket{LM}\bra{LM}.

(85)

The probabilities

In our model, the electronic density matrix involves only two energy levels, which allows for an interpretation in terms of an effective ‘thermal’ state.
The corresponding effective temperature can be defined through the Boltzmann weights associated with the unperturbed energies
E_{F}(L=1,2)

Such a thermal-state interpretation, however, is not generally applicable to three or more levels, and the density matrix should be regarded as an effective ‘nonequilibrium’ state, as in the case of phonons discussed below.

V.4.2
Reduced density matrix for phonons

Electron–phonon entanglement induces phonon excitations in the
L_{\rm tot}=1
ground-state multiplet, leading to an increasing weight of states with larger phonon number. Figure

To gain further insight, we consider the entanglement spectrum for phonons.
While in the context of nuclear physics bosonic degrees of freedom provide an effective description of a deformed shell structure, phonons in condensed matter systems constitute real physical degrees of freedom. We are therefore interested in their intrinsic properties.
We begin with the reduced density matrix for phonons:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ph}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}{\rm Tr_{el}}\sum_{M}|1M\rangle\hskip-1.42262pt\rangle\langle\hskip-1.42262pt\langle 1M|

\displaystyle P_{L_{\rm ph},\alpha}

\displaystyle=(2L_{\rm ph}+1)\ \lambda_{\rm ph}^{(L_{\rm ph},\alpha)}.

(90)

\displaystyle\xi_{L_{\rm ph},\alpha}=-\ln\lambda_{\rm ph}^{(L_{\rm ph},\alpha)},

(91)

\displaystyle\langle n_{d}\rangle

(92)

\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle_{L_{\rm ph},\alpha}

with increasing

We now discuss why the phonon angular momentum
L_{\rm ph}=1
is absent
for the reduced density matrix of the phonons.
Since the electronic angular momentum takes
L=1
and
L=2
, the condition for forming a total
angular momentum
L_{\rm tot}=1
state requires
L_{\rm ph}\leq 3
.
For bosons, the wave function must satisfy the totally symmetric condition under particle exchange.
As established in Ref.
Iachello and Arima 1987
, bosonic states constructed from
d
bosons are classified according to the quantum numbers of the group chain

\displaystyle U(5)\supset SO(5)\supset SO(3)\supset SO(2),

(94)

and the resulting phonon angular momentum never takes the value
L_{\rm ph}=1
.
More explicitly, for the two-boson state with
n_{d}=2
, the symmetric angular momentum coupling rule yields

\displaystyle[2\otimes 2]_{\rm sym}=4\oplus 2\oplus 0,

(95)

where
[\cdots]_{\rm sym}
denotes projection onto the totally symmetric subspace appropriate for bosons.
Similarly, for
n_{d}=3
, the angular momentum coupling gives

\displaystyle[2\otimes 2\otimes 2]_{\rm sym}=6\oplus 4\oplus 3\oplus 2\oplus 0,

(96)

none of which contains the
L_{\rm ph}=1
sector.
The absence of the
L_{\rm ph}=1
state persists to higher orders,
reflecting the underlying bosonic angular-momentum coupling constraints.
Although this group-theoretical argument may appear abstract at first sight, it can be understood more explicitly by examining the concrete form of the wave functions, as discussed in detail in Appendix
F
.

The above argument is based on purely bosonic systems. Although the
L_{\rm tot}=1
multiplet in our model consists of electron–phonon entangled states, in the limit
g\to 0
the expectation value of the boson number approaches
n_{d}=1
for the
(L_{\rm ph},\alpha)=(2,{\rm A})
sector, as shown in Fig.
5
(f). Similarly, one finds
n_{d}=2
for
(L_{\rm ph},\alpha)=(2,{\rm B})
and
n_{d}=3
for
L_{\rm ph}=3
as
g\to 0
. These results indicate that each
L_{\rm ph}
sector continuously connects, in the weak-coupling limit, to a well-defined
n_{d}
-boson configuration. Therefore, the structure of the
L_{\rm ph}
states observed in Figs.
5
(d)–(f) can be qualitatively understood from the angular-momentum coupling rules of the phonon degrees of freedom in the absence of electron–phonon interaction.

C

Quasispin selection rules

VI.1
Quasispin operators for electrons

The results of Sec.
V.3
, in particular the vanishing of the standard quadrupole in the electron–phonon-coupled
L_{\rm tot}=1
ground-state multiplet, can be
understood in terms of the quasispin selection rule
Judd 1967
;
Ceulemans 1994
.
To elucidate this point, we first define the operators

\displaystyle K_{+}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\langle 1m1m^{\prime}|00\rangle\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime}|00\rangle p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}p_{m^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}.

(97)

The prefactor
\sqrt{6}
originates from the factor
\sqrt{2s+1}\,\sqrt{2\ell+1}
evaluated at
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\ell=1
, as adopted in the definition of Ref.
Ceulemans 1994
.
We also define

\displaystyle K_{-}

\displaystyle=K_{+}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\gamma}D_{\gamma},

(98)

\displaystyle K_{z}

\displaystyle=[K_{+},K_{-}]/2=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\sigma}\quantity(p_{m\sg}^\dg p_{m\sg}-\frac 1 2),

(99)

which satisfies

\displaystyle[K_{z},K_{\pm}]

\displaystyle=\pm K_{\pm}.

(100)

We also define
K_{x,y}
by
K_{\pm}=K_{x}\pm\mathrm{i}K_{y}
.
These operators obey the standard
SU(2)
algebra.

Using these quasispins, the operators
p^{\dagger}
and
\tilde{p}
are identified as forming a quasispin tensor:

\displaystyle[K_{z},p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}]=\frac{1}{2}p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger},\ \ \ [K_{z},{\tilde{p}}_{m\sigma}]=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{p}_{m\sigma},

(101)

\displaystyle[K_{-},p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}]=\tilde{p}_{m\sigma},\ \ \ [K_{+},\tilde{p}_{m\sigma}]=p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}.

Hence the creation and annihilation operators are written in terms of the triple tensor as

\displaystyle p_{m,\sigma,{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}=p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger},~~~p_{m,\sigma,-{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}={\tilde{p}_{m\sigma}},

(102)

where the superscripts
(1,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
indicate the quantum numbers
(L,S,K)
.
With use of these tensors, one can define another triple tensor operators as

\displaystyle T_{M_{L},M_{S},M_{K}}^{\left({L,S,K}\right)}\equiv\left[{{p^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}}\otimes{p^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}}}\right]_{M_{L},M_{S},M_{K}}^{\left({L,S,K}\right)}

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{mm^{\prime}\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}\!\!\left\langle{1m1m^{\prime}\left|{LM_{L}}\right.}\right\rangle\left\langle{{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\sigma{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\sigma^{\prime}\left|{S{M_{S}}}\right.}\right\rangle\left\langle{{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\kappa{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\kappa^{\prime}\left|{K{M_{K}}}\right.}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times p_{m,\sigma,\kappa}^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}p_{m^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}.

(103)

For example, the angular momentum operator is constructed as

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}_{M}}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{2}\left[{{p^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}}\otimes{p^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}},{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}}}\right]_{M,0,0}^{\left({1,0,0}\right)}

\displaystyle=-2\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes\tilde{p}}\right]_{M,0}^{\left({1,0}\right)},

VI.2
Electron–phonon coupled case

We proceed to account for the selection rule in the electron–phonon coupled system.
The above analysis of quasispin operator indicates that the quadrupole operator with

VII
Summary and outlook

In this paper, we present a detailed analysis of a single-site model consisting of three electrons confined in a three-orbital system and coupled to Jahn–Teller phonons, which serves as a minimal model for fulleride materials in the Mott-insulating regime. We clarify the relationship between the Cartesian and
spherical tensor representations, highlighting their complementary advantages: the former provides intuitive physical insight, while the latter enables a systematic algebraic analysis based
on spherical tensor operators.

In the present system, the ground-state multiplet hosts internal degrees of freedom not captured by conventional quadrupole moments, but rather by composite quadrupoles constructed from products of higher-rank operators. We elucidate their tensorial structure and quantitatively evaluate the associated reduced matrix elements by means of detailed numerical calculations, while the selection rules obeyed by these tensor operators are systematically derived within the quasispin formalism. A detailed analysis of the entanglement induced by the electron–phonon interaction further reveals that, while a single-phonon state carries angular momentum
\ell=2
, the interacting multiphonon sector coupled to the electronic degrees of freedom is dominated by states with
L_{\rm ph}=2
and
L_{\rm ph}=3
. Thus, this work provides a concrete demonstration of the broad utility of angular-momentum tensor methods in condensed matter theory.

Spherical tensors provide a powerful framework for treating more complex interactions.
In fulleride systems, a small but finite spin–orbit coupling is present
Tosatti et al. 1996
, and the resulting fine structure can be described systematically within this formalism.
Moreover, in the context of nuclear physics, more elaborate Hamiltonians beyond simple fermion–boson coupling have been considered.
For example, parity-odd
f
bosons
Yoshinaga et al. 2024
can couple to fermionic degrees of freedom, as well as two‑fermion scattering processes associated with collective
d
‑boson modes
Teng et al. 2025
.
Similar extensions are expected to be relevant for fullerides, where parity-odd phonon modes indeed exist as molecular vibrations.
Such generalized electron–phonon couplings in the Hamiltonian can be formulated transparently within the spherical-tensor framework by constructing scalar operators such as
\big[[\mathscr{Q}\otimes\mathscr{Q}]\otimes d^{\dagger}\big]^{(0)}
and
\big[\mathscr{Q}\otimes[f^{\dagger}\otimes\tilde{f}]\big]^{(0)}
, in addition to the
\big[\mathscr{Q}\otimes d^{\dagger}\big]^{(0)}
term considered in this work.
Exploring these extensions, including the role of parity-odd phonon modes and higher-rank composite couplings, remains an interesting open problem from both physical and symmetry perspectives.

Appendix D
Spherical tensor formalism for
p

Below, we summarize the tensorial properties of the spin–orbital system.
Although some of these results have already been presented in the main text,
the formulation given here is more closely aligned with approaches commonly used in nuclear physics.
It thus provides a useful bridge between the theoretical frameworks of Cartesian and spherical tensor representations, and also offers a convenient framework for more complicated cases involving additional orbital degrees of freedom and/or spin–orbit coupling.
Subsections

D.4
Spectroscopic factor and overlap

Let us also consider the single-particle excitation spectrum as experimentally probed by the photoemission spectroscopy.
We first define the Green’s function

\displaystyle G(\mathrm{i}\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\tau\langle p(\tau)p^{\dagger}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}\tau},

(169)

where
\omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi T
is the fermionic Matsubara frequency.
Then the spectrum is given at low
T
by

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm Im\,}G(\varepsilon+\mathrm{i}0^{+})

(170)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f(\varepsilon)Z}\sum_{nm}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{n}}|\langle n|p^{\dagger}|m\rangle|^{2}\delta(\varepsilon+E_{m}-E_{n}),

(171)

where

The definitions of

and

are the same as those in Eq. (
78

This expression contains a matrix element
\langle n|p^{\dagger}|m\rangle
that connects a three-electron state

|m\rangle

We define the notation

\displaystyle{\left|{{{\left[{{{\left[{p\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({{L_{2}},{S_{2}}}\right)}\otimes p}}\right]}^{\left({L,S}\right)}}}\right\rangle_{U}}\equiv{\left[{{{\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]}^{\left({{L_{2}},{S_{2}}}\right)}}{\otimes p^{\dagger}}}\right]^{\left({L,S}\right)}}\left|0\right\rangle,

(172)

\displaystyle O_{{L_{2}},{S_{2}},{L^{\prime}_{2}},{S^{\prime}_{2}}}^{\left({L,S}\right)}

{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}S&{{S^{\prime}_{2}}}&{{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\end{array}}\end{array}}\right\},

and

\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}a&b&c\end{array}}\\
{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}d&e&f\end{array}}\end{array}}\right\}

6j
-symbol.

(179)

\displaystyle\times\left\langle{{{\left[{{{\left[{p\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({{{L^{\prime}}_{2}},{{S^{\prime}}_{2}}}\right)}}\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({L,S}\right)}}{{\left\|{{p^{\dagger}}}\right\|}_{D}}{{\left[{p\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({{L_{2}},{S_{2}}}\right)}}}\right\rangle.

(183)

From Eq. (
D.4

\displaystyle S_{{L_{2}},{S_{2}},{{L_{2}^{\prime}}},{{S_{2}^{\prime}}}}^{\left({L,S}\right)}=\frac{{N_{{L_{2}},{S_{2}}}^{\left({L,S}\right)}}}{2}

\displaystyle\times{\left|{\left\langle{{{\left[{{{\left[{p\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({{{L_{2}^{\prime}}},{{S_{2}^{\prime}}}}\right)}}\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({L,S}\right)}}\left|{{{\left[{{{\left[{p\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({{L_{2}},{S_{2}}}\right)}}\otimes p}\right]}^{\left({L,S}\right)}}}\right.}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}}.

(184)

This relation shows that the overlap matrix element is expressed in terms of the spectroscopic factor.

Appendix E
Quasispin formalism in fermion systems

We investigate the quasispin formalism that was first studied
in nuclear physics by A. K. Kerman
Kerman 1961
.
Here we apply the quasispin formalism to electrons in a
p
-shell (
{\ell=1}
).

E.1
quasispin operators

Using
p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}
and
{\tilde{p}_{m\sigma}}
in Eqs. (
37
) and (
39
),
quasispin operators
(
K_{\pm},K_{z})
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle{K_{+}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\Omega}{2}}{\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]^{\left({0,0}\right)}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\Omega}{2}}\sum\limits_{m,m^{\prime},\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}{\left\langle{1m1m^{\prime}\left|{00}\right.}\right\rangle}\left\langle{{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\sigma{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\sigma^{\prime}\left|{00}\right.}\right\rangle p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}p_{m^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger},

(192)

\displaystyle{K_{-}}

\displaystyle\equiv(K_{+})^{\dagger}=-\sqrt{\frac{\Omega}{2}}{\left[{\tilde{p}\otimes\tilde{p}}\right]^{\left({0,0}\right)}},\ \ \ {K_{z}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left({n-\Omega}\right),

(193)

where
n
is the number operator and
\Omega
(= 3) is
half the number of the maximally occupied electrons in the
p
-shell.
They satisfy quasispin commutation relations [Eq. (
131
)]:

\displaystyle\left[{{K_{+}},{K_{-}}}\right]{\rm{=2}}{K_{z}},~\left[{{K_{z}},{K_{\pm}}}\right]=\pm{K_{\pm}}.

(194)

Defining
{K_{x}}
and
{K_{y}}
by
{K_{\pm}}\equiv{K_{x}}\pm\mathrm{i}{K_{y}}
, one has the ordinary commutation relation for angular momentum:
\left[{{K_{k}},{K_{l}}}\right]{\rm{=}}\mathrm{i}{\varepsilon_{klm}}{K_{m}}\left({k,l,m=x,y,z}\right)
, leading to
the simultaneous eigenstates
\left|{K,{K_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle

(
\alpha
is any arbitrary quantum number) of
{\bm{K}^{2}}=K_{x}^{2}+K_{y}^{2}+K_{z}^{2}
and
{K_{z}}
.
Namely, one has
{\bm{K}^{2}}\left|{K,{K_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle=K\left({K+1}\right)\left|{K,{K_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle
and
{K_{z}}\left|{K,{K_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle={K_{0}}\left|{K,{K_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle

with
{K_{0}}=-K,-K+1,\cdots,K-1,K
.
Since the minimum of
{K_{0}}

is
-K
, the relation
{K_{-}}\left|{K,-K,\alpha}\right\rangle=0
is satisfied.
For any integer
p>0
, one has
\left|{K,-K+p,\alpha}\right\rangle={N_{pK}}{\left({{K_{+}}}\right)^{p}}\left|{K,-K,\alpha}\right\rangle
with

{N_{pK}}=\sqrt{{{\left({2K-p}\right)!}\mathord{\left/{\vphantom{{\left({2K-p}\right)!}{\left({2K}\right)!p!}}}\right.\kern-1.2pt}{\left({2K}\right)!p!}}}
.

The operator
\bm{K}
, although vectorial in form, is a scalar with respect to both the angular momentum
\bm{L}
and the spin
\bm{S}
, i.e., it is invariant under spatial and spin rotations:

\left[{{K_{i}},{L_{j}}}\right]=\left[{{K_{i}},{S_{j}}}\right]=0
.
Therefore, one has the simultaneous eigenstates for
{\bm{K}^{2}},{K_{z}},{\bm{L}^{2}},{L_{z}},{\bm{S}^{2}},{S_{z}}
denoted
as
\left|{K{K_{0}},L{L_{0}},S{S_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle
.

E.2
Construction of states with definite
K
and
{K_{0}}

In the following we classify the electronic states according to quasispin. For
one-particle system,
applying
{K_{z}}
to

p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}\left|0\right\rangle\equiv\left|{K,{K_{0}},m,\sigma}\right\rangle
, one has

{K_{z}}\left|{K,{K_{0}},m,\sigma}\right\rangle={K_{z}}p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}\left|0\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left({n-\Omega}\right)p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}\left|0\right\rangle=\left({-1}\right)p_{m\sigma}^{\dagger}\left|0\right\rangle
.
Thus
{K_{0}}=-1
.
Also, by applying
\bm{K^{2}}
, one obtains the quantum number K as

{\bm{K}^{2}}\left|{K,{K_{0}}\!=\!-1,\alpha}\right\rangle=2\left|{K,{K_{0}},\alpha}\right\rangle.

Thus, this state has
{K=1}
and is written as
\left|{{p_{m\sigma}}}\right\rangle=\left|{K\!=\!1,K_{0}\!=\!-1,m,\sigma}\right\rangle
.

Next, we consider the three-particle system.
As stated in Eqs. (
156
) and (
157

\displaystyle\left|{L=0}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{{\sqrt{2\Omega}}}{\left[{{{\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]}^{\left({1,1}\right)}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]^{\left({0,{\textstyle{3\over 2}}}\right)}}\left|0\right\rangle,

(195)

\displaystyle\left|{L=2}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{{\sqrt{\Omega}}}{\left[{{{\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]}^{\left({1,1}\right)}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]^{\left({2,{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}}\left|0\right\rangle,

(196)

\displaystyle\left|{L=1}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{{\sqrt{\Omega}}}{2}{\left[{{{\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]}^{\left({0,0}\right)}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}}\left|0\right\rangle,

(197)

where the third components of total orbital angular momentum and total spin are abbreviated to denote.

The
\left|{L=1}\right\rangle
state can be constructed by applying
{K_{+}}
to the one-particle state
\left|{{p_{m\sigma}}}\right\rangle=\left|{K=1,K=-1,m,\sigma}\right\rangle
.
Using the formula
\left|{K,-K+1,m,\sigma}\right\rangle={N_{p=1,K}}{K_{+}}\left|{K,-K,m,\sigma}\right\rangle
with

,
one has

\displaystyle\sqrt{{{\left({2K-1}\right)!}\mathord{\left/{\vphantom{{\left({2K-1}\right)!}{\left({2K}\right)!}}}\right.\kern-1.2pt}{\left({2K}\right)!}}}{K_{+}}\left|{{p_{m\sigma}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{{\sqrt{\Omega}}}{2}\left[{{{\left[{{p^{\dagger}}\otimes{p^{\dagger}}}\right]}^{\left({0,0}\right)}}\otimes p^{\dagger}}\right]_{m,\sigma}^{\left({1,{\textstyle{1\over 2}}}\right)}\left|0\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|{L=1}\right\rangle.

(198)

Thus
\left|{L=1}\right\rangle
has
K=1
and
{K_{0}}=0
,
while both
\left|{L=0}\right\rangle
and
\left|{L=2}\right\rangle
states have

and
{K_{0}}=0

E.3
Quasispin tensors in terms of creation and annihilation operators

Since Eq. (

E.5
Wigner Eckart theorem in quasispin space

We discuss concrete examples of the matrix elements.
Suppose the one-body operator
Q_{0}^{(1)}
has
K=1
and
K_{0}=0

For diagonal matrix elements, the Wigner–Eckart theorem gives

\displaystyle\left\langle{K,{K_{z}},\alpha\left|{Q_{0}^{\left(1\right)}}\right|K,{K_{z}},\alpha}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{{\left\langle{K,{K_{z}},1,0\left|{K,{K_{z}}}\right.}\right\rangle}}{{\sqrt{2K+1}}}\left\langle{K,\alpha{{\left\|{{Q^{\left(1\right)}}}\right\|}_{K}}K,\alpha}\right\rangle.

(207)

Here

\left\langle{\cdots{{\left\|{{Q^{\left(1\right)}}}\right\|}_{K}}\cdots}\right\rangle

indicates a reduced matrix element in quasispin space.
Explicit expression for the CG coefficient

\left\langle{K,{K_{z}},1,0\left|{K,{K_{z}}}\right.}\right\rangle

\frac{{{K_{z}}}}{{\sqrt{K\left({K+1}\right)}}}
.
Thus for

\left|{L=2}\right\rangle
and
\left|{L=0}\right\rangle

. Thus, for any
\left|L\right\rangle
state, one has
\left\langle{L\left|{Q_{0}^{\left(1\right)}}\right|L}\right\rangle=0
.

For the off-diagonal matrix element, there is the relation

{\langle L\!=\!1|Q_{0}^{(1)}|L\!=\!0\rangle}=0
, because
{Q_{0}^{\left(1\right)}}
has the rank (angular momentum)
L=2
.
Also, the two states
\left|{L=2}\right\rangle
and
\left|{L=0}\right\rangle
have

and

, and hence
\left\langle{L=2\left|{Q_{0}^{\left(1\right)}}\right|L=0}\right\rangle=0

The matrix element between

\displaystyle\left\langle{L=2,{m_{2}}\sigma\left|{{\mathscr{Q}_{M}}}\right|L=1,{m_{1}}\sigma}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{{\left\langle{1{m_{1}}2M\left|2\right.{m_{2}}}\right\rangle}}{{\sqrt{5}}}\left\langle{L=2\left\|\mathscr{Q}\right\|L=1}\right\rangle.

Talmi 1993
.

\lambda
