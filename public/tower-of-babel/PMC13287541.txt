Progressive intracranial venous reflux with cognitive decline in a hemodialysis patient: illustrative case
Authors: Hideki Nakajima, Yu Sato, Tetsushi Yago, Kazuhiko Tsuda, Hidenori Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Hemodialysis (HD) patients are at risk for central venous disease (CVD) due to long-standing arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) and repeated vascular access procedures.1–3 Central venous stenosis or occlusion may result in venous hypertension and the development of collateral or retrograde venous pathways.1 In rare cases, elevated venous pressure may propagate retrogradely into the intracranial venous system, leading to intracranial venous reflux and neurological manifestations.4

Most reported cases of HD AVF-related intracranial venous reflux have presented with acute or subacute neurological symptoms, often mimicking dural AVF (dAVF) or cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.2,4–7 Cortical venous reflux (CVR), when present, is generally regarded as a marker of aggressive venous hypertension.8 However, the longitudinal natural history of intracranial venous reflux in HD patients remains poorly characterized, and the mechanisms underlying progression from sinus-only reflux to cortical venous involvement are not well understood.

Here, we report the case of an octogenarian HD patient in whom intracranial venous reflux progressed over several years from a benign sinus-only pattern to overt CVR, ultimately manifesting as rapidly worsening cognitive impairment.

Illustrative Case

An 81-year-old woman with a 15-year history of chronic renal failure had been maintained on HD via a left upper extremity AVF. Seven years prior to presentation, brain MR angiography (MRA) incidentally demonstrated intracranial venous reflux from the left internal jugular vein (IJV) to the sigmoid and transverse sinuses (Fig 1A). Because no CVR was identified and the patient was neurologically asymptomatic, she was managed conservatively with observation. However, the patient later presented with rapidly progressive cognitive decline (Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] score decreased from 26 to 16 over a 6-month period), prompting further evaluation. Brain MRA revealed marked progression of intracranial venous reflux (Fig. 1B). She was subsequently admitted for further investigation and management. Arterial spin labeling MRI demonstrated increased perfusion within the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses (Fig. 2A arrow). Susceptibility-weighted imaging revealed prominent dilatation of cortical veins in the left cerebellum and left occipital lobe, indicating chronic venous congestion (Fig. 2B double arrows). CT angiography showed severe stenosis of the left brachiocephalic vein (BCV), which was compressed between the sternum and the brachiocephalic artery (Fig. 2C and D arrowheads). Left subclavian artery angiography demonstrated intracranial venous reflux, involving the superior petrosal sinus, vein of Labbé, straight sinus, vein of Galen, and cortical veins of the occipital lobe and cerebellum (Fig. 3A and B). Carotid ultrasound showed incompetence of the IJV valve. No intracranial arteriovenous shunt was identified. These findings were considered responsible for the patient’s worsening cognitive impairment, and endovascular treatment was planned to treat the severe stenosis of the left BCV.

Pretreatment angiography showed no antegrade venous flow from the left BCV to the superior vena cava (Fig. 4A). Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) was therefore performed (Fig. 4B). Although slight improvement in venous flow was achieved (Fig. 4C double arrowhead), residual severe venous outflow obstruction persisted. Because CVR remained unchanged after PTA, a staged surgical strategy was adopted. Another HD AVF was created in the right upper extremity 10 days later, followed by ligation of the left upper extremity AVF 2 months later. MRA obtained on the day after AVF ligation demonstrated complete resolution of intracranial venous reflux from the left IJV (Fig. 4D). Cognitive function gradually improved following AVF ligation, with the MMSE score increasing from 16 to 22 over approximately 3 months.

Intraoperative PTA and MRA findings after HD AVF ligation. A: Pretreatment angiogram demonstrating the absence of antegrade flow from the left BCV to the superior vena cava. B:PTA was performed for the BCV stenosis. C:Post-PTA angiogram showing only minimal improvement in venous outflow (double arrowhead), with persistent venous reflux. D: MRA was performed the day after HD AVF ligation and showed complete resolution of intracranial venous reflux from the left IJV.

Discussion

Observations

This case illustrates progressive intracranial venous reflux associated with a long-standing HD AVF, culminating in newly developed CVR and rapidly worsening cognitive impairment. Unlike most previously reported cases that presented with acute neurological deterioration, this patient demonstrated longitudinal conversion from a sinus-only reflux pattern to malignant cortical venous drainage over a 7-year interval. These findings highlight that HD AVF-related intracranial venous hypertension may evolve over time and that the emergence of CVR represents a critical turning point requiring definitive flow reduction.

CVD in HD patients is characterized by venous outflow obstruction, elevated venous pressure, and the development of collateral or retrograde venous pathways.1,3,9 Long-standing high-flow AVFs can cause vessel wall injury and promote endothelial proliferation, occasionally resulting in stenosis or occlusion in the central venous system, which can exacerbate venous hypertension and facilitate retrograde transmission of pressure into the cervical venous system.10 In many patients, such reflux remains asymptomatic or confined to the dural venous sinuses and may not immediately result in cortical venous involvement.3 In the present case, however, progressive left BCV stenosis impaired antegrade venous drainage over time, while sustained high-flow shunting imposed continuous hemodynamic stress. Although IJV valve incompetence is not uncommon in CVD, the cumulative burden of venous hypertension likely exceeded compensatory capacity, allowing progressive propagation of reflux beyond the dural sinuses.11,12 Serial imaging demonstrated stepwise extension of venous drainage from the sigmoid and transverse sinuses to the vein of Labbé, superior petrosal sinus, straight sinus, vein of Galen, and cortical veins. This evolution suggests that HD AVF-related intracranial venous reflux is not necessarily static and may progressively transition to a more aggressive venous outflow pattern if the underlying hemodynamic burden persists. T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging showed no parenchymal edema, consistent with predominantly venous congestion without significant tissue injury.

In the literature on dAVF, the presence of CVR is a well-established marker of aggressive venous hypertension and increased risk of hemorrhage or neurological decline.13 Importantly, dAVFs with CVR may also present with progressive cognitive impairment due to chronic venous congestion rather than acute hemorrhage.14–16 Although the underlying mechanism in the present case was extracranial AVF, the development of CVR can be understood within a similar hemodynamic framework in which impaired venous outflow leads to venous hypertension.17 In this case, progression of intracranial venous reflux occurred in an octogenarian and manifested primarily as rapidly worsening cognitive impairment. CVR itself may directly contribute to progressive cognitive impairment irrespective of its origin and may be overlooked in the absence of acute hemorrhage, particularly in older patients. Therefore, careful evaluation of extracranial venous outflow should be considered when CVR is identified without clear evidence of intracranial dAVF.

Endovascular treatment is frequently used for CVD in HD patients and has been reported to alleviate neurological symptoms in selected cases.1,2,9 However, its effectiveness depends on anatomical and hemodynamic factors.18 In this case, the left BCV was compressed between the sternum and the brachiocephalic artery, suggesting an extrinsic mechanical constraint limiting durable venous expansion. Furthermore, persistent high-flow shunting continued to impose substantial venous pressure despite angioplasty. The minimal improvement observed after PTA indicates that minimal restoration of venous patency alone was insufficient to reverse intracranial venous hypertension once CVR had developed. Definitive ligation of the HD AVF resulted in immediate resolution of intracranial venous reflux and sustained clinical improvement.11,18–20 This outcome suggests that reduction of shunt flow may represent a decisive therapeutic intervention when sufficient venous recanalization is impossible. That is, if CVR persists in spite of endovascular treatment, even in much older patients, the treatment strategy should be reevaluated in a timely manner, including consideration of definitive blood flow reduction. Management requires multidisciplinary collaboration, with treatment decisions balancing preservation of dialysis access against the risk of venous hypertension.

Although the present case provides longitudinal evidence of progression from sinus-only reflux to CVR, the natural history of HD AVF-related intracranial venous reflux remains insufficiently characterized. It is unclear which HD patients with CVD are at highest risk for progression to CVR. Furthermore, the optimal timing for intervention before the development of CVR has not been established. Accumulation of additional longitudinal cases will be necessary to clarify risk factors for progression and to define appropriate surveillance and treatment strategies.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that intracranial venous reflux associated with long-standing HD AVFs may progress over time from a sinus-only pattern to CVR, even in much older patients. The emergence of CVR may signal advanced venous hypertension and can present insidiously with progressive cognitive impairment rather than acute neurological deterioration. Recognition of this progression is critical, as definitive flow reduction may be required when endovascular treatment alone is insufficient. Accumulation of additional longitudinal cases will be necessary to better define risk factors for progression and optimal timing of intervention.
