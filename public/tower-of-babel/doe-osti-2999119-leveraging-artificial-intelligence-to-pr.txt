ABSTRACT

The goal of this project was to train an artificial neural network (ANN) to predict the fractional
composition and melting point of eutectic alloys using fundamental atomic properties as inputs.
The fundamental properties considered include atomic number, atomic weight, atomic radius,
valence electron concentration, electronegativity, and electron affinity. The project involved
several phases, starting with data preparation, where phase diagram data was harvested from the
ASM International database. Approximately 1300 binary eutectics were collected and cleaned to
ensure relevance and accuracy. A regression model was selected for training, utilizing a rectified
linear unit as the activation function. Various model configurations were evaluated for predictive
accuracy, with validation techniques employed to ensure robustness. The model demonstrated
predictive capabilities above random guessing and was able to achieve up to 11% accuracy under
certain conditions. An ablative test identified atomic radius and valence electron concentration as
critical inputs for model performance. Incorporating the melting point of atomic constituents
improved accuracy significantly, although ultimately the model’s predictive capability still fell
short of the 80% target. This report details the methodology, results, and implications of the
research, contributing to the understanding of employing artificial intelligence to predict the
phase transition behavior of eutectic alloys.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS

The development of eutectic alloys is a critical area of research in materials science, as these
alloys often exhibit desirable properties such as lower melting points, improved mechanical
characteristics, and enhanced thermal stability. Eutectic alloys are defined by their unique phase
diagrams, which demonstrate a sharp minimum in the liquidus line/surface. This sharp phase
transition at the eutectic point make them ideal materials for uses as liquid metal alloy ion beam
sources, for soldering and brazing of heterogeneous-integrated materials, or in advanced
manufacturing.

Traditionally, the prediction of eutectic compositions has relied on either a manual search of a
database of phase diagrams, which can be time-consuming and limited in scope, or on the use of
computational tools, which can be unreliable in some cases. These traditional methods often lead
to a trial-and-error approach in alloy design, which can be inefficient and costly. Moreover, the
complexity of phase diagrams, especially for multi-component systems, poses significant

 1

challenges in accurately predicting eutectic points. As a result, there is a growing need for
innovative approaches that can streamline the prediction process and improve accuracy.

This project aimed to leverage artificial intelligence, specifically artificial neural networks
(ANNs), to predict the fractional composition and melting point of eutectic alloys based on the
fundamental atomic properties of the alloys’ constituents. By utilizing machine learning
techniques, we sought to develop a tool capable of improving the efficiency of finding eutectic
alloys that have a favorable compositions and phase transition temperature for a given
application. The use of ANNs allows for the modeling of complex relationships between input
features and outputs, making them well-suited for this type of predictive task.

The specific questions addressed by this research included how fundamental atomic properties
can be effectively utilized to predict the characteristics of eutectic alloys, the predictive
capability of an ANN trained on these properties, and how different model configurations impact
the accuracy of predictions. The research was structured into several phases, beginning with data
preparation, where phase diagram data was harvested from the ASM International database. This
database contains extensive data on binary and ternary alloys, making it a valuable resource for
our study.

The initial milestone was harvesting the phase diagram data of eutectic alloys from the ASM
International database, which contains extensive data on both binary and ternary alloys. This
database is a valuable resource for researchers in materials science, providing access to a wealth
of information regarding material properties. For our purpose in particular, the phase diagrams of
binary and ternary alloys are able to be retrieved from the database. To facilitate data collection,
access to ASM’s proprietary Materials Property Data System (MPDS) was purchased, which
allowed for automated retrieval of phase diagram data in machine-readable JSON
format. Ultimately only binary alloys were considered, due to inadequacies in the machine-
readable files for ternary alloy phase diagrams which prevented them from being interpreted
automatedly.

Data cleaning was a critical step in ensuring the relevance and accuracy of the dataset. The ASM
database often contains multiple phase diagrams for a single binary alloy, some of which were
obtained computationally or were performed under non-standard pressure conditions, which can
lead to inconsistencies. To ensure the relevance of the data, only phase diagrams showing the
entire range of composition were considered. The eutectic point was selected using a "majority
rules" approach, where the phase diagrams that agreed on a eutectic point to within 5% the most
times were used. This method helped to remove irrelevant studies, which were typically singular
and contained phase diagrams that were markedly different from the majority. Ultimately,
manual verification of the harvested binary eutectic data was necessary due to occasional errors

 2

in the JSON files generated by the ASM Corel program, which in a few cases led to inaccurate
data being read in.

Following data preparation, the model selection process identified a regression model as the most
suitable approach for predicting the continuous outputs of fractional composition and melting
point. The choice of a regression model was driven by the nature of the prediction problem,
which required estimating numerical values rather than classifying data into discrete categories.
Hyperparameters for the model were chosen by using a predominantly trial-and-error method.

Model evaluation involved testing against random guessing and testing the ability to predict a
nonlinear function to ensure the model was working as expected and did not contain any bugs in
the code. The results indicated that the model could achieve predictive accuracy significantly
above random noise and could accurately predict a nonlinear relationship between input and
output vectors.

After optimization, a maximum model accuracy of 11% was obtained, which was well below the
targeted 80% success metric. This suggests that the ANN is able learn from the underlying
patterns in the data but is either missing information or unable to converge to the true
relationship. These results provide a promising avenue for future research in the prediction of the
phase transition behavior of eutectics.

An ablative test was conducted to determine the importance of different input features, revealing
that atomic radius, along with valence electron concentration, electronegativity, or electron
affinity, were the fundamental parameters that had the most substantial impact on model
performance. Furthermore, incorporating the melting point of atomic constituents into the input
set improved accuracy by over 50%.

The insights gained from this study can inform future research directions, including the
exploration of additional atomic properties or advanced modeling techniques that may further
enhance predictive capabilities.

In summary, this report provides a comprehensive overview of the research methodology,
results, and implications, contributing valuable insights into the application of artificial
intelligence in predicting the phase transition behavior of eutectic alloys. The integration of
machine learning techniques into predicting these characteristics represents a novel advancement
in the field of applying machine learning to materials’ science, offering new opportunities for
innovation and discovery.

The first phase involved data preparation, which included data collection. In the data
preparation phase, approximately 1300 binary eutectics were collected, cleaned, and validated to
ensure the accuracy of the data used for training the model. The data collection process involved
extracting phase diagram data from the ASM International database, which required purchasing
access to the MPDS (Materials Property Data System). This access allowed for automated
retrieval of phase diagram data in machine-readable JSON format. Examples of phase diagrams
for binary and ternary alloys are shown in Figure 1.

 4

Figure 1: Phase diagrams for a binary (left) and ternary (right) eutectic alloy. Eutectic points are identified as a sharp minimum
of the liquidus line in the case of a binary eutectic and as the convergence of three "eutectic valleys" on the liquidus projection of
a ternary system.

For the case of binary alloys, the ASM database mining program was utilized to extract the
liquidus line from the JSON files, identifying points where the gradient changes from positive to
negative. This process was essential for accurately determining the eutectic points of the alloys.

For ternary alloys, the JSON files containing the liquidus projections did not specify the labels
for isothermal lines or the lines corresponding to “eutectic valleys”, making it impossible to
automatedly determine whether a given material formed a eutectic and, if so, it’s eutectic point.
Therefore, the study was confined to predicting the eutectic points of binary alloys.

The ASM database mining program data_miner.py was utilized to extract the liquidus line from
the JSON files. This extraction process involved identifying points where the gradient of the
liquidus line changes from positive to negative, which is indicative of the eutectic point. The
correct identification of these points is crucial for obtaining accurate training and validation data.

Following data collection, the next step was data cleaning. The ASM database often contains
multiple phase diagrams for a single binary alloy, which can lead to inconsistencies in the data.
To ensure the relevance and accuracy of the dataset, only phase diagrams that showed the entire
range of composition were considered. This step was essential to avoid harvesting eutectic points
from non-relevant cases. The eutectic point was selected using a "majority rules" approach,
where the eutectic point most agreed upon by the phase diagrams was harvested. This method
ensured that the selected eutectic point was representative of the majority of the data, thereby
increasing the reliability of the dataset.

 5

Manual verification was ultimately necessary due to occasional errors in the JSON files
generated by the ASM Corel program. These errors could lead to inaccuracies in the data, which
would ultimately affect the performance of the ANN model. As a result, a thorough review of the
data was conducted to identify and correct any discrepancies. This meticulous approach to data
cleaning was critical in ensuring that the model was trained on high-quality data.

The inputs and outputs were scaled to range from 0 to 1, which is a common practice in neural
network training to improve convergence rates.

Once the data was prepared, the next phase involved data splitting. The dataset was randomly
split into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets, with randomization performed independently
for each run. This approach allowed for a robust evaluation of the model's performance, as the
validation set provided an unbiased assessment of how well the model generalized to unseen
data. The randomization process was essential in mitigating any potential biases that could arise
from the way the training and validation datasets were chosen.

The second phase of the project focused on model selection. The goal of the project was to
predict two continuous outputs: the fractional composition and the melting point of the eutectic
alloys. This task requires estimating numerical values rather than classifying data into discrete
categories, driving the decision to select a regression model.

In the third phase, model training was conducted. Hyperparameters such as node activation
function, evaluation metric, learning rate, learning algorithm, and number of training epochs
were tested to inform ultimate selection of hyperparameters. The rectified linear unit (ReLU),
widely used in neural networks due to its ability to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and
facilitate faster convergence during training, was selected as the activation function for the nodes
in the ANN. While the tanh activation function was also tested, it was found to be less effective
in this context. Adam was selected as the learning algorithm due to it’s superior performance, not
unexpected in a deep learning application. Other learning algorithms tested were stochastic
gradient descent (SGD), AdamW, and NAdam. The number of training epochs was allowed to be
automatedly determined with a patience value of 10 and a maximum of 1000 training epochs,
with the more successful models converging in on the order of 10 training epochs. Mean absolute
error was used as the loss function, with mean squared error also tested.

Various network shapes were tested during the training phase, including hourglass, diamond,
double diamond, and square configurations. This refers to the number of nodes per layer and the
shape that it makes (diamond shape has fewer nodes on the input/output ends and more nodes in
the central layers, hourglass is the opposite, square has constant number of nodes, etc.) Each
configuration was evaluated to determine its impact on predictive accuracy. We will show data
that indicates that the shape of the network did not substantially impact the model's predictive

 6

accuracy. This finding suggested that the model's performance was more dependent on the
quality of the input data and the training process rather than the specific architecture of the
network.

The fourth phase focused on model evaluation. The predictive accuracy of each model
configuration was assessed by comparing the number of correct predictions for the validation
dataset. An "accuracy metric" was defined as the average accuracy across models with 8-100
nodes and 10-33 layers, normalized to the best model's accuracy. This metric provided a clear
benchmark for evaluating the effectiveness of different model configurations and allowed for a
systematic comparison of results.

In the fifth phase, validation techniques were employed to ensure the robustness of the model.
Several tests were conducted to validate the model's performance. First, the model's accuracy
was compared against random guessing, demonstrating that the model had predictive capabilities
above random noise. This comparison was crucial for establishing the model's validity and
ensuring that it was not through some oversight acquiring a bias that made it good at guessing
the validation data.

Second, the model was tested for bugs by predicting outputs based on nonlinear functions of the
input. Using the input data contained in our spreadsheet of mined ASM data, we generated
output vectors whose elements were each a different nonlinear function of all inputs. We then
attempted to train the neural network, without further optimization of hyperparameters or other
changes to the code, on the data. This test aimed to ensure that the model could handle complex
relationships between inputs and outputs using the same number of training and validation data,
achieving up to 90% accuracy in some cases. This level of performance indicated that the model
was capable of learning meaningful patterns from the training data and there were no bugs in the
code.

Finally, the impact of bad training data was assessed by randomizing the elements of a specified
number of training output vectors. This analysis showed a significant reduction in predictive
power with the introduction of bad data, highlighting the importance of data quality in training
machine learning models. The results underscored the need for careful data management and
validation throughout the research process.

The sixth phase involved model interpretation, where an ablative test was performed to
evaluate the impact of selectively removing inputs on model performance. The results indicated
that atomic radius, along with either valence electron concentration, electronegativity, or electron
affinity, had the greatest influence on the model's accuracy. Additionally, incorporating the
melting point of atomic constituents into the input set improved accuracy by over 50%, although
the model still fell short of the 80% target. This finding suggests that while fundamental atomic

 7

properties are valuable for prediction, additional features may be necessary to achieve higher
accuracy.

In conclusion, the methodology employed in this research ensured that the ANN model was built
on a solid foundation of high-quality data and rigorous validation techniques. The insights gained
from this study advance our understanding of alloy behavior by pointing out which fundamental
properties of elements are most important in predicting the phase transition behavior of eutectics.

Evaluating ANN Models

First, we will evaluate the performance of the models based on the model “shape,” which we
refer to as the number of nodes per layer. Models with a diamond shape, for example, contain
only as many nodes as inputs/outputs on the input/output adjacent layers, with the number of
nodes in the layer constantly increasing as one moves towards the central layer, which contains a
defined maximum number of nodes. An hourglass-shaped model is the opposite of this case, with
a maximum number of nodes on the input/output ends and a minimum number of nodes (we used
3) in the center. Square-shaped models have the maximum number of nodes in each layer. A
double diamond-shaped model has the maximum number of nodes 25% and 75% of the way
through the layers, with diamond and hourglass characteristics at the extremes and the center,
respectively. Figure 3 shows the accuracy, defined as the percent of the time the model
accurately predicts both the fractional composition and melting temperature at the eutectic point,
plotted as a function of the number of layers and maximum number of nodes for the various

ANN shapes explored. The accuracy of the model is relatively unchanged, suggesting that the
inputs, rather than a specific model structure, are driving the predictive capability.

Figure 3: Accuracy, defined as the percent of the time the model accurately predicts, on the validation data, both the fractional
composition and the melting temperature at the eutectic point to within 5%.

Calculations were carried out on 8-200 maximum nodes and 1-100 layers. We show the relevant
subset of the calculations where the average accuracy changes appreciably. In particular, the
accuracy quickly drops to near-zero once the number of layers goes beyond 33, and the
performance increases and then stabilizes with the increase in the number of nodes. Investigation
of the output vectors from the models with very many (>35) layers showed that the model
converged to a configuration where it gave the same output irrespective of the input, suggesting
the learning algorithm was getting stuck in a local minimum.

From the calculated model accuracies, we obtain an average of the accuracy of models having
between 8-100 nodes and 10-33 layers. This value, normalized to the performance of a square-
shaped ANN with the entire set of input parameters used, is defined as the accuracy metric of a
given model configuration. This provides a benchmark to evaluate the changes in performance of
different ANN models.

To determine the impact of the various inputs on model performance, an ablative procedure is
used where the accuracy metrics of models containing various subsets of the input parameters are
evaluated. Table 2 shows the accuracy metrics of six models, each of which has one input
parameter removed. The results indicate that atomic radius, valence electron concentration,
electronegativity, and electron affinity have the biggest impact on the accuracy. Removing
atomic number or atomic weight has a negligible effect on the accuracy, but when both are
removed, the accuracy is significantly impacted.

Using the entire set of fundamental properties we are only able to achieve a maximum accuracy
of ~9%. Therefore, we consider the addition of other material properties into the model to
improve the model’s accuracy. The melting point of the constituents of an alloy, while a property
of materials and not of atoms, is an obvious choice, as it defines the extrema of the phase
diagram’s liquid line/surface. Figure 4 plots the accuracy matrix of a model run with all the
inputs plus the melting point of the constituents. The inclusion of melting point improved the
accuracy metric to 1.5, a 50% increase over the next best run without the melting point. While
still well below the 80% threshold, including the melting points provided a substantial boost to
the predictive accuracy.

Evaluating ANN Models

We now lay out the various tests performed to evaluate ANN model reliability. First, we rule out
the possibility that the accuracy observed is, in fact, merely the model being a “good guesser.”
To do this, we look at the accuracy matrix of a square model containing all inputs versus that of
the same model where the elements of the validation output vectors were changed to random
numbers between 0 and 1, shown in Figure 5. These results indicate that the model does exhibit
some predictive capability and that the changes in inputs are affecting that predictive power in a
meaningful way.

Figure 5: Accuracy matrices for a square-shaped ANN using all inputs (right) and the same model with the validation output
vectors changed to random numbers (left).

 13

Next, we will test that the code itself contains no bugs and is capable of generating, training, and
evaluating ANN models that have the capacity to predict complex relationships between inputs
and outputs, where they exist. To do so, we use the existing training dataset and code structure,
with the code modified only to change the elements of the training and validation output vectors
to a nonlinear function of the inputs. For simplicity, a model where each element’s atomic
number was the only input was used. The composition output element was set to x1×x2,
where x1 and x2 are the normalized atomic numbers of elements 1 and 2, respectively. The
melting point output element was set to (x1−x2)^2. Without further optimization, the model was
able to predict both output vectors of the validation set correctly up to 80% of the time when
using up to 100 nodes, shown in Figure 6. This shows that the model is capable of predicting
complex nonlinear relationships between inputs and outputs, and there are no errors being
introduced by bugs in the code.

Figure 6: Accuracy of the ANN model where each element of the output vectors was changed to be a different nonlinear function
of the inputs.

Now we analyze the dependence of the predictive accuracy on the introduction of spurious data
into the dataset. To do so, we generate bad data by changing the elements of a given number of
output vectors to random numbers between 0 and 1. These are randomly distributed amongst the
training and validation data, and the accuracy matrix is calculated to give the accuracy metric,
which is studied as a function of the number of data output vectors altered. The results are shown
in Figure 7, which plots the accuracy metric as a function of the number of output vectors
altered. This reveals an exponential drop in accuracy as the number of bad vectors is increased,
showing how just one or two erroneous training data points can cause a substantial decrease in

The research conducted in this project is expected to yield significant outcomes that will advance
the field of materials science, particularly in the use of machine learning to aid in the prediction
of the phase transition behavior of eutectic alloys. By leveraging artificial intelligence and
machine learning techniques, specifically artificial neural networks (ANNs), this project
attempted to predict the fractional composition and melting points of eutectic alloys. Although
the project did not meet the success metric of 80% accuracy, with the highest observed accuracy
reaching only 13%, the insights gained from this research are still valuable and impactful.

One of the primary anticipated outcomes is the development of a predictive model that, despite
its limitations, provides insights into the relationships between fundamental atomic properties
and eutectic behavior. The findings from the evaluation of various ANN models suggest that
specific atomic properties, such as atomic radius, valence electron concentration,
electronegativity, and electron affinity, play an important role in determining the eutectic
characteristics of alloys. By identifying these relationships, the results obtained from our model
can serve as a foundation for future studies aimed at improving predictive capabilities in this
area.

While the model's predictive accuracy fell short of the desired threshold, the fact that it achieved
accuracy above random noise levels indicates that there is a meaningful relationship between the
inputs and outputs. This finding is significant, as it suggests that the selected atomic properties

CONCLUSION

This research project has investigated the application of artificial intelligence, specifically
artificial neural networks (ANNs), to predict the fractional composition and melting points of
eutectic alloys based on fundamental atomic properties. Although the project did not achieve the
desired success metric of 80% accuracy, with the highest observed accuracy reaching only 11%,
it has provided valuable insights into the relationships between atomic properties and eutectic
behavior.

The findings indicate that specific atomic properties, such as atomic radius, valence electron
concentration, electronegativity, and electron affinity, significantly influence the eutectic
characteristics of alloys. While the predictive model did not yield a practical tool for immediate
time or cost savings in alloy development, it successfully demonstrated a meaningful relationship
between the selected inputs and outputs, as evidenced by the model's performance exceeding
random noise levels. This foundational understanding paves the way for future research aimed at
refining the model and enhancing its predictive capabilities.

The project has underscored the importance of data quality and the selection of relevant input
features in developing effective predictive models. Insights gained from this research can guide
subsequent studies that explore machine learning techniques for predicting eutectic behavior,
potentially leading to more accurate models in the future.

In conclusion, while the project faced challenges in achieving high predictive accuracy, it has
laid the groundwork for future investigations into the use of artificial intelligence in materials
science. The insights regarding the impact of atomic properties on eutectic behavior will inform
ongoing research efforts and contribute to a broader understanding of alloy design.
