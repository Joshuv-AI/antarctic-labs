Irisin treatment counteracts bone loss and muscle atrophy in aged mice by reducing sclerostin expression in bone and skeletal muscle
Authors: Clelia Suriano, Roberta Zerlotin, Michela Taurino, Manuela Dicarlo, Giuseppina Storlino, Paolo Pignataro, Onofrio D Terrevoli, Luisa Pellicani, Angela Oranger, Giorgio Mori, Giovanni Passeri, Silvia C Colucci, Graziana Colaianni, Maria Grano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver the past decade, the myokine irisin has been identified as the main molecular mediator of physical exercise, with a remarkable effect on muscle and bone. Given its anabolic role, we sought to determine whether prolonged treatment with irisin could be a therapeutic option to counteract age-related osteosarcopenia.MethodsTo this end, 12-month-old C57BL6 mice were treated with vehicle or irisin (s.c. 100 µg/kg/weekly) for 8 months. Femurs were analyzed by micro-computed tomography, 3-point-bending assay and histomorphometry. Vastus Lateralis muscles were subjected to histological and molecular analysis. To evaluate the effect of irisin on sclerostin, its expression was assessed in bone marrow, cortical bone and skeletal muscle ex vivo, and in C2C12 myoblasts in vitro.ResultsThe mice treated with irisin showed increase in cortical tissue mineral density (TMD) (p = 0.008), bone surface (BS) (p = 0.036), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) (p = 0.02), and number of trabeculae (Tb. N) (p = 0.0002) compared with vehicle-mice. Irisin decreased the number of osteoclasts (p = 0.0056), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase positive (TRAP+) osteocytes (p = 0.0292), and Sost expression in the bone marrow (p = 0.026) and cortical bone (p = 0.032). Furthermore, irisin preserved the morphological organization of muscle fibers and decreased the expression of muscle atrophy genes, Atrogin (p = 0.0168) and Muscle Ring-Finger Protein-1 (Murf1) (p = 0.0452). Notably, we observed a 2-fold lower sclerostin positivity in the muscle of irisin-treated mice than in vehicle mice (p = 0.026). In vitro experiments demonstrated that Sost was downregulated in C2C12 myoblasts treated with irisin either for 8 h (p = 0.0001) or intermittently for 1 week (p = 0.0004). On the other hand, sclerostin treatment in myoblasts affected their differentiation marker genes and downregulated the irisin precursor Fibronectin domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5) (p = 0.009).ConclusionsOverall, this study demonstrates that irisin is a systemic mediator capable of counteracting the effects of aging on the musculoskeletal system. Furthermore, our study provides evidence of sclerostin expression in skeletal muscle and the negative effect of this osteokine on myoblast differentiation. Therefore, the ability of irisin to downregulate sclerostin expression in both bone and skeletal muscle highlights its functional versatility, which could find application as a therapeutic approach for the treatment of osteosarcopenia.Keywords: Osteosarcopenia, Aging, Irisin, Sclerostin, Musculoskeletal system

Background

Aging is intrinsically associated with a coordinated decline in muscle mass and bone mineral density, a clinical phenomenon often described as osteosarcopenia. In recent years, the understanding of inter-organ communication has revealed that skeletal muscle is not merely a motor organ, but a dynamic endocrine tissue capable of secreting specific cytokines, termed myokines, in response to physical activity [1]. Among these, irisin has emerged as one of the most promising mediators in the muscle-bone communication [2–4]. Originally discovered in 2012, irisin is a 112-amino acid peptide derived from the proteolytic cleavage of the transmembrane protein fibronectin type III domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5) [5]. Although its initial function was linked to the browning of white adipose tissue and thermogenesis, research on murine models has demonstrated that bone is a primary and highly sensitive target of this myokine, with anabolic effects manifesting at dosages significantly lower than those required to influence fat metabolism [6].

Irisin is exceptionally conserved across species, with a 100% identical amino acid sequence between mice and humans, conferring significant translational value to studies conducted on murine models [7]. Its production increases significantly during aerobic exercise and muscle contraction [8]. However, the natural senescence process is characterized by a drastic reduction in circulating irisin levels and FNDC5 expression in muscle tissues [9]. In the serum of 24-month-old aged mice, used as a model of sarcopenia, a significant reduction in irisin levels has been observed [9], mirroring what has been observed in aged human population, where circulating irisin levels show a negative correlation with age and risk of fragility fractures [10, 11].

Exogenous administration of recombinant irisin has been tested in some studies to evaluate its ability to reverse senile bone loss in mice. Protocols vary significantly in terms of dosage, frequency, and duration, reflecting the complexity of the biological response to this myokine [12–13].

The efficacy of irisin in osteoporosis also derives from its ability to modulate bone resorption. Although irisin is primarily known as an anabolic agent promoting bone formation, it exerts crucial control over osteoclast activity. Irisin treatment indeed increases the expression of osteoprotegerin (OPG) by osteoblasts, thereby reducing osteoclast formation and activity [14, 15]. In murine models of menopausal and disuse osteoporosis, it has been observed that irisin increases the ratio between osteoblasts and osteoclasts, shifting the balance of bone remodeling toward a net gain in mass [16, 17]. Some studies indicate that irisin may also have direct action on osteoclast precursors, although this is strictly dependent on dose and treatment duration [18].

Additionally, our understanding of human physiology has recently undergone a paradigm shift with the recognition of the signaling pathway via myokine and osteokine secretion between muscle and bone tissues [19, 20]. This molecular communication, defined as “muscle-bone crosstalk,” is fundamental for maintaining the structural integrity of the locomotor apparatus [21]. Among the molecules identified in this complex signaling network, irisin and sclerostin emerge as two key players, whose interactions govern not only bone mineral density but also influence metabolic, cardiovascular, and oncological pathologies [22].

In a study on 3-month-old hind-limb unloaded mice, a murine model used to simulate the effects of disuse and weightlessness, irisin treatment reduced the expression of sclerostin, a potent inhibitor of bone formation that increases dramatically during disuse [16]. In another study on 3-month-old orchiectomized rats, an increase in sclerostin and a decrease in irisin serum levels were observed [23].

However, to date, few studies have evaluated the impacts of therapeutic treatment with irisin in elderly mouse models, including possible modulation of sclerostin synthesis, with particular attention to the musculoskeletal system as a unit. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the therapeutic potential of irisin to counteract osteosarcopenia in aged mice. To achieve this goal, we subjected 12-month-old mice to prolonged treatment with irisin (8 months) administered at a low dose (100 µg/kg) according to an intermittent protocol (once per week) via subcutaneous injection. The dosage of irisin, administered in mice according to a protocol that mimics moderate physical activity, (i.e. intermittently), has been shown to be successful in recapitulating the positive effects of the exercise on bone, brain and skeletal muscle [4, 16, 17, 24, 25]. In particular, the choice of this type of administration, compared to intraperitoneal or intravenous injections, would make it easier to replicate in human clinical practice, with greater patient compliance, the use of irisin as a possible therapeutic option for the treatment of osteosarcopenia.

Here we demonstrated that low-dose long intermittent treatment with recombinant irisin is effective in reducing age-related femoral bone loss in both cortical and trabecular compartments. In parallel, results showed that irisin preserves muscle fibers morphology and reduces the expression of atrophic genes. Mechanistically, irisin exerts its anabolic effect by reducing sclerostin synthesis in bone and muscle tissues. Ultimately, in vitro studies outline a model in which irisin and sclerostin interact in a negative feedback system.

Methods

Animals and experimental design

All experimental procedures were conducted in full compliance with the European Law Implementation of Directive 2010/63/EU. The experimental protocols were examined and approved by the Veterinary Department of the Italian Ministry of Health (Project: 12-2022-PR). Twelve-month-old male and female C57BL6/J mice (N = 35) (Charles River, Wilmington, MA, USA) were randomly distributed into two groups and treated subcutaneously with either sterile physiological saline filtered through a 0.22 µ membrane (vehicle, N = 15) or recombinant irisin (AG-40B-0128-C010, Adipogen International, San Diego, CA, USA) at a dose 100 µg/kg weekly (irisin, N = 19) for a period of eight months. The sample size was estimated through a power analysis using the G-power3, considering an effect size of 0.25, type I error = 0.05 and test power = 80% based on our previous data. To minimize allocation bias related to treatment timing or cage position, such as day on which animals were treated or their cage location, we used the method of randomization within blocks. Specifically, the experiment was divided into smaller sub-experiments, and treatments were randomized within each block. Mice were randomly allocated to blocks, homogeneous in age and sex, and treatments and analyses were performed by investigator blinded to the experimental groups. Both male and female animals were analyzed, and comparable outcomes were observed across sexes. Mice were housed in standard conditions in temperature-controlled rooms with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided a standard chow diet. Body weight was recorded weekly. After 8 months of treatment, body weight did not differ significantly compared with baseline in either the vehicle-treated group or the irisin-treated group (Table 1). The mortality rate during the 8-month treatment period, calculated as the number of dead mice/total mice × 100, was 16.66% in the vehicle group and 13.33% in the group of mice treated with irisin (Table 1). At the completion of the treatment protocol, mice were euthanized with CO2 and their tissues surgically excised. At the time of sacrifice, a visual inspection of the body revealed no lesions or masses. Moreover, macroscopic examination, including inspection of all orifices and dissection of the body cavities to assess the organs in situ prior to tissue collection, revealed no abnormalities.

Micro-CT analysis

Ex-vivo analysis of femurs by micro-computed tomography (microCT) was carried out to assess the impact of irisin on bone. The acquisition protocol, image analyses, parameters, and terminology followed the guidelines described by Bouxsein et al. [26] After fixation in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde, bone samples were preserved in 70% ethanol. The femurs were positioned vertically in the scanner, aligned along their longitudinal axis, and rotated during acquisition. Images were obtained using a Bruker Skyscan 1276 version 1.8 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). The acquisition parameters were Voxel size: 7.99 µ3, Peak tube potential: 60 kV, X-ray intensity: 160 µA, Aluminium foil: 0.5 mm, Sample rotation: 360°, Rotation step: 0.4°, Frame averaging: 2, Exposure time: 257 ms. Raw images were reconstructed with SkyScan reconstruction software (NRecon version 1.6.10.1) (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium), resulting in 3D cross-sectional image data sets employing a 3D cone beam algorithm. Reconstruction settings included an attenuation coefficient range of 0.01–0.11; correction for misalignments overlapping sub-scans; mild beam-hardening correction = 40%; and ring artifact correction = 5. Final images were recorded in 8-bit PNG format. To calibrate bone density measurement, three hydroxyapatite (HA) phantoms (0.25 and 0.75 g·cm− 3, and 2 mm diameter) were scanned using the same parameters, allowing the calculation of volumetric BMD. Structural indices were obtained with CT Skyscan analyzer (CTAn version 1.20.8.0) software (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). To identify the region of interest (ROI) for trabecular bone, an irregular anatomical region of interest near the endocortical boundary was chosen and drawn with an automatic algorithm. A region of interest of regular and uniform shape was selected for cortical bone. The cortical and trabecular portions of bone were split using a personalized processing algorithm in CTAn, which considered the different thicknesses of the structures. To analyze the femur, cortical bone was assessed at the level of the mid-shaft of the femur. The scan volume of the mid-shaft of the femur was centered on the midpoint of the bone, defined as half the distance from the distal condyle to the proximal point of the femoral head for all mice. Cortical bone analysis was performed utilizing a portion of 150 slices, starting 9 mm distal to the metaphysis. Trabecular bone analysis was performed, taking into consideration the region of the proximal metaphysis, starting from the portion proximal to the distal growth plate and extending distally for 200 slices. The cortical parameters included cortical bone mineral density (Ct. TMD), cortical bone surface (Ct.BS), periosteal bone perimeter (Ct. Per. Pm). The trabecular parameters included bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), Fractal Dimension (FD) and Degree of Anisotropy (DA).

3-point bending test of bone segments

After micro-CT scanning, the femurs were tested by means of a 3-point bending test. Mechanical testing was performed using a Zwick tensile testing machine (ZwickiLine Z1.0) (sn: 734188-2019, Zwick Roell, Ulm, Germany) equipped with a 200 N load cell and the specified test parameters as follows: distance of lower supports: 8 mm; pre-load: 0.1 N; speed until pre-load: 20 mm/min. A loading rate of 1 mm/min was applied in the medial-to-lateral direction. The software used to control the machine and for data acquisition and analysis was Zwich/Roell testXpert III version 1.4. The bones were stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C before testing and were harvested to be rehydrated and kept at room temperature in time before mechanical testing (> 30 min). The bone segments were carefully cleared of any remaining soft tissue. Before testing, all specimens were subjected to measurement of length and diameter at mid-shaft in the direction of breaking force using an appropriate caliper. During testing, femurs were placed similarly on the supports, with the distal end on the right, the proximal side on the left, and the posterior surface downward. The experimental setup and analytical approach followed the recommendations by Jepsen K.J. et al. [27]. The displacement (d) and force (F) obtained were used to calculate parameters delineating the structural properties of the whole bone.

Histological analysis of muscle

Muscle biopsies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, US) for 24 h, then transferred to a 15% sucrose solution and subsequently stored in a 70% ethanol solution. Muscle samples were dehydrated through graded ethanol concentration and embedded in paraffin. Histological sections were cut with a thickness of 5 μm using a standard microtome (RM-2155 Leica, Heidelberg, Germany). To perform staining procedures, paraffin was removed with Xylene solution, and the sections were rehydrated through decreasing ethanol concentrations. All reagents and dyes were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Longitudinal and transverse sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). On transverse sections we quantified fiber number and cross-sectional area (CSA), allowing measurement of skeletal muscle total area. Transverse sections were then stained with Picro-Sirius Red Stain to assess the percentage of collagen content in nonmyocyte area compared with skeletal muscle total area.

Stained sections were digitalized using the Aperio ScanScope CS whole-slide imaging system (Leica Biosystems) at the 20x magnification and stored as high-resolution digital images on the workstation associated with the instrument. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software, manually outlining the perimeter of each muscle fiber to obtain CSA measurements. Collagen deposition was quantified as the percentage of the red-stained area relative to the total tissue area, for each section, using color-based thresholding in ImageJ software version 1.53 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each section, at least 5 high power fields were analyzed by two independent observers.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was carried out on 5 μm-thick paraffin-embedded sections of vastus lateralis and femur samples. Following dewaxing, antigen retrieval was performed with treatment by protease treatment (Proteinase K; Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA) at a concentration of 0. 1 mg/mL in PBS for 2 min at 37 °C. After thorough washing in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 0.3% H2O2 (in PBS) for 30 min. Subsequently, sections were incubated with either a polyclonal rabbit anti-MyHC II antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab91506) or a rabbit anti-Sclerostin antibody (Antibodies, Cat#A10341) for 1 h at room temperature. After additional PBS washes, sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody (AB2; Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection IHC Kit, Abcam) at RT for 30 min, followed by incubation with streptavidin alkaline phosphatase (Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection IHC Kit, Abcam) at RT for 10 min. Finally, chromogenic detection was achieved using DAB at RT for 5 min, and slides with sections mounted with glycergel (Agilent Dako). The negative controls were processed using the same protocol, replacing the primary antibody with Antibody diluent (Abcam, Cat# ab64211). Stained sections were digitized using the Aperio CS2 scanner (Leica Biosystems) at 20x magnification. At least 5 high power fields were analyzed for each section. No Staining was detected when the primary antibody was omitted. The percentage of MyHC II and Sclerostin positive fibers was quantified using color-based thresholding on ImageJ software version 1.53 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Histological analysis of bone

After sacrifice, femurs were fixed in ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h and then maintained in ethanol 70%. Decalcification was carried out with 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) at 4 °C. Subsequently, the femurs were dehydrated with increasing ethanol concentration solutions and included in paraffin (A6330, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, U.S.). Paraffin blocks were cut using a standard microtome (RM-2155 Leica, Heidelberg, Germany) and 5-mm-thick section were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) (HHS32-1 L, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, U.S.) or with Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) (387 A Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, U.S.) and counterstained with Fast Green (42053, Millipore, Massachusetts, U.S.). All observations were conducted after scanning stained sections by Aperio CS2 (Leica Biosystems) at 20x and 40x magnifications. Three sections for each mouse and five high-power fields per section were analyzed for counting osteoclast number per bone perimeter (OCs N./BP), osteocyte number per bone area (OSy N./BA), and TRAP+ osteocyte number per bone area (TRAP + OSy N./BA). The analysis was performed using ImageJ software version 1.53 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Cell cultures

The cell line C2C12 (myoblasts) was purchased by ATCC (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and amplified up to passage P6. C2C12 were seeded at 10 × 103 cells/cm2 and cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) with 10% of fetal bovine serum (FBS), (Gibco, Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) until they reached confluence in a humidified atmosphere (37 °C, 5% CO2), (Hera cell 150, Themo-Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). To investigate the effect of irisin on Sost expression in myoblasts, C2C12 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL recombinant-irisin (AG-40B-0128-C010, Adipogen International, San Diego, CA, USA) for 8 h. To examine the effect of a longer intermittent exposure to irisin, myoblasts were treated with 100 ng/ml of irisin for 8 h, and then the medium was replaced by fresh medium without irisin. The procedure was repeated three times every 48 h. After 6 days cells were lysed and subjected to RNA extraction.

To study the effect of sclerostin in myoblasts, C2C12 cells were treated with 1 ng/ml recombinant Sclerostin (1589-ST, R&D system, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for 8 h and then cells were lysed and subjected to RNA extraction. To investigate whether sclerostin modulates the expression of the irisin precursor FNDC5, C2C12 myoblasts were plated at 5 × 103 cells/cm2 on 1-cm2 glass coverslip in 24-well plates with 10% FBS α-MEM (Gibco, Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). After 2 days, myoblasts were treated with 1 ng/ml recombinant Sclerostin for 24 h and then fixed with 10% formalin before carrying out immunocytochemistry.

Immunocytochemistry

Myoblasts fixed on glass coverslip were permeabilized with Triton X-100 0.1% in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and non-specific interactions were blocked with PBS-T (PBS + Tween-20) for 60 min at RT. After washing in PBS, myoblasts were incubated with the polyclonal rabbit anti-FNDC5 antibody (Abcam, Cat#ab181884) for 1 h at room temperature. After a thorough rinse in PBS, cells were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody (AB2; Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection IHC Kit, Abcam) at RT for 10 min; then with streptavidin alkaline phosphatase (Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection IHC Kit, Abcam) at RT for 10 min; finally cells were incubated with chromogen DAB at RT for 5 min, then counterstained with hematoxylin (HHS32-1 L, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, U.S.) and mounted in glycergel (Agilent Dako). For negative controls, the same method was followed replacing the primary antibody with Antibody diluent (Abcam, Cat# ab64211). Staining was never observed when the primary antibody was omitted. Percentage of FNDC5 positive myoblasts per total cellularity was calculated by using ImageJ software version 1.53 (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Real-time PCR

Muscle and bone samples were homogenized with ultra-turrax T8 (Ika, Staufenim Breisgau, Germany). Total RNA from tissue homogenates, and from whole bone marrow and myoblast lysates were extracted using spin columns (RNeasy, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was executed with iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) in the thermal cycler (My cycler; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Real-Time PCR on the CFX96 real-time system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) was performed with SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) for 40 cycles (denaturation 95 °C for 5 s; pairing/extension 60 °C for 10 s) after an initial 30-second phase for enzyme activation at 95 °C. The primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). All primers span an exon–exon junction. The primer sequences were as follows: Gapdh (S-acaccagtagactccacgaca, AS-acggcaaattcaacggcacag); beta-2-Microglobulin (S-tgctatccagaaaacccctca, AS-tttcaatgtgaggcgggtgg); Sost (S-tgcctcatctgcctacttg; AS-ggtctgcctccattctcc); Atf4 (S-gcctgactctgctgcttacattac; AS-cacgggaaccacctggagaag); OPG (S-gaccactcttatacggacag, AS-ctcacactcacacactcg); DKK1 (S-tctctatgagggcgggaaca; AS- acggagccttcttgtccttt); Fndc5 (S-gtgctgatcattgttgtggtccc, AS-atcatatcttgcggaggag), AS-attgttgtggtcctcttc); Pax7 (S-agccgagtgctcagaatcaag, AS-catccagacggttccctttgt); Myf5 (S-ctgacggcatgcctgaatgta, AS-caatccaagctggacacgga); MRF-4 (S-ctgctaaggaaggaggagcaaa, AS-acgatggaagaaaggcgctg); Myogenin (S-cccaacccaggagatcatttg, AS-cagttgggcatggtttcgtc); MyoD (S- atggcatgatggattacagcg, AS- tggagatgcgctccactatg); Atrogin (S- tgagcgacctcagcagttac, AS- gcgctccttcgtacttcctt); MuRF1 (S- accttcctctcaagtgccaag, AS- tcccaaagtcaatggccctc). The geometric mean of two housekeeping genes (Gapdh, B2M) was utilized to normalize the data. For each biological replicate we performed three independent technical replicates, and quantitative measures were calculated by the 2-ΔΔCT method and expressed as relative fold change from control.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) was used for statistical analysis and graph design. All data are shown as box-and-whisker plots with median and interquartile ranges, from max to min, with all data points shown. Based on normality assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, differences were evaluated using a parametric t-test or a non-parametric Mann-Whitney test, and a Pearson or Spearman test for correlations, respectively. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Using ROUT test, we identified extreme values as outliers and excluded them from statistical analysis.

Results

Irisin increases cortical and trabecular bone mass in aged mice

To determine whether irisin protects against age-related bone loss, we examined femurs of 20-month-old mice that had been treated for 8 months with vehicle or irisin (s.c. 100 µg/kg/weekly). To assess changes in bone structure, we performed micro-CT analysis on cortical and trabecular bone at femoral midshaft and in the proximal metaphysis region, respectively. As represented by microCT-generated section images (Fig. 1a), micro-CT analysis revealed that irisin treatment increased tissue bone mineral density (TMD) (p = 0.008) (Fig. 1b), bone surface (p = 0.036) (Fig. 1c), and periosteal perimeter (p = 0.04) (Fig. 1d) of cortical bone. We also observed an increase in trabecular bone volume fraction (BV/TV) (p = 0.02) (Fig. 1e) and number of trabeculae (Tb. N) (p = 0.0002) (Fig. 1f), without affecting trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) (p = 0.086) (Fig. 1g), consistently with a decrease of trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) (p = 0.006) (Fig. 1h) in Irisin-treated mice versus vehicle-mice. Noteworthy, irisin significantly increased fractal dimension (FD) (p = 0.0004) (Fig. 1i) and reduced the degree of anisotropy (DA) (p = 0.035) (Fig. 1j) in trabecular bone of irisin-treated mice compared with vehicle-mice, thereby indicating an improved microstructural organization of the trabeculae. Furthermore, to determine whether irisin treatment influenced the mechanical properties of the femurs, we evaluated stiffness (Fig. 1k), bending stress (Fig. 1l) and maximum load to fracture (Fig. 1m). Results showed that irisin treatment significantly increased the bending stress of femurs (p = 0.034) (Fig. 1l).

Irisin decreases the number of osteoclast and TRAP positive osteocyte in trabecular and cortical bone of aged mice

To understand which bone cells were involved in the action of irisin for bone mass recovery in aged mice, we performed a histological analysis of the femurs. As shown in Fig. 2a, osteoclast number (OCs N.) per bone perimeter (BP) decreased in trabecular bone of femurs from irisin-treated mice (p = 0.006) (Fig. 2b) compared with vehicle-mice. As shown in Fig. 2c, we also analyzed in cortical bone the number of viable osteocytes (Osy N.) per bone area (BA), which did not differ between vehicle- and irisin-treated mice (Fig. 2d). However, the number of TRAP-positive osteocytes (Fig. 2e) was significantly decreased in the femurs of irisin-treated mice (p = 0.03) (Fig. 2f).

Irisin downregulates the expression of Sost in bone marrow and in cortical bone of aged mice

To verify whether irisin treatment affected the expression of key transcription factors and proteins produced by osteoblast precursors, we analysed bone marrow isolated from femurs. Despite the effect of irisin on the expression of activating transcription factor 4 (Atf4) mRNA previously observed in marrow of young healthy mice [6], irisin treatment failed to increase Atf4 expression in the bone marrow of aged mice (Fig. 3a), nor did it modify osteoprotegerin (OPG) expression (Fig. 3b). Noteworthy, we observed a significant decrease of Sost mRNA expression, the gene codifying for sclerostin, in the bone marrow (p = 0.026) (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, by analyzing gene expression in cortical bone, we observed an irisin-mediated increase in OPG expression (p = 0.06) (Fig. 3d), although bordering on significance, and a marked downregulation of Sost (p = 0.032) (Fig. 3f), whereas the expression of Dickkopf-related protein 1 (Dkk1), the other inhibitor of the bone anabolic Wnt signalling pathway [28], remained unchanged (Fig. 3e).

Irisin preserves muscle fibers morphology and downregulates the expression of atrophy markers in skeletal muscle of aged mice

Histological analysis of longitudinal muscle fibers from the Vastus Lateralis showed that 8-month treatment with irisin preserved morphological organization of the fibers compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 4a). To decipher the molecular mechanism involved in the noticeable morphological preservation of muscle fibers in aged mice treated with irisin, we analyzed the expression of the primary genes responsible for muscle atrophy. Muscle atrophy F-box (Atrogin) and muscle RING-finger protein-1 (Murf1) are two muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases, which target specific protein substrates for degradation in the ubiquitin–proteasome system [29]. Results showed that irisin treatment decreased the expression of Atrogin (p = 0.017) (Fig. 4b) and Murf1 (p = 0.045) (Fig. 4c), thus suggesting a control over age-related muscle atrophy. Interestingly, in a linear correlation analysis, we noticed that the expression of Atrogin mRNA was positively correlated with that of Sost in bone marrow (r = 0.77; p = 0.014) (Fig. 4d), suggesting that there might be an irisin-mediated mechanism involving an effect of sclerostin in skeletal muscle. At the same time, histological examination of the Vastus Lateralis showed no changes in muscle fibrosis (Fig. 4e-f) or a possible fiber-type switch induced by irisin, as demonstrated by quantization of myosin heavy chain 2 (MyHC II) positive fibers per total muscle area (Fig. 4g-h).

Sclerostin is expressed by skeletal muscle of aged mice and is negatively affected by irisin treatment

Following an investigation into the expression of Sost in tissues and cells, we noticed that it was expressed at around 60% in muscle tissue and around 35% in myoblasts compared to the positive 100% reference of cortical bone (Fig. 5a). Through immunohistochemistry, we confirmed the expression of sclerostin in the Vastus Lateralis of aged mice treated with vehicle or irisin (Fig. 5b). Remarkably, quantification of the percentage of sclerostin-positive fibers showed its twofold decrease in the vastus lateralis of irisin-treated mice compared to vehicle-treated mice (p = 0.026) (Fig. 5c). In vitro experiments also demonstrated that Sost mRNA was downregulated in C2C12 myoblasts treated with irisin either for 8 h (p = 0.0001) (Fig. 5d) or intermittently for 1 week (p = 0.0004) (Fig. 5e).

Recombinant sclerostin affects the expression of myogenesis markers and FNDC5 in C2C12 myoblasts

To better understand whether Sclerostin expression in skeletal muscle influenced muscle cell proliferation and differentiation, we studied the key factors involved in these processes in myoblasts. Results showed that stimulation with recombinant sclerostin for 8 h decreased the expression of the paired box protein 7 (Pax7) (p = 0.0004) (Fig. 6a), the key transcription factor involved in maintaining the pool of muscle satellite cells. Sclerostin also downregulated the myoblast determination protein 1 (MyoD1) (p = 0.002) (Fig. 6c) and the Myogenin (p = 0.016) (Fig. 6e), transcription factors crucial for muscle cells differentiation. On the other hand, sclerostin upregulated the expression of myogenic factor 5 (Myf5) (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6b) and induced a tendency toward an increase in myogenic factor 6 (Mrf4) (p = 0.105) (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

Here we have demonstrated that long-term intermittent treatment with low dose of recombinant irisin is effective in reducing age-related femoral bone loss. More specifically, the femurs of irisin-treated mice showed an increase in cortical bone mineral density, bone surface, and trabecular bone mass compared to vehicle-treated mice. Notably, irisin significantly increased fractal dimension and reduced the degree of anisotropy, thus indicating that it is effective not only in increasing bone mass but also in improving the microstructural organization of trabecular bone.

The efficacy of irisin in counteracting senile osteoporosis derives from its ability to reduce the number of osteoclasts on bone surfaces. It is indeed known that, although irisin is primarily known as an anabolic agent promoting bone formation, it exerts indirect control over osteoclast activity by increasing OPG expression. 14,15

Additionally, we observe that irisin treatment reduced the number of TRAP-positive osteocytes on cortical bone. The expression of the TRAP enzyme in osteocytes has been attributed to the process of osteocytic osteolysis [30]. Osteocytes contribute to bone remodeling through the process of osteocytic osteolysis, whereby they remove matrix within their lacuno-canalicular network [31]. Previous studies demonstrated that this osteocytic osteolysis directly contributes to bone loss under conditions of high bone turnover, such as lactation and hyperparathyroidism [32], and additionally in the case of senile osteoporosis [33], as also suggested here for the mouse model of aging.

Concurrently, the results showed that irisin preserves muscle fiber morphology and reduces the expression of Atrogin and Murf1, thus suggesting control over age-dependent muscle atrophy. In accordance with these findings, a previous study observed that the expression levels of atrophic genes were increased in the quadriceps muscle of 22-month-old FNDC5 knockout mice [9]. Furthermore, previous literature demonstrated that irisin treatment mitigated muscle atrophy by blocking the increase of Atrogin and Murf1 in the murine model of chronic kidney disease [34]. In vitro studies revealed that exogenous irisin suppresses dexamethasone-induced transcription of Atrogin and Murf1 in C2C12 myotubes [35].

From a mechanistic perspective, our study demonstrates that irisin exerts its anabolic effect by reducing sclerostin synthesis in cortical bone, bone marrow, and skeletal muscle. This allowed us to hypothesize a model in which irisin and sclerostin interact in a negative feedback system. Accordingly, in vitro studies showed that Sost was downregulated in irisin-treated myoblasts and that sclerostin treatment reduced the expression of the precursor of irisin, FNDC5.

Scientific evidence suggests a complex bidirectional relationship between these two molecules. Irisin is released from muscle in response to physical activity and has the ability to profoundly influence bone metabolism [36]. Concurrently, physical activity reduces sclerostin levels, allowing the Wnt pathway to promote new bone formation, while sedentary lifestyle or disuse increases its synthesis, leading to bone loss [37]. The crosstalk between these two molecules represents a homeostatic mechanism that would allow the skeleton to respond in a coordinated manner to the state of muscle activity or inactivity [37]. For example, the irisin-sclerostin axis plays a crucial role in the metabolic complications of patients with type 2 diabetes, in whom circulating irisin levels are significantly reduced, while sclerostin levels tend to be elevated [38]. Experiments conducted during 10-day horizontal bed rest, a ground-based model to simulate microgravity, showed that irisin serum levels significantly decreased while sclerostin was increased at the end of the protocol [39].

One of the interesting discoveries in recent years concerns the origin of circulating sclerostin. Although it has long been considered an almost exclusive product of osteocytes, previous studies conducted on myogenic cell lines and murine primary muscle cells have unequivocally demonstrated that myocytes can synthesize and secrete sclerostin [40]. The molecular characterization of muscle sclerostin production revealed that the gene expression of Sost in muscle is dynamically modulated during the life cycle [40]. In particular, in vivo electro-transfer experiments of a plasmid containing the Sost gene into the hindlimb muscle of young mice confirmed that local muscle overproduction of sclerostin induces significant inhibition of bone growth in immediately adjacent skeletal structures [40]. This phenomenon suggests that sclerostin released from muscle may synergistically interact with that produced by osteocytes, enhancing the negative regulation of osteogenesis in response to periods of physical inactivity or disuse [41].

We also reveal here that myoblasts treated with sclerostin showed a reduction in the expression of Pax7, the key transcription factor involved in maintaining the pool of muscle satellite cells, alongside the reduction of MyoD1 and Myogenin, crucial transcription factors for differentiation into mature myocytes. Conversely, sclerostin upregulated the expression of Myf5, a transcription factor primarily involved in the very early phase of differentiation. This could mean that sclerostin blocks muscle cells in a proliferative phase, in addition to reducing the regenerative capacity provided by satellite cells.

From a muscle health perspective, emerging data suggest that pharmacological inhibition of sclerostin may have clinical benefits that extend beyond the skeleton. Analyses conducted on randomized clinical trials have indicated that romosozumab [42], a humanized monoclonal antibody designed to block sclerostin, could reduce the risk of falls by approximately 20% compared to controls [43]. Since LRP5/6 receptors are expressed in human muscle and canonical Wnt signaling has anabolic effects on muscle fibers, it is hypothesized that romosozumab may improve neuromuscular function through local activation of the Wnt pathway [43].

Limitations

Although our study supports the hypothesis that the irisin-sclerostin axis represents a crucial node in osteosarcopenia, it is necessary to acknowledge some potential limitations. Firstly, our results suggest that irisin may be one of the inhibitors of sclerostin transcriptional regulation in bone and muscle tissues, however, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying irisin-mediated modulation of the Sost gene promoter remain unknown. Additionally, although we have observed a reduction in the expression of atrophy-related genes, further analysis is needed to determine whether this is due solely to irisin or whether the reduction in sclerostin in muscle tissue is also involved. Furthermore, although we did not observe any adverse effects, signs of toxicity, or increased mortality rates in mice treated with prolonged administration of irisin, the translation of irisin-based therapies to humans requires further study. Consequently, long-term safety, feasibility of chronic administration, and efficacy of recombinant irisin remain key challenges for a possible therapy with irisin in human patients.

Conclusions

Altogether, this study demonstrates that irisin is a systemic mediator capable of counteracting the effects of aging on the skeleton in mice. Furthermore, its ability to modulate sclerostin highlights a functional versatility that has yet to be fully explored. Its intermittent administration could mimic the benefits of exercise in elderly individuals unable to perform intense physical activity, offering an innovative solution to combat senile osteoporosis and improve quality of life in the geriatric population.
