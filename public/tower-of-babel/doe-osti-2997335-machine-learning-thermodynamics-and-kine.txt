ABSTRACT
Atomistic defects play a pivotal role in functional and structural materials’ performance across a
myriad of technology applications. Quantitative prediction of the thermodynamics and kinetics
of defect formation and migration, respectively, typically requires accurate but expensive first-
principles approaches, such as density functional theory (DFT). Their computational expense
limits the throughput needed to perform high-throughput materials discovery/screening exercises
or to perform materials modeling tasks relying on extensive sampling techniques. Therefore, in
this Sandia National Laboratories Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project
(Project #229366), we developed a variety of machine learning techniques, trained on density
functional theory calculations, to accelerate the discovery and modeling of materials in which
vacancy and interstitial defects primarily dictate material performance. These include applications
such as metal oxides for water-splitting or mixed ionic-electronic conduction, metal hydrides
for hydrogen storage, and transition metal dichalcogenides for electronics, and the approaches
developed herein can further be applied to many other domains that similarly depend on materials’
thermodynamic and kinetic defect properties for their desired functionality.

1. INTRODUCTION

Vacancy defects dictate the critical properties by which a material’s utility is evaluated in a host of
technology applications. These include materials like metal oxides (MOs) and their high entropy
variants (HEOs), whose vacancy properties determine utility across a diverse domains spanning en-
ergy production [1] to next-generation electronics [2]. For materials like transition metal dichalco-
genides (TMDs), vacancy thermodynamics and kinetics substantially determine their resistance to
radiation and suitability as next generation electronics in extreme environments [3]. Discovery of
new and improved material solutions in these applications necessitates rapid computational assess-
ment and screening of these vacancy properties to down-select optimal candidates and narrow the
scope of expensive and time-consuming experiments.

Predicting isolated (point) and correlated (concentrated) vacancy formation enthalpies and vacancy
diffusion activation energies via high-accuracy and costly density functional theory (DFT) repre-
sents the currently required approach [4]. These thermodynamic and kinetic properties of vacancies
represent some of the first and foremost criteria by which a novel candidate material should be
assessed for its utility in defect-mediated applications. However, computational assessment of a
single compound containing many symmetry sites and diffusion paths may necessitate hundreds or
more DFT atomic relaxations, which may be intractable to evaluate even for handfuls of materials.
The challenge becomes even more acute with the combinatorial increase of configurational space
in multi-component/high-entropy materials, which constitute some of the best performing but more
difficult to understand systems.

Computationally efficient machine learning (ML) approaches, trained on sufficient DFT data, can
help alleviate such limitations. Previous work on ML-based prediction of vacancies has employed
carefully hand-engineered features, coupled with non-deep learning approaches for specific mate-
rials classes and applications [5, 6]. By contrast, models such as graph neural networks (GNNs),
which come with the cost or larger training data requirements, operate directly on a crystal structure
and had been extensively developed, since they obviate the need for intuiting important features
which are seldom generalizable across different material classes and applications [7]. These at-
tractive features of GNNs led to a remarkable rise in global property prediction tasks ranging from
compound formation enthalpies to vibrational density of states. However, noticeably lacking were
GNN models for directly predicting local material properties like vacancies from crystal structure
alone.

We therefore aimed to develop GNNs that bridge this gap to directly predict vacancy thermo-
dynamic (formation energies) and kinetic (migration activation energies) target properties. Our
explicit goal was to derive model architectures that would be applicable across differing materials
chemistry and structural space, and whose success depends primarily on the data upon which they
were trained and not the generalizability of the underlying architecture. These GNN-predicted

 13

defect properties, when exhaustively sampled within a given target material and coupled to down-
stream first-principles calculations, were used to connect with macroscopic observable behavior,
like temperature dependent non-stoichiometry or vacancy defect diffusivity (Chapters 2 and 3).
We additionally investigated cross-validation strategies that most fairly assess the generalizability
of these models’ out-of-distribution accuracy and its impact on materials discovery capabilities
(Chapter 4), as well as the practicality of state-of-the-art generative diffusion models to identify
novel candidate structures with desired defect properties (Chapter 5).
However, we also illustrate limitations of ML approaches when insufficient training data can
be generated. In material systems such as ABO3 rare-earth cobaltate perovskites, where subtle
complexities are encountered in converging the DFT calculations of host and vacancy defect
formation energy, insufficient data could be generated to train the defect surrogate models described
in Chapters 2 and 3. To study this important material class, regardless of its compatibility with
ML-accelerated techniques, we adopted a more low-throughput and targeted approach using DFT to
understand the oxygen vacancy thermodynamics and kinetics in the pure compounds (Chapter 10),
followed by experimental measurements of the oxygen vacancy diffusivity on pulsed layer deposition
(PLD) thin films for the pure compounds and high entropy variants with rare-earth A-site alloying
(Chapter 11).
Later on, recognizing that tweaking the GNN approach could render model applicable to interstitial
(e.g., nitrogen and hydrogen) defects in metal alloys as well, we additionally expanded the scope of
the project to predict formation energies of vacancies or interstitials in material classes such as metal
nitrides (Chapter 6), metal hydrides (Chapters 7 and 9), and metal hydridonitrides (Chapter 8).
These computationally efficient ML surrogate models enable sampling intensive first-principles
thermodynamic workflows to connect defect properties to macroscopic observables, such as the
metal hydride phase diagram, or pressure-composition-temperature (PCT) curve.
This report summarizes the key findings of the project across its individual thematic chapters,
which serve as executive summaries for more detailed articles referenced therein and published
across the course of the project.

2. DEFECT GRAPH NEURAL NETWORKS FOR MATERIALS
 DISCOVERY IN HIGH-TEMPERATURE ENERGY
 APPLICATIONS

We present a graph neural network (GNN) approach that fully automates the prediction of defect
formation enthalpies for any crystallographic site from the ideal crystal structure, without the
need to create defected atomic structure models as input. Using density functional theory (DFT)
reference data for vacancy defects in oxides, we trained a defect GNN (dGNN) model that replaces
the DFT supercell relaxations otherwise required for each symmetrically unique crystal site. The
dGNN approach is applicable to arbitrary structures with an accuracy limited principally by the
amount and diversity of the training data, and it is generalizable to other defect types and advanced
graph convolution architectures.

Figure 2-1 schematically shows the dGNN approach. Building off of the crystal graph convolution
neural network (CGCNN) approach [7], the model constructs a graph from a crystalline structure
(with atoms corresponding to nodes, and pairwise interactions corresponding to edges), and assigns

 15

an initial feature vector to each node corresponding to its element type. Feature vectors are updated
according to graph convolution functions, after which the defect site of interest is selected and
passed through a final multi-layer perceptron to compute the vacancy formation energy for that
particular site. Due to its prediction efficiency once trained, this provides a useful high-throughput
screening tool that will aid tackling future materials discovery problems in high temperature energy
applications and beyond.
For example, when interfaced with thermodynamic calculations of reduction entropies and asso-
ciated free energies, the dGNN model can be applied to the screening of oxides in the Materials
Project database, connecting the zero-Kelvin defect enthalpies to high-temperature process condi-
tions relevant for solar thermochemical hydrogen production and other energy applications.
For more details, we refer the reader to [8]: Witman, M. D. et al. Defect graph neural networks for
materials discovery in high-temperature clean-energy applications. Nature Computational Science
2023, 3, 675–686.
For subsequent experimental validation of this computational discovery workflow in the context of
thermochemical water-splitting materials, we refer the reader to [9]: Douglas, T. et al. Large-scale
experimental validation of thermochemical water-splitting oxides discovered by defect graph neural
networks. ChemRxiv 2025, 10.26434/chemrxiv-2025–pm7kn.

3. DEFECT DIFFUSION GRAPH NEURAL NETWORKS FOR
 MATERIALS DISCOVERY IN HIGH-TEMPERATURE ENERGY
 APPLICATIONS

The migration of crystallographic defects dictates material properties and performance for a plethora
of technological applications. Density functional theory (DFT)-based nudged elastic band (NEB)
calculations are a powerful computational technique for predicting defect migration activation
energy barriers, yet they become prohibitively expensive for high-throughput screening of defect
diffusivities. Building upon the defect graph neural network (dGNN) framework, we propose a de-
fect diffusion graph neural netwroks (d2 GNN) framework. Again without introducing hand-crafted
(i.e., chemistry- or structure-specific) descriptors, d2 GNN represents a deep learning approach to
train surrogate models for NEB energies of vacancy migration by hybridizing graph neural networks
with transformer encoders and simply using pristine host structures as input. With sufficient train-
ing data, computationally efficient and simultaneous inference of vacancy defect thermodynamics
and migration activation energies can be obtained to compute temperature-dependent vacancy dif-
fusivities and to down-select candidates for more thorough DFT analysis or experiments, as shown
schematically in Figure 3-1.

Starting from a host structure, the vacancy migration path network on a particular sublattice (in
this case all oxygen atoms) is enumerated for all paths up to a certain distance. Then the trained
d2 GNN model predicts the NEB sequence energies (Δ𝐻 𝑝𝑖 ), which yields both vacancy formation
and migration activation energies, for all paths. From this information, the temperature-dependent
diffusivity (𝐷 𝑇 ) can be computed for the target material [10]. Because of the execution efficiency of
d2 GNN, when used to screen a library of known and hypothetical metal oxides, we can extract key
quantities like the mean diffusivity at a given temperature 𝜆¯𝑇 , as shown in Figure 3-1. This permits
identification of fast, slow, or anisotropic vacancy diffusivity amongst screened materials.
This provides a promising materials discovery and modeling approach for applications in which
vacancy thermodynamics and kinetics are the primary drivers of material performance. One
such application is thermochemical water-splitting materials, where candidates with desired defect
thermodynamics, kinetics, and host stability properties can be more rapidly targeted from open-
source databases of experimentally validated or hypothetical materials using a d2 GNN screening
approach.

4. MATFOLD: SYSTEMATIC INSIGHTS INTO MATERIALS
 DISCOVERY MODELS’ PERFORMANCE THROUGH
 STANDARDIZED CROSS-VALIDATION PROTOCOLS

Machine learning (ML) models in the materials sciences that are validated by overly simplistic
cross-validation (CV) protocols can yield biased performance estimates for downstream modeling
or materials screening tasks. This can be particularly counterproductive for applications where
the time and cost of failed validation efforts (experimental synthesis, characterization, and testing)
are consequential. We propose a set of standardized and increasingly difficult splitting protocols
for chemically and structurally motivated CV that can be followed to validate any ML model
for materials discovery. Among several benefits, this enables systematic insights into model
generalizability, improvability, and uncertainty, provides benchmarks for fair comparison between
competing models with access to differing quantities of data, and systematically reduces possible
data leakage through increasingly strict splitting protocols.

Performing thorough MatFold CV investigations across increasingly strict chemical/structural split-
ting criteria, local vs. global property prediction tasks, small vs. large datasets, and structure
vs. compositional model architectures, some common threads are observed. However, several
marked differences exist across these exemplars, indicating the need for comprehensive, MatFold-
enabled analysis to fully understand each model’s generalization accuracy and potential for materials
discovery. Finally, a general-purpose, featurization-agnostic toolkit, MatFold, is provided to auto-
mate reproducible construction of these CV splits and encourage further community use in model
benchmarking (https://github.com/d2r2group/MatFold).

property models used to make multiple target predictions exist per model input structure, such

MatFold processes a set of base crystal structures, each of which may have multiple unique target
values per structure (i.e., defect formation energies for unique symmetry sites, work functions for
unique Miller surfaces, etc.). Nested (𝐾, 𝐿)-fold CV train/test splits are automatically generated
according to a variety of splitting criteria that increasingly test the out-of-distribution generalization
accuracy of the predictive model.
For more details, we refer the reader to [12]: Witman, M. D.; Schindler, P. MatFold: system-

6. INVESTIGATING THE EFFECTS OF LOCAL ENVIRONMENT
 ON NITROGEN VACANCIES IN HIGH ENTROPY METAL
 NITRIDES

High entropy metal nitrides are an important material class in a variety of applications, and the
role of nitrogen vacancies is of great importance for understanding their stability and mechanical
properties. We study six different high entropy nitrides with eight different metal species to build
a predictive model of the nitrogen vacancy formation energy. We construct sets of supercells
(schematically shown in Figure 6-1a) that maximize the number of unique nitrogen environments
for a given chemistry, and then use density-functional theory to calculate the energy density for all
nitrogen sites, and the vacancy formation energies (𝐸 𝑣 𝑓 ) for the highest, lowest, and a median subset
based on the energy densities. The energy density of nitrogen sites correlates with the vacancy
formation energies, for binary, ternary and high entropy nitrides.

Unlike in previous chapters where a graph neural network approach was required to predict vacancy

predictive and easily interpretable, and correlates with experimental data.

7. PHASE DIAGRAMS OF ALLOYS AND THEIR HYDRIDES VIA
 ON-LATTICE GRAPH NEURAL NETWORKS AND LIMITED
 TRAINING DATA

Efficient prediction of sampling-intensive thermodynamic properties is needed to evaluate material
performance and permit high-throughput materials modeling for a diverse array of technology
applications. To alleviate the prohibitive computational expense of high-throughput configurational
sampling with density functional theory (DFT), surrogate modeling strategies like cluster expansion
are many orders of magnitude more efficient but can be difficult to construct in systems with
high compositional complexity. As shown schematically in Figure 7-1, we therefore employ
minimal-complexity graph neural network (GNN) models that accurately predict and can even
extrapolate to out-of-train distribution formation energies of DFT-relaxed structures from an ideal
(un-relaxed) crystallographic representation. This enables the large-scale sampling necessary for
various thermodynamic property predictions that may otherwise be intractable and can be achieved
with small training data sets. Two exemplars, optimizing the thermodynamic stability of low-
density high-entropy alloys and the plateau pressure of hydrogen in metal alloys, demonstrate the
power of this approach, which can be extended to a variety of materials discovery and modeling
problems.

8. THERMODYNAMIC MODELING OF COMPLEX SOLID
 SOLUTIONS IN THE LU-N-H SYSTEM VIA GRAPH NEURAL
 NETWORK ACCELERATED MONTE CARLO SIMULATIONS

Metal hydrides are important across diverse applications such as hydrogen storage, batteries, gas
sensors, nuclear reactions and high-temperature superconductivity. Previous computational studies
of metal hydrides under extreme pressures, e.g., 𝑂 (102 ) GPa, usually treat them as stoichiometric
compounds without considering interstitial lattice disorder. As pressures become more moderate in
the 𝑂 (100 ) GPa and below range, hydrogen disorder at interstitial lattice sites becomes prominent,
e.g. in the N-doped Lu hydride that was recently claimed superconducting near 1 GPa. Further
adding compositional complexity from alloying and/or multi-element interstitial occupation makes
elucidating pressure- and temperature-dependent observables intractable by first-principles calcu-
lations alone. We therefore propose a lattice graph neural network (GNN) surrogate modeling
approach to predict configuration- and pressure-dependent equation-of-state properties (schemati-
cally shown in Figure 8-1).
Their efficiency permits Monte Carlo simulations to calculate Gibbs energies and pressure-
dependent phase diagrams, thereby revealing insights into the synthesis conditions required for
achieving desired phase equilibria. We demonstrate this concept for the compositionally complex
cubic Lu(H,N,Va)3 system where three constituents (hydrogen, nitrogen and vacancy) have disor-
dered multi-element interstitial occupancies and insights into pressure-dependent phase equilibria
are critically needed, e.g., N-doping levels can significantly lower dehydrogenation temperatures
and provide a new strategy to optimize hydrogen-storage alloys. This work can improve the thermo-
dynamic understanding of the Lu-H-N system and help rational synthesis of N-doped Lu hydrides,
but more generally demonstrates an efficient approach to model pressure-dependent thermodynam-
ics of multi-component solid solutions.
For more details, we refer the reader to [16]: Guan, P.-W. et al. Thermodynamic Modeling of
Complex Solid Solutions in the Lu - H - N System via Graph Neural Network Accelerated Monte
Carlo Simulations. PRX Energy 2025, 4, 033013.

9. PREDICTING PRESSURE-COMPOSITION-TEMPERATURE
 DIAGRAMS TO DISCOVER LOW STABILITY METAL
 HYDRIDES

Quantitatively accurate computational predictions of metal hydride thermodynamics are challeng-
ing but critical for alloy performance optimization across a multitude of technological domains,
including hydrogen storage, compression, purification, and getters. Recent machine learning
approaches have demonstrated great success in this area, but can potentially suffer from sev-
eral shortcomings since they rely on imbalanced experimental training data and can have poor
out-of-distribution (ood) test performance. Here we circumvent such pitfalls by developing a com-
putationally efficient, first principles-based workflow for direct prediction of metal hydride phase
equilibrium, i.e., the pressure-composition-temperature (PCT) diagram (Figure 9-1). We then
demonstrate its utility on predicting low stability hydrides derived from compositionally complex
C14 Laves phase AB2 alloys. Specifically, we computationally predict and then experimentally
validate an AB2 alloy series (𝑧 < 0.6 for Ti2−𝑧 Zr𝑧 CrMnFeNi) with ideal hydriding thermodynamics
for a two-stage metal hydride-based compressor for pressurizing boil off from liquefied hydrogen.
Importantly, this study lays the groundwork for accurate and efficient discovery/optimization of
ood, low-stability hydrides for which purely data-driven approaches lack sufficient accuracy.
For more details, we refer the reader to [17]: Way, L. et al. Efficiently predicting pressure-
composition-temperature diagrams to discover low-stability metal hydrides. ChemRxiv 2025, DOI:
10.26434/chemrxiv-2025-cfjgm.

10. FIRST-PINCIPLES CALCULATIONS OF OXYGEN VACANCY
 THERMODYNAMICS AND KINETICS IN RARE EARTH
 COBALTATES

We perform ab initio calculations of Rare-Earth Cobaltates (RECoO3 ) using Density Functional
Theory (DFT) with the SCAN+U functional to investigate host stability and the formation and
migration energies of oxygen vacancies in the low-temperature limit. We address significant chal-
lenges in converging the calculations due to competing phases with varying space-group symmetries
and the multiplicity of DFT solutions, which depend on the initial magnetic configuration of the
Co atoms. Our findings indicate that the ground state of LaCoO3 belongs to the monoclinic space
group (sg15), while other RECoO3 compounds stabilize with the orthorhombic crystal structure
(sg33).
These ground states exhibit a low-spin configuration for Co magnetic moments, which significantly
contributes to the total energy. Therefore, it is crucial to capture these low-spin moments consis-
tently, rather than relying on non-spin polarized calculations. In the presence of an oxygen vacancy,
adjacent Co atoms transition from a valency of 3+ to 2+, and their magnetic moments shift from
low-spin to high-spin. As the vacancy migrates, the total magnetic moment varies, complicating
the assessment of the diffusion barrier using the nudged-elastic band method. Our results enhance
the understanding of stability and defect properties in RECoO3 , emphasizing the importance of
magnetic configurations in determining material behavior.

11. OXYGEN DIFFUSIVITY MEASUREMENTS IN HIGH ENTROPY
 RARE EARTH COBALTATES

Defect formation and ion kinetics are a key part of understanding the functional properties of
emerging materials for energy storage, energy conversion, computing and many more applications.
Oxygen vacancy formation and diffusion, in particular, has proven to be crucial in the understanding
of many of the material properties, from their electronic transport to their redox performance and
ion-transport properties. Complex oxides, especially those of the ABO3 perovskite family (A=rare
earth, B=transition metal), are shown to be promising candidates and the possibility of tuning
them via doping or substitutions has opened a large material engineering space that is currently
being approached from numerous theoretical and experimental points of view. We have focused on
the effect of compositional complexity, as this route allows property tuning beyond traditional limits.

  AHEO samples during 18 O diffusion experiments. Grey spectra corresponds to reference. Black

In order to study oxygen diffusion, we have relied on isotope exchange between 16 O and 18 O. The
different isotope mass induces a shift of the main vibrational modes in the Raman spectra of the
material, mainly in the breathing mode (A2𝑔 around 670 cm−1 ), which allows fast an easy tracking
of the relative isotope content. To fully understand the mechanisms behind the observed oxygen

diffusion, several experimental routes have been explored.

Amongst those experiments we explored out-diffusion in thin films of pure LaCoO3 and a compositionally-
complex A-site cobaltite, where A is a mixture in equal fraction of five elements (La, Pr, Sm, Gd
and Y) sitting at the A-site in the perovskite structure. The out-diffusion is achieved by initially
annealing thin films in an 18 O rich atmosphere and then subsequent annealing steps in air. This is
seen in Figure 11-1 where Raman spectra for different annealing times are compared. The initial
shift, from gray to black, upon oxygen isotope substituted is gradually recovered with annealing
steps. The speed of diffusion in the high entropy compound is significantly reduced, as can be seen
in Figure 11-3.

To simplify the interpretation, we developed a quasi-1D diffusion system, utilizing free-standing
flakes and a combination of surface passivisation layers and Pt for increased surface kinetics. The

This experiment allows us to study the case when diffusion of the surface is the main limiting factor
and constitutes a novel way to directly measure oxygen diffusion.

results and is summarized in Figure 11-4. This method utilizes a HfO𝑥 layer grown in 18 O atmo-

12. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we have demonstrated how machine learning methods can be used to accelerate the
modeling and discovery of materials in applications in which defect properties are the primary
determinant of material utility. Focusing on vacancy thermodynamics and kinetics, Chapters 2
and 3 demonstrated how graph neural network-based surrogate models could drastically accelerate
predictions of vacancy formation energy and migration energies. By ensuring strict cross-validation
tests to assess their generalizability and accuracy (Chapter 4), these works led to novel materials
discovery capabilities which we specifically demonstrated in the identification of metal oxide
candidates for thermochemical water-splitting. Such screening exercises would have been entirely
intractable using only density functional theory. Furthermore, in Chapter 5 we demonstrated how
generative models for crystal structure prediction could be used to expand the chemical space
(beyond currently known or hypothetical crystal structures) upon which these surrogate models
could be applied to identify promising novel candidates. Finally, when the chemistry and structural
space of interest is comparatively more narrow than metal oxides writ large, such as in the exploration
of rock-salt high entropy metal nitrides, simpler and more interpretable linear machine learning
models were shown (Chapter 6) to be preferable surrogate models for predicting vacancy properties
needed in high-throughput materials screening.

Recognizing that variants of the graph neural network methods presented in Chapters 2 and 3 could
be tweaked to predict formation energies in interstitial defect materials, we applied graph neural
networks to predict the interstitial absorption energy landscape in metal hydrides and hydridonitrides
via high-throughput configurational sampling that would be intractable with density functional
theory. Coupled with increasingly complex first-principles thermodynamic approaches, from
direct sampling to pressure-dependent Monte Carlo simulations, these machine learning accelerated
workflows permitted calculation of the metal hydride phase diagram, i.e., pressure-composition-
temperature isotherms. By making such predictions tractable and accurate, we are able to perform
more accurate hydride modeling and discovery that will impact numerous applications, ranging
from hydrogen storage and compression to superconductivity.

Finally, we additionally investigated the vacancy defect properties in ABO3 metal oxide perovskites,
where B = cobalt and A = rare earth elements, due their potentially interesting properties in en-
ergy and electronics applications. However, the complexity of these materials poses a challenge
for converging density functional theory predictions of vacancy formation energies and migration
energies, even in the pure A-site compounds. Therefore, while we were able to computationally
elucidate interesting vacancy formation and migration energy trends (Chapter 10) as a function of
A element identity, high-throughput calculations for the high entropy A-site alloys were deemed
currently infeasible. As a consequence, this excluded possibilities to train surrogate machine learn-
ing models for more sampling intensive first-principles defect density or diffusivity calculations.
Nonetheless, such high entropy systems are readily synthesized as perfect thin films by pulsed

 37

layer deposition techniques, upon which we developed approaches to measure oxygen vacancy
diffusivity to elucidate how high entropy alloying affects defect transport in these material classes
(Chapter 11).
