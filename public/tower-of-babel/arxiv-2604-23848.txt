Abstract
. Gorenstein toric contact manifolds are good toric contact manifolds with zero first Chern class that are completely determined by certain integral convex polytopes called toric diagrams. The Ehrhart polynomial of these toric diagrams determines and is determined by the contact Betti numbers of the corresponding contact manifolds, i.e. the dimension of their cylindrical contact homology in each degree. In this paper we look into the following natural question: to what extent do these contact invariants determine the Gorenstein toric contact manifold? Flexibility is the norm and we illustrate it with the family of Gorenstein toric contact manifolds that arise as the prequantization of monotone iterated ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} -bundles, i.e. monotone Bott manifolds. In each dimension, the Ehrhart polynomial of their toric diagrams is equal to the Ehrhart polynomial of the cross-polytope, corresponding to the monotone prequantization of ℙ 1 × ⋯ × ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1} , and we describe the unimodular classification of these toric diagrams. On the rigidity side, we will show that the primitive prequantization of ℙ 1 × ⋯ × ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1} is rigid, i.e. completely determined as a Gorenstein toric contact manifold by its contact Betti numbers. More precisely, in each dimension, we show that its toric diagram, which we name small cross-polytope, is the unique toric diagram with its particular Ehrhart polynomial. We will also prove a rigidity result for a family of Gorenstein toric contact manifolds that arise as the primitive prequantization of monotone ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} -bundles over ℙ n − 1 \mathbb{P}^{n-1} .

1.
Introduction

Gorenstein toric contact manifolds are good toric contact manifolds with zero first Chern class.
They are in
1
-
1
correspondence with toric diagrams, i.e. integral simplicial polytopes with
unimodular facets (see Section
4
):

{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\longleftrightarrow(N^{2n+1}_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})\,.

The Ehrhart polynomial of
{\mathcal{D}}
counts its number of rational points and is defined by

L_{\mathcal{D}}(t):=\#(t\mathcal{D}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}),\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\,,

where
t\mathcal{D}=\{t\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}\}
. The Ehrhart polynomial

L_{\mathcal{D}}(t)
is indeed a degree
n
polynomial in
t
and, as proved in
[
4
]
, its
coefficients are contact invariants of
(N^{2n+1}_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})
. More precisely, if we write the Ehrhart
polynomial in the form

L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})\binom{t+n-k}{n}\,,

we have that the non-negative coefficients
h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
are the contact invariants of

(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})
given by

h^{\ast}_{n-k}({\mathcal{D}})=cb_{2k}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})-cb_{2(k-1)}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})\,,

where
cb_{\ast}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})
are the
contact Betti numbers
, i.e.

cb_{\ast}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})=\rank HC_{\ast}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})

where
HC_{\ast}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})
is the cylindrical contact homology with grading given by the SFT degree.
Note that for toric contact manifolds we have that
cb_{\ast}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})=0
whenever
\ast<0
or

\ast
is odd, which means in particular that the Ehrhart polynomial determines and is determined by
the contact Betti numbers.

Remark 1.1
.

As explained in
[
4
]
, despite some foundational technical difficulties in proving that cylindrical contact
homology is a well defined contact invariant, these contact Betti numbers are indeed well defined contact
invariants in many contexts. These include Gorenstein toric contact manifolds that have crepant
(i.e. with zero first Chern class) toric symplectic fillings and all the particular examples considered in this
paper have this property.

In this paper we address the following natural question:

To what extent do these contact invariants determine the Gorenstein toric contact manifold or,
equivalently, to what extent does the Ehrhart polynomial determine a toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}

up to integral translation and
GL(n,\mathbb{Z})
transformations (i.e. up to unimodular equivalence)?

Definition 1.2
.

A Gorenstein toric contact manifold is said to be
rigid
if it is completely determined, as a toric contact
manifold, by its contact Betti numbers. Equivalently,
(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})
is rigid if its toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}
is

Ehrhart determined
, i.e. whenever
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
is a toric diagram with
L_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}(t)=L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(t)
we have
that
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
is unimodularly equivalent to
{\mathcal{D}}
. A Gorenstein toric contact manifold is said to be
flexible

if it is not rigid. Two toric diagrams
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
are said to be
Ehrhart equivalent
if they have the
same Ehrhart polynomial, i.e. if
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(t)=L_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}}(t)
.

The Ehrhart polynomial
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(t)
determines the volume of
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
through its
t^{n}
coefficient.
When
n=1
the volume is the only invariant of a toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}
, up to unimodular equivalence,
and so we have that all Gorenstein toric contact
3
-manifolds are rigid. These are
S^{3}
with its standard
contact structure (toric diagram
=[0,1]
), and the Lens spaces
L^{3}_{p+1}(1,p)
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, with standard
quotient contact structure coming from
S^{3}
(toric diagram
=[0,p+1]
).

Any odd-dimensional sphere
S^{2n+1}
with its standard contact structure is an example of a rigid Gorenstein
toric contact manifold. In fact, its toric diagram is the unimodular simplex

{\mathcal{D}}=\conv\{\bm{0},\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,\ \text{where $\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{n}$ are
the canonical basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$,}

which is well-known to be Ehrhart determined.

Despite these examples, flexibility is the norm and this can be seen already when
n=2
. For example,
given
p\in\mathbb{N}
, the toric contact manifolds

(S^{3}\times S^{2},\xi_{p})\ \text{with toric diagram}\ {\mathcal{D}}_{p}=\conv\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(p+1,p+1)\}

and

S^{\ast}(L^{3}_{p+1}(1,q))\ \text{with toric diagram}\ {\mathcal{D}}_{p,q}=\conv\{(0,0),(1,0),(p+1,q),(p+1,q+1)\}\,,

for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
coprime with
p+1
, where
S^{\ast}(L^{3}_{p+1}(1,q))\cong L^{3}_{p+1}(1,q)\times S^{2}
is
the unit cosphere bundle of
L^{3}_{p+1}(1,q)
, have the same contact Betti numbers

cb_{0}=p\,,\ cb_{2}=2p+1\ \text{and}\ cb_{2k}=2p+2\,,\ \forall\,k\geq n\,.

See
[
1
,
3
]
for more information on these examples.

In this paper we will describe some families of flexible and rigid Gorenstein toric contact
manifolds that arise as the smooth prequantization of monotone toric symplectic manifolds.
These families are interesting both from the toric contact geometry and the Ehrhart theory
points of view.

Remark 1.3
.

Recall that a symplectic manifold
(M^{2n},\omega)
is said to be
monotone
when
the first Chern class
c_{1}(M)
is a multiple of
[\omega]\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{R})
. In the toric
context of this paper, that multiple is always positive and, by default, we will assume that it is equal
to
1
, i.e. a monotone toric symplectic manifold will mean
(M^{2n},\omega)
with
[\omega]=c_{1}(M)
.
Moreover, by default, the prequantization of a monotone toric symplectic manifold
(M^{2n},[\omega]=c_{1}(M))

will be the toric contact manifold
(N^{2n+1},\xi)
obtained as the
S^{1}
-bundle over
M^{2n}
with Chern class

\frac{1}{2\pi}[\omega]=\frac{1}{2\pi}c_{1}(M)\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
, with
\xi
being the horizontal distribution of a
connection
1
-form with curvature
\frac{1}{2\pi}\omega
.

As we recall in Section
4
, if
(M^{2n},[\omega]=c_{1}(M))
is
a monotone toric symplectic manifold, its moment polytope
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be
translated to be a reflexive Delzant polytope and its prequantization
(N^{2n+1},\xi)

is a Gorenstein toric contact manifold with toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}=-{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
where
{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}
is the polar dual of
{\mathcal{P}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{*}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}\leq 1,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{P}\}\,.

For example, when
M^{2n}=\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
with symplectic form
\omega

such that
[\omega]=c_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1})
, we have that
{\mathcal{P}}=[-1,1]^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

is the (hyper-)cube and
{\mathcal{D}}=\diamond_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the cross-polytope:

\diamond_{n}:=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x_{1}|+\cdots+|x_{n}|\leq 1\}\quad\text{(see Figure~\ref{fig:0}).}

The cross-polytope is not Ehrhart determined and our first family arises from a particular class of
toric diagrams that are Ehrhart equivalent to the cross-polytope (see Section
9
).
These turn out to correspond exactly to the Gorenstein toric contact manifolds that arise as prequantizations
of monotone Bott manifolds
B_{n}
(see Section
3
). Bott manifolds are iterated
\mathbb{P}^{1}=S^{2}
-bundles
of the form

B_{n}\xrightarrow{\pi_{n}}B_{n-1}\xrightarrow{\pi_{n-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}B_{1}=\mathbb{P}^{1}\xrightarrow{\pi_{1}}B_{0}=\{\text{point}\}

where

B_{i}=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}\oplus\mathbb{C})\ \text{with}\ \xi_{i}\rightarrow B_{i-1}\ \text{a $\mathbb{C}$-line bundle.}

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
(N^{2n+1},\xi)
be the prequantization of a monotone Bott manifold
(B^{2n},[\omega]=c_{1}(B^{2n}))
, with
reflexive moment polytope
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
{\mathcal{D}}=-{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be its toric diagram. Then

h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})=\binom{n}{k}\,,\ k=0,\ldots,n\,.

In particular, all these
(N^{2n+1},\xi)
are flexible and the corresponding toric diagrams provide a family of polytopes that are
Ehrhart equivalent to the cross-polytope, i.e. for any such toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have that

L_{\mathcal{D}}(t)=L_{\diamond}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{t+n-k}{n}\,.

In Section
9
we will describe and enumerate, up to unimodular equivalence, all these
toric diagrams. Note that when
n\geq 3
these are not all the toric diagrams that are Ehrhart equivalent
to the cross-polytope. Hence, when
n\geq 3
there are Gorenstein toric contact manifolds with the same
contact Betti numbers of a monotone prequantizattion of a monotone Bott manifold that are not any of those
manifolds.

Our second family arises from the fact that the first Chern class of the trivial Bott manifold

\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
is divisible by 2 and we can consider an integral symplectic form

\omega
with
[\omega]=c_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1})/2
. The corresponding prequantization
is still a Gorenstein toric contact manifold, whose toric diagram is what we call in this paper a small
cross-polytope, see Figure
2
and Section
7
. As we prove in Subsection
7.4
,
small cross-polytopes turn out to be Ehrhart determined and so we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

In any dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the prequantization
(N^{2n+1},\xi)
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1}

with symplectic form
\omega
such that
[\omega]=c_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{1})/2
is a rigid
Gorenstein toric contact manifold.

.

Small cross-polytopes share some interesting combinatorial properties with cross-polytopes. See

(N^{2n+1},\xi)
of Theorem

Our third family arises again from primitive prequantizations of monotone toric symplectic manifolds with
non-primitive first Chern class. The examples in this family are not rigid, but the family itself is rigid in the
sense that it contains all Gorenstein toric contact manifolds with these particular contact invariants. For
any
n\geq 2
, the monotone toric symplectic manifolds that give rise to this family are

M^{2n}_{k}=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k}\oplus\mathbb{C})\ \text{where}\ \xi_{k}\to\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\ \text{is a $\mathbb{C}$-line bundle
with $c_{1}(\xi_{k})=k\in\mathbb{Z}$.}

When
0\leq k<n
we have that
M^{2n}_{k}
is monotone, i.e. it has a toric symplectic form
\omega_{k}
with

[\omega_{k}]=c_{1}(M_{k}^{2n})
. When in addition
k\equiv n\pmod{2}
we have
that
c_{1}(M_{k}^{2n})
is divisible by
2
. The Gorenstein toric contact manifolds
(N^{2n+1}_{k},\xi)

in this family are the prequantizations of these
(M^{2n}_{k},\omega)
with

[\omega]=c_{1}(M_{k}^{2n})/2
and
0\leq k<n
,
k\equiv n\pmod{2}
. As we will show in
Section
10
, the Ehrhart polynomial of the corresponding toric diagrams
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

is independent of
k
and given by

L_{{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{t+n-j}{n}\,,\ \text{i.e. $h^{\ast}_{0}=\cdots=h^{\ast}_{n-1}=1$ and $h^{\ast}_{n}=0$.}

Moreover, we have the following rigidity theorem (cf. Theorem
10.9
).

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(N^{2n+1}_{\mathcal{S}},\xi_{\mathcal{S}})
be a Gorenstein toric contact manifold determined by a toric diagram
{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that

L_{{\mathcal{S}}}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{t+n-j}{n}\,.

Then
{\mathcal{S}}
is unimodularly equivalent to
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
for a unique
k\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
0\leq k<n
,
k\equiv n\pmod{2}
,
and
(N^{2n+1}_{\mathcal{S}},\xi_{\mathcal{S}})
is isomorphic as a toric contact manifold to the corresponding
(N^{2n+1}_{k},\xi)
.

Remark 1.8
.

All the rigidity examples considered in Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
arise from primitive
prequantizations of monotone toric symplectic manifolds with divisible (non-primitive) first Chern class. In other
words, their minimal Chern number is bigger than
1
, in fact equal to
2
in all these examples. It would be
interesting to find rigid examples without this feature but we do not know if they exist.

2.
Toric symplectic manifolds

A
toric symplectic manifold
is a closed symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
of dimension
2n

equipped with an effective hamiltomian
n
-torus action, i.e.

\tau:\mathbb{T}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{n}\hookrightarrow\Ham(M,\omega)\,.

The
n
hamiltonian functions
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
generating these
n
commuting circle actions
give rise to the
moment map

\bm{\mu}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,\ \bm{\mu}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})\,.

The Atiyah-Guillemin-Sternberg convexity theorem implies that the moment map image,
{\mathcal{P}}:=\bm{\mu}(M)
,
is the convex hull of the images of the fixed points of the action, i.e.
{\mathcal{P}}=\conv(\bm{\mu}(M^{\mathbb{T}^{n}}))
, and
can be described as a finite intersection of half-spaces:

{\mathcal{P}}=\cap_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\ \bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{j}+\lambda_{j}\geq 0\right\}\,,

where
\bm{\nu}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is the primitive integral interior normal to the
j
-th facet of
P
and

\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
determines its position relative to the origin.

{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the
moment polytope
of the toric symplectic manifold
(M,\omega,\tau)
.
It is a Delzant polytope and a complete invariant of
(M,\omega,\tau)

[
11
]
.
Note that the hamiltonian functions
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
are only determined up
to constants and hence the moment polytope
{\mathcal{P}}
is only determined up to translation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The real numbers
\lambda_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, can be used to determine the cohomology class of the
symplectic form
\omega
and we have that
[\omega]=c_{1}(M)
, i.e.
(M,\omega,\tau)
is
a monotone toric symplectic manifold, if and only if there is a translation of
{\mathcal{P}}
such that all the

\lambda_{j}
’s are equal to
1
, i.e.

{\mathcal{P}}=\cap_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\ \bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{j}+1\geq 0\right\}\,.

Example 2.1
.

When
n=1
there is only one toric symplectic manifold: the
2
-sphere
S^{2}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
equipped with an area form
and circle action given by rotation around an axis. For the monotone area form, with total area
4\pi
, we
have that

{\mathcal{P}}=[-1,1]=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\ -x+1\geq 0\right\}\cap\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\ x+1\geq 0\right\}\,.

The primitive, integral, interior normals to the facets of its moment polytope can be listed as the columns of the matrix

3.
Monotone Bott manifolds as toric symplectic manifolds

Bott manifolds, introduced in
[
12
]
, iterate the construction of Hirzebruch surfaces.
More precisely, they are iterated
\mathbb{P}^{1}=S^{2}
-bundles of the form

B_{n}\xrightarrow{\pi_{n}}B_{n-1}\xrightarrow{\pi_{n-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}B_{1}=\mathbb{P}^{1}\xrightarrow{\pi_{1}}B_{0}=\{\text{point}\}

where

B_{i}=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}\oplus\mathbb{C})\ \text{with}\ \xi_{i}\rightarrow B_{i-1}\ \text{a $\mathbb{C}$-line bundle.}

At each stage, we always have that
H^{\ast}(B_{i};\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{\ast}((S^{2})^{i};\mathbb{Z})
as graded abelian groups (but not as rings),
hence
\rank H^{2k}(B_{i};\mathbb{Z})=\binom{i}{k}
. In particular,
\rank H^{2}(B_{i};\mathbb{Z})=i
and so the Chern class
c_{1}(\xi_{i})
of
each
\mathbb{C}
-line bundle
\xi_{i}\rightarrow B_{i-1}
is determined by
i-1
integers
(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,i-1})
.

All Bott manifolds are toric symplectic manifolds determined by moment polytopes that are combinatorially equivalent to
hypercubes. For a Bott manifold
B_{n}
of dimension
2n
, the
2n
primitive, integral, interior normals of the corresponding
moment polytope are determined by the Chern classes
c_{1}(\xi_{i})
,
i=2,\ldots,n
. In an appropriate integral basis of

\mathbb{Z}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, these normals are given by the
n
coordinate basis vectors, i.e. the colums of the
(n\times n)

identity matrix
I_{n}
, and the
n
“opposite” vectors given by the colums of the lower triangular matrix

L(B_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}-1&\ &\ &\ &\ \\
a_{2,1}&-1&\ &\ &\ \\
a_{3,1}&a_{3,2}&-1&\ &\ \\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ \\
a_{n,1}&a_{n,2}&\cdots&a_{n,n-1}&-1\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

Necessary and sufficient conditions on
L(B_{n})
so that
B_{n}
is Fano, i.e. so that
c_{1}(B_{n})>0
, were given in
[
20
]
.
From the point of view of toric symplectic geometry, these are precisely the monotone Bott manifolds.

Theorem 3.1

(​​
[

]
)
.

B_{n}
is a monotone toric symplectic manifod if and only if
L(B_{n})
is such that for any colum
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

one of the folowing holds:

(1)

a_{j+1,j}=\cdots=a_{n,j}=0
;

(2)

\exists\ q\in\{j+1,\ldots,n\}\,:\ a_{q,j}=1
and
a_{i,j}=0
for all
i\in\{j+1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{q\}

4.
Gorenstein toric contact manifolds

In this section we will briefly recall the
1
-
1
correspondence between Gorenstein toric
contact manifolds, i.e. good toric contact manifolds (in the sense of
[
16
]
) with zero first
Chern class, and toric diagrams (defined below). We follow closely the presentations in
[
2
,
4
]
,
which contain further details.

Via symplectization, there is a
1
-
1
correspondence between co-oriented contact manifolds
and symplectic cones, i.e. triples
(W,\omega,X)
where
(W,\omega)
is a connected symplectic manifold
and
X
is a vector field, the Liouville vector field, generating a proper
\mathbb{R}
-action

\rho_{t}:W\to W
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
\rho_{t}^{\ast}(\omega)=e^{t}\omega
. A closed symplectic cone is a
symplectic cone
(W,\omega,X)
for which the corresponding contact manifold
N=W/\mathbb{R}
is closed.

A toric contact manifold is a closed contact manifold of dimension
2n+1
equipped with an effective
hamiltonian action of the standard torus of dimension
n+1
:
\mathbb{T}^{n+1}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
.
Also via symplectization, toric contact manifolds are in
1
-
1
correspondence with toric symplectic cones,
i.e. closed symplectic cones
(W,\omega,X)
of dimension
2(n+1)
equipped with an effective
X
-preserving
hamiltonian
\mathbb{T}^{n+1}
-action, with moment map
\bm{\mu}:W\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that
\bm{\mu}(\rho_{t}(w))=e^{t}\bm{\mu}(w)
,
for all
w\in W
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Its moment cone is defined to be
C:=\bm{\mu}(W)\cup\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

A toric contact manifold is
good
if its toric symplectic cone has a moment cone with the following properties.

Definition 4.1
.

A cone
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is
good
if it is strictly convex and there exists a minimal set
of primitive vectors
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
, with

d\geq n+1
, such that

(i)

{\mathcal{C}}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{d}\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{j}\geq 0\}
.

(ii)

Any codimension-
k
face of
{\mathcal{C}}
,
1\leq k\leq n
, is the intersection of exactly
k
facets whose set of
normals can be completed to an integral basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
.

The primitive vectors
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
are called the defining normals of the good cone
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

The analogue for good toric contact manifolds of Delzant’s classification theorem for closed toric
symplectic manifolds is the following result (see
[
16
]
).

Theorem 4.2
.

For each good cone
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
there exists a unique closed toric symplectic cone

(W_{{\mathcal{C}}},\omega_{{\mathcal{C}}},X_{{\mathcal{C}}},\bm{\mu}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
with moment cone
{\mathcal{C}}
.

The Chern classes of a co-oriented contact manifold can be canonically identified with the
Chern classes of the tangent bundle of the associated symplectic cone. The following proposition
gives a moment cone characterization for zero first Chern class, i.e. for the toric contact manifold
to be Gorenstein.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(W_{{\mathcal{C}}},\omega_{{\mathcal{C}}},X_{{\mathcal{C}}},\bm{\mu}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
be a good toric symplectic cone with
c_{1}(TW_{C})=0
.
Then there exists an integral basis of
\mathbb{T}^{n+1}
for which the defining normals
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}

of the corresponding moment cone
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
are of the form

\bm{\nu}_{j}=(\bm{v}_{j},1)\,,\ \bm{v}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,,\ j=1,\ldots,d\,.

Under this condition, we will encode the Gorenstein toric contact manifold whose symplectization is

(W_{{\mathcal{C}}},\omega_{{\mathcal{C}}},X_{{\mathcal{C}}},\bm{\mu}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
by the polytope

{\mathcal{D}}=\conv(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{d})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

Proposition 4.4
.

{\mathcal{D}}
is a toric diagram (cf. Definition
6.14
).

Remark 4.5
.

The conditions imposed on toric diagrams
{\mathcal{D}}=\conv(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
are equivalent to the corresponding cone
with normals
\bm{\nu}_{j}=(\bm{v}_{j},1)
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, being good.

Theorem 4.6
.

For each toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists a unique Gorenstein toric contact manifold
(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})
of
dimension
2n+1
.

The main examples in the context of this paper are the so-called prequantizations of monotone toric symplectic
manifolds.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
(M^{2n},\omega)\,,\ [\omega]=c_{1}(M)
, be a monotone toric symplectic manifold with moment polytope

{\mathcal{P}}=\cap_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\ \bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{j}+1\geq 0\right\}\,.

Then

{\mathcal{D}}=\conv(\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{d})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a toric diagram and the Gorenstein toric contact manifold
(N^{2n+1}_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})
is the prequantization of

(M^{2n},\omega)
.

Remark 4.8
.

In fact,
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a reflexive polytope and
{\mathcal{D}}=-{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}
is its polar dual
polytope.

5.
Contact invariants

As already described in the introduction, the coefficients of the Ehrhart polynomial of a toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

are contact invariants of the corresponding toric contact manifold
(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})
. In particular, if we write the Ehrhart
polynomial in the form

L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})\binom{t+n-k}{n}\,,

we have that the non-negative coefficients
h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
are the contact invariants of
(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})

given by

h^{\ast}_{n-k}({\mathcal{D}})=cb_{2k}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})-cb_{2(k-1)}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})\,,

where
cb_{\ast}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})
are the
contact Betti numbers
, i.e.

cb_{\ast}(N_{{\mathcal{D}}},\xi_{{\mathcal{D}}})=\rank HC_{\ast}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})\ \text{where $HC_{\ast}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})$
is the cylindrical contact homology.}

Recall that
HC_{\ast}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}})=0
whenever
\ast<0
or
\ast
is odd.

For the prequantization of monotone toric symplectic manifolds
(M^{2n},\omega)\,,\ [\omega]=c_{1}(M)
, with reflexive
moment polytope
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}=-{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a theorem in
[
7
]
implies
that

h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})=\dim H^{2k}(M;\mathbb{Q})\,.

(1)

See
[
4
]
for a direct toric proof and recall that
H^{\text{odd}}(M;\mathbb{Q})=0
for any toric symplectic manifold
M
.

5.1.
Contact invariants of the prequantization of monotone Bott manifolds

For the prequantization of monotone Bott manifolds, we have that
\dim H^{2k}(B^{2n};\mathbb{Q})=\binom{n}{k}
,

k=0,\ldots,n
, and so (
1
) implies Proposition
1.4
, i.e. the toric diagram
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

of the Gorenstein prequantization
(N^{2n+1},\xi)
of any monotone Bott manifold
(B^{2n},[\omega]=c_{1})
is such that

h^{\ast}_{k}({\mathcal{D}})=\binom{n}{k}\,,\ k=0,\ldots,n\,.

This implies that the non-zero contact Betti numbers of any such
(N^{2n+1},\xi)
are given by

cb_{2k}(N^{2n+1},\xi)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{n}{j}\,,\ k=0,\ldots,n\,,\ \text{and}\ cb_{2k}(N^{2n+1},\xi)=2^{n}\,,\ \forall k>n\,.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|cl}\hline\cr\ast=&\quad 0&\quad 2&\quad 4&\quad\cdots&\quad 2(n-1)&\quad 2n&\text{even}\,>2n\\
\hline\cr cb_{\ast}(N^{2n+1},\xi)&1&1+n&\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+1&\cdots&2^{n}-1&2^{n}&2^{n}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

6.
Polytopes and Ehrhart theory

In this section we introduce the language of polytopes and Ehrhart theory. We also define the classes of polytopes
that are most relevant for toric symplectic and contact geometry: Delzant polytopes, cones over them and toric
diagrams. This section provides a quick review of combinatorial results that will be used in the paper. Proofs and
technical details can be found in the indicated references.

6.1.
Polytopes, cones and polar duality

Here we give a quick introduction to polyhedra in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a thorough presentation of this subject see
for example
[
21
]
.

Definition 6.1

(Polytope)
.

A polytope
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the convex hull of a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\conv\{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\dots,\bm{v}_{m}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\lambda_{1}\bm{v}_{1}+\lambda_{2}\bm{v}_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{m}\bm{v}_{m}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1\text{ and }\lambda_{i}\geq 0,i=1,\dots,m\right\}.

This is commonly referred as the vertex or
\mathcal{V}
-representation of
\mathcal{P}
. Depending on the context,
it might be better to deal with the hyperplane or
\mathcal{H}
-representation of a polytope in which we define a
polytope by the intersection of a finite set of halfspaces delimited by hyperplanes, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:A\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i=0}^{d}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{x}\leq b_{i}\right\}

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This intersection is always a polyhedron but,
since it might be unbounded, it is not always a polytope. A polytope is a bounded polyhedron.

All polytopes can be expressed in both representations, although the problem of algorithmically converting
between them is non-trivial.

The interior of a polytope
\mathcal{P}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{\circ}
.

We call a polytope
integral
if all its vertices lie on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
An hyperplane

H=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}=b\}

is a supporting hyperplane of a polytope
\mathcal{P}
if

\mathcal{P}\subset\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}\leq b\}\text{ or }\mathcal{P}\subset\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}\geq b\}.

Definition 6.2

(Face)
.

A set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is face of a polytope
\mathcal{P}
if

\displaystyle F=\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{H}

for some supporting hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
.

The dimension of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
equals the dimension of the affine span generated by the points in
S
.
If
S
is a polytope and
\dim S=n
we refer to
S
as a
n
-polytope.

A
n
-polytope
{\mathcal{P}}
has faces of every dimension between
-1
and
n
, in particular:

•

\mathcal{P}
has a single face of dimension
n
, which is itself;

•

\mathcal{P}
has faces of dimension
n-1
, called facets;

•

\mathcal{P}
has faces of dimension
1
, called edges;

•

\mathcal{P}
has faces of dimension
0
, called vertices;

•

\mathcal{P}
has a single face of dimension
-1
, the
\emptyset
.

Throughout the text we will refer to the set of faces ordered by the (usual) inclusion as the face poset.
We will say that two polytopes are combinatorially equivalent if their face posets are isomorphic.

A useful construction on a polytope is
\cone(\mathcal{P})
that we define now.

Definition 6.3

(Cone over a polytope)
.

For a polytope
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\cone(\mathcal{P})=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{x}=\alpha\bm{y},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{P}\text{ and }\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\}.

We define the polar dual of a polytope
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 6.4

(Polar Duality)
.

For a polytope
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
its polar dual

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{*}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}\leq 1,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{P}\}

is also a polytope.

If
0\in\mathcal{P}
then
\mathcal{P}^{**}=\mathcal{P}
. The polar dual has several interesting properties.
Namely it suffices to check the inequalities in Definition
6.4
for the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
i.e. the facets of
\mathcal{P}^{*}

are defined by the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
. This gives rise to a useful property of polar duality, it induces a bijection between
faces of dimension
i
in
\mathcal{P}
and faces of dimension
n-i-1
in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Stretching this reasoning a bit more,
one can conclude that the face posets of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{*}
are anti-isomorphic.

Definition 6.5

(Reflexive polytope)
.

An integral polytope
\mathcal{P}
is said to be reflexive if
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is also integral.

Lastly, we cover the definition of simple and simplicial polytope.

Definition 6.6

(Simple polytope)
.

A
n
-polytope is simple if every vertex is contained in
n
edges.

Definition 6.7

(Simplicial polytope)
.

A
n
-polytope is simplicial if every facet is a
n-1
simplex.

Lemma 6.8
.

\mathcal{P}
is simple
\Leftrightarrow

\mathcal{P}^{*}
is simplicial.

6.2.
Ehrhart theory

In this subsection we define the basics of Ehrhart theory that will be used in the paper. For a more detailed exposition
and proofs see for example
[
5
]
.

Ehrhart theory studies functions that count the number of integer points in the (integer) dilates of a polytope
\mathcal{P}
.
Formally, we are interested in the function

L_{\mathcal{P}}(t):=\#(t\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}),\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

where
t\mathcal{P}=\{t\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{x}\in\mathcal{P}\}
.

Theorem 6.9

(Ehrhart’s Theorem)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is an integral
n
-polytope then
L_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
is polynomial in
t
with degree exactly
n
.

From now on, we will refer to
L_{\mathcal{P}}
as the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
.

The Ehrhart series of a polytope is the generating function whose coefficients are given by the Ehrhart polynomial, i.e.,

\Ehr_{\mathcal{P}}(z):=1+\sum_{t\geq 1}L_{\mathcal{P}}(t)z^{t}\,.

In fact, we can rewrite this series as a rational function of a special form, which leads to the next theorem and corollary.

Theorem 6.10

(Stanley’s non negativity theorem)
.

For an integral
n
-polytope

\Ehr_{\mathcal{P}}(z)=\frac{h^{*}_{n}z^{n}+h^{*}_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\dots+h^{*}_{1}z+1}{(1-z)^{n+1}}\,,

where
h_{1}^{*},h_{2}^{*},\dots,h_{n}^{*}
are non negative integers.

Corollary 6.11
.

L_{\mathcal{P}}(t)={t+n\choose n}+h_{1}^{*}{t+n-1\choose n}+\dots+h_{n-1}^{*}{t+1\choose n}+h_{n}^{*}{t\choose n}

and so the coefficients
h_{i}^{*}
are in fact the coefficients of the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
in the basis of the

n
-degree polynomials given by

\left\{{t+i\choose n},i\in 0,\dots,n\right\}.

The following theorem gives a particularly interesting characterization of reflexive polytopes.

Theorem 6.12
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{P}
is reflexive;

(2)

L_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}(t+1)=L_{\mathcal{P}}(t),\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, i.e., every lattice point
is in
\partial k\mathcal{P}
for a single
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
;

(3)

h_{k}^{*}=h^{*}_{n-k}
for every
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

The fact that
(1)\Leftrightarrow(3)
is often referred to as Hibi’s palindromic theorem.

6.3.
Delzant polytopes, toric diagrams and prequantizations

Let us introduce two classes of polytopes that are relevant in the context of toric symplectic and contact geometry.

Definition 6.13

(Delzant polytope)
.

A Delzant polytope is a simple
n
-polytope such that for every vertex the
n
primitive edge vectors form a basis of

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Definition 6.14

(Toric Diagrams)
.

A toric diagram is a simplicial
n
-polytope such that the
n
vertices of each facet can be translated to form a basis of

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

We will now define the prequantization combinatorial construction that relates certain Delzant polytopes with toric diagrams.

Definition 6.15

(Prequantization)
.

Start with a
n
-dimensional integral Delzant polytope
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by the
d
hyperplanes

\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{i}\geq-\lambda_{i}
,
\bm{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
i\in 1,\dots,d
.

(1)

Consider the cone over
\mathcal{P}\times\{1\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. This cone is defined by the hyperplanes

\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{\nu}_{i},\lambda_{i})\geq 0
, therefore for each facet of the cone the primitive inner normal vector is

(\bm{\nu}_{i},\lambda_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
.

(2)

Apply a linear transformation
T\in GL(n,\mathbb{Z})
to all the normals such that
T(\bm{\nu}_{i},\lambda_{i})=(\bm{v}_{i},1)
where

\bm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

When such a
T
exists, the
n
-polytope
\mathcal{D}=\text{conv}(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d})
is a toric diagram.

The following theorem gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of such a linear transformation
T
.

Theorem 6.16
.

The transformation
T
exists iff
r\mathcal{P}
is reflexive for some
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. In that case,

r\mathcal{D}
is also reflexive.

We point to Section 7.3 in
[
13
]
, keeping in mind that although this text never refers to the prequantization this
construction is tantamount to their dual cone.

From now on, we will refer to an integral polytope
\mathcal{P}
such that
r\mathcal{P}
is reflexive for some

r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
as a
Gorenstein
polytope of index
r
. In fact, reflexive polytopes are Gorenstein of index

1
and one is able to produce a theorem in the spirit of
Theorem 6.12
but for Goresntein polytopes.

Theorem 6.17
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{P}
is Gorenstein of index
r
;

(2)

L_{\mathcal{P}^{\circ}}(t)=L_{\mathcal{P}}(t-r),\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>r}
or

L_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\#\{\partial t\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}+L_{\mathcal{P}}(t-r)
;

(3)

h_{k}^{*}=h^{*}_{n+1-r-k}
for every
0\leq k\leq n+1-r
.

Note that
(3)
implies that the non zero coefficients of
h^{*}
are still a palindrome even though their center of
symmetry has changed.

We end this section with a basic example that illustrates what we discussed so far.

Example 6.18

(Basic Example)
.

The (hyper-)cube
[-1,1]^{n}
is Delzant and reflexive for every
n
. The prequantization of this polytope
has as toric diagram the cross-polytope

\diamond_{n}:=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x_{1}|+\cdots+|x_{n}|\leq 1\}\quad\text{(see Figure~\ref{fig:0})}

and we have that

L_{\diamond_{n}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\choose k}{t-k+n\choose n}\,,\qquad\Ehr_{\diamond_{n}}(z)=\frac{(1+z)^{n}}{(1-z)^{n+1}}\,.

7.1.
Construction and basic properties

We define the

\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\dots&0\\

Small cross-polytopes have the same face structure (face lattice) as cross-polytopes.

7.3.
Pseudo-bipyramids over small cross-polytopes

Cross-polytopes are often seen inductively as bipyramids over lower dimensional cross-polytopes.
Small cross-polytopes share a similar property. The usual definition of a bypiramid is essentially the
free sum with
[-1,1]
, however, since
\bm{0}\in\partial S_{n}
, this would then imply that some facets
of
\Bipyr(S_{n})
are no longer unimodular simplices, which in turn means it cannot be a toric diagram.
This motivates the definition of a more general construction, namely the pseudo-bipyramids.

Definition 7.5
.

(Pseudo-bipyramid)
Given a polytope
\mathcal{P}
we define its pseudo-bipyramid as
{\Pbipyr}(\mathcal{P},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})
as

\conv(\{(\bm{s}_{1},1),(\bm{s}_{2},-1)\}\cup\{(\bm{x},0)\mid\bm{x}\in\mathcal{P}\}).

where
\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}
are lattice points of
\mathcal{P}
(we will refer to them as special).

Lemma 7.6
.

To create toric diagrams from other (lower dimensional) toric diagrams,
\bm{s}_{1}
and
\bm{s}_{2}

should not lie on the same facet of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

If
\bm{s}_{1}
and
\bm{s}_{2}
were to lie on the same facet
F
of
\mathcal{P}
then the facet of

\text{P-bipyr}(\mathcal{P},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})
with vertices
(\bm{s}_{1},1),(\bm{s}_{2},-1)
and

\{(\bm{x},0)\mid\bm{x}\in V(F)\}
would not be simplicial since it would have
n+3
vertices and

\text{P-bipyr}(\mathcal{P},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})
is of dimension
n+1
.
∎

From an Ehrhart theory perspective, this construction is equivalent to the usual bipyramids: we are essentially
joining two pyramids that share the same base. This yields the following theorem.

Theorem 7.7
.

\Ehr_{\Pbipyr(\mathcal{P},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})}=\frac{1+z}{1-z}\Ehr_{\mathcal{P}}

By projecting
S_{n}
to the hyperplane
x_{1}=0
we get
S_{n-1}
. Figure
10
shows this construction with
the special vertices highlighted. We should also notice that instead of
x_{1}=0
we could have chosen any of the
hyperplanes
x_{2}=0
, …,
x_{n-1}=0
.

Lemma 7.8
.

The small cross-polytope
S_{n}
(up to reordering coordinates) can be constructed as

\Pbipyr(S_{n-1},(0,...,0),(0,...,0,1))

Remark 7.9
.

The choice of special vertices when constructing
S_{n}
from
S_{n-1}
does not really matter
in the case of small cross-polytopes. There are only
n-1
choices of pairs that do not share a
facet and all of them yield the same polytope (up to unimodular equivalence).

This construction not only proves Theorem
7.3
once again but also enables us to effortlessly
prove various inductive properties. The following theorem is an example.

Theorem 7.10
.

Small cross-polytopes admit a unimodular triangulation.

Proof.

By induction, the base case is trivial, for the inductive step note that for any
(n-1)
-simplex
\Delta
in the unimodular
triangulation of
S_{n-1}
then
\conv(\Delta,(\bm{0},1))
and
\conv(\Delta,(\bm{0},1,-1))
are unimodular
n
-simplices.
Taking these simplices over all

.
∎

7.4.
Small cross-polytopes are Ehrhart determined

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a toric diagram. If the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{D}
has
h^{*}
-polynomial

h^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}(z)=(1+z)^{n-1}
, then
\mathcal{D}
is unimodularly equivalent to the small cross polytope.

Proof.

The degree
n-1
of
h^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}(z)
indicates that
\mathcal{D}
is Gorenstein of index
2
. Thus, the
dilated polytope
2\mathcal{D}
contains a unique strictly interior lattice point
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Evaluating the Ehrhart polynomial at
t=1
gives the total number of lattice points in
\mathcal{D}
.
Because
\mathcal{D}
is Gorenstein of index
2
, its interior is empty of lattice points. Furthermore,
since
\mathcal{D}
is a toric diagram, its proper faces are unimodular simplices, meaning they contain
no boundary lattice points other than their actual vertices. Thus, the lattice points of
\mathcal{D}
are exactly
its vertices, giving
v=h^{*}_{1}+d+1=2d
vertices.

Next, we count the lattice points in
2\mathcal{D}
. For a unimodular
k
-simplex
\Delta
, the number of strictly
interior lattice points in its
t
-th dilation is given by the standard discrete volume formula

L_{\Delta^{\circ}}(t)=\binom{t-1}{k}
. At a dilation of
t=2
, any face of dimension
k\geq 2
contains exactly

\binom{1}{k}=0
interior points. Therefore, the lattice points in
2\mathcal{D}
consist solely of the center
\bm{c}
,
the
2n
vertices, and the strict midpoints of its
e
edges. Evaluating the Ehrhart polynomial at
t=2
gives

L_{\mathcal{D}}(2)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n-1}{i}\binom{n+2-i}{n}=2n^{2}+1\ \text{lattice points.}

Equating this to our structural geometric count
1+2n+e
yields exactly
e=2n(n-1)
edges.

Define the shifted polytope
\mathcal{T}:=2\mathcal{D}-\bm{c}
, which places the unique interior point at the origin

\mathbf{0}
. Its vertices are
\bm{v^{\prime}}_{i}=2\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{c}
, implying
\bm{v^{\prime}}_{i}\equiv-\bm{c}\pmod{2}
. Because
all
2d
vertices of
\mathcal{T}
share the same parity, the midpoint of any pair
\bm{u},\bm{w}\in\text{Vert}(\mathcal{T})

is an integer lattice point.

If
\bm{u}
and
\bm{w}
do not form an edge in
\mathcal{T}
, their connecting segment crosses the interior.
Since
\bm{0}
is the only interior lattice point, their midpoint must be
\bm{0}
, forcing
\bm{w}=-\bm{u}
.

We now look at the
1
-skeleton (the graph of vertices and edges) of
\mathcal{T}
. A complete graph on

2n
vertices has
\binom{2n}{2}=2n^{2}-n
edges. Since
\mathcal{T}
has
2n(n-1)=2n^{2}-2n
edges,
it is missing exactly
n
edges (non-adjacent pairs). Since a vertex can have at most one geometric antipode
crossing the origin, these
n
missing edges must be strictly disjoint, perfectly pairing all
2n
vertices into
geometric opposites.

We now translate this central symmetry back to
\mathcal{D}
. The opposite vertices in
\mathcal{T}
satisfy

\bm{w^{\prime}}_{i}=-\bm{v^{\prime}}_{i}
. Substituting our coordinate shift, we get
(2\bm{w}_{i}-\bm{c})=-(2\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{c})
.
Rearranging this gives
\bm{v}_{i}+\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{c}
. Geometrically, this confirms that the midpoint of any
opposite pair
(\bm{v}_{i},\bm{w}_{i})
in
\mathcal{D}
is exactly
\frac{\bm{c}}{2}
. Thus, the
2n
vertices of

\mathcal{D}
consist of
n
pairs symmetrically arranged around the half-integer center
\frac{\bm{c}}{2}
.

Let
F
be a facet of
\mathcal{D}
with vertices
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}
. Let
\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n}

denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Because
F
is a unimodular simplex, an affine unimodular
transformation maps
F
onto the hyperplane
x_{d}=0
such that its vertices are

\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{e}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and
\bm{v}_{n}=\bm{0}
.

Since the lattice distance from
\bm{c}
to
2F
is exactly
1
, the
n
-th coordinate of
\bm{c}
must be
\pm 1
.
After possibly applying a reflection across
x_{n}=0
we can guarantee it is
1
. We apply a further unimodular
shear parallel to
x_{n}=0
, which preserves
F
and maps
\bm{c}
exactly to the
n
-th basis vector
\bm{e}_{n}
.

The remaining
n
vertices of
\mathcal{D}
are now rigidly determined by the symmetry
\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{c}-\bm{v}_{i}
.
For
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we obtain
\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{e}_{n}-\bm{e}_{i}
, and for the origin,
\bm{w}_{n}=\bm{e}_{n}-\bm{0}=\bm{e}_{n}
.
This exact configuration of vertices, the basis vectors
\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n-1}
and origin
\bm{0}
, alongside
their shifted symmetrics
\bm{e}_{n}-\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n}-\bm{e}_{n-1}
and
\bm{e}_{n}
is precisely the standard
construction of the small cross polytope, proving uniqueness up to unimodular equivalence.
∎

8.
On prequantizations of Gorenstein Delzant polytopes

In this section we consider a construction similar to the one in the previous one, but now for a
general reflexive Delzant
n
-polytope
\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathcal{P}/r
is integral for some
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{P}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{D}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{P}/r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}}
preq.
- dual
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces/r}
preq.

Our main result in this context is the following theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

\Ehr_{\mathcal{D}}(z)=\frac{1-z^{r}}{1-z}\Ehr_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(z)\,.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a Gorenstein polytope of index
s
such that
\mathcal{P}/r
is integral for some

r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the toric diagram of the prequantization of
\mathcal{P}
and

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
the one of
\mathcal{P}/r
. We then have that

\Ehr_{\mathcal{D}}(z)=\frac{1-z^{rs}}{1-z^{s}}\Ehr_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(z).

Proof.

(Corollary)
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the toric diagram of the prequantization of the reflexive polytope

s\mathcal{P}=sr(\mathcal{P}/r)
. Applying
Theorem 8.1
twice, once to
\mathcal{P}

and once to
\mathcal{P}/r
, we have that

\frac{1-z^{s}}{1-z}\Ehr_{\mathcal{D}}(z)=\Ehr_{\mathcal{T}}(z)=\frac{1-z^{rs}}{1-z}\Ehr_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(z)

which proves the corollary. Note that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is Gorenstein of index
rs
.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

It is interesting, although direct, to check that the
h^{*}
polynomial of
\mathcal{D}
is exactly the sum of
r
copies of the
h^{*}
polynomial of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
such that each one has a shift of
s
indices to the right.
More precisely,

h^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}(z)=\left(1+z^{s}+z^{2s}+\dots+z^{s(r-1)}\right)h^{*}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(z)\,.

Furthermore, we note that the case
s=1
is of particular interest. In this case, we have a reflexive toric diagram
\mathcal{D}

and toric diagram
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that is Gorenstein of index
r
. The previous expression can be stated as

\sum_{j=x-r+1}^{x}h_{j}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=h_{x}^{*}(\mathcal{D})\,.

We assume throughout that
h_{x}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=h_{x}^{*}(\mathcal{D})=0
for
x<0
and for
x>n
. Taking consecutive differences
yields

h_{x-r}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})-h_{x}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=h_{x-1}^{*}(\mathcal{D})-h_{x}^{*}(\mathcal{D})\,.

Let
x=n-m+rk+r
and apply the palindromic property
h_{y}^{*}(\mathcal{D})=h_{n-y}^{*}(\mathcal{D})
to the right hand side to get

h_{n-m+rk}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})-h_{n-m+r(k+1)}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=h_{m-rk-r+1}^{*}(\mathcal{D})-h_{m-rk-r}^{*}(\mathcal{D})\,.

Summing both sides over
k\geq 0
we have

h_{n-m}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(h_{m-rk-r+1}^{*}(\mathcal{D})-h_{m-rk-r}^{*}(\mathcal{D})\right)\,,

recognizing the left hand side as a telescoping sum. In terms of contact Betti numbers, and
since
cb_{2i}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})=0
for all
i<0
, we have that

cb_{2i}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})=\sum_{m=0}^{i}(cb_{2m}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})-cb_{2(m-1)}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}))=\sum_{m=0}^{i}h_{n-m}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\,.

Substituting the expression for
h_{n-m}^{*}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
, exchanging the order of summation, and evaluating the inner
telescoping sum, we get

\displaystyle cb_{2i}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{i}\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(h_{m-rk-r+1}^{*}(\mathcal{D})-h_{m-rk-r}^{*}(\mathcal{D})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{i-rk-r+1}^{*}(\mathcal{D})\,.

Recall that
cb_{2i}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})=\rank HC_{2i}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})

and
h_{i}^{*}(\mathcal{D})=\dim H^{2i}(M_{\mathcal{P}};\mathbb{Q})
, where
M_{\mathcal{P}}
is the toric symplectic manifold with
reflexive Delzant polytope
{\mathcal{P}}=-{\mathcal{D}}^{\ast}
(cf. equation (
1
)). Thus, in this toric context,
Theorem
8.1
is the Ehrhart theory version of

HC_{2i}(N_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}H^{2(i-rk-r+1)}(M_{\mathcal{P}};\mathbb{Q})\,,

which is exactly Corollary 1.9 in
[
4
]
and also follows from a more general result in
[
7
]
.

Proof of Theorem

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a reflexive Delzant polytope such that
\mathcal{P}/r

Let us determine the toric diagrams of the prequantization of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}/r

Translate
\mathcal{P}
by a lattice vector such that
\bm{0}\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{P})

For the sake of this proof, we will only perform the first step of Definition

n

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
This clearly does not affect the integer points contained in the polytope compared to the final

If
\mathcal{P}
is defined by the
d

\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{i}\geq-\lambda_{i}
, with

\bm{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{i}\geq-\lambda_{i}/r

(\bm{\nu}_{i},\lambda_{i}/r)

Note that
\bm{\nu}_{i}

Lemma 8.4
.

The face poset (or combinatorial structure) of
\mathcal{D}
coincides with the face poset of

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

From the previous paragraph, we essentially have the "same" set of vertices but located in two

. More precisely, consider the linear map

Since linear isomorphisms preserve the face structure of polytopes,
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

are combinatorially equivalent.
∎

This lemma, together with the fact that
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

Lemma 8.5

\#\{\partial t\mathcal{D}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}=\#\{\partial t\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}

Proof.

From Lemma

coincide. Recalling that
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

are toric diagrams, we know that all their faces of dimension

are unimodular simplices.
This implies that there are unimodular triangulations (with no new vertices) of
\partial\mathcal{D}

and
\partial\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Since the polytopes share the same face lattice and every corresponding

\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
∎

9.
A class of toric diagrams Ehrhart equivalent to cross-polytopes

In this section we construct and classify a class of toric diagrams that share the Ehrhart series of the
cross-polytope
\diamond_{d}
. This class consists of the toric diagrams that arise from the
prequantization of monotone Bott manifolds (cf. Section
3
, in particular Theorem
3.1
).

9.1.
Iterative construction via pseudo-bipyramids

Recall that pseudo-bipyramids where defined in Definition
7.5
.
Denoting the set of
n
-dimensional polytopes in our class by
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we define the next iteration as

\mathcal{C}_{n+1}=\{\text{Pbipyr}(\mathcal{P},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})\mid\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{n},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathcal{P}\}.

and initialize
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with just one element, the toric diagram
[-1,1]
. For any polytope

\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, because it is Ehrhart equivalent to
\diamond_{n}
, the only lattice points
it contains are its
2n
boundary vertices and the origin as its unique interior point. Thus, when choosing
the points
\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}
for the next iteration, we are restricted to three cases:

•

\bm{s}_{1}=\bm{s}_{2}=\bm{0}
, which yields the standard bipyramid;

•

\bm{s}_{1}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{s}_{2}\in\partial\mathcal{P}
;

•

\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\in\partial\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 9.1
.

The third case (
\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\in\partial\mathcal{P}
) is redundant, as it always produces a polytope
unimodularly equivalent to one generated by one of the other cases.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}=\text{P-bipyr}(\mathcal{P},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})
where
\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\in\partial\mathcal{P}
.
We define a mapping
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by the transformation

\varphi(\bm{x},x_{n+1})=(\bm{x}-x_{n+1}\bm{s}_{1},x_{n+1})\,.

Because
\bm{s}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\varphi
is a unimodular transformation. Applying
\varphi
to the vertices
of
{\mathcal{Q}}
yields

•

\varphi(\bm{x},0)=(\bm{x},0)
for all
\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{P})
;

•

\varphi(\bm{s}_{1},1)=(\bm{s}_{1}-\bm{s}_{1},1)=(\bm{0},1)
;

•

\varphi(\bm{s}_{2},-1)=(\bm{s}_{2}+\bm{s}_{1},-1)
.

Therefore,
\varphi({\mathcal{Q}})
has vertices equivalent to a pseudo-bipyramid with special points at the origin and at

\bm{y}=\bm{s}_{1}+\bm{s}_{2}
. We must verify that
\bm{y}
is a valid lattice point in
\mathcal{P}
.
Because
\mathcal{P}
is Ehrhart equivalent to
\diamond_{n}
and its unique interior lattice point is the origin
\bm{0}
,
we know that
\mathcal{P}
is a reflexive polytope. Therefore
\mathcal{P}
can be defined as the intersection of
half-spaces
\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{F}+1\geq 0
, where
\bm{\nu}_{F}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is the primitive interior normal
to the facet
F
. Since
\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathcal{P}
, their dot products with
\bm{\nu}_{F}

are integers lower bounded by
-1
. By Lemma
7.6
, to ensure that the resulting pseudo-bipyramid
is simplicial, the special points
\bm{s}_{1}
and
\bm{s}_{2}
cannot lie on the same facet of
\mathcal{P}
. Therefore,
they cannot simultaneously satisfy
\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{F}=-1
for any given facet
F
. Because the dot products
are integers, for every facet
F
at least one of
\bm{s}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{F}
or
\bm{s}_{2}\cdot\bm{\nu}_{F}
must be
strictly greater than
-1
, meaning it must be
\geq 0
. By linearity of the inner product,

(\bm{s}_{1}+\bm{s}_{2})\cdot\bm{\nu}_{F}+1\geq 0
for all facets
F
. This proves that
\bm{y}=\bm{s}_{1}+\bm{s}_{2}

satisfies all facet inequalities and therefore is in
\mathcal{P}
. Since
\bm{y}
is the sum of two integer vectors, it is
a lattice point in
\mathcal{P}
, hence it is either
\bm{0}
or belongs to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{P}
.
Thus,
\varphi({\mathcal{Q}})=\text{P-bipyr}(\mathcal{P},\bm{0},\bm{y})
with
\bm{y}=\bm{0}
or
\bm{y}\in\partial\mathcal{P}

as desired.
∎

Theorem 9.2
.

The vertex set of any polytope in
\mathcal{C}_{n+1}
is of the form

\{(0,...,0,1),(\bm{y},-1)\}\cup\{(\bm{x},0)\mid\bm{x}\in\mathcal{P}\}\,,

for some
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

By the preceding Lemma, we only need to consider constructions where at least one of the new points is the origin.
∎

While this characterizes the coordinates perfectly, enumerating the family remains problematic since
different choices of
\mathcal{P}
or
\bm{y}
can yield toric diagrams that are unimodularly equivalent.

9.2.
Classification via Rooted
3
-Cacti

To systematically classify the toric diagrams in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
up to unimodular equivalence, we encode the
iterative pseudo-bipyramid construction into a purely combinatorial object, a rooted
3
-cactus graph. There
are several possible choices of combinatorial objects to carry out this classification. We chose rooted
3
-cacti
because they have already been related to monotone Bott manifolds in
[
10
]
.

A
3
-cactus (or triangle-cactus) is a connected graph in which every edge belongs to exactly one simple cycle,
and every such cycle is a triangle (
K_{3}
). We can naturally construct a bijection between the lattice points in
a polytope
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
and the vertices of a rooted
3
-cactus
T
, where the root
corresponds to the origin
\bm{0}
, and every other node corresponds to a non-zero boundary lattice point of
the polytope. Note that whenever a rooted
3
-cactus has
n
triangles then it has
3n-(n-1)=2n+1
vertices.

We define this mapping explicitly by building the graph incrementally, alongside the pseudo-bipyramid construction.
For the base case
n=1
, we start with the polytope
\mathcal{P}_{1}=[-1,1]\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. It contains exactly
three lattice points: the origin
0
, and the boundary points
-1
and
1
. The corresponding graph
G_{1}
is
initialized as a single triangle (
K_{3}

0
and the other two vertices represent
-1

and
1
. Suppose we have a
3

representing a polytope
\mathcal{P}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
To construct the next iteration
\mathcal{P}_{k+1}=\text{P-bipyr}(\mathcal{P}_{k},\bm{0},\bm{y})
, we first map

\bm{x}\mapsto(\bm{x},0)
, and then select an existing lattice point

\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}\cap\mathcal{P}_{k}\times\{0\}
,
which corresponds to a node in
G_{k}
(where choosing
\bm{y}=0
corresponds to selecting the root node).
This operation introduces exactly two new lattice points to the newly formed polytope:
\bm{v}_{k+1}:=\bm{e}_{k+1}

and
\bm{w}_{k+1}:=\bm{y}-\bm{e}_{k+1}
. In our graph, we represent this geometric addition by attaching a
new triangle directly to the node corresponding to
\bm{y}
. The two newly added vertices of this triangle naturally
represent
\bm{v}_{k+1}
and
\bm{w}_{k+1}
and we will later refer to
\bm{y}
as the parent of
\bm{v}_{k+1}
and

\bm{w}_{k+1}
. This procedure can be visualized in Figure
11
.

Notice the invariant of this construction: the two newly added vertices always perfectly sum to their parent,

\bm{v}_{k}+\bm{w}_{k}=\bm{y}
. Before proving the main equivalence theorem, we must establish a lemma regarding
this additive relationship.

Lemma 9.3
.

For any toric diagram
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, three distinct points
\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfy

\bm{a}+\bm{b}=\bm{c}
if and only if the corresponding vertices form a triangle in the associated
3
-cactus
G
, with
\bm{c}
as the parent
of the others.

Proof.

(\impliedby)
This follows directly from the construction.

(\implies)
Suppose
\bm{a}+\bm{b}=\bm{c}
for three distinct lattice points in
\mathcal{P}
. Because they are distinct,
neither
\bm{a}
nor
\bm{b}
can be the origin
\bm{0}
(otherwise we would have
\bm{a}=\bm{c}
or
\bm{b}=\bm{c}
).

Let
k\leq n
be the largest coordinate index where at least one of these vertices has a non-zero entry. All coordinates in our
construction belong to
\{-1,0,1\}
.

If
c_{k}\neq 0
, assume without loss of generality
c_{k}=1
. To satisfy
a_{k}+b_{k}=1
with coordinates restricted to
\{-1,0,1\}
,
exactly one of the summands, say
a_{k}
, must equal
1
. By the pseudo-bipyramid construction, there is a unique point in

\mathcal{P}
with a
k
-th coordinate of
1
and all subsequent coordinates
0
. This forces
\bm{a}=\bm{c}
, which contradicts
the assumption that
\bm{a}
and
\bm{c}
are distinct points.

Thus, we must have
c_{k}=0
, forcing
\{a_{k},b_{k}\}=\{1,-1\}
. This uniquely identifies
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
as the exact pair of
vertices added at dimension
k
. By Theorem
9.2
, these are
(\bm{0},1)
and
(\bm{y},-1)
. Their sum is
(\bm{y},0)
,
identifying
\bm{c}
as their parent vertex embedded in the
(k-1)

Lemma 9.4
.

The unimodular equivalence class of a polytope
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
depends only on its associated
3

G
,
and not on the specific sequence in which its triangles are constructed. Consequently, any leaf triangle of
G
can be assumed to
correspond to the final construction step in dimension
n
.

Proof.

The rooted structure of
G
imposes a natural partial order on its triangles, as any parent vertex must be generated before its children.
A valid geometric construction of
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to choosing a linear extension of this partial order, assigning each of the

n
triangles to a distinct step
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

By the pseudo-bipyramid construction, at each step
k\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, the operation embeds the
(k-1)
-dimensional base into

\mathbb{R}^{k}
and introduces two new vertices. Specifically, the two vertices added at step
k
take the values
\pm 1
exclusively
in the
k
-th coordinate. As a result, the geometric embedding of each triangle in
G
is entirely determined by the axis
\bm{e}_{k}

assigned at its creation step.

Suppose we generate two polytopes,
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, using two different valid construction sequences for the
exact same graph
G
. Because the set of triangles and their parent-child adjacencies are identical, the step sequence for

\mathcal{P}_{2}
is merely a permutation of the sequence for
\mathcal{P}_{1}
. Geometrically, this simply permutes the assignment
of the orthogonal basis vectors
\{\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n}\}
to the triangles of
G
. The linear transformation that maps the vertices
of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
to
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is exactly this permutation of the coordinate axis. Because permutation matrices have determinant

\pm 1
, this transformation is unimodular, therefore, any two valid attachment sequences for
G
generate polytopes that are unimodularly
equivalent.

This allows us to freely reorder the construction so that any chosen leaf triangle is generated at the final step
n
.
∎

Theorem 9.5
.

Two polytopes
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
are unimodularly equivalent if and only if their
corresponding rooted
3
-cacti
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic.

Proof.

(\implies)
Suppose
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are unimodularly equivalent. By definition, there exists an unimodular
transformation
U:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
mapping
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})
bijectively to
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{P}_{2})
.

All polytopes in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
are reflexive and the origin
\bm{0}
is their unique interior lattice point. For
U
to map

\mathcal{P}_{1}
to
\mathcal{P}_{2}
bijectively, it must map the interior to the interior, forcing
U(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
.
This guarantees that
U
is linear (possessing no translation component).

Because
U
is a linear bijection, the additive relation
\bm{a}+\bm{b}=\bm{c}
holds for a triplet of vertices in

\mathcal{P}_{1}
if and only if
U(\bm{a})+U(\bm{b})=U(\bm{c})
holds in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
By Lemma
9.3
, these sums identify the adjacencies of the
3
-cactus.

We claim the restriction
U|_{{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})}
acts as a strict, root-preserving graph isomorphism between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
First, since
U(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
, the root of the graph is preserved. Second, two distinct vertices
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in{\mathcal{V}}(G_{1})

share an edge if and only if they belong to the same triangle. By Lemma
9.3
, this occurs if and only if there exists
a third vertex
\bm{z}\in{\mathcal{V}}(G_{1})
such that one of the three vertices is the exact sum of the other two. Because
U
preserves
this exact additive relation, this holds if and only if the same equality holds in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
. This sum guarantees that

\{U(\bm{x}),U(\bm{y}),U(\bm{z})\}
forms a valid triangle in
G_{2}
. Therefore,
\{\bm{x},\bm{y}\}
is an edge in
G_{1}
if and only
if
\{U(\bm{x}),U(\bm{y})\}
is an edge in
G_{2}
, proving the isomorphism.

(\impliedby)
Suppose
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic via a root-preserving graph isomorphism

\phi:{\mathcal{V}}(G_{1})\to{\mathcal{V}}(G_{2})
. We will explicitly construct a unimodular matrix
U
such that

U(\bm{x})=\phi(\bm{x})
for all
\bm{x}\in{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{P}_{1})
, proceeding by induction on
n
.

For the base case
n=1
we have that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are single triangles corresponding to
[-1,1]
. The unimodular map

U=[1]
or
U=[-1]
trivially satisfies the claim depending on whether the isomorphism
\phi
fixes or swaps the vertices.

For the inductive step let
n>1
and assume the claim holds for dimension
n-1

is a finite
3

it contains at least one leaf triangle
\{\bm{y},\bm{v},\bm{w}\}
where
\bm{y}

By Lemma

n

\bm{v}=\bm{e}_{n}

\bm{w}=\bm{y}-\bm{e}_{n}
, producing the relation
\bm{v}+\bm{w}=\bm{y}
.
The isomorphism
\phi
maps this to a leaf triangle
\{\phi(\bm{y}),\phi(\bm{v}),\phi(\bm{w})\}
in
G_{2}
.
Applying Lemma
9.4
to
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, we can reindex its construction steps so this corresponding
mapped leaf is also added at the
n
-th step. Because it is attached to the parent
\phi(\bm{y})
, the newly added vertices

\{\phi(\bm{v}),\phi(\bm{w})\}
are exactly
\bm{e}_{d}
and
\phi(\bm{y})-\bm{e}_{n}
. Consequently,
\phi(\bm{v})
has
n
-th
coordinate
\alpha\in\{1,-1\}
and its first
n-1

\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.
Removing these leaf vertices from
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
leaves isomorphic
3
-cacti of dimension
n-1
. By the inductive hypothesis,
there exists a
(n-1)\times(n-1)
unimodular matrix
U^{\prime}
mapping all remaining base vertices perfectly, ensuring
U^{\prime}(\bm{y})=\phi(\bm{y})

We extend
U^{\prime}
to a
n\times n

U
by defining the action on the new basis vector as
U(\bm{e}_{n})=\phi(\bm{v})

Because all base vectors in
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
have a

Its determinant is

maps the

Because

\phi

Toric diagrams Ehrhart equivalent to a small bipyramid over the unimodular simplex

Let
n>1
be an integer and
\triangle_{n-1}
denote the standard unimodular simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
Consider the family of
n
-dimensional polytopes
\mathcal{P}_{k}
given by

\mathcal{P}_{k}:=\text{conv}\left(\{(n+k)\triangle_{n-1}-\bm{1}\}\times\{-1\},\{(n-k)\triangle_{n-1}-\bm{1}\}\times\{1\}\right)\,,

where
0\leq k<n
is an integer parameter. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is reflexive and Delzant by
looking at the hyperplane description of
\mathcal{P}_{k}
, which is given by:

•

x_{i}+1\geq 0,\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
;

•

-x_{n}+1\geq 0
;

•

-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}-kx_{n}+1\geq 0
.

The toric diagram
\mathcal{T}_{k}
of the prequantization of
\mathcal{P}_{k}
has vertex set

{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{T}_{k})=\{\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n},-\bm{e}_{n},\bm{v}_{k}\}

where
\bm{v}_{k}:=(-1,\dots,-1,-k)
.

Lemma 10.1
.

For any
0\leq k<n
, the toric diagram
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is a bipyramid with apices
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\bm{e}_{n},-\bm{e}_{n}\}
and
base
{\mathcal{E}}=\{\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n-1},\bm{v}_{k}\}
.

Proof.

Let
B(\mathcal{T}_{k})
be the barycenter of
{\mathcal{E}}
. We have the following affine dependency:

B(\mathcal{T}_{k}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{v}\in{\mathcal{E}}}\bm{v}=\left(\frac{n-k}{2n}\right)\bm{e}_{n}+\left(\frac{n+k}{2n}\right)(-\bm{e}_{n})

Since
0\leq k<n
, the coefficients are positive and sum to
1
.
By the following Lemma
10.2
,
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is a bipyramid.
∎

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}=\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n},\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2}\}
be the set of
n+2
vertices of a
n
-dimensional lattice polytope

{\mathcal{P}}
. If there exists an affine dependency of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\bm{v}_{i}=\mu_{1}\bm{y}_{1}+\mu_{2}\bm{y}_{2}\,,

where
\alpha_{i},\mu_{j}>0
for all
i,j
and
\sum\alpha_{i}=\sum\mu_{j}=1
, then
{\mathcal{P}}
is a lattice bipyramid with base

{\mathcal{E}}=\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}\}
and apices
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2}\}
.

Proof.

The point
\bm{x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\bm{v}_{i}=\mu_{1}\bm{y}_{1}+\mu_{2}\bm{y}_{2}
lies in the relative interior of both

\text{conv}({\mathcal{E}})
and
\text{conv}({\mathcal{A}})
. In a
n
-polytope with
n+2
vertices, the space of affine dependencies is exactly
one-dimensional. This unique dependency implies that
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}
are the only subsets of vertices whose convex hulls
intersect in the interior. A subset of vertices
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}
forms a face of
{\mathcal{P}}
if and only if its convex hull does not
intersect the interior of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Therefore,
{\mathcal{F}}
is a face if and only if it contains neither
{\mathcal{E}}
nor
{\mathcal{A}}
. The maximal such
sets (facets) are obtained by removing exactly one vertex from
{\mathcal{E}}
and one from
{\mathcal{A}}
. Specifically, the facets of
{\mathcal{P}}

are of the form
\text{conv}(({\mathcal{E}}\setminus\{\bm{v}_{i}\})\cup\{\bm{y}_{j}\})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
j\in\{1,2\}
.
This is precisely the combinatorial description of a bipyramid with apices
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
over a
(n-1)
-simplex
base with vertices
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 10.3
.

The
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is
h_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}^{*}(z)=1+2z+2z^{2}+\dots+2z^{n-1}+z^{n}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is a toric diagram we know there is a unimodular triangulation
T
for
\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
Furthermore
\mathcal{T}_{k}
is reflexive so taking the convex hull of the origin with each simplex of the boundary

\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}
induces a unimodular triangulation
K
of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
(see e.g. Exercise 10.3 of
[
5
]
).
Furthermore, we can use Theorem 10.3 of
[
5
]
to conclude that

h_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}^{\ast}(z)=h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}(z)\,,

where
h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}
is the
h
-polynomial of the boundary, i.e.

h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{j-1}z^{j}(1-z)^{n-j}

(2)

with
f_{j-1}=
number of
(j-1)
-dimensional faces of
\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}
(
f_{-1}=0
).

To compute
h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}(z)
, we determine the
f
-vector of the boundary complex. As established,

\mathcal{T}_{k}
is combinatorially a bipyramid over a
(n-1)
-simplex. For
0\leq j<n
, a
(j-1)
-face is either a

(j-1)
-face of the base
(n-1)
-simplex (yielding
\binom{n}{j}
faces) or the convex hull of a
(j-2)
-face of the base
with one of the two apices (yielding
2\binom{n}{j-1}
faces). The facets of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
(
j=n
) are exactly the
convex hulls of the
n
faces of dimension
(n-2)
of the base with each of the two apices, yielding
f_{n-1}=2n
.
This allows us to express the face numbers for all
0\leq j\leq n
as

f_{j-1}=\binom{n}{j}+2\binom{n}{j-1}-\delta_{j,n}\,,

where
\delta_{j,n}
is the Kronecker delta. Substituting this explicit
f
-vector in (
2
), we split the summation
into three parts:

h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}z^{j}(1-z)^{n-j}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j-1}z^{j}(1-z)^{n-j}-z^{n}\,.

The first sum is the binomial expansion of
(z+(1-z))^{n}=1^{n}=1
. For the second sum, we have

\displaystyle 2z\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}z^{i}(1-z)^{n-1-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{2z}{1-z}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}z^{i}(1-z)^{n-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{2z(1-z^{n})}{1-z}

\displaystyle=2z(1+z+z^{2}+\dots+z^{n-1}).

Recombining all the evaluated terms, we obtain

h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}(z)=1+2z+2z^{2}+\dots+2z^{n-1}+z^{n}\,.

Because this polynomial depends only on the dimension
n
, the reflexivity, and the combinatorial bipyramid structure,
it is invariant across all valid choices of
k
. Since
h_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}^{*}(z)=h_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{k}}(z)
, the result
follows.
∎

Theorem 10.5
.

For integers
0\leq k_{1},k_{2}<n
,
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}\cong\mathcal{T}_{k_{2}}
if and only if
k_{1}=k_{2}
.

Proof.

The result is trivial for
n=2
and we will now prove it for
n\geq 3
. Assume there exists an affine unimodular
transformation
\phi
mapping
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
to
\mathcal{T}_{k_{2}}
. Since
\phi
must preserve the
unique affine dependency from Lemma
10.1
, it maps the base barycenter

B(\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}})
to
B(\mathcal{T}_{k_{2}})
. This requires

\phi\left(\frac{n-k_{1}}{2n}\bm{e}_{n}+\frac{n+k_{1}}{2n}(-\bm{e}_{n})\right)=\frac{n-k_{2}}{2n}\bm{e}_{n}+\frac{n+k_{2}}{2n}(-\bm{e}_{n})\,.

The map
\phi
must preserve vertex degrees in the
1
-skeleton. The base vertices
{\mathcal{E}}
have degree
n+1
,
while the apices
{\mathcal{A}}
have degree
n
. Thus,
\phi
maps
{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{1}}
to
{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{2}}
and

{\mathcal{E}}_{k_{1}}
to
{\mathcal{E}}_{k_{2}}
. As
\phi
restricts to a bijection on the apices
{\mathcal{A}}_{k_{1}}={\mathcal{A}}_{k_{2}}=\{\bm{e}_{n},-\bm{e}_{n}\}
,
there are two cases. If
\phi
preserves the apices, then
(n+k_{1})/2n=(n+k_{2})/2n
, implying
k_{1}=k_{2}
. If
\phi

swaps the apices, then
(n-k_{1})/2n=(n+k_{2})/2n
, implying
-k_{1}=k_{2}
, which for
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
forces

k_{1}=k_{2}=0
.
∎

To apply the prequantization to the divided polytope
\mathcal{P}_{k}/2
, we must first ensure that the resulting

(1,\dots,1)

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{k}:=\mathcal{P}_{k}+(1,\dots,1)
which is given by:

•

;

•

-x_{n}+2\geq 0
;

•

-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}-kx_{n}+n+k\geq 0
.

We conclude that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}:=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{k}/2
is integral if and only if

. The inner normal
vectors of the cone over

are:

•

(\bm{e}_{i},0),\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
;

•

(-\bm{e}_{n},1)
;

•

(-1,\dots,-1,-k,\frac{n+k}{2})
.

We use the unimodular transformation in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by the matrix
T\in\text{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{Z})

T=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\

to map all these points to the hyperplane

. We obtain that the vertices of the corresponding toric diagram

are:

•

;

•

\bm{a}_{i+1}=\bm{e}_{i},\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}

•

;

•

\bm{a}_{n+2}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\bm{e}_{i}-k\bm{e}_{n-1}+\frac{n+k}{2}\bm{e}_{n}
.

Theorem 10.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a
n
-dimensional toric diagram. If its
h^{*}
-polynomial is
h_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\ast}(z)=1+z+z^{2}+\dots+z^{n-1}
,
then
{\mathcal{S}}
is unimodularly equivalent to
\mathcal{D}_{k}
for a unique
k\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
0\leq k<n
and

k\equiv n\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

From the given
h^{\ast}
-polynomial, the normalized volume of
{\mathcal{S}}
is
h_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\ast}(1)=n
.
The total number of lattice points is
L_{{\mathcal{S}}}(1)=h_{1}^{\ast}+n+1=n+2
. Since the degree of

h_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\ast}
is
n-1
and it is palindromic,
{\mathcal{S}}
is Gorenstein of index
2
thus contains no interior
lattice points. Because
{\mathcal{S}}
is a toric diagram, its facets are primitive, so all
n+2
lattice points are vertices.

Next, we determine the number of facets. The standard expansion of the Ehrhart polynomial dictates that the
coefficient of
t^{n-1}
in
L_{{\mathcal{S}}}(t)
is

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{S}})}\text{Vol}_{n-1}(F).

Because every facet
F
of a toric diagram is a unimodular simplex, the relative volume

\text{Vol}_{n-1}(F)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}
, making this coefficient exactly
\frac{f_{n-1}}{2(n-1)!}
. Using our specific

h^{\ast}
-polynomial, the Ehrhart polynomial is
L_{{\mathcal{S}}}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{t+n-j}{n}
, where the coefficient
of
t^{n-1}
is
\frac{n}{(n-1)!}
. Equating these gives a facet count of
f_{n-1}=2n
.

Any
n
-dimensional polytope with
n+2
vertices admits a unique (up to scaling) affine dependency

\sum\lambda_{i}\bm{v}_{i}=0
with
\sum\lambda_{i}=0
. Because every facet of
{\mathcal{S}}
is a simplex, no subset of

n+1
vertices can be affinely dependent. This strictly guarantees that
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
, meaning every vertex
participates in the dependency. This dependency therefore partitions the vertices into exactly two disjoint sets

I=\{\bm{v}_{i}:\lambda_{i}>0\}
and
J=\{\bm{v}_{j}:\lambda_{j}<0\}
. A subset of vertices forms a facet if and only if it is
obtained by removing exactly one vertex from
I
and one from
J
. Thus, the number of facets is given by the product

|I|\cdot|J|
. We are led to the system

|I|+|J|=n+2\quad\text{and}\quad|I|\cdot|J|=2n\,,

whose unique integer solution is
\{|I|,|J|\}=\{n,2\}
. A polytope whose vertices are partitioned in this way is,
by definition, a bipyramid over a
(n-1)
-simplex. Let
{\mathcal{E}}=\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
be the base vertices and

A=\{\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2}\}
be the apices.

The bipyramid
{\mathcal{S}}
is triangulated by
n
lattice simplices
S_{i}=\text{conv}(({\mathcal{E}}\setminus\{\bm{v}_{i}\})\cup{\mathcal{A}})
,
therefore the sum of their normalized volumes must be
n
. Since each
S_{i}
is a lattice simplex, its normalized volume must
be an integer greater than or equal to
1
. This forces the normalized volume of
S_{i}
to be
1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. The equality
of these volumes implies that the coefficients of the base vertices in the affine dependency are identical. The dependency relation
is thus

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{v}_{i}=\mu_{1}\bm{y}_{1}+\mu_{2}\bm{y}_{2}\,,

where
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}=n
. By setting
k=\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}
, we find
\mu_{1}=\frac{n-k}{2}
and
\mu_{2}=\frac{n+k}{2}
.
Up to swapping the apices
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
, we may assume
0\leq k<n
.

Finally, because
\text{Vol}(S_{n})=1
, the vectors
\{\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n-1}-\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1}\}

form a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for the lattice. Let
\phi
be the affine unimodular transformation that maps this basis to the basis
formed by the corresponding vertices of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
:

\phi(\bm{y}_{1})=\bm{a}_{n},\quad\phi(\bm{y}_{2})=\bm{a}_{n+1},\quad\phi(\bm{v}_{i})=\bm{a}_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq n-1\,.

Applying
\phi
to our dependency relation isolates
\phi(\bm{v}_{n})

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\bm{a}_{i}+\phi(\bm{v}_{n})=\frac{n-k}{2}\bm{a}_{n}+\frac{n+k}{2}\bm{a}_{n+1}\implies\phi(\bm{v}_{n})=\left(-1,\dots,-1,-k,\frac{n+k}{2}\right)\,.

For
\phi(\bm{v}_{n})
to be an integral lattice point, the coordinate
\frac{n+k}{2}
must be an integer, which forces the condition

k\equiv n\pmod{2}
. Therefore,
