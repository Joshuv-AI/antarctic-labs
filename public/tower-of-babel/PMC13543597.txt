Structural insights into the specificity of adaptor proteins Dok5 and Dok6 Docking to Trk receptors
Authors: Yingyao Wu, Xiaojiao Sun, Hongyue Chen, Bin Yin, Lin Hou, Boqin Qiang, Pengcheng Shu, Xiaozhong Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpecific neurotrophic signaling by Trk receptors relies on the selective recruitment of downstream adaptors such as Dok5 and Dok6, yet the structural basis underlying this selectivity has remained unresolved. Here, we elucidate the principles governing Trk-Dok specificity by integrating structural prediction with biochemical validation. Comparative analysis of AlphaFold3-predicted complexes reveals that the intracellular domains of Trk dimers exhibit model-based interpretation, matching the distinct pocket architectures of Dok5 and Dok6. Ligand-induced phosphorylation exposes critical Trk tyrosine residues, enabling high-affinity engagement by adaptor PTB domains. We identify a conserved interaction hotspot centered on Trk Tyr515/516 and Dok Tyr187 that anchors this recognition. Crucially, co-immunoprecipitation assays validate that the C-terminal domains of Dok proteins exert modulatory control via steric hindrance, a finding consistent with our structural model. Collectively, our study establishes spatial complementarity and phosphorylation-dependent exposure of docking sites as key principles governing Trk-Dok signaling specificity, providing new structural insights into neurotrophin signaling and its potential therapeutic modulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03004-w.Keywords: Trk receptors, Dok5/Dok6, Neurotrophin signaling, Phosphorylation-dependent binding, Structural specificity

Introduction

The tropomyosin receptor kinase (Trk) family of neurotrophin receptor tyrosine kinases, comprising TrkA, TrkB, and TrkC, plays a pivotal role in regulating neuronal survival [12], differentiation [2, 9], synaptic plasticity [1], and the formation of neural circuits [19]. These effects are mediated by binding specific neurotrophins: nerve growth factor (NGF) for TrkA [4, 8], brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) for TrkB [17], and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) for TrkC [7, 10, 20], triggering receptor dimerization and autophosphorylation at intracellular tyrosine residues [13]. These phosphorylation events serve as docking platforms for adaptor proteins and initiate downstream signaling cascades, including MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and PLCγ pathways [11, 14, 15], thereby orchestrating key processes such as neuronal survival, differentiation, and axonal pathfinding [2].

Although TrkA, TrkB, and TrkC share substantial structural similarity, with 48-56% sequence identity in their intracellular domains (ICDs), they elicit distinct signaling outcomes. Notably, TrkB and TrkC exhibit partially overlapping expression patterns in the developing cortex and hippocampus yet mediate divergent effects on synaptic connectivity and neuroplasticity [6]. This functional diversity is hypothesized to arise from selective recruitment of adaptor proteins to phosphorylated tyrosine docking motifs, but the precise molecular mechanisms governing adaptor specificity remain elusive.

Among the Trk-interacting adaptor proteins, Dok5 and Dok6, members of the downstream of kinase (Dok) family, are of particular interest due to their high neuronal specificity and critical roles in neurodevelopmental signaling [6, 16]. Both Dok5 and Dok6 contain a conserved pleckstrin homology (PH) domain and phosphotyrosine-binding (PTB) domain, which facilitate recruitment to activated Trk receptors. However, despite sharing ~ 69% sequence identity, Dok5 and Dok6 exhibit strikingly different binding profiles: Dok5 interacts with both TrkB and TrkC, whereas Dok6 preferentially binds TrkC and shows only weak affinity toward TrkB [6]. These differences imply that spatial structural compatibility rather than primary sequence homology may govern adaptor-receptor recognition.

Furthermore, previous studies have primarily focused on signaling consequences downstream of Dok recruitment, leaving the structural determinants of selectivity largely unexplored. For instance, while Dok5-mediated activation of MAPK/ERK signaling downstream of TrkB and TrkC is well established [16, 18], Dok6 binds specifically to the NPQY motif of the TrkC receptor via its PTB domain [6]. Further investigation revealed that Dok6 selectively mediates signaling and retrograde transport downstream of TrkC and Ret receptors, facilitating neurotrophin-Trk signaling, but the molecular basis for Dok6’s restricted TrkC preference remains poorly defined. Importantly, dysregulation of Trk receptor signaling has been implicated in numerous neurodevelopmental disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and psychiatric conditions. Therefore, understanding how adaptor proteins selectively couple to distinct Trk isoforms is essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying neuronal circuit formation and holds translational potential for targeted modulation of neurotrophic pathways.

In this study, we combined full-length phosphorylation modeling, high-resolution molecular docking simulations, and biochemical validation to systematically investigate the structural basis of Dok5/Dok6 selectivity toward mouse and human Trk receptors. We identified Trk Tyr516 as a critical phosphorylated docking hotspot and Tyr187 of Dok5/Dok6 as a potential key residue influencing adaptor engagement. Our results reveal that Dok5 achieves optimal complementarity to both TrkB and TrkC, whereas Dok6 adopts a bulkier conformational architecture that introduces steric clashes, thereby limiting productive binding to TrkB. By integrating computational modeling with biophysical and biochemical assays, we uncover a conformationally driven mechanism underlying adaptor selectivity and provide structural insights into how neurotrophic signaling specificity is encoded at the adaptor–receptor interface.

Results

Adaptor-receptor steric compatibility governs Trk signaling specificity

We first employed AlphaFold3 to predict and compare the structures of mouse TrkA, TrkB, and TrkC (hereafter termed mTrkA, mTrkB, and mTrkC, respectively). These receptors share a conserved domain architecture, comprising extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular domains (ICDs) (Fig. 1A, B, and S1). Structural analysis of ligand-induced dimers revealed marked differences in the steric accessibility of their ICDs. The ICD of mTrkA is highly sterically hindered in the dimeric state (Fig. 1C, black dashed circles), likely impeding stable adaptor binding. In contrast, mTrkB dimers adopt a partially open intracellular conformation, while mTrkC dimers exhibit the greatest degree of openness (Fig. 1E, and G, black dashed circles and orange lines). Phosphorylation of specific tyrosine residues critical for adaptor recruitment increases their solvent-accessible surface areas (highlighted by white dashed circles), thereby enhancing docking potential. Furthermore, regions undergoing secondary structural rearrangements post-phosphorylation (Fig. 1D, F, and H, brown circles) suggest local flexibility and pocket expansion that may facilitate adaptor recruitment. Notably, comparison of non-phosphorylated and tyrosine-phosphorylated Trk dimers underscored the impact of receptor activation. In the non-phosphorylated state, key residues (e.g., Tyr516, Tyr705, Tyr709, Tyr710, Tyr820) remain partially buried or occluded, restricting adaptor accessibility. Phosphorylation introduces negatively charged phosphate groups that induce local steric and electrostatic rearrangements, driving these tyrosines into solvent-exposed orientations (Fig. 1C, E, G, S1A, S1C, and S1E, white dashed circles). Structural alignments further demonstrate that the intracellular domains of human and mouse Trk receptors exhibit a high degree of conservation, particularly within the phosphotyrosine-centered docking motifs and their surrounding pocket geometries, underscoring that the conformational principles identified in mouse Trk dimers are structurally preserved across species (Fig. 2, and S2).

This gradient of structural accessibility indicates that the intrinsic conformational states of Trk ICDs strongly influence adaptor engagement. Consistent with this notion, AlphaFold-predicted structures of mouse Dok5 (mDok5) and mouse Dok6 (mDok6) (Fig. 3A, B, and G) show that both contain a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain and a phosphotyrosine-binding (PTB) domain, but differ substantially in their C-terminal structures. mDok5 exhibits a compact conformation with a 37.7 Å docking pocket (Fig. 3C, and D), whereas mDok6 displays an extended structure with a larger 45.0 Å pocket (Fig. 3E, and F). These structural distinctions, which are highly conserved in human homologs (Figures S3), likely underpin their receptor specificity. This conformational change generates optimal docking surfaces for the PTB domains of mDok5 and mDok6. Our data demonstrate that phosphorylation of these critical tyrosines facilitates interaction with Dok5/Dok6 PTB domains and markedly enhances Trk-Dok5/Dok6 complex formation in mice (Figures S4). An analogous, evolutionarily conserved mechanism is observed in human orthologs (Figures S5), underscoring phosphorylation as a pivotal regulatory step in Trk-Dok signaling complex assembly.

To further assess the reliability and dynamic features of these structural models, confidence metrics were analyzed. The kinase core and extracellular ligand-binding domains exhibited high to medium pLDDT scores (> 70), indicating stable structural predictions, whereas the C-terminal tails displayed low confidence (pLDDT < 70), suggesting intrinsic flexibility (Figures S6, S7). Predicted TM-scores (pTM) for Trk receptors ranged from 0.31-0.38 (ipTM 0.16-0.34), reflecting moderate reliability in overall topology but limited certainty in relative domain orientations. Similarly, Dok5/Dok6 core PH-PTB domains were highly ordered (pLDDT > 70) with pTM values of 0.63-0.65, whereas their C-terminal extensions were largely disordered, consistent with their predicted aligned error (PAE) maps indicating significant relative motion between N- and C-terminal regions.

This “rigid core + flexible tail” structural organization provides a spatial framework that could facilitate multi-site phosphorylation and selective adaptor engagement under dynamic signaling conditions.

A conserved tyrosine residue governs spatial selectivity of Trk-Dok engagement

While previous studies have established Dok5 and Dok6 as tyrosine-phosphorylated adaptors downstream of RTKs like c-Ret [3, 5], the structural basis for their specific recognition by Trk receptors remains unclear. To investigate the molecular mechanism of adaptor protein recognition, we performed site-specific phosphorylation modeling of tyrosine residues in the C-terminal region (Figure S8) and full-length (Fig. 4) mDok5 and mDok6, followed by molecular docking analyses with mouse Trk receptors.

Docking simulations revealed that full-length tyrosine-phosphorylated mDok5 readily interacts with phosphorylated mTrkB (Fig. 4A) and mTrkC (Fig. 4C) dimers, forming extensive hydrogen bonds and salt bridges. For the mDok5-mTrkB complex, key interactions included hydrogen bonds (e.g., mDok5 R185-mTrkB Y515, mDok5 Y187-mTrkB I510) and a potential salt bridge network involving mDok5 R19/K58/K90 and mTrkB D475 (Fig. 4A). Binding free energy calculations, performed using HawkDock Server V2 with MM/GBSA‑based estimation under an implicit solvent model, were used for qualitative and relative comparisons only. These calculations highlighted mDok5 R185 and Y187 as major contributors to complex stability (Fig. 4B). Similarly, for the mDok5-mTrkC complex, critical hydrogen bonds (e.g., mDok5 R185-mTrkC Y516, mDok5 Y187-mTrkC I511) and salt bridges (e.g., mDok5 K44-mTrkC E357/E360) were identified (Fig. 4C), with residues R185 and Y516 providing primary stabilization (Fig. 4D).

Notably, mDok6 exhibited specific binding to mTrkC (Fig. 4E), involving hydrogen bonds such as mDok6 Y187-mTrkC I511 and mDok6 M201-mTrkC Y516 (Fig. 4F). Binding free energy analysis confirmed these residues provide moderate but significant stabilizing interactions (Fig. 4F). A central finding is the conserved role of a tyrosine pair: mTrkB Y515/mTrkC Y516 (human TrkB/TrkC Y516) and Tyr187 in both Dok5 and Dok6. This interaction pattern was consistent across both full-length (Fig. 4) and C-terminal phosphorylation models (FigureS8 and Table S1), underscoring its robustness.

In summary, phosphorylation of key tyrosine residues within Trk ICDs is critical for stable complex formation with Dok5/Dok6. The identified conservation between mouse and human orthologs (Figs. 5, S5, S9, and Tables S1-S4) not only validates the mouse as a reliable model for human neurotrophin signaling but also suggests that evolutionary conservation of Trk-Dok interactions fine-tunes the strength, selectivity, and duration of neurotrophin signaling.

Tyrosine-dependent Trk-Dok interactions control complex formation

To validate the functional importance of the conserved tyrosine residues identified in our docking models, we performed co‑immunoprecipitation assays using site‑directed mutants (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, mutations of TrkB Y515 to phenylalanine, glutamic acid and alanine (Y515F/Y515E/Y515A) markedly reduced their association with Dok5. Similarly, mutation of TrkC Y516 to F, E or A impaired Dok5 binding (Fig. 6C). Parallel experiments with Dok6 confirmed that the same Y516 residue is critical for TrkC-Dok6 complex formation (Fig. 6D).

To further map the adaptor residues involved, we generated Dok5 R185A and Y187F mutants. The R185A and Y187F mutation significantly reduced TrkB binding (Fig. 6E). All of the Dok5 D150A, R185A and Y187F disrupted TrkC interaction (Fig. 6F). Notably, Dok6 Y187F similarly impaired TrkC binding (Fig. 6G). These results collectively establish that Trk Y515/Y516 and Dok Y187 form a conserved phosphotyrosine‑recognition module critical for complex formation.

Experimental validation of steric hindrance in Trk-Dok interactions

To further investigate how structural differences influence selective binding of Dok proteins to Trk receptors, we compared the amino acid sequences of full-length and truncated isoforms of mDok5 (Dok5, Dok5-L) and mDok6 (Dok6, Dok6-S). Sequence alignment revealed strong conservation within the PH and PTB domains, with notable divergence in the C-terminal regions, particularly in mDok5-L and mDok6, which possess extended sequences (Fig. 7A).

To assess the functional implications of these variations, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays in HEK293T cells co-expressing mouse Myc-tagged Dok isoforms with either mouse Flag-tagged TrkB or mouse Flag-tagged TrkC (Fig. 7B). Mouse wild-type Dok5 robustly interacted with mouse TrkB, whereas elongation of its C-terminal region (mouse Dok5-L) markedly reduced binding, suggesting that steric hindrance impairs complex formation. In contrast, mouse Dok6 displayed only minimal interaction with mouse TrkB, consistent with its larger structural size, and truncation of its C-terminal region (mouse Dok6-S) failed to appreciably enhance mouse TrkB binding. Similarly, extension of the mouse Dok5 C-terminal region weakened its interaction with mouse TrkC, while mouse Dok6 truncation did not improve binding.

We next performed co-immunoprecipitation assays in N1E-115 cells stably expressing lentiviral constructs encoding Myc-tagged mouse Dok isoforms together with Flag-tagged mouse TrkB or TrkC (Fig. 7C). Cells were stimulated with medium containing 100 ng/mL NT-3 for 15 min prior to lysis to activate Trk receptors. Wild-type mDok5 exhibited robust interaction with mTrkB, whereas the C-terminally extended isoform (mDok5-L) showed markedly reduced binding (Fig. 7D), suggesting that C-terminal elongation imposes steric constraints that impair receptor-adaptor complex formation. In contrast, mDok6 displayed minimal association with mTrkB (Fig. 7D), consistent with its more extended structural configuration, and truncation of its C-terminal region (mDok6-S) did not significantly enhance binding. Similarly, C-terminal extension of mDok5 reduced its interaction with mTrkC (Fig. 7D), whereas truncation of mDok6 failed to promote detectable binding (Fig. 7D). Consistent results were also obtained in HEK293T cells under overexpression conditions, further confirming that C-terminal structural variation modulates Trk-Dok interactions.

Quantification of co‑IP results from three independent experiments confirmed that mDok5 exhibits significantly stronger interaction with mTrkB than mDok5-L, whereas mDok6-S displays minimal binding to both mTrkB and mTrkC (Fig. 7E). Collectively, these findings indicate that although the C-terminal regions of mDok5 and mDok6 do not serve as primary interaction interfaces, their spatial conformations nonetheless modulate Trk receptor association. This highlights the crucial roles of receptor ICD topology and adaptor structural conformation in determining Trk-Dok docking specificity, consistent with the molecular pocket-adaptor compatibility model. Together, the results support a framework in which docking specificity is jointly governed by receptor pocket geometry and adaptor conformational constraints (Fig. 7F).

Coordinated evolutionary expansion of Dok and Trk gene families

To investigate the evolutionary relationship between DOK adaptor proteins and Trk receptors, we performed a comparative genomic analysis across representative invertebrate and vertebrate species using Ensembl Gene Gain/Loss Trees. Gene copy numbers were mapped onto a phylogenetic framework based on orthology relationships.

Our analysis showed that both DOK4/5/6 and Trk receptor (NTRK1/2/3) gene families first emerged in early vertebrates and underwent coordinated expansion during the two rounds of vertebrate whole-genome duplication (1R/2R), with additional duplication observed in teleost fishes (3R) (Figure S10). These findings support a potential co-evolutionary relationship between DOK adaptor proteins and Trk receptors.

Discussion

In this study, we provide structural, biochemical, and evolutionary evidence that adaptor selectivity in Trk receptor signaling is governed by spatial complementarity between receptor intracellular domains (ICDs) and the docking interfaces of Dok proteins. By integrating AlphaFold3-based structural modeling with mutational and biochemical analyses, we demonstrate that intrinsic conformational differences among Trk receptors establish a hierarchy of steric accessibility that directly influences adaptor engagement. Specifically, TrkB adopts a partially open ICD conformation that preferentially accommodates Dok5, whereas TrkC exhibits a more accessible configuration compatible with both Dok5 and Dok6. In contrast, TrkA remains sterically constrained, thereby limiting adaptor access and resulting in weak binding. These findings support a model in which receptor geometry acts as a primary determinant of signaling specificity.

Phosphorylation emerges as a central regulatory mechanism that dynamically controls adaptor recruitment. Structural comparisons between non-phosphorylated and phosphorylated Trk dimers reveal that key tyrosine residues are partially occluded in the inactive state but become solvent-exposed upon phosphorylation. This transition is driven by electrostatic repulsion and local conformational rearrangements induced by negatively charged phosphate groups, which collectively increase docking accessibility. Our data identify TrkB Tyr515 and TrkC Tyr516 as conserved phosphotyrosine docking sites critical for Dok recruitment. At the adaptor level, Tyr187 in both Dok5 and Dok6 plays a pivotal role in stabilizing receptor interaction, forming a conserved receptor-adaptor recognition module. This tyrosine-centered interaction pattern is consistently observed across modeling, docking, and mutational analyses, underscoring its robustness and evolutionary conservation.

A key insight from this study is the role of adaptor conformational plasticity in modulating receptor binding. Although the PH and PTB domains of Dok5 and Dok6 are highly conserved and serve as primary interaction modules, their C-terminal regions exhibit substantial structural divergence. mDok5 adopts a relatively compact conformation, whereas mDok6 displays a more extended architecture with a larger docking pocket. Functional validation using isoform variants further demonstrates that C-terminal extension of Dok5 (Dok5-L) significantly reduces binding to both TrkB and TrkC, supporting a steric hindrance model in which elongated structures interfere with productive receptor-adaptor interactions. In contrast, truncation of the Dok6 C-terminal region (Dok6-S) does not enhance binding, indicating that its reduced affinity arises from global structural constraints rather than tail length alone. These results highlight that the C-terminal regions, while not directly participating in binding, regulate adaptor accessibility by shaping overall conformational states. The results obtained from the N1E-115 lentiviral stable expression cell lines were consistent with those from the HEK293T overexpression system.

Our confidence analysis of AlphaFold3 models further supports this interpretation. The PH-PTB core domains exhibit high structural confidence, whereas the C-terminal regions show low pLDDT scores and high predicted aligned error, consistent with intrinsic disorder and conformational flexibility. This “rigid core + flexible tail” organization provides a structural basis for dynamic regulation, allowing adaptors to sample multiple conformations while maintaining a stable docking interface. Such flexibility may be critical for accommodating diverse receptor geometries and enabling context-dependent signaling.

Beyond structural and functional insights, our evolutionary analysis reveals that DOK4/5/6 adaptor proteins and Trk receptors (NTRK1/2/3) emerged and expanded coordinately during early vertebrate evolution, particularly through whole-genome duplication events. This co-expansion suggests a co-evolutionary relationship in which adaptor diversity and receptor specialization evolved in parallel to fine-tune neurotrophin signaling. The strong conservation of phosphotyrosine docking motifs and adaptor recognition residues between mouse and human orthologs further supports the physiological relevance and evolutionary stability of this signaling mechanism.

We note several limitations of the current study. Although AlphaFold3-based modeling and docking provide valuable structural insights, Trk ICDs are intrinsically dynamic and subject to complex phosphorylation-dependent regulation that may not be fully captured by static predictions. Our confidence analysis (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7) indicates that while the kinase core and PH‑PTB domains are predicted with high confidence, the C‑terminal regions exhibit low confidence and high predicted aligned error, consistent with intrinsic disorder. Therefore, the observed differences in “steric accessibility” should be interpreted as model‑based predictions rather than definitive static states. In addition, while our co-immunoprecipitation and signaling assays in N1E-115 cells provide functional validation, these systems rely on exogenous expression and may not fully reflect endogenous neuronal contexts, where protein abundance, localization, and signaling dynamics are tightly regulated. Future studies using primary neurons, in vivo models, or high-resolution structural approaches such as cryo-EM will be essential to further validate and extend these findings.

In conclusion, we propose a unified model in which Trk-Dok signaling specificity is determined by the interplay between receptor pocket geometry, phosphorylation-dependent exposure of docking sites, and adaptor conformational constraints. This pocket-adaptor compatibility framework provides a mechanistic basis for selective signal propagation downstream of Trk receptors and offers new insights into how structural organization encodes signaling specificity. These findings not only advance our understanding of neurotrophin receptor biology but also provide a conceptual foundation for targeting Trk-Dok interactions in neurological diseases.

Materials and methods

Sequence analysis

Amino acid sequences of mouse and human TrkA, TrkB, TrkC, Dok5, and Dok6 were retrieved from the UniProt database. Sequence alignments were performed using Jalview. Secondary structure elements were predicted and visualized using ESPript 3.0, which allowed mapping of conserved residues and structural motifs onto sequence alignments.

Structural modeling and molecular docking

The three-dimensional structures of mouse and human Dok5, Dok6, and Trk receptors were predicted using AlphaFold3. Site-specific phosphorylation at key tyrosine residues (Tyr516, Tyr705, Tyr709, Tyr710, and Tyr820) was manually introduced based on predicted structures using PyMOL and Maestro 4.0 (Schrödinger), generating both phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated receptor models for comparative analysis.

Protein-protein docking simulations were performed to evaluate interactions between Trk intracellular domains (ICDs) and full-length or truncated Dok5/Dok6 proteins. Docking was conducted using AlphaFold-based structural prediction and modeling approaches, with phosphorylated tyrosine residues considered as key interaction sites.

Binding free energy estimation and ranking of docking poses were performed using the HawkDock Server V2, which integrates molecular docking with MM/GBSA-based free energy calculations under an implicit solvent model. Due to the inherent limitations of this approach, including restricted sampling depth and simplified solvation treatment, the calculated binding free energies were used for qualitative and relative comparisons only, rather than as absolute quantitative measurements.

Structural visualization and analysis, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and steric effects, were performed using PyMOL.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

N1E-115 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Stable cell lines expressing Myc-tagged mouse Dok isoforms (mDok5, mDok5-L, mDok6, and mDok6-S) and Flag-tagged mouse Trk receptors (mTrkB or mTrkC) were generated lentiviral transduction. Briefly, cells were infected with lentiviral particles encoding the indicated constructs and selected with appropriate antibiotics to establish stable expression. Prior to lysis, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL NT-3 for 15 min to activate Trk signaling. Cells were then lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were incubated with Pierce™ Anti-DYKDDDDK Magnetic Agarose (Invitrogen) for 20 min at room temperature. After washing, immunoprecipitates (40 μg) were eluted and subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting with anti-Myc (CST, USA) and anti-Flag (Shanghai Genomics Technology, Ltd.) antibodies. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

For transient overexpression experiments in HEK293T cells, cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding Flag-tagged Trks (TrkB, TrkC in pcDNA3.1-Flag vector) and Myc-tagged Dok variants (Dok5, Dok5-L, Dok6, Dok6-S in pcDNA4.0-Myc vector) using Hieff Trans® Liposomal 2000 Transfection Reagent (Yeasen). At 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed and processed as described above.

Ortholog identification and comparative evolutionary analysis of DOK and Trk gene families

Orthologs of DOK4, DOK5, DOK6, NTRK1, NTRK2, and NTRK3 were identified using the Ensembl genome database (release 115). Human genes were used as references, and gene copy numbers across representative species were determined based on Ensembl Gene Gain/Loss Trees, from which orthology relationships were inferred. For each gene, the copy number was defined as the number of annotated protein-coding orthologs present in the genome of each species. Species were selected to represent key evolutionary transitions, including invertebrates, basal chordates, early vertebrates (cyclostomes), jawed vertebrates, ray-finned fishes, and tetrapods. The cephalochordate species (e.g., amphioxus) was not included due to the lack of corresponding annotations in the Ensembl Gene Tree dataset. A phylogenetic tree of selected species was generated using TimeTree (http://www.timetree.org) to provide an evolutionary framework for visualization. Heatmaps of gene copy numbers were generated in R using the ComplexHeatmap package, with values displayed within each cell. Animal silhouettes were obtained from PhyloPic (https://www.phylopic.org/).
