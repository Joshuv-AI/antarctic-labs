Parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children with special health care needs: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lingling Wang, Min Yang, Xuwen Wang, Qing Ye, Xu Yang, Shixin Xiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParents of children with special health care needs (CSHCN) usually hesitate to vaccinate their children, yet limited data on factors influencing parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children are available. Our study aimed to reveal parents’ willingness to immunize their children with the Expanded Programme on Immunization Vaccines and identify determinants influencing parents’ willingness.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey using a stratified sampling approach in Wuxi, China. Description methods were used to describe guardians’ willingness to vaccinate their children, and logistic regression analysis was employed to examine the influencing factors of parents’ willingness.ResultsA total of 904 parents/guardians were collected; 864 (95.58%) were willing to immunize their children. Univariate analysis revealed that parents’ knowledge of vaccines and their awareness of vaccination assessment clinics were factors that influenced vaccination willingness. The multivariate logistic regression results indicated that parental education level and awareness of vaccination assessment clinics were significant predictors of parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children.ConclusionHigher parental knowledge and use of vaccination consultation clinic services are associated with greater willingness to vaccinate among parents of children with special health care needs.Keywords: Children with special health care needs, vaccination, willingness, influencing factors

Introduction

Children with special health care needs (CSHCN) have congenital or acquired physical, developmental, behavioural or emotional disorders [1]. Due to their medical condition, they always have an increased risk of infection compared to the general population. Simultaneously, such individuals frequently fail to receive timely vaccine protection, resulting in heightened susceptibility to infection and subsequent adverse effects on health [2]. Although the national vaccination coverage rate has recently been maintained at over 95% [3], it has been a long-term issue for CSHCN to vaccinate adequately, and vaccination coverage among CSHCN remains suboptimal [2,4,5]. Outbreak surveillance data showed that children with underlying diseases face substantial infection risks in congregate settings, such as welfare centres and hospitals [6].

Furthermore, there has been little field evaluation of the factors influencing vaccine uptake among these populations. The underlying factors of incomplete vaccination are complicated and involve children, parents and vaccine providers [6]. Studies have been conducted to explore the willingness of vaccination staff to vaccinate children with medical conditions [7], and they suggested that vaccination providers demonstrate high willingness to vaccinate children with special health care needs after enhanced training. While, parents act as primary decision-makers and their hesitant attitudes directly influences the coverage rate of CSHCN [8,9]. There is no consensus on the definition of ‘vaccine hesitancy’, a recent review [9] argues that it should be understood as a specific psychological state rather than the final vaccination behaviour. Parental vaccine hesitancy (PVH) may result in children delaying or refusing vaccination, thereby increasing their susceptibility to preventable diseases. Extensive studies have examined PVH towards vaccines (e.g. varicella [10], pneumonia [11] and influenza [12], among healthy children, but much less attention has been paid to CSHCN. A review compared PVH rates between parents of children with chronic and/or immunocompromising conditions (CCIC) and parents of healthy children. It found a PVH prevalence of 25% in CCIC parents versus 13% in healthy controls, with higher odds observed for type 1 diabetes mellitus, autism spectrum disorder and disability [13]. Another study demonstrated that caregivers’ willingness to vaccinate their children with special health care needs with NIP and non-NIP vaccines was highest during the COVID-19 pandemic in China [14].

Recently, favourable measures such as providing immunisation advisory services have been introduced to promote vaccination among children with special needs. To date, there are still some gaps, such as delays or missed vaccines, in the implementation of immunization for CSHCN in practice. Therefore, we are interested in exploring the knowledge and attitudes of parents towards vaccination and related social supportive measures. Using a self-designed questionnaire, we investigated the underlying factors influencing their vaccination willingness, which is essential for tailoring interventions based on these factors and for increasing vaccination coverage among CSHCN.

Materials and methods

Study participants

We conducted a cross-sectional survey among parents/guardians of CSHCN when they took their children to community health service centres for routine National Immunization Program (NIP) immunization or children’s physical examinations, which every child should receive. Participants were selected randomly from 1 March 2025 to 31 September 2025, and a stratified sampling approach was adopted to cover all seven districts of Wuxi. Parents were recruited from each selected community health service centres, Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) local residents who have been residents for at least three consecutive months in Wuxi; 2) CSHCN were those who had ever or now with special health care needs involving physiological, pathological psychological or behavioural impairments; Exclusion criteria were: 1) parents with communication barriers such as language differences or cognitive impairments; 2) duplicate records due to twin pairs (only one child per pair was recruited). All participants voluntarily participated in the survey and provided their informed consent.

The sample size was determined using the statistical formula for sample size calculation: n=Z1-α/2 2p (1-p)/δ2, with the following parameters: willingness rate (p) set at 82%, referencing the 81.91%–90.34% NIP vaccination rate in children with congenital heart disease [5], a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an allowable error (δ) of 0.05 and a design effect (Deff) of 1.5 for stratified sampling. Therefore, the base sample size was 227. By incorporating the stratified sampling design effect, the adjusted sample size was calculated as n = 227 × 1.5 = 341. Finally, accounting for an assumed 20% nonresponse rate, the final required minimum sample size was determined to be 410.

All seven districts of Wuxi city were covered in this study, 130 participants were randomly recruited from each district to ensure geographic representativeness of the city. Within each district, community health service centres were randomly selected from the official registry. Based on the resident population, the number of CSHCNs selected per centre was determined. Parents or primary caregivers of CSHCN who visited the selected centres during the study period (from 1 March 2025 to 31 September 2025) were consecutively invited to participate. 910 participants were surveyed and we drop out 6 questionnaires including missing data. Finally, a total of 904 valid questionnaires were eligible for analysis.

Data collection

Field surveys and data collection were conducted by trained medical staff in community health service centres through online questionnaire. Although no standardized survey tools have been developed recently, questionnaire comprising of three sections was released by Wuxi Centre for Disease Control and Prevention based on literature review, expert consultation, and field interviews. After the initial version was developed, a pilot survey was conducted to refine and optimize each item, leading to the final version of the questionnaire. The first part was designed to collect information on children’s and parents’ sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age, health status, parents’ educational background, income and healthcare-related work experience. The second part was primarily about the parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children, and the third part was the factors influencing willingness (i.e. vaccine-related knowledge, attitudes and concerns about childhood vaccination assessment). To ensure acceptability and anonymity, a questionnaire was provided in an electronic format and delivered via WeChat, a widely used app in China. Following initial face-to-face communication, participants received the survey link through WeChat and independently completed it on their mobile devices before exiting community health service centres. Our team ensured quality control during data collection.

Study variables

We classified CSHCN into two groups: 1) special physiological status, refers to transient conditions such as physiological jaundice and premature infants, and 2) special pathological status, refers to confirmed chronic or disease-related conditions expected to last ≥12 months and requiring specialized care. The diseases included in this study were based on the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 11th Revision (ICD-11) but were not limited to the following disease categories: developmental anomalies, certain conditions originating in the perinatal period, diseases of the nervous system, diseases of the blood or blood-forming organs, diseases of the digestive system, diseases of the immune system, infectious diseases, diseases of the genitourinary system, diseases of the respiratory system, diseases of the skin, neoplasms and others. Diagnosis verification was based on medical records presented by parents/caregivers. Two researchers independently reviewed the original documentation (e.g. inpatient, outpatient records) and the primary/principal diagnosis documented was used for classification. Cases lacking supporting documentation were excluded.

Vaccination assessment, conducted by medical staff in the children’s vaccination consultation clinic (VCC), involves professional consultation for CSHCN and makes recommendations for vaccination. Wuxi opened its first VCC at the Children’s Hospital of Wuxi in December 2020. Participants’ attitudes towards VCCs were assessed using the following three criteria: a) don’t really know what it is, b) know some facts, c) have a good understanding (‘How much do you know about the vaccination consultation clinics?’).

Awareness and knowledge assessment

In this study, associated factors involved were measured using single-item self-report questions. Participants responded based on their subjective perception, concerned about the safety (‘Are you concerned about the safety of the vaccinations?’), knowledge level (‘How much do you know about the vaccination?’), anxiety of vaccination (‘Does your anxiety about childhood vaccination interfere with your daily life or make you feel nervous?’).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS, version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for descriptive statistics and chi-square tests, and SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) for Firth’s penalized logistic regression. Descriptive statistics were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The chi-square/Fisher’s exact test was used to compare vaccination willingness between groups. A Firth’s penalized logistic regression, adjusting for all variables, was conducted to identify factors associated with guardians’ willingness to vaccinate their children with NIP vaccines. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Institutional Review Board of the Wuxi Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (approval no. 202308), and all examinations and treatments were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects described in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were notified of the purpose and potential use of the collected data in an informed consent form. Only those who signed an electronic informed consent form were permitted to complete the questionnaire.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 904 parents were included: 437 (48.34%) of their children were boys and 467 (51.66%) were girls. 105 (30.09%) were 5 years old and 55 (15.71%) were 3 years old. Children with special pathological status accounted for 66.92%, and the majority (57.52%) of parents’ education levels were undergraduate or other equivalent levels. 9.29% of the parents majored in or worked in healthcare-related fields (Table 1).

Guardians’ willingness and associated factors

Among the 904 parents, 864 (95.58%) were willing to vaccinate their children. Univariate analysis showed that parents’ knowledge level of vaccines and their awareness of VCC are factors influencing their willingness to be vaccinated. Multivariate analysis revealed that parents’ education level and parents’ awareness of vaccination consultation clinics were predictors of higher willingness. Description of vaccination consultation acceptance

Of the 904 parents, 355 (39.27%) had previously visited the VCC for vaccination assessment. After the assessment, 242 (68.17%) of them relieved their anxiety. We further explored parental attitudes about vaccination assessment and participants could select multiple attitudes, 283 (79.72%) recognized its professionality; 42 (11.83%) thought that vaccination assessment was not convenient enough; and 9 (2.54%), 36 (10.14%) and 86 (24.23%) were not willing to seek vaccination assessment due to medical expenses, time costs and distance to medical care, respectively.

Discussion

Concerns about vaccination of CSHCN increase, except for the special health care needs children experienced, and the hesitancy of parents towards vaccination uptake is still an important factor that hinders the coverage of CSHCN. Few studies have been conducted in this field, particularly in vulnerable populations. In this survey, 95.58% of the parents involved were willing to have their children vaccinated, while a minority (4.42%) expressed unwillingness, although citizens were required to receive NIP vaccines as part of public health obligations. Our findings highlight that higher parental knowledge and use of vaccination consultation clinic services are associated with greater willingness to vaccinate, suggesting potential targets for future intervention.

In practice, delayed or refused vaccinations occur frequently among the CSHCN population. Relevant studies in Brazil [4] and Chile [15] indicated that 10.9% and 42.9% of CSHCN have not received or have not fully received the recommended vaccines, respectively. Studies conducted in two cities in China revealed that the delay rate of CSHCN vaccination was rather high (53.10% and 82.81%, respectively) [6,16]. Factors influencing their deferring vaccination include parents’ concerns about the risks associated with vaccination and social-medical support [17,18]. We further explored these factors and found that parental education level and their awareness of VCC are crucial for enhancing the willingness to vaccinate. Chen et al. [19] conducted a survey in children with congenital heart disease and similarly revealed that the age of children, parents’ education, annual household income, and having ever received professional vaccination guidance were all associated with vaccination coverage.

Our study identified parental education level and vaccine-related knowledge as significant factors associated with vaccination willingness among parents of CSHCN. These associations may be explained by differences in health literacy, information access and trust in medical systems, parents with higher education are more likely to understand vaccine benefits and risks, and are more favourable towards vaccination. Additionally, immunization consultation plays a vital role in overcoming the barrier to vaccination in CSHCN. In China, Several cities have established VCCs, and many previous studies have demonstrated that these measures achieve favourable outcomes in promoting vaccination among CSHCN [20]. In Wuxi, two VCCs were established and a system was created to refer to the CSHCN between immunization advisory clinics and community vaccination clinics. Notably, we found that parents who had ever sought immunization advice from the VCC or had clear knowledge or perception of vaccination assessment were more willing to achieve timely and appropriate vaccines for their children. Feng et al. [21] listed 10 proposed measures advised by the respondents to promote vaccination of CSHCN and mentioned that immunization advisory clinics with government endorsement in tertiary children’s hospitals were recommended to provide vaccination assessment. We further describe the attitudes of the participants who had visited the VCCs, and the results showed that 68.17% relieved their anxiety and 79.72% recognized its professionality. The integration of medical assessments and community-based interventions has been demonstrated to be significant in promoting CSHCN vaccination, studies conducted in the USA [22] and Canada [23] showed that family doctors and paediatric specialists could provide medical care with augmented services; they played a positive role in advocating for immunization for the CSHCN. However, other studies believe that the role of VCCs is limited [5,7,24]. In our study, 2.54%, 10.14% and 24.23% of those who had visited the VCCs were unwilling to seek a vaccination assessment due to medical expenses, time and distance cost, respectively, which added to the parents’ burden. Thus, further comprehensive measures should be implemented to enhance public health practices. For example, focus should be placed on diverse communication channels to implement health education to promote a comprehensive understanding of the benefits and risks of vaccination among parents of CSHCN, thereby improving parental vaccine confidence. In particular, there is an urgent need to develop detailed and unified vaccination guidelines for children with special health conditions, which will help enhance vaccination willingness and reduce hesitancy. Above recommendations are intrinsically consistent with the intervention framework proposed by a recent multi-study review, which highlights the role of trusted messengers, health literacy and structural reforms to reduce barriers in rebuilding vaccine confidence [25]. Notably, several findings in our study diverge from the existing literature. For instance, contrary to Doherty et al. [26] and Brumbaugh et al. [25] who found significant effects in general/special populations, household income and safety concerns emerged as significant predictors of vaccine hesitancy, we propose that this discrepancy may stem from the fundamental differences between the study populations. Specifically, parents of CSHCN are primarily focused on their child’s underlying medical condition rather than general vaccine concerns. Moreover, It is regrettable that we did not comprehensively assess multiple clinical factors (e.g. comorbidities, history of allergy), and further studies are needed to validate our findings and to clarify the differences from prior research.

High willingness rate (95.58%) was observed in the study and should be interpreted cautiously. First, social desirability bias may have led participants to overreport their willingness to vaccinate. Second, participants were recruited at childhood vaccination units, as a result, most included children likely had mild underlying health conditions and their parents were less likely to hold strong objections to vaccination, which may lead to an overestimation of willingness. Selection bias and self-report bias may result in wide 95% confidence intervals for some odds ratio estimates in our study. However, we would argue that the high rate may also partly reflect genuine parental concerns, as parents of CSHCN are typically highly attentive and motivated to protect their children from infections. Finally, the questionnaire used in this study was designed as a simple descriptive tool for those subjects in our study, rather than a structured, standardized or previously validated instrument. Therefore, measurement errors may exist, consequently, our findings should be interpreted with caution and be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, while the external validity of our findings should be further evaluated in future large-scale, community-based studies.

Conclusions

Higher parental knowledge and use of vaccination consultation clinic services are associated with greater willingness to vaccinate among parents of children with special health care needs.
