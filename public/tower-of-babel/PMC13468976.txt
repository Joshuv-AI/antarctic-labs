Diabetes, prediabetes, and mild cognitive impairment among older adults: the Hitachi Health Study II
Authors: Ami Fukunaga, Aoi Ito, Yosuke Inoue, Hitomi Inada, Tohru Nakagawa, Shuichiro Yamamoto, Maki Konishi, Tetsuya Mizoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimWe examined associations of diabetes, prediabetes, and glycemic markers with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in older adults, and whether these associations are modified by treatment status, diabetes duration, and glycemic control.MethodsWe used data from 2486 adults (mean age: 65 years) undergoing health check-ups and cognitive screening. Prediabetes and diabetes were defined by fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA1c, or history/medication use. Diabetes was divided into screen-detected and treated cases. Cognitive function was tested using a computerized test battery evaluating immediate memory, temporal orientation, delayed recall, and three-dimensional visual-spatial perception. Participants scoring ≤ 13 (range, 0–15) were considered to have MCI.ResultsOverall, 251 participants (10.1%) were classified with MCI. Compared with normoglycemia, odds ratios (95% CIs) were 1.45 (0.90, 2.33) for prediabetes and 1.67 (0.99, 2.81) for diabetes. When stratified by treatment status, the odds ratios were larger in treated diabetes than in screen-detected diabetes (1.77 [1.03, 3.06] vs. 1.48 [0.77, 2.84]). Larger odds ratios were also found among those with diabetes duration ≥ 10 years (2.26, 95% CI: 1.27, 4.02) and HbA1c ≥ 7% (1.90, 95% CI: 1.00, 3.60). Diabetes was associated with lower scores in temporal orientation and visual-spatial perception. Among participants without diabetes, higher FPG was associated with higher odds of MCI; the odds ratio for FPG 110–125 vs. <100 mg/dL was 1.85 (95% CI: 1.18, 2.89).ConclusionOlder adults with diabetes, particularly those with treated, longstanding, or uncontrolled diabetes, had a higher prevalence of MCI. Among those without diabetes, higher FPG was associated with MCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00592-026-02720-6.Keywords: Cognitive dysfunction, Glucose metabolism, Insulin resistance, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Diabetes is a major public health problem that increases the risk of life-threatening complications such as kidney disease, retinopathy, and neuropathy as a consequence of prolonged hyperglycemia [1]. Growing evidence suggests that diabetes also increases the risk of cognitive decline [1], through chronic hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and inadequate cerebral circulation [2]. Cognitive decline typically progresses gradually through an intermediate stage known as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) [3]. Given that MCI remains potentially reversible [3], examining its association with diabetes is essential for dementia prevention.

A meta-analysis of six cohort studies from the U.S., Australia, and Singapore has shown that older adults (mostly aged 70 years or older) with diabetes had a higher risk of MCI compared with those without diabetes [4]. An individual U.S. cohort study of adults aged 66–90 years has shown that poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%) and longer diabetes duration (≥ 5 years) were associated with a higher risk of MCI [5]. However, the evidence linking diabetes to MCI is lacking among relatively younger older adults in their 60s, an age when cognitive decline typically begins [6]. Additionally, previous studies defined diabetes based on self-report [7–9] and/or a single glycemic marker (FPG [10–13] or HbA1c [5, 14]), which have been shown to overlook 37% and 29% of diabetes cases, respectively [15]. It also remains unclear whether the association differs between newly identified diabetes and treated diabetes.

While accumulating evidence links diabetes to MCI [16], data are limited for prediabetes. Prediabetes has attracted attention not only due to its high transition rate to diabetes [1] but also due to its association with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [17] and cancer [18]. Elevated FPG levels have been associated with poorer cognitive performance [19–22], whereas results regarding HbA1c have been mixed, with some studies reporting null [23, 24] or inverse associations with cognitive function [25, 26]. These inconsistencies suggest that the association between prediabetes and MCI may differ depending on the definition of prediabetes. To our knowledge, no study has examined MCI in relation to prediabetes, which was defined by using both FPG and HbA1c, and to the subtype of prediabetes.

To address these research gaps, we examined the associations of diabetes, prediabetes, and glycemic markers with MCI among older Japanese adults, the majority of whom were in their 60s. We hypothesized that diabetes would be associated with a higher prevalence of MCI, particularly among those receiving treated diabetes, longer diabetes duration, or poor glycemic control, and that prediabetes defined by elevated FPG would show a stronger association with MCI than that defined by elevated HbA1c.

Methods

Design and participants of this study

This study’s data came from the Hitachi Health Study II baseline survey, which is an ongoing prospective cohort study of current and retired Hitachi, Ltd., employees and their spouses in Japan. Hitachi, Ltd. is a leading manufacturing company in Japan [27–29]. We used data collected during comprehensive health check-ups, which included blood tests and cognitive function assessments, conducted between April 2017 and March 2020.

Of the 2518 participants aged ≥ 60 years (as of the end of the fiscal year in which they underwent the health check-up), we excluded a participant who was referred to a clinic following cognitive screening and subsequently diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease (n = 1), a participant with missing data on the cognitive function test (n = 1), and participants who reported having a psychiatric disorder (n = 30). A total of 2486 participants (2274 men and 212 women) were included in the final analysis. The high proportion of males reflects the sex distribution of the employees in the collaborating company. Prior to data collection, participants were informed via workplace posters that their company-owned health data might be used for research purposes after anonymization. In accordance with Japanese ethical guidelines for epidemiological research, participants were given the opportunity to optout by notifying their occupational physicians. The data were anonymized at the Hitachi Health Care Center, the survey site, before being provided to the researchers at the Japan Institute for Health Security for this study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Japan Institute for Health Security (NCGM-G-002208) and by the Ethics Committee of the Hitachi Health Care Center.

Assessment of diabetes and prediabetes

FPG was determined by the glucose oxidase enzyme–electrode method (A&T, Tokyo, Japan), and HbA1c was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HLC-723G9, Tosoh, Tokyo, Japan). Antidiabetic treatment status, including diet therapy and medication, was confirmed by physicians during the health check-up. A history of diabetes was ascertained via a self-administered questionnaire embedded in the health check-up. According to the criteria of the American Diabetes Association [30], diabetes was defined as HbA1c levels of ≥ 6.5% or FPG levels of ≥ 126 mg/dL. Participants who self-reported a history of diabetes or who were receiving antidiabetic treatment were also classified as having diabetes. Prediabetes was defined as HbA1c levels 5.7–6.4% or FPG levels 100–125 mg/dL among individuals not classified as having diabetes. Normoglycemia was defined as FPG levels < 100 mg/dL and HbA1c levels < 5.7%. Serum insulin was determined by chemiluminescent immunoassay (Mitsubishi Chemical Medience Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Insulin resistance was estimated using the homeostastic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as (fasting serum insulin [mU/mL] × FPG [mg/dL])/405 [31].

We also grouped participants without diabetes based on their HbA1c (< 5.7, 5.7–5.9, or 6.0–6.4%) levels and FPG levels (< 100, 100–109, or 110–125 mg/dL), according to prediabetes diagnostic criteria established by several organizations: the American Diabetes Association (HbA1c 5.7–6.4% or FPG 100–125 mg/dL) [30], the World Health Organization (FPG 110–125 mg/dL) [32], and the International Expert Committee (HbA1c 6.0–6.4%) [33]. Participants with FPG-defined prediabetes were further classified according to the estimated duration of prediabetes (< 5 or ≥ 5 years) based on the FPG level measured 5 years before baseline. Those without FPG data from 5 years before baseline were treated as having missing data on prediabetes duration. We divided participants without diabetes into three HOMA-IR groups (< 1.6, 1.6–2.4, or ≥ 2.5) based on the criteria of the Japanese Diabetes Society [34].

The duration of diabetes was calculated using annual health check-up data collected for up to 10 years before baseline, including FPG, HbA1c, and self-reported diabetes, and was defined as the time from the year diabetes criteria were first met to baseline. Participants were categorized as having diabetes for < 10 years or ≥ 10 years. Those without health check-up data from 10 years before baseline were treated as having missing data on diabetes duration. Among participants with treated diabetes, participants were stratified by HbA1c level (< 7% or ≥ 7%), which is a treatment goal recommended by the American Diabetes Association Standards of Medical Care for many adults [35].

Assessment of MCI

Cognitive function was evaluated using a computerized screening test developed to identify individuals at risk of Alzheimer’s disease, called “The Forgetfulness Consultation Program” (MSP-1100, Nihon Kohden Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) [36, 37], which was derived from Hasegawa’s Dementia Scale (revised version) [38]. Participants completed the test by responding to prompts on a touch panel display, guided by text and voice instructions. The battery assessed four cognitive domains: immediate memory (3 points), temporal orientation (4 points), delayed recall (6 points), and three-dimensional visual-spatial perception (2 points). The total score ranged between 0 and 15, with higher scores indicating improved cognitive function. This test was developed and validated for identifying individuals at risk of Alzheimer’s disease with a cutoff score of 12/13 [36]. In community-based dementia prevention programs, a slightly higher threshold of 13/14 has been used to identify suspected MCI [39]. Thus, we defined MCI as a total score of ≤ 13 points.

Covariates

Data on lifestyle factors were obtained via a self-administered questionnaire embedded in the health check-up. Data included age, sex, job position, marital status, smoking status, alcohol drinking, sleep duration, and leisure-time physical activity. Physical activity was assessed by the question, “Do you exercise regularly?” with response options “no” (not exercising) or “yes” (exercising). Participants who answered “yes” were asked to select up to three physical activities they engaged in most frequently and to report the frequency (times per month) and duration (minutes per session) of each activity. Time spent on physical activity per week (hours/week) was estimated by multiplying the frequency and duration of each activity. Participants’ body height and weight were assessed using standardized protocols, while they wore light clothing and were barefoot. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²) and categorized as < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, or ≥ 30.0 kg/m². Blood pressure was measured using an automated sphygmomanometer. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or more, a diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or more, or the current use of antihypertensive drugs. Serum total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides were measured enzymatically. Dyslipidemia was defined as triglycerides of 150 mg/dL or more, LDL cholesterol of 140 mg/dL or more, HDL cholesterol of 40 mg/dL or less, or current use of a lipid-lowering medication.

Statistical analysis

We conducted multiple imputation using the chained equations approach under the assumption that data were missing at random [40]. All variables included in the main analysis were incorporated into the imputation model. We used predictive mean matching to create 20 imputed datasets, selecting the three nearest observed values. Each dataset was analyzed separately, and the results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules [41]. Proportions of missing data were 0.68% for marital status and 0.32% for sleep duration.

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by glycemic status were estimated using logistic regression, with normoglycemia serving as the reference group. We assigned dummy variables to glycemic markers and included them as a categorical variable in the model. Model 1 included adjustments for age and sex. Model 2 was further adjusted for job position, marital status, alcohol consumption, smoking status, leisure-time physical activity, sleep duration, BMI, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. We also examined the odds of MCI by treatment status (screen-detected diabetes or treated diabetes), diabetes duration (< 10 or ≥ 10 years), and HbA1c level (< 7% or ≥ 7%) among participants with diabetes.

To assess associations between glycemic status and domain-specific cognitive performance, we applied ordered logistic regression models with a categorical variable of glycemic status for scores in the following cognitive domains: immediate memory (range: 0–3), temporal orientation (0–4), delayed recall (0–6), and three-dimensional visual-spatial perception (0–2). Results were presented as beta coefficients with corresponding 95% CIs, representing the log-odds of achieving a higher score per glycemic category relative to normoglycemia.

Among participants without diabetes (n = 1893 for FPG and HbA1c; n = 1227 for HOMA-IR), logistic regression was used to estimate ORs (95% CIs) for MCI across categories of FPG, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR. P values for linearity were obtained using the Wald test by modeling each marker as a continuous variable [42], under the assumption that the odds of MCI increase linearly with increasing values of each marker.

To examine the impact of different cutoffs scores for MCI, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds of 14/15 to define MCI. To examine the impact of prediabetes duration, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis in which prediabetes defined by FPG was categorized according to duration (< 5 or ≥ 5 years). Statistical analyses were performed using Stata ver. 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants' characteristics

Among the 2486 participants (mean age: 65.1 years; 91.5% male), 1584 (63.7%) had prediabetes and 593 (23.9%) had diabetes. Among participants with diabetes, 40.8% (n = 242) had a diabetes duration ≥ 10 years. Of those with treated diabetes, 45.5% (n = 176) had an HbA1c ≥ 7%. Compared with participants with normoglycemia, those with prediabetes or diabetes tended to be male, older, and more likely to have higher BMI, hypertension, and dyslipidemia (Table 1). Participants with diabetes receiving antidiabetic treatment were more likely to be obese and had higher levels of FPG, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR than those with screen-detected diabetes (Table 1).

Diabetes, prediabetes, and MCI

We identified 251 cases of MCI (10.1%). In the fully adjusted model, participants with prediabetes and diabetes had higher, but not statistically significant, odds of MCI compared with those with normoglycemia. The ORs (95% CIs) were 1.45 (0.90, 2.33) for prediabetes and 1.67 (0.99, 2.81) for diabetes (Table 2). When stratified treatment status, this association became stronger in treated diabetes (OR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.03, 3.06) than in screen-detected diabetes (OR = 1.48; 95% CI: 0.77, 2.84) (Table 2). When stratifying by diabetes duration, we found that diabetes duration ≥ 10 years was significantly associated with higher odds of MCI (OR = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.27, 4.02; Table 2). When treated diabetes was further stratified by glycemic control, the association between diabetes and MCI was stronger in those with HbA1c ≥ 7% (OR 1.90, 95% CI 1.00, 3.60) than in those with HbA1c < 7% (OR 1.67, 95% CI: 0.90, 3.10).

When applying the alternative cutoff of 14/15 to define MCI, the ORs (95% CIs) were 1.36 (1.02, 1.82) for prediabetes and 1.79 (1.29, 2.48) for diabetes compared with normoglycemia (Table S1). When stratifying diabetes by treatment status, the ORs were 1.76 (1.16, 2.65) for screen-detected diabetes and 1.79 (1.26, 2.55) for treated diabetes.

Diabetes, prediabetes, and scores of each component in cognitive function tests

Participants with diabetes had significantly lower scores for temporal orientation (β = −0.86, 95% CI: −1.58, − 0.14) and three-dimensional visual-spatial perception (β = −0.49, 95% CI: −0.85, − 0.12), compared with those with normoglycemia (Table 3). There was no significant association between diabetes or prediabetes and scores for immediate memory and delayed recall. For diabetes vs. normoglycemia, the beta coefficients (95% CIs) were − 0.27 (− 1.24, 0.70) for immediate memory and − 0.58 (− 1.23, 0.06) for delayed recall (Table 3). When stratified by treatment status, beta coefficients (95% CIs) for temporal orientation were − 0.96 (− 1.79, − 0.13) for screen-detected diabetes and − 0.76 (− 1.52, 0.00004) for treated diabetes. Beta coefficients (95% CIs) for three-dimensional visual-spatial perception were − 0.45 (− 0.91, 0.02) for screen-detected diabetes and − 0.50 (− 0.90, − 0.11) for treated diabetes.

Discussion

Among older Japanese adults, those with diabetes had a non-significant but higher prevalence of MCI compared with those with normoglycemia. The association between diabetes and MCI was stronger in participants with treated diabetes, a diabetes duration of ≥ 10 years, and HbA1c ≥ 7%. Prediabetes was significantly associated with MCI only when defined by elevated FPG, but not by elevated HbA1c.

A meta-analysis of nine cohort studies showed that participants with diabetes had a 49% higher relative risk of MCI (95% CI: 26%–77%) [43]. In the present study, we found 67% higher odds of MCI associated with diabetes, although not statistically significant. Three of nine studies in the meta-analysis were conducted in relatively younger older adults (mean age: <70 years), showing a wide range of magnitudes of association [7, 9, 11]. In 4961 women in 25 countries, the OR of MCI was 1.78 (95% CI: 0.99, 3.20) for diabetes, defined by FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL or self-reported medication use [11]. In 549 Finnish adults, the OR of MCI was 1.55 (95% CI: 0.58–4.19) for self-reported diabetes [9]. In 1519 Chinese adults, the HR of MCI was 2.47 (95% CI: 1.92–4.19) for self-reported diabetes [7]. Our study extends the existing evidence of diabetes and MCI with a comprehensive assessment of diabetes using FPG and HbA1c in older adults, 85% of whom were aged < 70.

We found that the association of diabetes with MCI was stronger among participants with diabetes under treatment than among those with screen-detected diabetes. Although no previous study, to our knowledge, has examined this association by treatment status, studies on dementia and Alzheimer’s disease have reported the opposite results, suggesting protective effects of antidiabetic treatment on cognitive impairment. For example, a study reported a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease (HR: 1.56) in untreated diabetes identified by FPG but found no significant association in those with treated diabetes (HR not reported) [44]. Another study found HR (95% CI) for dementia was higher in diabetes without oral agents compared with those with oral agents (2.41 vs. 1.62) [45]. This discrepancy in findings may be attributable to differences in glycemic control in the treated group. In the previous studies, the treated group had lower FPG than the untreated group (128 vs. 144 mg/dL) [44], while in our study, the treated group had higher FPG (146 vs. 133 mg/dL) and HbA1c (7.1% vs. 6.4%) than the screen-detected diabetes group. Our finding of higher odds of MCI among those with diabetes duration ≥ 10 years and HbA1c ≥ 7% suggests that a more prolonged and poorly controlled diabetic state in this group explains the significant association. Chronic hyperglycemia has been shown to promote structural brain changes, including white matter lesions [46] and hippocampal atrophy [47], which are associated with cognitive impairment. Furthermore, different definitions of screen-detected diabetes may also explain the discrepancy. In our study, screen-detected diabetes reffered to participants newly identified with diabetes at health check-ups who were not receiving treatment, while in the previous study, it was defined as diagnosed with diabetes within a year but not taking antidiabetic medication [45]. Our findings highlight the importance of routine cognitive screening for individuals with diabetes, especially for those undergoing treatment.

We found that participants with diabetes had lower scores in temporal orientation and visual-spatial perception than those with normoglycemia. These findings may be attributed to diabetes-related structural changes in key brain regions involved in cognition. For example, a study in middle-aged adults reported that participants with diabetes had reduced hippocampal volume [48], a region critical for episodic memory, such as recalling past events [49]. In a study of participants with MCI and Alzheimer’s disease, decreased episodic memory performance was associated with lower scores in temporal orientation [50]. Furthermore, patients with diabetes have been shown to have a reduced volume of the occipital cortex [51], a brain region essential for visual-spatial processing.

We found a higher but non-significant odds of MCI associated with prediabetes, defined as FPG of 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c of 5.7–6.4% (OR: 1.45; 95% CI: 0.90, 2.33). While the lack of statistical significance is consistent with prior studies, the present study yielded a larger point estimate. In a study in 887 adults (mean age: 81 years), the HR of MCI in those with prediabetes, defined as random blood glucose 140–200 mg/dL, compared with those with < 140 mg/dL was 1.07 (95% CI: 0.43, 2.67) after adjustment for sociodemographic, baseline cognition, cardiometabolic profile, and APOE genotype [10]. Among 4961 postmenopausal women (mean age 66 years), prediabetes defined by FPG 110–125 mg/dL was not associated with MCI compared with < 110 mg/dL (OR: 1.06; 95% CI: 0.51, 2.20) after adjustment for education, race, and depressive symptoms [11]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in prediabetes definitions or confounder adjustments. Our study extends previous research by defining prediabetes using both FPG and HbA1c and examining its association with MCI while accounting for a wide range of known confounding factors, including lifestyle factors.

Among participants without diabetes, we found that elevated FPG was associated with MCI. This finding is consistent with a Chinese cross-sectional study [22] and a multiethnic cohort study [11]. There are possible explanations for this association. In vitro studies suggest that high glucose promotes oxidative stress and inflammation, which may disrupt neuronal function and synaptic plasticity [52]. In animal models, increased FPG leads to reduced hippocampal neurogenesis [53], impaired spatial learning ability [54], and reduced synaptic strengthening [54]. In humans, a prospective study in older adults without diabetes showed that higher FPG was associated with decreased volumes of hippocampus and amygdala [55]. On the other hand, we found no associations between HbA1c or HOMA-IR with MCI. The lack of association may be explained by the fact that HbA1c less than 7.0% is more likely to reflect postprandial glucose levels rather than FPG. Some studies have shown no association between postprandial glucose and cognitive performance [56, 57]. The lack of association between HOMA-IR and MCI may partly be explained by the fact that HOMA-IR reflects hepatic insulin resistance [58] rather than brain insulin resistance [59], which is associated with cognitive decline [60]. These findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of FPG as an early indicator of cognitive impairment among individuals without diabetes.

The American Diabetes Association recommends cognitive screening in older adults with diabetes for the early detection of MCI or dementia at the initial visit, annually, and as appropriate thereafter [61]. Our findings suggest that such screening may be particularly warranted in those with treated diabetes, a diabetes duration of ≥ 10 years, or HbA1c ≥ 7%. Furthermore, although the guidelines do not include recommendations for cognitive screening for prediabetes [61], our finding of a higher prevalence of MCI among those with FPG-defined prediabetes suggests that the potential need for cognitive screening may extend to the prediabetic stage.

The present study has several strengths, including the assessment of multiple glycemic indicators (FPG and HbA1c) to define diabetes and prediabetes and to examine their associations with MCI. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we assessed cognitive function using the MSP-1100 test battery, which was developed and validated for identifying individuals at risk of Alzheimer’s disease with a cutoff score of 12/13 [36], the validity of using a cutoff score of 13/14 to define MCI has not been established. This may have compromised the reliability of the outcome variable, leading to nondifferential misclassification bias and potentially weakening the true association [62]. However, given that a cutoff score of 12/13 effectively distinguishes patients with Alzheimer’s disease from healthy controls [36], a slightly higher cutoff of 13/14 may still be useful for identifying individuals with MCI. To address the uncertainty regarding the validity of the 13/14 cutoff, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative cutoff (14/15) and confirmed that results remained essentially unchanged. Second, although the MSP-1100 covers key cognitive domains [36], it does not include the domains of attention/calculation and language. The absence of these domains may have led to an underestimation of certain types of cognitive impairment. Third, the possibility of bias cannot be ruled out because of residual confounding and unmeasured factors such as education, income, APOE genotype, type of antidiabetic medications (e.g., insulin or oral agents), and medication burden other than those for diabetes. For example, SGLT2 inhibitors have been reported to improve cognitive function in patients with T2DM, possibly by reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [63, 64], while insulin use has been associated with increased dementia risk, possibly through hypoglycemia [65]. However, because all participants were employees of the same company, their educational backgrounds and income were likely to be relatively homogeneous. We attempted to address this limitation in an analysis by accounting for treatment status, including both antibiabetic medication and diet therapy. Fourth, participants were individuals undergoing comprehensive health check-ups and may have been relatively healthy, which could have limited the number of MCI cases detectable by the test battery. Fifth, the cross-sectional design of the study limits causal inference. The observed association may be explained by reverse causation. For example, cognitive impairment can lead to suboptimal glycemic control due to physical inactivity [66], an unhealthy diet [67], and inadequate medication adherence [68]. However, because MCI may not substantially alter lifestyle behaviors, and because we adjusted for several lifestyle factors, the likelihood of reverse causation may be limited. Sixth, our data were collected between 2017 and 2020. Given that diabetes treatment and glycemic control may have improved over time, the applicability of our findings to the current clinical setting may partly be limited. Finally, the data were obtained from a relatively healthy cohort derived from the workforce, consisting predominantly of older Japanese males (91%). This limits the generalizability of the findings to populations predominantly composed of women or those with different racial or ethnic backgrounds.

In conclusion, the present study of relatively younger older adults showed that the prevalence of MCI was higher among older adults with diabetes, particularly those with treated, longstanding, or uncontrolled diabetes. Diabetes was associated with lower scores in temporal orientation and visual-spatial perception. Among individuals without diabetes, prediabetes defined by both FPG and HbA1c was not associated with MCI; however, FPG-defined prediabetes was associated with MCI. Further studies are required to examine the prospective association between diabetes, FPG-defined prediabetes, and the incidence of MCI.
