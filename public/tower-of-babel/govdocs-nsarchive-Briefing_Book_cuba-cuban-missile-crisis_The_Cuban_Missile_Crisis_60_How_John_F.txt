<article about="/briefing-book/cuba-cuban-missile-crisis/2022-10-17/cuban-missile-crisis-60-how-john-f-kennedy" class="node node--type-briefing-book node--promoted node--view-mode-full">

<div class="node__content clearfix">

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington D.C., October 17, 2022 - </strong>In a secret “eyes only” memorandum for John F. Kennedy, written 60 years ago today at the outset of the Cuban Missile Crisis, U.N. Ambassador Adlai Stevenson admonished the president to abandon his initial plan to attack Cuba and to consider, instead, the diplomatic option of dismantling U.S. missile bases in Europe in return for the withdrawal of the Soviet missiles in Cuba. Air strikes against Cuba would “have such incalculable consequences,” he argued, “that I feel you should have made it clear [to your advisors] that the existence of nuclear missile bases anywhere is negotiable before we start anything.”</p>

<p>The memorandum, which was a follow-up to a private meeting Kennedy and Stevenson had on October 16 about the unfolding missile crisis, concluded with Stevenson’s mantra for U.S. diplomacy in the face of Soviet provocation: “Blackmail and intimidation <u>never</u>; negotiation and sanity <u>always</u>.”</p>

<div style=" padding:8px; margin:0px; left;  background-color: #FFD1A7">
<article class="align-center media media--type-image media--view-mode-original">>; negotiation and sanity <u>always</u>.”</p>

<div style=" padding:8px; margin:0px; left;  background-color: #FFD1A7">
<article class="align-center media media--type-image media--view-mode-original">

<div class="field field--name-field-media-image field--type-image field--label-hidden field__item">  <a href="/sites/default/files/2022-10/handwritten_draft_of_ambassador_stevensons_sign-off_to_kennedy.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/sites/default/files/2022-10/handwritten_draft_of_ambassador_stevensons_sign-off_to_kennedy.jpg" alt="handwritten draft of Ambassador Stevenson's sign-off to Kennedy" typeof="foaf:Image">
</a>
</div>

</article>

<p><em>A handwritten draft of Ambassador Stevenson's sign-off to Kennedy: "Blackmail and intimidation <u>never</u>; negotiation and sanity <u>always</u>."</em></p>
</div>

<p>Ambassador Stevenson’s early suggestion of negotiating a missile swap helped set the stage for the ultimate resolution of the dangerous crisis 11 days later, after President Kennedy agreed with Soviet premier Nikita Khrushchev to trade U.S. Jupiter missiles based in Turkey for the withdrawal of the newly installed Soviet medium- and intermediate-range ballistic missiles in Cuba. But the part of the accord that ended the crisis was kept TOP SECRET for decades. Officially, the Soviets agreed to pull the missiles out of Cuba in return for a U.S. pledge not to invade the country in the future. Publicly, the Kennedy White House promoted the narrative that the Soviets had "blinked" and retreated in the face of U.S. resolve and determination. Instead of receiving credit for his cogent advice, Ambassador Stevenson became the sacrificial lamb of the missile crisis.</p>ad "blinked" and retreated in the face of U.S. resolve and determination. Instead of receiving credit for his cogent advice, Ambassador Stevenson became the sacrificial lamb of the missile crisis.</p>

<p>To safeguard the secret of the&nbsp;<em>quid pro quo</em>&nbsp;that ended the crisis, administration leakers cast Stevenson as a “soft” appeaser who “wanted a Munich” and “to trade US bases for Cuban bases.” The disparaging allegations were made in an influential article, “<a data-saferedirecturl="https://www.google.com/url?q=http://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/wp-content/uploads/satevepost/1962-12-08-missile-crisis.pdf&amp;source=gmail&amp;ust=1665940998895000&amp;usg=AOvVaw3UJyRfN-5sXLv0Naf9TJV7" href="http://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/wp-content/uploads/satevepost/1962-12-08-missile-crisis.pdf" target="_blank"><b>In Time of Crisis</b></a>,” that appeared in <em>The&nbsp;Saturday Evening Post</em>&nbsp;in early December 1962. The two authors of the article, Kennedy’s close friend Charles Bartlett, and confidant Stewart Alsop, later admitted that the president had known about the story in advance of publication and had even participated in editing drafts. “My own guess, knowing him well, would be that he was not too displeased that this had turned up,” Bartlett later stated in an oral history, describing how he briefed the president on the accusations against Stevenson (that would later appear in the article) during a mid-November dinner in Middleburg, Virginia, where the Kennedy’s often spent weekends.</p>briefed the president on the accusations against Stevenson (that would later appear in the article) during a mid-November dinner in Middleburg, Virginia, where the Kennedy’s often spent weekends.</p>

<p>“Neither Stevenson nor the Kennedy-led secret diplomacy that resolved the most dangerous conflict in modern history have received full and due historical recognition,” according to Peter Kornbluh, who directs the Archive’s Cuba Documentation Project and authored an article in <em>Foreign Policy </em>magazine this week on Stevenson’s contribution to the missile crisis. “Stevenson’s approach, ‘Blackmail and intimidation never; negotiation and sanity always,’” Kornbluh noted, “is more relevant today than ever.”</p>

<p>On the 60th anniversary of the missile crisis, the Archive is posting a collection of declassified documents on Ambassador Stevenson’s critical role as crisis advisor to President Kennedy and on the political fallout from the “In Time of Crisis” article. The documents include: his October 17, 1962, private memorandum to Kennedy; his TOP SECRET recommendations for a broader U.N.-backed disarmament effort as a way of ending the missile crisis in Cuba (and avoiding missile crises elsewhere in the future); communications from Kennedy aide Arthur Schlesinger Jr. and Undersecretary of State George W. Ball defending Stevenson;&nbsp;and President Kennedy’s letter to Stevenson, in the wake of the <em>Saturday Evening Post</em> article, expressing “regret” at “the fuss” over the ambassador’s contribution to the crisis deliberations.</p>

</a>

<p><em>In the wake of the </em>Saturday Evening Post<em> controversy, </em>Time Magazine<em> published a cover story on Adlai Stevenson. It quoted him as stating: &nbsp;"We seem to be living in an era when anyone who is for war is a hero and anyone who is for peace is a bum." (</em><a href="http://content.time.com/time/covers/0,16641,19621214,00.html">Time Magazine</a><em>)</em></p>
</div>

<p>“Adlai’s strong and early advocacy of ‘exploring the possibilities of a peaceful solution’ provided Kennedy with a blueprint to do exactly that,” wrote the late historian, Martin J. Sherwin, in his comprehensive book, <em>Gambling with Armageddon</em>. “In fact, Stevenson’s suggestions of October 16 and 17 were congruent with nearly all the steps that the president followed in resolving the crisis.”</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>On October 16, 1962, the day the CIA&nbsp;briefed President Kennedy on the existence of Soviet ballistic missiles in Cuba, Kennedy met privately with his ambassador to the United Nations, Adlai Stevenson. Kennedy informed Stevenson that the initial consensus of his advisors, which the president supported, was to launch a series of airstrikes to take out the missiles. Stevenson advised the president to instead consider peaceful, diplomatic alternatives to convince Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev to remove the missiles. In this memorandum, written the next morning to advance his argument for the use of diplomacy over the use of force, Stevenson cites the “incalculable consequences” of airstrikes in Cuba and urges Kennedy to make clear to his advisors that “the existence of nuclear missile bases anywhere is negotiable before we start anything.” He expresses his “misgivings about the proposed course of action” and insists that “it should be clear as a pikestaff that the U.S. was, is, and will be ready to negotiate the elimination of bases and anything else,” before risking an escalation to nuclear war. Among his recommendations are that Kennedy opens back channels of communication with both Khrushchev and Fidel Castro.</p>

Simultaneously, the notes indicate, a different emissary would carry a note to Khrushchev, opening a channel to negotiate the withdrawal of the missiles.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In an effort to lobby President Kennedy to include a “political platform” in his October 22 public announcement of the U.S. response to the discovery of the missile sites, Ambassador Stevenson drafts a broad diplomatic plan to resolve the crisis and address the issue of nuclear weapons between the superpowers. His proposals call for negotiating the “neutralization and demilitarization” of Cuba, stationing United Nations peacekeeping and observer forces on the island, and organizing a summit meeting between Kennedy and Khrushchev to address nuclear weapons. As a “gesture showing our wisdom and good faith,” Stevenson recommends “an offer to exchange Guantanamo for removal of Soviet installations in Cuba.” He also suggests holding out the option of later negotiations on U.S. missile bases in Turkey and Italy. “Inclusion of a political program in the initial speech,” Stevenson argues in his memorandum, “will drive home the essential point: that the United States wants a political settlement, not an escalated military involvement.” Anticipating pushback against a conciliatory approach to resolving the missile crisis, Stevenson submits that, “The offer would not sound ‘soft’ if properly worded; it would sound ‘wise.’” At a meeting of his Executive Committee of advisors, however, Kennedy dismissed the broad proposal and the Guantanamo option, although he held out the prospect of further discussion on a missile trade.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In anticipation of President Kennedy’s public announcement of the discovery of the missiles and the U.S. response, Ambassador Stevenson writes his own draft of what Kennedy’s address to the nation, and the world, should include on diplomatic initiatives to manage the crisis. His draft section of speech focuses on diplomacy and the United Nations. It calls on the U.N. to dispatch observation teams not only to Cuba, but also to Italy and Turkey to “insure that no surprise attack could be mounted in any of these countries pending a permanent solution to the problem of foreign missile bases.” In Stevenson’s version, Kennedy would guarantee the territorial integrity of Cuba and thereby eliminate the Soviet rationale of using the missiles to defend the island against a U.S. attack. The Soviet Union would withdraw their missiles and all military personnel; in return, the United States would “evacuate our base at Guantanamo.” With respect to future disarmament talks, Stevenson’s proposed language said that the U.S. would consider the “elimination” of bases in Turkey and Italy.</p>

Kennedy notes that because his “personal friend” Charles Bartlett had co-authored the article, “everything controversial in his article would be laid at my door, whether I talked to him or not,” and conveys to Stevenson that “I did not discuss the Cuban crisis…with <em>any</em> newspapermen.” (In fact, Bartlett had shown Kennedy the passages on Stevenson prior to publication, giving him the opportunity to confirm or deny the charge that the U.N. ambassador “wanted a Munich” and had been the lone dissenter from the consensus of Kennedy’s other advisors.
