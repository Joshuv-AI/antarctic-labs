Event-preserving feature engineering for intermittent demand forecasting using SHOS
Authors: B Sendhil Nathan, Veera Siva Reddy B, C Chandrasekhara Sastry, Sachin Salunkhe, Robert Cep
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntermittent demand forecasting remains a persistent challenge in large-scale supply chains due to extreme sparsity, irregular demand occurrence, and highly variable demand magnitudes. While recent studies have increasingly emphasized architectural complexity to address these challenges, comparatively less attention has been directed toward the role of statistically grounded feature representation under sparse-demand conditions. This study introduces the Smoothed Hybrid Occurrence-Size (SHOS) framework as an event-preserving feature-engineering approach for intermittent-demand forecasting. SHOS decomposes demand into latent occurrence probability and conditional demand size components and generates adaptive, series-specific statistical representations using sparsity-aware exponential smoothing. Rather than functioning as a standalone forecasting model, SHOS operates as a feature-generation mechanism that embeds occurrence-size structure directly into supervised learning pipelines. The proposed framework was evaluated using a large-scale automotive aftermarket dataset consisting of approximately 56,000 dealer-part time series and approximately 1.4 million monthly observations generated after preprocessing and zero-padding. Models were evaluated using rolling-window cross-validation, signal-preservation analysis, and robustness studies across heterogeneous demand segments. Under the evaluated sparse-demand setting and model configurations, SHOS-augmented tree-based models substantially improved forecasting accuracy and stability relative to raw-feature baselines. In particular, the SHOS-augmented LightGBM configuration reduced the mean absolute error by approximately 46.3% relative to the baseline model, while the weighted mean absolute percentage error improved by more than 40%. Beyond conventional forecasting accuracy, the study additionally demonstrates that SHOS preserves event timing, peak structure, and frequency-domain characteristics that are often distorted by conventional smoothing approaches. The results further suggest that statistically grounded feature representations can substantially improve sparse-demand learning behaviour under the evaluated automotive aftermarket forecasting environment. Introduction

Intermittent demand forecasting remains a major challenge in supply chain and inventory management, due to long periods of zero demand interspersed with irregular, low-frequency demand events. This problem is especially critical in automotive aftermarket and spare-parts systems, where organizations manage large populations of slow-moving stock-keeping units (SKUs) across distributed dealer networks1–3. Under such conditions, inaccurate forecasts can simultaneously increase inventory holding costs, obsolescence risk, and service-level failures.

Classical intermittent-demand forecasting methods such as Croston’s method, the Syntetos-Boylan Approximation (SBA), and the Teunter-Syntetos-Babai (TSB) framework address this challenge by decomposing demand into occurrence and conditional size components4–6. These approaches remain widely used because they provide stable and interpretable forecasting behaviour under sparse-demand conditions. However, they operate primarily as univariate forecasting procedures and cannot directly exploit auxiliary variables or cross-sectional information increasingly available in modern enterprise systems.

Recent forecasting research has therefore increasingly explored machine-learning and deep-learning approaches capable of learning nonlinear relationships from large multivariate datasets7–10. Tree-based learners such as LightGBM and XGBoost have demonstrated strong predictive performance in tabular forecasting problems, while deep learning architectures aim to capture complex temporal structure7,8. However, intermittent-demand environments remain challenging for such models due to extreme sparsity, irregular demand occurrences, and weak temporal continuity, which limit the availability of stable learning signals.

At the same time, forecasting competitions and hybrid forecasting studies increasingly suggest that statistically meaningful feature representations may substantially improve forecasting performance even without highly specialized architectures11,12. This raises an important question in intermittent-demand forecasting: can statistically grounded feature representations improve sparse-demand learning behaviour more effectively than increasing architectural complexity alone.

To address this problem, this study introduces the Smoothed Hybrid Occurrence-Size (SHOS) framework as an event-preserving feature-engineering approach for intermittent-demand forecasting. SHOS decomposes intermittent demand into a latent-occurrence probability and a conditional-demand-size component via sparsity-aware adaptive smoothing. Rather than functioning as a standalone forecasting model, SHOS serves as a representation-generation framework whose outputs are directly incorporated into supervised learning pipelines.

The proposed framework is evaluated using a large-scale automotive aftermarket dataset containing approximately 56,000 dealer-part time series and approximately 1.4 million monthly observations after preprocessing and zero-padding. Forecasting models are evaluated using rolling-window cross-validation together with signal-preservation and robustness analyses under highly intermittent demand conditions. In the present study, the term “event-preserving” refers to the ability of the SHOS representation framework to retain meaningful demand-event characteristics, including occurrence timing, peak magnitude behaviour, and frequency-domain structure, while reducing instability arising from extreme sparsity.

The results demonstrate that SHOS-derived representations substantially improve forecasting accuracy and stability under the evaluated sparse-demand setting while preserving event timing and demand-structure characteristics that are often distorted by conventional smoothing approaches. Rather than proposing a universal replacement for complex architectures, the study investigates the role of statistically grounded feature representation within the evaluated automotive aftermarket forecasting environment.

Literature review

Classical intermittent-demand forecasting

Intermittent-demand forecasting methods are fundamentally based on the principle that demand occurrence and demand magnitude should be modelled separately. Croston’s method introduced this decomposition by independently smoothing inter-demand intervals and positive demand sizes4. Subsequent studies demonstrated that Croston’s estimator exhibits positive statistical bias, motivating several refinements13.

The Syntetos-Boylan Approximation (SBA) introduced a debiasing correction that improved forecast accuracy and inventory performance under intermittent demand5,14. Later, the Teunter-Syntetos-Babai (TSB) method replaced interval smoothing with direct smoothing of demand occurrence probability, enabling improved responsiveness to changes in demand frequency6,15. These approaches remain foundational because they explicitly preserve the occurrence-size structure characteristic of intermittent demand.

Despite their interpretability and robustness, classical methods remain limited by their primarily univariate formulation. They cannot naturally incorporate rich auxiliary information, such as product attributes, dealer behaviour, or cross-sectional demand patterns, which are increasingly available in modern supply-chain systems.

Machine learning and deep learning approaches

Machine-learning and deep-learning methods have increasingly been adopted for forecasting due to their ability to learn nonlinear relationships from large-scale multivariate data7–10. Tree-based ensemble learners such as LightGBM and XGBoost have demonstrated strong predictive performance in structured forecasting environments7,8, while neural network architectures aim to capture complex temporal dynamics16.

However, highly intermittent demand presents substantial challenges for purely data-driven learning approaches. Long sequences of zero demand reduce signal availability, increase noise sensitivity, and may destabilize sequential learning behaviour9,10. As a result, machine-learning performance under extreme sparsity often depends strongly on the quality of the underlying feature representation rather than solely on architectural complexity.

Hybrid forecasting and representation-learning gap

Recent forecasting research increasingly emphasizes hybridization strategies that combine statistical forecasting structure with machine-learning flexibility11,12,17,18. Existing hybrid intermittent-demand approaches generally integrate statistical estimators with machine-learning or deep-learning models through multi-stage, ensemble, or hurdle-based architectures.

Examples include Croston-LSTM hybrids, SBA-based feature integration within tree-based learners, and two-stage occurrence-size forecasting frameworks. While these methods often improve forecasting performance, they typically rely on architectural decomposition or model stacking to capture the structure of intermittent demand.

More recently, feature-based forecasting approaches have explored embedding statistical information directly into machine-learning pipelines as informative representations rather than treating it as a separate forecasting stage18. Nevertheless, there remains limited research on adaptive, statistically grounded feature representations specifically designed for highly sparse intermittent-demand environments.

The SHOS framework proposed in this study addresses this gap by generating adaptive occurrence-size representations that are incorporated directly into supervised learning models as input features. Unlike multi-stage hybrid architectures, SHOS performs hybridization at the representation level while preserving computational simplicity and forecasting scalability under sparse-demand conditions.

Theoretical basis of SHOS

Intermittent demand processes are fundamentally characterized by two distinct sources of uncertainty: whether demand occurs in a given period, and the magnitude of demand conditional on occurrence. This structure is naturally represented as a point process, where observed demand is generated by the interaction of a binary occurrence process and a conditional size process. Classical intermittent-demand models such as Croston-type estimators are built on this decomposition, reflecting the renewal nature of demand events separated by irregular intervals of inactivity. The SHOS framework adopts this same conceptual foundation, explicitly treating demand occurrence and demand size as separate latent components that evolve over time.

SHOS suppresses stochastic sparsity-induced noise without introducing substantial phase lag, peak attenuation, or distortion of intermittent event structure. Although SHOS is conceptually motivated by the occurrence-size decomposition principle introduced in Croston-type intermittent-demand methods, the SHOS framework does not directly reuse Croston forecast outputs as replacement predictions or embedded forecast estimators. Instead, SHOS independently generates adaptive latent representations of occurrence probability and conditional demand size through sparsity-aware smoothing and recency-adaptive updating mechanisms operating on the zero-padded demand series.

A central challenge in intermittent demand forecasting arises from the amplification of variance under extreme sparsity. When long sequences of zero demand dominate the series, direct smoothing or regression on raw demand values conflates non-occurrence with small magnitudes, leading to unstable estimates and high noise sensitivity. SHOS addresses this issue by smoothing the occurrence process independently of the size process. By updating the probability of demand occurrence at every time step, including zero-demand periods, SHOS stabilizes frequency estimation and prevents isolated demand events from exerting disproportionate influence. Conditional demand size, in contrast, is updated only when demand occurs, preserving meaningful magnitude information without contamination from inactivity periods. This separation acts as a variance-stabilizing mechanism, particularly effective in highly sparse regimes. The SHOS Decomposition and representation are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Importantly, SHOS does not perform smoothing via convolution or moving-average operations on the raw demand series. Instead, it applies event-preserving smoothing at the level of latent demand components. The timing of demand events is retained through the occurrence process, while magnitude information is preserved through conditional size updates. As a result, SHOS suppresses stochastic noise arising from sparsity without introducing phase lag or attenuating genuine demand bursts. This distinction explains why SHOS fundamentally differs from low-pass filtering approaches and why it can retain cyclical and burst-driven structure while improving statistical stability. These theoretical properties motivate the use of SHOS-derived occurrence and size estimates as statistically grounded input features for downstream machine learning models.

Unlike classical Croston-type estimators that explicitly model inter-demand intervals as renewal durations, the SHOS framework operates by adaptively smoothing latent occurrence probability and conditional demand size representations over a fixed temporal grid. Consequently, SHOS is less dependent on exact interval estimation and instead focuses on generating statistically stable occurrence-size representations suitable for supervised sparse-demand learning.

The SHOS algorithm: adaptive statistical priors

The SHOS (Smoothed Hybrid Occurrence-Size) algorithm is an adaptive statistical feature generation framework designed to operate robustly under extreme demand intermittency, where time series are dominated by long sequences of zero demand punctuated by sporadic, irregular non-zero observations. The algorithm decomposes intermittent demand into two latent components estimated at each time step: the probability of demand occurrence and the conditional demand size given that demand occurs. This formulation allows SHOS to update continuously over time, including during prolonged zero-demand periods, which is essential for maintaining stable estimates in highly sparse demand environments. Based on the balance between stability under sparse demand conditions and responsiveness under dense demand regimes, a fixed value of (k = 0.1) was adopted throughout this study as a stable and operationally practical default configuration.

SHOS versus TSB: bias mitigation and adaptivity

Although SHOS and the Teunter-Syntetos-Babai (TSB) method share a common conceptual foundation by modelling demand as the product of an occurrence probability and a conditional size, their estimation mechanisms differ fundamentally. In TSB, both components are updated using fixed exponential smoothing parameters, implicitly assuming homogeneous intermittency and stationarity across all series. While effective under moderate intermittency, this assumption can lead to delayed responsiveness and variance-induced bias in highly sparse or non-stationary demand environments.

SHOS addresses these limitations by introducing sparsity-adaptive smoothing, in which the degree of smoothing is dynamically scaled to series-specific intermittency. This reduces overreaction to isolated demand events in highly sparse series while maintaining responsiveness in less intermittent ones. Furthermore, the recency-sensitive adjustment mechanism enables SHOS to adapt more rapidly than TSB when demand reappears after prolonged inactivity, mitigating the delayed-response bias commonly observed in fixed-parameter smoothing approaches23.

Finally, the robust initialization strategy employed by SHOS stabilizes early estimates in short or highly intermittent series, further reducing estimation bias relative to classical methods. Collectively, these mechanisms enable SHOS to generate stable, informative, and adaptive estimates of demand occurrence probability and conditional size, making it particularly well suited for feature generation in anomalous intermittent demand scenarios.

Experimental study design

This section describes the methodological framework for studying event-preserving feature representation for intermittent demand forecasting. Rather than emphasizing model architecture, the experimental design is structured to isolate the effect of feature representation on learning behaviour under sparsity.

The experimental workflow described in the following subsections operationalizes this representation. Raw transactional data are aggregated to a monthly frequency and zero-padded to establish a consistent temporal grid. A comprehensive feature engineering process then generates a rich set of predictors, including SHOS-derived statistical features. Forecasting models are trained under a rolling-window cross-validation protocol that respects temporal ordering, enabling controlled evaluation of representation effects across heterogeneous demand regimes.

Dataset description

The experiments use a large-scale automotive aftermarket intermittent-demand dataset derived from dealer-level spare-parts sales transactions collected from a single national automotive aftermarket network. The original transactional records span the period from August 2023 to August 2025 and include approximately 59 dealers and 7,702 unique spare parts. Following preprocessing and dealer-part filtering, the dataset yielded approximately 56,000 active dealer-part demand series for forecasting analysis. Because raw transactional demand is extremely sparse and irregular, the original daily sales records were aggregated to a monthly frequency prior to forecasting analysis. Monthly aggregation was selected to reduce excessive day-level fragmentation while preserving the underlying intermittency structure relevant to spare-parts demand forecasting.

To establish a consistent temporal representation across all dealer-part combinations, the monthly demand series were zero-padded over the complete aggregation horizon. This preprocessing step produced a final forecasting dataset containing approximately 1.4 million monthly observations. All forecasting experiments, SHOS feature-generation procedures, rolling-window validation analyses, significance testing, and model comparisons reported in this study were conducted exclusively on this final processed monthly-aggregated dataset.

The zero-padding operation is statistically meaningful in the context of intermittent-demand forecasting because zero-valued observations explicitly represent periods of demand non-occurrence rather than missing information. These inactive periods contain essential occurrence-level information required for modelling renewal behaviour, demand sparsity, and event reactivation dynamics. Treating such periods as missing values would distort occurrence probabilities, alter sparsity distributions, and weaken the statistical representation of intermittent demand processes.

The present study interprets zero-valued periods as meaningful non-occurrence events rather than missing observations. This distinction is important because zero-demand intervals carry essential information regarding sparsity structure, renewal behaviour, and demand reactivation dynamics. Consequently, zero-padding was retained intentionally to preserve occurrence-level information required for SHOS-based feature generation and comparative sparse-demand learning24.

The processed dataset preserves the extreme sparsity characteristics of the original aftermarket demand environment. Across the active dealer-part series, the median Average Demand Interval (ADI) was approximately 25, while the median squared coefficient of variation (CV2) remained approximately 24, indicating highly intermittent and burst-driven demand behaviour. The forecasting target variable is the total monthly quantity sold for each dealer-part combination (PartQty). Additional covariates include static part-level attributes such as part price, weight, and volumetric cube dimensions, which were incorporated as auxiliary explanatory variables during feature generation and supervised learning.

This work additionally acknowledges that temporal aggregation may influence the observed renewal structure of intermittent demand. Finer-grained aggregation frequencies, such as weekly or daily representations, may preserve additional short-term event dynamics and inter-arrival variability. However, under the present large-scale dealer-part forecasting environment, such finer temporal resolutions substantially increased zero inflation, sparsity fragmentation, and noise sensitivity during preliminary exploratory analysis. The monthly aggregation adopted in this study, therefore, reflects an operationally motivated representation scale appropriate for the evaluated automotive aftermarket setting rather than a universally optimal temporal discretization strategy.

Because the dataset originates from a single national automotive aftermarket ecosystem, the reported findings should be interpreted within the context of the evaluated market structure, dealer behaviour, and sparsity characteristics. Extension of the SHOS framework to alternative industrial sectors, geographical markets, or multi-echelon supply-chain environments remains an important direction for future validation.

Monthly aggregation was selected as a practical compromise between temporal fidelity and statistical stability under highly sparse dealer-part demand conditions. At the raw daily transactional level, a substantial proportion of dealer-part series contained isolated low-volume events separated by extended inactive periods, resulting in highly fragmented temporal sequences with weak statistical learning signals. Aggregation to monthly frequency reduced excessive day-level fragmentation while preserving the broader intermittency structure required for representation-oriented forecasting and comparative model evaluation.

The present study interprets zero-valued periods as meaningful non-occurrence events rather than missing observations. This distinction is important because zero-demand intervals carry essential information regarding sparsity structure, renewal behaviour, and demand reactivation dynamics. No artificial imputation procedure was used to replace missing demand observations. Zero-padding was applied exclusively to represent valid inactive dealer-part periods within the aggregation horizon.

A structured feature engineering pipeline transforms the padded monthly data into a high-dimensional feature set. The pipeline includes:

Global and latent features: Dealer- and part-level historical averages together with low-rank latent embeddings derived from the dealer-part activity matrix were incorporated to capture cross-sectional interaction structure within the sparse-demand environment. The latent embeddings were intended to summarize broad dealer purchasing behaviour, part-level co-occurrence structure, and recurring interaction patterns across dealer-part combinations rather than to function as direct probabilistic demand estimators. To generate these representations, Truncated SVD with 12 latent dimensions was used due to its computational efficiency and numerical stability with large sparse matrices.

To strictly preserve temporal integrity and prevent data leakage, the Truncated SVD factorization was recomputed independently within each rolling-window cross-validation fold. Specifically, the dealer-part activity matrix was constructed exclusively from the training subset corresponding to the current fold, and the learned low-rank projection was subsequently applied to the associated test observations. No future demand information from the test horizon was used during latent embedding construction.

The present study additionally acknowledges that alternative sparse-factorization approaches such as zero-inflated probabilistic matrix factorization, Poisson factorization, or nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) may provide stronger probabilistic alignment with highly sparse count-based intermittent-demand data. However, the objective of the current work was to evaluate the contribution of SHOS-derived event-preserving statistical representations under a computationally scalable and controlled forecasting framework rather than to optimize latent factorization methodology itself. To isolate the effect of feature representation, baseline ML models are trained on base features only (temporal, global, and latent), while SHOS features are reserved for ablation studies.

Forecasting models and comparative architectures

To evaluate the effectiveness of SHOS-derived feature representations under intermittent demand, a diverse set of statistical, machine-learning, hybrid, and deep-learning forecasting configurations was examined under a unified experimental framework. All models were trained and evaluated using identical preprocessing procedures, rolling-window cross-validation folds, feature-generation protocols, and evaluation metrics to ensure fair and controlled comparison across architectures. The forecasting target for all supervised learning configurations was the monthly dealer-part demand quantity (PartQty). Unless otherwise stated, all models operated on the same zero-padded monthly-aggregated dataset described in section “Dataset description”.

Classical intermittent-demand methods were incorporated into the study under three distinct roles. First, Croston and TSB methods were evaluated independently as standalone statistical forecasting baselines. Second, Croston- and SBA-derived signals were incorporated within selected machine-learning hybrids as feature-level integration benchmarks. Third, SHOS-derived occurrence and conditional-size representations were generated independently through adaptive smoothing procedures and evaluated separately within the supervised learning framework. This separation allows the contribution of SHOS-derived representations to be distinguished from the influence of externally generated Croston-type statistical features.

The machine-learning (ML) models evaluated in this study include: LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest, Ridge Regression, and ElasticNet. In addition, deep-learning (DL) architectures were evaluated to examine the impact of sequential learning complexity under highly sparse demand conditions. These configurations include: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), and Transformer-based sequence models. For each architecture, two forecasting configurations were evaluated:

Base model: trained using temporal lag variables, intermittency indicators, latent embeddings, and static dealer-part attributes.

The SHOS framework, therefore, operates as an independently generated representation-learning mechanism rather than as a direct extension or embedded reuse of Croston forecast outputs. This framework operates exclusively as a feature-generation mechanism and does not alter the prediction target, loss function, or forecasting objective. This design ensures that any observed performance improvement can be attributed specifically to representation-level augmentation rather than architectural modification or hybrid forecast blending25. To further evaluate whether explicit architectural decomposition provides additional benefit beyond feature-level decomposition, a two-stage hurdle forecasting framework was also implemented. The hurdle configuration separates demand occurrence and conditional demand magnitude into two sequential learning stages:

Stage 1 (Occurrence model): A LightGBM classifier estimates the probability of observing non-zero demand, P(yt > 0), using the base feature set augmented with the SHOS occurrence representation .

The final forecast is obtained by multiplying the outputs of the occurrence and conditional-size stages, thereby generating an estimate of expected demand accounting for both event likelihood and demand magnitude. Gradient-boosted architectures (LightGBM and XGBoost) employed a Tweedie objective function to better model the zero-inflation and high skew of intermittent demand distributions.

Classical intermittent-demand methods were incorporated into the study under three complementary roles. First, the TSB framework served as the primary conceptual basis for SHOS because both approaches decompose intermittent demand into components of occurrence probability and conditional demand size. Second, standalone Croston and TSB implementations were evaluated directly as statistical forecasting baselines within the same rolling-window evaluation framework used for all supervised learning models. Third, hybrid configurations such as SBA-LightGBM, Croston-LightGBM, and related variants were evaluated as feature-level integration strategies within common machine-learning pipelines. These comparisons were intended to assess the relative effectiveness of statistical feature representations rather than to compare fundamentally different forecasting paradigms.

All preprocessing steps, latent embedding construction procedures, feature-generation pipelines, train-test splits, and evaluation folds were kept identical across all forecasting architectures unless explicitly stated otherwise. This controlled design ensures that observed performance differences arise from representation quality and model structure rather than inconsistencies in preprocessing, optimization, or evaluation methodology.

Hyperparameter configuration

All forecasting models use fixed hyperparameters determined through a preliminary hyperparameter optimization stage conducted prior to final evaluation. Hyperparameter selection was performed using rolling time-series cross-validation on a dedicated validation subset to preserve temporal ordering and avoid look-ahead bias. For each forecasting architecture, a grid-based search was conducted over the principal learning parameters, including tree depth, learning rate, regularization strength, number of estimators, and sampling ratios where applicable. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) on the validation folds was used as the primary optimization criterion because of its robustness under highly intermittent and skewed demand distributions.

After identifying the best-performing configuration for each architecture, the selected hyperparameters were fixed and consistently applied across all rolling-window evaluation folds. This design ensures fair comparison between forecasting models and isolates the effect of feature representation from optimization variability. For the SHOS framework, the sparsity-scaling parameter was fixed at (k = 0.1) based on preliminary sensitivity analysis across representative demand categories. The recency-aware update mechanism used a recency window of three consecutive inactive periods prior to adaptive boosting, while smoothing coefficients were capped at 0.9 to maintain numerical stability under extreme sparsity26.

Deep-learning architectures were additionally stabilized using fixed training configurations including: Adam optimizer, early stopping, dropout regularization, and fixed sequence-window construction across all folds. Deep-learning configuration summary

The evaluated deep-learning architectures were implemented under a standardized training and evaluation framework to ensure controlled comparison across all forecasting configurations. All models were trained using identical preprocessing pipelines, rolling-window validation folds, feature-generation procedures, and train-test splits in order to isolate the effect of representation quality from differences in optimization or data preparation.

The LSTM architecture employed a single recurrent hidden layer with dropout regularization and fixed sequence-window input construction to model temporal demand dependencies under sparse-demand conditions. The TCN configuration utilized causal temporal convolution blocks with residual connections and fixed dilation scheduling to capture localized temporal structure while preserving sequential ordering. The Transformer architecture employed a multi-head self-attention encoder with positional encoding and feed-forward projection layers to evaluate attention-based sequence learning behaviour in highly intermittent-demand environments.

All deep-learning models were optimized using the Adam optimizer together with mini-batch gradient updates, learning-rate stabilization, dropout regularization, and early stopping to improve optimization stability and reduce overfitting. Hyperparameters including hidden dimensions, dropout rates, learning rates, batch size, sequence-window length, and epoch limits were selected during preliminary rolling-validation tuning and subsequently fixed across all evaluation folds to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison between architectures27.

Despite these stabilization strategies, the evaluated deep-learning configurations remained sensitive to the extreme sparsity and prolonged inactive demand periods characteristic of the present automotive aftermarket forecasting environment. Consequently, the deep-learning results reported in this study should be interpreted within the context of the evaluated dataset characteristics, sparsity regime, feature representations, and rolling-window forecasting framework.

Evaluation protocol and metrics

Model performance is evaluated using a rolling-window cross-validation protocol that preserves temporal ordering and prevents look-ahead bias. In each fold, models are trained on a contiguous historical window and evaluated on the immediately following test window, thereby closely simulating real-world deployment conditions. All data-dependent transformations, including feature scaling and the construction of low-rank latent embeddings using truncated singular value decomposition (SVD), are fitted exclusively on the training data of each fold and then applied to the corresponding test data. This ensures strict temporal integrity and avoids information leakage. Model performance is reported as the mean and standard deviation of evaluation metrics computed across all eleven rolling validation folds.

Volatility retention and event-preserving behaviour

To assess whether SHOS provides an event-preserving representation rather than conventional low-pass smoothing, we evaluate its behaviour using time-domain, frequency-domain, and structural metrics that explicitly capture phase distortion, peak attenuation, and temporal dependence. These analyses are designed to determine whether SHOS suppresses informative volatility or preserves the timing and structure of demand events under intermittency.

In intermittent-demand forecasting, preserving the temporal structure of demand events is operationally important because replenishment and inventory decisions depend not only on aggregate forecast accuracy, but also on timely recognition of demand reactivation and event intensity. Excessive smoothing or temporal phase distortion can delay detection of genuine demand resurgence following prolonged inactivity, potentially increasing service-level risk and stockout exposure. Accordingly, the analyses presented in this subsection were intended to evaluate whether SHOS preserves informative event structure while reducing stochastic sparsity-induced noise.

Phase lag measures temporal displacement between smoothed and raw demand signals, peak preservation quantifies the fraction of original peak magnitude retained, and FFT similarity measures spectral alignment between signals in the frequency domain. Table 8 evaluates temporal and structural preservation properties across smoothing approaches. SMA exhibits a non-zero phase lag and substantial peak attenuation, consistent with low-pass filtering behaviour. In contrast, SHOS introduces zero phase lag for both cyclical and intermittent demand, indicating that the timing of demand events is fully preserved. Moreover, SHOS achieves higher peak preservation and greater similarity to the raw signal than SMA, demonstrating that noise reduction is achieved without suppressing informative demand bursts. Because SHOS determines the temporal representation, the SHOS+LGBM pipeline inherits these event-preserving properties without introducing additional temporal distortion35. While temporal metrics assess event alignment and peak fidelity, frequency domain analysis provides complementary insight into how much of the original signal’s spectral content is retained after smoothing.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that SHOS fundamentally differs from conventional low-pass smoothing. Rather than averaging demand values over time, SHOS performs event-preserving smoothing by operating on latent occurrence and conditional size components. This design enables effective noise reduction under sparsity while preserving cyclical structure, peak timing, and temporal dependence. These properties provide complementary evidence supporting why SHOS-derived features may improve learning stability and event responsiveness under intermittent demand without suppressing informative temporal structure.

Although improved responsiveness and event preservation may contribute to better replenishment awareness under intermittent demand, the analyses presented in this study were not intended to directly quantify inventory-policy performance or operational cost reduction. Rather, these metrics were evaluated as forecasting-oriented indicators of representation quality and sparse-demand learning stability.

Extreme sparsity stress test: responsiveness and recovery under demand reactivation

Intermittent demand forecasting models are particularly vulnerable under extreme sparsity, where long sequences of zero demand are followed by isolated demand reactivation events. In such regimes, learning systems trained on raw demand inputs often exhibit delayed response and unstable adjustment when demand resumes. To evaluate whether SHOS-derived feature representations mitigate these effects, we conducted an extreme sparsity stress test focusing on two operationally relevant criteria responsiveness, defined as the delay in reacting to demand reappearance, and recovery, defined as the time required for forecasts to stabilize following a demand event37.

Responsiveness is measured as the number of periods required for the forecast to exhibit a statistically meaningful increase following the first non-zero demand observation, while recovery measures the number of periods required for forecast error to return within a stable tolerance band after the reactivation event.

Table 10 reports stress test results across three sparsity bins. In the most extreme sparsity regime (sparsity ≥ 0.9), when augmented with SHOS-derived features, LightGBM exhibits a dramatic reduction in both responsiveness and recovery relative to LightGBM trained on raw demand features. Responsiveness is reduced by nearly an order of magnitude, indicating substantially faster reaction to demand reactivation, while recovery time is similarly compressed, reflecting more stable post-event adjustment. These improvements highlight SHOS’s ability to preserve actionable demand signals during prolonged periods of inactivity.

By explicitly encoding demand occurrence probability and conditional demand size, SHOS stabilizes learning during prolonged zero-demand periods while enabling rapid adaptation when demand resumes38. These findings reinforce the central claim of this study: the primary benefits of SHOS arise at the feature-representation level, improving responsiveness and recovery without increasing architectural complexity. These findings complement the volatility analysis in section “ Evaluation protocol and metrics” by demonstrating that SHOS not only preserves events, but also enables faster recovery when events reoccur after prolonged inactivity. From an operational perspective, faster responsiveness directly reduces the risk of stockouts following demand reactivation, while faster recovery prevents prolonged overreaction after isolated demand events, both of which are critical in spare-parts inventory planning.

SHAP interpretability analysis

To further understand the mechanisms underlying the observed performance improvements, we analyze model interpretability using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations). SHAP provides a consistent, game-theoretic framework for quantifying the marginal contribution of each input feature to the model’s predictions, enabling both global and local interpretability. This analysis focuses on the best-performing SHOS augmented machine learning model and examines which features drive forecast accuracy under extreme demand intermittency.

Figure 11 presents the global SHAP feature importance summary, ranked by mean absolute SHAP value across all validation folds. The results reveal that SHOS-derived features dominate the importance ranking. In particular, the smoothed occurrence probability and the smoothed conditional demand size consistently emerge as the most influential predictors, surpassing traditional lag-based features, rolling statistics, and static product attributes.

Global SHAP feature importance for the SHOS-augmented model, showing mean absolute contribution of each feature to forecast predictions across all validation folds.

The prominence of indicates that the model places substantial weight on the estimated probability of demand occurrence, reflecting the critical role of event timing in intermittent demand forecasting. Similarly, the importance of confirms that conditional magnitude estimation provides essential information once demand occurrence is anticipated. Together, these features closely align with the theoretical decomposition of intermittent demand into occurrence and size components, thereby validating the conceptual foundations of the SHOS framework.

In contrast, conventional temporal features such as raw lagged demand values and rolling averages exhibit lower and more dispersed SHAP contributions. This suggests that, in highly sparse settings, such features provide weaker, noisier signals than statistically smoothed occurrence-size representations. Static attributes and latent embeddings contribute additional explanatory power but play a secondary role relative to SHOS-derived signals.

Importantly, the SHAP analysis also highlights the interpretability advantage of the proposed approach. Unlike deep learning architectures, which make it difficult to trace feature contributions, the SHOS-augmented model provides transparent, domain-consistent explanations. Decision-makers can directly relate forecast outputs to intuitive quantities such as recent demand occurrence likelihood and expected conditional demand size, facilitating trust and adoption in operational settings39.

Overall, the SHAP results confirm that the performance gains achieved by SHOS-enhanced models are not driven by spurious correlations or overfitting, but by meaningful statistical representations that capture the fundamental structure of intermittent demand. This interpretability evidence complements the quantitative results presented in sections “Dataset description”–“Evaluation protocol and metrics” and reinforces the conclusion that SHOS provides both accuracy and explanatory value for large-scale intermittent demand forecasting.

Managerial Insights

The findings of this study have several direct implications for practitioners managing large-scale intermittent demand environments, such as automotive aftermarket, spare parts, and service logistics networks. Beyond forecasting accuracy, the proposed SHOS framework offers operational advantages in terms of robustness, interpretability, and deployability that are particularly relevant for real-world decision making.

First, SHOS enables materially improved forecast reliability for highly intermittent items without increasing system complexity. The empirical results show that SHOS-enhanced single-stage machine learning models outperform both standard ML baselines and more complex multi-stage or deep learning architectures. For practitioners, this implies that meaningful accuracy gains can be achieved without introducing additional modelling layers, custom architectures, or specialized training pipelines40. This is especially important in enterprise forecasting systems, where model simplicity directly translates to lower maintenance cost, faster retraining cycles, and easier integration with existing planning workflows.

Second, SHOS improves responsiveness to demand reactivation, reducing the risk of stockouts after prolonged inactivity. The extreme sparsity stress tests demonstrate that SHOS-based models recover more rapidly when demand reappears after long periods of zero demand. From an inventory management perspective, this behaviour directly addresses a common operational failure mode in intermittent-demand forecasting: delayed response to genuine demand signals. By stabilizing occurrence estimation while preserving event timing, SHOS supports earlier replenishment decisions when demand resumes, reducing service level failures without inflating inventory during inactive periods.

Third, SHOS preserves demand volatility and event structure, supporting downstream planning decisions. Unlike conventional smoothing or low-pass filtering approaches that distort peaks and introduce phase lag, SHOS retains the timing and magnitude of demand events while suppressing noise induced by sparsity. This property is particularly valuable for planners who rely on forecasts not only for point estimates, but also for understanding demand variability, planning safety stock, and evaluating risk. Preserving event-driven dynamics ensures that forecasts remain operationally meaningful rather than overly smoothed artefacts.

Fourth, the SHOS framework provides transparent and interpretable signals aligned with domain intuition. The SHAP analysis confirms that SHOS-derived features, with occurrence probability and conditional demand size as the dominant drivers, are the dominant drivers of model predictions. These quantities correspond to intuitive managerial concepts of the likelihood of demand and the size it is expected to be if it occurs. This interpretability facilitates trust, auditability, and stakeholder acceptance, particularly in settings where planners must justify decisions to procurement, finance, or service teams.

Finally, SHOS is operationally scalable and parameter-light, supporting large-scale deployment. The k-sensitivity analysis demonstrates that SHOS performance varies smoothly across a wide range of sparsity conditions and does not require fine-grained parameter tuning across product categories or lifecycle stages. As a result, a single global parameter setting can be applied across tens of thousands of SKUs, enabling consistent and scalable deployment in large assortments without manual intervention or per-item calibration14.

Taken together, these insights suggest that the primary managerial value of SHOS lies not only in improved forecast accuracy, but also in shifting the focus of intermittent-demand forecasting from architectural complexity to representation quality. By embedding a statistically grounded, event-preserving demand structure directly into the features, SHOS enables simpler, more robust, and more interpretable forecasting systems that closely align with the operational realities of intermittent demand planning.

Practical implications for inventory management

From an operational perspective, the performance characteristics of the SHOS-augmented model have direct implications for inventory planning and control in intermittent-demand environments. Forecast errors in such settings are inherently asymmetric: underprediction increases the risk of stockouts and service level failures, while overprediction leads to excess inventory, obsolescence, and unnecessary holding costs. The consistently low MAE and RMSE achieved by SHOS enhanced models indicate not only improved point accuracy, but also tighter error distributions, which are critical for managing this trade-off effectively.

For inventory managers, this reliability translates into greater confidence in replenishment decisions, particularly for slow-moving items where even small forecast deviations can have disproportionate cost impacts.

Equally important is the operational simplicity of the proposed framework. The use of a single-stage machine learning model augmented with SHOS-derived features reduces implementation complexity relative to multi-stage or hurdle-based pipelines. Such simplicity lowers barriers to deployment, facilitates routine retraining and monitoring, and improves transparency when forecasts must be communicated to planners and stakeholders. These considerations are especially relevant in large-scale supply chains that manage tens of thousands of SKUs amid frequent demand volatility.

The robustness of the SHOS-enhanced model under extreme sparsity further supports its practical applicability. Overfitting diagnostics and stress test evaluations confirm stable generalization across validation windows, indicating reliable out-of-sample performance. This stability is essential for dynamic inventory systems that must adapt continuously to evolving demand patterns without frequent model recalibration.

Taken together, these findings suggest that effective intermittent demand forecasting for inventory management does not require increasingly complex modelling architectures. Instead, embedding statistically grounded, event-aware representations of demand occurrence and magnitude into the forecasting pipeline enables simpler models to deliver more reliable and operationally useful predictions. This shift from architectural complexity to representation quality offers a practical pathway for improving inventory performance while maintaining system scalability and interpretability.

Conclusions and managerial implications

Intermittent demand forecasting remains one of the most operationally critical challenges in large-scale supply chains, particularly in aftermarket and spare-parts environments where thousands of slow-moving SKUs exhibit prolonged inactivity punctuated by irregular demand events. While recent research has largely focused on increasing architectural sophistication, the present study demonstrates that substantial and more robust gains can be achieved at the feature-representation level.

This work introduced the SHOS framework as an event-preserving feature engineering approach that explicitly decomposes demand into an occurrence probability and a conditional size component, and embeds these statistically grounded priors directly into supervised learning pipelines. Extensive large-scale experiments on more than 56,000 intermittent time series show that SHOS-derived features consistently reduce forecasting error by approximately 46.3% relative to conventional feature sets, while also improving generalization stability, responsiveness, and recovery under extreme sparsity.

Beyond predictive accuracy, the analysis demonstrates that SHOS preserves event timing, peak structure, and temporal dependence characteristics that are frequently distorted by conventional smoothing or deep sequence models. These properties explain why SHOS-augmented models outperform both raw-feature machine learning models and more complex two-stage or deep learning architectures. The findings indicate that, in highly intermittent regimes, representation quality dominates architectural complexity in determining forecasting performance.

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of the evaluated automotive aftermarket forecasting environment, the selected preprocessing strategy, and the forecasting configurations considered in the present investigation. The dataset originates from a single national automotive aftermarket network, and therefore, the reported behaviour may reflect market-specific sparsity characteristics, dealer behaviour, and inventory dynamics.

The present work focused primarily on point forecasting and event-preserving representation learning under highly intermittent demand conditions. Although the reported forecasting improvements, robustness behaviour, and signal-preservation characteristics are operationally relevant for intermittent-demand planning, the study did not explicitly evaluate probabilistic forecasting, uncertainty quantification, downstream inventory policies, service-level optimization, replenishment cost behaviour, or multi-echelon inventory performance. Consequently, the forecasting improvements reported here should not be interpreted as direct proof of inventory-level optimization performance.

In addition, the reported forecasting behaviour depends on the evaluated feature-generation framework, temporal aggregation strategy, and preprocessing configuration adopted in the present study. While SHOS demonstrated strong performance under the evaluated sparse-demand conditions, further validation is required across alternative spare-parts ecosystems, retail forecasting environments, geographical markets, and heterogeneous supply-chain structures.

Future work should therefore focus on extending SHOS toward probabilistic forecasting environments, integrating downstream inventory simulation and replenishment optimization, and evaluating representation-preserving sparse-demand forecasting across broader industrial and retail settings.

Scientific and strategic implications.

More broadly, this study contributes to a growing body of evidence that learning performance in sparse and intermittent systems is governed primarily by the quality of representation rather than by model complexity. The success of SHOS suggests that statistically grounded, event-aware feature engineering can reduce reliance on complex hybrid architectures, enabling simpler, more interpretable, and more scalable forecasting systems.

Limitations and future work.

Future work will extend SHOS toward adaptive parameter learning, automated lifecycle-aware representations, and integration with prescriptive inventory-optimization frameworks. Further evaluation across multi-echelon supply chains, real-time deployment environments, and additional industry sectors will help establish broader generalizability and operational impact.

The present study evaluates SHOS representations under a fixed monthly aggregation framework. Although monthly discretization improves statistical stability under highly sparse demand conditions, alternative temporal resolutions may preserve additional renewal dynamics and short-term demand structure. Future work should therefore investigate weekly aggregation, adaptive temporal discretization, and multi-resolution intermittent-demand representation frameworks to evaluate the sensitivity of SHOS-derived features to aggregation bucket size across different industrial forecasting environments.

The present evaluation framework focuses primarily on aggregate forecasting accuracy and representation-oriented comparative analysis under sparse-demand conditions. Future work should extend the evaluation protocol to include event-oriented intermittent-demand metrics, such as the sensitivity and specificity of nonzero-demand detection, conditional evaluation restricted to positive-demand observations, and Average Detection Delay (ADD) to measure responsiveness to demand reactivation after prolonged inactivity periods.

Future work should also investigate alternative representation-learning approaches for sparse dealer-part interaction matrices, including zero-inflated probabilistic matrix factorization, Poisson factorization, and nonnegative matrix factorization methods specifically designed for highly sparse count-based environments. Such approaches may further improve latent behavioural representation under extreme intermittent-demand conditions.
