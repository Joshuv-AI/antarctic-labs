Short-term Safety Profile of Trochanteric-Entry Intramedullary Lengthening Nails in Skeletally Immature Patients
Authors: Joshua T Bram, Stockton Troyer, Evelyn P Murphy, Austin T Fragomen, Taylor Reif, Jason S Hoellwarth, S Robert Rozbruch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are historical concerns with trochanteric-entry intramedullary femoral nailing in skeletally immature patients due to possible proximal femoral growth disturbance, including coxa valga. While more contemporary studies suggest improved safety with modern nail designs, data specific to femoral lengthening nails in patients with substantial growth remaining are limited. The purpose of this study was to evaluate proximal femoral morphology following trochanteric-entry intramedullary lengthening in skeletally immature patients.MethodsA retrospective institutional review board–approved case series was performed in skeletally immature patients with >2 years of growth remaining and a minimum of 1-year radiographic follow-up who underwent antegrade femoral lengthening with a Precice™ nail between 2017 and 2024. Radiographic parameters, including the neck-shaft angle (NSA), mechanical lateral proximal femoral angle (mLPFA), and articulotrochanteric distance (ATD), were measured preoperatively and at latest follow-up. Clinically meaningful coxa valga was defined as a ≥2 standard deviation increase in NSA from baseline. Linear regression analyses were performed to assess the effect of time remaining until skeletal maturity on proximal femoral morphology.ResultsTwenty-four femoral lengthenings in 22 patients (mean age 12.2 ± 2.3 years) were included with a mean radiographic follow-up of 23.4 ± 18.8 months. The mean age at nail insertion was 12.2 ± 2.3 years, and the mean lengthening was 46.4 ± 19.5 mm. There were no cases of infection, implant failure, or osteonecrosis. For the series, no significant differences were observed between pre and postoperative NSA, mLPFA, or ATD. One patient (4.2%) treated at age 8.6 years developed progressive coxa valga at long-term follow-up. Regression analysis demonstrated that each additional year of growth remaining was associated with a 1.5° increase in NSA, indicating that greater time remaining until skeletal maturity was associated with valgus-directed postoperative changes in proximal femoral morphology.ConclusionsTrochanteric-entry intramedullary femoral lengthening demonstrates a favorable safety profile in skeletally immature patients with >2 years of growth remaining, with a low risk of proximal femoral growth disturbance, including osteonecrosis. Younger patients with substantial growth remaining may be at higher risk for valgus proximal femoral changes, warranting routine radiographic surveillance until skeletal maturity.Key Concepts(1)Trochanteric-entry antegrade femoral lengthening appears safe in skeletally immature patients with >2 years of growth remaining, with no noted cases of osteonecrosis.(2)Clinically relevant proximal femoral growth disturbance was uncommon across the series, with no significant postoperative changes in the neck-shaft angle, mechanical lateral proximal femoral angle, or articulotrochanteric distance.(3)The youngest subset of patients with substantial growth remaining may be at higher risk for progressive coxa valga, necessitating routine postoperative radiographic surveillance until skeletal maturity.Level of EvidenceLevel 4 (case series)Keywords: Coxa valga, Precice, Femur, Intramedullary lengthening

Introduction

A common concern with trochanteric- or lateral-entry rigid nailing in young, skeletally immature patients is either the development of osteonecrosis [1] or injury to the greater trochanter apophysis, causing growth disturbance and subsequent coxa valga deformity [2,3]. Early experiences with intramedullary rods or nails in children were associated with the development of abnormal proximal femoral morphology in up to 30% of cases, including coxa valga and femoral neck narrowing, particularly in patients <13 years of age [3]. This led Raney et al. [2] to suggest that, in this age group, other methods of fracture treatment should be considered until subchondral sclerosis is visualized along the greater trochanteric physis. Similarly, it has been proposed that intramedullary nailing of the femur, even with lateral entry, should be employed primarily in patients >9 years of age [4].

However, numerous recent studies have been published suggesting a favorable safety profile of intramedullary nails in this patient population when treating femoral shaft fractures, rotational deformity, or performing femoral lengthening [[5], [6], [7]]. Knapik et al. [8] reported on the developmental patterns of the greater trochanter in children, observing that combined bony and cartilaginous trochanteric growth is essentially complete by age 7 and 8 years in females and males, respectively, positing that antegrade nail placement is feasible at ages ≥8 years. Several articles have evaluated postoperative changes in several measures of proximal femoral morphology, including the neck-shaft angle (NSA) and articulotrochanteric distance (ATD), without identifying postoperative abnormalities [6].

Therefore, the available literature suggests that when appropriate surgical technique is employed, the risk of clinically significant coxa valga and osteonecrosis after femoral nail placement in skeletally immature patients is low. One unique consideration, beyond fracture fixation where flexible nailing remains a viable option in the youngest cohort of patients, is the use of femoral lengthening nails in patients with congenital deficiencies that require earlier intervention. While many articles on femoral lengthenings generally report few to no cases of coxa valga, osteonecrosis, or other changes to proximal femoral morphology, they almost universally include a wide range of patients, including fully skeletally mature individuals [9,10]. Therefore, a case series reporting on proximal femoral morphology after lengthening nail placement in skeletally immature patients with >2 years of growth remaining is warranted. We hypothesized that there would be no cases of clinically relevant coxa valga development or osteonecrosis during short-term follow-up.

Methods

This was an institutional review board-approved retrospective case series of all skeletally immature patients who underwent antegrade femoral lengthening with the Precice™ nail from January 2017 to December 2024. Patients were treated by one of four limb lengthening and reconstruction specialists at a tertiary care orthopaedic specialty hospital. All had >2 years of growth remaining (females <12 years, males <14 years) at the time of surgery and a minimum of 1-year radiographic follow-up. Those with inadequate radiographic follow-up <12 months (n = 10), lengthening with another lengthening nail (n = 5), or prior surgeries about the proximal femur including prior femoral lengthenings over a nail or proximal femoral osteotomies (n = 0) were specifically excluded.

Patient demographic information and the reason for the lengthening procedure were obtained from the medical record. Prior operative procedures to the involved extremity were also identified for possible exclusion. Operative reports were reviewed to determine the length and diameter of the implant used. Clinic visits were then used to determine the amount of lengthening that was performed (in millimeters [mm]) as well as the duration of follow-up, the timing of removal of hardware or required subsequent procedures, and the occurrence of any complications. Complications specifically noted included infection, stiffness, and hardware failure. These were assessed using the Clavien-Dindo-Sink classification [11].

Surgical technique and postoperative protocol

Surgical planning includes calculating the shortest nail length and choosing the osteotomy level. The minimal length of the nail is calculated by measuring the distance from the nail insertion point to the proposed osteotomy site, and then the planned lengthening must also be added, plus 50 mm (length of the thick part of the nail that must remain in the proximal fragment) and 30 mm (length of the tip of the nail) [12]. Patients are positioned supine, with a bump under their ipsilateral hip. The proposed osteotomy level is first marked and measured from the tip of the greater trochanter, and a one centimeter stab incision is made at the osteotomy level. A multiple-drill-hole technique is utilized, using a 4.8-mm drill, which has been described in previous publications and additionally serves to vent the canal [[13], [14], [15]]. Then a 2.4 mm guide wire is inserted into the proximal femur, with the hip in an adducted position, using biplanar fluoroscopy.

A cannulated anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reamer is used to enter the proximal femur, and a flexible ball-tip guide wire is passed to the superior pole of the patella, and then serial reaming is performed. Steinmann pins are then placed posterior to the path of the nail in the lesser trochanter and in the distal femur, distal to the tip of the nail. These are placed to prevent malrotation after the osteotomy is completed. These are placed parallel in the axial plane if no rotational correction is being performed. The guide wire is removed and the nail is inserted proximal to the osteotomy site. The osteotomy is then completed using osteotomes and the nail is passed across the osteotomy site and seated. The distal screws are placed using the perfect-circle method and the proximal screws are placed using the jig. An iliotibial band release is then always carried out.

Postoperatively, patients are made partial weight-bearing and are instructed to start the lengthening on postoperative day 7 at a rate of 0.25 mm four times a day. Specific weight-bearing status is dependent on the nail diameter, with weight restrictions of 30 lbs, 50 lbs or 70 lbs. Every two to three weeks, patients are seen in clinic for monitoring of their hip and knee range of motion, neurovascular status, and the quality of the regenerate. Adjustments to the lengthening rate can then be made accordingly.

Radiographic parameters

A number of radiographic parameters (Fig. 1) were measured to evaluate for potential changes in the morphology of the proximal femur, including the neck-shaft angle (NSA), mechanical lateral proximal femoral angle (mLPFA), and articulotrochanteric distance (ATD). The NSA was assessed as the angle between 1) a line through the center of the femoral head and the center of the base of the femoral neck and 2) the long axis of the femur, which was determined using the centers of two circles in the proximal and distal femoral shaft [16]. The mean and standard deviation for the NSA in this study were consistent with those of prior evaluations of proximal femoral morphology in children [17,18]. Development of clinically meaningful coxa valga as a change from baseline morphology was therefore considered as a ≥2 standard deviation (determined from the overall cohort) increase in the NSA. The mLPFA was measured as the angle between the mechanical axis of the femur (i.e. a line from the center of the femoral head to the center of the knee joint) and the proximal femoral joint orientation line (a line connecting the center of the femoral head and the tip of the greater trochanter). The ATD was measured by drawing two lines perpendicular to the femoral shaft, one across the top of the femoral head and the other at the top of the greater trochanter. The ATD was then taken as the vertical distance between these two lines on a calibrated image in mm. Finally, femoral length was measured preoperatively and at the latest radiographic follow-up by measuring the distance from the top of the femoral head to the center of the knee joint along a line drawn across the femoral condyles using picture archiving and communication systems software on calibrated long-leg alignment radiographs. Postoperative radiographs were additionally scrutinized for the occurrence of osteonecrosis (sclerosis, collapse, etc.).

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were first calculated for all femora included rather than by patient. Paired t-tests were also used to compare radiographic parameters pre and postoperatively. The estimated time until skeletal maturity was determined by subtracting the patient age at surgery from 14 years for girls and 16 years for boys, respectively. These were chosen as commonly accepted ages for skeletal maturation, with strong correlations shown between skeletal and chronological age [19,20]. Simple linear regressions were then used to analyze the effect of time until estimated skeletal maturity on the change (i.e. delta) in the three radiographic variables from preoperative until the latest follow-up. The results of the regressions were then reported as the beta statistic and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh (Version 24.0, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.5.1 (R core team, 2025, http://www.R-project).

Results

A total of 24 femoral lengthenings across 22 patients were included for analysis (Table 1). Eight (33%) patients had undergone prior operative intervention for either lengthening or deformity correction. These included prior femoral and tibial lengthenings using a circular external fixator with medial distal femur and proximal tibia hemiepiphysiodesis for genu valgum (n = 1), bilateral distal femur hemiepiphysiodesis for genu valgum (n = 1), prior tibial lengthening using a circular external fixator and medial distal femur hemiepiphysiodesis (n = 1), prior femoral lengthening using a circular external fixator (n = 1), prior tibial lengthening over a circular external fixator with distal femoral and proximal tibial osteotomies, as well as ankle arthrodesis (n = 1), prior distal femoral, proximal tibial, and distal tibial osteotomies (n = 1), prior femoral lengthening using a circular external fixator with hemiepiphysiodesis of the lateral distal femur, proximal tibia, and distal tibia (n = 1), and trifocal tibial bone transport over a frame for segmental bone loss in the setting of an open fracture (n = 1). The mean age at nail insertion was 12.2 ± 2.3 years and 71% of surgeries were performed in male patients. The underlying diagnoses and reason for lengthening varied across the series, but the most common indication was congenital femoral deficiency. Thirteen (54%) of nails were 8.5 mm in diameter and eleven (46%) were 10.7 mm, with lengths ranging from 215 mm to 335 mm. Preoperative femoral length had a mean of 390.3 ± 46.8 mm (290.0–479.0). Lengthening occurred over a mean 47.2 ± 18.5 days to accomplish a mean lengthening of 46.4 ± 19.5 mm (18.0–80.0).

Overall radiographic follow-up averaged 23.4 ± 18.8 months. Twenty-two limbs (91.7%) underwent removal of the nail at a mean of 15.2 ± 6.1 months (Table 2). There were no complications related to infection, hardware failure, or osteonecrosis. Secondary procedures – aside from standard removal of hardware for either the nail or guided growth plates – included: anterior distal femoral hemiepiphysiodesis for a 15° knee flexion contracture (n = 1) that developed postoperatively (Clavien–Dindo–Sink 3), tibial lengthening over a Precice™ nail and subsequent flatfoot reconstruction (n = 1), bilateral medial distal femur and proximal tibia hemiepiphysiodesis for bilateral genu valgum (n = 1), exchange nailing from an 8.5 mm–10.7 mm Precice™ nail to accomplish an additional 70 mm of lengthening (n = 2), and distal tibia and fibula osteotomies with hindfoot arthrodesis for residual deformity (n = 1).

There were no significant changes in NSA (P = .068), mLPFA (P = .615), or ATD (P = .132) from preoperatively to the latest follow-up (Table 3). One patient (4.2%) with Russell–Silver syndrome developed a change in NSA of greater than two standard deviations into coxa valga (Fig. 2), which was managed with observation (Clavien–Dindo–Sink 1). After his initial lengthening at the age of 8.6 years using an 8.5 mm × 275 mm nail, his NSA increased from 141° to 159° at the latest radiographic follow-up at 79.1 months. His ATD similarly increased from 20 mm to 35 mm, and his mLPFA decreased from 79° to 64°. No other patients had a change in the other radiographic parameters from pre to postoperative beyond two standard deviations.

Linear regression analysis revealed that there was a significant relationship between time until skeletal maturity and delta NSA (beta = 1.5, 95% CI: 0.4 to 2.6, P = .010, r2 = 0.267), delta mLPFA (beta = −2.5, 95% CI: -3.8 to −1.1, P = .001, r2 = 0.395), and delta ATD (beta = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.0 to 2.8, P < .001, r2 = 0.493). For NSA, this indicates that for each additional year until estimated skeletal maturity, there is a positive 1.5° increase in the change in NSA (Fig. 3). Similarly, each additional year was associated with a 2.5° decrease in the change in mLPFA and a 1.9 mm increase in the change in ATD. Taken together, this reflects that nail placement in patients with greater growth remaining is associated with more valgus changes that decrease as skeletal maturity is reached.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a favorable safety profile for trochanteric-entry intramedullary lengthening using the Precice™ nail in skeletally immature patients with >2 years of growth remaining. Among 24 femoral lengthenings performed in 22 patients, there were no cases of infection, implant failure, or osteonecrosis. Importantly, no clinically meaningful changes in proximal femoral morphology were observed for the cohort, supporting the growing body of evidence that traditional- or lateral-entry trochanteric-entry rigid nailing can be performed safely in pediatric patients, including those with substantial growth remaining. However, caution should be used in the youngest subset of patients as one patient who underwent nail placement at 8 years of age went on to develop clinically relevant coxa valga, suggesting that perhaps these patients should receive routine monitoring until skeletal maturity.

Historically, rigid antegrade femoral nailing in children has been approached with caution. Early reports describing piriformis fossa start points documented clinically relevant proximal femoral deformities and osteonecrosis, leading to longstanding hesitancy toward rigid nailing in skeletally immature patients [1,2]. Trochanteric-entry nails have received less attention regarding osteonecrosis, but concerns remain over iatrogenic injury to the greater trochanter apophysis and subsequent development of coxa valga. More contemporary nail designs in children with lateral trochanteric entry points consequently have consistently demonstrated markedly lower complication rates with regard to osteonecrosis [4,6]. Similar to the present study, Dubin et al. [10] evaluated 247 patients across all age groups, including a large number of children, who underwent femoral lengthening using an intramedullary nail, reporting no cases of osteonecrosis. The absence of osteonecrosis in the present series therefore aligns with prior work demonstrating that trochanteric-entry using the Precice™ lengthening nail avoids compromise of the femoral head blood supply.

The absence of significant pre to postoperative changes in the NSA, mechanical lateral proximal femoral angle, and ATD further supports the safety of this technique in patients with >2 years of growth remaining. While prior series have reported small changes in the ATD following rigid nailing, these changes have generally not translated into clinically significant deformity. In a large retrospective cohort of skeletally immature patients with femur fractures treated with trochanteric-entry nails and a minimum of two-year radiographic follow-up, asymptomatic coxa valga occurred in only a small minority of skeletally immature patients (2.2%) and 15.1% of the cohort developing an increased ATD >5 mm (i.e. more valgus) [21]. These authors postulated that this was the result of either growth stimulation of the proximal femoral physis or growth retardation of the trochanteric apophysis. Other studies by Gordon et al. and Keeler et al. evaluating lateral trochanteric-entry both noted minimal, clinically insignificant changes in proximal femoral morphology following lateral trochanteric-entry nailing [4,6]. This suggests that while small growth changes occur, they are often not large enough to cause symptoms. The present study expands upon these observations by specifically focusing on patients with substantial growth remaining, a population that has been underrepresented in prior analyses.

One patient in this series developed progressive coxa valga, characterized by a substantial increase in the NSA (from 141° to 159°), a decrease in the mLPFA, and an increase in ATD over a long-term follow-up 6.6 years. This patient was notably younger than the cohort mean (age at surgery 8.6 years, ∼7–8 years of growth remaining) and had Russell–Silver syndrome, a condition associated with abnormal growth patterns but treated safely with limb lengthening [22]. While causation cannot be definitively established, this outlier highlights the importance of younger skeletal age, which may confer increased susceptibility to proximal femoral growth disturbance following rigid nailing. As the femoral head and greater trochanter are part of one combined structure with a continuous proximal femoral epiphysis that only later separates at older ages, it is possible that damage to the persistent lateral aspect of this physis with trochanteric-entry nailing leads to coxa valga development rather than damage to the apophysis itself [6,23].

The regression analyses provide further important insights into the relationship between skeletal maturity and proximal femoral remodeling. Greater time remaining until skeletal maturity was significantly associated with valgus-directed changes in all three radiographic parameters. The consistency of these associations suggests a biologically plausible growth-related effect, where disturbance of the greater trochanter apophysis (or the lateral proximal femoral physis) leads to valgus deformities at the youngest ages, whereas at older ages there is little further growth from the trochanteric physis [24]. Taken together, these findings indicate that while femoral lengthening with a trochanteric-entry nail is generally safe, patients with more remaining growth may experience greater radiographic remodeling. We therefore recommend that skeletally immature patients who undergo lengthening over an intramedullary nail should receive routine yearly radiographic monitoring until skeletal maturity.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and relatively small sample size limit the ability to detect a high number of potential growth disturbance cases and preclude multivariable risk modeling. Second, radiographic measurements were performed at varying follow-up intervals postoperatively, and although minimum follow-up criteria were enforced, not all patients were followed to skeletal maturity. Third, outcomes were solely assessed radiographically without capturing patient-reported outcomes, and subtle functional or symptomatic consequences of proximal femoral remodeling may not have been elucidated. Fourth, skeletal age was not assessed, as no routine hand radiographs were obtained, and therefore time to estimated maturity based on standard timelines (14 years in girls and 16 years in boys) was used. Fifth, patients with prior femoral lengthenings (either antegrade or retrograde) were excluded to most accurately characterize the effect of trochanteric-entry nail placement on proximal femoral morphology from a clean baseline, though this subpopulation warrants further evaluation as many of these children require multiple procedures. Last, radiographic measurements assessing hip morphology were performed on full-length hip-to-ankle imaging, where the beam is centered more distally. Despite these limitations, this series represents one of the largest focused evaluations in the published literature of intramedullary lengthening nails in skeletally immature patients with substantial growth remaining and provides clinically meaningful data to guide surgical decision-making.

Conclusion

Femoral lengthening using a trochanteric-entry intramedullary nail appears to be a safe and effective option in the majority of skeletally immature patients with >2 years of growth remaining. The risk of clinically significant proximal femoral deformity is low, and serious complications such as osteonecrosis were not observed. However, caution should be exercised in the youngest subset of patients, who may be at higher risk for valgus remodeling. These findings support the continued use of trochanteric-entry lengthening nails while emphasizing the importance of longitudinal radiographic monitoring until skeletal maturity.
