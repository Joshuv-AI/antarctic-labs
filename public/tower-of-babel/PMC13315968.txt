Primary pelvic extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma: a case report and comprehensive literature review of molecularly confirmed cases arising within the female genital tract
Authors: Anna C Hornbach, Sandhyarani Dasaraju, Alexandra M Schefter, Britt K Erickson
Published: 2026

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma is an aggressive mesenchymal neoplasm classified morphologically by undifferentiated small round cells and characteristic EWSR1 gene fusions, most commonly presenting as a bone lesion in young males (Dehner et al., 2024). Extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma (EES) is exceedingly rare with a lack of known risk factors. Its prevalence often follows a bimodal age distribution without established associations with race or sex, unlike Ewing sarcoma of the bone (Abboud et al., 2021). As the molecular signature of Ewing sarcoma has become better understood, the World Health Organization now designates the historical term “peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumor,” which described cases with neuronal differentiation, as part of the Ewing sarcoma family (Dehner et al., 2024).

Current knowledge of EES of the gynecologic tract is expanding, despite gynecologic oncology literature being predominately limited to isolated case reports or case series. However, there are significant diagnostic challenges associated with Ewing sarcoma given shared morphologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular features with intrinsic gynecologic malignancies such as high-grade endometrial sarcoma (Sharma et al., 2024; Shi et al. 2024). Management of Ewing sarcoma of the gynecologic organs follows National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for Ewing sarcoma of the bone, with multimodal therapy including neoadjuvant chemotherapy, wide resection and/or radiation for localized disease, and subsequent adjuvant chemotherapy (Biermann et al., 2026). EES of the gynecologic organs is most often described as a primary soft tissue tumor.

We present a rare case of primary pelvic EES of indeterminate origin invading the uterus, vagina, and bladder of a previously healthy 21-year-old female who required multidisciplinary surgical management. We provide a summary of existing literature describing Ewing sarcoma of the female genital tract, noting that this is one of few cases to report invasive disease in multiple pelvic organs and indeterminate origin of disease.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old previously healthy woman, with no notable medical, surgical or family history, presented to her primary care provider (PCP) with two weeks of flu-like illness including headaches, night sweats, body aches, weakness, fatigue and fevers up to 104.1 degrees Fahrenheit. She was presumed to have a viral infection after a brief resolution of symptoms was noted with conservative management. She returned to her PCP two weeks later, reporting ten days of red/orange colored vaginal discharge, pelvic pain, dysuria, urinary urgency and frequency, and low back pain. There was concern for urinary tract infection and/or pelvic inflammatory disease and she was sent promptly to the Emergency Department for further workup with a pelvic ultrasound.

Pelvic ultrasound revealed a large mass on the anterior uterus measuring 10.2 cm, with mass effect on the vagina concerning for a fibroid versus ovarian mass (Fig. 1). Right ovary and remaining uterus and endometrium were normal appearing. At that time, gynecology was consulted and she was admitted to a rural hospital for further workup. CBC was notable for leukocytosis with a WBC count of 13.3, mild thrombophilia with a platelet count of 485, and anemia with a hemoglobin of 10.1. CMP was notable for elevated globulin at 4.5, and urinalysis was reassuring against infection and showed 3–5 RBCs per HPF and occasional mucus.

A. Pelvic ultrasound demonstrating heterogenous mass (coronal view). B. MRI showing cystic and solid mass with bladder involvement and mass effect on the uterus and vagina. C. CT redemonstrating tumor and evidence of collecting system dilation.

The following day, MRI pelvis was obtained to further characterize the mass. MRI findings revealed a heterogenous cystic and solid mass in the mid pelvis located between the posterior bladder and anterior vagina measuring 9.6 × 9.4 × 7.8 cm with mass effect on the uterus and vagina (Fig. 1B). The mass appeared to be contiguous with the left anterolateral urinary bladder wall, with left ureteral obstruction and severe left hydroureter. Prominent nonspecific lymphadenopathy in the inguinal region and external iliac chain was present. The ovaries were normal appearing bilaterally. Given concern for malignancy based on imaging findings and ongoing urinary symptoms, urology was promptly consulted. CT Abdomen/Pelvis showed moderate left hydronephrosis and hydroureter secondary to the pelvic mass and cystoscopy showed no intraluminal bladder mass (Fig. 1C). A left ureteral stent was placed and the patient safely discharged home with plans for follow-up with surgical oncology and gynecologic oncology within a week.

She then saw gynecologic oncology within a week of hospital discharge. At that time, she continued to endorse night sweats, pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, urinary urgency and now constipation. On physical exam, there was a palpable mass between the pubic symphysis and umbilicus that was tender to palpation. Pelvic exam revealed blood‑tinged vaginal discharge and a distorted anterior vagina secondary to the mass which limited speculum exam. Bimanual exam revealed palpable large firm and fixed mass anterior to the vagina. The cervix was unable to be reached given posterior displacement. Rectovaginal exam revealed no rectal involvement of the mass. Tumor markers including CA 125, Inhibin B, CEA, CA 19–9 and LDH were within reference ranges. A multidisciplinary tumor board reviewed the case and recommended total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingectomy and possible cancer debulking and staging. The patient was agreeable to surgery, and the case was scheduled in combination with urologic oncology given possible need for ureteral and/or bladder resection.

Approximately one month after initial presentation to her PCP, the patient underwent an exploratory laparotomy, en bloc radical hysterectomy, bilateral salpingectomy (with ovarian preservation), anterior vaginectomy, distal left ureterectomy, partial posterior cystectomy with left ureteral reimplantation, bilateral ureteral stent placement, omental J flap, and bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy. Intraoperative pathology revealed small blue round cell tumor concerning for aggressive malignancy of unclear etiology. The operation was complicated by 2500 ml of blood loss requiring two units of blood intraoperatively and one additional unit of blood during admission. The patient recovered well in the post-operative period and discharged safely on post-op day six with bilateral ureteral stents and foley catheter in place.

Pathologic diagnosis

The surgical specimen revealed a firm mass measuring 11 cm in greatest dimension. The tumor was located along the anterior uterine wall, abutting the urinary bladder, and extending into the anterior vaginal wall. Grossly, the lesion was poorly circumscribed and exhibited an infiltrative growth pattern. Lymph nodes were negative for metastatic disease. On serial sectioning, the cut surface was heterogeneous, tan, white to brown, with extensive areas of necrosis and hemorrhage (Fig. 2A).

A. Gross tumor after resection. B: Histologic findings of undifferentiated small blue cells. C. CD99 Staining. D. NKX2.2 Staining.

Histological examination demonstrated a high-grade malignant neoplasm composed of sheets of undifferentiated small round cells with fine chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli (Fig. 2B). Immunohistochemical studies showed diffuse, strong membranous positivity for CD99 and nuclear positivity for NKX2.2 (Fig. 2C and D). These morphologic and immunophenotypic features were highly suggestive of Ewing sarcoma. All surgical margins were negative, with the closest margin at 1 mm at the anterior soft tissue/vaginal mucosal margin.

Cytogenetic analysis identified a chromosomal translocation, t(11;22)(q24;q12), the most common genetic abnormality associated with Ewing sarcoma. Subsequent molecular testing confirmed an EWSR1 gene rearrangement, specifically the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion transcript. This fusion results from the juxtaposition of the EWSR1 gene on chromosome 22 and the FLI1 gene on chromosome 11, generating an oncogenic transcription factor that drives tumorigenesis. The presence of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion is a defining molecular hallmark of Ewing sarcoma and confirms the diagnosis. The combined morphologic, immunohistochemical, cytogenetic, and molecular findings are diagnostic of a primary pelvic Ewing sarcoma.

Patient follow up

After evaluation by a sarcoma specialist, medical oncology planned for adjuvant chemotherapy beginning 4–6 weeks post-operatively given partial bladder resection. One month after surgery, PET scan showed no evidence of metastatic disease. Foley catheter and bladder stents were removed. The patient was offered consultation with a fertility specialist for potential oocyte cryopreservation, but she declined. The primary frontline treatment for Ewing sarcoma is vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide (VDC) alternating with cycles of ifosfamide and etoposide (IE). However, due to her partial bladder resection and concern for potential bladder toxicity from ifosfamide and cyclophosphamide, the patient received two cycles of vincristine, irinotecan, and temozolomide. For the third cycle, the patient was given VDC after confirmation of bladder healing via cystoscopy. She completed planned hospital admission for IE with mesna three weeks later and a subsequent fourth cycle of VDC/IE. Final duration of chemotherapy is pending upcoming imaging.

Discussion and literature review

We present a rare case of EES involving locally invasive disease into multiple pelvic organs including the uterus, vagina, and bladder in a previously healthy 21-year-old woman. This case required multidisciplinary surgical and oncologic care. Primary EES involving gynecologic organs is exceedingly rare, making this case notable for its unusual anatomical location and extensive pelvic organ involvement. This case highlights the importance of prompt imaging and local control of disease. Furthermore, early and accurate diagnosis of EES through molecular confirmation of the EWSR1 fusion gene is critical to guiding treatment according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines.

A thorough literature review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar with the following search terms:

“Ewing sarcoma,” “Ewing's sarcoma,” “peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumor” and “(uterus OR vagina OR cervix OR vulva OR broad ligament OR ovary).”

Demographic information, clinical presentation, tumor characteristics, treatment, and outcomes are summarized below for cases with definitive diagnosis through molecular testing confirming an EWSR1 gene fusion (Table 1). Data published in abstracts only were excluded. A total of 98 cases of molecularly confirmed EES involving gynecologic organs were included involving the vulva, vagina, uterine cervix, uterine corpus, broad ligament, ovary and fallopian tubes. Two cases were noted as “primary pelvic” without a clear origin from a single pelvic organ (Table 1).

As there is no universally established staging system for EES, we reported whether or not metastatic disease was present.

EES within the female genital tract often presents with non-specific symptoms such as abdominal/pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, or a palpable mass (Table 1). EES occurs throughout the spectrum of the female genital tract across the lifespan, with the youngest patient documented in the literature to be 3 years old (Xu et al., 2019). Gynecologic EES is aggressive, with a high incidence of mortality and many patients present with metastatic disease. However, in comparison to Ewing sarcoma of the bone, EES may have a more favorable prognosis (Abboud et al., 2021; Galyfos et al., 2016). Per our literature review, vulvar and uterine EES had the most favorable patient outcomes, with 66.7% (10/15) of patients surviving without evidence of disease at a mean follow-up period of 29.5 months and 71.4% (10/14) of patients surviving without evidence of disease at a mean follow-up period of 31 months, respectively. Considering vaginal EES, 60.0% (6/10) of patients survived without evidence of disease at a mean follow-up period of 16.3 months. For cervical EES, 40.1% (9/22) of patients survived without evidence of disease for a mean follow up period of 27.6 months. Prognosis for ovarian EES was poor, with only one of six patients surviving without evidence of disease at a follow-up period of 132 months.

Most care plans did not follow NCCN guidelines recommending neoadjuvant VDC/IE chemotherapy followed by local control including wide surgical excision or radiation and adjuvant chemotherapy. Instead, of patients with chemotherapy data available, only 54.1% (33/61) received first-line VDC/IE chemotherapy during their treatment. Additionally, 73.8% (48/65) of patients with available surgical data were treated with upfront surgery while 13.8% (9/65) underwent interval surgery after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Lastly, 12.2% (12/98) of patients were initially misdiagnosed with other gynecologic malignancies or benign leiomyomas. The variability in treatment regimens for EES of the female reproductive tract may be attributed to the major diagnostic challenges of this tumor including its rarity, requirements for tissue sampling to obtain definitive diagnosis with molecular testing, and overlapping features with intrinsic gynecologic malignancies. Furthermore, such variability in treatment regimens suggests the need for continued investigation into patient outcomes, especially regarding cases where NCCN guidelines are not initially followed.

Given the rarity of gynecologic EES, current knowledge is limited to case reports and short series. Much is unknown regarding pathogenesis and risk factors for EES. A 2020 institutional review of 302 cases within the Ewing sarcoma family of tumors found significantly increased prevalence of EES in women compared to men, and older age at presentation compared to Ewing sarcoma of the bone (25 years vs 16 years) (Rao et al., 2020). As knowledge of EES expands, fertility sparing treatments are becoming feasible as reported by a recent French study involving successful restoration of regular menses in a 25-year-old who underwent uterine transposition prior to pelvic radiation for recto-vaginal EES (Akladios et al., 2026). More research is required to further characterize gynecologic EES, investigate potential risk factors, and continue to decrease morbidity for patients through consideration of ovary-sparing and fertility-sparing treatments.

Conclusion

We report a unique case of EES in a previously healthy young woman with invasive disease in multiple pelvic organs including the uterus, vagina, and bladder which required multidisciplinary management and multimodal treatment. She underwent exploratory laparotomy with en bloc resection of the mass with ovarian preservation. After surgical management, she continues without evidence of disease on chemotherapy with a VDC/IE regimen as recommended by the NCCN for Ewing sarcomas. We also present a literature review of gynecologic extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma which summarizes existing case information including patient demographics, treatment regimens, and outcomes.
