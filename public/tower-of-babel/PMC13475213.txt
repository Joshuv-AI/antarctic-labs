Effects of a triple-strain Bacillus-based probiotic on cecal and ovarian colonization of Salmonella Enteritidis in commercial pullets
Authors: S Corray, M Schwartz, T Loeffler, A Meuter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA commercial triple-strain Bacillus-based probiotic (GALLIPRO® Fit) was evaluated for its ability to reduce cecal and ovarian colonization by Salmonella enteritidis (SE) in commercial layer pullets. A total of 250 day-old W-36 pullets were assigned to two dietary treatments on top of a standard diet: a non-supplemented control (CON) and a probiotic-supplemented (GALLIPRO® Fit) group (500 g/MT of feed; 1.6 × 106 CFU/g of finished feed; PRO). At 16 weeks of age, birds were orally challenged with 3.9 × 10⁸ CFU of a nalidixic acid-resistant SE strain. Cecal contents and ovaries were collected at 6, 10, 14, and 17 days post-infection (dpi) from 24 pullets per treatment group (n=192) for SE enumeration and prevalence assessment. Cecal SE counts were significantly reduced by 1.22 log₁₀ CFU/g in probiotic-fed birds compared to controls at 10 dpi (4.22 vs 5.44 log10 CFU/g; P = 0.0154), while no significant differences were observed at other time points. No differences in SE prevalence were detected in ceca or ovaries between treatments. These findings indicate that dietary supplementation with a multi-strain Bacillus-based probiotic may contribute to transient reductions in intestinal Salmonella Enteritidis load in layer pullets following a challenge, although effects on systemic invasion were not observed.Keywords: Bacillus, Probiotic, Pullets, Salmonella

Introduction

Salmonella enterica remains one of the leading causes of foodborne illness worldwide and continues to pose a significant challenge to poultry production and food safety. In the United States, Salmonella is estimated to cause approximately 1.35 million infections, 26,500 hospitalizations, and 420 deaths annually, representing a substantial public health and economic burden (CDC, 2025). Among the numerous serovars, Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar enteritidis (SE) is of particular importance due to its strong association with shell eggs and egg products (Whiley and Ross, 2015). In laying hens, SE can colonize the intestinal tract and subsequently invade internal organs, including the reproductive tract, leading to contamination of eggs prior to shell formation (Gast et al., 2003). This vertical transmission route represents a critical control point in the prevention of foodborne disease.

Multiple interventions are used to control Salmonella in poultry. SE is a major agent of foodborne illness linked especially to table eggs (Whiley and Ross, 2015). To address this, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration implemented the Egg Safety Rule (FDA, 2009), requiring producers to adopt preventative measures in poultry houses, perform environmental monitoring, and follow specified testing protocols. Although these measures have reduced risk, the egg industry continues to pursue additional pre-harvest food-safety strategies and technologies (De Cort et al., 2017). Pre-harvest interventions are crucial to poultry-product safety and quality (Van Immerseel et al., 2002) and include feed and water hygiene, pest control, biosecurity, vaccination, veterinary therapeutics, and feed additives (Trampel et al., 2014). These pre-harvest strategies are essential components of integrated food safety programs aimed at reducing the risk of egg contamination prior to processing and distribution (De Cort et al., 2017). However, these approaches do not completely eliminate SE colonization, and complementary strategies are required to further reduce pathogen load in poultry systems. With increasing concerns regarding antibiotic overuse and the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, probiotics are being explored as effective alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters for maintaining gut health and controlling enteric pathogens in poultry (Dibner and Richards, 2005; Shaji et al., 2023). Consequently, probiotics have received considerable attention as sustainable feed additives for improving intestinal health and reducing colonization by enteric pathogens. Probiotics are defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when administered in adequate amounts. In poultry, Bacillus-based probiotics are of particular interest due to their ability to form spores, allowing them to withstand feed processing conditions and persist in the gastrointestinal environment (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). Proposed mechanisms of action include competitive exclusion of pathogens, modulation of gut microbiota, enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity, and stimulation of host immune responses (Halder et al., 2024; Fuller, 1989). Previous studies have demonstrated that Bacillus-based probiotics can reduce Salmonella colonization in poultry; however, responses vary depending on probiotic strain composition, dosage, bird age, and experimental challenge conditions (Khan and Chousalkar, 2020). More recently, Sobotik et al. (2024) demonstrated that supplementation with the same commercial triple-strain Bacillus-based probiotic reduced cecal Salmonella Enteritidis colonization in experimentally challenged commercial layer pullets. However, information remains limited regarding the effects of this probiotic on both intestinal colonization and systemic invasion of S. Enteritidis, particularly in pre-lay pullets approaching the onset of lay, when colonization of the reproductive tract becomes increasingly relevant to egg safety. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to evaluate the effect of a commercial multi-strain Bacillus-based probiotic (GALLIPRO® Fit) on cecal colonization and ovarian invasion of SE in commercial layer pullets following experimental challenge. We hypothesized that probiotic supplementation would reduce intestinal colonization and decrease systemic invasion by SE compared with non-supplemented controls.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

A total of 250-day old W-36 chicks were obtained from Hy-Line Hatchery and randomly assigned to two dietary treatment groups at placement. Experimental groups were assigned to cage units in separate identical BSL-2 isolation rooms. Chicks were placed in cage units with wire floor raised on stainless steel decks at AAALAC-accredited Laboratory Animal Resources (LAR) at Iowa State University. Each experimental group was assigned to a single cage unit until the age of 8 weeks. At 8 weeks, pullets were assigned to 3-tier cage units to prepare for pre-lay light stimulation. Each cage unit contained 4 pullets and was equipped by one drinking nipple, a trough feeder and a manure collection tray.

A non-medicated corn and soybean meal based basal diet was formulated to meet nutritional requirements of the birds (ME 2900kcal/kg; CP 20% 0-8weeks, 18% 8-19 weeks). All feed was tested to confirm the absence of Salmonella prior to feeding, with no contaminants found and tests verified the absence of Salmonella. Pullets were assigned to treatments groups as follows: 1) Control (CON, no supplement); and 2) Control + Probiotic (PRO, GalliPro® Fit, 500 g/MT, 1.6 × 106 CFU/g of finished feed). Concentrations of Bacillus in the PRO group were verified to be at a minimum of 1.6 × 106 CFU/g using a culture-based method. All pullets were fed ad libitum with access to water throughout the study.

Salmonella challenge

Pullets were screened for Salmonella enteritidis (SE) by swabbing chick boxes upon placement as well as manure collection trays at 14 weeks of age to ensure a negative SE status prior to challenge. Each swab was placed in sterile sampling bag and gloves were changed between samples. Swabs were also collected from manure collection trays at 6 dpi to verify SE shedding associated with the experimental infection. At 16 weeks of age, pullets in both treatment groups (CON & PRO) received 1ml of SE inoculum containing 3.9 × 108 CFU of the SE challenge strain. Challenge doses were administered orally once to pullets using a dosing syringe and gavage tube. A loopful of a selected nalidixic acid resistant SE challenge strain stock was transferred to tryptic soy broth (TSB) and incubated overnight with shaking at 37° C. A 1:10 dilution was further incubated with shaking and concentration was adjusted by measuring optical density at 600 nm. Inoculum was harvested at OD reading indicative of 109 CFU/ml. Bacterial cells were washed twice, resuspended in sterile deionized water and used immediately. Actual concentration of challenge dose, verified by duplicate series of 10-fold dilutions and standard plate count method, was found to be 3.9 × 108 CFU/dose. At each sampling point (6, 10, 14, and 17 days post-infection), six cages were randomly selected from each treatment group, and all four birds within each selected cage (24 birds per treatment) were humanely euthanized for microbiological analysis. Each bird was sampled only once. Consequently, a total of 192 birds out of the 250 birds in the study were euthanized for sample collection (24 birds × 2 treatments × 4 sampling days). Birds housed in cages not selected for sampling remained in their respective treatment groups to maintain normal stocking density and husbandry conditions throughout the study. Cecal pouches and ovaries were aseptically collected from individual birds and transported to the laboratory on ice packs for analysis.

Microbiological analysis

Environmental swabs were processed for SE isolation using pre-enrichment in buffered peptone water, enrichment in Tetrathionate Hajna (TTH) broth and plating on Xylose Lysine Tergitol-4 (XLT-4) agar and Brilliant Green with Novobiocin (BGN) agar. Suspected colonies were further tested in Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) and Lysine Iron (LI) agar slants followed by serogrouping using appropriate O and H Salmonella antisera. The contents of cecal pouches were aseptically squeezed into sterile conical tubes and weighed. Sterile saline was added to prepare 1:10 weight per volume pipettable suspension. Ten-fold serial dilutions were prepared, and standard plate count method was conducted using XLT-4 agar plates containing 25 µg nalidixic acid/ml. Plates were incubated aerobically for 24 hr at 37° C and typical salmonella colonies were counted. Salmonella count was calculated by the following formula: CFU/g = (Number of colonies × dilution factor) / volume cultured. Randomly selected colonies from positive countable plates were serologically confirmed to be the SE inoculum strain to validate the accuracy of visual counts. Samples below detection limit of counting method were subject to culture to determine the presence or absence of SE. Negative samples were assigned zero Salmonella count. Ovaries were weighed and added to TTH broth containing 25 µg nalidixic acid/ml at a ratio of 1:10 weight to volume. Ovarian tissues were macerated with TTH using a stomacher and incubated at 37° C for 48 hr followed by streaking on XLT-4 agar plates containing 25 µg nalidixic acid/ml. Suspected colonies were further tested in TSI and Lysine Iron LI agar slants followed by serogrouping using appropriate O and H Salmonella antisera.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software LLC, San Diego, CA, USA). Normality of log₁₀-transformed Salmonella Enteritidis counts was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because several datasets did not satisfy assumptions of normality, treatment comparisons at each sampling day were conducted using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. Prevalence data were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Salmonella enumeration in cecal contents

Cecal Salmonella Enteritidis counts are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between treatments at 6, 14, or 17 dpi (P>0.05). However, at 10 dpi, pullets receiving the probiotic-supplemented diet exhibited significantly lower SE counts compared to the control group (4.22 vs. 5.44 log₁₀ CFU/g; P = 0.0154), representing a reduction of 1.22 log₁₀ CFU/g. This reduction corresponded to the mid-phase of infection, when intestinal colonization levels remained elevated across both groups. No treatment effects were observed at other sampling times or for cecal and ovarian SE prevalence.

Discussion

In the present study, supplementation with a multi-strain Bacillus-based probiotic resulted in a significant reduction in cecal Salmonella Enteritidis counts at 10 days post-infection, while no differences were observed at the other sampling time points. The reduction observed at 10 dpi suggests that probiotic supplementation may have influenced intestinal colonization during the mid-phase of infection, when pathogen loads remained elevated in both treatment groups. Bacillus-based probiotics are believed to exert their effects through multiple complementary mechanisms, including competitive exclusion, production of antimicrobial compounds, modulation of the intestinal microbiota, and enhancement of epithelial barrier function, and stimulation of innate and adaptive immune responses (Fuller, 1989; Tellez et al., 2012; Halder et al., 2024; Khan and Chousalkar, 2020). These mechanisms may have contributed to the temporary reduction in pathogen burden observed in the present study. The observed 1.22 log₁₀ reduction is consistent with previous studies which have reported reductions in Salmonella colonization following supplementation with Bacillus-based probiotics. Although significance was observed at only one sampling point, reductions of this magnitude may still be biologically meaningful because decreases in intestinal pathogen burden during peak colonization have the potential to reduce environmental contamination and horizontal transmission within flocks. Price et al. (2020) reported significant reductions in cecal Salmonella populations in laying hens receiving Bacillus-based direct-fed microbials following experimental challenge. Similar observations have been reported by Khan et al. (2020) and Sobotik et al. (2024), who demonstrated that supplementation with the same triple strain Bacillus-based probiotic significantly reduced cecal Salmonella Enteritidis counts in commercial layer pullets following experimental challenge. Collectively, these findings support the role of Bacillus-based probiotics as components of integrate pre-harvest Salmonella control strategies. The lack of effect at earlier (6 dpi) and later time points (14 and 17 dpi) suggests that the probiotic’s impact may be time-dependent and influenced by pathogen dynamics following challenge. Despite reductions in cecal SE counts, no differences were observed in ovarian colonization. This finding suggests that while the probiotic may influence intestinal colonization, it does not significantly affect systemic invasion under the conditions of this study. The low and inconsistent recovery of Salmonella Enteritidis from ovarian tissue observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that colonization of the reproductive tract is highly variable and influenced by bacterial virulence, host immune responses, inoculation route, and experimental challenge conditions (Gast, 1994; Gantois et al., 2009; Shaji et al., 2023).

The transient nature of the probiotic effect observed in this study suggests that its efficacy may be most pronounced during specific stages of pathogen colonization rather than throughout the entire infection period. Nevertheless, reductions in intestinal Salmonella load during peak colonization may still be relevant and could potentially reduce environmental shedding and transmission risk within flocks. Detection rates in ovarian tissue were generally low and sporadic across all sampling points, which may have limited the ability to detect treatment effects. Because ovarian invasion represents a later stage of systemic dissemination, reductions in intestinal colonization do not necessarily translate directly into measurable differences in ovarian colonization. Future studies should evaluate probiotic efficacy under commercial production conditions using pen-level replication, multiple inclusion rates, and longer observation periods extending through the onset of lay and peak egg production. Additional work should also investigate the effects of probiotic supplementation on environmental shedding, egg contamination, and host immune responses to better define the mechanisms underlying reductions in Salmonella Enteritidis colonization. Such studies will help determine how probiotic supplementation can be most effectively integrated into comprehensive pre-harvest food safety programs (Shaji et al., 2023).

Limitations

This study was conducted under controlled experimental conditions using a single probiotic inclusion level and a single challenge strain. Additionally, treatments were applied at the cage level without replication of experimental units, which should be considered when interpreting statistical outcomes. Future studies incorporating pen-level replication, multiple inclusion rates, and evaluation under commercial field conditions are warranted to further validate these findings. Although the reduction was observed at a single time point, decreases in intestinal Salmonella load during peak colonization may contribute to reduced environmental contamination and horizontal transmission within flocks. These limitations should be considered when extrapolating results to commercial production systems.

Conclusion

Supplementation with a triple-strain Bacillus-based probiotic reduced cecal Salmonella Enteritidis counts at 10 days post-infection but did not affect pathogen prevalence in cecal or ovarian tissues. These findings suggest a transient effect on intestinal colonization under the conditions of this study, with limited evidence for an effect on systemic dissemination. Because treatments were applied at the cage level without replication of experimental units and treatment effects were observed at only a single sampling point, additional studies are warranted to confirm these findings and evaluate their applicability under commercial production conditions
