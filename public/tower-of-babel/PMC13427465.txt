Impact of secretome containing extracellular vesicles from human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes
Authors: Yuki Saito, Kazuaki Takemura, Junya Aoyama, Sawako Hibino, Kouichi Hasegawa, Yoshiki Sawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCell transplantation therapy using human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) has shown promise for ischemic heart disease. Although paracrine effects mediated by the secretome are recognized as key mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of hiPSC-CMs, dynamic changes in the secretome profile during the manufacturing of hiPSC-CMs remain poorly understood. This study was aimed at elucidating the secretome dynamics of clinical-grade hiPSC-CMs to identify their “mode of action” (MoA) and “candidate quality attributes.”MethodsWe integrated multiomics profiling, including total RNA-sequencing, proteome analysis, and extracellular vesicle (EV)-derived microRNA-sequencing, with functional assays (cell proliferation, cell migration, and endothelial tube formation) to analyze samples obtained at different time points in the hiPSC-CM manufacturing process (Day 4–25).ResultsHigh-purity hiPSC-CMs (>95% cardiac troponin T-positivity) exhibited distinct transcriptomic maturation between Day 16 and Day 25, characterized by the upregulation of cardiac marker genes. Proteomic clustering revealed four distinct stages, with functional transition from proliferation-centric signaling to tissue-repair signaling. Notably, the purified Day 25 secretome showed a qualitative shift toward a platelet-derived growth factor- and stromal cell-derived factor 1-rich profile. Simultaneously, EVs from the Day 25 secretome were enriched in “myomiRs” (miR-133b, −208b, −499b) and multiple anti-proliferative miRNAs (let-7e-5p, miR-145-5p). Functionally, the Day 25 secretome significantly enhanced mesenchymal stem/stromal cell (MSC) migration and promoted the formation of mature, highly branched endothelial tubes compared with the secretome from earlier stages.ConclusionsThe hiPSC-CM secretome containing EVs undergoes programmed evolution during manufacturing, with functional transition from promotion of undifferentiated growth to complex tissue repair through stable angiogenesis and MSC recruitment. These findings establish a molecular foundation for the MoA of hiPSC-CM therapy and provide critical candidate quality attributes to ensure the potency and consistency of clinical-grade cardiac products.

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease remains a leading cause of mortality globally and is characterized by irreversible loss of cardiomyocytes (CM) and subsequent maladaptive ventricular remodeling [1]. To address this intractable pathology, cell-based regenerative therapies, particularly those using human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs), have emerged as a transformative frontier for restoring cardiac function [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. While early strategies focused primarily on direct cell replacement, the low long-term engraftment rates and risks of immunogenicity and arrhythmias have shifted the paradigm toward the paracrine hypothesis [[6], [7], [8]].

Recent evidence suggests that the therapeutic efficacy of hiPSC-CMs is largely mediated by their secretome—a complex cocktail of bioactive factors and extracellular vesicles (EVs) that orchestrates angiogenesis, suppresses fibrosis, and modulates inflammation in the host myocardium [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. The composition of this secretome is not static; it evolves dynamically during the differentiation and maturation of the cells [[13], [14], [15]]. This implies that the “paracrine potency” of hiPSC-CMs may be highly stage-specific; however, a high-resolution, multiomics map of these shifts in the secretome during the manufacturing of clinical-grade hiPSC-CMs is yet to be established.

Our group has established a robust process for manufacturing clinical-grade hiPSC-CMs, and our recent clinical trials have demonstrated their significant therapeutic potential in patients with heart failure [12,16]. However, to further optimize therapeutic outcomes and ensure product consistency, it is imperative to define the molecular “mode of action” (MoA) and identify the “candidate quality attributes” related to the secretome.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive longitudinal secretome analysis integrating total RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq), data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based proteomics, and extracellular vesicle-microRNA-sequencing (EV-miRNA-seq) with functional bioassays at several time points in the hiPSC-CM production timeline. By correlating cellular maturation with secretome potency, we aimed to identify a foundational molecular framework for next-generation cell-based regenerative medicine.

Materials and methods

Clinical-grade hiPSCs

The clinical-grade hiPSC line, QHJI14s04, was derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of a healthy donor homozygous for the most prevalent HLA haplotype in Japan [17]. The cell line generation was achieved using episomal plasmid vectors, and its subsequent maintenance was performed under feeder-free and xeno-free conditions at the CiRA Cell Processing Center (Kyoto University) [18,19]. Comprehensive quality control (QC), including sterility testing and identity verification, was conducted, and the master cell bank used in this study was established as described previously [12]. All cell manufacturing and banking operations were conducted in strict compliance with the Good Gene, Cellular, and Tissue-based Products Manufacturing Practices (GCTP/GMP) guidelines to ensure clinical applicability.

Differentiation and purification of CMs from hiPSCs

Cardiac differentiation was achieved following the procedure described previously [20]. Briefly, hiPSCs were seeded in microwell plates to form embryoid bodies, with the seeding day designated as Day 0. Differentiation into the mesoderm was induced for 4 days, and cardiac specification was initiated in spinner flasks on Day 4. Differentiation into CMs was induced from Day 10 to Day 16. Finally, from Day 16 to Day 22, the cells were cultured in a purification medium and further allowed to mature until Day 25 (Fig. 1A). This CM purification process effectively eliminated residual undifferentiated hiPSCs to mitigate tumorigenic risks. Conditioned medium was collected during medium changes, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, WA, USA) and used for subsequent analyses. Cell counts were measured using NucleoCounter NC-200 (ChemoMetec, Denmark).

Flow cytometry

hiPSC-CMs were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA). The cells were subsequently stained with Alexa Fluor 647 mouse anti-cardiac troponin T (cTnT) (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA). Alexa Fluor 647 Mouse IgG1, κ antibody (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was used as the isotype control. The cells were analyzed using a MACSQuant Analyzer 10 Flow Cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Data were analyzed using the FlowJo software (Tree Star, OR, USA).

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Library preparation was performed using the TruSeq stranded mRNA Library Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) in the paired-end mode (2x 101 nt). The sequenced reads were mapped to the human reference genome sequence (hg38) using HISAT2, ver. 2.2.0 [21]. The read counts were calculated using featureCounts, ver. 1.6.3 [22] and normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) using the Subio platform, ver.1.22 (Subio Inc., Kagoshima, Japan). Raw and processed data were deposited in the gene expression omnibus database under accession number GSE319631. Functional annotation was performed using gene ontology (GO; http://www.geneontology.org) database. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using limma [23], followed by ontology analysis.

EV-derived miRNAs were extracted from conditioned medium collected at each differentiation stage using the exoEasy Maxi Kit for Exosome Isolation (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Library preparation was performed using the NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting libraries were size-selected via agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (QIAGEN). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (New England Biolabs) in the single-read mode. The sequenced reads were mapped to the human mature miRNA sequences registered in miRBase release 22.1 [24] using Bowtie2, ver.1.2.2 [25]. The number of reads mapped to each miRNA was quantified using the idxstats function in SAMtools, ver.1.9 [26] and normalized using the Subio platform. Raw and processed data were deposited in the gene expression omnibus database under accession number GSE319632.

EV analysis

Concentration and size distribution of EV particles were determined via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a NanoSight Pro system (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Samples were diluted with distilled water to achieve the optimal particle concentration range for analysis and were measured according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The expression of representative EV markers (CD9, CD63, and CD81) was evaluated using Human Exosome ELISA Kits (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Proteome analysis

Conditioned medium during cardiac differentiation was collected as described above. The samples were prepared as described in a previous study [27]. Briefly, the cells were lysed in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) containing 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate and 20 mM NaCl using a BIORUPTOR BR-II (SONIC BIO Co., Kanagawa, Japan). The extracted proteins were quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, WA, USA) at 500 ng/μL. The protein extracts (20 μg) were reduced with 20 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine for 10 min at 80 °C followed by alkylation with 30 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark. Proteins were purified and digested using the sample preparation (SP3) method. Tryptic digestion was performed using 500 ng/μL Trypsin/Lys-C Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) overnight at 37 °C. The digests were purified using GL-Tip SDB (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The peptides were redissolved in 2% acetonitrile (ACN) containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and quantified using the BCA assay at 150 ng/μL.

The digested peptides were loaded directly on a 75 μm × 12 cm nanoLC nano-capillary column (Nikkyo Technos Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 40 °C and then separated with a 30 min gradient (mobile phase A = 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, B = 0.1% FA in 80% ACN), with 8% B at 0 min and 70% B at 30 min, at a flow rate of 200 nL/min using an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The eluted peptides were detected using a quadrupole Orbitrap Exploris 480 hybrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a normal DIA window. The MS1 scan range was set as full scan with m/z 490–745 at mass resolution of 15,000 for setting an Auto Gain Control (AGC) target for MS1 s as 3 × 106 and a maximum injection time of 23 ms. MS2 ions were collected at m/z > 200 at 30,000-resolution for setting an AGC target of 3 × 106, maximum injection time set at “auto,” and fixed normalized collision energy of 28%. The isolation width for MS2 was set to 4 m/z, and for the 500–740 m/z window pattern, an optimized window arrangement was used in Scaffold DIA (Proteome Software, Inc., Portland, OR, USA).

Raw data were searched against an in silico predicted spectral library using DIA-NN [28] (ver. 1.8.1, https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN). First, an in silico predicted spectral library was generated from the human protein sequence database (UniProt id UP000005640, reviewed, canonical, 20381 entries) using DIA-NN. The DIA-NN search parameters were as follows: protease, trypsin; missed cleavages, 1; peptide length range, 7-45; precursor charge range, 2–4; precursor mass range, 495–745; fragment ion m/z range, 200–1800; mass accuracy, 10 ppm; static modification, cysteine carbamidomethylation; enabled “Heuristic protein interferences,” “Use isotopologues,” “MBR,” and “No shared spectra.” Additional commands were set as follows: “mass acc cal 10,” “peak translation,” and “matrix spec q.” The protein identification threshold was set at <1% for both peptide and protein false discovery rates (FDRs). Statistical calculations and Pearson correlation coefficient heatmap analysis with hierarchical clustering were performed using Perseus v1.6.15.0 [29]. Proteomic analysis datasets were deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the jPOST partner repository [30] with dataset identifiers PXD074366 for ProteomeXchange and JPST004392 for jPOST.

Cell proliferation assay

Human adipose-derived normal mesenchymal stromal cells (Kurabo Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan) (AD-MSCs) were cultured using StemLife Basal Medium (Kurabo Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and StemLife MSC LifeFactors (Kurabo Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment, with medium changes performed every 2–3 days. Upon reaching subconfluency, the cells were passaged using the Passage Reagent Set (Kurabo Industries, Ltd.), and cells from the 1st to the 3rd passage were used for the experiments.

For the assay, AD-MSCs were seeded at a density of 1000 cells/well in a 96-well plate with StemLife Basal Medium and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. The following day, the medium was replaced with hiPSC- and hiPSC-CM-conditioned media (derived from Day 16 and Day 25) that had been concentrated two-fold using an Amicon Ultra-15, 3 kDa ultrafiltration membrane (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Cell proliferation was assessed daily from Day 1 to Day 3 post-addition using collection plates and Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Cell proliferation was measured according to the manufacturer's protocol provided with the product.

Cell migration assay

This assay was performed as previously described [31]. Briefly, AD-MSCs were seeded at a density of 95,000 cells/well in a 24-well plate with StemLife Basal Medium and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. The day after seeding, the medium was replaced with StemLife Basal Medium containing mitomycin C at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL, and the cells were incubated under the same conditions for 2 h. After treatment, scratches were created using a P1000 pipette tip. After washing with D-PBS (−) (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), iPS cell and hiPSC-CM conditioned media (derived on Day 16 and 25 of differentiation) were added, after being concentrated two-fold using an Amicon Ultra-15, 3 kDa ultrafiltration membrane. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment and captured every hour using time-lapse imaging for a total of 48 h. Time-lapse imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope and the corresponding software (BZ-X800, KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). The cell migration rate was calculated as the ratio of the cell-migrated area to the initial scratch area.

Angiogenesis assay

The endothelial cell tube formation assay was performed as previously described [32], with minor modifications. Pre-thawed Matrigel (Corning, 354234, NY, USA) was added at 125 μL to a 48-well plate and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for at least 30 min. Subsequently, 125 μL of hiPSC-CM conditioned media (derived from Day 16 and Day 25 of differentiation) that had been concentrated two-fold using an Amicon Ultra-15, 3 kDa was added to each well. DMEM (Nacalai Tesque) and DMEM supplemented with VEGF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; final concentration 30 ng/mL) were used as the negative and positive controls, respectively. Furthermore, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Kurabo Industries, Ltd., KE-4109), prepared in DMEM at 400,000 cells/mL, were seeded at 125 μL onto the samples and Matrigel and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment.

The medium was removed, and 250 μL of staining solution comprising DMEM and Calcein AM (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) dye was added. The wells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the staining solution was removed, and 250 μL of DMEM was added. Fluorescence images were captured using BZ-X800 and analyzed using the ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/) to quantitatively evaluate the length of the formed tubes and the number of junctions [33].

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of mean (SE), as indicated in the figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was evaluated using the Student's t-test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for comparisons among three or more groups. When the homogeneity of variances was confirmed, the Tukey–Kramer's post-hoc test was applied; otherwise, the Games–Howell's post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R, ver.4.4.3 software and Microsoft Excel.

Results

Differentiation maturity and transcriptomic profiles of hiPSC-CMs

To establish a molecular baseline for understanding the cellular transitions during differentiation, we first evaluated the differentiation kinetics and transcriptomic maturation of hiPSC-CMs. The stepwise manufacturing of hiPSC-CMs from hiPSCs was performed in four stages: mesoderm induction (Day 0–4), cardiac specification (Day 4–10), CM differentiation (Day 10–16), and CM purification and maturation (Day 16–25) (Fig. 1a). Cardiac differentiation was evaluated and confirmed at the end of each stage (Day 10, 16, and 25) via flow cytometry of the cTnT-positive cell population. The positivity rate increased as the differentiation stage progressed, reaching extremely high purity, exceeding 95%, by Day 25 after CM purification (Fig. 1b). Although the total cell count did not increase across the differentiation stages, the number of cTnT-positive cells plateaued after CM differentiation. This confirmed that the removal process during CM purification eliminated nearly all the cTnT-negative cells (Fig. 1c).

Having confirmed high CM purity, we hypothesized that the purification and post-purification phases would induce a profound transcriptomic maturation required for advanced cardiac function. RNA-seq was performed on hiPSCs and hiPSC-CMs (Day 16 and 25) to characterize the cellular properties before and after CM purification and maturation. Principal component analysis showed that the samples were separated according to the differentiation stage, suggesting reproducible expression profiles (Fig. 1d). Hierarchical clustering of the top 2000 genes revealed a clear separation (Fig. 1e). Compared to the Day 16 samples, the Day 25 samples showed statistically significant increases in the expression of TNNT2, CACNA1C, KCNH2, KCNJ2, and SCN5A, and an increasing trend was also observed for HCN4 (Fig. 1f). DEGs between the Day 16 and Day 25 samples were enriched in GO terms related to muscle contraction and the cardiovascular system (Supplementary Fig. 1a). These GO terms were associated with cardiac function and formed an interconnected network (Supplementary Fig. 1b). These findings demonstrated that intracellular transcriptomic machinery shifts toward functional maturation from Day 16 to Day 25, prompting us to investigate whether this cellular maturation is reflected in the intercellular signaling components, specifically EV-derived miRNAs.

Functional profiling of EV-derived miRNAs at each differentiation stage

Based on the transcriptomic maturation observed within the cells, we hypothesized that the components secreted into the extracellular space, particularly inside signaling vehicles such as EVs, would exhibit a corresponding stage-specific functional shift. miRNAs in the EVs present in the conditioned media of hiPSCs and hiPSC-CMs (Day 16 and 25) were identified using miRNA-seq analysis. A total of 267 miRNAs were specifically detected in the hiPSCs, 2 in hiPSC-CM Day 16, 6 in hiPSC-CM Day16–25, and 17 in hiPSC-CM Day 25 (Fig. 2a). Investigation of miRNAs for biological functions in hiPSC-CMs on Day 16 and 25 revealed that miRNAs specifically detected in the hiPSC-CM Day25 sample included those associated with CM markers, CM maturation, and cardiac function. Furthermore, this suggested the presence of multiple miRNAs with cell proliferation-suppressing functions (Fig. 2b). To further characterize the EVs, particle concentration and size distribution were assessed (Supplementary Fig. 3a and 3b). EV concentration was significantly elevated in samples derived from hiPSC-CM Day 16 compared with those derived from hiPSCs, whereas size distribution remained largely comparable across groups. The identification of these maturation-associated and anti-proliferative miRNA signatures within the EVs suggested a broader, programmed evolution of the entire secretome. This led us to comprehensively map the temporal changes of total secreted proteins across the differentiation process.

Sequential changes in protein components of the secretome

To achieve a high-resolution, holistic understanding of the paracrine factor dynamics accompanying cellular differentiation, we analyzed the total protein components of the secretome throughout the culture timeline. Proteomic analysis was performed to analyze the proteins released during the manufacturing of hiPSC-CMs (Day 4–25). Several thousand secreted proteins were identified in the conditioned medium at each medium change-point (Day 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, and 25). The temporal protein expression profiles were clustered into four distinct manufacturing stages (Fig. 3a). Similarly, the analysis of growth factors and cytokines revealed profiles corresponding to these four stages (Fig. 3b). GO analysis was performed for each stage and the identified biological processes were classified and color-coded according to their functional roles in differentiation, cell proliferation, cell migration, angiogenesis, and cardiogenesis (Fig. 3c). To further investigate the changes in clinically relevant secretome components between the defined differentiation milestones, we performed a comparative proteomic analysis of Day 16 and Day 25 secretomes across multiple independent batches (Fig. 3d). We specifically focused on factors associated with angiogenesis and cell migration [5,56]. Quantitative comparison revealed that the expression of ANGPT-1 and VEGF-B remained stable between Day 16 and Day 25. In contrast, several key proangiogenic and chemotactic factors, including the PDGF family (PDGF-A, –C, and -D), SDF-1, and VEGF-C, were significantly upregulated in the Day 25 secretome compared with that in the Day 16 secretome. Conversely, VEGF-A levels declined as differentiation progressed toward Day 25. Overall, our multiomics discovery pipeline (Sections 1, 2, 3.3) pointed toward a clear functional paradigm shift: from an early pro-proliferative state to a late tissue-repair/chemotactic state. To robustly validate these molecular predictions, we next subjected the secretomes to direct cell-based physiological activity assays.

Cell migration and proliferation activity of secretome at each differentiation stage

To evaluate the functional translational significance of the secretome, we focused our biological assays on Day 16 and Day 25, which represent two critical milestones in our clinical manufacturing process—the immediate completion of cardiac differentiation and the final purified/matured product, respectively. Driven by the proteomic and EV-miRNA findings, which indicated a decline in proliferation factors and an increase in cell-migration cues at the late stage, we sought to functionally validate the biological effects of the secretome on AD-MSCs. In the cell proliferation assay, AD-MSCs treated with hiPSC and hiPSC-CM Day 16 conditioned media exhibited a progressive increase in cell number over time. In contrast, AD-MSCs treated with hiPSC-CM Day 25 conditioned media showed no increase in cell number over time. These differences were statistically significant at the 4-day post-seeding time point (Fig. 4a and b), confirming the anti-proliferative molecular signature of the mature secretome. Notably, conditioned medium derived from hiPSC-CM Day 16 significantly enhanced cell proliferation compared with basal medium at Day 4 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). In the cell migration assay, a statistically significant difference in the cell migration rate at 48 h was observed between the hiPSC and hiPSC-CM conditioned media-treated groups (Fig. 4c and d). In addition, comparison with basal medium at 48 h demonstrated that conditioned media derived from both hiPSC-CM Day 16 and Day 25 significantly promoted cell migration (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Notably, this difference was more pronounced in the hiPSC-CM Day 25 conditioned medium-treated group, functionally validating the chemotactic potency predicted by the high expression of SDF-1 and the PDGF family on Day 25. Given that these factors are also heavily implicated in vascular remodeling, our final objective was to verify the angiogenic potential of this mature secretome.

Angiogenic potential of the hiPSC-CM secretome

To determine whether the qualitative shift from VEGF-A toward a PDGF- and VEGF-C-rich profile at Day 25 alters the structural morphology of newly formed blood vessels, we evaluated the functional angiogenic potential of the secretome. To assess the angiogenic potential, an endothelial tube formation assay was performed using HUVECs treated with the secretome of hiPSC-CM (Day 16 and Day 25) for 24 h (Fig. 5a–d). Quantitative image analysis revealed that the total tube length significantly increased in the Day 16 secretome group compared with that in the DMEM control (Fig. 5e). In contrast, the number of junctions was significantly higher in the Day 25 secretome group (Fig. 5f). Discussion

The therapeutic efficacy and safety of hiPSC-CM transplantation depends on the robustness and reproducibility of the manufacturing process [12]. In this study, the stepwise differentiation and purification protocol yielded hiPSC-CMs with a high purity of over 95% cTnT-positive cells by Day 25 (Fig. 1b and c).

The transcriptomic shift observed between Day 16 and Day 25 highlights the maturation process that occurs during the purification and post-purification phases. While Day 16 represented the completion of initial CM differentiation, our results showed that subsequent culture until Day 25 significantly upregulated the expression of key genes essential for cardiac function. In particular, the increased expression of TNNT2 (structural assembly) and CACNA1C, KCNH2, KCNJ2, and SCN5A (electrophysiological properties) indicated progression toward a more mature CM phenotype (Fig. 1f) [57]. The enrichment of GO terms related to muscle contraction and cardiovascular system development (Supplementary Fig. 1a and b) further supports the notion that these cells transition from immature myocytes to a more functionally integrated state.

These results suggest that the purification process employed in this study effectively removes cell populations, including undifferentiated cells, that pose a potential risk for CM transplantation, while promoting cellular maturation and enabling the high-purity recovery of more functionally competent CMs. Establishing a clear correlation between cellular maturation and gene expression dynamics is a prerequisite for identifying candidate quality attributes in the manufacturing process, thereby supporting a quality-by-design (QbD) approach for the production of clinical-grade hiPSC-CMs [16].

The secretome, comprising a diverse array of cytokines, growth factors, and EVs, serves as a sophisticated medium for intercellular signaling. EVs play a pivotal role in mediating precise and sustained regulatory effects by shielding their molecular cargo—miRNAs and proteins—from enzymatic degradation and delivering them to recipient cells [58]. Our miRNA-seq analysis of EV-encapsulated miRNAs revealed a distinct stage-specific signature, with a notable shift in the miRNA profile as differentiation progressed from hiPSCs to mature hiPSC-CMs (Fig. 2a).

The identification of specific miRNAs, such as miR-133b, miR-208b, and miR-499b, in the Day 25 EV fraction was highly significant. These miRNAs are well-recognized as “myomiRs,” which are predominantly expressed in cardiac and skeletal muscle and are essential for maintaining CM identity, contractile function, and functional maturation [59]. Notably, let-7b-5p, miR-145-5p, and miR-125b-5p detected in the Day 25 EV fraction exert paracrine effects, including the promotion of angiogenesis and protection of CMs [46,49,50]. Taken together, these findings suggest that CMs subjected to the purification process may acquire enhanced paracrine capacity after transplantation, thereby potentially contributing to myocardial regeneration. Furthermore, the presence of multiple miRNAs associated with the suppression of cell proliferation in the Day 25 EVs (Fig. 2b) provides a molecular basis for the functional transition observed in our secretome assays. During the maturation of hiPSC-CMs, CMs typically exit the cell cycle to prioritize structural and functional integration. The selective loading of proliferation-inhibiting miRNAs, such as let-7e-5p [47,48], miR-542-3p [51], miR-584-5p [52], and miR-193a-5p [53,54], into EVs suggests a coordinated mechanism for modulating the local tissue microenvironment.

In the context of cardiac repair, EV-mediated delivery of antiproliferative and promaturation signals may be essential for limiting fibroblast overgrowth or stabilizing the myocardial structure during the healing process. Therefore, these EV-derived miRNAs represent promising candidate quality attributes for monitoring the functional potency of hiPSC-CM products, bridging the gap between the manufacturing stages and therapeutic MoA.

The comprehensive proteomic profiling across 16 time points provided a high-resolution map of secretome dynamics during hiPSC-CM manufacturing. The clustering of several thousand secreted proteins into four distinct stages demonstrated that the hiPSC-CM secretome undergoes programmed transitions that closely mirror intracellular differentiation and morphogenesis [13,14] (Fig. 3a and b). Notably, the functional transition from proliferation-centric signaling in the early stages to tissue-repair and cardiogenesis signaling in stages 3 and 4 aligns with recent proteomic studies, suggesting that the cardiac microenvironment is dynamically regulated by stage-specific protein secretion [13,15]. This evolution suggests that the biological role of secretome matures from supporting initial “cell production” toward fostering “functional tissue integration.”

A key finding of this study was the qualitative enrichment of proangiogenic and chemotactic factors in the late-stage secretome (Day 25). Between Day 16 and Day 25, we observed a significant and reproducible upregulation of the PDGF family (PDGF-A, –C, and -D), VEGF-C, and SDF-1 (Fig. 3d). Although the total VEGF-A levels appeared to decline toward Day 25, this likely reflected the transition from the differentiation medium (containing exogenous VEGF-A) to protein-free purification and maturation media. However, the simultaneous and robust increase in other endogenous factors, particularly the PDGF family and SDF-1, highlights the intrinsic maturation of hiPSC-CMs and their enhanced capacity for endogenous paracrine signaling [9].

The shift toward a PDGF- and SDF-1-rich profile is clinically significant for myocardial regeneration. Although VEGF-A is a potent inducer of initial endothelial cell sprouting, excessive or isolated VEGF signaling often results in the formation of immature, leaky vessels [56]. In contrast, PDGF signaling is essential for the recruitment of pericytes and smooth muscle cells, which are critical for the stabilization and maturation of nascent vessels into functional vascular networks. Furthermore, the increased expression of SDF-1, a well-established homing factor for CXCR4-positive MSCs and other regenerative progenitors, suggests that the Day 25 secretome is specialized for “orchestrating” complex, multicellular tissue repair rather than merely inducing simple endothelial proliferation [60,61].

These results indicate that purified, late-stage hiPSC-CMs (Day 25) possess a secretome profile that is endogenously optimized for stable angiogenesis and host-mediated repair. By providing a more sophisticated cocktail of maturation-related factors, Day 25 hiPSC-CMs serve as a superior cellular source for transplantation therapies aimed at long-term functional restoration of the ischemic heart.

The physiological activity assays of conditioned media directly validated the functional implications of the stage-specific molecular shifts identified in our multiomics analysis. MSCs are recruited to sites of cardiac injury and proliferate in response to tissue damage, thereby reducing the progression of heart failure in animal models. Accordingly, MSCs were used for further investigation in this study [62]. In the cell proliferation assay, secretomes from hiPSCs and Day 16 hiPSC-CM significantly promoted AD-MSC expansion, whereas the Day 25 secretome did not exhibit such effects (Fig. 4a and b). This observation is highly consistent with the proteomic and EV-miRNA analyses, which revealed an abundance of pro-proliferative factors in the early to mid-differentiation stages and subsequent upregulation of cell cycle-suppressing miRNAs, such as let-7e-5p, miR-542-3p, and miR-584-5p, in the late maturation stage [47,51,52]. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that specific miRNAs, including miR-214-3p, modulate the proliferative capacity of mesenchymal and progenitor cells [34,35]. This transition suggests that, as hiPSC-CMs reach high purity and functional maturity, their paracrine role shifts from supporting general cellular expansion to fostering a microenvironment conducive to tissue stabilization and homeostatic maintenance.

In contrast to the proliferation results, the cell migration assay, performed using the in vitro scratch assay [31], revealed that the promigratory effect on AD-MSCs was more pronounced in the hiPSC-CM groups, particularly in the Day 25 secretome (Fig. 4c and d). Enhanced recruitment of MSCs is a crucial component of the endogenous repair mechanism in ischemic heart disease, because MSCs provide immunomodulatory support and essential angiogenic stimuli at the site of injury [9]. The synergistic effect of these proteins, coupled with migration-related miRNAs identified in the Day 25 EVs, likely accounts for the observed robust chemotactic response.

The endothelial tube formation assay, a well-established in vitro model for angiogenesis [32], provided functional confirmation of the proangiogenic potential identified in our multiomics profiling. Quantitative morphometric analysis using the Angiogenesis Analyzer [33] revealed that the Day 16 and Day 25 secretomes exhibited distinct patterns of angiogenic induction: the Day 16 secretome primarily enhanced the total tube length, whereas the Day 25 secretome significantly increased the number of junctions (Fig. 5e and f). These findings suggest that the influence of the hiPSC-CM secretome on vascular network formation evolves from supporting basic endothelial elongation to promoting increased structural complexity and branching as differentiation progresses.

This divergence in angiogenic profiles can be explained by the shifting composition of the secreted growth factors. In the earlier stage (Day 16), the presence of high levels of VEGF-A, both endogenous and potentially carried over from the differentiation medium, likely drove the rapid elongation of endothelial tubes. VEGF-A is a master regulator of endothelial cell proliferation and migration, which are the primary drivers of tube lengthening. In contrast, the increased number of junctions observed in the Day 25 group aligned with the significant upregulation of the PDGF family and VEGF-C identified in our proteomic analysis. The formation of junctions and a highly branched network is a hallmark of “mature” and functional angiogenesis [56].

Although the total tube length reflects the initial expansion of the vascular area, the number of junctions represents the connectivity and structural sophistication of the network. PDGF signaling, in particular, is critical for vascular remodeling and recruitment of mural cells, which stabilize these junctions and prevent vascular regression. Therefore, the Day 25 secretome can potentially facilitate the formation of robust and interconnected microvascular networks, although its precise behavior under comprehensive in vivo settings remains to be fully elucidated. These in vitro results suggest that purified, late-stage hiPSC-CMs act in a paracrine manner to trigger host angiogenesis and orchestrate the formation of a functionally mature vascular bed. This ability to promote a complex vascular architecture is essential for long-term survival and functional integration of transplanted myocardial tissue, ultimately contributing to the restoration of cardiac function in ischemic heart disease.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the manufacturing process of hiPSC-CM induces a “functional maturation” of the secretome. While early-stage cells promote undifferentiated growth, late-stage, purified hiPSC-CMs (Day 25) secrete a specialized cocktail of factors that are optimized for MSC recruitment and potentially stable angiogenesis. This functional divergence underscores the importance of differentiation stage selection in cell therapy; the Day 25 secretome, characterized by reduced pro-proliferative activity but enhanced chemotactic and maturation-inducing properties, offers a more targeted paracrine profile for myocardial regeneration, potentially maximizing the therapeutic efficacy while minimizing the risks associated with unregulated cellular proliferation.

It should be noted that while both the Day 16 and Day 25 secretomes significantly enhanced these cellular functions compared to the control, a direct statistically significant difference was not achieved between the two differentiation stages in the overall migration rate or total vascular network expansion (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). This marginal functional discrepancy can be attributed to the specific parameters of our clinical manufacturing protocol, wherein the Day 16 conditioned medium was harvested after a 48-h accumulation period, whereas the Day 25 CM was harvested after only a 24-h period. Consequently, the total protein concentration and factor accumulation time were halved in the Day 25 samples. The fact that the Day 25 secretome achieved equivalent or distinct morphological potency despite half the collection window strongly suggests a higher intrinsic secretion rate or enhanced specific activity of mature paracrine factors per unit of time. Future studies utilizing normalized protein concentrations will further refine the quantitative differences between these manufacturing stages.

Although these findings offer significant insights, this study had certain limitations. Our analyses were conducted primarily in vitro under controlled culture conditions. Consequently, the secretome dynamics may differ within the complex in vivo environment, where inflammatory milieus, hypoxia, and mechanical stress following transplantation could further modulate the secretory profile of hiPSC-CMs. In particular, these pathological stressors in the injured myocardium might dynamically stimulate transplanted cells, potentially altering or even augmenting the secretion of pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatory factors. Exploring how the ischemic microenvironment reshapes the secretome is critical to fully understand its translational efficacy. Future studies using ischemic heart disease models are required to validate the stability and paracrine efficacy of these stage-specific secretomes in living systems.

Although the precise biological reasons for CMs evolving to secrete this specific cocktail of factors require further investigation, it is highly probable that these secretome components are indispensable for the transition from progenitor stages to functional cardiac tissue integration. Therefore, within the context of myocardial regeneration, these dynamics represent a key MoA for hiPSC-CM transplantation therapy, which is currently under clinical development [11,16]. We anticipate that these paracrine mechanisms would work synergistically with direct cell replacement to repair the damaged myocardium, promote stable vascularization, and recruit endogenous repair cells, ultimately leading to the improvement of clinical symptoms and cardiac function in patients with ischemic heart disease [9,12].

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive map of secretome dynamics during the production of clinical-grade hiPSC-CMs. By integrating multiomics profiling with functional assays, we demonstrate that with purification and maturation, the paracrine profile of hiPSC-CMs shifts from supporting undifferentiated cell proliferation to orchestrating complex tissue repair through enhanced cell migration and mature angiogenesis. These findings establish a molecular and functional foundation for secretome-based MoA and reveal candidate quality attributes essential for the production of potent and consistent hiPSC-CM products for use in regenerative medicine.
