Parenting behaviours and autistic children’s internalizing and externalizing symptoms: a protocol of a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Krysta Andrews, Nawal Masood, Arooba Mansoor, Julie Gross, Irene Drmic, Teresa Bennett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAutistic children can experience elevated levels of emotional and behavioural problems, which adversely impact their psychosocial functioning. Positive parenting behaviours can significantly shape children’s emotional and behavioural developmental trajectories. However, for autistic children, these parenting behaviours may vary depending on their social, sensory and intellectual profiles. Literature on the association between parenting behaviours and autistic children’s internalising and externalising symptoms has been mixed. Thus, the main objective of this study will be to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies evaluating the effect of parenting behaviours on autistic children’s internalising and externalising symptoms.Methods and analysisIncluded studies have child samples between ages 6 and 17 years and a primary diagnosis of autism/Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), pervasive developmental disorder and/or Asperger’s syndrome as well as a measure of parenting behaviour. Primary outcomes are internalising and externalising symptoms. Substantive and methodological moderators will be examined. Searches will be conducted in MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Embase, Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global from their inception onwards, and we will also conduct backward/forward searching of eligible studies. Published and unpublished studies will be examined. Two independent reviewers will conduct title/abstract and full-text screening as well as data extraction. Risk of bias will be assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale and JBI Critical Appraisal tools. A random-effects meta-analysis model will be used, and summary effect sizes will be calculated. Moderation will be examined via Q-statistics and meta-regression to determine factors contributing to between-study variation in effect sizes.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required, as this review will use secondary data from published studies. Results will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations. The protocol is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024528043).Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42024528043.

Background

Child emotional and behavioural problems (eg, aggression, non-compliance and anxiety) are strong predictors of persistent functional difficulties into adulthood, as well as poorer academic outcomes and parent and family stress.13 Autistic children can experience elevated levels of emotional and behavioural problems (up to 70%),47 in addition to core impairment in sensory sensitivity, restrictive and repetitive behaviours, and social and communication challenges. About 30% of autistic people also experience co-occurring intellectual disabilities.8 9 Thus, identifying processes to reduce emotional and behavioural problems and their effects on autistic children and their families is essential.

Daily parent–child interactions are significant proximal influences shaping children’s developing emotional and behavioural functioning. Indeed, parent training programmes have been effective in reducing emotional and behavioural challenges in autistic children, with medium effect sizes reported.10 11 Specifically, positive parenting behaviours (eg, sensitivity, cognitive stimulation and behavioural support) have been extensively examined and linked to healthy cognitive12 and socioemotional13 outcomes in non-autistic children. However, for certain profiles of autistic children, social and communication difficulties could appear to hinder their ability to engage in meaningful interactions with their parents in patterns commonly expected from non-autistic children at different developmental stages.14 For example, by engaging in early joint attention, shared positive affect and play or, at older ages, in reciprocal conversation. Additionally, parents may need to adapt their own behaviours to tailor to the social, sensory and intellectual needs of their children, such as engaging in accommodation (eg, adapting situations the child finds difficult, adjusting expectations depending on the child’s mood), altering the environment, vigilance, reducing uncertainty (eg, planning for issues that could arise on outings), creating structure and familiarity and stimulating development.1518

Ku et al conducted a meta-analysis exploring parenting behaviours with neurodivergent children and non-autistic children. Notably, the parenting behaviours were based on the schemas derived from literature focusing on non-autistic children, which require consideration when interpreting the study results. The authors found that the use of “warm and supportive behaviours” (eg, positive affect and sensitivity) was comparable between groups14; however, directive or reactive behaviours (eg, aversion) occurred more with neurodivergent children.14 19 Therefore, identifying the parenting behaviours that reduce internalising and externalising symptoms in autistic children could play an important role in nurturing positive parent–child relationships and supporting the healthy development of emotional and behavioural functioning.

A growing number of studies have examined the association between parenting behaviours and internalising and externalising symptoms in autistic children. However, findings have been mixed. For example, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that of 11 studies examined, 9 did not find a significant link between positive parenting behaviours (eg, warmth/supportive parenting) and externalising behaviours in autistic children and 2 studies found significant effects such that positive parenting behaviours predicted greater externalising behaviours with small to moderate effects.20 Similarly, for reactive parenting behaviours (eg, psychological control, criticism and harsh punishment), the range of effects varied widely (ie, r=−0.6 to 0.63); with the majority of studies (k=14) finding that reactive parenting behaviours are associated with higher externalising behaviours and a smaller number of studies (k=2) linking them to fewer externalising behaviours.

This review provided insights into the scope of parenting practices explored in the literature and their association with autistic children’s externalising behaviours. However, there were notable gaps revealed by the review that we aim to address through the current systematic review and meta-analysis. First, internalising symptoms were not explored in the previous review; however, this is important to consider because parenting behaviours can differentially influence the development of internalising and externalising symptoms.21 For example, in a study with caregivers of 6–12-year-old children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), negative, controlling parenting behaviours uniquely predicted higher externalising behaviours, whereas ASD-adapted parenting behaviours (eg, stimulating development and adapting environment) predicted higher internalising behaviours.22 Altogether, the findings demonstrate the need to explore both internalising and externalising symptoms given the unique pathways through which parenting behaviours affect these outcomes and the insights it provides that can inform the development of more targeted and effective parenting programmes for different presentations of emotional and behavioural challenges. Thus, we will explore the association between parenting behaviours and externalising and internalising symptoms.

Second, given the inconsistencies in the effects identified by Suvarna et al20 and the broader literature, the current review aims to obtain precise estimates of the effect of positive parenting behaviours on children’s internalising and externalising symptoms to better understand the strength and direction of effects. Third, Suvarna et al20 highlighted several psychosocial factors that could potentially moderate the effect of parenting behaviours on autistic children’s externalising symptoms; however, formal statistical evaluation was not conducted. Further, given that families with neurodivergent children are more likely to experience psychosocial adversity,23 synthesising the literature and identifying plausible factors influencing the association between parenting behaviours and autistic children’s internalising and externalising symptoms (eg, parent psychopathology, socioeconomic factors, child age/sex and measurement approach) can provide more information on which parenting behaviours are effective and for whom. Thus, the current review aims to conduct formal statistical analysis to examine whether substantive moderators (eg, child age and sex, family sociodemographic, caregiver psychopathology) and methodological moderators (eg, study design, measurement approach) related to children’s internalising and externalising symptoms predict between-study variation in effect sizes.

Finally, the current review will also identify gaps within the parenting, autism and internalising and externalising symptoms literature, including whether studies examined use measures that have been validated in autistic samples.

Methods

This protocol is developed to adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P; see checklist in the online supplemental additional file 1)24 and the PRISMA 2020 statement.25 Protocol amendments will be tracked (including dates and rationales) and made available at the point of publication. The review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024528043).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were outlined using the PI/ECO (population, intervention/exposure, comparator, outcome) framework recommended by the Cochrane Handbook.26 The population of interest includes children aged 6–17 years. The child sample must have a primary diagnosis of autism/ASD, pervasive developmental disorder (PDD) or Asperger’s Syndrome according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders - Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) or DSM-5-Text Revision82729 that is determined with a clinical assessment, research assessment or documentation of a historical diagnosis. Children with primary diagnoses of a condition other than ASD will be excluded (eg, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and brain injury).

Studies will have a measure of parenting behaviours drawn from theoretical frameworks of attachment,30 behaviour management and social learning.31 Specifically, parenting behaviours could include positive parenting such as affective-emotional responsiveness (eg, warmth, contingent responding and acceptance), cognitive responsiveness (eg, rich verbal input and scaffolding), positive behavioural guidance (eg, positive discipline and reinforcement/praise) and/or emotion socialisation (eg, emotion coaching and validation). Additionally, reactive parenting (eg, hostility and intrusiveness) will be explored. Parenting behaviours tailored to the specific profiles of autistic children (eg, accommodation, managing uncertainty and vigilance)1518 will also be included, as these ASD-adapted parenting behaviours reflect a distinct and theoretically meaningful category within this review, demonstrating unique considerations of parenting autistic children and the adaptations parents may make to support their child’s social, sensory and intellectual needs. The primary outcomes include internalising (eg, anxiety and social withdrawal) and externalising (eg, aggression and property damage) symptoms.

Only studies available in the English language will be included. Additionally, both published and unpublished papers (ie, dissertations/theses) will be considered.

Information source and search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy for published articles and dissertations/theses was developed by the first author in consultation with a librarian and the research team. Five databases, including MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Embase, Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global, were searched from inception to May 2024 using key terms related to parenting behaviours, autistic children and child internalising/externalising behaviours. An updated search was conducted in December 2025. The search strategy was initially created in PsycINFO and MEDLINE and adapted to the other databases. Key terms are detailed in the online supplemental additional file 2. At the time of submission of this protocol, database searches are complete, and screening, including backward and forward chaining of eligible studies to identify any additional articles that meet the inclusion criteria, is ongoing. Data extraction and analyses are planned and have not yet been conducted.

Study records

Data management

Study records will be managed in Covidence, a web-based platform for systematic reviews. Relevant citations will be exported in RIS format and imported to Covidence, which supports the entire review process—from title/abstract screening to full-text assessment, data extraction and evaluations of bias. Covidence provides duplicate removal, an audit trail of actions, secure data storage and customizable data extraction templates, enabling consistent analysis across studies.

Selection process

To ensure consistency and accuracy in the study selection process, standardised protocols will be established at each stage, with calibration exercises (ie, pilot testing) conducted during title/abstract screening and full-text assessment. These exercises will involve all reviewers screening a predefined subset of records, applying inclusion and exclusion criteria uniformly. Percent agreement and kappa statistics will be calculated to assess interrater reliability, targeting a minimum of 80% agreement and a kappa of 0.6 or higher. If needed, the protocols will be refined based on these metrics.

Once the desired agreement is achieved, the formal screening will commence. Two independent reviewers will screen each citation, resolving discrepancies with a third reviewer before proceeding to full-text review. During the full-text assessment, two reviewers will again independently evaluate eligibility for each text, with a third reviewer resolving any conflicts. If full-text articles are inaccessible, the review team will contact study authors for access and any supplementary data needed for effect size extraction.

To maintain independence in effect sizes for the meta-analysis, a structured approach will identify and manage overlapping samples. All included studies will be reviewed for potential overlap, focusing on those with shared authorship or large-scale cohorts. In cases of overlapping datasets, priority will be given to the study most aligned with our research objectives, favouring larger samples and robust measurement tools.

Data collection process

Covidence provides customizable data extraction templates tailored to capture specific variables of interest for this review. To ensure accuracy and reduce bias, two reviewers will independently complete the data extraction process. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes include externalising symptoms (eg, aggression and non-compliance) and internalising symptoms (eg, somatic complaints and anxiety). We will also extract information on the measurement approach for each outcome, including self- and/or caregiver reports, direct assessment, behavioural observation ratings and clinical interviews.

The following steps will be taken to address multiple data points on outcomes within studies: (i) studies will be included if they measure one or both of internalising and externalising symptoms; (ii) if studies assess more than one relevant outcome within internalising or externalising symptoms (eg, two measures of internalising symptoms), all eligible effect sizes will be retained and robust variance estimation32 will be used to account for the dependence among effect sizes within studies33 34; (iii) for studies that include more than one relevant outcome using the same measurement instrument, we will examine measurement specificity (broad composite vs domain subscale) as a methodological moderator to determine whether the breadth of the outcome measure influences effect sizes and (iv) if multiple internalising and externalising outcomes were reported in a single study, they will be combined into a single variable (one composite for each of internalising and externalising), such that each sample will be represented once per meta-analysis.35

Risk of bias in individual studies

The risk of bias for included studies will be independently assessed by two reviewers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale for cohort studies and JBI Critical Appraisal Tools for Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs). Disagreements regarding the risk of bias judgments will be reviewed and resolved by a senior author.

Data synthesis

Qualitative synthesis

To facilitate an organised presentation of data, we will create summary tables that compile key characteristics of each study. Relevant information will include sample (eg, child age/sex, caregiver psychopathology and household sociodemographic); study characteristics (eg, design, sample sizes); parenting behaviour (eg, type of behaviour) and outcomes assessed (eg, internalising and externalising symptoms) as well as their measurement approach and informants. Furthermore, the summary tables will present the results of risk of bias assessments for each study, categorising them as low, moderate or high risk based on established criteria. Finally, we will use narratives as well as descriptive statistics (eg, means, frequencies) to describe heterogeneity across studies.

Quantitative analyses

Previous reviews informed the eligibility criteria10 11 14 20 and are designed to be sufficiently restrictive to justify a meta-analysis. The meta-analysis will include studies that provide suitable quantitative data that will be included in the meta-analysis (see details below). For studies where the data are not sufficient to compute effect sizes, study authors will be contacted to obtain requested data.

Comprehensive Meta-Analysis V.4 software36 will be used to calculate and analyse effect size data. To determine the overall effect of positive parenting behaviours on autistic children’s internalising and externalising symptoms, we will calculate correlations (r) for each study. Missing data will be requested from study authors where necessary. If data are not available, sensitivity analyses will be conducted using a conservative effect size of 0.0.

Publication bias will be assessed with funnel plots of the effect sizes against their SE and Duval and Tweedie’s37 “trim and fill” method.

Moderation analyses

To ensure viable estimates of between-sample variance, analyses will only be conducted for moderators with six or more observations per cell.35 Heterogeneity of the effect sizes will be assessed via Cochrane’s Q and I2 statistics. The Q statistic provides a ratio of observed variation to within-study variation of the effect sizes. The I2 statistic is an estimate of the proportion of variance that is attributable to real population differences.35 38

Categorical and continuous moderators will be assessed via Q-statistics and meta-regression, respectively.39 40 Substantive moderators will include child/caregiver age and sex, sociodemographic factors and caregiver psychopathology. Methodological moderators such as study design and measurement approach will also be examined (see table 1).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research protocol. As this study describes a protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis that will use secondary data from published studies, patient and public involvement is not applicable.

Ethics and dissemination

This protocol describes a systematic review and meta-analysis; therefore, ethics approval is not required. The protocol has been registered with PROSPERO (Registration Number CRD42024528043). Findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentations at relevant scientific meetings. Any protocol amendments will be reported in the final manuscript.

Discussion

Emotional and behavioural problems have pervasive effects on the well-being of autistic children and their families. The proposed review will provide a comprehensive synthesis of the literature on parenting behaviours that predict autistic children’s internalising and externalising symptoms and highlight implications for research and clinical practice with children and families. From a research perspective, this study will make significant, novel empirical and theoretical contributions to the parenting, autism and internalising and externalising symptoms literature. The review by Suvarna et al.20 included important parenting behaviours traditionally measured within the literature addressing general or at-risk mental health samples of children. There may be autism-specific parenting behaviours; however, that are important to understand and include in this canon. Additionally, it will provide further clarity on the specific parenting behaviours that are effective in reducing emotional and behavioural problems in autistic children. Specifically, by including studies measuring internalising symptoms in autistic children, a body of literature that has been less extensively explored compared with externalising symptoms. Further, it will foster our understanding of the gaps in the literature that may contribute to the variation in findings between studies, including the sociodemographics of recruited samples and the operationalisation and measurement of variables such as the frequency with which validated Autism/ASD-specific tools are used and thus the generalisability of extant findings. Relatedly, the severity and presentation of externalising and internalising behaviours may differ across certain profiles of autistic children, further warranting the need to explore whether the measures currently in use have the sensitivity to accurately capture this information. As such, the current review will also explore whether included studies use measures that have been validated in autistic samples.

From a clinical practice perspective, understanding the nuances of parent–child interactions can identify targets for clinical programmes to provide the necessary tools and resources to improve parent-child relationships in the autism population. In addition, examining the factors, such as parent psychopathology, that can modulate the impact of parenting behaviours on children’s internalising and externalising symptoms can provide insight into the key ingredients of multi-faceted approaches that parenting programmes may need to follow to provide comprehensive support for parent–child relationships and parental mental health. For the autism population, in particular, parenting programmes that incorporate resources for parental psychopathology may be essential given the elevated rates of mental health challenges in parents of autistic children.41 42 Our moderation analyses will help us to understand the factors that make certain parenting behaviours more effective in regulating children’s emotions and behaviours as well as the potential gaps in service for certain families with more complex needs in the autism community.

There are a number of limitations anticipated. First, the eligibility criteria only include quantitative studies to ensure manageable numbers and to collect the necessary effects to conduct a meta-analysis. However, qualitative studies can provide more in-depth contextual information (eg, of parenting behaviours and autism profiles) to better interpret the association between parenting behaviours and autistic children’s internalising and externalising symptoms. Second, we expect that many studies may not report all data needed for effect size calculations or moderator analyses. We will address these cases by contacting authors and conducting sensitivity analyses with imputed values. Third, due to limited resources/study team capacity, we are only including English-language studies, which may further limit the cultural and racial/ethnic diversity in the samples included and contribute to the gap in understanding effective parenting practices for autistic children cross-culturally. Findings from the review will be disseminated through conferences, social media outlets and publication in a peer-reviewed journal.
