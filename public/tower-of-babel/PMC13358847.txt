Fagopyrum acutatum extract attenuates influenza A virus-stimulated inflammation and acute lung injury in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Jinbing Liu, Yingxuan Zhou, Hanwei Huang, Conglin Li, Jiayi Ke, Lifu Liu, Yintong Chen, Taotao Gao, Xiaoli Liang, Youhua Xu, Zifeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFagopyrum acutatum is used in Chinese medicine for its heat-clearing, detoxifying and pus-expelling properties, particularly for respiratory diseases. However, the effect of Fagopyrum acutatum on inflammation induced by influenza A virus (IAV) remains unclear. This study aimed to assess how Fagopyrum acutatum extract (FAE) affected the inflammation underlying mechanisms of IAV in vitro and in vivo.MethodsThe pharmacodynamic components in FAE were identified using ultrahigh-performance-liquid-chromatography (UPLC)-Q-Exactive mass spectrometry (MS). The mechanism of FAE was predicted through RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis and confirmed via reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot. The protective effects of FAE on lung injury and systemic inflammation were evaluated based on survival rate, lung index, histopathological alterations in lung tissues, lung cytokine levels, leukocyte counts in peripheral blood and serum inflammatory factor levels.ResultsThere were 103 compounds (mainly carbohydrates and organic acids) identified in FAE. FAE reversed the IAV-induced expression of RIG-I, MDA5, TLR3, IP-10, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1 in A549 cells, and reduced NF-κB P65 and IKBα phosphorylation. In vivo, FAE improved survival rates, decreased lung index and alleviated pathological changes in lung tissue caused by IAV. Expression of MCP-1, TNF-α, IL-6, IP-10 in lung tissues of mice with influenza pneumonia decreased by FAE. In addition, FAE significantly improved H1N1-induced leukocyte alterations and serum cytokine levels.ConclusionsFAE effectively alleviates IAV-stimulated inflammation and lung damage via NF-κB signaling pathway.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Fagopyrum acutatum extract (FAE) significantly inhibits influenza A virus (IAV)-induced inflammation and lung injury both in vitro and in vivo through via NF-κB signaling pathway.

What is known and what is new?

• The excessive inflammatory response caused by influenza virus infection is the main reason for the damage to the body. FAE is a medicinal plant commonly used for respiratory diseases, which has multiple biological activities such as heat-clearing and detoxification.

• This study demonstrates that FAE exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting NF-κB pathway, thereby suppressing the expression of inflammatory factors and alleviating lung tissue damage.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This study demonstrates that FAE significantly inhibits the inflammatory response caused by IAV infection both in vivo and in vitro, and it is a promising anti-inflammatory therapeutic agent.

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is the major pathogen causing acute respiratory infections in humans, which is part of the Orthomyxoviridae family. Neuraminidase (NA) and hemagglutinin (HA), glycoproteins presented on the viral envelope, define the virus subtype (1). Subtypes such as H5N1 and H7N9 have led to pandemics and pose significant global public health threats (2). IAV typically causes seasonal outbreaks, particularly in winter and spring, with infected individuals displaying mild symptoms like sore throat, cough, runny nose, and fever. In severe cases, however, complications such as bronchitis, pneumonia, lung failure, and even death can occur, particularly in immunocompromised individuals or those with underlying diseases (3). The primary mechanisms of tissue damage in IAV infection are direct viral attack and excessive host inflammation. The production of cytokines and antiviral molecules is caused by the interaction of pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns that activate innate immune signal transduction (4,5). By activating and recruiting immune cells at the site of infection, cytokines and chemokines trigger the secretion of inflammatory factors. This cascade can result in a systemic release of cytokines, commonly referred to as “cytokine storm” (6). Cytokine storms exacerbate tissue damage, often leading to severe pathological outcomes such as diffuse alveolar damage and hyaline membrane formation (7).

Currently, the primary strategies for preventing and treating influenza include vaccines and four classes of antiviral drugs. Vaccination is a key preventive measure, with vaccines being developed based on surveillance of circulating virus strains by the World Health Organization. However, the effectiveness of vaccines is often reduced due to the influenza virus’s tendency to mutate through antigenic drift and antigenic shift (8). Additionally, vaccines tend to be less effective in vulnerable populations, such as the elderly, young children, and immunocompromised patients, who have lower immune responses to the virus (9). Neuraminidase inhibitors, such as Oseltamivir (10,11); influenza cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitors, such as Baloxavir (12); Hemagglutinin inhibitors, such as arbidol (13); and M2 ion channel blockers, such as Amantadine, are the primary antiviral drugs used to treat IAV infections. By blocking sialic acid cleavage, neuraminidase inhibitors prevent newly produced viruses from releasing host cells (14,15). M2 ion channel blockers inhibit viral RNA release into the host cell cytoplasm by binding to the M2 protein, thereby halting viral replication (16). Influenza cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitors work by inhibiting the endonuclease function of the viral PA protein, blocking viral replication (17). Hemagglutinin inhibitors, such as arbidol, prevent viral entry by interfering with the binding between the virus and host cell membranes. When administered early in infection, arbidol can also alleviate symptoms (13,18). Drug-resistant influenza virus strains have emerged as a result of extensive use of antiviral drugs, which has had a significant impact on their decreased efficacy. As a result, future IAV treatment strategies must involve the creation of novel antiviral agents.

Influenza has long been treated using traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) (19). TCM offers multi-component and multi-target therapies that can effectively address drug resistance resulting from viral mutations, and it treatments generally work through two primary mechanisms: antiviral and anti-inflammatory actions (20). For example, Lianhua Qingwen capsules and Jinhua Qinggan granules not only inhibit influenza virus replication in the lung but also reduce excessive inflammatory responses, playing an immunomodulatory role (21,22). Houttuynia (Houttuynia cordata Thunb.), Astragalus (Astragalus abbreviatus Kar. & Kir.), and Coptis (Coptis asplenifolia Salisb.) are common medicinal materials that inhibit virus replication and reduce the secretion of inflammatory factors. Some active ingredients in Chinese medicines, such as flavonoids, emodin, and baicalin, also exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting immune cell activation or blocking signaling pathways such as NF-κB (23). Therefore, it is very important to develop anti-influenza drugs based on TCM.

Fagopyrum acutatum [Fagopyrum acutatum (Lehm.) Mansf. ex K. Hammer] (The name has been verified using http://www.theplantlist.org.) is a genus Fagopyrum Mill in the Polygonum family, a medicinal plant of which its seeds and leaves are edible, while its roots are used in traditional medicine. It has the effect of clearing heat and detoxifying as well as removing pus and blocking blood stasis (24). Ancient Chinese medical texts, including Bencao Gangmu (Compendium of Materia Medica), document that Fagopyrum acutatum extract (FAE) with pointed leaves were specifically used for “descending qi” to treat coughing with reversed breathing and wheezing with chest fullness—symptoms highly consistent with influenza-induced respiratory inflammation. FAE is often used for lung injury, lung abscess, bronchitis and other respiratory diseases (25). FAE is an important component of the well-known Chinese patent medicine, Jizhi Syrup, which has the effects of clearing heat and transforming phlegm, diffusing the lung and suppressing cough (26). More than 100 compounds, including flavonoids, phenols, terpenes, and fatty acids, have been identified in FAE, with flavonoids being the primary active components that exhibit anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects (25,27). In addition, FAE inhibits the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced pulmonary inflammatory response and alleviates lung injury in mice, through regulating the TLR4/NLPR3 signaling pathway and reducing the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 (28). Meanwhile, FAE down-regulates the levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, ICAM-1, NF-κB P65 and MIP-2, thereby alleviating lung injury induced by Klebsiella pneumoniae in rats (29). Recent studies have shown that a procyanidin-enriched fraction from the rhizome of Fagopyrum acutatum can alleviate acute lung injury (ALI) caused by H1N1 infection (30). Because the specific mechanism by which FAE exerts anti-inflammatory effects in IAV infection is currently unknown, we examined the mechanism and inhibitory effect of FAE on excessive inflammation after influenza virus infection in vitro and in vivo. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0308/rc).

Methods

Cell and viruses

We used Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM)/F12 (1:1) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) to cultivate the human lung carcinoma cell line A549 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). We cultured the cell line with 5% CO2 at 37 ℃.

We maintained mouse-adapted virus A/FM/1/47 (H1N1) and influenza virus A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1) in a laboratory. We used specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicken embryos that were nine days old and propagated the virus at 37 ℃. We used low-speed centrifugation to clarify newly collected allantoic fluid 72 h after inoculation. We stored the embryos at −80 ℃. We used the Reed-Muench method to determine the 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) of the H1N1 in cells and the 50% lethal dose (LD50) in BALB/c mice (31). To develop a viral pneumonia mouse model, we used mouse adaptor strain A/FM/1/47 (H1N1).

Preparation of FAE

Anhui Huifeng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. provided Fagopyrum acutatum (batch number: 211101). The plant material was ground, and the resulting powder (148 g) was mixed with 10 volumes of water. The mixture was extracted twice using ultrasound at 45 Hz, with each extraction lasting 30 minutes. The extract was subsequently filtered and concentrated to 0.8 g/mL.

Chemical profiling of FAE

We used ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC)-Q-Exactive mass spectrometry (MS) to analyze the chemical components of FAE. We used an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) and Q-Exactive Orbitrap (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to conduct UPLC-MS. We used the Full Ms/dd-MS2 mode to collect the data. We used ACQUITY-p\-UPLC-HSS-T3-column, maintained at 45 ℃, to attain chromatographic separation. For the mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid-water) and B (acetonitrile), we used a gradient elution program as follows: 95%-5% B for 0–2 min; 70%-30% B for 2–4 min; 50%-50% B for 4–8 min; 20%-80% B for 8–10 min; 0%-100% B for 10–15 min; and 95%-5% B for 15–16 min. We used a sample injection volume of 5 µL, at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. We used a heated electrospray ionization source (HESI), with a scanning range of 100 to 1,200 m/z, to conduct the ion source. We set the mass spectrometer as follows: positive ion spray voltage 3,800 V and negative ion spray voltage 3,000 V; Aux gas heater temperature 350 ℃ and capillary temperature 320 ℃; Aux gas flow rate 8 Arb and sheath gas flow rate 35 Arb; PDA scanning range from 210 to 400 nm; and NCE/stepped NCE at 10, 20, and 40. We used Progenesis QI v3.0 software to conduct data analysis, including peak detection, baseline correction, integration, peak matching, retention time correction, and normalization. Qualitative analysis was conducted using the TCM database, based on accurate mass, second set fragmentation and isotope distribution. Substances identified were retained for further analysis if their total score exceeded 50 points or if their total score exceeded 40 with a secondary match score exceeding 50.

Cell viability assay

After FAE was incubated with the medium, we used the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay to determine the viability of Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells and A549 cells. Briefly, we inoculated MDCK cells and A549 cells at 2×104/well in 96-well plates. After incubation with 100 µL of medium for 24 h, we added two-fold gradient dilutions of FAE (0.080469–41.2 mg/mL) to treat the cells. We discarded the supernatant after 24 h of culture and diluted the CCK-8 regent 10 times after adding the medium. We measured absorbance after incubation for 4 h using a zymography instrument at 450 nm. We calculated the 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of FAE for MDCK and A549 cells.

Progeny virus titer detection

We inoculated the A549 cells into a six-well cell culture plate with 1×106 cells/well. The six wells included three groups treated with different concentrations of FAE, a high-concentration FAE group, a virus group, and the control group. We added 1 mL serum-free medium to the high-concentration FAE group and the control group. We added with 1 mL disease venom to the other groups, which were incubated for 2 h at 37 ℃. We added different concentrations of FAE to the subgroups after we discarded the supernatant. We added DMEM/F12 medium with 1.5 µg/mL N-tosyl-L-phenylalanine chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin to the viral group. At 24 and 48 h, we collected the cell supernatants. We used 96-well plates at 2×104/well to inoculate MDCK cells, which we cultured in a 37 ℃ incubator overnight. We discarded and washed the original medium twice with PBS. Then we added the collected cell supernatant, which we incubated for 48 h at 37 ℃. We used a hemagglutination assay to detect the TCID50 of the progeny virus.

Plaque reduction assay

We used six-well tissue culture plates to culture MDCK cells to monolayers. We used 100 plaque forming units (PFU)/well of IAV (H1N1) diluted in serum-free DMEM/F12 (1:1) to inoculate the cells, which we incubated for 2 h at 37 ℃. After we discarded the inoculum, we used 3 mL/well of DMEM/F12 (1:1) with 0.8% agarose and 1.5 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin (with or without the indicated concentration of FAE) to cover the cells. After we inverted the plate, we incubated the cells for 48 h at 37 ℃. To observe viral plaques, we fixed them with 10% formaldehyde and stained them with 0.1% crystal violet.

Transcriptome sequencing

LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China) conducted the mRNA sequencing procedure. The Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to isolate total RNA from A549 cells. After building the sequencing library, we used the Illumina-NovaSeq™-6000 platform to subject the samples to 2×150 bp paired-end sequencing (PE150).

Real-time semiquantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), RNA extraction, and reverse transcription

We used RT-qPCR to quantitatively analyze mRNA expression levels. Following the manufacturer’s instructions for Trizol reagent (Invitrogen), we extracted total RNA from A549 cell line or lung tissue. We used an ultra-microspectrophotometer (BioSun, Zhejiang, China) to measure the RNA concentrations at 260 nm. We used the PrimeScript-RT-Master-Mix (Takara, Shiga, Japan) to reverse-transcribe the extracted RNA into cDNA according to the following conditions: 15 min at 37 ℃, followed by 5 s at 85 ℃. We used the Premix Ex Taq (Probe qPCR) reagent (Takara) on an ABI7500 Real Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) to conduct PCR amplification in A549 cell according to the following conditions: 95 ℃ for 30 c, followed 95 ℃ for 5 s; 60 ℃ for 40 c for 40 cycles. We used SYBR Premix Ex Taq (AG, Wuhan, China) on the ABI7500 Real Time PCR System to perform the PCR in the lung tissue according to the following conditions: 30 s at 95 ℃, 10 s at 95 ℃ for 40 cycles, and 30 s at 60 ℃. The housekeeping gene was GAPDH. We completed the experiments in triplicate and used the 2-ΔΔCt method to calculate the relative mRNA expression levels. Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) synthesized the primers, see Table S1 for a list of sequences.

Western blot

Cell Signaling Technology provided the primary antibodies against RIG-I (3743), MDA5 (5321), phosphorylated NF-κB P65 (P-P65) (3033), NF-κB P65 (P65) (8242), phosphorylated IkBα (P-IkBα) (2859), and IkBα (4815), and the secondary antibodies (7074). We used RIPA lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors to extract protein from the A549 cells. We used the BCA assay to determine protein concentrations. We used 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) to boil and separate the samples, and then we transferred the samples to PVDF membranes. We used BSA dissolved in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) to block the membranes, which we incubated with the respective primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight (dilution: 1:1,000). We washed the samples with TBST three times, and then we incubated the membranes with secondary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature (dilution: 1:3,000). After we washed the samples three more times, we used the PXi-chemiluminescent-imaging-system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK) for protein detection. The loading control was GAPDH (5174). We used ImageJ software to quantitatively analyze the protein bands.

Animal experiments

Experiments were performed under a project license (No. W230012) granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Analysis and Testing Center, in compliance with National Institutes of Health’s guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China) provided the 6- to 7-week-old SPF BALB/c female mice, which weighed 18–20 g. We fed the mice at the Animal Experimental Center of the Guangzhou Analysis and Testing Center, which had an animal biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) facility. We sustained the mice at 24±2 ℃ with a humidity range of 50–70% under a 12 h light/dark cycle. The mice acclimatized for one week and then we randomly assigned them to one of six groups: H1N1 infection group, FAE low-dose group (50 mg/kg/day), FAE medium-dose group (100 mg/kg/day), FAE high-dose group (200 mg/kg/day), oseltamivir treatment group (80 mg/kg/day), and control group (uninfected).

We observed FAE’s protective effect against IAV (H1N1) in vivo by inducing pneumonia in the mice. The procedure was as follows: All mice were subjected to isoflurane anesthesia. The control group was intranasally injected with 50 µL PBS. The remaining mice were injected with 50 µL influenza A/FM/1/47 (H1N1) (2.5 LD50). Two days before the experiment, we intragastrically administered different doses of FAE, oseltamivir, or PBS to the mice for 7 days. We recorded survival rate from 0 to 14 days after infection. Following the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines, if a mouse lost more than 20% of its weight, we considered it to be dead and killed it using an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital 0.2 mL (50 mg/kg). We also infected mice with a lower viral dose (1 LD₅₀) to induce H1N1-mediated pneumonia and to explore the protective mechanisms of FAE. Subsequently, we treated the mice orally with FAE, PBS, or oseltamivir phosphate. Five days after infection, we killed and weighed the mice. We used ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes to collect their blood and used the mouse lung tissues to perform organ index analysis. We used 4% paraformaldehyde to fix the lung tissue and used hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to stain the tissue for histopathological analysis. We stored the remaining lung tissue at 80°C to assess the inflammatory mediator levels.

Lung index

We killed the mice and excised their lungs. After we rinsed the lungs twice with PBS, we used cotton pads to gently press-dry the samples before they were weighed. The lung index calculation was Lung index = [lung weight (g) / body weight (g)] × 100%.

Lung virus titer

We used TCID50 assays to determine virus titer in lung homogenate. We first homogenized the lung tissue in this assay in PBS, which we then centrifuged for 15 min at 1,500 g. To assess viral titer, we collected the supernatant. We added MDCK cells to 96-well plates and incubated the cells in 5% CO2 at 37 ℃. We conducted a hemagglutination assay as described previously to obtain viral titer (TCID50).

Examination of histopathology

We fixed the left lung tissue with 4% paraformaldehyde. We dehydrated the tissue and embedded it in paraffin. We prepared 4 µm thick sections and stained them with H&E. We used a microscope to examine the pulmonary lesions.

Luminex assay

We used a Luminex assay to measure the inflammatory mediator levels in the lung tissue. We purchased a BioRad (Hercules, CA, USA) multiplex detection kit for mouse cytokine (IL-6, IP-10, MCP-1, and MIP-1β) and performed the assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

We examined statistical significance using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (A threshold of P<0.05 was deemed to be statistically significant). We used GraphPad Prism v8.0.2 for data visualization and expressed data as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) (n≥3).

FAE notably callback IAV-associated pattern recognition receptors and cytokines in vitro by RNA-seq analysis

We used the CCK-8 assay to determine the nontoxic concentration range and to avoid the cytotoxic effects of high doses of FAE on A549 and MDCK cells. The calculated CC50 values for FAE in A549 and MDCK cells were 3.73 and 2.91 mg/mL, respectively (Figure 2A). To evaluate the antiviral activity of FAE against IAV infection, we conducted progeny virus reduction assays and plaque reduction assay. Figure 2B illustrates the progeny virus titers. At 24 and 48 h, we did not observe any reduced progeny virus titers after treatment of A549 cells infected with H1N1 with FAE. This result demonstrated that FAE had no inhibitory effect on viral replication. The plaque reduction assay further confirmed that FAE did not reduce influenza virus plaque formation, indicating no antiviral activity (Figure 2C).

We conducted RNA-seq analysis to assess the expression of H1N1-related inflammatory factors. We compared the mRNA expression profiles of the viral infection group and the FAE treatment group. Based on a P value <0.05 and a fold-change (FC) >1.5, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A volcano plot illustrates the overall changes in gene expression between the comparison groups (Figure 2D). DEGs were further analyzed using the LC Biotech online tool to create Venn diagrams, highlighting the number of common DEGs across different comparison groups (Figure 2E). The number of callback genes associated with all three groups was 1098, as shown in the Venn diagram. For the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis, we merged these 1,098 callback genes with the KEGG database. After enrichment analysis, we sorted results according to the P value and plotted the significance of the top 20 signalizing pathways in a bubble diagram (Figure 2F). We found that 19 DEGs were enriched in the influenza A pathway and 21 DEGs were enriched in the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway. The expression of important DEGs in IAV-associated pattern recognition receptors and IAV-associated inflammation were selected for heat map to analyze the expression differences (Figure 2G). After viral infection, several genes (e.g., RIG-I, MDA5, IL-8, IP-10, and IL-6) were significantly elevated, and after FAE treatment, transcript levels were alleviated, as shown in the heat map.

FAE decreases IAV-induced proinflammatory mediators by inhibiting NF-κB activation

In this study, we used RT-qPCR to evaluate inflammatory factors and determine whether FAE suppresses the upregulation of cytokine expression following IAV infection. The expressions of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IP-10, MCP-1, and TNF-α were upregulated markedly 24 h after infection in the virus-infected group of A549 cells (P<0.01). Remarkably, however, the levels of these inflammatory factors (P<0.05) decreased in a dose-dependent manner after treatment with FAE (0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL), particularly IL-8 (Figure 3A). Therefore, FAE may inhibit IAV-induced chemokine and cytokine upregulation, demonstrating its potential anti-inflammatory effects.

We also examined the signaling pathways responsible for influenza-induced inflammation. FAE treatment (0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL) significantly limited the mRNA expression levels of RIG-I, TLR3, and MDA5 in a manner that was dose dependent (Figure 3B). Additionally, in the virus-infected group, protein levels of RIG-I, MDA5, P-NF-κB P65, and P-IκBα increased notably (P<0.05). Treatment with FAE significantly downregulated RIG-I and P-NF-κB P65 expression compared with the virus group (P<0.05). In contrast, only at the maximum dose of FAE (1 mg/mL) were MDA5 and P-IκBα expression levels decreased (Figure 3C). According to these results, FAE blocked the activation of the NF-κB pathway, thereby inhibiting the expression of proinflammatory mediators and imposing its anti-inflammatory effects.

IAV-induced ALI alleviated by FAE

First, we monitored the survival rate of mice treated with FAE over 14 days. The survival rate was 10% for the FAE-treated group (50 mg/kg/day) and 70% for the oseltamivir treatment group (Figure 4A). We assessed the effects of FAE on IAV-stimulated lung injury by assessing the lung index and pathological alterations in lung tissue, both of which reflect the severity of lung inflammation in mice. The lung index for each group is presented in Figure 4B. The control group had a lung index of 0.74±0.03, which was remarkably lower than the viral infection group, which had a lung index of 1.5±0.12 (P<0.05). We treated the mice FAE (50, 100, and 200 mg/kg/day) or oseltamivir, and the lung index was reduced to 1.23±0.10, 1.2±0.17, 1.2±0.11, and 0.99±0.08. The results for the virus group were markedly higher (P<0.01). We also analyzed lung pathology to determine whether FAE had an impact on IAV-induced lung injury. The typical symptoms of lung injury were evident in the infected group, including extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar wall thickening, and pulmonary hemorrhage, as shown in Figure 4C. Inflammatory cell infiltration remarkably decreased after treatment with FAE (50, 100, and 200 mg/kg/day) or oseltamivir, and the alveolar structure normalized. These findings suggest that FAE offers protective effects against lung injury in IAV-infected mice.

FAE attenuates H1N1-induced leukocyte alterations and serum cytokine in peripheral blood

Blood indices are a critical measure for evaluating the progression and severity of viral infections. We collected blood samples from mice and analyzed peripheral blood cell composition and serum cytokine levels five days post-infection. Discussion

Influenza virus is a common respiratory pathogen, which has caused many seasonal epidemics and four world pandemics and has brought great challenges to human health (32). Although influenza can be managed with preventive measures such as vaccination and antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir, favipiravir, and arbidol, the rapid mutation of the virus has diminished the efficacy of existing vaccines and increased drug resistance (33). Influenza has been treated successfully by TCM. There are many biological components in TCM which could exert their effects through multi-target and multi-way such as anti-virus, anti-inflammation and immune regulation (31,34). Reportedly, IAV-induced ALI has been effectively alleviated by the anticomplement component of FAE (30). Nonetheless, its effects on IAV-stimulated inflammation have not been previously reported. This research provides evidence that FAE significantly inhibits IAV-induced inflammation and lung injury both in vitro and in vivo.

TCM comprises a wide range of bioactive ingredients that can treat diseases through various mechanisms. Carbohydrates and glycosides are the most abundant substances in FAE. Studies have demonstrated that numerous glycosides possess anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and other biological activities. The most abundant component is argininyl-fructosyl-glucose, which has been previously shown in studies to exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant physiological activities through the NF-κB pathway (35). In addition, Cordycepin is also an important glycoside in FAE, and it similarly inhibits NF-κB activation, thereby reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines (36). Amino acids and derivatives account for 21.11% of FAE components, and many of these substances have physiological activities such as anti-inflammatory effects. For example, L-arginine can exert anti-inflammatory effects by regulating NO production, inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation, and suppressing the production of inflammatory mediators (37). Additionally, FAE contains significant levels of flavonoids, including proanthocyanidins (38), catechin (39), narirutin (40), which inhibit the production of inflammatory factors by suppressing NF-κB pathway activation, thereby alleviating sepsis or LPS-induced ALI. Derivatives of organic acids are also essential—for example, the metabolite derived from citric acid arbidol: a broad-spectrum antiviral compound that blocks viral fusion and Succinic acid (41,42), also exhibit anti-inflammatory activity. Together, these four categories of substances account for 92.17% of FAE and work synergistically to provide potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, making FAE effective in addressing inflammation caused by viral infections.

As is well known, the excessive inflammatory response caused by IAV infection is one of the main reasons for tissue damage in the body. When IAV infects the body, it is first recognized by RIG-I, MDA5, and TLRs, which are pattern recognition receptors that can activate downstream signaling pathways to produce various inflammatory factors (43). Among them, RIG-I and MDA5 are members of the RIG-I-like receptor family. They can detect viral dsRNA and activate MAVS as well as IKK-kinase complex phosphorylation. As a result, NF-κB is activated, leading to nuclear translocation (44). TLR-3 is a member of the Toll-like receptor family and activates the TIR domain-containing adaptor protein. Because TLR-3 recognizes viral dsRNA, it can induce IFN-β production, promote the IKK complex, and activate NF-κB (45). Subsequently, NF-κB enters the cell nucleus and promotes the transcription of inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, TNF-α, IP-10, etc.) and chemokines (such as MCP-1, MIP-1β, etc.), thereby mediating the body’s immune response. This indicates that the NF-κB pathway is an important mechanism for the body to generate an immune response after IAV infection.

However, inflammatory responses are not always beneficial, and excessive inflammation can cause immune damage to the body’s normal tissues. After innate immune signal activation, immune cells (e.g., epithelial cells, alveolar macrophages, and neutrophils) secreted numerous chemokines and cytokines, which caused the inflammatory response to be unbalanced. Also known as a “cytokine storm” (46-48), Inflammatory factors such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IP-10, as well as chemokines like MCP-1 and MIP-1β, produced downstream of the NF-κB pathway, are key factors in the cytokine storm. IL-6 can regulate immune cell activity in the early immune response to promote protective immunity, but excessive IL-6 can lead to immune imbalance and trigger the activation of various other cell signaling pathways, resulting in increased levels of other cytokines and further amplifying inflammation (49). Excessive TNF-α can lead to increased oxidative stress in the body and a sustained rise in the expression of various pro-inflammatory factors (50). Additionally, IP-10 recruits and activates neutrophils, further exacerbating lung injury (51). The large amounts of MCP-1 and MIP-1β produced will recruit immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils to accumulate in the lungs, inducing ALI (52,53). On the other hand, the key factors causing the above situation included IL-6 and TNF-α, which made the NF-κB and JAK/STAT signaling pathways overactive, leading to excessive secretion of chemokines and cytokines, thereby further aggravating the inflammatory response (54).

Our study found that FAE can inhibit IAV-induced expression of MDA5, RIG-I, and TLR3 at both the transcriptional and translational levels. It also suppresses the phosphorylation of key proteins in the NF-κB pathway, including NF-κB p65 and IKBα, thereby limiting the expression of downstream inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IP-10, TNF-α, and chemokines like MCP-1 and MIP-1β, which helps alleviate the inflammatory response. Luminex assay results further confirm that FAE decreases serum cytokine and chemokine levels and inhibits systemic inflammatory responses. Future studies will systematically evaluate the temporal efficacy of FAE by monitoring viral load, inflammatory markers, and NF-κB activity at multiple time points post-infection to precisely define its window of therapeutic action. At the same time, FAE improves IAV-induced changes in mouse lung index, lung pathology, and peripheral blood leukocyte levels. The observation that FAE alleviates lung injury without reducing viral burden is clinically significant. Most current anti-influenza therapies (e.g., oseltamivir) target viral replication, whereas FAE appears to modulate host-pathogen interaction by dampening excessive inflammation. This ‘host-directed therapy’ approach addresses the immunopathology-driven tissue damage that contributes to severe influenza, independent of viral load reduction.

In short, FAE inhibits the activation of the NF-κB pathway, thereby suppressing the transcription and secretion of its downstream inflammatory factors and chemokines, modulating immune cells, improving pathological damage in lung tissue, and exerting immunoregulatory and anti-inflammatory effects. These findings suggest that FAE is effective in reducing both lung and systemic inflammation, making it a promising anti-inflammatory treatment. A limitation of the present study is that only a correlation has been established. Without reverse validation using NF-κB inhibitors or p65 siRNA, causality cannot be inferred for this pathway. Therefore, our conclusions remain correlational, and definitive causal relationships await future loss-of-function experiments.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrated the significant inhibitory effect of FAE on inflammatory responses induced by IAV both in vitro and in vivo. FAE downregulated the expression of key proteins in the NF-κB pathway, revealing its anti-inflammatory role, and blocked cytokine and chemokine production. Further investigation on FAE is warranted for its potential therapeutic applications as well as for its novel anti-inflammatory effect to treat IAV-induced infection.
