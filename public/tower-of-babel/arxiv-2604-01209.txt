Regularity theorems for random elliptic operators on domains
Peter Bella a , Julian Fischer b , Marc Josien c , Claudia Raithel d

Abstract
Regularity theorems à la Avellaneda-Lin are an indispensable part of the modern quantitative theory of stochastic homogenization. While interior regularity results for random elliptic operators have been available for a while, on general smooth domains the existing theory has until recently remained limited to Lipschitz estimates. We establish C 1 , α C^{1,\alpha} regularity results for random elliptic operators on bounded sufficiently smooth domains, as well as for scalar problems on convex polytopes. We, furthermore, prove a number of auxiliary results typically employed in the derivation of fluctuation bounds, such as a weighted Meyers estimate.

Introduction

In this contribution we derive regularity results for weak solutions
u\in H^{1}_{loc}({\mathcal{O}})
of elliptic PDEs and systems of the form

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad\,{\mathcal{O}},

(1.1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\quad\partial{\mathcal{O}},

where the coefficient-field
a:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is assumed to be bounded and uniformly elliptic –properties which are classically observed to be insufficient for guaranteeing the Hölder regularity of
u
, see for instance the counter-examples by Meyers
[
44
, Example 3]
and De Giorgi
[
28

. Within the context of homogenization, in which the operator
-\nabla\cdot a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla
is approximated by a constant-coefficient operator
-\nabla\cdot\bar{a}\nabla
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, it is, however, quite standard to aim at “transferring” regularity properties from the constant-coefficient homogenized operator back to
-\nabla\cdot a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla
for small enough
\varepsilon>0
. This type of strategy was introduced by Avellaneda and Lin in the setting of periodic homogenization in the 80s
[
12
,
13
]
, and more recently has been fruitfully exploited in stochastic homogenization
[
9
,
6
,
10
,
23
,
30
,
31
,
33
]
.

In the past decade, an extensive theory of quantitative stochastic homogenization has been developed. As large-scale regularity statements are a central ingredient in the derivation of quantitative homogenization estimates
[
9
,
10
,
30
,
33
]
, at this point the topic of large-scale regularity theories for random elliptic operators on the whole-space has been quite thoroughly explored. We refer to, e. g.,
[

,

,

,

,

,

]
concerning Hölder, Lipschitz, and

[

,

for higher-order (that is,

,

,

,

,

,

]
for nonlinear, degenerate, or parabolic variants.

On the other hand, much less is known concerning the topic of boundary regularity. In
[

]
large-scale Lipschitz estimates were shown for random elliptic operators with Dirichlet boundary data on sufficiently regular domains. For the particular case of a half-space
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, in

The main result of the present work –stated in Theorem

1.1
Boundary layers in periodic and stochastic homogenization

Two concepts central to quantitative homogenization theory are that of the
homogenization corrector
and the
two-scale expansion
. For the linear elliptic PDE

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla u^{\varepsilon})=\nabla\cdot f\quad\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.2)

the homogenization corrector
\phi_{i}\in H^{1}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as a sublinearly growing solution to the corrector equation

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla(x_{i}+\phi_{i}))

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad\,\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.3)

Note that its rescaling
\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot):=\varepsilon\phi_{i}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})
solves the rescaled corrector equation
-\nabla\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}))=0
, where we have abbreviated
a^{\varepsilon}(\cdot):=a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})
.

For elliptic, stationary, and ergodic random fields
a
, the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
of the solutions
u^{\varepsilon}
to (
1.2
) is given by the solution
\bar{u}
to a constant-coefficient
effective equation

-\nabla\cdot(\bar{a}\nabla\bar{u})=\nabla\cdot f

[
40
,
43
]
. Here, the
effective coefficient

\bar{a}
is determined as
\bar{a}e_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i})]
.

Given the solution to the homogenized problem
\bar{u}
, the
two-scale expansion

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale}}:=\bar{u}+\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}\partial_{i}\bar{u}

(1.4)

provides an approximate solution to the original PDE (
1.2
).
Many estimates in quantitative homogenization (both periodic and stochastic) follow from the energy estimate for the divergence-form elliptic equation satisfied by the “homogenization error”
u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale}}
.

In the case of the problem on a bounded domain
{\mathcal{O}}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, even with
u^{\varepsilon}=\bar{u}
on
\partial{\mathcal{O}}
the homogenization error fails to vanish on the boundary: In this case we have
u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale}}=-\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}\partial_{i}\bar{u}
on
\partial{\mathcal{O}}
, which is in general nonzero.

The avoid this mismatch in the boundary data of
u^{\varepsilon}
and
u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale}}
it is standard (for classical literature in the periodic setting see, e. g.,
[
1
,
27
,
26
]
) to adapt the ansatz
u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale}}
via a boundary layer corrector
\theta^{\varepsilon}_{i}\in H^{1}_{\rm{loc}}({\mathcal{O}})
. The latter is given as the weak solution of

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}\nabla\theta^{\varepsilon}_{i})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad\,{\mathcal{O}},

(1.5)

\displaystyle\theta^{\varepsilon}_{i}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}(\cdot/\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\text{on }\quad\partial{\mathcal{O}}.

The adjusted two-scale expansion then becomes

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale},\partial}:=\bar{u}+(\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon})\partial_{i}\bar{u}.

(1.6)

The adapted “homogenization error”, given by
u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale},\partial}
, now has homogeneous Dirichlet boundary data and solves the elliptic PDE

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale},\partial}))

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot((a^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon})-\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon})\nabla\partial_{i}\bar{u})+a^{\varepsilon}\varepsilon\nabla\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\partial_{i}\bar{u}

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad\,{\mathcal{O}},

(1.7)

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale},\partial}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\quad\partial{\mathcal{O}}.

Here,
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
is the so-called (whole-space) flux-corrector; in particular,
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
is a 3-tensor that is skew-symmetric in its last two indices and solves
\nabla_{k}\cdot\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{ijk}=e_{j}\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}(e_{i}+\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}_{i})-\bar{a}e_{i})
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
36
, Section 7.2]
.
Given suitable estimates on the correctors
\phi_{i}
,
\sigma_{i}
, and
\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
, the error equation (
1.7
) entails bounds on
u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{2-scale},\partial}
. As no suitable estimates on
\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
are available in the literature, our first task (in Proposition
3
below) is to derive suitable bounds for
\varepsilon\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon\nabla\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
.

Notation.

Throughout this paper, we consider the half-space
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}>0\}
. By
B_{r}(x)
we denote the ball of radius
r
centered at
x
and
B_{r}^{+}(x):=B_{r}(x)\cap\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
. When the ball is centered at
0
we simply write
B_{r}
and
B_{r}^{+}
respectively. The coordinate functions are given as
x_{i}:=x\cdot e_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
. When
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
we decompose
x=(x^{\perp},x^{\parallel})
, where
x^{\perp}=x_{1}
and
x^{\parallel}=(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})
.

For any
\delta>0
, we will use the convention
{\mathcal{O}}_{\delta}:=\{x\in{\mathcal{O}}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial{\mathcal{O}})<\delta\}
. When
{\mathcal{O}}
is a
C^{1,1}
-domain (not necessarily bounded), there exists
c({\mathcal{O}})
small enough such that for any
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{c({\mathcal{O}})}
there exists a unique point
p(x)\in\partial{\mathcal{O}}
that is the closest point to
x
. Notice that when
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, for
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
we have that
p(x)=x^{\parallel}
.

We frequently use the convention that
``\lesssim"
means
``\leq C(d,\lambda,\nu,\alpha),"
where
d
denotes the dimension,
\lambda>0
is the ellipticity ratio of the coefficient field
a
, and
\nu
and
\alpha
are from the Hölder regularity assumption (A4) below.

We use the notation
\mathcal{C}
to denote a generic random constant (respectively,
\mathcal{C}(x)
to denote a generic random field) whose value may change from line to line. Unless otherwise specified, the constant
\mathcal{C}
(respectively the random field
\mathcal{C}(x)
) will be subject to a uniform stretched exponential moment bound.

Aside from the caveat that, for us,
H^{1}_{\rm{loc}}({\mathcal{O}}):=\left\{v:v\in H^{1}(B_{r}(x)\cap{\mathcal{O}})\text{ for all }r>0,x\in{\mathcal{O}}\right\}
, we use standard notation for Sobolev spaces. In particular,
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
refers to the space of (in case of unbounded

Throughout the paper, we use the Einstein summation convention, i. e. we sum over repeated indices such as in

Discussion of main results

2.1
Assumptions

Throughout this contribution we make the following assumptions:

Assumptions on the domain.

For simplicity, we always assume that
d\geq 3
. Then, in different sections, we have varying assumptions on our domain:

•

In Sections
3
,
4
, and
6
, unless otherwise stated, we assume that
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is either a bounded
C^{1,1}
-domain or the half-space
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
.

•

In Section
5
, we assume that
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is either a convex cone or a convex polytope.

Assumptions on the ensemble.

We assume that the coefficient field,
a
, is defined on the whole-space and satisfies the following conditions:

(A1)

We assume that
\langle\cdot\rangle
-a. s. the heterogeneous coefficients
a
are
uniformly elliptic and bounded
. In particular, there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\displaystyle|a(x)v|

\displaystyle\leq|v|,

\displaystyle a(x)v\cdot v

\displaystyle\geq\lambda|v|^{2},

for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a. e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(A2)

The ensemble
\langle\cdot\rangle
is
stationary
. In particular, the law of
a
is invariant under spatial shifts; i. e. the statistics of
a
are spatially independent.

(A3)

We assume that
\langle\cdot\rangle
satisfies a quantitative ergodicity assumption in form of a
spectral gap inequality
in the following form: For any random variable
\xi
the estimate

\displaystyle\left<(\xi-\left<\xi\right>)^{2}\right>\leq\left<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{1}(x)}\big|\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial a}\big|{\textrm{d}}y\Big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right>

(2.1)

holds. Here,
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{1}(x)}\big|\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial a}\big|{\textrm{d}}y
is given as

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta a}\limsup_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\xi(a+h\delta a)-\xi(a)}{h}

(2.2)

with the perturbations
\delta a:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
being supported in
B_{1}(x)
and satisfying
\|\delta a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1
. When the random variable
\xi
is Fréchet differentiable then (
2.2
) is
\big|\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial a(x)}\big|
.

(A4)

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we assume that
a
is Hölder continuous with stretched exponential moments; i. e. there exist
\nu>0
and a stationary random field
\mathcal{C}(a,x)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\nu\mathcal{C}(a,x)^{\nu})]\leq 2

(2.3)

such that the Hölder bound

\displaystyle\sup_{y,z\in B_{1}(x)}\frac{|a(y)-a(z)|}{|y-z|^{\alpha}}\leq\mathcal{C}(a,x)

(2.4)

holds. Notice that the expectation on the left-hand side of (
2.3
) does not depend on the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
due to stationarity.

The assumptions (A1)-(A3) are a standard set of assumptions in stochastic homogenization –with the quantification of ergodicity (the qualitative assumption of which is canon in classical qualitative stochastic homogenization) via the spectral gap inequality (A3) being particularly amenable to the methods of
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. The assumption (A4) gives us access to (local) Schauder estimates –avoiding classical counterexamples to regularity at small scales–, whereas large-scale regularity properties are derived from the homogenization process. Assumptions (A1)-(A3) will be required for all of our main results, while assumption (A4) will only be needed for certain pointwise statements.

Since our assumptions on the ensemble are relatively standard, they are satisfied by a variety of relevant ensembles –e. g., (local nonlinear functions of) Gaussian ensembles satisfying suitably strong decorrelation estimates. To avoid excessive repetition, we choose to omit the details here and instead refer the interested reader to, e. g.,
[
17
, Section 2.1]
.

Remark 1

(Scalar PDEs versus systems)
.

While for notational convenience we employ scalar notation for the statement and the proofs of our results, with the exception of Theorem
8
our proofs also work in the case of linear elliptic systems and thus our results also apply
mutatis mutandis
to the case of linear elliptic systems.

Remark 2

(The case
d=2
)
.

Notice that we have avoided the case
d=2
. This is, in particular, for brevity –all of our results still hold for
d=2
, up to some logarithmic corrections.

2.2
A suboptimal decay estimate for the boundary layer corrector

In order to prove a large-scale regularity result for random
a^{\varepsilon}
-harmonic operators on bounded
C^{1,1}
-domains with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary data, we first require access to a suboptimal estimate for the decay of the boundary layer
\theta^{\varepsilon}
away from
\partial{\mathcal{O}}
. Since in
[
17
]
we require this suboptimal estimate as an ingredient for obtaining the optimal decay for the boundary layer in the case
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, we also state and prove our proposition for
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
.

Proposition 3
.

Adopt the assumptions (A1)-(A4), let
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, and let
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be either a bounded
C^{1,1}
-domain or
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
.
Let
\theta^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{loc}({\mathcal{O}})
denote the unique weak solution to (
1.5
) with
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B_{r}}\chi_{\mathcal{O}}|\nabla\theta^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0
.
Then there exists a random field
\mathcal{C}(a^{\varepsilon},x)
such that
\frac{\mathcal{C}(a^{\varepsilon},x)}{\varepsilon}
has stretched exponential moments (in the sense of (
2.3
), uniformly in
\varepsilon
and
x
) and the estimate

\displaystyle|\varepsilon\nabla\theta^{\varepsilon}(x_{0})|\lesssim_{d,\lambda,\nu,\alpha}\mathcal{C}(a^{\varepsilon},x_{0})\Big(1+\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x_{0},\partial{\mathcal{O}})}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{3}}

(2.5)

holds for any
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{O}}
.

In case of
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, the random field
\mathcal{C}(a^{\varepsilon},x)
is additionally stationary w. r. t. shifts tangential to
\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
.

The proof of Proposition
3
is given in Section
3
and, while relying on stochastic ingredients from
[
30
,
31
]
, is entirely deterministic here.

The main step for proving Proposition
3
is Lemma
9
, which is a spatially averaged decay estimate for the boundary layer corrector –the argument for which relies on a homogenization infused Campanato iteration to compare the boundary layer with a homogenized counterpart at large-scales. As already mentioned above, this is a standard technique in both periodic and stochastic homogenization.

We remark that the spatially averaged decay estimate of Lemma
9
holds under the assumptions (A1)-(A3). In order to obtain (
2.5
), the result of Lemma
9
must be post-processed under the additional inclusion of assumption (A4).

2.3
Regularity for random
a^{\varepsilon}
-harmonic functions on regular domains

Using the suboptimal decay of the boundary layer from Proposition
3
, we are then able to obtain the following large-scale regularity result for random
a^{\varepsilon}
-harmonic functions with homogeneous Dirichlet
boundary data on bounded
C^{1,1}
-domains or on
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
.
Note that like the original Avellaneda-Lin regularity theorems
[
12
,
13
]
and in contrast to standard Schauder theory (the latter of which is applicable on small scales
\lesssim\varepsilon
, assuming a Hölder regular coefficient field

), Theorem
4
establishes regularity results for the operator
-\nabla\cdot a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla
on scales
\gg\varepsilon
.

Theorem 4
.

Adopt the assumptions (A1) - (A3), let
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, and let
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be either a bounded
C^{1,1}
-domain or
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
. Then there exists a random field
r_{{\mathcal{O}}}^{*}(a^{\varepsilon},x)\geq\varepsilon
such that
\frac{r_{{\mathcal{O}}}^{*}(a^{\varepsilon},x)}{\varepsilon}
has stretched exponential moments (in the sense of (
2.3
), uniformly in
\varepsilon
and
x
) and such that the following holds true:

Given any
0<R<c({\mathcal{O}})
and any
x_{0}\in\overline{{\mathcal{O}}}
, let
u\in H^{1}_{\rm{loc}}({\mathcal{O}})
solve

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}\nabla u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad B_{R}(x_{0})\cap{\mathcal{O}},

(2.6)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\quad B_{R}(x_{0})\cap\partial{\mathcal{O}}.

Then for any
r
with
r_{{\mathcal{O}}}^{*}(a^{\varepsilon},x_{0})\leq r\leq R
, the excess-decay property

\displaystyle\inf_{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-A\cdot\nabla(x+\phi^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\theta^{\varepsilon})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{d,\lambda,\alpha,{\mathcal{O}}}\bigg(\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{2\alpha}+\frac{r^{2}}{c({\mathcal{O}})}\bigg)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{R}(x_{0})}|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.7)

holds.
Furthermore, for any
r
with
r_{{\mathcal{O}}}^{*}(a^{\varepsilon},x_{0})\leq r\leq R
the large-scale mean-value property

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{d,\lambda,{\mathcal{O}}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{R}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.8)

holds.

In case of
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, it holds that
c({\mathcal{O}})=\infty
and the random field
r_{{\mathcal{O}}}^{*}
is additionally stationary w. r. t. shifts tangential to
\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
.

We recall that in the particular case of the half-space
{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, Theorem
4
was already established in
[
25
]
, though without explicit quantification of the minimal radius
r_{\mathcal{O}}^{*}(a^{\varepsilon},x_{0})
.
The argument for Theorem
4
, contained in Section

, is similar to that of Lemma
9
. It also requires the result of Lemma

Combining the large-scale regularity of Theorem
4
with classical Schauder estimates at small scales (available to us by additionally imposing assumption (A4)), we obtain two useful corollaries.

Corollary 5
.

2.5
Regularity for
a^{\varepsilon}
-harmonic functions on convex polytopes

For the last result of this paper, we leave the setting of regular domains and consider instead the situation in convex polytopes.
The desire for a
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity theory also in this setting is quite natural –in particular, in practice material samples are not always smooth and often have corners and edges. Furthermore, convex polytopes make a natural appearance in numerical homogenization:
Representative volume elements (RVEs) for determining the effective properties of random media are often chosen as cubes; numerical homogenization approaches such as the local orthogonal decomposition method (LOD method) by Målqvist and Peterseim
[
41
]
also often utilizes cubes as domains of definition of the local basis functions. We refer to
[
24
,
35
]
for the analysis of the LOD method in the context of stochastic homogenization.

The issue separating the situation in a smooth domain from that in a general polytope is a lack of regularity in the homogenized picture. In particular, in domains with corners and edges it is standard practice to decompose a harmonic function in terms of a regular contribution and singular corner and edge contributions –an excess-decay only holding for the regular contribution (see, e. g.,
[
21
,
20
,
34
]
). In this paper, using the explicit scaling of the corner and edge contributions (which depends on the opening angles), we first observe that in the convex situation an excess-decay in fact holds for the full harmonic function –this is in contrast, e. g., to the situation encountered in
[
38
]
in which general opening angles (
<2\pi
) are considered. The presence of regularity in the homogenized setting then makes it possible to transfer regularity to
a^{\varepsilon}
-harmonic functions at large scales.
Imposing in addition our assumption (A4) to ensure regularity on small scales, we obtain the following pointwise estimate.

Theorem 8
.

Adopt the assumptions (A1)-(A4), let
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, and let
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex polytope. Denote by
E
the set of
(d-2)
-dimensional hyperedges of
\partial{\mathcal{O}}
.

Then there exists a constant
\delta=\delta(\lambda,{\mathcal{O}})
and a random field
r^{*}_{{\mathcal{O}}}(a^{\varepsilon},x)\geq\varepsilon
such that
\frac{r^{*}_{{\mathcal{O}}}(a^{\varepsilon},x)}{\varepsilon}
has stretched exponential moments (in the sense of (
2.3
), uniformly in
\varepsilon
and
x
) and such that the following holds true: Given any
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{O}}
, any
\rho
with
r^{*}_{{\mathcal{O}}}(a^{\varepsilon},x_{0})\leq\rho\leq c({\mathcal{O}})
, and any weak solution

to the scalar elliptic PDE

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a^{\varepsilon}\nabla u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0}),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0}),

the estimate

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x_{0})|\leq C\Big(\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x_{0},E)}{\rho}\Big)^{\delta}\Big(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{1/2}

In other words, the theorem states that the gradient of any

-harmonic function must decay whenever we approach a

Note that, in contrast, near any point on a face of the polytope the distance function

Proof of Proposition

We first remark that we may assume w. l. o. g that

{\mathcal{O}}\setminus\left\{0\right\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}

We now consider the

\omega

Denote by

form an increasing sequence diverging to

It is then a classical (and rather immediate, using the completeness of the basis

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{r\omega}|\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{2r}\cap{\mathcal{O}}}|\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\rho^{2}\Big(\frac{r}{\rho}\Big)^{2b_{1}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\rho\omega}|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S.

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\rho\omega}|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}S\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{R}\cap{\mathcal{O}}}|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{r}\cap{\mathcal{O}}}|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\Big(\frac{r}{\rho}\Big)^{2b_{1}-2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{R}\cap{\mathcal{O}}}|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.
