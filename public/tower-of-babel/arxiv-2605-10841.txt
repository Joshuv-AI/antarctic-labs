Constant time testability of first-order logic with modulo counting on finitary graphs
Isolde Adler, Jenny Stimpson

Abstract
This paper studies algorithmic meta theorems for property testing with constant running time in the bounded degree model. In (Adler, Harwath 2018) it was shown that on graph classes 𝒞 d w \mathcal{C}^{w}_{d} consisting of all graphs with both degree at most d d and treewidth at most w w , every problem expressible in monadic second-order logic with counting ( CMSO \operatorname{CMSO} ) is testable with polylogarithmic running time (where d , w ∈ ℕ d,w\in\mathbb{N} are fixed). It was left open whether this can be improved to constant running time. In this paper we give a positive answer for testing CMSO \operatorname{CMSO} on classes 𝒞 d c \mathcal{C}^{c}_{d} , where d d bounds the degree and c c bounds the component size. Our main result shows constant time testability of first-order logic with modulo counting ( FOMOD \operatorname{FOMOD} ) on 𝒞 d c \mathcal{C}^{c}_{d} . For our proof we tailor Hanf normal form of FOMOD \operatorname{FOMOD} to our setting, and we exhibit a number-theoretic ‘patchability’ condition that allows to infer global information on the input graph from a local sample of constant size. We believe that our ‘patchability’ might be of independent interest. The step from FOMOD \operatorname{FOMOD} to CMSO \operatorname{CMSO} then follows from a result by (Eickmeyer, Elberfeld, Harwath, 2017) on the expressive power of order invariant monadic second-order logic on classes of bounded treedepth. Keywords: Property testing, constant time algorithms, bounded degree model, algorithmic meta-theorems, first-order logic with modulo counting, monadic second-order logic.

Introduction

The past decades have seen a tremendous increase in the volume of stored data, posing a huge challenge to computational power. Moreover, the data is often stored remotely, making access costly. In this context, even just reading the whole input once may be infeasible.
Property Testing
addresses this by the design of algorithms
that derive global information on the structure of the input by only exploring a small number of
local parts of it. These algorithms are randomised and allow for a small error.
A property testing algorithm is required to
distinguish inputs that satisfy a given property from inputs that
are
\varepsilon
-far from satisfying the property with high probability correctly. Intuitively,
an input is called
\varepsilon
-far from satisfying a property if more than an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the input
needs to be modified in
order to make it satisfy the property. The exact definition of the distance measure depends
on the model.

Our work is based on the
bounded degree graph model
of property testing introduced
by Goldreich and Ron
[
11
]
.
This model considers graphs whose degree (
i. e.
the number of edges incident to any vertex)
is bounded from above by a constant
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Formally, a
property

P
is a class of graphs that is closed
under isomorphism.
Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Two
d
-bounded degree graphs
G
and
H
, both on
n
vertices,
are called
\varepsilon
-close
, if they can
be made isomorphic by modifying (adding or deleting) at most
\varepsilon dn
edges.
If
G
and
H
are not
\varepsilon
-close they are called
\varepsilon
-far
. For a property
P
of
d
-bounded degree graphs, a
graph
G
is
\varepsilon
-close to
P
, if
G
is
\varepsilon
-close to some member of
P
.
Otherwise
G
is
\varepsilon
-far from
P
.
The algorithms have
oracle access
to an input graph
G
of maximum degree
d
. Here we assume that
the vertex set of
G
is
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. More precisely, an algorithm gets the number
n
as input, and
it can access

via

to an oracle,
i. e.
for any specified vertex

and index

if it exists, and a special symbol

otherwise, in constant time.

A property

is
testable with query complexity

in the bounded degree model, if for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

and every input size

there is a probabilistic algorithm
A_{\varepsilon,n}
(called an
\varepsilon

that accepts

Until now, despite some effort, a
characterisation of the properties that are constant-query testable
in the bounded degree model is still elusive.

Towards this goal, Newman and Sohler
[
15
]
proved a general result,
showing that every property of planar graphs (and, more generally, of

hyperfinite
graphs) is
constant-query testable.
This theorem is a
meta-theorem
in the sense that it treats many properties simultaneously.

In this paper we are interested in the
running time
, which is measured as usual in terms of
n

For many individual properties is it easy to see that the query complexity of a tester dominates its running time.
However, while
a bound on the running time is also a bound on the query complexity, the converse is not true in general.
Indeed, using the Newman-Sohler theorem it is not hard to see that
there are undecidable properties
that are constant query testable, as is noted in
[
2
]
;
the idea is to let the number of vertices of edgeless graphs
represent the Goedel numbering of Turing machines, and to let the property be the class of
graphs representing a Turing machine that halts on empty input. Since this property is a
class of edgeless graphs it is hyperfinite and thus constant-query testable.

In practice,
sublinear running time is highly desirable
. This motivates our aim of providing
meta-theorems for property testing with constant
running time
.
Towards this we use the framework of logic, which allows to treat many properties simultaneously.
First-order logic on graphs
(\textsc{FO})
speaks about the edge relation and allows quantification over vertices as usual.
The logic
\operatorname{FOMOD}

Techniques.

Our proof techniques rely on a version of Hanf normal form for
\operatorname{FOMOD}
formulas, which is based on
a result by Nurmonen
[
16
]
and was further investigated in
[
12
]
. We carefully tailor it to our classes

\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{d}
in such a way that it can be translated into speaking about isomorphism types of connected components.
This allows us to reduce testing an
\operatorname{FOMOD}
-property
\varphi
to testing for satisfaction of a

k
-capped component histogram vector with modulo counting
. Such a histogram vector
\overline{v}
is indexed by the
isomorphism types of connected components of size at most
c
.
For each such component type
t

the entry in
\overline{v}[t]
is either an integer between
0
and
k-1
(
rare
types), or it is of the
form ‘
\geq k
and congruent
i
mod
m
’ (
frequent
types).
Here
k,i,m\in\mathbb{N}
are natural numbers with
i<m
, that only depend on
\varphi
. We say that a graph
G\in\mathcal{C}

satisfies

\overline{v}
if
for each isomorphism type
t
, the number of occurrences of components in
G
of type
t
obeys the entry

\overline{v}[t]
of the histogram vector
\overline{v}
.
Given access to an input graph
G\in\mathcal{C}
on
n
vertices (which we may assume is large), our algorithm
picks a constant number of vertices in
G
at random, explores their surrounding connected components
and thus discovers a constant size subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
.
If a component of
G^{\prime}
is rare according to
\overline{v}
, then the algorithm rejects. Otherwise it does an
arithmetic check to determine whether the graph is close to satisfying
\varphi
.
In the correctness proof, we check closeness by trying to ‘plant’ components as required by the rare components and the remainders in

\overline{v}
, then we fill the remaining of the
n
vertices with copies of a graph similar to
G^{\prime}

in order to preserve satisfaction of
\overline{v}
.
Finally, there may be a small number of vertices left to fill and we check for consistent ‘patchability’ using
arithmetic techniques including
the Frobenius Coin Theorem (cf.
e. g.

[
14
]
).

In
[

]

Logic

First-order logic
(FO) formulas on graphs are built from predicates for the edge relation and equality, using Boolean connectives
\land,\lor,\neg
as well as existential and universal quatifiers
\exists,\forall
, where the variables represent graph vertices. A

of FO is a formula in which every variable is in the scope of a quantifier. The semantics of FO are defined in the usual way. If a graph
G
satisfies a sentence
\varphi
we write
G\models\varphi

\varphi
of FO
defines
a graph property

if the members of the property are exactly the graphs which satisfy
\varphi
.

Example 3
.

The formula

\varphi=\exists x\Bigl(\forall y\bigl(\neg Exy\bigr)\Bigr)\land\exists x\exists y\Bigl(Exy\land\forall z\bigl((Exz\to z=y)\land(Eyz\to z=x)\bigr)\Bigr)

defines the property of graphs which contain both an isolated vertex and an isolated edge as connected components. Its negation
\neg\varphi
will be our running example.

First-order logic with counting
(
\operatorname{FOMOD}
) extends
FO
by modular counting quantifiers
\exists^{j(\operatorname{mod}m)}x

for a variable
x
and
j,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
j<\ell
.

Monadic second-order logic
(
\operatorname{MSO}
) extends

by also additionally allowing quantification over subsets of vertices.
Formally, we have two types of variables,

(denoted by small letters

) which are interpreted by vertices, and

(denoted by capital
letters

) which are interpreted by subsets of vertices. In addtion to the atomic first-order formula we also have the formula
Xx
saying

is an element of set

. Furthermore, we
have universal and existential quantification both over individual variables and set variables.

Monadic second-order logic with counting
(
\operatorname{CMSO}
) extends
\operatorname{MSO}
by modular counting quantifiers

and

and

Property testing

We will use the bounded-degree model for property testing introduced in
[
11
]
. A property testing algorithm
\mathcal{A}

G

i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, returns the
j
th neighbour of the vertex

i
has less than
j

We assume that oracle queries are answered in constant time.
An
edge modification

V(G)
. The
distance

\operatorname{dist}(G,H)
between graphs
G

Frobenius Coin Theorem

We will apply the following results of number theory to the question of whether a graph on a given number of vertices can be created as a disjoint union of pre-described connected component types. A
conical combination
of integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is a sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}
for some natural numbers
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
. A collection of integers is
setwise coprime
if their greatest common divisor is 1.
For integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
we write
\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
\operatorname{lcm}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
to denote their greatest common divisor and lowest common multiple respectively.

Theorem 8

(
[
14
]
)
.

For every setwise coprime subset of the positive integers
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
there exists a largest integer which is not expressible as a conical combination of
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
. This integer is called the Frobenius number.

Corollary 9
.

There exists a function
F:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}
which maps
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
to the largest multiple of

which is not expressible as a conical combination of
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.

Proof.

Notice that if
\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=g
, then the integers
\frac{1}{g}a_{1},\dots,\frac{1}{g}a_{n}
are setwise coprime and thus by the previous theorem there exists a largest integer
x
not expressible as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\left(\frac{a_{i}}{g}\right)

for any
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that

is
not
expressible as a conical combination of
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
.
Further for any integer

, the product
gy

is
expressible as a conical combination of
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
as

\frac{1}{g}a_{1},\dots,\frac{1}{g}a_{n}
.
