Copy-as-Decode: Grammar-Constrained Parallel Prefill for LLM Editing
Ziyang Liu

Abstract
Large language models edit text and code by autoregressively regenerating the full output, even when most tokens appear verbatim in the input. We study a decoding-layer mechanism, Copy-as-Decode , that recasts edit generation as structured decoding over a two-primitive grammar— <copy lines="i-j"/> references an input line range, <gen> … </gen> emits new content. A token-level FSM guarantees syntactic validity, and a serving-layer primitive updates the KV cache for each copy span via a single parallel-prefill forward rather than N N autoregressive steps, sharing the parallel-forward kernel of speculative decoding but with input tokens as the draft and program-enforced acceptance in place of probabilistic verification. The contribution is the mechanism together with an upper-bound analysis of its payoff that requires no end-to-end model training. We report two quantitative bounds and one correctness property. (i) Kernel speedup: on Qwen2.5-{1.5B, 7B}, copying N N tokens via parallel prefill is 6.8 × 6.8\times – 303 × 303\times faster than emitting them autoregressively ( N ∈ [ 8,512 ] N\in[8,512] , A100 80GB bf16). (ii) Copy ceiling: on ProbeEdit and HumanEvalPack-Fix (Py/JS), 74 74 – 98 % 98\% of gold tokens are reachable under the line-level primitive; composed with the empirical kernel s ⁡ ( N ) s(N) over each corpus’s exact span histogram this yields the closed-form wall-clock bound bnd exact = 29.0 × / 3.4 × / 4.2 × \mathrm{bnd}_{\mathrm{exact}}=29.0\times/3.4\times/4.2\times (ProbeEdit / Py / JS; 13.0 × 13.0\times pooled). A token-level extension reaches 91 91 – 99 % 99\% coverage with conservative floors of 4.5 4.5 – 6.5 × 6.5\times . (iii) Pipeline losslessness: oracle programs round-trip through the deterministic resolver on all 482 482 cases, an identifiability precondition that localizes any downstream failure to span selection rather than the mechanism. A perturbation study shows pooled EM drops from 100 % 100\% to 15.48 % 15.48\% under off-by-one noise, quantifying the span-selection precision required. A supervised fine-tuning pilot on Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B ( 131 131 – 385 385 oracle programs, 3 3 seeds, 99 99 held-out attempts per configuration) lifts HEvalFix-Py EM from 0 / 33 0/33 (untrained, any strategy) to 12 12 – 17 % 17\% (Wilson 95 % 95\% CI [ 7.0 , 26.0 ] [7.0,26.0] ), a learnability signal, not a production-accuracy span selector. We do not claim batched-serving integration or agentic multi-file coverage; these are scoped as direct follow-up.

Grammar over two primitives.
We replace free-form output with a programmatic grammar

program := ’<program>’ (copy | gen)* ’</program>’

copy := ’<copy lines="i-j"/>’

gen := ’<gen>’ free_text ’</gen>’

enforced by a token-level finite-state machine that restricts the model’s logits to tokens whose decoded prefix is consistent with the current grammar state. The decoder has three branches at each op boundary (start a copy, start a gen, end the program); inside a copy op the model picks only the numeric line indices; inside a gen op the model runs free until
</gen>

Copy spans cost one parallel prefill.
After the model commits to
<copy lines="i-j"/>

i..j

2
Method: Copy-as-Decode

Copy-as-Decode sits at the intersection of structured constrained decoding and speculative decoding (
Figure

1
). The mechanism has three components—a two-primitive grammar, a token-level finite-state machine that enforces it, and a parallel-prefill splice that realizes a copy span in one forward pass—each introduced below.

2.1
Grammar and Problem Formulation

Given an input document
D=l_{1}l_{2}\cdots l_{n}
(indexed by lines) and an edit instruction
I
, the goal is to produce the edited document
D^{\prime}
. Rather than autoregressively generating
D^{\prime}
token by token, we have the model emit a
program
over a two-primitive grammar:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Program}

\displaystyle::=\text{{<program>}}\;\mathrm{Op}^{*}\;\text{{</program>}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Op}

\displaystyle::=\mathrm{Copy}\mid\mathrm{Gen}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Copy}

\displaystyle::=\text{{<copy lines="}}i\text{{-}}j\text{{"/>}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Gen}

\displaystyle::=\text{{<gen>}}\;\textsc{FreeText}\;\text{{</gen>}}

A deterministic
resolver

\mathcal{R}
consumes a Program
P
and the immutable input
D
, and yields
D^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}(P,D)
. Line indices reference the
original

D
(no renumbering after edits).

Cost model.

Let
T
be the total token length of
D^{\prime}
,
T_{\mathrm{copy}}
the subset emitted by
<copy>
ops,
T_{\mathrm{gen}}=T-T_{\mathrm{copy}}
the subset emitted inside
<gen>
ops, and
K
the number of copy ops. Let
\tau
denote the single-token autoregressive decode latency on the target hardware (ms/token;
\tau\approx 24
ms on Qwen2.5-7B bf16 A100 80GB at a 1024-token prefix, Appendix
H
). Under full-regeneration the model incurs

L_{\mathrm{AR}}\;=\;\tau\cdot T.

(1)

Under Copy-as-Decode, it runs
T_{\mathrm{gen}}
autoregressive decode steps, one parallel-prefill forward per copy span of length
N_{k}
(cost
\tau\cdot N_{k}/s(N_{k})
, where
s(N)
is the empirical per-span speedup of §
4.1
), one short forced-template prefill per op opener/closer (cost
c_{\mathrm{forced}}
), and a mask construction plus CPU–GPU synchronization per op (cost
c_{\mathrm{mask}}+c_{\mathrm{sync}}
); Appendix
H
measures all three fixed costs. The total wall-clock is therefore

L_{\mathrm{CaD}}\;=\;\underbrace{\tau\cdot T_{\mathrm{gen}}}_{\text{gen decode}}\;+\;\underbrace{\tau\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{N_{k}}{s(N_{k})}}_{\text{copy splices}}\;+\;\underbrace{K\cdot(c_{\mathrm{mask}}+c_{\mathrm{sync}}+c_{\mathrm{forced}})}_{\text{fixed per-op overhead}}

(2)

and the realized speedup over full regeneration,
L_{\mathrm{AR}}/L_{\mathrm{CaD}}
, upper-bounds at the fixed-cost-agnostic closed form (dropping the third term, a valid inequality since
c_{\mathrm{mask}}+c_{\mathrm{sync}}+c_{\mathrm{forced}}\geq 0
)

\mathrm{Speedup}(D,D^{\prime})\;\leq\;\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{gen}}\;+\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_{k}/s(N_{k})}.

(3)

Measured overhead:

c_{\mathrm{mask}}=0.02\pm 0.01
ms,
c_{\mathrm{sync}}=0.23\pm 0.05
ms,
c_{\mathrm{forced}}=1.1\pm 0.2
ms per op on Qwen2.5-1.5B (Appendix
H
); for
K\leq 20
the combined overhead is
<30
ms,
<1\%
of a typical ProbeEdit edit, so (
3
) is tight. Two closed-form evaluations of (
3
) bracket the realized speedup:

•

Aspirational
(all spans length
\bar{N}
):
1/((1{-}f)+f/s(\bar{N}))
with
f=T_{\mathrm{copy}}/T
. Over-optimistic when short spans in the tail have smaller
s(N_{k})
than
s(\bar{N})
.

•

Conservative
(
s(N_{k})=s(m)
, minimum-viable splice length):
1/((1{-}f)+f/s(m))
.

Realized speedup lies between.
Table

2

\mathrm{bnd}_{\mathrm{exact}}

2.2
Resolver Semantics

The resolver is deterministic and pure:

1.

For each
<copy lines="
i
-
j
"/>
op, emit the verbatim text of
l_{i}\cdots l_{j}
from
D
, joined by newlines.

2.

For each
<gen>
s
</gen>
op, emit
s
with at most one leading and one trailing newline stripped (a readability allowance, preserving internal blank lines).

3.

Concatenate ops with a single newline separator.

The resolver’s default behavior on out-of-range or malformed indices is to raise, but a single flag toggles between strict (raise on any malformed op) and clipped (clip indices to
[1,n]
, emit a warning) semantics—we benchmark both modes explicitly in
Table

4
. It has no nondeterminism and no dependence on the model. §
4.3
measures
pipeline losslessness
: on all
482
(doc, gold) pairs across three corpora, oracle programs induced from line-level alignment reconstruct gold exactly under strict semantics—the strict-vs-clipped distinction matters only at deployment under a model that can emit invalid indices, not for the oracle-derivation pipeline itself.

Reserved literals and principled escape.

The grammar’s five structural substrings (
<copy
,
</copy>
,
<gen>
,
</gen>
,
</program>
) must not appear verbatim inside a
<gen>
-body. We handle this in two complementary places:

•

Preprocessing escape.
Each reserved substring in gold is replaced by a Unicode entity (e.g.
</gen>
\to
⟨/gen⟩
via U+27E8/U+27E9/U+2024, unused by all tokenizers we checked); resolver reverses before emit. For any gold
g
,
\mathrm{decode}(\mathrm{encode}(g))=g
and
\mathrm{encode}
is injective on inputs free of the entities—holds on
482/482
cases and
10{,}000
adversarial fuzz strings (Appendix
I
).

•

FSM end-of-gen detector.
Exact token-id-sequence match against the
3
-token Qwen BPE encoding of
</gen>
, not a substring check on decoded text. Byte-level overlaps split across token boundaries cannot mis-trigger the detector (Appendix
I
).

2.3
Token-Level Grammar-Constrained Decoder

A free-form decoder cannot be trusted to emit a valid Program: after a copy-span splice it has just “seen itself output” a long run of document-like tokens and naturally continues regenerating content. We enforce the grammar at the token level via a small FSM (5 states:
AWAIT_OP
,
AFTER_LT
,
COPY_LINES
,
GEN_BODY
,
CLOSE
; see Appendix
A
) that intersects the model’s logits with the set of tokens consistent with the current state. For Qwen2.5-family tokenizers (and any BPE tokenizer encoding the structural literals as fixed token sequences), the relevant token ids are fixed constants. The FSM alternates three step types:

•

Masked model steps
at decision points: argmax restricted to
\{\texttt{<},\texttt{</}\}
at op boundaries;
\{\texttt{copy},\texttt{gen}\}
for op type; digits plus
-
/
"
inside a copy’s line range.

•

Forced-template prefills
for fixed literal sequences (
_lines="
,
"/>
,
</gen>
,
</program>
), each spliced via a single parallel-prefill forward.

•

Free generation
inside
<gen>
bodies until the
3
-token Qwen encoding of
</gen>
is produced (Appendix
I
).

The structural portion is therefore never subject to model error; the model chooses only op types, line indices, and
<gen>
-body content. Parse rate is
100\%
by construction.

2.4
Copy-Span Splicing via Parallel Prefill

When the FSM exits the copy-line-index state with a chosen range
[i,j]
, we tokenize
l_{i}\cdots l_{j}
directly from
D
and append those tokens to the KV cache in a single forward pass:

\text{model}(\text{input\_ids}=[\![l_{i}\cdots l_{j}]\!],\;\text{past\_key\_values}=\mathrm{KV})

This is an ordinary transformer prefill forward over
N=|[\![l_{i}\cdots l_{j}]\!]|
tokens, not
N
decode forwards. The forward still computes all self-attention and feed-forward activations over the
N
tokens, but it does so in parallel within one launch, amortizing the per-step kernel overhead and, on modern hardware, running in the GEMM-bound regime rather than the memory-bandwidth-bound regime typical of single-token decode.

Copy-as-Decode shares the parallel-forward acceleration primitive of speculative decoding
(
Leviathan et al. 2023
;
Xia et al. 2023
;
Cai et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
but differs in the source of the
N
-token block (input document vs. draft model) and in the distributional contract (grammar commitment vs. probabilistic acceptance). It is not a distributional special case of speculative decoding; it reuses the parallel-prefill kernel but changes what is being decoded (detailed comparison in §
6
). The same grammar can also be used as a prompt format for closed models without KV-cache access (Appendix
Q
), which retains the output-token compression benefit but not the kernel speedup.

Perfect line-level span selection round-trips to gold on all

Pointer-decoder baseline.

A natural architectural alternative is a span-level pointer decoder
(
Vinyals et al. 2015
)
: attach three classification heads to a base LM (op-type, span-start, span-end), training end-to-end to emit the same {
copy
,
gen
} op sequence without serializing through XML text. The information content per
copy

Copy-as-Decode

3
autoregressive forward passes (op-type
+
start
+
end), while
Copy-as-Decode
pays
2
(FSM open/close)
+

1
(forced template prefill)
+

(parallel-prefill splice)
=

3+N/s(N)

\approx

is a format layered on any instruction-tuned LM. A token-level pointer (predict input-position at every copied token) is strictly dominated:
N
forward passes vs
N/s(N)
. The mechanism claim is thus not “copy is free”—a span-level pointer achieves that with architectural surgery—but “copy can be made format-level and still get most of the way there,” which is the deployable property.

Related Work

Speculative decoding and draft-based acceleration.

Blockwise parallel decoding
(
Stern et al. 2018
)
first observed that a single parallel forward can verify multiple draft tokens at once; speculative decoding
(
Leviathan et al. 2023
;
Xia et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
generalized this with a draft-model/target-model distinction and a probabilistic acceptance rule that preserves the target distribution. Medusa
(
Cai et al. 2024
)
replaces the separate draft model with extra prediction heads on the target; EAGLE
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024b

(
Miao et al. 2024
)
extends to tree-based draft expansion;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
explore multi-candidate and self-drafting variants; REST
(
He et al. 2024
)
retrieves drafts from a corpus. Across this family, drafts are produced by
some
learned predictor and accepted probabilistically to preserve the target distribution.

Copy-as-Decode shares the parallel-forward primitive but differs in two architectural respects: the “draft” is the input document (not a smaller-model prediction) selected by grammar commitment, and acceptance is unconditional (not probabilistic)—so Copy-as-Decode does not preserve speculative decoding’s free-form target distribution, redefining the decoded task as an edit program. In exchange it avoids draft-model training and obtains a trivial parallel-forward ratio equal to the AR-vs-PP kernel ratio. The two methods compose: within a
<gen>
body, any speculative-decoding scheme can further accelerate free-text generation.

Structured constrained decoding enforces an output grammar at the token level: PICARD

A parallel line of systems work focuses on driving per-step mask construction near zero: XGrammar
(
Dong et al. 2025
)
compiles a context-free grammar into an adaptive token-mask cache; SGLang
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
amortizes mask computation across requests via runtime sharing. Our FSM sits at the lower bound of this cost spectrum:
5

12

7

Edit formats and apply models.

Production tools use a variety of output formats for code and document edits. Aider
(
Gauthier 2024
)
uses search/replace blocks, where the model emits both the verbatim text to find and the verbatim text to replace; unified-diff emits the standard
@@

(
Cursor 2024
)
and Anthropic’s text-editor tool are closed-model apply pipelines whose training recipes are not public. Related work on editing as a task—SWE-bench
(

(
Cassano et al. 2024
)

(
Muennighoff et al. 2024

All of these require the model to re-emit structural context (search anchors, hunk headers, or the entire edited span) through ordinary autoregressive decoding. Copy-as-Decode differs in replacing the model-produced reference with a grammar-enforced one, and in splicing the referenced tokens into the KV cache rather than paying decode cost for them. §
4.4

Pointer networks
(

)
and copy mechanisms
(

(

)

(

)

(

(

Prompt-level structured output for edits.

Closed-model structured-output prompting
(
Liu 2026
)
has been studied as an edit format—the model emits a line-referencing program that a deterministic resolver expands. Our earlier work on CRP falls in this category; we treat it here as the prompt-level variant of Copy-as-Decode (Appendix
Q

7
Discussion and Conclusion

We recast edit generation as structured decoding over a two-primitive grammar, enforced by a token-level FSM and accelerated by a parallel-prefill splice that replaces
N
autoregressive decode steps with one forward. The contribution is the mechanism together with three measurable properties—two quantitative bounds and one correctness property—independent of model quality:
(i)
one parallel-prefill forward is
6.8\times
–
303\times
faster than
N
autoregressive decodes for
N\in[8,512]
on Qwen2.5-{1.5B, 7B};
(ii)

74
–
98\%
of gold tokens are realizable under the line-level primitive on ProbeEdit and HumanEvalPack-Fix, which composed with the kernel curve over each corpus’s exact span histogram yields the closed-form wall-clock bound
\mathrm{bnd}_{\mathrm{exact}}=29.0\times/3.4\times/4.2\times
(ProbeEdit / Py / JS,
13.0\times
pooled);
(iii)
oracle programs round-trip byte-exactly on all
482
cases—an identifiability precondition that localizes any downstream EM failure to span selection. A perturbation study (pooled EM
100\%\to 15.48\%
under off-by-one noise) gives an operational target for any trained selector.

Scope.

In scope: contiguous line-range copy (and, in the token-level extension of §
5
, contiguous token-range copy) from a single input document to a single output document, with
K\leq 20
ops per edit on the evaluated corpora. Out of scope for the present paper: multi-file and agentic workloads (SWE-bench-style); AST-granular edits whose operands are subtrees rather than spans; splice integration into the vLLM/SGLang scheduler (format-only latency gains on vLLM
0.19
continuous batching are measured in §
4.5
; paged-attention splice integration is scaffolded in Appendix
K
and deferred).

Limitations and future work.

The FSM’s fixed-id table is tokenizer-conditional and resolved once at load (Appendix
K
:
\geq 7/14
of our structural literals are single-token across Qwen2.5, DeepSeek-Coder, and CodeLlama-family tokenizers; fragmented tokenizers add
\approx 0.02
ms per literal). Reserved-literal substrings in gold are handled by a principled Unicode-entity escape (§
2.2
,
I
), so “the gold-audit holds on our corpora” is no longer the load-bearing claim. Line granularity loses coverage to sub-expression rewrites; the
74
–
98\%
vs.
93
–
99\%
line-vs-token gap (
Table

2
, Appendix
N
) is closed by the token-indexed
<copy tokens>
primitive, which we formalize and measure as an oracle-ceiling in this draft but whose realized selector training we defer. The pilot selector at
14
–
18\%
EM is a learnability signal, not a deployment result. Four concrete follow-ups: (i) scale the selector to
7
B QLoRA with larger corpora and span-end-targeted supervision (span-end accuracy is the dominant error, §
5
); (ii) productize the token-level
<copy tokens>
primitive under a trained selector; (iii) integrate the splice into vLLM/SGLang continuous batching with paged attention and report E2E latency/throughput at realistic QPS, above the format-only
2.1
–
3.3\times
floor we measure in §
4.5
; (iv) apply in code-editing agent loops and on SWE-bench-style benchmarks. Code released on acceptance.

Algorithm.

Appendix G
Mechanism Ablation: Grammar vs. Splice

The mechanism has two independently removable components: the token-level FSM (which guarantees parse rate) and the parallel-prefill splice (which provides kernel speedup). Their contributions act on different axes.

•

Remove the FSM, keep prompt-level grammar and the splice.
The decoder can drift out of the grammar mid-generation; our 4-case pilot shows prompt-level CRP yielding
0/4
parse on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct under greedy decoding (plausible copy tags but wandering line indices or missing closing tags). Wall-clock is below full regen because fewer tokens are emitted before cutoff, but EM collapses.

•

Remove the splice, keep the FSM.
The decoder still produces a syntactically valid program and the resolver still reconstructs gold from an oracle program; what is lost is the
s(N)
factor on copy spans. Wall-clock approaches full regeneration with output-compression advantage only—exactly the closed-model setting where KV-cache access is unavailable. Pipeline-losslessness is intact.

The FSM is required for correctness; the splice is required for speed.
Table

11
isolates the splice independently of model quality; §
4.3
establishes the resolver’s
482/482
oracle round-trip, isolating the FSM/grammar contract.

Round-trip guarantee.

•

Token-sequence end-of-gen detector.

The streaming decoder (

) uses exact token-id-sequence match against the Qwen BPE encoding of
</gen>
(3 tokens:
