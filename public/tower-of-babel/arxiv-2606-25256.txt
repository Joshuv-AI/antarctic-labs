Pre-Warm: Input-Conditioned Weight Initialization for Convolutional Neural Networks
Rowan Martinsn

Abstract
We introduce Pre-Warm, a simple yet effective zero-training-cost method for data-conditioned initialization of the first convolutional layer. Before the first forward pass, Pre-Warm extracts mean-centered local patches from a single training batch, clusters them with MiniBatchKMeans, applies inverse Manhattan spatial weighting, and uses the resulting centroids to initialize half of the first-layer filters (the remainder retain Kaiming initialization). We derive closed-form rules for all hyperparameters except a single insensitive scale parameter, though we derive a Kaiming parity bound on scale from patch dimensionality. For grayscale datasets we use Otsu’s foreground density; for natural color images we use the mean L2 norm of mean-centered patches. Both rules accurately predict the optimal patch count observed in grid search. Across five standard benchmarks - MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, SVHN, and CIFAR-100 - and 8-seed paired experiments, Pre-Warm yields statistically significant accuracy improvements over standard Kaiming initialization (p ¡ 0.05 on all datasets, p = 0.0007 on SVHN with 8/8 wins, p = 0.0033 on CIFAR-100 with 7/8 wins). The method adds negligible overhead, requires no architectural changes, and integrates into existing training pipelines with only a few lines of code. Pre-Warm demonstrates that even a lightweight, input-dependent signal can meaningfully improve optimization trajectories in modern convolutional networks.



1
Introduction

The choice of initial weights determines the starting geometry of the loss
landscape. Kaiming initialization
(
4
)
and Glorot initialization

(
2
)
are statistically principled - they preserve
activation variance across layers - but make no reference to the actual data the
network will process. Every dataset, regardless of its local structure, receives
an identical initialization drawn from a scaled random distribution.

We propose that even a lightweight, zero-cost signal from the data can meaningfully
inform the starting state of the first layer. This is not a claim about replacing
the optimization process, but about giving it a better starting point in a
principled and automatic way.

Pre-Warm targets only the first convolutional layer, adds no trainable parameters,
requires no gradient steps, and completes in under 0.1 seconds on a T4 GPU. The key
insight is that
k
-means on mean-centered local patches naturally discovers the
dominant edge and texture patterns in the image distribution - precisely what the
first convolutional layer is known to learn
(
3
)
. Seeding
those filters with data-derived centroids reduces the number of gradient steps
required to encode this structure.

2
Related Work

Data-dependent initialization

9
initialize
convolutional weights using statistics from mini-batch forward passes. LSUV

(
13
)
normalizes each layer’s output variance iteratively using
real data. Both methods operate at the dataset level and require forward passes
through the network. Pre-Warm is per-batch, requires no forward pass, and operates
entirely in input space at negligible cost.

Unsupervised pre-training

Greedy layer-wise pretraining

(
6
)
uses RBMs or autoencoders to provide informed initializations
before supervised fine-tuning. These methods are computationally expensive and have
been largely supplanted by modern normalization and residual architectures

(
5
)
. Pre-Warm recovers the core intuition - use unsupervised
structure to inform initialization at essentially zero cost.

Dictionary learning and sparse coding

15

showed that sparse coding on natural image patches produces Gabor-like filters
matching V1 receptive fields.
K
-means on local patches is a tractable
approximation of this process. Pre-Warm applies this observation directly to filter
initialization, with a principled derivation of how many patches to sample.

Meta-learning

MAML
(
1
)
learns an initialization that
adapts quickly to new tasks through second-order optimization. Pre-Warm can be
interpreted as a static, zero-cost approximation of this principle: conditioning
initial parameters on input statistics rather than learning the conditioning
mechanism. It recovers a fraction of the benefit at orders-of-magnitude lower cost.

3.4
Pre-Warm configuration

The conditioned runs apply Pre-Warm to

n_{\text{clusters}}=F/2=16

Grayscale datasets

(

n_{\text{patches}}=\lfloor(F/4)\cdot(1/d)\rfloor

; on Fashion-MNIST,
\tau^{*}=0.3809

and
n_{\text{patches}}=22
. The scale is
\sigma=0.20
for both datasets.

Color datasets

thresholds cannot distinguish. We instead derive
n_{\text{patches}}
from the mean
L2 norm of mean-centered patches - the same patches the conditioner already
extracts - via
n_{\text{patches}}=\lfloor(F/4)\cdot c\cdot\overline{\|\tilde{p}\|_{2}}\rfloor
with
c\approx 6.65
. On CIFAR-10 we formulated a prediction of
28
. On SVHN we formulated a prediction of
16
. For CIFAR-100 the estimated number of patches needed is
16
. The scale is
\sigma=0.25
for all
three color datasets, compensating for the threefold increase in patch
dimensionality (
3\times 3\times 3=27
versus
9
) and keeping conditioned
filter energy near the Kaiming standard-deviation scale for
C_{\text{in}}=3
.

3.5
Hypothesis and statistical protocol

A single paired experiment per dataset tests the following hypothesis: does Pre-Warm improve final test accuracy? For each seed we record the paired final accuracies of the conditioned and
Kaiming models and apply a one-sided paired
t
-test with the alternative

\mu_{\text{Pre-Warm}}>\mu_{\text{Kaiming}}
. We additionally report the
seed-level win count (the number of seeds for which Pre-Warm finishes ahead).

4
Method

4.1
Overview

Pre-Warm conditions the first convolutional layer of a network on a single batch of
training images before any gradient step is taken. The core pipeline is identical
across all datasets: extract local patches, mean-center them, run
MiniBatchKMeans, apply inverse Manhattan distance spatial weighting to the centroids,
normalize to a target scale, and write the resulting tensors into the first

n_{\text{clusters}}
filters of
conv1
. The remaining filters retain
Kaiming initialization, preserving the random diversity that allows the network to
represent patterns not captured by k-means.

The method has four hyperparameters:
patch_size
,
n_clusters
,

n_patches
, and
scale
. The central empirical contribution of this
section is that three of the four are governed by closed-form rules derived from
architecture dimensions and dataset statistics, leaving
scale
as the only
free parameter - and
scale
is empirically insensitive within a
dataset-type-specific range. We derive these rules in two stages: first via a
full grid search on Fashion-MNIST, which establishes the grayscale laws, then via
extension to natural color images.

4.2
Pipeline

Given the first training batch
\mathcal{B}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
and a first
convolutional layer with
F
output channels,
C_{\text{in}}
input channels, and
kernel size
K
, Pre-Warm proceeds as follows.

Step 1 - Patch extraction
Sample
n_{\text{patches}}
random

K\times K
patches per image. Each patch
p\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K\times C_{\text{in}}}
is flattened and mean-centered:

\tilde{p}\leftarrow p-\bar{p}

(1)

Mean-centering removes the DC brightness component, so the patch collection encodes
spatial structure (edges, textures) rather than absolute luminance.

Step 2 - Clustering
Run MiniBatchKMeans with

n_{\text{clusters}}=\lfloor F/2\rfloor
on the full patch collection

\{\tilde{p}\}
. This produces
n_{\text{clusters}}
centroid vectors

\{c_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}C_{\text{in}}}
.

Step 3 - Spatial weighting
Weight each element of each centroid by its
inverse Manhattan distance from the filter center

\mathbf{c}=\lfloor K/2\rfloor
:

w_{ij}=\frac{1}{1+|i-\mathbf{c}|+|j-\mathbf{c}|}

(2)

This down-weights corner pixels, consistent with how convolutional filters
concentrate discriminative energy near the receptive field center.

Step 4 - Normalization
Normalize the weighted centroid matrix to a
target standard deviation
\sigma
:

C\leftarrow\frac{C}{\mathrm{std}(C)+\varepsilon}\cdot\sigma

(3)

Step 5 - Assignment
Reshape each centroid to

(C_{\text{in}},K,K)
and copy into the first
n_{\text{clusters}}
filters of

conv1
. The remaining
F-n_{\text{clusters}}
filters retain Kaiming
initialization.

4.3
Hyperparameter Rules: Grayscale Datasets

We established the design rules via a grid search on Fashion-MNIST, sweeping all
four hyperparameters:

\texttt{patch\_size}\in\{3,5\}
,

n_{\text{clusters}}\in\{8,16,32,64\}
,

n_{\text{patches}}\in\{10,15,20,25,30,50,100\}
,
and
\texttt{scale}\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3\}
,
over 8 seeds each, evaluated by paired
t
-test on convergence loss at 500 steps.
The results were decisive on three of the four axes.

patch_size
=K
All
patch_size
=3
configurations produced
positive loss deltas; no
patch_size
=5
configuration achieved significance.
When
patch_size
>K
, centroids must be center-cropped to match the filter
shape, discarding the outer ring where edge-transition information is concentrated.
The rule is: patch size must equal kernel size.

n_{\text{clusters}}=F/2
Only
n_{\text{clusters}}=16
(
=F/2
)
achieved significant results. Too few conditioned filters (
n_{\text{clusters}}\ll F/2
) provide insufficient signal coverage; too many (
n_{\text{clusters}}\geq F
)
overwrite the entire filter bank, eliminating the Kaiming diversity that allows the
network to represent patterns not captured by k-means. Conditioning exactly half the
filter bank balances structured prior with stochastic exploration.

n_{\text{patches}}
: Otsu foreground density rule

Statistical significance appeared exclusively at
n_{\text{patches}}=25
and
disappeared at both
n_{\text{patches}}=20
and
n_{\text{patches}}=30
, revealing a
sharp empirical threshold. Below the threshold, informative patches are too sparse
per cluster for reliable centroid estimation; above it, background patches
- Fashion-MNIST images are approximately
65\%
background -
dilute centroids toward near-zero vectors that carry no more information than random
Kaiming weights. We derive the threshold from Otsu foreground density

(
16
)
. Otsu’s method estimates
\tau^{*}
as:

\tau^{*}=\arg\max_{\tau}\;\omega_{0}(\tau)\,\omega_{1}(\tau)\,[\mu_{0}(\tau)-\mu_{1}(\tau)]^{2}

(4)

giving foreground density
d=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}[x_{i}>\tau^{*}]
. The
patch count rule is then:

n_{\text{patches}}=\left\lfloor\frac{F}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\right\rfloor

(5)

Equation
5
arises from a dominance condition: with
F/2

clusters and foreground density
d
, the expected number of informative patches per
cluster is
n_{\text{patches}}\cdot d\cdot(2/F)
. Requiring at least 2 informative
patches per cluster and accounting for the effective patch dimensionality being
approximately
2
–
3
rather than the full
K^{2}
yields the
(F/4)
prefactor. On
Fashion-MNIST (
d=0.3602
) the formula predicts
n_{\text{patches}}=22
; a
confirmatory run at
n_{\text{patches}}=22
yielded
p=0.0025
, confirming the
formula is predictive rather than coincidental. Applied to MNIST (
d=0.1373
) with
no retuning, it predicts
n_{\text{patches}}=58
; a confirmatory run at this value yields
p=0.0109
(
7/8
wins), confirming the
formula generalizes across grayscale datasets without modification.

scale
\in[0.1,0.3]
Results were statistically significant across

\texttt{scale}\in\{0.1,0.2,0.25\}
at the optimal
n_{\text{patches}}
,
indicating that the method is insensitive to the exact scale value within this range.
We use
\sigma=0.20
for grayscale datasets throughout.

4.4
Extension to Color Datasets

Applying the grayscale rules directly to CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and SVHN required revisiting only
the
n_{\text{patches}}
formula and the scale. The
patch_size
=K
and

n_{\text{clusters}}=F/2
rules transfer unchanged: the geometric arguments that
govern them are architecture-level properties independent of channel count or pixel
distribution.

Scale
The grayscale scale of
\sigma=0.20
required a small upward
adjustment to
\sigma=0.25
for color images. The reason is dimensional: a

3\times 3\times 3
color patch spans
27
dimensions versus the
9
of a grayscale
patch. Global normalization distributes centroid energy over a larger space, so a
slightly larger scale is needed to maintain the same effective signal-to-noise ratio
relative to the random Kaiming weights of downstream layers. The optimal value

\sigma=0.25
sits near the Kaiming standard deviation for
C_{\text{in}}=3

(
\sigma_{\text{Kaiming}}=\sqrt{2/\text{fan\_in}}=\sqrt{2/27}\approx 0.272
),
suggesting that Kaiming parity is a useful upper bound for the scale.

n_{\text{patches}}
for color images: patch L2 norm rule

The Otsu-based formula in Equation
5
cannot be applied directly
to natural color images because its bimodal foreground/background assumption breaks
down. The mean L2 norm of mean-centered patches,
\overline{\|\tilde{p}\|_{2}}
, captures
exactly what the pixel distribution cannot: the average texture energy available to
k-means after brightness is removed. Patches with larger norm carry richer edge and
texture variation; k-means with a fixed codebook size
k=F/2
needs proportionally
more samples to populate a well-conditioned codebook over a high-energy distribution.
This motivates:

n_{\text{patches}}=\left\lfloor\frac{F}{4}\cdot c\cdot\overline{\|\tilde{p}\|_{2}}\right\rfloor\qquad c\approx 6.65

(6)

where
\tilde{p}
is a mean-centered
K\times K\times C_{\text{in}}
patch drawn
from the training set and
\overline{\|\tilde{p}\|_{2}}
is the empirical mean L2 norm
over a random sample. Importantly, this quantity is computed from the same patch
extraction step already performed inside
conditioned_init
, adding only a
single
np.linalg.norm
call before the k-means fit.

Equation
6
predicts
n_{\text{patches}}=27
for CIFAR-10
(
\overline{\|\tilde{p}\|_{2}}=0.522
) and
n_{\text{patches}}=16
for SVHN
(
\overline{\|\tilde{p}\|_{2}}=0.308
), leaving CIFAR-10 within
\pm 1
of its
grid-search optimum (
28
) and SVHN exactly at its optimum. Applied to CIFAR-100 with no retuning, the predicted number of patches is also
16
; running at this value yields
p=0.0033
(
7/8
wins),
confirming the formula regime transfers to the harder 100-class setting. By
contrast, all pixel-level formulas we experimented with ranked CIFAR-10 and SVHN in the wrong order,
predicting more patches for SVHN than for CIFAR-10 despite SVHN’s lower optimum.

5.3
Limitations and Future Work

While Pre-Warm demonstrates consistent gains across five standard benchmarks, several limitations remain. First, the method has only been evaluated on small-to-medium convolutional architectures and relatively simple datasets. Its effectiveness on larger models (e.g., ResNet, Vision Transformers) or more complex tasks (object detection, segmentation) is untested. Second, the current design conditions only the first convolutional layer; extending the approach to deeper layers or multiple layers simultaneously is a natural direction for future work.

Third, although the hyperparameter rules generalize well, the scale parameter still requires modest dataset-type-specific tuning (0.20 for grayscale vs. 0.25 for color). A fully automatic, theoretically grounded formula for scale remains an open question. Finally, the computational overhead, while negligible on modern hardware, could become more noticeable when training on extremely large datasets or when the first batch is not representative.

Despite these limitations, Pre-Warm provides a simple, practical, and theoretically motivated improvement to standard initialization practices with virtually zero cost.

6
Conclusion

We presented Pre-Warm, a lightweight, zero-training-cost method that conditions the initialization of the first convolutional layer on the statistics of a single training batch. By clustering mean-centered local patches and seeding half the filters with the resulting centroids, Pre-Warm injects meaningful data-dependent structure into the network while preserving the diversity of Kaiming initialization in the remaining filters.

Through careful empirical analysis on Fashion-MNIST, we derived simple closed-form rules for all hyperparameters except a single scale parameter. These rules successfully generalize to MNIST, CIFAR-10, SVHN, and CIFAR-100, yielding statistically significant accuracy improvements across all five benchmarks (p ¡ 0.05) with particularly strong results on SVHN (p = 0.0007, 8/8 wins) and CIFAR-100 (p = 0.0033, 7/8 wins,
+0.68
accuracy points on a 100-class task).

Pre-Warm demonstrates that even a minimal, input-aware signal at initialization can meaningfully improve optimization trajectories in modern convolutional networks. Its negligible computational cost, ease of implementation, and compatibility with standard architectures (including BatchNorm) make it a practical drop-in enhancement for a wide range of computer vision pipelines.

Future work includes extending the approach to deeper layers, exploring its interaction with larger architectures, and developing a fully unified theoretical framework for the scale parameter across diverse data regimes.
