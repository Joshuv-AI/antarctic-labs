Mechanical energy analysis identifies compensatory strategies in individuals with and without Non-specific chronic low back pain
Authors: Mohammad Yousefi, Mohsen Ramezani, Saeed Ilbeigi, Teddy Caderby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This quasi-experimental study aimed to identify biomechanical compensatory strategies during gait in individuals with non-specific chronic low back pain (NSCLBP) by analyzing mechanical energy expenditure (MEE) at the lower limb joints. Twenty-eight male participants (14 with NSCLBP and 14 healthy, mean age 27.9 ± 2.1 years) were assessed. Biomechanical data were collected using a three-dimensional motion analysis system and inverse dynamics method to calculate MEE in concentric, eccentric, and no-transfer (no energy transfer between segments) conditions for the ankle, knee, hip, and low back joints. The results revealed significantly higher eccentric MEE at the ankle, hip, and low back joints in healthy controls compared to NSCLBP patients (P≤0.05). No significant differences were found in concentric or no-transfer MEE at any joint, or eccentric MEE at the knee joint (P>0.05). These findings indicate disruptions in the eccentric energy flow at the low back, hip and ankle joints in individuals with NSCLBP, suggesting a reduction in mechanical efficiency. This highlights the importance of targeted rehabilitation interventions to improve gait efficiency in this population.

Introduction

Non-specific chronic low back pain (NSCLBP), defined as low back pain lasting 12 weeks or longer without identifiable structural causes, affects up to 83% of individuals with low back pain in industrialized countries1. This condition involves a complex interplay of biophysical, psychological, and social factors, complicating diagnosis and often leading to treatments that focus solely on symptom relief rather than addressing underlying movement impairments2,3. Studying gait biomechanics in NSCLBP is critical because altered movement patterns can reduce functional efficiency, increase disability, and impair quality of life4.

Research has consistently shown that NSCLBP alters gait biomechanics (for a review, see5. Compared to healthy individuals, those with NSCLBP exhibit a slower preferred walking speed, shorter stride length, reduced anti-phase coordination between the thorax and pelvis, decreased lumbar axial rotation, and lower lumbar flexion/extension and axial rotation moments during walking6–9. These kinematic and kinetic changes are commonly interpreted as biomechanical compensatory strategies intended to reduce pain or spinal loading during walking10. However, the extent to which these adaptations affect the mechanical efficiency of gait in individuals with NSCLBP remains poorly understood.

Mechanical energy expenditure (MEE) analysis is a method used to quantify biomechanical efficiency by assessing the total absolute mechanical work performed at a joint. MEE comprises two main components, which are fully expressed by equations (3) and Table 1: (1) concentric energy transfer between segments via concentric muscle action and (2) eccentric energy transfer between segments via eccentric muscle action or elastic structures of connective tissues11–16. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has examined the effect of NSCLBP on energy transfer across the lower limb joints during walking17. Specifically, these authors examined the mechanical energy compensation, defined as the ratio of the net joint MEE and the total absolute MEE at the joint. They reported that female athletes with NSCLBP exhibited a greater eccentric mechanical energy compensation coefficient at the hip joint, while no significant differences were found at the knee and ankle joints. These findings were interpreted as a potential compensatory strategy for dysfunction in the lumbosacral region.

Despite this important contribution, it is worth noting that this previous study focused exclusively on mechanical energy compensation, which reflects the proportion of muscle energy compensated by inter-segmental energy transfer16. However, it did not analyze joint MEE variables themselves and therefore provided no direct information on the amounts of energy transferred across joints (via concentric or eccentric actions) during walking. Such information may be particularly relevant for informing targeted rehabilitation approaches aimed at reducing NSCLBP-related disability.

Interestingly, it has been shown that individuals with NSCLBP exhibit increased lumbar paraspinal muscle activation during walking5, which may increase the trunk stiffness and explain the reduced dissociation between the thorax and pelvis during walking compared to healthy controls18. These changes in trunk stiffness could, in turn, impair energy transfer across lower limb joints, particularly at the low back and hip joints, in people with NSCLBP. However, this hypothesis has not yet been empirically tested.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of NSCLBP on MEE at the lower limb joints during gait. We hypothesized that NSCLBP disrupts normal energy flow due to lumbar muscle stiffness and/or altered movement patterns, which would be reflected in decreased MEE (concentric and/or eccentric), particularly at the low back and hip joints.

Methods

Participants

This quasi-experimental study recruited 28 male participants, including 14 with non-specific chronic low back pain (NSCLBP) and 14 healthy controls (mean age 27.9 ± 2.1 years). Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.1, Düsseldorf, Germany), assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), power of 0.80, and alpha of 0.05, requiring a minimum of 28 participants. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from local clinics and community centers in Tehran, Iran, between October 2023 and March 2024, with NSCLBP participants referred by physicians before outpatient physical therapy. NSCLBP diagnosis was confirmed by an experienced orthopedic physician based on clinical history and physical examination (pain lasting >12 weeks without red flags or specific pathology on imaging when performed). Participants were instructed to refrain from analgesic medication or any treatment for at least 24 hours prior to testing; none reported regular medication use. Limb dominance (preferred kicking leg) was recorded. Healthy controls had no history of low back pain or orthopedic, neurological, or cardio-respiratory conditions affecting neuromuscular function. The age range was limited to 20–45 years to reduce age-related variations in gait biomechanics, as older adults may show distinct movement patterns due to degenerative changes19. Only males were included to control for sex-related differences in gait kinematics and kinetics20. Athletes were not specifically excluded but were not recruited to ensure generalizability to the broader NSCLBP population. Eligible participants were cognitively intact and able to walk independently for at least 15 meters. Exclusion criteria included terminal illness, progressive neurological conditions, significant vision loss, acute pain, prior sacrolumbar or knee ligament surgery, complete ankle ligament rupture, or inability to walk21.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of University of Birjand (Ethics Approval Code: IR.BIRJAND.REC.1403.025). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to their inclusion in the study.

Height and body mass were measured to normalize biomechanical data. A 5-minute warm-up, including dynamic stretches (hip and knee stretches, leg swings) and light walking, prepared participants for gait analysis22. Nineteen 14-mm reflective markers were placed on anatomical landmarks using a modified Plug-in-Gait lower-limb model, suitable for gait analysis but limited for complex lumbar motion23,see Fig. 1). Markers were positioned at bilateral posterior and anterior superior iliac spines, mid-thigh (four-marker cluster), lateral and medial knee epicondyles, mid-shank (four-marker cluster), lateral and medial malleoli, calcaneus, second metatarsal head, and a three-pronged marker on the L3 vertebra spinous process by the same experienced researcher (intra-rater consistency ensured). Marker trajectories were captured at 100 Hz using an 8-camera infrared motion analysis system (Vicon, Oxford, UK). Ground reaction forces (GRFs) were recorded at 1000 Hz using a single force plate (Bertec Corp, Columbus, OH, USA). Participants maintained a static anatomical position for 3 seconds to establish a reference frame, followed by two practice walking trials at self-selected speed to ensure natural gait24. Data were collected from both lower limbs, with the average of three complete gait cycles per limb analyzed to ensure data reliability25. Because only one force plate was available, multiple walking trials were performed until at least three successful trials with complete foot contact were obtained separately for the right and left lower limbs (regardless of limb dominance). This procedure yielded a minimum of three complete gait cycles per limb with concurrent kinematic and kinetic data. Gait cycles were defined from ipsilateral heel-strike to ipsilateral heel-strike (detected from vertical ground reaction force).

Data processing and analysis

Kinematic and kinetic data were processed using Nexus v2.7 software and custom MATLAB scripts (the custom MATLAB scripts used for data analysis in this study are openly available in the GitHub repository: https://github.com/drmohammadyousefi2022-ux/Mechanical-Energy-Analysis-_MATLAB-code.git).

Joint mechanical power was calculated using the inverse dynamics approach, in which net joint moments and angular velocities are derived from marker trajectories and ground reaction forces. Mechanical energy expenditure (MEE) at each joint was then determined by integrating the absolute value of joint power over the duration of the stance phase. This approach allows quantification of energy generation and absorption across joints during gait.

To better understand energy transfer between adjacent segments, MEE was categorized into three primary conditions: concentric (energy generation), eccentric (energy absorption), and no-transfer (no net energy exchange between segments). These classifications were adapted and modified from previous biomechanical frameworks11–13,16,26,27. Six distinct modes of energy flow were defined based on the relationship between joint moment direction and angular velocity (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Independent-samples t-tests compared MEE between NSCLBP and healthy control groups for each joint and gait phase. The t-test was selected due to its suitability for comparing two independent groups with normally distributed data, verified by Shapiro–Wilk tests, and adequate power (0.80) for detecting medium effect sizes, as confirmed by the power analysis28. Homogeneity of variances was confirmed using Levene’s test. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d and interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), or large (0.8). Results

Differences in demographic and anthropometric characteristics, including age, height, body mass, body mass index, and gait speed, between the healthy and NSCLBP groups were assessed using independent-samples t-tests. No significant differences were observed, indicating comparable baseline characteristics between groups (Table 2). Gait speed was calculated using the trajectory of pelvic markers processed in MATLAB.

Mechanical energy expenditure (MEE) was analyzed across concentric, eccentric, and no-transfer conditions for the ankle, knee, hip, and low back joints (Aleshinsky, 1986a–e). Significantly higher eccentric MEE was observed in the ankle of healthy participants compared to those with NSCLBP (p = 0.001). Similarly, significant differences in eccentric MEE were identified in the hip (p = 0.02) and low back (p = 0.04) between healthy and NSCLBP groups. No significant differences were found in concentric or no-transfer MEE for any joint, nor in any MEE condition for the knee joint, suggesting that biomechanical differences were primarily associated with eccentric muscle activity in specific joints (Table 3).

The gait cycle was divided into five phases—weight acceptance (0–10%), mid-stance (10–50%), late-stance (50–60%), initial swing (60–80%), and terminal swing (80–100%)—to identify specific phases associated with differences in MEE (Kirtley, 2002). Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of NSCLBP on concentric and eccentric MEE at the lower limb joints during gait. We hypothesized that NSCLBP disrupts normal energy flow due to increased lumbar muscle stiffness and/or altered movement patterns, which would be reflected in reduced MEE, particularly at the low back and hip joints. Supporting this hypothesis, our main results revealed a reduction in eccentric MEE at these joints in individuals with NSCLBP compared to healthy participants. These findings suggest that energy transfer via eccentric muscle action is impaired in NSCLBP, indicating decreased mechanical efficiency of gait. These findings are further discussed in the subsequent paragraphs.

Eccentric MEE was observed at the ankle of healthy participants compared to those with NSCLBP during weight acceptance (0–10%) and mid-stance (10–50%) phases of the gait cycle (Table 4). Ankle power profiles (Fig. 2a) reflect similar patterns between groups, with greater MEE amplitude in the healthy group, consistent with more efficient gait. During weight acceptance, the tibialis anterior eccentrically controls ankle dorsiflexion. Increased plantar flexor stiffness in NSCLBP may lead to a plantarflexed position, reducing eccentric MEE of dorsiflexor muscles29 .

No significant differences in MEE were observed for the knee across any energy transfer conditions (Table 3). Knee power profiles (Fig. 2b) reflect similar patterns between groups, suggesting minimal impact of NSCLBP on knee biomechanics during gait.

In the hip, higher eccentric MEE was observed in healthy participants during mid-stance (Table 4). Concentric contractions predominated in the NSCLBP group, as presented in hip power profiles (Fig. 2c), whereas eccentric energy transfer was more prevalent in the healthy group, facilitating efficient movement. Hip moment profiles (Fig. 3a) exhibit similar patterns between groups. Differences in the horizontal plane during mid-stance were observed, as presented in hip joint angles (Fig. 3b), with internal rotation followed by external rotation in the healthy group, but consistent external rotation in the NSCLBP group. This reduced internal rotation aligns with prior studies of NSCLBP30,31.

During late-stance (50–60%), higher eccentric MEE was evident in the low back of the healthy group (Table 4). Low back power profiles (Fig. 2d) reflect eccentric energy transfer in the healthy group and concentric transfer in the NSCLBP group. A greater flexing moment in the sagittal plane was observed in the healthy group, with lumbar extension, while lumbar flexion occurred in the NSCLBP group, as presented in Fig. 3c and 3d. These findings suggest that sagittal plane motion influences low back biomechanics in NSCLBP.

Differences between groups included: (1) external hip rotation in the NSCLBP group during mid-stance, unlike the healthy group; and (2) lumbar flexion in the NSCLBP group during late-stance, compared to lumbar extension in the healthy group. These patterns may be associated with psoas major stiffness, which may contribute to external hip rotation and restrict hip extension, potentially causing lumbar flexion or excessive lordosis32. Reduced hip extension in NSCLBP, particularly during late-stance when the psoas major is maximally stretched, may explain lumbar flexion33.

The role of psoas major stiffness in NSCLBP remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Nevertheless, its potential influence on altered movement patterns, such as external hip rotation and lumbar flexion, is evident in hip and low back kinematics32,34. Furthermore, individuals with NSCLBP are known to exhibit increased activation of the lumbar paraspinal muscles during walking compared to healthy controls5, which may increase trunk stiffness and help explain the reduced dissociation between the thorax and pelvis observed during gait in this population18. However, further research is needed to determine whether these changes in trunk and psoas major stiffness contribute to the observed impairments in energy transfer across lower limb joints, particularly at the low back and hip, in people with NSCLBP. Gaining a better understanding of these mechanisms could inform the development of targeted rehabilitation strategies—such as specific stretching or strengthening exercises—to improve gait mechanical efficiency in individuals with NSCLBP.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (14 individuals with NSCLBP and 14 healthy controls) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although significant differences were observed, larger samples would increase statistical power and provide more robust estimates of the observed effects. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents any causal inferences regarding the relationship between NSCLBP and alterations in mechanical energy expenditure during gait. Longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to determine whether these biomechanical differences are a cause or consequence of NSCLBP and to assess their evolution over time or in response to rehabilitation.

To conclude, this study highlights altered energy transfer across lower limb joints during walking in individuals with non-specific chronic low back pain compared to healthy controls. The findings indicate reduced energy transfer via eccentric muscle action at the low back, hip, and ankle joints in individuals with low back pain, suggesting a decrease in mechanical efficiency. Assessing mechanical energy during gait offers valuable insight into the biomechanical compensatory strategies employed by individuals with low back pain. These insights may help guide rehabilitation professionals in developing interventions aimed at improving gait efficiency in this population.
