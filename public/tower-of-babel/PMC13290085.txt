CD69 blockade restores the bone marrow niche and delays leukemogenesis in a mouse model of Nras G12D-driven chronic myelomonocytic leukemia
Authors: Yuchen Li, Xu Jiang, Yinfeng Gu, Xianfeng Zha, Jie Wu, Xiuli Wu, Chunli Qi, Shaohua Chen, Yangqiu Li, Zhenyi Jin, Chengxiang Xia, Ling Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA subset of patients with chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) carries NRAS mutations, which are associated with shorter overall survival and an increased risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia. However, the effects of NRAS mutations on the bone marrow microenvironment (BME) remain unclear.MethodsWe used a CMML mouse model driven by a single Nras G12D allele mutation to investigate alterations in the BME and the potential role of CD69 in immune suppression. Nras G12D-mutated CMML mice were treated with an anti-CD69 monoclonal antibody. Flow cytometry, hematoxylin–eosin staining, and RNA sequencing were performed to evaluate treatment-related changes.Results Nras G12D-mutated CMML mice showed increased infiltration of regulatory T (Treg) cells and CD69+ T cells in the BME, whereas CD69 expression on peripheral blood T cells remained lower than that on bone marrow T cells. Anti-CD69 monoclonal antibody treatment was associated with reduced generation of granulocyte-macrophage progenitor cells, prolonged survival, and decreased Treg accumulation in the BME.ConclusionOur findings suggest that CD69 may serve as a biomarker of BME immunological dysfunction in CMML.

Introduction

Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a clonal hematopoietic malignancy exhibiting overlapping features of myelodysplastic neoplasms and myeloproliferative neoplasms. 1 Hallmarks of the disease include sustained peripheral-blood monocytosis, dysplastic hematopoiesis in the bone marrow (BM), and hepatosplenomegaly. The 2022 fifth-edition WHO classification categorizes CMML into two subtypes: CMML-1 (< 5% blasts in PB and < 10% in the BM) and CMML-2 (6%–19% blasts in blood or 10%–19% in BM). 2-4 Over 90% of patients harbor somatic mutations, 5 , 6 with activating lesions in RAS pathway genes – particularly NRAS and KRAS – serving as key initiating drivers in a subset of juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia and CMML. 7 , 8 NRAS mutations are significantly more prevalent in CMML-2 than in CMML-1 and correlate with shorter overall survival, increased risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and a poor prognosis. 9-11

Wang et al. previously reported that the activating Nras G12D mutation stabilizes the Nras protein in a constitutively active, GTP-bound conformation. This sustained activation aberrantly upregulates the Erk1/2 signaling axis, facilitating excessive proliferation and self-renewal of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and biasing their differentiation toward the myeloid lineage. 9 , 12 , 13 Additionally, Wang demonstrated that mice expressing Nras G12D exhibit quantitative and functional alterations in BM mesenchymal stem cells, thereby disrupting BME homeostasis. Niche disturbances serve as extrinsic cues that accelerate leukemia progression. 14 These findings indicate that Nras mutations exert cell-intrinsic oncogenic effects and influence disease progression by remodeling the supportive microenvironment. 15

Immune‒cell interactions within the BME maintain niche homeostasis. Although the intrinsic oncogenic molecular mechanisms of NRAS mutations are well-defined, there is insufficient evidence regarding the impact of these mutations on the tumor immune microenvironment.

CD69 has traditionally been regarded as an early lymphocyte activation marker; however, accumulating evidence indicates that CD69 also functions as an immunoregulatory receptor involved in lymphocyte retention, immune homeostasis, and antitumor immunity. A seminal study by Esplugues et al. demonstrated that CD69-deficient mice exhibited markedly reduced tumor growth and prolonged survival after challenge with MHC class I- tumors, and that the enhanced antitumor response was mediated by NK cells and T lymphocytes. 16 These findings provided an important rationale for considering CD69 not only as a marker of immune activation but also as a functional regulator of antitumor immune responses. In our previous work, we found that the enrichment of CD69+ tissue resident memory-like T cells (TRM-like) in the BM is a dependent risk factor for the overall survival of acute myeloid leukemia patients. 17 In this study, we also found that CD69 aberrantly high expression in the BM CD4 and CD8 T cells. Based on these observations, we investigated whether CD69 blockade could modulate the BM immune microenvironment and delay leukemic progression.

This study investigated the microenvironmental alterations associated with Nras G12D-driven CMML, with particular attention paid to the disruption of immune homeostasis in the bone marrow niche and aberrant CD69 expression. Anti-CD69 monoclonal antibody treatment was further applied in this model to assess its effects on leukemic progression and bone marrow niche abnormalities. These findings expand the current understanding of the role of CD69 in NRAS-mutant CMML and provide a rationale for exploring CD69-targeted intervention in this disease. A preprint version of this study has been posted previously. 18

Methods

Mice

This study included C57BL/6 mice. We generated LSL Nras G12D/+; Vav-Cre composite mice (CMML mice) that express the Nras G12D mutation only in the hematopoietic system and a small number of endothelial cells by crossing mice carrying the oncogenic Nras G12D mutation with Vav-Cre mice. Littermate LSL Nras G12D/+ mice lacking Cre recombinase served as controls and were regarded as wild-type (WT) mice. The genotypes of Nras G12D and Vav-Cre were identified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in all newborn mice. Genotyping was performed by PCR at postnatal day 10, and the mice were individually identified by toe clipping and tracked throughout the study using consistent cage cards and records. Nras G12D Vav-Cre (CD45.2) mice were generously provided by Professor Jin-Yong Wang’s group (Guangzhou Institute of Biomedicine and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences).

All the mice were bred and maintained in a specific pathogen-free animal facility under standard husbandry conditions with free access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jinan University (Approval No. 2022090605) and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal care and use.

For terminal sample collection, the mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), and blood was collected via the retro-orbital venous sinus. The mice were then euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, and bone marrow, spleen, and liver tissues were immediately harvested for subsequent analyses.

Mouse study design and anti-CD69 mAb treatment

A single mouse was considered the experimental unit. LSL Nras G12D/+; Vav-Cre mice were monitored for CMML-like disease development, and mice with peripheral blood (PB) CD11b+ cells ≥ 40% were considered diseased and enrolled in the CMML cohort. Most comparative analyses were performed using age-matched WT, CMML-PBS, and CMML-CD69Ab groups, whereas survival analysis used an isotype-treated CMML group as the control.

No a priori sample size calculation was performed. Sample sizes were determined empirically to allow statistical comparison, and exact n values for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends. The inclusion criteria were a correct genotype and, for treatment experiments, fulfillment of the predefined disease criterion (PB CD11b+ ≥ 40%). The exclusion criteria included an incorrect genotype, failure to reach the disease criterion during the observation period, injection failure, inadequate flow cytometry data quality, and other technical failures during the experiment.

A total of 91 CMML mice were used for treatment or disease-control experiments in this study. Among them, 12 mice were not enrolled because they developed CMML-like disease very late or did not reach the predefined disease criterion during the observation period. Nine mice were excluded from flow cytometry-based analyses because of unsuccessful flow cytometry experiments or inadequate flow cytometry data quality. One mouse was excluded because of injection failure, and the corresponding sample was not used for subsequent analyses. The remaining 69 mice fulfilled the predefined disease criterion of PB CD11b⁺ cells ≥ 40% and were enrolled in the treatment or disease-control cohorts. No animal was excluded on the basis of treatment response or survival outcome.

CMML mice received an anti-CD69 monoclonal antibody (InVivoMAb anti-mouse CD69, clone CD69.2.2, Bio X Cell, BE0330) via intravenous tail-vein injection at a dose of 500 μg per mouse every 2 weeks for a total of three doses. For survival experiments, CMML control mice received an isotype-matched IgG1 antibody (clone MOPC-21, Bio X Cell, BE0083) at the same dose and schedule. For other experiments, diseased CMML mice without antibody treatment were used as PBS controls, as indicated in Figure 2A.

The main outcome measures were overall survival and PB CD11b+ burden. Additional outcome measures included spleen size, Treg proportion, CD69 expression, progenitor composition, histopathological changes, cytokine levels, and transcriptomic alterations.

Mice were monitored in parallel with PB sampling. General activity, body condition, and PB CD11b+ levels were assessed throughout the experiment. A PB CD11b+ level approaching 70% was used as the humane endpoint for euthanasia. No unexpected adverse events were observed. To reduce potential confounding factors, comparisons were made between mice of similar age, and sample processing and flow cytometry acquisition were performed using the same workflow. No blinding was applied during the experiment or data analysis.

Flow cytometry

PB was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus of the mice. Bone marrow was harvested from the tibias and femurs, and spleen and liver tissues were collected after euthanasia. Spleen and liver tissues were minced and filtered through a 300-mesh filter into phosphate-buffered saline containing 2% fetal bovine serum (2% FBS/PBS) to obtain single-cell suspensions. BM was diluted with 5 mL of 2% FBS/PBS to prepare a single-cell suspension. Before antibody staining, the cells were incubated with TruStain FcX anti-CD16/32 antibody (clone 93, BioLegend, Cat# 101319) at 4 °C for 15 min for Fc receptor blocking. The fluorescent antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The samples were analyzed using an LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and sorted using a FACSAria III cell sorter (BD Biosciences). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA samples were extracted from cells derived from various tissue origins in different groups of mice, followed by reverse transcription. Additionally, qRT-PCR experiments utilized SYBR for double-stranded DNA amplification and quantification, with actin beta as the housekeeping gene. The relative gene expression levels were assessed using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to those in the control group. Supplementary Tables 2–3 present the associated primers.

Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining of mouse SP and bone tissue

The mice were euthanized, and the femurs and spleens were immediately dissected. Tissues were fixed separately in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight. Femurs were then decalcified in 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution (EDTA, pH 7.2–7.4) at room temperature with continuous agitation until completely softened. Both decalcified femurs and fixed spleens were processed through graded ethanol dehydration (75%, 85%, and 95% ethanol for 1–2 h each; absolute ethanol twice for 1.5 h each), cleared in xylene (two changes, 1 h each), and infiltrated with paraffin wax at 65 °C for 2–3 h (or overnight) using an automated tissue processor. Tissues were embedded in paraffin on a −20 °C cold plate. The paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4 µm thickness using a rotary microtome. The sections were floated on a 40 °C water bath, mounted onto glass slides, and dried at 60 °C overnight. For H&E staining, the sections were dewaxed with commercial xylene-free clearing agent (Servicebio, G1128) and rehydrated through graded ethanol to water. The slides were then stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for a few seconds, and rinsed with running water. After a brief rinse, the sections were washed with bluing solution for 1 min, followed by thorough washing under running tap water for 10 min. Subsequently, tissues were counterstained with eosin Y for 15–30 s, rapidly dehydrated through 95% ethanol and absolute ethanol, and cleared in xylene-free clearing agent. Finally, the slides were prepared using neutral balsam for microscopic examination.

Preparation of BM supernatants

BM supernatants were not obtained by bone marrow aspiration. Instead, the femurs and tibias were harvested, and the bone marrow was flushed with 2 mL sterile PBS per bone into pre-chilled tubes. The suspension was gently mixed and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the cells. The supernatant was carefully collected without disturbing the cell pellet, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until subsequent protein quantification and cytokine measurements. Because BM supernatants were generated by PBS flushing rather than aspiration, they may represent a diluted bone marrow fluid fraction; however, all samples were processed using the same PBS volume and identical workflow, allowing valid comparisons between groups.

Cytokine detection using flow cytometry multiplex bead assay

Cytokine levels in mouse BM supernatants were quantified utilizing a RayPlex Mouse Hematopoietic Stem Cell Bead Array (RayBiotech, RayPlex Mouse Hematopoietic Stem Cell Bead Array, Cat# FAM-HSC-1-48), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, standards and samples were incubated with the RayPlex Multiplex Bead Cocktail (antibody-coupled beads) and the Biotinylated Detection Antibody Cocktail, followed by incubation with Streptavidin–PE. After washing, the samples were analyzed on a FACS Canto™ II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Transcriptome sequencing and pathway enrichment analysis

Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells (the mix of Lin-IL-7R-Sca1-c-Kit+(LK) and Lin-IL-7R-Sca1+c-Kit+(LSK) cells) were isolated from the BM of WT, PBS-treated CMML, and anti-CD69 mAb-treated CMML mice and performed bulk RNA-seq (nWT = 2, nCMML = 3, nAnti-CD69 = 4). The Agilent Bioanalyzer was utilized to assess total RNA quality and quantity. Libraries were prepared using standard poly (A) selection and fragmentation protocols and sequenced on an Illumina platform. The raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and trimmed. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome utilizing HISAT2 for gene expression quantification. Differential expression analysis was performed utilizing DESeq2, identifying significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as those with adjusted P ≤ 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg) and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1. DEG functional enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler to assess Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was applied to GO and KEGG gene sets. Protein‒protein interaction (PPI) networks were created utilizing the STRING database.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0; GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R (version 4.1.0). The number of biological replicates (n) for each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legends or relevant Methods section. In animal experiments, n refers to individual mice. For bulk RNA-seq, the number of samples analyzed in each group is described in the relevant Methods section. Normality was assessed with GraphPad Prism. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas nonparametric tests were applied when appropriate. For comparisons among three groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test when applicable. For bulk RNA-seq, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, as described above. Significance is denoted by * for P < 0.05, ** for P < 0.01, *** for P < 0.001, **** for P < 0.0001, and ns for non-significant differences. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

BME of Nras G12D mutated CMML mice characterized by an inverted CD4+ and CD8+ ratio, increased Tregs, and high expression of CD69

We generated compound CMML mice (LSL Nras G12D/+; Vav-Cre) that express the Nras G12D mutation specifically in the hematopoietic system and in a few endothelial cells by crossing the mice carrying a conditional oncogenic Nras G12D mutation with Vav-Cre mice. Simultaneously, we utilized littermate LSL Nras G12D/+ mice as controls; these mice lack Cre recombinase expression and can be regarded as WT mice (Supplementary Figure 1A). At approximately 90 d post-birth (4 months of age), the proportion of CD11b+ cells in the PB of the above CMML mice significantly increased to approximately 40%, indicating the onset of leukemia. Additionally, the levels of CD11b+ cells in the SP and BM were significantly higher than those in WT mice (Supplementary Figure 1B, C). The significant increase in SP volume, length, and weight further supported the leukemia phenotype (Figure 1A).

We observed a notable decrease in CD8+ T cells and a concomitant increase in CD4+ T cells in the BM of the above CMML mice using flow cytometry (Figure 1B). CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the BM exhibited a significant upregulation of CD69 expression. Although T cells from PB and SP demonstrated increased CD69 expression, this increase was not as significant as that in the BM (Figure 1C). CD69 expression in PB T cells is commonly interpreted as an activation-associated marker; however, in tissue-derived T cells, CD69 may also reflect tissue-retention or residency-associated programs. 19 , 20 In addition, CD69 expression has been linked to Treg generation according to several pathways. 21 Owing to the observed inverse ratio of CD4+ to CD8+ T cells, we identified a significant elevation in the proportion of CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs in the BM of CMML mice, which was correlated positively with CD69 expression (Figure 1D).

The above findings suggest that T cells in the BM environment of CMML were characterized by an inverted CD4+ and CD8+ ratio, increased Tregs, and high expression of CD69.

Treatment with an anti-CD69 monoclonal antibody alleviated disease progression

In the subsequent experiments, LSL Nras G12D/+; Vav-Cre mice (CMML mice will be used hereafter to designate) received three intravenous doses of the anti-CD69 mAb via the tail vein, while control groups were given either isotype-matched IgG or PBS (Figure 2A). We selected 4-month-old CMML mice, characterized by a CD11b+ cell proportion of 40% or greater in the PB, for treatment with an anti-CD69 mAb. Simultaneously, CMML mice with similar criteria received injections of the same dose of the IgG isotype control. The treatment was administered biweekly for three sessions. PB CD11b+ cells were monitored at 5, 6, 7, and 8+ months of age. At 6 months, the anti-CD69 mAb-treated group exhibited a significantly lower proportion of CD11b⁺ cells in the PB compared to the control group (Figure 2B). At the same time point, anti-CD69 mAb treatment was associated with reduced spleen size and weight (Figure 2C) and a significantly decreased fraction of CD11b⁺ cells in the SP, whereas the fraction of CD11b⁺ cells in the BM did not significantly change (Figure 2D). The mice were euthanized when PB CD11b+ cell levels approached 70%, the survival threshold. The post-treatment survival period was the time from treatment initiation to the endpoint. The findings demonstrated that anti-CD69 mAb treatment extended the post-treatment survival duration in CMML mice by 163.5 d compared to 56 d in the isotype control group (Figure 2E). H&E staining revealed that the SP architecture in CMML mice was severely compromised, exhibiting increased infiltration of megakaryocytes, indicating increased extramedullary hematopoiesis. SP architecture was restored after anti-CD69 mAb treatment, with a distinct red and white pulp boundary and reduced megakaryocyte infiltration. H&E staining of the BM revealed an increased dysplastic megakaryocyte and a scarcity of blood sinusoids. Following anti-CD69 mAb treatment, dysplastic megakaryocytes were reduced, and blood sinusoids were increased (Figure 2F). These data indicate that anti-CD69 mAb treatment effectively mitigates splenomegaly in CMML mice, reduces the PB tumor burden, and significantly extends mouse longevity. Additionally, the observed histological changes confirmed anti-CD69 mAb treatment efficacy.

Increased erythropoiesis and decreased granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMPs) were observed after the anti-CD69 mAb administration

In addition to granulocyte and monocyte proliferation, dyserythropoiesis and dysmegakaryopoiesis are prevalent in CMML. After the mice were euthanized, the leg bones from the mice treated with the anti-CD69 mAb exhibited a redder coloration, and the BM pellet resembled that of WT mice (Supplementary Figure 2A). Subsequent flow cytometry analysis revealed that mice treated with the anti-CD69 mAb had more Ter119+ cells, from proerythroblasts to mature erythrocytes, and more stage Ⅴ mature erythrocytes (Ter119+CD71-CD44-) in the BM than untreated CMML mice (Figure 3A). These findings imply that anti-CD69 mAb treatment may aid in erythropoiesis recovery. We conducted flow cytometry analysis of BM CMP differentiation to examine whether the enhancement of mature erythrocyte production using anti-CD69 mAb is mediated through common myeloid progenitor cells (CMPs) differentiation in the BM. Our results revealed an increased proportion of megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs) in the BM of anti-CD69 mAb-treated mice; however, their absolute counts did not change. Simultaneously, the GMPs revealed a significant decrease in the number and proportion in the CMML-CD69Ab group (Figure 3B). These results revealed that GMP and MEP production are active in the BM of CMML mice. To further clarify the mechanism by which anti-CD69 mAb reduces GMP, we performed RNA-seq on Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells from the BM of mice in the WT, CMML-PBS, and CMML-CD69Ab groups (Supplementary Figure 2B). We found that all DEGs could be clustered into five clusters by clustering the DEGs from the three groups. Among these, Cluster-5 (C5) genes exhibited the most significant recovery in the CMML-CD69Ab group compared to the CMML-PBS group. Cluster-1 (C1) included some genes that returned to WT mice levels. Cluster-3 (C3) contained genes that remained abnormally expressed post-treatment, while Clusters 2 (C2) and 4 (C4) represented gene changes specific to the CMML-CD69Ab group (Figure 3C). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that C5 was enriched for multiple MAPK negative regulatory pathways (Figure 3D-E). Among the genes in these pathways, we observed that genes, including Dusp4, Dusp6, Spred2, and Spred3, were upregulated to varying degrees in the treated samples (Figure 3D). The dual-specificity phosphatases Dusp4 and Dusp6 dephosphorylate ERK1/2, limiting MAPK signaling pathway activation and cell proliferation. 22 Studies have demonstrated that Spred2’s EVH1 domain inhibits Ras activity, and its sprouty-related domain binds to the p85 subunit of PI3K, strengthening its inhibitory effect on the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway. 23 , 24 In the KEGG pathway enrichment, Rasa3/4 were upregulated after anti-CD69 mAb treatment (Figure 3E). These genes belong to the Ras-GAP family of proteins and can promote GTP hydrolysis, converting Ras into its inactive GDP-bound state, thereby inhibiting the excessive activation of the RAS signaling. These results indicate that anti-CD69 mAb treatment may inhibit the malignant GMP proliferation by upregulating these negative regulators to inhibit the RAS/RAF/MAPK signaling pathway.

The analysis of Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cell RNA-Seq data further showed that CD69 expression was upregulated in PBS-treated CMML mice compared to WT mice, which indicates that the inhibition of RAS/RAF/MAPK signaling may be due to the direct block of CD69 on Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells (Supplementary Figure 2C). The PPI network was further used to analyze putative CD69-interacting proteins. The PPI network constructed from DEGs between the CMML-PBS and WT groups revealed 20 putative CD69-interacting proteins (Supplementary Figure 2D), 16 of which were upregulated (Supplementary Figure 2E). The PPI network constructed from DEGs between the CMML-CD69Ab and CMML-PBS groups highlighted seven candidate CD69-associated proteins in Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells (Supplementary Figure 2F). Among the predicted CD69-associated edges, two showed reduced network weights in the CMML-CD69Ab group, whereas no edges showed an increase (Supplementary Figure 2G). Notably, Serpinb2 emerged as a candidate node within this CD69-associated network, displaying an apparent increase in the CMML-PBS group and a decrease after anti-CD69 mAb treatment. Importantly, CD69-associated proteins were not enriched among the DEGs when comparing the CMML-CD69Ab group with the WT controls. These findings suggest that Serpinb2 interaction with CD69 may play a role in enabling GMPs to proliferate and survive; however, this hypothesis requires further validation. Taken together, anti-CD69 mAb treatment may inhibit GMP expansion through direct or indirect modulation of the RAS/RAF/MAPK signaling pathway.

Anti-CD69 mAb treatment reduced the infiltration of Tregs and restored several cytokines in the BM

Given the aberrantly high CD69 expression on the BM T cells of CMML mice, we assessed the BM CD4/CD8 ratio and Treg levels following anti-CD69 mAb treatment. Although the CD4/CD8 ratio did not improve (Supplementary Figure 3), a significant reduction was observed in the Treg proportion. This decrease in the Treg proportion was not limited to the BM; it was observed in the PB, SP, and liver (Figure 4A), suggesting that anti-CD69 mAb treatment effectively reduced the systemic infiltration of Tregs.

The RNA-seq data of Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells revealed that anti-CD69 mAb treatment partially restored the expression of a subset of C1-associated genes toward WT levels. KEGG enrichment analysis of the C1 subset-associated genes showed prominent enrichment in cytokine-related and immune regulatory pathways (Figure 4B). Consistently, GSEA of DEGs between the CMML-CD69Ab group and the WT group showed that the “Cytokine Signaling in Immune System” pathway remained significantly enriched in anti-CD69 mAb-treated mice (Figure 4C). These findings indicate that the therapeutic effect of anti-CD69 mAb may involve fine-tuning of immune cytokines. To investigate this, we used flow cytometry-based multiplex cytokine assays to quantify hematopoiesis-related cytokines in the BM supernatant of treated CMML mice. We found that the immunosuppressive cytokine TGF-β was significantly decreased, whereas certain hematopoiesis-related cytokines, including stem cell factor (SCF) and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), were significantly increased after treatment (Figure 4D). The results indicate that anti-CD69 mAb treatment significantly enhanced the cytokine balance in the hematopoietic microenvironment, reducing the level of the immunosuppressive cytokine TGF-β. We used real-time quantitative PCR to measure the expression levels of CD69-related ligands in mice's BM mononuclear cells before and after anti-CD69 mAb treatment to determine if the microenvironmental repair effect is related to changes in CD69 ligand expression. The results revealed that the ligands Gal-1 and Myl9/12a were significantly decreased after anti-CD69 mAb treatment, while S100A8/9 was unchanged (Figure 4E).

The above findings indicate that anti-CD69 mAb may attenuate immunosuppression on the BME by reducing the systemic infiltration of Tregs and restoring relevant hematopoietic cytokine expression. Additionally, the block of CD69 may repair the BME by reducing Gal-1 and Myl9/12a recruitment of expressing immune cells to the BME.

Discussion

This study utilized the CMML mouse model with a single Nras allele mutation to demonstrate that the granulomonocytic proliferation associated with a solitary Nras gene mutation can establish an immunosuppressive BME. This environment is marked by Treg infiltration, increased TGF-β cytokine levels, and significant T cell immune dysregulation, as indicated by an inverted CD4/CD8 ratio and abnormally elevated CD69 expression. Furthermore, the levels of several hematopoiesis-related cytokines, including LIF, IL-3, and SCF, were significantly decreased. 25 , 26 These findings indicate that the accumulation of Nras-mutated cells can disrupt the homeostasis of the hematopoietic microenvironment, promoting CMML progression. Braf, a downstream signaling molecule of NRAS, contributes to the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in Braf-mutated tumors, which occurs by downregulating MHC class I molecules on tumor cell surfaces and enhancing chemokine production that recruits MDSC. 27 In colorectal cancer and triple-negative breast cancer, oncogenic KRAS/RAS-MAPK signaling has been reported to promote immunosuppressive microenvironment remodeling, partly through the recruitment or support of suppressive myeloid cells. 28 , 29 These findings suggest that the oncogenic effects of NRAS/KRAS pathway activation extend beyond cell-intrinsic signaling and may involve remodeling of the immune microenvironment. The process includes multi-dimensional remodeling of the immune microenvironment to establish an ecological system that facilitates the progression of malignant clones.

CD69 is recognized as a classic early marker of lymphocyte activation. However, recent evidence indicates that CD69 plays a significant role in regulating various immune responses. 17 , 30 During inflammatory responses, CD69 can suppress inflammation by regulating the differentiation balance between Treg and Th17 cells. Similarly, CD69 can facilitate the progression of inflammation by binding to its ligand Myl9/12a. In tumor animal models, CD69 deficiency or CD69-blocking antibodies can reduce tumor growth and metastasis, increase tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and boost anti-tumor immune responses. 30 Consistent with these observations, multiple preclinical studies in solid tumors have explored CD69-targeting strategies, including reports that anti-CD69 may enhance anti-tumor efficacy when combined with immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti-PD-1), as summarized in our review. 30 Given the theoretical role of CD69 in promoting Treg differentiation and our observed positive correlation between CD4+CD69+ T cells and Treg proportions in the BM of CMML mice, we hypothesized that anti-CD69 mAb treatment may delay disease progression in Nras-mutant CMML mice by modulating Treg generation and improving the immune microenvironment. The results demonstrated that anti-CD69 mAb treatment effectively prolonged CMML mice survival. Notably, the reduction in circulating CD11b⁺ cells was most evident at 6 months of age and was not consistently maintained at later monitoring time points, and PB CD11b⁺ levels did not return to WT-like levels. This suggests that anti-CD69 mAb treatment achieved a transient reduction in the peripheral leukemic burden rather than complete normalization. A plausible explanation is that the dosing intensity or treatment duration was insufficient to durably suppress residual disease, and that CD69 expression within Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells is limited to a small fraction of progenitors, allowing CD69- leukemia-initiating compartments to drive subsequent disease progression. Mechanistically, it repaired the BME by reducing the number of Treg cells and recovering several cytokine levels in the BM.

Building on these findings, we further explored ligand-associated mechanisms that may contribute to CD69-dependent microenvironmental changes. As previously reported, the interaction between Gal-1 and CD69 is primarily glycosylation-dependent and plays a crucial role in regulating the Treg/Th17 balance, inflammatory responses, and immune homeostasis. 30 In our study, CD69 blockade was associated with reduced Gal-1 mRNA levels in bulk BM MNCs. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether CD69-expressing T cells recruit Gal-1-expressing immune cells, including dendritic cells or macrophages, to the BM, thereby promoting Treg generation in CMML mice. Myl9/12a has been identified as another potential ligand for CD69, predominantly expressed on vascular luminal surfaces and within reticular structures at inflammatory sites, including the lungs and intestines. During inflammation, Myl9/12a forms a Myl9-network that facilitates the migration and retention of CD69-expressing activated T cells. This study further demonstrated that anti-CD69 mAb treatment was accompanied by reduced Myl9/12a mRNA levels in bulk BM MNCs. These findings suggest that CD69 blockade may dampen the ligand-associated inflammatory microenvironment in the BM, which could contribute to the microenvironmental changes observed after treatment.

Given that the anti-CD69 mAb treatment did not affect MEP generation, we hypothesize that the recovery of mature erythrocytes may result from other reasons, for example, the restoration of hematopoietic cytokines, which may facilitate erythroid differentiation beyond the MEP stage. This hypothesis may be supported by a previous study indicating that NRAS oncogene expression induces pathological effects similar to those of HRAS oncogene overexpression in AML and myelodysplastic neoplasms, both of which impair terminal differentiation of erythrocytes and promote aberrant proliferation of colony-forming unit-erythroid and early erythroblasts. 31

Another key finding of this study is that anti-CD69 mAb treatment significantly attenuated GMP in the BM, suggesting a previously unrecognized role of CD69 in regulating GMP survival. RNA sequencing revealed marked upregulation of CD69 in Lin-IL-7R-c-Kit+ cells from CMML mice. PPI analysis demonstrated an enhanced interaction between CD69 and multiple proteins, implicating CD69 in abnormal proliferation and survival of CMML cells. Notably, Serpinb2 emerged as one of the candidate nodes within this CD69-associated network and displayed a dynamic pattern between untreated CMML and anti-CD69 mAb-treated conditions. Prior studies have shown that SerpinB2 is inducible in macrophages under pro-inflammatory stimulation and is linked to transcriptional programs associated with cell movement and extracellular matrix interactions; moreover, SerpinB2 has been reported to restrain macrophage migration and to be associated with a resolution-phase signature, potentially via modulation of the uPA-plasmin system and downstream uPA receptor/integrin-related signaling. 32 These observations support the biological plausibility that SerpinB2 may reflect and potentially contribute to inflammatory myeloid states and microenvironmental remodeling. Importantly, our PPI analysis is hypothesis-generating and does not establish a direct physical interaction between CD69 and SerpinB2 or causality. In parallel, anti-CD69 mAb treatment induced the expression of several negative regulators of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway, including Dusp6, Spred2, Spred3, Rasa3, and Rasa4. In line with the established importance of MAPK deregulation in CMML, Kunimoto et al. showed that TET2 loss and NRAS mutation cooperatively drive CMML in part through epigenetic silencing of MAPK negative regulators such as SPRY2, thereby sustaining MAPK activation and myeloid transformation. 33 Although this study did not confirm abnormal CD69 expression on hematopoietic stem cells or progenitor cells at the protein level, previous studies have demonstrated that CD69 is broadly expressed across most hematopoietic lineages. Bujanover et al. demonstrated that acute immune stimulation significantly increases CD69 expression on HSCs, with its heterogeneity potentially reflecting functional diversity within the HSC population. 34 Additionally, another study identified a leukemia stem cell subset with elevated CD69 expression levels associated with enhanced self-renewal and colony-forming capacity, factors implicated in AML relapse. 35 These findings suggest that disrupting CD69-associated network programs in Nras cells may contribute to the reduced GMP burden observed after anti-CD69 mAb treatment, potentially via the modulation of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway.

The translational relevance of CD69-associated immune remodeling in human CMML remains to be clarified. Direct evidence for aberrant CD69 expression in BM T cells from primary CMML samples is currently limited. We have previously reported the accumulation of CD69+ TRM-like cells in the BM of patients with acute myeloid leukemia, particularly the monocytic subtype, which is associated with poor survival outcomes. 17 Whether the same immune microenvironment existed in CMML is worth further validation. Nevertheless, immune microenvironment abnormalities have been reported in CMML patients. In particular, bone marrow dendritic cell aggregates were shown to be associated with systemic immune dysregulation, disease progression, and regulatory T-cell phenotypic changes in CMML. 36 In addition, recent multi-omic analyses of primary CMML monocytes revealed metabolic and immune dysregulation linked to altered macrophage polarization. 37 These findings suggest that the CD69-associated T-cell changes, Treg accumulation, and cytokine imbalance observed in our Nras G12D-driven CMML model may represent a relevant immune axis that warrants further validation in patient-derived CMML samples.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Although anti-CD69 mAb treatment prolonged the survival of CMML mice and reduced the leukemic burden at certain time points, the relatively short treatment period may have limited the durability of its effects. In addition, the mechanisms by which CD69 blockade exerts its therapeutic effects remain incompletely defined, and several ligand- and network-associated findings reported here are still hypothesis-generating and await further validation. As this study was performed in a mouse model, the relevance of these observations to human CMML remains to be confirmed in patient-derived samples. Despite these limitations, our findings support a role for CD69 in bone marrow microenvironment dysregulation in Nras-mutant CMML and provide a basis for future mechanistic and translational investigation.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that anti-CD69 mAb treatment significantly prolongs survival in CMML mice. The molecular mechanisms underlying CD69-mediated regulation of CMML pathogenesis are still needed for future study.
