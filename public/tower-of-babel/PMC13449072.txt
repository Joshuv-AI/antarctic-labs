Long‐Term Associations Between Energy Expenditure and CGM‐Derived Metrics in Adults With Type 1 Diabetes: A Retrospective Analysis of the Syntactiq Cockpit Database
Authors: Katharina Bünzel, Sabrina Sanfilippo, Janis Schierbauer, Lukas Schuster, Harald Sourij, Martin Tauschmann, Ulrike Leb‐Stöger, Andreas Seemann, Gerhard Tschakert, Philipp Birnbaumer, Ali Kapan, Othmar Moser
Published: 2026

Background

Regular physical activity and exercise enhance glycaemic control in people with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and reduce the risk of diabetes‐related complications [1]. Exercise improves metabolic risk factors and muscle insulin sensitivity, leading to reduced insulin requirements [2], partly through increased skeletal muscle glucose uptake via insulin‐independent pathways involving GLUT1 [3]. Despite these benefits, fear of hypoglycaemia remains a major barrier to regular physical activity, as exercise‐induced and delayed hypoglycaemic events are common and difficult to predict [4].

In real‐world settings, physical activity and exercise are often estimated using step counts as a proxy for activity levels [5, 6]. However, step counts provide limited information on metabolic demand, and the relationship between energy expenditure and glucose dynamics in T1D remains insufficiently characterized, particularly over the long term.

Large‐scale datasets enable a more detailed assessment of this relationship. While prior studies such as the T1DEXI study [6] linked step count with glycaemic outcomes, energy expenditure reflects overall metabolic demand more comprehensively than step count alone [7]. When combined with CGM‐derived metrics, it allows direct investigation of the association between physical activity and glucose levels [8].

When combined with CGM‐derived metrics, it allows direct investigation of the relationship between physical activity and glucose levels. Therefore, the aim of this analysis was to investigate the long‐term impact of physical activity and exercise, quantified as weekly energy expenditure (kcal/week), on CGM‐derived metrics in a physically active cohort with T1D over a 6‐month period.

Methods

This study is a retrospective analysis of anonymised data collected between 2018 and 2024, voluntarily donated for scientific purposes by individuals with T1D using the Diabetes Cockpit application (Syntactiq, Austria). The application integrates health information from Apple Health [9], Nightscout [10] and/or applications for continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). This information includes step count, exercise minutes, calories burned, as well as CGM‐derived metrics such as mean glucose with standard deviation, time in range (TIR; 70–180 mg/dL), carbohydrate intake, and both basal and bolus insulin doses. The Diabetes Cockpit application does not display glucose values directly but summarizes them in various metrics and overviews. The sources for these metrics are the direct applications of the sensor systems and additional health metrics, offering clear visualizations and personalized insights to support optimizing diabetes management [11].

Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with T1D who used the Diabetes Cockpit application (Syntactiq, Austria) and provided consent for scientific use of their data.

The data was organized into non‐overlapping six‐month blocks. Only complete calendar months from the same user (“user‐months”) were included. A user‐month was eligible if it contained at least 21 days with ≥ 200 glucose readings per day and 21 days with recorded energy expenditure; overlap between these days was not required. This ensured at least 70% data coverage. The blocks were generated using a sliding window (i.e., every calendar month between 2018 and 2024 was checked as a possible starting month), whereby a block was only valid if all 6 consecutive months met the coverage criteria. For each user, the block with the highest data coverage was selected. In total, a maximum of 413 non‐overlapping blocks would have been possible for all users. For each participant, a “best single block” with the highest overall coverage was identified.

For detailed activity analyses, a stricter duplicate‐filtering procedure was applied, as some activities had been recorded more than once (e.g., when two applications were used simultaneously). After removing duplicates, workouts were categorized as strength, cardio, or other (e.g., yoga).

Energy expenditure was extracted from participants' movement data and defined as the estimated energy used above the resting metabolic rate. Participants were stratified into quartiles of weekly energy expenditure (< 2206, 2206–3056, 3057–4165, > 4165 kcal/week) to compare activity levels and explore dose–response relationships. To mitigate the impact of potential measurement errors or data gaps, all energy expenditure data were included in the analysis, as no standardized thresholds were predefined in our inclusion criteria.

The study protocol and all associated regulatory aspects were approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Bayreuth, Germany (approval number: 25‐036). The study was registered in the German Register of Clinical Trials (DRKS00039550).

The primary endpoint was the comparison of TIR across four groups defined by weekly energy expenditure (kcal/week) over 6 months. Secondary endpoints included additional CGM‐derived metrics: time in tight range (TITR; 70–140 mg/dL), time below range (TBR1: 54–70 mg/dL; TBR2: < 54 mg/dL), time above range (TAR1: 180–250 mg/dL; TAR2: > 250 mg/dL), coefficient of variation (CV), mean glucose, and total weekly energy expenditure. Correlations were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation. Group comparisons were performed using one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests with appropriate post hoc analyses. Exploratory linear regression analyses were additionally performed to quantify changes in TBR1 and TBR 2 per 1000 kcal/week increase in energy expenditure. Analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2); p < 0.05 was considered significant.

The mean glucose level was 141 mg/dL [128; 159] with a standard deviation of 45 mg/dL [36; 55] and CV of 31% [28; 36] (see Table S1). The overall median [IQR] energy expenditure was 3056 kcal/week [2205; 4165]. Defining the four groups based on the regular energy expenditure of the participants yielded the following median [IQR] values: Q1: 1589 kcal/week [887; 2011], Q2: 2592 kcal/week [2400; 2846], Q3: 3432 kcal/week [3166; 3700] and Q4: 5044 kcal/week [4561; 5498] (see Figure S1).

When comparing CGM‐derived metrics across the four physical activity groups, significant differences were found only for TBR2 (< 54 mg/dL) (p = 0.03). Post hoc analyses showed that TBR2 was significantly higher in the highest energy expenditure group (Q4) compared with the lowest energy expenditure group (Q1), with median values of 0.43% (IQR 0.11–0.78) versus 0.12% (IQR 0.00–0.37), respectively (p = 0.03). No significant differences were observed for the remaining CGM‐derived metrics (p > 0.05). Correlation analyses between overall kcal/week and CGM‐derived metrics showed significant correlations for TBR2 (r = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.08–0.37; p = 0.002), TBR1 (54–70 mg/dL) (r = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05–0.34; p = 0.008) and CV (r = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.04–0.33; p = 0.009; see Figure 1). Exploratory linear regression analyses showed that each increase of 1000 kcal/week in energy expenditure was associated with a non‐significant 0.067 percentage‐point increase in TBR1 (95% confidence interval (CI) −0.006 to 0.14, p = 0.073), while no association was observed for TBR2 (slope −0.027 percentage points, 95% CI −0.21 to 0.16, p = 0.771). For CV, the regression analyses demonstrated a small but statistically significant association between weekly energy expenditure and glycemic variability. The regression coefficient was 0.67 (95% CI 0.09 to 1.30, p = 0.024), indicating an increase of 0.67 percentage points in CV per additional 1000 kcal/week. However, the explained variance was low (R 2 = 0.029).

Discussion

In this study, it was found that within the observed cohort with T1D, a physically active lifestyle was associated with an increased risk of clinically relevant hypoglycemia. The Q4 group with the highest energy expenditure showed more time spent below range 2 (< 54 mg/dL) compared to the other groups. There are several possible reasons why hypoglycemia can occur more frequently during physical activity and exercise. Moderate‐intensity exercise typically stabilizes blood glucose in T1D via enhanced insulin‐independent uptake [12 ], whereas high‐intensity activity may first raise glucose (catecholamine release) before a subsequent drop [13]. It would have been important to find out whether the individuals in the study population were engaged in everyday and known or in unknown sporting activities, as this would have made an acute effect of exercise on insulin sensitivity more likely.

The correlation between overall kcal/week and the CGM‐derived metrics for TBR1 and TBR2 supports the assumption that there is a small but consistent association between higher energy expenditure and an increased risk of hypoglycemia.

The exploratory regression analyses further support the findings of the correlation analyses, suggesting that higher weekly energy expenditure was not associated with clinically meaningful increases in hypoglycemia risk. While a small positive trend was observed for TBR1, the association did not reach statistical significance, and no relationship was found for TBR2. A statistically significant association was observed between energy expenditure and glycemic variability; however, the effect size was small, with an increase of less than 1 percentage point in CV per additional 1000 kcal/week and a very low explained variance (R 2 = 0.029).

More frequent or more intense exercise physiologically elevates the likelihood of hypoglycemia, a finding that is well aligned with the existing literature [14], particularly when carbohydrate intake and insulin dose adjustments are insufficient. The observed effect of glucose variability, as reflected by the CV, was also significant over the 6‐month period. A statistically significant association was observed between energy expenditure and glycemic variability; however, the effect size was small, with an increase of less than 1 percentage point in CV per additional 1000 kcal/week and a very low explained variance (R 2 = 0.029). Overall, these findings suggest that higher physical activity levels may only have a minor impact on glycemic variability and hypoglycemia exposure in this cohort. Higher levels of physical activity were associated with greater glucose variability, indicating more short‐term fluctuations despite overall good diabetes management. This aligns with previous findings suggesting that increased CV is linked to a higher risk of hypoglycemia [15]. Although the absolute hypoglycemia values were statistically significant between groups, with TBR1 at 1.5% and TBR2 at 0.2% in total, they remain moderate from a clinical perspective. Nonetheless, it is important to emphasize that TBR2 events, glucose levels below 54 mg/dL, are considered safety‐critical due to the potential for neurological impairment [16] and should be avoided whenever possible.

As this is a retrospective observation conducted in a real‐world setting, the use of the app provides valuable insight into the participants' daily conditions, conferring a high degree of ecological validity. The cohort under consideration and the observational period provide sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences in CGM‐derived metrics. Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. The accuracy of the recorded movement data cannot be fully ascertained. Previous findings indicate that smartphone‐based applications exhibit lower accuracy compared to wrist‐worn smartwatches [17]. Similarly, with regard to data accuracy, it was not specified which CGM devices were used by the participants. The accuracy of different sensor systems varies to some extent, which may have influenced the results [18]. The retrospective design precludes causal inferences, as numerous potential confounders, such as insulin dosing, dietary intake, timing of exercise and exercise‐related adaptations, were not controlled for. In addition, self‐selection bias is likely, as many application users may be technologically proficient and possess greater knowledge of diabetes management. Finally, the type and intensity of physical activity and exercise were not systematically documented, which is relevant because different exercise modalities can interfere in various ways with the glucose (e.g., endurance exercise reduces glucose levels, whereas strength training tends to maintain more stable glycaemia responses [19]). The Diabetes Cockpit app lacked intensity metrics, limiting quantification in our dataset. Future studies should include objective intensity data to clarify its impact. Also limiting is the reliance on potentially inaccurate app‐based energy data and the absence of systematic documentation on activity type and intensity. Another limitation is the lack of carbohydrate intake data or insulin doses administered from the participants. With data of this kind, our current analysis would be significantly more meaningful.

Conclusion

This retrospective real‐world analysis confirms that physical activity and exercise are generally safe for adults with T1D, maintaining stable glycemic control over time. While higher energy expenditure slightly increased hypoglycemia and variability, absolute risks remained low. These results emphasize individualized management to safely incorporate exercise. App‐based real‐world data provide valuable insights into activity, exercise, and glucose control, supporting digital tools for personalized diabetes care.
