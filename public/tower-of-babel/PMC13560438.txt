Psychological and clinical associations of adverse childhood experiences in Chinese patients with eating disorders
Authors: Xiao Zhang, Chao Chen, Xueni Li, Qingmei Kong, Peihua Song, Darong Zhang, Tianmei Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEating disorders (EDs) are severe mental illnesses with significant morbidity and mortality. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are recognized risk factors, but their specific impact across ED subtypes and the mediating psychological mechanisms require further elucidation.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study (2008–2015), 970 outpatients with eating disorders (919 female) were assessed for adverse childhood experiences and clinical behaviors with the Eating Disorder Questionnaire, and for psychological traits with the Eating Disorder Inventory-1. This study used factorial ANOVA, Lasso regression, and mediation analysis to clarify the effects of ACEs and diagnostic subtypes, identify specific influencing factors, and explore the mediating role of psychological traits.ResultsACEs were nominally associated with most clinical behaviors except dieting; after correction, the most robust associations were observed for binge eating, laxative use, and several onset-age variables. Childhood emotional abuse most profoundly impacted psychological traits. The cumulative number of ACEs was associated with increased severity of psychological traits and a higher likelihood of clinical behaviors. Mediation analyses revealed that psychological traits significantly mediated the relationship between ACEs and clinical behaviors. For instance, ineffectiveness mediated the link from cumulative ACE count (mediation proportion = 0.481), “criticism and blame” (mediation proportion = 0.629), and “abandonment threats” (mediation proportion = 0.601) to binge eating.ConclusionsChildhood emotional abuse and cumulative ACEs significantly correlated with worsened psychological traits and clinical ED behaviors, with notable implications for binge-eating/purging symptoms. Several psychological traits showed substantial indirect associations between ACE exposure and clinical ED behaviors. These findings highlight the necessity of routine ACE assessment and trauma-informed interventions targeting these psychological vulnerabilities in ED treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40337-026-01689-5.

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are serious mental disorders with the highest mortality rates among all mental disorders [1, 2]. These illnesses often follow a chronic and debilitating trajectory, lasting for years, and contributing significantly to long-term disability, caregiver strain, and substantial healthcare expenditures [3, 4]. In recent years, a growing body of evidence has highlighted an increasing prevalence of EDs in Asian countries, including China, reflecting a concerning trend [5, 6]. EDs are categorized into four subtypes: anorexia nervosa, restricting type (AN-R); anorexia nervosa, binge-eating/purging type (AN-BP); bulimia nervosa (BN); and atypical eating disorders, also referred to as eating disorders not otherwise specified (EDNOS, DSM-IV category). These categories highlight the diverse nature of EDs, reflected in their distinct eating behaviors, psychological characteristics, and physical impacts [7]. Despite extensive research, the origins and mechanisms driving EDs remain poorly understood. Their development and persistence are believed to arise from a complex interplay of genetic, psychological, environmental, and sociocultural influences [8, 9]. Recent epidemiological evidence further suggests that the burden of eating disorders in China has continued to increase over the past decade, particularly among adolescents and young adults [10]. However, research examining adverse childhood experiences in Chinese patients with eating disorders remains limited. A recent study reported that childhood sexual abuse was associated with distinct psychological and clinical characteristics in Chinese patients with eating disorders [11].

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are potent non-specific risk factors for EDs, although associations between specific trauma exposures and clinical phenotypes are often overlapping and remain incompletely understood. According to the ACEs Pyramid Model, the impact of early life adversity unfolds through an escalating cascade [12]. In this framework, ACEs (Level 3) are hypothesized to disrupt neurodevelopment, leading to profound Level 5 impairments in social, emotional, and cognitive functioning. These impairments are represented by “psychological traits” - operationally defined by the Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI-1) subscales - which distinguish between ED-specific symptomatic attitudes (e.g., body dissatisfaction) and non-specific psychological vulnerabilities (e.g., ineffectiveness) [13, 14]. In this hierarchical framework, these impairments may be associated with increased likelihood of adopting health risk behaviors, where disordered eating acts as a functional, albeit maladaptive, mechanism to regulate overwhelming negative affect or to regain a sense of autonomy in the wake of trauma [15, 16]. Consequently, individuals may adopt Level 6 health risk behaviors—including bingeing, purging, or extreme restriction—as functional, albeit maladaptive, mechanisms to regulate trauma-induced emotional dysregulation or to regain a sense of control [17, 18].

Different ACE exposures may show partially overlapping associations with ED-related psychological and clinical phenotypes. For instance, childhood emotional abuse (CEA) is theorized to damage self-worth and bodily trust, fostering traits like ineffectiveness and interoceptive deficits [18, 19]. Furthermore, the established link between CSA and bulimic symptoms is often moderated by comorbid borderline personality traits and severe emotion dysregulation [20]. These correlates may facilitate an ‘impulsive-compulsive’ pathway, where binge-purge behaviors serve as a maladaptive response to the profound affective instability and identity disturbance rooted in early sexual trauma [21]. Similarly, childhood physical abuse (CPA) has been significantly associated with an increased likelihood of bingeing and purging behaviors, acting as a maladaptive strategy to manage the physiological arousal and loss of physical autonomy inherent to such trauma [22]. Childhood sexual abuse (CSA) is also frequently linked to binge-purge spectrum disorders (e.g., BN or AN-BP) as a means of managing internalized shame and dissociating from trauma-related bodily distress [12, 23]. Despite these theoretical associations, existing research in Chinese populations has often lacked integrated analyses to clarify which specific traits mediate the path from individual ACE types to clinical outcomes.

To address this, our study utilizes a large-scale clinical registry cohort collected between 2008 and 2015, representing a subsequent investigation based on the same registry data, to explore the interwoven nature of ACEs and ED pathology. Building on the ACEs Pyramid Model, we test two explicit hypotheses: (1) that cumulative ACE exposure exhibits a dose-response relationship with the severity of both Level 5 psychological traits and Level 6 clinical behaviors; and (2) that psychological traits (Level 5) may partially account for associations between ACE exposure and clinical symptoms (Level 6).

Methods and materials

Participants

In the present study, we analyzed data from the same cohort described in our previously published article, using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria [15]. Briefly, participants aged 9–35 years were consecutively recruited from the outpatient clinic of Peking University Sixth Hospital between 2008 and 2015 through the Eating Disorders Registration Project. Inclusion criteria were: (1) a diagnosis of an eating disorder according to DSM-IV criteria; (2) the ability to understand and complete the questionnaires; and (3) written informed consent provided by the participant or their legal guardian. Diagnoses were established according to DSM-IV criteria by attending-level psychiatrists or above following a structured clinical diagnostic procedure based on routine initial interviews and were independently reviewed by a second attending psychiatrist to ensure diagnostic consistency. Exclusion criteria were: (1) the presence of comorbid severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, organic mental disorders, intellectual disability); (2) current self-harm or suicidal behavior, or reported suicidal ideation, and major depressive disorder; (3) patients with severe physical conditions (e.g., severe malnutrition) or abnormal clinical findings (e.g., severe anemia, markedly elevated liver enzymes) deemed unsuitable for participation by the attending physicians.

Initially, 1,056 patients with eating disorders were enrolled. After excluding individuals who did not complete the questionnaires or declined research use of their data, 1,001 patients remained. Following additional screening for trauma-related variables, 970 participants (51 males and 919 females), including 290 adolescents and 680 adults, constituted the final analytical sample. Detailed information regarding the enrollment process and participant flow is provided in the Supplementary Methods and Figure S1.

Questionnaire

The Eating Disorder Questionnaire (EDQ), a self-assessment tool, was used in this study to collect comprehensive patient data. It gathered demographic details such as sex, age, education, and comorbidities, along with weight and height measured on-site. Additionally, it documented detailed behavioral symptoms, including the presence and onset age of dieting, binge eating, purging, and the use of diuretics, laxatives, or dieting pills. As such, its items were analyzed individually based on their specific clinical content.

In the EDQ questionnaire, seven items constitute the adverse childhood experiences questionnaire, asking participants, “Before you were 18, did any of the following happen to you? (Respond with Yes or No).” The questions include: (1) Criticism and Blame: Someone constantly criticized you and blamed you for minor things. (2) Physical Abuse: Someone physically beat you (hit, slapped, threw something at you, pushed you). (3) Threats of Harm: Someone threatened to hurt or kill you or do something sexual to you. (4) Abandonment Threats: Someone threatened to abandon or leave you. (5) Witnessed Domestic Violence: You witnessed one parent physically beating (hitting, slapping) the other parent. (6) Family Sexual Abuse: Someone from your family forced you to engage in sexual activities (e.g., unwanted touching, fondling, sexual kissing, or intercourse). (7) Non-Family Sexual Abuse: Someone outside your family forced you to engage in sexual activities (e.g., unwanted touching, fondling, sexual kissing, or intercourse).

The seven ACE items were derived from the EDQ clinical registry and were not based on a standardized ACE instrument such as the original ACE questionnaire [24] or the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [25]. For analytic purposes, these items were categorized into three theoretically informed subtypes aligned with established maltreatment frameworks [26], although they do not constitute psychometrically validated composites. Specifically, CEA included “Criticism and Blame,” “Threats of Harm,” and “Abandonment Threats”; CPA included “Physical Abuse” and “Witnessed Domestic Violence”; and CSA included “Family Sexual Abuse” and “Non-Family Sexual Abuse.”

The Eating disorder inventory-1 (EDI-1) is a globally recognized self-rating scale comprising 64 items, each rated on a six-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 6 (“always”) 1. These items are grouped into eight distinct subscales: Drive for Thinness, Bulimia, Body Dissatisfaction, Ineffectiveness, Perfectionism, Interpersonal Distrust, Interoceptive Awareness, and Fear of Maturity. Each subscale allows for the calculation of an average score, providing detailed insights into specific psychological dimensions of eating disorders. The scale has been validated for reliability and applicability in the Chinese context by local researchers [13, 27]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α coefficients were as follows: Drive for Thinness = 0.71, Bulimia = 0.85, Body Dissatisfaction = 0.89, Ineffectiveness = 0.80, Perfectionism = 0.60, Interoceptive Awareness = 0.76, Interpersonal Distrust = 0.61, and Maturity Fears = 0.70. These values indicate marginal to excellent internal consistency across subscales.

Analytical framework

This study investigated the impact of ACEs and diagnostic subtypes on psychological traits and clinical behaviors using a structured integration of confirmatory and theory-guided exploratory analyses. The analytic plan followed a hierarchical funneling logic designed to address three primary objectives: (1) Confirmatory assessment of the main and interaction effects of ACEs and diagnostic subtypes to test the pre-specified macro-level hypothesis of a cumulative dose-response relationship across all ED-related domains (Level 3 to Levels 5 and 6); (2) Exploratory identification of specific pathogenetic factors using Lasso regression analysis, utilized as a technique to handle multicollinearity and identify the most salient ACE conditions and psychological dimensions among the wide range of variables; (3) Exploratory testing of parallel mediation pathways, focusing on generating hypotheses regarding the specific mechanisms where Level 5 psychological traits may partially mediate associations between the Lasso-selected ACEs and Level 6 clinical symptoms; and (4) Robustness and sensitivity exploration, including item-level ACE association analyses, ACE co-occurrence analyses, latent class analysis (LCA), age-group interaction analyses, and mediation sensitivity analyses, conducted to further examine the overlap, heterogeneity, developmental stability, and robustness of ACE-related associations.

Demographic analysis and interaction effects

Demographic information was analyzed to summarize the basic characteristics of the sample, categorized by the presence or absence of ACEs. Chi-square tests were used to assess differences in categorical variables, including sex, comorbidity status, and the presence of specific clinical behaviors, while independent sample t-tests were applied to continuous variables such as age, BMI, Psychological Traits, and the onset age of clinical behaviors. To further elucidate group differences, univariate regression analyses were conducted with the number of ACEs as the dependent variable.

The interaction effects between ACEs and diagnostic subtypes on Psychological Traits and clinical behaviors were assessed using factorial ANOVA, implemented via the Univariate General Linear Model (GLM) in SPSS. This analysis assessed: (1) The main effects of diagnostic subtypes on Psychological Traits and clinical behaviors, (2) The main effects of ACEs on these variables, (3) The interaction effects between diagnostic subtypes and ACE history on these variables. To balance statistical rigor with transparency, all p values reported in the text and tables are nominal (uncorrected). However, statistical significance was determined using a stringent Bonferroni-adjusted threshold (α = 0.00625; 0.05/8) for the eight psychological traits to control for multiple comparisons. Similarly, the ten clinical behaviors were evaluated using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (α = 0.005; 0.05/10). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to identify specific group differences in psychological traits and clinical behaviors across diagnostic subtypes and ACE histories. For significant interaction effects, stratified analyses were conducted within each diagnostic subtype to investigate how ACE history influenced psychological and clinical behaviors in a diagnosis-specific context. Collectively, the main effects and interaction analyses provided a more precise characterization of how ACE exposure and diagnostic subtypes were associated with psychological traits and clinical behaviors.

Regression analysis of ACEs on psychological traits and clinical behaviors

To assess the independent effects of ACEs and other risk factors on psychological traits and clinical behaviors, Lasso regression analysis was employed. This approach, utilizing cross-validation, identified the most salient predictors under penalization. Coefficients were interpreted descriptively rather than inferentially. The regularization and cross-validation procedures inherently mitigate the risk of false-positive findings in high-dimensional analyses. The independent variables included individual ACE conditions (derived from responses to seven specific questions), demographic factors (age, sex, and diagnostic subtypes treated as dummy variables), and clinical factors (current BMI, and comorbidities). Each psychological trait (e.g., drive for thinness, body dissatisfaction) and clinical behavior (e.g., binge eating, purging) was analyzed as a dependent variable. To focus the main finding, only predictors with absolute values exceeding 0.2 were included in the main text. Results meeting the criterion 0 < |β| ≤ 0.2 are available in the Supplement tables. These findings lay the groundwork for subsequent mediation analyses.

Mediation effects: “ACE–psychological traits–clinical behaviors” pathway

Recognizing that ACEs significantly influence all eight psychological traits and have varying effects on clinical behaviors, we conducted mediation analyses using the lavaan package in R to investigate how psychological traits (M) mediate the relationship between ACEs (X) and clinical behaviors (Y). The analyses assessed the effects of ACEs at both the overall and individual levels, generating a total of 10 models. Within each model, eight psychological traits were tested simultaneously as parallel mediators; therefore, Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons (corrected α = 0.05/8 = 0.00625). Firstly, for the overall impact of ACEs in the full sample, we constructed separate models for each of the four clinical behaviors significantly influenced by ACEs (excluding dieting, as it was not significantly associated with ACE exposure). In these models, X = ACE count, M = eight psychological traits, and Y = clinical behaviors meeting the criteria. Secondly, for specific ACEs in the full sample, “Criticism and Blame,” “Abandonment Threats,” and “Family Sexual Abuse” exhibited β values exceeding |0.2| in their effects on psychological traits. Consequently, we developed six models where X = presence/absence of the three specific ACEs, M = eight psychological traits, and Y = clinical behaviors affected by the specific ACEs identified in regression analysis.

Robustness and sensitivity analyses

To address the limitation that the ACE indicators were non-standardized binary self-report items and that the predefined ACE subtype grouping had not been empirically validated, we conducted exploratory analyses using the original item-level ACE indicators. We first described the prevalence of the seven ACE items and their pairwise co-occurrence structure. We then used each of the seven ACE items as predictors in adjusted models examining associations with EDI-1 psychological traits, clinical behaviors, and ages at onset of clinical behaviors. Linear regression was used for continuous outcomes, and logistic regression was used for binary behavioral outcomes. All models were adjusted for age group (< 18 vs. >=18 years), sex, education level, diagnostic subtype, current BMI, and psychiatric comorbidity.

Because all seven ACE indicators were binary, we used latent class analysis (LCA) to identify data-driven ACE exposure patterns. The LCA included only the seven original ACE items and did not include diagnosis, EDI-1 variables, or clinical behavior variables. We fitted one- through five-class models and selected the final solution based on BIC, AIC, entropy, minimum class size, and clinical interpretability. After assigning individuals to their most likely latent class, we examined associations between latent class membership and diagnostic subtype, EDI-1 psychological traits, clinical behaviors, and ages at onset of clinical behaviors. Given the wide age range of the sample (9–35 years), we additionally tested whether associations between LCA class and key psychological or behavioral phenotypes were moderated by age group (< 18 vs. >=18 years).

Because some EDI-1 subscales conceptually overlap with eating-disorder symptoms, we conducted sensitivity analyses of the original mediation models. In the first set of models, we excluded EDI-Bulimia. In a second, more conservative set of models, we retained only relatively non-symptom-overlapping psychological traits, including Ineffectiveness, Perfectionism, Interpersonal Distrust, Interoceptive Awareness, and Fear of Maturity.

Results

Demographics and interaction effects

Demographics

The influence of ACEs on diagnostic subtypes, psychological traits, and clinical behaviors was apparent across the study sample. The prevalence of ACEs varied significantly by diagnostic subtype (χ² = 9.005, p = 0.029), with the highest rates observed in BN (69.11%) and EDNOS (63.41%), compared to AN-BP (61.74%) and AN-R (56.60%). Individuals with ACEs were more likely to have psychiatric comorbidities (χ² = 9.538, p = 0.002), higher current BMI (p = 0.032), and more severe scores across all eight psychological traits (all p < 0.001). Among 5 clinical behaviors, individuals with ACEs demonstrated higher prevalence rates of binge eating (χ² = 20.392, p < 0.001), purging (χ² = 12.598, p = 0.001), laxative use (χ² = 11.231, p = 0.003), and dieting pill use (χ² = 10.790, p = 0.001), alongside earlier onset ages for these 4 behaviors. Additional univariate regression analysis for these variables and ACE numbers further validated these findings, suggesting a dose-response effect, see Fig. 1. For more detailed results, refer to Table 1, Supplement Figure S2.

Main and interaction effects of ACEs and diagnosis

Significant diagnostic effects were observed across all psychological traits, with the strongest associations noted for Bulimia, Drive for Thinness, and Body Dissatisfaction (all p < 0.001). Clinical behaviors also showed significant diagnostic effects. These effects were most robust for Binge Eating and Purging (both p < 0.001). These findings are highly consistent with clinical observations, further confirming the reliability and validity of diagnostic classifications in distinguishing between different eating disorder phenotypes. ACEs exerted a pervasive and negative influence on all psychological traits, with a history of trauma being most strongly associated with heightened levels of Ineffectiveness, Perfectionism, and Interpersonal Distrust (all p ≤ 0.003). Among clinical behaviors, ACEs significantly impacted five variables. Notably, childhood trauma appeared to advance the disease course, as evidenced by the significantly earlier Dieting Pill Onset and Purging Onset Age (both p ≤ 0.001). No interaction effects remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. For more detailed results, refer to Supplement Table S1, S2.

Psychological traits

Among the three types of ACEs, childhood emotional abuse had the most profound and widespread impact on psychological characteristics in individuals with eating disorders. For “Criticism and Blame”, the most affected traits included Ineffectiveness (β = 0.330) and Perfectionism (β = 0.305). For “Abandonment Threats”, the most affected traits included Perfectionism (β = 0.299), Interoceptive Awareness (β = 0.286), Drive for Thinness (β = 0.280), Ineffectiveness (β = 0.263), and Bulimia (β = 0.245). These findings underscore the pervasive influence of emotional abuse on key psychological traits. Among childhood sex abuses, “Family Sexual Abuse” predominantly influenced Bulimia (β = 0.250), emphasizing its role in disordered eating and self-image issues.

The influence of diagnostic subtypes on psychological traits highlights notable differences across groups. Individuals with BN exhibited the strongest effects, with elevated scores in Bulimia (β = 1.022), Drive for Thinness (β = 0.567), Interoceptive Awareness (β = 0.244), and Body Dissatisfaction (β = 0.207). In the AN-BP group, the psychological traits also showed heightened effects, particularly in Bulimia (β = 0.849), Interoceptive Awareness (β = 0.410), Drive for Thinness (β = 0.398), and Ineffectiveness (β = 0.255). Conversely, AN-R consistently demonstrated lower psychological trait scores, with negative associations observed for Bulimia (β = −0.835), Drive for Thinness (β = −0.360), Interoceptive Awareness (β = −0.326), and Ineffectiveness (β = −0.262). These findings underscore the differential psychological profiles associated with each diagnostic subtype. The presence of comorbidities was associated with elevated scores across multiple psychological traits. Specifically, higher scores were observed for Ineffectiveness (β = 0.351), Interoceptive Awareness (β = 0.325), Fear of Maturity (β = 0.271), Drive for Thinness (β = 0.258), Bulimia (β = 0.233), Interpersonal Distrust (β = 0.232), and Perfectionism (β = 0.218). These findings highlight the pervasive impact of comorbidities on key psychological traits in individuals with eating disorders. Regarding sex differences, females exhibited notably higher scores compared to males, particularly in Drive for Thinness (β = 0.568) and Body Dissatisfaction (β = 0.366), suggesting that gender may play a significant role in the manifestation of these psychological traits.

Occurrence of clinical behaviors

In this section, we report the OR values. Among childhood emotional abuse, “Criticism and Blame” was associated with the occurrence of dieting pill use (OR = 1.613), binge eating (OR = 1.423), and dieting (OR = 1.351). “Abandonment Threats” was associated with the occurrence of dieting pill use (OR = 1.610), laxative use (OR = 1.576), and binge eating (OR = 1.495). Among childhood physical abuses, “Physical Abuse” was associated with the occurrence of dieting pill use (OR = 1.385). “Witnessed Domestic Violence” was linked to the occurrence of binge eating (OR = 1.612). Among childhood sexual abuses, “Family Sexual Abuse” was associated with an increased likelihood of dieting pill use (OR = 3.344), while also decrease the odds of dieting (OR = 0.735). “Non-Family Sexual Abuse” was associated with an increased likelihood of binge eating (OR = 1.302).

The influence of diagnoses on clinical behaviors reveals distinct patterns in eating disorders. AN-R is associated with a reduced likelihood of binge eating (OR = 0.498), dieting pill use (OR = 0.331), laxative use (OR = 0.592), and purging (OR = 0.380). In contrast, AN-BP is associated with an increased likelihood of dieting (OR = 1.692), binge eating (OR = 5.858), purging (OR = 4.228), and laxative use (OR = 1.675). BN increase the likelihood of dieting (OR = 1.495), binge eating (OR = 19.620), purging (OR = 10.627), laxative use (OR = 2.165), and dieting pill use (OR = 1.985). For sex, females demonstrated a higher likelihood of engaging in laxative use (OR = 1.859) and dieting (OR = 1.315); while exhibited less likelihood of purging (OR = 0.497). The presence of psychiatric comorbidities was associated with an increased likelihood of laxative use (OR = 1.477) and binge eating (OR = 1.326). Current BMI was positively associated with a higher likelihood of binge eating (OR = 1.259) and dieting (OR = 1.117). Education level was positively associated with a higher likelihood of binge eating (OR = 1.328).

Onset age of clinical behaviors

“Criticism and Blame” advances the occurrence of dieting pill use (β = −2.260) and dieting (β = −0.250). “Threats of Harm” was linked to earlier onset of dieting (β = −0.316) and binge eating (β = −0.498). Robustness and sensitivity analyses

Among the 970 patients, the most common ACE items were criticism/blame (47.8%), physical abuse (35.2%), and witnessing domestic violence (30.6%). Less frequent items included intrafamilial sexual abuse (2.8%) and extrafamilial sexual abuse (6.7%). Overall, 607 patients (62.6%) reported at least one ACE. The pairwise co-occurrence pattern among ACE items is shown in Figure S3 and Table S5.

In the adjusted item-level models, associations between ACEs and eating-disorder phenotypes were most evident for psychological traits, particularly for emotional-abuse-related items; item-level associations with behavioral outcomes were weaker (Table S6). Model comparisons supported a three-class LCA solution (Figure S4, Table S7). As shown in Figure S5, the three classes were labelled Low ACE (n = 517, 53.3%), Emotional-physical/domestic violence ACE (n = 328, 33.8%), and Multi-type ACE with threat/sexual exposure (n = 125, 12.9%). The Low ACE class showed low probabilities across all ACE items. The Emotional-physical/domestic violence ACE class was characterized mainly by higher probabilities of criticism/blame, physical abuse, and witnessing domestic violence. The Multi-type ACE with threat/sexual exposure class showed elevated probabilities across multiple ACE items, especially threat of harm, threat of abandonment, and sexual abuse-related items.

After adjustment for age group and other covariates, LCA class membership was significantly associated with several EDI-1 psychological traits (Table S8). Compared with the Low ACE class, the Multi-type ACE with threat/sexual exposure class showed higher scores on Interpersonal Distrust, Ineffectiveness, Interoceptive Awareness, Perfectionism, Drive for Thinness, and Bulimia. The Emotional-physical/domestic violence ACE class also showed higher scores on Perfectionism, Interpersonal Distrust, Ineffectiveness, and Interoceptive Awareness. These findings are consistent with the main analyses, in which emotional maltreatment and cumulative ACE burden were associated with more severe psychological traits.

For clinical behaviors, compared with the Low ACE class, the Multi-type ACE with threat/sexual exposure class was associated with higher odds of diet pill use (OR = 3.33, 95% CI: 1.54–7.19, q = 0.021) and binge eating (OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.22–4.51, q = 0.037). The Emotional-physical/domestic violence ACE class was also associated with higher odds of binge eating (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.16–2.68, q = 0.037). In onset-age analyses, the Multi-type ACE with threat/sexual exposure class was associated with earlier onset of laxative use (β = −1.04, 95% CI: −1.70 to −0.38, q = 0.021) and earlier onset of binge eating (β = −0.72, 95% CI: −1.24 to −0.20, q = 0.034; Table S8).

We found no evidence that age group significantly moderated the associations between LCA class and the main psychological or behavioral phenotypes; all FDR-adjusted q values for the interaction terms were nonsignificant (Table S9). In the mediation sensitivity analyses, the total indirect associations between ACE count and binge eating and between ACE count and laxative use remained after excluding EDI-Bulimia. When the mediator set was further restricted to relatively non-symptom-overlapping psychological traits, the statistical indirect associations from ACE count to binge eating and laxative use remained comparatively stable (Table S10).

Discussion

This study provides substantial evidence for the profound and multifaceted impact of ACEs on the psychological traits and clinical behaviors of individuals with EDs. Our findings indicate that ACEs not only associated with the core psychopathology of EDs but also exhibit strong statistical links to the presentation of specific clinical behaviors, with these relationships concurrently mirroring variations in key psychological traits. These results align with a growing body of literature underscoring the critical role of early life adversity in the clinical course of EDs [28]. The observed direct and indirect associations between ACEs and ED symptoms highlight the necessity of integrating trauma-informed perspectives into both assessment and treatment protocols.

Our investigation into the interplay between ACEs and diagnostic subtypes reveals that while ACEs exert a pervasive influence across all ED diagnoses, their specific impact on psychological traits and clinical behaviors can vary. This is consistent with research suggesting differential ACE profiles and clinical correlates across ED subtypes, such as higher rates of emotional abuse and household dysfunction in individuals with bulimia nervosa (BN) or binge-eating/purging spectrum disorders compared to restrictive anorexia nervosa (AN) [12, 29]. The findings underscore that a history of ACEs may be associated with the phenotypic expression of EDs, co-occurring, for instance, to more severe psychological distress and associated binge-eating or purging behaviors. However, these subtype-specific differences must be interpreted with caution given the well-documented phenomenon of diagnostic crossover and temporal instability in EDs. Longitudinal studies indicate that 20% to 50% of individuals migrate across diagnostic boundaries—particularly from restrictive to binge-purge patterns—within the first five years of illness [30]. Within the framework of the ACEs Pyramid Model [12], the “distinct” characteristics we observed likely represent fluid Level 6 health risk behaviors that manifest at specific clinical snapshots, whereas the underlying Level 5 psychological impairments (e.g., ineffectiveness and impaired interoceptive awareness) may serve as more stable, transdiagnostic vulnerabilities [31].

The prominent role of childhood emotional abuse in ED psychopathology resonates strongly with contemporary research. Emotional abuse, encompassing chronic criticism, blame, or threats, appears to be particularly insidious in fostering core psychological vulnerabilities such as ineffectiveness, perfectionism, and poor interoceptive awareness [16]. Network analysis studies have identified emotional abuse as a central node connecting childhood maltreatment to specific ED psychopathological features, including feelings of inadequacy and difficulties in identifying internal states [32]. These psychological traits have been identified as correlates and maintaining factors for disordered eating, suggesting that emotional abuse is associated with psychological vulnerability patterns that are central to the clinical presentation of eating disorders [33]. Furthermore, our findings regarding childhood physical abuse underscore its specific link to binge-eating behaviors. Theoretically, physical trauma disrupts a child’s sense of physical autonomy and physiological arousal regulation; consequently, individuals may adopt disordered eating (Level 6 behaviors) as a maladaptive, compensatory strategy to manage the heightened internal distress and loss of control inherent to such bodily violations [22, 34].

Furthermore, this study demonstrates a significant dose-response relationship, where the accumulation of ACEs was associated with greater severity of psychological distress and clinical behaviors. This finding is consistent with the cumulative stress model, which posits that the total burden of adversity is a critical determinant of subsequent mental health outcomes [35]. We found that psychological traits such as bulimia-related cognitions, ineffectiveness, and interoceptive deficits underlie the statistical associations between cumulative ACEs and behaviors like binge eating and purging, offer crucial insights into these pathways. Addressing these co-occurring psychological factors could thus be pivotal in mitigating the long-term consequences of cumulative childhood trauma. Importantly, nearly all eight psychological traits showed significant mediation effects. In particular, non-specific traits such as Ineffectiveness - a construct reflecting self-evaluation rather than eating behavior - emerged as the strongest mediators, showed a relatively large indirect-effect proportion in the single-mediator pathway between emotional abuse and binge eating. This pattern suggests that early trauma contributes to broad psychological impairment, rather than merely reflecting symptom overlap. However, given the cross-sectional nature of our data, these indirect pathways must be interpreted strictly as statistical associations rather than definitive causal or chronological mechanisms. While these findings align with our theoretical model, prospective longitudinal studies are required to establish true temporal ordering. Conceptually, within the transdiagnostic model of eating disorders [36], non-specific traits like Ineffectiveness reflect a deeply compromised core self-schema rather than mere secondary symptoms. Early adversities are statistically linked to these damaged psychological configurations, which strongly correlate with severe health-risk behaviors as maladaptive attempts to regulate distress and restore personal autonomy.

As for specific forms of emotional abuse, our results highlight “Criticism and Blame” and “Abandonment Threats” as particularly detrimental. Experiences of persistent parental criticism have been linked to the development of maladaptive perfectionism and increased risk for EDs, potentially driving core symptoms like an extreme drive for thinness or body dissatisfaction [37, 38]. Similarly, threats of abandonment, which can engender profound emotional insecurity and a diminished sense of self-worth, likely contribute to traits such as ineffectiveness and interpersonal distrust, which in turn mediate the path to disordered eating behaviors. These specific experiences can foster a desperate need for control, often manifested through the regulation of food and weight, as a compensatory mechanism [39]. The mediating role of psychological traits in these relationships underscores the complex cross-sectional associations between specific adverse emotional environments and ED symptoms. In contemporary Chinese society, child-rearing is traditionally anchored in parental authority, hierarchy, and high academic expectations. Recent evidence confirms that early childhood sex abuse is uniquely associated with distinct psychological distress in Chinese eating disorder patients [11]. Under such cultural pressures, affected individuals may covertly utilize the strict regulation of food and weight as a localized arena to regain personal control [6].

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the temporal relationship between ACEs, psychological traits, and ED development. Accordingly, the observed indirect effects should be interpreted as statistical associations consistent with the proposed theoretical framework, rather than as evidence of directional or mechanistic pathways. Second, relying on retrospective self-reports introduces potential recall bias, which may lead to underreporting or misclassification of ACE exposure. Such biases could either attenuate or artificially inflate the observed effect sizes, meaning the true impact of adversity on ED pathology might be more substantial or complex than currently captured in our models. Additionally, these binary items cannot fully capture the timing, severity, or chronicity of childhood trauma. Third, this sample was recruited from a single clinical center, potentially limiting generalizability. Fourth, we acknowledge a potential limitation of conceptual overlap in our mediation models, as certain EDI-1 traits (e.g., “Bulimia”) are closely aligned with the behaviors they mediate. Fifth, the use of DSM-IV criteria reflects the period of data collection (2008–2015). Although the EDNOS group in this study primarily presented with binge-eating symptoms, the lack of formal DSM-5 BED categorization is a limitation, and the findings should be interpreted within this historical context. Sixth, ACEs were assessed using seven binary self-report items embedded in the EDQ rather than a validated multidimensional instrument. This may limit severity differentiation and comparability with studies employing standardized measures. The categorization of ACE subtypes was theoretically informed but not empirically validated, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Seventh, the study employed the EDI-1 rather than later revisions (EDI-2 or EDI-3), which may limit direct comparability with more recent studies using updated versions. Eighth, as individuals with major depressive disorder were excluded at study initiation (2008), despite the high prevalence of depressive comorbidity in eating disorders, the present findings should be interpreted within the bounds of these historical inclusion and exclusion criteria. In addition, formal refusal rates were not systematically recorded, limiting our ability to fully assess potential selection bias. Ninth, as data were initially paper-based and later digitized, cases with severely incomplete records were not archived electronically. Owing to the elapsed time and document retention regulations, a detailed analysis of the missing cases was not feasible. Future longitudinal research is needed to delineate these complex pathways over time and examine how specific interventions may influence symptom patterns over time.

Conclusion

This study reinforces the significant and pervasive impact of adverse childhood experiences on the psychological and behavioral manifestations of eating disorders. Notably, emotional abuse and the cumulative burden of ACEs emerge as critical factors that shape core ED psychopathology—such as ineffectiveness, perfectionism, and impaired interoceptive awareness—and were associated with disordered eating behaviors. The identified mediating role of these psychological traits provides crucial targets for therapeutic intervention. These findings strongly advocate for the routine assessment of ACEs and the integration of trauma-informed, evidence-based interventions into the standard care for all individuals with eating disorders to address underlying vulnerabilities and improve long-term outcomes.
