The hidden bias of missing data: multiple imputation reveals geographic disparities and nutrition-WASH synergy as drivers of childhood diarrhea in Nigeria- a cross-sectional analysis of 33,924 children
Authors: Joseph Opeolu Ashaolu, Lydia Amin Kaisi, Gbenga Oluwaseyi Alabi, Omoyajowo A. Esther, Y. M. Sylvain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the determinants of childhood diarrhea in Nigeria, examining socio-economic, geographic, nutritional, and WASH factors, while systematically comparing Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) versus Complete Case Analysis (CCA) to demonstrate how missing data methods bias conclusions.MethodsA secondary cross-sectional analysis was conducted using the latest Nigerian Demographic and Health Survey (2018 NDHS) data on 33,924 under-5 children. The primary outcome was a caregiver-reported diarrhea incidence. Independent predictors were identified using multivariable logistic regression. Robustness was tested by sensitivity analyses through comparison between MICE, CCA and testing principal results under Missing Not at Random (MNAR) assumptions. Multi-dimensional risk heatmaps and correlation networks illustrated interactive risks.ResultsDiarrhea prevalence was 12.0%. Profound geo-economic and geographical disparities were uncovered; residing in the North-East was the strongest risk factor (aOR = 2.61), while wealth and maternal education demonstrated protective associations. There was a very high synergistic interaction between poor nutrition and WASH, with combined high-risk categories having the greatest prevalence of diarrhea (15.4%). Intake of eggs had a strong protective association (aOR = 0.87) that persisted under MNAR models. Sensitivity analyses revealed CCA-generated biased estimates, masking protective effects of education and inducing spurious risks for vitamin A and fruit consumption.ConclusionChildhood diarrhea in Nigeria is driven by a complex interplay of interacting factors. Effective control requires a mix of integrated, multi-sectoral interventions involving WASH, nutrition, and poverty concurrently, focusing on high-burden zones. Methodologically, advanced management of missing data is critical to produce valid evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07206-6.

Introduction

Diarrhea disease remains a persistent global health challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where diarrhea is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in under-5 children [1]. Despite the phenomenal gains made in the prevention and treatment of diarrhea in the last few decades, this menace continues to kill hundreds of thousands of children yearly, with the overwhelming majority of these deaths reported in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [2]. The impact of diarrhea disease also goes beyond acute mortality, being a significant contributor to childhood malnutrition, stunting, and intellectual disability, thus, sustaining cycles of poverty and inequality [3]. The pathogenesis of diarrhea is multicausal. It is a complex interplay between infectious agents, environmental factors, and host susceptibility. Understanding its subtle determinants is therefore crucial in formulating effective and focused public health interventions.

The most significant routes of diarrhea pathogen transmission are fecal-oral; thus, prevention measures have Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) facilities as their focal center. Lack of access to improved drinking water, inadequate sanitation facilities, and inadequate hygiene practices are established diarrhea disease determinants [4]. For example, dependence on unimproved water and unimproved toilet or shared sanitary facilities substantially elevates exposure to fecal and pathogenic contaminations. In addition, water collection time can limit a household’s capacity for keeping up hygienic practices, adding an additional level of risk [5]. Although the inherent value of WASH cannot be over-emphasized, the effectiveness of its protective value could fluctuate significantly in different socio-economic and geographic settings, which implies other factors intervene in these associations.

The most crucial modulator is nutrition. The term ‘bidirectional diarrhea-malnutrition relationship’ has a significant role in child mortality [6]. Malnutrition, especially micronutrient deficiency such as vitamin A and zinc, impairs immune status, making children vulnerable to infections such as diarrhea [7]. On the other hand, chronic diarrhea blunts nutrient absorption, suppresses appetite, and elevates metabolic needs, hence worsening underlying malnutrition. Apart from micronutrient supplementation, overall food variety and intake of nutrient-dense foods like eggs, meat, fish, fruits, and dark leafy green vegetables play a crucial role in the establishment and sustenance of resistance to infection. Nonetheless, the precise interaction between individual dietary constituents, WASH status, and diarrhea outcomes remains unclear and potentially could uncover significant synergistic risks.

Given these tangled determinants, a single-variable analytical technique is inadequate to identify the true risk terrain. The interactive effect of combined risk factors such as the union of poor WASH and nutrition, can be multiplicative rather than additive, producing hotspots of high risk that are undetectable when factors are considered separately [8]. For instance, the protection provided by good diet may be lost in a situation of massive fecal contamination, as the protective effect of a good toilet may be constrained for a malnourished child who is immunocompromised. Therefore, cross-validation of results on the basis of diverse methods of dealing with missing data through complete case analysis (CCA) or more sophisticated techniques like Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) [9] is not a statistical nicety but a necessary procedure to verify the robustness of detected protective and risk factors.

This study attempts to address these challenges by performing a systematic, multi-dimensional evaluation of the determinants of childhood diarrhea disease in an endemic context. We look beyond univariable risk factors to examine the intricate interdependencies and synergisms among nutrition, WASH, and socio-demographic variables. With a toolkit of analytical strategies including multivariable regression, multi-dimensional risk mapping, and correlation network analysis, we seek to provide a high-resolution portrait of vulnerability. Moreover, we systematically test the sensitivity of our results to alternative approaches to missing data handling, presenting a more valid and higher quality perspective on the determinants of diarrhea disease. The long-term aim of the study is to provide evidence to inform integrated, focused, and responsive public health interventions to alleviate the residual burden of diarrhea among children.

Methodology

Conceptual framework

This analysis was grounded in a multi-dimensional conceptual framework that conceptualizes childhood diarrhea as an outcome of the complex interplay of distal socio-economic and geographic factors with proximal environmental and nutritional factors. The framework integrates elements of the socio-ecological model and the UNICEF conceptual framework for child malnutrition, adapted for diarrhea disease. We hypothesize that household’s socio-economic status, such as represented by wealth, maternal education and geography, defined by region and residence-influence access to adequate WASH facilities and nutritious food. This access in turn directly affects a child’s pathogen exposure and nutritional susceptibility to infection. Guided by this framework, our analytical approach, including variable selection and the construction of composite indices, aimed to test these pathways and their interactions (Supplementary Figure s1).

Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study compared results of the most recent demographic and health survey (2018 DHS) of Nigeria. The survey used a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling design to allow for national representativeness. Enumeration areas were used as the primary sampling units, and systematic random sampling of household in clusters was utilized. Dietary intake was assessed via maternal 24-hour recall of specific foods given as complementary feeding (eggs, meat, fish, fruits, dark leafy vegetables, organ meats), consistent with standard DHS methodology.

A participant flow diagram (Fig. 1) details sample derivation. From the original NDHS 2018 dataset of 42,112 under-5 children, 41 children (0.12%) were excluded due to missing diarrhea outcome data. An additional 8,147 children (19.35%) were excluded due to missing data on key covariates (maternal education, wealth index, or region), yielding 33,924 children (80.6% of original) with complete covariate data. Of these, 14,759 children (35.0% of original) had complete data on all variables including dietary factors, forming the CCA sample. The remaining 19,165 children (45.5% of original) had missing dietary data but were retained for MICE, resulting in a final imputed analytical sample of 33,924 children. Ethical approval for the parent survey was received from the relevant institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to data collection.

Study variables

The primary outcome was diarrhea, defined by maternal report of three or more loose stools in 24 h within the two weeks preceding the survey. Independent variables were grouped into three domains including (a) Socio-demographic: Child’s age and sex; mother’s age and education; household wealth index; region; and urban/rural residence. (b) Nutritional: Vitamin A and iron supplementation in the past six months; consumption of specific foods (eggs, meat, fish, fruits, dark leafy vegetables, organ meats) introduced as complementary feeding. (c) WASH: Type of toilet facility (improved/unimproved, shared status); drinking water source (improved/ unimproved); and water collection time. The complete list of variables included in the multivariable models is provided in the result tables.

Water sources were categorized based on the JMP (WHO/UNICEF) [10] categorization system, where improved sources include piped water to dwellings/yard/plot, public tap/standpipe, borehole/tube well, protected dug well, protected spring, rainwater, and packaged water. Unimproved sources include unprotected dug well, unprotected spring, cart with small tank/drum, truck-delivered water, and surface water (rivers, dams, lakes, ponds, streams, canals, irrigation channels). Toilets were coded according to JMP [10] standards where an ‘improved’ toilet was defined as having a flush/pour flush to sewer systems, septic tank, or pit latrine; Ventilated Improved Pit Latrines (VIP); Pit latrine with slab; or Composting. An ‘unimproved’ toilet included those with flush/pour flush to another place other than sewerage, septic, or pit; Pit latrines without slab/open pit; Bucket toilets; Hanging toilet or latrine; and open defecation.

Wealth index in DHS was computed using principal component analysis of assets owned by the households (such as television, radio, bicycle, motorcycle, car, refrigerator, mobile phone), features of the house (types of floor, roof, and walls; number of rooms for sleeping; type of fuel used for cooking), and water and sanitation facilities, while the combined nutrition-WASH risk score was generated using the sum of standard binary variables (1 = presence of risk factor, 0 = absence of risk factor) for seven domains: (1) no intake of vitamin A supplements within the last six months, (2) no intake of iron supplements, (3) no intake of eggs, (4) no intake of meat, (5) use of an unimproved water source, (6) use of an unimproved toilet facility, and (7) water collection time exceeding 30 min. The cutoff value used in the classification process was determined a priori using the median cut-off point.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the 2018 NDHS, which covers all 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory of Nigeria. Descriptive analyses were undertaken, while bivariate analyses involving chi-square tests were carried out. We then fitted a logistic regression model involving all the variables in order to obtain the independent predictors’ aORs.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted for missingness, particularly for the dietary variables. MICE were the main method compared to CCA. The MAR assumption is plausible because the predictors of missingness included in the model are wealth index, maternal education, region, and urban/rural residence. However, the MAR assumption cannot be validated, so MNAR sensitivity analyses were performed as follows:

MNAR sensitivity analysis was undertaken using egg consumption, which alone among all food items had shown an independent relationship with diarrhea. The two scenarios considered were: (1) ‘mild MNAR’, where missing data on eggs were assumed to have been consumed by the child 10–15% less frequently than observed, and (2) ‘strong MNAR’ where eggs were assumed to be consumed 20–25% less frequently.

The rationale for selecting these ranges of percentages was based on the following three points. First, evidence shows that missing values on dietary intake occur more frequently in households experiencing food insecurity [11, 12] than in those whose eggs consumption is estimated. Second, the range 10–15% depicts a modest departure from the MAR hypothesis, whereas the 20–25% range assesses the relationship in case of a significant departure from MAR. Third, in accordance with the tipping point approach theory [13], the 25% upper boundary estimates deviation from MAR necessary for nullifying the observed protective association.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2 [14]. MICE was carried out using the ‘mice’ library (version 3.16.0) [15]. In line with DHS methodology [16], imputation model was set up using survey weights (v005), strata indicators (v022), and clusters (v021). The number of imputations generated was twenty (m = 20) according to the recommendation on datasets with 30–50% missingness [17].

After conducting imputations, analyses on each of the 20 imputed datasets were performed using weighted logistic regression that takes into account the PSUs, stratification, and sampling weight using the ‘svydesign’ function in the ‘survey’ package (R version 4.2-1) [18]. The results obtained were pooled according to Rubin’s rules [19] using the ‘pool’ function in the ‘mice’ package. Heatmaps, correlation networks, and risk matrices were plotted using ‘ggplot2’ (version 3.4.4), ‘corrplot’ (version 0.92), and ‘igraph’ (version 1.5.1).

Variable selection and sensitivity analyses

The variable ‘Given Internal Parasite Drugs’ was excluded from the final model due to high risk of reverse causality, as treatment is a consequence of illness. Its exclusion prevented confounding, particularly for Vitamin A supplementation estimates. In addition, a formal sensitivity analysis for Missing Not at Random (MNAR) data was conducted on egg consumption, the only dietary variable with a significant independent association with diarrhea. Using a pattern-mixture model, we created ‘Mild’ and ‘Strong’ MNAR scenarios, systematically assuming children with missing data had a 10–15% and 20–25% lower probability of egg consumption, respectively. Egg intake emerged as the only variable to undergo MNAR sensitivity analysis because egg intake was the only dietary variable in the multivariable analysis that had significant protective association with childhood diarrhea (aOR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.77–0.99). It was thus necessary to verify the robustness of this result against any violation of the MAR assumptions. The other dietary factors (i.e., meat, fish, fruits, and vegetable intake) were not significantly associated on their own in the main regression analysis, making sensitivity analysis for them less informative and potentially underpowered. Finally, advanced visualizations including a correlation network, a multi-dimensional risk heatmap, and a nutrition-WASH risk matrix, were used to illustrate the complex interactions between risk factors.

The imputation model

The imputation model included all analysis variables: the primary outcome (diarrhea status), all exposure variables (socio-demographic: age, sex, maternal education, wealth, region, residence; nutritional: vitamin A, iron, egg, meat, fish, fruit, vegetable consumption; WASH: water source, toilet type, water collection time), and auxiliary variables (maternal age, household size, number of under-5 children) to improve imputation precision. Diarrhea status was included in the imputation model to satisfy the ‘passive imputation’ requirement, as excluding the outcome would bias effect estimates toward the null. Convergence was assessed by (1) visualizing trace plots of imputed values across 20 imputation chains, which demonstrated stable means and variances after 10 iterations; and (2) examining variance inflation factors (VIF < 5 for all variables), indicating no convergence failures.

Multicollinearity within the independent variables was checked through the use of Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). The VIF scores for all the variables were less than 3 (range 1.02–2.84), demonstrating no issue of collinearity between the variables. The largest VIF score was 2.84 for wealth index vs. improved toilet facility.

Results

Descriptive and bivariate statistics

The final analytical sample included 33,924 under-5 children with a two-week diarrhea prevalence of 12.0% (n = 4,066) (Supplementary Table s1a, Supplementary Table s1b and Supplementary Table s3). The sample reflected significant socio-economic disparities: 45.4% of mothers never went to school, and 65.5% of children lived in rural areas. Key gaps in WASH and nutrition were observed; for instance, only 15.8% of the children received iron supplementation, and 52.2% used unimproved toilet facilities.

Bivariate analysis showed that diarrhea was highly correlated (p < 0.001) with nearly all variables except child’s sex (Table 1). Children with diarrhea were disproportionately among the most vulnerable: born to mothers who were not educated (57.2% versus 43.5%), of the poorest wealth quintile (33.2% versus 22.3%), and resided in the North-East region (39.6% versus 18.4%). Highest disease burden was among children 12–23 months (30.1% of cases) and 6–11 months (15.3%), while lower diarrhea prevalence was associated with supplementation (Vitamin A, iron) and consumption of foods like eggs and meat.

Multivariable logistic regression and sensitivity analysis

After controlling with multivariable models (Table 2), geographic and socio-economic factors were significant independent determinants. Residing in the North-East was the strongest risk factor (aOR: 2.56; 95% CI: 2.30–2.87), while South-West residence demonstrated a strong protective association (aOR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.54–0.75). Higher wealth and maternal education were also protective, with children from richer households having 41% reduced odds (aOR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.49–0.71), and highly educated mothers’ children with 26% reduced odds (aOR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.61–0.89) relative to their poorest and least educated counterparts respectively.

Robustness of key dietary finding

Due to a substantial missing rate (37.1%) for dietary data, the protective association of egg consumption was investigated assuming MNAR (Supplementary Table s2b). Under all assumptions, the association remained statistically significant, though the effect size dwindled from an aOR of 0.75 (MNAR) to 0.63 (Strong MNAR), making the finding robust across all MNAR scenarios.

Multi-dimensional and interactive risk visualization

Visualization tools captured the complex risk context. A regional bar chart confirmed the high burden in the North-East (Fig. 2a). Risk matrix also depicted synergistic effect; diarrhea prevalence was highest (15.4%) when risks of nutrition and WASH were high versus 9.6% when both were low (Fig. 2b). A correlation network identified education and wealth as hub nodes connected with protective WASH factors (Fig. 3a), while a multi-dimensional heatmap found the highest diarrhea prevalence clustering where low maternal education crossed over with specific age groups, having the highest composite nutrition-WASH risk score (Fig. 3b). A formal interaction term (Nutrition-Risk × WASH-Risk) added to the multivariable logistic regression model demonstrated a statistically significant association (aOR for interaction = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.18–1.52; p < 0.001), indicating a multiplicative synergistic effect beyond additivity. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) was 0.042 (95% CI: 0.021–0.063), confirming positive additivity.

Discussion

The enduring primacy of socio-economic and geographic disparities

The most significant conclusions drawn from this study are related to the underlying role of socio-economic status and geographical location. This establishes the graded inverse correlation between household wealth and diarrhea risk, as evidenced in both the bivariate (Table 1) and multivariable analysis (Table 2), agreeing with an enormous body of research [20]. Wealthier households are able to invest in protective infrastructure, such as better water and sanitation facilities in their homes, and are more likely to be able to afford nutrient-dense foods and medical care. The adjusted model also verifies that this protective association differs from other factors such as education and housing location, attributing economic capital as a principal determinant of health regardless of other variables.

Similarly, the protective association of maternal education, especially higher education, that remained significant after multivariable adjustment (aOR: 0.74), is consistent with evidence that already exists [21]. Educated mothers are likely to observe good hygienic practices, including proper handwashing and safe handling of food, and better command over health-related decisions, such as appropriate care-seeking for a sick child. However, sensitivity analysis (Table 3) showed an important caveat: this substantial protective association vanished under CCA, suggesting that the missing education or other covariate data were non-random but CCA likely imposed a selection bias which watered down this established association. This highlights the advantage of multiple-imputation methods such as MICE in maintaining the validity of estimates for large socio-demographic predictors [9].

Geographic region remains the strongest risk factor as children in the North-East region had 2.6 times odds of diarrhea than the reference region (Table 2). This is evident in Fig. 3a, with the North-East presenting the highest percentage of children with diarrhea. Such extreme regional disparities tend to reflect fundamental underlying inequities in management, investments in infrastructure, and the environment [11]. The North-East can be defined by poorer road access constraining health care accessibility, more water restrictions, or increased population densities which ensures pathogenic transmissions. This may also be explained by the long-term insurgency and conflict affecting the North-East. Interestingly, the protective effect of urban residence in univariate analysis reversed to a significant risk factor (aOR: 1.20) after multivariable adjustment. This suggests that the supposed benefit of urban residence is tempered by other factors, presumably the density of poverty, people, and poor sanitation in the slums of cities, which might generate high-risk conditions that ironically surpass those of some rural populations [22]. With adjusted wealth and education factors, the particular environmental risk of dense urban residence appears as a main driver of diarrhea. However, the lack of more granular urban data is a limitation to exploring further intricate association underlying this observation, a critical area for future spatial and qualitative study.

The change in direction from being a protective variable to being a risk factor after multivariable adjustment for urban-rural location requires attention. This trend is consistent with the “urban slum effect” found by Fink et al. [22] across 37 LMICs where urban dwelling is associated with increased wealth and education (both protective). However, when adjusted for wealth and education, the resulting increased risk for urban residence highlights the dangers inherent in crowded informal urban dwellings. Secondly, poor sanitation infrastructure in spite of dense populations, results in fecal pollution of living spaces. Third, poor water quality due to inconsistent access to pipe-borne water in peri-urban areas as well as insufficient waste disposal services provided grounds for disease vectors. These evidences imply that increased risks of diarrhea may result from urbanization in the absence of appropriate investments in WASH infrastructure. Future research on this correlation using combined DHS datasets with slum mapping is recommended.

Interplay of nutrition, WASH, and health-seeking behavior

The interactions between the dimensions of nutrition, WASH, and diarrhea explained expected synergisms and counterintuitive results that need careful interpretation. The risk matrix (Fig. 3b) offers strong evidence for a synergistic WASH–nutrition interaction. The step-wise increment of diarrhea prevalence from the low-risk/low-risk cell (9.6%) to high-risk/high-risk cell (15.4%) indicates the way deficiencies in one facet reinforce the other’s weaknesses. An immunocompromised child and therefore nutritionally at risk is less able to defend itself against pathogens which it becomes exposed to in an unclean setting [6] On the other hand, even with healthy diet, in a high fecal exposure setting, infection might not be avoided. This underscores the weaknesses of isolated interventions and highlights combined programming addressing nutrition and WASH concomitantly [8].

The protective association between egg consumption and diarrhea (aOR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.77–0.99) can be considered biologically plausible because eggs are a nutritionally dense food rich in bioavailable protein essential for repairing the intestinal mucosa; zinc (1.3 mg/egg) necessary for normal immune cell activity and capable of reducing diarrhea duration; vitamin A (retinol; 270 IU/egg) needed to maintain the integrity of intestinal epithelium; and choline (147 mg/egg) important for gut permeability. The stability of these associations in all MNAR analyses (aOR range: 0.75–0.88) further supports its plausibility. It is important to note however, that a causal statement or claims cannot be made yet. Reverse causation can occur where healthy children may have an increased appetite for eggs or where households that eat more eggs may be different from those who do not in some ways not measured (such as having their own food supply or eating a healthier diet). This is thus termed a ‘protective association’ rather than a ‘protective effect,’ and further investigation is needed using random control trials or natural experiments.

However, individual dietary and supplementation components, as probed, provided more nuanced outcomes. Egg consumption was associated with a very large protective tendency (aOR: 0.88), which is biologically plausible because eggs are rich in high-quality protein, zinc, and vitamin A, all of which play key functions in the immune system. Furthermore, we chose on purpose to exclude the variable ‘Received Internal Parasite Drugs’ from our final model. This variable, which indicated whether a child had received treatment for deworming, was considered to likely cause reverse causality and confounding by indication. Treatment is a consequence of a diagnosis of parasitic disease, which is itself a direct cause of diarrhea. Including this in the model would thereby flag up a high-risk subgroup (ill children who received care) rather than a true risk factor and could distort other variable estimates. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that its exclusion did not materially alter the effect estimates for our main socio-demographic, WASH, and nutritional predictors.

The sensitivity analysis (Table 3 and Supplementary Table s1a) complicates the story around some nutritional interventions. The false “risk” correlations between Vitamin A supplementation and fruit consumption only in the CCA model are a classic example of bias that may be generated by CCA methods. Perhaps systematically different missing data on those variables were missing, presumably from worse, more rural families with fewer health care and market ties. Leaving them out (as in CCA) produced a biased subsample where the left-behind children on supplements or mixed foods were underrepresented, leading to erroneous conclusions. The MICE model imputing these missing data based on other observed variables gives a less biased estimate with no significant effect, such as with mixed evidence of Vitamin A effectiveness on diarrhea [7]. This emphatically illustrates how methodological decisions can initiate or eliminate perceived risk factors. Thus, the potentially biased associations in the CCA model likely arise from systematic differences between complete cases and excluded observations.

Life-course susceptibility and the correlation web

Age analysis demonstrated a distinct life-course risk pattern, with the highest risk of diarrhea reported among the “12–23” and “6–11” age groups (aOR: 2.39, 2.42, respectively), as indicated by Table 2. This is the developmental period when children are becoming more mobile and inquisitive, making them more exposed to the environment. Simultaneously, this period makes them nutritionally vulnerable due to weaning from exclusive breastfeeding to introduction of complementary foods. The resulting reduction in risk with increasingly older age groups is caused by the acquired natural immunity and improved personal hygiene.

The correlation network (Fig. 3a) provides a systems-level, end-to-end representation of how these various factors are interrelated. It visually displays the regression result, indicating how important socio-economic determinants such as `Wealth Index` and `Mothers Educational Level` are located at the center of a hub, positively linked to protective determinants such as `Improved` toilet facilities and water, and negatively correlated with the outcome `Diarrhea Status`. The network indicates that diarrhea is not caused by individual determinants but appears as the effect of a network of determinants with correlations. This network is an effective instrument in speculating about indirect routes while comprehending the intricate child well-being ecology.

The multi-dimensional landscape of vulnerability

The multi-dimensional risk heatmap (Fig. 3b) transcends correlation to provide a nuanced portrait of intersectional vulnerability. It shows that the risk of diarrhea is not evenly distributed across categories. For instance, despite being a sweeping risk, the burden is further localized within certain age strata within the educational category. The heatmap indicates the areas of highest risk clusters at the intersection of lowest education (“Not Educated”) and certain ages, with a composite risk score overlaid (point size) to highlight that such children bear the highest aggregate WASH and nutrition burdens. This detailed perspective is invaluable to public health practice as it enables focused use of interventions among specific high-risk sub-populations, shifting from universal to equity-oriented and differentiated programming.

Policy and programmatic implications for Nigeria

This study has some policy and program design implications in the short run. First, sheer regional dominance necessitates sub-national, targeted planning. The North-East and other such high-burden states must be the priority of health ministry initiatives and stakeholder interventions with more targeted, context-specific interventions that meet the specific infrastructural and environmental needs of such states.

Second, WASH-nutrition synergistic risk necessitates programmatic silo-breaking. Combined “Nutrition-Sensitive WASH” interventions such as associating enhanced latrine promotion and handwashing with nutrition education and support to household food production, can achieve higher reductions in diarrhea compared to vertical, single-sector programs taken in isolation [8].

Third, the persistent socio-economic gradients also call for attention to social protection and poverty reduction. Even as direct health interventions are clearly paramount, sustained diarrhea disease elimination depends on more general policies that enhance family wealth, girls’ education opportunities, and limit spatial inequalities. Conditional cash transfers, for example, have been found to advance child health outcomes by easing budgetary pressures [23].

Lastly, methodologically speaking, our findings stand as a cautionary admonition for producers of research as well as users of evidence. The dramatic contrast between findings for the MICE and CCA models highlights the urgent need for employing the right methods for dealing with missing data. Researches that make use of CCA alone may end up drawing biased and wrong conclusions, potentially misdirecting public health policy.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations: (a) The cross-sectional study design does not allow causal inference. (b) There is potential for recall bias in terms of reporting diarrhea (recall period of two weeks) and food consumption (24-hour recall). The DHS uses standardized recall periods to mitigate this bias. Underreporting of diarrhea cases due to social desirability bias cannot be ruled out. (c) Reverse causality can occur, especially in case of diet variables. Children who suffer from diarrhea may have poor appetite or be provided with other foods (such as no egg/meat), which may result in biases against or toward the null. (d) Unmeasured confounders may also affect the observed associations. Some of these unmeasured variables are: quality of local health services, rotavirus vaccination status (unavailable in 2018 NDHS survey), breast-feeding apart from introduction of complementary foods, crowding at the household level, seasonal effects on data collection, and food preparation culture. (e) Despite the use of multiple imputations, there is still high missingness for dietary variables (37.1%). Although sensitivity analyses for MNAR showed the robustness of egg consumption, missingness due to unobserved variables cannot be removed using imputation. (f) The MAR assumption in the main MICE analysis is untestable. Although the sensitivity analyses show the robustness of egg consumption, it would take the collection of prospective data to confirm this. (g) Exploration-based nature of visualization techniques (heatmaps, correlation network plots, risk matrix analysis) makes such visuals exploratory in purpose rather than confirmatory. Hypothesis testing was done in regression models only. (h) Whether the results can be generalized beyond Nigeria or beyond recent years (after 2018) is not clear. In the period after 2018, Nigeria has seen continued conflict in North-East, COVID-19 pandemic, and economic instability that might have affected the diarrhea risk profile. Future studies need to use prospective cohort designs with multiple measurements of diet and WASH, include objective verification of diarrhea cases (for instance, through molecular diagnostics), and account for rotavirus vaccination status.

Conclusion

This study reaffirms that the burden of diarrhea disease among children is an expression of current socio-economic, geographic, and environmental disparities. Risk is not arbitrarily allocated but concentrates among the poorest, lowest educated, and inhabitants of particular high-burden areas. The interplay among nutritional status and WASH conditions is a compounded risk for the most vulnerable children. Our results most strongly support a multi-sectoral policy intervention that combines high-intensity WASH and nutrition interventions with other more general poverty-reduction and girls’ education strategies. Methodologic lessons about management of missing data are also a useful reminder to the epidemiologic community to use the best available analytic tools in ensuring evidence based public health action, with evidence that is as sound and consistent as possible.
