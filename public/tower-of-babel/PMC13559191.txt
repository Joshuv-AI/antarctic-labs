Levels of suggestibility are associated with distinct patterns of intrinsic functional neural connectivity
Authors: Menglu Chen, P J Alvarez Duran, Tatia M C Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuggestibility refers to an individual’s tendency to accept, believe, and act on information or ideas presented by others with limited critical evaluation or independent judgment. It reflects the extent to which a person’s behaviors, choices, thoughts, and memories can be shaped by external prompts, leading questions, or social influences. Despite its relevance to human behaviors, its neuropsychological basis remains poorly understood. In this study, we examined the behavioral, structural, and functional neural correlates of suggestibility in the domains of working memory and attentional control.MethodsWe employed the validated Multidimensional Iowa Suggestibility Scale (MISS) to measure the suggestibility of 42 healthy female participants. They were classified into the high suggestibility (HS) (n = 21) and low suggestibility (LS) (n = 21) groups based on their MISS scores. All participants underwent fMRI while performing working memory (N-back) and attentional control (Flanker) tasks, in addition to structural and resting-state scans.ResultsBehaviorally, the HS group exhibited reduced reaction time interference in the Flanker task, suggesting enhanced attentional control. Voxel-based morphometry revealed greater gray matter volume in the right superior frontal gyrus and left postcentral gyrus among HS participants. Task-based activation analyses showed increased activation in frontal, parietal, and temporal regions in the HS group. Resting-state regions of interest (ROI)-to-ROI connectivity analysis revealed a significant increase in connectivity between the salience network (right supramarginal gyrus) and default mode network (right lateral parietal cortex) in the HS group.DiscussionOur findings indicate that high and low levels of suggestibility are associated with distinct patterns of intrinsic functional connectivity. Also, suggestibility modulates attentional control, brain structure, and intrinsic functional connectivity. These findings provide important translational insights for applying suggestibility in therapeutic contexts. Future research should continue to elucidate the mechanisms underlying suggestibility and explore how these traits may be leveraged to enhance interventions across diverse populations.

Introduction

Suggestibility refers to an individual’s capacity to experience changes in perception, memory, or behavior in response to suggestion (Oakley and Halligan, 2013; Elkins et al., 2015). In everyday life, this trait manifests in various contexts: highly suggestible individuals may show enhanced responsiveness to placebo treatments, greater susceptibility to marketing and advertising messages, and increased tendency toward social conformity in group settings (Kotov et al., 2004). Suggestibility is increasingly recognized as a trait-like cognitive style, with individual differences persisting across adulthood (Piccione et al., 1989; Alexander et al., 2021). Clinically, individual differences in suggestibility predict treatment outcomes in hypnotherapy for chronic pain, anxiety, and procedural distress, as well as placebo responsiveness across medical contexts (Jensen and Patterson, 2014; Elkins et al., 2015). Understanding the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying suggestibility may therefore inform personalized treatment selection and guide the development of interventions tailored to individual suggestibility profiles (Raz, 2011). Overall, examining the cognitive and neural correlates of suggestibility in the general population is critical for advancing theoretical models of attention and executive control, as well as for informing clinical applications of hypnosis and suggestion-based therapies.

The neural basis of suggestibility likely involves large-scale brain networks, particularly the executive control network (ECN), the salience network (SN), and the default mode network (DMN) (Hoeft et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2017). These networks are thought to play distinct but interacting roles in supporting the cognitive flexibility, attentional control, and self-referential processing required for changes associated with suggestibility (Oakley and Halligan, 2013; Landry et al., 2017). For instance, increased ECN–SN coupling and reduced ECN–DMN connectivity have been reported in highly suggestible individuals, even outside formal induction contexts (Hoeft et al., 2012). Other functional MRI studies have shown that suggestibility is linked to enhanced functional integration between the SN and regions involved in executive function, supporting the notion that salience detection and cognitive control are critical for hypnotic responding (Jiang et al., 2017). Additionally, disruptions in DMN activity have been associated with reduced self-monitoring and increased absorption, both of which are characteristic of high suggestibility and hypnotic responsiveness (Demertzi et al., 2011; Deeley et al., 2013). Structural differences in frontal and temporal regions have also been reported (Huber et al., 2014), though findings remain somewhat inconsistent across studies. Recent meta-analyses suggest that while some individuals exhibit robust neuroanatomical markers of suggestibility, the variability across studies may reflect differences in experimental paradigms, measures of suggestibility, and sample characteristics (Landry et al., 2017). Overall, these findings highlight the importance of large-scale network dynamics in mediating individual differences in suggestibility.

From a behavioral perspective, highly suggestible individuals demonstrate altered cognitive processing in tasks related to attention and executive function. Notably, they may show enhanced interference control in paradigms such as the Flanker task, though results for other tasks, like the Stroop, have been less consistent (Cojan et al., 2015). These findings suggest that suggestibility may influence attentional control mechanisms in a task-specific way, potentially reflecting differences in resolution of perceptual vs. semantic conflict.

While many previous studies have relied on induction-based suggestibility measures, the present study adopted the Multidimensional Iowa Suggestibility Scale (MISS) (Kotov et al., 2004), a validated self-report measurement assessing general susceptibility to suggestion across cognitive and social domains. By leveraging this trait-based approach, we aimed to characterize the behavioral, structural, and functional neural correlates of suggestibility. We focused on cognitive domains previously linked to suggestibility, specifically attention and working memory, and we examined task performance, structural brain differences, and connectivity patterns at resting state and during task engagement. A classical N-back task paradigm was applied to measure working memory, and a Flanker task was used as an index of attentional control. Based on prior literature, we formulated the following directional predictions. First, at the behavioral level, we hypothesized that highly suggestible individuals would demonstrate reduced interference effects in the Flanker task [i.e. smaller reaction time (RT) difference between incongruent and congruent trials], reflecting enhanced attentional control during perceptual conflict resolution (Cojan et al., 2015). For working memory, given mixed prior findings, we did not make specific directional predictions but remained open to potential group differences in N-back performance. Second, at the neural level, we predicted that HS would be associated with increased resting-state functional connectivity between the SN and DMN, as well as enhanced ECN–SN coupling, based on previous evidence (Hoeft et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2017). Third, regarding brain structure, we expected group differences in gray matter volume within frontal regions implicated in executive control, though we remained agnostic about specific directionality given the variability reported across studies (Landry et al., 2017). Through this multimodal investigation, we aimed to clarify the cognitive and neural characteristics of suggestibility, thereby contributing to a broader understanding of individual differences in attention, control, and susceptibility to social and internal cues.

Materials and methods

Participants

Forty-two female participants aged between 18 and 40 years (mean age = 24.16, SD = 4.6) participated in this study. These participants were recruited from the Hong Kong University campus and the local community via email, advertisements, and social media. Inclusion criteria required participants to be physically and psychologically healthy, right-handed as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, native Chinese speakers fluent in English, and had normal or corrected vision. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, presence of metal implants or MRI contraindications, recent use of psychotropic medication, or relevant physical illnesses that could interfere with study results. Screening was conducted through online self-report questionnaires hosted on Qualtrics. From eligible participants, those scored at the top and bottom 10% on the MISS were classified into the high suggestibility (HS) (n = 21) and low suggestibility (LS) (n = 21) groups, respectively. All participants provided informed consent for participation. The study received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee for Non-Clinical Faculties at the University of Hong Kong (HREC Reference Number: EA123456).

Suggestibility assessment

Participants completed a battery of self-report questionnaires online via Qualtrics. The primary measure for participant categorization was the MISS, a validated 95-item self-report tool assessing trait suggestibility across domains such as consumer suggestibility, persuadability, sensation contagion, physiological reactivity, peer conformity, psychosomatic control, and stubborn opinionatedness. The total score from the MISS provided an overall measure of each participant’s susceptibility to suggestion, which was used to define the HS and LS groups for subsequent analyses. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale, yielding both subscale and total scores. The total MISS score served as the basis for classifying participants into HS and LS groups for subsequent analyses and the distribution of MISS score for the two groups is shown in Table 1.

Study procedure

This study employed a cross-sectional observational design to investigate the associations between suggestibility, cognitive functions, and brain structure and function in healthy female adults. Neuroimaging data were acquired, including three modalities: diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), and task-based fMRI. The functional MRI tasks included the Flanker and N-back paradigms, which were chosen for their sensitivity to suggestibility-related differences in executive control and attentional processes (Cojan et al., 2015). All participants attended a single session at the MRI center. Upon arrival, they received a detailed explanation of the procedures, provided written informed consent, and completed the experimental tasks while undergoing brain scanning. All participants demonstrated adequate task comprehension.

Experimental paradigms

Letter N-back task procedure

Participants completed an 8-min block-design letter-based N-back task during fMRI scanning to assess working memory load. The task consisted of 12 blocks, alternating pseudorandomly among three load conditions (0-back, 1-back, and 2-back), interleaved with jittered fixation periods of 8–12 s (Fig. 1). Each block began with a 2-s instruction screen indicating the current condition (e.g. “0-back”). Within each 27-s block, participants viewed a rapid sequence of 15 centrally presented capital letters, each displayed for 400 ms, followed by a 1400-ms interstimulus interval (ISI). Letters were randomly selected from a fixed set of visually symmetrical uppercase letters (A, H, I, M, O, T, V, W, X, and Y), all presented in white on a black background to minimize visual confounds and lateralization effects (Gazzaley and Nobre, 2012). Participants responded via button press using their right index finger when a target was detected. Task instructions varied by condition: 0-back: Press if the current letter is “X”. 1-back: Press if the current letter matches the one immediately before. 2-back: Press if the current letter matches the one presented two trials earlier. Block order, ISIs, and letter sequences were pseudorandomized (Friston et al., 1994) to minimize order effects and anticipatory responses (Fig. 1).

Flanker task procedure

In a separate 8-min fMRI run, participants performed an Eriksen flanker task to assess attentional control and response inhibition. Each trial began with a fixation cross displayed for a jittered interval between 1450 and 1550 ms, followed by a stimulus consisting of five horizontally aligned arrows (e.g. <<><<) presented for 2500 ms. Participants indicated the direction of the central (target) arrow by pressing the upper yellow button (leftward) or the right red button (rightward) on the response box. Stimuli were divided between 75% congruent trials (flanking arrows in the same direction) and 25% incongruent trials (flankers in the opposite direction), pseudorandomly ordered. To avoid visual or hemispheric bias, stimuli were rendered using symmetrical arrow characters and presented on a black background. ISIs, trial types, and arrow orientations were pseudorandomized to maintain task unpredictability and minimize habituation (Friston et al., 1994) (Fig. 1C).

Prior to fMRI scanning, all participants completed practice trials for both tasks to ensure comprehension and minimize performance variability due to task unfamiliarity. For the Flanker task, participants completed 20 practice trials (10 congruent and 10 incongruent) outside the scanner. For the N-back task, participants practiced each condition (0-back, 1-back, and 2-back) with 10 trials per block. Participants received trial-by-trial feedback during practice, and the experimenter verified that participants understood task instructions before proceeding. Only after successful completion of practice did participants enter the scanner for the main experimental session. This procedure is consistent with standard practices in fMRI studies using working memory and attentional control paradigms (Owen et al., 2005).

Behavioral data analysis

Behavioral performance on the N-back and Flanker tasks was assessed using accuracy (percentage correct) and mean RT (ms) for each condition. Cognitive load effects were quantified as load cost: incongruent minus congruent (Flanker) and 2-back minus 0-back (N-back). Participants with task accuracy below 50% were excluded from that task’s analysis, resulting in the exclusion of two participants from the Flanker task. Group differences in load cost (accuracy and RT) between HS and LS groups were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests. All analyses were performed in Python.

Imaging data acquisition

All images were acquired on a 3.0 T GE Signa Premier XT MRI scanner. The scanning protocol lasted ~60 min and included the following sequences: high-resolution T1-weighted structural imaging, resting-state functional MRI (fMRI), N-back task fMRI, and Flanker task fMRI. T1-weighted structural MRI: High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using a 3D BRAVO sequence with the following parameters: slice thickness = 1.0 mm, matrix = 240 × 240, field of view (FOV) = 24 cm, flip angle = 8°, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, and bandwidth = 31.25 Hz.

Resting-state functional images were acquired using a gradient echo EPI (Echo-Planar Imaging) sequence: TR (Repetition Time) = 1200 ms, TE (Echo Time) = 33 ms, flip angle = 90°, 60 axial slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, matrix = 80 × 80, FOV = 24 cm, multiband (HyperBand) acceleration factor = 4, and 425 volumes per run (8 min 30 s). During scanning, participants fixated on a centrally presented cross.

Task-based functional scans for both N-back and Flanker paradigms used the same gradient echo EPI parameters as resting-state fMRI: TR = 1200 ms, TE = 33 ms, flip angle = 90°, 60 slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, matrix = 80 × 80, FOV = 24 cm, and multiband acceleration = 4. Task stimuli were projected onto a screen and viewed through a mirror system attached to the head coil; responses were recorded using a VPixx response box.

The chosen MRI acquisition parameters were optimized to achieve high spatial and temporal resolution, whole-brain coverage, and robust signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The use of isometric voxels and multiband acceleration enabled rapid data acquisition without compromising spatial fidelity, thereby improving sensitivity to neural dynamics and precise localization of brain activity (Vu et al., 2017; Poser and Setsompop, 2018).

Structural MRI data analysis

Structural MRI images were analyzed using voxel-based morphometry (VBM) with SPM12. Preprocessing included segmentation of T1-weighted, normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, and spatial smoothing with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Quality control involved a combination of automated (interquartile range, IQR, of voxel intensities) and visual inspection, excluding statistical outliers and scans with artifacts. Whole-brain group comparisons of gray matter volume between HS and LS participants were performed using two-sample t-tests, with total intracranial volume as a covariate. Statistical significance was set at family-wise error (FWE) correction, P(FWE) < 0.05 with cluster size of k > 5. The final sample for VBM consisted of 20 HS and 18 low LS participants.

Data preprocessing for resting-state and task-based fMRI

Functional and anatomical MRI images were preprocessed using the CONN toolbox (release 22.a) and SPM12, following standard procedures. For each participant, functional images were first realigned with susceptibility distortion correction using fieldmaps and SPM’s realign & unwarp procedure. Slice timing correction was then applied. Outlier scans were identified using the Artefact Detection Tools (ART), defined as volumes with framewise displacement >0.5 mm or global BOLD signal change exceeding 3 SD. Both anatomical and functional images were subsequently segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid, and normalized to the MNI space via SPM’s unified segmentation and normalization algorithm, with resampling to 2-mm isotropic voxels using the IXI-549 tissue probability map. Finally, functional images were spatially smoothed using an 8-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

Following preprocessing, denoising was performed by regressing out confounding signals, including white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals (CompCor), head motion parameters and their first-order derivatives, outlier volumes, session effects, and linear trends within each run. A temporal bandpass filter (0.008–0.09 Hz) was then applied to the BOLD time series. Participants exhibiting excessive head motion (translation >3 mm, rotation >3°, or mean framewise displacement >0.3 mm) (Jenkinson et al., 2002) were excluded from further analyses to ensure data quality. After applying these criteria, the final sample for both resting-state and N-back task fMRI included 19 HS and 15 LS participants, and 18 HS and 14 LS participants for the Flanker task.

Task activation analysis

First-level general linear models (GLMs) were estimated in SPM12 (Friston et al., 1994) for both N-back and Flanker tasks, modeling task conditions and including motion parameters as nuisance regressors. Main contrasts of interest were 2-back > 0-back (N-back) and incongruent > congruent (Flanker). Group-level two-sample t-tests compared brain activation between HS and LS groups, with FWE correction (P < 0.05).

Resting-state functional connectivity analysis

ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity (RRC) analyses were performed to assess intrinsic connectivity among 15 regions of interest (ROIs) spanning the DMN, SN, and ECN, defined from a group-level HPC-ICA parcellation. Fisher z-transformed correlation coefficients between ROI pairs were computed and compared between groups using GLMs; False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied at P < 0.05.

Task-based functional connectivity analysis

Generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) analyses (McLaren et al., 2012) were conducted to estimate task-based functional connectivity between the same 15 ROIs for both the N-back (2-back vs. 0-back) and Flanker (incongruent vs. congruent) tasks. For each ROI pair, a gPPI model included the seed region’s BOLD timeseries as a physiological regressor, a boxcar regressor for task conditions as psychological regressors, and their interaction (psychophysiological regressor) with connectivity estimates quantified using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. At the group level, group differences between HS and LS participants were assessed using GLMs, with multiple comparisons correction (voxel-wise P < 0.001, cluster-level FDR P < 0.05).

Results

Behavioral performance between HS and LS

We compared N-back and Flanker task performance between HS and LS groups using independent-samples t-tests. For the N-back task, there were no significant group differences of the 2-back minus 0-back contrast (2–0 back) for either accuracy (t(32) = –0.50, P = 0.61, Cohen’s d = –0.161) or RT (t(32) = 0.70, P= 0.489, Cohen’s d = 0.224). These results show that suggestibility did not affect working memory performance.

For the Flanker task, there was no significant difference in accuracy (incongruent minus congruent) between HS and LS groups (t(30) = 1.49, P = 0.145, Cohen’s d = 0.484). However, a significant group difference emerged for RT, with LS participants exhibiting a larger RT difference between incongruent and congruent trials compared to HS participants (t(30) = 2.06, P = 0.046, Cohen’s d = 0.671). This finding suggests that individuals with higher suggestibility may experience reduced interference effects in RT during conflict processing (Fig. 2).

Suggestibility group differences in structural MRI (voxel-based morphometry)

VBM was conducted to compare gray matter volume between HS and LS participants. FWE correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, with significance set at P < 0.05 with a cluster size of k > 5 voxels. Two significant clusters were identified: HS participants showed greater gray matter volume in the right superior frontal gyrus (Brodmann Area (BA) 10; MNI coordinates [21, 48, 41], t = 6.36, P(FWE) = 0.026, k = 6) and in the left postcentral gyrus (BA 3; [–36, –27, 71]; t = 6.28, P(FWE) = 0.032, k = 7) (Fig. 3).

Suggestibility group differences in working memory and attention task activation

To examine whether suggestibility influenced task-related brain activation, two-sample t-tests were conducted between HS and LS participants for both the N-back and Flanker tasks. In the N-back task, HS participants exhibited greater activation than LS participants in the left superior frontal gyrus within the supplementary motor area (BA 6; MNI: [–18, –6, 64]; t = 3.78, k = 12), right orbitofrontal cortex (BA 11; [28, 28, –6]; t = 3.74, k = 19), bilateral superior parietal lobules (right BA 7; [28, –50, 50]; t = 3.66, k = 19; left BA 7; [–30, –52, 54]; t = 3.64, k = 14), right angular gyrus (BA 39; [50, –36, 28]; t = 3.67, k = 14), and the left inferior temporal gyrus (BA 20; [–48, –18, –8]; t = 3.68, k = 1). However, no significant clusters were found for the reverse contrast (LS > HS), and no effects were observed after FWE correction (P(FWE) > 0.885).

For the Flanker task, HS participants showed greater activation than LS participants in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) (BA 24; [0, 8, 30]; t = 3.95, k = 20), left insula (BA 13; [–38, –4, –2]; t = 3.86, k = 2), and left cingulate gyrus (BA 24; [–10, 14, 28]; t = 3.67, k = 7). On the other hand, LS participants showed greater activation in the right superior parietal lobule (BA 7; [36, –56, 54]; t = 3.86, k = 62). As with the N-back task, these clusters are not significant after correction for multiple comparisons (P(FWE) > 0.53), though the spatial distribution of uncorrected results suggests modest suggestibility-related modulation in regions linked to conflict monitoring and attention.

Suggestibility group differences in resting-state functional connectivity

To investigate suggestibility group differences in intrinsic functional connectivity, we conducted a resting-state ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis, with a connection-level threshold of P < 0.05 (uncorrected), and a cluster-level threshold of FWE corrected P(FWE) < 0.05. The right supramarginal gyrus in SN showed significantly higher intrinsic functional connectivity with the right lateral parietal (LP) (MNI: [47, –67, 29]) in the DMN among HS participants compared to LS participants (t(35) = 3.90, P = 0.0004, P(FDR) = 0.044). This significantly higher connection indicated a higher level of functional coupling between the SN and DMN during resting in individuals with HS.

Several additional functional connectivity involving the right supramarginal gyrus in SN showed nominal group differences, including increased connectivity with the posterior cingulate cortex in DMN (PCC, t(35) = 3.17, P = 0.0032) and the right lateral prefrontal cortex in the ECN (LPFC_R, t(35) = 2.81, P = 0.0081), as well as decreased connectivity with the left anterior insula in the SN (Insula_L, t(35) = –2.22, P = 0.0328). However, these connections were not significant after correction and should be considered exploratory. Overall, these findings suggest that HS is associated explicitly with increased intrinsic functional connectivity between the SN and DMN during resting-state, reflecting altered large-scale network organization in individuals with higher levels of suggestibility (Fig. 4).

Suggestibility group differences in task functional connectivity

No statistically significant group differences in task-based connectivity were found in the gPPI analyses for either the N-back or Flanker tasks.

Discussion

The key finding of this study was that HS is associated with enhanced resting-state functional connectivity between the SN and DMN. This intrinsic network difference converged with structural findings of increased frontal and sensorimotor gray matter and behavioral findings of reduced Flanker RT interference, together suggesting that suggestibility reflects stable neurocognitive architecture supporting enhanced cognitive flexibility. Notably, the absence of group differences in task-evoked activation or task-based connectivity indicates that these advantages stem from intrinsic network organization rather than requiring additional task-related neural recruitment. These multimodal findings provide a comprehensive account of suggestibility as a trait rooted in efficient intrinsic network coordination that manifests as superior attentional control. Theoretically, these results advance predictive processing models by suggesting that enhanced SN–DMN connectivity may facilitate more flexible updating of perceptual priors, enabling suggestions to override baseline expectations more readily (Friston, 2010). This architecture also aligns with network theories of absorption, which propose that suggestibility arises from flexible integration across networks supporting internally versus externally directed attention (Lifshitz et al., 2013).

Behaviorally, no significant group differences were observed in the N-back task, suggesting that working memory load effects were not modulated by suggestibility under the current design. In contrast, participants in the HS group demonstrated significantly reduced RT interference on the Flanker task compared to those in the LS group. This group-level difference suggests that individuals with a higher level of suggestibility may possess enhanced perceptual conflict resolution or attentional control abilities relative to those with LS. This pattern is consistent with previous research indicating altered interference processing in more suggestible groups (Cojan et al., 2015). Together, these results support the notion that suggestibility may influence attentional mechanisms, particularly in tasks involving perceptual, rather than semantic, conflict (Raz et al., 2005).

Structurally, VBM analyses revealed significantly greater gray matter volume in HS participants in the right superior frontal gyrus (BA 10) and the left postcentral gyrus (BA 3). The superior frontal gyrus is implicated in high-level executive processes and forms part of the DMN, which is associated with internally directed thought and self-referential processing (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2010; Spreng et al., 2014; Smallwood et al., 2021). Structural enhancement in this region among HS individuals may reflect altered self-monitoring or increased introspective capacity, processes previously linked to suggestibility (Oakley and Halligan, 2013). The postcentral gyrus, primarily involved in somatosensory processing, may relate to the embodied or sensorimotor dimensions of suggestibility, though this association needs further investigation (Derbyshire et al., 2009).

Resting-state ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analyses identified a significant effect in the right supramarginal gyrus of the SN. Specifically, this region exhibited increased functional connectivity with the right lateral parietal in the DMN in HS participants. These findings indicate enhanced network integration between the SN and DMN in suggestible individuals during rest. Such integration may facilitate more flexible attentional reallocation and more effective gating of internal and external information, processes that are crucial for adaptive cognition (Menon, 2011; Uddin et al., 2019). Although exploratory analyses revealed connections involving the PCC from DMN, the right lateral prefrontal cortex from ECN, and the left anterior insula from SN, these connections were not significant after corrections. Nevertheless, these trends may reflect broader alterations in intrinsic network dynamics associated with suggestibility, warranting further investigation in larger samples (Sridharan et al., 2008; Dixon et al., 2017).

In contrast, task-based connectivity analyses applying gPPI did not reveal any significant effects during either the N-back or Flanker tasks. This negative finding may be attributable to limited sample size, or it may suggest that suggestibility primarily modulates intrinsic (resting-state) rather than task-modulated functional connectivity. Previous studies have shown that the effects of suggestibility and related traits are often more pronounced in resting-state paradigms, whereas task-based functional connectivity may require stronger contextual manipulations to elicit detectable group differences (Oakley and Halligan, 2013; Landry et al., 2017). Future work employing more robust contextual manipulation, such as priming or suggestion cues, could help clarify whether suggestibility impacts dynamic connectivity under specific cognitive demands (Deeley et al., 2013; Jiang et al., 2017).

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, while the MISS provides a comprehensive measure of trait suggestibility, it differs conceptually from traditional induction-based scales, which may contribute to variability in findings across studies. Second, the exclusive use of a female-only sample increases internal validity by reducing gender-related variability but limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Moreover, as an exploratory study findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Future work should employ larger, more diverse samples to improve statistical power and generalizability. Third, while related psychological constructs such as absorption and dissociation were assessed, they were not included in the current analyses; incorporating these factors in future research may provide a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms underlying suggestibility.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that higher trait suggestibility is linked to superior attentional control (reduced Flanker interference), increased frontal and sensorimotor gray matter, and stronger SN–DMN resting-state connectivity. These findings reinforce the importance of interactions between the SN–DMN and support the perspective that suggestibility may be rooted in stable cognitive and neural traits. This has important implications for both basic research and clinical practice, particularly in areas involving suggestion, attentional control, and self-regulation. Future research should continue to elucidate the mechanisms underlying suggestibility and explore how these traits may be leveraged to enhance interventions across diverse populations.
