1.
Introduction

1.1.
Historical context and motivation

The interaction between automata theory and Diophantine approximation has produced a number of striking results in transcendence theory. A classical way to measure the combinatorial complexity of an infinite word
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
over a finite alphabet is via its subword complexity function
p(n)
, which counts the number of distinct blocks of length
n
occurring in
\mathbf{u}
. This notion has proved to be a powerful bridge between combinatorics on words, theoretical computer science and Diophantine approximation. For instance, the
b
-ary expansion of a normal real number satisfies
p(n)=b^{n}
for all
n
, reflecting maximal complexity. Ferenczi and Mauduit
[
8
]
established the transcendence of real numbers whose
b
-ary expansion has minimal complexity, namely
p(n)=n+1
for all
n\geq 1
. Such sequences are precisely the
Sturmian sequences
introduced by Morse and Hedlund
[
16
,
17
]
. Their work relies on a refinement of a theorem of Ridout
[
18
]
and has subsequently inspired a number of results exhibiting transcendental numbers with low combinatorial complexity; see, for instance,
[
4
,
5
,
19
,
2
]
.

A real number is said to be computable in time
T(n)
if its first
n
digits in some fixed base can be produced by a Turing machine in at most
T(n)
steps. Of particular interest are numbers computable in linear time, also referred to as real-time computable numbers. Rational numbers clearly fall into this class, and Hartmanis and Stearns
[
9
]
raised the natural question of whether irrational algebraic numbers can also exhibit such low computational complexity. Loxton and van der Poorten
[
11
,
12
]
proved that the base-
b
expansion of an irrational algebraic number cannot be generated by a finite automaton. Although an initial proof based on Mahler’s method
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
was proposed, it was later found to contain a substantial gap; see Becker
[
6
]
and the discussion in
[
20
]
. These developments culminated in the work of Adamczewski and Bugeaud
[
1
]
, who introduced powerful Diophantine methods, based on the Schmidt Subspace Theorem, to study the arithmetic nature of real numbers defined by low-complexity expansions.

Sequences generated by finite automata exhibit combinatorial properties which in turn lead to rigid arithmetic constraints on associated generating functions and infinite series. A fundamental illustration of this phenomenon is provided by Christol’s theorem, which asserts that a power series
A(X)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}X^{n}
with coefficients in a finite field of characteristic
p
is algebraic over the field of rational functions if and only if the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
p
-automatic. In a different direction, Adamczewski and Bugeaud proved that if
b\geq 2
is an integer and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a bounded automatic sequence of integers, then the real number

\alpha=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{b^{n}}

is either rational or transcendental; moreover,
\alpha
is rational if and only if
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is eventually periodic.

1.2.
Main results

The present work may be viewed as a natural continuation of this line of inquiry, in which we establish a multidimensional analogue of the above result. To describe our setting, let
r\geq 2
be an integer and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\geq 2
be multiplicatively independent integers, that is, the only solution
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
to

a_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots a_{r}^{m_{r}}=1

is
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})=(0,\dots,0)
; equivalently,
\log a_{1},\dots,\log a_{r}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. In this higher-dimensional setting, we show that analogous rigidity phenomena persist for real numbers defined by multidimensional automatic data.

For each
1\leq i\leq r
, let
(p_{n}(i))_{n\geq 0}
be a bounded sequence with values in
\mathbb{Z}
, and assume that
(p_{n}(i))
is
k_{i}
-automatic for some integer
k_{i}\geq 2
. Given a function

f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z},

we associate to these data the
r
-dimensional array
\mathbf{c}=(c_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}})
defined by

c_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f\bigl(p_{n_{1}}(1),\dots,p_{n_{r}}(r)\bigr),

and consider the corresponding real number

\alpha=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}\geq 0}\frac{c_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}}{a_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots a_{r}^{n_{r}}}.

The array
\mathbf{c}
is a special case of an
r
-dimensional automatic array in the sense of
[
3
, §14.2]
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
r\geq 2
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\geq 2
and
\alpha
be as above. Then
\alpha
is either rational or transcendental.

The study of such numbers brings together methods from the theory of automatic sequences and higher-dimensional Diophantine approximation. A key feature of automatic sequences is that they satisfy a stammering condition: arbitrarily long prefixes contain large repeated blocks with controlled overlap (cf.
[
1
, proof of Theorem 2]
). On the Diophantine side, the Schmidt Subspace Theorem provides a powerful framework for treating simultaneous approximation problems. For each
n
we define an auxiliary rational number
\alpha_{n}
by replacing each sequence
(p_{k}(i))_{k\geq 0}
with an eventually periodic sequence. These rationals
\alpha_{n}
admit explicit rational expressions with controlled denominators. The stammering property ensures that
\alpha_{n}
approximates
\alpha
with sufficient precision. These approximations are then shown, via a suitable application of the Subspace Theorem to preclude the possibility that
\alpha
is algebraic and irrational.

1.3.
Outlook

Our main result naturally motivates the following question: Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\geq 2
be multiplicatively independent integers. Given integers
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 2
and any bounded
[k_{1},\dots,k_{r}]
-automatic array
\mathbf{c}=(c_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}})
of integers, can one still expect the associated series

\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}\geq 0}\frac{c_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}}{a_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots a_{r}^{n_{r}}}

to be either rational or transcendental? We hope that the methods developed here will stimulate further investigation into multidimensional analogues of classical problems in transcendence theory.

2.
Automatic sequences and their properties

2.1.
Words and morphisms

Let
\Sigma
be a nonempty finite set, which we call an
alphabet
. For an integer
k\geq 2
, set
\Sigma_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
, interpreted as the set of residue classes modulo
k
. A (finite)
word
over
\Sigma
is a sequence
w=w_{0}\cdots w_{\ell}
with
w_{i}\in\Sigma
. Its
length
is given by
|w|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ell+1
. We write
\Sigma^{*}
for the set of all finite words over
\Sigma
, including the empty word
\varepsilon
. The set
\Sigma^{*}
is a monoid with respect to concatenation, i.e., for
x=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y=y_{1}\cdots y_{m}\in\Sigma^{*}
, set

xy\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}y_{1}\cdots y_{m}.

Given
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
z\in\Sigma^{*}
, we say that
x
is a
prefix
of
z
if
z=xy
for some word
y\in\Sigma^{*}
.

The set of all
infinite words
in the alphabet
\Sigma
is denoted
\Sigma^{\omega}
and consists of words of the form
w=w_{0}w_{1}\dots w_{n}\dots
. Given
x=x_{1}\dots x_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
, denote by
x^{\omega}
the infinite periodic word

x^{\omega}=xxx\cdots.

We may identify
\Sigma^{\omega}
with the set of functions from
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
to
\Sigma
, mapping
n\mapsto w_{n}
. We shall set
\Sigma^{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Sigma^{*}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}
.

Let
\Sigma
and
\Delta
be alphabets. A function
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Delta^{*}
is called a
morphism
if it respects the monoidal structure, i.e.,
\varphi(xy)=\varphi(x)\varphi(y)
for all
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
. Suppose that there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
such that

|\varphi(a)|=k\quad\text{ for all }a\in\Sigma.

In this case,
\varphi
is said to be
k-uniform
. A
1
-uniform morphism is called a
coding
and arises from a function
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\rightarrow\Delta
.

Suppose that
\Sigma=\Delta
and let
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
be a
k
-uniform morphism. Note that
\varphi
natural extends to a function
\varphi^{\omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{\omega}\rightarrow\Sigma^{\omega}
defined by

\varphi^{\omega}(w_{0}w_{1}\dots w_{n}\dots)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi(w_{0})\varphi(w_{1})\dots\varphi(w_{n})\dots.

Let
a\in\Sigma
be such that
\varphi(a)=ax
for some
x\in\Sigma^{*}
with
|x|=k-1
. Then
\varphi
is said to be
prolongable
on
a
. In this case the infinite word

w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi^{\omega}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=ax\varphi(x)\varphi^{2}(x)\dots\varphi^{n}(x)\dots

is fixed by
\varphi
. Indeed, one finds that

\varphi(w)=\varphi\left(ax\varphi(x)\varphi^{2}(x)\dots\varphi^{n}(x)\dots\right)=w.

2.2.
Languages and DFAOs

Given an alphabet
\Sigma
, a subset
L
of
\Sigma^{*}
is called a
language
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
deterministic finite automaton
(DFA) over
\Sigma
is a tuple

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},F),

where
Q
is a finite set (the set of states),
\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is the transition function,
q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state, and
F\subseteq Q
is the set of accepting states.

We can think of
\mathcal{A}
as a finite directed graph for which the states are vertices and the edges are labelled by elements in
\Sigma
. This graph inputs finite words in
\Sigma^{*}
and either accepts or declines them. In order to make this precise, we extend the transition function
\delta
to a map
\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\times\Sigma^{*}\to Q
according to the rules

\delta(q,\varepsilon)=q,\qquad\delta(q,aw)=\delta(\delta(q,a),w)

for
q\in Q
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
. Then a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is
accepted
by
\mathcal{A}
if
\delta(q_{0},w)\in F
. The
language recognized
by
\mathcal{A}
is

L(\mathcal{A})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\,w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\delta(q_{0},w)\in F\,\}.

Definition 2.2
.

A language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is called
regular
if there exists a DFA
\mathcal{A}
such that
L=L(\mathcal{A})
.

Given a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, it is natural to ask when it arises from a DFA. Further one would like to investigate the structure of the DFA with the minimal number of states. This can be achieved via the famous Myhill-Nerode theorem, which we now recall. Define an equivalence relation
u\sim_{L}v
for
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
if

uw\in L\iff vw\in L\quad\forall w\in\Sigma^{*}.

Theorem 2.3

(Myhill–Nerode)
.

With respect to notation above, the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is regular.

(2)

The relation
\sim_{L}
has finitely many equivalence classes.

One can think of a DFA
\mathcal{A}
as a machine which gives rise to a function
f_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, where
f(w)=1
if
w
is accepted and
f(w)=0
if not. We shall consider functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Delta
, where
\Delta
is a finite alphabet which arise from DFAs with output.

Definition 2.4
.

A
DFAO
(deterministic finite automaton with output) is a
6
-tuple

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\tau),

where
Q
is a finite set of states,
\Sigma
is the input alphabet,
\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is the transition function,
q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state,
\Delta
is a finite output alphabet, and
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\to\Delta
is the output function. If
\Sigma=\Sigma_{k}
, we call
\mathcal{A}
a k-DFAO.

As in the DFA case, the transition function
\delta
extends uniquely to a map

\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\times\Sigma^{*}\to Q

according to the rules

\delta(q,\varepsilon)=q,\qquad\delta(q,aw)=\delta(\delta(q,a),w),

for
q\in Q
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
. Consider the function
f_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Delta
defined by

f_{\mathcal{A}}(w)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau\left(\delta(q_{0},w)\right).

A function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Delta
that arises from a DFAO
\mathcal{A}
is called a
finite state function
.

Next we recall the definition of an automatic sequences in a finite alphabet
\Delta
. Given a nonzero integer
n\geq 0
, we write

n=\sum_{i=0}^{r}w_{i}k^{i}

with
w_{i}\in\Sigma_{k}
and
w_{r}\neq 0
. We associate to
n
the word

(n)_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=w_{0}w_{1}\cdots w_{r}\in\Sigma_{k}^{*}.

If
n=0
we simply set
(0)_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=0
. On the other hand, given a string
w_{0}\dots w_{j}\in\Sigma_{k}^{*}
, we set

(2.1)

[w_{0}\dots w_{j}]_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}w_{i}k^{i}.

Definition 2.5
.

A sequence
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with values in a finite alphabet
\Delta
is called
k
-automatic
if there exists a
k
-DFAO
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma_{k},\delta,q_{0},\mathfrak{A},\tau)
such that

a_{n}=\tau\bigl(\delta(q_{0},(n)_{k})\bigr)

for all
n\geq 0
.

Equivalently, a sequence is
k
-automatic if its
n
-th term is obtained by feeding the base-
k
expansion of
n
into a finite automaton with output and reading the resulting state through the map
\tau
. For background and further properties, see
[
3
, §4.3]
.

For a sequence
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, define the
k
-kernel of
\mathbf{a}
to be the set of subsequences

K_{k}(\mathbf{a})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(u_{k^{i}n+j})_{n\geq 0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i\geq 0\text{ and }0\leq j\leq k^{i}\}.

The following result shows that the
k
-automaticity property is detected by the
k
-kernel.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
k\geq 2
. The sequence
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
k
-automatic if and only if
K_{k}(\mathbf{a})
is finite.

In the case where
\mathbf{a}
takes values in
\{0,1\}
, this criterion follows from the Myhill-Nerode Theorem (i.e., Theorem
2.3
).

We recall the relationship between automatic sequences and sequences that are fixed under a morphism.

Theorem 2.7

(Cobham)
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence with values in a finite alphabet
\Delta
. Then
\mathbf{a}
is
k
-automatic if and only if there exist

•

a finite alphabet
\mathfrak{B}
,

•

a
k
-uniform morphism
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{B}^{*}\to\mathfrak{B}^{*}
,

•

a letter
b\in\mathfrak{B}
such that
\sigma(b)=bw
for some
w\in\mathfrak{B}^{*}
(i.e.,
\varphi
is prolongable in
b
as in §
2.1
),

•

and a coding
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{B}\to\Delta
,

such that
\mathbf{a}=\pi(\mathbf{b})
where
\mathbf{b}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma^{\omega}(b)
.

2.3.
Stammering sequences

Let
\Delta
be a finite alphabet. For a word
W
over
\Delta
, we denote by
|W|
its length. For any integer
\ell\geq 1
, we write
W^{\ell}
for
W\cdots W
, taken
\ell
-times. More generally, if
x>0
is a real number, we define

W^{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=W^{\lfloor x\rfloor}W^{\prime},

where
W^{\prime}
is the prefix of
W
of length
\lceil(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)|W|\rceil
. Here
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
and
\lceil\cdot\rceil
denote, respectively, the floor and ceiling functions.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence with values in
\Delta
, which we identify with the infinite word
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
w>1
be a real number. We say that
\mathbf{a}
satisfies
Condition
(*)_{w}
if there exist sequences of finite words
(U_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(V_{n})_{n\geq 1}
over
\Delta
such that:

(1)

For every
n\geq 1
, the word
U_{n}V_{n}^{\,w}
is a prefix of
\mathbf{a}
;

(2)

The sequence
\bigl(|U_{n}|/|V_{n}|\bigr)_{n\geq 1}
is bounded;

(3)

The sequence
(|V_{n}|)_{n\geq 1}
is strictly increasing.

A sequence
\mathbf{a}
satisfying Condition
(*)_{w}
for some
w>1
is called a
stammering sequence
.

It is easy to see that if
\mathbf{a}
is eventually periodic then it satisfies
(*)_{w}
for every
w>1
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a
k
-automatic sequence on a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, then
\mathbf{a}
is a stammering sequence.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.7
there exist a finite alphabet
\mathcal{B}
, a
k
-uniform morphism
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{B}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
, an infinite sequence
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{B}^{\omega}
which is a fixed point of
\sigma
, and a coding
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathbf{a}=\phi(\mathbf{u})
. It is clear that if
\mathbf{u}
satisfies Condition
(*)_{w}
, then so does
\mathbf{a}
. Thus it suffices to establish the result for
\mathbf{u}
.

Let
r=|\mathcal{B}|
. By the pigeonhole principle, the prefix of
\mathbf{u}
of length
r+1
contains two occurrences of the same letter. Hence it can be written in the form

W_{1}\,u\,W_{2}\,u\,W_{3},

where
u\in\mathcal{B}
and
W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}
are (possibly empty) words over
\mathcal{B}
. For each integer
n\geq 1
, define

U_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma^{n}(W_{1}),\qquad V_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma^{n}(uW_{2}).

Because
V_{n}
begins with
\sigma^{n}(u)
, we obtain that
U_{n}V_{n}^{\,1+1/r}
is a prefix of
\mathbf{u}
.

We now verify the conditions of
(*)_{w}
with
w=1+\frac{1}{r}
. Since
\sigma
is
k
-uniform, we have
|\sigma^{n}(W)|=k^{n}|W|
for any word
W
, and thus

\frac{|U_{n}|}{|V_{n}|}=\frac{|W_{1}|}{|uW_{2}|}\leq\frac{|W_{1}|}{1+|W_{2}|}\leq r-1,

so that the sequence
(|U_{n}|/|V_{n}|)
is bounded. Moreover since
|V_{n}|=k^{n}|uW_{2}|
, it follows that
|V_{n}|
is strictly increasing. Thus
\mathbf{u}
satisfies Condition
(*)_{1+1/r}
, and therefore is a stammering sequence. The same holds for
\mathbf{a}
.
∎

2.4.
r
-dimensional automatic arrays

In this section we recall the notion of multidimensional automatic arrays. By way of example, first consider the
2
-dimensional case. Let
k,l\geq 2
be integers, and let
\Sigma_{k}=\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
and
\Sigma_{l}=\{0,1,\dots,l-1\}
. We consider the product alphabet
\Sigma_{k}\times\Sigma_{l}
, consisting of pairs
[a,b]
with
a\in\Sigma_{k}
and
b\in\Sigma_{l}
. If

w=[a_{0},b_{0}][a_{1},b_{1}][a_{2},b_{2}]\cdots[a_{j},b_{j}]\in(\Sigma_{k}\times\Sigma_{l})^{*},

we define

[w]_{k,l}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigl([a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{j}]_{k},\;[b_{0}b_{1}\cdots b_{j}]_{l}\bigr),

where
[a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{j}]_{k}
and
[b_{0}b_{1}\cdots b_{j}]_{l}
are defined according to (
2.1
).
Conversely, given
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
, write
(m)_{k}=a_{0}\cdots a_{i}
and
(n)_{l}=b_{0}\cdots b_{j}
. We define a word
(m,n)_{k,l}\in(\Sigma_{k}\times\Sigma_{l})^{*}
by padding the shorter expansion with leading zeros, namely

(m,n)_{k,l}=\begin{cases}[0,b_{0}]\cdots[0,b_{j-i}][a_{0},b_{j-i+1}]\cdots[a_{i},b_{j}],&\text{if }j\geq i,\\
[a_{0},0]\cdots[a_{i-j},0][a_{i-j+1},b_{0}]\cdots[a_{i},b_{j}],&\text{if }i>j.\end{cases}

More generally, let
r\geq 2
be an integer, and let
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 2
be integers. For each
1\leq i\leq r
, set
\Sigma_{k_{i}}=\{0,1,\dots,k_{i}-1\}
, and consider the product alphabet

\Sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Sigma_{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times\Sigma_{k_{r}}.

Thus, elements of
\Sigma
are
r
-tuples
[a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(r)}]
with
a^{(i)}\in\Sigma_{k_{i}}
.

If

w=[a^{(1)}_{0},\dots,a^{(r)}_{0}]\cdots[a^{(1)}_{j},\dots,a^{(r)}_{j}]\in\Sigma^{*},

we define

(2.2)

[w]_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigl([a^{(1)}_{0}\cdots a^{(1)}_{j}]_{k_{1}},\dots,[a^{(r)}_{0}\cdots a^{(r)}_{j}]_{k_{r}}\bigr).

Conversely, given
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
, write
(n_{i})_{k_{i}}=a^{(i)}_{1}\cdots a^{(i)}_{\ell_{i}}
for each
i
. Let
\ell=\max_{i}\ell_{i}

3.
Diophantine tools

In this section we collect several results from Diophantine approximation that will be used later. We begin with classical one-dimensional approximation results, and then introduce the language of absolute values and heights, ending with Schlickewei’s refinement of Schmidt’s Subspace Theorem.

3.1.
Dirichlet and Roth

We first recall Dirichlet’s theorem on Diophantine approximation.

Theorem 3.1

(Dirichlet)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
Q\geq 1
. Then there exist integers
p,q
with
1\leq q\leq Q
such that

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{qQ}.

In particular, there exist infinitely many rational numbers
p/q
such that

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{q^{2}}.

Dirichlet’s theorem shows that every real number admits very good rational approximations. For algebraic numbers, however, such approximations cannot be substantially improved.

Theorem 3.2

(Roth)
.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational algebraic number. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, the inequality

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{q^{2+\varepsilon}}

has only finitely many solutions in rational numbers
p/q
.

Roth’s theorem is a far-reaching refinement of earlier results of Thue and Siegel. A higher-dimensional and powerful generalization is the Subspace Theorem, which we now describe.

3.2.
Absolute values and heights

Let
\mathbf{K}
be an algebraic number field of degree
d=[\mathbf{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Q}]
, and let
M(\mathbf{K})
denote the set of (normalized) places of
\mathbf{K}
. For
v\in M(\mathbf{K})
and
x\in\mathbf{K}
, we define an absolute value
|\cdot|_{v}
as follows:

(i)

If
v
corresponds to a real embedding
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{K}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, set

|x|_{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|\sigma(x)|^{1/d}.

(ii)

If
v
corresponds to a pair of complex embeddings
\sigma,\overline{\sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{K}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, set

|x|_{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|\sigma(x)|^{2/d}=|\overline{\sigma}(x)|^{2/d}.

(iii)

If
v
corresponds to a nonzero prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{K}}
, set

|x|_{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(N\mathfrak{p})^{-\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)/d}.

With this normalization, the family
\{|\cdot|_{v}\}_{v\in M(\mathbf{K})}
satisfies the
product formula

\prod_{v\in M(\mathbf{K})}|x|_{v}=1\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbf{K}^{\times}.

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbf{K}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
v\in M(\mathbf{K})
, define

|\mathbf{x}|_{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|_{v}^{2d}\right)^{1/(2d)},&\text{if }v\text{ is real infinite},\\[5.0pt]
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|_{v}^{d}\right)^{1/d},&\text{if }v\text{ is complex infinite},\\[5.0pt]
\max\{|x_{1}|_{v},\dots,|x_{n}|_{v}\},&\text{if }v\text{ is finite}.\end{cases}

Definition 3.3
.

The (multiplicative)
height
of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{K}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is defined by

H(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{v\in M(\mathbf{K})}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}.

This definition is independent of the choice of coordinates up to multiplication by a bounded factor, and plays a central role in Diophantine geometry. For each place
v
of, fix an extension
|\cdot|_{v}
to
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
.

3.3.
The Subspace Theorem

We now state the Subspace Theorem, due to Schmidt and its refinement by Schlickewei, which can be viewed as a multidimensional generalization of Roth’s theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(Subspace Theorem)
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be an algebraic number field, and let
m\geq 2
be an integer. Let
S\subset M(\mathbf{K})
be a finite set of places containing all infinite places. For each
v\in S
, let

L_{1,v},\dots,L_{m,v}

be linearly independent linear forms in
m
variables with algebraic coefficients. Let
\varepsilon>0
.

Then the set of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{K}^{m}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
satisfying

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{|L_{i,v}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{|\mathbf{x}|_{v}}\leq H(\mathbf{x})^{-m-\varepsilon}

is contained in finitely many proper linear subspaces of
\mathbf{K}^{m}
.

Since the polynomial
P_{n}(X,Y)
has integer coefficients it follows that
|L_{v,5}(\mathbf{x}_{n})|_{v}\leq 1
for any
v\in S_{0}
. Thus we find that

\Pi\ll a^{-(w-1)s_{n}}b^{-(w-1)s_{n}^{\prime}}\prod_{v\in S}|\mathbf{x}_{n}|_{v}^{-5}=a^{-(w-1)s_{n}}b^{-(w-1)s_{n}^{\prime}}H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-5}.

Note that for each nonarchimedian place
v
,
|\mathbf{x}_{n}|_{v}\leq 1
and therefore,

H(\mathbf{x}_{n})\leq|\mathbf{x}_{n}|_{\infty}.

On the other hand, since
P_{n}(X,Y)
has absolutely bounded coefficients and degree at most
r_{n}+s_{n}
in
X
and
r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime}
in
Y
, it follows that

|\mathbf{x}_{n}|_{\infty}\ll(r_{n}+s_{n})(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})a^{r_{n}+s_{n}}b^{r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime}}.

Let
C>0
be an absolute constant such that
r_{n}<Cs_{n}
and
r_{n}^{\prime}<Cs_{n}^{\prime}
. We find that

H(\mathbf{x}_{n})\ll(r_{n}+s_{n})(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})(a^{s_{n}}b^{s_{n}^{\prime}})^{C+1}

i.e.,

a^{-s_{n}}b^{-s_{n}^{\prime}}\ll(r_{n}+s_{n})^{1/(C+1)}(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})^{1/(C+1)}H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-1/(C+1)}

Therefore we find that

a^{-(w-1)s_{n}}b^{-(w-1)s_{n}^{\prime}}\ll\left((r_{n}+s_{n})(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-1}\right)^{\frac{w-1}{C+1}}.

Note that

H(\mathbf{x}_{n})\gg a^{r_{n}+s_{n}}b^{r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime}}

and therefore, for any constant
C^{\prime}>0
, we have that

H(\mathbf{x}_{n})\gg\left((r_{n}+s_{n})(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})\right)^{C^{\prime}}.

Therefore, we find that

(r_{n}+s_{n})(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})\ll H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\delta}

for any
\delta>0
.
Thus for any

\epsilon\in(0,\frac{w-1}{C+1})
, we have that

\left((r_{n}+s_{n})(r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime})H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-1}\right)^{\frac{w-1}{C+1}}\ll H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-\epsilon}.

We deduce that

\Pi\ll H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-5-\epsilon}

for all
n
. Consequently infinitely many of the
\mathbf{x}_{n}
lie on the same hyperplane. Passing to a subsequence, assume that all
\mathbf{x}_{n}
satisfy

(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4},u_{5})\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n}=0

where the vector
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4},u_{5})\in\mathbb{Q}^{5}
is not zero.
Thus we find that

u_{1}a^{r_{n}+s_{n}}b^{r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime}}-u_{2}a^{r_{n}}b^{r_{n}^{\prime}+s_{n}^{\prime}}-u_{3}a^{r_{n}+s_{n}}b^{r_{n}^{\prime}}+u_{4}a^{r_{n}}b^{r_{n}^{\prime}}-u_{5}P_{n}(a,b)=0.

Suppose that
u_{5}\neq 0
. Note that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{P_{n}(a,b)}{a^{r_{n}+s_{n}}b^{r^{\prime}_{n}+s^{\prime}_{n}}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{P_{n}(a,b)}{a^{r_{n}}(a^{s_{n}}-1)\,b^{r^{\prime}_{n}}(b^{s^{\prime}_{n}}-1)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}=\alpha.

Thus dividing by
a^{r_{n}+s_{n}}b^{r^{\prime}_{n}+s^{\prime}_{n}}
and letting
n\rightarrow\infty
we find that

u_{5}\alpha=u_{1}

and so
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
. Next suppose that
u_{5}=0
. Then the same argument gives
u_{1}=u_{5}\alpha=0
. Thus we find that for all
n
,

u_{2}b^{s_{n}^{\prime}}+u_{3}a^{s_{n}}=u_{4}.

Note that both
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
must be non-zero, otherwise it is easy to conclude that all
u_{i}=0
. If
u_{4}\neq 0
then divide by
u_{4}
to get

Ax+By=1

where
A,B
are fixed rational numbers and
x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=b^{s_{n}^{\prime}}
,
y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a^{s_{n}}
belong to finitely generated subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. It then follows that there are only finitely many solutions to this equation, see
[
10
, Theorem 3.1, Ch.8]
. Thus we find that
u_{4}=0
. In this case,

u_{2}b^{s_{n}^{\prime}}=-u_{3}a^{s_{n}}.

In particular,

(s_{n}^{\prime}-s_{m}^{\prime})\log b-(s_{n}-s_{m})\log a=0,

which is a contradiction. Thus we have shown that
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Next consider the
r
-dimensional case. Let
S_{0}
be the set of all finite primes dividing
a_{1}\cdots a_{r}
, and set
S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=S_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
. We consider the vector

\mathbf{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{2^{r}},x_{2^{r}+1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{2^{r}+1},

whose first
2^{r}
coordinates correspond to the
2^{r}
terms arising from the decomposition over subsets
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,r\}
, and whose last coordinate will specialize to
-P_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
.

For
v\in S_{0}
and
1\leq i\leq 2^{r}+1
, define

L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{i}.

For
v=\infty
, define

L_{\infty,2^{r}+1}(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{r}}x_{i}\Bigr)+x_{2^{r}+1},\qquad L_{\infty,i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{i}\quad(1\leq i\leq 2^{r}).

Order the collection of subsets
I\subset\{1,\dots,r\}
by
I_{1},\dots,I_{2^{r}}
. For each
n\geq 1
, define
\mathbf{x}_{n}
as follows. For each subset
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,r\}
, set

x_{I}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(-1)^{|I|}\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)+s_{n}(i)}\prod_{i\in I}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)},

and define

\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigl((x_{I})_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots,r\}},\;-P_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\bigr).

In other words, for
i\geq 2^{r}
set
x_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{I_{i}}
and
x_{2^{r}+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-P_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
.

By Proposition
4.1
we find that

L_{\infty,2^{r}+1}(\mathbf{x}_{n})=\left(\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)}(a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}-1)-P_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)}(a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}-1)\,(\alpha-\alpha_{n}).

Arguing as in the two-dimensional case, we have

\alpha-\alpha_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i}\geq r_{n}(i)+\lceil ws_{n}(i)\rceil+1\\
1\leq i\leq r\end{subarray}}\frac{c_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}-c^{(n)}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}}{a_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots a_{r}^{n_{r}}},

and hence

L_{\infty,2^{r}+1}(\mathbf{x}_{n})=O\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{-(w-1)s_{n}(i)}\right).

We now estimate

\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=1}^{2^{r}+1}\frac{|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x}_{n})|_{v}}{|\mathbf{x}_{n}|_{v}}.

As before, for
i\leq 2^{r}
and
v\notin S
, we have
|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x}_{n})|_{v}=1
, and hence by the product formula,

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=1}^{2^{r}}|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x}_{n})|_{v}=1.

Moreover, since
P_{n}
has integer coefficients, we have
|L_{v,2^{r}+1}(\mathbf{x}_{n})|_{v}\leq 1
for
v\in S_{0}
. Thus

\Pi\ll\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{-(w-1)s_{n}(i)}\cdot H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-(2^{r}+1)}.

On the other hand, since
P_{n}
has bounded coefficients and degree
\ll r_{n}(i)+s_{n}(i)
in each variable, we obtain

H(\mathbf{x}_{n})\ll\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{r}(r_{n}(i)+s_{n}(i))\Bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)+s_{n}(i)}.

Using
r_{n}(i)\ll s_{n}(i)
, we deduce

H(\mathbf{x}_{n})\ll\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{r}(r_{n}(i)+s_{n}(i))\Bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{(C+1)s_{n}(i)}

for some constant
C>0
.

It follows that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{-(w-1)s_{n}(i)}\ll H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-\varepsilon},

and hence for all
n
,

\Pi\ll H(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{-(2^{r}+1+\varepsilon)}.

Theorem
3.4
then implies that infinitely many of the
\mathbf{x}_{n}
lie in a proper subspace. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{r}}u_{i}x_{I_{i}}+u_{2^{r}+1}x_{2^{r}+1}=0

for all
n
, where not all
u_{i}
vanish. We set
u_{I_{i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=u_{i}
for ease of notation.

Suppose first that
u_{2^{r}+1}\neq 0
. Dividing by
\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)+s_{n}(i)}
and letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain

u_{2^{r}+1}\alpha=u_{J},

where
J=\emptyset
and hence
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Suppose now that
u_{2^{r}+1}=0
. Then

\sum_{I}u_{I}x_{I}=0,

where
I
ranges over the subsets of
\{1,\dots,r\}
. Dividing by
\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{r_{n}(i)}
, we obtain a relation of the form

\sum_{I}u_{I}\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}=0.

Let
I_{0}
be such that
u_{I_{0}}\neq 0
. We find that

\sum_{I\neq I_{0}}-\frac{u_{I}}{u_{I_{0}}}\frac{\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}}{\prod_{j\notin I_{0}}a_{j}^{s_{n}(j)}}=1.

Let
\widetilde{S}
be a finite set of places containing
S
such that
u_{I}/u_{I_{0}}
are
\widetilde{S}
-units.
As a consequence of the generalized
S
-unit theorem
[
7
, Corollary 7.4.3]
there is a finite set
\Phi
such that for all
n
, there is a set
I\neq I_{0}
such that

\frac{\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}}{\prod_{j\notin I_{0}}a_{j}^{s_{n}(j)}}\in\Phi.

Thus by the Pigeon hole principle, there exists a set
I^{\prime}\neq I_{0}
such that

\frac{\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}}{\prod_{j\notin I_{0}}a_{j}^{s_{n}(j)}}\in\Phi

for infinitely many
n
. Therefore there exists
n\neq m
such that

\frac{\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{s_{n}(i)}}{\prod_{j\notin I_{0}}a_{j}^{s_{n}(j)}}=\frac{\prod_{i\notin I}a_{i}^{s_{m}(i)}}{\prod_{j\notin I_{0}}a_{j}^{s_{m}(j)}}.

Therefore

\sum_{i\in I}(s_{n}(i)-s_{m}(i))\log a_{i}-\sum_{j\in I_{0}}(s_{n}(j)-s_{m}(j))\log a_{i}=0.

We write this relation as

\sum_{I}w_{i}\log a_{i}=0,

where

w_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}s_{n}(i)-s_{m}(i)&\text{ if }i\in I\setminus I_{0};\\
s_{m}(i)-s_{n}(i)&\text{ if }i\in I_{0}\setminus I;\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that the sequence
(s_{n}(i))_{n}
is strictly increasing and thus in particular,
s_{n}(i)\neq s_{m}(i)
for all
i=1,\dots,r
. Since
I\neq I_{0}
, not all coefficients are
0
and this gives us a contradiction since
\log a_{1},\dots,\log a_{r}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent. Thus
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
if
\alpha\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
and this completes the proof.
∎
