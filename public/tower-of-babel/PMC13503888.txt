Estimating time of death from smartwatch data: initial empirical results
Authors: Tanja Gosseling, Bartholomeus G H Latten, Jan Peter van Zandwijk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetermining the time of death is a critical factor in forensic death investigations. Currently, the most widely used method is rectal temperature measurement. However, there are many, often unknown, factors influencing the results of this method and furthermore it has the limitation that it can only be applied within a restricted timeframe. Therefore, there is a need for new alternative methods. The research question of this exploratory study is whether digital information from smartwatches allows for a reliable determination of the time of death. With this purpose in mind, we collected data from Garmin, Apple and Samsung smartwatches, worn by participants dying at a scheduled moment during a euthanasia procedure. Analysis of experimental data (N = 27) indicates that data registered by smartwatches showed changes around the participant’s death, allowing for an estimation of time of death. Although the number of variables and time granularity of registrations varied across types of devices, in most cases the difference between the time of death determined by the physician and the estimates derived from smartwatch data, was smaller than roughly 30 min. For unexplained reasons, however, in some instances there were gaps in the data recorded by smartwatches. This highlights the need for caution when applying this data for determining the time of death. Nonetheless, current first results do indicate that data from smartwatches has potential of providing relevant information about time of death in addition to existing methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61387-9.Keywords: Post-mortem interval, Time of death, Forensic death investigation, Smartwatches, Wearable device forensicsSubject terms: Public health, Medical research, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Determining the time of death (TOD) can be an important factor in both medical and forensic death investigations. Information about the TOD is often crucial in a forensic setting of a (potential) homicide, where a clear timeline of events needs to be established. Additionally, information about the time and the date somebody has died may help the bereaved in their grieving process.

Without reliable eyewitnesses, the TOD can be difficult to determine. In such cases, tactical information, for instance the time the person was last seen or camera footage, along with post-mortem changes in the body—rigor mortis (stiffness), livor mortis (lividity), and algor mortis (cooling of the body)—are used to estimate the time interval between death and the examination of the body, the so-called post-mortem interval (PMI). Currently, rectal body temperature measurement is a widely applied method for estimating PMI. Many models have been created[e.g.1–4], with the one developed by Henssge5,6 being the most common in practice. However, the results of such methods can be affected by various factors, including fluctuation of ambient temperature, wind flow, type of surface on which the body is found, clothing and whether the body is wet. As a result, calculation of PMI using temperature measurement often produces results with an interval of several hours (from ± 2.8 h up to ± 7 h5–7), which may often be too wide for practical purposes. Furthermore, once rectal temperature equals the ambient temperature – at room temperature typically occurring around three days post-mortem - the PMI cannot be calculated using this method. This also applies to a recently introduced more advanced method for determining PMI using a thermal photogrammetry and modelling approach, which shows an interval of 0.26 ± 1.38 h7. Also, other methods for determining the PMI relying on thanatochemical changes are being studied, such as changes in ocular potassium concentration8 and proteomics9–11.

Given the uncertainties associated with the currently employed methods, there is a critical need for alternative methodologies to ascertain the PMI or, preferably, the TOD. An interesting new opportunity for this may be emerging from the field of health-related consumer electronics. In the present era of technological innovation, wearable devices such as smartwatches, smart rings or other activity trackers are enjoying popularity and consequently deceased persons are increasingly often found wearing such devices. In a broader forensic context, digital traces from various types of consumer electronics have been found to have potential for inferring information about activities in the physical world12. In this study, we explore the possibility of wearable health-related consumer electronics in providing information concerning the TOD.

Using photoplethysmography (PPG)13 and information from sensors such as accelerometers and gyroscopes, wearable devices can collect several types of biometric data, including heart rate, blood oxygen saturation, or measures of physical activity, which are, importantly, all registered together with detailed timestamps. As biometric data collected by smartwatches are expected to show changes after death, these devices might offer interesting new investigative opportunities for estimating TOD. As data can remain stored on devices for longer periods of time, this could also potentially extend the time range for which estimates of TOD can be obtained beyond what is currently achievable with existing temperature-based techniques.

In living people, smartwatches have already been studied for their use in the diagnostics of medical conditions, such as cardiac arrhythmias14–16 and detection of epileptic seizures17. Furthermore, it has been investigated whether data from smartwatches can be used to make a model, that can predict death events in terminally ill patients18. In a clinical setting, a recent study19 has shown that specifically developed cardio watches can detect a medically induced circulatory arrest. However, the reliability and time range of estimates of the TOD using data from commonly available types of smartwatches is currently unknown.

To assess the potential of data from wearable devices for determining TOD, it is essential to collect data in a setting with known ground-truth, i.e. where TOD is independently determined by a physician. Such a study design presents substantial practical and ethical challenges, since it requires collecting data from individuals wearing a device in situations where death is anticipated. In this regard, cases in which a patient receives euthanasia could offer a unique opportunity, as the date and time of death are scheduled. In the Netherlands, euthanasia is a strictly regulated legal procedure, defined as “termination of life by a physician at the request of a patient”20. There are certain requirements that must be met before euthanasia can take place, among which there must be unbearable suffering without prospects of improvement. In the Netherlands, euthanasia is performed by first administering a coma inducing drug (thiopental or propofol) intravenously. Next, when the patient is in a deep coma, a muscle relaxant (rocuronium) is administered intravenously, which causes cessation of breathing and ultimately death. The euthanizing physician is legally required to record the exact times of administration of the drugs and the time of death.

The purpose of this exploratory study is to take the first steps in determining the applicability of data from commonly available wearable devices for estimating TOD. Hence, the research question of this paper is whether digital information from smartwatches allows for a reliable determination of the TOD, and, if so, with what margin? For this, patients who were scheduled to receive euthanasia were asked to wear a smartwatch during the procedure. Afterwards, we examined data recorded by these smartwatches for information allowing for estimation of TOD. Preliminary findings of this study have been published as an abstract21.

Results

Participants and euthanasia procedure

In the period between June 2024 and August 2024 a total of 75 euthanizing physicians were approached for the study and eventually 28 participants agreed to be included in the study. Details on inclusion criteria, participant recruitment and ethical approval are described in “Methods”. One of the participants had deceased of natural causes shortly before the euthanizing procedure. Since the time of death was not observed and confirmed by the physician, this patient was excluded from the further data analysis. Smartwatch data

Garmin Vivoactive 5

In nine experiments the participant wore the Garmin watch. In all cases, the watch produced consistent measurements throughout the period it was worn. Details on smartwatch configuration, data acquisition and processing can be found in “Digital methodology”. From analysis of processed data, it follows that the Garmin smartwatch has the capability to record data every minute and that time information is stored in the variable ‘timestamp’. Some biometric variables are collected every minute, while others are collected at a lower frequency. The following variables were identified as having a change in the period around the TOD, from which a Time of Last Registration (TLR) can be derived. The method employed to identify relevant variables from smartwatch data and the definition of TLRs can be found in “Data analysis”.

heart_rate: during wearing, (almost) every minute there is a value corresponding to number of heart beats per minute. When not worn, the device registers a value of 0 every 16 min, with no data being registered in between. For TLR we use the value of ‘timestamp’ corresponding to the last registration of a heart_rate > 0 in the period that the watch is worn.

reading_spo2: during wearing, (almost) every minute there is a value registered corresponding to the percentage of oxygenation. When not worn, the device produces no registration of reading_spo2. For TLR we use the value of ‘timestamp’ corresponding to the last registration of reading_spo2 in the period the smartwatch was worn, irrespective of the value being registered.

respiration_rate: during wearing, (almost) every minute there is a value registered corresponding to number of breaths per minute. When not worn, the device produces registrations of respiration_rate equal to -2.0 every minute. For TLR we use the value of ‘timestamp’ corresponding to the last registration of respiration_rate > -2.0 in the period that the watch is worn.

intensity: during wearing, there are registrations with variable time intervals in the range 0–10 min. When not worn, the device produces registrations with value 0 about every hour. For TLR we use the value of ‘timestamp’ corresponding to the last registration of intensity with any value.

body_battery_status: during wearing, (almost) every minute there is registration with constant value of 1.0. When not worn, the device produces registrations with a constant value of either 2.0 or 126.0 every minute. For TLR we use the value of ‘timestamp’ corresponding to the last registration of body_battery_status with value 1.0 in the period the watch was worn.

Apple Watch 9

In nine experiments the participants wore an Apple watch. Details on smartwatch configuration, data acquisition and processing can be found in “Digital methodology”. From analysis of processed data, it follows that the Apple smartwatch dynamically records data at variable time intervals ranging between a few seconds to ten minutes. For time information, we use the variable start_date. Based on the criteria from “Data analysis”, the following variables were identified as having a change in the period around the TOD.

Heart Rate: during wearing, consecutive values are recorded at variable time intervals up to ten minutes. Most frequently, the time intervals were a few seconds and roughly five minutes. Heart rate is registered in beats per second, which we converted to beats per minute for comparison with the Garmin and Samsung devices. When not worn, the device registers no data. For TLR we use the value of ‘start_date’ corresponding to the last registration of Heart Rate in the period that the watch is worn.

Active Energy Inspection of data reveals that the variable Active Energy shows some changes around the TOD. This variable contains noisy data which shows a slight trend transitioning from higher values before TOD towards lower values after TOD. Due to the noisy nature of the data, it is difficult to assign a TLR to this data. We did this by setting a threshold and used the value of ‘start_date’ corresponding to the last registration with a value of Active Energy < 0.5 in the period the watch was worn, but this is not necessarily very robust.

Effects of post-mortem movements, medical conditions and physical characteristics

Post-mortem movement of the arm of the participant by physician or family was reported seven times on the registration forms. These were small movements such as lifting the arm. In all cases, there were no noticeable phenomena present in the corresponding smartwatch data that could be connected to these events.

After removal and during transport of the watches, sometimes registrations of biometric data were observed in the Garmin device (see e.g. Figure 1). A likely explanation for this phenomenon is that measurements such as heart rate are triggered by movement of the device and therefore might show up as false positive registrations in such cases.

From inspection of the currently available data, no marked differences in TLRs were identified when separating the study population into categories based on medical conditions and physical characteristics, such as skin color and physique. Due to the small sample size no statistical analysis was performed.

Discussion

In this study, we set out to collect experimental data to examine to what extent TOD can be estimated from smartwatch data. The results of this study indicate that, in cases where the smartwatch produced continuous measurements during the period it was worn, times of last registration (TLR) of variables fall within a time interval of roughly 30 min before or after TOD as determined by the physician. This suggests that smartwatch data has the potential to provide estimates of the TOD with smaller time intervals than the traditional methods based on rectal body temperature (Henssge), which usually yield time intervals of several hours (from ± 2.8 h up to ± 7 h).

While thermal photogrammetry and modelling approaches show a relatively small interval of 0.26 ± 1.38 h for calculating the TOD, the applicability of these temperature-based methods is limited to the early postmortem period, as it can only be applied before the body temperature reaches the ambient temperature. In contrast, smartwatch data can be used in cases with a prolonged postmortem period. This suggest that the TOD may be estimated weeks, months of even years after death, provided that the data has been stored on the devices. Therefore smartwatch data could especially be useful in cases of a prolonged postmortem interval, where temperature-based methods are no longer applicable. In such cases, smartwatch data can be substantially more informative than TOD estimates based on observations of the body decomposition stages, which estimates the PMI in multiple days22.

In this study, differences between types of examined smartwatches were found concerning time granularity of registrations and the number of identified variables providing information on the TOD. Such heterogeneity between devices is fairly common in the digital forensic domain. Together with the rapid succession of hardware and software versions of the same device it poses substantial challenges as it complicates direct comparison and limits generalizability. Besides this, our findings contain some currently unexplained phenomena that could potentially impact applicability.

The Garmin Vivoactive 5 was found to register data with the highest time resolution and its data contains the most variables related to the TOD. TLRs derived from the data show a systematic tendency as they are almost all later than the TOD. For the Apple Watch 9, in three cases unexplained discontinuous Heart Rate registrations occurred. Since such cases are difficult to identify by looking at smartwatch data only, it might be somewhat risky to base an estimate of TOD solely on this data. Due to the noisy nature of Active Energy data, the estimate of TOD derived from this data involves a certain degree of uncertainty. For the Samsung Watch 6, only one variable related to the TOD was identified and the default time resolution of registration (10 min) was larger than for the other watches, thereby introducing a larger uncertainty in the estimate of TOD.

An element of uncertainty in this study might be the time of death as determined by the physician. Since dying is a gradual process, time of death is not a specific moment in time and therefore, the reported value of TOD may depend on the method used to determine it. In the Netherlands, there is no fixed protocol on how to determine the moment at which death has occurred, leaving this at the discretion of the physician. The variability of methods employed by physicians in this study may lead to some spread in the ground-truth values of TOD, the size of which is difficult to quantify. This spread may account to some extent for the differences between ground-truth TOD and estimates of TOD obtained from smartwatch data.

One of the limitations of this study is the relatively small number of participants. Nonetheless we find it particularly noteworthy that so many patients gave consent under the delicate circumstances associated with euthanasia. Small sample sizes limit the possibility for statistical analyses to determine the effects of, for example, physical characteristics. Therefore, some caution is warranted when interpreting the results and drawing strong conclusions should be avoided.

Due to the study design, our study population likely differs in several aspects from the population encountered in forensic death investigations. First, the study population consisted of mostly elderly and terminally ill participants who had reduced mobility or often were bedridden, while the forensic population will be younger, more healthy and more physically active. Therefore, variables related to physical activity, such as number of steps or walking velocity, were not registered frequently by the smartwatches during the observation period. In forensic cases these variables potentially could provide additional information to estimate the TOD. Also, participants in our population died of respiratory arrest leading to cardiac arrest due to the administration of drugs during the euthanasia procedure, while in the forensic population there might be other causes, such as extensive bleeding leading to drastically reduced blood pressure with an absent peripheral pulse. It is currently unknown how smartwatches react under such conditions and to what extent this affects data produced by devices. All these considerations impact the generalizability of our study results and consequently, caution is warranted when extrapolating the present results directly to a forensic population. However, despite these differences, we believe current findings nonetheless provide meaningful insights into data registered by smartwatches during the dying process, the mechanisms of which are also likely to occur in forensic cases.

In this study, we adopted a data-driven approach to selecting variables from smartwatch data providing information about the TOD. While this approach aligns with common digital forensic methodology, where traces are first identified and subsequently analyzed and validated to determine the inferences that can be drawn from them, at the same time it makes our findings mainly descriptive rather than explanatory. The extent to which observed changes in registrations by smartwatches are connected to underlying physiological changes or are caused by other reasons is currently unknown. Therefore, caution should be exercised when assigning a physiological or medical interpretation to these changes.

This research presents the first experimental data connecting registrations from smartwatches to ground-truth TOD and indicates the potential of using smartwatch data in post-mortem investigations. Given our current level of understanding and the limitations discussed above, the method shows potential to provide information on the TOD and to serve as a complementary source of information when combined with other techniques. All of the above considerations suggest that more research needs to be conducted in order to obtain a more complete understanding of the value of smartwatches for death investigations.

Methods

Ethics approval

In the Netherlands, scientific research involving patients is subject to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (Dutch: Wet Medisch Wetenschappelijk Onderzoek, WMO) if there is an infringement of the physical and/or psychological integrity of the participant23. In those cases, the study must be reviewed by an accredited Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC). In cases where it is not clear immediately if the WMO applies to that study, an MREC can perform a review to establish if a study is subject to the WMO. For this research, the Research Ethics Committee of the Maxima Medisch Centrum reviewed the study protocol (reference N24.017) and subsequently confirmed that the WMO does not apply to this research meaning further official approval by the committee was not required. Nevertheless, due to potential ethical considerations, the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Director Science and Technology of the Netherlands Forensic Institute (reference 25-DIR-131). The study was conducted according to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

Participant recruitment

For inclusion in this study, patients had to be at least 18 years of age, have an approved euthanasia procedure scheduled, and had to be able to wear a smartwatch around their wrist.

In the Netherlands, physicians are required to notify forensic physicians of a planned euthanasia. It was at this stage, that information about the study was provided to the euthanizing physicians. Physicians who showed interest in the study were subsequently approached by the researcher. The physician was asked to make an assessment whether the patient might be willing to consider participation and if the patient was sound of mind and capable of making an informed decision regarding participation. If so, the patient was approached by their physician about the study. In case of any doubt, the patient was not approached. Information folders about the study were provided and patients could make the decision whether or not to participate. Informed consent was obtained from all patients who decided to participate in the study.

Digital methodology

Selected smartwatches

For this study, three types of smartwatches were selected: Garmin Vivoactive 5 (software version 10.20), Apple Watch 9 (WatchOS 10.2) and Samsung Watch 6 (Wear OS 4.0). Selection of these specific devices was made based on input from digital specialists from the Dutch police as to which devices were the most popular, widely used, and where there was a method for data extraction. For practical reasons and to reduce the possibility of irregularities due to individual device behavior, multiple identical devices were used in this study for each type of smartwatch.

Smartwatch configuration

Before each experiment, the smartwatch was fully reset to factory settings to avoid possible influence of previous experiments on device behaviour. For the same reason, data from previous experiments was removed from the paired mobile phones for the Apple and Samsung watches (see “Smartwatch data acquisition and - processing”). After factory reset, smartwatches were manually configured using a fixed protocol, which included switching off all device behaviour that could disturb the participant, such as visual or vibrational notifications or alarms. Measurements which needed to be started manually (e.g. workouts or ECG measurements) were not used, so that no further actions regarding the smartwatch were required from the participants or their families.

We used the default settings for the frequency of registrations of measurements by the smartwatches. Types of data collected by the devices were left as they were after the factory reset, with the exception that we enabled continuous measurement of oxygen saturation on the Garmin smartwatch and continuous measurement of Stress on the Samsung smartwatch to collect these potentially relevant data.

After configuration, time on the device was synchronized. For Apple and Samsung devices, this was done by connecting to the paired mobile phone, which was connected to a Wi-Fi network. For the Garmin device, time was synchronized through GPS using the Timesync option in the configuration menu.

Smartwatch data acquisition and - processing

For the Garmin Vivoactive 5 smartwatch, data was acquired directly from the smartwatch by connecting it to a computer, where via the Media Transfer Protocol (MTP) the watch appears as separate drive ‘Vivoactive 5’. All relevant data is in the folder named GARMIN, which was copied as a whole from the drive. From pilot experiments it follows that the subfolder GARMIN/Monitor contains files with information that is automatically collected by the smartwatch in proprietary Garmin .FIT format. Files in this folder were processed and converted to .csv files for further analysis using scripts developed for this study. Here, variable names were assigned, based on information from the software developer kit provided by Garmin and analysis of the Garmin Connect App.

As it is currently not possible to acquire health data directly from Apple Watch 9 and Samsung Watch 6 devices, these devices were paired to a mobile phone. For Apple smartwatches, an iPhone 11 (i0S 17.4) and an iPhone 15 (iOS 17.1) were used for this purpose and for Samsung smartwatches a Samsung Galaxy S22 (Android 13) and Samsung Galaxy S22+ (Android 12) were used. After pairing via Bluetooth, data from the smartwatches was synchronized with the mobile phones, where it was stored in databases connected to health-related apps. For iPhone this database is healthdb_secure.sqlite, related to the Apple Health App, and for Samsung telephones it is SecureHealthData.db, related to the Samsung Health App. After synchronization, full file system extractions of the mobile telephones were produced using commercial forensic tools and from these extractions the databases mentioned above were obtained. Data in the database SecureHealthData.db is encrypted and was therefore first decrypted using commercial forensic tools before further processing. Finally, information from the databases healthdb_secure.sqlite and SecureHealthData.db pertaining to the smartwatches was further processed and converted to .csv files for further analysis using scripts developed for this study. In processing and data conversion of Apple and Samsung devices, original variable names as assigned by the manufacturer were retained in the processed data.

Protocol during euthanasia

Participants were instructed to wear the smartwatch on the opposite arm to the one where the IV for drug administration was located. The euthanizing physician was asked to remove the smartwatch approximately half an hour after the participant’s death.

Statistical analysis

In this study, a power analysis could not be performed because reliable estimates of effect size were unavailable at the time of study design, given the exploratory nature of this research and non-existent literature on the topic.

To evaluate applicability for estimating TOD and time ranges basic descriptive statistical analysis was performed on the differences between the TLRs derived from the features identified as described in “Data analysis” and TOD, as determined by the physician. Mean, standard deviation and confidence interval for the mean were calculated.

Inferential statistical analysis focused on the difference (TLR-TOD) for each variable. A non-parametric Friedman test was used to test whether the differences have the same probability distribution across variables within a single brand of smartwatch if there are at least three features. Only in case the overall test result was significant, post-hoc analysis was done with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples without correction for multiple comparisons. To compare differences (TLR-TOD) between devices, for each device the feature having the lowest mean was selected and a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallice test was performed. In case of a significant result, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were made using a Mann-Whitney U test without correction for multiple comparisons.

We used a confidence level of 95% for all intervals and tests.

Because of the small sample size, it was decided not to perform statistical tests aimed at investigating certain effects of post-mortem movements, medical conditions and physical characteristics on the variables.
