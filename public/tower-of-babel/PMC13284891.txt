On and Off‐Ramps: Residents Transferring Into and Out of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery
Authors: Angela Renne, Francis Deng, Jenny X Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo examine the characteristics of residents who transferred into and out of otolaryngology–head and neck surgery (OHNS) residency programs in the middle of their graduate medical training, we conducted a cross‐sectional study of 2019–2023 data from the Association of American Medical Colleges. During this 5‐year period, out of 1656 total applications, 58 residents transferred into OHNS (3.5%). Most residents originated from general surgery (n = 51, 87.9%). Among these, no applicants for whom we had information on their original applications (n = 52) had initially applied to OHNS exclusively, but all had applied to OHNS as one of multiple specialties. During this time, 13 residents transferred out of OHNS (0.8%); all residents transferred into non‐surgical specialties including anesthesiology (30.8%) and internal medicine (30.8%). We conclude that those who transferred into OHNS held pre‐existing interests in the field whereas those who transferred out may desire non‐surgical practices. These findings highlight the need to support alternative on and off‐ramps for OHNS training.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: career change, graduate medical education, match outcomes, physician workforce, residency transfer, switching specialties

Introduction

Otolaryngology–head and neck surgery (OHNS) is a competitive specialty, attracting applicants with strong academic and clinical performance [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Despite this competitiveness, residents may still enter or leave the specialty after beginning graduate medical training. Prior studies have characterized OHNS attrition [7, 8], but less is known about residents who transfer into OHNS from other fields or leave OHNS to pursue alternative career paths. This study evaluates recent trends in transitions into and out of OHNS training to better understand the specialty's on‐ and off‐ramps and identify opportunities for trainee support.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study using the AAMC ERAS Applicant Dataset linked to GME Track [9]. GME Track records each resident's initial and most recent specialty (as of November 12, 2024) and assigns a unique identifier to each applicant. This secondary analysis of deidentified data was exempt from the Johns Hopkins IRB.

The study population included residents who either initially matched into OHNS or transferred into OHNS from 2019 to 2023. Residents were categorized as transferring out (matched into OHNS and later switched) or transferring in (matched elsewhere and later entered OHNS). Application year and specialty entry year distinguished initial from subsequent matches. Descriptive statistics summarized characteristics of residents who transferred into or out of OHNS.

Results

From 2019 to 2023, 1656 residents entered OHNS. Thirteen residents left the specialty (0.8%) and 58 transferred in (3.5%), yielding a 4.5:1 ratio of transfers into compared with transfers out.

Residents Transferring Into OHNS

Most residents transferring into OHNS came from general surgery (n = 51, 87.9%), with smaller numbers from transitional year programs (n = 3, 5.2%), internal medicine (n = 2, 3.4%), integrated plastic surgery (n = 1, 1.7%), and anesthesiology (n = 1, 1.7%) (Table 1).

Application data were available for 52 residents; all had initially applied to OHNS, although none applied exclusively. Over half (n = 29, 55.8%) applied to both OHNS and general surgery, and a few paired OHNS with internal medicine or anesthesiology (n = 2 each, 3.8%). Nineteen residents (36.5%) applied to OHNS and two or more additional specialties. Transfer‐in residents were more likely to have trained at international medical schools compared to directly matched OHNS residents (6.9% vs. 1.3%, p = 0.001), with otherwise similar sex, race, and age distributions.

Residents Leaving OHNS

Across the same period, 13 residents transferred out of OHNS (Table 1). The most common destinations were anesthesiology and internal medicine (n = 4 each, 30.8%), followed by family medicine (n = 2, 15.4%). One resident each transferred to emergency medicine, psychiatry, and public health/general preventive medicine.

Application data were available for 11 residents. Seven (63.6%) had applied exclusively to OHNS, while four had cross‐applied to other specialties, including internal medicine, family medicine, psychiatry, and obstetrics/gynecology (one each).

Discussion

Although relatively few residents leave OHNS each year, post‐match transfers represent a pathway that new trainees enter the specialty. All residents who transferred in had previously cross‐applied to OHNS, suggesting that interest often begins early and that entry requires planning [4, 5, 6]. For educators, this supports continued engagement with trainees with sustained OHNS interest.

Residents transferring out of OHNS merit attention given the potential implications for trainee well‐being, workforce planning, and selection processes. Residents may leave due to evolving interests, lifestyle considerations, mentorship experiences, or increasing clarity about career goals. Importantly, all residents who transferred out entered non‐surgical specialties, suggesting that misalignment—if present—may relate more broadly to surgical practice rather than OHNS specifically. This pattern raises the possibility that “compassionate off‐ramps” including exposure to alternative career pathways such as medical OHNS–adjacent roles could better support resident well‐being [10, 11, 12]. More broadly, OHNS programs—like much of GME—may be affected by the longstanding “failure to fail” phenomenon, suggesting a need for supportive mechanisms to allow residents to explore alternative career paths when appropriate [13, 14] While causation cannot be inferred, this national descriptive analysis provides a framework for future qualitative research on specialty‐switching and career decision‐making.

Limitations

This study has limitations; its retrospective, descriptive design precludes causal inference. The dataset included only residents who applied through ERAS and recorded in GME Track from 2019 to 2023, limiting access to complete application histories. The number of transfers was small, restricting subgroup analyses. Because the analysis spanned 5 years, residents were followed for unequal durations, and some may still leave the specialty. PGY level could not be reliably determined, preventing assessment of transition timing. Finally, we lacked qualitative data on motivation, satisfaction, program culture, and mentorship, which may influence specialty‐switching [15, 16].

Conclusion

Most residents transferring into OHNS had previously applied to the specialty, reinforcing the role of early interest in shaping career trajectories. Residents leaving OHNS consistently transitioned into non‐surgical specialties, suggesting a need for proactive mentorship for trainees reassessing career fit. These findings may inform recruitment strategies and the development of supportive pathways for career realignment in graduate medical education.
