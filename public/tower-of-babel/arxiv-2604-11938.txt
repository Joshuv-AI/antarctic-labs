Sampling Colorings Close to the Maximum Degree: Non-Markovian Coupling and Local Uniformity
Vishesh Jain, Clayton Mizgerd, Eric Vigoda

Abstract
. Sampling graph colorings via local Markov chains is a central problem in approximate counting and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). We address the problem of sampling a random k k -coloring of a graph with maximum degree Δ \Delta . The simplest algorithmic approach is to establish rapid mixing of the single-site update chain known as the Metropolis Glauber dynamics, which at each step chooses a random vertex v v and proposes a random color c c , recoloring v v to c c if the resulting coloring remains proper. It is a long-standing open problem to prove that the Glauber dynamics has polynomial mixing time on all graphs whenever k ≥ Δ + 2 k\geq\Delta+2 . We prove that for every δ > 0 \delta>0 and all Δ ≥ Δ 0 ​ ( δ ) \Delta\geq\Delta_{0}(\delta) , if k ≥ ( 1 + δ ) ​ Δ k\geq(1+\delta)\Delta then the Glauber dynamics has optimal mixing time of O δ ​ ( | V | ​ log ⁡ | V | ) O_{\delta}(|V|\log|V|) on any graph of girth ≥ 11 \geq 11 and maximum degree Δ \Delta . Our approach builds on a non-Markovian coupling introduced by Hayes and Vigoda (2003) for the large-degree regime Δ = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Delta=\Omega(\log n) , in which updates at time t t may depend on and modify proposed updates at future times. A complete analysis of this framework requires resolving substantial technical obstacles that remain in the original argument, and extending it to the constant-degree regime introduces further difficulties, since non-Markovian updates may fail with constant probability. We overcome these obstacles by developing and analyzing a refined local non-Markovian coupling, and by establishing new local-uniformity results for the Metropolis dynamics, extending prior results for the heat-bath chain due to Hayes (2013). Together, these ingredients provide a complete analysis of the non-Markovian coupling framework in the large-degree regime, while simultaneously strengthening it substantially to obtain optimal mixing all the way down to the constant-degree setting.



1.
Introduction

A problem of long-standing interest in the fields of approximate counting and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is estimating the number of
k
-colorings of a graph
G=(V,E)
of maximum degree
\Delta
. Since the seminal work of Jerrum
[
15
]
, it has been an outstanding open problem to efficiently sample
k
-colorings when
k
is close to
\Delta
. Existing results achieve such guarantees only under strong structural assumptions, such as large girth (the length of the shortest cycle) or when
\Delta
grows with
n=|V|
. Progress on sampling
k
-colorings has also served as a driving force for the development of new algorithms for approximate counting and new techniques for analyzing the mixing times of MCMC algorithms. In this paper we make significant progress on sampling
k
-colorings when
k
is close to the maximum degree
\Delta
.

Throughout this paper, let
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
, and
a
k
-coloring is a proper vertex coloring
\chi:V\to[k]
such that for all
\{v,w\}\in E
,
\chi(v)\neq\chi(w)
.
For a graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
k\geq 2
, let

\Omega
denote the set of
k
-colorings of
G
, and let
\pi
denote the uniform distribution over
\Omega
.

In the approximate counting problem we are aiming for a fully-polynomial randomized approximation scheme (
\mathsf{FPRAS}
) for estimating
|\Omega|
. In the corresponding approximate sampling problem, which we study in this paper,
we are given as input a graph
G=(V,E)
of maximum degree
\Delta
, an integer
k\geq 2
, and an error parameter
\varepsilon>0
, and our goal is to sample a
k
-coloring from a distribution
\mu

which is within total variation distance
\varepsilon
of the uniform distribution
\pi
over
\Omega
, in time polynomial in
n=|V|
and
\log(1/\varepsilon)
; this then yields an
\mathsf{FPRAS}
for the approximate counting problem (e.g., see
[
18
,
13
,
16
]
).

The simplest and most widely studied approach for sampling colorings is the Glauber dynamics, which is the single-site update Markov chain. In this paper we analyze the Metropolis version of the Glauber dynamics, which we simply refer to as the Glauber dynamics.

Definition 1.1

(Discrete-time Metropolis Glauber Dynamics)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
k\geq 2
be the number of colors. Let
X_{0}
be an arbitrary proper
k
-coloring of
G
.
The dynamics evolves as a Markov chain
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
:

(1)

At each time step
t
, choose a vertex
u\in V
uniformly at random and a candidate color
c\in[k]
uniformly at random.

(2)

Define the candidate configuration
X^{\prime}
by

X^{\prime}(w)=\begin{cases}c&\text{if }w=u,\\
X_{t}(w)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

If
X^{\prime}
is a proper coloring, set
X_{t+1}=X^{\prime}
; else, set
X_{t+1}=X_{t}
.

It is straightforward to verify that the Glauber dynamics is ergodic whenever
k\geq\Delta+2
. Since the transitions are symmetric, the unique stationary distribution is
\pi
, the uniform distribution over
\Omega
.

Remark 1.2
.

An alternative version of the Glauber dynamics is the heat-bath Glauber dynamics. The main difference between the heat-bath and Metropolis dynamics is in the update probability. The heat-bath chain selects a color uniformly from the
currently available
colors
A(X_{t},v):=[k]\setminus\{X_{t}(w):w\in N(v)\}
, guaranteeing a valid move. In contrast, the Metropolis process attempts any color in
[k]
.

The mixing time
T_{\mathrm{mix}}
is the minimum number of steps, from the worst initial state
X_{0}
, to ensure that the distribution of
X_{T_{\mathrm{mix}}}
is within total variation distance
1/4
of the stationary distribution. Hayes and Sinclair
[
10
]
proved that for every constant
\Delta
, for any graph of maximum degree
\Delta
the mixing time is
\Omega(n\log n)
.
Therefore we say the Glauber dynamics has optimal mixing time when
T_{\mathrm{mix}}=O(n\log n)
.

The first major progress on sampling
k
-colorings was by Jerrum
[
15
]
who proved
O(n\log n)
mixing time of the Glauber dynamics whenever
k>2\Delta
. This was improved by Vigoda
[

]
to

, and further improved to

by
[

,
3

2

using a more general Markov chain known as the flip dynamics; this is currently the best poly-time sampling result which applies for general graphs.

Further progress on sampling
k
-colorings typically relies either on strong structural assumptions (such as large girth and constant
\Delta
) or applies only when
k/\Delta
is large. We briefly review the most relevant prior results.
Chen, Liu, Mani, and Moitra
[
5

proved that for every
constant

\Delta
and all

, for any graph with girth
\geq g(\Delta)
the Glauber dynamics has

mixing time. Regarding the girth requirement in
[

]
, in the simpler case when
k\geq(1+\delta)\Delta
, the required girth grows at least as
C(\delta)\log^{2}\Delta
.
Other notable results include polynomial mixing time of the Glauber dynamics when

for all
\Delta\geq 3
for triangle-free graphs
[
4
,
8
,
14
]
using spectral independence and a result of Dyer, Frieze, Hayes, and Vigoda
[

which establishes optimal mixing time of the Glauber dynamics when either:

and girth
\geq 5
, or

and girth

for earlier results in this direction.

Most closely related to our work, Hayes and Vigoda
[

In particular, our work requires revisiting the construction of the bounding chain,

and how these components are combined in the analysis.
In addition, we extend the local uniformity results of Hayes
[
12

to the Metropolis Glauber dynamics, which is also required but not established in
[

]
.
Furthermore, extending the Hayes–Vigoda non-Markovian coupling to the constant-degree regime introduces additional challenges in both the definition and analysis of the coupling.

Our main result establishes optimal mixing of the Glauber dynamics

.

For every

, there exist constants

1.1.
Technical Overview

A standard approach to proving rapid mixing of the Glauber dynamics is via path coupling
[
1
]
.
Consider a pair of colorings
X_{t},Y_{t}
that differ at a single vertex
z
. Let
c_{X}=X_{t}(z)
and
c_{Y}=Y_{t}(z)
denote the disagree colors. Under the identity coupling we update the same vertex
v_{t}
with the same attempted color
c_{t}
in both chains. For each
w\in N(z)
, there are at most two color choices which can create a new disagreement.
Since there are at least
k-\Delta
colors that can successfully recolor
z
in both chains, while each neighbor
w\in N(z)
can create at most two new disagreements, contraction requires
k-\Delta>2\Delta
, which yields rapid mixing when
k>3\Delta
.

In Jerrum’s coupling, for
v_{t}\in N(z)
, we pair color
c_{X}
in one chain with
c_{Y}
in the other chain. All other
(v_{t},c)
updates are coupled using the identity coupling.
Now each neighbor can create at most one new disagreement (from color
c_{Y}
in
X_{t}
and
c_{X}
in
Y_{t}
). Therefore, we need
k-\Delta>\Delta
, which yields Jerrum’s
k>2\Delta
bound.

Subsequent improvements rely on first burning-in the chains to avoid worst-case configurations, as utilized in
[
7
,
17
,
11
,
6
]
. In a star graph on
\Delta+1
vertices with center vertex
v
, the expected number of available colors for
v
in a random coloring is
\approx k\exp(-\Delta/k)
, since a color does not appear in the neighborhood with probability
(1-1/k)^{\Delta}\approx\exp(-\Delta/k)
. In fact, Dyer and Frieze
[
7
]
showed such a lower bound on the expected number of available colors for any triangle-free graph.
This heuristic leads to the condition
k\exp(-\Delta/k)>\Delta
, which holds for
k>1.764\Delta
.
This forms the basis of rapid mixing results for graphs of girth at least
5
; see
[
7
,
11
,
6
]
.
The additional girth assumption ensures that these estimates hold for the burned-in dynamics, rather than only in the stationary distribution.

Molloy
[
17
]
, and subsequently Hayes
[
11
,
12
]
, further improved this bound by proving stronger burn-in properties on the distance-2 neighborhood of a vertex (and hence further requiring girth
\geq 7
). In particular, for a neighbor
w\in N(z)
of the disagreement, by considering the probability that both colors
c_{X}
and
c_{Y}
appear in
N(w)\setminus\{z\}
, and hence there is no bad update for
w
, we obtain the bound
k\exp(-\Delta/k)>\Delta(1-\exp(-\Delta/k))^{2}
, which holds for
k>1.489\Delta
.

The remaining obstacle in the near-critical regime is handling singly-blocked configurations. Suppose that the disagree color
c_{Y}
appears in
N(w)\setminus\{z\}
, and the other disagree color
c_{X}
does not appear.
Then the coupled update for
w
which attempts color
c_{Y}
in
X_{t}
and color
c_{X}
in
Y_{t}
is blocked in
X_{t}
but succeeds in
Y_{t}
, creating a new disagreement.

Suppose instead we could modify the coloring of
Y_{t}
on
N(w)\setminus\{z\}
so that
c_{X}
appears and
c_{Y}
does not (which is the opposite of
X_{t}
). Then the same coupled update would be blocked in both chains, preventing this disagreement.
Consequently, a new disagreement at
w
can only occur if
c_{Y}
does not appear in
N(w)\setminus\{z\}
in
X_{t}
(and symmetrically for
c_{X}
in
Y_{t}
).
The coupling then requires
k\exp(-\Delta/k)>\Delta\exp(-\Delta/k)
, which holds for
k>\Delta
.

The goal of the non-Markovian coupling is to realize this ideal:
when
w\in N(z)
is singly blocked for one of the disagree colors in
X_{t}
then we’ll couple the update sequence for
Y_{t}
(at earlier times) so that it is singly blocked for the other disagree color. The challenge is ensuring the disagreements we introduce to obtain this do not propagate; this requires looking at and modifying updates at future times, hence the non-Markovian aspect of the coupling (see
Fig.

2
for an illustration.)

To implement this strategy, we first bring the chains into a well-behaved regime via a burn-in phase,
after which the refined coupling can be applied effectively.

Figure 1.
This chain is following the Jerrum coupling. Vertex
v_{t}
will attempt (blue/red) in the upper and lower chains respectively, leaving it as a (gray/red) discrepancy after time
t
.

Figure 2.
This chain is following the non-Markovian coupling. By changing past updates, when
v_{t}
attempts (blue/red) it will remain (gray/gray). We have prevented this discrepancy at the cost of introducing two “temporary” discrepancies in the neighborhood which we have cleverly chosen so that they will be fixed. The color “yellow” is somewhat arbitrary; it encodes that green was the original color of the other vertex to ensure validity of the coupling.

Stage I: Burn-in Phase:

To couple a pair
(X_{0},Y_{0})
that differ at a single vertex
z
, we first run a burn-in phase of
O(n\log{\Delta})
steps.
The burn-in phase transforms worst-case initial colorings into configurations that are locally close to the stationary distribution; this is formalized via local uniformity.
During this phase, we show that the initial disagreement at
z
does not spread beyond a ball of radius
\Delta^{c}
for a small constant
c\in(0,1/10)
,
allowing us, via a union bound, to conclude that all vertices in this local ball satisfy local uniformity with high probability. A key aspect here is that for a fixed (sufficiently large) time and a fixed vertex, local uniformity properties hold with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(\Delta))
, which is what permits us to take various union bounds over time and space. This aspect is also utilized in
[
6
]
.

One of our key technical ingredients is establishing local uniformity for the Metropolis Glauber dynamics.
A local uniformity result shows that after a suitable burn-in period, the distribution of colors in the neighborhood of every vertex is close to the distribution induced by a random coloring of a tree rooted at that vertex.

Previously, Hayes
[
12
]
established an analogous local uniformity result for the heat-bath Glauber dynamics. Hayes’s proof utilizes the similarity of the heat-bath dynamics to the stationary distribution, and it was unclear how to extend his proof approach to the Metropolis dynamics.
In particular, in the heat-bath dynamics the vertex update probabilities are independent of the current coloring, whereas in the Metropolis dynamics the probability that a proposed recoloring succeeds depends on the number of available colors at the chosen vertex. Controlling this dependency on the current configuration is the central difficulty in establishing the burn-in result. We discuss this in a bit more detail in
Section

1.2
.

We state here a simplified version of our burn-in result,
and refer the interested reader to
Theorem

5.2
for the general statement.
For a coloring
X_{t}
, and a vertex
v
, let

A(X_{t},v):=[k]\setminus X_{t}(N(v))

denote the set of
available colors
. The following result shows that after a suitable burn-in period, the number of available colors is determined only by the degree of
v
. The burn-in result requires girth
g\geq 7
as in Hayes
[
12
]
, where the girth is the number of edges in the shortest cycle.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon),C=C(\delta)
such that for all
\Delta>\Delta_{0}
,
k\geq(1+\delta)\Delta
, and for any graph
G=(V,E)
of maximum degree
\Delta
and girth
g\geq 7

we have the following. Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the discrete-time Metropolis Glauber dynamics. Fix
v\in V
. Suppose
T\geq n
and
T_{0}\geq Cn\log\Delta
. Then,

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exists t\in[T_{0},T_{0}+T]:\big||A(X_{t},v)|-ke^{-d(v)/k}\big|>\varepsilon k}}\right]\leq\frac{T}{n}e^{-\Delta/C}.

Before proceeding, let us interpret the conclusion of
Theorem

1.4
. As noted earlier, in a star graph the expected number of available colors for the center vertex
v
is
\approx k\exp(-d(v)/k)
.
(
1.1
)
shows that the number of available colors at
v
is sharply concentrated around this quantity.

The refinement of
Theorem

1.4
stated in
Theorem

5.2

establishes a finer-grained description of the distribution
of colors in the neighborhood of a vertex
v
. The probability that a neighbor
w\in N(v)
has exactly
i_{1}
neighbors colored
c_{1}
and
i_{2}
neighbors colored
c_{2}
is close to a Poisson distribution,
which shows that correlations between colors in the neighborhood are negligible.

Remark 1.5
.

Hayes
[
12
]
established the analog of
Theorem

1.4
(and the more general,
Theorem

5.2
) for the heat-bath dynamics.
The result of Hayes and Vigoda
[
9
]
relied on a local uniformity result for Metropolis Glauber dynamics; however Hayes’s proof approach does not extend to the Metropolis dynamics.

Stage II: Non-Markovian Coupling:

To translate local uniformity into rapid mixing, we refine the non-Markovian coupling introduced by Hayes and Vigoda
[
9
]
. We provide a high-level overview of the coupling here, and identify the key differences from
[
9
]
in the subsequent subsection
Section

1.2
.

Fix a pair
X_{0},Y_{0}
that differ only at a single vertex
z
. Let
{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
denote the coupling time, and
let

{\vec{\sigma}}=((v_{1},c_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}))

denote the update sequence defining the evolution from
X_{0}
to
X_{1},\dots,X_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}
. Thus, in the
transition
X_{t-1}\rightarrow X_{t}
we recolor
v_{t}
to color
c_{t}
if
c_{t}\notin X_{t-1}(N(v_{t}))
.

Our coupling defines a coupled update sequence:

F({\vec{\sigma}})={\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}=((v_{1},c^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c^{\prime}_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}})),

which will define the evolution from
Y_{0}
to
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}
. Notice that the vertex updates are the same for both chains, only the color updates possibly differ. We will ensure that
F
is bijective and hence this is a valid coupling.

We can now state our main coupling result, which we present in a slightly informal manner here in the introduction, and we refer the interested reader to a more formal statement in
Section

2
, which is presented after presenting the necessary definitions.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\delta>0
, and let
G=(V,E)
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
and girth
\geq 11
, where
\Delta>\Delta_{0}(\delta)
. Let
k\geq(1+\delta)\Delta
.
Consider a pair of colorings
X_{0},Y_{0}
which differ at a single vertex
z
and
which are “burned in” in the ball of radius
\Delta^{1/10}

around
z
.
Then, for
{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}=O_{\delta}(n)
,
there exists a
{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
-step coupling of the Glauber dynamics where

\mathbb{E}[|X_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\oplus Y_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}|]\leq 1/3.

A more precise version of
Theorem

1.6
is stated in
Theorem

2.4
in
Section

2
, where the coupling is formally defined and the requisite burn-in properties are formalized. We provide a high-level description of the coupling here.

We construct our global coupling
F
by composing local non-Markovian couplings.
For each time
1\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
we define a local coupling
f_{t}
which may modify updates at multiple times, both earlier and later than
t
.
Because these transformations act beyond the current step, the resulting construction is referred to as a non-Markovian coupling.

The high-level idea of the non-Markovian coupling is as follows. Suppose there is an update of vertex
v_{t}
at time
t
which potentially propagates a disagreement. We will ensure our disagreements propagate in a tree-like manner (in order to apply our non-Markovian coupling) and hence there will be a unique neighbor
p\in N(v_{t})
which differs. Let
\{c_{b},c_{u}\}=\{X_{t-1}(p),Y_{t-1}(p)\}
denote the disagree colors. The non-Markovian coupling will apply in the case where one of the disagree colors, say
c_{b}
, appears in
X_{t-1}(N(v_{t})\setminus\{p\})
, and hence is a blocked color; additionally, the other color
c_{u}
does not appear among the neighbors
N(v_{t})\setminus\{p\}
, and hence is an unblocked color.

In our non-Markovian coupling we create a pair of temporary disagreements in the neighborhood of
v_{t}
so that in chain
Y_{t-1}
the color
c_{u}
appears and
c_{b}
no longer appears (and hence in
Y_{t}
color
c_{u}
is now blocked and
c_{b}
is unblocked, thus the chain
Y_{t}
has the opposite behavior as
X_{t}
with respect to this pair of colors). Consequently, the coupled update
c_{b}
in
X_{t}
and
c_{u}
in
Y_{t}
is blocked in both chains, thereby preventing a disagreement that would occur under the standard coupling; this is the key mechanism enabling contraction in the near-critical regime, as outlined earlier.

Creating these temporary disagreements in a bijective manner is non-trivial.
Moreover, we must ensure that these temporary disagreements do not propagate.
We defer the detailed construction to
Section

2.1
.

Our construction differs significantly from Hayes and Vigoda
[
9
]
, where the non-Markovian coupling is applied either at all relevant times or not at all.
When
\Delta
is constant, a non-Markovian update may fail (i.e., cease to be bijective) with constant probability.
Consequently, our global coupling which is the composition of local couplings at each time
t
,
decides for each relevant time whether to apply the local non-Markovian coupling (when it is well-defined), or use the identity/Jerrum coupling if not.

We discuss some more aspects of our construction and analysis, and how they contrast with
[
9
]
in the next subsection.

1.2.
Comparison with previous work

We highlight several key issues in the construction and analysis of

[
9
]
and explain how our work addresses them.

Bounding chain

To apply our non-Markovian coupling we need to ensure the associated updates are sufficiently disjoint from each other. To achieve that we define a bounding chain
Z_{t}
(
Section

2.2
) where each vertex has a subset of colors at each time,
capturing the possible colors of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
under all coupling choices. The set of vertices with more than one color at a time, denoted as
\mathcal{P}
for
potential persistent
disagreements
, will be a superset of the disagreements between
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
(excluding the temporary disagreements introduced during the non-Markovian coupling).

Hayes–Vigoda utilize a different bounding chain, where the color sets
|Z_{t}(v)|
are of size at most
2
. However, for the bounding chain to be sufficiently powerful to capture all potentially propagating disagreements, one must allow the color set sizes
|Z_{t}(v)|
to grow, which we incorporate in our construction.
This presents an additional obstacle for maintaining that
|\mathcal{P}|
is sufficiently small with high probability (see
Proposition

4.2
).

Validity of the coupling

A central requirement of the non-Markovian framework is that the global
coupling map is bijective. In Hayes and Vigoda
[
9
]
,
this property is argued via a more intricate global construction.
In contrast, our formulation yields a simpler and more transparent validity
argument: by composing local transformations that are each explicitly
bijective, we obtain a global coupling whose bijectivity follows directly.
This local structure is also essential for handling failures of individual
non-Markovian updates in the constant-degree regime. See
Section

3
for details.

Measurability and drift analysis

Our aim is to analyze the expected Hamming distance at time
t
given the coupled updates at all times prior to
t
. However the coupling is looking ahead (and potentially modifying updates) at times after
t
; this appears somewhat contradictory. To overcome this, in Hayes–Vigoda they condition on the event that all non-Markovian updates succeed.
However, the coupling in Hayes–Vigoda restricts non-Markovian updates to a smaller time interval
{T_{\mathrm{m}}}=O(n)
around the particular update, which leads to higher failure probability of non-Markovian updates than claimed in
[
9
, Theorem 17]
; the subsequent drift analysis is therefore not complete as stated.

We rectify these issues as a byproduct of our improved coupling, which eliminates the smaller
{T_{\mathrm{m}}}
window, and our enhanced analysis; see
Section

4
for details.

Local uniformity

Let us briefly describe the proof overview of
Theorem

1.4
(and its more formal version
Theorem

5.2
) which shows that after a burn-in period, the Metropolis dynamics exhibits certain local uniformity properties.
Namely, we show that the distribution of colors in the neighborhood of a vertex
is well approximated for certain relevant statistics by the distribution induced by a random coloring of a tree
rooted at that vertex.

Similar results were proved for the heat-bath dynamics by Hayes
[
12
]
. We largely follow the proof strategy of
[
12
]
. The main strategy is to use the continuous time dynamics, fix a distinguished vertex
v
, and switch to a directed graph
G^{*}=G_{\mathrm{in}}(v,3)
(see
Definition

5.12
)
in which edges within
B_{3}(v)
are oriented towards
v
and all other edges are bidirected.
Then, conditioning on
\mathcal{F}
, the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the configuration outside
B_{2}(v)
, the colors of the vertices
w\in N(v)
become independent.
As in
[
12
]
, this auxiliary process allows us to analyze the local behavior around a fixed vertex
v
, after which we compare it to the original Glauber dynamics via a coupling argument.

However, analyzing the Metropolis dynamics introduces an additional difficulty.
For the continuous-time version of the heat-bath dynamics, each vertex
u
has a Poisson clock of rate
1
, and when its clock ticks it chooses a uniform random color in
A(X_{t},u)
. For the Metropolis dynamics,
we instead give each vertex
u
a Poisson clock of rate
|A(X_{t},u)|/k
, and when the clock ticks we choose a uniform random color in
A(X_{t},u)
; notice that this is the same process as the Metropolis dynamics but corresponds to ignoring rejected proposals.
However, information about the Poisson ringing of
u

reveals information about the configuration in
N(u)
.
To circumvent this dependency, we instead introduce another auxiliary process in which
u
has a Poisson clock of rate
\mathbb{E}[|A(X_{t},u)|\mid\mathcal{F}]/k
.
Under this auxiliary process, the events we analyze become conditionally independent given
\mathcal{F}
.
The auxiliary process can then be coupled with the standard Metropolis dynamics using Poisson thinning.

This auxiliary process allows us to recover the conditional independence structure used in Hayes’s analysis.
Using this framework, we establish the concentration bounds on
|A(X_{t},u)|
stated in
Theorem

1.4
(and more generally in
Theorem

5.2
), see
Remark

5.9
for more detailed discussion.
Similar auxiliary processes are also used to obtain conditional independence for various properties of our non-Markovian couplings; see
Section

6
.

Girth requirement.

The local uniformity argument requires tree-like structure up to radius
3
(which translates to girth
g\geq 7
),
while the coupling analysis requires that the discrepancy region and its

2
-neighborhood remain acyclic (see
Remark

4.5
).
These constraints combine to yield the girth

\geq 11
condition in our main result, namely
Theorem

1.3
.

The goal of this section is to construct the appropriate coupling. In

In order to apply path coupling with respect to Hamming distance for colorings, we need to expand the state space as is done in all prior works in the literature using path coupling for colorings. We consider the auxiliary state space:

\widehat{\Omega}:=[k]^{V},

which consists of all labelings, not only the (proper) colorings. The Metropolis update rule extends in a straightforward manner to
\widehat{\Omega}

and color

uniformly at random. For all

X_{t+1}(u)=\begin{cases}c&\mbox{if }c\notin X_{t}(N(u))\\
X_{t}(u)&\mbox{if }c\in X_{t}(N(u))\end{cases}

Notice that if
X_{t}\in\Omega
(i.e.,

is a proper coloring) then

\Omega
is a closed class for the chain on

\Omega
is exactly the original Metropolis Glauber dynamics, which has the uniform distribution on
\Omega

\Omega

\widehat{\Omega}
is used only in the path-coupling argument.

2.1.
Coupling Overview

Fix a pair of labelings
X_{0},Y_{0}\in\widehat{\Omega}
that differ only at a single vertex
z^{*}
.
Let
{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
denote the coupling time from
Theorem

2.4
.
Let

{\vec{\sigma}}=((v_{1},c_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}))

denote the update sequence defining
X_{0},\dots,X_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}
.
Thus, in the
transition
X_{t-1}\rightarrow X_{t}
we recolor
v_{t}
to color
c_{t}
if
c_{t}\notin X_{t-1}(N(v_{t}))
.

Given
X_{0},Y_{0}
and the update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}
, we will define a coupled update sequence:

{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}=((v_{1},c^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c^{\prime}_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}})),

which will define
Y_{0},\dots,Y_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}
. Thus the two chains always update the same vertex
v_{t}
at time
t
; only the
proposed colors may differ.

Before defining
{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}
, we first construct an auxiliary process

Z_{t}
, called the bounding chain (
Section

2.2
). This process depends only on

X_{0},Y_{0}
, and the original sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}
.
It is not itself a labeling: rather, for each vertex
v
, the value
Z_{t}(v)
is a set of colors, i.e.

Z_{t}(v)\subseteq[k]
.
The role of
Z_{t}
is to identify the region where disagreements between the two
chains may persist. More precisely, once the coupling has been defined, we will
show that (see
Proposition

3.9
)

\{X_{t}(v),Y_{t}(v)\}\subseteq Z_{t}(v)

for every vertex
v
and every time
t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
, except for the temporary
disagreements that are intentionally created during certain non-Markovian edits.
Consequently, apart from these temporary disagreements, any actual disagreement
between
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
can occur only at a vertex
v
with
|Z_{t}(v)|>1
.

This motivates the definition

\mathcal{P}_{t}:=\{v\in V:\ |Z_{t}(v)|>1\},

and we write

\mathcal{P}:=\bigcup_{t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\mathcal{P}_{t},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{\leq t}=\bigcup_{s\leq t}\mathcal{P}_{s}.

The set
\mathcal{P}_{t}
should be viewed as a set of
potential persistent
disagreements
at time
t
. The key point is that
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is determined before we define
the coupled sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}
: it is built only from
X_{0},Y_{0}
, and the update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}
, and it is designed to dominate the disagreements in every coupling
that we will later allow (again, ignoring the temporary disagreements deliberately introduced
inside a non-Markovian edit.)

The geometry of
\mathcal{P}
determines whether we attempt the non-Markovian
construction. If the induced subgraph on
\mathcal{P}
together with its local
neighborhood contains a cycle, then we do not use any non-trivial edits: we
simply take the identity coupling at all times, i.e.,

{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}={\vec{\sigma}}.

If, on the other hand, the subgraph is tree-like,
then we use a non-trivial coupling. Formally, later (
Section

2.4
) we will track a set
D_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{t}
of actual persistent
disagreements. A time
t
is potentially propagating if the updated vertex

v_{t}
lies on the boundary of the current disagreement set, say, with unique
neighbor
p\in D_{t-1}
, and the proposed color
c_{t}
is exactly the disagreeing
color across that edge.
In this situation the disagreement at
p
can spread to
v_{t}
. These are the times at which we
consider a non-Markovian edit. In particular, they are among the times when
the bounding chain indicates that the set of potential disagreements may grow.

At all other times we use simpler local couplings. If
v_{t}
is far from the
current disagreement set, or if
v_{t}
already belongs to it, then we use the
identity coupling. If
v_{t}
is adjacent to the current disagreement set but the
update is not potentially propagating, then we use the standard one-step
Jerrum coupling at time
t
; this modifies only the color choice at time
t

and leaves all other times unchanged. Even at a potentially propagating time,
we apply the non-Markovian map only when an additional local well-definedness
condition is satisfied; otherwise we again fall back to Jerrum’s coupling.

In summary, the full coupling will be defined as a composition of local maps
f_{t}
.
There are three kinds of local maps to keep in mind. The first is the
identity coupling, which leaves the color update unchanged. The second is a
standard one-step coupling, namely Jerrum’s coupling, where the coupled color
choice at time
t
is determined from the update at time
t
and the past
history. The third is our non-Markovian coupling: at certain exceptional times,
f_{t}
reads (and writes to) more coordinates than just
c_{t}
, so we might change the color choices
c_{t^{\prime}}
for times
t^{\prime}\neq t
and some of these times might be in the future
t^{\prime}>t
. However, none of these
local maps ever changes the vertex-update sequence
(v_{t})
.

We will define the full coupling

F=F_{X_{0},Y_{0}}:(V\times[k])^{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\to(V\times[k])^{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}

by composing these local maps at the appropriate times.

2.2.
Constructing the bounding chain

To apply our non-Markovian coupling, we want to ensure that the set of vertices involved in the non-Markovian updates induce an essentially tree-like subgraph. To this end, we will now introduce a “bounding chain” that defines a set
\mathcal{P}
which is a superset of all discrepancies that will occur between the subsequently coupled chains
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
(ignoring temporary disagreements). We will then define a predicate
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})
which is
\tt{True}
if certain conditions are satisfied (roughly, the discrepancy region
\mathcal{P}
and its local neighborhood evolve in a tree-like manner), and is
\tt{False}
otherwise.

Let
X_{0},Y_{0}\in\widehat{\Omega}
where
X_{0}\oplus Y_{0}=\{z^{*}\}

for some
z^{*}\in V
, and let
{\vec{\sigma}}={\vec{\sigma}}_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}=((v_{1},c_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}))

denote the update sequence defining
X_{0},\dots,X_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}
. For each such
X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}}
, we will define a bounding chain
(Z_{t})
where for all
v\in V,0\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
,

Z_{t}(v)\subseteq[k].

Let
Z_{0}
be defined by
Z_{0}(v)=\{X_{0}(v),Y_{0}(v)\}
for all
v\in V
. In particular,
|Z_{0}(z^{*})|=2
and
|Z_{0}(v)|=1
for all
v\neq z^{*}
.

For
t>0
we define
Z_{t}
inductively as follows.
For all
v\in V
, we decompose the colors
[k]
into three disjoint sets
A(Z_{t-1},v),B(Z_{t-1},v),H(Z_{t-1},v)
which correspond to the available, blocked, and potentially hazardous colors defined as follows:

\displaystyle A(Z_{t-1},v)

\displaystyle=[k]\setminus\bigcup_{w\in N(v)}Z_{t-1}(w)=\{c\in[k]:c\notin Z_{t-1}(w)\mbox{ for any }w\in N(v)\},

\displaystyle H(Z_{t-1},v)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in N(v):\\
|Z_{t-1}(w)|>1\end{subarray}}Z_{t-1}(w),

\displaystyle B(Z_{t-1},v)

\displaystyle=[k]\setminus(A(Z_{t-1},v)\cup H(Z_{t-1},v)).\text{ Thus, }B(Z_{t-1},v)\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in N(v):\\
|Z_{t-1}(w)|=1\end{subarray}}Z_{t-1}(w)

We emphasize that hazardous colors take precedence over blocked colors because
H(Z_{t-1},v)
is
meant to record the colors through which unresolved uncertainty can propagate: if
v
has a neighbor
w
such that
Z_{t-1}(w)=\{R,B\}
and another neighbor
w^{\prime}
such that
Z_{t-1}(w^{\prime})=\{R\}
, then color
R
is included in
H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})

and considered hazardous, not blocked. This is a deliberate over-approximation, and it ensures that later
local couplings can swap between colors carried by a discrepancy without
changing the evolution of the bounding chain.

We define
Z_{t}
, for
1\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
, based on the update
(v_{t},c_{t})
as follows:

(1)

For all
w\neq v_{t}
, set
Z_{t}(w)=Z_{t-1}(w)
.

(2)

If
c_{t}\in A(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
, set
Z_{t}(v_{t})=\{c_{t}\}
.

(3)

If
c_{t}\in B(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
, set
Z_{t}(v_{t})=Z_{t-1}(v_{t})
.

(4)

If
c_{t}\in H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
, do the following.

(a)

If
v_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
, set
Z_{t}(v_{t})=Z_{t-1}(v_{t})
.

(b)

If
v_{t}\notin\mathcal{P}_{t}
, then choose
w\in N(v_{t})\cap\mathcal{P}_{t}
according to some fixed, but otherwise arbitrary, total order. Set
Z_{t}(v_{t})=Z_{t-1}(w)\cup Z_{t-1}(v_{t})
.

For an intuitive understanding of these update rules, note that the sets
A(Z_{t-1},v)
,
B(Z_{t-1},v)
, and
H(Z_{t-1},v)

represent three different levels of certainty about what happens if the color

c_{t}
is proposed at the vertex
v_{t}
. If
c_{t}\in A(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
, then no neighbor of
v_{t}
can possibly use

c_{t}
, according to the information recorded in
Z_{t-1}
. Thus the proposal
is certainly legal, so after the update the color of
v_{t}
is completely
determined, and we may collapse to
Z_{t}(v_{t})=\{c_{t}\}
. If
c_{t}\in B(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
, then
c_{t}
is not available, but it also does not
appear in the color set of any unresolved neighbor. In this case no new uncertainty is created at
v_{t}
, so we simply
leave its set of possible colors unchanged:

Z_{t}(v_{t})=Z_{t-1}(v_{t}).
The interesting case is when
c_{t}\in H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
. By definition, this
means that some neighbor
w
of
v_{t}
has
|Z_{t-1}(w)|>1
and lists

c_{t}
as a possible color. Thus the proposal at
v_{t}
interacts with an
already unresolved part of the configuration. This is precisely the mechanism
by which uncertainty can spread from one vertex to another. In this situation
we deliberately over-approximate: rather than trying to determine the exact
outcome from the partial information in
Z_{t-1}
, we allow
v_{t}
both to keep
any of its previous possible colors and also to acquire any color that is
currently carried by an unresolved neighbor.

The purpose of the bounding chain is to track the region where
disagreements between the coupled chains may persist.
We therefore define the set of potential persistent disagreements using
Z_{t}
as follows. For
0\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
, let

\mathcal{P}_{t}=\{v\in V:|Z_{t}(v)|>1\},

and

\mathcal{P}=\cup_{0\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\mathcal{P}_{t},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{\leq t}=\bigcup_{s\leq t}\mathcal{P}_{s}.

Note,
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{z^{*}\}
where
z^{*}
is the initial disagreement between
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
and the set
\mathcal{P}_{\leq s}
“grows” from
z^{*}
. The next condition captures that this growth happens in a tree-like manner, outwards from
z^{*}
.

For the bounding chain
Z_{t}
on inputs
X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}}
, we set
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
if the following properties hold:

(1)

G[\mathcal{P}]

is acyclic. Furthermore, there are no three vertices
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
so that
G[\mathcal{P}\cup\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}]
has a cycle.

(2)

\forall\,1\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}},v_{t}\in\cup_{s<t}\mathcal{P}_{s}\implies c_{t}\notin H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
.

(3)

We have the size bound
|\mathcal{P}|\leq\exp(\exp(O({C_{\mathrm{cp}}})))
, where the implicit constant in
O(\cdot)
is universal, independent of any parameters.

Otherwise,
we set
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{False}
.

The condition
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\texttt{True}
singles out the update
sequences for which the potential disagreement region evolves in a controlled
way. Condition (1) is a geometric requirement. The set
\mathcal{P}
records all vertices
that ever become potentially persistently discrepant, and later our local couplings will
inspect and modify updates inside a radius-
2
neighborhood of this set.
Requiring
G[B_{2}(\mathcal{P})]
to be acyclic means that relevant
local environment of
\mathcal{P}
is tree-like, which will be crucially used in our construction. Condition (2) is a dynamical requirement. As the update rule above shows, a
hazardous update is the only kind of update that can enlarge the bounding chain vertex. The implication

v_{t}\in\bigcup_{s<t}\mathcal{P}_{s}\implies c_{t}\notin H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})

says that if the color
c_{t}
is potentially hazardous then the updated vertex was not a discrepancy at an earlier time, hence a vertex
v_{t}
already in the discrepancy set cannot be updated with a hazardous color. Intuitively, once a vertex has entered

\mathcal{P}
, later updates at that vertex may resolve its uncertainty or leave it
unchanged, but they are not allowed to make it hazardous again. Thus
\mathcal{P}_{\leq t}

grows outward from
z^{*}
in a “one-pass manner”, rather than repeatedly
re-propagating through vertices that are already in the discrepancy set. Finally, condition (3) ensures that the bounding chain does not grow too rapidly, and will be used in
Section

4
to show that our non-Markovian coupling is contractive.

Fact 2.5
.

Assume
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},\vec{\sigma})=\tt{True}
.

(1)

For every
t
, the induced subgraph
G[\mathcal{P}_{\leq t}]
is connected.

(2)

If
v_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\leq t-1}
, then
N(v_{t})\cap\mathcal{P}_{t-1}
is a singleton.

In particular, every vertex that first enters
\mathcal{P}
has a unique parent in the previously constructed set.

Proof.

For part (1), note that
\mathcal{P}_{\leq 0}=\{z^{*}\}
is connected. Suppose inductively that
\mathcal{P}_{\leq t-1}
is connected. If
\mathcal{P}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\leq t-1}
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise let
x\in\mathcal{P}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{\leq t-1}
. Then the update at time

is hazardous, so by definition of the bounding chain there exists a neighbor

with

, i.e.

y\in\mathcal{P}_{t-1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\leq t-1}
. Thus every new vertex is attached by an edge to the previously constructed set, and connectivity is preserved.

For part (2), existence of at least one neighbor in

follows from the same observation. For uniqueness, suppose that

had two distinct neighbors

\mathcal{P}_{\leq t-1}
is connected, so there is a path in

from

to
q
. Together with the two edges
pv_{t}
and

, this creates a cycle in

, contradicting Condition (1) in the definition of
\BC
is
\tt{True}
.
∎

2.3.
Local non-Markovian coupling

The bounding chain from
Section

2.2
identifies the region

\mathcal{P}
where persistent discrepancies may potentially occur and, when

\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\texttt{True}
, guarantees that this region grows
outward from the initial discrepancy in a controlled tree-like manner.
We now define the basic local operation that will be used to try to prevent a propagation
in the potential disagreement set
\mathcal{P}_{t}
from becoming a propagating in the actual disagreement set
X_{t}\oplus Y_{t}
. The resulting local
transformation, denoted
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
, is the basic building block of the
global coupling constructed later.

We begin with some preliminary definitions. For a set
S\subset V
and
v\in V
, let
N_{S}(v)
be the neighborhood of
v
in
G[(V\setminus S)\cup\{v\}]
, and similarly define
N_{S}^{2}(v)
. Notice that
N_{S}(v)=N(v)\setminus S
but
N_{S}^{2}(v)\neq N^{2}(v)\setminus S
in general. For simplicity, for a vertex

, we will write

.

For a vertex
w\in V
and time
0<t<{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
, define the last and next successful updates of
w
as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{w}^{-}

\displaystyle=\tau_{w}^{-}(t):=\max\{s:1\leq s\leq t,v_{s}=w,c_{s}\in A(X_{s-1},v_{s})\}

\displaystyle\tau_{w}^{+}

\displaystyle=\tau_{w}^{+}(t):=\min\{s:t<s\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}},v_{s}=w,c_{s}\in A(X_{s-1},v_{s})\},

whenever the sets are nonempty, and take
\tau_{w}^{-}=0
or
\tau_{w}^{+}={T_{\mathrm{cp}}}+1
otherwise. We define the update epoch of
w
around time

by

I(w,t)=\begin{cases}[\tau_{w}^{-},\tau_{w}^{+})&\mbox{if }\tau_{w}^{-}>0,\\
\mbox{undefined}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
t\in I(w,t)
and
X_{s}(w)
is constant (in particular, equal to
X_{t}(w)
) for all
s\in I(w,t)
. We also define the punctured interval

I^{\circ}(w,t):=I(w,t)\setminus\{t\}.

Fix initial labelings
X_{0},Y_{0}\in\widehat{\Omega}
that differ at a single vertex

, and fix an update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}\in(V\times[k])^{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
. Let
\{Z_{t}\}
denote the corresponding bounding chain, and let
\mathcal{P}=\cup_{t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\mathcal{P}_{t}
be its associated potential persistent discrepancy set. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}
denote the evolution of
X_{0}
according to
{\vec{\sigma}}
.

Condition 2.6
.

Our non-Markovian update is not defined unless the following conditions hold (and even if they are all satisfied, we may still require additional properties, see
2.8
).

•

1\leq t\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
is an update time such that
c_{t}\in H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
(i.e., the chosen update vertex

is attempting a hazardous update). In particular,

•

Let

denote the first element in

according to some fixed, but otherwise arbitrary, total ordering. We assume that

. (Whenever
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
,

is uniquely determined by

. We will only use our non-Markovian coupling whenever this holds, see the first bullet of
2.8
.)

•

Let

, so that

is a subset of

of size

according to the previous bullet.

We assume that

. In this case, let

denote the unique “blocked” color and let

denote the unique unblocked color.

•

For each

, we have
\tau_{w}^{-}>0
.

Remark 2.7
.

Notice that whether
2.6
is satisfied or not is fully determined by
(v_{s},c_{s})
for
1\leq s\leq t
. This is in contrast to the later condition
2.8

In the definitions below, for lightness of notation, we will omit the dependence on

and
H^{*}
in various places where there is no risk of confusion. We emphasize that we will only use these definitions on the event that
2.6
holds and further, that
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
(see the first bullet of
2.8
).

For a vertex
w\in N_{p}(v_{t})
, we denote the
exchangeable colors
for

as follows:

\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)=\{X_{t-1}(w)\}\cup\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{w}^{-}-1},w):\mbox{for all }s\in I^{\circ}(w,t),\mbox{ if }v_{s}\in N(w)\mbox{ then }c_{s}\neq c\},

whenever
\tau_{w}^{-}
is defined, and
\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)=\emptyset

In words,
\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)
denotes those colors

at its last successful recoloring (prior to

) and no neighbor of
w

regardless of updates attempted in the epoch.

We define an
avoided set

\mathsf{Avoid}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\{w\in N_{p}(v_{t}):\exists s\in I^{\circ}(w,t)\text{ where }v_{s}=v_{t},c_{s}=X_{s-1}(w)\}\cup\mathcal{P}.

If

during its epoch could also
change whether the proposed change to

is blocked at the corresponding time

. Excluding these vertices ensures that the local edit does not change any proposal at

, other than the intended modification at time

.

Next, for a color

, we define the set of neighbors which are
swappable for color

at time

\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c,t)=\{w\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t}):c\in\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)\}\setminus\mathsf{Avoid}_{\mathcal{P}}(t).

We define the
complementary neighbor
mapping

\alpha:\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{b},t)\to\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{u},t)

in the following manner.
Order
\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{b},t)=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\}
by decreasing
|\mathsf{Ex}(z_{i},t)|
and
\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{u},t)=\{z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}
by decreasing
|\mathsf{Ex}(z_{i}^{\prime},t)|
, breaking ties according to some arbitrary (but otherwise globally fixed) total order on
V
. For
i\leq\min\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}
, let
\alpha(z_{i})=z^{\prime}_{i}
; and for
i>\min\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}
, let
\alpha(z_{i})
be undefined.

For any
z\in\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{b},t)
for which
\alpha(z)
is defined, we further define the
complementary color
mapping

\beta_{z}:\mathsf{Ex}(\alpha(z),t)\rightarrow\mathsf{Ex}(z,t)

as follows. Let
\mathsf{Ex}(\alpha(z),t)=\{c^{\prime}_{1},\dots,c^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\}
and
\mathsf{Ex}(z,t)=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\}
denote ordered sets according to the natural order on

. For
1\leq j\leq\min\{m,m^{\prime}\}
, let
\beta_{z}(c^{\prime}_{j})=c_{j}
; and for
j>\min\{m,m^{\prime}\}
, let
\beta_{z}(c^{\prime}_{j})
be undefined.

Finally, we define the set of
blocking neighbors
as

B=\{w\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t}):X_{t-1}(w)=c_{b}\}.

Since
c_{b}\in X_{t-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t}))
by

, we have
|B|\geq 1
.

Condition 2.8

(non-Markovian well-defined)
.

The transformation
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
is applied only if
2.6
holds and furthermore the following hold:

•

\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
;

•

X_{t-1}(N_{p}(v_{t})\cap\mathcal{P})\cap H^{*}=\emptyset
;

•

B\cap\mathsf{Avoid}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\emptyset
;

•

the mapping

is defined on

;

•

for each
w\in B
, the color

\beta_{w}(X_{t-1}(\alpha(w)))
is defined and satisfies
\beta_{w}(X_{t-1}(\alpha(w)))\notin H^{*}
;

•

\alpha(B)\cap B=\{w\in B:\alpha(w)=w\}
.

Notice that the second bullet is equivalent to
B\subset\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{b},t)
. This follows as
c_{b}=X_{t}(w)
for all
w\in B
and
X_{t}(w)\in\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)
by definition of

.

We can now define our local non-Markovian coupling.

Definition 2.9

(local non-Markovian transformation)
.

Given
X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}}
and
t\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
satisfying
2.8
, the transformation

\NM_{t,H^{*}}({\vec{\sigma}})=((v_{1},c^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c^{\prime}_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}))

is defined as follows.

(1)

For each
w\in B
with
\alpha(w)=w
, set

c^{\prime}_{\tau_{w}^{-}}=c_{u}.

(2)

For each
w\in B
with
\alpha(w)\neq w
, set

c^{\prime}_{\tau_{w}^{-}}=\beta_{w}\big(X_{t-1}(\alpha(w))\big),\qquad c^{\prime}_{\tau_{\alpha(w)}^{-}}=c_{u}.

(3)

For every
w\in B\cup\alpha(B)
and every
s\in I^{\circ}(w,t)
with

v_{s}\in N(w)
and
c_{s}=X_{s-1}(w)
, set

c^{\prime}_{s}=c^{\prime}_{\tau_{w}^{-}}.

(4)

At time
t
set

c^{\prime}_{t}=H^{*}\setminus\{c_{t}\}=X_{t-1}(p).

(5)

For all other times
s
, set
c^{\prime}_{s}=c_{s}
.

Remark 2.10
.

The point of this coupling is that we introduce “temporary” discrepancies to block “persistent” discrepancies. However,
2.8
does not assume that
\tau_{w}^{+}
is defined for
w\in B
; thus, some of these temporary discrepancies may persist until time
{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
. This is intentional and is designed this way to overcome measurability obstacles. While these errors may not be fixed, they cannot propagate as we have checked that their new colors were exchangeable in
I(w,t)
.

The following simple lemma shows that the edits in the previous definition are pairwise disjoint, whenever
2.8
holds.

Fact 2.11
.

Suppose
2.8
holds. Let
W:=B\cup\alpha(B)
. Then:

(1)

W\subseteq N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
, and for every
w\in W
one has
N(w)\cap\mathcal{P}=\{v_{t}\}
;

(2)

If
u\in N(W)\setminus\{v_{t}\}
, then
N(u)\cap\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
and there is a unique
w\in W
with
u\in N(w)
;

(3)

W
is an independent set.

Proof.

The inclusion
W\subseteq N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
is immediate from the definitions of
B
and
\alpha
. Fix
w\in W
. Since
v_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
, we already have
v_{t}\in N(w)\cap\mathcal{P}
. If there were another vertex
q\in N(w)\cap\mathcal{P}
with
q\neq v_{t}
, then the connectedness of
G[\mathcal{P}]
from
2.5
(1) would give a path in
G[\mathcal{P}]
from
q
to
v_{t}
; together with the edges
qw
and
wv_{t}
, this would create a cycle in
G[\mathcal{P}\cup\{w\}]
, contradicting
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
. This proves (1).

If
u\in N(W)\setminus\{v_{t}\}
and
q\in N(u)\cap\mathcal{P}
, choose
w\in W
with
u\in N(w)
. Then the path in
G[\mathcal{P}]
from
q
to
v_{t}
, together with the edges
qu
,
uw
, and
wv_{t}
, creates a cycle in
G[B_{2}(\mathcal{P})]
, a contradiction. Hence
N(u)\cap\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
. If some
u\in N(W)\setminus\{v_{t}\}
were adjacent to two distinct vertices
w,w^{\prime}\in W
, then
w-v_{t}-w^{\prime}-u-w
would be a cycle in
G[B_{2}(\mathcal{P})]
, which is impossible. This proves (2).

Finally, if two distinct vertices
w,w^{\prime}\in W
were adjacent, then
w-v_{t}-w^{\prime}-w
would be a cycle in
G[B_{2}(\mathcal{P})]
. Hence
W
is independent.
∎

2.4.
Global Non-Markovian Coupling by Composing Local Couplings

Having defined our local non-Markovian coupling, we can now define our global (non-Markovian) coupling. As mentioned earlier, this will arise by composing three different types of maps: the identity map, the local non-Markovian map, and the following local Jerrum map.

Definition 2.12

(local Jerrum coupling)
.

Given
H\in\binom{[k]}{2}
,
t\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
, and

{\vec{\sigma}}={\vec{\sigma}}_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}}=((v_{1},c_{1}),\dots,(v_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}},c_{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}})),

define
\Jerrum_{t,H}({\vec{\sigma}})
to be the update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}=((v_{s},c_{s}^{\prime}))_{s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]}
where
c_{s}^{\prime}=c_{s}
for all
s\neq t
and

c_{t}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}H\setminus\{c_{t}\}&c_{t}\in H,\\
c_{t}&c_{t}\notin H.\end{cases}

Definition 2.13

(global coupling)
.

Our global coupling is a partial function
F:\widehat{\Omega}\times\widehat{\Omega}\times(V\times[k])^{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}\to(V\times[k])^{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}

\displaystyle(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{F}{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}

which maps an update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}
, which will be applied to
X_{0}
, to a corresponding update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}
, which will be applied to
Y_{0}
. The function

is defined only if
X_{0}
and

disagree at exactly one vertex,

.

If

, then set

(thus, we are using the identity coupling when the bounding chain “fails”). Otherwise, proceed as follows.

For
t\in\{0,1,\dots,{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}\}
, we will inductively define functions

with the initialization

, and sets

with the initialization

, the unique “root disagreement”. Let

We define

For
t\geq 1
, suppose we have already defined
D_{t-1}
and

.
Let

In particular,

(1)

. Set

.

(2)

(3)

Otherwise,

In this section we prove that the coupling

3.1.
Properties of local coupling

We will need the following properties of
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
in later proofs.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
, let
X_{s}
denote
X_{0}
evolved according to
{\vec{\sigma}}
and
X^{\prime}_{s}
denote
X_{0}
evolved according to
{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}=\NM_{t,H^{*}}({\vec{\sigma}})
.

(1)

For all
s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
with
v_{s}\notin(N^{2}_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})\cup\{v_{t}\})
,
c_{s}=c^{\prime}_{s}
.

(2)

For all
s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
the following hold:

(a)

If
v_{s}\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(B\cup\alpha(B))
then
c_{s}\in A(X_{s-1},v_{s})
if and only if
c_{s}^{\prime}\in A(X_{s-1}^{\prime},v_{s})
. Furthermore, if both chains succeed, then
c_{s}=c_{s}^{\prime}
.

(b)

X_{s}(v)=X_{s}^{\prime}(v)
unless
v\in B\cup\alpha(B)\cup\mathcal{C}_{s}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{s}
is the (possibly empty) component of
\mathcal{P}_{\leq s}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{t-1}
containing
v_{t}
.

These properties will be utilized in the later proof that the global coupling (formed by composing local couplings) is bijective, and hence a valid coupling. Perhaps the most subtle item in
Lemma

3.1
is
Item

2b
. Essentially, this states that at time
t
, we will have the discrepancies created on
B\cup\alpha(B)
as well as the discrepancy at
v_{t}
. As time progresses past
t
, those on
B\cup\alpha(B)
will not propagate outwards, and although
v_{t}
will propagate, it will do so in a contained manner, following the bounding chain
\mathcal{P}_{t}
. In particular, for
s<t
, we have
\mathcal{C}_{s}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Set

W:=B\cup\alpha(B),\qquad\chi(w):=X_{t-1}(w),\qquad\chi^{\prime}(w):=c^{\prime}_{\tau_{w}^{-}}\qquad(w\in W).

We first record two basic consequences of the definition of
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
.

For every
w\in W
one has

(3.1)

\chi^{\prime}(w)\in\mathsf{Ex}(w,t).

Indeed, if
w\in B
and
\alpha(w)\neq w
, then

\chi^{\prime}(w)=\beta_{w}(X_{t-1}(\alpha(w)))\in\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)
because
\beta_{w}
maps into
\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)
.
If
w\in\alpha(B)
, then
\chi^{\prime}(w)=c_{u}
and
w\in\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}(c_{u},t)
, so again
c_{u}\in\mathsf{Ex}(w,t)
.
If
w\in B
and
\alpha(w)=w
, then since
w
is in the codomain of

3.2.
Bounding chain properties

Here we show that the bounding chain satisfies certain symmetry properties, which will be used in the later proofs showing that the coupling is valid.

Lemma 3.3
.

\BC(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following symmetry properties.

•

\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\BC(Y_{0},X_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})
.

•

If
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
, let
{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}=F(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})
. Then
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime})=\tt{True}
. Furthermore, for all
t\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
, the sets
\mathcal{P}_{t}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
created by
(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})
and by
(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime})
are the same and further, the sets
Z_{t}(v)
and
\widetilde{Z}_{t}(v)
are also the same for all
v\in\mathcal{P}_{t}=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
.

Proof.

The first bullet is immediate, as examining the definition reveals no difference in how
\BC
handles its first two inputs.

The second bullet is somewhat more subtle. We will do this by analyzing the local functions
f_{t}
, and showing that none of them will meaningfully affect the evolution of the bounding chain
Z_{t}
. There are three possibilities for
f_{t}
.

•

f_{t}=\id
. This is trivial.

•

f_{t}=\Jerrum_{t,H}
for some
H\subset H(Z_{t-1},v_{t})
. This is also immediate from reviewing the definition of the bounding chain: a hazardous update never accesses which element of the hazardous set was actually attempted.

•

f_{t}=\NM_{t,H}
is non-Markovian. In this case, we must run an induction similar to proving
Lemma

3.1
. The key point is that in terms of updates,
\NM_{t,H}
affects
{\vec{\sigma}}
in the
2
-neighborhood at potentially any time in
[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]
but affects
{\vec{\sigma}}
in the
1
-neighborhood at only times in
[0,t)
(specifically,
\tau_{w}^{-}
for each
w\in N_{p}(v_{t})
). Thus the changes are all in the past, before any times at which
v_{t}
is hazardous (since
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
means that
v_{t}
was not yet hazardous). Thus the hazardous set

constructed in

does not see them.

Formally, let

\eta:={\vec{\sigma}}=((v_{s},c_{s}))_{s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]},\qquad\eta^{\prime}:={\vec{\sigma}}^{\prime}=\NM_{t,H}({\vec{\sigma}})=((v_{s},c^{\prime}_{s}))_{s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]}.

Let

For each

As in the proof of

, we have

(3.5)

\text{for every }w\in W\text{ and every }s\in I^{\circ}(w,t)\text{ with }v_{s}\in N(w),\text{ one has }c_{s}\neq\chi^{\prime}(w).

Because every

has no neighbor in
\mathcal{P}^{\eta}
. Thus
u
has no hazardous color in either bounding chain.

If
s\in I^{\circ}(w,t)
and
c_{s}=\chi(w)
, then by definition of
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
we have
c_{s}^{\prime}=\chi^{\prime}(w)
. In the reference run the proposal
\chi(w)
is blocked by the singleton neighbor
w
, and in the modified run the proposal
\chi^{\prime}(w)
is blocked by the same singleton neighbor
w
. Hence in both runs the update at time
s
leaves the state of
u
unchanged, so
u\notin\mathcal{P}_{s}^{\eta}=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{s}
and
(\dagger_{s})(3)
continues to hold.

In all other subcases,
c_{s}^{\prime}=c_{s}
. If
s\in I^{\circ}(w,t)
, then (
3.5
) gives
c_{s}\neq\chi^{\prime}(w)
, and because we are not in the previous subcase we also have
c_{s}\neq\chi(w)
. Thus the carried color of
w
is irrelevant for the proposal status at
u
, and the update type is the same in both bounding chains. So again
(\dagger_{s})(3)
holds.

Case 4:
v_{s}=v_{t}
.

If
s=t
, then
c_{t}^{\prime}=H^{*}\setminus\{c_{t}\}
and both colors lie in the same hazardous set
H^{*}\subseteq H(Z_{t-1}^{\eta},v_{t})
. Hence time
t
is hazardous in both sequences, and the resulting multivalued set at
v_{t}
is the same in both bounding chains:

\widetilde{Z}_{t}(v_{t})=H(Z_{t-1}^{\eta},v_{t})\cup Z_{t-1}^{\eta}(v_{t})=Z_{t}^{\eta}(v_{t}).

Thus
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}=\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\eta}
.

Now assume
s\neq t
. Then
c_{s}^{\prime}=c_{s}
. If
w\in W
and the two runs currently disagree on the singleton color of
w
, then necessarily
s\in I^{\circ}(w,t)
. In that case, (
3.6
) implies
c_{s}\neq\chi(w)
, while (
3.5
) implies
c_{s}\neq\chi^{\prime}(w)
. So the neighbor
w
blocks neither proposal. All other neighbors of
v_{t}
have the same bounding-chain state in the two runs by
(\dagger_{s-1})
. Therefore
c_{s}
has the same status in the two bounding chains, and the update at time
s
has the same effect in both.

These four cases exhaust all possibilities, so
(\dagger_{s})
holds for every
s\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
. In particular,

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{s}=\mathcal{P}_{s}^{\eta}\qquad\text{for all }0\leq s\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}},

and

\widetilde{Z}_{s}(u)=Z_{s}^{\eta}(u)\qquad\text{for every }u\in\mathcal{P}_{s}^{\eta}.

This proves that the two runs generate the same sets
\mathcal{P}_{s}
and the same bounding-chain values on those sets.

It remains to prove that
\eta^{\prime}
also satisfies
\BC
. Note first that Condition (1) in the definition of
\BC
depends only on the union of the sets
\mathcal{P}_{s}
, and that union is the same for
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
. For Condition (2), fix
1\leq s\leq{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
such that

v_{s}\in\bigcup_{0\leq r<s}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{r}=\bigcup_{0\leq r<s}\mathcal{P}_{r}^{\eta}.

By the case analysis above, the proposal at time
s
has the same type (available, blocked, or hazardous) in the two bounding chains. In particular,

c_{s}^{\prime}\in H(\widetilde{Z}_{s-1},v_{s})\iff c_{s}\in H(Z_{s-1}^{\eta},v_{s}).

Since
\eta
satisfies Condition (2) in the definition of
\BC
, the right-hand side is false, and therefore the left-hand side is also false. So
\eta^{\prime}
satisfies Condition (2). Finally, since we have shown that
\mathcal{P}={\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}
, Condition (3) is also satisfies, proving that
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},\eta^{\prime})=\tt{True}
.
∎

3.3.
Validity of local coupling

Here we prove that the local coupling
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
is bijective, and hence is a valid coupling.
In the lemma below, as throughout this section,
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
are two labelings with an update sequence
{\vec{\sigma}}\in(V\times[k])^{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}
. The discrepancy set
\mathcal{P}
follows condition
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\BC(X_{0},Y_{0},{\vec{\sigma}})=\tt{True}
and
t
satisfies
2.8
. Then

\NM_{t,H^{*}}(\NM_{t,H^{*}}({\vec{\sigma}}))~=~{\vec{\sigma}}.

Proof.

Set

\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}:=\NM_{t,H^{*}}(\vec{\sigma}),\qquad\vec{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}:=\NM_{t,H^{*}}(\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}).

We will show that
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}=\vec{\sigma}
.

The single-edit case in the proof of
Lemma

3.3
shows that the first pass preserves the bounding chain. Hence
\vec{\sigma}
and
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
generate the same sets
\mathcal{P}_{s}
and the same bounding-chain values on those sets. In particular, the auxiliary objects
p
,
H^{*}
, and the neighborhood
N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
are the same for the first and second pass.

Let

W:=B\cup\alpha(B),\qquad\chi(w):=X_{t-1}(w),\qquad\chi^{\prime}(w):=c^{\prime}_{\tau_{w}^{-}}\qquad(w\in W),

where
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}=((v_{s},c_{s}^{\prime}))_{s\in[{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}]}
.
By
Corollary

3.2
, the first pass changes the reference evolution on
W
exactly as follows: for each
w\in W
, the times
\tau_{w}^{-}
and
\tau_{w}^{+}
are unchanged, and on the whole interval
[\tau_{w}^{-},\tau_{w}^{+})
the color carried by
w
is changed from
\chi(w)
to
\chi^{\prime}(w)
.

Claim 3.5
.

For every
z\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
, the second pass sees the same epoch and the same exchangeable set as the first pass:

I^{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}}(z,t)=I^{\vec{\sigma}}(z,t),\qquad\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}}(z,t)=\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}}(z,t).

Consequently,

\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}}(c,t)=\mathsf{Swap}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\vec{\sigma}}(c,t)\qquad\text{for every }c\in[k].

Proof.

Fix
z\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
.

First suppose that
z\in W
.
By
Corollary

3.2
, the times
\tau_{z}^{-}
and
\tau_{z}^{+}
are unchanged by the first pass, so the epoch
I(z,t)
is unchanged as well.
Before time
\tau_{z}^{-}
, the two runs coincide on the whole neighborhood of
z
, so the availability set at the last successful recoloring time is the same in the two runs.
For every color
d\notin\{\chi(z),\chi^{\prime}(z)\}
, the first pass does not edit any proposal of color
d
made by a neighbor of
z
during
I^{\circ}(z,t)
, so membership of
d
in the exchangeable set is unchanged.
The color
\chi^{\prime}(z)
belongs to
\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}}(z,t)
by construction, and it belongs to
\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}}(z,t)
because it is the current color of
z
at time
t-1
under
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
.
The color
\chi(z)
belongs to
\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}}(z,t)
because it is the current color under
\vec{\sigma}
, and it belongs to
\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}}(z,t)
because every interior neighbor-attempt of
\chi(z)
was changed by the first pass to an attempt of
\chi^{\prime}(z)
.
Hence

\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}}(z,t)=\mathsf{Ex}^{\vec{\sigma}}(z,t).

Now suppose that
z\notin W
.
We claim that no coordinate edited by the first pass lies at
z
or at a neighbor of
z
during the open interval
I^{\circ}(z,t)
.
Indeed, step 1 of
\NM_{t,H^{*}}
edits only successful recolorings at vertices of
W
, and by acyclicity of
\mathcal{P}
, no vertex of
W
is adjacent to
z
.
Step 2 edits only proposals made at neighbors of vertices of
W
; but by acyclicity of
\mathcal{P}
, no such edited vertex can lie in
N(z)
.
Step 3 edits time
t
itself, but time
t
is excluded from
I^{\circ}(z,t)
by definition.
Therefore the successful recoloring times of
z
, the neighborhood states relevant at those times, and the entire neighbor-attempt pattern on
I^{\circ}(z,t)
are unchanged, so both the epoch and the exchangeable set are unchanged.

This proves the first two assertions.
For the swappable sets, note that their definition depends only on the exchangeable sets and on whether there exists
s\in I^{\circ}(z,t)
with
v_{s}=v_{t}
and
c_{s}
equal to the current color of
z
.
For
z\notin W
, all of these are unchanged.
For
z\in W
, the current color changes from
\chi(z)
to
\chi^{\prime}(z)
, but the first pass never edits a time with updated vertex
v_{t}
except time
t
itself, and time
t
is excluded from
I^{\circ}(z,t)
.
Moreover, by exchangeability neither
\chi(z)
nor
\chi^{\prime}(z)
is proposed by
v_{t}
on
I^{\circ}(z,t)
.
So the swappable sets are unchanged for every color.
∎

Let
B^{\prime}
denote the blocking set computed from
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
in the second pass.
By
Corollary

3.2
, every vertex
w\in B
carries the color

\chi^{\prime}(w)=\beta_{w}(X_{t-1}(\alpha(w)))\notin H^{*}

at time
t-1
under
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
, while every vertex
\alpha(w)
carries the color
c_{u}
. In the case that
\alpha(w)=w
, the second criterion takes precedence, i.e.
w
carries
c_{u}
.
No vertex of
N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})\setminus W
changes color under the first pass.
Since originally the only color from
H^{*}
present on
N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
was
c_{b}
, it follows that after the first pass the only color from
H^{*}
present on
N_{\mathcal{P}}(v_{t})
is
c_{u}
, and it appears exactly on
\alpha(B)
.
Therefore

B^{\prime}=\alpha(B),\qquad c_{b}^{\prime}=c_{u},\qquad c_{u}^{\prime}=c_{b}.

By
3.5
, the epochs
I(w,t)
are the same in the two passes.
Hence the complementary-neighbor map in the second pass is the inverse ordered matching:

\alpha^{\prime}(\alpha(w))=w\qquad\text{for every }w\in B.

Likewise, because the ordered exchangeable sets are unchanged, the complementary-color map in the second pass is the inverse ordered matching on those ordered sets:

\beta^{\prime}_{\alpha(w)}\bigl(\chi^{\prime}(w)\bigr)=X_{t-1}(\alpha(w))\qquad\text{for every }w\in B.

In particular, the second pass is well-defined.

We now compare the coordinates edited by the two passes.
At time
t
,

c^{\prime\prime}_{t}=H^{*}\setminus\{c^{\prime}_{t}\}=c_{t}.

For each
w\in B
, the second pass changes the two successful recolorings by

c^{\prime\prime}_{\tau_{w}^{-}}=c_{b}=\chi(w),\qquad c^{\prime\prime}_{\tau_{\alpha(w)}^{-}}=X_{t-1}(\alpha(w))=\chi(\alpha(w)).

These are exactly the original colors at those two times.
Finally, because

W=B^{\prime}\cup\alpha^{\prime}(B^{\prime})=\alpha(B)\cup B,

the second pass acts on exactly the same collection of epochs as the first pass, but with the carried colors reversed.
Thus every interior edit made in the first pass is undone in the second pass: each proposal of
\chi^{\prime}(w)
created by the first pass is changed back to a proposal of
\chi(w)
, for every
w\in W
.
All coordinates untouched by the first pass remain untouched by the second pass.
Hence

\vec{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}=\vec{\sigma},

which proves the lemma.
∎

and using (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}\mathbb{E}[\rho(t+1)-\rho(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}\sum_{p\in D_{t}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}\right)-\frac{1}{n}(1-\varepsilon e)}}\right]

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}\frac{\delta}{3n}|D_{t}|\leq-\frac{\delta}{3en}\rho(t).

Combining the generic bound (
4.7
) for the first
{C_{\mathrm{buffer}}}n
steps with the negative drift (

) for the remaining steps yields

(4.10)

\mathbb{E}[\rho({T_{\mathrm{cp}}})]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{e}{n}}}\right)^{{C_{\mathrm{buffer}}}n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\delta}{3en}}}\right)^{{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}-{C_{\mathrm{buffer}}}n}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e{C_{\mathrm{buffer}}}-\frac{\delta}{4e}{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}}\right).

Therefore, by choosing
{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}
sufficiently large after

has been fixed, we may ensure

(4.11)

\mathbb{E}[\rho({T_{\mathrm{cp}}})]\leq\frac{1}{9}.

Step 5: temporary discrepancies.

By
Proposition

4.9
,

\mathbb{E}[\nu({T_{\mathrm{cp}}})]\leq 1/9.

Step 6: Putting everything together.

Using (
4.5
), (
4.11
), and
Proposition

4.9
in (

), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X_{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}\oplus Y_{T_{\mathrm{cp}}}|\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{U}\}}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{9}+\frac{1}{9}+\frac{1}{9}=\frac{1}{3}

for all sufficiently large

∎

5.
Local Uniformity Properties of the Metropolis Glauber Dynamics

In this section, we establish local uniformity properties for the Metropolis Glauber dynamics. Local uniformity properties are those local properties (i.e. properties depending on a “small” neighborhood of a vertex) which hold with high probability for a uniformly random coloring. We show that the labelings generated by the Metropolis Glauber dynamics satisfy these local uniformity properties with high probability, after sufficiently many steps.

Similar results were obtained in
[
12
]
for the heat-bath version of the Glauber dynamics. While we follow the general approach of Hayes, there is a key aspect of the Metropolis dynamics which requires new ingredients to analyze, namely, the successful-refresh rate at a vertex
w
, which is
|A(X_{t},w)|/k
, is correlated with the colors in
S_{2}(v)
. In
Definition

5.16
, we introduce an auxiliary process
Z^{*}
to decouple this dependence. In fact, even for the heat-bath version, there seem to be some gaps in
[
12
]
, which can be fixed using our techniques.

All chains are run on the extended state space
\widehat{\Omega}=[k]^{V}
. For a labeling
X\in\widehat{\Omega}
and a vertex
u\in V
, we write

A(X,u):=[k]\setminus X(N(u))

for the set of colors available at
u
under
X
. We also use the variant

A_{v}(X,u):=[k]\setminus X(N(w)\setminus\{v\}),

which ignores the color at
v
.

Our main result establishes that the discrete-time Metropolis Glauber dynamics achieves local uniformity after sufficiently many time steps. We first define the
i
-times blocked subset.

Definition 5.1
.

For a labeling

which is
i

The auxiliary processes

Definition 5.12
.

Given a graph

•

The vertex set is

•

For any edge

–

–

In this directed context, neighbor means in-neighbor: for a vertex

Definition 5.13

(the natural directed chain

)
.

Let

is the continuous-time Metropolis dynamics on

Definition 5.14

(the recursive directed chain
X^{*}
)
.

Let

and let

be a labeling as before.
Let

obtained using the following construction.

(1)

Boundary phase: Note that the graph induced by
G^{*}
on

is an undirected graph.
Run the standard (reversible) continuous-time Metropolis Glauber dynamics on this induced graph for all times, with

.

(2)

Inward propagation to
S_{2}(v)
: Having fixed the trajectories on

, define the trajectories on
S_{2}(v)
as follows. For each

, conditionally on the already constructed trajectories of

(3)

(4)

.

In particular,

Remark 5.15
.

The role of

is that it starts from the actual initial labeling

and is therefore the natural object to compare to the original chain

. The role of

is different: because it is generated recursively from the outer trajectories, every term in the later definition of the bias field (

and is still equal to its initial value. Moreover, the construction of

ensures that for any vertex

Definition 5.16

(the auxiliary process

)
.

Fix

and the recursively defined process

as in

\mathcal{F}_{v}:=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X^{*}_{t}(x):x\notin B_{2}(v),\ t\in\mathbb{R}}}\right).

Define a process

by the following rules.

(1)

Outside

.

(2)

For each

(3)

Remark 5.17
.

In the heat-bath dynamics, one can condition on the update times in

without leaking information about the colors in

. For the Metropolis dynamics, this is not true as the update rate of

is proportional to

, and so revealing update times in

leaks information about colors in

. The purpose of the chain

is to fully decouple the update times in

with the colors of

: compare step (2) in the definition of

, where the rate is a conditional expectation over

, versus the rates in the definition of

.

Definition 5.18

.

For every

, there exist constants

The corresponding statements for

.
The only change this causes in Hayes’s result is that certain constants need to be adjusted by factors of

1-\exp(-\Delta/C)
on which

M_{w}\leq 2\eta\Delta\qquad\text{for every }w\in N(v).

Assume
\mathcal{E}_{2}
holds. Then for every
w\in N(v)
and every
t\in[T-a,T]
, the sets
A(X_{t}^{*},w)
and
A(Y_{t}^{*},w)
differ in at most
2\eta\Delta
colors, because only those children of
w
that have not refreshed by time
T-a
can still be discrepant. Since both sets have size at least
\rho k
, their intersection has size at least
\rho k/2
for
\Delta
large enough. Therefore, once we condition on the histories up to time
T-a
, the probability that a given
w\in N(v)
is still discrepant at time
T
is at most
e^{-\rho a/2}\leq\eta
, and these events are independent over distinct
w
. Another Chernoff bound gives an event
\mathcal{E}_{1}
such that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\mid\mathcal{E}_{2}]\leq\exp(-\Delta/C),

and on
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,

\#\{w\in N(v):X_{T}^{*}(w)\neq Y_{T}^{*}(w)\}\leq 2\eta\Delta.

Hence

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}]\geq 1-\exp(-\Delta/C).

We now prove the first conclusion. On
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, every neighborhood contains at most
\varepsilon\Delta
discrepant vertices. Indeed:

•

if
u\notin B_{3}(v)
, then
X_{T}^{*}=Y_{T}^{*}
on
N(u)
;

•

if
u\in S_{3}(v)
or
u\in S_{2}(v)
, then by girth at least
7
,
u
has at most one neighbor in
B_{2}(v)
, so
|(X_{T}^{*}\oplus Y_{T}^{*})\cap N(u)|\leq 1\leq\varepsilon\Delta
;

•

if
u=v
, then all discrepancies in
N(v)
are counted by
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, so the bound is
2\eta\Delta
;

•

if
u=w\in N(v)
, then the only possible discrepant neighbors are
v
and those children in
\mathcal{C}(w)
that failed to refresh by time
T-a
, so

|(X_{T}^{*}\oplus Y_{T}^{*})\cap N(w)|\leq 1+M_{w}\leq 1+2\eta\Delta\leq\varepsilon\Delta

for
\Delta
large enough.

This proves the neighborhood-discrepancy estimate.

For the color-specific estimate, fix
c\in[k]
. Since
X_{T}^{*}=Y_{T}^{*}
outside
B_{2}(v)
, it is enough to show that

\#\{z\in B_{2}(v):z\in X_{T}^{*}\oplus Y_{T}^{*},\ c\in\{X_{T}^{*}(z),Y_{T}^{*}(z)\}\}\leq\varepsilon\Delta

with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Delta/C)
.

The contribution from
N(v)
is already at most
2\eta\Delta
on
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, so only vertices in
S_{2}(v)
need further attention. Split them into two classes:

A_{c}:=\#\{z\in S_{2}(v):X_{T}^{*}(z)=c\neq Y_{T}^{*}(z)\},\qquad B_{c}:=\#\{z\in S_{2}(v):Y_{T}^{*}(z)=c\neq X_{T}^{*}(z)\}.

For
A_{c}
, note that if
X_{T}^{*}(z)=c\neq Y_{T}^{*}(z)
then
z
has had no successful update in
[0,T]
(in either chain, since those successful-update processes are identical at vertices of
S_{2}(v)
). Conditional on the outside trajectory, this has probability at most
e^{-\rho T}\leq e^{-\rho C}
; given that event, the value of
X_{T}^{*}(z)
is the color used at the last successful update of the two-sided chain
X^{*}
before time
0
, so the conditional probability that it equals
c
is at most
1/(\rho k)
. Thus each
z\in S_{2}(v)
contributes to
A_{c}
with conditional probability at most
e^{-\rho C}/(\rho k)
. Since
|S_{2}(v)|\leq\Delta^{2}
and
k\geq(1+\delta)\Delta
, the conditional expectation of
A_{c}
is at most
\eta\Delta
, so a Chernoff bound gives
\mathbb{P}[A_{c}>2\eta\Delta]\leq\exp(-\Delta/C)
.

For
B_{c}
, if
Y_{T}^{*}(z)=c\neq X_{T}^{*}(z)
then again
z
has had no successful update in
[0,T]
, so
Y_{T}^{*}(z)=Y_{0}^{*}(z)=X_{0}(z)=c
. Hence only vertices of
B_{2}(v)
that already have color
c
at time
0
can contribute. Since
X_{0}
is
400
-above-suspicion at
v
, there are at most
400\Delta
such vertices. Each survives with no successful update until time
T
with probability at most
e^{-\rho C}
, so
\mathbb{E}[B_{c}]\leq 400e^{-\rho C}\Delta\leq\eta\Delta
, and another Chernoff bound gives
\mathbb{P}[B_{c}>2\eta\Delta]\leq\exp(-\Delta/C)
.

Combining the contributions from
N(v)
,
A_{c}
, and
B_{c}
, we get the required bound, since
2\eta\Delta+2\eta\Delta+2\eta\Delta<\varepsilon\Delta
.
∎

5.2.
Lower bound on the number of available colors

Recall that (
5.1
) asserts a two-sided bound on the number of available colors
A(X_{T},v)
. As a first step, we will establish one side of the bound. We will first prove the result for
X^{*}
at a fixed time and then transfer it to
X
over an entire bounded-length interval.

Lemma 5.21
.

For every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, there exist constants
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
C=C(\delta,\varepsilon)
such that the following holds. Let
v\in V
and let
T\in[C,C+1]
.
Then,

\mathbb{P}[|A(X^{*}_{T},v)|\leq(1-\varepsilon)ke^{-d(v)/k}]=\exp(-\Delta/C).

Proof.

Condition on

\mathcal{F}_{v}:=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X^{*}_{t}(x):x\notin B_{2}(v),\ t\in\mathbb{R}}}\right).

Given
\mathcal{F}_{v}
, the colors
\{X^{*}_{T}(w):w\in N(v)\}
are independent, because in the directed graph
G_{\mathrm{in}}(v,3)
the branches below distinct neighbors of
v
are disjoint.

Fix
w\in N(v)
and
c\in[k]
. Let
\tau_{w}(T)
be the last successful refresh time of
w
before time
T
in
X^{*}
; this time exists almost surely. Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
and on
\tau_{w}(T)
,

\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{T}(w)=c\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\tau_{w}(T)]=\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}(T)^{-}},w)\}}{|A(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}(T)^{-}},w)|}\leq\frac{1}{\rho k}

by
5.5
. Hence

\sup_{c\in[k]}\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{T}(w)=c\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\leq\frac{1}{\rho k}.

Applying
Lemma

5.10
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
gives

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|A(X^{*}_{T},v)|\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}}}\right]\geq k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-1/(\rho k)}{e}}}\right)^{d(v)/k}=ke^{-d(v)/k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}}}\right)}}\right).

Since
d(v)/k\leq 1/(1+\delta)
, the error term
O(1/\Delta)
is uniform. For
\Delta
large enough,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|A(X^{*}_{T},v)|\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}}}\right]\geq(1-\varepsilon/2)ke^{-d(v)/k}.

A second application of
Lemma

5.10
yields

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|A(X^{*}_{T},v)|\leq(1-\varepsilon)ke^{-d(v)/k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}}}\right]\leq e^{-\Delta/C},

and averaging over
\mathcal{F}_{v}
proves the statement for
X^{*}_{T}
.
∎

Lemma 5.22
.

For every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, there exist constants
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon)
,
C=C(\delta,\varepsilon)
, and
R=R(\varepsilon,\delta)
such that the following holds. Let
v\in V
and suppose that
X_{0}
is
400
-above-suspicion for radius
R
at
v
. Then,

\mathbb{P}[\exists t\in[C,C+1]:|A(X_{T},v)|\leq(1-2\varepsilon)ke^{-d(v)/k}]=\exp(-\Delta/C).

Moreover, the same bound also holds with
X
replaced by
X^{*}
on
G_{\mathrm{in}}(v,3)
(in fact, without any assumptions on
X_{0}
).

Proof.

We first prove the statement for
X^{*}
. Choose a mesh size
h:=\frac{\varepsilon}{100\Delta}.

The interval
[C,C+1]
contains
O(\Delta/\varepsilon)
mesh points. By
Lemma

5.21
, the probability that the lower bound fails at one of those mesh points is at most
e^{-\Delta/C}
. Between two consecutive mesh points, the quantity
|A(X^{*}_{t},v)|
can change only when a vertex in
B_{2}(v)
refreshes successfully, and each such refresh changes
|A(X^{*}_{t},v)|
by at most
1
. The total successful-refresh rate in
B_{2}(v)
is at most
2\Delta^{2}
, so the number of such refreshes in an interval of length
h
is Poisson with mean
O(\varepsilon\Delta)
. By (
5.3
) and a union bound over the
O(\Delta/\varepsilon)
mesh intervals, with probability at least
1-e^{-\Delta/C}
each mesh interval contains at most
\varepsilon\Delta/4
such refreshes. On this event, if the lower bound holds at the mesh points with parameter
\varepsilon
, then it holds on the whole interval with parameter
2\varepsilon
.

For the original chain
X
, the statement now follows from
Proposition

5.20
and a similar mesh argument.
∎

5.3.
The bias field and its properties

This is the most technically difficult part in the proof of
Theorem

5.7
and also the one which differs the most from
[
12
]
due to the different bias field
P
and the auxiliary process
Z^{*}
.

The following notion of “bias field’ is similar, but not identical to the function
P(X,v,c)
in
[
12
, Section 2.4]
, as we explain below.

Definition 5.23

(Bias field)
.

Let
U=(U_{t})
be either the original metropolis chain
X
, the recursive directed chain
X^{*}
, or the auxiliary process
Z^{*}
. Let
v\in V
, let
T>0
, and let
c\in[k]
. For each
w\in N(u)
, let
\tau_{w}^{U}(T)
be the last successful refresh time of
w
before time
T
; in no such refresh has occurred, set
\tau_{w}^{U}(T)=-\infty
. Define

P(U_{T},v,c)=\sum_{w\in N(u)}\mathbf{1}\{\tau_{w}^{U}(T)>-\infty\}\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A_{v}(U_{\tau_{w}^{U}(T)^{-}},w)\}}{|A_{v}(U_{\tau_{w}^{U}(T)^{-}},w)|}.

Remark 5.24
.

This is slightly different from Hayes’ function
P(X_{T},v,c)

[
12
, Section 2.4]
, in which all colors on the right hand side are only accessed at time
T
with no mention of
\tau_{w}(T)
. We believe that the version above is what is actually needed for the later arguments in
[
12
]
as well.

Remark 5.25
.

In all our applications,
\tau_{w}^{U}(T)>-\infty
almost surely, so we will omit this factor for notational convenience. Additionally, almost surely,
A_{v}(U_{\tau_{w}^{U}(T)^{-}},w)=A_{v}(U_{\tau_{w}^{U}(T)},w)
, so we will not distinguish between
\tau^{-}
and
\tau
. Finally, note that for the processes
X^{*}
and
Z^{*}
on the directed graph
G^{*}=G_{\mathrm{in}}(v,3)
, for all
w\in N(v)
,

A_{v}(X_{t}^{*},w)=A_{G^{*}}(X_{t}^{*},w)=A_{G^{*}}(Z_{t}^{*},w)=A_{v}(Z_{t}^{*},w),

where we have used that
X_{t}^{*}=Z_{t}^{*}
outside
B_{1}(v)
.

The next lemma provides a comparison between the bias fields for
X^{*}
and
Z^{*}
.

Lemma 5.26
.

For every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
there exist constants
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
C=C(\delta,\varepsilon)
such that the following holds. Let
v\in V
and
T\in[C,C+1]
. Then one can couple
X^{*}
and
Z^{*}
so that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigl|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in N(v):\ \tau_{w}^{X^{*}}(T)\neq\tau_{w}^{Z^{*}}(T)}}\right\}\bigr|>\varepsilon\Delta}}\right]\leq e^{-\Delta/C}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exists c\in[k]:\ \bigl|P(X^{*}_{T},v,c)-P(Z^{*}_{T},v,c)\bigr|>\varepsilon}}\right]\leq e^{-\Delta/C}.

Proof.

Condition on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
. For each
w\in N(v)
, couple the refreshes of
X^{*}
and
Z^{*}
by a common Poisson point process on
[0,T]\times[0,1]
of intensity
1
: at a point
(t,s)
, the chain
X^{*}
refreshes
w
if
s\leq|A(X^{*}_{t},w)|/k
, the chain
Z^{*}
refreshes
w
if
s\leq\lambda_{w}(t)
, and whenever both refresh we use the same new color.

Let
B_{w}
be the event that before time
T
there is a point
(t,s)
at which exactly one of the two chains refreshes
w
. If
B_{w}
does not occur, then the two chains use exactly the same refresh times and colors at
w
, so in particular
\tau_{w}^{X^{*}}(T)=\tau_{w}^{Z^{*}}(T)
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
, the event
B_{w}
is dominated by a Poisson random variable with mean

\mu_{w}:=\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl||A(X^{*}_{t},w)|-\mathbb{E}[|A(X^{*}_{t},w)|\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\bigr|\,dt.

Hence

\mathbb{P}[B_{w}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mu_{w}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}].

For each fixed
t
, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
the variable
|A(X^{*}_{t},w)|
is a Lipschitz function of the independent colors of the vertices in
N(w)\setminus\{v\}
, and
Lemma

5.10
implies

\mathrm{Var}\bigl(|A(X^{*}_{t},w)|\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}\bigr)=O(k).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigl||A(X^{*}_{t},w)|-\bigg(\mathbb{E}[|A(X^{*}_{t},w)|\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\bigr|\bigg)\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}}}\right]=O(\sqrt{k}),

and integrating over
t\in[0,T]
yields

\mathbb{P}[B_{w}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\Delta}}.

For distinct
w\in N(v)
, the events
B_{w}
are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{F}_{v}
, because the branches below the different neighbors of
v
are disjoint. Hence the total number

B:=\sum_{w\in N(v)}\mathbf{1}_{B_{w}}

is conditionally stochastically dominated by a binomial random variable with parameters
\Delta
and
C/\sqrt{\Delta}
. Its mean is
O(\sqrt{\Delta})
, so a Chernoff bound gives

\mathbb{P}[B>\varepsilon\Delta\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\leq e^{-\Delta/C},

and the statement follows from the law of total probability.
On the complement of this event, the definitions of
P(X^{*}_{T},u,c)
and
P(Z^{*}_{T},u,c)
differ in at most
\varepsilon\Delta
summands, and by
Lemma

5.22
each summand is bounded by
C/\Delta
. Thus

\sup_{c\in[k]}\bigl|P(X^{*}_{T},u,c)-P(Z^{*}_{T},u,c)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon.

This proves the claim.
∎

Next, we compare the bias fields for
X
and
X^{*}
. This is the analogue of
[
12
, Corollary 30]
for our version of bias field.

Lemma 5.27
.

For every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
there exist constants
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
C=C(\delta,\varepsilon)
such that the following holds. Let
v\in V
and
T\in[C,C+1]
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exists c\in[k]:\ \bigl|P(X^{*}_{T},v,c)-P(X_{T},v,c)\bigr|>\varepsilon}}\right]\leq e^{-\Delta/C}.

Proof.

For lightness of notation, let
\tau_{y}:=\tau_{y}^{X}(T)
and
\tau_{y}^{*}:=\tau_{y}^{X^{*}}(T)
.
Let
D=\{y\in N(u)\mid\tau_{y}\neq\tau_{y}^{*}\text{ or }\tau_{y}<0\}
. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|P(X_{t}^{*},u,c)-P(X_{t},u,c)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in N(u)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{y}^{*}}^{*},y)\}}{|A(X_{\tau_{y}^{*}}^{*},y)|}-\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{y}},y)\}}{|A(X_{\tau_{y}},y)|}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in N(u)\cap D}+\sum_{y\in N(u)\setminus D}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{y}^{*}}^{*},y)\}}{|A(X_{\tau_{y}^{*}}^{*},y)|}-\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{y}},y)\}}{|A(X_{\tau_{y}},y)|}}}\right|

We bound the first sum

\sum_{y\in N(u)\cap D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{y}^{*}}^{*},y)\}}{|A(X_{\tau_{y}^{*}}^{*},y)|}-\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X_{\tau_{y}},y)\}}{|A(X_{\tau_{y}},y)|}}}\right|\leq|N(u)\cap D|\cdot\frac{2}{A_{\mathrm{min}}}\leq\frac{2e\Delta\varepsilon}{k}\leq 6\varepsilon.

For the second sum, notice that if
y\notin D
, then
0<\tau_{y}=\tau_{y}^{*}
. At this point, we conclude using the same argument as in the proof of
[
12
, Corollary 30]
.
∎

We will prove local relations for the bias fields for
X,X^{*},Z^{*}
. We will first establish such a relation for
Z^{*}
, and then transfer it to
X^{*}
and
X
via the previously established comparison results.

Lemma 5.28
.

For every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, there exist constants
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
C=C(\delta,\varepsilon)
such that the following holds. Let
v\in V
, let
T\in[C,C+1]
, and let
c\in[k]
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|P(Z_{T}^{*},v,c)-\sum_{w\in N(v)}\frac{\exp(-P(X_{\tau_{w}}^{*},w,c))}{|A(X_{\tau_{w}}^{*},w)|}}}\right|>\varepsilon}}\right]=\exp(-\Delta/C),

where
\tau_{w}:=\tau_{w}^{Z^{*}}(T)
.

Proof.

Condition on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
and on the collection of last ring times

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tau_{w}:w\in N(v)}}\right\}.

For
w\in N(v)
, write

\xi_{w}:=\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A_{v}(Z^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)\}}{|A_{v}(Z^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)|}.

Then

P(Z^{*}_{T},v,c)=\sum_{w\in N(v)}\xi_{w}.

Because
Z^{*}
and
X^{*}
coincide on
S_{2}(v)
, the denominator is

|A_{u}(Z^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)|=|A_{u}(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)|,

where we emphasize that the time
\tau_{w}
on the right hand side is still
\tau_{w}^{Z^{*}}(T)
.
Moreover, for fixed
w
the event
\{c\in A_{v}(Z^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)\}
depends only on the colors of the vertices in
N(w)\setminus\{v\}
, and those vertices lie in pairwise disjoint branches for different
w
. Hence, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
and on the times
\tau_{w}
, the random variables
\xi_{w}
are independent over
w\in N(v)
.

Fix
w\in N(u)
and write
\tau=\tau_{w}=\tau_{w}^{Z^{*}}(T)
. For each
z\in N(w)\setminus\{v\}
, let
\sigma_{z}
be the last successful refresh time of
z
before
\tau
in the chain
Z^{*}
(or
X^{*}
, since these chains coincide outside of
B_{1}(v)
). Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{v}
and the times
\{\sigma_{z}:z\in N(w)\setminus\{v\}\}
, the indicators

\mathbf{1}\{X^{*}_{\tau}(z)=c\}\qquad(z\in N(w)\setminus\{v\})

are independent, and

\mathbb{P}[X^{*}_{\tau}(z)=c\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\{\sigma_{y}\}]=\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X^{*}_{\sigma_{z}},z)\}}{|A(X^{*}_{\sigma_{z}},z)|}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[c\in A_{v}(X^{*}_{\tau},w)\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\tau,\{\sigma_{z}\}}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\prod_{z\in N(w)\setminus\{v\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\mathbf{1}\{c\in A(X^{*}_{\sigma_{z}},z)\}}{|A(X^{*}_{\sigma_{z}},z)|}}}\right)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-P(X^{*}_{\tau},w,c)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{z\in N(w)\setminus\{v\}}\frac{1}{|A(X^{*}_{\sigma_{z}},z)|^{2}}}}\right)}}\right).

On the event from
Lemma

5.22
, every denominator is at least
(1-2\varepsilon)ke^{-1/(1+\delta)}
, so the quadratic error is
O(\Delta/k^{2})=O(1/\Delta)
. Hence

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[c\in A_{v}(X^{*}_{\tau},w)\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\tau,\{\sigma_{z}\}}}\right]=e^{-P(X^{*}_{\tau},w,c)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}}}\right).

Dividing by the denominator
|A_{v}(X^{*}_{\tau},w)|
and taking conditional expectations gives

\Biggl|\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi_{w}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\{\tau_{y}:y\in N(v)\}}}\right]-\frac{e^{-P(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w,c)}}{|A_{v}(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)|}\Biggr|\leq\frac{C}{\Delta^{2}}.

Summing over
w\in N(v)
yields

(5.4)

\Biggl|\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P(Z^{*}_{T},v,c)\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\{\tau_{y}\}}}\right]-\sum_{w\in N(v)}\frac{e^{-P(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w,c)}}{|A_{v}(X^{*}_{\tau_{w}},w)|}\Biggr|\leq\frac{C}{\Delta}.

Finally, on the same lower-bound event each
\xi_{w}
is bounded by
C/\Delta
, and the family
(\xi_{w})_{w\in N(u)}
is conditionally independent. Hoeffding’s inequality therefore gives

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Bigl|P(Z^{*}_{T},v,c)-\mathbb{E}[P(Z^{*}_{T},v,c)\mid\mathcal{F}_{v},\{\tau_{y}\}]\Bigr|>\varepsilon/2\ \middle|\ \mathcal{F}_{v},\{\tau_{y}\}}}\right]\leq e^{-\Delta/C}.

Combining this with (
5.4
) proves the lemma.
∎

.

and

Proof.

This follows by combining

Corollary 5.30
.

For every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, there exist constants
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}(\delta,\varepsilon)
,

, and

such that the following holds. Let

, and assume that

is
400
-above-suspicion for radius

at

, the following holds simultaneously for every vertex

, every time

\ell_{j},\ \log m_{j}\leq 3^{j}\bigl({C_{\mathrm{cp}}}^{2}+\log(200{C_{\mathrm{cp}}})+2\bigr).

In particular,

\ell_{100{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\leq\exp(O({C_{\mathrm{cp}}})),\qquad m_{100{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\leq\exp(\exp(O({C_{\mathrm{cp}}}))).

Proof.

Set

A:=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{200{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}{\gamma}}}\right)={C_{\mathrm{cp}}}^{2}+\log(200{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}),\qquad u_{j}:=3^{j}(A+2).

We claim that for all
j
,

\ell_{j}\leq u_{j},\qquad\log m_{j}\leq u_{j}.

This is immediate for
j=0
, since

\ell_{0}=2\leq A+2=u_{0},\qquad\log m_{0}=0\leq u_{0}.

Now assume
\ell_{j},\log m_{j}\leq u_{j}
. Since
u_{j}\geq u_{0}=A+2
, we also have
A\leq u_{j}
. Hence

\ell_{j+1}=2\ell_{j}+\frac{A+\log m_{j}}{\log 25}\leq 2u_{j}+\frac{2u_{j}}{\log 25}<3u_{j}=u_{j+1},

because
\log 25>2
.

Also,

\log m_{j+1}=A+\log m_{j}+\frac{\ell_{j+1}}{100}\leq u_{j}+u_{j}+\frac{u_{j+1}}{100}<3u_{j}=u_{j+1}.

This closes the induction.

Finally,

\log m_{100{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\leq 3^{100{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}(A+2)=\exp(O({C_{\mathrm{cp}}})),

since
A={C_{\mathrm{cp}}}^{2}+\log(200{C_{\mathrm{cp}}})=O({C_{\mathrm{cp}}}^{2})
. Exponentiating gives

m_{100{C_{\mathrm{cp}}}}\leq\exp(\exp(O({C_{\mathrm{cp}}}))).\qed

Appendix B
Proof of
6.1

Let
\kappa:=\varepsilon m
, and for notational simplicity assume that
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
; inserting floors and ceilings does not change the argument. Also write

A_{t}:=\sum_{i\leq t}X_{i},\qquad B_{t}:=\sum_{i\leq t}Y_{i},\qquad\mu_{t}:=\sum_{i\leq t}p_{i}.

Then
R=A_{j}
, while
\operatorname{index}(R)
is the first time
B_{t}
reaches the value
R
.

The proof has two ingredients. First, for a uniformly random
j
, the monotone sequence
(p_{i})
is typically almost constant on the window
[j-2\kappa,j+2\kappa]
. Second, for such a
j
, the random index
\operatorname{index}(R)
is typically within distance
\kappa
of
j
. Putting these together gives the desired comparison between
p_{j}
and the nearby values
p_{\operatorname{index}(R)\pm\kappa}
.

We begin with the first point. For
2\kappa+1\leq j\leq m-2\kappa
, set

\kappa_{j}:=p_{j-2\kappa}-p_{j+2\kappa}\geq 0.

Since
(p_{i})
is nonincreasing, these
\kappa_{j}
measure the total variation of the sequence on windows of radius
2\kappa
. Summing over all interior
j
, we obtain a telescoping estimate:

\sum_{j=2\kappa+1}^{m-2\kappa}\kappa_{j}=\sum_{j=2\kappa+1}^{m-2\kappa}p_{j-2\kappa}-\sum_{j=2\kappa+1}^{m-2\kappa}p_{j+2\kappa}=\sum_{i=1}^{m-4\kappa}p_{i}-\sum_{i=4\kappa+1}^{m}p_{i}.

Hence

\sum_{j=2\kappa+1}^{m-2\kappa}\kappa_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{4\kappa}p_{i}-\sum_{i=m-4\kappa+1}^{m}p_{i}\leq 4\kappa,

because each
p_{i}\leq 1
. Since
j
is uniform in
[m]
, this implies

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j\notin[2\kappa+1,m-2\kappa]}}\right)\leq\frac{4\kappa}{m}=4\varepsilon,

and, by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa_{j}>\varepsilon^{1/2}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{m\varepsilon^{1/2}}\sum_{j=2\kappa+1}^{m-2\kappa}\kappa_{j}\leq\frac{4\kappa}{m\varepsilon^{1/2}}=4\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Therefore, with probability at least
1-4\varepsilon-4\varepsilon^{1/2}
, we have simultaneously

j\in[2\kappa+1,m-2\kappa]\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{j-2\kappa}-p_{j+2\kappa}\leq\varepsilon^{1/2}.

On this event, monotonicity of
(p_{i})
yields

(B.1)

p_{j}-\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq p_{j+2\kappa}\leq p_{j}\leq p_{j-2\kappa}\leq p_{j}+\varepsilon^{1/2}.

We now turn to the second point. Fix
j\in[2\kappa+1,m-2\kappa]
. We claim that with high probability,

(B.2)

|\operatorname{index}(R)-j|\leq\kappa.

To see this, let

\lambda:=\frac{\gamma\kappa}{4},

and consider the event

E_{j}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{|A_{j}-\mu_{j}|\leq\lambda,\ |B_{j-\kappa}-\mu_{j-\kappa}|\leq\lambda,\ |B_{j+\kappa}-\mu_{j+\kappa}|\leq\lambda}}\right\}.

Since
A_{j}
,
B_{j-\kappa}
, and
B_{j+\kappa}
are sums of independent Bernoulli variables, Hoeffding’s inequality gives

(B.3)

\mathbb{P}(E_{j}^{c})\leq 6\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{m}}}\right)=6\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}\kappa^{2}}{8m}}}\right)=6\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}}}\right).

Assume now that
E_{j}
occurs. Because every
p_{i}\geq\gamma
, we have

\mu_{j}-\mu_{j-\kappa}=\sum_{i=j-\kappa+1}^{j}p_{i}\geq\gamma\kappa,\qquad\mu_{j+\kappa}-\mu_{j}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{j+\kappa}p_{i}\geq\gamma\kappa.

Using
R=A_{j}
, it follows that

B_{j-\kappa}\leq\mu_{j-\kappa}+\lambda\leq\mu_{j}-\gamma\kappa+\lambda=\mu_{j}-\frac{3\gamma\kappa}{4}<\mu_{j}-\lambda\leq A_{j}=R,

and similarly

B_{j+\kappa}\geq\mu_{j+\kappa}-\lambda\geq\mu_{j}+\gamma\kappa-\lambda=\mu_{j}+\frac{3\gamma\kappa}{4}>\mu_{j}+\lambda\geq A_{j}=R.

Thus
B_{j-\kappa}<R\leq B_{j+\kappa}
. Since
B_{t}
is nondecreasing and increases only by steps of size
0
or
1
, it must hit the value
R
at some time between
j-\kappa+1
and
j+\kappa
, i.e.

|\operatorname{index}(R)-j|\leq\kappa,

which proves (
B.2
) on the event
E_{j}
.

Finally, we combine the two ingredients. Suppose that
j
satisfies the conclusion of the first part, and that
E_{j}
also occurs. Then (
B.2
) implies

j\leq\operatorname{index}(R)+\kappa\leq j+2\kappa,\qquad j-2\kappa\leq\operatorname{index}(R)-\kappa\leq j.

Since
(p_{i})
is nonincreasing, this gives

(B.4)

p_{j+2\kappa}\leq p_{\operatorname{index}(R)+\kappa}\leq p_{\operatorname{index}(R)-\kappa}\leq p_{j-2\kappa}.

Combining (
B.4
) with (
B.1
), we conclude that

p_{j}-\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq p_{\operatorname{index}(R)+\kappa}\leq p_{\operatorname{index}(R)-\kappa}\leq p_{j}+\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Recalling that
\kappa=\varepsilon m
, this is exactly

p_{j}-\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq p_{\operatorname{index}(R)+\varepsilon m}\leq p_{\operatorname{index}(R)-\varepsilon m}\leq p_{j}+\varepsilon^{1/2}.

It remains only to estimate the failure probability. The first part fails with probability at most
4\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^{1/2}
, while by (
B.3
), conditional on any fixed
j
, the event
E_{j}
fails with probability at most

6\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}}}\right).

Hence

\mathbb{P}(\text{failure})\leq 4\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^{1/2}+6\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}}}\right).

Under the regime
m\gg\varepsilon^{-1}\gg\gamma^{-1}
, this quantity tends to
0
. Therefore the stated inequalities hold with high probability. ∎
