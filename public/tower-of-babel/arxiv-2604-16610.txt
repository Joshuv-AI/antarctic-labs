Fairness Constraints in High-Dimensional Generalized Linear Models Journal: Computational Statistics & Data Analysis
Yixiao Lin, James G. Booth

Abstract
Machine learning models often inherit biases from historical data, raising critical concerns about fairness and accountability. Conventional fairness interventions typically require access to sensitive attributes like gender or race, but privacy and legal restrictions frequently limit their use. To address this challenge, we propose a framework that infers sensitive attributes from auxiliary features and integrates fairness constraints into model training. Our approach mitigates bias while preserving predictive accuracy, offering a practical solution for fairness-aware learning. Empirical evaluations validate its effectiveness, contributing to the advancement of more equitable algorithmic decision-making.



1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Algorithmic decision-making pervades our lives, profoundly shaping outcomes in
areas such as email filtering, image tagging, personalized news feeds, credit
scoring, and job assessments. While these systems offer efficiency and
convenience, they also raise significant concerns regarding transparency,
accountability, and fairness. These concerns are especially pressing when the
historical data used to train such systems reflect biases associated with
sensitive attributes, such as gender, race, or religion. When unchecked, these
biases can lead to systematically unfair treatment of certain groups and
perpetuate existing societal inequities.

A large body of work in fair machine learning seeks to mitigate these risks by
constraining or modifying learning algorithms so that predictions satisfy
group-based fairness notions, such as demographic parity or equalized odds.
However, two practical challenges remain relatively underexplored. First, most
methods assume that sensitive attributes are directly observed and available
throughout the development and deployment of a system. In many real-world
applications, this assumption fails due to privacy concerns, ethical
considerations, or legal restrictions
(
8
)
. Second,
modern applications often involve high-dimensional feature spaces in which
only a small subset of predictors is truly informative. Variable selection is
therefore essential; yet, the interaction between feature selection, fairness
constraints, and unobserved sensitive attributes is poorly understood.

In this paper, we address these two challenges simultaneously. We consider
settings in which sensitive attributes are multi-category, unobserved, and
must be inferred from auxiliary predictors, while the main prediction task is
modeled using generalized linear models (GLMs) in either low- or
high-dimensional regimes. Our goal is to construct prediction rules that are
both accurate and fair with respect to the latent sensitive attribute, while
retaining the interpretability and sparsity benefits of modern variable
selection methods.

1.2
Related work

Existing approaches to algorithmic fairness can be broadly categorized into three classes:

1.

Preprocessing approaches.

These methods modify the training data before model fitting in order to mitigate discriminatory patterns, for example, through reweighting, resampling, or data transformation to balance the representation of sensitive groups
(
11
;
15
;
10
)
.

2.

In-processing approaches.

These methods incorporate fairness constraints, regularization terms, or other fairness-aware objectives directly into the learning procedure so that model training jointly optimizes predictive performance and fairness criteria
(
32
;
1
;
16
;
19
)
.

3.

Post-processing approaches.

These methods adjust the outputs of an already trained model to satisfy fairness criteria, which is especially useful when retraining the model or modifying the original learning algorithm is infeasible
(
14
;
27
;
28
)
.

Despite their diversity, most of these methods rely on access to sensitive attributes, either to define fairness criteria or to implement interventions. When such attributes are unavailable, fairness becomes substantially more challenging. Existing work has considered fairness under missing protected attributes, proxy-sensitive features, or latent demographic structure
(
7
;
35
;
34
;
22
;
31
;
12
;
26
)
. For instance,
35
proposes a probabilistic graphical model in which a variational autoencoder estimates latent sensitive attributes from relevant features, and fairness interventions are then applied to the learned representation. While this proxy-based strategy is promising, it typically assumes prior knowledge of which features are relevant for recovering the sensitive attribute, an assumption that may be unrealistic in complex applications with many covariates and intricate dependency structures. More broadly, different fairness notions may be incompatible with one another or with predictive calibration in general settings
(
18
;
27
;
5
)
.

Fairness in high-dimensional settings, particularly when combined with
variable selection, has also received limited attention.
30

studies fairness-aware clustering and introduces
\ell_{1}
-regularization to
promote sparsity in feature selection. However, analogous developments for
supervised learning—such as regression or classification with fairness
constraints and automatic variable selection—are far less mature.
High-dimensional predictors exacerbate the difficulty of ensuring fairness:
not only may many features carry implicit information about group membership,
but selecting a small subset of predictors can itself introduce or amplify
disparities if it preferentially retains group-informative variables.
For broader overviews of fairness in machine learning, see
24
and
6
.

1.3
Contributions

In response to these challenges, we develop a unified framework for
fairness-aware generalized linear models when multi-category sensitive
attributes are unobserved and predictors may be high-dimensional. Our main
contributions are threefold.

1.

Latent sensitive attribute estimation via mixture models.

Our first contribution is to model the unobserved sensitive attribute through a latent mixture structure, estimated via an EM-type procedure
(
9
;
23
)
. We assume that a subset of predictors depends on an unobserved
multi-category sensitive attribute and model this dependence using either
Gaussian mixture models or mixtures of product-multinomials. We derive
EM-based estimators for the corresponding parameters and use the posterior
class probabilities as estimates of the sensitive attribute. To improve
robustness when many predictors are only weakly related to the sensitive
attribute, we also propose a simple screening strategy that estimates the
sensitive attribute from each candidate predictor separately and selects the estimate that yields the best
predictive performance or fairness according to an appropriate criterion.

2.

Fairness-aware GLMs with variable selection.
Given the
estimated sensitive attribute, we regress the predictors on its posterior
expectation and construct residuals that are less informative about group
membership. We then fit fairness-aware GLMs using these residuals: a
constrained least-squares formulation for continuous responses that limits
the coefficient of determination obtained by regressing predictions on the
estimated sensitive attribute, and a penalized logistic regression
formulation for binary responses that incorporates a fairness penalty
based on centered correlations. In high-dimensional settings, we integrate
this fairness machinery with the SEMMS empirical Bayes variable selection
procedure
(
3
)
, yielding sparse, fairness-aware GLMs that
automatically select a small set of predictive features.

3.

Theoretical and empirical characterization of
accuracy-fairness trade-offs.
Compared with existing fairness-aware learning approaches
(
20
;
32
;
34
)
, our method addresses the practically important setting in which the sensitive attribute is not directly observed. We study the conditions under which the
mixture models for the predictors are identifiable and derive explicit
expressions and bounds for the probability of correctly recovering the
sensitive attribute under both Gaussian and categorical mixtures. For
regression, we obtain closed-form expressions for the coefficients of
determination before and after regressing out the sensitive attribute,
thereby quantifying the loss of predictive power induced by fairness
constraints and characterizing the fundamental accuracy-fairness
trade-off in our framework. Through extensive simulations and applications
to benchmark datasets, including ADULT, COMPAS, and ARRHYTHMIA, we validate
these theoretical insights and show that our approach can substantially
reduce group disparities with only a modest loss in predictive accuracy.

1.4
Notation and problem setup

We now formalize the problem setup and introduce the notation used throughout the
paper. Each
p
-dimensional vector is treated as a column vector and identified
with a
p\times 1
matrix. Let
n
denote the number of observations. The

i
th observation is denoted by
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

is the
p
-dimensional feature vector and
y_{i}
is the response. The response
can be either continuous or binary, i.e.
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
or

y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Let

\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{X}=[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}

denote the response vector and feature matrix, respectively.

We assume the existence of an unobserved multi-category sensitive attribute

A_{i}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
for each observation. For convenience, we also use
one-hot encoding

\boldsymbol{a}_{i}=(a_{i1},\dots,a_{iK})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{K},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{ik}=1,

and collect these into the
n\times K
matrix

\mathbf{A}=[\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}]^{\top}
. The sensitive attribute
A_{i}
is not
observed, but we assume that a subset of predictors carries information about
it. Specifically, we partition the columns of
\mathbf{X}
as

\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{X}^{\boldsymbol{a}},\mathbf{X}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}}]
, where
\mathbf{X}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
consists of predictors
whose distribution depends on the sensitive attribute and

\mathbf{X}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}}
consists of predictors that are conditionally independent
of
A_{i}
.

Our main prediction model is a generalized linear model. Let

\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}(y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and
g(\cdot)
denote a link function. Then we consider linear predictors of the form

g(\mu_{i})=\eta_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}x_{ij},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})^{\top}
is a vector of regression
coefficients. We study two regimes:

1.

a
low-dimensional
setting in which
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is an unknown but
fixed parameter vector and
p<n
; and

2.

a
high-dimensional
setting in which
p
may exceed
n
and
sparsity is induced through an empirical Bayes prior of the form

\beta_{j}=u_{j}\gamma_{j}
, where
u_{j}\sim N(\mu_{u},\sigma^{2})
and

\gamma_{j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
with

\Pr(\gamma_{j}=g)=\pi_{g}
for
g\in\{-1,0,1\}
. In this case, the

j
th predictor is effectively included in the model only if

\gamma_{j}\neq 0
, as determined by the SEMMS algorithm

(
3
)
.

To estimate the latent sensitive attribute, we model the rows of

\mathbf{X}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
as independent draws from a finite mixture distribution, the
components of which correspond to the levels of
A_{i}
. In particular, we assume that

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}\mid A_{i}=k\sim f_{k}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}),\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(2)

where
f_{k}
is either a multivariate Gaussian density with component-specific
means (and possibly covariances) or a product-multinomial model with
component-specific probability vectors. Writing

\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{K})^{\top}
for the mixing proportions, the marginal density
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}
is then

f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}).

We use the EM algorithm to obtain maximum likelihood estimates of

\boldsymbol{p}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, and we take the resulting posterior
probabilities

\hat{a}_{ik}=\Pr(A_{i}=k\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}),\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

as estimates of the entries of
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
. Collecting these into

\hat{\mathbf{A}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}]^{\top}
, we then regress
\mathbf{X}
on

\hat{\mathbf{A}}
and compute the residual matrix

\hat{\mathbf{U}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}]^{\top}
. The final step is to solve an
optimization problem that balances fairness, as measured by the dependence of
predictions on
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
, against predictive accuracy, as measured by the
ability of
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
to predict
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Each of these steps is explained in more detail in Sections
2

and
3
. Section
4
presents results from
simulation studies, while Section
5
demonstrates the approach
on real datasets. Section
6
concludes with a discussion of
our findings and directions for future work.

2
Methodology

In this section, we describe our three-stage procedure: (i) estimating the
latent sensitive attribute from auxiliary predictors using mixture models;
(ii) constructing residualized predictors that are less informative about
group membership; and (iii) fitting fairness-aware generalized linear models
(GLMs), with optional variable selection via SEMMS in high-dimensional
settings.

2.1
Estimating the latent sensitive attribute

Given the parameters
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
, the posterior
probabilities for the latent classes are

a_{ik}=\Pr(A_{i}=k\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}})=\frac{p_{k}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}p_{\ell}f_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\ell})},\qquad k=1,\dots,K.

(3)

We collect these into the soft group-membership vector

\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}=(a_{i1},\dots,a_{iK})^{\top}
and estimate it by substituting
maximum likelihood estimates for
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
into
(
3
). Writing

\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}=(\hat{a}_{i1},\dots,\hat{a}_{iK})^{\top}
for the resulting estimate
of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}
, we obtain the estimated sensitive attribute matrix

\hat{\mathbf{A}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}]^{\top}
. We estimate model parameters using the EM algorithm
(
9
)
, a standard approach for latent-variable and finite-mixture models
(
23
)
.

EM algorithm.

At
iteration
t+1
, the E-step computes the current posterior probabilities

\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\hat{p}_{k}^{(t)}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}^{(t)})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\hat{p}_{\ell}^{(t)}f_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\ell}^{(t)})},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,\;k=1,\dots,K,

(4)

and the M-step updates the mixing proportions and component parameters via

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{k}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)},\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\log f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
. The EM iterations are repeated
until convergence. We denote the final estimates by

(\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K})
and set

\hat{a}_{ik}=\Pr\bigl(A_{i}=k\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}\bigr)

as in (
3
). Collecting the rows yields the estimated
sensitive attribute matrix
\hat{\mathbf{A}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}]^{\top}
.

In what follows we consider two specific choices for the component densities

f_{k}
: a Gaussian mixture model for continuous predictors and a product
multinomial mixture for categorical predictors.

2.1.1
Gaussian mixtures

When the predictors in
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}
are continuous, we use a Gaussian
mixture model with a shared covariance matrix. Specifically,

f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})=\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=|2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are the common covariance matrix.
The M-step updates (
6
) simplify to

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}},\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(t+1)})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(t+1)})^{\top}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}}.

(8)

2.1.2
Categorical mixtures

When
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}
consists of
D
independent categorical predictors with

m_{d}
levels for the
d
th predictor, we represent each predictor by

(m_{d}-1)
dummy variables and obtain a design matrix

\mathbf{X}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{\boldsymbol{a}}}
with

p_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}(m_{d}-1)
. For each component
k
, we let

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{kd}=(\pi_{kd1},\dots,\pi_{kdm_{d}})^{\top}
denote the probability
vector for the
d
th categorical predictor, with

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{d}}\pi_{kd\ell}=1
. The component distribution factorizes
as

f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m_{d}}\pi_{kd\ell}^{\,z_{id\ell}},

where
z_{id\ell}=1
if the
d
th categorical predictor of observation
i

takes its
\ell
th level and
z_{id\ell}=0
otherwise. Here

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{kd}\}_{d=1}^{D}
.

The M-step update (
6
) yields the usual weighted
multinomial maximum likelihood estimator:

\hat{\pi}_{kd\ell}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}z_{id\ell}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(t+1)}},\qquad k=1,\dots,K,\;d=1,\dots,D,\;\ell=1,\dots,m_{d}.

(9)

2.2
Selecting relevant predictors for mixture modeling

In practice, estimating
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
using all predictors can be inaccurate when
many columns of
\mathbf{X}
are weakly related to the sensitive attribute or not
related at all. Including such predictors inflates variability without
improving the identifiability or estimation accuracy of the mixture model. To
mitigate this, we adopt a simple screening strategy.

For each candidate predictor (or small subset of predictors)
j
, we fit a
mixture model using only the corresponding columns
\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
, compute
posterior probabilities
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{(j)}
, and obtain an estimate

\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}
. Using
\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}
, we carry out the residualization and
fairness-aware modeling steps described in Sections
2.3

and
2.4
–
2.5
, obtaining
predictions
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(j)}
for the primary response.

In regression problems, we select the estimate
\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}
that maximizes
the correlation between
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(j)}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
:

j^{\star}=\argmax_{j}\operatorname{Cor}(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{y}).

In binary classification problems, we instead use a group-wise mean distance
fairness measure. For a given
j
, let

w_{i}^{(j)}=\max_{k}\hat{a}_{ik}^{(j)}-\frac{1}{K},

and define the soft group
k
as

S_{k}^{(j)}=\{i:\hat{a}_{ik}^{(j)}\geq\hat{a}_{i\ell}^{(j)}\text{ for all }\ell\neq k\}.

We compute the maximum absolute difference between weighted group means:

\mathrm{MD}_{j}=\max_{1\leq k,\ell\leq K}\left|\frac{\sum_{i\in S_{k}^{(j)}}w_{i}^{(j)}y_{i}}{\sum_{i\in S_{k}^{(j)}}w_{i}^{(j)}}-\frac{\sum_{i\in S_{\ell}^{(j)}}w_{i}^{(j)}y_{i}}{\sum_{i\in S_{\ell}^{(j)}}w_{i}^{(j)}}\right|.

(10)

Smaller values of
\mathrm{MD}_{j}
indicate more similar performance across
the estimated groups. Depending on the application, we may choose
\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}

to either minimize
\mathrm{MD}_{j}
(emphasizing fairness) or to balance it
against predictive accuracy.

In the sequel, we assume that a particular estimate
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
has been
selected by one of the above criteria and describe how to construct fair
predictive models conditioned on
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
.

2.3
Residualization

The residualization step is intended to reduce the influence of variables most strongly associated with the latent sensitive structure, in the spirit of removing discriminatory or proxy information before downstream prediction
(
11
;
15
;
34
)
.

Given the estimated sensitive attribute matrix
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
, we seek predictors
that remain informative about
y
but are less directly associated with group
membership. To this end, we regress the full feature matrix
\mathbf{X}
on

\hat{\mathbf{A}}
. Specifically, we consider the linear model

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}+\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{U},

where
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional vector of ones,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is a

p
-dimensional intercept vector,
\mathbf{B}
is a
K\times p
matrix of regression
coefficients, and
\mathbf{U}
is an
n\times p
matrix of residuals. We estimate

(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{B})
by ordinary least squares, column-wise or jointly, and define the
residualized predictors as
\hat{\mathbf{U}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}]^{\top}
.

Intuitively,
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
contains the variation in
\mathbf{X}
that is orthogonal (in a
least-squares sense) to the estimated sensitive attribute
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
. By
design, linear functions of
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}
are less correlated with
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}

than linear functions of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, which makes them a natural basis for
fairness-aware modeling.

2.4
Fairness-aware regression

Our constrained formulation is related to prior fairness-constrained learning approaches in regression and classification
(
20
;
32
;
1
)
.

We first consider the case of a continuous response
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Using
the estimated sensitive attribute
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}
and residualized predictors

\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}
, we define the linear predictor

\eta_{i}=\beta_{0}+\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(11)

where
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is an intercept and

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are coefficient vectors.
The corresponding predictions are
\hat{y}_{i}=\eta_{i}
.

Our fairness criterion is based on the empirical coefficient of
determination obtained by regressing
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\dots,\hat{y}_{n})^{\top}

on
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
. Let
\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}
denote the sample
covariance matrices of the rows of
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
, respectively. It
can be shown (see
(
21
)
) that the
R^{2}
of the
regression of
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
on
(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\mathbf{U}})
and the proportion of variance
explained by
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
alone can be expressed in terms of

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}\boldsymbol{\beta}
. We
enforce fairness by constraining the fraction of explained variance
attributable to
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
to be at most
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

Formally, we solve the constrained least-squares problem

\min_{\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}\quad\text{subject to }R^{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\mathbf{A}})\leq\varepsilon,

(12)

where
R^{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\mathbf{A}})
is the coefficient of determination of the
regression of
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
on
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
. After centering the variables, problem
(
12
) is equivalent to minimizing

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})

(13)

subject to the quadratic constraint

(1-\varepsilon)\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\varepsilon\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq 0.

(14)

When
\varepsilon=0
, the constraint (
14
) forces

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{0}
, which corresponds to a regression model that is fair with
respect to
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
(predictions depend only on
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}
). For

\varepsilon
close to
1
, the constraint becomes weak, and the solution
approximates the unconstrained least-squares estimator. Intermediate values of

\varepsilon
trace out an explicit accuracy–fairness trade-off.

In the low-dimensional setting (
p<n
), the problem
(
13
)–(
14
) can be solved using standard
quadratically constrained quadratic programming. In the high-dimensional
setting (
p\gg n
), we combine this fairness formulation with the SEMMS
empirical Bayes variable selection prior described in
Section
1.4
, using the fairness constraint to guide
the selection toward predictors that are both predictive and less
group-informative.

2.5
Fairness-aware binary classification

We now consider binary responses
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Using the same linear
predictor as in (
11
), we define the success
probability using the logistic link:

\pi_{i}=\Pr(y_{i}=1\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\eta_{i})}.

Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n})^{\top}
. The standard logistic negative
log-likelihood is

\ell_{\mathrm{logit}}(\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{y_{i}\log\pi_{i}+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\pi_{i})\right\}.

To encourage fairness, we penalize the dependence between the predictions and
the estimated sensitive attribute. Following
33
, we use a
correlation-based measure

C(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})=\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\bigr\|_{2},

where
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}

is the centering matrix. Large values of
C(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
indicate that
the predicted probabilities differ substantially across groups, while small
values indicate more similar predictions.

We therefore minimize the penalized objective

\min_{\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ell_{\mathrm{logit}}(\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})+\lambda\,C(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}),

(15)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a tuning parameter that controls the trade-off
between predictive accuracy and fairness. When
\lambda=0
,
(
15
) reduces to standard logistic regression with

(\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})
as predictors. As
\lambda
increases, the penalty
forces
C(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
toward zero, yielding progressively fairer
classifiers at the cost of higher classification error.

Because
C(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
is non-smooth at the origin, we employ a smooth
approximation

C_{\delta}(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})=\sqrt{\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\delta},

for a small
\delta>0
, and optimize the resulting smooth objective using
gradient-based methods (e.g., quasi-Newton or adaptive first-order
algorithms). In high-dimensional settings, we again couple this fairness
penalty with the SEMMS prior to obtain sparse, fairness-aware logistic
regression models.

In both regression and classification, varying the tuning parameters

\varepsilon
in (
14
) and
\lambda
in
(
15
) yields explicit accuracy–fairness trade-off curves, as
illustrated in the simulation and real-data studies in
Sections
4
and
5
.

3
Theory

In this section, we develop theoretical results for the two main components of our framework. First, we study identifiability of the mixture model used to infer the sensitive attribute and characterize the accuracy with which the latent classes can be recovered. Although identifiability of finite Gaussian mixture models is well established in the literature
(
23
)
, our setting requires additional analysis because the sensitive attribute is unobserved and connected to the observed predictors through a generalized linear model. We consider both Gaussian and categorical mixtures, and show how classification performance depends on the separation between mixture components and the class prior probabilities.

Second, we examine how removing the linear dependence between the predictors and the latent sensitive attribute affects performance in a downstream regression task. Using
R^{2}
as a measure of explanatory power, we derive closed-form expressions that make the trade-off between predictive accuracy and fairness explicit.

3.1
Identifiability

While the identifiability of finite Gaussian mixture models is well understood, identifiability for categorical mixture models is more delicate and often poses substantial challenges.
We begin with a simple motivating example and then provide a general counting argument for the product-multinomial case.

Consider first the simplest categorical mixture predictor

x_{i}^{a}\;=\;a_{i}Z_{i1}+(1-a_{i})Z_{i2},\qquad a_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p),\quad Z_{i1}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\theta_{1}),\quad Z_{i2}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\theta_{2}).

This model involves
n_{\text{mix}}=3
parameters
(p,\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
, but the observable
x_{i}^{a}
takes only two possible values:
0
and
1
. Hence, the distribution of
x_{i}^{a}
yields only one free probability (since probabilities must sum to one). In other words, the model is over-parameterized relative to what can be learned from the data, and the parameters are not identifiable.

In the general case, suppose we have a
K
-component categorical mixture with
D
independent categorical variables, where the
d
th variable has
M_{d}
levels. The mixture model then contains

n_{\text{mix}}\;=\;(K-1)+K\sum_{d=1}^{D}(M_{d}-1)

free parameters:
K-1
mixture proportions and, for each of the
K
classes,
\sum_{d=1}^{D}(M_{d}-1)
free parameters in the conditional multinomial probabilities.
On the other hand, the joint distribution of the
D
categorical variables has
\prod_{d=1}^{D}M_{d}
possible combinations and therefore requires

n_{\text{joint}}\;=\;\prod_{d=1}^{D}M_{d}-1

parameters to be specified. For the mixture model to be identifiable from the joint distribution, the number of parameters implied by the mixture must not exceed the degrees of freedom in the joint distribution, that is,

\prod_{d=1}^{D}M_{d}-1\;\geq\;(K-1)+K\sum_{d=1}^{D}(M_{d}-1).

For example, if
K=2
and
D=2
, and both variables have the same number of levels
M_{1}=M_{2}=M
, this inequality requires
M\geq 4
; alternatively, if
K=2
and
D=3
, it suffices to have
M_{d}=2
(i.e., three binary variables).
This counting argument illustrates that, unless the product
\prod_{d}M_{d}
is large enough relative to the number of mixture components
K
, categorical mixtures may fail to be identifiable.

3.2
Accuracy in determining the sensitive variable: Gaussian mixture

We now turn to the accuracy with which the latent sensitive attribute can be recovered when the predictors follow a Gaussian mixture.
Throughout this subsection, we assume that the mixture parameters are known and focus on the probability that the posterior mode classifier correctly recovers the true component label.

Theorem 3.1

(Multivariate Gaussian mixture)
.

Consider the case in which

[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\mid A_{i}=k]\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

and define the minimum Mahalanobis separation

\delta_{\min}^{2}\;=\;\min_{1\leq k,\ell\leq K}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell}).

Let
\delta_{\min}=\sqrt{\delta_{\min}^{2}}
. Then:

1.

As the minimum separation between component means grows without bound,

\lim_{\delta_{\min}\to\infty}\hat{A}_{i}=A_{i}

almost surely; that is, the posterior classifier recovers the true latent class with a probability tending to one.

2.

The probability of correctly classifying an individual under the posterior mode rule is

\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=A_{i}\bigr)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\prod_{l\neq k}\left\{1-\Phi\left(\frac{1}{\|\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{kl}\|}\log\Big(\frac{p_{l}}{p_{k}}\Big)-\frac{\|\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{kl}\|}{2}\right)\right\},

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
and

\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{kl}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}
for all
k,l
, and
\Phi(\cdot)
denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.9.1
.

In the special case where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}
is univariate and

[x_{i}^{a}\mid A_{i}=j]\sim N(\mu_{j},\sigma_{e}^{2}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,K,

it is intuitive that the larger the separation between component means, the easier it becomes to identify the true latent class. The next result provides an explicit separation condition that guarantees a desired level of classification accuracy.

Theorem 3.2

(Univariate Gaussian mixture)
.

Let

\delta_{\min}=\min_{1\leq j,\ell\leq K}\frac{|\mu_{j}-\mu_{\ell}|}{\sigma_{e}}

denote the minimum pairwise
standardized
separation between component means in a univariate Gaussian mixture. Then:

1.

The probability of correctly classifying an individual under the posterior mode rule is

\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=A_{i}\bigr)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\prod_{l\neq k}\left\{1-\Phi\left(\frac{\sigma_{e}}{\mu_{k}-\mu_{l}}\log\Big(\frac{p_{l}}{p_{k}}\Big)-\frac{\mu_{k}-\mu_{l}}{2\sigma_{e}}\right)\right\}.

2.

Given a target error level
\alpha>0
, let
z_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)
,
p_{\max}=\max_{j}p_{j}
, and
p_{\min}=\min_{j}p_{j}
. Define

\alpha^{\prime}=1-(1-\alpha)^{1/(K-1)}.

If

\delta_{\min}=\sqrt{2\log\!\left(\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}\right)}+z_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{2}-z_{\alpha^{\prime}},

(16)

then

\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=A_{i}\bigr)>1-\alpha.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.9.2
.

Thus, for Gaussian mixtures, both the separation between component means and the imbalance in class probabilities jointly determine how accurately the latent sensitive attribute can be recovered from
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}
.

3.2.1
Accuracy in determining the sensitive variable: categorical mixture

We next consider a product-multinomial mixture model for which the predictors are categorical. Again, we assume that all mixture parameters are known and quantify the probability that the posterior mode classifier correctly identifies the true latent class.

Let
D
denote the number of categorical predictors, and let the
d
th predictor have
M_{d}
categories. For component
k
, let

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k,d}=(\theta_{k,d,1},\ldots,\theta_{k,d,M_{d}})^{\top}

denote the probability vector governing the distribution of the
d
th predictor, with

\theta_{k,d,j}=\Pr(X_{d}=j\mid A=k)
.

Theorem 3.3

(General categorical mixture)
.

Assume that all mixture parameters are known. The probability of correctly estimating the sensitive attribute from a product-multinomial mixture under the posterior mode rule is

\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=A_{i}\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\sum_{\text{all combinations }j^{*}}\left[\prod_{l\neq k}\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{\prod_{d=1}^{D}\frac{\theta_{k,d,j^{*}_{d}}}{\theta_{l,d,j^{*}_{d}}}>\frac{p_{l}}{p_{k}}\right\}\;\prod_{d=1}^{D}\theta_{k,d,j^{*}_{d}}\right],

where the inner sum runs over all possible category combinations

j^{*}=(j^{*}_{1},\ldots,j^{*}_{D})
with
j^{*}_{d}\in\{1,\ldots,M_{d}\}
, and

\mathbbm{1}\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function.
The proof is given in Appendix
A.10.1
.

In the simplest case,

X=aZ_{1}+(1-a)Z_{2},\qquad a\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p),\quad Z_{1}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\theta_{1}),\quad Z_{2}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\theta_{2}),

the expression becomes more explicit.

Theorem 3.4

(Binary categorical mixture)
.

In the binary case described above,

\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=A_{i}\bigr)=(m-n)\big(p\theta_{1}-(1-p)\theta_{2}\big)+n(2p-1)+(1-p),

where

m=\mathbbm{1}\!\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\geq\frac{1-p}{p}\right),\qquad n=\mathbbm{1}\!\left(\frac{1-\theta_{1}}{1-\theta_{2}}\geq\frac{1-p}{p}\right),

and
m,n\in\{0,1\}
are indicator variables (not the sample size).
The proof is given in Appendix
A.10.2
.

From this expression, we see that as the component probabilities become more extreme, for example,
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\to 1
or
p\to 0
(or
p\to 1
), we approach a best-case scenario with near-perfect classification. Otherwise, when the two components are not well separated and the prior probabilities are balanced, performance can be no better than random guessing.
Combined with the identifiability discussion in Section
3.1
, this highlights that the accurate recovery of the sensitive attribute from categorical predictors requires both sufficient component separation and an identifiable mixture model. This logic is summarized schematically in Figure
1
.

p
or
\theta
. The blue blocks indicate intermediate conclusions, while the red blocks represent the resulting probabilities.

3.3
Loss of predictive power and the accuracy–fairness trade-off

We now investigate how enforcing fairness by removing the linear association between the predictors and the sensitive attribute impacts predictive performance.
Following the procedure described in Section
2.3
, we regress the predictors on the estimated sensitive attribute and work with the residuals; here, we analyze the resulting loss in
R^{2}
.

Model 3.1
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
,

y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}}
,

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}}
denotes the block of predictors related to the sensitive attribute, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}}
denotes the remaining predictors.

Assume

[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\mid A_{i}=j]\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}),\quad j=1,\ldots,K,

which can be written compactly as

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{i},

with
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p_{a}}
,
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
the
K
-dimensional one-hot representation of
A_{i}
, and

\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}\times p_{a}}
.
Furthermore, assume
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz})
and define

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\mid A_{i}=k\sim N\bigl((\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k};\mathbf{0}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bigr),

with

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}\times p_{a}}
is the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{X}^{a}
,

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}\times p_{z}}
is the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{X}^{z}
, and

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}\times p_{z}}
is the cross-covariance between
\mathbf{X}^{a}
and
\mathbf{X}^{z}
. Here
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
is the covariance matrix of the one-hot sensitive attribute.

Let
R_{x}^{2}
denote the
R^{2}
when regressing
\boldsymbol{y}
on the full predictor vector
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}^{z})
, and let
R_{a}^{2}
denote the
R^{2}
after the fairness adjustment—i.e., after regressing
\boldsymbol{x}
on
\boldsymbol{a}
and using the residuals
\boldsymbol{u}
as predictors. The following result gives asymptotic expressions for both quantities.

Theorem 3.5

(Linear regression case)
.

Under Model
3.1
,

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

and

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

The proof is given in Appendix
A.11.1
.

In the basic case where
x_{i}^{a}
is univariate and
A_{i}
is binary, these expressions simplify substantially.

Theorem 3.6

(Univariate Gaussian case)
.

Consider the simplest version of Model
3.1
in which

[x_{i}\mid A_{i}=1]\sim N(\mu,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\qquad[x_{i}\mid A_{i}=0]\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\qquad A_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p),

and
y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
. Then

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\big(p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\big)}{\beta_{1}^{2}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which is the
R^{2}
obtained by regressing
y
on
x
, and

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which is the
R^{2}
obtained after the fairness adjustment, i.e., after regressing
y
on the residuals
u
from regressing
x
on
A
.
The proof is given in Appendix
A.11.2
.

From these expressions, we observe that the term
p(1-p)\mu^{2}
appears in both the numerator and denominator of
R_{x}^{2}
, but is absent from
R_{a}^{2}
. As
\mu
increases, corresponding to greater separation between the distributions of
x
for different values of
A
, this term grows, leading to the following behavior:

1.

As
\mu\to\infty
, the explanatory power of
x
increases and
R_{x}^{2}\to 1
, indicating that
x
essentially fully captures the variation in
y
.

2.

In contrast,
R_{a}^{2}
decreases toward zero because, after removing the effect of
A
, the remaining residual variation in
x
carries little information about
y
.

3.

When
\mu\to 0
, implying no separation between the groups,
R_{x}^{2}
and
R_{a}^{2}
become approximately equal, since
x
no longer meaningfully encodes the sensitive attribute.

This analysis highlights a fundamental accuracy–fairness trade-off: increasing
\mu
improves the predictive accuracy of models that exploit the sensitive structure in
x
, but once fairness interventions remove the component associated with
A
, the explanatory power of the remaining predictors diminishes, leading to smaller
R_{a}^{2}
. This is consistent with the broader literature showing that fairness constraints may induce nontrivial trade-offs with predictive accuracy or calibration
(
25
;
18
;
27
)
.

6
Conclucsion

In this paper, we propose a novel approach to fairness-aware variable selection by integrating sparsity and fairness constraints into a unified framework. Our method estimates sensitive attributes in the absence of explicit labels and removes their influence from predictive models to ensure fairness. Through theoretical analysis and empirical evaluations of Gaussian and categorical mixture models, we demonstrate that our approach effectively mitigates bias while preserving predictive performance.

Experimental results on benchmark datasets, including ADULT, COMPAS, and ARRHYTHMIA, confirm the trade-off between fairness and accuracy. With a proper choice of regularization parameters, our method significantly reduces bias with only a minor decrease in predictive accuracy, outperforming existing fairness-aware methods.

Future work will explore extending this framework to high-dimensional settings and scenarios where categorical and Gaussian variables are not independent, as well as investigating fairness-aware methods under adversarial conditions. By addressing fairness concerns without requiring explicit sensitive attributes, our approach contributes to the broader effort of developing ethical and interpretable machine learning models.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
3.2

We first treat the binary case and then extend the argument to the general
K
-class setting.

A.1.1
Binary case

Claim.

Suppose

[x_{i}\mid A_{i}=1]\sim N(\mu,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\quad[x_{i}\mid A_{i}=0]\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\quad A_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p),

and let

\hat{a}_{i}=\frac{p\,\phi(x_{i};\mu,\sigma_{e})}{p\,\phi(x_{i};\mu,\sigma_{e})+(1-p)\,\phi(x_{i};0,\sigma_{e})},

where
\phi(\cdot;\mu,\sigma_{e})
denotes the
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma_{e}^{2})
density. Then:

1.

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu/\sigma_{e}\to\infty}\hat{A}_{i}=A_{i}
almost surely, i.e., the posterior mode classifier recovers the true class with a probability tending to one.

2.

Given
\alpha>0
, if

\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{e}}\;\geq\;\max\!\left(\sqrt{2\log\!\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\right)+z_{\alpha}^{2}}\;-\;z_{\alpha},\;\sqrt{-2\log\!\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\right)+z_{\alpha}^{2}}\;-\;z_{\alpha}\right),

where
z_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)
, then

\Pr\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\}=A_{i}\bigr)>1-\alpha.

Proof.

It is convenient to work with a standardized version of the model. Define

x_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{x_{i}}{\sigma_{e}},\qquad\mu^{\prime}=\frac{\mu}{\sigma_{e}},

so that

[x_{i}^{\prime}\mid A_{i}=1]\sim N(\mu^{\prime},1),\qquad[x_{i}^{\prime}\mid A_{i}=0]\sim N(0,1).

We drop the prime in what follows and write
\mu
for
\mu^{\prime}
and
\sigma=1
.

Write
x_{i}
as

x_{i}=\begin{cases}\mu+\varepsilon_{i},&A_{i}=1,\\[2.0pt]
\varepsilon_{i},&A_{i}=0,\end{cases}\qquad\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,1).

(1) Perfect classification as
\mu\to\infty
.

If
A_{i}=1
, then

\hat{a}_{i}=\frac{p\exp\{-\varepsilon_{i}^{2}/2\}}{p\exp\{-\varepsilon_{i}^{2}/2\}+(1-p)\exp\{-x_{i}^{2}/2\}}=\frac{p}{p+(1-p)\exp\{-(x_{i}^{2}-\varepsilon_{i}^{2})/2\}}.

Using
x_{i}=\mu+\varepsilon_{i}
,

x_{i}^{2}-\varepsilon_{i}^{2}=(\mu+\varepsilon_{i})^{2}-\varepsilon_{i}^{2}=\mu^{2}+2\mu\varepsilon_{i}.

Hence

\hat{a}_{i}=\frac{1}{1+\Big(\dfrac{1-p}{p}\Big)\exp\{-(\mu^{2}+2\mu\varepsilon_{i})/2\}}.

As
\mu\to\infty
, the exponent term
\exp\{-(\mu^{2}+2\mu\varepsilon_{i})/2\}\to 0
almost surely, so
\hat{a}_{i}\to 1
when
A_{i}=1
.

If
A_{i}=0
, a similar calculation yields

\hat{a}_{i}=\frac{1}{1+\Big(\dfrac{1-p}{p}\Big)\exp\{-(\mu^{2}-2\mu\varepsilon_{i})/2\}},

and the exponent again tends to
0
almost surely as
\mu\to\infty
, implying
\hat{a}_{i}\to 0
when
A_{i}=0
. Thus
\hat{A}_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\}\to A_{i}
almost surely as
\mu\to\infty
.

(2) Explicit condition for an error level
\alpha

We now bound

\Pr\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\}=A_{i}\bigr)=p\,\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\mid A_{i}=1)+(1-p)\,\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}<1/2\mid A_{i}=0).

Case
A_{i}=1
.

We have

\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\mid A_{i}=1)=\Pr\!\left(\frac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}>\frac{1-p}{p}\;\middle|\;A_{i}=1\right),

where
f_{1}=\phi(x_{i};\mu,1)
and
f_{0}=\phi(x_{i};0,1)
. Using

\frac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}=\exp\bigl\{(x_{i}^{2}-\varepsilon_{i}^{2})/2\bigr\}=\exp\bigl\{\mu(\mu+2\varepsilon_{i})/2\bigr\},

we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\mid A_{i}=1)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\mu(\mu+2\varepsilon_{i})/2>\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}>\frac{1}{\mu}\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)-\frac{\mu}{2}\right).

If

\frac{1}{\mu}\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)-\frac{\mu}{2}\leq z_{\alpha}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu\geq\sqrt{2\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)+z_{\alpha}^{2}}-z_{\alpha},

then
\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\mid A_{i}=1)\geq 1-\alpha
.

Case
A_{i}=0
.

Analogously,

\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}<1/2\mid A_{i}=0)=\Pr\!\left(\frac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}<\frac{1-p}{p}\;\middle|\;A_{i}=0\right),

where now
x_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}
and
f_{1}=\phi(x_{i};\mu,1)
,
f_{0}=\phi(x_{i};0,1)
. A similar calculation yields

\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}<1/2\mid A_{i}=0)=\Pr\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}<\frac{1}{\mu}\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)+\frac{\mu}{2}\right).

If

\frac{1}{\mu}\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)+\frac{\mu}{2}\geq z_{1-\alpha}=-z_{\alpha}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu\geq\sqrt{-2\log\Big(\frac{1-p}{p}\Big)+z_{\alpha}^{2}}-z_{\alpha},

then
\Pr(\hat{a}_{i}<1/2\mid A_{i}=0)\geq 1-\alpha
.

Combining the two cases, if

\mu\;\geq\;\max\!\left(\sqrt{2\log\!\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\right)+z_{\alpha}^{2}}-z_{\alpha},\;\sqrt{-2\log\!\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\right)+z_{\alpha}^{2}}-z_{\alpha}\right),

then each conditional probability is at least
1-\alpha
, and hence

\Pr\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\hat{a}_{i}>1/2\}=A_{i}\bigr)>1-\alpha.

Reintroducing
\sigma_{e}
replaces
\mu
by the standardized separation
\mu/\sigma_{e}
and yields the claimed condition in Theorem
3.2
for the binary case.

A.1.2
K
-class case

We now extend the argument to the general univariate Gaussian mixture with
K
components:

[x_{i}\mid A_{i}=j]\sim N(\mu_{j},\sigma_{e}^{2}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,K,

with class probabilities
p_{j}=\Pr(A_{i}=j)
. Let

\delta_{\min}=\min_{1\leq j,\ell\leq K}\frac{|\mu_{j}-\mu_{\ell}|}{\sigma_{e}},\qquad p_{\max}=\max_{j}p_{j},\quad p_{\min}=\min_{j}p_{j},

and define
\alpha^{\prime}=1-(1-\alpha)^{1/(K-1)}
as in Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
(2).

Working again with standardized variables so that
\sigma_{e}=1
(the general case follows by scaling), write
x_{i}=\mu_{j}+\varepsilon_{i}
when
A_{i}=j
, with
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,1)
. Then

\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=A_{i}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\Pr\bigl(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=j\mid A_{i}=j\bigr)p_{j}.

Fix
j
and consider the event
\{\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=j\mid A_{i}=j\}
. Under
A_{i}=j
, we have

\frac{f_{j}}{f_{\ell}}=\exp\!\left\{\frac{(\mu_{j}+\varepsilon_{i})^{2}-(\mu_{\ell}+\varepsilon_{i})^{2}}{2}\right\}=\exp\!\left\{\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}-\mu_{\ell}^{2}+2(\mu_{j}-\mu_{\ell})\varepsilon_{i}}{2}\right\},\quad\ell\neq j,

where
f_{\ell}
is the
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell},1)
density evaluated at
x_{i}
. Then

\hat{a}_{ij}=\frac{p_{j}f_{j}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}p_{\ell}f_{\ell}},\qquad\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{f_{j}}{f_{\ell}}>\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{j}}\ \ \forall\,\ell\neq j.

Hence

\displaystyle\Pr(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=j\mid A_{i}=j)

\displaystyle=\Pr\Bigl(\frac{f_{j}}{f_{\ell}}>\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{j}}\ \forall\,\ell\neq j\;\Bigm|\;A_{i}=j\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{\ell\neq j}\Pr\Bigl(\frac{f_{j}}{f_{\ell}}>\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{j}}\;\Bigm|\;A_{i}=j\Bigr).

A direct calculation yields

\Pr\Bigl(\frac{f_{j}}{f_{\ell}}>\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{j}}\;\Bigm|\;A_{i}=j\Bigr)=\Pr\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}>\frac{1}{\mu_{j}-\mu_{\ell}}\log\Big(\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{j}}\Big)-\frac{\mu_{j}-\mu_{\ell}}{2}\right).

Using the definitions of
\delta_{\min}
,
p_{\max}
, and
p_{\min}
, we obtain the uniform lower bound

\Pr\Bigl(\frac{f_{j}}{f_{\ell}}>\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{j}}\;\Bigm|\;A_{i}=j\Bigr)\;\geq\;\Pr\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}>\frac{1}{\delta_{\min}}\log\Big(\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}\Big)-\frac{\delta_{\min}}{2}\right)=:q.

Thus

\Pr(\arg\max_{l}\hat{a}_{il}=j\mid A_{i}=j)\geq q^{K-1}.

If we choose
\delta_{\min}
such that

q=\Pr\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}>\frac{1}{\delta_{\min}}\log\Big(\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}\Big)-\frac{\delta_{\min}}{2}\right)\geq 1-\alpha^{\prime},

with
\alpha^{\prime}=1-(1-\alpha)^{1/(K-1)}
, then
q^{K-1}\geq 1-\alpha
. This is achieved whenever

\delta_{\min}\;\geq\;\sqrt{2\log\!\left(\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}\right)+z_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{2}}\;-\;z_{\alpha^{\prime}},

which is exactly the condition stated in Theorem
3.2
(2) after reinstating
\sigma_{e}
and viewing
\delta_{\min}
as the minimum standardized separation. This completes the proof.

A.2
Proof of Theorem
3.6

Consider the model

[x_{i}^{a}\mid A_{i}=1]\sim N(\mu,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\qquad[x_{i}^{a}\mid A_{i}=0]\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\qquad A_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p),

and

y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}^{a}+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),

with
x_{i}^{a}
independent of
\varepsilon_{i}
. Let
q=1-p
.

For the simple linear regression of
\boldsymbol{y}
on
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
with an intercept, the regression sum of squares explained by
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
is

\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})=\frac{\big[(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}-\mathbf{1}\,\overline{x^{a}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}\big]^{2}}{(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}-\mathbf{1}\,\overline{x^{a}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{a}},

where
\overline{x^{a}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{a}
.
The total sum of squares is

\mathrm{TSS}=(\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{y})^{\top}\boldsymbol{y},\qquad\bar{y}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}.

The corresponding coefficient of determination is

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})}{\mathrm{TSS}}.

Now suppose
A_{i}
is observed, and we remove its effect from
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
by regressing
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
on
\boldsymbol{a}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})^{\top}
and taking the residuals. Under the Gaussian mixture representation

x_{i}^{a}=\mu A_{i}+e_{i},\qquad e_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),

we can write in vector form

\boldsymbol{x}^{a}=\mu\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{e},

and the residuals from regressing
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
on
\boldsymbol{a}
are, up to estimation error

\boldsymbol{u}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_{B})\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\approx\boldsymbol{e},

where
\mathbf{H}_{B}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{B}
is the design matrix in
2.3
. Let
\overline{u}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}u_{i}
and
\overline{e}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}e_{i}
. Then the regression sum of squares when regressing
\boldsymbol{y}
on
\boldsymbol{u}
is

\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{\big[(\boldsymbol{u}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{u})^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}\big]^{2}}{(\boldsymbol{u}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{u})^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}}.

Because the centering projection
(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})
and the projection
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_{B})
onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{a})
commute, and
\boldsymbol{e}
is already orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{a}
, we have

\boldsymbol{u}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{u}=(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_{B})\boldsymbol{e}=(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{e}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{e},

and

(\boldsymbol{u}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{u})^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{e}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_{B})\boldsymbol{e}=(\boldsymbol{e}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{e})^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}.

Hence

\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{u})=\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e}).

We denote

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{u})}{\mathrm{TSS}}=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e})}{\mathrm{TSS}}.

We now derive the large-sample limits of
\mathrm{TSS}
,
\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})
, and
\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e})
.

Limit of
\mathrm{TSS}

Since

y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mu A_{i}+\beta_{1}e_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}[y_{i}]=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mu p,

we have, for large
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\,\mathrm{TSS}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}=\mathbb{E}\Big[(y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[y_{i}])^{2}\Big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[(\beta_{1}\mu(A_{i}-p)+\beta_{1}e_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})^{2}\Big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}^{2}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Limit of
\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})
.

Note that

x_{i}^{a}=\mu A_{i}+e_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{a}]=\mu p,

so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}-\mathbf{1}\,\overline{x^{a}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(x_{i}^{a}-\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{a}])y_{i}\big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(\mu(A_{i}-p)+e_{i})(\beta_{1}\mu A_{i}+\beta_{1}e_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})\big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}-\mathbf{1}\,\overline{x^{a}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(\mu(A_{i}-p)+e_{i})^{2}\big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Hence

\frac{1}{n}\,\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})=\frac{\big(\beta_{1}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})\big)^{2}}{p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})}=\beta_{1}^{2}\big(p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\big)+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

so

\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})=n\,\beta_{1}^{2}\big(p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\big)+O_{p}(n^{1/2}).

Thus

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a})}{\mathrm{TSS}}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\big(p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\big)}{\beta_{1}^{2}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which is the expression stated in Theorem
3.6
for
R_{x}^{2}
.

Limit of
\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e})
.

We now replace
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
by
\boldsymbol{e}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[e_{i}]=0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}(\boldsymbol{e}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{e})^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(e_{i}-\mathbb{E}[e_{i}])y_{i}\big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[e_{i}(\beta_{1}\mu A_{i}+\beta_{1}e_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})\big]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

and

\frac{1}{n}(\boldsymbol{e}-\mathbf{1}\,\bar{e})^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}=\mathbb{E}[e_{i}^{2}]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})=\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Therefore

\frac{1}{n}\,\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e})=\frac{\big(\beta_{1}\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})\big)^{2}}{\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})}=\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

so

\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e})=n\,\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{1/2}).

Combining with the expression for
\mathrm{TSS}
yields

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{e})}{\mathrm{TSS}}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which coincides with the expression in Theorem
3.6
for
R_{a}^{2}
. As
\mu\to\infty
,
R_{x}^{2}\to 1
, and
R_{a}^{2}\to 0
claimed.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Recall Model
3.1
. For
i=1,\ldots,n
,

y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}}
,

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}}
,
and
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
are independent of
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})
.

The block of predictors related to the sensitive attribute satisfies

[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\mid A_{i}=j]\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}),\quad j=1,\ldots,K,

which can be written as

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{i},

with
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p_{a}}
,
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
the one-hot representation of
A_{i}
, and

\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}\times p_{a}}
.
We write
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
so that

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\mathbf{X}^{a})=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}\times p_{a}}

is the covariance of
\mathbf{X}^{a}
.
Similarly,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}\times p_{z}}
is the covariance of
\mathbf{X}^{z}
, and

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}\times p_{z}}
denotes the cross-covariance between
\mathbf{X}^{a}
and
\mathbf{X}^{z}
:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}^{a},\mathbf{X}^{z}).

Define the full predictor vector

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
R_{x}^{2}
denote the
R^{2}
from regressing
\boldsymbol{y}
on the full predictor
\boldsymbol{x}
, and let
R_{a}^{2}
denote the
R^{2}
after the fairness adjustment, i.e., after regressing
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
on
\boldsymbol{a}
and using the residuals as predictors (together with
\boldsymbol{x}^{z}
). We derive large-sample expressions for both.

Population
R^{2}
for the full model

In the linear model with intercept,

y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}+\varepsilon_{i},

the conditional mean is

m_{x}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}):=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i}]=\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}.

The
R_{x}^{2}
is

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}\big(m_{x}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\big)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(y_{i})}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})}{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}},

since
\varepsilon_{i}
is independent with variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
.

Using the covariance decomposition,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}
is scalar and

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}
, we can write the cross-term as
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}
, with the understanding that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
encodes the symmetric contribution. Substituting

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}
, we obtain

\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}.

Therefore,

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

By standard arguments (consistency and
\sqrt{n}
-rate of the OLS estimator, plus the law of large numbers for sample covariances), we have

R_{x}^{2}=R_{x}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which yields the stated expression for
R_{x}^{2}
in Theorem
3.5
.

Population
R^{2}
after fairness adjustment

The fairness adjustment regresses
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
on
\boldsymbol{a}
and replaces
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
by the residuals. At the population level,

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{a}_{i}]=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{i},

so the residual from regressing
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}
on
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
is exactly

\boldsymbol{u}_{i}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}-\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{a}_{i}]=\boldsymbol{e}_{i}.

The adjusted model therefore uses predictors
(\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})
in place of
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})
.

The corresponding conditional mean becomes

m_{a}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}):=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}]=\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z},

because the term
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
has been removed. The population analogue of
R_{a}^{2}
is then

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}\big(m_{a}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})\big)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(y_{i})}.

Note that

\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(m_{a}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}))=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z},

since
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}
and the cross-covariance with
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}
is still
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
.

The marginal variance of
y_{i}
remains

\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(y_{i})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2},

since the data generating process for
y_{i}
has not changed.

Hence

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

As before, the sample
R_{a}^{2}
computed from regressing
\boldsymbol{y}
on the empirical residuals
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{X}^{z}
is a
\sqrt{n}
-consistent estimator of
R_{a}^{2}
, so

R_{a}^{2}=R_{a}^{2}+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which yields the expression for
R_{a}^{2}
stated in Theorem
3.5
. This completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem

(hat-matrix argument)

Let
\mathbf{H}_{1},\mathbf{H}_{2},\mathbf{H}_{a}
denote the hat matrices introduced above:

\mathbf{H}_{a}=\mathbf{Z}_{a}(\mathbf{Z}_{a}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{a})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{a}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{Z}_{a}=[\mathbf{1}_{n},\mathbf{A}],

\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H}_{a})\mathbf{X}^{a}
is the residualized version of
\mathbf{X}^{a}
.

Recall that, for a linear model with an intercept, the usual
R^{2}

We show that these ratios converge to the expressions stated in Theorem

Write the model as

\boldsymbol{y}=\beta_{0}\mathbf{1}_{n}+\mathbf{X}^{a}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\mathbf{X}^{z}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\qquad\mathbf{X}^{a}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{E},

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{a}}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{z}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p_{a}}
,
\mathbf{A}
collects the one-hot vectors
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
,
\mathbf{E}
collects the noise vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
, and
\mathbf{X}^{z}
collects the
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z}

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i})

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{z})

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{X}^{a},\mathbf{X}^{z})

\mathbf{A}_{0}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})\mathbf{A},\quad\mathbf{E}_{0}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})\mathbf{E},\quad\mathbf{U}_{0}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})\mathbf{U},\quad\mathbf{X}^{z}_{0}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\bar{\mathbf{J}})\mathbf{X}^{z},

A.9
Proofs for Gaussian mixture classification accuracy

A.9.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

For the first statement, note that the Bayes classifier under known mixture parameters assigns observation
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}}
to the class

\hat{A}_{i}=\arg\max_{k}\;p_{k}\,\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\,\boldsymbol{a}};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}),

where
\phi(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})
is the multivariate Gaussian density. Standard arguments for Gaussian discriminant analysis show that, for
k\neq\ell
,

\log\frac{p_{k}\phi(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})}{p_{\ell}\phi(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e})}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell}\bigr)+\log\frac{p_{k}}{p_{\ell}}.

When
\delta_{\min}\to\infty
, the Mahalanobis distance between any two means diverges. Conditioning on
A_{i}=k
, the misclassification probability can be bounded by a Gaussian tail probability whose argument grows with
\delta_{\min}
; hence the misclassification probability tends to zero and
\hat{A}_{i}\to A_{i}
almost surely.

For the second statement, consider
A_{i}=k
. After whitening, let

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{x}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
.
Pairwise comparison between components
k
and
\ell
reduces to comparing a univariate Gaussian projection along the direction of
\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k\ell}
. The event that component
k
is preferred to
\ell
under the posterior mode rule can be written as

\langle\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\|\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k\ell}\|}\log\Bigl(\frac{p_{\ell}}{p_{k}}\Bigr)+\frac{\|\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k\ell}\|}{2},

which yields a tail probability of the form

1-\Phi(\cdot)

because
\langle\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k\ell},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle
is univariate normal under
A_{i}=k
.
Treating the pairwise comparison events across
\ell\neq k
via this representation and multiplying their probabilities yields the product form in the theorem; averaging over
k
with weights
p_{k}
gives the stated expression.
∎

A.9.2
Proof of Theorem
3.2

Proof.

In the univariate case with

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xz}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{z}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

Standard laws of large numbers for quadratic forms in Gaussian variables imply that the empirical
R^{2}
converges to these population quantities at a rate
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, yielding the stated asymptotic expansions.
∎

A.11.2
Proof of Theorem
3.6

Proof.

In the univariate case,

X\mid A=1\sim N(\mu,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\quad X\mid A=0\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\quad A\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p),

so

X=\mu A+E,\qquad E\sim N(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),\ \ A\perp E.

Then

\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X)=\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\mu A)+\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(E)=\mu^{2}p(1-p)+\sigma_{e}^{2}.

Under the linear model
y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
, the population
R^{2}
for regression on
X
is

R_{x}^{2}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\beta_{1}X)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\beta_{1}X)+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X)}{\beta_{1}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X)+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}},

which yields the expression in the theorem upon substituting
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(X)
.

For the fairness-adjusted
R^{2}
, note that regressing
X
on
A
removes the component
\mu A
and leaves the residual
U=E
, whose variance is
\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(U)=\sigma_{e}^{2}
. The corresponding population
R^{2}
is

R_{a}^{2}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\beta_{1}U)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Var}}(\beta_{1}X)+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}=\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}}{\beta_{1}^{2}\{p(1-p)\mu^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}},

which is the second expression in the theorem.
The
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
terms again arise from the standard asymptotics of sample covariances.
∎
