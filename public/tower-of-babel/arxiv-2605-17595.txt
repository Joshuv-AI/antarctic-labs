Elasticity of Orders from the S S -relative Davenport Constant: an Arithmetic Application of a Number-Theoretic Investigation
Jared Kettinger and Grant Moles

Abstract
. Orders in algebraic number fields have long been objects of central interest in algebraic number theory. Despite non-maximal orders failing to be Dedekind, the present authors have previously shown that the structure of the ideal class group may still contain enough information to determine elasticity. In this paper, we develop the S S -relative Davenport constant, which builds on previous work by M. Skałba. Although Skałba’s original construction was defined to aid in the study of binary quadratic forms, we show that this related invariant is the exact tool needed to tackle the question of elasticity in non-integrally closed orders. In particular, we investigate the elasticity of orders 𝒪 \mathcal{O} whose conductor ideal I = ( 𝒪 : 𝒪 K ) I=(\mathcal{O}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{O}_{K}) is prime as an ideal of 𝒪 \mathcal{O} , as well as orders in quadratic number fields with primary conductor. We also give conditions under which 𝒪 \mathcal{O} will have the same elasticity as the full ring of integers 𝒪 K \mathcal{O}_{K} .



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. A
G
-sequence

\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\}
of (not necessarily distinct) elements of
G
is called a
zero-sum sequence
if
g_{1}+g_{2}+\dotsm+g_{n}=0
. We use
D(G)
to denote the smallest positive integer
m
such that any
G
-sequence of length
m
must have a zero-sum subsequence. Equivalently, we may define
D(G)
as the largest positive integer
m
such that there exists a zero-sum sequence of length
m
with no proper zero-sum subsequence. This group invariant is known as the
Davenport constant
of
G
.

The Davenport constant was first introduced by Rogers (
[
24
]
) and later popularized by Davenport (
[
7
]
) in connection with the arithmetic of rings of integers. In particular, when
G
is taken to be the class group of a ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
D(G)
is a sharp upper bound for the length of the prime factorization (counting multiplicity) of the ideal
(\alpha)
for an irreducible element
\alpha\in\text{Irr}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
. More generally, the Davenport constant is the precise tool we need to take advantage of uniqueness of ideal factorization—allowing us to discern arithmetic information about an element
\alpha
from the ideal
(\alpha)
. Consider the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
R
be an atomic domain and
\alpha\in R
a nonzero nonunit. The
length set
of
\alpha
in
R
is

\ell_{R}(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid\alpha=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{k}\textup{ for some }\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\in\textup{Irr}(R)\}.

The
elasticity
of
\alpha
in
R
is

\rho_{R}(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\left\{\frac{m}{n}\>\middle|\>m,n\in\ell_{R}(\alpha)\right\}.

The
elasticity
of
R
is

\rho(R)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\{\rho_{R}(\alpha)|\alpha\in R\text{ a nonzero nonunit}\}.

Recall that an integral domain in which every nonzero nonunit factors uniquely into primes is called a
unique factorization domain
(UFD). Note that the elasticity of a domain is a measure of the failure of unique factorization—in particular, factorization length. The following is a classic result of Narkiewicz (
[
19
]
) which demonstrates how the Davenport constant of the class group
\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
of a ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
determines this factorization invariant.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{K}
be a ring of integers which is not a UFD, then

\rho(\mathcal{O}_{K})=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K}))}{2}.

For a non-maximal order
\mathcal{O}
of a number field
K
, the relationship between
D(\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O}))
and
\rho(\mathcal{O})
is more tenuous as
\mathcal{O}
fails to be Dedekind, since
\overline{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
. As we will see, this results in the generalized class group
\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
containing only information about those ideals relatively prime to the conductor ideal
(\mathcal{O}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{O}_{K})
. However, recent work by the present authors in
[
13
]
has shown that, in the case when
(\mathcal{O}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{O}_{K})
is a prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, the elasticity of the order is still completely determined by the structure of
\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
. In this paper, we consider the more general case when
(\mathcal{O}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{O}_{K})
is a prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}
. In order to do so, we will introduce a variant of the Davenport constant which not only informs our present inquiry, but also provides a natural language for the results of
[
13
]
.

The Davenport constant has long been studies as a purely combinatorial or number-theoretic object (see
[
21
]
or
[
3
]
, respectively). It has also inspired numerous variants and generalizations in the area of factorization theory. Interestingly, multiple generalizations have been defined and studied from a strictly number-theoretic point of view which were later found to have profound arithmetic implications. For example, Adhikari and Rath introduced weighted Davenport constants in 2006 (
[
1
]
), after which they were widely studied. It was not until 2014 that Halter-Koch gave an arithmetic interpretation of these Davenport constants in
[
11
]
. Furthermore, in 2022, Boukheche et al. demonstrated in
[
2
]
that these invariants completely determine the arithmetic of the normset of a ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
for a Galois number field
K
.

Similarly, in 1993, Skałba introduced the relative Davenport constant as part of his investigation of numbers with a unique representation by a binary quadratic form (
[
25
]
). It turns out, when we generalize this invariant from elements to sets, we find precisely the tool we need to study the arithmetic of non-maximal orders.

In Section 2, we will focus on developing this group invariant and proving results which will inform our arithmetic inquiries. In Section 3, we will study orders whose conductor ideal is prime in the order, culminating in a characterization of their elasticities in Theorem
3.14
. In Section 4, we briefly apply these results to settle prior conjectures on orders whose elasticity is equal to that of its integral closure. Finally, in Section 5, we restrict our attention to orders in quadratic number fields, allowing for a more complete characterization of elasticity in Theorem
5.8
. We conclude with Theorem
5.16
, which settles a particular case of a well-known conjecture on the elasticity of intermediate orders.

To remain consistent with previous work on this topic and to emphasize the relationship between a (non-maximal) order and the full ring of integers, we will adopt the notation
R
to refer to an order in a number field
K
and
\overline{R}
to refer to its integral closure
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

2.
The
S
-Relative Davenport Constant

A natural generalization of the Davenport constant was first introduced by Skałba in
[
25
]
to answer a question on binary quadratic forms. He further explored this invariant in its own right in
[
26
]
. Before defining it, we introduce some relevant terms. For a finite abelian group
G
, a
G
-sequence
\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\}
is called
irreducible
if it has no proper zero-sum subsequence. Furthermore, if
\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\}
also fails to be a zero-sum sequence, we call it
primitive
. The
relative Davenport constant of
G
with respect to
a
, denoted
D_{a}(G)
, is the largest positive integer
m
such that there exists an irreducible
G
-sequence
\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{m}\}
with
g_{1}+g_{2}+\dotsm+g_{m}=a
. We will presently develop an analogue of the relative Davenport constant replacing the element
a
with a subset. Taking advantage of the groundwork done in
[
25
]
, we are able to give explicit formulas for this constant for certain subsets of arbitrary cyclic groups which will translate to strong arithmetic results in later sections.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. We call the sequence of elements
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
an
S
-sum sequence
in
G
if
g_{1}+\dots+g_{n}\in S
. If
S
is the singleton set
S=\{g\}
for some
g\in G
, we may use the term
g
-sum sequence
rather than
\{g\}
-sum sequence for convenience. Note that this agrees with the existing definition of a zero-sum sequence.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. We define the
S
-relative Davenport constant
, denoted
D_{S}(G)
, to be the minimal natural number
n
such that any
S
-sum sequence
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
of length
n
in
G
must contain a zero-sum subsequence.

It should be observed that this definition differs from the generalization introduced in
[
14
]
which uses similar notation. The following proposition demonstrates the relationship between the S-relative Davenport constant and the original invariant.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then
D_{G}(G)=D(G)
.

Note that the
S
-relative Davenport constant and Skałba’s original construction generalize the two equivalent definitions of
D(G)
—in terms of sequences of minimal and maximal lengths respectively. With this in mind, we introduce a
“small”
version of our invariant.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. We define the
small
S
-relative Davenport constant
, denoted
d_{S}(G)
, to be the largest whole number
n
such that there exists an
S
-sum sequence in
G
of length
n
which has no zero-sum subsequence. If every
S
-sum sequence in
G
has a zero-sum subsequence, we define
d_{S}(G)=0
.

The terms small and large Davenport constant are used analogously throughout the literature. Namely,
d(G)
denotes the largest possible integer
\ell
such that there exists a
G
-sequence of length
\ell
with no
0
-subsequence—proper or otherwise. For abelian groups, we have
d(G)+1=D(G)
, but this fails in general for non-abelian groups. For details, see
[
8
]
. The following results demonstrate that we avoid this bifurcation for the
S
-relative Davenport constant of abelian groups as well.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. Then
d_{S}(G)=0
if and only if
S=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Recall that
d_{S}(G)
is defined to be
0
exactly when every
S
-sequence in
G
has a zero-sum subsequence. Assume
d_{S}(G)=0
, and let
g\in S
. Then the
S
-sequence
(g)
must contain a zero-sum subsequence, and thus
g=0
. Therefore,
S=\{0\}
.

Now assume that
S=\{0\}
. Then any
S
-sequence in
G
must by definition also be a zero-sum sequence and thus contains a zero-sum subsequence. Then
d_{S}(G)=0
.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. Then
D_{S}(G)=d_{S}(G)+1
.

Proof.

First, assume that
d_{S}(G)=0
; that is, assume
S=\{0\}
. Then since any
S
-sequence of length
1
in
G
must contain a zero-sum subsequence (namely, the entire sequence),
D_{S}(G)=1=d_{S}(G)+1
.

Now assume that
d_{S}(G)=d>0
. Then there is some
S
-sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})
in
G
of length
d
which has no zero-sum subsequence. For each
1\leq k\leq d
, let
h_{k}=g_{k}+g_{k+1}+\dots+g_{d}
, and consider the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{k-1},h_{k})
. Since
g_{1}+\dots+g_{k-1}+h_{k}=g_{1}+\dots+g_{d}\in S
, this is still an
S
-sequence in
G
. Furthermore, any subsequence sum of
(g_{1},\dots,g_{k-1},h_{k})
is clearly a subsequence sum of
(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})
. Then since the original sequence does not have a zero-sum subsequence, neither does this new sequence. Thus, for every
1\leq k\leq d
, there exists an
S
-sequence in
G
of length
k
with no zero-sum subsequence, so
D_{S}(G)>d
.

Finally, let
n=d_{S}(G)+1
and consider an
S
-sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
in
G
. If this sequence has no zero-sum subsequence, then by definition,
d_{S}(G)\geq n
, a contradiction. Then any
S
-sequence in
G
of length
n
must contain a zero-sum subsequence, so
D_{S}(G)\leq n=d_{S}(G)+1
. Then
d_{S}(G)<D_{S}(G)\leq d_{S}(G)+1
, so
D_{S}(G)=d_{S}(G)+1
.
∎

Considering Definition
2.4
, one might reasonably expect that we have
d_{\{g\}}(G)=D_{g}(G)
, the relative Davenport constant with respect to
g
defined by Skałba. While this is true for
g\neq 0
, we should be careful to recall that
D_{0}(G)=D(G)
and
d_{\{0\}}(G)=0
. Notably, these invariants are almost identical for singletons. Hence, we will be able to take advantage of results on
D_{g}(G)
to calculate
d_{\{g\}}(G)
and subsequently
D_{S}(G)
. We presently develop a few simple results about these constants which will be helpful in the following sections.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}
nonempty subsets of
G
. Then
d_{S_{1}}(G)\leq d_{S_{2}}(G)
and
D_{S_{1}}(G)\leq D_{S_{2}}(G)
.

Proof.

If
d_{S_{1}}(G)=0
, then the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise, let
d=d_{S_{1}}(G)
and
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\}
be an
S_{1}
-sequence in
G
with no zero-sum subsequence. Then
g_{1}+\dots+g_{d}\in S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}
, so this is also an
S_{2}
-sequence of length
d
with no
0
-subsequence. Then
d_{S_{1}}(G)\leq d_{S_{2}}(G)
. From this, it immediately follows that
D_{S_{1}}(G)=d_{S_{1}}(G)+1\leq d_{S_{2}}(G)+1=D_{S_{2}}(G)
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. Then

D_{S}(G)\leq D(G).

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group with subgroup
H
, and let
G^{\prime}=\large\nicefrac{{G}}{{H}}\normalsize
. Let
S^{\prime}\subseteq G^{\prime}
and
S=\{s\in G|s+H\in S^{\prime}\}
(i.e. letting
\pi+H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to G^{\prime}
be the natural projection modulo
H
,
S=\pi^{-1}(S^{\prime})
). Then
d_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime})\leq d_{S}(G)
and
D_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime})\leq D_{S}(G)
.

Proof.

First, if
d_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime})=0
, then the result is trivial. Otherwise, we let
d=d_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime})
and select an
S^{\prime}
-sequence
\{g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{d}^{\prime}\}
in
G^{\prime}
with no zero-sum subsequence. For each
1\leq i\leq d
, we select some
g_{i}\in G
such that
g_{i}^{\prime}=g_{i}+H
; then
(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{d})+H=g_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+g_{d}^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
. Then by definition of
S
,
g_{1}+\dots+g_{d}\in S
. Furthermore,
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\}
cannot have a zero-sum subsequence in
G
, since this would force
\{g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{d}^{\prime}\}
to have a zero-sum subsequence in
G^{\prime}
. Then there exists an
S
-sequence in
G
with no zero-sum subsequence of length
d
, so
d_{S}(G)\geq d=d_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime})
. It immediately follows that
D_{S}(G)=d_{S}(G)+1\geq d_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime})+1=D_{S^{\prime}}(G^{\prime}).

∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
S
a nonempty subset of
G
. Also let
\sigma\in\textup{Aut}(G)
. Then
d_{S}(G)=d_{\sigma(S)}(G)
. In particular, if
H
is a subgroup of
G
and
\sigma\in\textup{Aut}_{H}(G)
(i.e.
\sigma
is an automorphism of
G
such that
\sigma(H)=H
), then for any
g\in G
,
D_{g+H}(G)=D_{\sigma(G)+H}(G)
.

Proof.

First, let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
be an
S
-sequence in
G
with no zero-sum subsequence; in particular, let
s\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g_{1}+\dots+g_{n}\in S
. Then
\{\sigma(g_{1}),\dots,\sigma(g_{n})\}
is a
\sigma(S)
-sequence in
G
; specifically,
\sigma(g_{1})+\dots+\sigma(g_{n})=\sigma(s)\in\sigma(S)
. Moreover, since
\sigma
is an automorphism, then if there is a zero-sum subsequence, say (after possibly reordering)
\{\sigma(g_{1}),\dots,\sigma(g_{k})\}
, then
\sigma(g_{1}+\dots+g_{k})=0
and thus
g_{1}+\dots+g_{k}=0
, a contradiction. Then
\{\sigma(g_{1}),\dots,\sigma(g_{n})\}
is a
\sigma(S)
-sequence in
G
of length
n
with no zero-sum subsequence, so
d_{S}(G)\leq d_{\sigma(S)}(G)
. The reverse inequality follows immediately from the observation that
S=\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(S))
, with
\sigma^{-1}\in\textup{Aut}(G)
. Then
d_{S}(G)=d_{\sigma(S)}(G)
.

The rest of the result follows from the observation that for any subgroup
H\leq G
,
\sigma\in\textup{Aut}_{H}(G)
, and
g\in G
, we have
\sigma(g+H)=\sigma(g)+H
.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group,
S
a nonempty subset of
G
, and
-S
the set of additive inverses of elements of
S
. Then
D_{S}(G)=D_{-S}(G)
. In particular, for any subgroup
H
of
G
and coset
\alpha\in\large\nicefrac{{G}}{{H}}\normalsize
,
D_{\alpha}(G)=D_{-\alpha}(G)
.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from the previous theorem, since
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to G
defined by
\sigma(g)=-g
for all
g\in G
is an automorphism of
G
which fixes any subgroup
H
.
∎

For the remainder of this section, we consider the case when
G
is cyclic and our sets are taken to be cosets of subgroups. First, consider the following result of Skałba on the relative Davenport constant of a cyclic group.

Lemma 2.12
.

[
25
, Theorem 2]

Let
G
be a finite cyclic group and
a\in G
, then

D_{a}(G)=\begin{cases}|G|&\text{for }a=0\\
|G|-|G|/|a|&\text{for }a\neq 0\end{cases}

where
|a|
denotes the order of
a
.

Notably, since
d_{\{g\}}(G)=D_{g}(G)
for all
g\neq 0
and
d_{\{0\}}=0
, this result actually demonstrates one way in which the small
S
-relative Davenport constant behaves more nicely than the relative Davenport constant: in all cases,
d_{\{g\}}(G)=\absolutevalue{G}-\absolutevalue{G}/\absolutevalue{g}
.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
G
be a finite cyclic group of order
n
and
H=\langle m\rangle\leq G
(without loss of generality, we assume
m|n
). Let
\alpha\in\large\nicefrac{{G}}{{H}}\normalsize
and
g\in\alpha
such that
1\leq g\leq m
. Then

d_{\alpha}(G)=d_{\{\gcd(g,m)\}}(G)=n-\gcd(g,m).

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
; we will treat the elements of
G
as integers. Since
d_{\alpha}(G)=\max\{d_{\{a\}}(G)|a\in\alpha\}
, it will suffice by Lemma
2.12
to show that
\gcd(g,m)\leq\gcd(a,n)=\frac{n}{\absolutevalue{a}}
for every
a\in\alpha
, with equality for some
a\in\alpha
. The inequality is straightforward; letting
d=\gcd(g,m)
, we note that there must exist
k,x,y\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n=km
,
g=xd
, and
m=yd
. Then
n=km=(ky)d
, and for any
a=g+rm\in\alpha
,
a=xd+ryd=(x+ry)d
. Then
d
is a common divisor of
n
and
a
, so
d=\gcd(g,m)\leq\gcd(a,n)
for every
a\in\alpha
. We simply need to show that
\gcd(g,m)=\gcd(a,n)
for some
a\in\alpha
.

First, assume that
n=p^{r}
for some prime
p
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
m
is a divisor of
n
, then
H=\langle m\rangle=\langle p^{s}\rangle
for some
0\leq s\leq r
. We will denote
\gcd(g,n)=p^{a}
; since
1\leq g\leq m
, we know that
0\leq a\leq s
. Since
p^{a}
is the largest power of
p
dividing
g
and
a\leq s\leq r
, this immediately tells us that

\gcd(g,m)=\gcd(g,p^{s})=p^{a}=\gcd(g,n).

Then
\gcd(g,m)=\gcd(g,n)
with
g\in g+H=\alpha
, as desired.

Now dropping the assumption than
n
is a prime power, we write
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
for distinct primes
p_{i}
and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq i\leq k
. Since
m
is a divisor of
n
, then necessarily
m=p_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{b_{k}}
for some
0\leq b_{i}\leq a_{i}
. Decomposing our cyclic group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, we note that

G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}^{a_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p_{k}^{a_{k}}}.

Considering the image of
H
under this isomorphism and applying the Chinese Remainder Theorem yields

H\cong\langle p_{1}^{b_{1}}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle p_{k}^{b_{k}}\rangle\leq\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}^{a_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p_{k}^{a_{k}}}.

Now for each
1\leq i\leq k
, we define
g_{i}
such that
g_{i}\equiv g\textup{ (mod }p_{i}^{b_{i}})
and
1\leq g_{i}\leq p_{i}^{b_{i}}
. Using the case when
n
was a prime power, we note that
\gcd(g_{i},p_{i}^{b_{i}})=\gcd(g_{i},p_{i}^{a_{i}})
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, we can now select
a\in G
such that
a\equiv g_{i}\textup{ (mod }p_{i}^{a_{i}})
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Since
a\equiv g_{i}\equiv g\textup{ (mod }p_{i}^{b_{i}})
for each
1\leq i\leq k
, we have that
a\equiv g\textup{ (mod }m)
, and thus
a\in g+H=\alpha
. We note the following:

\gcd(g,m)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\gcd(g,p_{i}^{b_{i}})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\gcd(g_{i},p_{i}^{b_{i}})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\gcd(g_{i},p_{i}^{a_{i}})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\gcd(a,p_{i}^{a_{i}})=\gcd(a,n).

Then
\gcd(g,m)=\gcd(a,n)
for some
a\in\alpha
and
\gcd(g,m)\leq\gcd(a,n)
for every
a\in\alpha
, so

d_{\alpha}(G)=\max\{d_{a}(G)|a\in\alpha\}=\max\{n-\gcd(a,n)|a\in\alpha\}=n-\gcd(g,m)=d_{\{\gcd(g,m)\}}(G).

∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
G
be a finite cyclic group and
H\leq G
. For any
\alpha,\beta\in\large\nicefrac{{G}}{{H}}\normalsize
such that
\beta\in\langle\alpha\rangle
,
d_{\alpha}(G)\geq d_{\beta}(G)
.

Proof.

Identifying
G
with
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and treating the elements of
G
as integers, we let
H=\langle m\rangle
for some divisor
m
of
n
and select
g_{1}\in\alpha
and
g_{2}\in\beta
such that
1\leq g_{1}\leq m
and
1\leq g_{2}\leq m
. Since
\beta\in\langle\alpha\rangle
, then there exists some
a\in\alpha
and
s\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
g_{2}\equiv sa\textup{ (mod }m)
. Since
\alpha=a+H=g_{1}+H
, we can therefore select
t\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
g_{2}=sg_{1}+tm
.

Now let
d_{1}=\gcd(g_{1},m)
and
d_{2}=\gcd(g_{2},m)
. Since
d_{1}
is a common divisor of
g_{1}
and
m
, it must also divide
g_{2}=sg_{1}+tm
. Thus,
d_{1}
is a common divisor of
g_{2}
and
m
, so
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
. By Theorem
2.13
, this gives:

d_{\alpha}(G)=n-d_{1}\geq n-d_{2}=d_{\beta}(G).

∎

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
G
be a finite cyclic group and
H_{1}<H_{2}\leq G
. For some
g\in G
, we denote
\alpha=g+H_{1}
and
\beta=g+H_{2}
; in particular, note that
\alpha\subsetneq\beta
. Then
d_{\beta}(G)=d_{\beta\backslash\alpha}(G)
. Equivalently, there exists a
\beta
-sequence
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\}
of length
d=d_{\beta}(G)
with no
0
-subsequence in
G
whose sum does not lie in the proper subset
\alpha
.

Proof.

Since
\beta\backslash\alpha\subset\beta
, then Proposition
2.7
immediately tells us that
d_{\beta}(G)\geq d_{\beta\backslash\alpha}(G)
. We will show the reverse inequality holds as well.

As usual, we identify
G
with
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
with subgroups
\langle m_{1}\rangle
and
\langle m_{2}\rangle
for some divisors
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
of
n
. Since
H_{1}
is a proper subgroup of
H_{2}
, we note that
m_{2}
must be a proper divisor of
m_{1}
. We factor these integers into primes:

n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}};\quad m_{1}=p_{1}^{c_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{c_{k}};\quad m_{2}=p_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{b_{k}}.

Since
m_{2}
must be a proper divisor of
m_{1}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
b_{1}<c_{1}
.

Following the construction from Theorem
2.13
, we let
1\leq g\leq m_{2}
be an element of
\beta
and select
g_{i}\equiv g\textup{ (mod }p_{i}^{b_{i}})
with
1\leq g_{i}\leq p_{i}^{b_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. This gives
\gcd(g_{i},p_{i}^{b_{i}})=\gcd(g_{i},p_{i}^{a_{i}})
, so selecting
b\in G
such that
b\equiv g_{i}\textup{ (mod }p_{i}^{a_{i}})
for every
1\leq i\leq k
gives an element
b\in\beta
such that
\gcd(g,m_{2})=\gcd(b,n)
. If this element
b
happens to lie outside of
\alpha
already, then

d_{\beta}(G)=n-\gcd(g,m_{2})=n-\gcd(b,n)=d_{\{b\}}(G)\leq d_{\beta\backslash\alpha}(G).

Now assume that this element
b
we constructed in the previous step lies in
\alpha
. Since we assumed that
b_{1}<c_{1}
, we now choose
g_{1}^{\prime}=g_{1}+p_{1}^{b_{1}}
and
b^{\prime}\in G
such that
b^{\prime}\equiv g_{1}^{\prime}\textup{ (mod }p_{1}^{a_{1}})
and
b^{\prime}\equiv g_{i}\textup{ (mod }p_{i}^{a_{i}})
for
2\leq i\leq k
. Note that
b^{\prime}\equiv b\textup{ (mod }m_{2})
, so
b^{\prime}\in\beta
. However, note that
b^{\prime}-b\equiv p_{1}^{b_{i}}\textup{ (mod }p_{1}^{c_{1}})
, so
b^{\prime}\not\equiv b\textup{ (mod }p_{1}^{c_{1}})
. In particular, since
b\in\alpha
,
b^{\prime}\notin\alpha
. However, since
\gcd(g_{1}^{\prime},p_{1}^{b_{1}})=\gcd(g_{1}^{\prime},p_{1}^{a_{1}})
, it follows exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2.13
that
\gcd(g,m_{2})=\gcd(b^{\prime},n)
. Then once again,

d_{\beta}(G)=n-\gcd(g,m_{2})=n-\gcd(b^{\prime},n)=d_{\{b^{\prime}\}}(G)\leq d_{\beta\backslash\alpha}(G).

Thus,
d_{\beta}(G)=d_{\beta\backslash\alpha}(G)
.
∎

The previous two corollaries seem as if they should naturally hold for any abelian group
G
, but the more general results defy simple proof. We include them here as conjectures; we will see later that in cases where these conjectures hold, the main results of this paper may actually be stated much more simply.

Conjecture 2.16
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
H\leq G
. For any
\alpha,\beta\in\large\nicefrac{{G}}{{H}}\normalsize
such that
\beta\in\langle\alpha\rangle
,
d_{\alpha}(G)\geq d_{\beta}(G)
.

Conjecture 2.17
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
H_{1}<H_{2}\leq G
. For some
g\in G
, we denote
\alpha=g+H_{1}
and
\beta=g+H_{2}
; in particular, note that
\alpha\subsetneq\beta
. Then
d_{\beta}(G)=d_{\beta\backslash\alpha}(G)
. Equivalently, there exists a
\beta
-sequence
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\}
of length
d=d_{\beta}(G)
with no zero-sum subsequence in
G
whose sum does not lie in the proper subset
\alpha
.

Remark 2.18
.

Recall that given an order
R
in a number field,
\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})\cong\large\nicefrac{{\textup{Cl}(R)}}{{\ker(\tau)}}\normalsize
, where
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\textup{Cl}(R)\to\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
is defined by
\tau([J])=[J\overline{R}]
(where
J
is an integral ideal of
R
which is relatively prime to the conductor ideal
I
of
R
). As we will see throughout this paper, we will often specifically be interested in
D_{S}(G)
and
d_{S}(G)
when
G=\textup{Cl}(R)
for some order
R
and
S=\tau^{-1}(\{[J]\})
, the set of all ideal classes in
\textup{Cl}(R)
whose image under
\tau
is
[J]\in\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
(that is, given an ideal
J
in
\overline{R}
, we let
J^{\prime}\in[J]
be an ideal relatively prime to the conductor ideal
I
and let
S=[R\cap J^{\prime}]\ker(\tau)
). In this case, we will often use the notation
D_{J}(\textup{Cl}(R))
and
d_{J}(\textup{Cl}(R))
as shorthand for
D_{S}(\textup{Cl}(R))
and
d_{S}(\textup{Cl}(R))
. In particular, when referring to
S=\ker(\tau)
itself, we will often use
d_{\overline{R}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
and
D_{\overline{R}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
.

3.
Elasticity of Orders from the
S
-Relative Davenport Constant

With this understanding of the
S
-relative Davenport constant of a group, we now turn our attention to a common application of the original Davenport constant: determining the elasticity of an order in a number field. Classically, it was proven in
[
19
]
that when
R
is a ring of integers in a number field, we may use
D(\textup{Cl}(R))
, the Davenport constant of the ideal class group of
R
, to determine
\rho(R)
, the elasticity of
R
. In
[
13
, Theorem 3.2]
, this was extended to determine the elasticity of any order
R
in a number field whose conductor ideal
I=(R\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\overline{R})
is prime in
\overline{R}
. In hindsight, one will see almost immediately that this result can be stated in terms of the
S
-relative Davenport constant.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
P
, where
P
is prime as an ideal of
\overline{R}
. Then:

(1)

if
P
is principal in
\overline{R}
, then

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2},\frac{D_{\overline{R}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}\right\};

(2)

otherwise,

\rho(R)=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}.

In this section, we will find a natural generalization of part (1) of this result. In particular, we will determine the elasticity of any order
R
in a number field
K
whose conductor ideal
I
is principal as an ideal of
\overline{R}
and prime as an ideal of
R
(note that this covers but does not imply the case when
I
is prime in
\overline{R}
). To see how to handle this, we first produce a characterization of when
I
is prime in
R
that will help us partition this problem into two pieces.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

I
is a prime ideal of
R
;

(2)

I=R\cap P
for every prime ideal
P
of
\overline{R}
containing
I
;

(3)

I=R\cap P
for some prime ideal
P
of
\overline{R}
.

Proof.

Assume that
I
is a prime ideal of
R
, and let
P
be a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
containing
I
. Then
R\cap P
is a prime ideal of
R
containing
I
. Since
\dim(R)=1
and
I\neq\{0\}
, then
R\cap P=I
. Thus,
(1)\implies(2)
. The implications
(2)\implies(3)
and
(3)\implies(1)
are immediate.
∎

In order to quickly handle the case when
I
is a non-primary ideal of
\overline{R}
, we defer to the following result of Halter-Koch.

Proposition 3.3
.

[
10
, Corollary 4]

Let
R
be an order in an algebraic number field,
\overline{R}
its integral closure, and
I
its conductor ideal.

(1)

If for some prime ideal
P
of
R
there is more than one prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
P
, then
\rho(R)=\infty
.

(2)

If for every prime ideal
P
of
R
there is exactly one prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
P
, then
R
has accepted (finite) elasticity.

Note that by
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
, it is sufficient to check only the prime ideals containing
I
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. If
I
is a prime ideal of
R
such that
I
is a non-primary ideal of
\overline{R}
, then
R
has infinite elasticity.

Proof.

Assume that
I
is a prime ideal of
R
which is a non-primary ideal of
\overline{R}
. Then there exist distinct prime ideals
P
and
Q
of
\overline{R}
which contain
I
; it follows immediately from Propositions
3.2
and
3.3
that
R
has infinite elasticity.
∎

We now turn our attention to the case when
I
is a primary ideal of
\overline{R}
which is prime in
R
. By Proposition
3.2
, this is equivalent to
I
being a power of a prime ideal
P
of
\overline{R}
with
I=R\cap P
. As mentioned previously, we will be aiming to generalize part (1) of Proposition
3.1
; that is, we will also be assuming that
I
is a principal ideal of
\overline{R}
. Before determining the elasticity of an order with such a conductor, we present lemmata that will be useful throughout this section and those that follow. The first three lemmata ensure that prime factorization of ideals in
R
relatively prime to the conductor behaves very similarly to prime factorization of ideals in
\overline{R}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
5
, Theorem 3.6]

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. If
J
is an ideal of
R
which is relatively prime to
I
, then
J
is an invertible ideal of
R
.

Lemma 3.6
.

[
5
, Corollary 3.11]

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. Any ideal of
R
which is relatively prime to
I
has unique factorization into prime ideals of
R
relatively prime to
I
. Moreover, all but finitely many prime ideals in
R
are relatively prime to
I
.

Lemma 3.7
.

[
22
, Theorem 2.6]

Let
R
be an order in a number field. Then every ideal class in
\textup{Cl}(R)
contains infinitely many prime ideals of
R
.

The next two lemmata will help us relate ideals of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to the conductor to the ideals of
R
relatively prime to the conductor. In particular, there is a one-to-one correspondence between these ideals, and this correspondence respects primality and prime factorization.

Lemma 3.8
.

[
13
, Lemma 2.6]

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. If
J
is an ideal of
R
comaximal to
I
, then
J\overline{R}\cap R=J
; if
J
is prime, then
J\overline{R}
is prime as well.

Moreover, if
J
is an
\overline{R}
-ideal comaximal to
I
, then
(R\cap J)\overline{R}=J
; if
J
is prime, then
R\cap J
is prime as well.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. If
A
is an ideal of
R
relatively prime to
I
with prime ideal factorization
A=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
, then
A\overline{R}
is an ideal of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
with prime ideal factorization
A\overline{R}=(Q_{1}\overline{R})\cdots(Q_{k}\overline{R})
.

Moreover, if
J
is an ideal of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
with prime ideal factorization
J=P_{1}\cdots P_{k}
, then
R\cap J
is an ideal of
R
relatively prime to
I
with prime ideal factorization
R\cap J=(R\cap P_{1})\cdots(R\cap P_{k})
.

Proof.

Let
A
be an ideal of
R
relatively prime to
I
. By Lemma
3.6
, we can factor
A
into a product of prime ideals of
R
, say
A=Q_{1}\dots Q_{k}
. Then

A\overline{R}=(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k})\overline{R}=(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k})\overline{R}^{k}=(Q_{1}\overline{R})\dots(Q_{k}\overline{R}).

Since Lemma
3.8
tells us that each
Q_{i}\overline{R}
is a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
, this is the (unique) prime factorization of
A\overline{R}
. Moreover, since
A
is relatively prime to
I
, then there exist
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in I
such that
\alpha+\beta=1
. Since
\alpha\in A\overline{R}
as well, then
A\overline{R}
is also relatively prime to
I
.

Now let
J
be an ideal of
\overline{R}
be an ideal of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
with prime ideal factorization
J=P_{1}\cdots P_{k}
. Since
J
is relatively prime to
I
, then there exist
\alpha\in J
and
\beta\in I
such that
\alpha+\beta=1
. Since
\alpha=1-\beta\in R\cap J
, then
R\cap J
is also relatively prime to
I
. By Lemma
3.6
, we can therefore factor
R\cap J
into a product of prime ideals of
R
, say
R\cap J=Q_{1}\dots Q_{n}
. By the previous part of this proof and Lemma
3.8
,

J=(R\cap J)\overline{R}=(Q_{1}\overline{R})\cdots(Q_{n}\overline{R}),

with each
Q_{i}\overline{R}
a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
. Since prime ideal factorization in
\overline{R}
is unique, then
n=k
and (after potential reordering),
P_{i}=Q_{i}\overline{R}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Thus,

R\cap J=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}=(Q_{1}\overline{R}\cap R)\cdots(Q_{k}\overline{R}\cap R)=(R\cap P_{1})\cdots(R\cap P_{k}).

∎

These final lemmata will give us ways to more easily construct irreducibles in
R
and elements in
R
of large elasticity. Of particular note here is Lemma
3.13
, which demonstrates exactly how the
S
-relative Davenport constant will be useful to us.

Lemma 3.10
.

[
13
, Lemma 2.7]

Let
R
be an order in a number field
K
with conductor ideal
I
. Let
\alpha\in R
be a nonzero nonunit with
\rho_{R}(\alpha)>1
, and suppose that
\alpha=\beta\pi
for some
\beta\in\overline{R}
and
\pi\in R
, with
\pi
a prime element of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
. Then
\beta\in R
and
\rho_{R}(\beta)\geq\rho_{R}(\alpha)
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. Let
\alpha\in\overline{R}
, and let
\alpha\overline{R}=P_{1}\cdots P_{s}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{t}
be the (unique) factorization of
\alpha\overline{R}
into prime ideals of
\overline{R}
, with each
P_{i}\mid I
and
Q_{j}\nmid I
for
1\leq i\leq s
and
1\leq j\leq t
. Then
\alpha
has a nonunit divisor lying in
R
which is relatively prime to
I
if and only if
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{t}]\}
has a zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
.

Proof.

First, assume that
\alpha
has some nonunit divisor
r\in R
, with
r
relatively prime to
I
. Then necessarily (after potential reordering), there exists
1\leq k\leq t
such that
r\overline{R}=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
. By Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
,

rR=(rR)\overline{R}\cap R=r\overline{R}\cap R=(R\cap Q_{1})\cdots(R\cap Q_{k}),

so
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{k}]\}
is a zero-sum subsequence of
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{t}]\}
in
\textup{Cl}(R)
.

For the converse, assume that
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{t}]\}
has a zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
; without loss of generality, assume that this zero-sum subsequence is of the form
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{\ell}]\}
for some
1\leq\ell\leq t
. Then for some
r\in R
,
rR=(R\cap Q_{1})\cdots(R\cap Q_{\ell})
. By applying Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
, we get

r\overline{R}=(rR)\overline{R}=(R\cap Q_{1})\overline{R}\cdots(R\cap Q_{\ell})\overline{R}=Q_{1}\dots Q_{\ell}.

Now letting
\beta\in\overline{R}
be a generator of
P_{1}\cdots P_{s}Q_{\ell+1}\cdots Q_{t}
, there exists a unit
u\in U(\overline{R})
such that
\alpha=(u\beta)r
. Thus,
\alpha
has a nonunit divisor lying in
R
which is relatively prime to
I
(namely,
r
).
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field with conductor ideal
I
. If
\alpha\in R
is a nonzero, nonunit relatively prime to
I
, then
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq\max\{1,\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}\}
. Moreover, this inequality is sharp.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in R
be relatively prime to
I
. If
\rho_{R}(\alpha)=1
, then the result follows trivially; then assume that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)>1
. Let
\alpha=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m}=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}
be two factorizations of
\alpha
into irreducibles of
R
. Since each
\pi_{i}
and
\tau_{j}
must be relatively prime to
I
as well, then Lemma
3.5
ensures us that each
\pi_{i}R
and
\tau_{j}R
(as well as each prime factor of these ideals) is an invertible ideal of
R
(and thus represents an ideal class in
\textup{Cl}(R)
). Since we are only looking for an upper bound on the elasticity of
\alpha
, Lemma
3.10
tells us that it will suffice to show the result in the case that each
\pi_{i}
and
\tau_{j}
is a non-prime irreducible.

Let
k
be the number of prime ideals in the prime factorization of
\alpha R
. Since each
\tau_{j}
is a non-prime irreducible, then each
\tau_{j}
must factor into at least two prime ideals of
R
; thus,
2n\leq k
. Since each
\pi_{i}
is an irreducible element of
R
, then if
\pi_{i}R=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{s}
is the prime factorization of
\pi_{i}R
in
R
, then
\{[Q_{1}],\dots,[Q_{s}]\}
must be a zero-sum sequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
with no proper zero-sum subsequence. Thus,
\pi_{i}R
can have at most
D(\textup{Cl}(R))
prime factors, so
k\leq m\cdot D(\textup{Cl}(R))
. Then
2n\leq m\cdot D(\textup{Cl}(R))
, so
\frac{n}{m}\leq\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}
. Since this inequality holds for every pair of factorizations of
\alpha
,
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}
. Thus,
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq\max\{1,\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}\}
.

To show that this inequality is sharp, note that any irreducible element of
R
has elasticity 1. Then it will suffice to show that when
D(\textup{Cl}(R))\geq 2
, there exists an element
\alpha\in R
relatively prime to
I
such that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}
. To this end, let
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d}]\}
be a zero-sum sequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
with no proper zero-sum subsequence of length
d=D(\textup{Cl}(R))
. By Lemma
3.7
, we can select a prime ideal
Q_{i}
of
R
relatively prime to
I
from each ideal class
[A_{i}]
. Moreover, we can select a prime ideal
Q_{i}^{\prime}
of
R
relatively prime to
I
from each ideal class
[A_{i}]^{-1}
. Since
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d}]\}
(and thus
\{[A_{1}]^{-1},\dots,[A_{d}]^{-1}\})
is a zero-sum sequence with no zero-sum subsequence, this ensures that
Q_{1}\cdots Q_{d}
,
Q_{1}^{\prime}\cdots Q_{d}^{\prime}
, and each
Q_{i}Q_{i}^{\prime}
are principal ideals of
R
generated by an irreducible element of
R
. Let
\beta
,
\gamma
, and
\pi_{i}
, respectively, be these irreducible generators. Then for some unit
u\in U(R)
,
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\beta\gamma=(u\pi_{1})\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{d}
, so
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\geq\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}
. By the previously shown inequality and the fact that each
Q_{i}
and
Q_{i}^{\prime}
is relatively prime to
I
,
\alpha
is an element of
R
relatively prime to
I
with elasticity exactly
\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field whose conductor ideal
I
is prime as an ideal of
R
and both principal and primary as an ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is, for some
\pi\in R
and prime ideal
P
of
\overline{R}
,
I=P^{a}=\pi\overline{R}
and
I=R\cap P
. If
\tau\in\textup{Irr}(R)
and
\tau\in P
, then
\tau\overline{R}
factors into at most
d_{P^{j}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+j
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
, where
j\in\mathbb{N}
is the largest power of
P
such that
\tau\in P^{j}
. Moreover, this upper bound is achieved for each
a\leq j<2a
.

Proof.

First, note that since
R\cap P=I
, then for any
\tau\in\textup{Irr}(R)
such that
\tau\in P
,
\tau\in I=P^{a}
. Also note that if
\tau\in P^{2a}=I^{2}=(\pi^{2})
, then for some
\beta\in\overline{R}
,
\tau=\pi(\pi\beta)
. Since both
\pi
and
\pi\beta
are nonunits in
R
, then
\tau
cannot be irreducible. Then letting
j\in\mathbb{N}
be maximal such that
\tau\in P^{j}
, we have that
j=a+i
for some
0\leq i<a
. We can now factor the ideal
\tau\overline{R}
into prime ideals,
\tau\overline{R}=P^{j}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
, where each
Q_{i}
is relatively prime to
I
. Now assume that
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{k}]\}
has a zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
. By Lemma
3.11
, this means that
\tau
must have a nonunit divisor lying in
R
which is relatively prime to
I
, and thus
\tau=\beta\gamma
with
\gamma\in R\backslash P
a nonunit and
\beta\in P^{j}\subseteq I\subseteq R
. Then
\tau
reduces in
R
, a contradiction. Then no such
0
-subsequence can exist, so
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{k}]\}
is a sequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
with no zero-sum subsequence for which
[Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}]=[P^{j}]^{-1}
. By definition of the small
S
-relative Davenport constant and Corollary
2.11
,
k\leq d_{P^{j}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
. Thus, the number of prime divisors of
\tau\overline{R}
is
k+j\leq d_{P^{j}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+j
.

Now to show that this upper bound is achieved, let
a\leq j<2a
and
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d}]\}
be a sequence of length
d=d_{P^{j}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
in
\textup{Cl}(R)
with no zero-sum subsequence such that
\{[A_{1}\overline{R}],\dots,[A_{d}\overline{R}]\}
is a
[P^{j}]^{-1}
-sequence in
\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
(such a sequence must exist by definition of the
S
-relative Davenport constant and the discussion above). By Lemma
3.7
, we can assume without loss of generality that
A_{i}
is a prime ideal of
R
relatively prime to
I
for each
1\leq i\leq d
, and by Lemma
3.8
, each
Q_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A_{i}\overline{R}
is a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
. Then let
\alpha\in\overline{R}
be any generator of the (principal) ideal
P^{j}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{d}
. Since
j\geq a
, then
\alpha\in I\subseteq R
.

Assume toward a contradiction that
\alpha=\beta\gamma
for some nonunits
\beta,\gamma\in R
. Since
R\cap P=I=P^{a}
and
j<2a
, then
\beta
and
\gamma
cannot both be elements of
P
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\beta\in P
and
\gamma\notin P
. Then
\alpha
has a nonunit divisor (namely,
\gamma
) lying in
R
which is relatively prime to
I
, so Lemma
3.11
tells us that
\{[R\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R\cap Q_{d}]\}=\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d}]\}
must have a zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R)
, a contradiction. Then
\alpha\in\textup{Irr}(R)
such that
\alpha\overline{R}
has exactly
d+j
prime ideal factors.
∎

With these results in hand, we are now ready to present the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field whose conductor ideal
I
is prime as an ideal of
R
and both principal and primary as an ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is, for some
\pi\in R
and prime ideal
P
of
\overline{R}
,
I=P^{a}=\pi\overline{R}
and
I=R\cap P
. Then

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2},\frac{D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{i}{a}\middle|0\leq i\leq a-1\right\}.

Proof.

For ease of notation, we will let
M
refer to the maximum presented above. To show equality, we will first show that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\geq M
for some nonzero, nonunit
\alpha\in R
; then that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq M
for every nonzero, nonunit
\alpha\in R
. First, note that if
M=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}
, then
D(\textup{Cl}(R))\geq 2
; by Lemma
3.12
, we can select a nonzero, nonunit
\alpha\in R
relatively prime to
I
such that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}=M
.

Now assume for some
0\leq i\leq a-1
that
M=\frac{D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{i}{a}
. By Lemma
3.13
, we can select
\tau\in\textup{Irr}(R)
such that
\tau\overline{R}=P^{j}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{d}
, where
j=a+i
,
d=d_{P^{j}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
, and each
Q_{i}
is a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
. Similarly, we can let
\tau^{\prime}
be any generator of the (principal) ideal
\tau^{\prime}\overline{R}=P^{j}Q_{1}^{\prime}\cdots Q_{d}^{\prime}
, where
[R\cap Q_{i}^{\prime}]=[R\cap Q_{i}]^{-1}
for each
1\leq i\leq d
. In the same way that we showed that
\tau
was irreducible in the proof of Lemma
3.13
, we can conclude that
\tau^{\prime}\in\textup{Irr}(R)
. Finally, let
\pi_{i}
be a generator of the principal ideal
Q_{i}Q_{i}^{\prime}
for each
1\leq i\leq d
. Since
[\pi_{i}\overline{R}\cap R]=[R\cap Q_{i}][R\cap Q_{i}^{\prime}]=[R]
, then we can without loss of generality assume that
\pi_{i}\in R
. Moreover, since each
R\cap Q_{i}
is non-principal, each
\pi_{i}\in\textup{Irr}(R)
. Thus, for some unit
u\in U(\overline{R})
,

\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\tau\tau^{\prime})^{a}=(u\pi)\pi^{2j-1}\pi_{1}^{a}\cdots\pi_{d}^{a}.

Since the left-hand side of this expression is a product of
2a
irreducibles in
R
and the right-hand side of this expression is a product of
2j+ad
irreducibles in
R
, then

\rho_{R}(\alpha)\geq\frac{2j+ad}{2a}=\frac{d_{P^{j}}(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}+\frac{a+i}{a}=\frac{D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{i}{a}=M.

Thus,
\rho(R)\geq M
.

We now need to show that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq M
for every nonzero, nonunit
\alpha\in R
. Let
\alpha\in R
be a nonzero nonunit, and assume we have two factorizations of
\alpha
into irreducibles of
R
:
\alpha=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m}=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}
. If
\rho_{R}(\alpha)=1
, then
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq M
trivially. Then assume that
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\neq 1
; without loss of generality, we may also assume that
n>m
. By Lemma
3.10
, this allows us to assume that none of the
\pi_{i}
’s or
\tau_{j}
’s are prime elements of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
. After possibly reordering, let
0\leq s\leq m
and
0\leq t\leq n
such that
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{s},\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{t}\in P
and
\pi_{s+1},\dots\pi_{m},\tau_{t+1},\dots\tau_{n}\notin P
. If
\tau_{1}\overline{R}=P^{j}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
for some
j>a
and prime ideals
Q_{i}
relatively prime to
I
, then note that
\alpha^{a}=(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{m})^{a}=\tau_{1}^{a}(\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{n})^{a}
. Since
\tau_{1}^{a}\overline{R}=(P^{a})^{j}(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k})^{a}
, then for some generator
\beta\in\overline{R}
of
(Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k})^{a}
,
\tau_{1}^{a}=(\pi\beta)\pi^{j-1}
, a product of at least
j>a
irreducibles in
R
. Then
\alpha^{a}
can be written simultaneously as a product of
am
irreducibles in
R
and strictly more than
an
irreducibles of
R
, so
\rho_{R}(\alpha^{a})>\frac{n}{m}
. Since we are only interested in elements of large elasticity, it will suffice to show that
\frac{n}{m}\leq M
when
\tau_{j}
lies in
P^{a}\backslash P^{a+1}
for each
1\leq j\leq t
.

Let
k
be the (unique) number of prime ideal factors of
\alpha\overline{R}
. For
1\leq\ell\leq t
, note that
\tau_{\ell}\overline{R}
has exactly
a
factors of
P
. Furthermore, for
t+1\leq\ell\leq n
, since
\tau_{\ell}\notin P
, then
\tau_{\ell}\overline{R}
has at least two prime ideal factors, and these primes must be distinct from
P
. Then the number of prime factors distinct from
P
in the prime ideal factorization of
\alpha\overline{R}
is
k-ta\geq 2(n-t)=2n-2t
.

For
1\leq\ell\leq s
, note that
\pi_{\ell}\overline{R}
must have at least
a
factors of
P
but no more than
2a-1
; for each
1\leq\ell\leq s
, let
1\leq i_{\ell}<a
such that
\pi_{\ell}\overline{R}
has exactly
a+i_{\ell}
factors of
P
. Thus,
ta=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(a+i_{\ell})
. By Lemma
3.13
, each
\pi_{\ell}
for
1\leq\ell\leq s
can have at most
d_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=d_{P^{i_{\ell}}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
prime factors distinct from
P
. Similarly, by the proof of Lemma
3.12
, each
\pi_{\ell}
for
s+1\leq\ell\leq m
can have at most
D(\textup{Cl}(R))
prime factors (distinct from
P
). Thus,

2n-2t\leq k-ta\leq\sum_{j=1}^{s}d_{j}+(m-s)D(\textup{Cl}(R)).

Since
ta=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(a+i_{\ell})
, we have that
2t=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}2+\frac{2i_{\ell}}{a}
. Then

2n\leq 2t+\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}d_{\ell}+(m-s)D(\textup{Cl}(R))=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\left[(d_{\ell}+2)+\frac{2i_{\ell}}{a}\right]+(m-s)D(\textup{Cl}(R)).

Dividing by 2, this yields

n\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\left[\frac{D_{P^{i_{\ell}}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{i_{\ell}}{a}\right]+(m-s)\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}.

Note that the right-hand side of this inequality is now a sum of
m
terms, each of which is bounded above by
M
; then
n\leq mM
, so
\frac{n}{m}\leq M
. Then
\rho_{R}(\alpha)\leq M
for every nonzero, nonunit
\alpha\in R
, so
\rho(R)\leq M
. Since we already showed the reverse inequality, we can conclude that
\rho(R)=M
.
∎

Along with Proposition
3.4
, this characterizes the elasticity of any order
R
whose conductor ideal
I
is principal in
\overline{R}
and prime in
R
. Moreover, if
I
is a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
,
I
will automatically be a prime ideal of
R
; then case (1) of Proposition
3.1
is exactly equivalent to Theorem
3.14
when
a=1
.

It is worth noting that Theorem
3.14
, along with Corollary
2.8
, actually gives quite a narrow range for the potential elasticities of any order of the given form.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field whose conductor ideal
I=P^{a}
is prime as an ideal of
R
and principal as an ideal of
\overline{R}
. Then

\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}\leq\rho(R)\leq\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{a-1}{a}<\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}+\frac{3}{2}.

Theorem
3.14
also simplifies greatly in the case that
\overline{R}
is a PID.

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field whose conductor ideal
I=P^{a}
is prime as an ideal of
R
. Also assume that the integral closure
\overline{R}
is a PID. Then

\rho(R)=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{a-1}{a}.

Proof.

Since
\overline{R}
is a PID, then
I
is automatically a principal ideal of
\overline{R}
. Furthermore, since
D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))=D_{\textup{Cl}(R)}(\textup{Cl}(R))=D(\textup{Cl}(R))
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then by Theorem
3.14
,

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2},\frac{D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{i}{a}\middle|0\leq i\leq a-1\right\}=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{a-1}{a}.

∎

Even when
\overline{R}
is not a PID, we can still make Theorem
3.14
easier to use by noting that, of course,
\frac{i}{a}
increases with larger values of
i
. Thus, if two different values of
i
give identical values of
D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
, we need only look at the term with the larger value of
i
. In particular, we can make use of Proposition
2.10
to reduce the values of
i
we must check. Moreover, we can use Corollary
2.11
to obtain the following.

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field whose conductor ideal
I
is prime as an ideal of
R
and both principal and primary as an ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is, for some
\pi\in R
and prime ideal
P
of
\overline{R}
,
I=P^{a}=\pi\overline{R}
and
I=R\cap P
. If
D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R))\geq D_{P^{i}}(\textup{Cl}(R))
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2},\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{a-1}{a}\right\}.

Note that this will hold whenever Conjecture
2.16
applies to
G=\textup{Cl}(R)
,
H=\ker(\tau)
, and
\alpha=[P]
. In particular, by Corollary
2.14
, this will hold whenever
\textup{Cl}(R)
is cyclic.

To illustrate the importance of the condition that
I
is a prime ideal of
R
, we provide an illustrative example that demonstrates the failure of the theorem when
R\cap P\neq I
.

Example 3.18
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
,
R_{1}=\mathbb{Z}[2\sqrt{2}]
, and
R_{2}=\mathbb{Z}[9\sqrt{2}]
. The conductor ideals of these orders are
I_{1}=(R_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{O}_{K})=(2)=(\sqrt{2})^{2}
and
I_{2}=(R_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{O}_{K})=(9)=(3)^{2}
, both of which are squares of prime ideals.
\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
is trivial (i.e.
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is a PID) and
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are both locally associated orders (see the table at
[
17
]
), so
\textup{Cl}(R_{1})\cong\textup{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\cong\textup{Cl}(R_{2})
. Now since
R_{1}\cap(\sqrt{2})=I_{1}
, we can use Corollary
3.16
to conclude that
\rho(R_{1})=\frac{3}{2}
. On the other hand, note that in
R_{2}
,
(9+9\sqrt{2})(9-9\sqrt{2})=-3^{4}
, with each factor being irreducible. Then
\rho(R_{2})\geq\frac{4}{2}=2>\rho(R_{1})
(and in fact,
\rho(R_{2})=2
; this is shown later in Example
5.13
).

We conclude this section with an additional example of an elasticity calculation when the ring of integers is not a PID.

Example 3.19
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]
, where
\alpha
is a root of
f(x)=x^{4}-x^{3}+5x^{2}-2x+1
. Using the database at
[
15
]
and SageMath, we note the following:

•

\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

•

3\overline{R}=P^{2}
, where
P=(3,-1+6\alpha-\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{3})
is a non-principal prime ideal.

•

Letting
I=3\overline{R}
and
R=\mathbb{Z}+I
, we have that
R
is an order in
K
with conductor ideal
I
and
R\cap P=I
.

•

\absolutevalue{U(\quot{\Rbar}{I})}=72
and
\absolutevalue{U(\quot{R}{I})}=2
.

•

U(\overline{R})=\{\pm\alpha^{k}|k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and
\alpha^{12}
is the first power of
\alpha
lying in
R
, so
\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R)}}=12
.

Therefore,

\absolutevalue{\Cl(R)}=\absolutevalue{\Cl(\Rbar)}\cdot\frac{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{\Rbar}{I})}}{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{R}{I})}\cdot\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R)}}}=2\cdot\frac{72}{2\cdot{12}}=6.

Since
\textup{Cl}(R)
must be abelian, then
\textup{Cl}(R)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{6}
. In this copy of
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
,
\ker(\tau)
corresponds to the set
\{0,2,4\}
; since
P
is non-principal in
\overline{R}
, then by Corollary
3.17
, we have:

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2},\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right\}=\max\left\{\frac{6}{2},\frac{7}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right\}=4.

4.
Orders with
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})

Before continuing in our efforts to find the elasticities of additional orders, we take a brief diversion to refine old results and answer old conjectures. Of particular interest in this section will be orders
R
for which
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
. In
[
6
, Theorem 3.4]
, it was shown that
\rho(R)\geq\rho(\overline{R})
for any order
R
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
that, for
R
to be an HFD (i.e. for
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})=1
), it is necessary that
R
is an associated order. It was conjectured in
[
16
, Conjecture 5.2.8]
that
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
would imply that
R
is associated more generally. Though we will show later in this section that this conjecture fails in general, it turns out that
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
does imply that
R
is a locally associated order (see
[
18
]
for full definitions and characterizations).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field
K
. If
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
, then
R
is a locally associated order.

Proof.

First, note that if
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})=1
, then it is already known that
R
must be an associated (and thus locally associated) order. Then assume that
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})>1
. In this case, Lemma
3.12
gives us:

\rho(\overline{R})=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(\overline{R}))}{2}\leq\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2}\leq\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R}).

Then
D(\textup{Cl}(R))=D(\textup{Cl}(\overline{R}))
; by
[
6
, Lemma 3.3]
, it follows that
\textup{Cl}(R)\cong\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
.
∎

As an interesting corollary, we can actually use this fact to show the converse of
[
6
, Theorem 3.8]
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
R
be an order in a number field
K
whose conductor ideal
I
is a radical ideal of
\overline{R}
. Then
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
if and only if
R
is an associated order.

Proof.

The proof that
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
when
R
is an associated order is handled in
[
6
, Theorem 3.8]
. We will show the converse. Since
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
, then Theorem
4.1
tells us that
R
must be locally associated. Furthermore, since
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})<\infty
, then Proposition
3.3
tells us that for any two prime ideals
P
and
Q
dividing
I
,
R\cap P\nsubseteq Q
. Since
I
is radical, this is sufficient to show that
R
is ideal-preserving. Since
R
is both ideal-preserving and locally associated,
[
18
, Corollary 4.13]
tells us that
R
is an associated order.
∎

We now use Theorem
3.14
to answer
[
16
, Conjecture 5.2.8]
in the negative.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
R
be a locally associated order in a number field
K
with principal conductor ideal
I=P^{a}
, where
P=\pi\overline{R}
is principal and prime as an ideal of
\overline{R}
and
a\geq 2
. Also assume that
R\cap P=I
and
\rho(\overline{R})\geq\frac{2a-1}{a}
. Then
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})
, but
R
is not an associated order.

Proof.

First, note that since
R\cap P=I=P^{a}\neq P
, then
R
is by definition not an ideal-preserving order; then
R
is not associated. Since
P
is principal, then we can conclude by Corollary
3.17
that

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R))}{2},\frac{D_{\overline{R}}(\textup{Cl}(R))+1}{2}+\frac{a-1}{a}\right\}.

Since
R
is locally associated, then
\textup{Cl}(R)\cong\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
; therefore,
D(\textup{Cl}(R))=D(\textup{Cl}(\overline{R}))
and
D_{\overline{R}}(\textup{Cl}(R))=1
. Then

\rho(R)=\max\left\{\rho(\overline{R}),\frac{2a-1}{a}\right\}=\rho(\overline{R}).

∎

Example 4.4
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}[79\sqrt{79}]
be the index
79
order in the quadratic number field
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{79}]
. In this case,
\overline{R}=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{79}]
and
I=(79)=(\sqrt{79})^{2}
. Using the table at
[
17
]
, we can verify that
R
is locally associated but not ideal-preserving, so
R\cap(\sqrt{79})=I
. Since
\rho(\overline{R})=\frac{3}{2}
, then Proposition
4.3
tells us that
\rho(R)=\rho(\overline{R})=\frac{3}{2}
.

5.
Elasticity of Orders with Primary Conductor in Quadratic Fields

The results in Section 3 certainly allow us to find the elasticity of orders that were previously unapproachable, but the restrictions on the conductor ideal (that
I
is a prime ideal of
R
which is principal in
\overline{R}
) serve to somewhat limit their scope. However, when we restrict to the realm of orders in quadratic number fields, we can relax these conditions somewhat. Not only will every order automatically have a conductor ideal which is principal in
\overline{R}
(generated by an integer), the order will actually be uniquely determined by its conductor ideal. We will also be able to take advantage of the fact that
R\cap P
is very predictable. Moreover, as we will see, orders in
\overline{R}
lying between
R
and
\overline{R}
will have nice properties which we can utilize. Ultimately, this will allow us to find the elasticity of any order of index
p^{k}
for prime
p
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
in any quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
.

As before, we begin by compiling lemmata of which we will make frequent use that apply specifically to the quadratic case. Throughout this section,
K
will refer to a quadratic number field
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
for some squarefree
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
R_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[n\alpha]
will refer to the unique order of index
n
in
K
, where
\alpha=\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}
if
d\equiv 1\textup{ (mod }4)
and
\alpha=\sqrt{d}
otherwise. For ease of notation when working with multiple orders within the same number field, we will still use the notation
\overline{R}
to refer to the integral closure of
R_{n}
, i.e.
\overline{R}=R_{1}=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
,
p\in\mathbb{Z}
a prime dividing
n
, and
P
any prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
p
. Then:

(1)

if
p
is inert or split in
K
and
p^{k}|n
, then
R_{n}\cap P^{k}=p^{k}\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}
;

(2)

if
p
is ramified in
K
and
p^{k}|n
, then
R_{n}\cap P^{2k-1}=R_{n}\cap P^{2k}=p^{k}\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p^{k}|n
, and let
I_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p^{k}\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}
. Since
R_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[n\alpha]=\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}
, then
I_{k}\subseteq R_{n}
immediately. If
p
is either inert or split in
K
, then
I_{k}\subseteq p^{k}\overline{R}\subseteq P^{k}
, so
I_{k}\subseteq R_{n}\cap P^{k}
. If
p
is ramified, then
I_{k}\subseteq(p^{k}\overline{R})\subseteq P^{2k}\subseteq P^{2k-1}
, so
I_{k}\subseteq R_{n}\cap P^{2k}\subseteq R_{n}\cap P^{2k-1}
. We must now show the reverse inclusions.

Assume that
p
is either inert or split in
K
, and let
\beta=a+b\alpha\in R_{n}\cap P^{k}
. Since
\beta\in R_{n}
, then
n|b
; since
p^{k}|n
and
p^{k}\in P^{k}
, this means that
p^{k}|b
as well, so
b\alpha\in P^{k}
. Then
a=\beta-b\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\cap P^{k}=p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
, so
\beta\in I_{k}
. Therefore,
R_{n}\cap P^{k}\subseteq I_{k}
. Similarly, if
p
is ramified, we let
\beta=a+b\alpha\in R_{n}\cap P^{2k-1}
. Since
\beta\in R_{n}
, we follow a similar logic as above to conclude that
a\in\mathbb{Z}\cap P^{2k-1}=p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,
\beta\in I_{k}
, so
R_{n}\cap P^{2k}\subseteq R_{n}\cap P^{2k-1}\subseteq I_{k}\subseteq R_{n}\cap P^{2k}
.
∎

This lemma, along with Proposition
3.3
, allows us to immediately narrow our focus to orders whose index has no split prime factors.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Then
R_{n}
has infinite elasticity if and only if there exists a prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
which divides
n
and splits in
K
.

Proof.

Assume that
p|n
for some split prime
p
. Then there are two distinct prime ideals
P
and
Q
of
\overline{R}
which lie over
p
. By Lemma
5.1
, we have
R_{n}\cap P=p\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}=R_{n}\cap Q
, and thus
R_{n}
has infinite elasticity by Proposition
3.3
.

Otherwise, for any two distinct primes
P
and
Q
dividing
I=n\overline{R}
,
P
and
Q
lie over distinct primes
p
and
q
of
\mathbb{Z}
. Using Lemma
5.1
,
R_{n}\cap P=p\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}\neq q\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}=R_{n}\cap Q
. Again using Proposition
3.3
, we conclude that
R_{n}
has finite elasticity.
∎

The following lemma will allow us to make use of another feature of quadratic orders: the predictability of which elements conduct others into the order.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
, and let
n=mk
for some
m,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

(1)

R_{n}+m\overline{R}=R_{m}
;

(2)

The set of elements in
\overline{R}
conducting
m
into
R_{n}
is exactly
R_{k}
; that is,

R_{k}=\{\beta\in\overline{R}|m\beta\in R_{n}\}.

Proof.

Since
R_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[n\alpha]=\mathbb{Z}+n\alpha\mathbb{Z}
and
\overline{R}=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]=\mathbb{Z}+\alpha\mathbb{Z}
, then note that
R_{n}=\mathbb{Z}+n\overline{R}
. Thus,
R_{n}+m\overline{R}=\mathbb{Z}+n\overline{R}+m\overline{R}=\mathbb{Z}+m\overline{R}=R_{m}
. This completes the proof of (1).

For (2), we note that for any
\beta=a+b\alpha\in\overline{R}
,
m\beta=ma+mb\alpha\in R_{n}
if and only if
n|mb
. Since
n=mk
, this holds if and only if
k|b
, which is true if and only if
\beta\in R_{k}
. Thus,
\beta
conducts
m
into
R_{n}
if and only if
\beta\in R_{k}
.
∎

As we will see in the main theorem of this section and the corollaries that follow, finding the elasticity of a quadratic order can be much more complicated if Conjecture
2.17
fails. The following lemma allows us to still handle this case.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Also assume that the prime (integer) divisors of
n
are all either ramified or inert in
K
. Finally, let
b
be a divisor of
n
and
a
be a proper divisor of
b
. If
U(R_{a})=U(R_{b})
, then
\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_a)}<\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}
. In particular, if every unit in
\overline{R}
is contained in
R_{b}
, then
R_{b}
is not a locally associated order.

Proof.

Since
a
is a proper divisor of
b
, then
b=ak
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{1\}
. Let
p
be a prime divisor of
k
, and note that if
U(R_{a})=U(R_{b})
, then
U(R_{b})\subseteq U(R_{ap})\subseteq U(R_{a})=U(R_{b})
. Moreover,
\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_a)}\leq\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_{ap})}\leq\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}.
Then it will suffice to show the result when
b=ap
for a prime integer
p
.

By
[
20
, Theorem 12.12]
, we know that for any order
R
in an algebraic number field with conductor ideal
I
:

\frac{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R)}}{\absolutevalue{\Cl(\Rbar)}}=\frac{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{\Rbar}{I})}}{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{R}{I})}\cdot\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R)}}}.

Then:

\frac{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}}{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_a)}}=\frac{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}}{\absolutevalue{\Cl(\Rbar)}}\cdot\frac{\absolutevalue{\Cl(\Rbar)}}{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_a)}}=\frac{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{\Rbar}{(b)})}}{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{R_b}{(b)})}\cdot\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R_b)}}}\cdot\frac{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{R_a}{(a)})}\cdot\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R_a)}}}{\absolutevalue{U(\quot{\Rbar}{(a)})}}.

If we assume that
U(R_{a})=U(R_{b})
, then
\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R_a)}}=\absolutevalue{\quot{U(\Rbar)}{U(R_b)}}
. Thus, we can cancel this factor from the above equation and apply
[
18
, Theorem 6.2]
to obtain

\frac{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}}{\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_a)}}=\frac{L(b,d)}{L(a,d)},

where
L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{N}
is the function defined in
[
18
, Definition 6.1]
. From this definition of
L
, we can see that if
p\nmid a
, then
L(b,d)=L(ap,d)=L(a,d)\cdot L(p,d)
, with
L(p,d)=p
if
p
is ramified and
L(p,d)=p+1
is
p
is inert. If
p|a
, then we can write
a=p^{r}k
for some
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
; again by the definition of
L
, we have
L(b,d)=L(p^{r+1}k,d)=p\cdot L(a,d)
. In either case, note that
L(b,d)>L(a,d)
, and thus
\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}>\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_a)}
.

By letting
a=1
, we can see that if
U(\overline{R})=U(R_{b})
(i.e. every unit in
\overline{R}
is contained in
R_{b}
), then
\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_b)}\neq\absolutevalue{\Cl(\Rbar)}
, and thus
R_{b}
is not locally associated.
∎

As in Lemma
3.13
, we now characterize the irreducible elements of
R_{n}
and determine their prime ideal factors in
\overline{R}
. To keep the proofs of these results as simple as possible, we handle this in stages.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Also assume that the conductor ideal
I=n\overline{R}
is a primary ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is,
n=p^{a}
for some ramified or inert prime
p
and
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
P
be the unique prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
p
. Then an element
\pi\in P
lies in
\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
if and only if there exist
1\leq j\leq a
and
\alpha\in\overline{R}
with no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
such that
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
and one of the following cases holds:

(1)

j=1
and
\alpha\in U(R_{n})
;

(2)

\alpha\notin P
and
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}\backslash R_{p^{a-j+1}}
;

(3)

j=a
,
p
is ramified in
K
, and
\alpha\in P\backslash P^{2}
.

Proof.

First, we will show that any element
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
which satisfies the above conditions is necessarily an irreducible element of
R_{n}
; then we will show the converse. In case (1), we have that
\pi=p\alpha
for some
\alpha\in U(R_{n})
(note that such an
\alpha
will necessarily have no nonunit divisors). Clearly
\pi\in R_{n}
as a product of two elements of
R_{n}
. If
p
is inert, then
\pi
is irreducible in
\overline{R}
and therefore irreducible in
R_{n}
as well. Otherwise,
(p)=P^{2}
and any nontrivial factorization
\pi=\beta\gamma
in
R_{n}
would necessarily have
\beta,\gamma\in R_{n}\cap P\backslash(p)
. However, Lemma
5.1
tells us that
R_{n}\cap P\subseteq(p)
, so
\pi
must be irreducible in
R_{n}
.

In case (2),
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
for some
1\leq j\leq a
with
\alpha\notin P
and
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}\backslash R_{p^{a-j+1}}
. By Lemma
5.3
, any such
\pi
must lie in
R_{n}
. Using Lemma
5.1
, we know that any nontrivial factorization of
\pi
in
R_{n}
must necessarily be of the form
\pi=(p^{k}\beta)(p^{j-k}\gamma)
for some
0\leq k\leq j
and
\beta,\gamma\in\overline{R}
. Assume without loss of generality that
k\leq j-k
. If
k=0
, then
\alpha=\beta\gamma
with
\beta
a nonunit lying in
R_{n}
, contradicting the fact that
\alpha
has no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
. Then
1\leq k\leq j-k\leq j-1
. Once again using Lemma
5.3
, we know that
\beta\in R_{p^{a-k}}\subseteq R_{p^{a-j+1}}
and
\gamma\in R_{p^{a-(j-k)}}\subseteq R_{p^{a-j+1}}
. Therefore,
\alpha=\beta\gamma\in R_{p^{a-j+1}}
, a contradiction. Then
\pi\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
.

In case (3),
p
is ramified and
\pi=p^{a}\alpha
with
\alpha\in P\backslash P^{2}
. If
\pi
factors nontrivially in
R_{n}
as
\pi=\beta\gamma
, then exactly one of
\beta\overline{R}
or
\gamma\overline{R}
must be exactly divisible by an odd power of
P
. However, Lemma
5.1
assures us that this is impossible if this prime power is less than
2a
. Then without loss of generality, we can assume that
\beta\in P^{2a+1}
and
\gamma\notin P
. However, this again means that
\gamma
is a nonunit element of
R_{n}
which divides
\alpha
, a contradiction. Thus,
\pi\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
.

We now show the converse. Assume that
\pi\in P
is an irreducible element of
R_{n}
. Since
\pi\in P
, Lemma
5.1
assures us that
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\nmid\alpha
. Moreover, if
j<a
, then the same lemma tells us that
\alpha\notin P
. We also note that if
j>a
, then
\pi=p^{j-a}(p^{a}\alpha)
is a factorization of
\pi
in
R_{n}
, a contradiction. Then either
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
for some
1\leq j\leq a
with
\alpha\notin P
; or
p
is ramified and
\pi=p^{a}\alpha
for some
\alpha\in P\backslash P^{2}
. In any case, note that if
\alpha
were to have a nonunit factor
\beta\in R_{n}
(i.e. if
\alpha=\beta\gamma
for some
\gamma\in\overline{R}
), then this
\beta
must necessarily lie outside of
P
. Thus,
\beta+I\in U(\large\nicefrac{{R_{n}}}{{I}}\normalsize)
, so
(\pi+I)(\beta+I)^{-1}=p^{j}\gamma+I\in R_{n}+I
. Then
p^{j}\gamma\in R_{n}
as well, contradicting the irreducibility of
\alpha
in
R_{n}
. We conclude that
\alpha
must have no nonunit divisors lying
R_{n}
.

All that remains to show is that if
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
for some
1\leq j\leq a
with
\alpha\notin P
, then either case (1) or case (2) holds. Note that since
p^{j}\alpha=\pi\in R_{n}
, then Lemma
5.3
tells us that
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}
. Then suppose that
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j+1}}
. By the same lemma, we know that
p^{j-1}\alpha\in R_{n}
, and thus
\alpha=p(p^{j-1}\alpha)
. If either
p^{j-1}
or
\alpha
is a nonunit, this contradicts the irreducibility of
\alpha
. Then either
j=1
and
\alpha\in U(R_{n})
(note here that
n=p^{a}=p^{a-j+1}
) or
\alpha\notin R_{p^{a-j+1}}
.
∎

We now define a notation that will help us more easily describe the possible number of prime ideal factors of a principal ideal of
\overline{R}
generated by an irreducible element of
R_{n}
.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Also assume that the conductor ideal
I=n\overline{R}
is a primary ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is, that
n=p^{a}
for some ramified or inert prime
p
and
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
P
be the unique prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
p
. For each
1\leq j\leq a
, we define the constant
d_{j}
as follows:

d_{j}=\begin{cases}d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n})),&\textup{if }U(R_{p^{a-j}})\neq U(R_{p^{a-j+1}});\\
d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n})),&\textup{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here,
\tau_{i}
refers to the surjective function
\tau_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\textup{Cl}(R_{n})\to\textup{Cl}(R_{p^{i}})
defined by
\tau_{i}([J])=[JR_{p^{i}}]
(for proof of surjectivity, see
[
6
, Lemma 2.13]
). Note that Lemma
5.4
guarantees that when
U(R_{p^{a-j}})=U(R_{p^{a-j+1}})
,
\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})
is nonempty.

As we will see in the proof of the following statement,
d_{j}
is defined in such a way that it will give the length of the longest sequence
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d}]\}
in
\textup{Cl}(R)
with no zero-sum subsequence such that
\alpha\overline{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(A_{1}\cdots A_{d})\overline{R}
is principal and
\alpha
can be chosen from
R_{p^{a-j}}\backslash R_{p^{a-j+1}}
. It is especially worth noting that in cases when Conjecture
2.17
holds,
d_{j}
will simply be equal to
d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
(in particular, Corollary
2.15
guarantees that this holds when
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
is cyclic).

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Also assume that the conductor ideal
I=(n)
is a primary ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is,
n=p^{a}
for some ramified or inert prime
p
and
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
P
be the unique prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
p
. Let
\pi\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
, and let
p^{j}
be the largest power of
p
dividing
\pi
in
\overline{R}
. Finally, let
d_{j}
be as defined in Definition
5.6
for each
1\leq j\leq a
. Then:

(1)

If
j=0
, then
\pi\overline{R}
factors into at most
D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
.

(2)

If
j\geq 1
and
p
is inert, then
\pi\overline{R}
factors into at most
j+d_{j}
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
.

(3)

If
j\geq 1
,
p
is ramified, and
\pi\notin P^{2a+1}
, then
\pi\overline{R}
factors into at most
2j+d_{j}
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
.

(4)

If
p
is ramified and
\pi\in P^{2a+1}
, then
\pi\overline{R}
factors into at most
2a+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
.

Moreover, these upper bounds are achieved for each
1\leq j\leq a
.

Proof.

From Lemma
5.1
, we know that if
p\nmid\pi\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
(i.e. if
j=0
), then
\pi\notin P
. Item (1) follows immediately as a porism to the proof of Lemma
3.12
; this also shows that the upper bound given in (1) is achievable.

Otherwise, we have that
\pi
is an irreducible element of
R_{n}
lying in
P
, so we can apply Lemma
5.5
to write
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
for some
1\leq j\leq a
and
\alpha\in\overline{R}
with no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
. If
j=1
and
\alpha\in U(R_{n})
, then
\pi\overline{R}
factors either into
1\leq j+d_{j}
prime ideal factors if
p
is inert or
2\leq 2j+d_{j}
prime ideal factors if
p
is ramified.

Now assume that
\pi
falls under case (3) of Lemma
5.5
; that is,
j=a
,
p
is ramified in
K
, and
\alpha\in P\backslash P^{2}
. Then
\pi\overline{R}=P^{2a+1}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
for some prime ideals
Q_{i}
of
\overline{R}
distinct from
P
. Since
\pi
is irreducible in
R_{n}
, it must be the case that
\pi
has no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
which are relatively prime to
I
. Otherwise, if such a divisor
\beta
were to exist, we could factor
\pi=\beta\gamma
with
\beta\in R_{n}
a nonunit and
\gamma\in P^{2a+1}\subseteq R_{n}
a nonunit, contradicting the irreducibility of
\pi
. By Lemma
3.11
, the sequence
\{[R_{n}\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R_{n}\cap Q_{k}]\}
must have no zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
. Since
\alpha\overline{R}=PQ_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
is principal, then
\{[Q_{1}],\cdots,[Q_{k}]\}
must be a
[P]^{-1}
-sequence in
\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
, meaning that

. Thus,
\pi\overline{R}
factors into at most
2a+1+d_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))=2a+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
, completing the proof of item (4).

To see that the upper bound in (4) is achieved, we use the definition of
d_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
to select a
[P]^{-1}
-sequence
\{[Q_{1}],\dots,[Q_{d}]\}
of length
d=d_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
in
\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
with each

a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
relatively prime to
I
such that
\{[R_{n}\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R_{n}\cap Q_{d}]\}
has no zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
. Then let
\pi=p^{k}\alpha
, where
\alpha
is a principal generator of the ideal
PQ_{1}\cdots Q_{d}
. Since
R_{n}\cap P\subseteq P^{2}
,
\alpha\in P\backslash P^{2}
, and
\{[R_{n}\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R_{n}\cap Q_{d}]\}
has no zero-sum subsequence, it must be the case that
\alpha
has no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
. By case (3) of Lemma
5.5
,
\pi
is an irreducible in
R_{n}
such that
\pi\overline{R}
factors into exactly
2a+1+d=2a+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
.

The only case remaining to consider is when
\pi
falls under case (2) of Lemma
5.5
; that is, when
\alpha\notin P
and
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}\backslash R_{p^{a-j+1}}
. In this case, we factor
\alpha\overline{R}=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
, with each
Q_{i}
a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
distinct from
P
. Since
p^{j}\alpha=\pi\in R_{n}
, then Lemma
5.3
tells us that
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}
. Then
\alpha R_{p^{a-j}}=(R_{p^{a-j}}\cap Q_{1})\cdots(R_{p^{a-j}}\cap Q_{k})
is principal. Furthermore, since
\alpha
has no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
, Lemma
3.11
tells us that
\{[R_{n}\cap Q_{1}],\dots,[R_{n}\cap Q_{k}]\}
must have no zero-sum subsequence in
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
. Since this sequence must also lie in
\ker(\tau_{a-j})
, it follows that
k\leq d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
. Moreover, assume that
U(R_{p^{a-j}})=U(R_{p^{a-j+1}})
. By Lemma
5.4
, this also means that
\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_{p^{a-j}})}<\absolutevalue{\Cl(R_{p^{a-j+1}})}
, so
\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})
is a nonempty subset of
\ker(\tau_{a-j})
. Suppose that

\displaystyle\alpha R_{p^{a-j}}

\displaystyle=(R_{p^{a-j}}\cap Q_{1})\cdots(R_{p^{a-j}}\cap Q_{k})

\displaystyle=[(R_{p^{a-j+1}}\cap Q_{1})\cdots(R_{p^{a-j+1}}\cap Q_{k})]R_{p^{a-j}}

\displaystyle=(\beta R_{p^{a-j+1}})R_{p^{a-j}}=\beta R_{p^{a-j}}.

Then there exists some

such that

, so

k\leq d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
. In any case, we see that

, so

factors into at most

prime ideals of
\overline{R}
if

is inert and at most

prime ideals of
\overline{R}
if

is ramified, completing the proofs of items (2) and (3).

All that remains to show is that the upper bounds in items (2) and (3) are achievable for each
1\leq j\leq a
. Assume first that
U(R_{p^{a-j}})=U(R_{p^{a-j+1}})
; we note again that by Lemma

,
\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})
is a nonempty subset of
\ker(\tau_{a-j})
. Then by definition of the small
S
-relative Davenport constant, there exists a sequence
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d_{j}}]\}
in
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
of length
d_{j}=d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
which has no zero-sum sequence in
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
whose product lies in
\ker(\tau_{a-j})
but not in
\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})
. Now for
1\leq i\leq d_{j}
, let
Q_{i}
be a prime ideal of
\overline{R}
distinct from
P
such that
R\cap Q_{i}\in[A_{i}]
. By definition of the
\tau_{i}
’s, the construction of the sequence
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d_{j}}]\}
, and Lemma
3.11
, this means that there is a principal generator
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}\backslash R_{p^{a-j+1}}
of the ideal
\alpha\overline{R}=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{k}
which has no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
. By Lemma
5.5
, the element
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
is an irreducible in
R_{n}
such that
\pi\overline{R}
factors into exactly
j+d_{j}
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
if
p
is inert and
2j+d_{j}
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
if
p
is ramified.

If
U(R_{p^{a-j}})\neq U(R_{p^{a-j+1}})
, then we proceed in a very similar manner. The only difference is that we now relax the condition that
\{[A_{1}],\dots,[A_{d_{j}}]\}
must avoid being a
\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})
-sequence. This allows us to select such a sequence of length

. We again find a principal generator
\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j}}
of the ideal

and note that
\alpha
must have no nonunit divisors lying in
R_{n}
. If
\alpha\notin R_{p^{a-j+1}}
already, then
\pi=p^{j}\alpha
is an irreducible in
R_{n}
such that
\pi\overline{R}
factors into exactly

prime ideals of
\overline{R}
if

is inert and
2j+d_{j}
prime ideals of
\overline{R}
is

\alpha\in R_{p^{a-j+1}}
, then we select a unit
u\in U(R_{p^{a-j}})\backslash U(R_{p^{a-j+1}})
. If
\alpha u=r\in R_{p^{a-j+1}}

\alpha+(p^{a-j+1})\in U(\large\nicefrac{{R_{p^{a-j+1}}}}{{(p^{a-j+1}}}\normalsize))
,

u+(p^{a-j+1})=(r+(p^{a-j+1}))(\alpha+(p^{a-j+1}))^{-1}\in\large\nicefrac{{R_{p^{a-j+1}}}}{{(p^{a-j+1})}}\normalsize.

Then

We now take advantage of these observations to find the elasticity of any quadratic order with primary conductor ideal.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
R_{n}
be the index
n
order in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Also assume that the conductor ideal
I=(n)
is a primary ideal of
\overline{R}
; that is,
n=p^{a}
for some ramified or inert prime
p
and
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
P
be the unique prime ideal of
\overline{R}
lying over
p
. Finally, let
d_{j}
be as defined in Definition
5.6
for each
1\leq j\leq a
. If
p
is ramified, then

\rho(R_{n})=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2},a+\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2},j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}\middle|1\leq j\leq a\right\}.

If
p
is inert, then

\rho(R_{n})=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2},j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}\middle|1\leq j\leq a\right\}.

In particular,
\rho(R_{n})\geq a
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a nonzero, nonunit element of
R_{n}
. If
\rho_{R_{n}}(\alpha)=1
, then the elasticity is trivially bounded above by the given maxima. Otherwise, we can use Lemma
3.10
to remove any prime element factors of
\alpha
relatively prime to
P
and produce an element with at least as large elasticity. After doing so, we factor
\alpha=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{s}=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{t}
, with each
\pi_{i},\tau_{j}\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
. If
p
is ramified, then Lemma
5.1
tells us that each
\tau_{j}\overline{R}
must factor into at least two prime ideal factors. Furthermore, Lemma
5.7
tells us that each
\pi_{i}\overline{R}
can have at most
M=\max\left\{D(\textup{Cl}(R)),2a+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R)),2j+d_{j}|1\leq j\leq a\right\}
prime ideal factors. Then letting
r
be the number of prime ideal factors of
\alpha\overline{R}
,
s\cdot M\geq r\geq 2t
, so
\frac{t}{s}\leq\frac{M}{2}
. Since this serves as an upper bound for the elasticity of any
\alpha\in R_{n}
,

\rho(R_{n})\leq\frac{M}{2}=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2},a+\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2},j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}\middle|1\leq j\leq a\right\}.

To see that this upper bound is achievable, we note that Lemma
3.12
guarantees the existence of an element
\alpha\in R_{n}
with elasticity at least
\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
. We simply need to show that elements of
R_{n}
can be constructed with elasticity at least
a+\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
and at least
j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}
for each
1\leq j\leq a
. By Lemma
5.7
, there is some
\beta\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
such that
\beta\overline{R}=P^{2a+1}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{d}
, where
d=d_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
. Now for each
1\leq i\leq d
, we can select some prime ideal
Q_{i}^{\prime}
in
\overline{R}
such that
[R_{n}\cap Q_{i}^{\prime}]=[R_{n}\cap Q_{i}]^{-1}
. By Lemmas
3.11
and
5.5
, any generator
\gamma\in R_{n}
of
\gamma\overline{R}=P^{2a+1}Q_{1}^{\prime}\cdots Q_{d}^{\prime}
must also be irreducible in
R_{n}
. Moreover, for each
1\leq i\leq d
, we can select an element
\pi_{i}\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
which generates
\pi_{i}\overline{R}=(Q_{1}Q_{1}^{\prime})
. Then for some unit
u\in U(\overline{R})
,

\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(u\beta)\gamma=p^{2a+1}\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{d}

is an element of
R_{n}
whose elasticity is at least
a+\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
.

Roughly the same argument shows that if
M=2j+d_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq a
, an element of
R_{n}
can be constructed with elasticity at least
\frac{M}{2}
. This is done by letting
\beta\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
be a principal generator of
P^{2j}Q_{1}\cdots Q_{d_{j}}
,
\gamma\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
a principal generator of
P^{2j}Q_{1}^{\prime}\cdots Q_{d_{j}}^{\prime}
, and
\pi_{i}\in\textup{Irr}(R_{n})
a principal generator of
Q_{i}Q_{i}^{\prime}
for each
1\leq i\leq d_{j}
. The only substantial difference in this case is that if
M=2j+d_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq k
, then we must be careful about the introduction of the unit
u
. In this case, however, we note that if there is any
j<i\leq a
for which
U(R_{p^{a-i}})\neq U(R_{p^{a-i+1}})
, then by Proposition
2.7
,
d_{j}\leq d_{\ker(\tau_{a-i})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))=d_{i}
. This implies that
M=2j+d_{j}<2i+d_{i}
, a contradiction, so we immediately get that
U(\overline{R})=U(R_{p})=\cdots=U(R_{p^{a-j}})
; that is, every unit
u\in U(\overline{R})
lies in
R_{p^{a-j}}
. Now when we produce the element

\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(u\beta)\gamma=p^{2j}\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{d_{j}},

we note that
u\in R_{p^{a-j}}=R_{n}+p^{a-j}\overline{R}
and
\beta\in p^{j}\overline{R}
. Then
u\beta\in R_{n}
, meaning that
\alpha
is still an element of
R_{n}
whose elasticity is at least
j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}
. This concludes the proof in the case that
p
is ramified.

Now assume that
p
is inert. The proof that the desired elasticity is achieved by an element of
R_{n}
is nearly identical to the previous case, with the notable difference being that
P
is now a principal prime ideal generated by
p\in R_{n}
. All that remains to show is that this is an upper bound on the elasticity of any element of
R_{n}
. Let
M=\max\{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}),2j+d_{j}|1\leq j\leq a\}
, and consider a nonzero, nonunit element
\alpha\in R_{n}
factored into irreducibles of
R_{n}
in two ways:
\alpha=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{s}=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{t}
. As before, we will assume that
\rho_{R_{n}}(\alpha)>1
and that none of the
\pi_{i}
’s or
\tau_{j}
’s are prime unless they generate
P
. Let
r
be the total number of prime ideals of
\overline{R}
in the factorization of
\alpha\overline{R}
;
x
the number of factors of
P
in this factorization; and
y
the number of factors of other prime ideals. Immediately,
r=x+y
. Now for each
1\leq i\leq t
, note that
\tau_{i}\overline{R}
must have at least one prime ideal factor if
\tau_{i}\in P
; otherwise,
\tau_{i}\overline{R}
must have at least two prime ideal factors distinct from
P
. That is,
2(t-x)+x=2t-x\leq r
.

Now considering the left-hand side of the equation, let
x_{i}
be the number of factors of
P
in the prime ideal factorization of
\pi_{i}\overline{R}
for each
1\leq i\leq s
, and let
y_{i}
be the number of prime ideal factors distinct from
P
in this factorization. Then
x=\sum_{i=1}^{s}x_{i}
and
y=\sum_{i=1}^{s}y_{i}
. If
\pi_{i}
does not lie in
P
, then note that
x_{i}=0
and
y_{i}\leq D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
. On the other hand, if
\pi_{i}
is exactly divisible by
p^{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq k
, then
x_{i}=j
and
y_{i}\leq d_{j}
by Lemma
5.7
. Then
2t-x\leq r=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(x_{i}+y_{i})
, and thus

\frac{t}{s}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{s}(x_{i}+y_{i})+x}{2s}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{s}(2x_{i}+y_{i})}{s}.

Since this final fraction is the average of the terms
2x_{i}+y_{i}
, which for each
1\leq i\leq s
must be equal to either
D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
or
2j+d_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq k
, then this average is bounded above by
M
, the largest possible value of any individual term. Since this forms an upper bound for the elasticity of any element of
R_{n}
, we get

\rho(R_{n})\leq\frac{M}{2}=\max\left\{\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2},j+\frac{r_{j}}{2}\middle|1\leq j\leq a\right\}.

Since this upper bound is also achieved as the elasticity of an element of
R_{n}
, this completes the proof in the case that
p
is inert.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

One will notice that in the proof of the last statement, we observed that, by Theorem

, for any

. This immediately gives rise to the following corollary.

As a final note, we turn our attention to a pair of conjectures. The first asks whether, given a half-factorial order
R
in a number field
K
, any order
T
with
R\subseteq T\subseteq\overline{R}
must also be an HFD. This conjecture seems to have first appeared (albeit indirectly) in
[
12
]
in 2005. In 2023,
[
23
]
suggested that a counterexample should exist, though an immediate corollary to
[
9
, Theorem 6]
shows that such an example could not appear in any quadratic number field. The question was also of central interest in
[
4
]
. In this paper, it was shown that the conjecture holds whenever the conductor ideal

of

is a radical ideal of
\overline{R}
.

The second, far stronger statement was conjectured in
[
16
]
. It asks whether, given any order
R
in a number field

, any order
T
with

must satisfy

. Although this question is likely far from being settled in full generality, it follows from Theorem
5.8
and a simple set-theoretic lemma that this conjecture must hold when
R
is a quadratic order with primary conductor. Moreover, we can find a characterization of when the elasticity of
R
and
T
must be equal; namely, when the elasticity of
R
(and of
T
) is achieved by an element relatively prime to the conductor.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
A
,
B
, and
C
be sets with functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\to B
and
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B\to C
. Let
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq B
and
S\subseteq C
. The following hold:

(1)

(g\circ f)^{-1}(S)=f^{-1}(g^{-1}(S))
;

(2)

f^{-1}(S_{2})\backslash f^{-1}(S_{1})=f^{-1}(S_{2}\backslash S_{1})
.

Proof.

Note that by definition of the preimage under a function,
x\in(g\circ f)^{-1}(S)
if and only if
(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))\in S
. This holds if and only if
f(x)\in g^{-1}(S)
, or equivalently,
x\in f^{-1}(g^{-1}(S))
. Thus,
(g\circ f)^{-1}(S)=f^{-1}(g^{-1}(S))
.

For the second item, note that
x\in f^{-1}(S_{2})\backslash f^{-1}(S_{1})
if and only if
f(x)\in S_{2}
but
f(x)\notin S_{1}
. The fact that this is equivalent to
x\in f^{-1}(S_{2}\backslash S_{1})
is obvious. Then
f^{-1}(S_{2})\backslash f^{-1}(S_{1})=f^{-1}(S_{2}\backslash S_{1}).

∎

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
R_{m}
and
R_{n}
be the index
m
and
n
orders, respectively, in the quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
. Also assume that
R_{m}\subsetneq R_{n}
(so
n|m
) and that
R_{m}
(and therefore
R_{n}
) has primary conductor ideal in
\overline{R}
; that is,
n=p^{a}
and
m=p^{b}
for some inert or ramified prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a<b
. Then
\rho(R_{m})\geq\rho(R_{n})
, with equality if and only if
\rho(R_{m})=\rho(R_{n})=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
(that is, the maximum elasticity is achieved by an element relatively prime to
p
).

Proof.

When finding the elasticity of the quadratic order
R_{n}
with primary conductor ideal, there are three quantities we need to consider:

\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2};\quad a+\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2};\quad j+\frac{d_{j}}{2},\>1\leq j\leq a.

If we show that the corresponding quantities for
R_{m}
can only grow, the result will follow.

First, we define surjective homomorphisms
\tau_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\textup{Cl}(R_{n})\to\textup{Cl}(R_{p^{i}})
and
\sigma_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\textup{Cl}(R_{m})\to\textup{Cl}(R_{p^{j}})
for
0\leq i\leq a
and
0\leq j\leq b
such that
\tau_{i}([A])=[AR_{p^{i}}]
and
\sigma_{j}([B])=[BR_{p^{j}}]
. In particular, note that
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau_{0}
maps from
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
to
\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
;
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma_{0}
maps from
\textup{Cl}(R_{m})
to
\textup{Cl}(\overline{R})
;
\sigma_{a}
maps from
\textup{Cl}(R_{m})
to
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
; and
\sigma_{i}=\tau_{i}\circ\sigma_{a}
for all
0\leq i\leq a
. Since
\sigma_{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\textup{Cl}(R_{m})\to\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
is a surjective homomorphism, it follows that
D(\textup{Cl}(R_{m}))\geq D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
.

For the second quantity, recall by the definition of our terminology that
D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))=D_{A}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
, where
A=\tau^{-1}(\{[P]\})
. Similarly,
D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m}))=D_{B}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m}))
, where
B=\sigma^{-1}(\{[P]\})
. By part (1) of Lemma
5.15
, this tells us that
B=\sigma_{a}^{-1}(A)
. Then treating
\sigma_{a}
as the projection of
\textup{Cl}(R_{m})
onto
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
and using Theorem
2.9
,

D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))=D_{A}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))\leq D_{B}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m}))=D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m})).

Thus,
a+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))<b+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m}))
.

Now let
1\leq j\leq a
, and recall the definition of
d_{j}
for
R_{n}
:

d_{j}=\begin{cases}d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n})),&\textup{if }U(R_{p^{a-j}})\neq U(R_{p^{a-j+1}});\\
d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n})),&\textup{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For ease of notation, we will similarly define
d_{j}^{\prime}
to be the corresponding values for
R_{m}
for
1\leq j\leq b
(that is, replace
a
and
\tau
in definition for
d_{j}
with
b
and
\sigma
, respectively). Since
\ker(\tau_{a-j})=\tau_{a-j}^{-1}(\{[R_{p^{a-j}}]\})
and
\ker(\sigma_{a-j})=\sigma_{a-j}^{-1}(\{[R_{p^{a-j}}]\})
, then item (1) from Lemma
5.15
tells us that

\ker(\sigma_{a-j})=\sigma_{a-j}^{-1}(\{[R_{p^{a-j}}]\})=\sigma_{a}^{-1}(\tau_{a-j}^{-1}(\{[R_{p^{a-j}}]\})=\sigma_{a}^{-1}(\ker(\tau_{a-j})).

Again treating
\sigma_{a}
as the projection of
\textup{Cl}(R_{m})
onto
\textup{Cl}(R_{n})
and using Theorem
2.9
,

d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))\leq d_{\ker(\sigma_{a-j})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m})).

By the same argument and using item (2) from Lemma
5.15
, we know that

\ker(\sigma_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\sigma_{a-j+1})=\sigma_{a}^{-1}(\ker(\tau_{a-j}))\backslash\sigma_{a}^{-1}(\ker(\tau_{a-j+1}))=\sigma_{a}^{-1}(\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})).

Once again applying this Theorem
2.9
,

d_{\ker(\tau_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\tau_{a-j+1})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))\leq d_{\ker(\sigma_{a-j})\backslash\ker(\sigma_{a-j+1})}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m})).

Then for
1\leq j\leq a
, we have
d_{j}\leq d_{(b-a)+j}^{\prime}
. Thus, for the third quantity which determines the elasticities of
R_{n}
and
R_{m}
, we have

j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}<(b-a)+j+\frac{d_{(b-a)+j}^{\prime}}{2},\>1\leq j\leq a.

By Theorem
5.8
, it follows that
\rho(R_{n})\leq\rho(R_{m})
.

Now suppose that
\rho(R_{m})=\rho(R_{n})
. If
\rho(R_{n})\neq\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
, then Theorem
5.8
tells us that
\rho(R_{n})
must instead be equal to either
a+\frac{D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
or
j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}
for some
1\leq j\leq a
. However, our earlier arguments showed us that
b+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{m}))>a+D_{P}(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))
and
(b-a)+j+\frac{d_{(b-a)+j}^{\prime}}{2}\geq j+\frac{d_{j}}{2}
. In particular, this means that
\rho(R_{m})>\rho(R_{n})
, contradicting our assumption. Then
\rho(R_{m})=\rho(R_{n})=\frac{D(\textup{Cl}(R_{n}))}{2}
. The converse is immediate, completing the proof.
∎
