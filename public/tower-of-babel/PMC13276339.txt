Acute respiratory decompensation revealing occupational baritosis: A case report
Authors: Ghizlane El Aidouni, Mohammed Taloubi, Manal Merbouh, lamyae A Sayad, Houssam Bkiyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBaritosis is a rare pneumoconiosis caused by chronic inhalation of barium sulfate dust and is traditionally considered a benign condition characterized mainly by radiological abnormalities and minimal functional impairment. However, prolonged exposure may lead to severe respiratory failure and cor pulmonale. We report the case of a 73-year-old man with a history of prolonged occupational exposure to barite and heavy smoking who presented with progressive dyspnea and acute respiratory deterioration. Clinical examination revealed hypoxemic respiratory failure and digital clubbing. Chest computed tomography demonstrated diffuse bilateral micronodular opacities with high attenuation consistent with pneumoconiosis. Arterial blood gas analysis showed hypercapnic respiratory failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed pulmonary hypertension with right ventricular dysfunction compatible with cor pulmonale. The patient was managed with oxygen therapy, bronchodilators, diuretics, corticosteroids, and empiric antibiotic therapy. This case challenges the traditional concept of baritosis as a purely benign pneumoconiosis and highlights its potential progression to clinically significant interstitial lung disease with pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure. Baritosis was suspected in an appropriate occupational context, namely the occupational history which remains essential for diagnosis.Keywords: Baritosis, Pneumoconiosis, Acute respiratory failure, Cor pulmonale, Occupational lung disease, Case report

Introduction

Pneumoconioses are interstitial lung diseases caused by inhalation and deposition of inorganic dust particles, leading to chronic pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis [1]. Among them, baritosis is caused by exposure to barium sulfate, commonly encountered in mining, drilling, and industrial manufacturing. Traditionally, baritosis has been considered a benign pneumoconiosis characterized by radiological abnormalities without functional impairment [2]. Nevertheless, isolated reports suggest that prolonged exposure may be associated with chronic respiratory symptoms and progressive lung disease.

We report a case of acute respiratory decompensation revealing advanced pulmonary involvement in a patient with remote occupational barite exposure, complicated by cor pulmonale.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old man from eastern Morocco was admitted for acute exacerbation of chronic dyspnea. His past medical history included heavy smoking (38 pack-years, stopped 14 years earlier) and occupational exposure to barite for approximately 10 years, discontinued 40 years before admission. He had no history of tuberculosis, no prior imaging studies, occupational surveillance imaging, or echocardiograms and he has never been previously diagnosed with COPD or interstitial lung disease. He had never received home oxygen therapy. The patient was followed for chronic pulmonary heart disease and was treated with diuretics, spironolactone, and beta-blockers.

Dyspnea had progressively developed over 5 years, initially graded as mMRC stage I and worsening to stage III during the previous 6 months (Table 1). Ten days before admission, he developed dyspnea at rest associated with productive cough and muco-purulent sputum, without fever or night sweats.

On admission, the patient was conscious but in respiratory distress. Oxygen saturation was 80% on room air, improving to 91% with 6 L/min oxygen via face mask. Digital clubbing was present. Lung auscultation revealed diffuse bilateral fine “velcro-like” crackles extending to the mid-lung fields. Hemodynamic status was stable (blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, heart rate 90 bpm). There was no peripheral edema.

Electrocardiogram (ECG) Findings: Sinus rhythm with right axis deviation, right atrial enlargement and right branch block (RBB).

Arterial blood gas analysis under oxygen therapy showed hypercapnic respiratory failure: pH 7.37, PaCO₂ 51 mmHg, PaO₂ 65 mmHg, and HCO₃⁻ 26 mmol/L. Laboratory tests showed mild inflammatory syndrome (CRP 22 mg/L) and marked polycythemia. Renal function and electrolytes were within normal limits (Table 2).

Chest radiography demonstrated a diffuse bilateral interstitial pattern composed of nodular and micronodular opacities involving both lung fields (Fig. 1).

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed right ventricular dysfunction with septal flattening, pulmonary hypertension, dilated inferior vena cava with reduced respiratory variation, small pericardial effusion, and bilateral pleural effusions, consistent with right heart failure. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is preserved, estimated at approximately 65%, indicating normal left ventricular systolic and diastolic function. Presence of dyskinesia with a D-shaped septum and paradoxical septal motion, suggestive of right ventricular pressure overload, no significant valvular disease was indicated.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary embolism and confirmed diffuse interstitial involvement (Fig. 2).

A bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed with histopathological confirmation of baritosis.

The patient was treated with oxygen therapy, nebulized bronchodilators, systemic corticosteroids, diuretics, anticoagulants, and empirical antibiotics. No admission to intensive care or intubation was required.

He was discharged from the hospital 12 days after admission, equipped with a home oxygen concentrator. Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension was considered.

The clinical picture was consistent with acute respiratory distress following baritosis disease, complicated by cor pulmonale.

This care report follow the care case report guidelines [3].

Discussion

Baritosis is an uncommon form of pneumoconiosis resulting from chronic inhalation of barium sulfate dust. Occupational exposure typically occurs in barite mining, grinding, and industrial processing activities. In contrast to classical fibrogenic pneumoconioses such as silicosis or asbestosis, baritosis has historically been considered a nonfibrotic benign pneumoconiosis characterized primarily by the accumulation of radiopaque particles within the pulmonary parenchyma. However, symptomatic cases have been reported, particularly after prolonged or intense exposure [[2], [3], [4]].

In our case, the diagnosis of baritosis was confirmed, while eliminating all other affections, namely COPD or smoking-related lung disease, silicosis due to silica impurities, pneumoconiosis due to mixed dust, prior infection, the other occupational pneumoconioses, pulmonary vascular and chronic thromboembolic disease.

Radiologically, baritosis is characterized by diffuse micronodular opacities with high radiodensity on chest radiography and high-resolution computed tomography. These radiological findings reflect the accumulation of barium sulfate particles within the alveolar spaces and interstitium. High-resolution CT usually demonstrates diffuse, well-defined micronodules with a reticulonodular pattern involving both lung fields without significant architectural distortion [5].

Although baritosis has historically been considered a benign condition with minimal functional impairment, several reports have described symptomatic patients presenting with progressive dyspnea, chronic cough, and radiological abnormalities. In some cases, coexisting inhalation of other mineral particles, including silica impurities present in barite mines, may contribute to pulmonary fibrosis and worsen respiratory outcomes [[6], [7]].

In the present case, the patient had a significant occupational exposure to barite combined with a history of heavy smoking. The presence of digital clubbing, diffuse crackles, hypercapnic respiratory failure and diffuse high-attenuation micronodular opacities on CT imaging strongly supports the diagnosis of baritosis. The associated development of pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction reflects chronic hypoxic vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling leading to cor pulmonale.

Only a limited number of cases describing clinically significant respiratory failure associated with baritosis have been reported in the literature [8].

There is no specific therapy for baritosis. Management is supportive and focuses on oxygen supplementation, treatment of acute exacerbations, and management of complications such as cor pulmonale. Corticosteroids and antibiotics are commonly used during acute decompensation, although evidence remains limited. Careful occupational history remains essential in patients presenting with unexplained diffuse pulmonary micronodules and chronic respiratory symptoms [9].

This case highlights that the findings were compatible with baritosis. However, the diagnosis was established based on clinical, radiological, professional, and histological criteria.

Conclusion

Baritosis is a rare occupational pneumoconiosis that is usually considered benign. However, Long-term occupational exposure to barite dust may occasionally lead to clinically significant interstitial lung disease. This case emphasizes that baritosis was suspected in the appropriate occupational context. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management are essential to reduce morbidity, prevent progression to advanced cardiopulmonary complications and improve quality of life.
