Dietary Adaptation in Paranthropus robustus Postcanine Teeth
Authors: M C O'Hara, S Chapple, P Mahoney, W S McGraw, Gary T Schwartz, M M Skinner, D Guatelli‐Steinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesRecent studies of Paranthropus robustus crania identified temporal trends in features associated with increased bite force production, coinciding with environmental shifts toward more arid conditions. To test whether the evolution of postcanine teeth also reflects increasing mechanical demands associated with hard food consumption, we analyzed trends in the architecture of P. robustus postcanine teeth, including crown area, tooth shape, and average (AET) and relative (RET) enamel thickness.Materials and Methods2D mesial sections were created from microCT scans of upper premolars (n = 24), upper molars (n = 14), and lower molars (n = 16) from multiple South African sites. Descriptive statistics, Mann–Whitney U tests, and Jonckheere‐Terpstra tests were used to identify significant differences over time, with Holm‐Bonferroni corrections applied for multiple comparisons.ResultsSignificant temporal trends existed in premolar morphology with increased crown area (CA), absolute crown strength (ACS), overall enamel thickness (AET and RET), and increased enamel thickness over the occlusal half of the tooth. Upper molars increased in CA but no other measured variable. Lower molars showed no significant temporal trends in any measured variable.DiscussionThese results suggest rapid dental evolution in response to dietary pressures, with varying and independent rates of change between different tooth types. These findings support the hypothesis that selection resulted in shifts to P. robustus dental morphology to increase mechanical resistance, consistent with an expanding dietary niche that incorporated increasingly challenging foods.Keywords: dental evolution, dietary adaptation, Durophagy, enamel thickness, hominin evolution, microevolution, Paranthropus robustus

Introduction

Several dental features may relate to the consumption of mechanically resistant or hard‐object foods (durophagy) in non‐human primates, including hominins. Large tooth size (Constantino et al. 2012; Constantino et al. 2011; Janis and Fortelius 1988), low‐crowned, “bunodont” teeth (Constantino et al. 2009; Kay and Hylander 1978; Kay 1981; Luke and Lucas 1983; Spears and Crompton 1996), thick enamel (L. Martin 1985; Janis and Fortelius 1988; Dumont 1995; Pampush et al. 2013), unevenly distributed enamel (Gantt 1986; Molnar and Gantt 1977; Macho and Thackeray 1992, 1993; Schwartz 2000; Kono 2004; Lucas et al. 2008a; Thiery et al. 2017a, b, c), and high degrees of enamel decussation (Popowics et al. 2001, 2004; Lucas et al. 2008b) have all been suggested to protect teeth against cracks and/or crack propagation during hard‐object feeding and thereby enhance the functional longevity of molars. These aspects of dental form are thought to represent adaptive responses to durophagous diets (Jolly 1970; Kay 1981; Janis and Fortelius 1988; Constantino et al. 2019).

Paranthropus robustus is characterized by distinctive craniodental features, including molarized premolars and thick enamel, traditionally interpreted as adaptations for processing mechanically challenging foods (Bailey and Wood 2007; Williams 2015). A recent study (Martin et al. 2021) compared P. robustus crania from Drimolen (2.04–1.95mya) to those from Swartkrans Member 1 Hanging Remnant and Kromdraai B (~1.8mya). The authors identified a distinct temporal trend such that later (geologically younger) P. robustus were characterized by features including larger teeth and more anteriorly positioned sagittal crests and zygomatic roots, which are associated with production of greater bite forces (Martin et al. 2021). A drying period and expansion of shrubland‐type vegetation in this region of South African is also documented between Drimolen and Swartkrans, leading Martin et al. (2021) to suggest that the lineage of P. robustus adapted to a diet that became increasingly more mechanically challenging over this time period:

…those morphological changes that are functionally related to the performance of the feeding apparatus […] are dietary adaptations. Specifically, we infer (but cannot prove) that they resulted from ecologically driven directional or, perhaps, variability selection favouring increased efficiency at producing and withstanding higher bite forces. This probably expanded the P. robustus dietary niche to include more foods that are mechanically challenging to process, allowing increased ecological generalization. (Martin et al. 2021: 43)

Martin et al.'s (2021) analysis presents an excellent framework for evaluating whether dental form in P. robustus followed a similar trajectory. Given their claim that P. robustus was evolving in an environment becoming increasingly arid and was therefore capable of processing a more mechanically challenging foods with higher bite forces (Martin et al. 2021), we propose that, over time P. robustus teeth should evince morphological changes in tooth size, tooth shape, and/or enamel thickness that are consistent with an increased reliance on mechanically challenging foodstuffs.

Dental Morphology and Durophagy

Many aspects of tooth size, tooth shape, and enamel thickness associated with durophagy may be related to body size or phylogenetic traits other than those necessary for durophagy: to prevent teeth from cracking and be more fracture resistant than the food being consumed (Constantino et al. 2011; Lucas et al. 2008a, b; Luke and Lucas 1983). For example, thick enamel is more resistant to crack formation compared to thinner enamel, but thick enamel also lengthens the functional lifespan of enamel prior to dentin and pulp exposure with abrasive diets (Rabenold and Pearson 2011; Pampush et al. 2013). Likewise, larger teeth are generally more resistant to cracks under high masticatory loading (Janis and Fortelius 1988; Constantino et al. 2012; Constantino et al. 2011).

Teeth, and specifically enamel, are under strong selection pressure because enamel forms the occlusal surface and therefore makes direct contact with food objects, and any wear or damage accrued is permanent, as enamel does not remodel. During mastication, a food bolus is comminuted, that is, reduced to smaller, digestible sizes between the enamel surfaces of opposing teeth. If food is not properly processed due to inadequate or compromised teeth, nutrients may be inaccessible to an organism, leading to undernutrition (King et al. 2005). When hard foods are consumed, objects must be reduced without inducing mechanical failure of the teeth that process them. Primates with similar dietary needs for crushing and grinding, compared to shearing, share similar molar morphologies (Kay 1975; Gantt 1986; Guy et al. 2015; Thiery et al. 2017a, b, c). Selective pressures are therefore proposed to be based on the diet and masticatory needs of a species, as teeth are required to adequately process food throughout an individual's life (Janis and Fortelius 1988).

Tooth Size

Given the same morphology, a larger tooth can protect against enamel cracks compared to a smaller tooth of similar morphology because wider occlusal surfaces sustain lower stresses per unit area compared to teeth with smaller occlusal surfaces (Janis and Fortelius 1988). In primates, absolutely larger teeth, particularly wider teeth, can withstand higher occlusal loads before enamel cracks appear (Constantino et al. 2012; Constantino et al. 2011; Constantino et al. 2009; Lawn and Lee 2009). When comparing the critical load required to generate margin fractures in sea otters, Paranthropus boisei, and H. sapiens molars, Constantino et al. (2011) concluded that because of their significantly larger and more thickly‐enameled teeth, P. boisei molars could withstand nearly three times the load of molars from H. sapiens . While large teeth may be selected for in certain lineages for a variety of reasons, tooth size also tends to increase with body size across species. Thus, in large‐bodied species, large teeth will withstand high loads, potentially serving as an exaptation (Gould and Vrba 1982) to durophagy, as Schwartz et al. (2020) suggest may be the case for gorillas whose teeth are adapted to leaf‐eating but in at least one population, can and do consume hard Coula edulis nuts (van Casteren et al. 2019).

Tooth Shape

Tooth shape, specifically the geometric aspects of occlusal form such as cusp relief, may protect postcanine teeth during high loading. Molars are defined as “bunodont” when they possess low (low‐relief or “short”), rounded cusps (Swindler 2002). Limited shearing crests and relatively flat (low relief) crowns dissipate high occlusal forces generated when crushing hard/tough foods, preventing cracks and facilitating grinding (Jolly 1970; Kay 1981). Similarly, a relatively blunt cusp is suggested to be ideal for cracking a hard object and then reducing it via grinding (Jolly 1970; Luke and Lucas 1983; Constantino et al. 2009). Thus, low‐relief molars with obtuse cusp angles are more effective at crushing objects and more resistant to high loads than those with highly angled and tall cusps (Kay and Hylander 1978; Spears and Crompton 1996). Species that crush hard objects are therefore expected to have low‐relief (short) molars with more bunodont cusps and more obtuse cusp angles.

Enamel Thickness

There have been at least three major claims about how enamel thickness varies between species and how it may relate to the ability to resist fracture: (1) Durophagy is often associated with thick enamel (usually measured with the index of relative enamel thickness, “RET”) (L. Martin 1985; Dumont 1995; Pampush et al. 2013), (2) durophagy will be associated with thickened enamel over cusp tips (Kono 2004; Lucas et al. 2008b; Chai 2018), and (3) the “functional” sides of teeth are bigger/thicker than the “non‐functional” side (Kay 1975; Molnar and Gantt 1977; Khera et al. 1990; Schwartz 2000). This study examines these aspects of enamel thickness and its distribution in P. robustus and tests for evidence of directional change over time.

Enamel thickness has historically been measured using either summary measures (e.g., L. Martin (1985)'s indices of average enamel thickness, AET, and relative enamel thickness, RET) that model enamel as uniformly thick (or thin) across the crown, or by evaluating its complex distribution by using linear and regional measures of thickness at various locations on the crown (Grine and Martin 1988; Schwartz 2000). Thick enamel protects against wear in abrasive diets, and in diets that include hard foods (see Pampush et al. (2013)). Primates that specialize in consuming hard, resistant foods have been reported to have thicker indices of relative enamel thickness (RET) when compared to closely related soft‐object feeding primates (Dumont 1995; McGraw et al. 2012).

According to Lucas et al. (2008b), an adaptation to durophagy involves an “uneven” distribution of enamel whereby cusp tips are reinforced with thickened enamel. This expectation is partially due to the fact that occlusal forces are maximized at cusp tips (Chai 2018). This thickening, or reinforcement, is suggested to protect the cusp tips from cracking. Thiery et al. (2017a, b, c) tested this hypothesis by measuring the unevenness of enamel in non‐human primate upper second molars and found that more “uneven” enamel distribution is related to hard object feeding in cercopithecid monkeys. That study, however, did not report the specific regions of the enamel cap that were thickened with respect to durophagy.

Schwartz (2000) found that upper lingual molar cusps have thicker enamel than their buccal counterparts, especially in H. sapiens and Pongo. Maxillary lingual and mandibular buccal cusps have been referred to as “functional” or “loading” cusps and, in general, functional cusps tend to be more thickly enameled (Molnar and Gantt 1977; Schwartz 2000). During Phase I of the chewing cycle, food objects are crushed between the upper and lower functional/loading cusp tips (protocone and protoconid, respectively) and the opposing tooth's occlusal basin (Kay 1975; Schwartz 2000). While the term “functional” is misleading given that both sides of the tooth are used during the breakdown of a food bolus, it is a useful short‐hand for indicating the side of the tooth crown most engaged during crushing activities, which is particularly relevant for hard object feeding.

Because cusp tips approach the opposite occlusal basin when crushing a food bolus, hard object feeders should show evidence of enamel thickening in protocone and protoconid cusp tips and in their occlusal basins relative to their lateral walls (Macho and Berner 1994; Schwartz 2000; Kono 2004; Lucas et al. 2008a; Le Luyer et al. 2014). Alternatively, a recent study of enamel thickness and fracture potential suggests that cuspal enamel may be thickened simply as a developmental sequala of selection for thicker enamel on lateral crown walls which would strengthen a crown against margin fractures (Schwartz et al. 2020).

Goals

This study examines whether mechanically relevant aspects of the postcanine dentition in P. robustus follow a temporal trajectory that is equivalent to aspects of the cranium that are related to generating masticatory loads (Martin et al. 2021), and thereby reflect an additional anatomical signal of adaptations to changing dietary demand. Specifically, we propose that if P. robustus incorporated greater proportions of hard‐object foods in its diet in response to an increasingly arid environment, we should see evidence that over that same time interval, the postcanine teeth became larger, with flatter or more bunodont cusps, more thickly enameled, and/or characterized by thicker enamel concentrated across the occlusal basin and cusp tips.

We test the following hypotheses:

H1 (Tooth size and shape): If P. robustus postcanine teeth evolved toward greater resistance to mechanical loading, then the crown area and absolute crown strength (ACS) should increase, and cusp geometry should shift toward being flatter (i.e., more bunodont in the geologically younger samples).

H1 (Tooth size and shape): If P. robustus postcanine teeth evolved toward greater resistance to mechanical loading, then the crown area and absolute crown strength (ACS) should increase, and cusp geometry should shift toward being flatter (i.e., more bunodont in the geologically younger samples).

H2 (Whole‐crown enamel thickness): If durophagy increased over time, average enamel thickness (AET) and relative enamel thickness (RET) should increase in geologically younger samples.

H2 (Whole‐crown enamel thickness): If durophagy increased over time, average enamel thickness (AET) and relative enamel thickness (RET) should increase in geologically younger samples.

H3 (Enamel distribution): If structural reinforcement was concentrated at sites of peak occlusal loading, linear and regional enamel thickness should increase preferentially over the functional cusp tips and occlusal basin relative to the lateral and marginal walls, producing a more “uneven” distribution of enamel through time.

H3 (Enamel distribution): If structural reinforcement was concentrated at sites of peak occlusal loading, linear and regional enamel thickness should increase preferentially over the functional cusp tips and occlusal basin relative to the lateral and marginal walls, producing a more “uneven” distribution of enamel through time.

Given prior evidence that enamel thickness is more evolutionarily labile than crown size or shape (Hlusko 2016; Hlusko et al. 2004; Ungar and Sponheimer 2011), we further predict that H2 and H3 will show stronger and more consistent support than H1. We analyze temporal trends in cusp shape, crown size and strength, and enamel thickness distribution across different tooth types to test whether any observable changes align with expectations for adaptations to more mechanically resistant foods.

Materials and Methods

Sample

We examined digital 2D slices through the mesial cusps of P. robustus upper and lower postcanine teeth (n = 54), including lower first molars (LM1, n = 8), lower second molars (LM2, n = 8), upper first molars (UM1, n = 8), upper second molars (UM2, n = 6), upper third premolars (UP3, n = 11), and upper fourth premolars (UP4, n = 13). Specimens derive from the Drimolen Main Quarry (2.04–1.95 Ma), Swartkrans Members 1 Hanging Remnant (2.0–1.8 Ma), Member 2 (1.71–1.1 Ma), and Member 3 (1.3–0.6 Ma), and Kromdraai B (~1.8 Ma) (Table 1 and Table S5, (Herries and Adams 2013; Herries et al. 2020)). To facilitate the study of temporal trends, we organized the specimens into three time periods: (1) “Earlier” which includes the Drimolen specimens, (2) “Member 1 HR” which includes specimens from Swartkrans Member 1 HR, and (3) “Later,” which contains specimens from Kromdraai B and Swartkrans Members 2 and 3 (see Table 1).

Two‐dimensional slices across the mesial cusps were generated using the methods established by Skinner et al. (2013; 2015) using Dragonfly 22.0. Tooth Size

Tooth size was evaluated using three measurements, defined below: crown area (CA), bi‐cervical diameter (BCD), and enamel dentin junction (EDJ) length (Figure 1, Table 1). In this study, CA is represented by the total area of the entire crown (i.e., the enamel cap and dentin) that is exposed between the outer enamel surface and BCD of the 2D slice. BCD is defined as the length of a line that extends between the cemento‐enamel junction on each side of the virtually sectioned tooth. EDJ length is the length a line running along that tissue interface from one cervical margin to the other (Table 1, Figure 1). A tendency to increase the amount of hard object, or mechanically resistant, foods should correspond to increasing CAs, EDJ lengths, and BCDs because larger tooth crowns are better able to dissipate high occlusal loads (Janis and Fortelius 1988; Constantino et al. 2012; Constantino et al. 2011; Lawn and Lee 2009).

Tooth Shape

Tooth shape was evaluated using two direct measurements and two variables calculated from other measurements. Two measurements of cusp angle were taken on the functional (Fangle) and non‐functional (Nangle) cusps. Cusp angles were measured as the included angle between the lowest point of the occlusal basin, the maximum extent of the cusp tip, and a point on the lateral wall where a line tangent to the occlusal basin and parallel to the BCD intersects with the outer enamel surface (see Figure 1B). Teeth that are more bunodont can better dissipate high loading forces, so increasing durophagy should be associated with cusp angles becoming more obtuse (Constantino et al. 2009).

To compare the degree of bunodonty between species while attempting to scale for overall differences in tooth size, a crown shape index (CSI) was calculated as the square root of CA divided by BCD (Table 3). Values greater than or equal to 1 represent teeth that are equally tall or taller than they are wide, while values less than 1 represent teeth that are wider than they are tall. Low‐cusped, bunodont crowns will be wider than they are tall, so increasing levels of durophagy should be associated with reductions in the CSI values and would typically be less than 1.

Whole Crown Enamel Thickness

Overall enamel thickness and its distribution across the crown are broken down into six categories: total enamel thickness across the virtual section through the crown, linear enamel thicknesses, regional enamel thicknesses, proportional linear enamel thicknesses scaled to AET, and proportional regional enamel thicknesses scaled to AET.

Average enamel thickness (AET) and relative enamel thickness (RET) were established by L. B. Martin (1983) as estimates of whole crown enamel thickness. AET is calculated by dividing the enamel cap area by the EDJ length (see Table 1, Figure 1C), while RET corrects AET for scaling effects by dividing the latter by the square root of dentin area and multiplying by 100. Thus, RET is a unitless index and is often used to compare overall enamel thickness between species of various body/tooth sizes, while AET is appropriate only when comparing teeth within a species because it is not scaled in any way (Smith, Kupczik, et al. 2012a). Durophagous groups are predicted to have greater AET and RET values compared to non‐durophagous groups because thick enamel is expected to be protective against cracking during hard object feeding. Thus, if there is an increase in durophagy over time, then AET and RET values in P. robustus should increase from geologically older to younger points along the P. robustus lineage in South Africa.

Linear Enamel Thickness

Unlike the indices described above, linear enamel thickness measurements provide information about the distribution of enamel over the crown by quantifying the exact thickness in specific areas. More than 30 linear measurements have been used in the literature to measure enamel thickness (see Figure S1). Depending on the tooth's morphology, some measurements are more comparable than others, but when comparing among species, it is important to recognize that small differences in morphology can exaggerate differences in linear enamel thickness values between species (Smith et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2005; Suwa and Kono 2005). For example, if the cusp tip sits directly above the dentin horn in one species, the cusp tip enamel thickness will be smaller than one where the cusp tip sits lateral to the dentin horn. Skinner et al. (2015) note the substantial variation in measurements of enamel thickness depending on locations of measurement, and tooth types that have been used over the years and call into question the biological homology of the data that have been published (Figure S1).

In this study, we use both linear and regional enamel thicknesses (see below). Linear enamel thickness at the functional and non‐functional cusp tips, functional and non‐functional lateral walls, and the deepest point of the occlusal basin is measured (Table 2, Figure 1D). Linear enamel thickness at the occlusal basin (LOB) was measured as the distance between the lowest point of the EDJ within the occlusal basin and the lowest point of the outer enamel surface (Macho and Berner 1994; Macho and Thackeray 1992, 1993; Schwartz 1997, 2000; Kono‐Takeuchi et al. 1998; Olejniczak and Grine 2006). Linear enamel thickness at the functional and non‐functional cusp tips (LFT and LNT, respectively) was measured as the distance between the apex of the dentin horn and the maximum extent of the outer enamel surface at that cusp (Beynon and Wood 1986; Grine and Martin 1988; Macho and Thackeray 1992, 1993; Macho and Berner 1994; Spoor et al. 1993; Schwartz 1997, 2000; Schwartz et al. 1998; Kono and Suwa 2005; Kono‐Takeuchi et al. 1998; Ulhaas et al. 1999; Shimizu 2002; Dean and Schrenk 2003; Grine et al. 2005; Olejniczak and Grine 2006; Feeney 2009; Mahoney 2010; Le Luyer et al. 2014). Linear enamel thickness at the functional and non‐functional lateral wall midpoints (LFM and LNM, respectively) was measured at the midpoint of the lateral wall's EDJ; a line perpendicular to the EDJ was measured from the EDJ midpoint to the outer enamel surface. Cusp tip measurements (LFT and LNT) were not taken on crowns that had been reconstructed due to wear (O'Hara and Guatelli‐Steinberg 2021).

According to Lucas et al. (2008a), durophagous groups will have thick enamel over the cusp tips, so LFT and LNT are expected to be greater in durophagous species. However, it should be noted that interspecific body size and tooth size variation may make it difficult to identify durophagous signals in raw linear enamel thickness values. Given that hard objects are crushed between cusp tips and the opposing tooth's occlusal basin, the occlusal basin (LOB) is also expected to be thicker in durophagous species (Constantino et al. 2009). No specific expectations are predicted for the linear lateral wall margin measurements, but they may also trend thicker in durophagous species, as thicker marginal enamel may reduce margin fractures (Schwartz et al. 2020).

Regional Enamel Thickness

Linear enamel thickness measurements are limited in their scope and can only provide information about a single point on an enamel crown. Thus, enamel thickness is also measured in zones or “regions” of the crown that roughly correspond to different stages of the chewing cycle to better represent the amount of enamel underlying masticatory forces.

We defined six regions in our study: functional and non‐functional cervical (RFC and RNC), lateral (RFL and RNL), and occlusal zones (RFO and RNO) (Table 2, Figure 1D). The linear enamel thickness measures form the borders of the regions of enamel thickness to be measured. For example, the functional lateral region (RFL) is contained between the LFM and LFT measurement (Figure 1D). Each region's enamel thickness is calculated by dividing the area of enamel within the region by the EDJ length within the region (Table 2). Regional measurements were not taken on crowns that had been reconstructed to “extensive” wear that extended into the dentin horns (O'Hara and Guatelli‐Steinberg 2021).

The regional enamel thickness measures are based loosely on previous regions established by Kono (2004) and (Skinner et al. 2015) 2D regional buccal, occlusal, and lingual AET system. However, each of those three regions is divided into two, and cusps are referenced by functional status rather than side. This was done, in part, because Schwartz (2000) identified differences in linear enamel thickness on the buccal and lingual lateral walls, with the functional cusp side tending to have thicker enamel. Dividing the tooth into six parts (cervical, lateral, and occlusal thirds on both buccal and lateral sides) will also allow for increased power in identifying distribution trends across the crown. Keeping the measurements consistent across all tooth types and classes will make it possible to analyze inter‐ and intra‐tooth trends. Crushing and grinding occur on both the functional and non‐functional walls of the occlusal basin, so durophagous groups are expected to have thicker enamel in the RFO and RNO regions (Constantino et al. 2009). Thus, RFO and RNO are expected to be thicker in durophagous groups compared to non‐durophagous, but no significant differences are expected for RFC, RFL, RNL, or RNC.

Linear Enamel Thickness Scaled to AET

While linear and regional enamel thickness measurements are useful for identifying thickness and distribution of enamel within a group, variation in body and tooth size complicates raw values when comparing between groups. Thus, scaled variables were created to allow for the relative thickness and distribution of enamel to be compared between groups.

The five linear measures of enamel thickness were each divided by the tooth's AET to create 5 new variables: pLFM, pLFT, pLOB, pLNT, pLNM (Table 3). The calculated values represent the percent by which the region's enamel thickness differs from the whole crown enamel thickness (adapted from Kono (2004)). For example, a value of 0.75 would indicate the region was 75% as thick as the whole crown and a value of 1.50 would indicate the region was 50% thicker than the whole crown AET. These variables therefore provide a way to compare enamel distribution between crowns with differing crown sizes. Predictions for the linear enamel thickness values scaled to AET are the same as for the raw linear enamel thickness values: durophagous groups are expected to “stack” a greater proportion of their enamel along cusp tips and the occlusal basin, so the pLFT, pLOB, and pLNT values are expected to be significantly greater than non‐durophagous groups.

Regional Enamel Thickness Scaled to AET

The same method for scaling linear enamel thickness values was applied to the regional enamel zones to create 6 new variables: pRFC, pRFL, pRFO, pRNO, pRNT, pRNC (Table 2). As with the regional measures, pRFL, pRFO, and pRNO are expected to be thicker in durophagous groups compared to non‐durophagous, but no significant differences are expected for RNL, RFC, or RNC.

Statistical Analysis

Mean and standard deviations were calculated for each of the measured variables (Table 2). Tooth types are grouped (upper premolar, upper molar, and lower molar) for each site and between older and younger sites with pooled periods (Older = Drimolen, SK Member 1 HR; Younger = Kromdraai B, SK Member 2, SK Member 3) (Tables S6–S33). Jonckheere‐Terpstra tests were performed to identify whether significant trends in dental form could be identified between individual sites; Mann–Whitney U tests were used for two‐group (Older vs. Younger) comparisons. The Jonckheere‐Terpstra test was chosen because it can detect ordered differences among ordered groups, it is appropriate for temporal sequence data, it does not require that data follow a normal distribution, and it does not require equal sample sizes across groups. To control for family‐wise error rate across multiple simultaneous comparisons within each variable category, Holm‐Bonferroni correction was applied. The Holm‐Bonferroni procedure ranks all p‐values from smallest to largest and applies sequentially adjusted significance thresholds, providing greater statistical power than standard Bonferroni correction. Separate analyses were conducted for individual tooth types as well as pooled tooth types (upper premolars, upper molars, and lower molars). Tables S6–S33 document all analyses and comparisons.

Results

All measured values are reported in the Supporting Information (file: ALL_DATA.xlxs). Descriptive statistics for (a) all teeth and (b) teeth sorted by individual tooth type and pooled tooth type are reported by site and by chronological period (older vs. younger) for tooth size (Tables S6–S9), tooth shape (Tables S10–S13), whole crown enamel thickness (Tables S14–S17), linear enamel thickness (Tables S18–S21), proportional linear enamel thickness (Tables S22–S25), regional enamel thickness (Tables S26–S29), and proportional regional enamel thickness (Tables S30–S33). Holm‐Bonferroni significance is documented in each of the Tables S1–S33; Table 3 reports the measured variables with Holm‐Bonferroni significant temporal trends.

Tooth Size and Shape

Upper molar comparisons of CA, BCD, EDJ, Fangle, Nangle, CSI, and ACS revealed only one significant directional trend over time: CA (Table 3, Figure 2, Tables S6–S13). Given that there are so few molar specimens younger than Swartkrans Hanging Remnant, molar analyses should be considered exploratory. The two Drimolen UM1s and SK47 (Member 1HR) have the three smallest CAs in the upper molar sample, while the Kromdraai specimen (TM 1517) has the largest crown area. While BCD, EDJ, Fangle, Nangle, CSI, and ACS average means also increase across the time periods, none of those trends were significant, possibly due to low sample sizes (see Figure 2 for ACS increase). Lower molar comparisons revealed no significant directional trends over time (Table 3, Tables S6–S13).

Box plots of lower molar, upper molar, and upper premolar crown area (CA) (top left), ACS (top right), AET (bottom left), and RET (bottom right) values across the five sites. Gray arrows indicate significant directional trends over time identified after Holm‐Bonferroni corrections.

Upper premolar CA and ACS both increased over time (Table 3, Figure 1, Tables S6–S9) perhaps because, all other things being equal, wider teeth will have higher ACS values. Like the upper molars, two early premolars from Drimolen (DNH 59 and DNH 22) had the smallest reported CA and ACS, while the Later SKX 26625 from Swartkrans Member 3 had the largest CA and highest ACS. Mean values for cusp geometry (i.e., Fangle and Nangle) decreased over time while CSI increased, but these trends were not significant (Tables S10–S13).

Enamel Thickness

Upper and lower molar AET and RET means increased over time, but neither trend was significant (Figure 2, Tables S14–S17). Upper premolar AET and RET, on the other hand, both exhibited significant directional trends, increasing over time (Figure 2, Table 3, Tables S14–S17). UP4s increased in AET and RET by site and by chronology, but none of the UP3 trends were significant (Table 3, Tables S14–S17).

Linear Enamel Thickness

No significant trends in upper or lower molar linear enamel thicknesses (LFM, LFT, LOB, LNT, LNM) or proportional to AET (pLFM, pLFT, pLOB, pLNT, pLNM) were identified (Tables S18–S25). An increase in upper molar LNT was identified between the Drimolen and Member 1HR samples (p < 0.040), but it was not significant after the Holm‐Bonferroni correction (Table S20). A decrease in LFM and pLFM was found in two lower molar analyses, but did not reach Holm‐Bonferroni significance (Tables S21 and S24).

Upper premolar linear enamel thicknesses increased at LFT and LNT (Figure 3, Table 3, Tables S18–S21). The trends were only significant with Holm‐Bonferroni correction when upper premolars were combined, though UP4s achieved raw p‐values less than 0.05 in several analyses. A proportional decrease in thickness was identified at the margin of the functional cusp wall (pLFM) and non‐functional cusp wall (pLNM) when upper premolars were combined and compared across individual sites (Table S24).

Box plots of lower molar, upper molar, and upper premolar linear cusp tip thickness (top, LFT and LNT), proportional cusp tip thicknesses (middle, pLFT, pLNT), and proportional lateral margins (bottom, pLFM, pLNM) across the five sites. Gray arrows indicate significant directional trends over time identified after Holm‐Bonferroni corrections.

Regional Enamel Thickness

No significant trends in upper or lower molar regional enamel thickness measured directly (RFC, RFL, RFO, RNO, RNL, RNC) or proportional to AET (pRFC, pRFL, pRFO, pRNO, pRNL, pRNC) were identified (Figure 4, Tables S26–S33). An increase in pRFO was identified in UM1s between Drimolen and Swartkrans Member 1HR, but it was not significant after the Bonferroni correction (Table S30).

Box plots of lower molar, upper molar, and upper premolar regional thickness measurements across five sites: Top, RFC and RFL; middle, RFO and RNO; bottom, RNL. Gray arrows indicate significant directional trends over time identified after Holm‐Bonferroni corrections.

Upper premolar regional thickness increased significantly at RFC, RFL, RFO, RNO, RNL across individual sites (Figure 4, Table 3 and Tables S26–S29). UP4s followed the same trend, but only reached Holm‐Bonferroni significance for RFL (Table 3 and Table S26). Proportional means tended to increase, but none of the trends were significant (Tables S30–S33).

Discussion

P. robustus postcanine teeth underwent significant changes across the lineage's chronology, particularly in occlusal enamel features. Our results provide some support for the three hypotheses. H1, predicting increases in crown size and a shift toward flatter, more bunodont cusp geometry, received partial support: CA increased significantly in both upper premolars and upper molars, while ACS increased significantly only in upper premolars. However, no cusp shape variables showed significant directional trends in any tooth type. H2, predicting increases in whole‐crown enamel thickness, was supported in upper premolars, where both AET and RET increased significantly over time, but not in upper or lower molars. H3, predicting a shift toward thicker enamel concentrated over the functional cusp tips and occlusal basin relative to the lateral and marginal walls, received the strongest support, but only in upper premolars (Figure 5). Linear and regional enamel thicknesses increased over the occlusal basin and cusp tips in premolars (LFT, LNT, RFC, RFO, RNO, RNL, Figure 5), with a proportional decrease along the marginal walls (pLFM, pLNM, Figure 5). Within upper premolars, enamel thickness and its distribution (H2, H3) showed more consistent evidence of directional change than did tooth size and shape (H1).

(middle) Line graph of linear and regional proportional values from the three time periods. Horizontal black line at y = 1 represents the AET. Black arrows indicate significant trends. (right) Cartoon of a tooth showing the locations where significant positive (red) and negative (trends) were observed. Bottom: Representative premolars from three sites.

Based on these findings, we suggest that dental features of different tooth types (molars and premolars) may show different rates of detectable morphological change. However, we note that the molar results are exploratory given the limited number of later samples. In just a few hundred thousand years, P. robustus premolars underwent a significant directional transformation in both tooth size and enamel thickness, while P. robustus molars increased slightly only in size (CA). This difference suggests that selective pressures may act more strongly on certain tooth types, possibly due to their specific roles in food processing. Indeed, Fannin et al. (2025a) demonstrated that in non‐human primates, premolar size is more strongly associated with chewing performance than first molar size.

Our findings complement recent research by Martin et al. (2021), who identified distinct temporal trends in P. robustus craniofacial form from Drimolen (2.04–1.95 mya) compared to those from both Swartkrans Member 1 Hanging Remnant and Kromdraai (~1.8 mya). The morphological changes they identified, including larger teeth and more anteriorly positioned sagittal crests and zygomatic roots, are consistent with a masticatory configuration that could produce and resist greater magnitude bite forces. Our dental evidence supports their assertion that these changes represented adaptations to a more durophagous diet. Thus, the changes in premolars, particularly the increased CA and ACS, along with thicker and redistributed enamel, may relate to increasing molarization of premolars, a characteristic feature of P. robustus (Bailey and Wood 2007; Leece et al. 2022; Robinson 1954). This molarization process appears to have occurred during periods of environmental aridification, possibly reflecting adaptation to a diet with more resistant or mechanically challenging foods. We note that behavior changes such as selecting for more mechanically challenging foods likely predated the morphological change in premolars (Fannin, Seyoum, et al. 2025b).

The results of this investigation are important for several reasons. First, they suggest rapid evolution over short time periods in response to environmental shifts. While enamel thickness was the aspect of dental crowns that was most frequently identified as shifting, CA also increased in both molars and premolars, potentially indicating that multiple dental features can respond to selective pressures, though perhaps at different rates. It seems that the distribution of enamel thickness is fairly evolvable and that some aspects of it are linked to shifts in the internal architecture of crowns that are likely linked to durophagy. Based on the present study, it seems that the distribution of enamel thickness may be the aspect of crown anatomy most responsive to dietary changes on short evolutionary timescales within hominins. If true, our results align nicely with previous research on the evolvability of dental design (Hlusko 2016; Hlusko et al. 2004).

Multiple studies have connected hard object feeding with thick enamel in primates (Lucas et al. 2008a, b; Kay 1975; Dumont 1995; Pampush et al. 2013; McGraw et al. 2014; Guatelli‐Steinberg et al. 2022). Beyond whole‐crown estimates of enamel thickness (i.e., AET and RET), the amount of enamel covering the cusp tips and occlusal basins has received considerable attention in relation to hard object feeding. Thicker enamel over the cusps has sometimes been construed as “uneven” enamel and linked to hard object feeding (Lucas et al. 2008b; Constantino et al. 2009; Thiery et al. 2017a, b, c). Generally, thicker cusp tips and/or thicker enamel in the occlusal basin have been expected in species with hard diets as a structural adaptation to resist fracture by limiting crack formation and propagation (Constantino et al. 2009; Chai 2018; Schwartz et al. 2020). Kono and Suwa (2008) emphasize the importance of occlusal enamel thickness as a potential adaptation for high masticatory loads when chewing hard/large food objects. Additionally, thick lateral walls help dissipate the high stresses that occur during food processing (Benazzi et al. 2013; Benazzi et al. 2016). In our upper premolar sample, this pattern is reflected in significant increases in RFC, RFO, RNO, and RNL across sites, along with increases in RFL at both the UPR and pooled premolar levels (Table 3). Here, we highlight changes in occlusal and cervical enamel during a period when diet was likely becoming harder for P. robustus.

The environmental context of the changes in crown architecture that we have identified is particularly significant. The shift toward more arid conditions and the expansion of shrubland vegetation likely created new dietary resources, and thus challenges, that required rapid evolutionary responses. The ability of P. robustus to adapt its dental morphology, particularly in premolars, demonstrates the species' evolvability in the face of environmental change. This likely played a crucial role in the species' survival across approximately 1 million years of environmental fluctuation, during which it successfully exploited an increasingly challenging diet.

Isotopic signals from the enamel of hominins and penecontemporaneous mammals have revealed that P. robustus consumed a significant proportion of C4 plants, such as tropical grasses, sedges, and underground storage organs (USOs), which were abundant in its environment during periods of climatic variability (Yeakel et al. 2007; Grine et al. 2012; Sponheimer et al. 2006). Stable carbon isotopes of P. robustus dental enamel indicates a mixed C3/C4 diet, with approximately 25%–35% C4 resource consumption (Lee‐Thorp et al. 2007). Laser ablation profiles reveal a highly flexible diet with substantial intra‐tooth δ13C variation of up to 5‰, indicating seasonal to interannual dietary shifts between C3‐dominated and C4‐enriched foods (Sponheimer et al. 2006). The temporal trends identified here coincide with significant environmental changes in South Africa during the early Pleistocene. Strontium analysis of P. robustus teeth from Swartkrans and Kromdraai indicates exploitation of both savanna and riparian woodland habitats, with approximately 30% of strontium derived from woodland environments (Sillen et al. 2025), speaking to both variation in habitat and flexibility of diet. Recent research has documented a pronounced drying period and expansion of shrubland‐type vegetation during this time (Caley et al. 2018). P. robustus likely inhabited a combination of ever‐changing and ever‐drying environments, including both open grasslands and wooded areas, requiring a flexible dietary repertoire that could shift to accommodate changing environmental conditions. One benefit of having thick enamel covering the entire occlusal surface of a tooth is that it is protective against both hard and abrasive foods (Constantino et al. 2011; Constantino et al. 2009; Lawn and Lee 2009; Lee et al. 2009), so while we emphasize the evolution of upper premolar enamel thickness distribution in relation to hard object feeding, it could have been useful when faced with abrasive foods.

Dental microwear texture analysis corroborates the flexible dietary strategy inferred from isotopic data. P. robustus teeth exhibit significantly higher complexity (Asfc) and lower anisotropy (epLsar) measures than does Australopithecus africanus, consistent with more frequent consumption of hard, brittle foods (Scott et al. 2005). In fact, P. robustus scores significantly higher than A. africanus for 11 of 16 texture variables related to feature complexity, size, and depth (Peterson et al. 2018). High variance in complexity values have been suggested to reflect diets in which hard objects may have been consumed periodically as fallback foods during resource scarcity (Ungar and Sponheimer 2011). On the other hand, P. robustus and the sooty mangabey ( Cercocebus atys ), an habitual hard‐object feeder (McGraw et al. 2014), share multiple similarities in dental form (Guatelli‐Steinberg et al. 2023; Scheinblum et al. 2025). Their similarities may suggest similar feeding regimes (Scheinblum et al. 2025; Guatelli‐Steinberg et al. 2023). When comparing P. robustus microwear by site, however, Peterson et al. (2018) found no significant mean differences in molar microwear, a signal that would have suggested increases in hard‐object feeding. They did note that the variance in microwear features differed among sites, but it was not possible to track trends in dental microwear over time. Although we cannot specifically connect the temporal trends found in our sample to significant temporal trends in microwear, we are confident that the pattern of tooth size and enamel thickness changes observed in Paranthropus robustus would have served it well in a changeable habitat.

The consistent signal in this study is that differences were found in upper premolar crown form, not upper or lower molars, with the exception of CA increasing in upper molars (Table 3, Figure 2). The lack of significant trends in lower molars, despite more lower molars (n = 16) than upper molars (n = 14) may reflect weaker selective pressures on lower molars, or may reflect limited statistical power given the small representation from Drimolen and the younger sites (Table S5). The asymmetry in results found in upper premolars versus either set of molars, though, gives some support to the idea that selection may have been active preferentially on premolars. Our goal was to include the molar data as a set of preliminary observations that should provide the basis for future analyses that include larger samples, especially given the many new samples that have been recovered and described from continued excavations at Drimolen (e.g., see Leece et al. 2022; Leece et al. 2023). Depending on those new findings and their morphology, it may be worth considering that the temporal trends identified here may reflect population level or intraspecific diversity within P. robustus .

Conclusions

The rapid dental evolution observed in P. robustus between 2.04 and 0.6 Ma, evident primarily in upper premolars rather than molars, provides insight into the pace and pattern of hominin adaptation to environmental change. Upper premolar CA, ACS, AET, and RET all increased over this interval, accompanied by a redistribution of enamel toward occlusal and cuspal regions. Upper molars showed a more limited response, with only CA increasing significantly, while lower molars showed no significant temporal trends in any measured variable. The varying degrees of change between different dental features and tooth types suggest a complex pattern of dental evolution, where some features were more responsive to selective pressures than others. These results have implications for understanding the mechanisms of dental adaptation in other hominin lineages. Future studies may benefit from examining whether similar patterns of dental evolution occur in other hominin species during periods of environmental change.
