Table 1 summarizes the key design parameters used in the two-dimensional waveguide–resonator coupling model.
The model is based on a 0.80 µm–high and 2.00 µm–wide Si3 N4 bus waveguide placed 0.10 µm away from a lithium–
niobate (LN) resonator of radius 2.48 mm. At the operating wavelength of 1.55 µm, the ordinary and extraordinary
refractive indices of LN are nLN,o = 2.211 and nLN,e = 2.138, while the waveguide core index is nSiN = 2.20.
The imaginary part of the LN refractive index is taken as kLN = 10−8 , yielding a material-limited quality factor of
Qmat = 1.11 × 108 . Based on this geometry, the predicted free spectral range is 8.70 GHz.

1.4 TM & TE Mode Coupling Comparison
Figure 7 shows the effective index neff of the fundamental TE00 and TM00 modes as a function of waveguide core width
wcore . For TE00 , the effective index starts near neff ≈ 1.2 at small widths (wcore ∼ 0.4 µm) and increases rapidly before
saturating around neff ≈ 1.8 for wcore ≳ 1.5 µm. In contrast, TM00 begins at a higher effective index (neff ≈ 1.45)
and exhibits a more gradual increase, saturating earlier near neff ≈ 1.7. These results indicate that TE00 is more
sensitive to waveguide width than TM00 , though both modes eventually reach strong confinement at large widths. At
wcore = 0.8 µm where the waveguide core becomes a square, the effective mode indice of TE and TM fundamental
mode converge.

 5

Figure 8 presents the confinement percentage of the TE00 and TM00 modes within the waveguide core. There exists a
positive correlation between the effective index and the confinement percentage: TM00 confinement rises from about
85% at narrow widths and levels off near 94% as the core widens. TE00 , however, increases more sharply, surpassing
TE00 confinement around wcore = 0.9 µm and stabilizing close to 96% at larger widths.

 Figure 9: Comparison of electric field norm of the fundamental TE00 and TM00 modes.

Figure 9 compares the electric field norm distributions of the fundamental TE00 and TM00 modes in the waveguide. The
TE00 mode (left) exhibits a highly symmetric field profile that is strongly confined within the rectangular waveguide
core, with limited penetration into the cladding. In contrast, the TM00 mode (right) shows weaker confinement, as its
field distribution extends further into the cladding region, particularly along the vertical direction.
This comparison highlights that the TE00 mode experiences stronger guiding due to its higher degree of confinement
within the core, whereas the TM00 mode is less confined and therefore has a larger evanescent field. The weaker
confinement of TM00 provides greater overlap with external structures, which can enhance coupling efficiency to a
resonator but comes at the cost of weaker mode confinement compared to TE00 .

Figure 10: The above plot gives respectively the percentage of transmission through the waveguide and the coupling
into the resonator under TM fundamental mode, which adds up to be 1 at all wavelengths. Such a spectrum is absent
for TE fundamental mode as no resonance over 20% coupling is found.

Figure 10 shows the transmission (red) and coupling (blue) spectra of the waveguide–resonator system under the TM00
fundamental mode. The transmission spectrum exhibits sharp dips near the resonance wavelengths, corresponding to
strong coupling into the resonator, with a maximum coupling efficiency approaching 80% (not the global optimum).

 6

The sum of transmission and coupling remains close to unity across the scanned wavelength range, confirming energy
conservation between the two output channels.
In contrast, no such high-coupling resonance is observed for the TE00 mode. For TE, the strongest resonant features
correspond to less than 20% coupling efficiency, indicating that the TE fundamental mode couples far less effectively
to the resonator compared to the TM fundamental mode.
The coupling behavior in Figure 10 can be related to the confinement of the guided modes within the waveguide
core. The TM00 fundamental mode exhibits weaker confinement compared to TE00 (see Fig. 8), which allows a larger
fraction of its field to extend into the cladding. This enhanced evanescent tail increases the overlap with the resonator
mode, enabling strong coupling resonances with efficiencies up to 80%. By contrast, the TE00 mode is more tightly
confined in the core, leaving a smaller evanescent field available for interaction. As a result, its resonant coupling
never exceeds 20%, and no spectrum with strong resonance comparable to TM00 is observed.
However, confinement alone may not fully explain the disparity between TE and TM coupling, and there exists some
other problems that require finer model setup and simulations. The lithium–niobate (LN) resonator is a birefringent
material, and thus the excited TE and TM modes experience different effective refractive indices. This birefringence
would lead to distinct phase matching conditions, which can further enhance or suppress coupling depending on po-
larization. Therefore, the observed difference between TE and TM resonances likely arises from a combination of
confinement effects and birefringence-induced phase mismatch, and more detailed simulations are needed to isolate
their relative contributions. However, according the eigenfrequency study in section 1.2, the simulation fails to distin-
guish the TE and TM fundamental whispering gallery mode, as only one fundamental mode is found.

Notes on the 3-D computation. To resolve the whispering-gallery fields and the evanescent coupling region in three
dimensions, the mesh must be sub-wavelength, especially for the waveguide and the surface of the resonator. In
practice we set the global maximum of the mesh to be λ0 /2, and even finer sizing across the gap to capture the field
and coupling characteristics accurately. Such a mesh size makes a full 3D fiber-resonator system computationally
expensive in COMSOL, so the working geometry in Table 3 is intentionally confined: the disk radius is reduced to
∼ 10 µm and the cladding domain is kept to a few core widths. This limits the total degrees of freedom while still
preserving the local physics in the coupling zone.

2.2 Coupling performance under waveguide thickness sweep
Effect of waveguide thickness on the TM spectra. Figure 17 overlays the transmission spectra obtained at a set
of thickness ratios r = h/wcore . The key observation is that the spectral shapes do not evolve coherently with the
thickness. As r (waveguide thickness with respect to the waveguide width) is varied, the positions of the resonance
dips shift drastically and their depths change irregularly; increasing the height does not simply broaden or deepen
asingle resonance; rather, distinct resonant features move in and out of the limited wavelength window in a way that
depends on the exact phase-matching conditions for that height.

No clear relation between thickness and optimum transmission. The summary plot in Fig. 19 collects, for each
height, the minimum transmission T min found within its local spectrum. The curve oscillates between low and moderate
values with no obvious trend: minima are near r ≈ 2 and r ≈ 4 are followed by noticeably higher points T min at nearby
thickness. Thus, within the scanned range and resolution, there is no reproducible relationship between the waveguide
thickness and the optimum transmission.

3 Conclusion
The COMSOL simulations demonstrate some fundamental characteristics of the modes as well as distinct differences
in coupling performance between the fundamental TE00 and TM00 modes in the SiN–LiNbO3 waveguide–resonator
system. The TE00 mode is more tightly confined within the waveguide core, which results in limited evanescent overlap

and weak coupling to the resonator. In contrast, the TM00 mode exhibits weaker confinement, allowing a larger fraction
of the field to extend into the cladding and overlap with the resonator, thereby achieving strong coupling resonances
with efficiencies exceeding.
The coupling behavior is also strongly influenced by system geometry. Separation scans reveal a critical gap (d x ≈
0.20–0.22 µm) where critical coupling occurs, with smaller separations leading to over-coupling and larger separations
leading to under-coupling. Adjusting the waveguide thickness significantly shifts resonance positions but does not
yield a clear monotonic trend in coupling efficiency, reflecting the complexity of phase-matching conditions.
However, confinement alone cannot fully account for the disparity between TE and TM coupling. The birefringence
of the LN disk introduces polarization-dependent effective indices, leading to phase mismatch that further modifies the
coupling dynamics. More refined models and 3D simulations with larger resonator sizes will be required to capture
these effects and to approach experimental configurations more accurately.
In summary, the reduced 2D and 3D COMSOL models capture the essential physics of waveguide–resonator coupling:

strong TM-dominated resonances, sensitivity to waveguide–resonator separation, and complex geometry-dependent
phase matching. At the same time, computational limitations and material anisotropy point toward future refinements
for more quantitative predictions.
