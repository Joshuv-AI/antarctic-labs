The fracture resistance of elastic networks increases with the density of defects like a random walk
Antoine Sanner, Luca Michel, David S. Kammer

Abstract
Disordered spring networks are a well-established model system to study fracture in a wide range of materials, from ceramics to polymer networks and mechanical metamaterials, across length scales from the atomistic to the macroscopic. A central quantity characterizing fracture is the apparent fracture energy G c G^{\mathrm{c}} , which measures the resistance to the propagation of a preexisting dominant crack. While it is well established that disorder can increase G c G^{\mathrm{c}} through crack arrest by local inhomogeneities, its dependence on the degree of disorder remains poorly understood. Here, we study the effect of varying concentrations of missing bonds on crack propagation of an otherwise perfect two-dimensional triangular network of springs. For a given network with a fixed concentration of missing bonds, the apparent fracture energy G c ​ ( a ) G^{\mathrm{c}}(a) increases with crack advance a a . This behavior can be explained by mapping the effect of the missing bonds onto an equivalent local fracture energy landscape Γ loc ​ ( a ) \Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a) and applying established theories linking planar crack arrest with fluctuations in Γ loc ​ ( a ) \Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a) . For increasing fraction of missing bonds ν \nu , the standard deviation of the fluctuations of Γ loc \Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}} increases with ν \sqrt{{\nu}} , which we explain by considering a random-walk-like superposition of perturbations caused by individual missing bonds. We demonstrate that as a consequence of crack arrest by fluctuations in Γ loc \Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}} , the average G c ​ ( a ) G^{\mathrm{c}}(a) follows the same ν \sqrt{{\nu}} scaling. Furthermore, we observe that the probability density of Γ loc \Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}} has an exponential tail leading to a logarithmic increase of G c ​ ( a ) G^{\mathrm{c}}(a) with crack advance a a . Our results quantitatively link microstructural disorder to macroscopic fracture energy and paves the way for quantitative predictions of the fracture energy in a wide variety of materials.



I
Introduction

Defects and disorder are ubiquitous in materials and profoundly influence their resistance to fracture. The nature of the dominant defects, however, varies widely across material classes. Microscopic voids govern the fracture properties in brittle materials, such as glasses and ceramics
Griffith and Taylor 1920
;
Lawn 1993
;
Curtin and Futamura 1990
; manufacturing imperfections often dominate in the case of mechanical metamaterials
Bhuwal et al. 2023
;
Zaiser and Zapperi 2023
;
Romijn and Fleck 2007
;
Karapiperis and Kochmann 2023
;
Fulco et al. 2025
; while the heterogeneity of polymer networks typically controls fracture in the case of rubber-like materials
Tauber et al. 2022
;
Arora et al. 2020
;
Sakai et al. 2010
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Barney et al. 2022
. Understanding how disorder influences fracture resistance is not only of fundamental interest but also has practical implications for the design of fracture-resistant materials. To study these effects in a controlled setting and gain fundamental insights relevant across a range of materials and scales, spring network models can be used. In such models, defects and disorder can be introduced via missing or weakened bonds in lattices
Curtin and Futamura 1990
;
Urabe and Takesue 2010
;
Hartquist et al. 2024
;
Broedersz et al. 2011
, random bond strengths
Kahng et al. 1988
;
Shekhawat et al. 2013
, or amorphous network structures
Arora et al. 2022
.

A central question in this context is how the presence and density of defects govern the competition between crack nucleation and crack propagation, and whether disorder ultimately toughens or weakens a material. Using spring network-like models, it has been established that in the absence of a preexisting crack, defects promote crack nucleation, and thus lower fracture resistance
Alava et al. 2006
;
Shekhawat et al. 2013
. When a dominant crack is already present, failure depends on how the defects affect the propagation of this dominating crack, which leads to a distinct and more complex dependence of failure on the presence of defects. When the defect density is large, the material is weakened by further increasing defect densities
Hartquist et al. 2024
;
Fulco et al. 2025
;
Chouzouris et al. 2026
, following the same trend as in the absence of the dominating crack. At low defect densities in precracked systems, however, increasing disorder may enhance resistance to crack propagation and thereby increase the ultimate stress of the material
Curtin and Futamura 1990
;
Urabe and Takesue 2010
;
Chouzouris et al. 2026
.

This disorder-induced resistance to the propagation of a dominant crack can be quantified by the apparent fracture energy

G^{c}
, where we adopt the term
“apparent”
to emphasize that

G^{c}
differs from the surface energy in the ideal equilibrium case considered by Griffith
Griffith and Taylor 1920
. In heterogeneous brittle materials,

G^{c}
typically increases with crack advance
a
because the crack is pinned by microstructural heterogeneity
Kendall and Cottrell 1975
;
Charles and Hild 2002
;
Hossain et al. 2014
;
Lebihain et al. 2021
;
Sanner et al. 2024
;
Sanner et al. 2025
;
Curtin and Futamura 1990
,
and it therefore ultimately exceeds the ideal value. Quantitative predictions of the toughening caused by crack pinning have been obtained in the case of a heterogeneous landscape of
local
fracture energy,
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}

Curtin and Futamura 1990
;
Charles and Hild 2002
;
Roux et al. 2003
;
Roux and Hild 2008
;
Patinet et al. 2013
;
Démery et al. 2014
. However, a similar quantitative framework for toughness arising from elastic heterogeneity, such as missing bonds, remains incomplete. A promising approach is to map the elastic heterogeneity onto an equivalent
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
landscape and apply planar crack pinning theories
Joanny and de
Gennes 1984
;
Sanner et al. 2024
, which have been partially explored in triangular spring networks
Curtin and Futamura 1990
. However, a theoretical understanding of how fluctuations in
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
depend on defect density is still lacking.

Here, we use a spring-network model to investigate how multiple randomly placed missing bonds affect the propagation of a preexisting crack in a triangular spring network. We show that for a given elastic heterogeneity induced by a fraction of missing bonds
{\nu}
, the standard deviation of the equivalent
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
grows as
\sqrt{{\nu}}
. We explain this scaling based on the random-walk-like superposition of the perturbations induced by individual missing bonds. These enhanced local fluctuations lead to a
systematic
increase in the apparent fracture energy
G^{\mathrm{c}}(a)
for large crack advances
a
, because crack arrest is governed by the largest values of
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
encountered along the crack path.
Our results establish the local mechanisms through which disorder enhances fracture resistance and provide a quantitative framework for connecting microscopic defect statistics to macroscopic toughness.

In the undeformed state, all springs have rest length

When deformed, the force in a spring is

A spring breaks abruptly when its strain

This choice promotes straight crack propagation while keeping the simulation domain moderate in size, in line with our prior work

For simulations dedicated exclusively to crack initiation, we consider a domain of width

To analyze crack propagation, we consider a system with dimensions

L_{y}=200\,\sqrt{3}/2\,\ell_{r}

We impose uniform vertical displacements on the top and bottom boundaries while constraining all lateral displacements of the boundaries to zero.

Loading proceeds in increments of vertical displacement

After each increment, we relax the network to static equilibrium using the FIRE minimization algorithm implemented in LAMMPS

Springs that exceed

After each removal, equilibrium is recomputed, and the process is repeated until no further springs break.
The next displacement increment is then applied.

We define the apparent fracture energy

In practice, we compute

This measure is independent of crack length and becomes exact in the limit

Because of the independence on the crack length, prescribing vertical displacement is equivalent to imposing an energy release rate.

We begin by considering a reference case: a pre-cracked but otherwise perfect triangular spring network.
We gradually increase the driving force for crack propagation,
i.e.
, the elastic energy release rate
G

The first bond to break is at the crack tip (Fig.

2

Next, we consider networks with a fraction
{\nu}=0.2

Fracture response varies significantly between realizations (Fig.
2

In contrast to the perfect network, rupture of the first bond does not trigger complete failure. Instead, the crack advances intermittently, alternating between unstable growth and arrest,

Although we analyze the full function

g^{\mathrm{c}}(a)=(G^{\mathrm{c}}(a)-\Gamma^{\mathrm{perfect}})/\Gamma^{\mathrm{perfect}}

(2)

which measure the fracture energy deviation of a given system relative to the perfect network.

The initiation and failure fracture energies are affected in two contrasting ways by the presence of the missing bonds.
While the initiation fracture energy

\Gamma^{\mathrm{perfect}}

\left\langle g^{\mathrm{c}}(0)\right\rangle\simeq 0

Thus, we see that in the regime of

As discussed in the next section, the resulting intermittent crack propagation is a direct signature of crack arrest caused by local heterogeneity.

IV
Local fracture energy fluctuations arrest the crack

We now show that the increase in the apparent fracture energy,
G^{\mathrm{c}}(a)
, with crack advance,
a
, originates from crack arrest induced by spatial fluctuations of the local fracture energy, and we derive a theoretical prediction for this increase following Refs.
Curtin and Futamura 1990
;
Charles and Hild 2002
.
For simplicity, and only in this section, we restrict the analysis to a straight crack path by allowing bond breaking only along the initial crack plane (
y=0
).
This protocol differs from all other simulations presented in this paper, where the crack path is not prescribed and typically deviates slightly from a straight line.
As shown at the end of this section, predictions assuming a straight path remain accurate even in those cases, indicating that crack arrest is the dominant toughening mechanism in the regime we study here, while crack path roughness plays only a minor role.

IV.1
Local fracture energy
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}

We first quantify the landscape of local fracture energy
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a)
that governs the arrest of the crack.
To obtain
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
, we simulate forced crack growth by artificially extending the crack length, rather than letting it propagate spontaneously. For each crack position, the applied displacement is increased gradually until the bond at the crack tip reaches the failure threshold
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{max}}=1
, and the corresponding elastic energy release rate is taken to define
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
at that point. The resulting values of
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
fluctuate due to the varying constellations of missing bonds encountered as the crack advances, as illustrated by the light-blue bars in Fig.
3
.

Next, we return to the case of spontaneous crack propagation and explain how local fluctuations in
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a)
lead to
G^{\mathrm{c}}(a_{\mathrm{f}})\geq G^{\mathrm{c}}(0)

is related to
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a)

\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}

In general, the crack advances only if the applied

is greater than or equal to the local fracture energy
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
. Thus, from the initial crack position at
a=0
, propagation will occur when
G
reaches the local fracture energy
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
at that point,
i.e.

G^{\mathrm{c}}(0)=\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(0)
(see Fig.
3
-I). Considering the realization shown in Fig.
3
, the local fracture energy at

is slightly lower than that of
a=0

as long as
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n\ell_{\mathrm{x}})<G

\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
exceeds
G

3

\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}

Consequently, the largest peak in local fracture energy encountered up to position

determines the applied energy release rate required for propagation, which defines the apparent fracture energy

G^{\mathrm{c}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})=\underset{1\leq n\leq m}{\max}\,\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n\ell_{\mathrm{x}})\penalty\ .

(3)

In particular, this means that the failure fracture energy
G^{\mathrm{c}}(a_{\mathrm{f}})
is always greater than or equal to the initiation fracture energy.
Moreover, for
m=a_{\mathrm{f}}/\ell_{\mathrm{x}}=200
it is extremely unlikely that all 200 values of
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
lie below the mean
\langle\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}\rangle=\Gamma^{\mathrm{perfect}}

This explains why in our simulations we never observe

IV.3
Statistical distribution

Having established the deterministic relationship between
G^{\mathrm{c}}
and
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
on the example of a specific realization of the network, we use Eq.
3
to predict the statistical distribution of the normalized apparent fracture energy,
g^{\mathrm{c}}(a)
, for a given value of
{\nu}
.
This derivation is particularly simple because the applied elastic energy release rate,
G
, is independent of crack length, and it corresponds to a special case of the more general formulations presented in Refs.
Curtin and Futamura 1990
;
Charles and Hild 2002
.

To predict the statistics of
g^{\mathrm{c}}(a)
, we determine the statistical properties of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a)=(\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a)-\Gamma^{\mathrm{perfect}})/\Gamma^{\mathrm{perfect}}
under two simplifying assumptions.
First, we assume statistical translational invariance, meaning that the (cumulative) probability distribution is independent of the crack advance
a
, which holds when the crack tip is far from the boundaries. Due to the arbitrary initial crack length considered in this work, this assumption results in the observation that
\mathcal{P}[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}]
must be equivalent to
\mathcal{P}[g^{\mathrm{c}}(0)]
, and we note that the latter distribution can be easily obtained from simulations on crack initiation (Fig.
2
d).. Second, we assume that
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(a)
is spatially uncorrelated,
i.e.
, that
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
and
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
are independent random variables for
n\neq m
. Appendix
C
confirms that correlations between
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
and
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}((n+d)\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
are indeed negligible even for
d=1
.

We now derive the cumulative probability distribution of the energy release rate,
\mathcal{P}[g^{\mathrm{c}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})](g^{\star})=P(g^{\mathrm{c}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})<g^{\star})
,
i.e.
, the probability that
g^{\mathrm{c}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
is smaller than a given value
g^{\star}
.
From Eq.
3
, the condition
g^{\mathrm{c}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})<g^{\star}
is met only when all
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n)
encountered along the path

are smaller than
g^{\star}
. Since the
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(n\ell_{\mathrm{x}})
are independent and identically distributed, this occurs with probability

\mathcal{P}[g^{\mathrm{c}}(m\ell_{\mathrm{x}})](g^{\star})=\left(\mathcal{P}[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}](g^{\star})\right)^{m}.

(4)

Using Eq.
4
, and taking the distribution of
\mathcal{P}[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}]
to be equal to the
\mathcal{P}[g^{\mathrm{c}}(0)]
extracted from our simulations, we can make predictions for the median of

and the cumulative distribution
\mathcal{P}[g^{\mathrm{c}}(a_{\mathrm{f}})]

This agreement is especially remarkable given that the theory assumes a straight crack path, whereas the simulated crack path deviates slightly from straightness (Appendix
G

Thus, in the regime considered here, crack arrest by local fluctuations in

is the dominant toughening mechanism, while crack path roughness plays only a minor role.

However, the predictions shown in Fig.
2

\mathcal{P}[{\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}}]

{\nu}

In the next section, we present a model that predicts the CDF
\mathcal{P}[{\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}}]
for a given proportion of missing bonds,

In the previous section, we showed that the failure fracture energy
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})
is determined by the largest peak in the local fracture energy
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
encountered along the crack path, and, for a given fraction of missing bonds, we determined the link between the distributions of local fracture energies and apparent fracture energies that induce crack propagation.
Here, we show that the fluctuations in
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
, and hence the expected maximum
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
and
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})
, increase as
\sqrt{{\nu}}
with the fraction
{\nu}
of missing bonds.
We rationalize this scaling by first analyzing the perturbation caused by a single missing bond, which we denote
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
, and then showing that the contributions of multiple missing bonds add up stochastically in a random-walk-like manner.

Figure
4
a shows the probability density function of the change in local fracture energy caused by removing one bond,
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
(details in Appendix
D
).
The probability density
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
has a sharp peak around
0
because the influence of a missing bond decays rapidly with distance
Sanner et al. 2025
, so that many possible locations lead to a value of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
close to
0
.
More importantly,
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
is nearly symmetric about
0
, indicating that the probability of increasing or decreasing the fracture energy of the system by removing a single bond is comparable.

For a given realization of the system with
M>1
missing bonds, corresponding to removing a fraction
{\nu}=M/N
of the
N
bonds initially present in the network, we can approximate the total fracture energy perturbation
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
by summing the individual contribution of each missing bond in that realization,
i.e.
,

\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}=\sum_{i=1}^{M={\nu}N}\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1,i},

(5)

where each random variable
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1,i}
is independent and independently distributed according to the single-bond distribution
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
.

The perturbations induced by each removed bond are stochastic and accumulate as the number of removed bonds increases, such that
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
is a random variable with
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}]
that broadens in the manner of a random walk, which is indeed what we observe in our simulations, see Fig.
4
b.
More quantitatively, the variance of
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}]
is expected to grow linearly with
M
, because
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
is the sum of
M
independent random variables.
Consequently, the standard deviation of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
will be given by

\sigma(M)=\sqrt{M}\,\sigma_{1},

(6)

where
\sigma_{1}
is the standard deviation of the single-bond distribution
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
.
While it seems that
\sigma
depends on the system size,
N
, because
M={\nu}N
, this effect is canceled out by the fact that
\sigma_{1}\propto 1/\sqrt{N}
,
see Appendix
D
. Hence the corresponding system-size independent expression is

\sigma({\nu})=s_{1}\sqrt{{\nu}}.

(7)

The constant
s_{1}=\sigma_{1}\sqrt{N}
captures the fluctuations induced by a single bond removal and, in contrast to
\sigma_{1}
, is independent of the system size.
This prediction is in excellent agreement with the result from brute force simulations of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}

We now analyze the functional form of the distributions of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}

As in the previous section, we approximate
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
as the superposition of many independent perturbations caused by the missing bonds. By the central limit theorem, this suggests that the center of the probability density function
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}]
should approach a Gaussian form for sufficiently large
{\nu}

\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
by the prediction for its standard deviation

{\nu}\geq 0.05

p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
(Fig.
4
a) is non-Gaussian. Because the largest peaks in
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
arrest the crack, the upper tail of
p[{\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}}]
controls the statistics of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}
and thus the evolution of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
with crack advance.

We now discuss the expected functional form of the cumulative distribution
\mathcal{P}[g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)]
based on the planar crack arrest model and on
\mathcal{P}[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}]
.
Equation
3
shows that the value of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
is the maximum of the local
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
along the crack path, and thus the distribution of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
should converge to a limiting extreme-value distribution for large crack advances and in particular for

g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})
is determined by the largest of many values of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
, it is mostly determined by the upper tail of

leads to a Gumbel extreme-value distribution for
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})

so that a perfect Gumbel distribution appears as a straight line.
To confirm that the exponential behavior of the tail of the distribution determines
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})

a Gumbel distribution with mode (the most likely value of the random variable

Hence, the median of

In summary, the energy release rate

We showed that this dependence is a direct consequence of extreme value statistics applied to the exponential tail in the probability density of

An open question for future work is to identify the physical origin of the exponential tail, or, even better, to predict it analytically rather than fitting it empirically.

We can obtain insight into the probable origin of the fat tail of

by noticing
that the variance of the single bond removal distribution,

(-1,1)
. Such a random walk leads to a binomial distribution, which has fat tails similar to our data, although we note that the tails in this case are not a perfect exponential.
This illustrates that the exponential is not necessarily the functional form expected theoretically, but we chose it for our fit in the interest of simplicity and because yields an excellent prediction of the increase of
\left<g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)\right>
with

VII
Discussion

In this work, we have simulated the fracture of triangular networks with random missing bonds,
showing that the applied energy release rate increases with the fraction of missing bonds as
\sqrt{{\nu}}
and with the crack advance as
\ln a
.
We linked this increasing
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
to a crack arrest mechanism, where the largest peak in
local
fracture energy,
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
, determines the apparent fracture energy,
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
.
The probability distribution of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
at a given crack advance
a
is thereby linked to the distribution
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}]
via extreme value statistics,
and the increase in
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
with
\sqrt{{\nu}}
is the direct consequence of the upper tail of
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}]
scaling with
\sqrt{{\nu}}
.
This
\sqrt{{\nu}}
scaling of the fluctuations
is a result of the stochastic superposition of the perturbation by each removed bond analogous to a random walk.

The mechanisms of crack arrest and defect-induced perturbations identified here are not specific to the triangular lattice geometry and are therefore expected to extend to other lattice structures and, more generally, to continuum systems, where missing bonds correspond to microvoids or microcracks.
Our results may thus be relevant for a broad class of disordered materials, provided that the defect density remains low and fracture proceeds along a nearly straight crack path.

We discuss below some further implications of our findings and the limitations of our work.

Size-effect

The logarithmic increase of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
with crack advance implies that the fracture energy required to split the sample entirely,
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(L_{\mathrm{nc}})
, has a logarithmic size effect.
That is, for a strip of given height, the critical force to split it grows logarithmically with the uncracked length
L_{\mathrm{nc}}
.
This behavior contrasts with the size effect in disordered materials without a pre-existing crack, where the critical stress decreases with system size
Weibull 1939
;
Urabe and Takesue 2010
;
Alava et al. 2006
.
The difference arises because, with a pre-existing crack, defects arrest the crack and enhance toughness, whereas failure without a crack is controlled by nucleation at the largest microcracks.

Existence of a maximum fracture energy

Since
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
is mainly influenced by missing bonds near the crack tip, one might expect that there exist an upper bound for the maximal possible
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
, corresponding to the constellation of missing bonds around the crack tip that is optimal for toughening.
If such a bound exists, it is expected to be found after sufficiently long crack advance,
i.e.
,
G^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
would saturate for large
a
instead of increasing logarithmically without bounds.
We did not observe such a saturation in our simulations (Figs.
6
a,c), meaning that this upper bound either does not exist or can only be reached after even longer crack propagation. If a sample was large enough for
G^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
to reach saturation, the size-effect mentioned in the previous paragraph would not exist and the saturated value could be considered a material property.

Limitations of the theory

The main simplifying assumptions in our theory are (i) that the crack propagation is planar, (ii) that the values of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
are uncorrelated along the crack propagation distance, and, (iii) that perturbations of individual missing bonds are independent and superpose linearly into the total local fracture energy,
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
.

First, we discuss the assumption of planar crack propagation. In reality, the crack path is rarely perfectly straight, exhibiting occasional switches to the neighboring plane for small fractions of missing bonds, and a more pronounced roughness for larger fractions of missing bonds, see Fig.
13
in Appendix
G
.
One may expect that crack path roughness increases the fracture energy beyond the prediction of our planar crack arrest model due to several mechanisms
such as: (i) the increase in number of broken bonds due to the increased arclength of the rough crack path
Urabe and Takesue 2010
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Persson and Tosatti 2001
, (ii) mode mixity due to misorientation of the crack
Mirkhalaf et al. 2014
;
Faber and Evans 1983a
;
Faber and Evans 1983b
, and (iii), unbroken bonds bridging accross the crack faces
Bower and Ortiz 1991
;
Mirkhalaf et al. 2014
. However, we argue below that in our case, these mechanisms are negligible compared to the crack arrest by local heterogeneity.

We can completely exclude the increase in number of broken bonds mechanism,
because the number of broken bonds actually
\emph{decreases}
as the result of removing bonds from the network, see Fig.
14
in Appendix
H
.
The typical argument linking fracture energy to the number of broken bonds is based on energy conservation, which does not hold here. The process of crack propagation is unstable, dissipating energy, and is governed by arrest by strong obstacles.

Concerning the bridging chains mechanism, we found in a previous study with a single missing bond that toughening by bridging bonds plays an important role for critical strains of the bonds
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{max}}<0.3
. For the current study, we deliberately chose
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{max}}=1
to avoid these effects and reduce the roughness of the crack path. We can hence expect stronger deviations between the planar crack arrest theory and the simulations for smaller
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{max}}
, which needs to be verified in future work. The regime of small failure strain is for example especially important for brittle architected materials
Quintana-Alonso and Fleck 2009
;
Lingua et al. 2026
and for ceramics
Curtin and Futamura 1990
.

Overall, good match between our theory and our numerical results suggests that in our case the crack arrest by local elastic heterogeneity described by our theory dominates compared to all those mechanisms.

The second important assumption in the crack arrest theory is that
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
is uncorrelated from one crack tip position to the next. This assumption is in fact very good, since the correlation of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
decays below 0.5 at a distance of one bond and is nearly zero at a distance of two bonds, see Appendix
C
. This small correlation length suggests that the local fracture energy is dominated by a few defects very close to the crack tip.

The third important approximation in our theory concerns the prediction of the standard deviation of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
,
\sigma({\nu})
.
We computed the perturbations caused by removing one bond at different positions from a perfect network
and added them up (stochastically) to obtain the total perturbation
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
. In reality, when one bond has already been removed, the elasticity of the network has changed and hence the response to the second removed bond will be different. While this effect is negligible when the two removed bonds are far apart, it makes a noticeable difference in some cases where the bonds are close to each other and close to the crack tip. This effect becomes increasingly important at high
{\nu}
, where it becomes likely that two neighbouring bonds are missing and furthermore the overall elasticity of the network changes significantly.
Nevertheless, the distributions computed using the assumption of linear independent superposition of single bond perturbations are in excellent agreement with the result of direct simulations, see Appendix
E
.

Broader range of fraction of missing bonds

In this paper, we focused on the relatively narrow range of missing bond fractions
{\nu}\leq 0.2
, and we expect both the planar crack arrest model and our prediction of the fluctuations of
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(0)
to break down for large
{\nu}
.
A similar study that introduced weak bonds instead of missing bonds
Hartquist et al. 2024
observed that after an initial increase with the fraction of weak bonds,
the fracture energy eventually decreases, so that very heterogeneous networks end up weaker than the perfect network.
We attribute this opposite trend at large disorder to a different failure mode, where bonds break not only at the crack tip, but instead breakage occurs across a larger region around the crack tip. Accordingly, completely different modeling approaches, such as percolation models, were used to predict the toughness in this delocalized failure regime
Arora et al. 2020
;
Hartquist et al. 2024
.

While our numerical simulations could in principle probe the
{\nu}>0.2
regime, we did not manage to reach system size convergence at these concentrations, preventing us from determining the
intrinsic
crack resistance curve
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a)
.
Missing bond fractions
{\nu}>0.2
require larger system sizes,
possibly due to approaching rigidity transition at
{\nu}=1/3
,
where the lengthscale of stress heterogeneity diverges
Maxwell 1864
;
van Hecke 2009
;
Broedersz et al. 2011
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Dussi et al. 2020
.

Generalization to three dimensions

The situation in three dimensions is qualitatively different because the crack tip is a line rather than a point. The strong sensitivity to fluctuations in local fracture energy that we observed here are inherent to the fact that the crack tip is a point,
so that at a given instant of crack propagation, crack growth is determined by a single value of the local toughness.
Hence, a single extreme value of local toughness on the crack path is enough to lead to a large failure fracture energy.
The picture is different in three dimensions, where the crack tip is a line, so that the crack growth is determined by the average of the toughness encountered along the crack front. This averaging along the crack front attenuates the fluctuations, and in the limit of a straight crack front interacting with many uncorrelated heterogeneities,
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}
would be equal to the average
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
. However, in reality fluctuations do not average out completely because the crack front can deform, allowing sections of the front to move independently as if they were independent two-dimensional systems. The result is a partial averaging out of the fluctuations that leads to
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})\sim\sigma({\nu})^{2}\sim{\nu}
, see Refs.
Larkin and Ovchinnikov 1979
;
Imry and Ma 1975
;
Robbins and Joanny 1987
;
Roux et al. 2003
;
Démery et al. 2014
;
Sanner et al. 2024
, which is smaller and is a different scaling to
g^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})\sim\sigma({\nu})\sim\sqrt{{\nu}}
, which we observed here. Three-dimensional network simulations need to be carried out to verify this speculation.

VIII
Conclusion

We have shown that randomly removing bonds in a triangular spring network increases the failure fracture energy
G^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})
,
with the increase in
G^{\mathrm{c}}_{\nu}(a_{\mathrm{f}})
being proportional to the square root of the fraction of removed bonds
\sqrt{{\nu}}
.
This increase in toughness is the result of the pinning of the crack by the microstructural heterogeneity created by the missing bonds,
which can be described by a local fracture energy landscape,
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}(a)
.
Our main finding is that the standard deviation of
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
increases with
\sqrt{{\nu}}
as a result of the
the stochastic superposition of the perturbations caused by each removed bond.
Furthermore, we observe that the probability density of
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\nu}
has an exponential upper tail,
which leads to a logarithmic increase of the apparent fracture energy with crack advance.
Our work paves the way to link fracture behaviour to microstrctural features in a variety of disordered materials.

Appendix D
Determination of the single bond perturbation probability density distribution
p[{\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}}]
and its dependence on the system size

We determine the distribution of the change in local fracture energy
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
caused by removing a single bond from a perfect network.
The system dimensions are the same as used for the simulations of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}=g^{\mathrm{c}}(0)
, namely
L_{\mathrm{x}}=400\,\ell_{\mathrm{x}}
,
L_{\mathrm{y}}=200\,\ell_{\mathrm{x}}
,
L_{\mathrm{c}}=200\,\ell_{\mathrm{x}}
, which contains a total number of bonds
N\approx 60000
. Hence, computing
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(0)
for each of the
N
possible choice of missing bond would be extremely costly, and is unnecessary because only missing bonds near the crack tip have a signigicant influence on
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}(0)
.

We approximate the entire
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
by only computing
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
for the
N_{\mathrm{w}}\simeq 500
possible missing bonds within a square window close to the crack tip.
The histogram of the obtained
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
values yields the probability density of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
knowing that the missing bond is within the window, which we denote
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\in\mathrm{w}}]
.

The probability density for the case where the missing bond can be anywhere,
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
, is the sum of probability densities for the cases where the missing bond is within the window
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\in\mathrm{w}}]
and outside of the window
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\notin\mathrm{w}}]
, weighted by the respective probabilities
N_{\mathrm{w}}/N
and
1-N_{\mathrm{w}}/N
of the missing bond being within or outside of the window:

p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}](g^{\star})=\frac{N_{\mathrm{w}}}{N}p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\in\mathrm{w}}](g^{\star})+\left(1-\frac{N_{\mathrm{w}}}{N}\right)p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\notin\mathrm{w}}](g^{\star})\penalty\ .

(16)

If we choose
N_{\mathrm{w}}
large enough, the perturbation caused by a missing bond outside of the window is negligible, so that
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\notin\mathrm{w}}]
, can be approximated by a Dirac distribution,

p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\notin\mathrm{w}}](g^{\star})\simeq\delta(g^{\star})\penalty\ .

(17)

Equations
17
and
16
imply that only the removed bonds near the crack tip determine the shape of the tails of
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}](g^{\star})
.

We now use Eq.
16
to discuss the implications of the choice of the system size
N
on
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
and in particular the standard deviation
\sigma_{1}
of
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
. We keep
N_{\mathrm{w}}
fixed and furthermore assume that
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\in\mathrm{w}}]
is independent of
N
and hence remains unchanged.
Increasing the system size reduces the probability that the removed bond is within the window,
N_{\mathrm{w}}/N
, and hence the contribution of
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1\in\mathrm{w}}]
to
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
.
For the variance
\sigma_{1}^{2}
of
p[\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}]
, this implies that

\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{w}}}{N}\sigma_{1\in\mathrm{w}}^{2}\penalty\ ,

(18)

where we used the additivity of the variance and that the variance of the Dirac distribution is 0.
Hence,

\sigma_{1}\propto 1/\sqrt{N}.

(19)

It follows from Eq.
18
that
s_{1}=\sigma_{1}\sqrt{N}
is independent of the system size
N
.

Finally, we verify that
s_{1}=\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{w}}}\,\sigma_{1\in\mathrm{w}}
converges to a window-size–independent limit as the window used to compute
\gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}_{1}
is enlarged.
As shown in Fig.
10
, the values of
s_{1}
become nearly identical once the window extends beyond approximately
r_{\mathrm{w}}\simeq 3
.
This indicates that the perturbations from bonds outside this region are negligible.
The results presented in the main text are obtained using a square window centered at the crack tip spanning
12\times 12
unit cells, corresponding to the largest window shown in Fig.
10
, and are therefore well converged.

Appendix G
Roughness of the crack path

While crack arrest theory presented in the main text assumes a perfectly straight crack path, actual crack paths are rarely perfectly straight in our simulations, see Fig.
13
. Even for the smallest fraction
{\nu}=0.01
, the crack occasionally jumps to a neighboring row of bonds, and the crack path becomes highly tortuous for
{\nu}=0.2
. Even for the roughest case, the crack propagation consists of several straight sections separated by scattering events. Because of the nearly translational invariance of the geom
\Gamma^{\mathrm{loc}}
landscape that is statistically similar than in the straight case.
At the scattering events, the crack deviates from the straight path because an off-the-plane bond breaks at a smaller
G
than the bond in the crack plane. Hence, the critical energy release rate required to advance the crack is smaller when allowing the crack to deviate from the plane than when forcing it to remain straight
Abid et al. 2019
.
Therefore we expect our planar crack pinning theory to be an upper bound for the actual fracture energy. However, the numerical results for non-straight crack propagation are virtually indistinguishable from this upper bound, indicating that this effect might be very weak.

Our simulations contradict with this expectation: while the fracture energy increases, the number of broken bonds actually decreases with increasing fraction of missing bonds, see Fig.
