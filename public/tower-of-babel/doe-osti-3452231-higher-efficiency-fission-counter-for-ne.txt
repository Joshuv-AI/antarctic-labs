 ABSTRACT

 Fission chambers containing 235U have been used for detection of neutrons for many decades. In these
chambers 235U is electroplated on a metal which is one plate of an ionization chamber. Since the range of
fission products from induced fission in the chamber is extremely short, these deposits from electroplating
are so thin that the amount of 235U results in low neutron detection efficiency and the chamber needs high
internal 235U surface area to increase efficiency. A typical ~8 in. long, ~2 in. diameter fission counter only
contains < 2 g of 235U total, resulting in very low detection efficiency per incident neutron impinging on
the outer surface of the detector. However, because of their large electronic pulses from fission products
compared to gamma rays it is very easy to discriminate against gamma rays impinging on the detector.
These types of chambers are produced commercially and are used worldwide in nuclear research and
nuclear power reactors. ORNL has made a higher efficiency fission ionization chamber by using many
thin concentric annuli to increase the area. A higher efficiency fission counter would be useful and can be
made by incorporating 235U into a glass or ceramic scintillator. For nearly 100 years the green color in
antique glass was produced by incorporation of depleted uranium (up to 20 wt. %) into a glass. The
concept for this scintillation-optical-based fission detector is to dope a scintillator (either ceramic or glass)
with 235U. Glass scintillators are commercially available doped with as much as 8 wt. % 6Li for neutron
detection. In the 235U doped scintillator the fission product pulses would be double the size of those in an
ion chamber since all fission products produce light in the scintillator whereas in the ionization chamber
half do not go into the ionizing gas volume. These higher efficiency fission chambers would be very
useful for a wide variety of research, nuclear research, and nuclear power reactor applications. Because of
their smaller size and weight, they should be useful for space application such as monitoring of space
nuclear power reactors. This report reviews existing fission chamber technology and suggests the
development of these small high-efficiency fission chambers. Other fission isotopes could be used for
other research applications.
 1. BACKGROUND

 The first fission chambers were built in Germany in 1939 [1] and since then there have been a wide
variety of shapes and sizes. In these ionization chambers there is an ionizing gas and highly enriched
uranium coated parallel metallic plates subjected to a voltage to collect the ions produced by the fission
products from, say 235U. The 235U deposit on these plates must be extremely thin to allow the fission
products to escape into the gas to produce ionization. The range of 235U fission products is < 10 microns.
As a result of the thin deposits the efficiency for detection of neutrons is very limited. There has been a
very small spiral fission chamber built by Houghterp of LANL [2] where they took a thin long metallic
plate electroplated on one side with 235U and wound it in a spiral to make small (0.5 in diameter
cylinders,0.5 in height) fission chambers. Williams of ORNL [3] built large chambers. A typical fission
chamber in use for monitoring the neutron level in nuclear power reactors is built by Westinghouse Co
(Model WL6376). The chamber contains 1.72 grams of 235U and has a diameter of 2-3/32 inches and
length of 8 in. with an active area of 6 in. In contrast, a 6.0 in. long, 2.0 in. diameter ceramic scintillator
(density 6.5 g per cc) of this size based on the technique we propose, would contain ~160 grams of highly
enriched uranium, which would result in 90 times higher efficiency than the WL6376 fission counter. For
a glass scintillator with a density of 2.5 g/cc the increase in efficiency would be 35. Obviously, fission
chambers containing other fissile isotopes could be built by doping scintillators.

 2. CONCEPT

 Two concepts for this fission counter used for neutron detection for nuclear power reactors, and
neutron counting in educational and research applications are described: highly enriched uranium in thin

scintillation fibers transmitting to a photomultiplier tube (PMT) for conversion to electronic pulses and
the other smaller surface-to-volume ratios like a cylinder with a radius of 1 in and a length of 6 in.

2.1 FIBER OPTIC CONCEPT

 This concept for uses 235U doped glass-scintillator fibers with optical transmission properties coupled
to a photomultiplier which could be some distance away and is shown in Figure 1.

 Starting from the inside, there is the 235U doped scintillator which absorbs neutrons inducing fission in
the uranium with the resulting fission products producing light in the scintillator. The light is read out by
the photomultiplier and converted to an electrical pulse that is detected by appropriate commercially
available equipment. All fission products from induced fission produce light, whereas in the standard
ionization fission counters only half contribute to the electrical pulse. For very thin glass fibers the fission
products escaping the surface would be captured in a thin layer of scintillator not containing any 235U. For
thick scintillation-optical based fission counters, this layer is not needed. The thin layer of reflector
surrounds the scintillator and assures that more of the light produced by the uranium fission products
reaches the photomultiplier. These are then enclosed in protection layers as appropriate. Adjacent layers
of fibers rotated 90 degrees to each other could be used as a spatial detector of slow neutrons as used at
the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS). For this application, the fibers should be about half the distance
travelled by the fission products of neutron induced fission (tens of µm). This type of configuration could
provide a high spatial resolution source (mm to hundreds of µm resolution) of neutrons and gamma rays
from induced fission in the uranium by bundling fibers in the desired sizes.

2.2 LARGE SCINTILLATOR MOUNTED DIRECTLY ON THE PHOTOMULTIPLIER

 This concept would be used for a wide variety of applications and could replace existing fission
counters supplied commercially. The concept is illustrated in Figure 2. The 235U doped glass scintillator is

surrounded by a thin reflective layer which in turn is enclosed by a protective metal layer that also
encloses the photomultiplier. Of course, there are cables connected to the photomultiplier (typically one
or two), for bias voltage and for signal output. A typical dimension of the uranium doped scintillator
could be 2.0 in. diameter and 6 in. long.

2.3 PHOTON DETECTION

 To detect the emitted scintillation light, suitable photon detectors have been identified. Conventional
photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) may be used. Since they tend to be bulky, fragile, and require about a
thousand volts bias, silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) may be used in their place. The latter are available
with sensitive areas from 1 mm2 to several cm2 (by means of arrays), are very robust to shock and
magnetic fields, and require only a few 10’s V of bias. Either device can provide the fast (~nanosecond)
timing desired. Additionally, SiPMs are an order of magnitude or more less expensive than PMTs. A fast
amplifier and/or a fast comparator may be used coupled to the photon detector to generate the presence
signals that identify the presence of an event in the scintillator. The readout is, thus, extremely simple and
requires low power (mW) to operate. The obvious choice is a SiPM because of size and lower voltage
requirements, but a conventional PMT may be used when higher radiation tolerance is needed.

 3. ADVANTAGES

 The main advantages would be higher efficiency by a factor of ~100 for 8 wt. % 235U or more,
depending on how much 235U can be diluted in the glass or ceramic scintillator matrix. Li-containing glass
scintillators are commercially available with 8 wt. % Li. Glass or ceramic scintillation-based fission
counter with 8 wt. % 235U would have a factor of ~90 more efficiency than commercial ionization fission
chambers (WL6376) for essentially the same active length. Higher 235U content would increase efficiency
even further.

 Another advantage is that the proposed devices could be smaller: for the same efficiency as WL6376
they could be 90 times smaller in active volume.
  Of course, the figures quoted do not include external effects such as light detection efficiency,
coupling, etc.

 4. MEASUREMENT PROCEDURE TO TEST THE 235U DOPED SCINTILLATION
 DETECTOR

 The pulses of the scintillator from the photomultiplier should be viewed on an oscilloscope and stored
electronically with and without a neutron source present. A pulse height distribution curve should be
acquired on a multichannel analyzer and plotted vs. discriminator setting without the neutron source. The
gamma ray from the natural activity of the 235U (186 KeV energy) will be detected by the scintillator.
Adjust the voltage in the PMT so that the gamma rays from the natural activity of the 137Cs produces a
signal of 150 millivolts. Then set the discriminator at 50 millivolts which will be 1/3 of the 137Cs gamma
ray or 220 KeV. The threshold results in a 1 MeV threshold for prompt neutrons and 0.22 MeV for
gamma rays. This gamma ray threshold discriminates against the natural activity of 235U. Place a neutron
source adjacent to the 235U doped scintillator and measure and record the pulse height distribution and
then compare the performance with a conventional fission chamber for detecting neutrons. In practice,
higher thresholds should be sufficient for detecting fission fragments, but this procedure is meant for
assessing maximum sensitivity.

 5. DEVELOPMENT PLAN

 There are several detector manufacturers that have glass scintillators doped with neutron absorbers.
ORNL Tech Transfer should contact manufacturers to see if there is interest in commercialization or the
235

 6. CONCLUSIONS

 Scintillation-optical-based fission chambers may have much higher efficiencies than present
commercial fission chambers for neutron counting, a factor of ~100 or more for the same active volumes
or they could be made smaller by a factor of ~100 or more for the same efficiency.
