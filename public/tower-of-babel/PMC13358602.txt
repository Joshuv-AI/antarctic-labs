CT-based deep learning predicts immunotherapy response in esophageal squamous cell cancer patients
Authors: Tongxin Li, Rutao Fan, Zhili Liu, Shanshan Xu, Xiaoqin Zhang, Zhou Xu, Jian Yi, Ziyan Wei, Wei Wu, Yi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunotherapy has emerged as a promising addition to neoadjuvant therapy for locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (LA-ESCC), yet treatment response varies substantially among patients. Reliable biomarkers for predicting response to immunochemotherapy (ICT) remain lacking. We aimed to develop and externally validate a deep learning model for predicting immune Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (iRECIST) response to ICT in LA-ESCC.MethodsThis retrospective, dual-center study included 290 patients with pathologically confirmed LA-ESCC. Of these, 264 patients from The First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University were randomly divided into training (n=185) and internal testing cohorts (n=79), and 26 patients from Banan District People’s Hospital of Chongqing served as an external testing cohort. A deep learning-based model was developed using pretreatment contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) images to predict short-term iRECIST-defined therapeutic response. Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs), Brier scores, calibration intercept and slope, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe model achieved AUCs of 0.853 (95% CI: 0.795–0.906), 0.794 (95% CI: 0.683–0.886), and 0.759 (95% CI: 0.549–0.925) in the training, internal testing, and external testing cohorts, respectively. Calibration was acceptable in the training and internal testing cohorts but weakened in the small external cohort (slope =0.36; 95% CI: −0.02 to 0.75), and DCA showed greater net benefit than “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across a range of threshold probabilities, with wider variability in the external cohort.ConclusionsIn this dual-center retrospective cohort, a deep-learning model based on pretreatment contrast-enhanced CT showed exploratory predictive value for short-term radiological response to ICT in LA-ESCC. Given the small external cohort and single-region design, the present results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, and prospective multi-regional validation is required before any clinical use.Keywords: Computed tomography (CT), deep learning, immunotherapy response, esophageal cancer

Highlight box

Key findings

• A pretreatment contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT)-based three-dimensional (3D) deep learning model predicted short-term immune Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors-defined response to immunochemotherapy in locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (LA-ESCC), achieving areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.853, 0.794, and 0.759 in the training, internal testing, and external testing cohorts, respectively. Calibration and decision-curve analyses supported exploratory utility, although the external cohort was small.

What is known and what is new?

• Immunochemotherapy is a promising neoadjuvant treatment for LA-ESCC, but treatment response varies substantially. Existing biomarkers, including programmed death-ligand 1 expression, microsatellite instability, and tumor mutational burden, have limited and inconsistent predictive performance.

• This study developed and externally tested an image-only 3D convolutional neural network incorporating multi-scale spatial attention and channel attention modules to predict immunochemotherapy response from pretreatment contrast-enhanced CT images.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This CT-based deep learning framework may provide a noninvasive candidate tool for pretreatment risk stratification and individualized treatment planning. It should not yet be used for routine clinical decision-making, and larger multi-regional prospective validation with pathological and survival endpoints is required.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, ranking sixth in overall cancer deaths (1). The predominant histological subtype, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), accounts for more than 90% of cases (2-5).

In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown remarkable antitumor efficacy across multiple cancer types, offering new therapeutic opportunities for patients with ESCC. However, the clinical benefits of immunotherapy vary substantially among individuals. For non-responders, immunotherapy not only fails to provide meaningful efficacy but also imposes high financial costs and exposes patients to the risk of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Therefore, accurately identifying patients who are likely to respond to immunotherapy prior to treatment is crucial for achieving personalized therapy, maximizing clinical benefit, and minimizing unnecessary toxicity.

Currently, there is a lack of reliable biomarkers for predicting the response to immunochemotherapy (ICT). Although molecular markers, such as microsatellite instability (MSI), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and tumor mutational burden (TMB) (6), have been widely used in clinical practice, their predictive value remains limited and testing can be costly. Numerous studies have explored genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and radiomics to identify biomarkers associated with immunotherapy response (6-8). However, single-omics approaches often fail to fully capture the multidimensional heterogeneity of tumors across molecular, cellular, and structural levels. With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, deep learning models have shown great potential by automatically extracting complex and subtle features from medical data.

In this study, we aimed to develop a deep learning model integrating pretreatment contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) imaging to predict immunotherapy response in patients with locally advanced ESCC (LA-ESCC). This approach seeks to provide a reliable, noninvasive tool to support individualized treatment decision-making in the era of precision oncology. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0805/rc).

Methods

Ethical approval

The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study received ethical clearance from the institutional ethics committees of The First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University (approval No. [A] KY2025056) and Banan District People’s Hospital of Chongqing (approval No. BNLL-KY-2025-050), with a waiver of written informed consent owing to its retrospective design. Clinical and imaging data were obtained from the hospital information systems of the two participating institutions, and the primary study endpoint was treatment response.

Patient enrollment

Patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy between January 2020 and June 2022 at The First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University and between January 2020 and December 2022 at Banan District People’s Hospital of Chongqing were retrospectively included. The inclusion criteria were: (I) pathologically confirmed ESCC; and (II) completion of two to four cycles of combined ICT. The exclusion criteria were: (I) poor-quality CT images with significant artifacts; (II) absence of baseline CT imaging prior to initiation of immunotherapy; (III) presence of concomitant malignancies other than the index cancer; (IV) receipt of radiotherapy during the course of treatment; (V) use of immunosuppressive agents within the preceding 2 weeks; (VI) active or untreated human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; and (VII) history of active autoimmune disease requiring systemic therapy within the past 2 years. Clinical variables and pretreatment contrast-enhanced chest CT images were collected for all eligible patients. The study design is illustrated in Figure 1.

A total of 264 patients with LA-ESCC from The First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University were included and randomly divided into training and internal testing cohorts at a ratio of 7:3 using stratified sampling based on treatment response. An additional 26 patients from the Banan District People’s Hospital of Chongqing were enrolled as an external testing cohort for independent validation.

CT image acquisition

All examinations were conducted on Siemens CT systems (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), encompassing three distinct scanner models: the Sensation 16, the Somatom Definition AS 64-slice helical computed tomography unit, and the Somatom Definition Flash operating in dual-source helical mode. Before each examination, patients were instructed to perform a breath-hold at the end of inspiration to reduce artifacts attributable to respiratory motion. Patients were positioned supine with the head entering the gantry first, and both upper extremities were elevated overhead.

Technical acquisition settings included a tube potential ranging from 100 to 120 kV, with tube current modulation applied in an automatic mode, and a helical pitch factor between 1.2 and 1.5. For contrast-enhanced acquisitions, a nonionic iodinated contrast medium, iopamidol with an iodine concentration of 300 mg/mL, was intravenously administered at a volume of 80 to 100 mL and a flow rate of 3.5 to 4.0 mL/s. The resulting image sets contained between 116 and 560 cross-sectional slices, reconstructed at thicknesses varying from 0.50 to 2.00 mm.

Prior to transfer from the institutional picture archiving and communication system (PACS), all CT datasets underwent de-identification procedures to strip personal health information. Exported images were maintained in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format.

Treatment protocol

All patients were scheduled to receive at least two cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. Each treatment cycle lasted 3 weeks, with both immunotherapeutic and chemotherapeutic agents administered via intravenous infusion on day 1 of each cycle. Chemotherapy consisted primarily of paclitaxel (175 mg/m2) combined with a platinum-based agent (cisplatin or carboplatin, 70–75 mg/m2). One of the following ICIs was administered: sintilimab, pembrolizumab, or camrelizumab, each at a recommended dose of 200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks.

Response evaluation

Treatment response was evaluated 4 weeks after completion of therapy by two experienced thoracic surgeons, based on pretreatment and posttreatment contrast-enhanced CT scans. Tumor response was determined according to the immune Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (iRECIST) (9). An immune complete response (iCR) was defined as the disappearance of all target lesions, with all lymph nodes reduced to less than 10 mm in short-axis diameter and no appearance of new measurable lesions. An immune partial response (iPR) referred to a reduction of at least 30% in the sum of target lesion diameters compared with baseline measurements.

Immune progressive disease (iPD) encompassed both immune unconfirmed progression (iUPD) and immune confirmed progression (iCPD). iUPD was defined as an increase of at least 20% (and an absolute increase of ≥5 mm) in the sum of target lesion diameters or the emergence of new or clearly progressing non-target lesions. Patients initially categorized as iUPD were reassessed after 4–8 weeks; if progression was confirmed upon re-evaluation, the case was classified as iCPD, while patients who did not meet the criteria for progression were reassigned based on subsequent imaging findings. Immune stable disease (iSD) was defined as a change insufficient to qualify as either iPR or iUPD.

Patients achieving iCR or iPR were classified as the response group, whereas those with iSD or iCPD were designated as the non-response group. All evaluations were performed only when disease status remained stable for at least 4 weeks after the completion of combined immunotherapy. In cases of iUPD detected during the first evaluation, final classification was determined according to follow-up imaging. Any discrepancies between the two initial reviewers were resolved by consultation with a senior thoracic surgeon.

Model development

A single-modality three-dimensional (3D) convolutional neural network (CNN) was designed to analyze volumetric CT data for lesion-focused feature extraction. As shown in Figure 2, the architecture comprises an attention-enhanced backbone, batch normalization, adaptive pooling, and a classification head. The backbone integrates a multi-scale spatial attention-3D (MSA-3D) module and a channel attention-3D (CA-3D) module. The MSA-3D uses multi-kernel convolutions (1×1×1, 3×3×3, 5×5×5) to capture local and global spatial dependencies, while the CA-3D recalibrates inter-channel relationships via global pooling and shared 1×1×1 convolutions followed by sigmoid normalization.

Overview of the model architecture. Schematic representation of the proposed deep learning framework. The model integrates multi-scale spatial and channel attention mechanisms to extract discriminative imaging features from pretreatment contrast-enhanced CT scans for predicting therapeutic response to nICT. 3D, three-dimensional; CT, computed tomography; nICT, neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy.

The refined features are processed through a 3D ResNet-18 encoder that extracts hierarchical volumetric representations. Batch normalization and adaptive average pooling generate compact global embeddings, followed by dropout (0.2) to prevent overfitting. Finally, a fully connected layer with softmax activation produces probabilistic predictions. This integrated 3D framework effectively combines spatial and channel attention to enhance feature discriminability and interpretability for volumetric CT-based medical image analysis.

Model training

The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e−4, weight decay of 1e−5, and batch size of 16. Training was performed for a maximum of 100 epochs with early stopping (patience =15) based on validation loss, monitoring the loss trajectory to prevent overfitting and reduce unnecessary computational overhead. Data augmentation included random rotation (±15°), horizontal flipping, and intensity jittering. The loss function was binary cross-entropy, and class weights were computed based on the inverse class frequency to address potential label imbalance. All training was implemented using PyTorch 2.1 on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 6000 GPU.

Tumor segmentation

Tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated on cross-sectional CT images by two independent observers (thoracic surgeons with >5 years of experience) using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0; http://www.itksnap.org/). Both observers performed independent delineations on the full cohort, blinded to clinical outcomes. The inter-observer intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for tumor volume was ICC =0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.94. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior radiologist, whose judgment served as the tiebreaker, and the consensus ROI was used for all subsequent training and evaluation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-sided statistical tests were applied, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. For comparisons among three cohorts, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables with equal variances, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied otherwise. Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as the median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution.

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages [n (%)]. When the expected frequencies in all contingency table cells were ≥5, the Pearson χ2 test was used; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test or Monte Carlo simulation-based χ2 test was applied. The development of the deep learning model was implemented using Python version 3.10.12.

Probability outputs from the deep-learning model were dichotomized at the threshold that maximized the Youden index on the training cohort; the resulting threshold was fixed and applied identically to the internal and external testing cohorts and was not re-optimized in either held-out cohort. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score were obtained from 1,000 stratified resamples. The Brier score was computed as the mean squared deviation between predicted probability and observed outcome. Model calibration was assessed by fitting a logistic recalibration model, where an intercept (a) of 0 and a slope (b) of 1 indicate perfect calibration.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 290 patients with pathologically confirmed LA-ESCC were included in the study and were randomly divided into a training cohort (n=185), internal testing cohort (n=79), and an external testing cohort (n=26). The overall cohort consisted of 83.4% male and 16.6% female patients, with a mean age of 65.9±7.6 years. The distributions of sex and age were comparable among all subsets.

Regarding lifestyle factors, 64.5% of patients were current or former smokers, and 62.8% were current or former drinkers. In terms of physical performance, most patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1, with 54.5% scoring 0 and 45.5% scoring 1 across the overall cohort. The tumor location was most commonly in the middle thoracic esophagus (45.5%), followed by the lower (33.4%) and upper (21.0%) segments. The majority of patients presented with locally advanced disease, with 69.3% having clinical tumor (cT)3 tumors and 46.6% having clinical node (cN)1 nodal involvement. According to the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system, stage III disease accounted for 63.1% of all patients, followed by stage II (26.9%) and stage IV (10.0%). Regarding treatment response, 62.1% of patients achieved an iPR/iCR (partial or complete response), while 37.9% exhibited iCPD/iSD (progressive or stable disease). Model performance

As illustrated in Figures 3-5 and Table 2, the deep learning model showed favorable discrimination across all datasets, with progressively wider CIs as cohort size decreased. In the training cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.853 (95% CI: 0.795–0.906), accuracy 0.773 (95% CI: 0.708–0.832), sensitivity 0.705 (95% CI: 0.616–0.786), specificity 0.877 (95% CI: 0.808–0.946), and F1-score 0.790 (95% CI: 0.722–0.849). In the internal testing cohort, AUC was 0.794 (95% CI: 0.683–0.886), accuracy 0.684 (95% CI: 0.570–0.785), sensitivity 0.612 (95% CI: 0.469–0.735), specificity 0.800 (95% CI: 0.633–0.933), and F1-score 0.706 (95% CI: 0.590–0.805). In the external testing cohort, AUC was 0.759 (95% CI: 0.549–0.925), accuracy 0.692 (95% CI: 0.500–0.885), sensitivity 0.684 (95% CI: 0.474–0.895), specificity 0.714 (95% CI: 0.429–1.000), and F1-score 0.765 (95% CI: 0.581–0.914). The corresponding Brier scores were 0.173, 0.197, and 0.240. The 95% CIs were noticeably wider in the external testing cohort, reflecting the limited sample size; in particular, the calibration slope of 0.362 (95% CI: −0.024 to 0.747) suggests over-dispersed probability estimates in the external setting and indicates that absolute predicted probabilities should be interpreted cautiously at the individual-patient level.

Ablation study

To evaluate the contribution of each proposed module, we performed a stepwise ablation analysis, and the results are summarized in Table 3. The baseline model using plain 3D ResNet-18 achieved AUCs of 0.812, 0.753, and 0.701 in the training, internal testing, and external testing cohorts, respectively. Adding the MSA-3D module improved performance to AUCs of 0.835, 0.778, and 0.731, with the most pronounced gain observed in the external testing cohort (+0.030). Further incorporating the CA-3D module yielded the full model with AUCs of 0.853, 0.794, and 0.759, representing an additional improvement of +0.018, +0.016, and +0.028 across the three cohorts. Compared with the baseline, the full model achieved a net improvement of +0.041 in the external testing cohort. These findings confirm that both the MSA-3D and CA-3D modules contribute synergistically to the model’s discriminative power, with the channel attention mechanism providing complementary gains particularly in the generalization to external data.

Calibration and clinical utility

Calibration was acceptable in the training and internal testing cohorts (calibration slopes =1.57 and 1.15, respectively); however, calibration in the small external cohort was weaker (slope =0.36), and the corresponding plot deviated visibly from the ideal line. The external-cohort calibration estimate is therefore exploratory and should be interpreted in light of the limited sample size. Calibration plots are shown in Figure 6A-6C.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) showed that, across a wide range of threshold probabilities, the model provided greater net benefit than the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies in the training and internal testing cohorts. In the small external testing cohort, the same trend was observed, although the curve fluctuated more visibly because of the limited sample size; this finding should therefore be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory of clinical applicability. Discussion

Neoadjuvant ICT (nICT) has emerged as a promising and increasingly adopted treatment strategy for LA-ESCC (10). Recent clinical evidence suggests that incorporating immunotherapy into the neoadjuvant setting can further improve outcomes; for example, the ESCORT-NEO/NCCES01 trial demonstrated that adding immunotherapy to chemotherapy significantly increased the pathological complete response (pCR) rate (28.0% vs. 4.7%) (11). In current clinical practice, the selection of immunotherapy is primarily guided by PD-L1 expression within tumor tissues (12), as higher PD-L1 levels are generally associated with better responses (13). However, the immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation of PD-L1, typically quantified by the combined positive score (CPS) or tumor proportion score (TPS) (14), is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and often lacks satisfactory predictive performance (15). Moreover, PD-L1 quantification varies considerably depending on the staining protocols and antibodies used, and several studies have reported low inter-observer reproducibility even among certified pathologists (16,17).

Previous studies have reported that a substantial proportion of patients fail to benefit from immunotherapy, and in our cohort, 62.1% (180/290) of patients achieved an iPR or iCR while 37.9% (110/290) did not, indicating that more than one-third of patients may not derive meaningful benefit. Therefore, developing a reliable, noninvasive, and reproducible method to predict treatment response before therapy remains an urgent clinical need.

In this study, we proposed a deep learning model that uses purely imaging data as its input. The model demonstrated stable discriminative ability and balanced performance between sensitivity and specificity across the training, internal testing, and external testing cohorts. Calibration analysis showed acceptable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes in the training and internal testing cohorts but weaker agreement in the small external cohort, while DCA indicated that the model achieved a higher net benefit than the “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies over a range of threshold probabilities; this DCA finding should likewise be regarded as exploratory in light of the limited external sample.

Several prior studies have explored similar approaches. Qi et al. (18) developed a multimodal deep learning model in 223 patients to predict pCR after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, achieving an AUC of 0.89, outperforming both radiomics (AUC =0.70) and clinical models (AUC =0.63). Liu et al. (19) integrated histopathology, CT imaging, and clinical variables to predict PD-L1 status (AUC =0.836), immunotherapy response (AUC =0.80), and survival risk stratification (AUC =0.802), though their limited sample size restricted generalizability. Zhang et al. (20) developed a voxel-level radiomics-based deep learning model for predicting pCR after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, achieving an AUC of 0.92. It is important to note that this was a retrospective study without prospective validation; thus, multicenter prospective trials are warranted to further confirm the model’s clinical effectiveness and safety boundaries.

From a clinical perspective, our model could facilitate risk stratification. For patients predicted to have limited benefit, clinicians may consider adjusting treatment strategies earlier to avoid unnecessary delays, cost, and the risk of irAEs. Conversely, for patients predicted to respond favorably, continued immunotherapy under guideline-based management, combined with regular imaging follow-up reassessment, may help optimize therapeutic timing and outcomes. Because the model relies solely on routinely available contrast-enhanced CT images, it could in principle be added to existing imaging workflows; however, the limited size and geographic scope of the external cohort preclude any conclusion regarding clinical readiness, and the present results should be regarded as exploratory. Broader multi-regional validation in independent populations is therefore warranted before clinical adoption.

It is also important to acknowledge that the predicted endpoint here is short-term iRECIST-defined radiological response rather than pCR, major pathological response, R0 resection, or overall survival. iRECIST is the international consensus framework for response assessment in ICI-treated solid tumors and was the only endpoint obtainable for the entire cohort, as pathological and survival endpoints were available only in a small surgical subgroup. Although prior studies have linked short-term iRECIST/RECIST response to longer-term survival, response classified by imaging remains an intermediate surrogate, and the present model should not be interpreted as a predictor of pathological response or prognosis. Prospective studies with pre-specified pathological and survival endpoints are required to establish clinical validity.

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort is heterogeneous in ICI agent, cycle number, and CT acquisition parameters, and no formal harmonization (e.g., ComBat) was applied; the small sample size, particularly in the external cohort, does not permit stable scanner-, ICI-, or cycle-stratified analyses, and residual acquisition- or protocol-related signal cannot be fully excluded. Second, the “black-box” nature of deep learning restricts interpretability. Third, the study is retrospective and lacks prospective validation. Future work will address these limitations through prospective multi-regional validation, ComBat-based image harmonization, stratified analyses, and feature-attribution methods to improve interpretability.

Conclusions

In this exploratory dual-center retrospective study, a deep-learning model trained on pretreatment contrast-enhanced CT showed promising discrimination for short-term radiological response to ICT in LA-ESCC. Because external validation was limited to 26 patients from a single neighboring institution, the model is not yet clinically applicable, and prospective multi-regional validation is required.
