 Executive Summary
 The objective of this project was to develop a revolutionary process and a unique compact
catalytic membrane reactor (MR) for producing ammonia (NH 3) from nitrogen (N2) and
hydrogen (H2). The process used a novel catalytic MR, containing two key components: i) nano-
engineered, low-cost ruthenium (Ru)-based catalyst, and ii) highly NH 3 selective, Na+-gated
nanochannel membrane on hollow fiber support. The developed catalytic MR with integrated
reaction and separation, along with a high packing-density hollow fiber MR configuration,
enables a compact modular system for NH3 synthesis at desired scales.
 The technical scopes of this project included highly active Ru-based catalyst development,
high separation performance sodium ion (Na+)-gated nanochannel membrane development, lab-
scale and prototype MR testing for NH3 synthesis, techno-economic analysis (TEA), and
commercialization plan development. Key findings and conclusions are as follows:
  The final optimized catalyst, composed of 0.2 wt.% Ru with co-promoters (Ba, Cs, Dy)
 supported on mixed Pr-Dy oxides, achieved ammonia synthesis rates >36 mmol
 NH3/gcat/hr at 400 °C and 30 bar and demonstrated excellent stability.
  Optimized membranes showed excellent separation performance of NH 3 over H2 and N2
 under harsh NH3 synthesis conditions (300-400 °C and >20 bar) and demonstrated
 excellent stability for > 120 h, with NH3 flux >1.25 mol/(m2·h) and NH3/H2 selectivity
       >250 at 350 °C.
  Membrane reactors achieved a maximum N2 conversion of 47% with 7.4 mmol/gcat/h
 NH3 synthesis rate at the optimized operating conditions and demonstrated good stability
 over 120 hours.
  A prototype with multiple membrane modules achieved 0.17 kg/day NH 3 production with
 a synthesis rate at 14.83 mmol NH3/gcat/h under optimized operating conditions.
  Detailed high-level TEA showed that the developed MR technology reduces energy
 consumption by over 85%, operating costs by 83%, and unit production cost by over 20%
 ($331.58 vs. $417.99/tonne NH3) for NH3 synthesis compared to the conventional HB
 process (baseline), indicating its economically viable.
  A thorough sensitivity analysis identified membrane flux, N 2 conversion rate, and
 operating pressure as key players for further performance improvement.
  Numbers of potential customers/investors were identified and their needs and
 opportunities for collaboration were actively explored through direct communication. A
 comprehensive commercialization plan was completed, and an industrial partnership was
 established for the next phase development and demonstration.
 The developed innovative, compact, modular MR and process for NH 3 synthesis provides a
transformative technology for significantly improving reaction efficiency, lowering energy and
carbon intensity, and reducing cost of unit operations by process intensification. It will also
contribute to the advancement of green NH3 production technologies, especially in the context of
industrial decarbonization.

1. Introduction
 The overall objective of this project is to develop a revolutionary process and a unique
compact catalytic MR for producing NH3 from N2 and H2. The proposed catalytic MR with
integrated reaction and separation, along with a high packing-density hollow fiber membrane
reactor configuration, enables a compact modular system for NH 3 synthesis at desired scales to
achieve technical goals of: 1) 80-90% energy consumption decrease for NH 3 synthesis and >80%
operating cost decrease (relative to the baseline Haber-Bosch process); 2) >50% single-pass N 2
conversion; 3) <10% performance degradation of catalytic MR in 120 h. The proposed program
will advance the technology from the current TRL 2 to TRL 4 and deliver a 0.2 kg/day prototype
system to DOE.
 In this project, we propose to develop a revolutionary NH3 synthesis process using a
catalytic MR at moderate temperatures (300-400 ℃) and pressures (10-70 bar) and demonstrate
continuous NH3 production. In the proposed process (Figure 1), mixed N 2/H2 stream is
compressed to 10-70 bar and then pre-heated via a cross-exchanger that recovers heat from the
reactor exit stream. The preheated mixture enters the MR for continuous NH 3 production. The
NH3 permeated through the membrane is cooled via the cross-exchanger. The NH 3 stream is
further cooled to liquid for storage. The unreacted high-pressure gas mixture (i.e., N 2, H2, and
very small amount of NH3) is recycled back to the reactor inlet.

 Figure 1. Diagram of the proposed catalytic MR process.

 The process uses a novel catalytic MR (Figure 2) containing two key components: i) highly
NH3 selective, Na+-gated nanochannel membrane on hollow fiber support, [1] and ii) nano-
engineered, low-cost Ru-based catalyst (Ru loading ≤0.2 wt.%). The highly efficient Ru-based
catalyst activates N2 and H2 at moderate temperatures (300-400 ℃) and pressures (10-70 bar)
and converts them to NH3, while our Na+-gated nanochannel membrane separates NH3 from N2
and H2 in situ, shifting the thermodynamic equilibrium towards continuous NH 3 formation. The
membrane has high selectivity of NH3 over N2 and H2, which enables high purity of permeated
NH3, minimizing or eliminating the need for further purification. This integrated one-step
approach intensifies a process that would otherwise require multiple reaction beds, recycle of a
large amount of unreacted gases, and a subsequent energy-intensive NH 3 separation step.

 5

 Figure 2. Diagram of catalytic MR incorporated with Na+-gated, nanochannel hollow fiber membrane for in-situ NH3
 separation.

 The proposed technology aims to develop highly energy efficient process for NH 3 synthesis
by addressing challenges of the current state-of-the-art in NH 3 synthesis including extreme
operating conditions, low N2 conversion rate, and energy intensive NH3 separation and unreacted
gases recycling. Compared to the start-of-the-art Haber-Bosch (HB) process for NH 3 synthesis,
the proposed technology, when fully developed, could reduce energy consumption by 80-90%
and operating cost by 80%. Implementation of the developed catalytic MR at commercial scale
would lead to a significant cost reduction. The proposed catalytic MR utilizes high packing-
density hollow fibers capable of achieving a compact system with a small footprint.
 Market Transformation’s primary goal is to penetrate into the current HB process industry
and to expand into the renewable energy industry and industries with free or low-cost H 2 by
developing highly energy efficient, modular systems for low-cost NH 3 synthesis and reducing
the barriers impeding full technology commercialization. Our strategy is to i) use renewable
energy industry or industry with H2 as the waste or byproduct as a test bed for early adoption of
MR for NH3 synthesis; ii) demonstrate the value proposition of MR for NH 3 synthesis through
collection and analysis of performance and cost data; and iii) develop models, tools, and
templates for users to provide best practices in real-world implementation.

2. Background
 Ammonia is one of the largest-volume chemicals synthesized in the world, consuming ~2%
of the world's energy and generating 1% of global CO2 emission. [2] However, the NH3
synthesis from HB process is energy and capital-intensive due to high reaction temperature and
high pressure, low NH3 yield, cryogenic NH3 purification process, and reheating of the large
quantity of the recycled gas mixture. The NH3 synthesis process efficiency will be greatly
improved if the reaction can be operated at milder conditions and produced NH 3 can be directly
separated from the reaction system using a MR to break the equilibrium limitation and eliminate
the expensive cryogenic condensation process. Our proposed MR incorporating highly selective
Na+-gated nanochannel membrane and highly active Ru-based catalyst is such a technology
which allows operation at moderate temperatures and pressures, enables to overcome the
thermodynamic and kinetic barriers to achieve high N 2 conversion and NH3 yield, and eliminates
cryogenic condensation by in situ NH3 separation. The proposed technology is less energy-
intensive (>80% improvement) and more economical than the HB process.
 This project has three budget periods (BPs). Specific goals for each BP are as follows:

 BP1: development and optimization of key individual components (i.e., membrane, catalyst,
 and MR) to achieve:
 6

  Highly active Ru-based catalyst for high rate NH3 synthesis (>6.0 mmol NH3/gcat/h) at
 moderate temperatures (300-400 ℃) and pressures (>10 bar);
  High separation performance Na+-gated nanochannel membrane (NH3 flux >0.75
 kg/m2/h, and selectivities >100 for NH3 over H2 and N2 at 300-400 °C and >10 bar) for
 effective separation of NH3; and
  Completion of MR testing unit design and fabrication.

 BP2: lab-scale MR testing and prototype design and construction to achieve:
  N2 conversion >50% and >8 mmol NH3/gcat/h production rate at proposed operating
 conditions (reaction temperatures: 300-400 ℃; pressures: 10-70 bar); and
  Completion of prototype system design and fabrication per lab-scale testing results.

 BP3: prototype system (0.2 kg/day) testing to achieve:
  N2 conversion >50% and >8 mmol NH3/gcat/h production rate at proposed operating
 conditions (reaction temperatures: 300-400 ℃; pressures: 10-70 bar) for 120 h with
 <10% performance degradation;
  Validation of achievement of technical goals through process simulation and detailed
 Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA); and
  Delivery of final market transformation plan.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Catalyst development
3.1.1 Data Mining of Ammonia Synthesis Literature for Machine Learning
 Ammonia synthesis catalyst performance data reported in the literature were mined in order
to be used as training data for machine learning. Over 100 research articles published from 1970
to 2020 found through online resources such as Elsevier, Wiley, and ACS were reviewed. After
excluding articles that included complex synthesis processes and gaps in the reported data, 50
research publications were selected for data mining. It should be noted that these research
articles only included heterogeneous catalysts for conventional thermo-catalytic NH 3 synthesis in
laboratory-scale fixed bed catalytic reactors. Moreover, the steady-state synthesis rate of NH3
(mmol of NH3/gcat/h) is only considered as the metric for catalyst performance since it is the
most common and widely used metrics for all the publications. The vast majority of the
datapoints relating the synthesis rate of NH3 to the operational variables are in the form of
images in the literature. Hence, a plot digitizing software is used to convert graphical data to
numerical data. [3] Due to the different types of units used in the literature, they are preprocessed
and converted to the same unit.
 Total 1816 experimental datapoints, relating the catalyst design and operational variables
(features) to the synthesis rate of NH3 (label) were extracted. A summary of the NH3 synthesis
datapoints according to the reaction temperature and pressure is illustrated in Figure 3.

 7

 Figure 3. Overview of NH3 synthesis datapoints according to reaction conditions.

 Heterogeneous catalysts in the dataset include elements with a maximum of three supported
on oxides or activated carbon. The highest NH3 synthesis rate in the dataset is 760 mmol of
NH3/gcat/h, which is obtained by utilizing over 22 wt.% Ru supported on activated carbon
promoted by Ba and Cs, at 63 bar (6.3 MPa) pressure and 400 °C temperature with over 480,000
mL/gcat/h space velocity. [4] The highest NH3 synthesis rate for Ru based catalyst with Ru ≤ 1
wt.% in the current dataset is 9.3 mmol of NH3/gcat/h by 1 wt.% unpromoted Ru supported on
ceria at 10 bar (1 MPa) pressure and 400 °C temperature with 36,000 mL/g cat/h space velocity.
[5] However, this rate is obtained by a CO reduction step before the standard H 2 reduction step,
which promoted strong metal-support interaction. The synthesis rate dropped to 4.1 mmol of
NH3/gcat/h without the CO reduction step. [6]

3.1.2 Catalyst Synthesis and Testing
Benchmark Catalyst Activity
 Benchmark 2 wt.% Ru supported on MgO and Al 2O3 were synthesized using incipient
wetness impregnation. The support was first dried at 105 ºC for 1 hour before impregnation. An
appropriate amount of RuCl3 was dissolved in deionized (DI) water and an aliquot of solution
was then added to the dried support under magnetic stirring until incipient wetness was achieved.
The impregnated support was then dried at 105 ºC for 10 minutes, after which the process was
repeated until the entire solution was impregnated onto the support. The catalysts were then dried
at 105 ºC for 24 hours. For other catalyst formulations the same procedure was followed by
dissolving RuCl3 along with other metal precursors in the same solution. The dried catalyst was
then reduced at 400 ºC for 1 hour at 1 bar using a stochiometric mixture of H 2 to N2 gas mixture
for NH3 synthesis (3:1) in a single channel reactor. Ammonia synthesis rate measurements then
followed at 300, 350, 400, and 450 ºC at different pressures, H 2 to N2 ratios and space velocities.
 The 2 wt.% Ru/Al2O3 and 2 wt.% Ru/MgO benchmark catalysts were tested for activity in a
single channel plug flow reactor, which was built for this project. The results of these tests can
be seen in Figure 6. In general, the benchmark catalysts showed little activity across the
temperature range.

Machine Learning Predictions
 A machine learning model was trained using 1507 datapoints extracted from the literature
relating to the NH3 synthesis rate and features affecting the rate. An extra trees regressor model
was selected for predictions due to the highest accuracy (lowest mean absolute error) during the
training. The predicted vs measured NH3 synthesis rates for the model are shown in Figure 7.
Most datapoints appear close to the parity line, where the measured and the predicted synthesis
rates agree. Some datapoints appear above the parity line and some below, meaning that for
some catalyst formulations the model is overpredicting and underpredicting for some.

 Figure 7. Predictions by extra trees regressor compared to measured synthesis rates.

 To gain insight into the extra trees regressor model and interpret the accuracy gained by the
model, a metric known as feature importance can be used. The top ten most important features of
the extra tree regressor model are shown in Figure 8. Pressure and temperature are the two most
important features which make sense since those two features are the two most important
features determining the NH3 synthesis rate for any catalyst.

 10

 Figure 8. Feature Importance for extra tree regressor.

 The trained extra trees regressor machine learning model was then used to predict the NH 3
synthesis rates of 35,282 hypothetical catalyst formulations at 10 bar, H 2:N2 ratio of 3:1 and
24,000 mL/gcat/h space velocity at 300, 350 and 400 ºC. These formulations are formed by
different combinations of 5 different alkali metals (loadings from 1 to 12 wt.%) and different 53
metallic elements (loadings from 1 to 12 wt.%) along with 1 wt.% Ru supported on MgO. The
predictions of NH3 synthesis rates of all the 35,282 catalyst formulations are plotted in Figure 9.
This figure known as a ‘violin plot’ illustrates the density of the points by the enclosed grey area
at each temperature across all NH3 synthesis rates. The vast majority of the datapoints in the plot
are concentrated between 2 to 5 mmol NH3/gcat/h. Ru and Co containing catalysts are predicted
to be the best catalyst candidates across all three temperatures. This result can be expected since
both Ru and Co are active metals for NH3 synthesis.

 The Pr2O3 support was synthesized by a precipitating method. A 0.5 M aqueous solution of
praseodymium nitrate hexahydrate (Sigma Aldrich) was added at a rate of 2 mL/min to excess
ammonium hydroxide (28%) solution (Sigma Aldrich), while undergoing constant mixing. After
three hours, the precipitate was allowed to come to rest at the bottom of the beaker of the
solution overnight and then was filtered with a Buchner funnel followed by multiple washes of
deionized water. The precipitate, which was mostly praseodymium hydroxide, was dried at 105
°C and calcined under air at 700 °C for five hours. Following this, praseodymium oxide support
was added to a solution of ruthenium carbonyl (Fisher Scientific) in excess tetrahydrofuran
(Sigma Aldrich). While mixing, the tetrahydrofuran was allowed to evaporate. The remaining

catalyst was calcined under argon at 350 °C for five hours to remove the carbonyl ligands and
then reduced under hydrogen at 500 °C for one hour.
 As can be seen in Figure 18 and in comparison, with Table 1 and Table 2, the 3 wt.%
Ru/Pr2O3 displayed significantly higher production rates (11.83 mmol NH 3/gcat/h at 400 °C, 30
bar, 7,200 mL/gcat/h, H2:N2 ratio of 3:1) than the previously best performing MgO supported
catalysts. Further, unlike the MgO supported catalysts, this catalyst showed increasing NH 3

Active Learning for Accelerating Catalyst Optimization
 The machine learning (ML) model developed based on literature data, as explained in
previous, was used to predict the NH3 synthesis activity of the catalysts tested in the high
throughput (HT) reactor. The HT reactor tested 43 catalyst formulations by varying the second
promoter (2 wt.%) for a catalyst with Ru (1 wt.%), Cs (2 wt.%) supported on Pr 2O3. This search
space included second promoters namely, Li, Na, K, Rb, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Sc, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co,
Ni, Cu, Zn, Al, Ga, Y, Zr, Ag, Cd, In, Sn, Hf, Re, Ir, Pb, Bi, La, Ce, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy,
Ho, Er, Tm, Yb and Lu. The activities of these catalysts were predicted at 400 °C, 30 bar, H 2/N2
ratio of 1 at a space velocity of 36000 mL/gcat/h. The top 5 catalysts with the highest activity
according to the ML model were Zn, Mn, Cr, Mg, and Cd, and their predicted vs. measured
activity is shown in Figure 32.
 23

Figure 32. Predicted vs measured NH3 synthesis rate (400 °C, 30 bar, H2/N2 =1, 36000 ml/hr/gcat) of top 5 catalysts according
 to ML predictions.

 As seen in Figure 32, ML predictions were not accurate. Only 2 catalysts with Mg and Cd
were predicted with reasonable accuracy, and the catalyst that was predicted to have the highest
activity with Zn as a promoter had no measurable activity. These inaccuracies were not
surprising since there were no training data for NH 3 synthesis activity of Ru catalysts with
promoters and supported on Pr2O3 to be extracted from literature. Hence, more experimental data
in this search space should be integrated into the machine learning model to improve the
accuracy of the predictions.
 To reduce the number of experiments another ML method named active learning (AL) was
tested. As seen in Figure 33. ML model trained in literature data proposes to run an experiment
(catalyst formulation with a different promoter to be tested in the reactor). Three experimental
directions can be proposed.
  Maximum Uncertainty (MU): The formulation predicted with the maximum
 uncertainty for NH3 synthesis activity.
  Maximum Expected Improvement (MEI): The formulation predicted with the
 maximum value for NH3 synthesis activity.
  Maximum Likelihood Improvement (MLI): The formulation predicted with the
 maximum value for NH3 synthesis activity considering the uncertainty.

 Figure 33. Active learning (AL) process.

 After the experiment is run, the data is integrated into the ML model and re-trained with
experimental and literature data. Then a new experiment to run is proposed. Likewise, these
steps are iteratively executed until a catalyst with the desired goal is experimentally found. The
results of the AL are shown in Figure 34. Here the best experimentally found NH 3 synthesis rate
is plotted vs. each new experimental run for the three types of experiments (MU, MEI and MLI).
The experiment to run is marked as the promoter in the catalyst in the MEI pathway, which
 24

required the minimum number of experiments (18 out of 43) compared to other methods to reach
the best NH3 synthesis rate in the search space with the Ba promoted catalyst. Hence, AL showed
that about 60% of experiments in the search space are not necessary and reduced the time and
resources required to find the best catalyst in the search space.

Identifying catalyst descriptors via machine learning
 A machine learning model was developed using data extracted from literature to understand

 30

the role of support for the future design of better supports. The model is interpreted using SHAP
(SHapley Additive exPlanations) to determine how the individual descriptors would affect the
NH3 synthesis rate as the descriptor values are being changed. SHAP calculates the contribution
or effect of each descriptor to the prediction of the machine learning model for every datapoint in
the model. The variation of this effect versus the descriptor values was plotted for each
descriptor used in the machine learning model. A volcano like relationship was observed for the
SHAP value of the mean value of the support metal nitride formation energy when its value was
changed (Figure 43a).
 This descriptor describes the strength of acidity on the metal oxide surfaces since the acidity
of metal oxide is experimentally characterized by doing NH3-Temperature Programmed
Desorption. The strength of the acid sites of the metal oxide is determined by how strong the
metal oxide is bonded to the NH3 or by the metal-nitrogen bond strength. The formation enthalpy
of the metal nitride is descriptive of the strength of the metal-nitrogen bond. Increasing absolute
values of the formation enthalpy of the metal nitride means increasing metal-nitrogen bond
strength which corresponds to acid sites with increasing strength.

Figure 43. (a) Variation of the SHAP value of the mean value of the support metal nitride formation energy (b) Some literature
 data points for synthesis rate (5 wt.% Ru, 400 °C, 10 bar) of Ru/oxide catalysts versus the same descriptor [7].

 This variation of the acidity descriptor suggests an optimal acidity value corresponding to
the optimal NH3 synthesis rate. In fact, plotting some of the literature data points for synthesis
rate instead of the SHAP value for Ru/oxide catalyst under the same reaction conditions also
shows a volcano like relationship (Figure 443b). These trends were experimentally validated by
performing NH3-Temperature Programmed Desorption with mass spectroscopy (10 °C/min from
100 °C) on selected metal oxides for different sides of the volcano trend, which are Yb 2O3, CeO2
and Er2O3. As seen in Figure 44 the expected increase in acidity Er2O3 > CeO2 > Yb2O3,
expected from Figure 43b is also seen in experimental data. Ammonia is desorbed at increasing
temperatures demonstrating that the strength of the acid sites and the number of different types
of acid sites increases with increase in the support metal nitride formation enthalpy.

Membrane preparation and characterization

the procedure reported in our previous publication. [16]

solution, following by drying and heat annealing and finally hydrothermal synthesis at 80 °C for

Ion exchange on Na+-gated nanochannel membranes with Cs+ and K+

 42

 Since the cation (i.e., Na+) plays a critical role on determining the gas permeation property
of the Na+-gated nanochannel membrane, it is expected that the membrane performance can be
altered by changing different cations in the membrane layer. To reduce the H 2 and N2
permeances, a larger monovalent cation than Na+ is preferred based on the assumption that larger
cations tend to occupy more space in the zeolitic framework and result in smaller accessible
pores to those molecules.
 The cesium carbonate and potassium carbonate were used as salts for providing the Cs + and
K+, respectively. Before the ion-exchange, a certain amount of salt was dissolved to make
solutions with the molar concentration of Cs + to be 0.01 M, 0.05 M and 0.1 M, respectively.
Solutions with different molar concentrations of Cs + were used for investigating the influence of
Cs+ concentration on the membrane performance. For potassium, only solution with K +
concentration of 0.1M was prepared. To prevent the formation of the defects during the ion-
exchange, methanol was used as the solvent to dissolve salt. For ion-exchange, the membranes
were immersed in the above prepared solutions for different times (1.5 hours, 2 hours, 8 hours,
and 20 hours) at room temperature to investigate the influence of the ion-exchange time on the
membrane separation performance. Please note that we kept stirring the solutions during the ion-
exchange process, and the membranes were washed thoroughly with deionized water after the
ion-exchange. After drying the membranes at 80 °C overnight, they were sealed and fixed inside
the membrane module for testing.
Ion-exchange with Cs+
 Figure 60 shows the single gas permeation performance of the membranes after ion-
exchange with different concentrations of Cs+ at room temperature for 8 hours. The membrane at
concentration of 0 refers to the Na+-gated nanochannel membrane without ion-exchange.
According to Figure 60, H2 permeance decreased upon the Cs+ exchange and decreased with the
increase of the Cs+ concentration. For example, H2 permeance for Na+-gated nanochannel
membrane without ion-exchange was 1.98×10-9 mol/m2/s/Pa; after ion-exchanging with 0.01 M,
0.05 M, and 0.1 M of Cs+, it decreased to 1.90×10-9, 7.86×10-10, and 2.71×10-10 mol/m2/s/Pa,
respectively. Although N2 permeance after ion-change with 0.01 M (5.52×10-10 mol/m2/s/Pa) and
0.05 M Cs+ (3.98×10-10 mol/m2/s/Pa) solutions were higher than the Na+-gated nanochannel
membrane without ion-exchange (6.83×10-11 mol/m2/s/Pa), the membrane after ion-exchange
with 0.1 M Cs+ solution showed almost the same N2 permeance (7.71×10-11 mol/m2/s/Pa) with
the membrane without ion-exchange. The results indicated that the ion-exchange indeed changed
the membrane composition and altered the gas permeation behavior.
 There are several possible reasons for the gas permeation change. First, there might be some
defects formed during the ion-exchange, which was already reported in the literature. [17, 18, 19]
Although we had chosen methanol as the solvent and tried to prevent the formation of defects,
we cannot exclude this possibility. The much higher N 2 permeance could result from the
formation of defects. With the increase of the salt concentration, more Cs + tends to precipitate
inside the defect and results in a smaller H2 and N2 gas permeances. Another possible reason for
this change is that the effective pore size was changed by Cs +, and the membrane pores were co-
gated by Na+ and Cs+. Cs+ has a larger radius and occupies more space inside the unit cell of
zeolite, therefore the effective pore size decreased by incorporating Cs + and became smaller and
smaller when the membrane was exposed to higher concentrations of Cs +. This explains why the
membrane after ion-exchange with 0.1 M Cs + solution had the lowest gas permeance. The third
possibility is the combined effect of those two reasons. To further understand the reason behind
it, more characterizations and comparison experiments are needed.

linear increase over the whole temperature range, while the membranes after ion-exchange
showed first a mild increase in the temperature range of 200-300 °C and then a steep increase in
the temperature range of 300-400 °C. In general, the membranes after the ion-exchange showed a
slightly mild permeance increase, compared with the pristine Na +-gated nanochannel membrane.

Membrane Si/Al ratio tuning of the Na+-gated nanochannel membranes
 The Na+ content and the hydrophilicity of a zeolitic framework is determined by the Si/Al
ratio of the zeolite. Normally, more Al and thus lower Si/Al ratio will result in more Na + content
and more hydrophilic membrane surface. More Na + content will increase the NH3 permeance and
decrease the H2 and N2 permeances. More hydrophilic surfaces will enable stronger NH3
adsorption at higher temperature, which will increase the NH 3 permeance and block the
adsorption and permeation of H2 and N2 through the membrane. Due to the strong correlation
between Si/Al ratio and the Na+ content and hydrophilicity, it is expected that changing the Si/Al
ratio might alter the separation performance of the Na+-gated nanochannel membranes.

 46

 To tune the membrane Si/Al ratio, we simply changed the recipe of the membrane synthesis
gel by adding more aluminum to the synthesis solution. To find a good amount of additional
aluminum, we started by increasing the aluminum by 10 % and 20 % and slightly tuned the
aluminum amount based on the membrane performance. The only difference from preparing a
regular Na+-gated nanochannel membrane is that more aluminum was added. All the other
treatments were identical to that of preparing the regular Na +-gated nanochannel membranes.
 For tuning the Si/Al ratio in the membrane layer, we increased the aluminum amount by 10
% and 20 %, respectively, when preparing the synthesis gel for membrane growth. After the
membrane synthesis, the 20 % higher aluminum recipe did not result in good membranes, as the
solution did not turn clear before the membrane synthesis, and the membrane surface was very
rough after the synthesis. We only tested the membrane prepared by the synthesis gel with 10 %
more aluminum added. The single gas permeation and NH3 separation performance are listed in
Table 11 and Table 12, respectively. From the single gas permeation data, the membrane’s
performance was very similar to that of the regular Na+-gated nanochannel membrane. For the
mixture separation performance, the membrane showed higher NH 3 permeance (6.02×10-8
mol/m2/s/Pa, NH3 flux of 0.51 kg/m2/h) than that of the regular Na+-gated nanochannel
membranes (2-5×10-8 mol/m2/s/Pa), while the H2 and N2 permeance did not change too much.
As a result, the NH3/H2 and NH3/N2 selectivities were 320 and 950, respectively, at 200 °C and
21 bar.

Membrane performance at different NH3 feed temperature
 Due to the limitations of the feed tank gas mixture pressure, the effect of feed temperature
was performed at 21 bar. Figure 64 shows the effect of feed temperature on the NH 3 flux with
20% NH3 mixture. At constant feed pressure of 21 bar, the NH3 permeance at 300 C was
3.1410-8 mol/m2/s/Pa and slightly decreased to 2.6010-8 mol/m2/s/Pa at 400 C, while the flux
also decreased from 0.65 kg/m2/h to 0.56 kg/m2/h. The temperature did not affect the NH3
permeance and flux as much as for the H2 and N2 permeances, which resulted in a decrease in
membrane selectivity. The H2 permeance increased from 8.6910-11 mol/m2/s/Pa to 4.1810-10
mol/m2/s/Pa and the N2 permeance was from 9.7410-12 mol/m2/s/Pa to 4.5410-11 mol/m2/s/Pa,
when the temperature increased from 300 C to 400 C. Under these increasing feed temperature
conditions, the membrane selectively suffered with NH 3/H2 selectivity from 362 to 62 and
NH3/N2 selectivity from 3,322 to 574. It can be taken from the current results that the feed
temperature had a small effect on the flux but large effect on selectivity, as the adsorption of NH 3
on the membrane surface got weaker and thus lowered the blockage of both H 2 and N2. Thus, it
is suggested to perform the membrane separation process at around 300 C. The selectivity meets
the target of >250 according to Milestone 5.2.2. However, the membrane flux still needs to be
improved to fully achieve the target of >1.5 kg/m 2/h. One way to achieve higher flux is to
minimize the selective layer thickness by using a diluted seeding solution.

3.3 MR and prototype testing
3.3.1 Lab-scale catalytic MR testing system design and construction
Lab-scale catalytic MR testing system design
 We designed a lab-scale catalytic MR testing system. The process flow diagram (PFD) for
the system is shown in Figure 69. The system includes feed gases (H 2 and N2) delivery system,
sweep gas (He) delivery system, mass flow controllers (MFCs), a MR, tubular furnace, and an
on-line GC for composition analysis. In this design, hydrogen in stream and nitrogen in stream 2
are mixed after passing through mass flow controllers, check valves, and ball valves. The mixed
hydrogen and nitrogen then are fed through stream 3 and into the MR. The pressure relief in
stream 3 is added to limit the maximum pressure and protect the system. Helium in stream 4 is
sweep gas, which passes through permeate side (NH3-rich) of membrane then exits through
stream 5. The reactant gases at the retention side (NH3-depleted) exit the MR through stream 6.
The stream 6 then is cooled and depressurized by passing through chiller and back pressure
regulator to stream 7. Both stream 5 and stream 7 are analyzed by GC to quantify their
composition.

 Figure 69. Process flow diagram (PFD) of the MR testing system.
 54

 A gas delivery and MR testing system were constructed, as shown in Figure 70. The system
can be tested up to 70 bar. The gas delivery system was done leakage test, and all connections
are free leakage. The pressure relief valve and MR testing part are in a well-ventilated fume hood
to ensure vented gases from relief valve and any potential NH 3 and H2 leakage from the reaction
to be ventilated. The MFCs, valves, and regulators have been checked to ensure to work as
needed. The MFCs for N2, H2, and argon were calibrated. The furnace was validated to ensure
the uniformity of the temperature.

Hazard and Operability Analysis (HAZOP)
 We conducted a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of our 0.2 kg/day prototype
testing system to identify and assess potential hazards to personnel and equipment. HAZOP was
developed using a deviation-by-deviation approach, identifying 7 nodes, including the
inlet/outlet gas streams, furnace, MR, vacuum pump, ventilation, and product analysis system.
Each node was carefully examined, considering process parameters, causes of deviations,
consequences, safeguards, actions, and personnel responsibilities.
 The safe operation and handling of our prototype testing system relies on several critical
factors, such as the proper orientation of valves, the adequate functioning of the compressed gas
cylinder regulators, and appropriately installed leak-free piping fixtures. Ensuring the accurate
and proper functioning of our GC and furnace controllers is also crucial for the proper operation
of our testing system. By following the protocols described in our HAZOP report, we can ensure
the safe and effective operation of our prototype testing system.

Sensitivity Analysis
 To identify bottlenecks and cost drivers in our technology, a comprehensive sensitivity
analysis was further refined for key parameters, including operating pressure, reaction
temperature, conversion rate, membrane flux, Ru concentration, and permeate pressure within
the MR for NH3 production. These variables were examined for their influence on process
performance and economic outcomes.
 The sensitivity analysis examined the following parameters based on TEA results to explore
optimization opportunities for the NH3 synthesis process:
 1) Conversion rate: Adjust conversion rate of N2 (current conversion rate is 25%).
 2) Ru concentration: Adjust Ru concentration of the catalyst used in the MR (current Ru
 concentration is 0.6 wt.%)
 3) Na+-gated membrane flux: adjust membrane flux of the Na+-gated membrane (current
 membrane flux is 1.5 kg/(m2·h)).
 4) Permeate pressure: Adjust permeate side pressure of Na+-membrane (current
 permeate pressure of Na+-MR is 0.6 bar).
 5) Reaction temperature: Adjust NH3 synthesis reaction temperature (current reaction
 temperature of NH3 synthesis is 400 °C).
 6) Operating pressure: Adjust pressure of NH3 synthesis (current operating pressure of
 NH3 synthesis is 30 bar).
 These parameters represent potential performance improvements, offering insight into the
ongoing R&D and the potential application of emerging technologies. Each variable has a direct
impact on unit production costs, operating expenses, and energy consumption, providing a
pathway for further cost reduction and process optimization.
 The results of the sensitivity analysis and their impact on unit production costs are
summarized in Figure 95. The base case shows a unit production cost of $332/tonne of NH 3. The
sensitivity analysis reveals that the unit production cost is most influenced by membrane flux, N 2
conversion rate, and operating conditions (temperature and pressure). For instance, increasing N 2
conversion rate by 45% reduces the production cost to $305/tonne NH 3, while lower conversion
rate or suboptimal operating conditions (e.g., reduced pressure or temperature) increases costs.
The concentration of Ru in the catalyst has a relatively smaller impact on production costs, while
the permeate pressure shows minimal influence. The analysis suggests that improving membrane
performance, conversion rate, and optimizing operating conditions are essential to achieving cost
reductions in NH3 production.

 75

 Figure 95. Overall sensitivity analysis of key parameters for NH3 unit production cost.

 The effects of various parameters on energy consumption are outlined in Figure 96. The
analysis highlights that operating pressure has the largest effect on energy use, with higher
pressures leading to increased energy consumption. Conversely, lower pressures, such as 20 bar,
reduce energy demands. Permeate pressure also plays a critical role, as it affects the load on the
vacuum pump: higher permeate pressure eases the vacuum pump's workload, reducing energy
consumption, while lower permeate pressure increases the pump's workload, leading to higher
energy use. N2 conversion rate has a moderate impact, with increases causing a slight rise in
energy use. Reaction temperature, permeate pressure, and conversion rate adjustments can help
reduce energy consumption, while Ru concentration and membrane flux have minimal impact.
Optimizing operating pressure and membrane permeate pressure are thus key to improving
energy efficiency in NH3 synthesis.

lower pressures (e.g., 20 bar) lower operating costs. Similar to energy consumption, permeate
pressure affects the vacuum pump's load, influencing operating costs-higher permeate pressure
reduces the pump's workload and costs, while lower permeate pressure increases them. N 2
conversion rate has a moderate effect on operating costs, with slight increases or decreases
linked to minor cost changes. Reaction temperature, permeate pressure, and conversion rate

improvements can lower operating costs, while Ru concentration and membrane flux exhibit

Process robustness
 The evaluation for the improvement of process robustness was conducted based on the
following equation:
 Improvement of process robustness = (improvement of pressure range + improvement of
temperature range)/2×100%.
 The current HB process requires high temperatures (450-500 ºC) and pressures (200-300
bar) in order to achieve high single-pass conversion yield 10-15%. Compared to the HB process,
our testing has demonstrated high conversion rates (>15%) at much lower temperatures (330-430
ºC) and pressures (10-70 bar), leading to the significant improvement of the process robustness
(30%).

 Throughout the project, the following major accomplishments have been achieved:
 1) Ammonia synthesis catalyst development:
  A number of new promoters for NH3 synthesis was determined. In addition to cesium,
 barium, and potassium are beneficial for NH3 synthesis, several lanthanides including
 lutetium, dysprosium, and europium, were also found to be effect promoters. Our
 work also revealed that barium could act as a hydrogen donor during the reaction.
  A series of low-Ru loading catalysts with outstanding performance have been
 Developed. The final optimized catalyst, composed of 0.2 wt.% Ru with co-
 promoters (Ba, Cs, Dy) supported on mixed Pr-Dy oxides, achieved NH 3 synthesis
 rates >36 mmol NH3/gcat/h at 400 °C and 30 bar and demonstrated excellent stability.
 83

  The process for unpromoted catalysts uses the associative mechanism was developed
 through the work of this project.
 2) Membrane Development:
  High quality membranes with <5 mm thickness were prepared on porous ceramic
 support.
  Optimized membranes showed excellent separation performance of NH 3 over H2 and
 N2 under harsh NH3 synthesis conditions (300-400 °C and >20 bar).
  Excellent membrane stability, with NH3 flux >1.25 mol/(m2·h) and NH3/H2
 selectivity >250 at 350 °C, was demonstrated for > 120 h
 3) MR for NH3 Synthesis:
  MRs achieved a maximum of 47% N2 conversion with 7.4 mmol/gcat/h NH3 synthesis
 rate at moderate conditions.
  MR for NH3 synthesis demonstrated good stability over 120 h with <10%
 performance degradation.
  A prototype with multiple membrane modules achieved 0.17 kg/day NH 3 production
 with a maximum synthesis rate at 14.83 mmol NH 3/gcat/h under optimized operating
 conditions.
 4) Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA):
  A detailed high-level TEA was performed using SuperPro Designer based on a
 production capacity of 2,000 tonnes/day. This included detailed capital and
 operational cost modeling, unit production cost assessment, and sensitivity analyses.
  The proposed technology showed a total energy consumption of only 0.83 GJ/tonne
 NH3, an 85.9% reduction compared to the conventional HB process. The new
 technology achieves a 20.7% lower unit production cost than HB ($331.58 vs.
 $417.99/tonne NH3), with potential for further reduction upon optimization. The total
 capital investment and annual operating costs were reduced by 82% and 83%,
 respectively, compared to HB.
  A thorough sensitivity analysis identified membrane flux, N 2 conversion rate, and
 operating pressure as key levers for further performance improvement.
 5) Market Analysis and Commercialization Planning

5. Benefits Assessment
 The compact modular process incorporating high active, low content Ru-based catalyst and
high-performance Na+-gated nanochannel membranes developed in this project offers substantial
energy, economic, and environmental benefits upon potential commercialization:

 84

  Energy savings: The current HB process compresses reactants to approximately 200 bar
 of pressure. Due to the limitations of the catalysts and thermodynamics, pressure is
 increased to produce useful effluent NH3 concentrations. The energy required to
 pressurize this system is incredibly high. Usage of the developed catalyst, membrane, and
 MR system instead would reduce many of the issues that the industrialized process has.
 The catalysts developed met and exceeded the required operating conditions of the novel
 system. These catalysts have been shown to be stable and can operate under off/on
 system changes, which will allow the system to run when renewable energy is available
 or when the load on the power grid would be low. The highly stable and high-
 performance Na+-gated nanochannel membrane under NH3 synthesis condition enables
 separate NH3 in situ, boosting N2 conversion and collecting product simultaneously. TEA
 for our developed process shows that our technology reduced energy consumption by
 over 80% compared to the HB process, significantly lowering energy demands in
 industrial NH3 production after commercial implementation.
  Process simplification: Our MR integrates reaction and separation and can separate NH3
 with reactants in-situ, making permeated NH3 with high purity, so eliminating or
 minimizing further purification by cryogenic condensation. The in situ separation of NH 3
 also eliminates the reheating of the unreacted N2 and H2 before recycling. The compact,
 integrated MR design minimizes unit operations, improving plant operability, safety, and
 reducing maintenance needs.
  Modular & scalable: The high packing-density hollow fiber membrane configuration of
 our reactor enables a compact modular system and simple manufacturing. The basic
 building unit is an individual module that can be duplicated as needed. This advantage
 makes our technology suitable for deployment in decentralized locations, particularly
 beneficial for renewable hydrogen integration and remote agricultural applications.
  Cost competitiveness: Due to the utilization of low Ru concentration catalyst and high-
 performance Na+-gated nanochannel membrane for in situ product separation, high
 reaction efficiency achieved by MR, and process simplification, our technology could
 reduce the unit production cost of NH3 by over 20%, which can improve market
 competitiveness and resilience of decentralized production systems.
  Reduced emissions: Lower energy use, particularly by eliminating high-pressure
 compression and cryogenic condensation steps, implies a substantial reduction in
 associated CO2 emissions, especially when powered by low-carbon electricity.
6. Lessons Learned
 Several important lessons emerged during the project, informing future research and
development:
  Catalyst development: The key takeaways from the catalyst development are three-fold.
 Using literature-based data for machine learning is not an effective approach. The data is
 too heterogeneous to compare between different running conditions, synthesis
 parameters, and reactors. As a result, machine learning for catalyst development should
 be used in tandem with an experimental set-up. Active learning is effective, but only
 when the data are taken using a single reactor with a single synthesis setup. Second, high-
 throughput screening is an effective method for determining useful promoters. The
 cesium and barium were found to be good again, which lines up well with the consensus
 of catalyst development in literature. However, the tool also yielded novel promoters that
 had not been studied. Finally, the combination of in situ spectroscopy and kinetic
 85

 analyses revealed the role of the promoters and how the mechanism changed for
 promoted and unpromoted catalysts, highlighting their potential for use in tandem.
  Membrane development: Seeds for Na+-gated nanochannel membrane synthesis may
 limit membrane scale-up in the future. More economic synthesis methods for nano-seeds
 need to be developed. The membrane’s flux and selectivity were also identified as
 critical factors dictating not only NH3 productivity but also overall process efficiency
 and economic viability. High NH3 permeation flux is essential for achieving
 meaningful separation and conversion enhancement under equilibrium-limited
 conditions. However, sustaining this performance under harsh thermal and chemical
 environments remains a key challenge. Future work must prioritize advanced
 membrane materials that offer both high permeance and selectivity while exhibiting
 long-term thermal, mechanical, and chemical stability.
  MR design and optimization: MRs that integrate both catalyst and membranes are
 significantly more complex than traditional fixed-bed reactors. Their design must
 carefully balance trade-offs among membrane areas, catalyst properties, system
 pressure drops, and integration with upstream and downstream unit operations.
 Operating conditions should be optimized to meet for the distinct performance
 requirements of both the membrane and the catalyst. These operating parameters are
 essential not just for maximizing MR performance but also for improving overall
 energy efficiency and ensuring effective membrane and catalyst integration.
7. Conclusions
 The catalyst development in this project was useful for scientific understanding, but also for
real-world applications. The catalysts met and exceeded all project objectives and fulfilled the
requirements for use in the MRs. Further, they are stable and can operate under start-up/shut-
down procedures, which are useful for decarbonization. In addition, the work here showed that
active machine learning and high-throughput screening can yield similar results for beneficial
catalysts, and that the role of the promoters used can be determined with detailed analyses.
 The membrane developed in this project demonstrated high separation performance and
excellent stability for NH3 separation. This highly NH3-selective membrane, capable of in situ
separation under realistic reaction conditions, offers significant advantages for improving the
NH3 synthesis process.
 The innovative, compact, and modular MR developed in this project integrates both catalyst
and membrane into a single unit, enabling simultaneous reaction and separation. Lab-scale and
prototype testing has demonstrated N2 conversion and NH3 yield that are 3–5 times higher than
those of the conventional HB process under mild operating conditions. Long-term testing has
also confirmed the excellent stability of the MR. Due to its high reaction efficiency, in situ NH₃
recovery, and moderate operating conditions, this transformative MR simplifies and advances
NH3 production while enhancing energy efficiency. TEA indicates that the developed MR
process can substantially reduce both energy and carbon intensity while also lowering operating
and unit production costs, making it economically viable. With further optimization, scale-up,
and commercialization, this technology has the potential to replace the conventional HB process.
It will also play a key role in advancing green NH3 production technologies, particularly in
support of industrial decarbonization efforts.

8. Future Work/Recommendations
 Based on current findings, to further advance this technology towards commercialization,
the following areas are recommended for future research and development.
 Further catalyst development is required before commercialization. Before roll-out, the
addition of other promoters such as potassium and lutetium should be further explored to
produce an even better catalyst that can operate at even lower temperatures than the cesium-
barium promoted catalyst. In addition, detailed kinetic analyses should be conducted over a
range of temperatures and pressures to create an understanding of where the best synergistic
effects of the catalyst and MR would be. Additionally, further research should be conducted to
explore possible ways to reduce the relatively rare praseodymium oxide, thereby supporting cost
reduction and increased availability. Finally, further development of the catalyst should be
undertaken to use more commercially available precursors to determine the commercial viability
of the catalyst.
 For Na+-gated nanochannel membrane, future work for its further development would be to
develop low-cost methods for nano-seeds synthesis. Low cost and scalable methods for nano-
seeds synthesis can reduce the costs of membrane manufacturing, thereby lowering the overall
cost of MR and capital cost of NH3 production. Additionally, NH3 permeance plays a critical role
for the commercial application. High permeance can reduce the area needed, making the reactor
more compact and cheaper. Reducing membrane thickness by further optimizing membrane
synthesis conditions can further increase NH3 permeance. Furthermore, scalability and
reproducibility of membrane synthesis are vital to its commercialization. Demonstration of
membrane scalability to 80-100 cm length with >80% reproducibility should be conducted in
next step.
 Further studies on the interactions between membrane and catalyst, as well as mass and heat
transfer, flow dynamics, and overall behavior within the MR are essential to refine its design and
enable successful scaleup and commercialization. The interplay between catalyst activity and
membrane separation is particularly crucial in MRs. Future work will focus on matching the
catalyst’s activity profile with the membrane's permeation characteristics to fully leverage
reaction-shift benefits. This may involve optimizing catalyst placement (e.g., proximity to
membrane surface), adjusting packing density, or fine-tuning residence time to synchronize
reaction kinetics and separation dynamics. Computational tools such as Computational Fluid
Dynamics (CFD) analysis can be employed to support and accelerate these efforts. Based on
insights from these studies, the design of MRs can be further improved in order to maintain
favorable driving forces across the membrane, optimizing flow configurations, and controlling
temperature and pressure conditions to maximum catalyst activity and membrane separation
performance. To move toward commercialization, scaling up the MR from current lab-scale and
small prototype (100-200 g/day NH3) to a large prototype (10- 20kg/day NH3) is necessary. This
scale-up will help identify and address challenges related to thermal gradients, sealing, and
membrane module integration, and allow for validation of the MR performance at a practical
production scale.
