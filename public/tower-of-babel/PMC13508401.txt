Area-level socioeconomic status and children’s body weight: examining the role of unhealthy food environments
Authors: Luis Carmona-Rosado, Julia Díez, Luis Cereijo, José Manuel Díaz-Olalla, Luisa N Borrell, Manuel Franco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocioeconomic status (SES) is associated with inequities in children’s body weight. Yet little is known about whether features of the urban food environment modify this association. We assessed whether and to what extent the availability of unhealthy food retailers modifies the association between area-level SES and weight status among children, overall and by sex.MethodsWe conducted an ecological study using individual data from a city-wide representative survey of 3505 children, aged 3 to 12 years living in Madrid, Spain. We measured area-level SES at the census-section level using a composite measure and categorized it into tertiles, with T3 representing the highest area-level SES. Our outcome variables were overweight and obesity, measured using anthropometric data on height and weight. As a potential effect modifier, we examined the availability of unhealthy food retailers around children’s households. Using Geographic Information Systems, we calculated 400-m street-network buffers and categorized unhealthy retailers’ availability into tertiles. We used Poisson regression models with robust standard errors to estimate prevalence ratios (PR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusting for age, sex, and population density.ResultsOverall, the prevalence estimates for overweight and obesity were 26.2% and 16.6%, respectively. Compared with children living in high-SES areas (T3), those living in the lowest-SES areas (T1) showed a higher prevalence of overweight (PR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.51) and obesity (PR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.75, 3.03). Among children with the lowest exposure to unhealthy food retailers, overweight prevalence was higher for children in low-SES areas than in high-SES areas (PR = 1.44; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.96). For obesity, the prevalence was also higher among children living in low- (T1; PR = 3.96, 95% CI: 2.36, 6.65) and medium-SES areas (T2; PR = 2.40; 95% CI: 1.42, 4.07) relative to those living in high-SES areas (T3). We found a statistically significant interaction between area-level SES and unhealthy food retailer availability for obesity on both additive and multiplicative scales.ConclusionOur findings suggest that the urban food environment modifies the association between area-level SES and childhood obesity, whereas the evidence was less consistent for overweight. The persistence of higher obesity prevalence in lower-SES areas, even where exposure to unhealthy food retailers was lower, indicates that food environment interventions may not be sufficient to reduce socioeconomic inequities unless complemented by other structural approaches that address social disadvantage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01351-5.Keywords: Childhood obesity, Overweight, Socioeconomic inequalities, Urban food environment, Urban health

Background

Worldwide, the prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity has increased over the past few decades [1]. In Spain, the prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity has remained stable at approximately 40% over the last decade, placing the country among those with the highest estimates in Europe [2]. The magnitude of this burden has significant implications for both population health and economic systems [1].

Socioeconomic status (SES) in childhood is also a major determinant of obesity and related health inequities [3–5]. Evidence from different European countries shows that low SES is associated with a higher prevalence of both overweight and obesity in children [5–8]. Data from Spain also suggest that these patterns have persisted over the years [9].

Consistent with existing theoretical frameworks, childhood obesity is shaped by determinants operating at multiple levels [10–12]. Among these, the retail food environment is particularly relevant, given its association with diet quality [13–15]. Indeed, evidence suggests that the availability of and access to unhealthy food are key obesogenic influences in youth [16]. Previous studies have shown that children living in unhealthier food environments (e.g., those with a greater density of fast-food outlets or convenience stores) tend to have a higher weight status than their counterparts living in healthier areas [17–19]. These obesogenic determinants are also socially patterned, with children from low-SES backgrounds often having greater access to unhealthy foods or beverages than their more socioeconomically advantaged peers [15, 17, 20–22]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that these inequities differ by sex/gender. For instance, increased availability of convenience stores and supermarkets has been associated with higher weight status in girls than in boys [23, 24]. Kranjac et al. also showed that the influence of neighborhood-level SES on obesity was greater in girls than in boys [25].

In addition to their independent association with obesity, built and social environmental characteristics are also related to one another [26]. Previous research has shown that unhealthy food outlets are more concentrated in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas [20, 27–29]. However, few studies have assessed the role of the unhealthy food environment in the association between area-level SES and childhood obesity. In New Zealand, Egli et al. found an association between neighborhood-level SES and weight status, but no association between unhealthy food outlets and dietary behaviors [30]. In England, Cetateanu et al. reported that unhealthy food environments explained only 1% to 2% of the association between area-level socioeconomic disadvantage and weight status among children aged 10 to 11 years [31]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has assessed whether the food environment modifies the association between SES and weight status in childhood. Therefore, we examined whether area-level SES was associated with childhood weight status and whether this association was modified by an unhealthy urban food environment, overall and by sex.

Methods

Study design and setting

This ecological study used data from a city-wide representative survey and publicly available secondary databases. Individual-level data were obtained from the ‘Encuesta sobre la situación nutricional de la población infantil en la Ciudad de Madrid’ (Survey on the nutritional status of children in the city of Madrid), a survey of children aged 3 to 12 years living in Madrid in 2017 [32]. We derived environmental data for the same year from previously validated administrative sources [33].

This study covered the entire city of Madrid, the largest city in Spain and one of the most socio-spatially segregated cities in Europe [34]. In 2017, the city had 3,165,541 residents, including 268,682 children aged 3 to 12 years. Administratively, Madrid is divided into 21 districts, 128 neighborhoods, and 2,443 census sections. The latter are the smallest geographical units for which population data are available, with an average population of 1,323 residents. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement (see Supplementary Materials) [35].

Participants

The survey recruited 5,961 children aged 3 to 12 years using a multistage cluster sampling design. The sampling units at each stage were schools, classrooms, and students. Schools were selected using proportional probability sampling. Of the 84 schools invited to participate, 60 agreed to do so (response rate: 71.4%). Classrooms were selected using simple random sampling. Finally, children from the selected classrooms were invited to participate. Further details on the sampling strategy and data collection procedures are available elsewhere [32].

From the original study population, we included children with recorded home addresses (n = 3,989). We then excluded those with missing data on weight or height (n = 484), yielding a final analytical sample of 3,505 children. In the supplementary materials, we depict the participant flowchart and show that the analytical sample did not differ from the full survey sample.

Outcome: overweight and obesity

Overweight and obesity were assessed using objectively measured height (cm) and weight (kg), collected by trained personnel using validated instruments and following a standardized written protocol. For each child, we calculated the age- and sex-specific body mass index (BMI) z-score according to the World Health Organization (WHO) growth reference curves to define weight status [36]. Consistent with current definitions, we classified children as overweight if their BMI was more than +1 standard deviation (SD) above the mean and as having obesity if their BMI was more than +2 SD above the mean [37]. We operationalized both overweight and obesity as binary variables (yes/no).

Exposure: area-level socioeconomic status (aSES)

Our independent variable was area-level socioeconomic status (aSES). Consistent with a previous systematic review [38], we used area-level SES (aSES) as an indicator of socioeconomic conditions [39] based on a composite index previously developed and applied in Madrid [40]. This index captures the economic and social conditions of residents and households and comprises seven indicators: (1) percentage of workers in manual or unqualified occupations, (2) percentage of part-time workers, (3) percentage of workers in temporary jobs, (4) average housing price per square meter, (5) percentage of people aged over 25 years with primary education or less, (6) percentage of people aged over 25 years with university education or higher, and (7) percentage of registered unemployed individuals among those aged 16 to 64 years. All data were obtained from secondary databases for 2017. Further details on the indicators and data sources are provided in the supplementary materials and elsewhere [40, 41]. The index was constructed at the census section level and linked to each child based on the geolocation of the entrance to their residential building to capture contextual influences.

Based on the data distribution of the index, we categorized aSES into tertiles, with the lowest tertile (T1) indicating children living in census sections with the greatest socioeconomic disadvantage [42].

Effect modifier: unhealthy food environment

Based on previous research, we proxied the unhealthiness of the food environment using the availability of unhealthy food outlets [22]. Unhealthy food outlets included fast-food outlets, convenience stores, bakeries, ice cream shops, kiosks, churro shops, and supermarkets. We selected these outlet types because they have been previously identified in the scientific literature as components of obesogenic environments, given their association with unhealthy eating patterns [43] and a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity [18, 19, 44]. After processing the food outlet data, we identified 6,625 unhealthy food outlets in the study area.

Using Geographic Information Systems (GIS), we first geocoded the address of each unhealthy food outlet. We then quantified the number of unhealthy outlets within a 400-m street-network buffer around each child’s home address (see Supplementary Materials). We selected a 400-m buffer because it approximates a 5-minute walk [45]. We operationalized this variable into tertiles of low, medium, and high unhealthy outlet availability. T1 (0 to 9 outlets) represented the lowest availability of unhealthy food outlets, T2 (10 to 23 outlets) represented intermediate availability, and T3 (24 to 85 outlets) represented the highest availability.

We collected food outlet data for 2017 from a secondary database curated by Madrid City Council. This database is routinely maintained for statistical, licensing, and inspection purposes and has been validated for research use [33].

Covariates

As covariates, we included age (in years), calculated from the date of birth reported in the survey. We also included reported sex (girls/boys) because gender identity was not collected in the survey. Both variables were reported by the children’s caregivers. We also included population density (population/km2) at the census section level, obtained from the municipal population register of Madrid City Council for 2017.

Statistical analyses

We first conducted survey-weighted descriptive analyses of the study population, overall and by area-level socioeconomic status (aSES). Second, we used Poisson regression models with robust variance to calculate the association between aSES (low, medium, and high) and overweight and obesity. We used Poisson regression rather than log-binomial regression because it yields unbiased prevalence ratio estimates when the outcome prevalence is common (e.g., >10%) and avoids convergence issues [46, 47]. We fitted both crude and adjusted models, controlling for age, sex, and population density. Results are presented as prevalence ratios (PRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs).

To examine the potential effect modification by the unhealthy food environment, we tested an interaction term between aSES and unhealthy food availability in the model adjusted for age, sex, and population density. In addition, we examined interaction on both additive and multiplicative scales. For this analysis, we dichotomized aSES and unhealthy food outlet availability into high and low categories. For the additive scale, we calculated the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and its confidence interval using the Delta method, whereas for the multiplicative scale, we estimated the ratio of prevalence ratios [48].

We performed all statistical analyses using R software (version 4.3.2), and all geographical analyses using QGIS software (version 3.28.12). We used the survey package to account for sampling weights [49] and the interactionR package to estimate the measures required for effect modification analysis [42].

Results

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the study population, overall and by aSES. Overall, children had a mean age of 7.3 years, and almost half were girls (49.1%). The prevalence estimates for overweight and obesity were 26.2% and 16.6%, respectively. Children were exposed to a median of 13 unhealthy food outlets in their residential environment (interquartile range [IQR]: 6, 28). Compared with children living in high-SES areas (10.1%), those living in low-SES areas had a higher prevalence of obesity (26.0%). They were also exposed to a greater availability of unhealthy food outlets than children living in more advantaged areas.

We observed that aSES was associated with obesity and overweight before and after adjustment for age, sex, and population density. However, these associations, although stronger for obesity, were attenuated after adjustment. Compared with children living in high-SES areas, those living in low- and medium-SES areas had a 31% (PR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.51) and 16% (PR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.35) higher prevalence of overweight, respectively (Table 2). For obesity, these estimates were 2.30 (95% CI: 1.75, 3.03) for low and 1.58 (95% CI: 1.18, 2.12) for medium-SES areas relative to those living in high-SES areas. We stratified this association by sex for overweight (p for interaction = 0.02) and obesity (p for interaction = 0.75; Table 2). Among boys, we found statistically significant associations for both overweight (low-aSES: PR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.22, 1.77; medium-aSES: PR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.11, 1.65) and obesity (low-aSES: PR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.77, 3.54; medium-aSES: PR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.19, 2.53). Among girls, we only observed a statistically significant association for obesity in those living in low-SES areas (PR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.39, 3.21).

Figure 1 shows the association between aSES and overweight and obesity according to the availability of unhealthy food outlets. We observed statistically significant interactions between aSES and the unhealthy food environment for both overweight and obesity (p for interaction = 0.03 and p < 0.001, respectively). Among children with the lowest exposure to an unhealthy food environment, the prevalence of overweight was higher among those living in low-SES areas than among those living in high-SES areas (PR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.92), whereas the association for medium-SES areas was weaker and not statistically significant (PR = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.85, 1.54). The prevalence of obesity was higher among those living in low-SES (PR = 3.65, 95% CI: 2.16, 6.17) and medium-SES areas (PR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.31, 3.83) than among those living in high-SES areas. Overall, the associations between lower aSES and obesity were stronger than those observed for overweight.

We also observed statistically significant associations for children living in food environments with medium availability of unhealthy food outlets (Fig. 1). Compared with children living in high-SES areas, we found a higher prevalence of overweight (PR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.09, 1.93) among those living in low-SES areas. For obesity, PRs were greater among children living in low- (PR = 3.36, 95% CI: 2.00, 5.62) and medium-SES areas (PR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.21, 3.53) than among those living in high-SES areas. For children with the highest exposure to unhealthy food outlets, we found no statistically significant association with overweight or obesity.

Table 3 shows the prevalence ratios of overweight for each combination of aSES and the availability of unhealthy food outlets. We found that children living in areas with low availability of unhealthy food outlets and low SES had a prevalence ratio of 1.31 (95% CI: 1.14, 1.50) relative to those living in areas with low availability of unhealthy food outlets and the highest aSES. We also observed similar results for children living in areas with high availability of unhealthy food outlets and high SES (PR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.12, 1.69) and those living in areas with high availability of unhealthy food outlets and low SES (PR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.33, 1.86). We did not find evidence of effect modification on either the additive scale (RERI = -0.11, 95% CI: -0.41, 0.19) or the multiplicative scale (PR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.70, 1.10).

Discussion

In this study, approximately one in four children were living with overweight, and one in six with obesity. Compared with children living in high-SES areas, those living in more disadvantaged areas had a 31% higher prevalence of overweight and a 130% higher prevalence of obesity. Moreover, we found that the unhealthy food environment modified the association between area-level SES and both overweight and obesity. In less unhealthy food environments, the prevalence of overweight was 41% higher among children living in low-SES areas than among those living in high-SES areas.

The prevalence of obesity was also substantially higher among children living in low- and medium-SES areas than among those living in high-SES areas. Moreover, we found a statistically significant interaction on both the additive and multiplicative scales for obesity, indicating that the combined effect of low area-level SES and an unhealthy food environment was lower than expected based on their individual effects. As such, unhealthy food environments appeared to show a relatively stronger association in higher-SES areas, thereby attenuating area-level socioeconomic inequalities in obesity. In contrast, we found no association between area-level SES and the prevalence of overweight or obesity in the unhealthiest food environments.

Our findings on the association of area-level SES with childhood overweight and obesity are consistent with those of previous studies [38, 50]. Despite differences in how area-level SES has been measured, most studies report a higher prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity in more disadvantaged areas than in more socioeconomically advantaged areas. Although international evidence suggests a plateau in the overall prevalence of childhood obesity [51, 52], our findings underscore the continued presence of socioeconomic inequities, supporting previous studies highlighting this issue in Spain [2, 9].

Regarding the negative interaction observed on both the additive and multiplicative scales for obesity, this finding was contrary to our expectation for the combination of an unhealthier food environment with lower area-level SES; however, this pattern is consistent with previous studies. For example, a cohort study conducted in the Netherlands assessed whether the availability of fast-food outlets moderated the association between area-level SES and body mass index in adults [53]. Similar to our findings, they reported a weaker association between area-level SES and BMI among those with greater exposure to fast-food outlets. This pattern may be explained by two complementary mechanisms. First, in high-SES areas, even small absolute increases in obesity may produce relatively large prevalence ratios because baseline prevalence estimates are lower. Second, in low-SES areas, the prevalence of obesity may approach a saturation point, such that additional exposure to unhealthy food outlets does not substantially increase obesity prevalence. This is consistent with the findings of previous research conducted among adult populations, where the association between unhealthy food outlets and BMI was attenuated in high-poverty areas [54].

Our findings also suggest that obesity prevention strategies should not be the same across urban settings but should be adapted to the local context [55]. Mediterranean cities often have distinctive retail food environments characterized by greater compactness and higher retail density. This urban compactness may help explain the high exposure to unhealthy food observed in our study, even in high-SES areas. Indeed, previous studies have also reported higher food store densities in Spain than in the UK [56] or the US [57]. This saturation may help explain why unhealthy food outlets appear to have their strongest relative effect in higher-SES neighborhoods, where the baseline obesity prevalence is lower. This differential pattern was captured in the significant interaction observed on both the additive and multiplicative scales for obesity.

Our findings have several implications for public health practice. First, in compact cities, an unhealthy food environment may not amplify area-level socioeconomic inequalities in obesity but may attenuate them instead. These findings suggest that regulating unhealthy food outlets alone is unlikely to be sufficient to reduce health inequalities. While ‘zoning laws’ to restrict unhealthy food retail establishments may reduce obesity prevalence overall [58], our findings suggest that such interventions may have unequal effects across neighborhoods. Second, the observed interaction indicates that tackling obesity in low-SES areas requires more than food environment interventions alone. The limited additional risk associated with unhealthy food outlet availability in these areas suggests that obesity prevalence may already be shaped by multiple co-occurring disadvantages. Therefore, effective strategies should simultaneously address both structural and intermediate socioeconomic determinants of health. Such an integrated approach is essential to ensure that obesity prevention efforts reduce, rather than widen, socioeconomic inequalities.

This study had several limitations. First, we only considered the retail food environment around participants’ residential locations. Second, we were unable to account for other potentially influential factors (e.g., online delivery). However, we used a previously validated official City Council database that covers the entire physical food environment of Madrid. Third, we measured SES using a composite index at the census section level, while we defined the unhealthy food environment using a 400-m buffer around each child’s home. Consequently, the spatial boundaries of these two measures did not fully overlap. Although census-section SES provided the most appropriate available indicator of neighborhood socioeconomic conditions, incorporating food environment information from adjacent census sections may have introduced some misclassification, particularly for households located near administrative boundaries. Moreover, this study used area-level SES to capture the influence of context. Although we acknowledge the possible individual variability within each area, we did not include household socioeconomic status because our interest was in area-level SES, given that the unhealthy environment variable was at the area level. Finally, this study relied on data from 2017. However, evidence on unhealthy food environments and health outcomes remains limited in the Spanish context, and our analytical approach, examining the food environment as an effect modifier of the association between area-level socioeconomic status and childhood weight status, is novel. Despite these limitations, this study has important strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the modifying effect of an unhealthy food environment on the association between SES and overweight/obesity among children. Second, we examined these associations in a city-wide representative sample of school-aged children. Third, weight status was measured objectively by qualified personnel using validated instruments. Fourth, we used street-network buffers, which better approximate children’s potential exposure to unhealthy food retailers. Fifth, we assessed unhealthy food environments using GIS to generate individualized neighborhood exposure measures based on each participant’s residential location.

Conclusions

We observed a strong association between area-level SES and the prevalence of overweight and obesity in a representative sample of children aged 3 to 12 years in Madrid. This association varied according to residential exposure to unhealthy food outlets. Among children living in areas with lower exposure to unhealthy food outlets, the association between area-level SES and obesity was stronger than among those living in areas with higher exposure. We also observed that unhealthy food availability significantly modified the association between area-level SES and obesity. These findings suggest that unhealthy food environments may have a greater relative effect in higher-SES areas while contributing less to obesity prevalence in more socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, where other co-occurring disadvantages may play a larger role. Our findings improve understanding of how social inequities and the unhealthy food environment jointly shape childhood overweight and obesity. These findings can help inform future research and practice by suggesting that food environment interventions alone may be insufficient to reduce socioeconomic inequalities in childhood obesity.
