Introduction

How will the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) affect pharmaceutical innovation? Projections of the IRA's impact on new drug development vary enormously, and much of this variation traces to a single parameter: the elasticity of innovation with respect to market size. Using its structural simulation model, the Congressional Budget Office projected that the IRA's price negotiation provisions would result in roughly 13 fewer new drugs over 30 years out of approximately 1300 expected, implying an effective elasticity of roughly 0.2.1,2 Conversely, an analysis of a precursor bill with similar pricing provisions by Philipson and Durie, which applied an elasticity of approximately 1.5 derived from averaging across prior studies, projected 135 fewer new drugs over a comparable horizon.3 This roughly 10-fold difference in the projected loss of new drugs, from essentially the same policy, is driven almost entirely by the choice of elasticity.

The direction of this relationship is well established. Economic theory and a growing body of empirical evidence confirm that reducing pharmaceutical revenue can slow research and development (R&D) investment and ultimately lead to fewer new drugs.4-8 But the magnitude remains uncertain, with estimates ranging from as low as 0.23 to as high as 4.4,7 The sources of this variation are numerous: studies differ in identification strategy, outcome variable, and independent variable.

Two of these sources are both first-order in magnitude and routinely conflated in policy discussions: whether innovation outcomes are counted as US-launched or globally-launched drugs, and whether market size is measured with US-only or global revenue. Indeed, prior work has speculated that higher US prices should imply a larger elasticity with respect to US revenue, though this conjecture remains untested and theoretically ambiguous.9 Similarly, studies looking only at US drug launches (instead of global launches) may find different results because those 2 margins are not the same.

Crucially, this means that policy evaluations should not uncritically rely on “off-the-shelf” estimates. The appropriate elasticity depends on at least 2 dimensions: the policy being evaluated and the margin of interest. Evaluating how a US price reform affects US-launched drugs calls for a different estimate than evaluating how a global coverage expansion affects global drug development. In addition, the type of policy reform itself matters: price reductions translate dollar-for-dollar into profit reductions, whereas market expansions (such as broadening insurance coverage) increase profits only on the net margin on each additional unit sold.

In this article, we bridge this gap between economic theory and empirical measurement by demonstrating how and why policy counterfactuals require specific, tailored elasticity estimates. We contribute to the literature in 2 ways. First, we present a conceptual framework that cleanly disentangles the global R&D development decision (innovation) from the local market entry decision (access). Existing frameworks often conflate the invention of a new molecule with its availability in a given country; our model separates these margins explicitly, illustrating why different ways of measuring innovation and market size yield entirely different elasticity estimates.

Second, we follow the approach of Dubois et al. as a transparent vehicle for demonstrating these distinctions empirically.7 Rather than producing a new headline estimate—or attempting to reconcile the full range of published values—we hold the estimator fixed and systematically vary how innovation and market size are measured to match the mechanisms in our framework. This lets us assess, within a single dataset and a single method, how much of the variation in published elasticities is attributable to measurement choices rather than to differences in research design or data.

Data and methods

Analytic framework

We begin by presenting a conceptual framework for the margins that determine whether a new molecule is brought to market. Consider a global pharmaceutical industry facing a menu of potential R&D projects. Each project is characterized by: (1) its ex-ante expected revenue, which in turn may depend on its clinical or commercial baseline value; and (2) its cost of development, capturing both the financial cost of running the necessary trials and the probability that the project will not succeed.

Successful R&D projects become drugs that can be sold (for simplicity) in 2 markets: the United States and the Rest of World (ROW). United States and ROW differ in their patient population size, price level, fixed cost of entering the market (ie, the requirements to obtain marketing approval), and cost of selling once the drug has marketing approval. Products will be developed only if expected global profits exceed development costs, and launched in a given market only if the local operating profits cover the fixed costs of entry.

Historically, the US market features both high potential rewards, in the form of high prices, and high entry hurdles, in the form of stringent FDA trial requirements (true on average, despite expedited routes available for select high-priority drugs) and large marketing costs to navigate a fragmented healthcare system. Conversely, sub-segments of the ROW market may offer lower rewards, but also lower regulatory and commercial barriers to entry. As a result, we expect that some drugs with low expected revenue and low cost of development may be developed only for the ROW market, while only products with sufficiently high expected revenue will seek approval in the US market. This setup highlights why a single, uniform measure of “innovation elasticity” is insufficient for policy analysis. In particular, the model yields 2 margins that analysts must account for when estimating policy-appropriate elasticities, plus a third caveat about the scope of applicability of any elasticity derived from realized revenue variation.

Global innovation is not the same as US availability. Not all drugs launch in all countries.10,11 In our comprehensive sample, more than half of all molecules are never available in the United States (see Summary Statistics). In the language of the model, these are products with sufficiently low cost of development to justify being developed, but with expected US revenue too low to cover the cost of US market entry. If the ROW market expands, it may fund additional low-cost, marginal drugs, increasing the number of drugs available globally, but not necessarily those available in the United States. Conversely, expanding the US market delivers a dual effect: it increases global profits, funding new R&D, and lowers the effective US entry hurdle, potentially drawing in existing ROW-only drugs.

The source of revenue matters but the relative magnitude of the US-vs-ROW elasticity is theoretically ambiguous. Revenue from different sources may have different impacts on R&D incentives. The conventional view is that higher US prices imply higher per-unit profit margins, so that a dollar of US revenue should have a larger effect on innovation than a dollar of ROW revenue; a recent synthesis explicitly advances this conjecture and notes that it has not been tested.9 However, there are reasons to believe that the cost of selling drugs in the United States is also substantially higher. The United States is 1 of only 2 OECD countries permitting broadcast direct-to-consumer advertising (DTCA) of prescription drugs—a $9.6 billion channel in 2016 alone—and maintains roughly twice the pharmaceutical sales-force intensity per physician as the 5 largest European markets combined.12-14 Conversely, the EU explicitly prohibits DTCA.15 Moreover, the US payer system involves intermediaries—wholesalers, pharmacy benefit managers, pharmacies, and insurers—estimated to capture roughly 40% of each retail drug dollar.16,17 Most other developed markets, by contrast, use centralized price setting with regulated distribution margins, substantially reducing these costs.18 The net implication for the relative impact of United States vs ROW revenue on innovation is therefore theoretically ambiguous.

Scope caveat: prices vs volumes. Any elasticity estimated from observed revenue variation must also be interpreted with care when applied to price-control policies. This is not a margin we can vary within our data, but a boundary on what such an elasticity can directly answer. Specifically, we must distinguish between policies that alter prices and those that alter patient volume. When market size growth comes from volume (eg, Medicaid expansion), the resulting increase in profits depends on the size of the expansion times the marginal profit per unit. This marginal profit may be low for populations like Medicaid enrollees, who receive drugs at relatively lower prices. Consistent with this logic, Garthwaite, Sachs, and Stern find that Medicaid expansions—which increased patient volume but at low reimbursement rates—did not meaningfully increase pharmaceutical R&D investment.8 Conversely, a policy that implements a price control, like the IRA, or the more recently proposed GENEROUS, GLOBE, and GUARD models, shifts profits one-to-one, because each dollar of revenue lost is exactly one dollar of profit lost.19,20 Price controls may therefore have more uniform effects across markets, since a given revenue reduction from European price controls shrinks the global R&D budget by the same amount as an equivalent reduction from US price negotiations (net of tax considerations).

The empirical analysis that follows varies the first 2 margins across 3 nested drug samples and 2 measures of market size. The third item is not something we can vary within our data; we return to it in the Discussion, where we describe how a volume-derived elasticity should be rescaled before being applied to a price-control policy.

Data

Our main source of data is the MIDAS database maintained by IQVIA—a quarterly-level panel reporting sales of all drugs in a large set of countries. Our version of the database includes information for all branded drugs marketed at any point between 1998q1 and 2023q4, and breaks down sales in the United States and outside the United States (henceforth, ex-US), closely matching our theoretical framework. Appendix A lists all countries included in the ex-US bucket. Sales in each country are reported in nominal dollars using the end-of-quarter exchange rate (we adjust nominal dollars to 2023 dollars using US CPI data from the St. Louis Federal Reserve).21 The data also reports the initial launch date and loss-of-exclusivity (LOE) date for all products; for ex-US observations, these dates refer to the earliest launch and LOE date, respectively. Drugs are defined by a molecule, product name, aggregated form (oral, injectable, other), and the 4-digit Anatomical Therapeutical Chemical code (ATC-4). The ATC is a WHO classification that divides drugs into a five-level hierarchy; the first level (ATC-1) identifies the broad anatomical or therapeutic group (eg, “L” for antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents). We aggregate the data at the molecule level, selecting the earliest available launch and LOE date, and the ATC-4 with the most sales.

We combine this data with information on the availability of drugs in 8 additional markets (Australia, Brazil, Canada, the EU, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, and the United Kingdom), representing some of the largest markets in the world outside the United States. This set also closely matches the set of countries used in Dubois et al.7 allowing for the cleanest comparison of our results to theirs. Relative to Dubois et al.7 we do not have data on drug availability in China and Turkey, because it was not easily accessible through publicly available sources. Data on availability in these 8 markets comes from publicly available drug lists from regulatory authorities and aggregator websites (see Appendix B).

Empirical approach

Our main analysis consists of a period-class-level Poisson regression of number of molecules on log market size. We define periods to be 4-year intervals starting in 1980, and classes at the level of an ATC-1. Since there are 13 ATC-1s and our data covers 1980-2023, we have a total of 143 observations. Standard errors are clustered at the ATC-1 class level to allow for arbitrary within-class correlation across periods. We also report specifications that omit one or both sets of fixed effects.

While the specification with both period and ATC-1 fixed effects is our preferred estimate, we also report specifications that drop the class fixed effects and therefore identify the elasticity from variation across therapeutic classes—the same variation used by cross-sectional and instrumental-variables studies such as Acemoglu and Linn (2004) and Lichtenberg (2007). We report these alongside our preferred specification to confirm that the differences across our measurement choices persist across identifying-variation strategies.

We calculate market size as the 20-year present-discounted value of total revenue across all drugs launched in a given period and ATC-1. Since we do not observe revenue before 1998 or after 2023, we impute it by estimating age-revenue curves from the observed data; Appendix C provides further details. We use 5% as the discount rate, following Dubois et al.7 Notice that this approach implies that market size is mechanically zero if no products are launched. To capture the revenue for hypothetical launches in a given class and period, we impute market size as a linear interpolation of market size in the periods before and after any such gap.

Sample and variable definition

To show how estimated elasticities vary depending on how key variables are defined, we present results using 3 different samples of drugs and 2 different measures of market size. The 3 samples are: (1) all drugs launched in any country included in the MIDAS data, (2) all drugs launched in the United States, or in any of the 8 markets listed in the data section, and (3) all drugs launched in the United States. The 2 measures of market size are based on global drug revenue and US drug revenue. In each of the 6 specifications, we construct both the dependent variable (number of molecules launched) and the explanatory variable (market size) using only the drugs in that sample.

Results

Summary statistics

Our final sample includes 2965 total molecules launched between 1980 and 2023. Of these, 1402 are available in the United States, 973 are not available in the United States but are available in at least one of the 8 other largest markets for which we have data, and 590 are available only outside of these top markets.

Figure 2, Panel A shows molecule counts by ATC-1 category, stratified by launch geography. The largest therapeutic classes by molecule count are antineoplastics, cardiovascular, and alimentary tract and metabolism (which includes diabetes drugs). The smallest are antiparasitic products—reflecting their orientation toward developing-country diseases—and systemic hormones, a narrow category that excludes sex hormones, insulins, and hormone products used in oncology. US penetration varies substantially across classes: most antineoplastics, blood and blood-forming organs, and systemic hormones are available in the United States. Conversely, respiratory, cardiovascular, and dermatological classes have notably lower US penetration, consistent with many molecules in those areas not generating sufficient expected revenue to justify the cost of US market entry.

Figure 2, Panel B plots average annual revenue per molecule by years since launch, stratified by launch tier, and shows 3 things at once. First, revenues follow the classic age profile familiar from the pharmaceutical literature: they ramp up over roughly a decade as adoption grows, plateau, and then decline over time as newer therapies become available and IP protections expire. Second, the 3 tiers are enormously far apart in level: US-launched drugs peak at roughly $1.35B per year, drugs launched only in the Top-8 peak below $0.1B, and drugs launched only outside the United States and Top-8 are essentially flat at the bottom of the chart. Hence, the higher revenue of US-launched drugs is not simply driven by access to the US market: even abroad, these drugs earn far more, consistent with the framework's prediction that only the highest-revenue drugs clear the US entry hurdle. These same age profiles are also the object we use to impute revenue outside the 1998-2023 window (see Appendix C).

Three patterns emerge from the results. First, and contrary to a widely held conjecture in the literature, we do not find evidence that molecule launches are differentially responsive to US revenue relative to global revenue. As discussed above, this prediction is theoretically ambiguous once the higher US costs of selling are accounted for. Our regressions provide the first within-dataset test of this conjecture: within each sample, the elasticities estimated using US-only market size are statistically indistinguishable from those using global market size. This null is consistent with the view that higher US costs offset much of the gross-margin advantage implied by higher US list prices. Regardless of mechanism, the finding suggests that analysts should not assume that shocks to US revenue translate into larger innovation effects than equivalent shocks to global revenue.

Second, expanding the sample to drugs launched anywhere in the world yields lower elasticities. Under our preferred specification (column 4), the elasticity for the global sample is roughly 0.21, against 0.26 for drugs launched in the United States or one of the 8 largest ex-US markets—a difference that is statistically significant under both revenue measures (Table 2)—and 0.28-0.30 for drugs launched in the United States, higher still but estimated less precisely. This pattern is consistent with the conceptual framework: because barriers to entry outside the United States are lower, many ROW-only drugs require relatively little revenue to justify development. These marginal drugs are less sensitive to revenue incentives, pulling down the elasticity in the broader sample. Drugs that clear the higher US entry hurdle, by contrast, are more responsive to market size because they are drawn from the upper part of the expected-revenue distribution, where revenue considerations are most binding.

Third, estimated elasticities decline substantially when ATC-1 fixed effects are included. Adding class fixed effects roughly halves the estimates in the all-drugs sample and reduces them by about one-third for US-launched drugs, while period fixed effects have comparatively little impact. This implies that a substantial share of the cross-sectional correlation between market size and innovation reflects persistent differences across therapeutic areas—some classes consistently attract more R&D and generate more revenue over time. Whether this variation represents a genuine market-size response or reflects unobserved differences in scientific opportunity cannot be resolved within our design, though we conservatively treat column 4 as our preferred estimate.

These results raise a practical question: given the range of estimates across measurements and specifications, which elasticity should an analyst use for a given policy question? We address this in the Discussion, where we map the rows of Table 1 onto common policy scenarios.

Discussion

The central takeaway of this paper is that the elasticity of pharmaceutical innovation with respect to market size varies based on how the 2 quantities are measured, in a way consistent with basic economic theory. Across 6 combinations of sample and revenue measure, our estimated elasticities range from 0.21 to 0.30—a spread that can translate into large quantitative differences in policy projections.

For analysts evaluating a specific policy, 2 decisions determine which estimate applies. First, what is the outcome of interest: the effect of the policy on drugs available to US patients, or the effect on the global portfolio of R&D activity? If the former, the relevant sample is US-launched drugs, and the relevant elasticity is correspondingly higher. If the latter, the global sample and its lower elasticity are more appropriate. Second, does the policy operate through prices or through volumes? Our estimates rely on revenue variation that more closely resembles volume-driven shifts in profit than one-for-one price changes. As discussed in the analytic framework, volume expansions increase profits only to the extent of the variable-profit share on marginal units, whereas price changes flow through to profits one-for-one. It follows that, when applied to a price-based reform, a volume-based elasticity should be scaled up by the inverse of the variable-profit share.

For concreteness, we illustrate this adjustment using a figure reported by Dubois et al.7 who cite industry consultations suggesting that variable costs account for roughly 50% of drug revenue, implying a variable-profit share of approximately 50%.7 We use it for illustration only; it should be replaced with a better-grounded estimate when available. Under this illustrative 50% figure, the 0.21-0.30 range we found across the 6 sample and revenue combinations would translate into a corresponding range of roughly 0.42-0.60. As an example, a US price reform like the IRA's Medicare negotiation, evaluated for its effect on global innovation would use the all-drugs elasticity on US revenue (0.22, scaled to 0.44). Table 3 applies this logic to several policy scenarios (we note that the point of the exercise is not to endorse these specific numbers, but to make explicit the mechanical adjustment that any volume-derived elasticity requires when applied to a price-control policy).

A secondary finding carries a direct implication for policy projections: because innovation responds no more strongly to US revenue than to global revenue, an analyst evaluating a policy that operates on US revenue need not apply a US-specific premium to the elasticity—contrary to a common assumption that higher US prices imply a larger US response. A dollar of US revenue and a dollar of global revenue carry similar weight for innovation.

Reconciling our estimates with the broader literature

Our preferred estimate is close to the most comparable prior study (Dubois et al., 2015), which uses a similar (but shorter) panel and within-class strategy, finding an elasticity of 0.23 (our most comparable estimate is 0.26).7 A more recent analysis by Dubois (2025) revisits these questions on a comparable 1997-2021 panel, decomposing innovation's response into region-specific revenue elasticities for the United States, the EU, and the rest of the world.22 Its regional decomposition is complementary to our US-vs-global comparison, though differences in outcome definition and identification mean the point estimates are not directly comparable to ours. The wider dispersion of published elasticities—from near zero to roughly 4—reflects differences in research design that our study is not built to reconcile, such as the outcome measured (trials vs launches). One pattern, though, holds both in our table and in the literature: estimates identified from cross-class variation run higher. In our results, this shows up in columns 1-2 of Table 1; in the literature, cross-class studies such as Lichtenberg (2007) and Acemoglu and Linn (2004) report higher elasticities—roughly 0.5 and 3-4, respectively—against the within-class estimates of Dubois et al. (2015) and our own near 0.2-0.3.4,7,23

Limitations

Several limitations warrant discussion. Our estimates are observational, not experimental. Identification comes from cross-class variation in revenue over multi-year periods, and time-varying within-class confounders—such as shifts in scientific opportunity or disease burden—remain a concern. Market size itself is endogenous, since it is a function of which drugs were launched; we mitigate this concern by using a forward-looking, present-discounted revenue measure following Dubois et al.7 but cannot fully rule out reverse causality. Our estimates capture long-run equilibrium relationships and may not apply to the short-run dynamics of a specific policy change, given the long lead times inherent in pharmaceutical R&D. Most importantly, the scope of this paper is conceptual: we identify the measurement issues that separate valid from invalid applications of existing elasticities and illustrate their quantitative importance, but we do not attempt to resolve the deeper identification problem of distinguishing price-driven from volume-driven innovation responses. That problem remains an important open question for future work.

Conclusion

Our analysis yields a clear practical message: there is no single, universal “elasticity of innovation.” By providing a conceptual framework that helps interpret estimates under different measurement choices, and by demonstrating the quantitative importance of these choices, this paper offers analysts and policymakers a more precise toolkit for evaluating the innovation consequences of pharmaceutical pricing reforms.
