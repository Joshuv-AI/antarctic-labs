RESUMO: As cidades periféricas costumam abrigar universidades públicas das quais se espera
que promovam o desenvolvimento urbano; no entanto, os mecanismos que ligam as instituições
âncora à transformação territorial ainda são pouco compreendidos. Este estudo examina como
a mediação institucional molda o desenvolvimento urbano em contextos de capacidade estatal
limitada, utilizando a implantação de uma universidade federal na Amazônia brasileira como
um caso interpretativo com metodologia mista. Pesquisas domiciliares (n = 53), entrevistas
semiestruturadas, estatísticas descritivas e análise de conteúdo revelam falhas persistentes de
governança, déficits de infraestrutura e fraca coordenação institucional. Em vez de atuar
exclusivamente por meio de sua presença econômica, a universidade surge como uma
instituição âncora capaz de mediar as relações entre as comunidades e os governos locais. Os
resultados apresentam uma explicação institucional para o desenvolvimento urbano periférico,
destacando a capacidade de coordenação como um mecanismo fundamental que conecta as
instituições âncora à transformação territorial.

ABSTRACT: Peripheral cities often host public universities expected to promote urban
development, yet the mechanisms linking anchor institutions to territorial transformation
remain poorly understood. This study examines how institutional mediation shapes urban
development in contexts of limited state capacity, using the implementation of a federal
university in the Brazilian Amazon as an interpretive mixed-methods case. Household surveys
(n = 53), semi-structured interviews, descriptive statistics, and content analysis reveal
persistent governance failures, infrastructure deficits, and weak institutional coordination.
Rather than acting solely through economic presence, the university emerges as an anchor
institution capable of mediating relations between communities and local governments. The
findings advance an institutional explanation of peripheral urban development, highlighting
coordination capacity as a critical mechanism connecting anchor institutions to territorial
change.
Keywords: Urban Development. Community Perception. Public University. Institutional
Coordination. Peripheral Amazon.

INTRODUÇÃO
Urban development is significantly shaped by the presence of higher education
institutions, which reshape city structures by attracting skilled labor, intensifying housing
demand, and increasing the need for public services. The literature frequently links these
dynamics to an integrated model of urban development that aligns economic growth, social
inclusion, and environmental sustainability.
Recent scholarship, however, often assumes a straightforward, positive correlation
between university presence and urban outcomes, treating higher education institutions as
inherently transformative agents (Costa e Silva et al., 2024; Pinsky; Gomes; Kruglianskas,
2019). In peripheral contexts, universities more commonly function as “anchor institutions”,

organizations whose capacity to drive urban change depends less on their physical footprint
and more on their ability to weave institutional networks, generate territorial legitimacy, and
engage with local state capacities that are frequently constrained (Fongwa, 2021; Harris; Holley,
2016; Macedo; Monteiro Neto; Vieira, 2022; Queiroz; Garcia, 2018).
In the Amazon, infrastructural deficits, dispersed settlement patterns, and socioenvironmental conflicts constrain the applicability of generalized theoretical frameworks
(Costa; Silva, 2019; Santos, 2017; Sousa; Santos; Almeida, 2021b; Viana; Freitas; Giatti, 2016).
Within such settings, universities do not automatically catalyze urban development. In
peripheral territories marked by limited state capacity, infrastructural shortfalls, and
institutional fragmentation, their impact depends on the presence of territorial mediation
mechanisms that can connect communities, public authorities, and academic institutions.
Consequently, the urban footprint of universities emerges from relational processes,
institutional coordination, social legitimacy, and community engagement, rather than from
mere physical proximity.
To test this premise, the present study examines the establishment of the Federal
University of Western Pará (UFOPA) campus in Alenquer, Pará, a municipality grappling
with structural deficits in sanitation, transportation, and urban infrastructure. Under these
conditions, the university operates as a potential catalyst for socio-spatial transformation
(Araújo et al., 2023; Da Silva et al., 2023; Sousa; Santos; Almeida, 2021b). Understanding how
local residents perceive the institution’s role makes it possible to assess whether its activities
contribute to a more inclusive urban identity and foster civic engagement in territorial
transformation initiatives (Cullen, 2009; Ikeda, 2020; Jacobs, 1969, 1970; Lynch; Camargo, 1997).
This study investigates how anchor institutions interact with local governance
structures in peripheral settings, specifically analyzing the ways in which UFOPA acts, or
encounters limits in acting, as an institutional mediator of urban development in Alenquer. By
adopting this lens, the research advances our understanding of the mechanisms that connect
higher education institutions to their surrounding territories in peripheral contexts, while
offering practical insights for designing urban development strategies in regions of high socioenvironmental complexity.

2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
2.1 Urbanization, Urban Landscape, and Community Participation
Rapid urban expansion, coupled with deficits in territorial planning, creates
asymmetries in public service provision and local governance. This reality demands analytical
tools that bridge spatial configuration and institutional capacity. Drawing on the work of
(Cullen, 2009; Jacobs, 1970; Lynch; Camargo, 1997), this study operationalizes the relationship
between territorial perception, urban experience, and community mediation.
Lynch (1997) argues that citizens’ cognitive mapping of urban space rests on the
environment’s legibility and imageability, which are structured by paths, landmarks,
boundaries, and nodes. This study treats legibility as an analytical premise: when residents
struggle to read their territory spatially, it tends to correlate with perceptions of public
disinvestment and a weakening of place-based identity. Thus, residents’ descriptions of street
networks and the distribution of public facilities are treated as indicators of their cognitive
capacity to appropriate the neighborhood.
Complementing this, (Cullen, 2009) shifts the focus to sequential perceptual experience,
emphasizing how movement rhythms and visual continuity shape how space is lived.
Narratives about commuting, accessibility, and the experience of the campus surroundings are
therefore analyzed as indicators of experiential coherence and sociospatial integration. This
approach moves beyond mere physical description to capture how the formal quality of the
environment enables or constrains perceived inclusion.
(Jacobs, 1970) introduces the idea that effective urban planning depends on bottom-up
processes, and that the success of such processes requires shared responsibility among local
actors. From this perspective, community participation functions as institutional mediation:
when channeled through formal mechanisms, it translates diffuse demands into public agenda
priorities, thereby reducing information asymmetries between officials and residents
(Almeida; Carlos; Silva, 2016; Avritzer, 2011; Ikeda, 2020; Milani, 2008). Within this study,
residents’ knowledge of and engagement with participatory institutions serve as a central
mediating variable. Without community organization, the university’s potential to act as a
catalyst rarely translates into effective urban policy.

2.2 The Role of Universities in Urban Development
Embedding university campuses within urban territories produces ambiguous spatial
externalities. On one hand, an academic presence stimulates local markets, intensifies
knowledge circulation, and attracts infrastructure investment (Costa e Silva et al., 2024;
Macedo; Monteiro Neto; Vieira, 2022; Queiroz; Garcia, 2018). On the other, it can drive up real
estate markets and accelerate gentrification, necessitating compensatory governance
mechanisms (Costa E Silva et al., 2024). Since the 1960s, governments across various
institutional models have recognized universities as anchors of regional development,
particularly in historically underinvested areas (Silva, 2019). Over time, these institutions have
progressively taken on the role of territorial coordinators, aligning government, industry, and
communities around shared agendas (Peters, 2015; Souza, 2018a).
Scholarship on “anchor institutions” argues that universities play a strategic territorial
role, particularly in peripheral cities, where they frequently assume institutional coordination
functions that partially substitute for weak local state capacity (Fongwa, 2021; Harris; Holley,
2016). Under these conditions, their effectiveness hinges on their ability to generate social
legitimacy, coordinate local actors, and establish permanent channels of interaction between
communities and public authorities.
Yet this coordination is conditional. It depends on municipal institutional capacities
that are often deficient in peripheral contexts: strategic leadership, technical expertise within
municipal departments, integrated management instruments, and meaningful social
participation (Peters, 2015; Souza, 2018a). Without these mechanisms, universities tend to
operate as territorially disconnected entities, unable to convert academic capital into tangible
urban transformation. This risk is magnified in the Amazon, where territorial dispersion,
limited municipal technical capacity, and overlapping governmental jurisdictions hinder the
internalization of academic externalities.
In Alenquer, Pará, weak institutional capacity is well-documented in studies on
intersectoral health coordination (Da Silva et al., 2023), education (Silva et al., 2021), and waste
management and public sanitation (Garcia et al., 2021; Sousa; Santos; Almeida, 2021b). This
structural deficit limits the municipality’s ability to absorb the impacts of the university’s
presence. In this context, UFOPA is gradually redefining its third mission, taking on
interinstitutional coordination roles that fill local governance gaps and mediate between the

3. METHODOLOGY
This study adopts an interpretivist approach, supplemented by auxiliary mixed
methods. The primary aim of this research design is to understand the meanings people ascribe
to their experiences and actions within a specific social context (Creswell, 2010). To generate
these initial insights, we used an interview guide alongside semi-structured questionnaires.
Data collection took place across 53 households in the Independência neighborhood, a
developing peripheral area that houses the UFOPA campus in Alenquer, Pará. The inferences
drawn are directly valid for this specific context. While empirically bounded to Alenquer, the
findings are interpreted analytically as expressions of recurrent institutional mediation
mechanisms in peripheral cities characterized by low state capacity and uneven urbanization.
It should be noted, however, that these interpretations do not constitute statistical
generalizations, but rather situated theoretical approximations.
The municipality covers a total area of 23,645.452 km², with 13.61 km² of urbanized land
and approximately 74,836 inhabitants. As of 2022, only 5.49% of the population was formally
employed. Additionally, 18,355 families are registered in the Brazil’s Unified Social Registry, of
whom 13,809 live in poverty, highlighting persistent socioeconomic inequalities (IBGE, 2022).
The UFOPA campus surroundings encompass the Esperança and São Francisco
neighborhoods, which, together with Independência, make up Brazilian Institute of Geography
and Statistics (IBGE) census tract 150040405000051. This tract reflects a challenging reality: 353
families are registered in the social assistance system, 258 of whom live in extreme poverty,
underscoring the severity of local socioeconomic disparities. Given the high level of social
vulnerability, this condition demands strategic interventions (IBGE, 2022).

Because the Independência neighborhood is still expanding, there are relatively few
residents in the immediate campus vicinity. Nevertheless, the 53 households interviewed
represent more than 15% of all residences in the census tract. Framing residents as producers of
territorial knowledge, we selected participants randomly. Most residents were willing to
cooperate, which facilitated the administration of the semi-structured questionnaires and the
gathering of necessary research data.
To ensure alignment between epistemological assumptions and methodological
procedures, data analysis was structured across three complementary levels. First, quantitative
data from the questionnaires served a contextualizing function, characterizing residents’
socioeconomic profiles and mapping the objective conditions of the territory through
descriptive statistics (Hair et al., 2013).
Second, qualitative data from interviews were used interpretively to uncover the
meanings participants attached to their experiences of urban space. Finally, at a third level, the
Iramuteq software was employed to organize and systematize discursive patterns through
lexicometric analysis.
Choosing content analysis supported by Iramuteq goes beyond a technical exercise in
lexical counting. Epistemologically, we adopt the premise that language structures social
thought. The words residents use to describe their neighborhood and the university reveal the
collective cognitive frameworks through which they organize their reality. The software thus
aids in identifying discursive patterns, though their interpretation ultimately rests on
theoretical analysis.
We applied Descending Hierarchical Classification (DHC) and similarity analysis to
identify not only recurring themes but also fields of semantic association (Camargo; Justo, 2013;
Sousa, 2021, p. 202). When residents link “University” with “Hope,” but also with “No”
(negation/denial), it reveals an epistemological tension between the institution’s official
project and lived reality.
The method, therefore, serves to expose the contradictions of urban development, where
official narratives of progress collide with everyday experiences of neglect. We employed a
Descending Hierarchical Classification test, which groups words according to their degree of
correlation. Through this method, we identified the core discussion themes, mapping the data
into a topical structure.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1 Institutional Context: The Creation of UFOPA and the Alenquer Campus
The UFOPA was established in 2009 under Federal Law No. 12,085/2009, as part of a
national policy to expand and decentralize higher education across the Amazon region
(BRASIL, 2009). The new institution absorbed infrastructure, academic programs, and
personnel from the Federal University of Pará (UFPA) and the Federal Rural University of the
Amazon (UFRA), consolidating its main campus in Santarém while opening satellite campuses
in six strategic municipalities across western Pará: Alenquer, Itaituba, Juruti, Monte Alegre,
Óbidos, and Oriximiná. By 2024, the university served 5,497 enrolled students and 630
scholarship recipients through the Forma Pará program (UFOPA, 2024) (Figure 1).

4.2 Infrastructure Conditions in the Campus Vicinity
The campus was established in an area marked by structural deficiencies in basic urban
infrastructure. Unpaved access roads severely restrict mobility, a problem that intensifies
during the rainy season when flooding and soil erosion become frequent.
Rua João Ferreira, the main access route, features a compacted dirt surface and
inadequate or nonexistent storm drainage, which accelerates soil degradation and heightens
flood risks (Figure 3). The lack of sidewalks and traffic signage further compromises pedestrian
safety, disproportionately affecting children, the elderly, and people with disabilities.

Similar conditions prevail on Rua Beatriz do Vale, where the campus is located (Figure

conditions, fostering dust accumulation, stagnant water, and debris, with direct consequences
for public health. Public lighting is unevenly distributed, particularly along campus access

From an urban morphology perspective, these material deficits reduce spatial legibility
(Lynch; Camargo, 1997) and weaken affective community ties (Cullen, 2009). The absence of
positive visual landmarks, irregular street layouts, and a lack of basic urban services combine
to create a territory with low intelligibility, complicating navigation, spatial appropriation, and
the formation of a shared territorial identity.
These material conditions establish the objective backdrop that shapes both the
campus’s academic operations and local perceptions of development and the university’s
institutional role, a theme that directly intersects with the sociodemographic profile and
community participation capacity examined below.

associate its establishment with improvements in road paving, street lighting, and sanitation
regularization, suggesting that the university’s physical presence would elevate the
neighborhood’s visibility among public officials.
Furthermore, the availability of local higher education, eliminating the need to relocate,
is viewed as a mechanism for social mobility, expanding access to professional qualifications
and the formal labor market. This perception aligns with literature that frames the university
as a vital bridge between local communities and policymakers, one capable of mediating
dialogue, fostering strategic partnerships, and informing the development of policies tailored
to regional realities (Lynch; Camargo, 1997; Macedo; Monteiro Neto; Vieira, 2022; Pinsky;
Gomes; Kruglianskas, 2019).

This dual linkage suggests that negation functions on two discursive levels: an
acknowledgment of preexisting infrastructure deficits and a frustration over the lack of
immediate, institution-driven transformation.

4.5 Integrated Discussion: Mechanisms of Mediation Between the University and Urban
Development
The findings indicate that the territorial impact of anchor institutions in peripheral
cities emerges from institutional mediation mechanisms rather than automatic urban causality.
In the case studied, the interaction among the university, the community, and public
authorities proved contingent upon low local state capacity, fragile participatory structures, and
the ongoing need to build territorial legitimacy.
The first vector concerns institutional coordination. The results demonstrate that
perceptions of urban inadequacy stem primarily from weak intersectoral articulation within
municipal management. In this context of constrained state capacity, the university is
envisioned as a potential integrator bridging community demands and governmental responses,
corroborating studies on the role of higher education institutions in settings of institutional
fragility (Costa e Silva et al., 2024; Macedo; Monteiro Neto; Vieira, 2022; Pinsky; Gomes;
Kruglianskas, 2019).
The second vector encompasses conditional community legitimacy. The similarity
analysis shows that recognition of UFOPA as a driver of transformation (in education,
employment, and political mediation) is not automatically consolidated. Social acceptance
depends on tangible deliverables and transparency in decision-making processes. The physical
presence of the campus, therefore, does not guarantee credibility per se; credibility must be
continuously built through coordinated extension programs and meaningful participation in
local governance forums.
The third vector addresses local civil organization. The absence of established
participatory structures in the Independência neighborhood (e.g., resident associations,
councils) undermines the university’s capacity to function as a mediator. Without organized
interlocutors, the systematization of community demands becomes fragmented and episodic.
Establishing robust channels for community participation thus emerges as a prerequisite for
realizing UFOPA’s coordinating potential, a finding that aligns with literature on social capital
and urban development (Jacobs, 1970).

ability of anchor institutions to generate effective territorial coordination within urban

5 CONCLUSION
This study examined how anchor institutions interact with local governance structures
in peripheral contexts of uneven urbanization, using the case of UFOPA in Alenquer, Pará, as
an empirical expression of the institutional mediation mechanisms at work in the Brazilian
Amazon. Empirically, the study documents that urban development is perceived as constrained
by structural deficits in basic services, which residents attribute to the municipal government’s
limited coordinating capacity. This finding reinforces the literature on institutional fragility in
Amazonian municipalities and demonstrates that perceived quality of life is directly tied to the
effectiveness of local public administration.
Theoretically and analytically, the research demonstrates that expectations placed on
the university are structurally conditional. While residents acknowledge UFOPA’s potential
to expand opportunities and mediate political dialogue, this recognition coexists with a
skepticism grounded in a history of administrative sluggishness and a lack of measurable
outcomes. Institutional legitimacy, therefore, is not a given but an ongoing process that
depends on transparency, territorially grounded extension initiatives, and integration with
municipal governance.
As a conceptual contribution, the findings indicate that the presence of a university
campus in peripheral contexts does not operate as an automatic catalyst for development. Its
impact is mediated by an institutional triangulation: (i) the university’s own capacity for
articulation; (ii) the municipal administration’s willingness to incorporate it as a strategic
partner; and (iii) the existence of a local associative fabric capable of systematizing demands
and holding stakeholders accountable. A breakdown in any of these links compromises the
realization of the institution’s transformative potential, regardless of its academic excellence.
The study’s limitations lie in its geographical and temporal scope, which restrict the
generalizability of the findings to the Independência neighborhood and the campus’s early
implementation phase. The institution’s future consolidation may shift perception dynamics,
warranting longitudinal investigations.
