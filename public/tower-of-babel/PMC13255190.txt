A comparison between dissociative subtypes of PTSD and CPTSD among trauma-exposed adolescents
Authors: Yajie Bi, Chengqi Cao, Wei Chen, Yu Liu, Ping Liu, Shu Luo, Bin Zhang, Huiyi Qin, Ying Tang, Yun Pan, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The dissociative subtype of posttraumatic stress disorder (D-PTSD) was recognized in DSM-5, and a dissociative subtype of complex posttraumatic stress disorder (D-CPTSD) in ICD-11 was recently proposed. Introduction

In the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11; World Health Organization, 2018), complex posttraumatic stress disorder (CPTSD) was introduced as a diagnosis distinct from posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) to recognize the impact that chronic or repeated trauma can have on self-organization-related mechanisms. Given that CPTSD is a newly recognized mental disorder in ICD-11, further research into its correlates and features is essential to build both basic and clinical knowledge on this psychiatric condition.

Prior research has documented a close relationship between dissociation and CPTSD, both historically and aetiologically (e.g. Herman, 1992; Leiva-Bianchi et al., 2023; Luxenberg et al., 2001; Vonderlin et al., 2018). Empirical studies have found a moderate-to-strong association between dissociative symptoms and CPTSD (Fung et al., 2023; Hamer et al., 2024). However, only a subset of individuals with CPTSD (ranging from 28.6% to 76.9%) experience clinically significant dissociative symptoms (Fung et al., 2023). Studies employing latent class analysis (LCA) methods have identified a distinct subgroup characterized by a high risk of encountering both CPTSD and dissociative symptoms, accounting for 27.4% to 35.4% of CPTSD cases in both adult and adolescent samples (Hyland et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). This subgroup also exhibits the poorest mental health outcomes, the highest levels of functional impairment, and the most severe childhood trauma experiences. These findings suggest that a dissociative subtype of CPTSD (D-CPTSD) may exist within the ICD-11.

Although the diagnostic logic of ICD-11 CPTSD and PTSD in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) differs, Lehrner and Yehuda (2020) compared the two sets of criteria and revealed that the DSM-5 PTSD criteria encompass all symptoms included in the ICD-11 definition of CPTSD. This raises an important question: what is the relationship between DSM-5 D-PTSD and ICD-11 D-CPTSD? Given the substantial progress made in understanding the mechanisms and interventions associated with DSM-5 D-PTSD (e.g. Misitano et al., 2024; Schiavone et al., 2018; Vancappel et al., 2025), exploring this question could help to clarify whether the knowledge on DSM-5 D-PTSD could be extended to ICD-11 D-CPTSD. The current study aims to compare the dissociative subtype of ICD-11 CPTSD with that of DSM-5 PTSD in a sample of trauma-exposed adolescents. In the current study, the operational definition of D-PTSD involves simultaneously screening for probable PTSD and dissociation, while D-CPTSD involves screening for probable CPTSD and dissociation.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The current study re-analyzed a database of 57,984 trauma-exposed adolescents that had been used in several previous studies (Bi et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024). Adolescents participated in the current study voluntarily after providing electronic informed consent jointly with their guardians. This research protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Deyang people’s hospital (2021-04-152-K01). Details of the data collection procedures and demographic characteristics of the sample were reported in the aforementioned studies.

Measures

The individuals with DSM-5 D-PTSD or ICD-11 D-CPTSD were screened with the Global Psychotrauma Screen for Teenagers (GPS-T; Grace et al., 2021) in the current study. The GPS is a psychometric instrument designed to assess a wide range of reactions to trauma, selecting the most representative symptoms from valid and reliable measures for screening specific types of traumatic stress reactions (e.g. DSM-5 PTSD measured by Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5, PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015; ICD-11 CPTSD measured by International Trauma Questionnaire, ITQ, Cloitre et al., 2018) (Olff et al., 2020). Empirical studies have demonstrated that individuals screened for PTSD, D-PTSD, and CPTSD using the GPS-T exhibit high consistency with those identified by the PCL-5, the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5), and the ITQ, whose items are fully aligned with the official diagnostic criteria for DSM-5 PTSD, D-PTSD, and ICD-11 CPTSD (Grace et al., 2023; Pinto et al., 2024). The Chinese version was developed through a two-stage process involving forward translation and back-translation by two independent translators.

The PTSD subscale in GPS-T consists of five ‘Yes’ (1) or ‘No’ (0) questions. A cutoff score of 3 on the GPS-T PTSD subscale has been shown to effectively screen for probable PTSD with high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy (Grace et al., 2023; Pinto et al., 2024). In the current sample, the PTSD subscale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.80. The CPTSD subscale of the GPS-T comprises seven ‘Yes’ (1) or ‘No’ (0) questions. A cutoff score of 5 on the CPTSD subscale effectively screens for probable CPTSD (Grace et al., 2023; Primasari et al., 2024). In the current sample, the CPTSD subscale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.83. Trauma-related dissociative symptoms were assessed using the dissociation subscale of the GPS-T, which includes two ‘Yes’ (1) or ‘No’ (0) questions measuring depersonalization and derealization, respectively. Previous studies have demonstrated that a cutoff score of 1 is optimal for identifying probable trauma-related dissociation (Pinto et al., 2024). Functional impairment was assessed with a single functioning rating item in the GPS-T, rated on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (poor, indicating severe functional impairment) to 10 (excellent, indicating minimal functional impairment).

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and major depressive disorder (MDD) were assessed using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006) and the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001), respectively. Items on both the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Extremely). A cutoff score of 10 on both scales has been recommended in previous studies to identify probable cases of GAD and MDD (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). The Chinese versions of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 have demonstrated good psychometric properties in adolescent populations (e.g. Leung et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2021). In the current sample, Cronbach’s α for both the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 was 0.91.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0. First, prevalence estimates and 95% confidence intervals for D-PTSD and D-CPTSD were calculated and compared using the z-test. Second, as D-PTSD and D-CPTSD were treated as dichotomous variables, Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was employed to assess the level of agreement in classifying individuals into the same diagnostic category. Agreement was interpreted as very high for Kappa values above 0.80, high for values between 0.60 and 0.79, moderate for 0.40–0.59, low for 0.20–0.39, and very low for values below 0.20 (Kaluźniak-Szymanowska et al., 2021). Third, independent samples t-tests were used to compare functional impairment between the D-PTSD and D-CPTSD groups. Finally, z-tests were employed to examine differences in comorbidity rates of MDD and GAD between the two groups.

Results

Descriptive statistics

In the current study, the mean scores of the PTSD subscale, CPTSD subscale, dissociation subscale, GAD-7 and PHQ-9 were 1.0 (SD = 1.5, range: 0–5), 1.5 (SD = 2.0, range: 0–7), 0.2 (SD = 0.5, range: 0–2), 3.6 (SD = 3.9, range: 0–21) and 4.4 (SD = 4.9, range: 0–27). The prevalence of the probable D-PTSD (8.4%, 95% CI: 8.2–8.6%) was higher than that of the probable D-CPTSD (6.9%, 95% CI: 6.7–7.1%) (z = 9.45, p < .001, Cohen's h = 0.056).

Agreement between D-PTSD and D-CPTSD

The agreement between D-PTSD and D-CPTSD was extremely high, as indicated by a Kappa coefficient of 0.90, exceeding the 0.8 threshold. Additionally, all individuals with D-CPTSD met the criteria for D-PTSD, comprising 82.4% of the D-PTSD group.

Functional impairment between D-PTSD and D-CPTSD groups

The mean functional score of the D-CPTSD group was 4.6 ± 2.4, while that of the D-PTSD group was 4.9 ± 2.5. These results indicate that the D-CPTSD group exhibited greater functional impairment compared to the D-PTSD group (t = 4.62, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.099).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the DSM-5 D-PTSD and ICD-11 D-CPTSD in a large sample of trauma-exposed adolescents. The study found that the prevalence of DSM-5 D-PTSD was higher than that of ICD-11 D-CPTSD. Previous reviews have reported that the prevalence of D-PTSD is approximately 20% in non-clinical samples (Hansen et al., 2017; White et al., 2022). Relatively, the only two studies related to ICD-11 D-CPTSD found that the prevalence of D-CPTSD was 10.0% in adults and 5.3% in adolescents (Hyland et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), lower than that of D-PTSD. Consistent with previous findings, the current study supports the notion that the prevalence of ICD-11 D-CPTSD might be lower than that of DSM-5 D-PTSD. However, the small effect size suggests that this difference in prevalence between D-PTSD and D-CPTSD requires further confirmation.

The current study also found an extremely high agreement between ICD-11 D-CPTSD and DSM-5 D-PTSD. Specifically, all individuals with D-CPTSD met the criteria for D-PTSD, and those with D-CPTSD represented the majority (over 80%) of the D-PTSD population. These results indicate that ICD-11 D-CPTSD might be part of, and constitute the majority of, DSM-5 D-PTSD cases. These findings align with previous theoretical and empirical research. It has been documented that both the expansion of DSM-5 PTSD criteria and the development of ICD-11 CPTSD aim to better conceptualize common posttraumatic symptoms such as affective dysregulation, interpersonal difficulties, and negative self-concept. Therefore, ICD-11 CPTSD diagnostic features are largely captured within DSM-5 PTSD criteria (Lehrner & Yehuda, 2020). Moreover, two person-centred studies identified subgroups of D-CPTSD characterized by more severe CPTSD and dissociative symptoms in both adult and adolescent samples, but did not find a distinct class with elevated ICD-11 PTSD and dissociation symptoms (Hyland et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Additionally, although the D-CPTSD group exhibited greater functional impairment and higher comorbidity rates with GAD and MDD than the D-PTSD group in the current study, the small effect sizes (all Cohen’s d and Cohen’s h were lower than 0.2) suggested that the differences in functional impairment and comorbidity with other psychiatric conditions between D-CPTSD and D-PTSD warrant further verification. In summary, these integrated findings suggested that ICD-11 D-CPTSD might be a part of DSM-5 D-PTSD.

The current findings provided empirical support for extending basic, clinical, and practical knowledge of D-PTSD to D-CPTSD. For instance, neurobiological studies have supported the notion that D-PTSD is correlated with emotion overmodulation (Schiavone et al., 2018), a mechanism that might also apply to D-CPTSD. Furthermore, therapies effective for alleviating D-PTSD symptoms, such as dissociation-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (DF-CBT; Vancappel et al., 2025), might also be applicable to D-CPTSD.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, a self-reported questionnaire survey was used as the most economical way to collect data from a large sample. Therefore, additional studies using clinician-rated measures are needed. Second, the current sample consisted of individuals exposed to a specific trauma (i.e. the COVID-19 pandemic and lockdown) from China. Given that these factors might influence dissociation reporting (Krüger, 2020; Schimmenti, 2018), future studies should use samples exposed to various trauma types from other cultural backgrounds to examine the generalizability of these findings. Third, the PTSD and CPTSD symptoms assessed with the GPS-T could not precisely mirror the DSM-5 and ICD-11 diagnostic criteria, and only one item was used to measure functional impairment in the GPS-T. Future replication studies using measures that capture DSM-5 PTSD and ICD-11 CPTSD diagnostic criteria, along with multi-validating assessments of functional impairment, are warranted. Lastly, the Chinese version of GPS-T has not been systematically validated, and its psychometric properties need to be thoroughly examined in future studies.

Conclusion

Despite the aforementioned limitations, the present study is the first to compare the symptomatic characteristics of DSM-5 D-PTSD and ICD-11 D-CPTSD. The findings suggest that ICD-11 D-CPTSD might be a part of DSM-5 D-PTSD, and inform that the basic, clinical, and practical knowledge of DSM-5 D-PTSD might be extended to ICD-11 D-CPTSD.
