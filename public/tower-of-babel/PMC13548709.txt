The relationship between climate change anxiety, death anxiety, and psychological resilience in older adults with chronic illnesses
Authors: Cansu Mine Aydın, Özlem Karabulutlu, Tuğba Kavas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study employed a descriptive, a cross-sectional and analytical (correlational) design to determine the relationship between climate change anxiety, death anxiety, and psychological resilience among older adults with chronic illnesses living in Kars, located in eastern Turkey.Materials and methodsThe study was carried out from March 2025 to November 2025 with the participation of 386 older adults living in Kars. The study data were collected online using an “Information Form,” the “Climate Change Anxiety Scale (CCAS),” the “Psychological Resilience Scale (PRS),” and the “Death Anxiety Scale (DAS).” The data obtained in the study were analyzed using the DataBeeg 1.0 program. The chi-square test, Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis H test, Bonferroni test for difference comparison, and Spearman rank correlation analysis were used in the evaluation of the data. The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.ResultsThe study found that the mean scores for the climate change anxiety scale (3.61 ± 0.56), psychological resilience scale (2.79 ± 0.46), and death anxiety scale (13.13 ± 2.13) were moderate. A low-level negative correlation (r = − 0.124, p = 0.015) was found between the Climate Change Anxiety Scale and the Psychological Resilience Scale, while a low-level positive correlation (r = 0.258, p < 0.001) and a moderate negative correlation (r = − 0.307, p < 0.001) were observed between the Psychological Resilience Scale and the Death Anxiety Scale (p < 0.05). Female participants’ climate change anxiety and death anxiety scale scores were found to be statistically significantly higher than those of male participants (p < 0.05).ConclusionHigh climate change anxiety and death anxiety were associated with decreased psychological resilience in older adults with chronic diseases. It is recommended that intervention programs be implemented that take into account socio-economic characteristics and gender differences to improve the health status of older adults, increase their quality of life, develop health systems, and reduce psychological effects.Keywords: Climate change, Chronic Disease, Death Anxiety, Psychological Resilience, Old Age

Introduction

The increase in average life expectancy within societies is leading to a rapid aging of the population [1]. Aging is a complicated process that, over time, leads to a decline in function at the cellular level and a reduction in quality of life [2]. This process results in a gradual decline in physical and mental capacity, an increased risk of disease, and ultimately, death [3]. As individuals age, physiological changes (such as hearing loss, cataracts, osteoarthritis, etc.) and psychological changes, such as loneliness, cognitive decline, and reduced environmental adaptation, are observed [1]. Furthermore, the prevalence of chronic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, dementia, and depression increases with the aging process [2, 3]. A study conducted by the National Council on Aging in 2022 reported that 94.9% of individuals aged 60 and over have at least one chronic condition [4]. Individuals are more likely to suffer from multiple chronic conditions simultaneously as they age [3]. Chronic conditions are health problems that progress slowly, require long-term and ongoing care, and affect individuals throughout their entire lives. Factors such as stressors resulting from rapid urbanization, technological advancements, reduced physical activity, and changing dietary habits are increasing the incidence and prevalence of chronic diseases [5]. Climate change is one of the factors that can lead to a worsening of symptoms in individuals with chronic diseases and the development of new diseases in those without [6]. The effects of climate change on human health are multifaceted and negatively impact both physical and mental health [7]. Multiple chronic conditions, advanced age, susceptibility to dehydration, and reduced sensory perception constitute significant risk factors against the adverse effects of climate change. As a result of climate change, extreme weather conditions (hurricanes, typhoons, heatwaves, rainfall, etc.), soil pollution, increased ultraviolet radiation, rising sea levels, and ocean acidification are occurring [8]. This situation leads to the emergence of infections arising from water use and food supply, vector-borne diseases, infectious diseases, allergies, cancer, respiratory diseases, neurological disorders, and stress-related illnesses [7]. People over 65 are among the vulnerable groups most affected by climate change. Older adults experience physiological changes and socio-economic inequalities that exacerbate the adverse effects of climate change [7, 8]. For example, due to rising temperatures, a reduced sweating response in the thermoregulatory process and changes in cutaneous blood flow in older adults increase the morbidity and mortality of chronic diseases. Temperature fluctuations contribute to cardiovascular, digestive, genitourinary, and respiratory diseases in older adults, thereby increasing the rate of emergency department visits and, particularly among those aged 85 and over, the rate of hospital admissions [7]. Furthermore, following events such as droughts, floods, and fires, many illnesses arise as a result of malnutrition caused by older people being unable to access food easily [9]. Furthermore, while the psychological effects of climate change may not be immediately apparent, they do lead to the emergence of various mental health issues [10]. In particular, the consequences of the changes brought about by climate change and the uncertainties regarding the future can lead to psychological problems such as fear, post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, complicated grief, guilt, moral trauma and depression in individuals [7, 10]. Older adults, in particular, face mental health issues following climate-related disasters because they are confronted with the loss of their homes, families, loved ones and social circles [8]. The study found that depression, anxiety, insomnia and post-traumatic stress disorder were prevalent among older adults affected by hurricanes, floods and wildfires [11]. Ageing and chronic illnesses increase the incidence of psychological problems. The decline in physical strength, the loss of productivity, and the struggle with various illnesses lead older adults to perceive themselves as vulnerable and to feel that they are drawing closer to death. Psychological problems affect older adults with chronic diseases more severely. They may even cause mental changes such as fear of death. Fears like uncertainty, suffering, losing loved ones, the loss of the body, and loneliness heighten death anxiety [12]. Negative psychological consequences are seen in older adults who are aware that life is finite and death is inevitable and who experience fear of death [13]. In these situations, it is essential for an individual to have a high level of psychological resilience in order to effectively manage adverse situations and withstand anxiety and fear (such as natural disasters, epidemics, accidents, etc.). Psychological resilience is regarded as an important personality trait in safeguarding an individual’s mental health. It is noted that individuals with a high level of psychological resilience possess protective factors such as autonomy, self-help skills and self-efficacy, and that these are effective in coping with adverse situations [14]. Kaya and Aksakallı’s [15] study determined that individuals possessing traits such as extroversion, emotional stability and responsibility experience less anxiety regarding climate change, whilst Duman and colleagues’ [16] study found that those experiencing anxiety about climate change have lower levels of psychological resilience. The current literature makes significant contributions to understanding the bidirectional relationships between ageing, chronic illness, death anxiety, psychological resilience and climate change anxiety; however, it remains limited in explaining the multivariate and holistic nature of these relationships. In particular, there are few studies that examine climate change anxiety, death anxiety and psychological resilience together in chronically ill older adults, and evaluate the relationship between these variables within the same model. This study aims to contribute to the literature and to future research in this field by examining the relationships between climate change anxiety, death anxiety and psychological resilience within a holistic model. Furthermore, it will contribute to healthcare organisations recognising the impact of climate change anxiety and death anxiety on the psychological resilience of older adults, and to the development of sustainable objectives for safeguarding their mental and physical health. This study will be examined based on three hypotheses.

H1. Climate change anxiety is significantly related to death anxiety.

H1. Climate change anxiety is significantly related to death anxiety.

H2. Climate change anxiety is significantly related to psychological resilience.

H2. Climate change anxiety is significantly related to psychological resilience.

H3. Death anxiety is significantly related to psychological resilience.

H3. Death anxiety is significantly related to psychological resilience.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was designed as an observational study with a cross-sectional and analytical (correlational) design, conducted in Kars, located in eastern Turkey. The research design, sample selection, variables, data collection and reporting of statistical analyses were carried out in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [17].

Study population and sample

The study population consisted of 26.203 individuals aged 65 and over (men: 12.241, women: 13.962) registered in Kars province in the 2024 Turkish Statistical Institute Address-Based Population Registration System [18]. In the study, a purposive sampling method—one of the non-probability sampling methods—was used [19], The sample size was calculated using the formula for calculating a sample with a known population (n=Nt2pq/d2 (N-1)+ t2pq). Using the parameters p, q = 0.5, t = 1.96, and d = 0.05 in this formula, the sample size was calculated as 379 at a 95% confidence level. Considering the possibility of data loss, the study was completed with 386 participants (men: 192, women: 194). The inclusion criteria for participants were: being over 65 years of age, having a diagnosed chronic disease (hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, etc.), voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study, and being literate (literate, but without a formal qualification) in Turkish. Diagnoses of psychiatric disorders and severe cognitive impairment were assessed based on participants’ self-reports; those experiencing such issues and those who completed the questionnaire incompletely or incorrectly were excluded from the study. Older adults who approved informed consent forms participated in the study using an online collection method.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using the “Information Form,” “Climate Change Anxiety Scale,” “Psychological Resilience Scale,” and “Death Anxiety Scale.”

Information Form: Researchers conducted a literature review to develop a 20-question questionnaire covering sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, income status, etc.), chronic health conditions (duration of chronic illness, regular medication use, smoking status, general health status, etc.), and climate change-related characteristics (level of knowledge about climate change, sources of information, etc.) [9, 12].

Climate Change Anxiety Scale (CCAS): The scale developed by Stewart [20] to measure climate change anxiety was adapted into Turkish by Gezer and İlhan [21]. The scale has two subscales, 10 items, and a five-point Likert scale. On the scale, the “Never” option is scored as 1, and the “Always” option as 5. A total score of 10–50 can be obtained from the scale, and a high score indicates a high level of climate change anxiety. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was determined to be 0.91. The Cronbach’s alpha value for this study was found to be 0.91.

Brief Psychological Resilience Scale (BPRS): The scale developed by Smith et al. [22] was adapted into Turkish by Doğan [23]. The scale has 6 items and a five-point Likert scale. In the scale, the option ‘I completely disagree’ is scored as 1, and the option ‘I completely agree’ is scored as 5. Items 2, 4, and 6 are reverse-coded. The total score ranges from 6 to 30 on the scale, and a high score indicates a high level of psychological resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was calculated as 0.81. The Cronbach’s alpha value for this study was found to be 0.73.

Death Anxiety Scale (DAS): The validity and reliability study of the scale developed by Templer [24] was conducted by Akça and Köse [25]. The scale consists of 15 items and a 2-point Likert scale. On the scale, “yes” is scored as 1 point and “no” is scored as 0 points. The total score on the scale ranges from 0 to 15 points. A score of 7 or more on the scale indicates high death anxiety. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was determined to be 0.86. The Cronbach’s alpha value for this study was found to be 0.74.

Data collection method

The data for this study were collected from older adults via an online survey form between March 2025 and November 2025. The older adults were asked to sign an informed consent form in a digital environment. Participants who had difficulty using digital tools or reading the questions were provided technical support by the researcher in a face-to-face setting, upon request, within the same standard protocol. This support was limited to reading the questions and response options in the survey form directly to the participants and resolving issues related to links or device usage; no explanations or additional comments were provided regarding the questions. The researcher recorded the participants’ responses in the online form without any guidance. All participants, except those requiring technical support, were expected to complete the survey form on their own. During the data collection process, careful attention was paid to ensuring that participants’ responses were not influenced and that the reliability of the measurements was maintained. This method aimed to enhance the accessibility of the data collection process and reduce the risk of potential selection bias.

Data evaluation

The data obtained in the study were analyzed using the DataBeeg 1.0 program. Descriptive statistical methods (number, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were used to evaluate participant characteristics and calculate the total scores of the scales. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics, skewness and kurtosis values, and graphical methods (histograms, Q-Q plots) were used to assess whether the data followed a normal distribution. A p-value less than 0.05 in the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicates that the data are not normally distributed. However, the literature notes that this test may be overly sensitive, particularly with large samples [26]. Upon examining the results, it was observed that the skewness and kurtosis values for the climate change concern and psychological resilience variables fell within acceptable limits (± 2) or were close to this threshold, whereas the skewness (-2.380) and kurtosis (7.819) values for the death anxiety variable were significantly outside these limits. Based on these results, it was concluded that the assumption of normality was not fully met in the dataset, and non-parametric tests were preferred in the analyses [27] (Table 1). As the data were not normally distributed, and for nonparametric test analysis, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for comparisons between two independent groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for comparisons between more than two independent groups. In these tests, interpretations were based on median values [27]. A Bonferroni correction was applied to control for the probability of error in multiple comparisons [28]. A chi-square test was conducted to examine the relationships between categorical variables. Spearman’s correlation was used to determine the direction and strength of the relationship between variables. Based on Cohen’s [29] study, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were classified as weak (r = 0.10), moderate (r = 0.30), and strong (r = 0.50). In interpreting effect sizes, the following values were used: r (0.10 low, 0.30 moderate, 0.50 high) for the Mann–Whitney U test, η² (0.01 low, 0.06 moderate, 0.14 high) for the Kruskal–Wallis test, and Cramér’s V (0.10 low, 0.30 moderate, 0.50 high) for the Chi-square test (0.10 low, 0.30 moderate, 0.50 high) were used as reference values [29]. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical aspects of the study

Prior to commencement of the study, ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Non-Interventional Research at the Faculty of Health Sciences, Kafkas University, on March 10, 2025, and numbered 81829502.903/17. Informed consent was obtained from the participating older adults prior to the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration.

Results

The study found that 50.3% of participants were female, 85.8% were married, 99.1% lived with their spouse, 99.2% had children, and 69.2% had three or more children. It was determined that 71.5% of the participants were aged 65–74, 68.1% were literate, 72.3% did not work in any job, 70.7% had income less than their expenses, 72.8% had social security, 57.8% had an extended family, 88.6% lived in their homes, and 51.3% lived in villages. It was determined that 50.5% of participants smoked, 56% had a chronic illness lasting 6–10 years, and among those with existing chronic diseases, 75.1% had hypertension, 48.4% had heart disease, 18.9% had respiratory diseases, 16.6% had diabetes, and 18.7% had other diseases (such as arthritis and cerebrovascular disease). It was determined that 96.1% of participants take medication regularly, and 49.5% rated their general health as neither good nor bad. It is observed that 97.2% of respondents are aware of climate change, 72.8% learned about it from television, and 62.4% responded, “I think the place where I live will be affected a lot by climate change.

Table 2 presents the means, standard deviations, and correlation analysis of the scales used in the study. The climate change anxiety scale had a mean of 3.61 ± 0.56 points, the psychological resilience scale had a mean of 2.79 ± 0.46, and the death anxiety scale had a mean of 13.13 ± 2.13. A low-level, negative, and statistically significant relationship was found between the climate change anxiety scale and the psychological resilience scale (r=-0.124, p = 0.015). A low-level, positive, and statistically significant relationship was found between the Climate Change Anxiety Scale and the Death Anxiety Scale (r = 0.258, p < 0.001). A moderate, negative, and statistically significant relationship was found between the Psychological Resilience Scale and the Death Anxiety Scale (r=-0.307, p < 0.001). The results obtained from the analysis indicate that Hypotheses H1, H2, and H3 are supported.

Discussion

This study indicates that older adults with chronic illnesses tend to have moderate-to-high levels of climate change anxiety, high levels of mortality anxiety, and psychological resilience levels that are close to moderate. A positive relationship was found between climate change anxiety and death anxiety, and both variables were determined to be negatively related to psychological resilience. The study’s hypotheses, supported by these results, underscore the connection between climate change, death anxiety, and psychological resilience. There are limited studies in the literature that examine these variables together. Therefore, this paper is one of the pioneering studies examining the relationship between climate change anxiety, death anxiety, and psychological resilience in older adults. This study suggests that climate change anxiety and fear of death represent significant psychosocial issues among older adults and constitute risk factors that can undermine their psychological resilience. The results of this study are discussed within the framework of the variables of psychological resilience, fear of death, and climate change anxiety.

Climate change is a major barrier to healthy aging. In older adults, the presence of multiple chronic diseases, frailty, cognitive impairment, and polypharmacy increases the likelihood of being affected by climate change [8]. Chen et al.‘s [30] study found that older adults with chronic illnesses experience greater climate change anxiety due to their increased health vulnerability. Erkan et al.’s [31] study and the present study have supported the literature by finding a significant positive association between scores for climate change anxiety and scores for death anxiety (Table 2). As one of the few studies examining the relationship between climate change anxiety and death anxiety in older adults with chronic illnesses, it has contributed to the literature. Based on these results, it is believed that advancing age and having a chronic illness are associated with increased climate change anxiety and heightened death anxiety.

Climate-related threats such as heat waves and air pollution cause older adults to feel helpless and emotionally exhausted, negatively affecting their coping strategies [8]. Previous studies, including this one, have identified a statistically significant negative correlation between scores for climate change anxiety and scores for psychological resilience [16, 32] (Table 2). Consistent with the literature, this result suggests that participants experience difficulties in coping with adverse situations and in adapting to climate change. Therefore, it is believed that climate change anxiety constitutes a risk factor that can weaken an individual’s psychological resilience.

Death is a situation that comes to mind alongside chronic illness and causes anxiety in individuals due to its uncertainty [33]. The study found that older adults experiencing death anxiety exhibited reduced psychological resilience and negative psychological outcomes [13]. The negative correlation found in this study between participants’ death anxiety scores and psychological resilience scores supports the existing literature (Table 2). In this context, it is thought that factors such as the chronicity and uncertainty of the illness may have a negative impact on the mental state of older adults, which could be related to death anxiety.

In this study, while it was determined that female participants’ scores for climate change anxiety and death anxiety were higher compared to males, no significant difference was found in terms of psychological resilience scores (Table 3). Previous studies have found that female university students have higher anxiety about climate change than male students [34], and that older female adults in China have higher death anxiety scores than older male adults [35]. The literature supports the results of this study. It is hypothesized that this result stems from the different sociocultural roles of men (such as not openly acknowledging fears related to death) and women (such as caring for the family and the fear of leaving their children) in society. The literature presents different results regarding the relationship between gender and psychological resilience. While Aygün and Boz [36] found that men have higher levels of psychological resilience compared to women, Meng et al. [37] obtained results to the contrary. This result suggests that the relationship between the gender variable and psychological resilience is influenced by numerous factors. It is anticipated that factors such as experienced traumas, stress, mental state, and social support may form a complex network of relationships with psychological resilience, alongside the gender variable. This study stands out among the limited number of notable studies in the literature due to its inclusion of older adults with chronic illnesses and its examination of gender differences.

The literature indicates that the relationship between anxiety, worry, and smoking behavior is complex. It has been reported that individuals experiencing high levels of anxiety or worry are more prone to smoking. It has even been noted that individuals may turn to smoking to reduce their anxiety and worry [38]. Another study reported a correlation between increased psychological resilience and a decrease in smoking habits [39]. According to the results of this study, while non-smokers had higher climate change anxiety scores compared to smokers, their psychological resilience scores were found to be higher (Table 4). According to the literature, the fact that non-smokers have both high climate change anxiety scores and high psychological resilience scores will contribute new insights to the literature. This result indicates that even older adults with chronic illnesses may experience anxiety despite having high psychological resilience and that coping strategies may not always be sufficient.

The global development of health and technology systems highlights the extension of human lifespan, the rapid growth of the older population, and the necessity of strengthening psychological resilience among older adults [40]. In this study, it was determined that the participants’ average psychological resilience score was close to the moderate level (Table 2). In previous studies, the average psychological resilience scores of older adults were found to be at a moderate-to-high level, a result that is similar to this study [14, 36]. In this context, it is believed that the fact that the majority of older adults have extended families and children positively influences their ability to cope with adverse situations. It has been noted that psychological resilience in older adults is associated with various demographic factors (gender, education, income level, etc.) [40], and similar results were found in this study. Participants with a college degree or higher exhibited higher psychological resilience scores than literate participants in this study (Table 3). In the study by Aygün and Boz [36], it was noted that older adults with a college degree had higher psychological resilience scores compared to those with other educational levels. The study’s results are consistent with the literature. It suggests that an increase in educational level among older adults may enhance their ability to cope with negative events and strengthen their psychological resilience.

Consistent with the study by Bartley et al. [41], this study also found a positive and significant relationship between older adults’ income level and their psychological resilience (Table 3). Additionally, this study determined that those with income equal to their expenses scored higher than the group with income below their expenses, while no significant difference was observed for those with income exceeding their expenses. This result indicates that other variables, such as social support, stress, and individual characteristics, may also influence the relationship between income level and psychological resilience. Changes that occur in old age, chronic illnesses, and a decline in physical strength cause individuals to feel closer to death and experience death anxiety [40]. In this study, it was determined that the participants’ average death anxiety score was high (Table 2). Another study reported that the average death anxiety score among older adults was at a moderate level [42]. In a study conducted among individuals with chronic illnesses, however, the average anxiety about death score was found to be high [33]. It is believed that the participants in this study, which supports the literature, were influenced by having a chronic illness, increased thoughts about the end of life, and reduced treatment adherence.

In the study by Tel and colleagues [42], death anxiety scores were found to be lower among older adults with a high school education or higher. The result in this study that there is a relationship between increasing educational level and decreasing death anxiety supports the literature (Table 3). It is assessed that in older adults with chronic illnesses and low educational levels, coping with uncertainty, experiencing a loss of control, having health-related anxieties, and facing difficulties in finding meaning in life may influence death anxiety.

In particular, the increase in healthcare costs associated with chronic diseases among low-income retirees or older adults makes old age a more difficult period [30]. In the previous study and in this study, a negative association was found between increasing income levels and death anxiety scores [43] (Table 3). It is believed that older adults with chronic illnesses and low incomes experience higher levels of death anxiety due to factors such as difficulty accessing healthcare services and a lack of transportation options.

The absence of a definitive cure for chronic diseases and the presence of serious complications intensify death anxiety. In the study by Semerci et al. [44], participants with chronic diseases were found to have duration of 6 months to 5 years and 6 to 10 years, respectively, and no relationship with death anxiety was examined. This study contributes to the literature by finding a relationship between death anxieties and having a chronic illness for 1–5 years and 6–10 years (Table 4). This result, offering a new perspective that could contribute to the existing literature, suggests a link between older adults and the psychological adjustment and adaptation process that develops over time in the context of chronic illness. In the study conducted by Şanal and Hamarta [2] and in this study, death anxiety was found to be high among older adults with chronic illnesses who were medicated continuously. In this regard, the novelty of the disease process, the lack of a definitive cure for it, and the need for lifelong medication in individuals with chronic diseases are thought to be associated with death anxiety.

Being aware of climate change causes individuals to recognize their vulnerability and confront their inevitable mortality, thereby increasing their death anxiety and concern [45]. This study revealed that individuals who possess knowledge about climate change exhibit higher levels of both climate change anxiety and death anxiety. Participants’ levels of death anxiety were actually higher when they got their information from television or their environment (Table 4). In particular, individuals who learn about climate change from the media may develop prejudice and contribute to the spread of misinformation, which negatively affects society [46]. This result is thought to be associated with the fact that older adults experience increased anxiety about death due to the rise in mortality rates and emergency department visits caused by climate change, as well as the misinformation they receive from the media.

The observed consequences of climate change and its potential future impacts are causing psychological issues such as fear, stress, and anxiety among older adults [21]. In this study, participants’ scores for anxiety related to climate change were found to be at a moderate-to-high level (Table 2). In Demir et al.’s [47] study, it was determined that adult women had moderate-level anxiety about climate change, and in Semenderoğlu et al.’s [34] study, university students also had scores close to the moderate level. It is observed that there are a limited number of studies in the literature addressing climate change anxiety among older adults; however, current results suggest that climate change may affect the mental health of older adults.

In the context of climate change, individuals having sufficient economic resources increases their sense of security and provides protection against impending crises. In particular, individuals need economic resources to prepare for disasters and overcome challenges. The study noted a negative correlation between an increase in income level and anxiety regarding climate change [48]. This study is one of the few that examines the relationship between older adults’ income levels and their anxieties about climate change. The majority of older adults do not have income that exceeds their expenses and have limited economic resources, suggesting that socioeconomic factors may influence their anxieties about climate change.

In this study, it was found that those with knowledge about climate change exhibited higher levels of climate change anxiety compared to those without such knowledge (Table 3). Previous studies have also found that climate change anxiety is higher among individuals with a higher level of climate change awareness [34]. This result is noteworthy and suggests that it increases the perception of a threat to life and uncertainty about the future among older adults. Additionally, this study found no significant difference between education level and climate change anxiety scores (Table 3). Studies in the literature have shown a relationship between increasing education levels and rising climate change anxiety [47, 48]. It is believed that this result is affected by the fact that the majority of the study sample consisted of literate older adults.

In this study, it was determined that some of the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics differed by gender (Table 5). It has been determined that men consume more cigarettes than women [49]—a factor contributing to the rising prevalence of chronic diseases worldwide—and are more likely to suffer from conditions such as COPD [50]. In this study, the smoking rate among men was found to be higher than that of women. A correlation was identified between women and the absence of chronic respiratory disease. The results of this study are consistent with those of other studies. Accordingly, it is believed that the higher rate of smoking among men in society influences the prevalence of chronic diseases and leads to a higher incidence of respiratory diseases compared to women.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the online data collection method may have limited the participation of older adults who do not have access to digital devices or the internet, or who have lower levels of digital literacy. The selection of individuals with specific characteristics (those aged 65 and older, literate individuals, residents of Kars, etc.) using a purposive sampling method may limit the sample’s ability to represent the population. The cross-sectional design precludes the drawing of causal inferences. The fact that the study was conducted exclusively with older adults residing in Kars also limits the generalizability of the results to the older population in other regions. Therefore, the results should be carefully evaluated when generalized to broader populations. For future studies, it is recommended to use qualitative and mixed-methods designs to obtain more effective results and to address the evaluation of older adults’ coping mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, older adults with chronic illnesses were found to have moderate-to-high levels of climate change anxiety, high levels of death anxiety, and moderate levels of psychological resilience. It revealed a positive correlation between climate change anxiety and death anxiety and a negative correlation with psychological resilience. It was determined that older adults’ climate change anxiety, death anxiety, and psychological resilience are closely related to the duration of chronic illness, continuous medication use, knowledge about climate change, and sociodemographic characteristics. In particular, the results indicate that levels of climate change anxiety and death anxiety may vary by gender, while psychological resilience appears to be unaffected by gender and may be more closely related to individual and environmental factors. Both climate change anxiety and psychological resilience levels are high among non-smokers, possibly because individuals’ coping mechanisms are not always sufficient to deal with adverse situations. A relationship has been found between income and levels of climate change anxiety, death anxiety, and psychological resilience. Inadequate socioeconomic factors lead to difficulties in accessing healthcare services and a lack of transportation for older adults, negatively affecting both their physical and mental health. These results indicate that climate change in older adults is not only an environmental concern but also an important public health issue that causes increased death anxiety and decreased psychological resilience. It leads to a decline in the quality of life for older adults and has negative effects on them both biologically and psychologically. The positive relationship between climate change anxiety and fear of death reveals that older adults perceive climate change as a threat. This situation is related to their increased psychological vulnerability. Furthermore, it reveals that harmful habits such as smoking are more prevalent among older adult men, as evidenced by results related to men’s smoking behavior and the prevalence of chronic respiratory diseases. Therefore, it is suggested that programs to educate and counsel older adults with chronic diseases be included in regular medical care that smoking cessation efforts focus on risks specific to men that awareness about climate change and its effects on health be raised, and that educational programs featuring meditation or mindfulness for managing stress be set up. It is recommended that education and intervention programs for older adults be implemented in primary care settings by nurses, psychologists, physicians, and social workers in the form of individual counseling and group education sessions. Such approaches will contribute to supporting healthy aging in the population, strengthening health systems, reducing health expenditures, and improving quality of life. This study reveals that addressing climate anxiety, chronic illness, fear of death, and psychological resilience together in older adults may contribute to a more holistic approach to mental and physical health.
