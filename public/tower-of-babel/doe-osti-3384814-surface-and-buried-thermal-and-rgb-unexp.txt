Executive Summary
This document provides a description of a data collection campaign of unexploded ordnance (UXOI) set. The dataset captures a
controlled UAV imaging campaign designed to support detection of UXO across varied environmental conditions. Data were
collected during three campaigns in Norris and Northeast Knoxville, Tennessee, using RGB, and thermal sensors mounted on Parrot
UKR. In total, the dataset contains 9925 images,26 full-motion video, and approximately 81.99 GB of data, collected across late
spring/summer conditions, every hour during sunlight, and multiple surface contexts, including tall grass, short grass, gravel, as well
as buried in sand, and other gravel mixtures.

The collection was designed to capture thermal and visual variability relevant to UXO detection in agricultural land, bare earth, and
subsurface. Review of the imagery showed that ordnance was most detectable during periods of changing solar input, especially
approximately 10-60 minutes after sunrise, approximately 20-60 minutes after sunset, and 2-3 min after cloud cover interrupted
prolonged solar heating. These conditions increased thermal contrast because many ordnance items retained or released heat
differently than the surrounding vegetation and ground surface.

This dataset provides a useful resource for developing and evaluating airborne UXO detection methods under realistic field
conditions. All ordnance used in the study was inert, and thermal behavior may differ from that of live ordnance. In addition,
variation in ordnance type, composition, and placement introduced differences in thermal response that should be considered when
interpreting results.

Experimental Design and Data Collection
Data were collected at two separate locations: two in Norris, Tennessee, and one at L & M Landscape Material Supply, LLC in NE
Knoxville, Tennessee. The Norris campaign was conducted over two days, while the L & M campaign was completed in the late
afternoon till sunset on one single day. The timing of the Norris campaigns was planned to capture variability in temperature and
weather conditions, specifically representing late spring/summer environments of a temperate grassland.

During the Norris campaigns, inert pieces were positioned in taller vegetation and marked with red survey flags. This strategic
placement was intended to emulate how UXO might be encountered in real-world environments. All training ordnance placed in
taller grass was marked with red survey flags. Additional inert ordnance was also placed in areas of shorter grass. Inert ordnance was
placed in the mid-afternoon, followed by test flights and equipment checks.

During the L & M campaign, inert ordnances were either buried or placed on the surface of varying sizes of gravel or a mixed
material to simulate minefields in urbanized areas including roadways. The site featured multiple material stockpiles comprised of
mulch, gravel, river rock, and compost as well as heavily trafficked access roads compacted by heavy machinery. Data capture
occurred every hour across one evening in late June from 5pm to 8pm. Data were collected using a drone-mounted thermal sensor
at an altitude of 10-15 meters above ground level. Higher wind velocity was recorded during the L & M campaign which lead to
challenging FMV captures and should be considered when interpreting the dataset.

Three M20 practice heavy anti-tank mines were buried in either fine aggregate, crusher run, or 1.27 cm gravel rock. They were
buried at a depth of 2.54 centimeters and flagged at each burial site. Nine ordnances were placed on top of a flat area of mixed
materials including sand, dirt, and various rock types. These ordnances included a Panzer Panzerfaust 60 rocket head replica, 2
60mm M766 mortars, M880 81mm mortar, 4 PFM-1 makeshift landmines, and a MK76 practice bomb. In addition, an M30 training
grenade, BLU-26 submunition, M67 fragmentation grenade, 81mm M301 mortar, M1A1 training anti-tank mine, and two 60mm
M766 mortars were placed on the sloped surface of fine aggregate, crusher run, or ½ inch gravel mounds.

For the Norris campaign, flights were then conducted on day 1, from ~3 PM every hour until sunset. Followed by hourly flights from
sunrise to ~3 PM the following day. Flights were programmed at ~33 m to obtain a resolution of ~2.60 cm/pixel. Furthermore, to aid
in the creation of orthomosaics, flights were programmed to have between 80-85% front and side overlap. Day 2 consisted of similar
flight intervals, starting at about 10 AM to 3PM. Partly cloudy conditions during the morning prevented flight during peak morning
temperature change from 7 AM to 10AM. This flight schedule was designed to capture a full diurnal cycle, from sunrise to sunset.
Although the schedule was not ideal, similar weather and temperature conditions across both days of each campaign allowed us to
capture thermal variability between the ordnance and the ground surface.

The Norris collection site consisted of a freshly cut (~1 km2) and unkept field grass (~5 km2). Unkept portions ranged in height from
~1-1.5 meters while the manicured portions were ~3-5 cm. The grasses present in the area consisted of Verbesina occidentalis (i.e.,
Yellow Cornbeard), Asprera spica-veni (i.e., Loose Silky-bent), and Bromus diandrus (i.e., Broncho grass). These grasses displayed
typical bimodal phenological behavior based on whether the data were collected in warmer months versus winter conditions.
Vegetation was similar at both collection sites.

Equipment and Inert Ordnance Description
Aircraft and Sensor Payload

The Parrot ANAFI UKR sensor package combines electro-optical RGB imaging with long-wave infrared thermal sensing on a stabilized
three-camera IR/EO gimbal. Its visible-spectrum payload uses two EO sensors, a wide camera and a telephoto camera, supporting 4K
or FHD video, JPEG and DNG still imagery, ISO 100 to 12,800, and up to 35x digital zoom. The thermal payload is a FLIR Boson 640
thermographic camera, operating in the 8 to 14 micrometer IR band with a 640-class thermal sensor and thermal sensitivity of 60
mK or better. Parrot specifies temperature measurement from -40 °C to +250 °C, and the system can fuse thermographic and RGB
information in the FreeFlight 8 application for paired visual and thermal interpretation.

Inert Ordnance

Inert UXO were donated on loan for experimental use by several sources: private collectors donated several US made landmines,
grenades, and artillery pieces, plus a modern German Panzerfaust, anti-tank mines, guided rockets, cluster munitions.

Inert training ordnance used in these collection campaign included:

M30 training grenade, 60mm M766 mortar, BLU-26 Submunition, MK76 practice bomb, 81mm M880 mortar, M1A1 training anti-
tank mine, M20 practice heavy anti-tank mine, 81mm M301 mortar, BLU-3 submunition (pineapple bomb), Panzerfaust 60 rocket
head replica, M67 fragmentation hand grenade, and a handmade PFM-1 butterfly mine made with polyethylene.

Description of Data
This dataset consists of a combination of thermal full-motion video (FMV), and pre-programmed RGB and thermal images including
26 MP4 (FMV), 9925 images, and totaling 81.99 GB of data.

The Parrot UKR files use a numeric acquisition identifier followed by a sensor or product descriptor and, when applicable, a within-
sequence frame number. The general pattern is [capture ID]_[product type]_[frame number].[extension]. For example, files
beginning with 0000143 belong to the same acquisition or collection group, while the middle descriptor identifies the product as
visible-gpslapse, thermal-gpslapse, or blended-gpslapse. These labels indicate whether the file is a visible RGB image, a thermal
image, or a blended visible and thermal product generated during a GPS-lapse sequence. The final four-digit number, such as _0001
or _0206, records the frame position within that sequence and allows corresponding visible, thermal, and blended products to be
matched across the same capture group. Standalone products omit the frame number when they represent a single capture rather
than a sequence, as in 0000144_thermal.PNG. Video files follow a simpler related pattern, [capture ID]_video.MP4, where the
numeric ID identifies the video acquisition and the _video suffix distinguishes it from still-image products. Overall, the convention
organizes Parrot UKR media by acquisition ID first, sensor or derived product second, and sequence position third, making it possible
to group related RGB, thermal, blended, and video files programmatically.

Observations and Caveats
Observations

Over the course of these collection campaigns and manual inspection of imagery, several operational practices became apparent
and were validated. Inspection of thermal imagery revealed that ordnance was most easily detectable when environmental
conditions allowed thermal equilibrium to occur, followed by the addition or removal of direct sunlight. These environmental
changes occurred during three types of events: (1) approximately 30-60 minutes after sunrise, (2) approximately 30-60 minutes after
sunset, and (3) after prolonged periods of sunshine followed by cloud cover that blocked solar input. Event 3 produced the most
rapid change because most of the ordnance tested had much higher thermal inertia than the surrounding vegetation. As expected,
tall grasses dissipated heat more quickly than very short grass or bare ground. This phenomenon was expected as observed in past
collections. Exact temperature changes for each ordnance type were not recorded because of the quantity and variety of ordnance,
differences in ordnance placement, and the limited number of data collectors.

Images were captured at the Norris and L and M sites of subsurface mines in a variety of materials. During the Norris campaign, a
M20 anti-tank landmine and M30 grenade were buried 1 inch underneath play sand in a small plastic pool. Likewise, during the L
and M campaign a variety of landmines with steel, aluminum, cast iron, and plastic encasings were buried in either fine aggregate
(less than 4.75 mm), crusher run gravel rock (i.e. mixture of stone dust and 1.9 cm), or 1.27 cm gravel rock. These rock types consist
of quartz, basalt, limestone, granite, or limestone rock fragments.

The images suggest clearer detection is reliant on time of day, cloud cover, size of rock granules, and moisture of groundcover.
Similar to surface UXO, subsurface UXO are more defined in the images during three types of events: (1) approximately 30-60
minutes after sunrise, (2) approximately 30-60 after sunset, and (3) after prolonged periods of sunshine followed by cloud cover that
blocked solar input.

The clearer detection is inferred to result from the lower thermal inertia of the mine casing material relative to the surrounding
material. During times of temperature change we also observed differing levels of detection based on surrounding material. In sandy
like materials, such as play sand and crusher run gravel rock, the subsurface UXO were more visible. However, in grains of gravel
such as fine aggregate and 1.27 cm gravel rock, detection of subsurface UXO were less defined. Lastly, during a preliminary study at
the L and M site we buried various types of UXO in 1 inch (2.54 cm) to 5 inches of damp sand (12.7 cm). Despite a similar time, frame
and environmental conditions to those in our dry-sand investigation, the mines buried beneath damp sand were not detectable in
the captured images. Of all of the ordnances, the ones buried in dry sand and crusher run gravel rock were the most clearly defined,
therefore detectable, in the collected images.

Limitations, Disclaimers, and Data Usage

Several factors should be understood by teams using these data. First, all ordnance used in this work was inspected by EOD
specialists and verified to be inert. Although these conditions were necessary at this stage of the research, removal of explosive
materials from the ordnance almost certainly altered object density and, therefore, produced thermal behavior different from that
of live ordnance. Second, the ordnance used in this data collection varied widely in type. As expected, differences in ordnance
composition (i.e., steel, aluminum, Bakelite, rubber, plastic, etc.) resulted in different thermal behavior under these conditions.
