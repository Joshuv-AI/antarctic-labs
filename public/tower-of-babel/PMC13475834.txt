Therapeutic challenge in a 5-year-old girl with Gorlin-Goltz syndrome and cardiac fibroma
Authors: Agnieszka Pskit-Hanuszczak, Tomasz Książczyk, Radosław Pietrzak, Mariusz Furmanek, Mariusz Kuśmierczyk, Bożena Werner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gorlin-Goltz syndrome (GGS) is a rare hereditary autosomal dominant condition. Cardiac fibromas are one of the clinical manifestations of this disease. The presence of these benign heart tumors may lead to life-threatening complications. We present the case of a 5-year-old girl with GGS and a large cardiac fibroma who had ventricular fibrillation during respiratory tract infection. Several options of treatment were considered in the secondary prevention of cardiac arrest. A partial resection of the tumor was performed to prevent recurrence of malignant arrhythmia. The loop recorder implanted postoperatively didn’t record any episodes of sustained ventricular arrhythmia in a 9-month follow-up. In conclusion, cardiac fibromas, especially with the presence of infections, increase risk of ventricular arrhythmias. Partial resection of tumor can be effective and relatively safe in the secondary prevention of ventricular fibrillation in patients with large cardiac fibromas with GGS.

INTRODUCTION

Gorlin-Goltz syndrome (GGS), also referred to as basal cell naevus syndrome, is a rare hereditary autosomal dominant condition. The mutation in the tumor suppression patched-1 (PTCH1) gene is the most common cause of GGS.[1] The clinical manifestations of GGS include multiple basal cell carcinomas, odontogenic keratocysts of the jaw, and involvement of other organ systems.[2] Cardiac fibromas, which are benign heart tumors, occur in approximately 3% of GCS cases and may lead to ventricular outflow obstruction, arrhythmia, or heart failure.[3]

CLINICAL SUMMARY

We report the case of a 5-year-old girl diagnosed with hereditary GGS (father’s pathogenic PTCH1 mutation) and cardiac fibroma in the neonatal period. The girl had multiple dysmorphic features, including macrocephaly, polydactyly, and natal tooth. A large (50 mm × 30 mm) cardiac tumor attached to the posterolateral wall of the left ventricle and a patent arterial duct were found in echocardiography. Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed the presence of the tumor and revealed a second smaller tumor (16 mm × 10 mm) in the anterior and lateral wall of the left ventricle. The tumors did not cause any impairment in the hemodynamics, including no obstruction of blood flow to or from the left ventricle. At the age of 2 months, the girl underwent percutaneous patent arterial duct closure and pericardial drainage a month later. Ventricular extra beats were recorded in electrocardiography. Holter monitoring revealed episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, for which metoprolol therapy was initiated, resulting in satisfactory arrhythmia control.

At the age of 5 years, during the respiratory syncytial virus infection, a sudden episode of ventricular fibrillation occurred, resulting in cardiac arrest [Figure 1a]. Prompt resuscitation and cardiac defibrillation restored circulation. Then, sustained ventricular tachycardia with hemodynamic instability was observed. Three cardioversions and intravenous amiodarone administration resulted in conversion to the sinus rhythm. No subsequent neurological deficits were found.

Echocardiography showed a large tumor (68 mm × 35 mm) in the postero-lateral wall of the left ventricle, infiltrating subvalvular apparatus of the mitral valve [Figure 1b]. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed the fibroma infiltrating the lateral and inferior walls of the left ventricle, protruding into the posterior part of the mitral valve. Another heart tumor was visualized in the anterolateral wall of the left ventricle, adhering to the main fibroma. The tumors measured 78 mm × 52 mm × 31 mm and 23 mm × 17 mm, respectively [Figure 1c].

Due to the episode of life-threatening arrhythmia, pharmacotherapy and implantation of implantable cardioverter defibrillator were considered in secondary prevention of ventricular fibrillation. Another available therapeutic option was surgical removal of tumour. Thorough analysis of imaging studies excluded the possibility of total tumor resection due to the high risk of mitral valve impairment. The decision to perform a partial resection of the tumor to prevent recurrence of life-threatening arrhythmia was made.

The cardiac fibroma (confirmed histologically) was partially resected from the free wall of left ventricle with no complications [Figure 1d-f]. The mitral valve remained intact. During the postsurgical electrophysiological study, no ventricular arrhythmia was evoked. For long-term surveillance, an implantable loop recorder was placed for continuous arrhythmia monitoring. During 9 months of postsurgical follow-up, no sustained ventricular arrhythmia was detected, and there was no increase in tumor size during regular echocardiographic assessment.

DISCUSSION

Cardiac fibromas, the second most common heart tumors in children, occur in approximately 1 in 280,000 live births. Cardiac fibromas are one of the clinical manifestations of GGS and are associated with a high risk of malignant arrhythmias leading to sudden cardiac death.[4] They act as intramyocardial fibrotic scars that disrupt ventricular conduction.

There is no well-established standard of management for this condition. In the presented patient, ventricular fibrillation occurred during a respiratory infection. This indicates a higher risk of arrhythmia during the inflammatory process and increased sympathetic nervous system activity.

The most common treatment strategies include pharmacotherapy, typically with beta-blocker and amiodarone (this combination targets several electrophysiologic mechanisms involved in tumor-related ventricular tachyarrhythmias), cardioverter-defibrillator implantation (which is challenging in small children, but highly effective in preventing sudden cardiac death), and surgical intervention. Complete surgical resection of the fibroma is not feasible in cases with infiltration of the mitral valve apparatus, as this carries a high risk of permanent damage to mitral valve structures, which may lead to mechanical valve implantation. In our case, partial tumor resection was performed to reduce arrhythmogenic substrate and preserve cardiac function. The absence of recurrence of arrhythmia during the follow-up period, with continuous heart rhythm monitoring using an implantable loop recorder, supports the validity of this approach. The outcome of the surgical treatment is consistent with similar approaches described in the literature.[56]

CONCLUSION

The presented case emphasizes that cardiac fibromas, although rare, pose a significant risk of malignant arrhythmias, especially in the presence of additional triggers such as infections. The absence of arrhythmia recurrence postoperatively points that partial tumor resection can be an effective and relatively safe strategy for arrhythmia prevention in patients with large cardiac fibromas associated with GGS.
