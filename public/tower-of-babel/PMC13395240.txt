The age-group difference in psychological motivations for penile girth enhancement surgery in Chinese men: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yafei Zhang, Zhen Wang, Wei Wu, Yanbin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPenile girth enhancement (PGE) surgery has seen a global rise, with motivations shifting from purely medical indications to psychological and socio-cultural drivers. Data from Chinese populations, particularly concerning the psychological profiles and age-group differences among men seeking PGE, remain scarce. This study aimed to investigate the psychological and cultural mechanisms driving the demand for PGE in Chinese men, with a focus on age-group differences.MethodsA single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted on 136 men presenting for consultation regarding PGE at the Department of Urology of a major teaching hospital in Hefei. Participants were divided into 2 age-based groups: Young (<30 years, n = 81) and Older (≥30 years, n = 55). Measures included the Masculine Gender Stress Inventory Score (MGSIS), Appearance-related Masculinity Stress (MGRSS-A), Social Comparison, Sexual Self-Esteem (SSE), and a composite Psychological Distress index (GAD-7/PHQ-9). Statistical analyses included independent-samples t-tests, binary logistic regression, and a structural equation model (SEM) to explore the psychological mechanism.ResultsThe total sample size was 136. Young men exhibited significantly higher scores in Social Comparison (mean: 68.78 vs 49.96, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.52), Appearance-related Masculinity Stress (mean: 79.62 vs 53.03, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.45), and Psychological Distress (mean: 62.80 vs 42.06, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.88) compared to the Older group. Logistic regression identified Psychological Distress (OR = 1.08 per unit, 95% CI: 1.01-1.15, P = .021) as an independent predictor of surgical intent. The SEM confirmed a significant mediation pathway from age-group difference to psychological distress and then to surgical intent, with a standardized indirect effect of β_std = –0.25 (95% BCI: –0.40 to –0.10).DiscussionThe demand for PGE in young Chinese men is predominantly driven by psychological and cultural stress related to appearance and social comparison, marking a paradigm shift from traditional functional or pathological concerns. This age-group difference underscores the influence of modern media and social environment on male body image. Clinically, mandatory pre-operative psychological screening and the establishment of clear referral pathways for men with high psychological distress are essential to ensure ethical practice and optimize patient outcomes in PGE surgery.

Introduction

Global rise of penile girth enhancement and changing clinical profiles

The field of cosmetic genital surgery has expanded significantly in recent decades, with penile girth enhancement (PGE) becoming one of the most frequently requested procedures among men globally.1,2 Historically, penile augmentation procedures were sometimes considered for men reporting severe distress related to penile size, including those meeting criteria for penile dysmorphic disorder (PDD). However, current clinical consensus considers PDD a contraindication for surgery; these patients should be referred for psychiatric or psychological evaluation rather than being offered surgical intervention.3,4 However, a growing body of literature, predominantly from Western countries, suggests a notable shift in the underlying motivations for seeking these procedures.5,6 The profile of the typical patient is evolving from one with a clear medical or psychiatric diagnosis to an individual driven by psychological and social factors, such as a desire for improved self-confidence, enhanced aesthetic appearance, and a perceived need to conform to idealized standards of masculinity.7,8 This transition reflects a broader societal trend where cosmetic surgery is increasingly viewed as a means of self-improvement and social advantage rather than solely a corrective measure.9,10 Despite this global trend, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding the specific psychological and cultural dynamics at play within the rapidly modernizing Chinese male population.11,12

Gaps in existing literature

Existing research on men seeking PGE is characterized by several limitations. First, much of the focus has been disproportionately placed on the pathological extreme, namely, PDD.13,14 While PDD is a critical concern, this narrow focus overlooks a large cohort of men who, while not meeting the full diagnostic criteria for a mental disorder, are nonetheless experiencing significant subclinical psychological distress and social pressure that drives their surgical intent.15–17 Second, and most critically for this study, there is a profound lack of data from China.17 The psychological and cultural constructs of masculinity, body image, and social comparison are highly context-dependent, and findings from Western cohorts may not be directly applicable to Chinese men, who operate within a distinct social and media environment.18,19 Finally, the literature lacks a cohort perspective and a robust psychological–cultural mechanism model to explain the observed shift in motivations. Understanding how media exposure and differing socialization pathways influence younger age groups is crucial for contemporary clinical practice.20

Theoretical framework

To address these gaps, this study is grounded in 3 established psychological theories:

Body Image Theory: This theory posits that an individual’s perception and attitude towards their own body shape and size significantly influence their psychological well-being and behavior.21,22 For men seeking PGE, a negative genital self-image, even in the absence of objective size deficiency, can be a powerful motivator for surgical intervention.23

Masculinity Stress Theory: This framework suggests that men experience psychological distress when they perceive themselves as failing to meet culturally prescribed masculine norms.24,25 In the context of PGE, this stress can manifest as anxiety related to sexual performance, physical appearance, and social dominance, all of which are often linked to penile size in cultural narratives.26

Study hypotheses and objectives

Based on the theoretical framework and identified literature gaps, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1 (Internal Comparison): Young men (<30 years) will exhibit significantly higher levels of Social Comparison, Masculinity Stress, and Psychological Distress compared to older men (≥30 years) seeking PGE consultation in China.

H1 (Internal Comparison): Young men (<30 years) will exhibit significantly higher levels of Social Comparison, Masculinity Stress, and Psychological Distress compared to older men (≥30 years) seeking PGE consultation in China.

H2: Psychological variables (Masculinity Stress, Social Comparison, Psychological Distress) will be independent predictors of surgical intent, while self-reported penile girth will not.

H2: Psychological variables (Masculinity Stress, Social Comparison, Psychological Distress) will be independent predictors of surgical intent, while self-reported penile girth will not.

H3: The relationship between Age-Group Difference and Surgical Intent will be significantly mediated by Psychological Distress, establishing a “Psychological-Cultural Mechanism Model” for PGE demand.

H3: The relationship between Age-Group Difference and Surgical Intent will be significantly mediated by Psychological Distress, establishing a “Psychological-Cultural Mechanism Model” for PGE demand.

The primary objectives of this study are 2-fold: First, to identify the psychological characteristics and age-group differences among Chinese men seeking PGE consultation (Internal Comparison), and second, to contextualize these findings against the existing Western literature to highlight the unique cultural drivers in China (International Comparison).

Therefore, this study aimed to answer the following refutable research question: Among Chinese men seeking penile girth enhancement surgery, do younger men (<30 years) and older men (≥30 years) differ significantly in their levels of social comparison, appearance-related masculinity stress, and psychological distress, and does psychological distress mediate the relationship between age-group difference and surgical intent?

Study design and participants

This was a single-center, outpatient, cross-sectional study conducted at the Department of Urology of a major teaching hospital in Hefei, China, between September 2023 and September 2025.

Penile girth measurement

Participants were instructed to measure penile circumference at the mid-shaft using a flexible measuring tape, during erect state, following standardized written instructions with diagrams. Self-reported values were used for analysis.

Measures and questionnaires

All participants completed a standardized, self-administered questionnaire package.

Demographic and clinical variables

Data collected included age, partner status (married/co-habiting vs single/dating), education level, and sexual experience. Participants were also asked to provide their self-reported penile girth (circumference in cm) as a key covariate to assess the “non-pathological” nature of the cohort. Surgical intent was measured as a binary variable (Yes/No) based on the patient’s expressed desire to proceed with the surgery after the initial consultation.

Men with a measured erect girth below 8.0 cm were excluded from analysis, as this falls below the fifth percentile of normative values reported for Chinese men.

Definition of age-based groups

Based on the hypothesis of a media-driven shift in motivations, a clear cut-off point was required to delineate distinct socialization pathways. We defined the age-based groups as:

Young age group: Men aged <30 years (n = 81)

Older age group: Men aged ≥30 years (n = 55)

This cut-off is theoretically justified by the differential exposure to the pervasive, unfiltered social media and online content environment that became dominant in China around the late 2000s and early 2010s. Men under 30 have largely undergone their critical socialization period entirely within this new media landscape, leading to a potentially distinct set of body image and masculinity norms compared to their older counterparts. We acknowledge that this cross-sectional grouping reflects age-group differences or cohort differences rather than a true generational effect, and we will use cautious language in the discussion.

Results

Baseline characteristics and surgical intent

A total of 136 men were included in the final analysis. The Young age group (<30 years) comprised 81 men (59.6%), and the Older age group (≥30 years) comprised 55 men (40.4%). The mean age of the total cohort was 33.09 ± 12.89 years. As expected, the mean age was significantly different between the Young group (23.66 ± 3.23 years) and the Older group (46.97 ± 8.39 years) (P < .001). Self-reported penile girth (mean 11.48 ± 0.77 cm) did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (P = .391).

Overall, 73.5% (n = 100) of the men expressed a definite intent to proceed with PGE surgery. The rate of surgical intent was significantly higher in the younger age group (89.0%) compared with the older age group (50.9%) (χ2 = 25.4, P < .001). Other baseline characteristics, including partner status, education level, and sexual experience, showed no significant differences between the 2 age groups (Table 1; Figure 1).

Discussion

Principal findings

The current study, based on a cohort of 136 Chinese men seeking PGE consultation, reveals a clear shift in the primary motivation for the procedure, particularly when comparing different age groups. The most striking finding is the significantly higher level of psychological distress, appearance-related masculinity stress, and social comparison among the Young age group (<30 years) compared to the Older age group (≥30 years). This suggests that the demand for PGE has largely transitioned from being a function-oriented concern to a psychological and social-driven phenomenon in the younger cohort.29,30 Furthermore, the mediation analysis confirmed that the age-group difference in surgical intent is significantly explained by the mediating role of psychological distress, supporting our proposed “Psychological-Cultural Mechanism Model.” We distinguished our sample from PDD patients by excluding those who met DSM-5 criteria for BDD. Our participants reported distress that was subclinical in nature—situationally triggered (eg, by social media exposure) and focused on social comparison rather than a fixed, delusional belief about deformity.

The very large effect observed for appearance-related masculinity stress suggests that this is not merely dissatisfaction but a source of significant psychological burden.31

Age-group difference versus age effect (internal comparison)

While our cross-sectional design cannot definitively distinguish between a true generational (cohort) effect and a simple age (life-cycle) effect, the magnitude of the differences observed strongly suggests a cohort influence. The large effect sizes for MGRSS-A (d = 2.45) and Social Comparison (d = 1.52) are difficult to explain solely by the natural aging process. We hypothesize that men under 30, having undergone their formative years entirely within the era of ubiquitous social media and high-speed internet, have been exposed to a different set of social norms and comparison targets than their older counterparts.34 This differential media exposure and socialization pathway is the likely driver of the observed age-group difference, a term we use cautiously to reflect the methodological limitation while acknowledging the substantive shift.35 This internal comparison within the Chinese cohort clearly demonstrates a paradigm shift in motivation from the older, more traditional concerns to the younger, more psychologically and socially driven concerns.

Cultural implications and comparison with Western literature (international comparison)

Past vs present (cohort-based shift)

The data clearly illustrate a shift in the primary concern. The Older age group, while still seeking PGE, shows a less extreme psychological profile and a relatively higher score on Function Expectation (though still lower than Appearance Expectation). This suggests their motivation may be closer to the historical, function-oriented model.36 In contrast, the Young age group’s overwhelming focus on appearance and extreme psychological distress marks a clear departure, defining the contemporary profile of the PGE patient in China.

China vs Western literature

Our findings contrast sharply with much of the Western literature, which remains heavily PDD-centric.13 While PDD is a concern globally, our cohort’s primary driver is subclinical psychological distress rooted in collective and relational masculinity stress—a concept particularly relevant in Chinese culture. Western masculinity often emphasizes individual achievement and dominance; Chinese masculinity, however, frequently incorporates relational and collective aspects where one’s appearance and success are tied to family honor and social standing.15–17 The amplified social comparison in China’s high-pressure social environment translates the pressure to “succeed” into the pressure to “look successful,” which includes conforming to physical ideals. This cultural context explains why social comparison and appearance stress are such powerful mediators in our Chinese cohort, a nuance often missed in Western PDD–focused studies.37–39 This comparison highlights that while the outcome (seeking PGE) is the same, the underlying psychological mechanism is distinctively shaped by the Chinese cultural context. In contrast to our findings, Western studies have emphasized PDD as the primary driver of penile size concerns, with a focus on individual psychopathology. However, more recent Western research has begun to identify subclinical motivators. For example, a 2022 US-based study found that 62% of men seeking penile enhancement reported social media as a source of concern, similar to our observations. A key difference is that Western samples tend to show weaker associations with collective/relational masculinity stress, which played a prominent role in our Chinese cohort—consistent with the more interdependent self-construal typical in East Asian cultures. While our study focused on Chinese men, the phenomenon of social media-driven body image concerns is not unique to China. Similar patterns have been documented across multiple countries (eg, South Korea, Brazil, the United States) and for various aesthetic procedures (eg, rhinoplasty, pectoral implants), suggesting a broader global trend where digital media reshapes body image norms across cultures. Our findings contribute to this international literature by providing data from a previously understudied large popula tion.

Clinical implications

Currently, preoperative evaluation for PGE primarily focuses on anatomical suitability and surgical risks. Our findings suggest an adjunctive approach: Age-group-specific psychological screening. We do not advocate universal psychiatric referral for all candidates. Rather, we propose:

Younger men (<30 years) with high Social Comparison or MGRSS-A scores should receive psychoeducation about media literacy and, if distress is severe, a referral to a mental health professional.

Older men (≥30 years) with elevated PHQ-9/GAD-7 scores should be evaluated for treatable anxiety or depression before surgical consideration.

This stratified approach differs from current practice by targeting screening resources to those most likely to benefit, rather than applying a blanket policy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study using a convenience sample of men who presented for consultation at a urology clinic. Findings may not generalize to the broader Chinese male population or to men seeking PGE in other clinical settings (eg, private cosmetic clinics). Second, the sample is subject to selection bias, as participants were already motivated to seek surgical consultation and may differ systematically from men with similar concerns who do not seek consultation. Third, the reliance on self-reported measures, including penile girth, introduces potential reporting bias. we did not directly assess participants’ perceptions of what constitutes “normal” penile dimensions. Such normative misperceptions—often propagated by social media and pornography—may be a key mediator linking social comparison to distress. Future studies should include validated measures of perceived norms. Finally, while we used established scales, the Social Comparison scale was adapted for genital appearance, and although psychometric properties were acceptable, further validation is warranted. Additionally, this study lacked a control group of men not seeking girth enhancement surgery, which limits our ability to attribute the observed psychological patterns specifically to the surgical-seeking population rather than to broader generational trends in body image concerns.

Future directions

Future research should include longitudinal studies to track the stability of motivations and the long-term psychological outcomes of PGE. Multi-center and cross-cultural studies are needed to validate the “Psychological-Cultural Mechanism Model” in diverse populations. Finally, comparative studies assessing the efficacy of psychological interventions versus surgical intervention for men with high appearance-related distress are crucial for evidence-based practice.

Conclusion

This study confirms a paradigm shift in the motivation for PGE surgery in Chinese men, particularly in the younger cohort, where the primary driver is psychological distress mediated by appearance-related masculinity stress and social comparison, rather than functional concerns. This finding necessitates the integration of mandatory psychological assessment into the pre-operative protocol for PGE to ensure ethical practice and address the underlying psychological–cultural drivers of this growing demand. The clear age-group difference in motivation within China, coupled with the distinct psychological profile compared to Western cohorts, underscores the need for culturally sensitive clinical guidelines.
