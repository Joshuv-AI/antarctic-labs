Levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation to 8 years after first delivery according to vaginal parity: prospective cohort study with an 8‐year recall extension
Authors: F. Siafarikas, J. Šaltytė Benth, J. Stær‐Jensen, C. Reimers, K. Bø, M. Ellström Engh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe first aim was to describe longitudinal changes in levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation in the first pregnancy to 1 and 8 years after first delivery, according to subsequent vaginal parity in women with vaginal delivery or Cesarean section (CS) at first delivery. The second aim was to assess the effect of subsequent vaginal delivery on levator hiatus area after first vaginal delivery.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study with an 8‐year recall extension, 183 initially nulliparous women underwent three‐ and four‐dimensional transperineal ultrasound examination at 21 weeks' gestation and 1 and 8 years after first delivery. The levator hiatus area at rest, during maximum pelvic floor muscle contraction and during maximum Valsalva maneuver was assessed at each timepoint. Participants with multiple vaginal deliveries (vaginal multiparae), only one vaginal delivery (vaginal primiparae), CS for all deliveries and vaginal birth after CS were compared. The differences between study groups in levator hiatus area over time were assessed using a linear mixed model.ResultsOverall, vaginal multiparae showed a statistically significant non‐linear increase in levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation to 8 years after first delivery (P < 0.001). In contrast, participants who delivered exclusively by CS showed a decrease in levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation to the 8‐year follow‐up. Subsequent vaginal delivery after an initial vaginal delivery did not affect the levator hiatus area during Valsalva maneuver. At the 1‐year and 8‐year follow‐up after the first vaginal delivery, levator hiatus area at rest remained unchanged in vaginal multiparae but decreased in vaginal primiparae. At the 8‐year follow‐up, vaginal multiparae had a larger hiatus area during pelvic floor muscle contraction than did vaginal primiparae.ConclusionsIn this study, the levator hiatus area was influenced by the mode of the first delivery and by subsequent vaginal deliveries. Overall, the levator hiatus area increased from 21 weeks' gestation to 8 years in participants with at least one vaginal delivery and decreased in those who delivered exclusively by CS. Subsequent vaginal delivery after a first did not seem to affect the levator hiatus area measured during Valsalva maneuver. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

The levator ani muscle forms a sling that borders the levator hiatus, an opening through which the pelvic organs may prolapse. The levator hiatus can be assessed using three‐dimensional (3D) and four‐dimensional (4D) transperineal ultrasound, and a larger levator hiatus area has been observed in women with symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse and in those with prolapse recurrence 1 , 2 , 3 . Pregnancy increases the size of the levator hiatus, but this change is temporary 4 , 5 , 6 and has not been found to increase the risk of pelvic organ prolapse 7 , 8 . However, a persistent enlargement of the levator hiatus area as a result of extensive stretching and/or tearing of the levator ani muscle during vaginal delivery 9 , 10 , 11 is suspected to contribute to the development of female pelvic organ prolapse 3 , 8 , 12 . However, it may take decades after childbirth before symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse occurs 13 .

The effect of a first pregnancy and delivery on the levator hiatus area up to 1 year postpartum has been described previously by our research group and others 11 , 14 , 15 , 16 . Less is known about the dimensions of the levator hiatus beyond the first postpartum year and whether subsequent vaginal deliveries impact the levator hiatus area. Given the association between increased levator hiatus area following childbirth and symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse later in life, longitudinal data on the levator hiatus area in normal childbearing populations may provide insights into the mechanism underlying prolapse development.

The aim of this study was two‐fold. First, we aimed to describe longitudinal changes in the levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation in the first pregnancy until 1 year and 8 years after the first delivery, according to subsequent vaginal parity among women who had a vaginal delivery or Cesarean section (CS) at first delivery. Second, we aimed to assess the effect of subsequent vaginal delivery on levator hiatus area after a first vaginal delivery. Our null hypothesis was that there is no difference in levator hiatus area during Valsalva maneuver between women with and those without a subsequent vaginal delivery.

METHODS

This was a single‐center prospective cohort study with an 8‐year recall extension conducted at our center (Akershus University Hospital, Lørenskog, Norway). Between January 2010 and October 2012, 300 nulliparous women at 17–19 weeks' gestation were recruited and followed‐up until 1 year after their first delivery 4 , 15 , 17 . Inclusion criteria were a singleton pregnancy and the ability to speak a Scandinavian language, and exclusion criteria were a previous pregnancy of > 16 weeks' gestation and serious maternal or fetal pathology. Patients who had a preterm delivery (< 32 weeks) or stillbirth at first delivery were also excluded. To assess long‐term changes in pelvic organ support, a follow‐up of the study population was performed between October 2017 and June 2020 18 , 19 . Participants from the initial cohort were contacted by telephone and invited for follow‐up assessment, scheduled at least 6 months after their most recent delivery. Participants who underwent pelvic floor surgery during the follow‐up period and those with missing data regarding subsequent delivery were excluded. Hence, the final cohort included in the analysis was a convenience sample constrained by the responders from the original 2010–2012 cohort. All included women gave written informed consent for participation in the initial cohort study and the 8‐year follow‐up. Ethical approval was obtained from the Regional Ethics Committee (REK South‐East; refs.: 2009/170 and 2017/89) and the hospital's data protection officer (refs.: 17‐055 and 17‐086) for both parts of the study.

From 6 weeks to 6 months after their first delivery, 139 participants took part in a concurrent randomized controlled trial (RCT) aiming to explore the effect of pelvic floor muscle training on pelvic floor anatomy and function. No association between pelvic floor muscle training with levator hiatus area and levator ani avulsion was found 20 .

Participants were examined at 21 weeks in the first pregnancy and at 1 year and 8 years after their first delivery. Assessments included 3D and 4D transperineal ultrasound examination of the pelvic floor using the GE Kretz Voluson E8 ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria) with a 4–8‐MHz curved‐array 3D/4D ultrasound transducer (RAB4‐8l/obstetric). Ultrasound volumes were acquired at rest, during maximum pelvic floor muscle contraction and during maximum Valsalva maneuver. The ability to perform a correct pelvic floor muscle contraction was assessed by visual observation and vaginal palpation to evaluate squeeze and inward movement by a trained physical therapist. Verbal instructions, biofeedback using ultrasound and repetition were used to optimize the Valsalva maneuver and avoid co‐contraction of the levator ani muscle 21 . Each maneuver was recorded three times and the ultrasound volumes were analyzed offline (4D View; GE Healthcare). The levator hiatus area, defined as the area bordered by the levator ani muscle, the pubic symphysis and the pubic inferior ramus, was measured in the axial plane using render mode 22 around the plane of minimal hiatal dimensions (Figure 1). The ultrasound volume acquired during maximum pelvic floor muscle contraction, defined as the volume with the shortest anteroposterior diameter (from the posteroinferior margin of the pubic symphysis to the levator ani muscle) in the midsagittal plane, and the ultrasound volume acquired during maximum Valsalva maneuver, defined as the volume with the longest anteroposterior diameter in the midsagittal plane, were chosen for analysis. Rest position was defined as the most caudal position of the levator ani muscle before a muscle contraction.

Three‐dimensional transperineal ultrasound imaging around plane of minimal hiatal dimensions, showing levator hiatus (LH) area (dotted yellow line (measured using render mode)) during pelvic floor muscle contraction in patient with subsequent vaginal delivery after first vaginal delivery at (a) 21 weeks' gestation in first pregnancy (LH area, 7.50 cm2), (b) 1 year after first delivery (LH area, 7.86 cm2) and (c) 8 years after first delivery (LH area, 8.59 cm2).

Two trained investigators (F.S., J.S.‐J.) performed the examinations and analyzed the ultrasound volumes according to the methodology described above. The inter‐rater reliability for levator hiatus area was excellent (intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.96) 23 , 24 . Levator ani avulsion was analyzed and classified according to previously described methodology 25 , 26 , 27 . The investigators were blinded to previous ultrasound findings, participants' demographic and obstetric data, and their participation in the RCT on the effect of pelvic floor muscle training.

The exposure was subsequent vaginal delivery, according to delivery mode (vaginal delivery or CS) at first delivery. Four study groups were defined: vaginal multipara (vaginal delivery at first delivery and at least one subsequent vaginal delivery); vaginal primipara (vaginal delivery at first delivery without subsequent vaginal delivery, i.e. primiparous or multiparous with CS as subsequent delivery); CS (women who only had CS (primiparous or multiparous)); and vaginal birth after CS (VBAC) (women with CS at first delivery and subsequent vaginal delivery). The outcomes measured were levator hiatus area at rest, during maximum pelvic floor muscle contraction and during maximum Valsalva maneuver, assessed at 21 weeks' gestation in the first pregnancy, 1 year after first delivery and 8 years after first delivery.

Demographic information and data from the first delivery were extracted from electronic maternal medical records. An electronic questionnaire, which included questions about any additional deliveries, was completed by the participants 8 years after their first delivery.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and STATA version 18 (StataCorp. LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Demographic and obstetric data, as well as the levator hiatus area, are presented as n (%) or mean ± SD, as appropriate. A linear mixed model with random intercepts for participants was used to assess the differences between the study groups in levator hiatus area over time. The model included fixed effects for the timepoints and study groups, and the interaction between them. Standard residual diagnostics were performed. Results are shown graphically. As a post‐hoc analysis, to be interpreted as purely exploratory, the mean between‐group differences in levator hiatus area with 95% CI at each timepoint were estimated. Missing data for the outcome variables were handled using the inherent maximum likelihood estimation of the linear mixed model, which uses all available observations under the missing‐at‐random assumption. No imputation procedures were applied.

The differences in change in levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation to 1 and 8 years after first delivery and the between‐group differences, extracted from the linear mixed model, were used to test our hypothesis. For sensitivity analysis, the linear mixed models described above were adjusted for levator ani avulsion. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. An a‐priori power calculation was not performed.

RESULTS

Study population

Of the 300 participants enrolled in the study at 21 weeks, nine were excluded from the study (five for preterm delivery or stillbirth at first delivery, one owing to pelvic floor surgery in the follow‐up period and three owing to missing data regarding subsequent delivery) and 108 participants dropped out of the study at 1 or 8 years after first delivery (Figure 2). Overall, 183 participants attended the ultrasound examination 8 years after first delivery and were included in the final cohort. Ten patients did not attend the 1‐year follow‐up examination and therefore ultrasound data for these patients 1 year after first delivery were missing. There were no missing data for specific variables of interest among the final 183 participants.

Overall, there were no significant differences in levator hiatus area between vaginal multiparae and the other study groups (Tables 2 and S1), nor between any of the other study groups according to post‐hoc analysis (Table S3), at 21 weeks. There was a significant non‐linear trend observed in the vaginal‐multipara group, with an increase in levator hiatus area at rest, during pelvic floor muscle contraction and during Valsalva maneuver from 21 weeks' gestation to 1 year after first delivery and from 21 weeks' gestation to 8 years after first delivery (P < 0.001 for all) (Tables 2 and S1). The change in levator hiatus area at rest from 21 weeks' gestation to the 8‐year follow‐up was significantly different between the vaginal‐multipara group and vaginal‐primipara group (P = 0.03) (Table S1). In the CS group, the levator hiatus area decreased from 21 weeks' gestation to the 8‐year follow‐up. In the VBAC group, we observed an increase in levator hiatus area from the 1‐year to 8‐year follow‐up at rest, during pelvic floor muscle contraction and during Valsalva maneuver. Compared with vaginal multiparae, there was no significant difference in levator hiatus area at rest, during pelvic floor muscle contraction or during Valsalva maneuver at the 8‐year follow up (Table S3).

Levator hiatus area and subsequent vaginal delivery

The levator hiatus area during Valsalva maneuver did not differ significantly between the vaginal‐primipara and vaginal‐multipara groups at 1 and 8 years after first delivery (Table S3). Post‐hoc analysis (data not shown) demonstrated that, from the 1‐year to 8‐year follow‐up, the levator hiatus area during Valsalva maneuver remained unchanged in participants with and without subsequent vaginal delivery after first vaginal delivery. There was no significant difference in change in the levator hiatus area during Valsalva maneuver from 1 to 8 years between the study groups.

Levator hiatus area at rest also did not differ significantly between the vaginal‐primipara and vaginal‐multipara groups at 1 and 8 years after first delivery (Table S3). Post‐hoc analysis (data not shown) demonstrated that, from the 1‐year to 8‐year follow‐up, the levator hiatus area at rest remained unchanged in the vaginal‐multipara group but decreased significantly in the vaginal‐primipara group (P = 0.005). The difference in the change in the levator hiatus area at rest from the 1‐year to 8‐year follow‐up was statistically significant when comparing vaginal primi‐ and multiparae (P = 0.006).

While at the 1‐year follow‐up after first delivery there was no statistically significant difference in levator hiatus area during pelvic floor muscle contraction between the vaginal‐multipara group and the vaginal‐primipara group, at the 8‐year follow‐up the levator hiatus area was significantly larger in the vaginal‐multipara group (mean difference, 0.89 (95% CI, 0.05–1.72) cm2) (Table S3). The change in levator hiatus area during contraction from 1 to 8 years after first delivery did not differ significantly between study groups.

The sensitivity analysis adjusting for levator ani avulsion (Tables 2 and S1) showed similar results, with only minor alterations, and the overall conclusion remained unchanged.

DISCUSSION

Overall, vaginal multiparae showed a statistically significant non‐linear increase in levator hiatus area from 21 weeks' gestation to 1 year and to 8 years after first delivery. In contrast, participants who delivered exclusively by CS showed a decrease in levator hiatus area at the 8‐year follow‐up. Subsequent vaginal delivery after a first did not seem to affect the levator hiatus area measured during Valsalva maneuver.

This study is one of few providing a comprehensive description of longitudinal changes in levator hiatus area from first pregnancy through to long‐term follow‐up in women who had a vaginal delivery at the first delivery (vaginal primiparae and vaginal multiparae), women who had CS for all deliveries and women who had VBAC 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 . Despite limited comparability due to differing research questions and statistical methods, our results are consistent with prior studies that observed that women with at least one vaginal delivery had a larger levator hiatus area than women with CS for all deliveries 28 , 29 . Furthermore, in the VBAC group, our results demonstrate a larger increase in levator hiatus area compared with vaginal multiparae from the 1‐year to 8‐year follow‐up, but no significant difference in the levator hiatus area at 8 years. This finding is consistent with a prior study designed specifically to assess the effect of vaginal birth after CS31. However, the small sample size in the VBAC group makes these findings highly susceptible to individual outliers. In addition, the absence of levator ani avulsion in this specific subgroup should be interpreted with extreme caution, as it is unlikely to be representative of the broader VBAC population.

Although epidemiological data link vaginal multiparity with an increased risk of pelvic organ prolapse 32 , 33 , previous ultrasound studies have not shown this association 34 , 35 . Our data support previous ultrasound findings and suggest that the first vaginal delivery impacts the size of the levator hiatus area during Valsalva maneuver, with no further significant changes after subsequent vaginal deliveries by the 8‐year follow‐up. However, despite the strongest associations between levator hiatus area and pelvic organ prolapse being reported for measurements obtained during Valsalva maneuver 1 , the levator hiatus size at rest and during pelvic floor muscle contraction may likewise be important for pelvic support. Research on pelvic floor muscle training has shown that improvements in muscle strength reduce prolapse stage and symptoms and decrease levator hiatus area both at rest and during contraction 36 . In our study, the impact of subsequent vaginal delivery on the levator hiatus area at rest was revealed through our longitudinal approach. While the levator hiatus area at rest did not change from the 1‐year to 8‐year follow‐up for the vaginal‐multipara group, it decreased significantly in participants who did not have any subsequent vaginal deliveries. Previous studies point in the same direction: one study found that the levator hiatus area at rest was larger in vaginal multiparae compared with vaginal primiparae 3–5 years after their first delivery 28 , and a decrease in the levator hiatus area at rest was observed in vaginal primiparae at 4‐year follow‐up in a UK cohort 29 .

We also found an association between levator hiatus area during pelvic floor muscle contraction and subsequent vaginal delivery. In our study, the levator hiatus area during pelvic floor muscle contraction was significantly larger at the 8‐year follow‐up in participants with multiple vaginal deliveries compared with those without subsequent vaginal delivery. Our findings may be biased by the higher rates of levator ani avulsions in the vaginal‐multipara group. To account for this, we controlled for levator ani avulsion in our linear mixed model and the results remained unchanged.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study lie in the longitudinal design and inclusion of participants from an unselected pregnant population. However, the study experienced a substantial dropout rate of 36.0%. Non‐participation may have led to selection bias, as individuals experiencing pelvic floor symptoms may have been more or less likely to respond to the 8‐year recall invitation. This may impair the validity and generalizability of the study results. Nevertheless, our study population was comparable with the background population delivering at our center and non‐attenders. Transperineal ultrasound measurements were assessed by trained clinicians and researchers.

No a‐priori power calculation was performed, therefore it remains unclear whether the available sample size was powered adequately for the interaction analyses performed, particularly for the small VBAC subgroup (n = 11). Furthermore, the large number of comparisons in the post‐hoc analysis increased the risk of Type‐I errors. We did not adjust for multiple testing in our analyses; results with P‐values < 0.01 should be interpreted with caution. We specify clearly which analyses were performed post‐hoc and thus are to be interpreted as exploratory. Moreover, the heterogeneity regarding the number of subsequent deliveries and the delivery mode in subsequent deliveries is a limitation. To increase the sample size, group allocation was based on the assumption that pregnancy has a negligible effect on the levator hiatus area; while hormonal changes and mechanical loading during pregnancy may increase the levator hiatus area 6 , evidence suggests that this increase is temporary 4 , 5 , 6 and that pregnancy is not associated with pelvic organ prolapse 7 , 37 .

Furthermore, the average time interval from the last delivery to the 8‐year follow‐up was shorter for the vaginal‐multipara group and the VBAC group compared with the vaginal‐primipara group. This may have contributed to an underestimation of the differences between groups. It remains unclear whether the trends illustrated in our findings will continue over time or whether they will stabilize. Thus, whether the resting levator hiatus area in vaginal primiparae could be influenced by this longer interval since the last obstetric trauma remains speculative. Ideally, the levator hiatus area should have been measured after each delivery.

Conclusions

In our study, the levator hiatus area was influenced by the mode of first delivery and by subsequent vaginal deliveries. In general, the levator hiatus area increased from 21 weeks' gestation to 8 years after first delivery in participants with at least one vaginal delivery, and decreased in those who delivered exclusively by CS. Subsequent vaginal delivery after a first did not seem to affect the levator hiatus area measured during the Valsalva maneuver.
