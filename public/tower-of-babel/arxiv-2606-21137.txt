Introduction

Free probability was introduced by Voiculescu in the 1980s as a noncommutative probability theory in which the notion of independence is replaced by a noncommutative counterpart called freeness. Although this theory was initially developed in the context of operator algebras, it has found many applications.
One of the first striking connections was with random matrix theory, when
Voiculescu showed that independent GUE matrices become asymptotically free in the limit as their size tends to infinity
[
23
]
.
Strong asymptotic freeness strengthens this by asserting that the norms of polynomials in independent random matrices converge to the norms of the corresponding free operators in the reduced free product
C^{*}
-algebra. This was first proved for GUE matrices by Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen
[
11
]
, and quantitative forms were later developed by Collins, Guionnet and Parraud
[
9
]
.

One common route from polynomial norm convergence to spectral estimates is the
linearization trick, which replaces a polynomial by a larger self-adjoint linear
pencil with matrix coefficients. However, the strong-convergence proofs
themselves use several different mechanisms. Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen’s
original proof uses a master equation for the Stieltjes transform of the
spectrum; Collins, Guionnet and Parraud’s approach relies on interpolating
between the GUE and a free product; other techniques rely on moment
methods
[
1
]
, matrix concentration
[
4
]
, and Markov
inequalities and interpolation techniques
[
7
,
8
,
20
]
.

The goal of the present paper is different. We study directly a linear
matrix-coefficient model and show how far one can obtain non-asymptotic
spectral-edge bounds using only Gaussian concentration and comparison
inequalities. This model is natural in its own right: it is a block Gaussian
matrix with a macroscopic covariance pattern. The resulting bound is not sharp
in general, but it is sharp in the regime of uniformly bounded positive
coefficients and
N=o(M)
.

Specifically, we focus on the linear matrix-coefficient model

H_{M}=A_{0}\otimes I_{M}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\otimes G_{i},

where
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
are Hermitian. In the
free limit,
M^{-1/2}G_{i}
is replaced by a free semicircular variable

s_{i}=l_{i}+l_{i}^{*}
, where
l_{i}
are the canonical creation operators on full Fock
space. The following edge formulas are extracted from Lehner’s semicircular
operator-norm formula.

Theorem 1.1

(Edge formulas from Lehner’s semicircular formula)
.

Let
l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}
be the canonical creation operators on full Fock space and
let
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
with
a_{0}=a_{0}^{*}
and
a_{i}=a_{i}^{*}
. Put

x=a_{0}\otimes I+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes(l_{i}+l_{i}^{*}).

Then

\lambda_{\max}(x)=\inf_{z\succ 0}\lambda_{\max}\left(z+a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}z^{-1}a_{i}\right)

and

\lambda_{\min}(x)=\sup_{z\prec 0}\lambda_{\min}\left(z+a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}z^{-1}a_{i}\right).

Lehner states the corresponding operator-norm formula in

[
14
, Corollary 1.5]
. The same proof gives the right-edge formula above;
the left-edge formula follows by applying the right-edge formula to
-x
.

We apply this with
m=N
and
a_{i}=A_{i}
. Define the free right and left edges by

\rho_{+}:=\inf_{Z\succ 0}\lambda_{\max}\left(A_{0}+Z+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}Z^{-1}A_{i}\right)

(1)

and

\rho_{-}:=\sup_{Z\prec 0}\lambda_{\min}\left(A_{0}+Z+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}Z^{-1}A_{i}\right).

(2)

We also write

\rho_{*}=\max\{\rho_{+},-\rho_{-}\}

for the norm of the corresponding free self-adjoint operator.
Our goal is to show
how far one can go using only Gaussian concentration and comparison inequalities.
The main result is the following finite-dimensional expected operator-norm
estimate.

{restatable*}

[Main theorem]theoremmainthm

Assume that

A_{i}\succeq 0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

and assume
M\geq N
. Then

\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho_{*}+9\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

The constant
9
in Theorem
1
is universal: it does not depend on

n,N,M
, or on the coefficient matrices.

Corollary 1.2

(Asymptotic consequence)
.

Let
n
be fixed. For each
M
, let

A_{0,M},A_{1,M},\ldots,A_{n,M}\in M_{N_{M}}(\mathbb{C})
be Hermitian matrices with

A_{i,M}\succeq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and assume
N_{M}=o(M)
. Suppose
there is a constant
K
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|A_{i,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K

for all
M
. Let
\rho_{+,M}
,
\rho_{-,M}
be the free right and left
edges defined in (
1
)–(
2
) for the coefficient
family
A_{0,M},A_{1,M},\ldots,A_{n,M}
, and set

\rho_{*,M}=\max\{\rho_{+,M},-\rho_{-,M}\}.

Let
H_{M}
be formed from these coefficients as in Theorem
1
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho_{*,M}+o(1).

In particular, if
\rho_{*,M}\to\rho
, then

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
,

\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho_{*,M}+9\sqrt{\frac{nN_{M}}{M}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i,M}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

The last factor is at most
\sqrt{n}\,K
, while
n
is fixed and

N_{M}=o(M)
. Hence the error term is
o(1)
.
∎

When
n=N=1
and
A_{0}=0
, the upper-edge part of our argument reduces to the
classical upper bound

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(M^{-1/2}G)\leq 2+O(M^{-1/2})

for a normalized GUE matrix
G
. This scalar case is closely related to the
classical concentration-and-comparison estimates for Gaussian matrix norms; see,
for example, Davidson–Szarek
[
10
, Section II.c]
and the
2018 first edition of
Vershynin
[
21
, Section 7.3]
.
For inhomogeneous matrices with independent entries, see also
van Handel
[
19
, Section 4]
. In the present proof,
the Davidson–Szarek estimate enters at the end through a rectangular
block-Gaussian singular-value bound, while the full-process comparison is a
separate step. Sharper non-asymptotic edge deviation estimates for the GUE,
which in particular imply

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(M^{-1/2}G)\to 2
, were obtained by
Ledoux
[
13
]
and Aubrun
[
3
]
.
The factor
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}
in the error term is the same row/column square-function
quantity that appears in the endpoint noncommutative Khintchine inequality. For
a free semicircular family
(s_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, the classical operator-space form
of this inequality gives

\left\|\sum_{i}a_{i}\otimes s_{i}\right\|\leq K\max\left\{\left\|\sum_{i}a_{i}^{*}a_{i}\right\|^{1/2},\left\|\sum_{i}a_{i}a_{i}^{*}\right\|^{1/2}\right\},

see, for example, Pisier
[
17
, (9.9.8)–(9.9.9)]
and
Buchholz
[
6
, Remark 1]
; for self-adjoint coefficients
this reduces precisely to
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}
.
Another point of the argument that may be of independent interest is
Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, which give dual forms of
Lehner’s edge quantities over density matrices. After completion of this work,
we learned of independent related work of Kunisky
[
12
]
,
which develops semidefinite-programming formulations of Lehner’s formulas and
also discusses such dual formulations.

The proof of Theorem
1
proceeds through separate estimates for the
two spectral edges. The first ingredient is the following upper-edge estimate.

[Upper-edge estimate]propositionupperedgeprop

Assume that

A_{i}\succeq 0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

and assume
M\geq N
. Then

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\rho_{+}+6\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

The second ingredient is the corresponding lower-edge estimate. For

C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, write

E_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(C^{1/2}A_{i}C^{1/2})^{2}.

(3)

The dual variational formula, proved in

\rho_{-}=\min_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)-2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\}.

{restatable*}

[Left-edge estimate]propositionleftedgeprop

Assume that

A_{i}\succeq 0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

and assume
M\geq N
. Then

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\min}(H_{M})\geq\rho_{-}-6\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

The asymptotic consequence of Theorem
1
should be viewed in the
context of the strong convergence phenomenon for random matrices; see

[
20
]
for a recent survey. In its greatest generality, this
asymptotic statement is not new. Recent work of Chen–Garza-Vargas–van Handel

[
8
, Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2]
proves strong convergence
for polynomials in GUE, GOE, and GSE matrices with matrix coefficients of
dimension
D_{M}=\exp(o(M))
. This covers, in particular, the regime
N=o(M)

considered here, and does so even for signed coefficients and arbitrary
fixed-degree noncommutative polynomials. This work builds on the general
framework introduced in
[
7
]
. Earlier results in this direction
include Collins–Guionnet–Parraud
[
9
]
, which gave the
tensor-coefficient regime
N=o(M^{1/3})
, and the subsequent work of
Parraud
[
15
]
, which reaches
N=\exp(o(M^{2/3}))
.

The point of the present paper is different. In the special case of a linear
self-adjoint pencil with positive coefficients, we give a direct
concentration-based proof of the finite-dimensional estimate

\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\left\|A_{0}\otimes I+\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes s_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}+O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\left\|\sum_{i}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}\right),

where the free norm is identified explicitly by Lehner’s variational formula.
This gives a short quantitative route to the
N=o(M)
upper bound, without using
a master equation or resolvent asymptotic expansions. The positivity assumption
on the non-constant coefficients is essential for our sharp Sudakov comparison
argument; in particular, the result should not be read as an input to a
linearization proof of general strong convergence, where signed coefficients and
full spectral convergence are unavoidable. Rather, the point is that the
geometry of the underlying Gaussian processes already produces the free
probability edge formulas in this positive-coefficient block model.

. Section
5

the block-Wishart estimate needed to control the comparison process and proves
Proposition
1
. Section
6

7

1
.
Finally, Section
8
explains why the direct Sudakov approach does
not extend sharply to signed Hermitian coefficients.

2
Setup and preliminaries

Throughout,
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
are Hermitian. Positivity
assumptions are stated explicitly in the results where they are used. We write

\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
for the operator norm,

\|X\|_{1}=\Tr|X|
for the trace norm, and

\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle_{F}=\Rea\Tr(X^{*}Y)

for the real Frobenius inner product.

The GUE matrices
G_{i}
are normalized by

\mathbb{E}\left\langle G_{i},K\right\rangle_{F}^{2}=\|K\|_{F}^{2}

for every Hermitian
K\in M_{M}(\mathbb{C})
. Equivalently, diagonal entries are real
standard Gaussians and off-diagonal real and imaginary parts have variance

1/2
. Throughout the paper, a standard complex Gaussian means a random
variable of the form

X+iY,

where
X,Y
are independent real Gaussians with law
N(0,1/2)
, and

N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes the real Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and
variance
\sigma^{2}
. Thus, if
\Gamma
is a standard complex Gaussian matrix
and
L
is a deterministic matrix of the same size, then

\mathbb{E}\left\langle\Gamma,L\right\rangle_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|L\|_{F}^{2}.

Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}|\Tr(\Gamma^{*}L)|^{2}=\|L\|_{F}^{2}
.

Let

\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{C\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C}):C\succeq 0,\ \Tr C=1\}

be the density matrices. With
E_{C}
as in (
3
), define

\phi(C)=\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2}).

We identify
\mathbb{C}^{N}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{M}
with
\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}
by

\vecop(S)=\sum_{a,b}S_{ab}(e_{a}\otimes e_{b}).

Then

(A\otimes G)\vecop(S)=\vecop(ASG^{\top}).

Consequently, for Hermitian
A
and
G
,

\left\langle\vecop(S),(A\otimes G)\vecop(S)\right\rangle=\Tr(S^{*}ASG^{\top}).

For
M\geq N
set

\mathrm{St}_{N,M}=\{W\in M_{N,M}(\mathbb{C}):WW^{*}=I_{N}\}.

Thus elements of
\mathrm{St}_{N,M}
are coisometries.

Lemma 2.1

(Variational characterization of the trace norm)
.

Let
A\in M_{N,M}(\mathbb{C})
and assume
M\geq N
. Then

\|A\|_{1}=\sup_{W\in\mathrm{St}_{N,M}}\Rea\Tr(A^{*}W).

This is an immediate consequence of the duality between the Schatten

1
-norm and the spectral norm
[
24
, (1.173)]
, together with the
singular value theorem
[
24
, Theorem 1.6]
. Equivalently, it
follows from von Neumann’s trace inequality in the form of

[
5
, Theorem IV.2.5]
.

Definition 2.2

(Gaussian process)
.

Let
T
be an index set. A (real-valued) Gaussian process indexed by
T
is a
family of real random variables
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
such that for every

t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in T
, the random vector

(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})

is jointly Gaussian. The process is centered if
\mathbb{E}X_{t}=0
for every

t\in T
.

In our applications, the processes arise from real parts
of complex Gaussian inner products, and their sample paths are continuous.
We use the following standard comparison inequality.

Theorem 2.3

(Sudakov–Fernique with deterministic drift)
.

Let
T
be a compact metric space. Let
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
and

(Y_{t})_{t\in T}
be centered Gaussian processes whose sample paths are
almost surely continuous. Suppose that for every
s,t\in T
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X_{s}-X_{t})^{2}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{s}-Y_{t})^{2}\bigr].

Then, for every continuous deterministic function
a:T\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\{a(t)+X_{t}\}\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\{a(t)+Y_{t}\}.

Proof.

This is Vitale’s deterministic-shift extension of the
Sudakov–Fernique comparison: see
[
22
, (4), Section 2]
.
Strictly speaking, Vitale states the result for denumerable index sets;
the compact-continuous version follows by restricting to a countable
dense subset of
T
, using the continuity of the sample paths.
∎

Our use of Theorem
2.3
follows a fixed strategy.
First, we rewrite
\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})
as the supremum of a Gaussian process
indexed by density matrices and Stiefel coisometries; see Equation
(
4
). We then compare the original process

X_{C,W}
in Equation (
5
) with the comparison process

Z_{C,W}
in Equation (
6
), whose supremum can be evaluated
through the trace-norm variational formula.
Second, we verify the increment domination required in
Theorem
2.3
. For positive coefficients this
verification is reduced to the deterministic two-fiber estimate of
Lemma
4.1
, and is carried out in the proof of
Proposition
4.2
.

3
Variational formulas over density matrices

The goal of this section is to prove dual variational formulas for the free
edges
\rho_{+}
and
\rho_{-}
over density matrices. The right-edge formula is
needed in the full-process comparison in the proof of
Proposition
1
.

In this section, we will need to exchange the order of a supremum and an infimum in a variational formula. For this, we recall the following minimax theorem of Sion
[
18
]
.
The minimax step below may be viewed as a matrix-coefficient version of

[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 3.1

(Sion’s minimax theorem)
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of a topological vector space,

Y
be a convex subset of a topological vector space,
and suppose either
X
or
Y
is compact.
Suppose

f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
is such that, for each
y\in Y
, the map

x\mapsto f(x,y)
is upper semicontinuous and concave, and for each

x\in X
, the map
y\mapsto f(x,y)
is lower semicontinuous and convex.
Then

\sup_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}f(x,y)=\inf_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y).

For
Z\succ 0
define

B(Z)=A_{0}+Z+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}Z^{-1}A_{i},\quad D(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}CA_{i}.

Writing

f(C,Z)=\Tr(A_{0}C)+\Tr(CZ)+\Tr(D(C)Z^{-1}),

we have

\rho_{+}=\inf_{Z\succ 0}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}f(C,Z).

Lemma 3.2

(Weighted trace AM–GM)
.

For
C,D\succeq 0
,

\inf_{Z\succ 0}\{\Tr(CZ)+\Tr(DZ^{-1})\}=2\Tr\bigl[(C^{1/2}DC^{1/2})^{1/2}\bigr].

Proof.

If
C\succ 0
, put
Y=C^{1/2}ZC^{1/2}
. Then

\Tr(CZ)+\Tr(DZ^{-1})=\Tr Y+\Tr(C^{1/2}DC^{1/2}Y^{-1}).

The matrix trace AM–GM identity

\inf_{Y\succ 0}\{\Tr Y+\Tr(EY^{-1})\}=2\Tr(E^{1/2})

follows by expanding

\Tr\bigl[(Y^{1/2}-E^{1/2}Y^{-1/2})(Y^{1/2}-E^{1/2}Y^{-1/2})^{*}\bigr]\geq 0

and then taking
Y=E^{1/2}+\eta I
,
\eta\downarrow 0
.

For singular
C\succeq 0
, the upper bound follows from the positive-definite
case applied to
C+\eta I
. Since
C+\eta I\succeq C
,

\inf_{Z\succ 0}\{\Tr(CZ)+\Tr(DZ^{-1})\}\leq\inf_{Z\succ 0}\{\Tr((C+\eta I)Z)+\Tr(DZ^{-1})\}=2\Tr\bigl[((C+\eta I)^{1/2}D(C+\eta I)^{1/2})^{1/2}\bigr],

and then
\eta\downarrow 0
. For the lower bound, apply the positive-definite
case to
C+\eta I
. For every fixed
Z\succ 0
,

\Tr(CZ)+\Tr(DZ^{-1})+\eta\Tr(Z)\geq 2\Tr\bigl[((C+\eta I)^{1/2}D(C+\eta I)^{1/2})^{1/2}\bigr].

Letting
\eta\downarrow 0
gives the desired lower bound for this fixed
Z
,
and then taking the infimum over
Z
proves the singular case.
∎

Proposition 3.3

(Dual form of Lehner’s right edge)
.

For Hermitian
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
,

\rho_{+}=\,\max_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\phi(C).

Proof.

For
Z\succ 0
,

\lambda_{\max}(B(Z))=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\Tr(CB(Z))=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}f(C,Z).

The set
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is compact and convex, and the positive-definite cone is convex.
Moreover,
f
is affine in
C
and convex in
Z
. Lemma
3.1
,
applied with the compact variable
C
, gives

\rho_{+}=\inf_{Z\succ 0}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}f(C,Z)=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\inf_{Z\succ 0}f(C,Z).

For fixed
C
, Lemma
3.2
gives

\inf_{Z\succ 0}f(C,Z)=\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr\bigl[(C^{1/2}D(C)C^{1/2})^{1/2}\bigr]=\phi(C).

Therefore

\rho_{+}=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\phi(C).

The supremum is attained because
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is compact and
\phi
is continuous.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(Dual form of Lehner’s left edge)
.

For Hermitian
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
,

\rho_{-}=\min_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)-2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\}.

Proof.

By Lehner’s edge formulas,

\rho_{-}(A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})=-\rho_{+}(-A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}).

Applying Proposition
3.3
with deterministic coefficient
-A_{0}

gives

\rho_{-}=-\max_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{-\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\},

which is the stated minimum.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The use of Sion’s minimax theorem in Proposition
3.3
is not needed
for the main theorem. The estimates below only require the upper bound

\max_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\phi(C)=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\inf_{Z\succ 0}f(C,Z)\leq\inf_{Z\succ 0}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}f(C,Z)=\rho_{+},

which is just the elementary direction of the minimax inequality. Sion’s
theorem is used only to identify this upper bound with the full dual formula.

4
Full-process comparison

The goal of this section is to prove Proposition
4.2
. Namely,
we introduce the two processes that will be compared by
Theorem
2.3
and verify the required increment
domination.

For
C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
put

B_{i}(C)=C^{1/2}A_{i}C^{1/2},\qquad E_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}(C)^{2}.

This agrees with the notation in (
3
).

Lemma 4.1

(Two-fiber Stiefel increment)
.

Let
P,Q\succeq 0
be
N\times N
matrices and let
W,V\in\mathrm{St}_{N,M}
.
Then

\|W^{*}PW-V^{*}QV\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\|PW-QV\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

This is a direct application of Araki–Yamagami’s Hilbert–Schmidt
inequality
[
2
, Theorem 1]
,

\||X|-|Y|\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}\,\|X-Y\|_{F}.

Indeed, since
WW^{*}=VV^{*}=I_{N}
and
P,Q\succeq 0
,

|PW|=(W^{*}P^{2}W)^{1/2}=W^{*}PW,\qquad|QV|=(V^{*}Q^{2}V)^{1/2}=V^{*}QV.

Applying the cited inequality with
X=PW
and
Y=QV
gives the claim.
∎

Every
S\in M_{N,M}(\mathbb{C})
with
\|S\|_{F}=1
has
C_{S}=SS^{*}\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
. Since
M\geq N
,
we may write
S=C_{S}^{1/2}W_{S}
with
W_{S}\in\mathrm{St}_{N,M}
; if
C_{S}
is singular we
extend the partial coisometry arbitrarily on
\ker C_{S}
.
Thus, in distribution,

\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})=\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N},\,W\in\mathrm{St}_{N,M}}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle G_{i},W^{*}B_{i}(C)W\right\rangle_{F}\right\}.

(4)

Here we used the fact that
G
and
G^{\top}
have the same distribution for a GUE matrix
G
.

Let
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n}\in M_{N,M}(\mathbb{C})
be independent standard complex
Gaussian matrices. We compare the centered Gaussian processes indexed by

(C,W)\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\times\mathrm{St}_{N,M}
defined by

X_{C,W}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle G_{i},W^{*}B_{i}(C)W\right\rangle_{F}

(5)

and

Z_{C,W}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\Gamma_{i},B_{i}(C)W\right\rangle_{F}.

(6)

Proposition 4.2

(Full-process Sudakov comparison)
.

Assume
M\geq N
and
A_{i}\succeq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+\frac{2}{\sqrt{M}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}(C)\Gamma_{i}\right\|_{1}\right\}.

Proof.

Since
A_{i}\succeq 0
, also
B_{i}(C)\succeq 0
for every
C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
.
For two indices
(C,W)
and
(C^{\prime},V)
,

\mathbb{E}|X_{C,W}-X_{C^{\prime},V}|^{2}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|W^{*}B_{i}(C)W-V^{*}B_{i}(C^{\prime})V\|_{F}^{2}.

By Lemma
4.1
, this is at most

\frac{2}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|B_{i}(C)W-B_{i}(C^{\prime})V\|_{F}^{2}.

On the other hand, the normalization of the complex Gaussian entries gives

\mathbb{E}|Z_{C,W}-Z_{C^{\prime},V}|^{2}=\frac{2}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|B_{i}(C)W-B_{i}(C^{\prime})V\|_{F}^{2}.

Theorem
2.3
therefore gives, with zero drift,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{C,W}X_{C,W}\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{C,W}Z_{C,W}.

Applied with the deterministic drift
(C,W)\mapsto\Tr(A_{0}C)
, the same theorem
gives the inequality used here:

\mathbb{E}\sup_{C,W}\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+X_{C,W}\}\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{C,W}\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+Z_{C,W}\}.

For fixed
C
and fixed
\Gamma_{i}
,

\sup_{W\in\mathrm{St}_{N,M}}\Rea\sum_{i}\Tr(\Gamma_{i}^{*}B_{i}(C)W)=\left\|\sum_{i}B_{i}(C)\Gamma_{i}\right\|_{1},

by Lemma
2.1
. This proves the claim.
∎

5
The block-Wishart estimate and the upper edge

The full-process comparison still contains a supremum over
C
inside the
expectation. The following proposition controls it uniformly by using a single
block Gaussian matrix. We state it first with the quantity
\Psi
defined below, rather than
with
\rho_{+}
, because this form will also be used for the lower edge with
deterministic coefficient
-A_{0}
.

Lemma 5.1

(Positive-part estimate for the largest Wishart eigenvalue)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
d\times M
random matrix with independent standard real or
standard complex Gaussian entries. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}-1\right)_{+}\right]\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{d}{M}}.

Proof.

We first record the tail bound

\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\Gamma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\sqrt{M}+\sqrt{d}+t\right\}\leq e^{-t^{2}/2}.

For real Gaussian entries this is the non-asymptotic singular-value estimate
of Davidson and Szarek
[
10
, Theorem II.13]
, after
rescaling their normalization. For standard complex Gaussian entries in the
convention
\mathbb{E}|\gamma|^{2}=1
, the
expectation bound

\mathbb{E}\|\Gamma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{M}+\sqrt{d}

follows from the complex Hilbert-space form of Chevet’s
inequality
[
16
, Section 43]
. Since the map

\Gamma\mapsto\|\Gamma\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is 1-Lipschitz for the real Frobenius norm,
Gaussian concentration on the underlying real coordinates gives the displayed
tail bound; with our complex normalization the concentration exponent is in
fact
e^{-t^{2}}
, so the weaker bound above follows.
Since

\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}=\frac{\|\Gamma\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{\sqrt{M}},

the tail-integral formula gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}-1\right)_{+}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{M}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d}{M}}+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2M}}\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{d}{M}},

where the last inequality uses
d\geq 1
.
∎

Proposition 5.2

(Uniform control of the comparison process)
.

Assume
M\geq N
and
A_{i}\succeq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Define

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle:=\max_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\},

\displaystyle B_{\max}

\displaystyle:=\max_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2}).

Then

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}\left(A_{0}\otimes I_{M}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\otimes G_{i}\right)\leq\Psi+6B_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}

and

B_{\max}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.2
,

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+\frac{2}{\sqrt{M}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}(C)\Gamma_{i}\right\|_{1}\right\}.

Introduce the block row matrix

R_{C}=[B_{1}(C)\ B_{2}(C)\ \cdots\ B_{n}(C)]\in M_{N,nN}(\mathbb{C})

and the block Gaussian matrix

\Gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{1}\\
\Gamma_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\Gamma_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in M_{nN,M}(\mathbb{C}),

where
\Gamma
is an
nN\times M
standard complex Gaussian matrix.
Then

\sum_{i}B_{i}(C)\Gamma_{i}=R_{C}\Gamma,\qquad R_{C}R_{C}^{*}=\sum_{i}B_{i}(C)^{2}=E_{C}.

For every
C
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\|R_{C}\Gamma\|_{1}=\Tr\left[\left(R_{C}\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}R_{C}^{*}\right)^{1/2}\right].

Since

0\preceq R_{C}\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}R_{C}^{*}\preceq\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}R_{C}R_{C}^{*}=\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}E_{C},

and
X\mapsto\Tr(X^{1/2})
is monotone on the positive semidefinite cone, we get

\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\|R_{C}\Gamma\|_{1}\leq\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2}).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{C}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\}.

For every
\alpha\geq 0
, the pointwise bound

\sup_{C}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\alpha\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\}\leq\Psi+2(\alpha-1)_{+}B_{\max}

follows directly from the two cases
\alpha\leq 1
and
\alpha>1
.
Therefore

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\Psi+2B_{\max}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left\|\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{*}}{M}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}-1\right)_{+}\right],

By Lemma
5.1
with

d=nN
, this gives

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\Psi+6B_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}.

It remains to bound
B_{\max}
. Since

E_{C}^{1/2}=(R_{C}R_{C}^{*})^{1/2},

we have

\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})=\|R_{C}\|_{1}.

Writing

R_{C}=C^{1/2}[A_{1}C^{1/2}\ A_{2}C^{1/2}\ \cdots\ A_{n}C^{1/2}],

the Schatten Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\|R_{C}\|_{1}\leq\|C^{1/2}\|_{2}\left\|[A_{1}C^{1/2}\ A_{2}C^{1/2}\ \cdots\ A_{n}C^{1/2}]\right\|_{2}.

As
\Tr C=1
, the first factor is one, while the square of the second is

\Tr\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}CA_{i}=\Tr C\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Taking the supremum over
C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
proves

B_{\max}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

∎

\upperedgeprop

Proof of Proposition
1
.

By Proposition
5.2
,

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\Psi+6B_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}.

Proposition
3.3
gives
\Psi=\rho_{+}
. The bound

B_{\max}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}
from Proposition
5.2
completes the
proof.
∎

Remark 5.3

(Multiplicative form when
A_{0}\succeq 0
)
.

Under the additional assumption
A_{0}\succeq 0
, the additive error in
Proposition
1
also admits the cleaner multiplicative form

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(H_{M})\leq\left(1+3\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\right)\rho_{+}.

Indeed, in the proof of Proposition
5.2
, if

\alpha=\|\Gamma\Gamma^{*}/M\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}
, then
A_{0}\succeq 0
gives the
pointwise estimate

\sup_{C}\left\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\alpha\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\}\leq(1+(\alpha-1)_{+})\rho_{+}.

For
\alpha>1
, this follows by writing

\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\alpha\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})=\alpha\{\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\}+(1-\alpha)\Tr(A_{0}C),

and using
(1-\alpha)\Tr(A_{0}C)\leq 0
. The case
\alpha\leq 1
is immediate.
Lemma
5.1
then yields the displayed bound. We keep the
additive form in Proposition
1
, as it does not require

A_{0}\succeq 0
.

6
The left edge

We also record the corresponding lower-edge estimate. The point is that the
Sudakov comparison itself does not require positivity of the deterministic
coefficient; the lower edge is obtained by applying the upper-edge estimate to

-H_{M}
.

\leftedgeprop

Proof.

Since
G_{i}
and
-G_{i}
have the same
distribution,

-H_{M}=-A_{0}\otimes I_{M}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\otimes G_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-A_{0}\otimes I_{M}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\otimes G_{i}.

Therefore Proposition
5.2
, applied with deterministic
coefficient
-A_{0}
, gives

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\max}(-H_{M})\leq\max_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left\{-\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})\right\}+6\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

Using
\lambda_{\max}(-H_{M})=-\lambda_{\min}(H_{M})
, this is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{\min}(H_{M})\geq\rho_{-}-6\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

∎

7
Lipschitz control and proof of the main theorem

Lemma 7.1

(Lipschitz control of spectral observables)
.

The maps

(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})\mapsto\lambda_{\max}(H_{M}),\qquad(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})\mapsto\lambda_{\max}(-H_{M}),\qquad(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})\mapsto\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

are Lipschitz with respect to the real Euclidean norm on the GUE inputs induced
by the Frobenius norm,

\|(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})\|_{\mathrm{Euc}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta_{i}\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

with constant

L\leq\frac{\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{M}}.

Proof.

If
G_{i}
is replaced by
G_{i}+\Delta_{i}
, with each
\Delta_{i}
Hermitian, then
the corresponding perturbation of
H_{M}
is

\Delta H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\otimes\Delta_{i}.

By the variational characterization of
\lambda_{\max}
and by the triangle
inequality for the operator norm, all three observables in the statement
change by at most
\|\Delta H\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. We bound this operator norm using the
Frobenius norm of the perturbations. Since
\Delta H
is Hermitian, it is enough
to test Rayleigh quotients. Let
S\in M_{N,M}(\mathbb{C})
with
\|S\|_{F}=1
. Under the
vectorization convention from Section
2
,

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\vecop(S),\Delta H\vecop(S)\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\Tr(S^{*}A_{i}S\Delta_{i}^{\top})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta_{i}\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|S^{*}A_{i}S\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

By the ideal property of the Frobenius norm and since

\|S\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|S\|_{F}=1
,

\|S^{*}A_{i}S\|_{F}\leq\|S\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|A_{i}S\|_{F}\leq\|A_{i}S\|_{F}.

Thus

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|S^{*}A_{i}S\|_{F}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A_{i}S\|_{F}^{2}=\Tr S^{*}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right)S\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Taking the supremum over such
S
gives

\|\Delta H\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta_{i}\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

which proves the claim.
∎

\mainthm

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Write

X=\lambda_{\max}(H_{M}),\qquad Y=-\lambda_{\min}(H_{M})=\lambda_{\max}(-H_{M}).

Then

\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max\{X,Y\}.

Let

\mu=\max\{\rho_{+},-\rho_{-}\}+6\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

Then, Proposition
1
and
Proposition
1
give
\mathbb{E}X\leq\mu
and
\mathbb{E}Y\leq\mu
.

We now use Gaussian concentration in the real Euclidean coordinates
corresponding to the Frobenius norm and our GUE normalization.
By Lemma
7.1
and Gaussian concentration, the centered
random variables satisfy, for every
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\{X-\mathbb{E}X\geq t\}\leq e^{-t^{2}/(2L^{2})},\qquad\mathbb{P}\{Y-\mathbb{E}Y\geq t\}\leq e^{-t^{2}/(2L^{2})},

where

L\leq\frac{\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{M}}.

Since
\mathbb{E}X\leq\mu
and
\mathbb{E}Y\leq\mu
, we have

\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max\{X,Y\}\leq\mu+\max\{X-\mathbb{E}X,\,Y-\mathbb{E}Y\}.

Therefore, by the standard maximal inequality for two subgaussian random
variables, or directly by the union bound and tail integration with

\min\{1,2e^{-t^{2}/(2L^{2})}\}\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/(2L^{2})}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\max\{X-\mathbb{E}X,\,Y-\mathbb{E}Y\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/(2L^{2})}\,dt

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}\,L\leq 3L.

Thus

\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\mu+3L.

The Lipschitz correction is smaller than the block-Wishart error term and is not
the bottleneck in the estimate. Indeed,

L\leq\frac{\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{M}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2},

so

\mathbb{E}\|H_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho_{*}+9\sqrt{\frac{nN}{M}}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}.

∎

8
Obstruction to a sharp inequality in the case of signed coefficients

In this final section, we discuss what remains true when the
A_{i}
are Hermitian but not
positive. This section is meant to delimit the scope of the concentration method
rather than to prove the full signed Lehner formula.

Let
A_{i}=A_{i}^{*}
and
B_{i}(C)=C^{1/2}A_{i}C^{1/2}
. Then
B_{i}(C)=B_{i}(C)^{*}
and

B_{i}(C)^{2}=C^{1/2}A_{i}CA_{i}C^{1/2}.

Consequently,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}(C)^{2}=C^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}CA_{i}\right)C^{1/2}.

Thus the fixed-fiber expression

\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr\left[\left(C^{1/2}D(C)C^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}\right]

and the variational formula over
C\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
remain valid without positivity of the
individual
A_{i}
.

The positivity assumption enters only through the sharp increment estimate. For
signed Hermitian
B
, the inequality

\|W^{*}BW-V^{*}BV\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\|B(W-V)\|_{F}^{2}

is false. Already for
N=M=2
, take

W=I_{2},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

A direct computation gives

\|B-V^{*}BV\|_{F}^{2}=8\sin^{2}\theta,\qquad 2\|B(I-V)\|_{F}^{2}=8(1-\,\cos\theta).

The desired inequality would require
\sin^{2}\theta\leq 1-\cos\theta
, which fails for small nonzero
\theta
.

For a fixed Hermitian
B
one still has the elementary estimate

W^{*}BW-V^{*}BV=W^{*}B(W-V)+(W-V)^{*}BV,

and hence

\|W^{*}BW-V^{*}BV\|_{F}\leq 2\|B(W-V)\|_{F}.

After squaring, this gives the non-sharp bound

\|W^{*}BW-V^{*}BV\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4\|B(W-V)\|_{F}^{2}.

At a fixed fiber, the Sudakov argument then yields a comparison with a Gaussian
process whose amplitude is larger by a factor
\sqrt{2}
. Thus the deterministic
comparison constant

\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2})

would be replaced by

\Tr(A_{0}C)+2\sqrt{2}\,\Tr(E_{C}^{1/2}),

which is valid but no longer recovers Lehner’s sharp constant. Extending such a
signed argument to the full process would require an increment estimate for two
different matrices
B_{i}(C)
and
B_{i}(C^{\prime})
, which is precisely where the
positive-coefficient proof uses the Araki–Yamagami inequality.

To conclude, the concentration method gives a sharp and robust
positive-coefficient theorem, together with non-sharp signed estimates. However,
proving the full signed Lehner formula appears to be out of reach of the
Sudakov–Fernique comparison.
