End-to-end robust system discovery in electrical dynamical systems using scientific machine learning
Authors: Jhelum Chakravorty, Nicolò Ripamonti, Tor Laneryd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystem discovery is an important part of the power systems asset management process. In this paper, we introduce an end-to-end approach for robust system discovery for a class of electrical dynamical systems with polynomial dynamics. To that end, we provide a theoretical analysis of the problem setting and the solution approach using a particular Scientific Machine Learning method called Physics-Informed Machine Learning. We introduce model architecture and training and validation methods for deterministic as well as probabilistic approaches to predict the solution to pertinent inverse problems and propose a sampling method to make the predictions robust to data sparsity. With empirical evidence drawn from a case study of thermal modeling of electrical induction machines, we establish the merits of the proposed method and discuss the implications of this research on possible future directions. In particular, we benchmark our proposed end-to-end method with multiple deterministic and probabilistic approaches and show that it outperforms all but one baseline in terms of performance in a range of 6% to 78% reduction of validation error. The drop in performance (increase in validation error) in our end-to-end probabilistic approach compared to a Bayesian approach is compensated by a large reduction (86%) in the computation time, making our method more balanced in terms of performance and cost. The experimental results establish that our end-to-end approach outperforms the baseline methods by demonstrating consistent balancing among performance and computation cost.Subject terms: Computational science, Thermoelectric devices and materials, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Electrical and power dynamical systems, such as power transformers and shunt reactors, semiconductors, converters, etc., are guided by one or more physical laws. Examples include electromagnetic induction laws for thermal modeling and partial discharge, acoustics, Maxwell’s equations for electromagnetic field behavior, Joule heating principles, electrodynamic coupling mechanisms, electrostatic field theory, magnetohydrodynamics for cooling fluid interactions, and nonlinear material characteristics that affect core saturation. Often, the current practices for system analysis and discovery are limited to simulation-based and analytical modeling-based approaches, which are costly, can fail in the face of uncertainty, are sensitive to data sparsity, and call for repeating the analysis from scratch with any change in the underlying assumptions. In this paper, we look at the problem of system discovery of a few classes of electrical and power system dynamics. Also known as the inverse problem, where the cause of a dynamical phenomenon is predicted from the observed effect, this is usually a difficult problem due to a lack of ground truth data for the cause. In this paper, we use the terms system discovery and inverse solutions interchangeably.

We focus on developing an approach to predict the inverse solution from the observed effect in a robust and automated way. To that end, we invoke an emerging avenue of Machine Learning, the Scientific Machine Learning (SciML), that augments the traditional data-driven approach with system-specific domain knowledge. While SciML is quite wide in its definition (equation discovery methods, differentiable physics simulators, multi-fidelity modeling techniques, DeepONet and Fourier Neural Operators that learn mappings between function spaces, and Bayesian methods for uncertainty quantification in scientific predictions), in this paper we restrict our attention to Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) that uses scientific knowledge encoded in the physical laws in terms of mathematical models such as Ordinary or Partial Differential Equations (ODEs/PDEs) and incorporates these laws in the training of the predictive model as additional constraints in the objective function.

Robust system discovery includes satisfactory predictions of the cause in the face of uncertainty. However, uncertainty quantification (UQ) in inverse problems is a challenging task, that addresses the variability in the data and the model to predict the cause of an event by quantifying the uncertainty in the measured effect and relating it to the uncertainty in the cause. When the measured data are noisy or the mathematical model for the underlying dynamics is imperfect, it becomes important to quantify the uncertainties inherent in any inferences derived from the solutions of the inverse problems, which also helps identify and regularize any ill-posedness in the solution of the inverse problem, i.e., conditions violating existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence of solutions on data. For the inverse problems relating to condition monitoring in power devices, this is instrumental for understanding the uncertainties in the observed effect in inaccessible components by measuring the values of the accessible interconnected parts, which makes the prediction accurate as well as robust to varying reliability of the data quality. Furthermore, for some electrical dynamical system discovery applications, the data available is sparse because of poor accessibility of certain regions for data procurement. It is important that the predictions of system dynamics is robust with data sparsity.

This research can benefit electrical condition monitoring applications by providing a robust framework for inverse problem solutions. In electrical acoustics, this approach could help identify partial discharge locations from limited acoustic emissions data. For high-voltage equipment monitoring, the methodology enables more accurate source localization from electromagnetic signals, while transformer vibration analysis could leverage this framework to identify mechanical or core issues from external measurements. The methodology extends beyond electrical systems to various industrial applications. For industrial process monitoring, it could identify anomalies in chemical reactions without direct access to reaction chambers. In structural health monitoring, this approach could locate weaknesses from limited sensor data, while in semiconductor manufacturing, it could identify process variations from indirect measurements. In each case, the robust inverse solution methodology and uncertainty quantification provide valuable decision support, reducing downtime, preventing catastrophic failures, and optimizing maintenance intervals across critical infrastructure. This framework’s ability to handle sparse and noisy data while providing confidence metrics improves decision-making in predictive maintenance scenarios. The developed inverse solution methodology and uncertainty quantification enhance manufacturing processes by enabling data-driven predictive maintenance of electrical systems, reducing downtime, and optimizing maintenance intervals across critical manufacturing infrastructure. The end-to-end approach proposed in this paper lays the ground for enhanced condition monitoring, root cause analysis, and system discovery in the face of uncertainty through detailed analysis and empirical evidence. This methodology can be applied to dynamical systems where the underlying physics is known or can be modeled, and the data availability is limited and may be noisy, sparse, or inaccessible. The applicability of the proposed method in diverse realistic scenarios is primarily affected by the balance between performance and computation cost that this approach offers, which is established through experimental results in the paper. Some example applications that can benefit from the proposed unified framework include, but are not limited to, condition monitoring in diverse electrical and power systems assets1, model order reduction for manufacturing2 and system discovery3,4.

Scientific Machine Learning (SciML) encompasses a diverse range of methods beyond Physics-Informed Machine Learning. Equation discovery methods like SINDy5 (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics) extract governing equations directly from data without prior knowledge of system structure. Differentiable physics simulators6 enable end-to-end optimization by embedding physical constraints in differentiable computational graphs. Operator learning approaches such as DeepONet7 and Fourier Neural Operators8 learn mappings between function spaces, effectively capturing solution operators for families of PDEs rather than solving individual instances. Bayesian methods for scientific computing provide robust uncertainty quantification in predictions while incorporating domain knowledge through carefully designed priors. PIML, a type of SciML method that integrates theoretical knowledge and observational data, has shown remarkable success in handling inverse problems related to Partial Differential Equations (PDEs)9. In situations where only parts of the system description, i.e., initial or boundary conditions and coefficients, are available, PIML excels at estimating missing inputs through insights derived from collected data10. Unlike traditional approaches11, PIML can work with limited, noisy data by integrating domain knowledge with mathematical models12. Consequently, PIML solutions often claim increased accuracy, faster training, comprehensibility, and generalization, thanks to the interplay between machine learning capabilities and physical laws. Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) is a way to implement PIML by integrating a Deep Neural Network (DNN) along with a mathematical model such as an ODE/PDE.

Rules for measurements of power losses in transformers is described in international standards and guides13,14, including determination of uncertainties15. A body of work exists that reviews of transformer losses16,17, combines experiments and computation18 and describes the use of thermal cameras to estimate losses19–22. The method presented in ref. 18 estimates parameters to compute the temperature distribution on the transformer top using multi-objective optimization. The work in ref. 20 studies on the reliability of thermal measurements by comparing the core loss values calculated using thermal measurements with the same directly measured using magnetic methods. The work in ref. 21 focuses on solving Maxwell’s equations using measured infrared images of temperature distribution in a physics simulator to compute core losses, where access to measure ground truths is also available. The authors of ref. 19 proposes a method to estimate losses in a high-frequency planar transformer by first calibrating the measurement equipment (thermal camera) by heating transformer components using a DC power supply, and then estimating power losses in the tested transformer under predefined loads using the heating curves obtained during the calibration step. These estimates are compared with ground truth calculated using established theoretical electrical relationships. The approach of ref. 22 uses thermographic images to calculate total losses in dry-type air-core reactors using the calorimetric method. In contrast with these related works for estimating power loss in a device, we propose a nonlinear predictive tool (PIML) that can estimate directly from measured temperature distributions, can create an estimate of the heat loss without any direct comparison with ground-truth (hence being applicable even in the absence of ground truth), and is much faster than the physics simulators to predict the heat losses during inference.

Moreover, our approach computes the predictive model’s confidence in its estimation through an uncertainty quantification method, which adds to the model’s interpretability. Uncertainty quantification is a research area occurring in mathematical modeling, probability, statistics and machine learning. In the probabilistic setting, Bayesian approaches such as Bayesian networks23, quantify the uncertainties in the predictions. However, Bayesian approaches are usually computationally expensive, e.g., with Monte Carlo search24 or training Bayesian Networks25. In contrast, our approach for quantifying uncertainty provides a computationally cheaper way to estimate uncertainty with Gaussian parameterization.

Methods

We use PINNs as a predictive tool for solving the heat loss localization inverse problem in power devices, using limited medium-quality data and partial knowledge of heat diffusion dynamics. We introduce uncertainty quantification (UQ) for PINN models for electrical dynamical systems. Throughout the paper, we present different training losses balancing computation cost (model size and convergence time) against prediction accuracy, while improving model performance and interpretability.

Given the absence of ground truth data, we develop an indirect validation method for trained PINN models. A key aspect of our contribution is the development of a validation strategy in case of real-world scenarios where direct ground truth for the inferred quantities is inaccessible. We acknowledge that our proposed indirect validation method carries the assumption that the physical model, in this case, the heat diffusion equation, is a sufficiently accurate representation of the true thermal dynamics. This can introduce a bias, as the validation primarily confirms the PINN’s success in learning to invert the assumed physical laws, rather than the laws of the real-world system. This challenge, known as model discrepancy, is a fundamental and well-known issue in the field of inverse problems, where simplified models are a practical necessity for computational tractability. Our contribution, therefore, lies in providing a complete, computationally efficient framework that delivers valuable insights under these practical constraints. While this emerging research direction offers limited baseline comparisons, our comprehensive analysis of the case study involving inverse heat diffusion problem establishes foundations for applying PIML and UQ to wider ranges of dynamical systems. We acknowledge that explicitly addressing model discrepancy could be a natural extension of the proposed work.

End-to-end automated and robust system discovery for electrical dynamical systems

We recognize that an end-to-end automated system discovery approach for electrical and power dynamical systems should leverage information from measured observations (effects) and complete or partial knowledge of the underlying system dynamics and provide predictions of the cause with satisfactory performance (e.g., accuracy) even in the presence of limited, unreliable, or moderate-quality data. Moreover, analyses such as uncertainty quantification in effect and predicted cause and robustness analysis with data sparsity can provide useful insights towards explainable predictions that may help human experts interpret the outputs and take actions accordingly.

While traditional deep learning26 minimizes the mismatch between network output and target values by fine-tuning the parameters θ, inverse problems present a unique challenge: the target cannot be directly measured. Instead, PIML compensates for this lack of ground truth by learning to predict the cause through the physical dynamics that connect them to measurable effect distributions.

To ensure the accuracy of the prediction, it is necessary that the neural network satisfies the physics governed by the PDE. Application of PIML for predicting local heat loss distribution in electromagnetic induction devices

In this section, we develop the end-to-end approach to the robust solution for the inverse problem in a case study of thermal diffusion problems arising from local heat losses in electrical induction machines such as power transformers and shunt reactors. The heat losses in power devices represent both an economic loss and an unnecessary emission of greenhouse gases. Raising the temperature level reduces the remaining useful life (RUL) through accelerated thermal aging and may require an additional cooling system. An important first step in reducing heat losses is to localize where in the power device they occur.

For power transformers, the total losses are the difference between the input and output power, and their measurement is relatively straightforward. The distribution of heat losses is more difficult to obtain. Instead, measuring the temperature distribution and calculating the heat losses that would cause this thermal behavior is an example of an inverse problem, where one estimates the cause of an event by observing the effect of the same. In this paper, we present an approach to solve this inverse problem using PIML.

Data acquisition and processing

Measurement data are gathered through tests conducted in a factory test room on two devices: a 90 MVA power transformer (3-minute test) and a 120 MVAr shunt reactor (10-minute test) under full voltage starting from thermal equilibrium. These short durations ensure uniform, constant temperature in the cooling media. Measurements use a FLIR A70 thermal camera (Teledyne FLIR) with 29∘ field of view, capturing thermal images at frequencies between 1−30Hz with 640 × 480 resolution. The accuracy of the camera is ±2∘C ( ± 3. 6∘F) or ±2% of reading, for ambient temperature 15 ∘C−35 ∘C (59∘F − 95∘F) and object temperature above 0 ∘C (32∘F). The sensitivity of the thermal camera, given by Noise Equivalent Temperature Difference (NETD) is < 35mK (milliKelvin). The test setup for the 90 MVA transformer is depicted in Fig. 2b27.

Since the measurements of thermal cameras, although spatially dense, can have measurement noise, it is important to validate the use of thermal cameras as a tool for measuring data. Since thermocouples are used widely for data measurements and they provide sufficiently precise point-wise measurements, we use the thermocouple data from given points on the tank wall as reference values and compare them with the thermal camera measurements to calibrate the latter as a reliable tool for data procurement. Methodology for PIML

This section describes the main components of the methodology for the application described in the Application of PIML for predicting local heat loss distribution in electromagnetic induction devices section. In particular, we discuss the architectures of PIML predictive models for estimating dynamics and heat distribution and their training and validation approaches with and without uncertainty quantification. PIML solves inverse problems directly from measurements, eliminating physics simulators to solve underlying dynamics, offering a computationally cheaper and faster parametric nonlinear model. This technology has limited equivalent methods for comparison in industrial applications.

Although focused on heat loss localization inverse problems21, PINN-based approaches show notable computational benefits for forward problems as well. We also test the method with synthetic examples of Gaussian and Sinc functions, where PINNs achieve prediction times two orders of magnitude faster than traditional physics simulators while maintaining accuracy in heat loss estimation.

Probabilistic PINN model (Pro-PINN)

In the deterministic PINN, as in ref. 11, the dynamics of the physical system are predicted, and the point-wise estimates of the variables of interest are given by the PINN model. In contrast, to facilitate UQ in PINN, we assume that the variables of interest are probabilistic and the PINN model estimates the parameters of a parameterized probability density. In particular, for the thermal diffusion model, we assume that the temperature and load loss (heat) are normally distributed, and the mean and variance of the Gaussian distribution are estimated using a PINN model. History-aware training. Previous studies38 have shown that many of the critical issues regarding the accuracy of PINNs that may arise from PDE-based regularization and make the problem ill-conditioned can be resolved by optimizing the objective function or the training regime over time and keeping the network structure unchanged. Active-sampling and robustness with data sparsity

Active learning (AL) is an iterative supervised ML method that queries a source of information interactively to label new data points for desired outcomes40. While we typically assume data availability through high spatial density camera images, this may not always hold true. To enable PINN models to handle occasional data sparsity reasonably well and to maintain the performance level, we introduce an active learning approach that iteratively selects device wall positions where PINN approximation performs the worst. These points guide optimal sensor (e.g., thermocouple) placement when camera data collection becomes impractical or impossible.

In our active sampling method, we select mAC locations maximizing the objective function per iteration, where we use Mean Squared Error (MSE) (17) for temperature approximation. To prevent selecting samples too close to each other, we exclude locations within dp of already sampled points and db of boundaries. Inspired by simulated annealing41, we optimize dp by training a set of PINNs with different dp values at each iteration, starting from identical initial weights and biases but using slightly different datasets influenced by the corresponding dp. The Results and discussion section demonstrates that active sampling achieves better training accuracy compared to a baseline of randomly selecting points.

Comparison with classical and neural network-based solution approaches

Inverse problems are essentially optimization problems that can be solved using classical closed-form or iterative methods. In a deterministic setting, classical approaches employ a regularization method to diminish the effect of ill-posedness (e.g., non-existence, non-uniqueness or instability of solution). Most prominent regularization methods include the least-square method (e.g., Ridge regression or Tikhonov regularization), singular value decomposition method or variational approaches (e.g., total-variation regularization). In a probabilistic setting, the inverse problem can be viewed as learning the conditional probability of the cause given the noisy observations. The Bayesian approach is applied to use an assumed prior to learn this posterior distribution43. While the classical methods can provide closed-form solutions, there are some challenges that make these classical methods costly to implement. These challenges include the complexity of the nonlinear optimization problem, especially in a non-convex space, the convergence speed, and singularity (ill-posedness) in the inverse solutions. In the Bayesian approach, the main challenge is that it is computationally very expensive (an NP-hard problem to find an exact solution). While methods such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) can be employed to find approximate solutions44, they are sample-inefficient due to the need to multiple roll-outs of the forward model. The computation and time cost of these methods make them unsuitable for real-time applications.

A data-driven approach to solve inverse problems can be a good alternative to the classical methods due to their ability to give good sub-optimal solutions with a higher speed of convergence. In recent years, DNNs have been used to predict the forward problem, which is then used to iteratively predict the system parameters12. This approach solves the inverse problem in a deterministic setting with a shared network. We extend this setup to two variants of network architecture (separate and shared) and analyze their performances in the deterministic setting, and add the probabilistic framework and robustness analysis to the solution approach. Our probabilistic setting is computationally efficient, which predicts two Gaussian parameters rather than employing an expensive Bayesian inference method such as MCMC, as in ref. 44. Regularized ensemble Kalman inversion (EKI) method45 proposes to deal with dynamics where physics models typically do not have readily available adjoint capabilities.46 (DEKI) addresses the scalability of EKI to higher dimensions by introducing dropouts. Conformal inverse optimization approach47 proposes learning an uncertainty set for unknown parameters and prescribes new decisions that are well-aligned with human intuition.

We compare some of the aforementioned approaches with our end-to-end (E2E) approach, both qualitatively and quantitatively. For qualitative comparison, we ask if the approaches address the inverse problem in deterministic and probabilistic settings, if the approach can support higher-order polynomial dynamics, if full PDE solver is required to compute the solution and whether the method tracks full posterior (for probabilistic approaches). For quantitative comparison, we compare the validation MSE and time cost for training and inference of select approaches with ours.

Comparison with classical methods

In this section, we compare our end-to-end (E2E) approach with a range of deterministic as well as probabilistic classical methods to benchmark the performance and cost of these methods for the power transformer test case. For the fairness of comparison, we report the training and inference times as measures of the computational cost rather than convergence rates, since the underlying optimization methods are quite different, which affects the convergence rates. For example, for one benchmarking method, one iteration requires a simple gradient propagation, another method requires solving a set of partial differential equations, and yet another approach requires solving a linear system. The qualitative as well as the quantitative results show that the E2E methods perform competitively with the baseline methods, often demonstrating better balance in performance and computation cost than the baseline methods.

A qualitative comparison for our end-to-end (E2E) framework with several baseline methods is presented in Table 1b. The comparison is based on key properties, including whether the methods provide deterministic or probabilistic solutions, allow approximations of high-order polynomial dynamics, require the use of a full solver, and keep track of posteriors for probabilistic approaches. The last two properties are relevant for computation costs, where the requirement of a full solver and need to keep track of the posterior distributions make a method costly. For instance, Table 1b shows that Tikhonov regularization is purely deterministic and does not require the use of a full solver, making it computationally inexpensive, while methods like Bayesian Neural Network Monte-Carlo (BNN-MC) offer probabilistic setting but keep track of the posterior, making the computational cost high. In contrast, the proposed E2E framework is the only approach that addresses all the evaluated criteria, offering a unified framework for the deterministic as well as probabilistic settings, balancing between good performance and relatively low computational cost due to no requirement of a full solver and keeping track of the posterior distribution, since it rather estimates the parameters of the Gaussian posterior. We summarize the benchmarking methods and a comparative study below.

Deterministic baseline methods

We apply Tikhonov regularization for a deterministic approach, which is serves as a classical baseline for regularization. We implement the Tikhonov method by solving the augmented normal equations using conjugate gradient descent, as described in ref. 48. Its performance, however, is critically dependent on the manual selection of a regularization parameter, γ, as noted in ref. 49. For our problem, we notice that even a small value (0.01) has a significant impact on the accuracy, and oversmoothing of physical features. As our second deterministic baseline, we apply Ensemble Kalman Inversion (EKI)50, which presents a derivative-free, iterative optimization framework whose cost scales with the ensemble size (J = 100 in our case) and the number of iterations. The method iteratively updates the ensemble through a prediction-correction scheme, computing empirical covariance matrices at each iteration. Our implementation requires approximately 20 iterations to converge, taking 45 minutes of training time, following the reference methodology proposed in ref. 50. The method achieves a validation MSE of 4.9 × 10−2, offering a reasonable balance between accuracy and computational cost. A key characteristic of EKI is the subspace property, which constrains the solution to the linear span of the initial ensemble, making its performance highly dependent on the quality of the initial guess.

Probabilistic baseline methods

We use Bayesian MCMC, BNN-MC, and BNN-Dropout as the baselines for the probabilistic approach. It is clear from the results that the implicit regularization offered by the stochastic formulation of our approach is more natural and offers a distinct improvement in accuracy. Fully probabilistic methods like Bayesian MCMC51 aim to approximate the entire posterior distribution of the unknown heat source. We implement full Bayesian inference using the preconditioned Crank-Nicolson MCMC method with a Gaussian process prior for sampling the posterior distribution of heat sources, following44. While theoretically robust, MCMC is exceptionally costly, often requiring tens of thousands of sequential forward model evaluations after an initial burn-in period of discarded samples, rendering it impractical for real-time applications. The process requires approximately 50000 samples with a burn-in period of 10000 iterations to adequately explore the posterior distribution in our infinite-dimensional parameter space. Training time reaches 850 minutes on our hardware. This aligns with findings in ref. 51, which reports that traditional MCMC methods for PDE-constrained inverse problems can be several times slower due to the need for repeated forward model evaluations. The high computational cost makes MCMC less practical for real-time industrial applications, despite a higher rigor in uncertainty quantification. Recent studies on surrogate-accelerated MCMC have shown that replacing expensive PDE solvers with neural network surrogates can reduce costs, but at the expense of introducing approximation errors. Neural network-based Bayesian methods exhibit a similar problem: the traditional Monte Carlo (MC) approach places distributions over each network weight, doubling the number of trainable parameters and leading to high computational and memory costs. Following52, we implemented BNN-MC using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) for posterior estimation over network weights. Our implementation employed 8 hidden layers with 128 neurons per layer, using Gaussian priors with zero mean and unit variance on all weights and biases. HMC sampling has been performed with 100 leapfrog steps per iteration and step size ϵ = 0.001. We collect 10000 posterior samples after a burn-in of 2000 iterations, requiring 189 minutes of training time. In contrast, the MC Dropout(BNN Dropout) approximation offers a cheaper alternative by keeping dropout active during inference, as suggested in ref. 53, but this efficiency comes at the cost of theoretical rigor. During training, a dropout rate p = 0.1 has been applied to all hidden layers for 50,000 epochs, requiring 38 minutes. At inference time, dropout remains active, and we perform 100 forward passes to approximate the posterior predictive distribution. This yields an inference time of approximately 400 ms per sample, faster than full BNN-MC but still substantially slower than deterministic methods.54 show that while dropout helps prevent overfitting, it provides less theoretically grounded uncertainty estimates compared to full Bayesian treatments.

Discussion

We establish through theoretical analysis and empirical evidence five key merits of PIML in heat loss localization: (i) the importance of data processing in prediction accuracy, (ii) an efficient way to balance trade-offs among prediction accuracy, computation and storage costs through PIML model architecture variants, (iii) a history-aware training method improving accuracy by incorporating relevant past information, (iv) a computationally efficient UQ method enhancing model interpretability and enabling robust sampling analysis, and (v) an indirect, reconstruction-based validation using multi-physics simulation. This methodology shows potential for extension to components where measurement data procurement is challenging or of lower quality.

In the context of estimating heat losses from temperature measurements using inverse problems, it is crucial to recognize that achieving a perfect fit between model predictions and experimental data may not be desirable. A perfect fit could imply that the model is capturing not only the underlying physical phenomena but also the noise inherent in the measurements. This phenomenon, often referred to as overfitting, can lead to misleading conclusions about the system’s behavior. In practical applications, especially in thermal imaging and heat transfer analysis, measurement noise is an unavoidable reality that can influence the accuracy of parameter estimations. The presence of noise necessitates a careful balance in model training, where the goal should be to capture the essential thermal dynamics while remaining robust against measurement uncertainties. If the model aligns too closely with noisy data, it risks losing its predictive capability and generalizability. Instead, a more prudent approach involves leveraging regularization techniques or incorporating prior knowledge about the system to mitigate the effects of noise. This ensures that the model remains focused on the underlying physics rather than fitting random fluctuations in the data. It has been shown that thermal measurement noise could pose a challenge in estimating boundary conditions and heat loss rates55. In our approach, by leveraging PIML and by introducing a probabilistic framework, we manage to capture the dynamical behavior of the heat losses with good accuracy and robustness. The predictive accuracy of the probabilistic approach (Pro-PINN) is shown and compared with that of its deterministic counterpart in Table 1a. However, instead of reducing the accuracy further, which may lead to overfitting, this approach maintains the robustness of prediction by leveraging an inherent trade-off in its training method, which is reflected in Fig. 8, which depicts the de-noising effect in the temperatures reconstructed from predicted heat loss without overfitting to the noise present in the measured temperature, which requires no additional steps of filtering of the training data.

In inverse problems with noisy measurements, achieving a perfect model-data fit is not desirable as it indicates overfitting, i.e., capturing not just underlying physics but also measurement noise, which can lead to misleading conclusions about system behavior. When working with noisy measurement data, it is crucial to capture essential thermal dynamics while maintaining robustness against uncertainties to ensure generalizability. Previous research has shown thermal measurement noise impacts boundary condition and heat loss rate estimation55. Our probabilistic PIML approach (Pro-PINN) successfully captures heat loss dynamics with both accuracy and robustness. Table 1a and Fig. 8 establish that Pro-PINN achieves reduced overfitting and more accurate and robust prediction in the presence of noisy data.

Conclusion

This paper presents an end-to-end approach to predict the cause of the observed effect in electrical and power dynamical systems and quantify the uncertainties for a class of dynamics (governed by polynomial spatiotemporal ODEs or PDEs). To that end, we use Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) approach with Neural Networks as the predictive models, develop the theory for the uncertainty, and address the robustness of the predictions in the presence of sparse data. Our empirical results with simulated as well as real data demonstrate PIML’s ability to accurately predict heat distribution beyond current physics simulation capabilities, while its uncertainty quantification enhances prediction accuracy and interpretability, enabling expert investigation of root causes. It introduces an end-to-end approach to balance the cost and performance, hence providing solutions that can be applied to near-real-time applications. While the history-aware training of models makes the training data-efficient, the quantitative analysis of the robustness and uncertainties makes the solutions more accurate and interpretable by users. By providing a unified framework for automated and robust system discovery in both deterministic and probabilistic settings, our work establishes a foundation for future research combining domain knowledge with data-driven methods for complex electrical assets, and thus lays the ground for more enhanced manufacturing and asset monitoring processes using combined data and domain-knowledge-driven approaches. The end-to-end approach to automated and robust system discovery introduced in this paper can contribute to future research in modeling multi-physics systems, such as the modeling of current-carrying capacity of underground and overhead power cable lines that require modeling of the physics involving the electrical, electromagnetic, and thermal dynamics, and modeling electrical contacts in electrical and electronic devices requiring modeling of pressure, electrical, and thermal dynamics.
