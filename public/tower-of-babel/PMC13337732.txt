Review of Quality Attributes and Analytical Methods Used for Comparative Analytical Assessment of Monoclonal Antibodies as Part of Successful Biosimilar Approvals in the United States and European Union
Authors: Rakesh Aithal, Olivia M Majedi, Diane McCarthy, Fouad Atouf, Niomi Peckham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have revolutionized therapeutic treatments by their ability to target specific antigens, leading to enhanced clinical outcomes over other drugs. They are one of the largest modalities within the growing biotherapeutics space and are indicated for a range of diseases. Though transformative, mAbs are still not readily accessible to many patients globally because of their high costs. An increasing number of mAbs are losing patent exclusivity, which has opened the door for the development of biosimilars that could drive down costs and ensure increased access to these life-saving drugs. Regulators approve biosimilars after conducting a rigorous evaluation similar to any other biologic medicine to ensure the safety, quality, and efficacy of these products. To establish biosimilarity, extensive comparative analytical and clinical studies of the biosimilar product with the approved reference product is a regulatory expectation. Growing acceptance from regulators to potentially waive clinical efficacy studies when robust evidence for similarity with reference product is established from analytical, functional, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies will have a major impact on reducing the time and cost of developing biosimilars. Comparative analytical assessment includes side-by-side analysis of the biosimilar with the reference product to demonstrate similarity regarding their physicochemical and functional characteristics. This is usually achieved by identifying product quality attributes (PQAs) that could impact clinical safety and efficacy and applying orthogonal analytical methods to characterize these attributes to identify any differences between the products. This review identifies the common PQAs studied for approved mAb biosimilars in the United States and the European Union through to the end of 2024. We have also compiled the data for the analytical methods used to characterize these attributes and identified a subset of methods universally used among biosimilar applicants. Finally, a brief overview of the risk-based analysis of attributes is summarized from the regulatory submissions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40259-026-00786-w.

Key Points

Comparative analytical assessment data of monoclonal antibody (mAb) biosimilars approved in the United States (US) and European Union (EU) were reviewed from publicly available regulatory submission documents.Results are summarized for the quality attributes included in the analytical packages of approved mAb biosimilars in the US and EU and analytical methods used for the characterization of these attributes.Regulatory agencies are moving towards reducing the requirements for clinical efficacy studies and increasing emphasis on a robust analytical package with evolving regulatory framework. This study will help in informing the analytical package to be included in the comparative analytical assessment during biosimilar development.

Introduction

Biosimilars are follow-on biological products of an approved reference product having a similar structure and functional activity. As of December 2024, the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved 63 biosimilars, including 43 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and their derivatives, 7 Fc-fusion proteins, and 13 protein therapeutics [1]. Zarxio (filgrastim-sndz), a biosimilar to NEUPOGEN (filgrastim) was the first biosimilar product to receive approval from the FDA in 2015 through the 351(k) biosimilar pathway established under the Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act [2]. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) has approved more than 90 biosimilars including 54 mAbs, 6 Fc-fusion proteins, 29 protein therapeutics, 5 peptide therapeutics, and 1 complex carbohydrate product [3]. It is important to note that peptides and complex carbohydrates in the US are not generally regulated as biologics. The first biosimilar medicine approved in the European Union (EU) was OMNITROPE (a biosimilar recombinant human growth hormone) in 2006 [3].

The overarching goal of the development of biosimilars is to reduce production time and costs enabling increased patient access to biologic drugs used to treat a wide range of diseases. Because of the reduced discovery phase and clinical studies required, the development of a biosimilar costs around US$100–250 million and takes approximately 7–8 years, compared with about US$1–1.8 billion in costs and 12 years for the development of a novel biological product [4]. Since the approval of the first biosimilar in 2015, there has been a savings of about US$36 billion in the US alone for patients and the healthcare system from the adoption of biosimilars [5].

Due to the complexity of the structure and manufacturing process involving living systems, the development process for biosimilars has been challenging from a production, analytical, clinical, and regulatory perspective. Well characterized reference materials together with scientifically sound analytical methods with adequate specificity and sensitivity to characterize product quality attributes (PQAs) are required to ensure the consistent quality of these products. The intent of this review is to compile the analytical methods employed to assess quality attributes of mAb therapeutics as part of successful biosimilar filings, which can be useful in building consensus on the PQAs characterized and appropriate analytical methods used for each attribute, thereby steering the efforts of those developing biosimilars.

The term ‘biosimilar’ or ‘biosimilarity’ is defined in the Public Health Service (PHS) Act “in reference to a biological product that is the subject of an application under [section 351(k)]” to mean “that the biological product is highly similar to the reference product notwithstanding minor differences in clinically inactive components” and that “there are no clinically meaningful differences between the biological product and the reference product in terms of the safety, purity, and potency of the product” (section 351(i)(2) of the PHS Act) [6, 7].

Principles for demonstrating the comparability of biotechnological/biological products before and after manufacturing changes including considerations on selecting relevant quality attributes and analytical techniques for the drug substance or drug product are recommended in guidance documents [8, 9]. Although the principles in these guidance documents can be applied for biosimilarity assessment, the recommendations provided in these guidance documents are limited to an assessment of the comparability of a biological product by the same manufacturer before and after a manufacturing process change, in which case extensive knowledge and information about the product and the existing process, including established controls and acceptance parameters, exist. However, demonstrating that a proposed product is highly similar to a reference product manufactured by a different manufacturer typically will be more complex and will likely require more extensive and comprehensive data where knowledge on the manufacturing process and process controls used in the reference product manufacturing is lacking [7]. Both comparability and biosimilarity assessment studies use batch release tests performed with validated methods and extended characterization tests, which are not necessarily validated to characterize relevant quality attributes to ensure the quality, safety, and efficacy of the product.

In April 2015, the FDA released two guidance documents to support the development of biosimilars, entitled “Scientific Considerations in Demonstrating Biosimilarity to a Reference Product” and “Quality Considerations in Demonstrating Biosimilarity of a Therapeutic Protein Product to a Reference Product” [10, 11]. The latter was replaced with an updated guidance document in 2025 to provide recommendations on the design and evaluation of comparative analytical studies intended to support biosimilar filings, and is titled “Development of Therapeutic Protein Biosimilars: Comparative Analytical Assessment and Other Quality-Related Considerations; Guidance for Industry” [7].

The FDA also released a draft guidance in 2017 on statistical analysis for the similarity assessment. This information on statistical analysis is now included in the guidance document “Development of Therapeutic Protein Biosimilars: Comparative Analytical Assessment and Other Quality-Related Considerations” [7]. This includes information on the classification of analytical methods into tiers based on risk assessment. The tier would determine the amount of data required and the statistical analysis recommended; tier 1 contains the most stringent requirements and would evaluate the highest risk attributes, with tier 3 being the lowest.

In the EU, the EMA is responsible for evaluating the majority of the applications to market biosimilars, and the approval is through a centralized authorization procedure [3]. The EMA defines a biosimilar as a “biological medicinal product that contains a version of the active substance of an already authorized original biological medicinal product (reference medicinal product) in the European Economic Area. Similarity to the reference medicinal product in terms of quality characteristics, biological activity, safety, and efficacy based on a comprehensive comparability exercise needs to be established [12].”

In October 2005, the EMA published a document titled “Guideline on Similar Biological Medicinal Products,” and updated this guideline in October 2014 [12]. In addition to this document, the EMA has produced multiple documents that are general to all biosimilars, and also provides additional documents that contain guidelines specific to certain types of biosimilar products, which is in contrast to the FDA providing only general guidance documents [13].

Another important regulatory note is the concept of interchangeability. FDA will determine the biological product to be interchangeable with the reference product if FDA determines that the information submitted in the application or the supplement is sufficient to show that the biological product “is biosimilar to the reference product” and “can be expected to produce the same clinical result as the reference product in any given patient” and that “for a biological product that is administered more than once to an individual, the risk in terms of safety or diminished efficacy of alternating or switching between use of the biological product and the reference product is not greater than the risk of using the reference product without such alternation or switch (section 351(k)(4) of the PHS Act) [6].” The FDA published a guidance document on the requirements for interchangeability in May 2019 titled “Considerations in Demonstrating Interchangeability with a Reference Product: Guidance for Industry [14].” However, based on the experience of the agency with the clinical performance of approved biosimilars, the agency published a draft guidance document in June 2024 to update the interchangeability requirements based on the agency’s current scientific approach: “Considerations in Demonstrating Interchangeability with a Reference Product: Update; Guidance for Industry [15].”

The EMA defines interchangeability as “the possibility of exchanging one medicine for another medicine that is expected to have the same clinical effect.” Heads of Medicines’ Agencies and EMA consider that once a biosimilar is approved in the EU it is interchangeable, which means the biosimilar can be used instead of its reference product (or vice versa) or one biosimilar can be replaced with another biosimilar of the same reference product. But the decision on how to implement interchangeability is not under the purview of EMA and is the responsibility of the individual member states [3, 16].

Hung et al. [17] provided a review of the general strategies used for approval of the first five biosimilars by the FDA, which included three mAbs, one Fc-fusion protein, and one protein therapeutic, with a focus on clinical data. Extensive analytical characterization was performed for all five biosimilars. In addition, applicants included three to five pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) studies and up to two clinical studies demonstrating efficacy. Currently, most biosimilar applications focus on the demonstration of analytical similarity by performing extensive physicochemical and functional characterization in comparison to the reference product, with more limited clinical data to compare PK/PD, immunogenicity, and efficacy.

The International Pharmaceutical Regulators Programme biosimilars working group summary report from a workshop on streamlining biosimilar development highlights the strong consensus reached among the attendees (including global regulators and industry representatives) on re-evaluating the need for comparative clinical efficacy studies [18]. There was also a broad agreement that clinical efficacy studies are not sensitive enough to detect anything but very large analytical differences between the proposed biosimilar and reference product, and a very large analytical difference would likely cause a proposed biosimilar to not be able to meet the “highly similar” standard based on analytics alone [18]. Also, the experience gained by the developers and regulators from the time the first biosimilar was approved in 2006 until present and the advances in the analytical methods to detect changes relevant to clinical safety and efficacy have led to discussions evaluating the necessity of clinical efficacy studies in the biosimilar development programs [18–21]. Further, the experience from biosimilar filings indicate that in most cases clinical efficacy studies do not add additional value in regulatory decision making and increase the cost for the biosimilar developers [18–21]. Therefore, streamlining of the biosimilar development programs is recommended with a major emphasis on the quality of the analytical data package and PK studies, with clinical efficacy studies required only in exceptional cases [18–21]. In line with the recommendations, several regulatory guidance documents have been updated to indicate that clinical efficacy studies are typically not required for biosimilar approvals when a stringent analytical comparability data package and clinical PK data are available [22–25]. Further, an International Council for Harmonisation expert working group has been established to develop a multidisciplinary guideline that describes a risk-based framework for determining the utility of clinical efficacy studies in biosimilar development programs [26]. This guideline aims to help identify the specific situations in which clinical efficacy studies would or would not be scientifically warranted to inform a conclusion of biosimilarity [26]. In both the US and the EU, each applicant must demonstrate that their product’s minor differences do not have any relevant clinical impact and it is still “highly similar” to the reference product. While these guidance documents do have differences, both mention overlapping categories such as physicochemical characterization, biological activity assessment, impurities, stability studies, and clinical PK trial [7, 27, 28]. They both also state the goal is to demonstrate the proposed product is similar to an already approved product and explain the potential effect any differences have on safety or efficacy [7, 27]. In many cases, applicants have submitted to the US and the EU for the same biosimilar and have included a comparison to both the US-approved and EU-approved originator product in the application.

The guidance documents give applicants a pathway and attributes to consider evaluating when developing biosimilars. However, the guidance documents do not provide any requirements for the use of specific analytical methods in demonstrating analytical comparability but recommend using orthogonal methods for a given product quality attribute [7, 27]. Applicants commonly test many attributes such as amino acid sequence, molecular weight, post-translational modifications (including glycosylation), higher-order structure (HOS), size variants, charge variants, protein content, and biological activity as part of their comparative analytical assessment. The flexible nature of the guidelines means the analytical package used can differ between applicants, even if those applicants produce the same drug. In addition, newer analytical technologies may be introduced in the development of a biosimilar that are not used by the originator. Advancements in the analytical methods used in comparative analytical assessment studies for different critical quality attributes (CQAs) over a period from 2010 to 2021 are outlined in an excellent review from Nupur et al. [29]. Applicants may use various analytical methods to characterize biosimilars, and it is up to the applicant to select the best ones for each attribute of their product with appropriate scientific justification.

To get a better understanding of the product attributes and analytics that have been specifically used in the industry as part of successful biosimilar filings, we reviewed publicly available summary documents including product quality reviews, summary reviews, and chemistry reviews from the US and public assessment reports from the EU. After reviewing these documents, we compiled a list of product attributes assessed and the methods used to establish analytical similarity in these successful filings. In this article, we summarize the product attributes characterized, and the methods used as part of the regulatory filings. We will limit the scope of this review to only mAbs as these form the majority of approved biosimilars. Methods

This examination began by reviewing the list of biosimilars approved by the FDA. Then we searched against the FDA purple book database for each biosimilar and reviewed several types of documents including product quality reviews, summary reviews, and chemistry reviews. The FDA prepares these review documents with information the applicant submitted to the FDA during the biologics license application filing.

These documents contain comparative analytical assessment data, which show the attributes and test methods each applicant used to compare their biosimilar to the reference product. We gathered the information from this section of the reviews and compiled the information for further analysis. Out of the 43 approved mAb biosimilars in the US, publicly available review documents containing data for comparative analytical assessment were available for 40 biosimilars and were considered in this study (Note: comparative analytical assessment data was not available for one adalimumab biosimilar [SIMLANDI], and two ustekinumab biosimilars [STEQEYMA and SELARSDI] at the time of drafting the article).

We widened the scope of this review to include biosimilars approved in the EU by the EMA. The EMA website was our database for approved biosimilars. The EMA website can be filtered for biosimilar medicines and reports applications that have been granted approval, denied approval, or withdrawn approval. Assessment reports documenting the comparability data were available for 53 out of the 54 biosimilars approved for mAbs in the EU (Note: comparative analytical assessment data was not available for AMGEVITA—a biosimilar to adalimumab at the time of drafting the article). In this review, a total of 93 review documents from FDA and EMA filings were considered to summarize the product attributes studied and the analytical methods employed for studying a product attribute.

Overall, there was a consistent grouping of analytical methods used in the available review documents; however, this varied slightly among applicants. We made the decision to categorize assays based on what was most often done by the applicants, adding additional categories for ease of presentation. We noted that acronyms and scientific vernaculars used to describe specific techniques varied. We combined assays using the information available in the filing based on scientific judgement.

Results

Guidance documents from the FDA and the EMA outline general criteria on the use of comparative analytical assessment to establish biosimilarity. Comparative analytical assessments include physicochemical characterization, functional characterization, similarity of degradation profile under accelerated and stressed conditions, similarity of long-term stability profile at intended storage condition, product-related impurities, and forced degradation studies. Functional characterization includes both Fab-dependent activities which are molecule specific depending on its target antigen and mechanism of action; and Fc-dependent activities which are typically common for full-length antibodies (Note: Fc-dependent activity can vary based on IgG subclass).

In the following section, we present a summary of the CQAs included for characterization studies and analytics used in biosimilarity assessment from successful applications for both the US and the EU. Again, we drew this summary from publicly available summary reports and reviews and assumed that additional details of the methods were contained in the filings submitted to the regulatory agencies. Note that only the CQAs and analytics used in comparative analytical assessment studies are included in this study. Results are not included for analytics used for overall quality (e.g., process impurities, stability profiles) which are not used in comparative studies of the proposed biosimilar with the originator reference product. Common CQAs for mAb biosimilars included in the comparative analytical assessment are shown in Fig. 1 and details for analytical methods used for testing these attributes are discussed in the following sections.

Primary Structure and Molecular Weight

Primary structure refers to the linear sequence of amino acid arrangement in the heavy and light chains of a mAb. This CQA must be characterized as part of biosimilarity assessment. Figure 2 shows a summary of the analytical methods used for characterization of primary structure and molecular weight. The methods used include reduced peptide mapping with UV and/or mass spectrometry (MS) detection, Edman degradation, molecular weight analysis by MS, and extinction coefficient determination.

Primary structure analysis. Methods used for primary structure determination of mAb biosimilars approved in the US and EU. EU European Union, mAb monoclonal antibody, US United States

Peptide mapping under reducing conditions is the widely applied methodology to demonstrate similarity of the biosimilar candidate’s amino acid sequence to the reference product. All the 40 US and 53 EU applications included peptide mapping as part of analytical similarity assessment to demonstrate an identical amino acid sequence between the biosimilar and reference product. Peptide mapping was most often performed with trypsin digestion, with several applicants using other site-specific proteases including Lys-C, Asp-N, Glu-C, and chymotrypsin for digestion to obtain complete amino acid sequence coverage. Peptide mapping coupled with tandem MS was also most often used to confirm the identity of N- and C-terminal sequences.

Edman degradation was included in 9 out of the 40 applications in the US and 13 out of the 53 in the EU. This was either used as a standalone method to confirm the identity of the N-terminal sequence or used in combination with peptide mapping to obtain complete coverage for the mAb sequence. With the exceptional resolution and mass accuracy achieved by the modern high-resolution MS (HRMS) instruments, a majority of the applicants preferred peptide mapping with MS detection for amino acid sequencing.

Molecular weight determination (mass analysis) by MS was included in the majority of the US and EU filings. Molecular mass analysis at the intact level where the masses of the major glycoforms were compared was included in 38 of the 40 US and 45 of the 53 EU filings. Heavy chain and light chain mass comparisons were performed by reduced mass analysis in 24 US and 26 EU filings. Deglycosylated mass analysis either at the intact or reduced level after the removal of N-linked glycans using Peptide-N-Glycosidase F (PNGaseF) was included in 17 US and 19 EU filings. Mass analysis at the subunit level after the digestion of the mAb with site-specific proteases such as immunoglobulin-degrading enzyme of Streptococcus pyogenes was included in eight US and six EU filings. Most of the applicants used reverse phase chromatography (RPC) coupled with MS to perform mass analysis; however, several applications also used size-exclusion chromatography coupled with MS (SEC-MS).

Extinction coefficient determination together with amino acid analysis for accurate protein content estimation was included in 18 of the 40 US and 28 of the 53 EU applications.

Post-translational Modifications

Post-translational modifications have the potential to impact the bioavailability, efficacy, immunogenicity, and stability of a biologic drug. Characterization of these modifications is a critical aspect of product knowledge since they are a source of product heterogeneity; and monitoring them is required to demonstrate process consistency [30]. We observed that analysis of N-terminal and C-terminal variants, oxidation, deamidation, glycation, and aspartic acid isomerization were often included in the analytical comparability assessments in both US and EU filings (Fig. 3).

Post-translational modifications included in mAb biosimilar approvals in the US and EU. EU European Union, mAb monoclonal antibody, US United States

N-terminal variant analysis was included in 30 of the 40 US and 40 of the 53 EU filings, which typically includes monitoring of pyroglutamate formation, presence of signal peptide sequences, and truncation variants. C-terminal variants monitored include C-terminal lysine and C-terminal amidation and this was included in 31 US and 37 EU filings. Oxidation, mostly observed on methionine residues, was included in 36 US and 42 EU filings. Deamidation of asparagine was studied in 34 US and 39 EU filings. Peptide mapping with MS detection (typically tandem MS) was the commonly used method to extensively characterize the above-mentioned modifications at the amino acid level, while ion exchange chromatography (IEX) and/or capillary isoelectric focusing/imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (cIEF/icIEF) are used for quality control purposes once thorough product knowledge is established.

Aspartic acid isomerization to isoaspartic acid was monitored in 10 of the 40 US and 17 of the 53 EU filings. Methods commonly used in the filings to study isomerization included reduced peptide mapping with MS detection and an isoaspartate detection kit, with a few applicants using papain with hydrophobic interaction chromatography–high-performance liquid chromatography (HIC-HPLC) and RPC analysis. Glycation resulting from the reaction between reducing sugars and the primary amine groups in the mAb was included in 21 US and 27 EU filings. Analytical methods to characterize glycation included mass analysis by liquid chromatography MS (at intact, reduced, or subunit level), boronate affinity chromatography, and reduced peptide mapping with MS detection.

Other modifications such as succinimide intermediate, thioether linkage, and trisulfide bonds are less commonly included in the similarity data. Acetylation, methylation, and norleucine incorporation are reported in similarity data for the antibody fragment ranibizumab, which is expressed in Escherichia coli cells.

Disulfide Bond and Free Cysteines

Formation of native disulfide bonds are a CQA of mAbs since they have the potential to impact structure, function, and the stability of the molecule [31]. Disulfide bond analysis demonstrating a similar pairing of the cysteine residues in a biosimilar to the reference product is critical and was included in 39 of the 40 US and 49 of the 53 EU filings. Analysis was performed by peptide mapping under non-reducing conditions with MS detection to identify peptides with disulfide linkages.

Level of free cysteines resulting from incomplete disulfide formation or reduction of existing disulfide bonds can impact the stability, aggregation propensity, and function of the mAb [30]. Free thiol content analysis was included in 30 US and 46 EU filings. Ellman’s assay with spectrophotometric detection was the most routinely used method for free thiol content estimation, but several applicants also used fluorometric detection.

Applicants studied disulfide isomers for the biosimilar developed in the US and EU for denosumab reference product—a mAb belonging to immunoglobulin G (IgG) subclass 2 (IgG2) known to have three different disulfide bond-related isomers named as IgG2-A, IgG2-B, and IgG2-A/B [32]. It was also included in a biosimilar (Bkemv) developed in the US for eculizumab, a humanized IgG2/4 antibody. In addition to non-reduced peptide mapping with MS detection, applicants used RPC to analyze the disulfide isomers. RPC was used to separate and quantitate peaks representing the three different disulfide isomers in denosumab (IgG2) and eculizumab (IgG2/4) products.

Higher-Order Structure

HOS is a CQA directly contributing to the clinically relevant biological functions of protein therapeutics and refers to the overall three-dimensional structure of the protein encompassing the secondary, tertiary, and the quaternary structure [33]. HOS assessment is a regulatory requirement mentioned in both the US and EU guidance documents as part of a meaningful comparative analytical assessment. HOS assessment includes the application of multiple orthogonal methods to demonstrate similarity of the secondary structure, tertiary structure, and thermal stability of the biosimilar to the reference product. CD circular dichroism, DSC differential scanning calorimetry, ELISA enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, EU European Union, Fab fragment antigen-binding, FTIR Fourier transform infrared, H/D hydrogen/deuterium, mAb monoclonal antibody, NMR nuclear magnetic resonance, US United States, UV ultraviolet

Secondary structure was commonly studied by far-ultraviolet circular dichroism (far-UV CD) spectroscopy (included in 25 of the 40 US and 48 of the 53 EU filings), and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (included in 28 US and 48 EU filings).

Near-UV CD spectroscopy was the most-used method to characterize the tertiary structure (included in 29 US and 47 EU filings), followed by intrinsic fluorescence measurement (included in 16 US and 25 EU filings). Orthogonal methods less commonly used for tertiary structure characterization included hydrogen/deuterium (H/D) exchange MS, extrinsic fluorescence measurement, protein conformation array enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), X-ray crystallography, and 1-D/2-D nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.

Thermal stability comparison was frequently used as part of the HOS assessment and was included in 36 of the 40 US and 46 of the 53 EU filings. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was the preferred method used for studying thermal stability, whereas one applicant in the EU used differential scanning fluorimetry for the study.

Antibodies belonging to the IgG4 subclass have a tendency to dissociate into half molecules and re-assemble to form novel bivalent antibodies, a process termed as Fab-arm exchange that could impact its biological activity [34]. Fab-arm exchange kinetics were studied using fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) under physiologically relevant conditions for the biosimilar developed in the US and EU for natalizumab reference product—a mAb belonging to the IgG4 subclass.

Subvisible Particles

Subvisible particles are an important CQA of injectable protein therapeutics and must be controlled to ensure safety and stability of biotherapeutics. These particles can be composed of aggregated proteins, degradation products from excipients, silicone oil from syringes, or other contaminants from the manufacturing or drug delivery process [35]. Microflow imaging was the most utilized method to analyze subvisible particles and is included in 17 of the 40 US and 26 of the 53 EU filings. A light obscuration/high-accuracy particle counting method was included in ten US and nine EU filings. Resonant mass measurement was less commonly used and included in one US and four EU filings. Several applicants used more than one method to validate the results.

Protein Concentration

Protein concentration is a general attribute that needs to be controlled since it can potentially impact the efficacy, safety, and PK of the biotherapeutic. Protein concentration is determined to demonstrate similar strength in comparison to reference products. All the 40 applications included protein concentration determination as part of the biosimilarity assessment in the US filings, and it was included in 52 of the 53 EU filings. UV absorbance at 280 nm using the experimentally determined extinction coefficient was the most common method used whereas theoretical extinction coefficient was used by few applicants. UV-visible spectrophotometer with a fixed pathlength has been the most used method. UV-Vis spectrophotometer with variable pathlength technology such as the SoloVPE system has also been used in filings for protein concentration measurement.

Size Variants

Size variants such as fragments and aggregates are considered CQA for a mAb since they can impact safety and efficacy; and need to be analyzed as part of comparative analytical assessment. We have grouped analytical methods used for characterization of aggregation and fragmentation together as size variants, as shown in Fig. 5. SEC with UV detection was the most common method used for the quantitation of size variants including the high molecular weight species and low molecular weight species. SEC was included in 37 of the 40 US and 50 of the 53 EU filings. Most of the applicants used SEC with the high-performance liquid chromatography system (SE-HPLC). However, there were seven applicants in the US and three applicants in the EU who used SEC with the ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (SE-UHPLC).

Orthogonal methods for size variant analysis included SEC with multi-angle light scattering detector (SEC-MALS), analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC), dynamic light scattering (DLS), field-flow fractionation (FFF), and sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). SEC-MALS was included in 24 US and 28 EU filings. AUC was performed by 27 applicants in the US and 40 applicants in the EU, and in a majority of the filings included sedimentation velocity analytical ultracentrifugation (SV-AUC) except for one applicant each in the US and EU using sedimentation equilibrium analytical ultracentrifugation (SE-AUC). DLS was used in 11 US and 21 EU filings, and FFF was included in 6 US and 5 EU filings. SDS-PAGE was included by five applicants in the US and 11 applicants in the EU.

For comparability of fragments, capillary electrophoresis–sodium dodecyl sulfate (CE-SDS) was the preferred method, either using reducing or non-reducing conditions. rCE-SDS included in 37 US and 50 EU filings was used to mainly quantitate the percentage of heavy chain, light chain, non-glycosylated heavy chain, and fragments. nrCE-SDS was used in 35 US and 48 EU filings to determine the percentage of intact IgG and fragments. All the applicants in the US and EU performing nrCE-SDS also included rCE-SDS in their filings.

Charge Variants

Charge variants include the acidic and basic variants of the main species and may impact the safety, efficacy, and PK profile of the product. Acidic variants typically include modifications like deamidation, sialylation, and glycation, and basic variants include the presence of C-terminal lysine, uncyclized N-terminal glutamine, and C-terminal amidation. They are the most common source of product heterogeneity and hence need to be controlled within defined limits [36].

Charge variant analysis was performed primarily by ion exchange chromatography (IEX) and capillary isoelectric focusing as shown in Fig. 6. IEX was performed by 33 of the 40 applicants in the US and 48 of the 53 in the EU. A majority of the applicants in the US and EU used cation exchange chromatography for charge variant analysis, except for the application of anion-exchange chromatography by two applicants each in the US and EU to study charge variants of biosimilars developed for eculizumab reference product—an IgG2/4 kappa isotype antibody with a relatively low isoelectric point.

Methods used for determination of charge variants for mAb biosimilars approved in the US and EU. cIEF capillary isoelectric focusing, CZE capillary zone electrophoresis, EU European Union, icIEF imaged capillary isoelectric focusing, IEX ion exchange chromatography, mAb monoclonal antibody, US United States, 2D-DIGE two-dimensional difference gel electrophoresis

Capillary isoelectric focusing which includes both capillary isoelectric focusing (cIEF) and imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (icIEF) was used in 24 US filings and 37 EU applications. Data shows icIEF (included in 15 US and 21 EU filings) to be the more preferred method over cIEF (included in 9 US and 16 EU filings). 20 US and 34 EU applicants have included both IEX and cIEF/icIEF in their filings. Gel-based isoelectric focusing was included in four US and six EU filings. Capillary zone electrophoresis and two-dimensional difference gel electrophoresis (2D-DIGE) were not commonly used but included in few filings for charge variant comparability.

Hydrophobic Variants

Hydrophobic variants of mAbs can result from conformational changes induced by various post-translational modifications including oxidation; they may impact the stability and efficacy of the molecule. Though not widely used in comparability assessment, hydrophobic variants are characterized mainly by HIC or RPC. HIC was included in 9 of the 40 U.S. and 14 of the 53 EU filings; and RPC was included in three US and seven EU filings.

Glycosylation

Glycosylation, specifically the N-linked glycosylation on the conserved asparagine residue on Fc region, impacts the effector functions, PK, stability, and immunogenicity of the mAb. Characterization of the N-linked glycan profile is necessary to ensure product safety and quality and is generally included in the comparability assessment exercise. Multiple methods were included by the applicants to study the site of glycosylation, released N-linked glycan profile, total sialic acid content, and the monosaccharide composition analysis as shown in Fig. 7.

N-linked glycosylation analysis. Attributes studied for N-linked glycosylation analysis in mAb biosimilar approvals in the US and EU. EU European Union, mAb monoclonal antibody, US United States

N-linked glycan analysis was included in 34 of the 40 US filings with a majority of the applicants (30) using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) with fluorescence detection alone or in some cases coupled with MS detection. In the EU, 48 of the 53 applicants included N-linked glycan profile in their comparability study with the majority (46) again using the HILIC method. The two US and five EU applications that did not contain glycan profile were the biosimilars of ranibizumab reference product, which is an antibody fragment (Fab) and is not glycosylated. High-performance anion-exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) was included in three US and two EU filings for N-linked glycan profile. A single applicant in the US included RPC with fluorescence and MS detection for N-linked glycan profile. Analysis of α1–3-galactosylation, which can potentially cause adverse immune reactions [37], was included in 8 US and 12 EU filings, with all the applicants using HILIC except one in which capillary gel electrophoresis with laser-induced fluorescence method was used.

Glycosylation site determination was mainly performed by peptide mapping coupled with MS detection and was included in 16 US and 21 EU filings. Monosaccharide composition analysis to quantitate the levels of individual sugar residues was performed by 8 US and 17 EU applicants with methods including RPC with fluorescence detection, HPAEC-PAD, HILIC-MS, and gas chromatography with MS detection (GC-MS). Terminal sialylation of the Fc glycan is another critical modification impacting the effector functions and PK properties of the mAb [38]. Sialic acid content was determined by 11 US and 22 EU applicants using either RPC with fluorescence detection with 1,2-diamino-4,5-methylenedioxybenzene (DMB) labeling or HPAEC-PAD analysis.

Biological Activity

As comprehensive functional characterization is called out in the regulatory guidance, biological activity assays reflective of the product’s intended mechanism or mechanisms of action (MoA) are a significant part of establishing biosimilarity. Therefore, functional assays employed should reflect clinically relevant MoAs known for the reference product to the extent possible; and multiple functional assays are recommended to be performed as part of comparative analytical assessment [7]. For this review, we have not included potency or binding assays that are molecule specific but have evaluated the assays used to characterize Fc-dependent functions of mAbs as they are generally included in the regulatory filings for most of the mAbs. In Fig. 8, we show the Fc-mediated effector functions and binding assays included by the applicants in their filings.

Fc-dependent functions characterized in mAb biosimilar approvals in the US and EU. Fc fragment crystallizable, ADCC antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, ADCP antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, CDC complement-dependent cytotoxicity, C1q complement component 1q, EU European Union, FcRn neonatal Fc receptor, FcγR Fc gamma receptor, mAb monoclonal antibody, US United States

Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) mediated by the interaction of Fc domain of the antibody predominantly with the FcγRIIIA receptor expressed on natural killer (NK) cells [39] was included in 36 of the 40 US filings except for the two biosimilars each of ranibizumab which is an antibody fragment (Fab) without the Fc region and eculizumab (IgG2/4) which lacks ADCC activity. An ADCC assay was also included in 45 of the 53 EU filings, except for the biosimilar filings of ranibizumab, eculizumab, and omalizumab. Applicants in both the US and EU used peripheral blood mononuclear cell-based assay, NK cell-based assay, or reporter gene assay formats to characterize the ADCC activity of the mAb. In several cases, the applicants used either two or all the three assay formats mentioned above to study ADCC activity using orthogonal approaches. Two assay formats were used by 13 US and 15 EU applicants; and three assay formats were used by two applicants each in the US and EU.

Complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) is another effector function that is part of the MoA of certain mAbs and is mediated by the interaction of Fc domain with the complement component C1q [39]. A CDC assay was included in the majority of the filings with 35 applicants in the US and 43 applicants in the EU using it in their comparability assessment. A C1q binding assay primarily performed using the ELISA method was included in 38 US and 47 EU filings, with two applicants each in the US and EU using the biolayer interferometry (BLI) method. Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis induced by interaction of the mAb’s Fc region with the FcγRIIa receptor on macrophages [40] was included in 7 US and 13 EU filings. Generally, if the Fc-dependent effector functions such as ADCC and CDC activities are potential MoA of the mAb, they are analyzed as a high-risk attribute by quantitative analysis in comparison to the reference product. Where the effector functions are not part of the MoA, the lack of activity in comparison to the reference product is demonstrated.

All the applicants in the US and EU performed an FcγR binding assay to complement the cell-based assays except for the biosimilar filings of ranibizumab, which lacks the Fc region. FcγRIIIa (158V), FcγRI, and FcRn binding assays were included in all the 38 US and 48 EU filings studying FcγR binding. FcγRIIa (131H) binding was included in 34 US and 45 EU filings. FcγRIIIa (158F), FcγRIIa (131R), FcγRIIb, and FcγRIIIb binding are included in 33 US filings each and in 45, 43, 44, and 38 EU applications, respectively. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was the most common method to study binding kinetics in both the US and EU. Additional assays that were less commonly used include AlphaLISA or AlphaScreen, BLI, ELISA, FRET, or cell-based binding assays.

Forced Degradation Studies

In addition to the comparative assessment of physicochemical and functional quality attributes, the comparative evaluation of stability under accelerated and stressed temperature storage conditions together with comparative forced degradation studies is recommended in the current guidance documents in both the US and the EU [7, 27]. The guidelines recommend these studies be performed using multiple stress conditions to study any product differences regarding the degradation profiles that may need additional evaluation. Commonly used stress conditions in both the US and EU filings include elevated temperature, light exposure, oxidative stress, low pH, and high pH conditions, as shown in Fig. 9. Mechanical stress such as agitation and freeze-thaw conditions were less commonly used in comparative assessment. Only one applicant in the US compared degradation profiles with exposure to metal ions in their filing.

Risk Ranking

Regulatory agencies recommend using a risk-based approach to rank quality attributes based on their potential to impact safety, efficacy, bioavailability, and immunogenicity. Applicants are recommended to develop a risk assessment tool considering two factors: (a) potential impact of a quality attribute on clinical performance established from publicly available information and applicants’ own characterization data of the reference product; and (b) the degree of uncertainty surrounding a certain quality attribute to impact clinical performance. The risk scores for attributes should be proportional to the patient risk, and be clearly defined, justified, and supported by literature and data provided. All the 40 applicants in the US used a risk-based approach to rank PQAs as either high, moderate, or low risk. Risk-based rankings of the PQAs were clearly defined in 33 of the 53 EU filings from the information provided in the publicly available assessment reports.

We observed several trends in the summary of the risk rankings found in the regulatory submission documents (see Table 3) derived from the one EU and 19 US filings in which the tiers for the analytical methods were available. As recommended by agencies, applicants typically used a risk-based approach for statistical evaluation of analytical results using a tiering approach, with tier 1 attributes representing the greatest risk and impact on clinical performance, tier 2 posing a moderate risk, and tier 3 having the lowest risk. Tier 1 and 2 attributes are generally tested using quantitative assays with statistical evaluations such as equivalence testing or the quality range approach to demonstrate biosimilarity. Tier 3 attributes are tested using qualitative assays with visual data comparisons. Certain attributes such as primary sequence, though highly critical, are placed in tier 3 tests since they are not amenable for quantitative analysis.

Discussion

More than 200 mAb therapeutics are approved globally and nearly 1400 mAbs are in clinical development for multiple indications including cancers, autoimmune diseases, and infectious diseases [41]. With their ability to target specific antigens, mAbs have had a significant impact on addressing unmet medical needs, and they constitute one of the largest modalities under the biopharma market. However, equitable access to patients is a challenge due to the complexity of the manufacturing process, high development costs, and the high cost of the innovator product. Biosimilars—follow-on biologics highly similar to the approved reference product—could increase market competition, resulting in decreased costs and increased patient access.

Changes in the regulatory framework for biosimilar approvals and patent expiration/loss of exclusivity have led to an increase in the number of applications for biosimilar products. A major focus of a biosimilar development program is to demonstrate analytical similarity of the biosimilar product in comparison to the reference product. Inherent heterogeneity is expected in certain quality attributes of mAbs since they are produced in living systems and drift in certain quality attributes over time have been reported in innovator products during post-manufacturing changes [42–44]. While it is not expected that all the quality attributes of the biosimilar product will be identical to the reference product due to the distinct manufacturing processes employed, any differences observed between the biosimilar and reference product need to be described and appropriately justified to have no impact on the clinical performance of the product [7, 27]. Several excellent review articles discuss the strategies for biosimilar development, including quality attributes and methods included in analytical comparability assessment, scientific, and regulatory requirements [29, 45, 46]. Also, a white paper by Vandekerckhove et al. [47] provides a detailed explanation of rational CQA selection, criticality assessment, tiering of quality attributes, and analytical methods including several case studies. However, there is a gap in the availability of information on the quality attributes studied and the suite of analytical methods included in successful regulatory submissions.

In this review, we have conducted extensive analysis of the publicly available regulatory submission documents to explore the quality attributes studied and analytics used to support biosimilar approvals in the US and the EU. This in-depth analysis from successful filings will provide biosimilar manufacturers with additional insights into the biosimilar development process regarding the PQAs commonly studied and the most-used analytical methods to characterize a given attribute. Since analytical characterization is the foundation of comparability, the findings from this study would also be useful for evaluating potential changes to mAb products that may arise after manufacturing changes [36, 48, 49].

Data from regulatory filings identify the mAb quality attributes routinely included for comparative analytical assessment, such as primary structure, post-translational modifications, disulfide bond, free thiol content, HOS, thermal stability, product-related variants, glycosylation, protein concentration, and functional activities. Comparative forced degradation studies are also performed by a majority of the applicants, with thermal, photo, oxidative, and pH stress being commonly used. Some of the other attributes, such as hydrophobic variants, subvisible particles, monosaccharide analysis, and total sialic acid content are less commonly included in comparative data. Certain attributes included in filings are also specific to the IgG subclass, such as disulfide isomers in case of denosumab and eculizumab biosimilars belonging to the IgG2 and IgG2/4 subclass, respectively, and Fab-arm exchange kinetics in case of a natalizumab biosimilar representing the IgG4 subclass.

Regulatory expectations for the biosimilarity assessment are to use orthogonal analytical methods to study a particular quality attribute. This review also finds that some assays are nearly universally included in analytical comparability compared with other methods in both the US and EU. Intact mass analysis and peptide mapping under reducing condition for primary structure determination, non-reduced peptide mapping for disulfide bond analysis, HILIC with fluorescence detection for N-linked glycan profiling, DSC for thermal stability analysis, far-UV CD and FTIR for secondary structure determination, near-UV CD for tertiary structure comparison, SEC and CE-SDS for size variant analysis, IEX for charge variant analysis, and UV280 absorbance for protein concentration determination are the most-used methods for physicochemical characterization. Apart from product-specific functional assays, cell-based assays to study ADCC and CDC activity and binding assays to study interaction with C1q, FcγR, and FcRn are the most common biological activity assays included. Binding to C1q was typically studied by an ELISA assay whereas binding to FcγR and FcRn was studied mainly by an SPR-based assay.

Although this article focuses on regulatory submissions in the US and EU, findings from this study on the quality attributes investigated as part of biosimilarity exercises aligns with the guidance from the World Health Organization and other regulators [22, 50–52]. All these guidance documents recommend comparative data be provided on primary structure, higher-order structure, post-translational modifications including glycosylation, product-related substances/impurities, and biological activity. They also recommend the use of scientifically sound analytical methods that are reliable and sensitive to detect relevant differences in the quality attributes between the biosimilar and reference product. The use of orthogonal methods having different physicochemical or biological principles to collect data for complex attributes is also recommended in these guidance documents.

Analysis of the data shows a trend to utilize advanced techniques to phase out some of the earlier methods with low throughput, high variability, and less sensitivity. For example, gel-based SDS-PAGE and iso-electric focusing separations are less commonly used and have largely been replaced with capillary-based separations which are more precise and yield better resolution and sensitivity. Low throughput methods such as Edman degradation have been replaced with high throughput MS-based methods to confirm protein sequence. We also observed that tertiary structure determination has the greatest number of orthogonal methods used in the filings, which may be a result of the limited capacity of the analytical methods to provide atomic-level resolution, which therefore requires multiple analytical approaches to establish similarity.

Biosimilar sponsors apply a suite of analytical methods to perform a comprehensive comparative analytical assessment in order to identify any minor differences in the PQAs relevant for clinical performance between the biosimilar and reference product. Inherent heterogeneity present in biotherapeutics requires the application of multiple orthogonal methods to characterize key physicochemical and functional attributes [53]. Future analytical methods for biosimilar characterization are shifting toward more efficient, sensitive, and high-resolution techniques able to establish identity and detect subtle differences in post-translational modifications and higher-order structure, thereby reducing the need for extensive clinical trials. Some of the key emerging techniques include the multi-attribute method, advanced mass spectrometry techniques (including native MS, ion mobility MS, and HDX-MS), NMR for HOS characterization, and two-dimensional liquid chromatography separations.

Conclusions

Biosimilar developers need to conduct comprehensive comparability studies, with a major emphasis on demonstrating analytical similarity including physicochemical and functional characteristics. In order to understand any significant structural or functional differences between a biosimilar and the reference product, it is necessary to have a thorough understanding of the capabilities and limitations of the analytical methodologies used to establish biosimilarity. Applicants must also select analytical methodologies with adequate sensitivity and specificity to detect, characterize, and in some instances quantitate differences between the originator and the proposed biosimilar. Multiple orthogonal methods for most attributes have been used in successful applications in the US and EU to demonstrate analytical comparability, with some methods more universally used across applicants than other methods. In this review, we have summarized publicly available data from the regulatory filings in the US and EU to identify common quality attributes studied and analytical methods typically used to study these attributes. This information provides meaningful insights into the analytical comparability aspect of biosimilar development. Robust comparative analytical assessment data, supported by PK/PD studies, has become the foundation of biosimilar development, and can be leveraged to reduce the need for extensive, costly, and time-consuming clinical efficacy studies. Greater alignment on the PQAs to be included in comparative analytical assessment and appropriate test methods can further improve efficiency and encourage new biosimilar development.
