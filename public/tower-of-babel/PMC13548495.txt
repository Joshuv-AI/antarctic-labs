An exploration of demographic and conceptual factors related to attitudes towards dementia
Authors: Swapneel Mirani, Shi Tang, Tessa Williams, Briony Harden, Martin Orrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile negative attitudes towards dementia are well documented, there is a lack of understanding of the factors that influence these attitudes. This study examines whether demographic factors, wisdom, and attitudes towards aging are associated with dementia attitudes.MethodsThis study used an online, quantitative, and cross-sectional survey design to explore factors that are correlated with attitudes towards dementia in the United Kingdom. The survey includes questions on demographic information, the Dementia Attitudes Scale (DAS), Three-Dimensional Wisdom Scale-12 Items (3D-WS-12), and the Attitudes to Aging Questionnaire (AAQ).ResultsBeing a professional in dementia care, wisdom, and positive attitudes to aging were significantly correlated with more positive attitudes toward dementia. Introduction

Background and rationale

People with dementia often encounter stigma and discrimination due to negative misconceptions from both the public and themselves [1–4]. Some of the most common public stigmas about people with dementia are that they are dangerous and can lose control of themselves [3]. As negative stereotypes or labeling of a group can lead to internalization of these ideas, self-stigma can make people with dementia feel ashamed, vulnerable, invisible, and lower their self-confidence [4, 5]. Despite increased public awareness in recent years, many societies still have misunderstandings about dementia [5].

Previous research has consistently demonstrated that public attitudes toward dementia play a crucial role in shaping the lived experiences of people with the condition, influencing social inclusion, help-seeking behaviors, quality of care, and overall quality of life [6–8]. Negative attitudes have been observed across different cultural contexts and age groups geographically, suggesting that dementia-related stigma is a widespread and persistent issue [9–12]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated these challenges, intensifying social discrimination and reinforcing harmful stereotypes about dementia, particularly around perceptions of vulnerability and burden [13–15]. In healthcare settings, gaps in dementia knowledge, limited training, time constraints, and difficulties in interdisciplinary collaboration among healthcare professionals have also been identified as contributors to stigma and suboptimal care [16, 17].

Alongside demographic and experiential factors, growing attention has been given to psychological and conceptual factors that may influence attitudes toward dementia. One such construct is wisdom, commonly defined as expert knowledge about the fundamental pragmatics of life, focusing on how people think about theoretical problems [18]. Wisdom has been associated with greater empathy, emotional regulation, tolerance of uncertainty, and the ability to integrate multiple perspectives when making judgments [19–20]. Given that stigma toward dementia is often rooted in fear, lack of knowledge, and reliance on simplified or prejudicial beliefs, it would be expected that individuals with higher levels of wisdom may demonstrate greater understanding of dementia and form more compassionate and well-balanced judgments about the condition. Moreover, wisdom encompasses not only cognitive capacities but also affective qualities such as empathy and compassion, which may be particularly relevant to dementia attitudes given that stigma often operates through emotional and interpersonal channels. It manifests as social avoidance, discomfort, and fear rather than purely factual misunderstanding [36]. Individuals with greater wisdom may therefore be better positioned to respond to people with dementia with emotional openness and acceptance, rather than fear or pity.

Together, this body of evidence highlights that attitudes toward dementia are shaped by a range of demographic, experiential, and psychological factors, yet the mechanisms underlying these associations remain incompletely understood.

Methods

Study design

This study used an online, quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to explore factors that were associated with attitudes toward dementia in the United Kingdom. The questionnaire was developed for the study and used in other research, but these studies have not been published yet. As a result, an English version has been attached and will be available as a supplementary file. Eligible participants were recruited through the Join Dementia Research (JDR) platform, a free online platform that connects volunteers interested in aging and dementia-related studies across the United Kingdom. Eligibility criteria established by JDR required participants to be aged 18 or older and proficient in English. The participants had to sign the information sheet to show informed consent and then provide a code memorable to them so their answers could be located if they wished to withdraw their data. Data were collected between 17th July and 6th September 2024. Ethical approval was given by the Unit of Mental Health and Clinical Neurosciences ethics committee at the University of Nottingham (ID: 3170).

Procedure

Eligible volunteers participated in this survey online through JDR. The surveys asked demographic questions on age, gender, experience of dementia, ethnicity, and then moved to questionnaires on wisdom and attitudes to aging and dementia. After submitting their responses, participants were directed to a page of the debrief sheet, where they could either enter their details in a separate survey for a prize draw or simply close the window to end the survey.

Measures

Dementia Attitudes Scale (DAS)

Attitudes towards dementia were assessed using the 20-item DAS [21], which measures dementia knowledge and social comfort with affective, behavioral, and cognitive components of an attitude. Responses were recorded on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The overall score ranges from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes. The DAS demonstrated high internal consistency in previous research (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) [21].

Attitudes to Aging Questionnaire (AAQ)

Attitudes toward aging were measured using the 24-item AAQ [22], which includes three subscales: psychosocial loss (PL), physical change (PC), and psychosocial growth (PG). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. For items 1–7, the scale ranges from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and for items 8–24, the scale ranges from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (extremely true), with the AAQ_PL subscale reverse scored. The overall score ranges from 24 to 120, while the subscale scores range from 8 to 40. Higher scores indicate more positive attitudes to aging, and each subscale has been found to have good internal consistency (PL= 0.81, PC= 0.81, PG= 0.74) [22].

Three-Dimensional Wisdom Scale – 12 items (3D-WS-12)

Wisdom was assessed using the 3D-WS-12 [23], which captures cognitive, reflective, and affective dimensions of wisdom. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Questions 5, 9, and 10 are reversely coded. Total scores range from 12 to 60. Higher scores mean a greater level of wisdom. The 3D-WS-12 has been found to have good reliability values of 0.73 and 0.63 for subscales [23].

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using Stata (version 18.5). Descriptive statistics were computed for each scale and subscale of key demographic variables (age, gender, experience, and ethnicity). The participants grouping for experience was based on the most current experience they indicated when completing the survey, but this does not mean they do not have any other experience with dementia. Pearson correlations assessed bivariate relationships between DAS and potential factors that might be associated (age, wisdom, AAQ subscales). Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVAs were used to examine differences in DAS scores by gender and experience group. Multiple linear regression models were then used to identify the potential factors that are correlated attitudes toward dementia, as measured by the DAS total score. Covariates were entered in blocks: first demographic variables (age, gender, and experience), followed by psychological variables (wisdom subscales, AAQ subscales). Standardized beta coefficients (β) and adjusted R² values were reported.

Although Stata automatically excluded cases with missing values, a review of the raw dataset showed at most, 30 participants’ data was missing from any variable. Therefore, missing values were addressed using simple mean imputation so that all cases could be retained in the analyses. The only variable that was not imputed was age. Simple mean imputation involves replacing missing values with the overall mean of the respective variable. This approach was considered appropriate because the missing data were determined to be completely at random and the proportion of missing data was minimal. While multiple imputation is generally preferred for its ability to preserve variance and reduce bias, it requires assumptions about the missing data mechanism that are difficult to verify without auxiliary variables. Given that missing data affected fewer than 1% of cases (≤ 30 of 3,144 participants) and sensitivity analyses showed no substantive differences, simple mean imputation was deemed sufficient for this dataset. Additionally, simple mean imputation is considered a beneficial method of mean imputation for large datasets. However, it should be stated that simple mean imputation is not considered the best method of mean imputation, because it can artificially reduce the standard deviation (SD) from the mean, potentially leading to inaccurate results.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 3,144 participants were included in the final analysis. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 97 years (M = 64.25, SD = 12.00). The sample was predominantly female (n = 2204, 70.10%), with 29.45% male. Nearly all participants identified as White (n = 3058, 97.26%), so we could not conduct analyses involving ethnicity. In terms of dementia-related experience, nearly half the sample (48.35%) reported knowing or having known someone with dementia, 26.21% were family carers of people with dementia, 13.77% were professionals or staff in dementia care, and 3.98% were people with dementia.

For people who were classified as having ‘Other’ experience (n = 242, 7.70%), the experiences included those who had no experience of dementia, those who believed they had dementia but were not formally diagnosed, those diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment, members of the community that did not provide a service, or people who had simply stated ‘Other’.

Results showed that females scored significantly higher on the Total DAS than males, t(3130) = 11.33, p < .001, showing more positive attitudes towards dementia compared to males. The mean DAS score for females was 108.87 (SD = 14.38), compared to 102.48 (SD = 14.47) for males, a difference of 6.39, 95% CI [5.28, 7.50].

On the Dementia Knowledge subscale, females also had higher scores than males, t(3130) = 13.95, p < .001. Similarly, a significant gender difference was observed for the Social Comfort subscale, t(3130) = 6.90, p < .001. Female participants reported higher comfort levels (M = 50.18, SD = 10.38) compared to males (M = 47.43, SD = 9.65), with a mean difference of 2.75, 95% CI [1.97, 3.53].

Mean differences of dementia attitudes across experience groups

A one-way ANOVA indicated that the mean DAS scores differed significantly across the five experience groups, F(4, 3125) = 126.49, p < .001. The highest mean DAS scores were found among Professional/Staff participants (M = 114.08, SD = 14.66), followed by Family caregivers (M = 112.09, SD = 13.04). The lowest scores were found in the “Other” category (M = 94.62, SD = 13.36).

ANOVA revealed significant differences of Dementia Knowledge scores across experience groups, F(4, 3125) = 54.40, p < .001. Professional/Staff participants again had the highest mean (M = 60.23, SD = 6.82), while the “Other” group had the lowest (M = 53.66, SD = 6.68).

A one-way ANOVA also found a significant link between experience and the Social Comfort subscale, F(4, 3125) = 121.90, p < .001. Professionals/Staffs (M = 53.85, SD = 10.27) and Family caregivers (M = 53.00, SD = 9.18) reported higher levels of social comfort compared to “I know/knew someone with dementia” (M = 47.23, SD = 9.52) and “Other” (M = 40.96, SD = 9.26).

Association between dementia attitudes and age

A simple linear regression was conducted to examine whether age was related to DAS total score. Results showed that age was significantly correlated with DAS (β = -0.129, SE = 0.022, p < .001, R² = 0.011). Subscale analysis revealed that age was significantly linked to the Dementia Knowledge subscale (β = -0.128, SE = 0.010, p < .001, R² = 0.0497), but had no significant relationship with Social Comfort (β = -0.001, p = .952).

Association between dementia attitudes, attitudes to aging, and wisdom

There was a small but significant positive correlation between overall DAS and overall AAQ scores (r = .292, p < .001), meaning more positive attitudes to aging were correlated with more positive attitudes towards dementia (Table 2). Among the correlations, DAS and its subscales were most strongly correlated with Psychosocial Growth (r = .318, p < .001; r = .274, p < .001; r = .271, p < .001).

Discussion

Key findings

Overall, this study provides greater insight into potential factors that are related to the public’s attitudes towards dementia and offers practical implications for stigma-reduction campaigns, dementia education, and policy reform. Our findings show that age, gender, experience, attitudes towards aging, and wisdom are significantly associated with attitudes towards dementia.

Although the majority of associations in this study were statistically significant, effect sizes were generally small to moderate. For example, age accounted for approximately 1% of variance in DAS scores (R² = 0.011), and correlations between DAS and wisdom or AAQ subscales ranged from r = .12 to r = .32. Given the large sample size (N = 3,144), statistical significance alone should not be interpreted as evidence of strong practical importance. However, even modest effect sizes may carry meaningful implications at a population level, small shifts in dementia attitudes across large groups could aggregate into meaningfully reduced stigma, particularly when targeted through scalable public health interventions. Furthermore, the relatively stronger associations observed for affective wisdom (r = .318) and psychosocial growth (r = .318) compared to cognitive wisdom (r = .150) suggest that these constructs may be more promising targets for intervention, even if absolute effect sizes remain moderate.

Older age was significantly correlated with more negative attitudes towards dementia, even when controlling for experience and gender. Previous research has shown mixed findings of the relationship between age and dementia attitudes with some studies suggesting that younger people tend to have greater knowledge but less positive attitudes towards dementia [24–27]. Our finding might be explained by the perspective that old people tend to connect dementia with older age, which may lead to greater fear of dementia compared to younger people [26, 27].

Professionals and family caregivers exhibited the most positive views and highest knowledge scores, while those in the “other” category reported the lowest. This trend is consistent with earlier evidence showing that direct experience, particularly through caregiving or clinical work, can significantly reduce stigma [27–29]. These results also support international guidance from WHO’s QualityRights framework highlighting lived experience and participatory approaches as tools to shift discriminatory mindsets [30, 31].

Females had significantly higher scores on the DAS total and both subscales (Dementia Knowledge and Social Comfort), holding more positive attitudes towards dementia. These findings echo earlier literature indicating that women may also engage more in caregiving roles, increasing familiarity and empathy toward people with dementia [27, 32].

Multiple regression analyses confirmed that attitudes to aging and wisdom were significantly correlated to more positive attitudes towards dementia independently. Previous studies have confirmed that individuals’ attitudes to aging were associated with attitudes to dementia [33]. Similarly, Webster et al. [34] argued that wisdom supports broader mental health and social connectedness, which may in turn support more positive understanding of marginalized groups such as people with dementia—a pattern consistent with our findings. Among the three wisdom dimensions, affective wisdom showed the strongest association with positive dementia attitudes. This pattern is theoretically meaningful. Dementia stigma operates substantially through emotional and interpersonal channels. The stronger role of affective wisdom suggests that the capacity for empathy, compassion, and emotional regulation may be more central to shaping dementia attitudes than analytical reasoning or self-reflection. The more interpersonal and affective dimension of the DAS was more strongly associated with wisdom and aging attitudes than the Dementia Knowledge subscale, suggesting that relational aspects of dementia attitudes are more amenable to influence by psychological traits. These findings align with research highlighting empathy as a key predictor of positive dementia attitudes [27, 43, 44] and suggest that stigma-reduction interventions may benefit more from empathy-building and emotional engagement approaches than from didactic knowledge-based education alone. Programs that cultivate emotional understanding, such as contact-based interventions or perspective-taking exercises, may therefore be particularly promising avenues for future work [29, 45].

In the AAQ subscales, Psychosocial Growth showed the strongest positive correlation with DAS scores. This result supports the idea that people who view aging as an opportunity for growth or wisdom may hold more inclusive views toward aging-related conditions like dementia [22]. This finding highlights a meaningful link between personal development and openness toward dementia, which is consistent with the theoretical overlap between positive psychology and anti-stigma efforts [32, 35]. Social Comfort with dementia is more strongly related to AAQ and 3D-WS-12 than Dementia Knowledge. This means that aspects of dementia attitudes that are more interpersonal and affective are more likely to be influenced by aging attitudes and wisdom traits. This supports the findings of Russ [36], who differentiates between the cognitive and emotional sides of dementia stigma, and highlights the importance of effective education and experience to reduce social discomfort.

This study supports the idea that positive psychology plays a role in reducing stigma and improving attitudes towards aging and dementia. The relationships between psychological growth and positive dementia attitudes corroborated other findings regarding the importance of strengths-based constructs in terms of hope, resilience, a sense of independence and social engagement [36, 37]. As we now observe the potential role of wisdom and attitudes towards aging in dementia attitudes, more implications based on positive psychology could take these factors into account.

Furthermore, the increasing interest in the application of a human rights-based approach to dementia care was also embedded in personal beliefs and attitudes [30, 31, 38]. Knowing how psychological variables like wisdom might relate to support for a rights-based approach to dementia care would help inform educational interventions and policy aimed at reducing stigma and improving quality of life for people with dementia.

Limitations

A few participants could not complete the survey because sometimes the questionnaire was not fully displayed on their screens. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal and long-term conclusions. For instance, while wisdom and positive aging attitudes were associated with more positive dementia attitudes, it is equally plausible that pre-existing positive dementia attitudes shape how individuals perceive aging and develop wisdom-related traits over time. Additionally, the regression prediction models were low, suggesting further factors may moderate these findings. Although we observed multiple significant associations, causal inference would still require more rigorous experimental or quasi-experimental designs [39]. Furthermore, the study had limited diversity in terms of ethnicity and gender. The sample was 70% female, and given that females in this study scored significantly higher on the DAS than males, this imbalance likely inflates the overall mean DAS score and may overestimate the positivity of dementia attitudes relative to the general population. The sample was also overwhelmingly White (97.26%), precluding any ethnicity-based analyses. As dementia stigma and attitudes have been shown to vary across cultural and ethnic contexts [5, 12, 40], findings regarding the role of wisdom and aging attitudes may reflect patterns specific to a Western, predominantly White population and should be interpreted with caution in other cultural settings. Due to insufficient sample sizes, individuals identifying their genders as “prefer not to say” or “other” were excluded from gender-related analyses. These exclusions also reflect a lack of diversity in gender in dementia research, which was previously noted in work on LGBT cultural competency in dementia care [41].

Future direction

Future studies should focus on the possibility of interventions targeting psychological factors to decrease dementia stigma. Based on Jeste et al.‘s [42] results, interventions focusing on wisdom and resilience may be beneficial for older adults, improving their quality of life by decreasing levels of perceived stress and loneliness, and wisdom-related traits such as emotional regulation, acceptance of divergent values, and empathy can improve the idea of ageism [42]. In addition to wisdom and aging attitudes, many other studies mention empathy as important psychological factors [27, 38, 43, 44]. For example, Hassan et al. [27] found that higher levels of empathy are positively associated with dementia attitudes in adolescents. Therefore, future studies could be conducted to examine the direct correlation between other psychological factors and attitudes to dementia among a more diverse population, which helps with more comprehensive and human rights-based solutions to stigma.

As our study emphasizes, increasing personal contact with people with dementia more broadly may be another way to improve stigma. Advocacy organizations and programs such as Dementia Friends UK have already made an effort in personal outreach to people with dementia (https://www.dementiafriends.org.uk/). For example, by simply attending an info session or watching a short video, people are encouraged to commit to a small action such as being more patient in a shop queue or talking more calmly and clearly to people with dementia [45]. To maximize the scale of such approaches, more research is needed to develop and test whether personal exposure through online websites or other mass communication platforms like social media is effective in reducing stigma under different cultures and geographic contexts globally [46–48].

Our data however does not account for the richness of personal stories, cultural meanings, or changing perspectives that may explain participants’ answers. Future studies could consider using the human rights-based approach PANEL (Participation, Accountability, Non-discrimination, Empowerment, Legality) as guidance to conduct more qualitative research [49, 50]. The clear relationship between social experience and attitudes supports the notion that participatory, human-rights-oriented models may improve how dementia is viewed and treated in society and reminds us that people with dementia are not receivers of care but rights-holders [51, 52].

Conclusion

This study highlights that wisdom and aging beliefs are positively correlated with better attitudes toward dementia. Future research should be done to better understand potential causal relationships. Our results at present support that targeting aging beliefs and wisdom in intervention contexts may be a promising avenue for stigma-reduction, though causal evidence is needed. Taken together, these insights point toward a comprehensive dementia awareness and human rights-based approach to transforming public attitudes and improving the lived experience of those affected by dementia.
