Disentangling direct and indirect genetic pathways to neurodevelopmental risk: brain structure and behavior in a population‐based parent–offspring trio study
Authors: Fin J E van Uum, Yllza Xerxa, Alexander Neumann, Jean‐Baptiste Pingault, Charlotte A M Cecil, Mina Shahisavandi, Koen Bolhuis, Boglarka Kovacs, Henning Tiemeier, Ryan L Muetzel, Neeltje E M van Haren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are highly heritable, yet the pathways linking parental genetic liability to child outcomes remain poorly understood. Traditional designs cannot easily separate genes transmitted to the child from environmental influences shaped by parental genotypes.MethodsIn 3,270 families from the Generation R Study, we applied a mother–father–offspring trio design to disentangle direct genetic effects from indirect parental influences (‘genetic nurture’). Polygenic scores (PGSs) included a transdiagnostic neurodevelopmental score (NDV) and disorder‐specific scores for ADHD, ASD, and major depressive disorder (MDD). Offspring brain structure (cortical surface area, subcortical gray matter, cerebellar volume) at age 9 and behavioral problems at age 14 were assessed. Structural equation modeling was used to test direct, indirect, and brain‐mediated genetic pathways.ResultsTransdiagnostic NDD genetic liability predicted multiple behavioral domains, primarily via direct genetic effects, with similar influence on internalizing and externalizing problems. ADHD PGS effects closely paralleled those of the NDV PGS, whereas ASD and MDD PGSs showed more domain‐specific associations. Notably, maternal, but not paternal, MDD genetic liability was indirectly related to behavioral problems across domains, independent of the child's own genetic risk. This maternal pathway was partly reporter‐specific but robust for attention problems across both parent and child reports. Global brain measures did not mediate any of the identified genetic pathways.ConclusionsIntergenerational NDD risk is predominantly driven by direct genetic transmission. However, maternal depression liability exerts additional influence through environmentally mediated pathways. The absence of brain‐mediated effects suggests that the gross neural phenotypes examined here do not capture the mechanisms underlying these pathways. Our findings highlight the importance of maternal influences on child NDD outcomes and underscore the need for longitudinal, multimodal research designs that integrate genetic, caregiving, and fine‐grained neural phenotyping data to inform targeted prevention and early intervention strategies for at‐risk families.Keywords: Neurodevelopmental disorders, polygenic scores, intergenerational transmission, brain structure, ADHD, MDD

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), are among the most prevalent child psychiatric conditions, affecting educational achievement, employment, health, and social relationships (Scandurra et al., 2019). ASD affects an estimated 0.7–3% of the population, whereas ADHD is more common, with a prevalence of 5–11% (Francés et al., 2022). These conditions frequently co‐occur and share cognitive, social, and emotional features (Morris‐Rosendahl & Crocq, 2020; Thapar, Cooper, & Rutter, 2017). Rather than mapping onto discrete diagnostic boundaries, this overlap is increasingly understood as reflecting shared, pleiotropic genetic liability that spans neurodevelopmental and broader psychopathology domains (Hughes et al., 2023). This perspective motivates integrative frameworks that consider both disorder‐specific and shared genetic and environmental influences.

A central challenge in elucidating NDD etiology is disentangling direct from indirect genetic transmission of risk from parent to child. Direct transmission refers to risk alleles passed from each parent to the child, directly influencing offspring phenotype. Indirect transmission occurs when non‐inherited parental alleles affect child outcomes via environments shaped by parental genetics (Kong et al., 2018). Trio designs, which jointly analyze parent and offspring genomes, allow these effects to be separated (Pingault et al., 2022).

Evidence from genomic parent–offspring trio studies indicates that direct genetic effects explain the majority of variance in neurodevelopmental and related behavioral traits, including ADHD (Cheesman et al., 2020; Eilertsen et al., 2022; Hegemann et al., 2025; Pingault et al., 2022; Voronin et al., 2024). At the same time, these studies also provide evidence for indirect genetic influences operating via the family environment. For example, maternal genetic liability for traits such as neuroticism has been shown to predict child ADHD symptoms beyond inherited risk (Pingault et al., 2022). Clarifying the relative contribution of direct versus indirect pathways is therefore essential, as prevention and intervention strategies may differ depending on whether risk is transmitted primarily through genetic inheritance or through environmentally mediated mechanisms.

The contributions of the present study are twofold. First, we address phenotypic overlap across NDDs by applying a transdiagnostic polygenic score (PGS). PGSs, derived from genome‐wide association studies (GWAS), aggregate common variants weighted by effect sizes (Choi, Mak, & O'Reilly, 2020). Given the strong correlations between PGSs for different NDDs (Li et al., 2020), their genetic architecture reflects both pleiotropy and polygenicity. Accordingly, transdiagnostic scores such as the neurodevelopmental (NDV) PGS, which captures shared risk variance across ADHD, ASD, major depressive disorder (MDD), and Tourette's syndrome (Lee et al., 2019), offer a way to quantify broad NDD liability (Shi et al., 2023). Although MDD is not conventionally classified as an NDD, its genetic risk overlaps with these disorders, loading on the same genetic factor (Lee et al., 2019). To reflect this genetic architecture, we include MDD risk within the NDV PGS, prioritizing genomic patterns over diagnostic categories. By focusing on this transdiagnostic measure, we aim to clarify how shared genetic liability contributes to heterogeneous NDD manifestations during adolescence.

Second, we integrate neurobiological markers into a triadic genetic framework. Structural brain alterations are consistently observed in children and adolescents with NDDs (Hoogman et al., 2019; van Rooij et al., 2017) and tend to correlate with polygenic liability (Alemany et al., 2019). Genes contributing to the NDV factor are preferentially expressed in brain tissue during prenatal development and influence processes such as neurogenesis and synapse formation (Lee et al., 2019). These findings suggest that genetic vulnerability may be expressed through early neurodevelopmental variation that contributes to later‐emerging behavioral profiles, including both NDD‐proximal traits (e.g., attention and social functioning) and broader dimensions of psychopathology. In this context, neuroimaging markers provide an opportunity to examine whether childhood brain structure lies on the developmental pathway linking genetic liability to later behavioral outcomes.

In summary, this study aims to (1) evaluate the transmission profile of a transdiagnostic NDD PGS, (2) assess the relative contributions of direct and indirect genetic pathways, and (3) examine whether childhood brain morphology mediates these associations across adolescence. Mediation analyses are embedded in a developmental framework in which parental and offspring genetic liability precede childhood brain structure and subsequent adolescent behavioral outcomes, enabling assessment of mediation along both direct and indirect genetic pathways. Clarifying these processes may refine our understanding of how genetic and environmental influences intersect in NDD risk, ultimately informing prevention and intervention strategies in child psychiatry.

Methods

Sample description

This study draws on data from the Generation R study, a population‐based cohort study located in Rotterdam, the Netherlands (Kooijman et al., 2016). The cohort comprises children born to mothers with expected delivery dates between April 2002 and January 2006, with developmental assessments conducted from fetal life onwards via longitudinal follow‐up. Comprehensive assessments are conducted at each measurement point, including biological samples, neuroimaging, and behavioral evaluations. Genotyping is performed for the child as well as both parents.

From the initial Generation R cohort (N trios = 9,901), unique mother–father–offspring triads were identified by randomly excluding siblings from the dataset (N trios = 9,153). Only families with complete genetic data, following PGS computation and filtering, were retained, yielding 3,270 unique triads (N participants = 9,810). Offspring were aged 12–16 years at the time of questionnaire assessment.

The study received approval from the Erasmus MC Medical Ethical Committee, and informed consent was obtained from both parents and children aged 12 and older. The sample demographics closely reflect the population of Rotterdam, encompassing a wide range of ethnic backgrounds. The primary inclusion criterion for this study was the availability of complete genetic data for all members of the family triad (mother, father, child). Missing data for outcome, mediator, and covariate variables were permitted to maximize the sample size and are addressed in the results section.

Measures

Behavioral assessment

Psychiatric symptoms at age 14 were assessed using the child‐reported Youth Self‐Report (YSR) and the parent‐reported Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) (Achenbach & Rescorla, 2001). CBCL questionnaires were completed primarily by mothers (>99%), with the remainder completed by fathers or other caregivers. The YSR contains 112 items and the CBCL 113 items, each rated on a three‐point scale: 0 = not true, 1 = somewhat or sometimes true, and 2 = very true or often true, referring to behaviors over the preceding 2 months. Both instruments adopt a dimensional approach to common mental health problems, such as attention difficulties and social challenges (Krueger et al., 2018).

Our primary focus is on the externalizing and internalizing broadband scales (Achenbach, Ivanova, Rescorla, Turner, & Althoff, 2016). The externalizing scale captures behaviors such as aggression and rule‐breaking, while the internalizing scale assesses symptoms of anxiety and depression. These domains are frequently observed in NDDs and serve as important indicators of symptom severity (Bauminger, Solomon, & Rogers, 2010). In addition, we include the attention problems and social problems syndrome scales, which are particularly relevant for NDD phenotypes: the attention problems subscale is used as a proxy for ADHD symptoms, and the social problems subscale is associated with ASD (Ooi, Rescorla, Ang, Woo, & Fung, 2010). Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity within each domain.

The YSR and CBCL have demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with test–retest reliability coefficients of .92 and .85, and internal consistency values of .94 and .92 (Achenbach, 2010; Achenbach & Rescorla, 2001). Both measures have been validated across 23 societies, including the Netherlands (Ivanova et al., 2010). The use of multiple informants enhances behavioral assessment by enabling cross‐informant comparisons and integrating perspectives from different contexts.

MRI image acquisition

The MRI acquisition protocol for Generation R has been described in detail elsewhere (White et al., 2017). In brief, neuroimaging data were collected at age 9 using a 3 Tesla Discovery MR750w scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with an 8‐channel receive‐only head coil. A coronal inversion recovery fast spoiled gradient recalled (IR‐FSPGR) sequence with ARC acceleration was used. The key scanning parameters were TR = 8.77 ms, TE = 3.4 ms, TI = 600 ms, flip angle = 10°, field of view (FOV) = 220 × 220 mm, acquisition matrix = 220 × 220, slice thickness = 1 mm, and 230 slices were acquired.

MRI image processing

Structural T1‐weighted MRI data were processed using FreeSurfer (v6.0). The following standard preprocessing steps were applied: removal of non‐brain tissue, B1 inhomogeneity correction, whole‐brain tissue segmentation, and cortical surface reconstruction, which underwent visual inspection for quality control (Muetzel et al., 2019). Scans that failed quality control, exhibited significant artifacts, or contained substantial incidental findings were excluded from statistical analysis (N = 275).

For each hemisphere, global brain metrics were extracted and averaged across hemispheres to yield single values for cerebellum volume, subcortical gray matter volume, and cortical surface area. These metrics were selected a priori based on both empirical and mechanistic considerations. Empirically, neuroimaging studies of neurodevelopmental disorders tend to report small but widely distributed structural differences at the global level (Hoogman et al., 2019; Van Rooij et al., 2017). Mechanistically, gene‐expression analyses indicate that genetic variants contributing to the NDV cluster show preferential expression in the cerebellum, particularly during early brain development (Hughes et al., 2023).

Genotype data preprocessing and quality control

Genotype data for the Generation R Study were generated using Illumina 610 K and 660 K arrays for GenR3 children, GSA‐MD v2.0 arrays for GenR4 children, and GSA‐MD v2.0/v3.0 arrays for parents (Ghatan et al., 2024; Jaddoe et al., 2007; Medina‐Gomez et al., 2015). Standard quality control (QC) procedures were applied, including filters for call rate, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, excess heterozygosity, and concordance between genetically inferred and reported sex and relatedness.

Genotype imputation was conducted using the 1,000 Genomes Project reference panel (Phase 3, Version 5) (Delaneau, Marchini, & Zagury, 2011; Howie, Donnelly, & Marchini, 2009). Post‐imputation, autosomal SNPs were retained if they had an imputation quality score (R2) ≥ 0.80 and minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 1%. This resulted in 7,673,245 SNPs for participants genotyped on Illumina 610 K/660 K arrays (GenR3), 9,508,795 SNPs for GSA‐MD v2.0 (GenR4), and 9,520,497 SNPs for the parent cohort.

PGS calculation and harmonization

Polygenic scores were calculated using LDpred2‐auto (Privé, Arbel, & Vilhjálmsson, 2020), based on the largest available GWAS summary statistics of European ancestry for major phenotypes: major depressive disorder (MDD) (Adams et al., 2024), ADHD (Demontis et al., 2023), and ASD (Grove et al., 2019). Due to limited GWAS sample size, an individual disorder‐specific PGS was not computed for Tourette syndrome.

The NDV PGS was based on previously published work by Hughes et al. (2023). In that study, genomic structural equation modeling (gSEM) was used to model the genetic covariance structure across multiple psychiatric disorders, identifying a latent genetic factor capturing shared liability across ADHD, ASD, MDD, and Tourette syndrome. For this latent factor, gSEM yields SNP‐level effect estimates representing each variant's contribution to NDV, derived as linear combinations of disorder‐specific GWAS effects weighted by their respective factor loadings.

Imputed genotype dosage data in PLINK BED format served as input for the computation of individual‐level PGSs. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was modeled using the European‐ancestry UK Biobank LD reference panel (1,054,330 HapMap SNPs), as recommended by LDpred2 developers (Privé, Albiñana, Arbel, Pasaniuc, & Vilhjálmsson, 2023).

Across the GWAS summary statistics and LD reference panel, approximately 924,000 to 979,000 SNPs overlapped with those available in GenR3 children, 964,000 to 1,036,000 SNPs in GenR4 children, and 964,000 to 1,036,000 SNPs in the parent cohort, ensuring consistent LD estimation and alignment with GWAS ancestry across all subcohorts.

Ancestry adjustment and harmonization

Given the multi‐ethnic composition of the cohort, 20 genomic components were derived to capture population structure, via multidimensional scaling (MDS) for children and principal component analysis (PCA) for parents using PLINK software (Chang et al., 2015; Purcell et al., 2007).

To correct for residual ancestry differences and potential batch effects, PGS values were residualized and z‐standardized within each genotyping subcohort (GenR3 children, GenR4 children, and GenR parents), using the first 20 genomic components. Residualization accounts for ancestry‐ and platform‐specific variation, while z‐standardization controls for differences due to minor variations in SNP availability or genotyping batch. These steps minimize confounding from population stratification and technical artifacts. Scripts for genotype preprocessing and PGS computation are publicly available (GitHub link).

Statistical analysis

We examined relationships among genetic, neuroimaging, and behavioral data using structural equation modeling (SEM) implemented in the lavaan package (version 0.6.17) (Rosseel, 2012) in R (version 4.3.2). Descriptive statistics (N, mean, SD, minimum, maximum, median) were calculated for quantitative variables; categorical demographics are reported as frequencies (N, %). All variables were standardized prior to model estimation.

SEM models were estimated using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) to accommodate non‐normality in behavioral data. Missing data were handled using a two‐tiered approach: listwise deletion was applied for descriptive statistics and correlation analyses, whereas all SEMs were estimated using full‐information maximum likelihood (FIML), allowing all available data to contribute to parameter estimation.

Within the FIML framework, auxiliary demographic variables (mother's education, partner's education, paternal age at conception, and marital status) were included only to improve estimation of missing outcome and neuroimaging data under a missing‐at‐random assumption. These variables were not included as covariates in the structural models and therefore did not condition the estimated genetic pathways.

In the genetic models, covariates were restricted to the child's age at assessment and maternal age at conception. Other demographic characteristics, including parental socioeconomic indicators, were not included as covariates because they are partially influenced by parental genotype and may therefore lie on the indirect genetic pathway rather than function as exogenous confounders in a trio design. Conditioning on such variables would risk attenuating indirect genetic effects. Intracranial volume (ICV) was included in all models involving brain metrics. Behavioral outcomes were residualized for child sex prior to modeling.

The analytic approach began with models testing simple genetic–behavioral and genetic–MRI associations (Figure 1a). These were followed by genetic trio models that simultaneously estimated direct genetic effects (child PGS → outcome) and indirect effects (parental PGS → outcome) (Figure 1b). Finally, trio mediation models incorporated paths from child and parental PGSs to brain metrics and from brain metrics to behavioral outcomes, with indirect effects calculated as the product of PGS → MRI and MRI → outcome paths for each genetic pathway (Figure 1c). In this way, the approach separates direct and indirect genetic influences into brain‐mediated and non–brain‐mediated effects. Mediation effects are reported, with total effects corresponding to those found in the trio models.

In total, four primary regression models, four trio models (one per behavioral outcome), and 12 mediation models (four behavioral outcomes × three brain metrics) were estimated. In all primary analyses, behavioral problems were modeled as latent factors capturing shared variance between CBCL and YSR domain scores, with the YSR loading fixed at 1 and the CBCL loading freely estimated to allow for scaling differences between informants.

Single‐informant outcomes (CBCL‐only or YSR‐only) were used exclusively in predefined sensitivity and robustness analyses, which also examined individual PGS components (ADHD, ASD, MDD). In an additional sensitivity analysis, paternal age was included as a covariate alongside maternal age in the models. Maternal and paternal age at conception were moderately correlated within couples (r = 0.63; N = 3,265), leaving a substantial proportion of variance in paternal age independent of maternal age. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05.

False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied a priori within clearly defined families of hypothesis tests, reflecting both the polygenic score (transdiagnostic vs. disorder‐specific) and the genetic pathway under investigation (direct child, maternal indirect, paternal indirect effects). Specifically, FDR correction was performed separately for (i) primary association models, (ii) trio transmission models, and (iii) trio mediation models, and within trio models was applied independently to direct and indirect genetic pathways rather than across all parameters jointly. This strategy was chosen to control type I error while preserving power to detect indirect genetic effects. Effect sizes are reported as standardized regression coefficients. Full model specifications are provided in Appendix S1, with a summary of model classes, covariates, and FDR families provided in Table S1. Complete results (i.e., uncorrected and unstandardized estimates) are reported in Appendix S2.

Preliminary analyses

Preliminary analyses showed moderate correlations between parent‐reported (CBCL) and self‐reported (YSR) behavioral outcomes (r = .41–.55), as well as moderate correlations across behavioral domains (r = .23–.64; Figure 2a; Table S2). Genetic validity checks revealed the expected correlations between parental and offspring polygenic scores (r = .48–.51), confirming valid intergenerational genetic links (Figure 2b; Table S3). Correlations among disorder‐specific PGSs were generally modest (e.g., ADHD–ASD: r = .29; ADHD–MDD: r = .27; ASD–MDD: r = .13), whereas the transdiagnostic NDV PGS showed stronger associations with its constituent PGSs (ADHD: r = .66; ASD: r = .54; MDD: r = .44; Figure 2b; Table S3).

Association models

Initial association models (Figure 1a) indicated significant relationships between the child NDV PGS and all behavioral domains. The strongest effect was observed for externalizing problems (β = .178, SE = .027, p = 1.67 × 10−12), followed by attention problems (β = 0.159, SE = 0.026, p = 2.87 × 10−7), internalizing problems (β = .149, SE = .029, p = 5.75 × 10−7), and social problems (β = .122, SE = .031, p = .008). All associations remained significant after FDR correction for four comparisons (Tables S4a and S4b). Disorder‐specific PGSs were also significantly associated with behavioral outcomes after FDR correction for 12 comparisons (3 PGSs × 4 outcomes), except for the ASD PGS and attention problems (β = .026, SE = .029, p_FDR = .321; Tables S5a and S5b).

The child NDV PGS showed no significant associations with brain structure metrics, including total cortical surface area (β = −0.031, SE = 0.014, p_FDR = 0.078), cerebellar volume (β = −.022, SE = .022, p_FDR = .462), or subcortical gray matter volume (GMV) (β = −.003, SE = 0.017, p_FDR = .875). Among disorder‐specific PGSs, only the MDD PGS was significantly associated with brain structure, showing a negative association with subcortical GMV (β = −.051, SE = 0.017, p_FDR = .018) after correction for nine comparisons (3 PGSs × 3 brain metrics; Tables S6a and S6b).

Main analysis 1: Direct and indirect genetic transmission

Using trio models, child, father, and mother PGSs were simultaneously related to child behavioral outcomes, applying a latent factor structure consistent with the initial association models (Figure 1b). Significant direct effects of the child's NDV PGS on behavioral problems were observed: externalizing (β = .140, SE = .037, p_FDR = .001), attention (β = .128, SE = .038, p_FDR = .006), social (β = .116, SE = .041, p_FDR = .024), and internalizing problems (β = .100, SE = 0.038, p_FDR = .015) (Figure 3; Tables S7a and S7b). No significant indirect effects were detected for maternal or paternal NDV PGSs, and parental NDV PGSs were uncorrelated (β = .022, SE = .017, p_FDR = .202).

Main analysis 2: Neural mediation effects

Trio mediation models (Figure 1c) revealed no significant mediation effects of brain structure on the relationship between PGSs and behavioral outcomes, indicating that structural brain measures did not account for the genetic effects observed in the trio models (Tables S10a and S10b). In exploratory analyses separating parent‐ and self‐reports (24 comparisons: 2 raters × 3 brain metrics × 4 outcomes), modest mediation effects appeared but did not survive FDR correction. For example, the direct genetic effect of the NDV PGS on parent‐reported internalizing problems showed suggestive mediation by cerebellar volume (uncorrected p = .046; β = .011, SE = .006), which was non‐significant after correction (p_FDR = .57) (Figure 5).

Sensitivity analysis: Paternal age

Incorporating paternal age at conception as a covariate, alongside maternal age, did not significantly alter the overall results, and all inferences remained consistent with the previous models. For further details, please refer to Appendix S2.

Discussion

This study employed a genetic trio design to address three central questions: (1) how transdiagnostic genetic risk for neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) relates to adolescent behavioral problems, (2) the relative contributions of direct versus indirect genetic transmission, and (3) whether childhood brain structure mediates these effects.

Across analyses, genetic associations with adolescent behavior were driven predominantly by direct effects of the child's genotype. This pattern is consistent with evidence from genomic parent–offspring trio studies indicating that common genetic variation underlying neurodevelopmental traits is transmitted primarily through direct inheritance, while indirect parental genetic effects tend to be more trait‐specific rather than widespread (Cheesman et al., 2020; Eilertsen et al., 2022; Hegemann et al., 2025). Within this transmission framework, the broad phenotypic reach of the transdiagnostic NDV PGS is consistent with genome‐wide evidence for pleiotropy and shared genetic architecture across neurodevelopmental conditions, whereby directly inherited genetic liability contributes to multiple correlated symptom domains (Demontis et al., 2023; Grove et al., 2019).

Disorder‐specific PGSs largely followed this general pattern but also revealed meaningful differentiation: ADHD showed the strongest and most consistent direct associations across behavioral domains, whereas ASD‐related genetic risk was confined to externalizing problems and MDD genetic risk was selectively associated with internalizing symptoms. Notably, the MDD PGS was the only score to exhibit evidence of indirect transmission, with maternal (but not paternal) genetic liability predicting child behavioral outcomes across domains, highlighting that indirect genetic effects may emerge in specific phenotypic contexts rather than as a general feature of neurodevelopmental risk.

Maternal MDD risk and child development

By distinguishing direct from indirect transmission, our study points to a maternal‐specific pathway through which genetic liability for depression contributes to offspring difficulties. These indirect effects suggest that part of the covariance between maternal risk and child behavior does not reflect shared inherited alleles, but rather environmental conditions shaped by maternal genetic predispositions. Maternal depression risk may influence parenting behaviors, stress regulation, or attachment processes (Bernard‐Bonnin, 2004). Broader contextual factors, such as marital instability and socioeconomic disadvantage, may amplify these influences (Fergusson & Lynskey, 1993). In this sense, maternal genetic liability for MDD may function not only as a biological risk but also as a proxy for intergenerational social determinants of mental health, shaping the environments in which children grow up and broadly contributing to attention, internalizing, and externalizing difficulties.

The absence of paternal indirect effects strengthens the case for maternal specificity. Mechanisms may operate both prenatally, via stress hormones, medication, or substance use during pregnancy (Davis et al., 2007; Koc, Marroun, Stricker, Muetzel, & Tiemeier, 2023), and postnatally, through the quality of mother–child interactions, consistent with mothers' typically greater caregiving involvement in contemporary families (Connell & Goodman, 2002; Pietikäinen et al., 2019). These results highlight maternal risk pathways as potential leverage points for early intervention, particularly in families where maternal depression risk is elevated.

An important caveat is potential reporter bias. Maternal depression risk may influence how mothers perceive and report their children's behavior, potentially inflating problem scores (Berg‐Nielsen, Vika, & Dahl, 2003; Gartstein, Bridgett, Dishion, & Kaufman, 2009). In the present sample, the vast majority of CBCL assessments were completed by mothers, resulting in a highly unbalanced informant distribution. As a consequence, stratifying analyses by parent informant was not feasible, as father‐completed CBCLs were too sparse to yield stable or interpretable estimates.

Consistent with the possibility of reporter bias, most maternal indirect effects were attenuated when child self‐reports were used instead of parent reports. In contrast, attention problems remained significantly associated across both informants, suggesting that this domain may be less susceptible to rater‐specific bias and may reflect a more robust intergenerational association. Taken together, these findings indicate that the observed maternal indirect effects are robust within the primary modeling framework but should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect a combination of environmentally mediated genetic influences, informant‐specific processes, and limited statistical power rather than a single explanatory mechanism.

Brain structure and genetic transmission

We found no evidence that global brain metrics mediated genetic effects on adolescent behavior. This finding should be interpreted in light of the fact that neurodevelopmental disorder–related neuroimaging differences are typically subtle and broadly distributed, particularly in population‐based samples. Although structural deviations in cortical surface area, subcortical regions, and the cerebellum have been reported in NDDs (Hoogman et al., 2019; Van Rooij et al., 2017), the weak associations observed here are consistent with prior work showing that univariate global volume measures often show limited correspondence with behavioral outcomes and may fail to capture distributed or microstructural substrates of genetic liability (Genon, Eickhoff, & Kharabian, 2022). More sensitive neural phenotypes, such as multimodal imaging approaches (Vieira et al., 2024), normative modeling of developmental deviations (Marquand et al., 2019; Marquand, Rezek, Buitelaar, & Beckmann, 2016), or indices of myelination (Khelfaoui, Ibaceta‐Gonzalez, & Angulo, 2024), may therefore be required to detect mediating processes. Accordingly, data‐driven and fine‐grained neuroimaging approaches represent an important priority for future research, particularly in larger samples optimized for imaging–genetic mediation analyses.

Equally important is the timing of measurement. Genes within the NDV cluster show peak cerebellar expression during prenatal development (Hughes et al., 2023), making it plausible that polygenic risk exerts its strongest influence on early neurodevelopmental trajectories rather than on global structural volumes measured later in childhood. In this context, null mediation effects may reflect that the examined brain metrics do not explain additional variance in adolescent behavioral outcomes beyond genetic liability already indexed by the polygenic scores, rather than indicating an absence of neurobiological involvement. Longitudinal studies integrating gene‐expression data with neuroimaging across infancy, childhood, and adolescence are therefore needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of these pathways (Biagioni, Baronchelli, & Fossati, 2024).

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, because this is a population‐based cohort, the right‐skewed distribution of NDD symptoms may have attenuated associations. Selection bias is also a concern, as families with fewer difficulties were more likely to provide complete data. Replication in clinical samples will be important to establish generalizability (Hughes et al., 2023; Shakeshaft et al., 2023).

Second, although a sample of 3,270 trios is large for imaging genetics, it remains modest for detecting indirect effects, as trio models decompose variance into multiple components and often require >10,000 trios for full power (Pingault et al., 2022). Trio mediation models are also not designed to establish causality; quasi‐experimental approaches will be needed to strengthen inference about indirect genetic effects (Baud, McPeek, Chen, & Hughes, 2021), as statistically significant indirect effects may be influenced by factors beyond the anticipated environmental and biological pathways (Baud et al., 2021).

Third, the selected brain metrics may not have been sufficiently sensitive to detect mediating processes, suggesting that more refined neural phenotypes could be necessary to capture these effects.

Finally, the NDV PGS in this study was driven mainly by ADHD risk, with a smaller contribution from ASD risk, despite comparable heritability estimates (Brikell, Kuja‐Halkola, & Larsson, 2015; Tick, Bolton, Happé, Rutter, & Rijsdijk, 2015). This likely reflects the greater statistical power of ADHD GWAS and differences in discovery versus target samples. The heterogeneity of ASD phenotypes also complicates PGS prediction (Grove et al., 2019). Future work should continue to refine transdiagnostic genetic measures that balance power with specificity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that direct genetic effects predominate in the transmission of neurodevelopmental disorder risk across adolescence. Maternal genetic liability for depression confers additional risk through environmentally mediated mechanisms that may involve both prenatal and postnatal influences. Within the global structural brain measures examined, we found no evidence of mediation. Nonetheless, this finding should be interpreted in the context of this study's population‐based sample and the coarse nature of the brain metrics used. These findings advance understanding of how genetic and environmental influences intersect across generations and identify maternal pathways as potential targets for prevention. Future progress will depend on richer neural phenotypes tracked longitudinally and on designs that integrate genetic nurture with measured environments to guide early interventions.
