Modelling the coevolution of opinion dynamics and decision making in social dilemmas
Ella C. Davidson, Lorenzo Zino, Ming Cao, Mengbin Ye

Abstract
This paper proposes a mathematical model for the coevolution of actions and opinions for a population facing a social dilemma. In particular, we assume each person participates in a Public Goods Game (PGG), with their action being to cooperate or defect, and holds an opinion about which action they prefer. We propose a payoff function that combines the PGG with the Friedkin–Johnsen model from opinion dynamics to form a coevolutionary game. According to a discrete-time process, players asynchronously update their actions and opinions, aiming to maximise their individual payoff for the coevolutionary game using myopic best-response. We study the equilibria and provide conditions for the existence of the all-defection and all-cooperation consensus equilibria. We also establish conditions for global convergence to the all-defection equilibrium.



I
Introduction

The emergence and persistence of widespread cooperation within a population when faced with a social dilemma is a fundamental problem of interest in biological, social and behavioural sciences
[
14
,
6
]
. This is partly because classical insights from game theory suggest that defection (also known as free riding) is often the best strategy from the perspective of a selfish rational individual. And yet, cooperation, which provides greater benefits for the entire population, is observed to persist in many real world scenarios.

Collective action, i.e. the mobilisation of a large group to participate in a movement in pursuit of institutional or societal change, is a classical social dilemma
[
4
]
. Participating in collective action is costly at an individual level and requires enough individuals to have a chance of success, but when successful, collective action often brings benefits to the entire population. More interestingly, there are cases of progressive movements (e.g. same-sex marriage and Black Lives Matter), in which many people participate even though they are not part of the repressed minority that would most benefit from the movements’ success.

Various factors that allow cooperation to emerge and persist over time have been proposed and studied. These include, and are not limited to, peer punishment
[
1
]
, reciprocity
[
10
,
20
]
, and social influence
[
19
]
. While these factors are social in nature, a key psychological factor in determining a person’s action is their own belief
[
12
]
. This is true for many contexts, including especially social dilemmas and collective action, as observed empirically within the literature on the social psychology of protest movements
[
23
]
. Moreover, generally, a protester’s beliefs, attitudes, and psychological drivers (all of which evolve as the movement gains or loses traction) are central to their engagement with a movement
[
16
,
18
]
.

Despite empirical evidence of an interdependence between an individual’s action and opinion, there have been limited efforts to combine the two into a unified mathematical model. The continuous-opinion discrete-action (CODA) model framework provided important initial contributions
[
17
,
5
]
, operating under the assumption that an individual’s action is a direct quantisation of their opinion, limiting the possibility of capturing the fundamental tension between cooperation and defection in social dilemmas. More recently, a mathematical model was proposed using a unified game-theoretic formulation, in which individuals played coordination games on a network and exchanged opinions on preferred strategies
[
7
]
. The resulting coevolution of actions and opinions was studied analytically, and emergent phenomena such as unpopular norms and polarisation were identified
[
7
]
.
The model was extended by
[
15
]
to consider an anti-coordination game instead.

In this paper, we consider the coevolutionary dynamics of actions and opinions of a group of people in the context of social dilemmas.
In more detail, individuals participate in a Public Goods Game (PGG), a classical game commonly used for modelling social dilemmas due to its flexibility and broad applicability
[
3
]
, and decide whether to cooperate or defect. An individual’s strategic decision to cooperate or defect coevolves with their opinion (or attitude/belief) towards cooperation. In
[
20
]
, a PGG was integrated within a continuous-time opinion dynamics model, yielding a framework similar to CODA models. Players revise their opinion based on a realistic opinion dynamics model that accounts for social interactions and the payoff of a PGG, and then make decisions on their actions (the amount to contribute to the public good) based on their opinion in a stochastic manner. This, however, illustrates a one-way relationship, not an interdependent coevolution of actions and opinions.

Here, we adopt the game-theoretic coevolutionary approach
[
7
]
and propose a joint payoff function for the coevolutionary game that combines i) the payoff from the PGG, ii) opinion dynamics evolving through social influence (inspired by the Friedkin–Johnsen model
[
8
]
), and iii) a term that captures an individual’s desire for consistency between their action and opinion. We consider a population of players interacting on a network to share opinions, revising their actions and opinions asynchronously at discrete timesteps using myopic best-response updating. Our work contrasts the coevolutionary models in
[
7
,
15
]
, which consider coordination and anti-coordination games rather than social dilemmas, and offers a novel lens to study the emergence and persistence of cooperation in social groups.

The main contributions of the paper are as follows. We propose a novel coevolutionary game, and obtain the explicit expression for the best-response updating, showing how a player’s best-responding action and opinion are shaped by others’ opinions and the PGG parameters. We study the equilibria, providing sufficient conditions for the existence of the all-defection and all-cooperation equilibria, and identify a sufficient condition for the all-defection equilibrium to be the unique equilibrium with a global stability property. The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section
II
presents the coevolutionary game and associated dynamics. Section
III
contains the main results and Section
IV
concludes the paper.

II-B

The PGG and Actions

We consider the set of players
\mathcal{V}
participating in a PGG. Each player can decide whether to contribute to the public good or not, referred to as “cooperate” or “defect”, respectively. We denote player
i
’s decision with the term
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, with
x_{i}=0
and
x_{i}=1
representing player
i
deciding to defect and cooperate, respectively. All
n
player decisions are held in the action vector
\boldsymbol{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

If player
i
decides to cooperate, they contribute a unit amount to the public good. Regardless of whether they have cooperated or not, each player receives a reward, with the amount depending on how many players have cooperated.
Thus, a player’s payoff depends on the player’s own action and the actions of the other
n-1
players. Here, we consider a simple implementation of the PGG, with all players contributing a unit amount to the same public good if they cooperate, and a constant
r
, with
1<r<n
, that represents the public good multiplier
[
3
,
11
]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\pi_{i}^{a}(x_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{-i})

\displaystyle=x_{i}\Bigg[\dfrac{r\left(\sum^{n}_{j=1,j\neq i}x_{j}+1\right)}{n}-1\Bigg]

\displaystyle+(1-x_{i})\dfrac{r}{n}\sum\nolimits^{n}_{j=1,j\neq i}x_{j},

(2)

where the first term (which is non-zero if player
i
cooperates) accounts for the share of the total reward received by player
i
if they cooperate, less their unit contribution), and the second term (which is non-zero if player
i
defects) accounts for the share of the total reward received by player
i
if they defect.

II-C

Opinion Dynamics

To model the evolution of the players’ opinions we opt for the use of the empirically validated Friedkin–Johnsen (FJ) model
[
8
]
. Each player
i
of a population
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,n\}
is assigned a continuous random variable
y_{i}\in[0,1]
, which represents player
i
’s opinion. All
n
of the players’ opinions are held in the opinion vector
\boldsymbol{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]^{\top}\in[0,1]^{n}
. Players share their opinion on a social network
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\boldsymbol{W})
, where the entry
w_{ij}
of
\boldsymbol{W}
represents the social influence of player
j
on player
i
. As detailed in the literature
[
21
]
, the FJ model can be formulated as a game
[
9
]
, where player
i
’s payoff for adopting an opinion
{y}_{i}
, given an opinion vector
\boldsymbol{y}
is defined as

\pi_{i}^{o}(y_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})=-\dfrac{1}{2}(1-\gamma_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{i}(y_{i}-u_{i})^{2},

(3)

where the parameter
\gamma_{i}\in[0,1]
represents player
i
’s level of attachment to their constant prejudice
u_{i}\in[0,1]
. The best-response to this payoff yields the FJ opinion dynamics model
[
8
]
. Note that the FJ model, as standard in opinion dynamics, allows
w_{ii}>0
(self-loops in
\mathcal{G}
), but in a game-theoretic context corresponds to the non-standard concept of playing a game against oneself. This can be addressed by a minor adjustment to Eq. (
3
), see
[
7
]
.

II-D

Coevolutionary Model

Coevolutionary Game

We are now in a position to introduce the coevolutionary game that combines the public goods and opinion dynamics game in an interdependent manner. In this setting, each player plays a PGG, where they can decide to defect,
x_{i}=0
, or to cooperate,
x_{i}=1
. Simultaneously, each player holds an opinion,
y_{i}\in[0,1]
, representing their level of support (or preference) for the action of cooperating in the PGG. Specifically, a player
i
fully supporting (preferring) defection and cooperation correspond to
y_{i}=0
and
y_{i}=1
, respectively. We make the important distinction between whether a player chooses to defect or cooperate (
x_{i}
) and whether a player supports it (
y_{i}
), noting that these two may not always be aligned. Player
i
’s strategy is defined as
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
, and we refer to the vector
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
as the
state of the system
and strategy profile interchangeably.

Given the state of the system
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, we propose that the coevolutionary game has the following payoff function for player
i
selecting strategy
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
:

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(z_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{-i})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}\pi_{i}^{a}(x_{i},\boldsymbol{x_{-i}})+\beta_{i}\pi_{i}^{o}(y_{i},\boldsymbol{y_{-i}})

\displaystyle\qquad-\dfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{2},

(4)

with
\pi_{i}^{a}(x_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{-i})
and
\pi_{i}^{o}(y_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})
from Eq. (
II-B
) and Eq. (
3
), respectively.
The parameters
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\lambda_{i}\in[0,1]
represent player
i
’s relative weight of actions, opinions, and desire to ensure consistency between their action and opinion (self-consistency), respectively. Without any loss in generality, we assume that
\lambda_{i}=1-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}
, from which Eq. (
II-D
) can be readily interpreted as a convex combination of the three terms, one term proportional to the payoff of the corresponding PGG, one term proportional to the payoff of the corresponding opinion dynamics, and a third term that accounts for self-consistency. A major conceptual foundation of our model is to explicitly distinguish between action and opinion, while introducing the self-consistency term to reduce the total payoff as the player’s action and opinion become less aligned. All player payoffs for the coevolutionary game are stored in the vector
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\pi}=[\pi_{1},\cdots,\pi_{n}]^{\top}
. We can now formally define the coevolutionary game as follows.

Definition 2

(Coevolutionary game)
.

The coevolutionary game is
\Gamma(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\pi})
, played by the set of players
\mathcal{V}
, on
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\boldsymbol{W})
, with strategy set
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}\times[0,1]
and payoff vector function
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, where entry
\pi_{i}
is defined in Eq. (
II-D
).

In order to characterise the best-response strategies to the coevolutionary game, we introduce a
discriminant
quantity:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(z_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{-i})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}\left(\dfrac{r}{n}-1\right)

(5)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}\bigg(\gamma_{i}u_{i}+(1-\gamma_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}y_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg).

The following result provides the best-response explicitly, by utilising the discriminant
\delta_{i}
. The proof is in the Appendix.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the coevolutionary game from Definition
2
. Then, given a state
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
, for a player
i\in\mathcal{V}
,
(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i})\in\mathcal{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z}_{-i}))
if and only if

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{s}_{i}(\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}))

(6)

\displaystyle\bar{y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\beta_{i}(1-\gamma_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}y_{j}+\beta_{i}\gamma_{i}u_{i}+\hat{s}_{i}(\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}))\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}},

(7)

with

\hat{s}_{i}(\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})\leq 0\\
1&\text{if }\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

(8)

Remark 1
.

When
\delta_{i}=0
, the set of best responses comprises two elements: one with
\bar{x}_{i}=0
and one with
\bar{x}_{i}=1
, and the corresponding values of
\bar{y}_{i}
, which differ, as per Eq. (
7
).

Finally, while the
\delta_{i}
is presented in Eq. (
5
) as a function of
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, it turns out it is in fact only a function of
\boldsymbol{y}
. This property will play a key role in our analysis of the updating dynamics in the sequel. We note that Eq. (
5
) suggests a one-way relationship between the players’ opinions and actions, as we observed in
[

]
. However, the formulation of the payoff in Eq. (

) does not. We conjecture that this results from the linear payoff formulation of the PGG; if a non-linear PGG payoff were implemented, this may no longer be the case.

Player Dynamics

Let us now define the update dynamics for the players, who are repeatedly playing the coevolutionary game. At each discrete timestep
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we posit that a set
\mathcal{R}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of players becomes active and revises their strategy, and
\mathcal{R}(t)
satisfies the following property.

Assumption 1
.

(Revision sequence). There exists a constant
T<\infty
such that
\cup_{s=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{R}(t+s)=\mathcal{V},
for any
t\geq 0
.

Remark 2
.

Assumption
1
covers many synchronous and asynchronous update rules. Synchronous updating corresponds to
\mathcal{R}(t)=\mathcal{V}
for all
t
, while asynchronous updating corresponds to
|\mathcal{R}(t)|=1
, for all
t
. The condition of
\cup_{s=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{R}(t+s)=\mathcal{V}
mimics typical assumptions in opinion dynamics with time-varying networks
[
21
]
, and ensures that each player activates at least once within a time window of
T
, and that this occurs infinitely often as
t\to\infty
.

We assume that active players in
\mathcal{R}(t)
will always update their future action and opinion by adopting the best-response strategy for the coevolutionary game given
\boldsymbol{z}(t)
. Hence, at timestep
t
, each player
i\in\mathcal{R}(t)
updates their state as

z_{i}(t+1)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}(\pi_{i}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z}_{-i}(t)))

(9)

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is the set of best-responses strategies computed in Proposition
1
. Here, we make a mild assumption that if a player’s best response comprises two elements (see Remark
1
), they will opt to defect due to inherit selfishness. Therefore, based on Proposition
1
, an active player
i
with state
z_{i}(t)=(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t))
is updated as

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle=s_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))

(10)

\displaystyle y_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\beta_{i}(1\!-\!\gamma_{i})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}y_{j}(t)\!+\!\beta_{i}\gamma_{i}u_{i}\!+\!s_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}

(11)

where

s_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))\leq 0\\
1&\text{if }\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))>0,\end{array}\right.

(12)

with
\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))
defined in Eq. (
5
). Any player
j\notin\mathcal{R}(t)
is inactive, and thus
z_{j}(t+1)=z_{j}(t)
.

We now point out some important features of the player update dynamics, and relevant interpretations within the context of the modelling problem.

First, notice from Eq. (
10
) that
x_{i}(t+1)
is determined solely by
\delta_{i}
, which is a function of
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
. In other words, the players decision to defect or cooperate can potentially (but not necessarily) be influenced by other players’ opinions, but not actions. However,
\delta_{i}
also contains the parameters
r,n,\alpha_{i}
which are associated with the PGG, and as we illustrate in the sequel, there are parameter values for which the sign of
\delta_{i}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{y}
. This is a key departure from the coevolutionary model in
[
7
]
, which considered coordination games on networks with Friedkin–Johnsen opinion dynamics, and the equivalent
\delta_{i}
that determined a player’s action was dependent on both
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Second, a player’s new opinion is a convex combination of their neighbours’ current opinions, their existing prejudice, and their new action. This is a natural extension of the Friedkin–Johnsen model, which considers a convex combination of the former two quantities. It also illustrates how a player’s new opinion is impacted by both their neighbours’ current opinions and their own decision, illustrating the coevolving nature of the dynamics. Because
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}=1
, the dynamics are clearly influenced by the relative weighting that a player places on the PGG (
\alpha_{i}
), the opinion dynamics (
\beta_{i}
) and the need to be self-consistent (
\lambda_{i}
).

In summary, the
coevolutionary game

\Gamma=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\pi})
is defined on a network
\mathcal{G}
as in Definition
2
. The
coevolutionary model
is the dynamical system arising from the game being played repeatedly over discrete time-steps by the players, in which active players at each time-step update their action and opinion using a best-response approach.

III
Main Results

Here, we study the equilibria of the system, characterizing them as Nash equilibria of the coevolutionary game, discussing the existence of some equilibria of interest (namely, those in which the entire population defect or cooperate, respectively), and show that under certain parameter conditions, there is global convergence to a unique equilibrium.

III-A

Classification of Equilibria

To characterise the equilibria of the coevolutionary model, we introduce some useful terminology. A state
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})
is an equilibrium if Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
11
) hold with
x_{i}(t+1)=x_{i}(t)=x_{i}^{*}
and
y_{i}(t+1)=y_{i}(t)=y_{i}^{*}
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
.

Our results begin by relating the equilibria of the coevolutionary model to Nash equilibria of the coevolutionary game. We omit the proof for brevity, noting it can be obtained directly from Definition
2
and Eqs. (
10
)–(
12
).

Proposition 2
.

Under Assumption
1
, the set of equilibria of the dynamics for the coevolutionary model in Eqs. (
10
)–(
12
) coincides with the set of Nash Equilibria of the coevolutionary game in Definition
2
.

Henceforth, our analysis is written from the perspective of characterising the equilibria for the coevolutionary model, but as indicated above, this provides equivalent characterisation of the Nash equilibria for the coevolutionary game. In this paper, we derive results under the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

All players have zero prejudice, i.e.
\gamma_{i}=0,\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
, and
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\lambda_{i}\in(0,1),\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
.

This assumption allows us to the focus on the role of the variables
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},
and
\lambda_{i}
, which are the parameters of the PGG, opinion dynamics, and self-consistency. Extending the results to allow for
\gamma_{i}>0
is an important future direction, to examine the impact of attachment to existing prejudice.

To better interpret the results, let us define three types of consensus that can arise.

Definition 3

(Classification of consensus states)
.

A state
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is said to be an action consensus if
x_{i}=x_{j}\ \forall i,j\in\mathcal{V}
. For any arbitrary
\boldsymbol{y}
, we call the action consensus state
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y})
as the all-defection state, and
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{y})
as the all-cooperation state. Similarly, we define an opinion consensus as
y_{i}=y_{j}\ \forall i,j\in\mathcal{V}
. Then, for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{x}
, the state
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{x},c\boldsymbol{1})
is an opinion consensus state for any
c\in[0,1]
. Finally, we call
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})
the all-defection consensus state, and
\boldsymbol{z}=(\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1})
the all-cooperation consensus state.

We begin our equilibria analysis by showing that any equilibrium in which there is action consensus must in fact have an opinion consensus.

Proposition 3
.

Consider the coevolutionary model under Assumption
2
, and let
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(x^{*},y^{*})
be an equilibrium of the model. There holds
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
. That is, an equilibrium
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
with an action consensus must in fact also have an opinion consensus.

Proof.

We first show that
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}\Rightarrow\penalty\ \boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
. At an equilibrium
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})
,
x_{i}^{*}=s(\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*}))=0
for all
i
. Thus, Eq. (
11
) at
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
evaluates, for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
, to be

y_{i}^{*}=\dfrac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}\sum\nolimits_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}y_{j}^{*}.

(13)

Let us define
\beta_{i}/\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}
as the
i^{th}
diagonal entry of the diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.
We write the set of
y_{i}^{*}
equilibria equations as
(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{W})\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
, with

\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
obviously a solution. To prove it is in fact the unique solution, we prove the matrix
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{W}
is invertible, yielding
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{W}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{0}
. Assumption
2
implies that
\beta_{i}/(\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i})<1
, and thus
\boldsymbol{\phi W}
is a nonnegative matrix with all row sums strictly less than 1. Standard nonnegative matrix theory yields that
\rho({\boldsymbol{\phi}W})<1
(e.g. by application of
[
13
, Corollary 8.1.29]
). Thus,
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi W}
cannot have a zero eigenvalue and is in fact invertible.

Next, we show
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}\Rightarrow\penalty\ \boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
. The hypothesis that
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
implies that
s_{i}(\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*}))=1
must hold for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
.
Hence, at an equilibrium
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
, there must hold

y_{i}^{*}=\dfrac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}\sum\nolimits_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}y_{j}^{*}+\dfrac{\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}.

(14)

The set of equilibrium equations can thus be expressed as
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{y}^{*}+(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi})\boldsymbol{1}
.
The fact that
\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}
implies that
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
is a solution. We proved above that
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi W}
is invertible, allowing us to rewrite
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi W})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\phi})\boldsymbol{1}
. Clearly, the solution
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
is unique, and must be
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
2
, the all-defection consensus state
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})
is always an equilibrium of the coevolutionary model. Moreover, it is the unique equilibrium of the coevolutionary model if there holds

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}\leq 2\alpha_{i}\left(1-\frac{r}{n}\right),

(15)

for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
. If instead

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i}}>2\alpha_{i}\left(1-\frac{r}{n}\right),

(16)

for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
, then the all-cooperation consensus state
(\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{1})
is an equilibrium.

Proof.

To prove the first statement, we need to show that at an equilibrium
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})
, it is always true that i)
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and ii)
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
. The first part is immediate from Proposition
3
. For the second part, observe that when
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
, Eq. (
5
) evaluates to be

\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=\alpha_{i}\bigg(\frac{r}{n}-1\bigg)-\frac{\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}}{2(\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i})}\leq 0

(17)

because
r<n
(recall
r
is the public good multiplier). In other words,
x_{i}^{*}=0
for all
i
, hence
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})
is always an equilibrium of the coevolutionary model.

To prove the second statement, notice that if

\dfrac{\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})\Big(1-\dfrac{r}{n}\Big)}{\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\geq 1,

(18)

then
\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})\leq 0
holds independent of the particular value of
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
, making
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})
the unique equilibrium. Rearranging Eq. (
18
) yields Eq. (
15
).

To prove the all-cooperation consensus state is an equilibrium we must show that at an equilibrium
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})
, i)
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
, and ii)
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
. The first part is obtained from Proposition
3
. For the second part, observe that under the assumption
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{1}
, Eq. (
5
) evaluates to be

\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=\alpha_{i}\bigg(\frac{r}{n}-1\bigg)+\frac{\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}}{2(\beta_{i}+\lambda_{i})}.

(19)

For a player to cooperate,
\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})>0
. Setting the condition in Eq. (
19
) to be greater than zero and rearranging yields Eq. (
16
). Therefore, if Eq. (
16
) holds for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
then the all-cooperation consensus state is an equilibrium. ∎

Interestingly, the conditions in Eq. (
15
) and Eq. (
16
) depict a trade-off between the role of self-consistency and opinion dynamics (left-hand side) and of the PGG (right-hand side). Stable cooperation seems possible when self-consistency and opinion dynamics can compensate for the fact that defection is inherently more advantageous for the single individual in the PGG. Note that when Eq. (
15
) does not hold, there may be other equilibria besides the all-defection and all-cooperation consensus states, with a mixture of defectors and cooperators.

III-B

Convergence

Theorem
1
identifies conditions on the individual-level parameters that imply that
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})
is the unique equilibrium. Now, we prove that the same conditions yield convergence to said equilibrium for all initial conditions.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the coevolutionary model under Assumptions
1
and
2
. Suppose that
\boldsymbol{W}
is symmetric and irreducible, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}
is undirected and connected. Suppose further that Eq. (
15
) holds for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
.
Then, for all
(\boldsymbol{x}(0),\boldsymbol{y}(0))
, there holds
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
t\geq T
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\boldsymbol{0}
, where
T
is given in Assumption
1
.

Proof.

In this proof, we first establish convergence of
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
to
\boldsymbol{0}
in finite time. Then, we show that once
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
has converged,
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
will converge asymptotically to
\boldsymbol{0}
.

Observe that if Eq. (
15
) holds for all agents, then
\delta_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))\leq 0
, for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
and
t\geq 0
, irrespectively of
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
. In other words, if player
i
is active at timestep
t
, their best-response will involve defection for the PGG (i.e.,
x_{i}(t+1)=0
).
Under Assumption
1
, after
T
timesteps, there holds
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\boldsymbol{0},\forall t\geq T
. In the following, without loss of generality, we focus on analysis of
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
for
t\geq T
.

Given that the action vector is at the all-defection action consensus state,
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\boldsymbol{0}
, for all
t\geq T
, the payoff that an arbitrary but fixed player
i
receives for having action
0
and opinion
s\in[0,1]
for any time
t\geq T
simplifies to

\pi_{i}((0,s),\boldsymbol{z}_{-i})=-\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\beta_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}w_{ij}(s-y_{j})^{2}+\lambda_{i}s^{2}\bigg].

(20)

Now, consider the following potential function:

\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{y})=-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\dfrac{w_{ij}}{2}(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}+\dfrac{\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}}y_{i}^{2}\right].

(21)

Our hypothesis that
\boldsymbol{W}
symmetric, i.e.
w_{ij}=w_{ji}
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{V}
, means that
\Phi
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Phi(y_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\bigg]-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\beta_{i}}y_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{i\}}w_{k\ell}(y_{k}-y_{\ell})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\sum_{k\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\beta_{k}}y_{k}^{2}\bigg].

(22)

Notice here that we have simply rewritten
\Phi
to highlight a fixed but arbitrary player
i\in\mathcal{V}
.

By exploiting Eq. (
20
) and Eq. (
III-B
), it can be verified that given two opinions,
s
and
s^{\prime}
for player
i
, there holds

\pi(s,\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})-\pi(s^{\prime},\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})=\beta_{i}(\Phi(s,\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})-\Phi(s^{\prime},\boldsymbol{y}_{-i})).

(23)

Verify that the quadratic form of Eq. (
21
) is
\Phi(\boldsymbol{y})=-\frac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{W}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\boldsymbol{y}]
,
where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1}/\beta_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}/\beta_{n})
is a positive diagonal matrix. Note that
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{W}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a symmetric nonsingular
M
-matrix
[
22
, Theorem 4.31]
, and thus positive definite, which means
\Phi
has a unique maximum equal to
0
, precisely at the maximiser
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Let player
i
be the active player at time
t\geq T
. From Definition
1
, it is clear that the best-response dynamics implies that
\pi_{i}((0,y_{i}(t+1),\boldsymbol{z}_{-i}(t))\geq\pi_{i}((0,y_{i}(t),\boldsymbol{z}_{-i}(t))
, with a strict inequality if
y_{i}(t+1)\neq y_{i}(t)
. This implies, from Eq. (
23
), that
\Phi(\boldsymbol{y}(t+1))-\Phi(\boldsymbol{y}(t))\geq 0
, with a strict inequality if
y_{i}(t+1)\neq y_{i}(t)
. In other words, every timestep in which the active agent revises their opinion to a different value results in an increase in the potential
\Phi
. Since
\Phi(\boldsymbol{y}(t))
is a monotonically non-decreasing sequence, and is bounded from above by
0
, the monotone convergence theorem
[
2
]
leads to the conclusion that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\Phi(\boldsymbol{y}(t))=\Phi^{*}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})
. Our final step is to prove that
\Phi^{*}=0
, or that
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
. To see this, observe that in the limit
t\to\infty
,
\Phi(t+T)=\Phi(t)
, which is equivalent to
\boldsymbol{y}(t+T)=\boldsymbol{y}(t)
. From Assumption
1
, this means that every agent has activated and has not changed their opinion value. In other words,
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
has reached an equilibrium
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
. We concluded earlier that
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
t\geq T
, and combined with Proposition
3
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}=\boldsymbol{0}
, which completes the proof.
∎

IV
Conclusion

This paper introduced a mathematical model that examined the coevolution of opinions and actions in social dilemmas. We established explicit expressions for a player’s best-response for the coevolutionary game and analysed the model’s equilibria, focusing on the all-defection and all-cooperation consensus states. A sufficient condition for global stability of the all-defection consensus equilibrium was identified. These promising results suggest several avenues of future research. First, we aim to establish an explicit region of attraction for the all-cooperation consensus equilibrium. Second, extending from Theorem
1
, we are interested in identifying conditions for the existence of equilibria with a mixture of cooperators and defectors. We also aim to show that the dynamics will always converge to an equilibrium, as extensive simulations support this conjecture.
