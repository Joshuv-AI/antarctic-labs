Unmasking the role of a thoracic arachnoid web and watershed ischemia in rapidly progressing paraplegia: illustrative case
Authors: Therese Phua, Ishwarya Nair, Paul Nichols, Saif Yousif, Patrick Donnelly, Joseph Garcia-Redmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSpinal arachnoid webs (SAWs) are an uncommon cause of spinal cord compression, typically presenting with an insidious onset of symptoms. Their role in acute neurological deterioration is not well established, and the underlying pathophysiology remains debated.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe the case of a 62-year-old male who presented with acute thoracic back pain and rapidly progressive unilateral lower limb weakness, evolving to paraplegia within hours. The initial deficit was highly atypical for a dorsally located compressive lesion, creating significant diagnostic uncertainty. Imaging revealed a T5–7 dorsal arachnoid web, which was considered an incidental finding given the atypical presentation. Subsequent imaging demonstrated web enlargement and the development of an owl’s-eye T2 hyperintensity, indicative of anterior spinal cord ischemia. Emergency surgical decompression and arachnolysis were performed, leading to partial neurological recovery.LESSONSThis case challenges the conventional understanding of SAWs as slowly progressive lesions and highlights the diagnostic complexity introduced by atypical presentations. The location of the web in a vascular watershed zone of the midthoracic spine likely contributed to the development of cord ischemia, which was suspected to be the primary driver of the patient’s fulminant course. This case underscores the importance of considering SAWs in the differential diagnosis of acute myelopathy. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2682

A spinal arachnoid web (SAW) is an abnormal band of intradural arachnoid membrane1,2 that causes focal tethering of the spinal cord. SAWs are rare lesions that share pathophysiological features with spinal arachnoid cysts (SACs), including alterations of the arachnoid membrane and disruption of CSF flow, suggesting potentially overlapping mechanisms.2,3 Some consider SAWs to represent an uncommon variant or incomplete form of SACs,1,4 proposing that arachnoid webs may arise from remnants of a collapsed arachnoid cyst.2 While often incidental, they may cause progressive myelopathy, radiculopathy, or back pain, corresponding to their size and location.2,5

Arachnoid webs are most commonly reported in the dorsal thoracic spine. Proposed mechanisms for acquired SAWs include prior trauma, infection, and iatrogenic procedures such as surgery.6,7 One hypothesis suggested that occult subarachnoid hemorrhage (secondary to trauma) may result in pooling of blood products in the thoracic subarachnoid space, provoking inflammation (arachnoiditis) and subsequent arachnoid adhesion/web formation.1 Acquired webs often feature thickened, inflammatory arachnoid tissue, suggesting a different underlying process from their congenital counterparts. In contrast, congenital arachnoid webs and cysts are thought to arise from developmental abnormalities of the arachnoid membrane.1,6,8,9

Symptom generation is thought to occur via spinal cord compression and changes to CSF mechanics/gradients1, 3 that sometimes result in the development of syringomyelia.2,4 In the thoracic spine, where vascular supply is relatively tenuous, such compression may cause vascular compromise, potentially leading to spinal cord ischemia.10,11 This case report explores the intersection of mechanical and vascular pathophysiology in a patient with a fulminant and atypical presentation.

Illustrative Case

A 62-year-old male presented to a peripheral emergency department with acute thoracic back pain and left lower limb weakness on waking that morning. He denied preceding infection, systemic symptoms, recent vaccination, or travel. He had no past medical history (including vascular risk factors) and was not taking regular medications. One week earlier, he reported left-sided chest and scapular pain following exertion while carrying a rucksack, which was diagnosed as musculoskeletal in nature. There was no clear history of trauma.

Initial examination (1430) demonstrated normal upper limb function. In the left lower limb, strength was reduced (hip flexion [L2] Medical Research Council grade 2/5, knee extension [L3] 2/5, ankle dorsiflexion [L4] 2/5, great toe extension [L5] 2/5, plantar flexion [S1] 4/5), but it was preserved in the right lower limb. Tone was normal. Sensation (light touch, pinprick, temperature, and vibration) was intact bilaterally. Proprioception was not assessed. Both patellar reflexes were 2+ but ankle reflexes were absent bilaterally. The left plantar response was extensor, and there was no response on the right. Peripheral pulses (posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis) were normal in both lower limbs.

Urgent MRI of the spine (at 1720) revealed a dorsal thoracic arachnoid web spanning T5–7 with mild cord compression but no intrinsic cord signal abnormality (Fig. 1A). At 2130 (7 hours after presentation), the patient’s condition had deteriorated with complete paralysis of his left lower limb (0/5) and new right-sided weakness (4/5 in L2–S1). Sensation (light, pinprick, temperature, and vibration) remained intact. Tone and reflexes were unchanged compared to prior assessment. There was no clonus.

Repeat MRI and MR angiography (at 0200, about 12 hours postpresentation) showed no change in the cyst. The absence of cord edema on T2-weighted imaging argued against venous congestive myelopathy, and no evidence of a vascular malformation was seen. He was referred to the neurology department at our tertiary hospital for further investigation and was awaiting transfer.

By 0700 (now 17 hours since presentation), he had proceeded to complete bilateral lower limb paralysis. Reflexes were now absent at the knees and ankles. Difficulty obtaining plantar responses was documented. Gross light touch remained intact, but a complete sensory examination was not performed. A catheter was placed for urinary retention. There was no documentation of anal tone assessment.

A lumbar puncture in the emergency department demonstrated a mildly elevated protein (520 mg/L, reference range 150–500 mg/L) with otherwise unremarkable findings. No opening pressure was recorded. Extensive infectious and autoimmune screening was negative (Supplemental Appendix). A working diagnosis of acute inflammatory neuropathy was considered, and intravenous immunoglobulin was planned but not initiated at the referring center due to the lack of availability.

The patient was reexamined at 1620 (26 hours from presentation) on arrival to our tertiary center. His upper limb assessment remained intact. He remained paralyzed in both lower limbs with absent knee and ankle reflexes. Light touch sensation was preserved, but pinprick was reduced. Proprioception was intact and vibration was not assessed. There was no clonus.

Privigen 10% 45 g was administered at 1955 and a 5-day course was planned. Nerve conduction studies performed the following morning were reported as nondiagnostic. There was evidence of bilateral asymmetric peroneal neuropathy (nonlocalized) with corresponding absent activity in muscles innervated by this nerve. There was no evidence of an acquired demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy. Tibial F-waves were within normal limits. It was suggested that the study be repeated in 1–2 weeks (see the Supplemental Appendix for a full report).

On examination at 1030 (32 hours from presentation), a sensory level to T5 for temperature was identified. A third MRI examination (36 hours from presentation) revealed interval progression of the arachnoid web, now spanning T5–T8 with significant cord compression (Fig. 1B). New T2 hyperintensity with an owl’s-eye appearance at T5–6 was evident, consistent with anterior spinal cord ischemia (Fig. 2A). Intravenous immunoglobulin was stopped after 2 doses and urgent neurosurgical intervention was undertaken. FIG. 2. The patient underwent an emergency T4–6 laminectomy (40 hours from presentation). Intraoperatively, thickened arachnoid adhesions forming a web were identified (Video 1) and resected, restoring CSF flow, confirmed with intraoperative ultrasound (Video 2). Histopathological analysis showed benign arachnoid tissue. There were no postoperative complications.

Preoperatively, the patient was classified as T4 American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grade B. There was no immediate postoperative improvement; however, by postoperative day 9, he had improved to T3 AIS grade C. On discharge from spinal rehabilitation, he was T4 AIS grade D.

Discussion

Observations

This case presents a diagnostic and management challenge by illustrating a fulminant neurological decline secondary to a thoracic SAW, which typically has an indolent course. The patient’s rapid progression from unilateral weakness to paraplegia is highly atypical and necessitates reevaluation of the pathophysiology underlying symptomatic SAWs. We propose a dual pathophysiological model in which acute spinal cord ischemia, occurring within a vulnerable vascular watershed zone, contributed to rapid neurological deterioration in addition to mass effect from the arachnoid web.

Diagnostic Dilemma: Unilateral Motor Deficit and Dorsal Compression

The initial presentation of isolated unilateral motor weakness without sensory involvement posed a significant diagnostic challenge. Dorsally located compressive lesions typically produce bilateral motor and sensory deficits due to symmetric compression of the posterior columns and corticospinal tracts. The preserved sensation and the striking asymmetrical motor involvement were highly atypical and initially led clinicians to doubt whether the web was the causative lesion.

In retrospect, the unilateral presentation may be explained by asymmetric vascular compromise rather than direct mechanical compression. The anterior spinal artery (ASA), which supplies the anterior two-thirds of the cord, including the anterior horns and corticospinal tracts, may have been preferentially affected on one side due to anatomical variations in sulcal artery distribution or asymmetric tethering of these vessels during cord displacement.12,13 This would result in predominantly motor, pain and temperature deficits (from anterior horn and lateral column involvement) while sparing the dorsal columns (posterior column–medial lemniscus pathway), thereby preserving proprioception and fine touch. Evidence Supporting the Acquired Nature of the Arachnoid Web

Multiple findings support an acquired, posttraumatic, and inflammatory etiology rather than a long-standing congenital lesion. In their systematic review of secondary SACs, Wang et al.14 identified trauma as one of the three most common etiologies, with a median latency of 12–30 months between inciting event and diagnosis for extradural and intradural cysts. A literature review by Vattipally et al. further suggested that SAWs are underdiagnosed most likely due to their slow onset of symptoms, with a mean duration of symptom onset being greater than 3 years.15

Posttraumatic arachnoid inflammation has been proposed as a mechanism for arachnoid pathology. Lesoin et al.16 described spinal arachnoid inflammation following trauma as a cause of focal arachnoid pathology, mediated by mechanical disruption or microhemorrhage within the arachnoid membrane. This is followed by an inflammatory response that leads to adhesion formation between the arachnoid and dura.17 These adhesions may disrupt CSF flow and contribute to focal arachnoid pathology. Zigic et al.17 characterized this process as “post-traumatic focal adhesive arachnoiditis,” a noninfectious inflammatory condition in which bleeding induces adhesion formation between the spinal cord and dura, potentially causing web formation and CSF obstruction.

In keeping with the inflammatory process, vasospasm should be considered as a contributing factor to ischemic injury. Vasospasm is a main mediator of secondary injury in the setting of spinal cord injury.18,19 Miller et al.20 demonstrated specific elevated inflammatory markers in the CSF of patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage complicated by vasospasm, supporting a possible association between inflammation and vascular spasm.20 In our case, such CSF inflammatory markers were not assessed and initial spinal MR angiography demonstrated no evidence of vascular malformation or vasospasm, and thus this was not further pursued.

The patient’s presentation 1 week following an episode of acute scapular and chest pain after carrying a rucksack represents an unusually rapid progression but is highly suggestive of a traumatic precipitant. This may reflect acute extension of a preexisting subclinical web triggered by minor trauma and exacerbated by a posttraumatic inflammatory process or, alternatively, the formation of a new web via an acute inflammatory cascade. The possibility of a parallel mechanism should also be considered instead of purely independent mechanical and ischemic processes.

The patient had no antecedent history of back pain, radiculopathy, or neurological symptoms prior to the rucksack-carrying incident. A long-standing congenital lesion of the size eventually documented on MRI (90 mm in craniocaudal length) would be expected to exhibit some degree of chronic symptomatology. The sudden onset of symptoms following a discrete traumatic event argues against a preexisting large web and favors either de novo formation or acute expansion/extension of a previously asymptomatic web. There is certainly the possibility of a concurrent inflammatory/arachnoiditis occurring secondary to trauma, which may also have fueled web enlargement.

A case described by Tsukamoto et al.21 experienced acute neurological deterioration following a minor traffic accident, in which imaging identified a small arachnoid web. The authors proposed that even small arachnoid webs, when confounded by stressors (trauma), may disrupt compensatory CSF dynamics, resulting in neurological decline.21

Surgical findings held the most compelling evidence for an acquired etiology. Intraoperatively, the web consisted of thickened arachnoid tissue with adhesions to the lateral dural walls and the dorsal surface of the spinal cord. This pathological appearance is characteristic of posttraumatic or postinflammatory arachnoiditis rather than a simple congenital duplication of the arachnoid membrane.22,23 Although there is insufficient literature to differentiate the gross appearance of congenital from acquired arachnoid webs, congenital SACs are typically described as thin-walled, translucent structures with minimal adhesions, whereas acquired cysts demonstrate fibrotic, thickened membranes and extensive adhesions resulting from prior hemorrhage, inflammation, or mechanical disruption of the arachnoid.14,24 Drawing from this parallel, it is reasonable to hypothesize that arachnoid webs may exhibit similar differences.

Importantly, resection of symptomatic arachnoid webs typically results in neurological improvement or cure and must be done to restore CSF flow.1,2,15,25 In this case, the presence of a superimposed ischemic injury likely limited recovery, underscoring the importance of early recognition and intervention.

Taken together, the temporal relationship to minor trauma, absence of prior symptoms, intraoperative findings of fibrotic adhesions, and established mechanisms of posttraumatic arachnoid inflammation support an acquired etiology. We propose that minor mechanical stress led to arachnoid disruption or microhemorrhage, initiating an inflammatory cascade that resulted in adhesion formation, web development, and ultimately rapid neurological deterioration.

Mechanism of Rapid Expansion and Ischemic Insult

The critical element in this case is the anatomical location of the insult. The web, spanning T5–8, is situated within the midthoracic spinal cord (T4–9), a region notorious for its tenuous vascular supply. This segment is a recognized vascular watershed zone. Lying between the well-perfused cervical and lumbar enlargements, it receives sparse radiculomedullary arterial input,10,26 rendering it particularly vulnerable to perfusion compromise.

External compression in this region can have devastating vascular consequences. Studies by Epstein11 and Alshareef et al.27 have demonstrated that dorsal compression can paradoxically cause the most significant reduction in blood flow within the ASA territory.11,27 This occurs due to distortion of the cord and tethering of the delicate sulcal arteries that arise from the ASA to supply the anterior horns. The dramatic appearance of the owl’s-eye sign on the patient’s third MRI examination is radiological evidence of this ischemic process. This sign represents cytotoxic edema in the anterior horn motor neurons, which have high metabolic demand and are exquisitely sensitive to hypoperfusion from an ASA territory insult.28,29 The timing of its appearance, coinciding with the patient’s most profound neurological deficit and the documented web enlargement, provides a clear pathophysiological link between compression, ischemia, and clinical presentation.

Lessons

Synthesizing these findings, we propose that the patient’s clinical course resulted from a two-hit process. The first insult involved rapid expansion of a likely acquired, posttraumatic/inflammatory arachnoid web. The second, more critical insult was the vascular compromise within a midthoracic watershed zone, leading to anterior spinal cord ischemia and infarction. The resulting neurological deficit is therefore better characterized as an ischemic myelopathy precipitated by mechanical compression and potential inflammatory arachnoid pathology, rather than a purely compressive myelopathy. However, it is possible that the two events occurred concurrently. The initial unilateral presentation likely reflected asymmetric vascular involvement, which subsequently progressed to bilateral infarction.

This case offers several crucial lessons. Minor trauma may act as a precipitating factor for symptomatic progression of arachnoid webs. SAWs should not be universally dismissed as incidental findings in patients with acute or progressive neurological symptoms, even when the presentation is atypical. Finally, unilateral deficits in the setting of dorsal compression should raise suspicion for vascular mechanisms rather than direct mechanical injury alone, especially in the midthoracic spine. Recognition of this dual pathology is important as timely surgical intervention may prevent irreversible ischemic spinal cord injury and, in isolated SAWs, may restore neurological function.
