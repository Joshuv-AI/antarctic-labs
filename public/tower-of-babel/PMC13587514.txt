Antimicrobial peptide CAP18 as a natural alternative to antibiotics: effects on gut health, immunity, and meat quality in broiler chickens
Authors: Pooneh Jebalbarezi, Mohsen Afsharmanesh, Azadeh Lohrasbi-Nejad, Mohammad Salarmoini, Mohammad Khajeh Bami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with an antibiotic or the antimicrobial peptide CAP18 on growth performance, immune response, intestinal microbiota, gut morphology, and meat quality in broiler chickens. A total of 480 one-day-old Ross 308 broilers were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments in a completely randomized design with five replicates of 24 birds each: (1) basal diet (control), (2) basal diet + 500 mg/kg antibiotic (5% oxytetracycline), (3) basal diet + 10 mg/kg CAP18, and (4) basal diet + 20 mg/kg CAP18.ResultsSupplementation with CAP18 at either 10 or 20 mg/kg maintained body weight gain, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio compared with the control or antibiotic treatments. However, CAP18 improved intestinal health by increasing the ileal lactic acid bacteria-to-coliform ratio (P < 0.05) and enhancing ileal morphology, with birds receiving 20 mg/kg CAP18 showing reduced crypt depth and a higher villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (P < 0.05). Meat quality was also enhanced in CAP18-fed birds, as indicated by lower thiobarbituric acid reactive substances, reduced cooking loss, and higher water-holding capacity compared with the antibiotic group (P < 0.05). Moreover, at 42 days, serum IgG levels were elevated in birds receiving 20 mg/kg CAP18, demonstrating enhanced humoral immunity (P < 0.05).ConclusionsIn conclusion, dietary CAP18 at 10 mg/kg maintained growth performance while promoting a healthier intestinal microbiota, enhanced meat quality, and strengthened immune responses compared with the antibiotic treatment.Keywords: Antibiotic, Antimicrobial peptide, Broiler chickens, Immune response, Intestinal microbiota

Background

Antibiotics have been widely used in poultry diets to prevent disease and promote growth due to their antimicrobial properties [1, 2]. However, increasing concerns over antibiotic resistance and residues in animal products have prompted the search for safe and effective alternatives [3]. Among these alternatives, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have gained considerable attention. AMPs are small, cationic molecules with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity that differs from conventional antibiotics. They act by disrupting microbial membranes or interfering with intracellular targets, thereby reducing the risk of resistance development [4, 5]. Beyond their antimicrobial effects, AMPs modulate immune responses, shape gut microbiota composition, and support intestinal health [6, 7]. In broilers, AMPs have been reported to enhance nutrient digestibility, immune performance, gut morphology, and meat quality [8, 9].

CAP18, a cathelicidin originally isolated from rabbit neutrophils, exhibits potent antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and can neutralize lipopolysaccharides [5, 10, 11]. Its cationic properties allow it to bind bacterial surfaces, causing membrane lysis without promoting resistance. Despite its promise, studies evaluating CAP18 as an alternative to antibiotic growth promoters in broilers remain limited. Previous research indicates that AMPs can improve feed efficiency, modulate intestinal microbiota, enhance villus architecture, and reduce enteric pathogen load [12]. However, data on recombinant AMPs such as CAP18, especially as a direct alternative to antibiotics and the optimal dietary level of this peptide in broiler diets, are scarce.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary supplementation with CAP18 and an antibiotic on growth performance, intestinal microbiota, gut morphology, immune responses, and meat quality in broiler chickens, to assess the potential of CAP18 as a natural alternative to conventional antibiotic growth promoters.

Materials and methods

Birds, experimental design, and diets

A total of 480 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks were obtained and reared for 42 days under standard management and environmental conditions. Birds were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments in a completely randomized design, with five replicates per treatment and 24 birds per replicate. Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study. Experimental diets were based on corn and soybean meal and formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of Ross 308 broilers for starter (1–10 days), grower (11–24 days), and finisher (25–42 days) phases (Aviagen, 2014) [13] (Table 1). The dietary treatments were as follows: (1) basal diet without additives (control), (2) basal diet + 500 mg/kg antibiotic (5% oxytetracycline), (3) basal diet + 10 mg/kg CAP18, and (4) basal diet + 20 mg/kg CAP18. The antimicrobial peptide CAP18 was produced recombinantly, lyophilized as an rCAP18-containing cell-free culture supernatant, and mixed thoroughly into the basal diet. An oxytetracycline premix containing 5% active substance was added to the basal diet to provide 25 mg of active oxytetracycline per kilogram of feed. This sub-therapeutic level was selected based on levels commonly used for antibiotic growth promoters in poultry nutrition. All diets were provided in mash form throughout the experimental period. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and approved by Veterinary Faculty of Shahid Bahonar University of Kerman (approval number: IR.UK.VETMED.REC 2019-03-05).

Antimicrobial peptide

The antimicrobial peptide CAP18 was produced recombinantly using a single X-33@rCAP18 clone that was prepared with our team previously using the Pichia pastoris expression system [11, 14]. The clone was cultured in 10 mL YPD medium (yeast extract 1% (W/V), peptone 2% (W/V), and dextrose 1% (W/V) at 30 °C and 150 rpm for 16 h. Cells were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min and transferred into 5 mL YPG medium (yeast extract 1% (W/V), peptone 2% (W/V), and glycerol 1% (V/V)) for overnight, 150 rpm. After sufficient cell proliferation, the culture was harvested by centrifugation and inoculated into 100 mL of YPM medium (yeast extract 1% (W/V), peptone 2% (W/V), and methanol 0.5% (V/V)), while the OD600 of new medium reached 1 and incubated at 28 °C for 54 h. Secretory expression of rCAP18 was induced by adding 0.5% (v/v) methanol every 24 h. The culture medium was harvested 54 h after the induction process started. The supernatant, rich in secreted rCAP18, was separated from the cell pellet by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm and 4 °C, yielding approximately 41 mg/L of recombinant CAP18. The molecular weight and identity of rCAP18 were confirmed as an approximately 17.5 kDa band by Tris-Tricine SDS-PAGE and anti-His Western blotting after Ni-NTA purification, with a purity of approximately 85% [11, 14]. The supernatant including rCAP18 was lyophylized before being incorporated into the experimental diets.

Growth performance

Birds were group weighed on days 1, 10, 24, and 42 to determine body weight gain (BWG) for the starter (1–10 days), grower (11–24 days), finisher (25–42 days), and overall (1–42 days) periods. Feed intake (FI) was calculated as the difference between feed offered and feed residuals for each experimental unit. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the ratio of FI to BWG and was adjusted for mortality, which was recorded twice daily.

Carcass traits

On day 42, two birds were randomly selected from each experimental unit (10 birds per treatment), weighed, and slaughtered by cervical dislocation to assess carcass characteristics. The carcass, thighs, breast, proventriculus, liver, pancreas, heart, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius were separated, weighed, and expressed as a percentage of live body weight. The left-side thigh muscles (biceps femoris and semitendinosus) from each bird was collected, placed in plastic bags, and stored at − 20 °C for 30 days for subsequent meat quality analyses.

Meat quality

The antioxidant status of meat was assessed by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) following the method of Buege and Aust [15], with modifications described by Khajeh Bami et al. [16]. Briefly, 0.5 g of thigh muscle was homogenized with 2.5 mL of a mixture containing 0.375% TBA (Sigma-Aldrich, T5500), 15% TCA (Merck, K46451107), and 0.25 N HCl using a Potter-Elvehjem homogenizer. The mixture was prepared by adding 2.05 mL of 0.25 N HCl, 15 g of TCA, and 0.375 g of TBA, and bringing the final volume to 100 mL with distilled water. The homogenate was heated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 10 min, cooled immediately under tap water, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Epoch, Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). TBARS values were expressed as mg malondialdehyde per kg of meat.

Meat pH was measured according to Jang et al. [17]. A 5 g sample was homogenized in 25 mL of distilled water for 1 min, and pH was recorded using a digital pH meter.

Water-holding capacity (WHC) was determined following Castellini et al. [18]. Briefly, 1 g of meat was placed on filter paper inside a falcon tube, centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 4 min at 4 °C, and dried at 70 °C. WHC was calculated based on retained water.

Drip loss (DL) was determined as the percentage weight loss of meat samples (without external fat and connective tissue) after 24 h of storage at 4 °C, following Christensen [19]. Cooking loss (CL) was measured as the percentage weight difference before and after cooking meat samples in a water bath at 85 °C for 10 min [20].

Intestinal microflora

At day 42, two birds were randomly selected from each experimental unit and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Ileal digesta was collected aseptically, placed in sterile plastic bags, and stored at − 80 °C until microbial analysis. For microbial enumeration, the digesta samples were serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) were cultivated on MRS agar using dilutions from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶ and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Coliforms (COL) were cultivated on MacConkey agar using dilutions from 10⁻² to 10⁻⁵ and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h [20, 21]. Microbial counts were expressed as log10 colony forming units per gram of digesta.

Intestinal morphology

The same birds euthanized for intestinal microbiota analysis were used for gut histomorphology. Approximately 1-cm segments of the ileum and jejunum were collected, flushed with saline, and fixed in 10% buffered formalin. Samples were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological analysis. Slides were examined using an optical microscope (Micromaster, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and analyzed with Image Pro-Plus software (v4.5; Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA) [22]. The following intestinal morphology parameters were measured: villus height, villus width, crypt depth, villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH/CD), epithelial cell layer thickness, and goblet cell density). At least 10 measurements per variable were recorded for each bird to ensure accuracy.

Immune response

Sheep red blood cells (SRBC) were used as a model antigen to evaluate humoral immune responses in broilers [23]. On 35 days, two birds per experimental unit were injected intramuscularly in the breast with 1 mL of 0.5% SRBC suspension. Blood samples (2 mL per bird) were collected from the brachial vein 7 days after injection (day 42). Sera were separated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Total and IgG anti-SRBC antibodies (mercaptoethanol resistant antibodies) were quantified using hemagglutination assays in U-bottomed 96-well microplates [22, 24]. IgM titers were calculated as the difference between total and IgG titers.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design with four dietary treatments and five replicates per treatment. Data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in Minitab statistical software (version 22, Minitab Inc., State College, PA, USA). Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test at a significance level of 0.05 to evaluate differences among the four treatment groups.

Results

Growth performance

BWG, FI, and FCR of broilers fed different treatments are presented in Table 2. During the starter (d 1–10), grower (d 11–24), finisher (d 25–42), and overall period (d 1–42), no significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed among treatments for BWG, FI, or FCR. Numerically, birds fed the antibiotic-supplemented diet showed slightly higher BWG during d 11–24 and marginally improved overall FCR compared with the control and peptide groups. Supplementation with CAP18 at both 10 and 20 mg/kg resulted in performance values comparable to the antibiotic group and control, with no consistent dose-dependent response. Overall, dietary CAP18 did not adversely affect growth performance and produced results similar to the antibiotic and control treatments throughout the trial. Mortality during the study was low (2%) and the deaths were not associated with any specific treatment.

Meat quality parameters

The effects of dietary treatments on broiler meat quality are presented in Table 4. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in TBARS, CL, and WHC, while DL and meat pH were unaffected (P > 0.05). Birds in the antibiotic group showed the highest TBARS value, indicating greater lipid peroxidation, whereas both levels of CAP18 (10 and 20 mg/kg) reduced TBARS to levels comparable to the control (P = 0.001). CL was significantly higher in the antibiotic group compared with both CAP18 treatments, with the control intermediate (P = 0.011). Similarly, WHC was lower in the antibiotic group compared with the control and CAP18 treatments (P = 0.007), suggesting that CAP18 supplementation supported better meat water retention. Overall, CAP18 supplementation improved oxidative stability and water holding properties of broiler meat relative to the antibiotic treatment, producing values similar to or better than the control.

The effects of dietary treatments on ileal microbiota are shown in Table 5. COL and LAB counts were not significantly influenced by treatments (P > 0.05). However, the ratio of LAB to COL was significantly affected (P = 0.009). Birds supplemented with CAP18 at both 10 and 20 mg/kg had a higher LAB /COL ratio compared with the antibiotic group, while the control group showed intermediate values. These findings indicate that CAP18 supplementation promoted a more favorable microbial balance in the ileum relative to antibiotic treatment, by enhancing the relative abundance of beneficial LAB compared with coliforms.

Intestinal morphology

The effects of dietary treatments on ileal morphology are summarized in Table 6. Villus height was not significantly influenced by treatments (P = 0.197). However, significant differences were observed for crypt depth (P = 0.010), VH/CD (P = 0.033), epithelial cell layer thickness (P = 0.029), and goblet cell density (P < 0.001). Birds supplemented with 20 mg/kg CAP18 exhibited the shallowest crypt depth compared with the control and antibiotic groups, resulting in a significantly higher VH/CD. Epithelial cell layer thickness was highest in the antibiotic and 10 mg/kg CAP18 groups compared with the control and 20 mg/kg CAP18 treatments. Goblet cell density was reduced in both CAP18 groups relative to the antibiotic and control diets. Overall, supplementation with 20 mg/kg CAP18 improved ileal morphology by enhancing the VH/CD, indicating more efficient absorptive potential.

Birds receiving 20 mg/kg CAP18 exhibited the highest IgG levels compared with the antibiotic group, while the control and 10 mg/kg CAP18 groups were intermediate. Discussion

In the present study, dietary supplementation with the antimicrobial peptide CAP18 at either 10 or 20 mg/kg did not significantly influence BWG, FI, or FCR compared with the control or antibiotic groups. These findings suggest that CAP18 supplementation maintained growth performance at levels comparable to antibiotic use, thereby supporting its potential as an alternative growth promoter. Similar to our observations, Li et al. [25] and Daneshmand et al. [12] reported that AMPs do not always elicit consistent improvements in broiler performance, although they can sustain growth comparable to antibiotics by enhancing gut health and nutrient utilization rather than directly stimulating FI. The absence of significant growth promoting effects from CAP18 in this study may be related to differences in peptide type, dose, and experimental conditions. Previous studies demonstrated variable responses to AMPs: Zhang et al. [8] reported that specific AMPs yielded growth performance comparable to antibiotics, whereas others observed stronger growth benefits from antibiotic supplementation [26]. Importantly, our results confirm that CAP18 did not impair performance, aligning with reports that AMPs can maintain growth outcomes while simultaneously offering additional health benefits [27, 28].

In the current study, dietary supplementation with CAP18 at 10 or 20 mg/kg did not significantly influence carcass yield, breast or thigh percentages, or the relative weights of internal organs compared with the control and antibiotic treatments. These findings indicate that CAP18, similar to antibiotics, does not adversely affect carcass composition or organ development. Consistent with our results, Daneshmand et al. [12] and Zhang et al. [8] reported that supplementation with AMPs generally maintains carcass characteristics without marked alterations in yield. Given the lack of statistical differences, the present results suggest that CAP18 functions primarily to preserve normal carcass growth rather than to enhance yield beyond basal or antibiotic-supplemented diets.

Dietary supplementation with CAP18 significantly increased the ratio of LAB to COL in the ileum compared with the antibiotic group, while total counts of LAB and COL were not significantly altered. This indicates that CAP18 selectively promotes beneficial microbial populations while suppressing potential pathogens, supporting a healthier intestinal environment. Mechanistically, antimicrobial peptides are known to disrupt bacterial membranes through electrostatic interactions, thereby inhibiting Escherichia coli without negatively affecting commensal Lactobacillus species [8, 29]. In contrast, antibiotic supplementation reduced the LAB/COL ratio, likely due to its broader spectrum effects on gut microbiota. These results highlight that CAP18 may be more targeted than conventional antibiotics in modulating gut microbial balance, promoting intestinal homeostasis and potentially contributing to improved nutrient absorption and immune function. The findings align with previous studies demonstrating that AMPs enhance beneficial microbial populations while limiting pathogenic colonization in broilers [8].

In the present study, broilers supplemented with 20 mg/kg CAP18 exhibited a shallower crypt depth and an increased VH/CD compared with the control and antibiotic groups, while villus height alone was not significantly affected. These morphological changes suggest enhanced absorptive capacity and improved intestinal health, which may contribute to more efficient nutrient utilization. However, a lower goblet cell density was also detected in the CAP18‑treated birds. Goblet cells are essential for mucin production, maintenance of the mucus barrier, epithelial protection, and innate mucosal defense. Reduced goblet cell density may reflect several biological possibilities, including reduced inflammatory stimulation, altered mucosal turnover, or potentially compromised mucosal protection. Therefore, the biological significance of reduced goblet cell density remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Some previous studies have shown that bioactive peptides and antimicrobial peptides can modulate gut morphology by promoting villus development and reducing crypt hyperplasia [30–32]. The effects of AMPs on gut morphology, however, are influenced by peptide type, dosage, and experimental conditions [33]. These findings suggest that CAP18 may enhance nutrient absorption and intestinal efficiency.

Improving meat quality—including physicochemical traits such as oxidative stability, WHC, and CL is an important objective alongside production performance. Feed additives, including antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), can influence these traits by modulating oxidative status, muscle integrity, and microbial balance [34]. In the present study, thigh muscle TBARS values were significantly lower in broilers supplemented with 10 and 20 mg/kg CAP18 compared to the antibiotic group, indicating reduced lipid peroxidation and improved cellular protection. This aligns with previous studies showing that bioactive peptides decrease malondialdehyde content in broiler meat relative to antibiotics and controls [35, 36]. AMPs enhance antioxidant enzyme activities such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), which mitigates oxidative damage and preserves meat quality [32]. Improved WHC observed in CAP18 supplemented birds supports better juiciness and texture, likely due to preservation of proteolytic enzyme activity and protein integrity [37, 38]. DL was reduced in the 10 mg/kg CAP18 group, and CL was lower in the 20 mg/kg CAP18 group relative to the antibiotic treatment, highlighting dose dependent improvements in meat quality. In contrast, antibiotic supplementation tended to increase CL, consistent with reports linking some antibiotics to reduced water retention and altered muscle structure [39]. Collectively, these results indicate that CAP18 improves meat quality by enhancing oxidative stability, water retention, and postmortem muscle integrity. WHC is another essential quality factor linked to juiciness and tenderness. Our findings revealed that WHC was lower in the antibiotic group compared with both the AMP‑treated and control groups. This difference may be related to alterations in muscle proteolytic processes, as reported by Huff‑Lonergan and Lonergan [38]. Enhanced WHC correlates with lower DL and CL, which we also observed, further confirming the protective effects of AMPs on meat texture and moisture retention [40]. Conversely, antibiotic supplementation increased CL, possibly due to altered muscle protein integrity [39].

The immunomodulatory effects of CAP18 were evident in the present study. Birds supplemented with 20 mg/kg CAP18 exhibited significantly higher serum IgG levels at day 42 compared with the antibiotic group, while IgM and total immunoglobulin levels were not significantly altered. These results indicate that CAP18, particularly at the higher dose, enhances humoral immunity, reflecting an improved capacity to produce antibodies [41]. The observed immune benefits are likely linked to CAP18’s positive effects on gut health and microbial balance. By promoting beneficial LAB and reducing pathogenic COL, AMPs create an intestinal environment that supports both innate and adaptive immune function [8, 42]. Previous studies using other antimicrobial peptides including Microcin C7, LLV peptides, and flaxseed-derived bioactive peptides reported similar increases in IgG and IgM concentrations in broilers [31, 43, 44]. AMPs have also been shown to enhance lymphocyte proliferation and immunoglobulin levels in other livestock species [45, 46].

Conclusion

Supplementing broiler diets with the antimicrobial peptide CAP18 at 10 and 20 mg/kg maintained growth performance at levels comparable to antibiotic and control treatments, without adverse effects on BWG, FI, or FCR. Importantly, compared with the antibiotic group, CAP18 enhanced meat quality by reducing lipid oxidation (lower TBARS), improving WHC, decreasing CL, and stabilizing muscle pH. Birds receiving CAP18 also exhibited a higher LAB-to-COL ratio in the ileum and elevated serum IgG levels compared with the antibiotic group. Overall, CAP18 demonstrated potential physiological and meat quality benefits while maintaining growth performance comparable to control and antibiotic treatments. The study was conducted under non-challenge conditions; therefore, while CAP18 shows promise as a functional feed additive, further mechanistic studies and challenge trials are required.
