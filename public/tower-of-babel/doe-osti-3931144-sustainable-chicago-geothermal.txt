 Executive Summary
The goal of this project was to evaluate and deploy a community geothermal pilot project
in the City of Chicago from 2023-2027 in the West Woodlawn neighborhood of Chicago,
a disadvantaged community facing high energy burdens from skyrocketing gas heating
and electric bills. The project team - a Chicago-based coalition of community, city, labor,
and energy leaders - is seeking to evaluate and demonstrate an equitable and just
transition to building decarbonization in dense, urban environments in cold climates. The
project will deploy a shared community geothermal network across four city blocks,
containing more than 200 multi-family and single-family residential households. The
community geothermal system seeks to demonstrate the Better Heat model, a new
carbon-free, modular, community-focused heat utility that can decarbonize the city’s
residential and small commercial building sectors.

Introduction
The project lead, Blacks in Green (™), an environmental and economic development non-
profit, together with the Chicago-based coalition of community, city, labor, and energy
leaders, are submitting this Report for the Sustainable Chicago Geothermal Project for
Budget Period 1 (Development).
During the Planning and Design Budget Period 1 (BP1) the project team designed a
community-scale geothermal heating and cooling systems in the West Woodlawn
neighborhood of Chicago, finalized site location and footprint, assessed the geothermal
resources, analyzed environmental and permitting needs, assessed planned usage,
conducted techno-economic feasibility analysis and local stakeholder engagement and
identified workforce and training development needs.
As required by the Funding Opportunity Announcement (FOA), this Report contains:
 1. A narrative description of Budget Period 1 Accomplishments.
 2. Full engineering feasibility and technical design for the GDHC system: Appendix
 A of this report;
 3. Technical, economic, and environmental assessment: Appendix B of this report.

Project Background
The Sustainable Chicago Geothermal project is part of the West Woodlawn community’s
Sustainable Square Mile effort, launched in 2010, and centered around the effort to build
a local living economy where most core neighbor needs are met within walking distance
and where visitors are grounded in local currency, culture, energy, goods, and knowledge.
The project lead, Blacks in Green, an environmental and economic development 501c3
non-profit, is tackling pollution and poverty with The Sustainable Square MileTM approach
to close the racial health and wealth gap by teaching the risks of climate crisis and
opportunities of the new green economy. The project is a critical next step to rapidly
advance a complete green makeover currently underway for a 73-block area in the West
Woodlawn Neighborhood as a model of The Sustainable Square Mile™. The project team
coalition is made up of the following entities:
 ● Community Leaders: Blacks in Green (“BIG”), City of Chicago, Citizens Utility
 Board (“CUB”)
 ● Workforce Leaders: Illinois AFL-CIO, Climate Jobs Illinois
 ● Analysis/Design Leaders: University of Illinois, The Accelerate Group
 ● Deployment Leaders: dbHMS, Geoexchange

Project Goals
The core project-specific goals are as follows:
Goal 1: Demonstrate the technical capability of shared geothermal networks to operate
within the spatial footprint of dense urban environments.
Goal 2: Demonstrate the economic feasibility of shared geothermal networks to reduce
costs of decarbonization compared to “behind-the-meter” geothermal system
deployments and air-source heat pumps with electric resistance back-up heating
systems.
Goal 3: Demonstrate models for supporting community energy leadership, and
ownership, over energy solutions for environmental justice, low-income, and
disadvantaged communities.
Goal 4: Demonstrate effective workforce partnerships to build capacity for and accelerate
adoption of neighborhood-scale decarbonization projects leveraging multiple trades.

Task 3.0 Initial System Design:

Task 3.1 Create Preliminary Technical Design: The project team created several analyses
to compute the capacity for ground source thermal storage in public rights of way to
determine the feasibility of the model. The project team identified alleyways in Chicago
as a primary option for exploration, due to the availability of space, the proximity to
household/business HVAC infrastructure at the rear of buildings, and the lower cost of
repair and replacement of alleyway surfaces vs. streets. The project team analyzed
several different typical block designs present in Chicago, compared to building demand
and heating and cooling needs, to determine this capacity. The team developed, and
updated throughout the BP1, layouts to reflect refinement of the technical model, and the
integration of equipment in the customer-side package design. The typical alley layouts
and building designs are represented in Appendix A, as present in the site footprint. The
project team evaluated multiple pipe and connection layout options, compared centralized
borefields to distributed boreholes, vertical boreholes compared to angled boreholes,
evaluated distributed pumps as compared to a per-module centralized pump house, and
settled on a system design that meets the project constraints. The system layout was
loaded into a shared GIS resource for use in development of a permitting plan.

 a) The Project Team found that typical Chicago alleys, at 16’ wide, can support
 between 60-90 boreholes, staggered on opposite sides of the alley, at 15-ft.
 distance on-center. For the two Chicago blocks in this project, one alley can
 support 62 boreholes (Module J), and the other alley can support 76 boreholes
 (Module K & L) due to its “T” design.
 b) Based on the surface constraints, the Project Team developed an initial technical
 model that would evaluate up to 150 M (450-492 feet) depth for vertical wells, and
 a horizontal loop depth of 6.5 ft.

Task 4.0 Technical Analysis:

Tasks 4.1 Assess geothermal resource: The Project team utilized a thermal response
tests and analysis from the Obama Presidential Center, located about one mile from the
project location, as well as gathered data from previous boring work done in nearby
neighborhoods, as well as drill logs from Illinois Water and Related Wells Map (ILWATER)
to compile the initial analysis to assess the geothermal resource and begin to develop
conceptual thermogeology models. The project team used this geological data input into
its simulations for Task 4.2.
The subsurface stratigraphy was estimated using existing drilling logs for water wells and
engineering test holes provided by the Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS) and
geologic map of the Jackson Park quadrangle (Curry et al., 2023). The boreholes used
for the analysis are shown in Figure 2. A typical stratigraphic profile for the West
Woodlawn neighborhood (assuming isotropic, homogeneous, and horizontal layers) is
shown in Appendix B. The subsurface geology reveals that the area is primarily underlain
by dolomite, which overlies gray shale. Above this bedrock, the site is covered by
relatively thin surficial glacial deposits, consisting of silt, sand, and till. The thickness of
these sedimentary layers varies across the site, generally ranging between 17 and 20
meters. The detailed results are in Appendix B.

Field Work

Horizontal-to-vertical spectral ratio (HVSR) passive seismic data was acquired in the
study area by the ISGS as part of an ongoing geologic mapping project in Metropolitan
Chicago funded by the US Geological Survey. The HVSR method is used to estimate the
depth of the bedrock (Karahalios et al., 2021) where a 6-inch-deep hole is excavated and
the receiver which records the ambient seismic waves for approximately 20 minutes is
placed. In the study area the bedrock was found to be between 17.3 m and 19.3 m below
ground surface.
Samples from a continuous core from a borehole located 6 miles from the project site
completed for Chicago’s deep tunnel project were subjected to a series of laboratory
experiments, including measurements of the thermal properties (thermal conductivity,
thermal diffusivity, and volumetric specific heat capacity using a Hot Disk® TPS 1500
thermal property analyzed.

effort. During Q2, the team was able to leverage preliminary sets of building loads gained
from the Foundational Study work. The project will eventually model all individual alley
blocks as individual modules and then combined together as multiple aggregated loops.
Preliminary results of a single alley simulation demonstrate the alley maintains sufficient
pipe capacity to support preliminary modeled loads. For the analyses conducted in Q2,

the project team modeled the deployment of a single pipe design that presumed a run
through of building loads sequentially. Further analysis is focused on interspersing
building loads with boreholes to identify the change in outlet temperatures. The project
team evaluated a specific module with building loads to determine whether the system
design conditions would support preliminary modeled loads. For the early analysis, with
36 boreholes, at a range of velocity, outlet temperatures at individual buildings were

deployment of a single pipe design that presumed a run through of building loads
sequentially, interspersing building loads with boreholes under multiple scenarios to
determine under what scenarios boundary conditions would not be reached. The
successful analysis showed that with 60 boreholes at velocity of 1.1 m/s, the module
analyzed was within the design range of the system.
ASHRAE recommends maintaining loop operating temperatures between 4°C and 32°C
to optimize system efficiency and protect the performance of the heat pump. This range
is particularly effective for ground-source heat pumps, as it ensures sufficient heat
exchange between the ground and the system without causing thermal stress on the heat
pump. Operating below 4°C could lead to freezing risks, while exceeding 32°C may
overheat the system, reducing efficiency. Kavanaugh and Rafferty (2014) emphasize this
range in their work, noting that it strikes a balance between performance, longevity, and
cost by allowing the heat pump to operate within its optimal thermal limits. The loop
operating temperatures considered for the design are shown in Table 2.

Preliminary ASHRAE Analysis
The Project team sought to evaluate whether the design of the project - distributed wells
and loads - would result in a different set of demands for wells and the horizontal loop
system The Project Team ran a comprehensive simulation of the specific Sustainable
Chicago Geothermal system modules within the project footprint
The ASHRAE analytical method developed by Philippe, Bernier, and Marchio (2010) was
applied to calculate the required borehole length for the geothermal system. This method,
which is based on steady-state heat transfer principles, provides practical guidelines for
borefield sizing by considering ground thermal properties and building loads. The
calculation incorporates the g-function concept to estimate the thermal interaction
between the boreholes and the surrounding ground. By using a simplified approach, the
method calculates the necessary borehole length to meet the building’s heating and
cooling demands over time, ensuring that the system operates within acceptable fluid

Task 4.4 Customer-Side Package Design: The team began work to analyze the individual
building typology within the site footprint to develop a standard set of customer-sited
equipment that best leverages the community-scale geothermal system and can
interconnect with the ground-source loops. A significant challenge has emerged in the
assessment of an initial design package. Most of the buildings within the project footprint
are three-story buildings with boiler heat feeding distributed radiators. Little to any
buildings possess central air, and air conditioning, if any, is supplied by window AC units.
The design team has worked to source potential solutions for heat exchange and heat
pump solutions for these building types. However, the preferred method (mini-split
systems connecting to a heat exchanger through water-sourced exchange with the
geothermal network) no longer exists on the market for residential single-phase buildings.
The only such systems the project team could identify were three-phase systems, more
typical to commercial buildings. The project team has evaluated alternative options (water
source systems that rely on internal piping), located at the existing radiator locations, but
the cost could be prohibitive (up to $90k per building).

After reviewing alternatives, the Project Team concluded that the most cost-efficient
approach would be to retrofit the properties to construct ducted central air systems where

Building Interconnection
The shared geothermal system components, including the boreholes, horizontal loop, and
pump-house, will be largely located in the public right-of-way in the alleys. However, the
community-owned system in the Sustainable Chicago Geothermal design extended into
each individual building as well. The “Geothermal System” will include all components
from the underground pipe connection to the horizontal loop through to a heat exchanger
located within each building that represents the “point-of-interconnection” for a customer
to the system. This building interconnection package includes the dual-pipe spur from the
shared loop into the building, outdoor below grade valve boxes with shutoff valves on
each pipe going into and out of the building, a BTU meter connected to each pipe going
to and from the heat exchanger, and circulating pumps.

Single Bore Field Alternative
In the Single Bore Field Alternative, the project team used the ASHRAE method to
determine
the number of vertical boreholes to serve the total, aggregate load of all buildings. In this
design, a pipe system was run from the bore field (still in the alleys) back to the pump

and then distributed out to the buildings through a separate loop.
Total cost of Single Bore Field Alternative (overnight capital cost): $7,804,260
Individual Boreholes Alternative

For the Individual Boreholes Alternative Scenario, the intent was to determine the
feasibility of locating individual geothermal systems on each property. Based on ASHRAE
analysis, each building would need approximately 3 boreholes to serve its loads. In a
geographical review of the site footprint, however, only 20 of the existing buildings had
enough private land to accommodate the required number of vertical boreholes to support
the building’s loads. In most of those cases, the available land included an existing
concrete driveway pad, patio, or other ground structure that would have to be replaced.
Based on this analysis, the Individual Boreholes Alternative scenario was deemed to be
infeasible and non-compliant with the goals of supplying geothermal energy to buildings
in the project footprint.

However, the Project Team still wanted to conduct an analysis of the Individual Boreholes
Alternative to determine cost savings of the community/alley approach. The Project Team
used ASHRAE calculations for each household load, disregarded land use constraints,
and priced out individual systems.

Total cost of Individual Boreholes Alternative (ignore space, overnight capital cost):
$7,229,370

Task 5.3 Design of geothermal utility ownership and rate structure: The Project Team
looked into multiple options for ownership of the community geothermal system and
ultimately decided to pursue the creation of a new, non-profit ownership entity modeled
on a clean energy cooperative structure. Such a model allows for and enables the
qualities of the energy system identified by the community through the Community
Foundation and Foundational Study phase:

 ● The desire for community ownership and control over energy resources;
 ● The desire to retain financial resources within the community, and
 build wealth and equity within its members;
 ● The need to overcome distrust in energy actors by community members;
 ● Preserving the ability of households to capture rebates under the IRA’s
 Home Electrification and Appliance Rebates (Section 50122) and Home
 Efficiency Rebates (Section 50121);
 ● Enabling the applicability of IRA Investment Tax Credits to the project;
 ● Ensuring the entity does not require a special franchise or regulatory regime; and
 ● Reinvest any profits back into the system or as bill reductions to participants;
However, due to the fact that rules from the US Treasury on investment tax credit
definitions and implementation are still pending and have a significant impact on the tax
impacts of the ownership structure, the Project Team plans to finalize the design of the
ownership entity during early phases of Budget Period 2. A pro forma financial plan for
such an ownership entity is provided in Appendix B.

Rate Structures

The Project Team evaluated several rate structures for billing for the thermal energy
supplied / exchanged through the community geothermal system to each
customer/building/household. In order to ensure principles of fairness and to encourage
efficiency, the Project Team settled on a design where customers pay more the more they
use the system. The preliminary rate structure is as follows:

 ● Customer Charge: fixed fee of $5/customer/month
 ● Variable Rate: based on monthly BTU usage set at a rate to ensure a 30%

Technical Assessment
Led by the Prairie State Institute at the University of Illinois, the Project team completed
a comprehensive analysis of the project location to a) determine if a geothermal system
deployment was feasible in the proposed location, b) developed a physics-based model
for the community scale system to determine whether a shared system was efficient, and
whether a distributed laid and borehole system was more efficient than a bore field design,
and c) developed an assessment tool for easier calculation of borehole requirements for
shared geothermal deployments in Chicago alleys. The core activities are summarized
as follows:
Tasks 4.1 Assess geothermal resource: The Project team utilized a thermal response
tests and analysis from the Obama Presidential Center, located about one mile from the
project location, as well as gathered data from previous boring work done in nearby
neighborhoods, as well as drill logs from Illinois Water and Related Wells Map (ILWATER)
to compile the initial analysis to assess the geothermal resource and begin to develop
conceptual thermogeology models. The project team used this geological data input into
its simulations for Task 4.2.
Horizontal-to-vertical spectral ratio (HVSR) passive seismic data was acquired in the
study area by the ISGS as part of an ongoing geologic mapping project in Metropolitan
Chicago funded by the US Geological Survey. The HVSR method is used to estimate the
depth of the bedrock (Karahalios et al., 2021) where a 6-inch-deep hole is excavated and
the receiver which records the ambient seismic waves for approximately 20 minutes is
placed. In the study area the bedrock was found to be between 17.3 m and 19.3 m below
ground surface.
Task 4.2 Create physics-based model for community scale system: The project team
calibrated a physics-based model to simulate subsurface multiphysics processes (heat
transfer, water flow, and deformation) at community scale, a first-of-its-kind modeling
effort.
The Project team sought to evaluate whether the design of the project - distributed
borehole configuration and loads - would result in a different set of demands for the
boreholes and the horizontal loop system The Project Team ran a comprehensive
simulation of the specific Sustainable Chicago Geothermal system modules within the
project footprint.
Task 4.3 Create preliminary assessment tool for project replication: The Project
developed a preliminary assessment tool for replication across modules and across
similar geographies to provide a simple but enhanced tool for quickly determining the
number of boreholes required for alley deployment on a city block in Chicago, based on
building types.
The attached Technical Analysis, compiled by the project team, provides details on the
approach and results.

INTRODUCTION
Fossil fuels for commercial and residential buildings contributes to approximately 30% of
the total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the United States (USEPA, 2024).
Decarbonizing the country’s buildings is a necessity for meeting; 1) future carbon
emission goals; 2) improve air quality; 3) offer equitable access to lower energy costs;
and 4) transition to clean, renewable energy sources. Shallow geothermal system
networks offer a clean energy solution that can harvest geothermal energy and meet the
thermal demands of buildings (Baser and McCartney, 2015). A single residential or
commercial building geothermal system can reduce energy consumption and GHG
emissions; however, despite numerous efforts the upfront costs are still high (Neves et
al., 2020; Farzanehkhameneh et al., 2020). Community-scale geothermal systems can
address these challenges and lead to faster transition to clean energy. Meng et al. (2019)
presented a case study using a numerical model to assess the thermal impact and long-
term sustainability of a neighborhood-scale geothermal system. The results showed
limited thermal anomalies over time and confirmed the economic and environmental
viability of the project. Meng et al. (2019) suggested a site-specific analysis to evaluate
the long-term sustainability of the project. Zeh et al. (2021) proposed that using large-
scale geothermal systems shift heating and cooling loads seasonally, leading to flexibility
in the heating network. Garcia-Gil et al. (2020) suggested that the transition from fossil
fuel-based heating and cooling systems cannot be achieved by technological
improvement alone; instead, it requires the implementation of scientifically informed
policies to effectively exploit the underground resources. Therefore, they presented an
adaptive management framework structure and a governance model and provided a
roadmap for policymakers.

BACKGROUND
The project aims to deploy a shared community geothermal network in the West
Woodlawn, a neighborhood on the South Side of Chicago, and the broader “Sustainable
Square Mile”. The neighborhood area, covering 12 city blocks, includes over 100
structures of 4 building types, with a mix of residential and commercial uses. The vision
of this project is to establish a scalable community geothermal system the supports
residents in building a local green economy to satisfy the neighborhood needs to improve
their health and wealth. The shared geothermal network will be deployed in Chicago’s
alley ways and public rights-of-way. The boreholes and underground pipes will connect
to geothermal heat pumps at each building. The existing heating, ventilation, and air
conditioning system would be used to heat and cool the spaces. A foundational study was
conducted to collect baseline household and community data, including energy and water
use, bill data, building envelope and safety issues, as well as information on barriers to
adoption of clean energy technologies. This information is used to estimate the thermal
loads for the buildings in the neighborhood. For the final build-out, offering a modular
development approach will allow the residents and businesses to opt-in over time.

Conclusions
This geothermal project for the West Woodlawn neighborhood has advanced the building
decarbonization efforts in dense and urban environments in temperate climates. The
Technical Assessment provides a detailed description of the analytical approach used for
site evaluation, system design, sensitivities, constructability, and technologies for the
community geothermal system. This study holds significant importance for several
reasons: 1) this is one of the first research-integrated systems designed using advanced
numerical tools; 2) design charts were developed for a community-scale geothermal
system for the first time; and 3) real-time monitoring and data collection are integrated in
the design efforts. Future phases of the project would include simulating the subsurface
heat transfer and water flow processes. The design charts being developed will provide
a straightforward tool that could be used in future designs elsewhere. Furthermore, for
larger district- or community-scale projects, or installations in geologically complex
regions, the added accuracy of advanced numerical modeling can significantly improve
system design and long-term performance.
N.B: The full Technical, Economic, and Environmental Assessment is in the Geothermal
Data Repository (GDR)
