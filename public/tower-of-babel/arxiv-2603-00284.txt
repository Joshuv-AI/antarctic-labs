Abstract
Résumé One considers polynomial optimization problems with compact feasible set 𝛀 \mathbf{\Omega} defined by SOS-concave polynomials g j g_{j} , and with a globally non-convex polynomial objective f f . We show that if f f is strongly convex on 𝛀 \mathbf{\Omega} , or SOS-convex on 𝛀 \mathbf{\Omega} when the constraints g j g_{j} are at most quadratic, then the Moment-SOS hierarchy converges in finitely many steps, without à priori knowledge of this hidden (local) convexity. In addition, in the latter case, the exact order for which the relaxation is exact is provided by the degree of a Putinar-like certificate of convexity. This demonstrates that a general-purpose hierarchy can adapt to favorable hidden properties of a specific instance without being informed of them, yielding certified global minimizers.



keywords
Polynomial optimization; Moment-SOS hierarchy; Semidefinite relaxations; Hidden convexity.

Introduction

Given multivariate polynomials
f,g_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
,
j=1,\ldots,m
,
consider the polynomial optimization problem (POP)

\displaystyle f^{*}\,=\,\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Omega}}\,f(\mathbf{x}),

(1.1)

where the feasible set
\mathbf{\Omega}=\{\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:g_{j}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\,,\>j=1,\ldots,m\,\}
is compact and convex, but
f
is
not
convex.
If
f
is convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, Problem (
1.1
) is convex but not a
“convex program“
as the latter
definition usually assumes that
f
and the
-g_{j}
’s are convex
on the whole space

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This distinction is not a subtlety.
Indeed, suppose that the
g_{j}
’s are concave polynomials and that the Slater’s condition holds. Then
the KKT-optimality conditions

\displaystyle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{*})-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{*}_{j}\nabla g_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\,=\,0,\>\lambda^{*}_{j}\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0,\>j=1,\dots,m\,,\>{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}\geq 0,

(1.2)

at local minimizer
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
(for some
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}\geq 0
) have a quite different interpretation depending on whether
f
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

or on
\mathbf{\Omega}
only. In the former case,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})-f^{*}\,:=\,f(\mathbf{x})-f^{*}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{*}_{j}\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\geq\,0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.3)

that is,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is also a global minimizer of the
Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})
on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, because
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})
is convex and

\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})=0
.
In the latter case,
while convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
,
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})

is not necessarily convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(typically when
\mathrm{deg}(f)>\max_{j}\{\mathrm{deg}(g_{j})\}
).
Consider, for instance,
f^{*}=\min\{\,x^{3}:\>x(1-x)\geq 0\}

with
(x^{*},\lambda^{*})=(0,0)
.

For algorithms which converge to a local
minimizer
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
that satisfies (
1.2
), global optimality of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is guaranteed if

f
is convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
. But if this (local) convexity on
\mathbf{\Omega}
is hidden, one is left with an à priori local minimizer
only. Recall that checking whether a given polynomial is convex is NP-hard
[
1
]
.

The Moment-SOS hierarchy

Let
g_{0}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto 1
be a constant polynomial. Recall that the degree-
n
semidefinite relaxation of moment-SOS hierarchy to solve the POP (
1.1
), reads :

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\min_{{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}}\,\{\,\phi(f):\,\phi(1)=1,\>\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0,\>j=0,\ldots,m\},

(1.4)

where
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=(\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{2n}}
,
\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
is the degree-
(n-d_{j})
localizing matrix associated with
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
and the polynomial
g_{j}
, and
d_{j}=\lceil\mathrm{deg}(g_{j})/2\rceil
,
j=0,\ldots,m
,
d_{f}=\lceil\mathrm{deg}(f)/2\rceil
. Then,
\rho_{n}\leq f^{*}
for
all
n\geq\max[d_{f},\max_{j}d_{j}]
, and if the quadratic module defined by the
g_{j}
’s is Archimedean (see discussion around (
2.8
)),
\rho_{n}\uparrow f^{*}
as
n\to+\infty
.
For more details on the Moment-SOS hierarchy and its applications, the interested reader is referred to, for example,
[
9
]
.

Remark 1.1

As proved in
[
10
]
, finite convergence is a
generic
property of the Moment-SOS hierarchy
in the general case, with no convexity assumption, neither on
f
nor on the
g_{j}
’s. However, when
considering a specific instance
(f,g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
of POP, one does not know whether that instance
is regular, even if theoretically, given the class
(m,s)
of
POPs (
1.1
) with
m
inequality constraints and with
\sup[\mathrm{deg}(f),\mathrm{deg}(g_{j})]<s
,
genericity could be tested (but at a prohibitive
computational cost).

Convex and SOS-convex POPs

Convex or SOS-convex problems form a typical class for which
the Moment-SOS hierarchy has
finite
convergence.
For instance, if
f
and
-g_{j}
are SOS-convex (see (
1.5
)), finite convergence takes place at the first semidefinite relaxation of the hierarchy
[
7
]
.
If
f
is only convex, finite convergence is guaranteed, but without knowing at which step of the hierarchy convergence takes place. We remind that, while convexity is hard to detect
[
1
]
, SOS-convexity can be, in principle, checked efficiently.
Of course, when the information that
f
and
-g_{j}
are convex is known à priori,
some algorithms can be more efficient than the Moment-SOS hierarchy, but the interesting observation is
when the convexity of
f
is
hidden
.

Indeed, the Moment-SOS hierarchy blindly
recognizes
SOS-convex POPs,
i.e., without à priori knowledge of this SOS-convexity
, as it converges at the first step of the hierarchy. It is a nice feature of a general purpose method to be able to adapt its behavior by benefiting from strong properties of some specific instances, without being “informed” of such properties.

In
[
5
]
, the authors have extended some results of
[
7
]
.
They prove finite convergence of the Moment-SOS hierarchy
for convex programs when (i) Slater’s condition holds, (ii) the quadratic module associated with the
g_{j}
’s is Archimedean, and (iii)
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}^{*})\succ 0
at every minimizer
\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Also, if

\mathbf{\Omega}\subset\mathbf{B}(0,R)
, the same result holds under conditions (ii) and (iii) (but now satisfied by
the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
rather than by
f
).
Two key ingredients in
[
5
]

are a result of Scheiderer
[
11
]
and the (convex) Farkas Lemma

[
3
, Prop. 5.3.1]
.
Finally, it is shown in
[
6
]

that if both
f
and

\mathbf{\Omega}
are convex (without the
g_{j}
’s are not necessarily concave), all points that satisfy
the KKT-optimality conditions are global minimizers.

Contribution

Let us start by introducing the notion of
SOS-convexity on
\mathbf{\Omega}
. Namely, a polynomial

f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
is said to be SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
if

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.5)

for some integers
r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}

and matrix polynomials
\mathbf{L}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]^{d\times r_{j}}
,
j=0,\ldots,m
.

This definition is a natural extension of global SOS-convexity on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(introduced in

[
4
]
, see also
[
7
]
)
to local SOS-convexity on
\mathbf{\Omega}
. Notice that (
1.5
) can be viewed as a Putinar-type
algebraic certificate
that

f
is convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
.
In particular, if
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, then by
the Putinar’s matrix Positivstellensatz
[
8
, Theorem 2.19]
, (
1.5
) holds and so
f
is SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
. In principle,
checking whether (
1.5
) holds can be done efficiently by solving a semidefinite program of finite size determined by an à priori bound on the maximum degree
appearing in entries of matrix polynomials
\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
.

The scope of this work is determined by the class of POPs for which :

—

the
g_{j}
’s are all SOS-concave and so
\mathbf{\Omega}
is convex. Typical examples of such feasible sets include
the simplex, the Euclidean unit ball, convex polytopes, intersection of ellipsoids, etc.

—

f
is
not
convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but
is SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, i.e., (
1.5
) holds.

Hence, the resulting POPs define convex optimization problems (but not convex programs in the standard sense, since
f
is not convex on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

Our main results are as follows.

(i)

If
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
1

1

Recall that a polynomial
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
if and only if there exists
\mu>0
such that
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})-\mu\mathbf{I}\succeq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
.
, then the Moment-SOS hierarchy converges in finitely many steps.

(ii)

If, in addition,
\deg(g_{j})\leq 2
for all
j=1,\ldots,m
, then finite convergence also holds under the weaker assumption that
f
is SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
(i.e., (
1.5
) holds). Moreover, an explicit lower bound on the order
n
of an exact relaxation is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1+\max\left\{\max_{j=1,\dots,m+1}\{a_{j}+1\},a_{0}\right\},\,\text{with}\,a_{j}:=\max_{(k,l)\in\{1,\dots,d\}\times\{1,\dots,r_{j}\}}\deg(\mathbf{L}_{j}{(k,l)}),

(1.6)

where
\mathbf{L}_{j}{(k,l)}
denotes the polynomial entry
(k,l)
of the matrix
\mathbf{L}_{j}
from (
1.5
).

A key feature is that neither result requires
à priori
verification of the corresponding convexity assumption : one need not check that
f
is strongly convex in
(i)
, nor that (
1.5
) holds in
(ii)
.
In other words, the convexity may be
hidden
.
Nevertheless, for the standard Moment-SOS relaxations (
1.4
) (with no ad hoc modification), finite convergence still occurs.

Crucial for our proofs and of independent interest, we provide a Jensen-type inequality for
polynomials that are SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, and linear functionals
\phi
whose associated localizing matrices
\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
,
j=0,\ldots,m
, are positive semidefinite (thus an extension beyond probability measures on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, but for a restricted class of convex functions).

We emphasize once again that,
if one knows that
f
is convex on

\mathbf{\Omega}
, then running a local algorithm that converges to a KKT point
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
might be enough, because
(
1.2
) provides
\mathbf{x}^{*}
with a certificate of global optimality on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, and there is no need to run
the Moment-SOS hierarchy. But if one is unable to check beforehand whether

f
is convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
(an NP-hard problem), then the local KKT-optimality conditions are not sufficient
to ensure that
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a global minimizer.

2.1
Notation, definitions and preliminary result

Let
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be the ring of
d
-variate polynomials in the variables

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, and let
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
be the vector space of polynomials of total degree at most
n
. Let
\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]
be the cone of sum of squares (SOS) polynomials and let
\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]_{n}:=\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]\cap\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2n}
.
For
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, let

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
, and denote by
\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}=\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\leq n\}
the finite set of multi-indices of size
s(n):=\binom{d+n}{n}
. Let
\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}}

be the vector of all monomials with total degree bounded by
n
.
For a real symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
,
the notation
\mathbf{A}\succeq 0
(resp.
\mathbf{A}\succ 0
) means that
\mathbf{A}
is positive semidefinite
(resp. positive definite).
A polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{n}
is written
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x})\,=\,\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,=\,\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\rangle\,,
with vector of coefficients
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=(f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s(n)}
in the basis
\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
. A
d
-dimensional unit sphere is denoted by
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Euclidean ball centered at origin and having radius
R>0
is denoted by
\mathbf{B}(0,R)
.

Riesz linear functional.

Given a sequence
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=(\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
(in bold), its associated Riesz functional is the linear mapping

\phi:\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]\to\mathbb{R}
(not in bold) defined by :

\displaystyle f\>(=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})\quad\mapsto\phi(f)\,=\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,=\,\langle\mathbf{f},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\rangle.

(2.1)

A sequence
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
has a
representing
measure if its associated Riesz linear functional
\phi
is a (positive) Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in which case,

\displaystyle\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,d\phi,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(2.2)

Given a sequence
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=(\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
and a polynomial
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]\ni g:\mathbf{x}\mapsto g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}g_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\,\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
, we define the new sequence

g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
via

\displaystyle(g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,:=\,\phi(\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\,g)\,=\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}g_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\,\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\,,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(2.3)

so that its associated Riesz linear functional, denoted by
g\cdot\phi
, satisfies

\displaystyle g\cdot\phi(f)\,=\,\phi(g\,f),\quad\forall f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}].

(2.4)

Moment matrix.

The
moment
matrix of order
n\in\mathbb{N}
associated with a sequence

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=(\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
(or, equivalently, with the Riesz linear functional
\phi
),
is the real symmetric matrix of size
s(n)\times s(n)
denoted by
\mathbf{M}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})
(or
\mathbf{M}_{n}(\phi)
)
with rows and columns indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}
, and whose entry

({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
corresponds to
\phi(\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})=\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
, for every
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{M}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})
depends only on moments
\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
of degree at most
2n
.
Moreover, if
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
has a representing measure
\phi
, then

\mathbf{M}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0
because
\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{M}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})\,\mathbf{f}\rangle=\int f^{2}d\phi\geq 0
, for all
f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{n}
.

Localizing matrix.

With
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
as above and
g\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
(with
g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\gamma}g_{\gamma}\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}
), the
localizing
matrix associated with
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}

and
g
is the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{n}(g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
associated with the sequence
g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
. That is,

\mathbf{M}_{n}(g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
is the real symmetric matrix
with rows and columns indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}
, and whose entry
({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is just

(g\cdot\phi)_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=\phi(\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}g(\mathbf{x}))
, that is,

\mathbf{M}_{n}(g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}})({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{\gamma}g_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\phi_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
, for every
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{n}
.
If
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
has a representing measure
\phi
whose support is contained in the set
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:g(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}
then
\mathbf{M}_{n}(g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0
for all
n
, because

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{M}_{n}(g\cdot{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\,\mathbf{f}\rangle\,=\,g\cdot\phi(f^{2})\,=\,\phi(f^{2}g)\,=\,\int f^{2}\,g\,d\phi\,\geq\,0,\quad\forall f\,\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{n}.

(2.5)

SOS-convex polynomials.

Definition 2.1

A polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
is
(globally) SOS-convex
if its Hessian
\nabla^{2}p
is a matrix-SOS.
That is, there exists a matrix polynomial
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]^{d\times r}
such that

\nabla^{2}p(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x})^{T}
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A polynomial
p
is SOS-concave if
-p
is SOS-convex.

Definition 2.2

With
\mathbf{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in (
1.1
), a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
is
SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
if its Hessian
\nabla^{2}f
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.6)

for some matrix polynomials
\mathbf{L}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]^{d\times r_{j}}
, with
r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
for
j=0,\dots,m
.

Quadratic module

Definition 2.3

Let

\mathbf{g}=(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]^{m+1}
. The set
Q(\mathbf{g})\subset\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
defined by

\displaystyle Q(\mathbf{g})\,:=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sigma_{j}\,g_{j}:\>\sigma_{j}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}],\,j=0,\dots,m\right\}.

(2.7)

is called a quadratic module generated by
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
. Its truncated version of order
n\in\mathbb{N}
, namely

Q_{n}(\mathbf{g})\subset\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{n}
, reads

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\mathbf{g})\,:=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sigma_{j}\,g_{j}:\>\sigma_{j}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}],\>\mathrm{deg}(\sigma_{j}g_{j})\,\leq n,\>j=0,\ldots,m\right\}.

(2.8)

When both
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
and the quadratic module
Q(\mathbf{g})
is Archimedean, then by Putinar’s matrix Positivstellensatz
[
8
, Theorem 2.22]
, equation (
2.6
) holds, that is,
f
is SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
. Recall that
Q(\mathbf{g})
is said to be Archimedean if there exists
R>0
such that
R^{2}-||\mathbf{x}||^{2}\in Q(\mathbf{g})
.

2.2
Main result

Since
\mathbf{\Omega}
is compact, we may and will assume that

\mathbf{\Omega}\subset\mathbf{B}(0,1)
possibly after re-scaling. Hence, we can include in the definition of

\mathbf{\Omega}
the redundant quadratic constraint
g_{m+1}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
, with
g_{m+1}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto 1-\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
(which is SOS-concave).
This ensures that the quadratic module
Q(\mathbf{g})
in (
2.7
) is Archimedean.
Therefore, throughout the paper, we assume the following :

Assumption 2.4

(i)

The set
\mathbf{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in (
1.1
) is compact, and
g_{m+1}
is one of the defining constraints of
\mathbf{\Omega}
;

(ii)

For every
j=1,\ldots,m+1
, the constraint
g_{j}
is SOS-concave (hence
\mathbf{\Omega}
is convex) ;

(iii)

Objective function
f
not convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but satisfies (
1.5
), i.e., it is SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
.

A Jensen inequality for linear functionals

We may now state the main result underpinning our analysis of finite convergence of the Moment–SOS hierarchy under Assumption

2.4
.

Lemma 2.5

Let Assumption
2.4
hold,
and let
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{2n}}
be such that

\displaystyle\phi(1)=1\,\text{and}\,\>\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\,\succeq 0,\quad j=0,\ldots,m+1.

(2.9)

(i)

If
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, and if
n
is large enough, then

\displaystyle\phi(f)\,\geq\,f(\phi(\mathbf{x})),\quad\text{where}\quad\phi(\mathbf{x}):=(\phi(x_{1}),\ldots,\phi(x_{d}))\in\mathbf{\Omega}

(2.10)

(ii)

If
\mathrm{deg}(g_{j})\leq 2
for all
j=1,\ldots,m+1
, then
(
2.10
) holds as soon as
\displaystyle 2n\geq 2+\max_{j=1,\dots,m+1}\{2a_{j}+2\}
and
\displaystyle 2n\geq\max_{j=0,\dots,m+1}\{2a_{j}+2\}
, or equivalently,
\displaystyle n\geq 1+\max\left\{\max_{j=1,\dots,m+1}\{a_{j}+1\},a_{0}\right\}
.

Proof 2.1
.

(i)

Observe that
\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0\Rightarrow\phi(g_{j})\geq 0
, and
since
g_{j}
is SOS-concave, by
[
8
, Theorem 5.13]
,
0\leq\phi(g_{j})\leq g_{j}(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
, which turn implies that

\phi(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
.

Next, let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
be fixed, arbitrary. Then, as
\phi
is linear and satisfies
\phi(1)=1
, we get
\phi(f-f(\mathbf{z}))=\phi(f)-f(\mathbf{z})
. We will prove that

\displaystyle\phi(f-f(\mathbf{z}))\,\geq\,\phi(\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rangle)\>=\,\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{z}),\phi(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{z})\rangle,

(2.11)

and therefore substituting
\mathbf{z}
with
\phi(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
, one obtains the desired result (
2.10
).

Observe that

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{z})=\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rangle+\Bigg\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}),\left(\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\,\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{z}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))\,dt\right)\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\Bigg\rangle.

(2.12)

As the maximum degree (in
t
) of the entries of the integrand

(1-t)\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{z}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))
is
2d_{f}-1
, the entire integral from (

) may be computed using a cubature formula for Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, supported on
s

t_{1},\ldots t_{s}\in[0,1]
, with weights
0<{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. The formula being exact for polynomials
of degree
2d_{f}-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{z})=\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}\Bigg\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}),\left((1-t_{k})\,\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))\right)\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\Bigg\rangle.

(2.13)

Next for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
and each
k=1,\ldots,s
,
\mathbf{z}+t_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
, and so

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))^{\top}g_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))

(2.14)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]^{d\times d}.

(2.15)

Observe that
each entry of
\mathbf{H}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
is a polynomial in
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
of degree at most
\max_{j}[2a_{j}+2d_{j}]
.
Moreover, since, by assumption,
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{H}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\mathbf{u}\rangle\,>\,0\,,\quad\forall\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\>\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{\Omega}.

(2.16)

Moreover, as the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}))
is a continuous function of its entries,
it is also a continuous function of
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
,
and as
\mathbf{\Omega}
is compact, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\mathbf{H}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\succeq\,\delta\mathbf{I}

for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
.
Therefore
by Putinar’s matrix Positivstellensatz
[
8
, Theorem 2.22]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\mathbf{V}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\mathbf{V}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})^{\top}g_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\mathbf{W}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\mathbf{W}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})^{\top}g_{j}(\mathbf{z}),

(2.17)

for some matrix polynomials
\mathbf{V}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{W}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]^{d\times r_{kj}}
with
r_{kj}\in\mathbb{N}
,
and whose
entries themselves are polynomials of degree at most
\delta_{kj}
. Introduce

\displaystyle S_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(1-t_{k})\,\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{V}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\mathbf{V}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\rangle\,,

(2.18)

\displaystyle T_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(1-t_{k})\,\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{W}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\mathbf{W}_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\rangle.

(2.19)

Then, we can write

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{z})=\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rangle+\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}S_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right)\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k\,}T_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right)\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z}).

(2.20)

Observe that
S_{kj},T_{kj}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]
with degree bounded by
2\ell^{*}:=2+\max_{j,k}\delta_{kj}
, and that the same is true for

\sigma_{j}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}S_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
and
\psi_{j}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}T_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
, i.e.,
\sigma_{j},\psi_{j}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]_{\ell^{*}}

So let
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
be as in (
2.9
), with
2(n-d_{j})\geq 2\ell^{*}
, and let

\mathbf{z}:=\phi(\mathbf{x})
.
Recall that
\phi(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
and so
g_{j}(\phi(\mathbf{x}))\geq 0
for
j=0,\dots,m+1
.
Next, substituting
\mathbf{z}:=\phi(\mathbf{x})
(a constant vector) into (
2.20
) yields,

\displaystyle\phi(f)-f(\phi(\mathbf{x}))\,=\,\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{z}),\phi(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle+\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\phi\left(\sigma_{j}\,g_{j}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\phi(\psi_{j})\,g_{j}(\phi(\mathbf{x})).

(2.21)

Since for all
j=0,\dots,m+1
,
\sigma_{j},\psi_{j}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]_{\ell^{*}}
and since
\mathrm{deg}(\sigma_{j}\,g_{j})\leq 2n
, we have
\phi(\sigma_{j}\,g_{j})\geq 0
and
\phi(\psi_{j})\geq 0
. Therefore, we obtain
\phi(f)\geq f(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
, which is the desired result.

(ii)

If
g_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{2}
and
g_{j}
is concave, then for every
k=1,\dots,s
,

\displaystyle 0\,\leq\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))-(\,t_{k}\,(g_{j}(\mathbf{x}))+(1-t_{k})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z}))\,=:\,q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]_{1},

(2.22)

because all nonnegative quadratic polynomials are SOS. Therefore, in (
2.15
), we can replace

g_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))
with
t_{k}\,(g_{j}(\mathbf{x}))+(1-t_{k})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z})+q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
,
and let

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))\mathbf{L}_{j}(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))^{\top}\,=:\,\Delta_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,\in\,\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]^{d\times d}.

(2.23)

We then obtain

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\Delta_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\left(t_{k\,}g_{j}(\mathbf{x})+(1-t_{k})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z})+q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right),

(2.24)

for every
k=1,\ldots,s
.
Hence, letting
\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}):=\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}),\Delta_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\rangle
, we observe that

\sigma_{kj}
is SOS with

\mathrm{deg}(\sigma_{kj})\leq 2(a_{j}+1)
. Next,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}),\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{z}+t_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}))\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\rangle\,&=t_{k}\,\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x})\\
&+(1-t_{k})\,\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z})+\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}),\end{split}

(2.25)

for every
k=1,\ldots,s
.

Let

\displaystyle 2n-2\geq\max_{j=1,\dots,m+1}\{2a_{j}+2\}
and
\displaystyle 2n\geq\max_{j=0,\dots,m+1}\{2a_{j}+2\}
. Then
with
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{\Omega}
fixed, and as
\mathbf{M}_{n-1}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0
(recall that
d_{j}=1
for quadratic
g_{j}
),

\phi(\theta\,g_{j})\geq 0
for all
\theta\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]_{n-1}
.
Therefore, for every
k=1,\ldots,s
,

\displaystyle\phi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,=\,\phi(\sigma_{k0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}))+\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}\phi(\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x}))\,\geq\,0.

(2.26)

Similarly, as
g_{j}(\mathbf{z})\geq 0
is a constant, and
\mathbf{M}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z})\right)\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\phi(\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}))\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z})\,\geq\,0.

(2.27)

Finally, as
q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]_{1}
, we also have

\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]_{a_{j}+2}
. Hence,
recalling that, by assumption,
\mathbf{M}_{n}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0
, and since

, we conclude that

\displaystyle\phi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right)\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}\phi(\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}))\,\geq\,0.

(2.28)

As the above is valid for all

, using
\mathbf{z}:=\phi(\mathbf{x})
, one obtains the desired result

\displaystyle\phi(f)-f(\phi(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}t_{k}\,\underbrace{\phi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{\geq 0}+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}(1-t_{k})\,\underbrace{\phi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,g_{j}(\mathbf{z})\right)}_{\geq 0}

(2.30)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\gamma_{k}\underbrace{\phi\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\sigma_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,q_{kj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\right)}_{\geq 0}\,\geq\,0.

2.3
Finite convergence of the Moment-SOS hierarchy

Recall that, under Assumption
2.4
,
the quadratic module (
2.7
) associated with
\mathbf{g}
is Archimedean, and that the
n
-th moment semidefinite relaxation of POP (
1.1
) has the following form :

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\,\{\,\phi(f):\>\phi(1)=1,\>\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\,\succeq 0,\,j=0,\ldots,m+1\}.

(2.31)

Theorem 6
.

Let Assumption
2.4
hold.
Then, for every
\displaystyle n\geq n_{\min}:=\max\left\{\max_{j=0,\ldots,m+1}d_{j},d_{f}\right\}
, the semidefinite relaxation (
2.31
) has an optimal solution
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(n)}
. In addition :

(i)

If
f
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, then there exists
n\geq n_{\min}
such that
\rho_{n}=f^{*}=f(\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))
.

(ii)

If
\mathrm{deg}(g_{j})\leq 2
for every
j=1,\ldots,m+1
, then, as soon as
\displaystyle n\geq 1+\max\left\{\max_{j=1,\dots,m+1}\{a_{j}+1\},a_{0}\right\}
, we have
\rho_{n}=f^{*}=f(\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))
, i.e., the point
\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
is globally optimal in (
1.1
).

Proof 2.2
.

(i)

Recall that
g_{m+1}(\mathbf{x})=1-\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
and that
\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{m+1}}(g_{m+1}\,\phi)\succeq 0
. This implies that
\phi(x_{i}^{2n})\leq 1
for every
i=1,\ldots,d
. Hence, by
[
8
, Proposition 2.38]
,

\displaystyle|\phi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\,\leq\,\max\{1,\max_{i=1,\dots,d}\phi(x_{i}^{2n})\}\,\leq\,1,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{2n}.

(2.32)

Therefore, the feasible set of (
2.31
) is compact, implying that (
2.31
) has an optimal solution
\phi^{(n)}
.
By construction,
\rho_{n}\leq f^{*}
for all
n\geq n_{\min}
.

Next let
\phi^{(n)}
be an optimal solution, hence
satisfying
\phi^{(n)}(f)=\rho_{n}\leq f^{*}
.
Observe that
\mathbf{M}_{n-d_{j}}(g_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\succeq 0\Rightarrow\phi^{(n)}(g_{j})\geq 0
, and since
g_{j}
is SOS-concave,

0\leq\phi^{(n)}(g_{j})\Rightarrow g_{j}(\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\geq 0
. Therefore

\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
, which in turn implies
f(\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\geq f^{*}
.

Finally, for
n
large enough, Lemma
2.5
(i)
guarantees that

\displaystyle f^{*}\,\leq\,f(\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\,\leq\phi^{(n)}(f)\,=\,\rho_{n}\leq\,f^{*},

(2.33)

which yields the desired result.

(ii)

As in the proof of
(i)
, but one now invokes Lemma
2.5
(ii)
.

When solving (
2.31
) at step
n\geq n_{\min}
of the Moment-SOS hierarchy,
it is always recommended to check whether

f(\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\leq\rho_{n}
at an optimal solution
\phi^{(n)}
. Indeed
if the
g_{j}
’s are SOS-concave then

\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\phi^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{\Omega}
is a global minimizer as
f(\mathbf{x}^{*})\leq\rho_{n}\leq f^{*}
.
Theorem
6
states that under
the hidden convexity of Assumption
2.4
, it is guaranteed to happen at some
n\geq n_{\min}
.

Finally, we give a toy example that illustrates the main notions introduced above, as well as the properties of the associated hierarchy of semidefinite relaxationsS.

Example 1

(Illustration when
d=2
)

Consider the following optimization problem

\displaystyle f^{*}\,=\,\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Omega}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,\underbrace{1-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}_{:=g_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\geq 0\}}\,f(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{6}x_{1}^{3}+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2}.

(2.34)

Observe that the quadratic constraint
g_{1}
is SOS-concave, therefore,
\mathbf{\Omega}
is convex, but also compact. In addition,
Q(\mathbf{g})
is Archimedean by construction. A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}+1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(2.35)

The function
f
is clearly not convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Next, define the SOS polynomials
\sigma_{0}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}+1)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]_{1}
and
\sigma_{1}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]_{0}.

Then the following identity holds
exactly
:

\displaystyle x_{1}+1=\sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})+g_{1}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\,\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(2.36)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\,\underbrace{g_{0}(\mathbf{x})}_{:=1}\;+\;\mathbf{S}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,g_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{x})&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}g_{1}(\mathbf{x})

(2.38)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{0}\mathbf{L}_{0}^{\top}+\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{L}_{1}^{\top}g_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{L}_{0}(\mathbf{x}):=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1+x_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{x_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}&0\\[2.84526pt]
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\,\mathbf{L}_{1}(\mathbf{x}):=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\[2.84526pt]
0\end{bmatrix},

which certifies that
f
is SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
.
Finally, since
\deg(g_{1})=2
,
a_{0}=1
, and

, we deduce that the moment semidefinite relaxation for the problem (
2.34
) will be exact as soon as

Indeed, we have,

-\frac{2}{3}=\rho_{2}=f^{\star}=f(\phi^{(2)}(\mathbf{x}))

3
Conclusion

We have shown that for a class of POPs with some hidden convexity,
the Moment-SOS hierarchy has finite convergence with a guarantee to provide a global minimizer.
Importantly, while the feasible set
\mathbf{\Omega}
is identified as convex (because its
defining polynomials are SOS-concave), the objective
f
is not assumed to be convex on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
but SOS-convex on
\mathbf{\Omega}
, without the knowledge of this SOS-convexity being explicit. As this hidden convexity is hard to check,
any minimizer on
\mathbf{\Omega}
(even global) provided by a (local) algorithm
is lacking a guarantee to be a global minimizer.
Our results complement existing work on convex POPs and highlight a remarkable aspect of the Moment–SOS hierarchy : it can blindly adapt to convex structure in the instance at hand, even when this structure is neither known nor identified in advance. Future work could investigate whether this phenomenon persists for structured variants of the Moment-SOS hierarchy designed to exploit structural properties of considered problems, such as symmetries or different sparsity types.
