Comparison of penetration depth into dentinal tubules and push-out bond strength of zinc oxide and copper doped zinc oxide nanoparticles sealers : an in-vitro study
Authors: Narges Farhad Mollashahi, Eshaghali Saberi, Amin Motameni Tabatabaei, Sina Radfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe present study aimed to evaluate dentinal tubule penetration depth and push-out bond strength of root canal sealers based on conventional zinc oxide - eugenol (ZOE), Nano ZOE, and Cu-doped Nano ZOE.MethodsThis in-vitro study was conducted on 90 extracted human single-rooted teeth. All samples were prepared using the crown-down technique and irrigated with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite and 17% EDTA. Root canal obturation was performed using the single-cone technique with three sealers: ZOE, Nano ZOE, and Cu-doped Nano ZOE. The specimens were randomly allocated into two equal experimental sets (n = 45 each). The first set was used for evaluation of dentinal tubule penetration depth using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM), while the second set was used for assessment of push-out bond strength. In each set, samples were equally divided into three groups (n = 15 per group) based on the type of sealer. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, LSD post hoc test, and independent t-test.ResultsThe nano zinc oxide sealer showed the highest tubular penetration depth, while the conventional zinc oxide sealer showed the lowest. Significant differences were observed among the evaluated sealers in tubular penetration depth at the coronal (P < 0.001) and apical (P < 0.001) thirds. In addition, penetration depth decreased significantly from the coronal to the apical thirds in all evaluated sealers. Regarding push-out bond strength, the Cu-doped zinc oxide group was significantly higher than the nano zinc oxide group, and the latter group was significantly higher than the conventional zinc oxide group (P = 0.012).ConclusionNano zinc oxide sealer demonstrated superior dentinal tubule penetration, while Cu-doped zinc oxide exhibited the highest push-out bond strength, highlighting the potential influence of nanoparticle modification on the performance of zinc oxide–based endodontic sealers.

Introduction

Root canal sealers are necessary to seal the space between the dentinal wall and the obturating interface. Sealers also fill irregularities in the main, lateral and accessory canals, and spaces between gutta percha points used in lateral compaction [1]. Nowadays, various endodontic sealers are available that are divided into different groups based on their chemical structures. On the other hand, zinc oxide–eugenol (ZOE)-based sealers have been used for several decades, considering their desirable physical and chemical characteristics [2]. Previous studies have focused on improving the physicochemical and biological properties of endodontic sealers by incorporating bioactive additives and reducing particle size, which can enhance flow, antimicrobial activity, and penetration into dentinal tubules [3–6]. In the meantime, nanotechnology has revolutionized all fields of science, including dentistry. This technology includes preparing materials and functional structures in size range of 1–100 nm (nanoparticles) that are used for diagnosing and treating several diseases [7, 8].

Compared to macroscopic materials, nanoparticles have improved properties due to their increased surface area and quantum effects [9, 10]. Nanotechnology has been applied in the quality improvement of cements used as sealers in endodontics [11]. Moreover, the addition of zinc oxide or chitosan nanoparticles to the zinc oxide sealers’ composition can prevent the biofilm formation at the dentin-sealer interface, decrease cytotoxicity, and improve the sealing effectiveness and antibacterial properties of sealers based on zinc oxide [12–14]. ElKateb et al. reported that the addition of nanoparticles to sealers could improve their flow depending on the nanoparticle size. Consequently, the sealers containing nanoparticles showed better penetration into dentinal tubules [15].

Recently, some researchers have tried to change the structure of zinc oxide nanoparticles by doping using elements like Fe, Mn, Co, Ni, and Cu [16]. Doping has been extensively used for nanoparticle alterations to achieve increased optical, electrical, and biological activities [17] (Cu) Copper (Cu), a transition metal, is widely used as a dopant to tailor the physicochemical properties of zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles. Evidence suggests that Cu doping can significantly enhance electrical conductivity, magnetic and biological properties, gas-sensing performance, optical behavior, mechanical strength, and photocatalytic activity of ZnO nanoparticles [9, 18, 19].

Cu-doped ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated antibacterial, antifungal and antioxidant potential [19]. It exhibits enhanced cytotoxicity and anticancer activity compared to ZOE Nanoparticles [20]. This research explores a novel and cost-effective type of ZOE based sealer, which is referred to as Cu-doped Nano ZOE. Therefore, the aim of present study was to perform a comprehensive comparison of the dentinal tubule penetration depth and push-out bond strength of conventional zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE), nano-ZOE, and Cu-doped nano-ZOE sealers. The findings of this study may contribute to improving the properties of endodontic sealers and enhancing the quality of root canal treatment. The null hypothesis was that there would be no statistically significant differences among the tested sealers in terms of dentinal tubule penetration depth and push-out bond strength.

Materials and methods

The protocol of this in vitro study was approved by the research and ethics committee of Zahedan University of Medical Sciences (IR.ZAUMS.REC.1400.413). Ninety human-extracted single-rooted mandibular premolars were selected and collected for the present study. Based on the classification of Vertucci, all teeth were type I, and their root canal morphology was confirmed using mesiodistal and buccolingual radiographs. All the teeth were extracted due to periodontal disease or orthodontic. They had fully developed roots, without any severe curvature, caries, internal or external resorption, fractures or cracks and calcification. Teeth with crown fracture, caries, enamel cracks in the collar area, root resorption, short roots, open apex, restorations, and previous endo treatment were excluded from the present study.

The sample size was estimated based on previous similar in vitro studies published between 2000 and 2020, which commonly used 10 specimens per group to detect differences in bond strength and dentinal tubule penetration [21–24]. To enhance statistical reliability and reduce type II error, the sample size was increased to 15 specimens per group in the present study [25]. Although a formal power analysis was not performed, the selected sample size was considered sufficient to detect meaningful differences based on previous evidence. The specimens were randomly allocated into the experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence (Random Allocation Software), ensuring equal distribution of samples among the groups.

Sample preparation

The teeth underwent mechanical cleaning from dental plaques using a curette. Afterward, they were put in 0.5% chloramine to get disinfected and were then kept in 4 ° C normal saline. Before the root canal preparation, the crown was cut at the Cement-Enamel Junction (CEJ) using a diamond disc, and the working length was standardized to 12 ± 1 mm using a 10# K-file (Dentsply, Maillefer, Switzerland). The measurement was performed when the file’s tip became visible from the apical foramen. Then, the working length was considered 1 mm shorter.

All teeth were prepared using the crown-down technique, 10# and 15# K-files (Dentsply, Maillefer, Switzerland) as hand instruments, and ProTaper rotary instruments (Dentsply, Maillefer, Switzerland). Moreover, the final preparation was performed using an F2 file (ProTaper Universal, Dentsply, Maillefer, Switzerland). F2 was selected as the master apical file to standardize apical preparation and preserve the original canal anatomy while avoiding excessive dentin removal and over-enlargement. It should be noted that the teeth with canals wider than an F2 file were excluded from the study. Apical diameter was confirmed up to size F2 using a master apical file to ensure consistency among all specimens. Canal taper uniformity was maintained by following the ProTaper Universal instrumentation protocol for all samples.

After using each file, the root canal was irrigated with 5 ml of 5.25% NaOCl solution (MORVABON Trading & Manufacturing Co., Iran). Moreover, irrigation was performed with 3 ml of 17% EDTA solution (MORVABON Trading & Manufacturing Co. Iran) after the preparation to eliminate the smear layer. Finally, the root canals were rinsed with 10 ml of normal saline and dried using F2 paper points (Meta Biomed Inc. South Korea). The teeth were then randomly divided into 6 groups of 15.

Sealer preparation and doping procedure

The Cu-doped ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized using a chemical doping method in which Cu²⁺ ions were incorporated into the ZnO crystal lattice under controlled synthesis conditions. The doping process ensured uniform distribution of copper ions within the ZnO structure. Nano-ZnO particles had an average size of 1–100 nm, as confirmed by FE-SEM analysis. The final sealer formulations were prepared by mixing ZnO-based powder with eugenol-based liquid at a powder-to-liquid ratio of 3:1 (w/w), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Study groups

Three of 6 groups were used for assessing the tubular penetration depth. These groups were named G1, G2, and G3 and were filled with conventional zinc oxide, nano zinc oxide, and Cu-doped zinc oxide sealers, respectively. Moreover, the other 3 of 6 groups were used for assessing the push-out bond strength. Also, one tooth was completely covered with wax (1 mm from the apical side) and was considered as the negative control. Additionally, a positive control group (standard commercially available zinc oxide eugenol sealer without nanoparticles) was included for comparison purposes.

Based on the manufacturer’s instructions, the sealers were prepared before root canal filling and were inserted into the root canals using a lentulo. Moreover, root canal filling was performed using the single-cone technique with F2 gutta-percha (Meta Biomed Inc., Republic of Korea). Then, the excessive gutta-percha was cut from the CEJ site using a plugger, and condensing was performed. The walls were then cleaned of excess sealer using cotton dipped in 95% ethanol to prevent disturbing the composite polymerization. For tooth restoration, the etching process was performed using 37% phosphoric acid (MORVABON Trading & Manufacturing, Iran) for 20 s, followed by washing for 30 s. Then, the bonding agent (FGM Dental Products, Brazil) was performed, followed by light curing for 40 s. Finally, the composite (FGM Dental Products, Brazil) was placed, followed by another light curing for 40 s. All samples were initially placed in a wet sponge to simulate the periapical environment and maintained for 3 days to ensure complete setting of the sealers. Subsequently, all specimens were stored in normal saline at 4 °C under 100% humidity conditions throughout the experimental period to prevent dehydration and preserve structural integrity. All measurements were independently performed by two blinded observers who were unaware of the group allocation to minimize observer bias.

For assessing the tubular penetration depth, 46 teeth underwent longitudinal cuts in the buccolingual orientation (Fig. 4a) using a diamond disc (diameter: 21 mm, thickness: 0.3 mm, D$Z, Germany) and water and air as cooling agents. Afterward, the samples were dehydrated by soaking in ethanol and mounted on aluminum bases, and the cut sections underwent Au-coating with argon (JEOL JFC-1200 Fine Coater, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 4b). Afterward, the sections were evaluated under the SEM (JEOL JSM-5510, Tokyo) at a 250x-1000x magnification, and the deepest point of penetration from the canal wall to the point of maximum sealer penetration [26] was measured and recorded in µm by two observers (Figs. 6, 7, and 8). In each specimen, sections corresponding to each root third (coronal, middle, and apical) were evaluated, and penetration depth measurements were performed in these regions. For each tooth, the deepest point of sealer penetration from the canal wall to the maximum extent of penetration into the dentinal tubules was measured and recorded in micrometers (µm). This parameter was selected to evaluate the maximum penetration ability of the sealer within the dentinal structure.

For assessing the push-out bond strength, 45 teeth were cut off horizontally at the middle part of the canal with a thickness of 1 ± 0.1 mm using a CNC device (RADOX Industrial Group, Pezhwak Radin Sanat Danesh) under water spray (Fig. 4-c) [24–30], while the Universal appliance (Santam, stm20 model, Iran) (Fig. 4-d) with a capacity of 50 kg and an accuracy of 10 g was used for measurement [27]. The selected plunger diameter was slightly smaller than the canal diameter in the specimens to prevent direct contact with the dentinal walls and to ensure uniform stress distribution across the sealer surface. All specimens were prepared exclusively from the middle third of the root in order to eliminate the influence of anatomical variations in the coronal and apical regions and to standardize the experimental conditions among samples. Using a cylindrical plunger with a diameter of 1.1 mm that moved in the apical orientation parallel to the longitudinal tooth axis at a speed of 5 mm/min, a force was applied to the obturation material until displacement (Fig. 4-e). Then, the maximum force applied before displacement was recorded in Newton using the Universal appliance. Finally, the push-out bond strength (MPa) was calculated by dividing the obtained force (N) by the bond surface using the following formula:

Data analysis and description

After data collection, statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 22. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To compare dentinal tubule penetration depth and push-out bond strength among the three study groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. In cases where overall significant differences were observed, pairwise comparisons were performed using the LSD post hoc test. To compare differences between root thirds (coronal, middle, and apical) within each sealer group, the independent t-test was used. To improve the accuracy of the analysis and reduce the risk of Type I error, the results of post hoc tests were reported and interpreted alongside the overall pattern of intergroup differences. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Root canal filling is a critical factor determining the success of endodontic treatment, directly affecting the related results [28]. Thus, it is essential to use materials able to create an impermeable seal for this treatment. Studies investigating the tubular penetration depth, microleakage, and sealing effectiveness of these materials play an important role in their development. The sealer’s penetration into the dentin tubules is beneficial because it makes a mechanical bond between the sealer and root canal dentin. Moreover, tubular penetration is affected by the dentin tubules’ dimensions and the sealer’s particle size [29]. Thus, the smaller particles have deeper penetration, probably decreasing the chance of microleakage [30, 31]. This finding suggests that reducing particle size through nanotechnology may enhance the adaptability of sealers to the dentinal structure, thereby improving the sealing ability and potentially increasing the long-term success rate of root canal treatment.

The present study used nanotechnology for making nano zinc oxide and Cu-doped zinc oxide sealers. The use of nanoparticles in endodontic sealers has several benefits, including improved physical and chemical characteristics, increased antibacterial properties, reduced microleakage, and enhanced biocompatibility, which have been addressed by various studies [14, 32, 33]. Furthermore, the modification of ZnO with copper ions may contribute to changes in surface energy and chemical reactivity of the material, which could further influence its interaction with dentinal walls and improve adhesion.

According to the tubular penetration depth assessments using SEM in the present study, the nano zinc oxide sealer at the coronal one-third had the highest tubular penetration depth (103.4 ± 16.67), while the conventional zinc oxide sealer at the apical one-third had the lowest tubular penetration depth (56.52 ± 12.81). Moreover, a study by Zand et al. reported that nano calcium hydroxide sealer had a higher tubular penetration compared to conventional calcium hydroxide sealer [34]. Also, a study by Elkateb et al. investigated the tubular penetration depth between 4 types of sealers, reporting significantly higher penetration depth in sealers containing nanoparticles (silver and zinc oxide) compared to sealers without nanoparticles and the control group [15].

In addition to the particle size, the sealer’s penetration depth is affected by the tubular dimensions. According to previous studies, the penetration depth varies in different parts of the root canal: cervical, middle, or apical. Vasiliadis et al. reported the highest tubular penetration depth in the coronal part of the middle third of the root canal [35]. Moreover, the present study reported a higher mean tubular penetration depth in the coronal one-third compared to the middle one-third and in the latter compared to the apical part. It has been shown that the coronal and middle parts have more dentinal tubules with larger sizes compared to the apical region, which improves the sealer’s penetration into the tubules [29, 36]. Also, the sclerotic dentin of the apical region is another cause of the lower tubular penetration in this area [37].

On the other hand, the tubular penetration ability of sealers is also affected by their physicochemical properties [38, 39]. Kokkas et al. reported that the tubular penetration depth was higher in resin-based sealers, such as AH-Plus, compared to zinc oxide-based sealers [40], which can be explained by the higher flow and lower film thickness of resin-based sealers [41]. However, other studies did not report a significant difference in penetration depth by smear layer removal [42–44]. Also, a study by Evans et al. reported that using gutta-percha and sealer without removing the smear layer led to improved seal [45].

In the present study, we removed the smear layer and obturated the canals using the single-cone technique, which may lead to incomplete obturation due to a partial mismatch of gutta-percha cones with canal walls, resulting in decreased tubular penetration depth due to decreased vertical or lateral pressure. However, using heated gutta-percha may increase the tubular penetration of root canal sealers [36]. Therefore, other root canal obturation techniques may affect the results.

Various methods have been described for evaluating the quality of dental material bonding to dentin, such as shear, compressive, tensile, flexural, and push-out bond strength assessments. Among these methods, push-out bond strength assessment, also called dislodgement resistance, has been proven efficient and reliable [46]. This test is a relevant prognostic test for evaluating the sealer’s bonding to canal walls and core materials [47].

According to the present study results, the nano ZOE sealer had the greatest penetration, whereas the Cu-doped ZOE sealer had the greatest push-out bond strength. This divergence suggests that dentinal tubule penetration and push-out bond strength represent related but fundamentally distinct material properties. While penetration is mainly governed by particle size and canal anatomy, bond strength is additionally influenced by interfacial chemistry and cohesive properties of the sealer. Therefore, Cu doping may have altered interfacial behavior, particle interaction, and internal cohesion of the sealer in ways that are not reflected by penetration measurements alone.

a study by Saghiri et al. reported a higher push-out bond strength in the nanoparticle sealer group compared to other groups [48]. This improvement in bond strength may be attributed to the incorporation of Cu ions into the ZnO lattice, which can enhance surface reactivity and increase chemical interaction with dentinal hydroxyapatite. In addition, Cu-doping may modify the surface energy and wettability of the sealer, facilitating deeper penetration into micro-irregularities of dentin and resulting in stronger mechanical interlocking at the dentin–sealer interface.

The higher push-out bond strength observed in the Cu-doped zinc oxide and nano zinc oxide groups can be attributed to the reduced particle size and increased surface-to-volume ratio, which enhances the available surface area for adhesion. However, the Cu-doped zinc oxide sealer demonstrated significantly higher bond strength compared to the nano zinc oxide sealer, which suggests that Cu doping provides additional reinforcement beyond nanoscale effects alone. This may be related to improved crystal lattice stability, enhanced interparticle cohesion, and stronger chemical interaction at the dentin–sealer interface.

In the present study, novel nano zinc oxide and Cu-doped zinc oxide sealers were evaluated; however, available evidence on these materials remains limited. Moreover, comparisons of push-out bond strength with previous studies should be interpreted with caution, as results may be influenced by differences in experimental protocols, including canal taper preparation, obturation technique, and plunger dimensions. Therefore, direct comparison across studies may not be fully reliable.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vitro design of the study does not fully reproduce the complex biological and clinical conditions of the oral environment. Second, only a single obturation technique (single-cone technique) was used, which may have influenced sealer penetration and bonding behavior. Third, no comparison was made with contemporary resin-based or bioceramic sealers, which limits the clinical relevance and generalizability of the findings. In addition, the study did not include long-term aging or thermomechanical loading, both of which are important factors affecting the durability of endodontic materials under clinical conditions. Furthermore, key physicochemical properties such as flow, film thickness, solubility, and setting time were not evaluated, limiting a more comprehensive understanding of material behavior. Biological properties, including cytocompatibility and antimicrobial activity, were also not assessed, which are essential for determining the clinical safety and performance of these materials. Finally, although the findings suggest improved dentinal tubule penetration and bond strength, they should be considered preliminary. Clinical translation of these results should be made with caution, and further in vivo and long-term studies are required to confirm their clinical applicability.

Conclusion

Considering the limitations of the present study, nano zinc oxide sealer showed greater dentinal tubule penetration compared to Cu-doped zinc oxide and conventional zinc oxide sealers. In contrast, Cu-doped zinc oxide sealer demonstrated higher push-out bond strength than both nano zinc oxide and conventional zinc oxide sealers under the in vitro conditions of this study. These findings indicate that nano zinc oxide was associated with greater dentinal tubule penetration, whereas Cu-doped zinc oxide exhibited higher push-out bond strength under the specific in vitro conditions of this study. These effects suggest different influences of nanoparticle modification and copper doping on sealer behavior. Further studies are required to determine whether these advantages persist after aging and thermomechanical stress and whether they translate into clinically relevant benefits.
