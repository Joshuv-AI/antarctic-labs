A reinforcement learning-enhanced fuzzy multi-objective equilibrium optimization framework for multiple sequence alignment
Authors: Hamidreza Hosseini, Najme Mansouri, Behnam Mohammad Hasani Zade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) is a fundamental task in bioinformatics, underpinning comparative genomics, structural analysis, and evolutionary inference. However, MSA remains a challenging multi-objective optimization problem due to the need to simultaneously maximize alignment accuracy, preserve conserved regions, and control gap proliferation, particularly in large and heterogeneous sequence collections. In this work, we propose MOFSACEO-MSA, a novel hybrid optimization framework for multiple sequence alignment that integrates a fuzzy multi-objective evaluation scheme with the Equilibrium Optimizer (EO) and a Soft Actor–Critic (SAC)–based adaptive control mechanism. The proposed framework formulates MSA as a dynamic multi-objective optimization problem, in which alignment quality is assessed using complementary residue-level and column-level criteria, including Sum-of-Pairs score, column conservation, entropy, and gap statistics. Fuzzy membership functions are employed to harmonize competing objectives into a unified optimization landscape, while EO provides robust global exploration. To further enhance adaptability, SAC dynamically regulates key EO parameters during the search process, enabling an effective balance between exploration and exploitation across datasets of varying size and heterogeneity. Extensive experiments werew conducted on diverse biological sequence datasets, with a primary focus on RNA benchmarks, including structured families from Rfam, large-scale repositories from RNAcentral and GenBank, and organism-specific tRNA datasets from GtRNAdb. Comparative evaluations against classical alignment tools (ClustalW, MAFFT, MUSCLE, PRANK, KAlign, and T-Coffee), metaheuristic methods (SAGA, Sequoya and EAFSA), and a reinforcement learning–based approach (RLALIGN) demonstrate that MOFSACEO-MSA consistently achieves competitive or superior Sum-of-Pairs scores while significantly reducing gap proportions and maintaining compact alignment lengths. Notably, the proposed framework exhibits improved robustness on large and highly heterogeneous datasets, where existing methods often suffer from excessive gap insertion or unstable convergence. Overall, MOFSACEO-MSA provides a flexible and extensible optimization paradigm that effectively bridges evolutionary search and reinforcement learning for high-quality multiple sequence alignment, with demonstrated effectiveness on challenging RNA alignment tasks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57298-4.

Introduction

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) is a core computational task in bioinformatics, enabling comparative analysis of biological sequences for evolutionary inference, structural characterization, and functional annotation. Despite its central role, MSA remains computationally challenging due to its NP-complete nature1, which necessitates the use of heuristic and approximate optimization strategies for practical applications.

Clustal Omega2, MAFFT3, and MUSCLE4 are three widely used alignment tools that take advantage of progressive and iterative heuristics. These methods are highly efficient and perform well on moderately sized and relatively homogeneous datasets. However, their reliance on fixed scoring schemes and implicitly single-objective optimization—typically centered on the Sum-of-Pairs (SP) score5—introduces important limitations when applied to complex biological datasets. In particular, the inability to simultaneously balance competing alignment criteria often results in suboptimal trade-offs between pairwise consistency, column conservation, and gap distribution.

These limitations become particularly evident in multiple sequence alignment scenarios involving high sequence heterogeneity and structural variability. While classical dynamic programming methods (Needleman-Wunsch, Smith-Waterman) provide optimal solutions for pairwise alignment, MSA is NP-hard and requires heuristic approximations. Established MSA tools such as MAFFT and Clustal Omega employ greedy progressive strategies that, while computationally efficient, suffer from three key limitations: (1) they get trapped in local optima due to their deterministic nature, (2) they optimize a single objective function without balancing conflicting goals (e.g., maximizing conservation while minimizing gaps), and (3) they fail on highly divergent sequences or datasets with complex structural constraints. As demonstrated in Sect. “Results and discussion”, these limitations manifest as practical failures: widely used classical aligners such as MAFFT, ClustalW, and PRANK often introduce excessive gaps when applied to large and heterogeneous datasets, and in some cases produce “NaN” (no alignment) results on challenging RNA datasets (Table 4). For instance, gap proportions exceed 70% in RNAcentral collections and approach 90% in highly diverse Dictybase datasets, accompanied by severe degradation of SP scores, which frequently become strongly negative. Even in short and structurally conserved tRNA datasets, classical methods exhibit gap percentages above 20–30%, indicating limited control over alignment compactness. Reinforcement learning–based approaches such as RLALIGN show competitive SP scores on moderately sized and structured datasets; however, their performance deteriorates under increased heterogeneity, often relying on increased gap insertion and longer alignments to maintain normalized scores. These observations highlight that fixed-objective and fixed-policy optimization strategies struggle to generalize across datasets with diverse structural and evolutionary characteristics6.

Existing efforts to improve MSA performance have explored more flexible optimization strategies, including stochastic and adaptive approaches7–10. While these methods enhance the exploration of the alignment search space, they often rely on fixed parameter configurations or implicitly prioritize specific objectives, limiting their robustness across datasets with varying characteristics. Similarly, adaptive strategies based on reinforcement learning have shown promise in guiding alignment decisions11–13, but their effectiveness remains sensitive to dataset scale and heterogeneity, and they may still produce alignments with excessive gap insertion or unstable column structure.

Taken together, these challenges highlight a fundamental issue: MSA is inherently a multi-objective optimization problem, where alignment accuracy, conservation, compactness, and gap control must be considered simultaneously. Importantly, the relative importance of these criteria is not fixed but depends on the properties of the input sequences. Therefore, effective MSA frameworks should not only integrate multiple objectives but also adapt their optimization behavior dynamically during the search process.

Nevertheless, addressing MSA as a multi-objective problem alone is insufficient in practice. Fixed or manually tuned objective weightings often lead to unstable optimization behavior when dataset characteristics vary, particularly in heterogeneous datasets. Likewise, global optimization methods may lose effectiveness without adaptive control, while reinforcement learning–based strategies without explicit multi-objective balancing tend to over-optimize surrogate rewards, frequently resulting in excessive gap insertion or elongated alignments. These observations indicate that robust MSA requires a tightly integrated framework that simultaneously supports flexible objective aggregation, effective global search, and adaptive parameter control.

To address these challenges, we propose MOFSACEO-MSA, a hybrid optimization framework that formulates MSA as a fuzzy multi-objective and adaptively controlled search problem. The proposed approach combines three complementary components. First, a fuzzy multi-objective evaluation model enables flexible aggregation of alignment quality metrics, allowing smooth trade-offs between competing objectives without relying on rigid weighting schemes. Second, an enhanced Equilibrium Optimizer (EO)14 provides a robust global search mechanism capable of exploring complex alignment landscapes. Third, a Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) reinforcement learning agent15 dynamically adjusts key optimization parameters based on feedback from the search process, enabling adaptive behavior across datasets with different levels of heterogeneity.

Although the proposed framework is general and applicable to multiple sequence alignment tasks across different molecule types, this study focuses on RNA datasets as a challenging evaluation scenario due to their structural variability and sequence diversity. Experimental results on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that MOFSACEO-MSA achieves improved pairwise alignment consistency, significantly reduced gap usage, and more compact alignments, while maintaining stable conservation and entropy profiles. These findings suggest that integrating fuzzy multi-objective modeling with adaptive optimization provides a more balanced and robust solution to the MSA problem.

Related works

Biological sequence alignment is mainly based on well-known tools such as ClustalW16, Clustal Omega2, a guide tree–based progressive aligner optimized for scalability, MAFFT3, an FFT-based alignment framework designed for fast and accurate large-scale sequence analysis, MUSCLE4, an iterative refinement method focused on improving alignment accuracy, t-Coffee17, a consistency-based alignment approach that integrates information from multiple pairwise alignments, PRANK18, a phylogeny-aware alignment method that explicitly models insertion and deletion events, and KALIGN19, a fast alignment algorithm based on the Wu–Manber approximate string-matching strategy. Multiple sequence alignment can be efficiently performed using heuristic algorithms. However, these methods generally rely on fixed optimization strategies and therefore cannot simultaneously satisfy multiple alignment objectives. In recent years, artificial intelligence–based approaches have led to more adaptive and accurate solutions. It has been shown that methods based on swarm intelligence optimization20, hidden Markov models21, and deep learning22 can reduce alignment time while improving alignment quality.

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of RNA has been extensively studied in many fields, including optimization, evolutionary computation, and machine learning. The complexity of evolutionary relationships embedded within RNA families is often difficult to capture using classical deterministic algorithms due to compensatory mutations, noncanonical base pairings, and high structural constraints. Due to this, a large body of research has focused on developing more robust optimization frameworks, such as metaheuristic-based alignment strategies and, more recently, deep reinforcement learning. These two major directions of research are summarized in this section.

Metaheuristic-based approaches for MSA

In contrast to progressive or consistency-based aligners, metaheuristic algorithms enable exploration of large combinatorial search spaces and adaptability to non-linear scoring surfaces. In MSA algorithms, candidate alignments are iteratively refined using natural, biological, or physical search operators.

For alignment of multiple sequences, Notredame and Higgins23 proposed a software package called SAGA. A quasi-evolutionary objective function evaluates multiple alignment quality to improve fitness. SAGA automatically combines or modifies alignments between generations. SAGA optimizes sums of pairs better than some common alternatives. Optimal solutions and alignment accuracy are compared to reference alignments based on sequences with known tertiary structures.

Notredame and Higgins24 introduced a genetic algorithm–based approach for aligning homologous RNA sequences while considering both primary sequence similarity and secondary structural information. To address this problem, they developed the RAGA and PRAGA software packages. RAGA extends the earlier SAGA framework for multiple sequence alignment by incorporating evolutionary optimization strategies into RNA alignment tasks. PRAGA further enhances this approach by executing multiple genetic algorithms in parallel and periodically exchanging candidate solutions to improve search diversity and convergence. A key characteristic of these methods is their ability to optimize objective functions that simultaneously account for sequence conservation and RNA secondary structure features, including pseudoknots. Experimental evaluations on eukaryotic small-subunit rRNA datasets demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed evolutionary optimization strategy for structurally complex RNA alignment problems. Due to their stochastic optimization capability and their explicit incorporation of structural information, SAGA-based approaches were considered relevant comparative baselines for evaluating the optimization behavior of the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework.

The Enhanced Artificial Fish Swarm Algorithm (EAFSA) has been proposed by Tawfeek et al.10. Besides improving alignment accuracy and preserving sequence integrity, EAFSA also reduces computational costs by rapidly searching for similar fragments. An evaluation of the proposed EAFSA using benchmark RNA datasets is carried out in comparison with other available state-of-the-art alignment techniques.

A more accurate method of aligning multiple protein sequences can be developed with less time consumption, according to Tawfeek et al.25. The proposed method overcomes the most challenging aspects of MSA problems by utilizing the Artificial Fish Swarm (AFS) algorithm. The AFS ensures high accuracy in a timely manner. Machine learning, artificial intelligence, and computer vision are all examples of applications that use AFS. Essentially, it mimics the way fish search for food naturally. The proposed mechanisms of AFS, such as preying, swarming, following, moving, and leaping, increase execution speed and accuracy. The artificial fishes are accurate because of their sense organs that aid them in gathering information and vision from the environment. These features make the proposed AFS suitable for large-scale data alignment operations and make alignment operations more efficient. When compared with well-known algorithms, AFS appears to be the best choice for multiple sequence alignment based on its implementation and experimental results.

Chaabane26 proposed an approximate method for multiple sequence alignment (MSA) based on metaheuristics and pairwise dynamic programming. In order to overcome local optimum problems, particle swarm optimization (PSO) and simulated annealing (SA) are applied. Dynamic programming is used to improve worst solution quality and accelerate convergence. The proposed method produces good alignments based on simulations of the BAliBASE benchmark.

Ibrahim et al.7 proposed a simplified evolutionary-based alignment method without considering non-dominated sequences. Through the exclusion of non-dominated solution sets from their optimization method, they were able to reduce computational complexity. Their primary objective functions are the Sum of Pairs and Total Conserved Column. During the alignment process, they represented chromosomes as integers to enhance the computational efficiency. Extensive experiments are conducted using the SABmark and BAliBASE datasets in order to compare their method with existing ones. They produced superior solutions compared to their predecessors, according to the results. Their model’s improvement is statistically significant using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\:<\:0.05$$\end{document}). Multiple Sequence Alignment in bioinformatics can be advanced with this comprehensive approach.

Belattar et al.27 proposed a novel framework for aligning multiple DNA sequences using genetic algorithms and asynchronous advantage actor critics. First, alignments were generated using the T-Coffee method. Genetic algorithms were then used to recursively fragment the pre-aligned sequences. In this section, the short subalignments are introduced to the A3C model. Multiple alignments are best obtained by fusing subalignments. The proposed approach was compared to T-Coffee, MAFFT, and RLALIGN based on the Balibase 3.0 benchmark. Based on experimental results, the proposed approach is capable of producing solutions of competitive quality.

According to Zhu et al.28, MSA can be performed as a multiobjective optimization problem. To solve MSA, MOMSA uses an evolutionary multiobjective algorithm based on decomposition. The MOMSA algorithm was improved by developing a new population initialization method and a mutation operator. Several evolutionary algorithm-based alignment methods were compared to MOMSA, including VDGA, GAPAM, and IMSA, as well as progressive alignment methods like MSAprobs, Probalign, MAFFT, Procons, Clustal omega, T-Coffee, Kalign2, MUSCLE, FSA, Dialign, PRANK, and CLUSTALW. To test these alignment algorithms, BAliBASE 2.0 and BAliBASE 3.0 are used. Based on statistical analyses, MOMSA achieved better alignments than VDGA and GAPAM, achieved better TC scores than IMSA, and was comparable to the leading progressive alignment approaches.

Chowdhury et al.29 developed a bi-objective sequence alignment using genetic algorithms (BSAGA). This approach is unique because of its selection process. The Sum of Pairs is used to select part of the population, while Total Conserve Columns is used to select the remainder. The chromosomal coding used in an alignment only represented the gap positions. It is possible to improve a search technique even with longer sequences by using such a representation. To compare BSAGA’s alignment score to other relevant alignment methods, they used BAliBASE and SABmark. There is no doubt that the BSAGA performs better than other methods based on a Wilcoxon sign test.

Benitez-Hidalgo et al.30 introduced Sequoya, an evolutionary algorithm–based framework for MSA implemented in Python. The platform supports visualization, parallel computation, and interactive optimization, facilitating efficient exploration of high-quality alignment solutions.

Several other metaheuristic approaches have been proposed for MSA, including particle swarm optimization–based methods31,32, genetic algorithm frameworks33,34, population-based optimization techniques35, hybrid metaheuristic approaches36, and multiobjective evolutionary algorithms37,38. These methods aim to improve alignment quality by enhancing population diversity, balancing multiple objectives, or combining global and local search strategies. A comparative summary of the reviewed metaheuristic approaches is provided in Appendix Table A1.

Deep learning and reinforcement learning-based approaches

Recent advances in deep learning and reinforcement learning have enabled adaptive MSA frameworks capable of learning alignment strategies directly from biological sequence data.

In 2016, Mircea et al.39 reported the first application of Reinforcement Learning in Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA). Aligning sequences in order is similar to finding an optimal alignment order using the progressive alignment algorithm. They used a reinforcement learning method based on dynamic programming-Q-learning to calculate the optimal alignment order. Through Q-learning, this pioneering work models the alignment task as a sequential search problem. Each state corresponds to a biological sequence composed of the nucleotides A, C, T, G, and the gap symbol ( -). RL agents identify the best alignment by navigating the state space - which consists of all possible permutations of input sequences - and finding the best path. The agent constructs a complete permutation by sequentially selecting each input sequence exactly once. Reward signals are quantified by alignment scores. Based on a reference alignment, this score is derived by combining the values for matches, mismatches, and gaps across the sequences, facilitated by a positional matrix.

Jafari et al.40 solved an NP-complete problem using deep reinforcement learning (DRL). The aim of Multiple Sequence Alignment is to maximize the similarity between DNA, RNA, or proteins. Multiple sequence alignment is the first step towards constructing phylogenetic trees in many bioinformatics problems. For the estimation phase, multiple sequence alignment is modelled as a DRL problem. The actor-critic algorithm is used with experience replay to accelerate convergence. By combining deep Q-learning and Q-networks, it is possible to overcome the complexity of other approaches. The authors compared their approach with well-known heuristic methods, including MAFFT, ClustalW, and others using eight real-life datasets.

According to Ramakrishnan et al.41, RLALIGN is a reinforcement learning-based framework developed for solving the multiple sequence alignment (MSA) problem. The method is built upon the Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C) architecture, where alignment generation is modeled as a sequential decision-making process. Because MSA does not naturally contain a predefined terminal goal state, several modifications to the standard A3C framework were introduced to improve training stability and alignment convergence. RLALIGN can accurately align moderately long sequences and extend to longer sequences through additional heuristic strategies. In many cases, the generated alignments achieve accuracy comparable to well-established alignment algorithms. Furthermore, the framework demonstrates robustness to different scoring schemes and can be applied to local realignment and other combinatorial optimization problems. Due to its adaptive learning capability and reinforcement learning-driven optimization strategy, RLALIGN was selected in this study as a representative learning-based baseline for comparison with the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework.

Dotan et al.42 improved the BetaAlign approach by estimating multiple alignment alternatives and determining the one that maximizes certainty. In order to better characterize BetaAlign, the following analyses were performed: (i) analyzing training time and size; (ii) testing transfer learning; (iii) comparing evolutionary dynamics with performance; (iv) evaluating transformer architectures. Furthermore, they introduced subspace learning as a method for training on a subspace of indel parameters. Additionally, they demonstrated that their approach could be used to predict sequence lengths for other tasks based on the embedding obtained by the model.

Song et al.43 introduced an optimal sequence alignment method based on reinforcement learning. Through deep reinforcement learning, the DQNalign algorithm learns and aligns sequences. Their work combined x-drop and DQNalign algorithms to perform local alignment without human intervention. In the proposed algorithm, the alignment direction is continuously selected until the DQNalign process is terminated by the x-drop algorithm. As compared to conventional local alignment algorithms, this algorithm was more linearly complex. They proposed a method that maintained linear complexity while being comparable to the conventional alignment method in terms of identity and coverage.

Additional studies have explored various reinforcement learning and deep learning architectures for sequence alignment, including Q-learning–based methods, actor–critic frameworks, attention-enhanced deep reinforcement learning, convolutional neural networks, and transformer-based models44–48. These approaches generally improve representation learning and alignment quality but often require large training datasets and substantial computational resources. A comparative summary of the reviewed learning-based approaches is provided in Appendix Table A1.

To ensure a comprehensive and fair evaluation, a diverse set of baseline methods was selected, covering multiple methodological categories in multiple sequence alignment. Classical progressive aligners such as ClustalW and MAFFT were included due to their widespread use, computational efficiency, and strong practical performance across biological sequence alignment tasks. Metaheuristic-based approaches such as SAGA were selected to represent global optimization strategies capable of exploring complex and highly non-linear alignment landscapes. In addition, the recently proposed EAFSA10 method, which was specifically designed for RNA multiple sequence alignment using an enhanced artificial fish swarm optimization framework with fuzzy multi-objective evaluation, was included as an RNA-focused metaheuristic baseline for comparison with the proposed framework. Furthermore, the reinforcement learning–based method RLALIGN was incorporated to provide a direct comparison with adaptive learning-based alignment frameworks.

This selection ensures that the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework is evaluated against representative methods spanning deterministic heuristics, stochastic optimization, and learning-based approaches, thereby enabling a balanced and methodologically diverse comparison.

The goal is to find an alignment configuration A that maximizes biological consistency while minimizing unnecessary insertions, deletions, and mismatches. Because the scoring surface of MSA is highly multimodal and influenced by both sequence conservation and indel patterns, the alignment problem is inherently multi-objective. MOFSACEO-MSA models alignment quality using three major, biologically validated objectives:

(1) minimizing mismatches among nucleotides,

(2) minimizing gap costs, and

(3) maximizing similarity scores across aligned positions.

The proposed method, MOFSACEO-MSA, optimizes alignments for these sequences by jointly considering several biologically-motivated objectives and aggregating them with fuzzy membership functions. These objectives must be optimized simultaneously, as improvements in one criterion typically degrade another (e.g., lowering mismatches may increase gap usage).

Fuzzy multi-objective functions

Fuzzy membership function

In the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework, each of the three objectives is transformed into a fuzzy membership value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:\:\in\:\:\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right]$$\end{document}, expressing the degree to which an alignment satisfies that objective. Unlike linear membership functions, which enforce a strict boundary interpretation, we employ Gaussian and sigmoid S-shaped functions, as they provide smoother transitions, better modeling of uncertainty, and superior sensitivity to small changes in alignment quality—properties essential for biosequence optimization.

To formalize this, let x denote the numerical value of an objective function (e.g., mismatch count, total gap penalty, or alignment similarity score). We use two families of membership functions:

1- Gaussian Decreasing Membership Function (for minimizing objectives). Used when smaller values are better, e.g., mismatches and gap penalties.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{dec-\mathrm{G}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{s}}\left(x;c,\sigma\:\right)=\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{{\left(x-c\right)}^{2}}{2{\sigma\:}^{2}}\right]$$\end{document}

where, c is ideal (best) value of objective (usually 0), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} is dispersion controlling how fast membership decreases as the objective worsens. As x gets closer to c (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x\to\:c$$\end{document}), the membership degree tends to 1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:membership\:\to\:\:1$$\end{document}) (the most complete membership), and as x gets much larger than c (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x\gg\:c$$\end{document}), the membership degree gradually and continuously approaches 0 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:membership\:smoothly\:\to\:\:0$$\end{document}).

2- Sigmoid Increasing Membership Function (for maximizing objectives). Soft actor–critic controller for dynamic parameter adaptation

The stochastic nature of EO makes its performance highly dependent on hyperparameter selection (mutation strength, Lévy scaling, equilibrium pool size, reseeding probability, etc.). MOFSACEO-MSA embeds these parameters in a continuous reinforcement learning controller based on the Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) algorithm. SAC is chosen due to its entropy-regularized formulation, which suits continuous control in noisy optimization environments. SAC is an architecture equipped with an actor, a critic, and a selector. The selector determines the most promising action at the current state based on the last estimate from the critic15.

EO–SAC synergy

The integration of EO with SAC forms a tightly coupled, self-adaptive optimization ecosystem. EO supplies a population of structurally diverse candidate alignments, exploring complex regions of the search space through mass-balance dynamics, Lévy flights, and elitist recombination. However, EO’s performance depends strongly on hyperparameters that govern mutation amplitude, exploration pressure, and reseeding intensity—parameters that vary significantly across RNA families and alignment landscapes. SAC provides a learning-driven mechanism to automatically modulate these controls. By continuously observing population diversity, fuzzy satisfaction levels, and convergence trends, the SAC agent learns to regulate EO’s behavior throughout the optimization process. Early stages emphasize high-entropy exploration to broaden search coverage, whereas later stages enforce low-entropy exploitation, stabilizing convergence toward high-quality alignments. The synergy produces a dynamic optimization pipeline capable of escaping deep local optima, accelerating convergence, and consistently improving alignment accuracy across datasets—advantages unattainable by either EO or SAC alone.

Experiments and evaluation

We evaluate the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework through a comprehensive set of experiments designed to assess alignment quality, robustness, and practical effectiveness across diverse RNA datasets. The experimental analysis focuses on comparing MOFSACEO-MSA with widely used baseline alignment methods using standard and well-established evaluation metrics. To better understand the contribution of each component of the proposed framework, additional analyses are conducted to examine the roles of the fuzzy multi-objective model, the enhanced Equilibrium Optimizer search strategy, and the SAC-based adaptive parameter tuning mechanism. All experiments are repeated using multiple random initializations to ensure stability and reproducibility of the results, and performance is reported using average values to provide reliable comparative insights. In addition to alignment accuracy, computational behavior such as convergence trends and resource efficiency is also examined to demonstrate the applicability of MOFSACEO-MSA in realistic bioinformatics workflows.

To quantitatively assess the contribution and necessity of individual components in the proposed MOFSACEO‑MSA framework, a structured and controlled ablation study was designed. Baseline seed‑driven alignments without EO‑Pro enhancement (Baseline-Seed and EO-SP) were employed to isolate the effect of canceling the enhanced EO‑based search mechanism. EO‑based variants with reduced objective formulations, including EO-LinearMO (static linear‑weighting of SP and CS) and EO-LinearMO + Gap, were used to evaluate the impact of replacing the full fuzzy multi‑objective model with simplified objective representations and explicit gap penalization. The full MOFSACEO‑MSA configuration integrates all components, including fuzzy multi‑objective optimization, EO‑Pro enhancement, and SAC‑based adaptive parameter control. All ablation variants were executed on the same RNA benchmark datasets under identical preprocessing and hyperparameter settings, to ensure fair and reproducible comparison. Detailed quantitative results, along with component‑wise contribution, synergy, and irreplaceability analyses, are presented in Sect. “Ablation study and component contribution analysis”.

Representative benchmark families and their biological descriptions are presented in Appendix (Table A2).

To ensure consistency and fairness across datasets, all sequences were subjected to a standardized preprocessing procedure prior to alignment. Duplicate sequences were first removed to eliminate redundancy, followed by sequence similarity filtering using a 90% identity threshold to reduce over-representation of highly similar sequences while preserving evolutionary diversity. Sequences containing excessive ambiguity (e.g., more than 5% undefined nucleotides such as “N”) were excluded. For large-scale datasets such as RNAcentral (e.g., Lncbase and snoRNA collections), representative subsets were selected when necessary to maintain computational feasibility while preserving the overall diversity of the data. All remaining sequences were retained in their original orientation and no structural annotation preprocessing was applied, allowing the evaluation to reflect the intrinsic capability of the alignment algorithms under realistic conditions.

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework across datasets of varying difficulty, we quantified the alignment complexity of each dataset using three key factors known to influence MSA performance: (1) the number of sequences, (2) the average sequence length, and (3) the degree of size variability among sequences. These factors collectively capture both the structural and evolutionary heterogeneity inherent in families.

Each factor was assigned a weight reflecting its relative impact on alignment difficulty—40% for the number of sequences, 30% for mean sequence length, and 30% for sequence-length dispersion—to compute an overall Complexity Score for each dataset. The selected weights reflect the dominant influence of sequence count on combinatorial complexity, while still accounting for sequence length and heterogeneity. This composite score provides a normalized measure of dataset difficulty, enabling fair comparison of algorithmic performance across heterogeneous families.

Statistical characteristics of the datasets, including sequence counts, sequence-length statistics, and Complexity Scores, are provided in Appendix (Table A3).

Evaluation metrics

Alignment quality was evaluated using complementary metrics that capture alignment accuracy, conservation, gap behavior, and alignment consistency.

Sum-of-pairs (SP) score

The Sum of Pairs (SP) score is one of the most widely adopted evaluation metrics for multiple sequence alignment, as it directly quantifies the consistency of residue pairing across all sequences. The SP score evaluates an alignment by summing the scores of all possible sequence pairs at each aligned position, thereby reflecting the global alignment quality at the pairwise level.

The SP metric is particularly effective for evaluating the ability of an alignment algorithm to preserve evolutionary signals across multiple sequences. However, since SP values scale with the number of sequences and alignment length, raw SP scores alone may not be sufficient for cross-dataset comparisons. For this reason, a normalized variant of the SP score is introduced in the subsequent subsection.

Gap percentage (Gap Metric)

Gap introduction is an unavoidable component of multiple sequence alignment, as insertions and deletions (indels) are fundamental evolutionary events. However, excessive gap usage can artificially inflate alignment length, distort homologous relationships, and reduce the biological interpretability of the resulting alignment. Reference-based metrics

While internal evaluation metrics such as SP score and Column Score provide insight into alignment quality, they do not necessarily guarantee biological correctness. To address this limitation, reference-based metrics are employed by comparing the predicted alignments against curated ground-truth alignments from benchmark datasets. In this study, we adopt two widely used reference-dependent measures: Sum-of-Pairs Score (SPS) and Total Column (TC) score.

SPS (Sum-of-Pairs Score w.r.t. reference alignment)

Convergence and early-stopping criteria

The optimization process was considered converged when no significant improvement in the objective function was observed over a predefined stagnation window. Specifically, convergence was detected when the improvement in the best SP score remained below a threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document} for 200 consecutive iterations.

In addition to the stagnation criterion, population diversity was continuously monitored during optimization. When diversity decreased below a predefined threshold while the objective function remained unchanged, the optimization process was interpreted as approaching premature convergence. In such cases, the EO re-initialization and diversification mechanisms described in Sect. “Jumping and re-initialization mechanism” were activated to restore exploration capability.

For computational consistency across all benchmark experiments, a maximum iteration limit was also imposed. Therefore, optimization terminated when either:

Implementation details and environment configuration

The proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework was implemented in Python (version 3.9). All experiments were conducted on a Windows 10 operating system using a system equipped with 16 GB of RAM and an Intel Core i5-12400 F CPU.

The reinforcement learning component based on the Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) algorithm was implemented using PyTorch (v2.8.0) and Stable-Baselines3 (v2.7.0). Numerical computations and data processing were performed using NumPy (v2.0.2) and SciPy (v1.13.1), while sequence handling and bioinformatics-related operations were supported by Biopython (v1.85). The fuzzy multi-objective evaluation module was implemented using scikit-fuzzy (v0.4.2).

Additional libraries such as scikit-learn (v1.6.1) were used for auxiliary data processing and analysis. Visualization and plotting were carried out using Matplotlib (v3.9.4). The MAFFT tool (v7.505) was employed to generate initial alignments used for hybrid encoding and baseline comparison.

All experiments were executed under consistent software and hardware configurations to ensure reproducibility. The complete source code, along with the exact dependency versions and implementation details, will be made available upon publication (or upon reasonable request).

Ablation study and component contribution analysis

To quantitatively evaluate the contribution, necessity, and interaction of individual components within the proposed MOFSACEO‑MSA framework, a controlled ablation study was conducted on two RNA benchmark datasets with distinct biological characteristics: baciSubt2‑mature‑tRNAs and Mitochondria. These datasets were selected to represent two complementary alignments. The baciSubt2‑mature‑tRNAs dataset contains short and highly conserved sequences, where excessive gap insertion rapidly degrades alignment quality. In contrast, the Mitochondria dataset consists of longer and moderately heterogeneous sequences, in which robustness to structural variation and parameter adaptability become more critical.

All ablation experiments were performed under strictly identical experimental conditions, including the same MAFFT‑generated seed alignment, preprocessing pipeline, datasets, and hyperparameter configuration. Consequently, all observed performance differences can be attributed exclusively to the activation or removal of specific architectural components. This design enables a fair and interpretable quantification of component‑level contributions.

Alignment quality was evaluated using four complementary indicators: Sum‑of‑Pairs (SP) score, Column Score (CS), gap percentage, and alignment length (AL). Together, these metrics capture both alignment accuracy and structural compactness. Quantitative component contribution analysis

Effect of removing EO-Pro enhancement

Across both datasets, the Baseline-Seed configuration exactly reproduces the MAFFT baseline in terms of SP score, CS score, gap ratio, and alignment length. The EO-SP variant also yields identical results on both datasets, indicating that EO optimization driven solely by the SP objective is insufficient to escape the local optimum imposed by the seed alignment.

These results indicate that evolutionary optimization driven by a single objective, without EO‑Pro enhancement and without richer objective modeling, is insufficient to escape the local optimum imposed by the seed alignment. EO‑Pro enhancement is therefore a necessary prerequisite for achieving meaningful performance gains.

Effect of replacing fuzzy multi‑objective optimization with linear weighting

Introducing multi‑objective guidance through EO-LinearMO, implemented via a static linear‑weighting formulation, leads to consistent improvements in alignment compactness on both datasets. On baciSubt2‑mature‑tRNAs, the SP score increases from 139,582 to 143,752, while the gap ratio decreases from 23.56% to 22.14%. Similarly, on the Mitochondria dataset, the SP score improves to 1,052,296 and the gap ratio decreases from 10.84% to 9.80%.

However, these gains are accompanied by a notable reduction in CS values on both datasets, indicating that fixed linear weighting cannot fully balance competing alignment criteria. This observation highlights the inherent limitation of static objective weighting and motivates the adoption of more flexible multi‑objective formulations.

Role of explicit gap awareness

The EO-LinearMO + Gap variant further improves alignment quality by explicitly penalizing excessive gap insertion. On baciSubt2‑mature‑tRNAs, the SP score increases to 148,258 and the gap ratio is reduced to 21.86%. On the Mitochondria dataset, gap percentage decreases further to 9.28%, accompanied by reductions in alignment length.

These results demonstrate that gap‑aware modeling plays an increasingly important role as sequence length and heterogeneity increase, complementing objective‑level optimization.

Impact of removing SAC-based adaptive control

Comparing EO-LinearMO + Gap with the MOFSACEO-MSA isolates the contribution of SAC‑based adaptive parameter control. Incorporating SAC yields the strongest performance improvements across all metrics. On the tRNA dataset, the SP score increases substantially from 148,258 to 166,749, CS improves to 0.2381, and alignment length is reduced to 99. On the Mitochondria dataset, SAC further increases the SP score to 1,073,864 and produces the most compact alignment (length 118).

These improvements indicate that adaptive parameter control enables dynamic balancing between exploration and exploitation, which cannot be achieved through static parameter settings.

Synergy, irreplaceability, and joint optimization value

The ablation results demonstrate that no individual component—EO‑Pro enhancement, linear‑weighting objective formulation, gap‑aware penalization, or SAC‑based adaptive control—can independently achieve the performance of the full MOFSACEO‑MSA framework. Instead, the observed gains arise from their non‑additive synergistic interaction.

Linear weighting provides a minimal multi‑criteria optimization signal, gap awareness constrains excessive insertion behavior, EO‑Pro enables effective exploration of the solution space, and SAC dynamically adapts optimization behavior to dataset‑specific characteristics. Removing any single component leads to measurable degradation in SP score, CS, or alignment compactness, thereby confirming the irreplaceability of each component and the joint optimization value of the proposed framework.

Results and discussion

Comprehensive performance evaluation and comparative analysis

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework and discusses its performance relative to state-of-the-art classical, metaheuristic, and reinforcement learning–based multiple sequence alignment methods. The analysis focuses on alignment accuracy, robustness across heterogeneous datasets, and statistical significance of the observed improvements.

The proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework was systematically evaluated across a diverse and representative collection of RNA datasets, encompassing structurally conserved families, large-scale heterogeneous repositories, and organism-specific tRNA collections. This selection was designed to assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method under varying levels of sequence length, diversity, and evolutionary complexity.

Alignment performance was evaluated using a comprehensive set of quantitative metrics that jointly capture pairwise accuracy, column-wise conservation, alignment compactness, and overall biological plausibility. Specifically, we employed the Sum-of-Pairs (SP) score, its normalized variant (SP_Normalized), the Column Score (CS), Alignment Length (AL), Gap Percentage, Average Shannon Entropy (Avg_Entropy) and Average Conservation Score (Avg_Conservation). Together, these measures provide a multi-level assessment of alignment quality, spanning residue-level agreement, conservation of homologous columns, gap management efficiency, and information-theoretic consistency.

In all alignment experiments, a uniform scoring scheme was applied to ensure fair and unbiased comparison across methods. Matches between identical, non-gap nucleotides were rewarded with a score of + 2, mismatches were penalized by − 1, and gap insertions were assigned a penalty of − 2. This scoring configuration was consistently used in the computation of SP-based metrics and reflects a balanced trade-off between rewarding conservation and discouraging excessive gap introduction.

The effectiveness of the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA algorithm was examined through extensive comparisons with a broad spectrum of established multiple sequence alignment approaches. The comparative results are reported in three dedicated tables. Finally, Table 4 contrasts MOFSACEO-MSA with the reinforcement learning-based method RLALIGN, thereby elucidating the advantages gained by integrating soft actor–critic reinforcement learning with equilibrium optimization and fuzzy decision modeling.

Collectively, these comparisons aim to demonstrate the consistent performance gains, superior gap management, and balanced conservation achieved by MOFSACEO-MSA across diverse alignment paradigms, underscoring its effectiveness as a unified optimization framework for RNA multiple sequence alignment.

Comparison between MOFSACEO-MSA and metaheuristics methods.

The Sum-of-Pairs (SP) score reflects the overall residue-level agreement across all aligned sequence pairs and remains one of the primary indicators of alignment quality. Across nearly all evaluated datasets, MOFSACEO-MSA consistently achieves higher SP scores than SAGA, Sequoya, and the recently introduced EAFSA framework. This advantage is particularly evident in structured RNA families such as RF03962, RF00062, and RF00014, where the proposed framework produces noticeably higher alignment agreement while maintaining compact alignments. In organelle-derived datasets such as Mitochondria and Plastids, MOFSACEO-MSA also achieves the highest SP values, indicating improved robustness in the presence of conserved but evolutionarily noisy sequence patterns. Although all methods experience substantial degradation on extremely heterogeneous RNAcentral datasets (e.g., Lncbase and Dictybase), MOFSACEO-MSA consistently exhibits less severe deterioration than the competing metaheuristic methods. These observations suggest that the EO–SAC optimization strategy provides a more stable exploration mechanism and better avoids premature convergence than traditional evolutionary approaches, including fish swarm–based optimization methods such as EAFSA.

The normalized SP score (SP_Normalized) provides a scale-independent evaluation criterion that enables fair comparison across datasets with different sequence counts and lengths. MOFSACEO-MSA attains the highest normalized SP values in the majority of datasets, particularly within the Rfam and GenBank collections. For example, on the Mitochondria dataset, MOFSACEO-MSA achieves a normalized SP score of 0.76 compared with 0.70 for SAGA, 0.66 for Sequoya, and 0.69 for EAFSA. Similar improvements are observed for Plastids and RF03962. However, several datasets exhibit smaller margins or isolated cases where alternative methods obtain competitive normalized SP values. For instance, Sequoya slightly exceeds MOFSACEO-MSA on RF00014 and certain tRNA datasets. This behavior likely reflects differences in alignment compactness and normalization sensitivity, where methods generating longer alignments with additional gaps may occasionally increase pairwise matching opportunities after normalization. Nevertheless, these localized improvements are generally accompanied by substantially higher gap percentages and alignment lengths, suggesting that the higher normalized SP values are obtained at the expense of biological compactness and structural plausibility. Overall, the proposed framework achieves a more balanced trade-off between pairwise agreement and alignment compactness.

Column Score (CS) evaluates the proportion of perfectly aligned columns and is particularly sensitive to structural consistency and conserved motif preservation. MOFSACEO-MSA generally achieves more stable and biologically meaningful CS values across datasets than SAGA and EAFSA. Although Sequoya occasionally attains slightly higher CS values in specific Rfam families such as RF03962 and RF00014, these gains are typically associated with substantially longer alignments and increased gap insertion. In contrast, MOFSACEO-MSA preserves competitive CS performance while maintaining more compact alignments. On large and heterogeneous datasets such as Lncbase, snorna_database, and Dictybase, the proposed framework demonstrates improved column preservation relative to the other metaheuristics, suggesting that the fuzzy multi-objective formulation effectively balances column conservation and alignment compactness. The relatively stable CS behavior across diverse datasets also indicates that the SAC-guided optimization process improves robustness against initialization sensitivity and operator instability commonly observed in classical evolutionary algorithms.

Gap-related metrics reveal one of the clearest advantages of the proposed framework. MOFSACEO-MSA consistently produces lower gap percentages than SAGA, Sequoya, and EAFSA across most datasets, particularly within the Rfam and GenBank collections. For example, on the Mitochondria dataset, the proposed method reduces the gap percentage to 9.15%, compared with 18.6% for SAGA, 22.4% for Sequoya, and 18.1% for EAFSA. Similar trends are observed for RF03962, Plastids, and baciSubt2-mature-tRNAs. This behavior is especially important for RNA alignment tasks because excessive gap insertion may artificially inflate similarity measures while simultaneously disrupting biologically meaningful structural regions. Classical evolutionary approaches frequently rely on gap proliferation as a mechanism for escaping local optima, leading to overextended alignments and reduced interpretability. In contrast, the SAC-guided adaptive parameter control in MOFSACEO-MSA dynamically regulates exploration intensity, enabling the algorithm to improve SP and conservation metrics without excessive alignment expansion.

Average column entropy measures alignment uncertainty and reflects the degree of dispersion within aligned columns. Lower entropy values generally indicate more stable and consistent alignments. Across most datasets, MOFSACEO-MSA achieves entropy values equal to or lower than those of SAGA, Sequoya, and EAFSA, particularly in Rfam and GenBank datasets. In highly heterogeneous collections such as Lncbase and Dictybase, entropy values increase for all methods due to extreme sequence variability; however, the proposed framework maintains comparatively controlled entropy growth. This suggests that MOFSACEO-MSA achieves a better balance between alignment flexibility and structural consistency, avoiding the excessive randomness that may arise in purely stochastic optimization frameworks. The lower entropy values further support the observation that the fuzzy multi-objective evaluation stabilizes the search process and encourages biologically coherent column formation.

Average conservation directly reflects the biological plausibility of the resulting alignments by quantifying how well conserved regions are preserved across sequences. MOFSACEO-MSA consistently achieves equal or higher conservation values than the competing metaheuristic methods across nearly all datasets. The improvement is particularly notable in organelle-derived and tRNA datasets, where biologically meaningful conservation patterns are expected to remain stable despite evolutionary divergence. Although Sequoya and EAFSA occasionally produce comparable conservation values, these cases are generally associated with significantly longer alignments and higher gap usage, indicating a trade-off between conservation and compactness rather than a genuine improvement in alignment quality. The consistently strong conservation performance of MOFSACEO-MSA demonstrates that the integration of fuzzy multi-objective optimization with EO–SAC adaptive control effectively aligns optimization behavior with biologically relevant sequence patterns.

Taken together, the comparative analysis demonstrates that MOFSACEO-MSA achieves a more balanced optimization of alignment accuracy, compactness, conservation, and robustness than existing metaheuristic-based approaches, including SAGA, Sequoya, and the recent EAFSA framework. While competing methods occasionally achieve competitive performance in isolated metrics or specific datasets, they generally exhibit higher variability, increased gap inflation, and reduced structural consistency. The proposed framework maintains superior overall stability because the EO-based global exploration, fuzzy multi-objective evaluation, and SAC-driven adaptive parameter control operate synergistically to guide the optimization process toward biologically meaningful alignment configurations. Consequently, MOFSACEO-MSA represents a robust and scalable alternative for complex RNA multiple sequence alignment tasks, particularly for heterogeneous and structurally challenging datasets.

Comparison between MOFSACEO-MSA and RLALIGN.

The SP score comparison indicates that MOFSACEO-MSA and RLALIGN achieve largely comparable performance across most datasets, particularly for structured and moderately sized families. In Rfam datasets (RF03962, RF00062, RF00014), both methods reach identical or near-identical SP values, confirming that RLALIGN is effective in optimizing pairwise residue consistency in relatively well-defined alignment landscapes. However, for large-scale and heterogeneous datasets such as RNAcentral (Lncbase, Dictybase), MOFSACEO-MSA consistently shows less severe SP degradation compared to RLALIGN, suggesting improved robustness under extreme diversity. In GenBank datasets, especially Plastids and Archaea, MOFSACEO-MSA maintains slightly higher SP values, indicating better preservation of evolutionary signal in long genomic sequences. Overall, while RLALIGN is competitive, MOFSACEO-MSA demonstrates more stable SP behavior across dataset scales and complexities. The normalized SP scores further highlight the stability of MOFSACEO-MSA. For most datasets, the two methods exhibit similar normalized values; however, RLALIGN occasionally reports higher SP_Normalized scores, notably in GenBank datasets such as Mitochondria and Archaea. This behavior reflects RLALIGN’s tendency to normalize alignment quality through increased gap usage and longer alignments.

Column Score analysis reveals a consistent advantage for MOFSACEO-MSA in preserving fully conserved columns. In Rfam datasets, MOFSACEO-MSA achieves slightly higher CS values, reflecting improved structural coherence. For challenging RNAcentral datasets, both methods exhibit reduced CS, yet MOFSACEO-MSA maintains comparable or marginally better values in Lncbase and Dictybase. In tRNA datasets, the difference becomes more pronounced: RLALIGN shows a noticeable increase in CS for araTha1-filtered-tRNAs, but this gain is accompanied by substantially higher gap penalties, indicating a trade-off between column conservation and alignment compactness. Overall, MOFSACEO-MSA demonstrates more consistent column-level conservation across datasets.

Gap-related metrics clearly differentiate the two approaches. MOFSACEO-MSA consistently introduces fewer gaps than RLALIGN in almost all datasets, particularly in RF03962, RF00062, and RF00014. While the absolute differences appear moderate, they are systematic and persist across dataset categories. In RNAcentral and GenBank datasets, RLALIGN exhibits a tendency toward increased gap insertion, which contributes to higher normalized SP values but at the expense of alignment compactness. The lower gap scores of MOFSACEO-MSA indicate more controlled gap management, aligning better with biological expectations.

Average entropy values show that MOFSACEO-MSA generally produces alignments with equal or slightly lower entropy, particularly in Rfam and GenBank datasets. This suggests reduced uncertainty and more consistent residue distributions across alignment columns. Although RLALIGN performs competitively in some RNAcentral subsets, its entropy values remain marginally lower in most cases, reflecting increased variability introduced by gap-rich alignments. The results confirm that MOFSACEO-MSA achieves more coherent column distributions, especially in structured datasets.

Both methods achieve high average conservation in structured datasets; however, MOFSACEO-MSA consistently matches or slightly exceeds RLALIGN across most benchmarks. In GenBank Plastids and Mitochondria, conservation scores are nearly identical, but MOFSACEO-MSA maintains these values with fewer gaps and shorter alignments. In tRNA datasets, conservation levels are comparable, though RLALIGN occasionally reports marginal gains that coincide with increased gap penalties. This suggests that MOFSACEO-MSA achieves conservation in a more biologically parsimonious manner.

By integrating fuzzy multi-objective evaluation with EO-driven global search and SAC-based adaptive control, MOFSACEO-MSA achieves more stable SP performance, improved gap regulation, and compact alignments without sacrificing conservation or column integrity. These findings underscore the advantage of hybrid optimization frameworks over purely reinforcement learning–based approaches for robust multiple sequence alignment across diverse RNA datasets.

The additional complexity of MOFSACEO-MSA is motivated by the limitations of conventional alignment strategies on highly heterogeneous and structurally challenging datasets. Although classical methods such as MAFFT, ClustalW, and PRANK remain highly efficient for relatively homogeneous sequences, the experimental results indicate that their performance degrades substantially as dataset complexity increases. In several RNAcentral and GenBank datasets, conventional methods produced unstable alignments, excessive gap insertion, or failed to generate valid outputs under the evaluation pipeline (Table 2). In contrast, MOFSACEO-MSA consistently produced stable alignments across all evaluated datasets. Furthermore, while improvements over existing methods are moderate on relatively simple datasets, substantially larger gains are observed on difficult datasets. For example, Table 4 shows that MOFSACEO-MSA improved the SP score on the Dictybase dataset by 59.8% compared with RLALIGN, demonstrating that the proposed framework becomes increasingly beneficial as sequence heterogeneity increases. In addition, the ablation analysis indicates that removing individual framework components reduces alignment quality by approximately 3–13%, confirming the complementary contributions of fuzzy multi-objective evaluation, EO-based global optimization, and SAC-driven adaptive parameter control. Although the proposed framework introduces additional computational overhead compared with traditional progressive aligners, this trade-off is justified in challenging alignment scenarios where robustness, compactness, and alignment quality are prioritized over minimal execution time.

Theoretical significance and broader applicability of the proposed framework

Beyond its empirical performance in multiple sequence alignment, the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework has important theoretical implications for solving complex optimization problems in bioinformatics. At its core, the method introduces a unified paradigm that integrates fuzzy multi-objective modeling, metaheuristic global optimization, and reinforcement learning–based adaptive control within a single optimization loop. This combination enables the simultaneous handling of conflicting objectives, uncertainty in biological data, and dynamic adaptation of search strategies—three fundamental challenges that are pervasive across computational biology.

The fuzzy formulation provides an interpretable mechanism for balancing competing biological criteria, such as alignment accuracy, structural compactness, and conservation, without requiring rigid objective prioritization. At the same time, the Equilibrium Optimizer ensures effective exploration of high-dimensional and multimodal search spaces, while the Soft Actor–Critic controller dynamically adjusts optimization behavior based on the evolving state of the search process. This results in a self-adaptive optimization system capable of maintaining robustness across datasets with widely varying characteristics.

Importantly, this framework is not restricted to RNA multiple sequence alignment. Its design is inherently generalizable and can be extended to other bioinformatics problems that involve complex trade-offs and large search spaces. For example, in protein structure prediction, the framework can be adapted to jointly optimize energy functions, structural constraints, and evolutionary signals. In gene expression analysis, it can be applied to multi-objective clustering, feature selection, and gene regulatory network inference, where trade-offs between accuracy, sparsity, and biological interpretability must be carefully managed. Similarly, tasks such as RNA secondary structure prediction, motif discovery, and comparative genomics can benefit from the ability to integrate heterogeneous objectives and adaptively control the optimization process.

From a broader perspective, the proposed method demonstrates how combining fuzzy logic with reinforcement learning and metaheuristics can lead to a new class of intelligent optimization systems tailored to the challenges of modern bioinformatics. This positions MOFSACEO-MSA not only as a high-performance alignment tool but also as a flexible and extensible computational framework for addressing a wide range of biological sequence analysis problems.

Error analysis and dataset-specific performance variability

Although the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework achieved superior overall performance across most benchmark datasets, several datasets—particularly certain GenBank families—exhibited slightly lower Normalized SP scores compared with RLALIGN. A detailed analysis suggests that this behavior is primarily associated with the high sequence heterogeneity and evolutionary variability present in these datasets.

Unlike curated RNA family databases containing relatively conserved structural motifs, GenBank datasets frequently include sequences with substantial divergence, irregular insertion/deletion patterns, and inconsistent evolutionary distances. Under such conditions, methods that aggressively maximize pairwise similarity may achieve higher normalized SP values at the cost of excessive gap insertion and reduced alignment compactness.

In contrast, the proposed framework performs multi-objective optimization by jointly balancing SP maximization, entropy minimization, conservation preservation, and gap control through adaptive fuzzy weighting. Consequently, the optimizer may occasionally favor biologically compact alignments with lower gap inflation rather than aggressively maximizing pairwise normalized similarity alone. This trade-off explains why RLALIGN achieves marginally higher normalized SP scores in some heterogeneous datasets while simultaneously producing higher gap ratios.

Another contributing factor is the dependence of the proposed editable-gap representation on the quality of the initial seed alignment. In highly divergent datasets, early alignment uncertainty introduced during initialization may propagate into later optimization stages despite the adaptive refinement strategy. Nevertheless, the proposed MOFSACEO-MSA framework remains more stable overall and demonstrates stronger generalization across datasets with varying structural complexity.

These observations highlight an important practical consideration: maximizing a single alignment metric does not necessarily guarantee biologically optimal or structurally compact alignments. The proposed framework instead prioritizes balanced optimization across multiple biologically relevant objectives.

Overall, MAFFT remained the most computationally efficient method in terms of execution time and memory consumption. Across most datasets, MAFFT completed alignment within 1–50 s while maintaining memory usage close to 5 MB. This behavior is expected because MAFFT relies on highly optimized progressive heuristics specifically designed for fast large-scale alignment.

ClustalW and PRANK exhibited substantially lower scalability. Although both methods performed adequately on smaller datasets such as RF00070 and araTha1-tRNAs, they failed to complete several larger or structurally heterogeneous datasets, including 5srRNAdb, Bacteria, crw, evlncrnas, and tarbase. In addition, their runtime increased rapidly with dataset complexity. For example, on the srpdb dataset, ClustalW required 114.35 s, while PRANK required 301.02 s. These observations indicate that traditional progressive alignment strategies become increasingly unstable and computationally demanding when sequence diversity and dataset size increase.

Compared with classical methods, both RLALIGN and MOFSACEO-MSA required higher computational cost due to their iterative optimization procedures and adaptive search mechanisms. Nevertheless, the proposed framework demonstrated competitive scalability relative to RLALIGN and achieved lower runtime on several challenging datasets. For example, on the Bacteria dataset containing 7,291 sequences, MOFSACEO-MSA completed execution in 1254.34 s compared with 1654.34 s for RLALIGN. Similarly, on the evlncrnas dataset, which contains extremely long RNA sequences, the proposed framework reduced runtime from 1253.31 s to 1037.06 s. Comparable reductions were also observed on snorna_database (332.62 vs. 365.27 s) and srpdb (816.11 vs. 1016.11 s). These results suggest that the EO–SAC integration improves optimization efficiency and convergence behavior on complex datasets.

Memory analysis further demonstrates the practical scalability of the proposed framework. Although MOFSACEO-MSA incorporates additional optimization components, its memory consumption remained consistently lower than RLALIGN on most large-scale datasets. The largest reduction was observed on the crw dataset, where RLALIGN required 3103.26 MB while MOFSACEO-MSA required 1933.40 MB. Similar improvements were observed on Bacteria (1311.81 MB vs. 909.59 MB), tarbase (816.13 MB vs. 497.45 MB), and snorna_database (528.63 MB vs. 139.13 MB). Even on datasets with extremely long sequences such as evlncrnas, the proposed method maintained relatively moderate memory usage (104.71 MB) compared with RLALIGN (174.16 MB). These findings indicate that the proposed optimization framework improves search effectiveness without introducing disproportionate memory overhead.

The additional computational cost of MOFSACEO-MSA primarily arises from three components: (1) EO-based population exploration, (2) repeated fuzzy multi-objective evaluation, and (3) SAC-based adaptive parameter updates. Among these components, EO population optimization represents the dominant computational factor because candidate alignments must be repeatedly evaluated throughout the optimization process. SAC-based adaptive control introduces additional overhead; however, this mechanism improves convergence stability and reduces unnecessary alignment expansion, contributing to improved alignment compactness and robustness on heterogeneous datasets.

From a computational complexity perspective, the proposed framework scales approximately with both alignment length and optimization population size, since each optimization iteration requires repeated evaluation of candidate solutions. Consequently, runtime increases for datasets containing thousands of sequences or extremely long RNA molecules. However, unlike several conventional methods that failed on highly heterogeneous datasets, MOFSACEO-MSA successfully generated valid alignments across all evaluated benchmarks, demonstrating improved robustness and practical applicability under difficult alignment conditions.

Importantly, the proposed framework is inherently suitable for parallelization. Candidate alignment evaluation within the EO population is independent across individuals and can therefore be executed concurrently using multi-core CPU or GPU architectures. In addition, SAC policy evaluation and critic updates rely on tensor-based operations that are naturally compatible with GPU acceleration. Therefore, future implementations based on distributed EO evaluation or multi-GPU SAC training may substantially reduce runtime and further improve scalability for large-scale genomic and transcriptomic analyses.

Overall, the computational analysis demonstrates that MOFSACEO-MSA introduces moderate additional computational overhead relative to highly optimized progressive aligners such as MAFFT. However, this trade-off is compensated by improved robustness, scalability, reduced memory usage relative to RLALIGN on large datasets, and the ability to consistently generate stable alignments on heterogeneous RNA datasets where several conventional approaches either degrade substantially or fail completely.

Limitations and applicability boundaries

Despite the strong performance of MOFSACEO-MSA across diverse datasets, several limitations should be acknowledged to better contextualize its applicability. First, the integration of fuzzy multi-objective evaluation, equilibrium optimization, and Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) control introduces additional computational overhead compared to classical progressive alignment methods. While this cost remains manageable for moderate-scale datasets, it may become a limiting factor for extremely large-scale problems involving thousands of long sequences, where computational efficiency and memory usage become critical constraints.

Second, although the proposed framework is designed to balance multiple alignment objectives, its advantages are more pronounced in datasets with moderate to high complexity and structural variability. In very short or highly conserved sequence sets (e.g., small tRNA families), where the alignment space is relatively simple, the benefits of adaptive multi-objective optimization may be less significant compared to lightweight heuristic methods.

Third, the performance of the framework is influenced by the interaction between its components, particularly the SAC-based adaptive controller and the fuzzy objective formulation. Although the parameter sensitivity analysis in Sect. “Parameter sensitivity analysis” demonstrates overall robustness, the method may still require careful parameter tuning when applied to datasets with highly atypical characteristics or extreme heterogeneity.

In terms of applicability boundaries, MOFSACEO-MSA is particularly well-suited for complex alignment scenarios characterized by high sequence diversity, structural constraints, and competing optimization criteria. However, for ultra-large datasets or real-time applications where computational efficiency is the primary concern, simpler or highly optimized heuristic approaches may remain preferable.

Future work will focus on addressing these limitations by exploring parallel and distributed implementations to improve scalability, developing more efficient policy-learning strategies to reduce reinforcement learning overhead, and investigating adaptive data-driven mechanisms for automatic parameter configuration. In addition, extending the framework to incorporate structural priors or pretrained sequence embeddings may further enhance performance, particularly in challenging biological contexts.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

To better understand the practical behavior of the proposed fuzzy multi-objective formulation, we conducted a systematic parameter sensitivity analysis focusing on four key membership parameters: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{M}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{G}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{S}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{S}$$\end{document}. These parameters govern how mismatches, gap penalties, and alignment scores are transformed into fuzzy membership values (Sect. “Fuzzy membership function”), and therefore directly influence the balance between alignment accuracy, structural compactness, and robustness to sequence variability.

A central aspect of the score-based membership function is its dataset-dependent scaling. The upper bound \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{S}$$\end{document} represents the theoretical maximum alignment score for a given dataset and depends on the number of sequences, alignment length, and match reward. The midpoint parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{S}$$\end{document} is defined relative to this bound (i.e., as a fraction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{S}$$\end{document}), ensuring that the region of highest sensitivity adapts naturally to the scoring scale of each dataset. In contrast, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{S}$$\end{document} controls the steepness of the transition and is treated as a normalized coefficient that regulates selection pressure independently of absolute score magnitude. This distinction enables a flexible yet stable parameterization across datasets with very different characteristics.

Given the diversity of RNA sequence families—ranging from highly structured and conserved RNAs to long, heterogeneous non-coding sequences—a single fixed parameter configuration is unlikely to be universally optimal. Therefore, this analysis aims to (i) quantify the influence of each parameter on alignment outcomes, (ii) identify their relative importance, and (iii) provide interpretable, dataset-specific parameter configurations.

Parameter priority and dataset-specific recommendations

The results in Table 10 reveal clear and consistent patterns across datasets. For RF03962, which represents structurally conserved RNA sequences, the strict (and optimal) configuration achieves the highest SP (20372) and lowest gap ratio (2.78%), confirming that strong constraints are beneficial in this context. Notably, the performance of the proposed method is comparable to, and in some aspects more structurally consistent than, MAFFT.

For the GtRNAdb dataset, a slightly more flexible configuration yields the best results, indicating that even highly conserved sequences benefit from moderate relaxation of constraints. In contrast, the Dictybase dataset demonstrates a fundamentally different behavior: flexible parameter settings significantly improve SP and CS scores compared to strict configurations, highlighting the importance of adaptability in heterogeneous RNA families.

Overall, the sensitivity analysis establishes a clear hierarchy among the parameters. The gap sensitivity parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{G}$$\end{document} has the strongest impact on alignment structure, followed by the mismatch parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{M}$$\end{document}, which primarily influences alignment accuracy. Importantly, the results demonstrate that parameter effects are both dataset-dependent and interpretable, enabling principled tuning rather than trial-and-error adjustment. For conserved RNA families, stricter parameter settings are recommended to preserve structural integrity, whereas for heterogeneous datasets, more flexible configurations provide improved robustness and alignment quality.

In summary, the proposed fuzzy membership framework is not only theoretically well-founded but also practically tunable. By linking parameter behavior to biological characteristics of the data, the method offers a transparent and adaptable approach to multiple sequence alignment across diverse RNA scenarios.

Conclusion and future work

In this study, we introduced MOFSACEO-MSA, a novel hybrid framework for multiple sequence alignment that integrates fuzzy multi-objective evaluation with the Equilibrium Optimizer (EO) and a Soft Actor–Critic (SAC)–based adaptive control mechanism. The proposed method was designed to explicitly address the inherent multi-objective nature of MSA by jointly optimizing alignment accuracy, gap management, and column-level conservation, while dynamically adapting search behavior during optimization.

Comprehensive experimental evaluations conducted on diverse RNA datasets—including structured families from Rfam, large-scale heterogeneous collections from RNAcentral and GenBank, and organism-specific tRNA datasets from GtRNAdb—demonstrated the effectiveness and robustness of MOFSACEO-MSA. Across classical alignment tools, metaheuristic approaches, and reinforcement learning–based methods, the proposed framework consistently achieved competitive or superior Sum-of-Pairs (SP) scores, while maintaining lower gap penalties, higher column conservation, and more compact alignments. In particular, comparisons with RLALIGN highlighted that MOFSACEO-MSA attains comparable alignment accuracy without relying on excessive gap insertion or alignment expansion, reflecting a more biologically plausible optimization behavior.

The integration of fuzzy membership functions enabled flexible and interpretable aggregation of heterogeneous objectives, while the EO–SAC synergy provided both global exploration and adaptive exploitation. This combination proved especially beneficial for complex and large-scale datasets, where purely heuristic or purely learning-based methods often exhibit instability or sensitivity to hyperparameter settings.

Despite these promising results, several directions remain open for future research. First, incorporating RNA secondary structure information, such as base-pairing probabilities or thermodynamic stability scores, into the fuzzy objective formulation may further enhance alignment quality in structurally conserved regions. Second, extending the SAC controller to a multi-agent or hierarchical reinforcement learning setting could improve scalability and convergence in ultra-large datasets. Third, future work may explore transfer learning or continual learning strategies to reuse knowledge across related RNA families, reducing computational cost and improving generalization. Finally, integrating MOFSACEO-MSA into downstream bioinformatics pipelines—such as phylogenetic inference, RNA structure prediction, or functional annotation—would provide additional insight into its practical impact and biological relevance.
