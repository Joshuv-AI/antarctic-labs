Towards Tight Bounds for Streaming Attention
Justin Y. Chen MIT Ying Feng MIT Piotr Indyk MIT Michael Kapralov EPFL Ekaterina Kochetkova EPFL Boris Prokhorov EPFL

Abstract
The attention mechanism is a cornerstone of modern transformer architectures. However, its expressive power comes at the cost of quadratic runtime and linear space usage. In particular, the classical transformer architecture explicitly stores all previously seen input elements (tokens) in order to generate the next one. The problem of implementing a transformer in limited space, known as KV cache compression , has received much interest over the past few years, spurring the development of powerful heuristics. Recent works of Haris et al, COLT’25 and Kochetkova et al, NeurIPS’25, formalized KV cache compression as the streaming attention approximation problem, providing both upper bounds (based on discrepancy theory) and information theoretic lower bounds. However, those papers left open a significant gap between the upper and lower bounds. For example, the space usage of their algorithms increases with the precision parameter, but the lower bound does not get stronger. In this work, we revisit the streaming attention approximation problem and provide nearly tight bounds on its space complexity. On the algorithmic side, we achieve the result through a surprisingly tight interplay between three distinct methods for kernel density estimation: discrepancy-based coreset constructions (e.g., Charikar-Kapralov-Waingarten’24), the polynomial method (e.g., Greengard-Rokhlin’87, Alman-Song’23), and space partitioning (e.g., Andoni-Laarhoven-Razenshteyn-Waingarten’17, Charikar-Kapralov-Nouri-Siminelakis’20). On the lower bound side, our main technical contribution is a new technique for using the INDEX problem with a large amount of side information that we hope will prove useful in other high dimensional geometric estimation problems.

Introduction

Transformer models
38

(1)

Technical Overview

In this section we define our model of computation and problems studied formally, present our results and outline the main techniques used to achieve them.

Throughout, we use
\operatorname{exp}(\cdot,\cdot)
to refer to the core kernel (henceforth referred to as the
softmax kernel
) associated with attention computation. For key and query vectors
k,q
,
\operatorname{exp}(k,q)=e^{\langle k,q\rangle}
. For the full key sequence
K=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})
,
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\langle k_{i},q\rangle}
is the sum of the kernel over all keys.
We will refer to
\exp(K, q)
as the
normalizing constant
as it serves the purpose of normalizing the weights
\operatorname{exp}(k_{i},q)
given to each value vector
v_{i}
in the attention equation (
Equation

1
). In the following technical overview we will mostly focus on the problem of computing
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)
, as the techniques needed for this task transfer to the more general attention problem with only minor modifications.

In both our upper and lower bounds, we will make use of polynomial expansions of the exponential function. Recall the standard Taylor expansion:
e^{x}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{l}}{l!}
. We denote the truncated kernels
\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(k,q):=\sum_{l=0}^{t}\frac{\langle k,q\rangle^{l}}{l!}
and
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q):=\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle k,q\rangle^{l}}{l!}
, such that:

\operatorname{exp}(K,q)=\sum_{k\in K}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\langle k,q\rangle^{l}}{l!}=\sum_{k\in K}\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(k,q)+\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q).

We start by formally defining the problem of computing the normalizing constant
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)
in the streaming model.
We note that this is exactly an instance of the
kernel density estimation problem
(KDE for short) that has received much attention in its own right in the recent literature
13
;
6
;
7
;
37
;
11
;
24
;
12
. We note, however, that most of the aforementioned works consider shift-invariant kernels, whereas softmax KDE is a version of maximum inner product search.

Definition 2.1

(Streaming softmax KDE, i.e. computing
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)
)
.

The algorithm receives keys
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a stream and at every point
j=1,\ldots,n
,
given any fixed
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, outputs an estimate
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{j},q)
of
\operatorname{exp}(K_{j},q)
that satisfies

\displaystyle|\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{j},q)-\operatorname{exp}(K_{j},q)|\leq\epsilon\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K_{j},q)

(3)

with probability at least
1-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
for any fixed query
q
. Here
K_{j}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{j})
is the sequence of keys received by time
j
.

Definition 2.2

(Streaming attention)
.

The algorithm receives a sequence of keys, values and queries
(k_{j},v_{j},q_{j}),j=1,\ldots,n
in a stream and at every point
j
the algorithm must output
\hat{z}_{j}
such that

\|\hat{z}_{j}-\mathsf{Attn}(K_{j},q_{j},V_{j})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\cdot\|\operatorname{softmax}(K_{j},q_{j})\|_{2}\|V_{j}\|_{F},

with probability at least
1-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, where
K_{j}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{j})
and
V_{j}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j})
.

2.1
High temperature regime (small

).

Our main result (see

To put this in perspective, it is useful to recall the collection of techniques that have been used for

In contrast to these results, we show that the

in

(setting

in (

, our upper bound yields space complexity of

\approx 1/\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Our techniques: upper bound.

Given a stream of at most
n
data points (denoted by
K
), the data structure maintains:

1.

a sketch
\sum_{k\in K}\psi(k)
for some feature map
\psi
, and

2.

a coreset
C\subset K
,

such that for any query
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some fixed integer

of our choice, one can use the sketch
\sum_{k\in K}\psi(k)
to approximate
\sum_{k\in K}\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(k,q)
, and the coreset
C
to approximate
\sum_{k\in K}\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)
.

We will give the constructions of
\psi
and
C
, and then show that there exists

such that the total space usage of

\sum_{k\in K}\psi(k)
and
C
is
\approx(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{\log\log(1/\eps)}{\log d}}
. Formally, we show the existence of a pair of embeddings
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
, for the keys and the queries respectively, that approximate the low degree part of the exponential function well:

\quantity|\Bigl\langle\sum_{k \in K} \psi_1(k), \psi_2(q) \Bigr\rangle- \sum_{k \in K} \expker_{\leq t}(k, q)|\leq\epsilon\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K,q),

and that
\sum_{k\in K}\psi_{1}(k)
can be stored in
\tilde{O}\Bigl(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\Bigr)
bits of space. The embeddings are simple: we store low moments of the dataset of keys, similarly to the Fast Multipole Method. The formal guarantees are given in
Lemma

5.1
in Section
5.1
.

Given the aforementioned sketch of the dataset
K
, namely
\sum_{k\in K}\psi_{1}(k)
, it remains to design a small space compression of
K
that allows one to approximate
\sum_{k\in K}\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)
. We accomplish this with a
coreset
(a small subset) of
K
for the truncated kernel
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}
. Specifically, we show that there exists
C\subset K
such that with high probability, for any fixed
q

\Bigl|\sum_{k\in K}\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)-\frac{|K|}{|C|}\cdot\sum_{k\in C}\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)\Bigr|\leq\epsilon\sum_{k\in K}\operatorname{exp}(k,q).

The existence of such a coreset follows by noting that for any integer
t\geq 0
, the kernel

\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q):=\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle k,q\rangle^{l}}{l!}

is positive definite. This means that if we construct an embedding of keys and queries into Hilbert space such that
(a)
the dot products in the Hilbert space are equal to the kernel similarities
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)
and
(b)
the Hilbert space norms of the embedded keys and queries are appropriately small, then by Banaszczyk’s theorem (a deep result in discrepancy theory
**
**

**

The idea of constructing coresets for kernel density estimation using Banaszczyk’s theorem was pioneered in
34
, and the recent work of
12
gave a powerful asymmetric construction of embeddings (i.e., different embeddings for keys and queries), leading to surprisingly efficient KDE data structures for smooth kernels.
),
there is a coreset that approximates the KDE value of the keys at every query well. The Hilbert space norms of the embeddings govern the size of the coreset (they bound the
\gamma_{2}
norm of the truncated kernel), and, intuitively, they are small exactly because
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}
is a truncated kernel. Specifically, a careful analysis yields Lemma

, which states that our truncated kernel
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}
admits an

-approximate coreset

that can be stored in space

\tilde{O}\left(\frac{e^{r^{2}}}{\epsilon}\cdot\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2l}}{l!}\right).

Finally, it remains to choose

to minimize the total space usage of the embedding based approach and the coreset size, i.e.

\min_{t}\left\{\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}+\frac{e^{r^{2}}}{\epsilon}\cdot\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2l}}{l!}\right\}.

(5)

The ultimate value of the cutoff point

that we use is

, resulting in a total space complexity of
\tilde{O}((1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{\log\log(1/\eps)}{\log d}+o(1)})
, as claimed.
The quick approximate derivation for this amounts to truncating the series to the dominant first term, ignoring the

and

For

for a constant

can be found in Section

Our techniques: lower bound.

Our lower bound uses a reduction from the communication problem of
INDEX
. In the
INDEX
problem Alice holds a binary string
\mathbf{x}
of length
n
and Bob has an index
i\in[n]
. Alice sends Bob a message
m
, after receiving which Bob should be able to output
\mathbf{x}_{i}
with a constant advantage over random guessing, i.e. with
1/2+\Omega(1)
success probability. The
INDEX
problem requires
\Omega(n)
communication from Alice to Bob, even if Alice and Bob share randomness and even if the input string
\mathbf{x}
contains exactly half zeros and half ones.
Although reductions from
INDEX
are commonly used in streaming algorithms, see e.g.,
23
;
20
;
27

We start by presenting a lower bound for approximating the normalizing constant
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)
in the streaming model, and then obtain a lower bound for approximating
\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)
by reduction.

Let
ALG
be a streaming algorithm that computes an approximation
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K,q)
to the normalizing constant
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)
as per Definition
2.1

\left|\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K,q)-\operatorname{exp}(K,q)\right|\leq\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K,q).

We show how to use
ALG
to obtain a communication efficient protocol for
INDEX

\mathbf{x}
of length
2n
.
Let
r^{\prime}:=r/2
, where
r
is the upper bound on the Euclidean norm of keys and queries in our setting. Using the shared randomness, Alice and Bob sample a point set
K=\{k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{2n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

. Upon receiving her input
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}

to Bob. In order to find out whether
\mathbf{x}_{i}=1
or
\mathbf{x}_{i}=0

\begin{split}\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)&=\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot e^{k_{i}^{T}q}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{2n}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot e^{k_{j}^{T}q}=\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot e^{r^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{2n}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot e^{k_{j}^{T}k_{i}},\end{split}

(6)

and therefore it is natural to hope that the sum over
j\neq i
contributes a ‘noise’ term that is smaller than
e^{r^{2}}
. If that were the case, Bob would simply threshold
\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)
, concluding that
\mathbf{x}_{i}=1
if
\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)\geq\theta
, and that
\mathbf{x}_{i}=0
if
\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)<\theta
, for a threshold
\theta
. This intuition is further supported by the fact that the random Gaussian vectors
k_{j}
and
k_{i}
are quite close to orthogonal with high probability. This is the approach used in
20
;
27
. However, one can verify that this intuition is only correct for extremely large values of
r
. Therefore this natural, but quite basic reduction is in fact not sufficiently powerful to give tight dependence on
r
and
1/\epsilon
.

Side information.

The problem with thresholding
\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)
in order to learn
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is that the noise term in (
6
) is too large. Therefore, we need to find a way to reduce it. To this end, Alice sends some side information about
\mathbf{x}
to Bob, in the form of the low-degree part of the dataset
K_{\mathbf{x}}
, essentially mimicking our upper bound approach. Just like in our upper bound, we write

\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)=\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\langle k,q\rangle^{l}}{l!}=\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(k,q)+\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)

and define feature maps
\psi_{1}(k)=(k^{\otimes l}/l!)_{l=0}^{t}
and
\psi_{2}(q)=(q^{\otimes l})_{l=0}^{t}
such that

\exp_{\leq t}(k,q)=\left\langle\psi_{1}(k),\psi_{2}(q)\right\rangle
.
Then Alice sends Bob the sketch
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{1}(k)
, from which Bob can compute

\exp_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)=\left\langle\sum_{k_{i}\in K}\psi_{1}(k),\psi_{2}(q)\right\rangle.

Signal to noise ratio and the Hermite expansion.

The hope for this approach is that the truncated softmax kernel
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,q)
has smaller values and therefore introduces less noise. Specifically, the truncated version of (
6
) now becomes

\begin{split}\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)&=\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{i},k_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{2n}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{j},k_{i}).\end{split}

(7)

The noise term contributed by the sum on the rhs is indeed smaller now.
However, so is the signal term
\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{i},k_{i})
.
In principle, it is not clear which of the terms is reduced faster. This concern turns out to be well founded: if we use Taylor expansions as above, the signal drops too fast relative to the noise for us to achieve the desired bound. However, a more careful choice of the polynomial expansion, specifically, the use of the Hermite basis, allows us to carry out this approach. The detailed analysis is presented in Section
5.2
.
Here we just mention that the upper and lower bounds match quite tightly because the expression for the upper bound (
Equation

5
) is the same as for the lower bound, namely

\tilde{\Omega}\quantity(\max_{t} \min\left\{ \frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}, \frac{1}{\eps}\cdot\sum_{l=t+1}^\infty\frac{r^{2l}}{l!} \right\}).

2.2
Low temperature regime (large
r
)

In this section we outline our results and techniques for the ‘low temperature’ regime of large
r
, namely when
r^{2}=\Omega(\log n)
. In this regime the exponential dependence on
r
becomes important, and we resolve the optimal dependence up to lower order terms in the exponent.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
r^{2}=\Omega(\log n)
, i.e.
e^{r^{2}}=n^{\Omega(1)}
. Then the Softmax KDE problem (
3
) can be solved using

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\quantity(\frac{e^{r^2(1 + o(1))}}{\eps})

memory. Furthermore, this bound is almost tight for any
d=(\log n)^{1+\Omega(1)}
, i.e. there is a

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}\quantity(\min\qty{n, \frac{e^{r^2(1 - o(1))}}{\eps}})

lower bound for the streaming space complexity.

Remark 2.2
.

This result also holds for the Streaming Attention problem (
Definition

2.2
), but the lower bound holds only under additional assumption
r^{2}<(\log n)/2
. See
Theorem

7.2
for the formal statement and
Sections

7.1
and
7.2
for details of the reduction.

Our starting point is the algorithm BalanceKV (
27
), an online discrepancy minimization method based on
3
that maintains a coreset approximating the dataset in all directions simultaneously. While this mechanism uses basic geometric properties of the dataset such as the points being bounded by
r
in Euclidean norm, it is largely oblivious to the distribution of keys inside the Euclidean ball of radius
r
. In particular, for inputs consisting of tight clusters, it is strictly more effective to run separate instances of BalanceKV on each cluster rather than on the dataset as a whole. This is the observation that leads to our improvement over
27
in terms of the dependence on
r
, which we also show to be optimal. We now give more details of the approach.

Pseudorandom datasets.

A key tool in our algorithm is a
pseudo-randomization procedure
that decomposes the dataset into two types of components: random-like datasets and dense datasets contained in balls whose radius is smaller by a nontrivial factor, e.g., by a factor of
2
. We show that both types admit very compact coresets. The precise definition we use follows
4
(see also
11
):

Definition 2.3

(Pseudo-random dataset; slightly specialized version of Definition
6.1
)
.

A dataset
K
of
n
keys of Euclidean norm bounded by
r
is
(r,\Delta,\tau)
-
pseudo-random
††
††

††

Our definition follows
4
;
11
, but with
K
in a ball instead of a sphere. This makes our notion somewhat counterintuitive. For instance, a dataset that is highly concentrated around
c
is pseudo-random. We nevertheless retain the term to emphasize the connection with the earlier literature.
if

\displaystyle\forall q,\|q\|_{2}\leq r\quad|\{k\in K\mid\left\langle k,q\right\rangle>\Delta r^{2}\}|\leq\tau n.

(8)

We find the dense regions as per (
8
), i.e. small spherical caps containing a nontrivial fraction of the points, in the input dataset and recurse on them. These dense regions are by definition contained in small spherical caps of the original Euclidean ball of radius
r
, and therefore each such region can be enclosed by a Euclidean ball of a constant factor lower radius. Once the radius is reduced by at least a factor of
2
in the sequence of recursive calls, we can stop the recursion because a stronger coreset for the dataset in question can be constructed – see discussion below. The other termination condition is that the dataset is pseudorandom as per Definition
2.3
. In this case, we have a strong lower bound on the value of our softmax estimate. Intuitively, the dataset is close to random with respect to the query, in the sense that softmax KDE value cannot be much smaller than for a random dataset; see
Lemma

6.3
for more details. This stronger lower bound on softmax KDE value directly implies stronger coreset size bounds due to the multiplicative nature of our approximation guarantee.

We first present the coreset construction that we will invoke later in the recursive analysis. Although our main object is the kernel
\operatorname{exp}(\cdot,\cdot)
, it is useful to formulate the lemma for a general kernel
\gamma
, since, during the recursive calls, we work with shifted and rescaled versions of
\operatorname{exp}

Lemma
(Restated from Lemma

)
Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive definite kernel, and let

be a predefined subset of the domain. There is a randomized algorithm

which receives

and

\textsc{BaseCompress}(\gamma,K)
outputs

\left|\gamma(K,q)-2\gamma(\widetilde{K},q)\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\max_{u\in U}\gamma(u,u)\cdot\max_{v\in V}\gamma(v,v)}\cdot\log(|K|/\delta)\right).

\textsc{BaseCompress}(\gamma,K)
runs in
O(|K|^{2}\cdot T)
time, where
T
is the maximum time to access
\gamma(k,q)
.

This lemma crucially relies on algorithms for discrepancy minimization that have been the subject of a beautiful line of work in theoretical computer science, e.g.

;

;

3
.

Low radius dataset as a leaf.

In this case our algorithm for constructing coresets crucially exploits the asymmetry in Lemma
4.3
above, i.e. the fact that the error term on the right hand side depends on the product of kernel norms
\sqrt{\gamma(u,u)}
of
u\in U
and
\sqrt{\gamma(v,v)}
of
v\in V
.

To see why this asymmetry helps, compare a dataset
K\subset B(c,r/2)
(
B(c,r/2)
stands for the Euclidean ball centered at
c
with radius
r/2
) with its recentered version
K-c\subset B(0,r/2)
. Finding coresets for
K
and
K-c
is essentially the same approximation problem, since

\exp(K,q)=\exp(\langle c,q\rangle)\cdot\exp(K-c,q),

so any weighted coreset for one yields the same multiplicative guarantee for the other. Yet, since our coreset construction primitive uses embeddings of keys to determine the coreset, the actual choice of embeddings matters a lot. When
K
is far from the origin, the common offset
c
creates a large global bias in the kernel values and embeddings, worsening approximation quality. After recentering
K
to
K-c
and rescaling
K-c
and
q
so that both fit inside a smaller radius ball, however, this shared bias disappears, and the algorithm only has to capture the local variation inside the cluster. The fact that the small cluster has low radius leads to a smaller coreset.

More precisely, any dense part
K^{\prime}
of the dataset partition
\mathcal{K}
given by
Lemma

6.2
is contained in a ball
B(c^{\prime},r^{\prime})
much smaller than the query domain
B(0,r)
. If we first shift and rescale it to

\sqrt{\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}}\,(K^{\prime}-c^{\prime}),

and rescale the query domain accordingly, then both the key and query domains lie in

B(0,\sqrt{rr^{\prime}})
. Applying our basic coreset construction in these coordinates
yields a multiplicative approximation error proportional to

\frac{\exp(\langle c',q\rangle)\cdot\exp(rr')}{\exp(\langle c',q\rangle)\cdot\operatorname{exp}\left(\sqrt{\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}}(K^{\prime}-c^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}}q\right)}=\frac{\exp(rr')}{\operatorname{exp}\left(\sqrt{\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}}(K^{\prime}-c^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}}q\right)}.

Since we do not assume any additional structure of the shifted and rescaled
dataset or query, the best universal lower bound on the denominator is

\exp(-rr')
. Thus the multiplicative error is bounded by

\frac{\exp(rr')}{\exp(-rr')}=\exp(2rr').

By contrast, applying the coreset construction directly to the original dataset
only guarantees an error term of order

\exp\bigl(r^{2}/2+(r^{\prime}+\|c^{\prime}\|_{2})^{2}/2\bigr)\geq\exp(r^2).

Since the best universal lower bound on
\operatorname{exp}(K^{\prime},q)
is
\exp(-r^2)
, this
gives the worst-case multiplicative guarantee

\frac{\exp(r^2)}{\exp(-r^2)}=\exp(2r^2),

which is worse than
\exp(2rr')
as
r^{\prime}<r
.

See
Figure

6.1
,
Lemma

6.4
for more details. Finally, we would like to note that while the step getting us from a coreset construction, or, rather, a compression algorithm, to an actual streaming algorithm is essentially an application of the classical Merge-and-Reduce framework, this application is nontrivial, for example, in how it interacts with the pseudorandomification procedure above – see

for more details.

Pseudorandom dataset as a leaf.

Open Problems

Our work naturally leads to several interesting open questions.

•

Adaptivity: KV caching is often deployed in scenarios where the input tokens are generated sequentially via a feedback loop, where the attention mechanism is iteratively reapplied to the tokens produced earlier. In this setting, the input stream is generated adaptively, so it would be interesting to develop adversarially robust
10
streaming algorithms for this task.

•

Gaussian kernel density estimation: the currently best results for Gaussian kernel density estimation
11
use independent random sampling, incurring a
1/\epsilon^{2}
dependence on the precision parameter
\epsilon
. Recent work of
12
was able to use discrepancy methods together with geometric space partitioning instead, achieving a
1/\epsilon
dependence for
smooth
kernels (kernels decaying inverse polynomially with distance). There are challenges in extending the approach of
12
beyond smooth kernels, but we hope that our present work for the softmax kernel, which is intimately connected to the Gaussian kernel, may offer insights.

4.1
Multivariate Polynomials

In this section, we formalize the polynomial sketching primitive used throughout the paper.
Proposition

4.1
bounds the number of monomials in a degree-
t
polynomial, while
Lemma

4.2
uses this bound to obtain a finite-dimensional embedding for degree-
t
polynomials in the inner product. These together give a compact way to store the low-degree part of the kernel and will be used repeatedly later in the paper.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a multivariate polynomial with degree at most
t
. The number of monomials of
f
is at most

\displaystyle{d-1\choose d-1}+{d\choose d-1}+\dots+{d+t-1\choose d-1}={d+t\choose t}\leq\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}.

Moreover, when
d=d(t)=\omega(t^{2})
, this bound is
(1+o_{t}(1))\frac{d^{t}}{t!}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\gamma(k,q):=\sum_{l=0}^{t}a_{l}\left\langle k,q\right\rangle^{l}
be a degree-
t
polynomial in the inner product
\langle k,q\rangle
for some
t
and
a_{l}
. Fix an arbitrary
r>0
. There exist embeddings
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
such that for any finite set
K\subset\mathcal{B}_{r}
and any
q\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
, we guarantee

\quantity|\Bigl\langle\sum_{k \in K} \psi_1(k), \psi_2(q) \Bigr\rangle- \sum_{k \in K} \gamma(k, q)|\leq\delta,

Moreover,
\sum_{k\in K}\psi_{1}(k)
can be stored in
O\Bigl(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\log\frac{|K|\cdot(d+t)^{t}\cdot\max_{l}|a_{l}|\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t}}{\delta}\Bigr)
bits of space, and
\psi_{1}(k)
can be evaluated in
O\Bigl(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\log\frac{|K|\cdot(d+t)^{t}\cdot\max_{l}|a_{l}|\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t}}{\delta}\Bigr)
time.

Proof.

Fixing
K
, we can write
\sum_{k\in K}\gamma(k,q)
as a function of
q
is a multivariate polynomial from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
with degree at most
t
.
We let

\psi_{1}(k):=\quantity(a_l \cdot k^{{\otimes l}})_{l=\overline{0..t}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi_{2}(q):=\quantity(q^{{\otimes l}})_{l=\overline{0..t}}

where
x^{\otimes k}
denotes the
k
-th tensor power of the vector of
x
, then

\gamma(k,q)=\sum_{l=0}^{t}a_{l}\left\langle k,q\right\rangle^{l}=\sum_{l=0}^{t}a_{l}\left\langle k^{\otimes l},q^{\otimes l}\right\rangle=\left\langle\psi_{1}(k),\psi_{2}(q)\right\rangle.

Using
Proposition

4.1
, the embedding dimension of
\psi_{1}
after gluing together repeating monomials is
m=O\quantity(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!})
. Regarding the bit required per coordinate, we round the embedding vectors and argue that it gives the desired accuracy of approximation. Every coordinate of
k^{{\otimes l}}
,
q^{{\otimes l}}
are bounded by
\max\{r,1\}^{t}
.
Suppose we store a rounded vector
z
such that
\|z-\sum_{k\in K}\psi_{1}(k)\|_{\infty}\leq\eta
, then

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Bigl\langle z,\psi_{2}(q)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\sum_{k\in K}\psi_{1}(k),\psi_{2}(q)\Bigr\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot\|z-\sum_{k\in K}\psi_{1}(k)\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\psi_{2}(q)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot\eta\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t}

Therefore, to guaranty the total error
\delta_{0}
, we can pick
\eta=\frac{\delta_{0}}{m\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t}}
. Bounding over the sum of all keys, the bit complexity to store a single coordinate for total error
\delta_{1}
is

O\quantity(\log\frac{ |K|\max_l |a_l| \max\{r, 1\}^t \cdot(m\cdot\max\{r, 1\}^t)}{\delta_1}).

Multiplying with
m=O\quantity(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!})
gives the final space complexity. The evaluation time of
\psi_{1}
is the time to write down these bits, thus is the same as the space complexity.

4.2
Coresets for kernel density estimation (KDE)

Our upper bounds in both the high- and low-temperature regimes are based on discrepancy minimization for KDE. If
\gamma
is positive definite (
Definition

4.1
), then
\gamma(k,q)
can be written as the inner product of embeddings
\psi(k)
and
\psi(q)
in a Hilbert space
H
. Viewed this way, constructing a coreset that approximates
\gamma(K,q)
for an unknown query

amounts to selecting a coreset of vectors
\{\psi(k)\}_{k\in K}
whose sum has approximately the same projection as
\sum_{k\in K}\psi(k)
onto an unknown direction. This is precisely the

, and in

we formalize this connection and use it to build a compression algorithm.

Definition 4.1
.

\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive definite kernel if there exists a feature map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to H

such that
\gamma(x,y)=\left\langle\psi(x),\psi(y)\right\rangle_{H}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive definite kernel, and let
U\times V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a predefined subset of the domain. There is a randomized algorithm
BaseCompress
which receives
\gamma
and
K\subset U
as inputs.
\textsc{BaseCompress}(\gamma,K)
outputs
\widetilde{K}\subset K
such that
|\widetilde{K}|\leq|K|/2
and for any
q\in V

1-\delta
,

\left|\gamma(K,q)-2\gamma(\widetilde{K},q)\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\max_{u\in U}\gamma(u,u)\cdot\max_{v\in V}\gamma(v,v)}\cdot\log(|K|/\delta)\right).

\textsc{BaseCompress}(\gamma,K)
runs in
O(|K|^{2}\cdot T)
time, where
T
is the maximum time to access
\gamma(k,q)
.

Remark 4.1
.

In this paper, we by default apply

to the softmax kernel. Therefore, we omit the first parameter
\gamma
and write
\textsc{BaseCompress}(K)
.

Proof.

We reduce the problem to the inner-product setting, where the following result is known:

Theorem 4.4

(Theorem 9 of
12
. Adapted from
3
)
.

Let
\gamma_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
\gamma_{0}(k,q):=\langle k,q\rangle
and let
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. There is a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which receives
K\subset U
as input, and outputs
\widetilde{K}\subset K
such that
|\widetilde{K}|\leq|K|/2
and for any fixed

1-\delta

\left|\gamma_{0}(K,q)-2\gamma_{0}(\widetilde{K},q)\right|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\max_{u\in U}\gamma_{0}(u,u)\cdot\max_{v\in V}\gamma_{0}(v,v)}\cdot\log(|K|/\delta)\right).

Furthermore,
\mathcal{A}
does not require explicit access to
K,q
; it only needs oracle access to
\gamma_{0}(k,q)
.
\mathcal{A}
runs in
O(|K|^{2}\cdot T)
time, where
T
is the maximum time to access
\gamma_{0}(k,q)
.

It remains to reduce our setting to this inner-product case. Since
\gamma
is positive definite, there exists a feature map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to H
into a Hilbert space
H
such that

\gamma(x,y)=\langle\psi(x),\psi(y)\rangle_{H}\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We consider the subspace
\mathcal{P}
spanned by
\{\psi(k):k\in K\}
and
\psi(q)
. Choosing an orthonormal

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}
isometrically with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some

. Under this identification, each
\psi(k)
and
\psi(q)
becomes a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}

\gamma(k,q)=\langle\psi(k),\psi(q)\rangle_{H}=\langle\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}=\gamma_{0}(\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q))

Therefore,

\mathcal{A}
only requires oracle access to inner products, which is exactly the kernel evaluations
\gamma(k,q)

for

being the evaluation time of
\gamma(k,q)
.

4.3
Hermite Polynomials

Let
\{\text{He}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
denote the sequence probabilists’ Hermite polynomials; i.e.

\text{He}_{l}(x)=(-1)^{l}e^{x^{2}/2}\cdot\frac{d^{l}}{dx^{l}}(e^{-x^{2}/2}).

And let
\varphi_{l,\lambda}(x)
denote the rescaled version
\varphi_{l,\lambda}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{l!}}\text{He}_{l}\quantity(x / \lambda)
with rescaling factor
\lambda>0
. Below are all the facts we use about Hermite polynomials. Proposition
4.6
is the fundamental property of Hermite polynomials (see, e.g., Corollary 3.1 of
1
). Proposition
4.7
and
4.9
can be found in Chapter 18 of
16
.

Proposition 4.6
.

For any
\lambda>0
,
\{\varphi_{l,\lambda}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in

for
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2})
.

Proposition 4.7

(Explicit Coefficient Formula)
.

\text{He}_{l}(x)
is a polynomial of degree exactly
l
, with the explicit formula
\text{He}_{l}(x)=l!\sum_{h=0}^{\left\lfloor l/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{h}}{h!2^{h}}\frac{x^{l-2h}}{(l-2h)!}
.

Corollary 4.8
.

\text{He}_{l}(x)=x^{l}-\frac{l(l-1)}{2}x^{l-2}+l!\sum_{h=2}^{\left\lfloor l/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{h}}{h!2^{h}}\frac{x^{l-2h}}{(l-2h)!}
. When
x=\omega(l)
, the last term is upper bound by

, so
\text{He}_{l}(x)=x^{l}\pm O(l^{2}x^{l-2})=(1\pm o(1))x^{l}
.

Proposition 4.9

(Generating Function)
.

The exponential generating function of
\text{He}_{l}(x)
is

\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{l}}{l!}\text{He}_{l}(x)=e^{xz-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}.

Corollary 4.10
.

Substituting
x
with
x/\lambda
and
z
with
\lambda
, the generating function gives

e^{x}=e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{\sqrt{l!}}\varphi_{l,\lambda}(x).

Corollary 4.11

For any
x
, the function

.

Proof.

Substituting
x
with
x-s
, the generating function gives

\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{l}}{l!}\text{He}_{l}(x-s)=e^{(x-s)z-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}.

For

On the other hand,

by Taylor’s theorem.
Comparing the coefficients of

gives

This, multiplied with
\frac{2e^{r^{2}}}{\epsilon}
, is the second term in the upper bound of the space. We also observe that
2e^{r^{2}}=(1/\epsilon)^{o(1)}
in our regime. Therefore, the total space usage is

\tilde{O}(1/\epsilon\cdot(1/\epsilon)^{o(1)}\cdot(1/\epsilon)^{-\frac{\log\log(1/\eps)}{\log d}(1+o(1))}.

In particular, because
d\leq\log^{a}n
and
\log(1/\epsilon)=\Theta(\log n)
,
we have

\frac{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}{\log d}\geq\frac{1}{a}-o(1),

thus the space usage is

\tilde{O}\!\left((1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}+o(1)}\right)
as claimed.

Remark 5.1
.

As shown in
Lemma

5.1
and
Lemma

5.2
, the update time of this algorithm is at most quadratic in the storage space.

5.2
Lower Bound

We will use a reduction from the communication problem of
\texttt{INDEX}_{2n,n}
to prove a space lower bound on the Streaming KDE problem (
Definition

2.1
).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
S_{n,d,r,\epsilon}
denote a deterministic upper bound on the space, measured in bits, of any (randomized) data structure for the Streaming KDE problem with parameters
n,d,r,\epsilon
and a failure probability
1/100
.
Assume that
r=\log^{o(1)}n
,
d=\Theta(\log^{a}n)
for some constant
a>2
, and
n^{-100}<\epsilon<n^{-c}
for an arbitrarily small constant
c>0
.

Then, there always exists some
m
such that
\min\{(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}-o(1)},n\}\leq m\leq n
, and that there is a communication protocol with a message size
S_{n,d,r,\epsilon}+o(m)
which solves the
\texttt{INDEX}_{2m,m}
problem with probability at least
9/10
.

This lemma, combined with the bound
R^{\text{pub},\to}(\texttt{INDEX}_{2m,m})=\Omega(m)
from
Theorem

4.12
implies the lower bound of
Theorem

2.1
.
The rest of the section is dedicated to proving
Lemma

5.3
.

The reduction.

Assume that we have chosen an appropriate
m
, which we will describe later. Let
r^{\prime}:=r/2
. Using the public randomness, Alice and Bob agree on a point set
K=\{k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{2m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
k_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left\lparen 0,\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{d}I_{d}\right\rparen
i.i.d. for each
k_{i}\in K
.
Given her input string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{2m}
from the
\texttt{INDEX}_{2m,m}
problem, Alice defines a corresponding dataset
K_{\mathbf{x}}=\{k_{i}\in K\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}=1\}
. Then she constructs her message to Bob, which contains two components:
for the first component, Alice builds a streaming KDE data structure with parameters
n,d,r,\epsilon
. This takes
S_{n,d,r,\epsilon}
bits. Note that for
m\leq n
, such a KDE data structure can indeed solve the instance with the dataset
K_{\mathbf{x}}
. Also, note that the use of the data structure requires that the input points are promised to have norm at most
r
. On the other hand, the point set
K
contains vectors which are sampled from
\mathcal{N}\left\lparen 0,\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{d}I_{d}\right\rparen
with unbounded worst-case norm. We make the following standard argument:

Claim 5.4
.

With
.99
probability over the random choice of
K
, all vectors
k\in K
satisfy

\|k\|_{2}\leq r
.
Similarly, for any single
v\sim\mathcal{N}\left\lparen 0,\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{d}I_{d}\right\rparen
, with
.99
probability,
\|v\|_{2}=(1\pm O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}))r^{\prime}

Proof.

We write each
k_{i}\in K
as
k_{i}=\frac{r^{\prime}}{\sqrt{d}}g_{i}
for i.i.d.
g_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then applying
Proposition

4.13
and a union bound give

\|g_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\pm\Theta(\sqrt{\log m})=\sqrt{d}\pm O(\sqrt{\log n})
simultaneously for all
g_{i}
with
.99
chance. On this event,
\|k_{i}\|_{2}\leq\frac{r^{\prime}}{\sqrt{d}}(\sqrt{d}\pm O(\sqrt{\log n}))\leq r
for
d\gg\log n
and sufficiently large
n
.

Similarly, applying
Proposition

4.13
on scaled
v
gives
\|v\|_{2}\leq\frac{r^{\prime}}{\sqrt{d}}(\sqrt{d}\pm\Theta(1))
, which is
(1\pm O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}))r^{\prime}
as claimed.
∎

For the second component of Alice’s message, similar to the upper bound proof, Alice defines a feature map associated with the degree-
t
truncation of the exponential kernel and sends the aggregated sketch of
K_{\mathbf{x}}
. However, instead of truncating the Taylor expansion as in
Lemma

5.1
, here we will truncate the Hermite expansion of the exponential kernel. Concretely, recall from
Corollary

4.10
that the exponential function can be written as

e^{x}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}c_{l}\varphi_{l,\lambda}(x),

where
c_{l,\lambda}:=e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{\sqrt{l!}}
and
\varphi_{l,\lambda}
is the rescaled
l
-th Hermite polynomial with rescaling factor
\lambda
.
We will set
\lambda=\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{\sqrt{d}}
.
We denote

T_{\leq t}(x):=\sum_{l=0}^{t}c_{l,\lambda}\varphi_{l,\lambda}(x),

and

T_{>t}(x):=\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}c_{l,\lambda}\varphi_{l,\lambda}(x).

The goal of the second component is to allow Bob to approximate

\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{\leq t}(\langle k,q\rangle)
at his query
q
. We formalize this in the following lemma:

Lemma 5.5
.

For any integer
t\geq 0
, there exist embeddings
\psi_{A},\psi_{B}
such that

\left\lvert\left\langle\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(q)\right\rangle-\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{\leq t}(\langle k,q\rangle)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon m,

and that
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k)
can be stored in
O\left\lparen\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\left\lparen t\log(d+t)+\log(n/\eps)\right\rparen\right\rparen
bits of space.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

4.2
, it suffices to give polynomial bounds on the coefficients
\{a_{l}\}_{l=0}^{t}
of
T_{\leq t}
.
The coefficients of
T_{\leq t}
have magnitude upper bounded by
t\cdot\max_{l}^{t}|c_{l,\lambda}|
times the maximum coefficient norm of
\varphi_{l,\lambda}
. These coefficients are given in
Proposition

4.7
, therefore this expands to:

\displaystyle\max_{l=0}^{t}|a_{l}|

\displaystyle=t\max_{l=0}^{t}\left\lvert e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{\sqrt{l!}}\left\lparen\sqrt{l!}\sum_{h=0}^{\left\lfloor l/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{h}}{h!2^{h}}\frac{(1/\lambda)^{l-2h}}{(l-2h)!}\right\rparen\right\rvert

\displaystyle=t\max_{l=0}^{t}\left\lvert e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\left\lparen\sum_{h=0}^{\left\lfloor l/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{h}}{h!2^{h}}\frac{\lambda^{2h}}{(l-2h)!}\right\rparen\right\rvert.

Recall that we chose
\lambda=r^{\prime 2}/\sqrt{d}
. Since
d=\Omega(\log^{a}n),a>2
and
r^{\prime}=(\log n)^{o(1)}
, we have
\lambda=o(1)<1
for all sufficiently large
n
.
Therefore,

\max_{l=0}^{t}|a_{l}|\leq O(t)\cdot\max_{l=0}^{t}\left\lvert\sum_{h=0}^{\left\lfloor l/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{h}}{h!2^{h}}\frac{1}{(l-2h)!}\right\rvert\leq O(t).

∎

After receiving the KDE data structure of
K_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k)
, Bob queries the data structure with
q
and computes
\langle\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(q)\rangle
, where
q=k_{j}\cdot\frac{r^{\prime}}{\|k_{j}\|}
for
j\in[2n]
being his input in the
INDEX
problem.

With
.99
chance, the KDE data structure outputs
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)
with

|\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)-\operatorname{exp}(K_{x},q)|\leq\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)\leq\Theta(\epsilon m).

In the last inequality we used
\exp(k, q)\leq e^{r^{2}}=n^{o(1)}=\epsilon^{o(1)}
and rescaled
\epsilon\to\epsilon^{1-o(1)}
to unclutter notation. This doesn’t affect the final bound since
o(1)
is already in the exponent.
Equivalently,

\left|\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)-\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\left(T_{\leq t}(\langle k,q\rangle)+T_{>t}(\langle k,q\rangle)\right)\right|\leq\Theta(\epsilon m),

thus

\left|\left(\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)-\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{\leq t}(\langle k,q\rangle)\right)-\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q\rangle)\right|\leq\Theta(\epsilon m).

Since
\langle\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(q)\rangle
approximates
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{\leq t}(\langle k,q\rangle)
up to
\epsilon m
error,

S:=\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)-\left\langle\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(q)\right\rangle

approximates
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q\rangle)
up to
\Theta(\epsilon m)
. We argue that Bob can decide whether
x_{j}=1
(i.e., whether
k_{j}\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
) by thresholding
S
. This follows from the next lemma:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
q_{1}=k_{j_{1}}\cdot\frac{r^{\prime}}{\|k_{j_{1}}\|}
, for some
k_{j_{1}}\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
and let
q_{2}=k_{j_{2}}\cdot\frac{r^{\prime}}{\|k_{j_{2}}\|}
for some
k_{j_{2}}\in(K\setminus K_{\mathbf{x}})
. Given
t
such that

•

t=o(\sqrt{d})
,

•

m\frac{(t+1)!}{d^{t+1}}=o(1)
, and

•

\epsilon m=o(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!})
,

with
.96
probability,

\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)-\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{2}\rangle)=\omega(\epsilon m).

Observe that
q_{1}
corresponds to the case
x_{j}=1
and
q_{2}
corresponds to
x_{j}=0
. Therefore,
Lemma

5.6
implies that in these two cases,
S
has an
\omega(\epsilon m)
gap after tolerating
\Theta(\epsilon m)
approximation error, thus Bob can distinguish the two cases.

Proof (of
Lemma

5.6
).

It suffices to show that with high probability,

T_{>t}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle)=\omega(\epsilon m)+\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{2}\rangle)-\sum_{k\in(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j_{1}}\})}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle).

We proceed by lower bounding
T_{>t}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle)
(
5.7
) and upper bounding
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{2}\rangle)-\sum_{k\in(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{q_{1}\})}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)
(
5.8
).

Claim 5.7
.

In the success case of
5.4
,
T_{>t}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle)=\Omega\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right)
.

Proof.

Since
q_{1}
is a scaled version of
k_{j_{1}}
,
\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle=\|k_{j_{1}}\|_{2}\|q_{1}\|_{2}
.
Also, we note that
\|q_{1}\|_{2}=\|k_{j_{1}}\|_{2}\frac{r^{\prime}}{\|k_{j_{1}}\|_{2}}=r^{\prime}
by definition.
Therefore, by
5.4
applied to
\|k_{j_{1}}\|_{2}
,
\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle=\left(1\pm O(1/\sqrt{d})\right)r^{\prime 2}
.

Then using the Hermite polynomial expansion (
Corollary

4.10
), we can expand as follows:

\displaystyle T_{>t}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l=t+1}^{\infty}c_{l}\varphi_{l,\lambda}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\sum\limits_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{l!}\text{He}_{l}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\left(\underbrace{\frac{\lambda^{t+1}}{(t+1)!}\text{He}_{t+1}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)}_{=:\alpha}+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{l=t+2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{l!}\text{He}_{l}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)}_{=:\beta}\right)

We will show that
\alpha=\Omega\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right)
and
\beta\geq 0
. Then the conclusion follows immediately.
Since we set
\lambda=\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{\sqrt{d}}
and
t=o(\sqrt{d})
, in the success case of
5.4
,
\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda\geq\left(1-O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)\sqrt{d}=\omega(t)
.

Corollary

4.8
gives

\displaystyle\text{He}_{t+1}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)

\displaystyle=(1\pm o(1))(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)^{t+1},

so

\alpha=\Omega\left(\frac{\lambda^{t+1}}{(t+1)!}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)^{t+1}\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{\left(1-O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)^{2t+2}r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right).

The last inequality follows from
\left(1-O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)^{2t+2}\geq e^{-O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)(2t+2)}=e^{-o(1)}
.

It remains to show that
\beta\geq 0
. Let
f(s):=e^{(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)s-s^{2}/2}
, then the generating function (
Proposition

4.9
) implies

\beta=\sum\limits_{l=t+2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{l!}\text{He}_{l}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda)=f(\lambda)-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{t+1}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{l!}\text{He}_{l}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda);

applying
Corollary

4.11
at
s=0
, this is further
\beta=f(\lambda)-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{t+1}\frac{\lambda^{l}\cdot f^{(l)}(0)}{l!}.
On the other hand, by Taylor’s theorem (with Lagrange remainder),

f(\lambda)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{t+1}\frac{\lambda^{l}\cdot f^{(l)}(0)}{l!}+\frac{\lambda^{t+2}\cdot f^{(t+2)}(\xi)}{(t+2)!}

for some
\xi\in(0,\lambda)
. Together, these give

\beta=\frac{\lambda^{t+2}\cdot f^{(t+2)}(\xi)}{(t+2)!}
. At this point, we can apply
Corollary

4.11
again at
s=\xi
and get

\beta=\frac{\lambda^{t+2}\cdot\text{He}_{t+2}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda-\xi)f(\xi)}{(t+2)!}.

To reach our desired conclusion that
\beta\geq 0
, it suffices to argue that
\text{He}_{t+2}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda-\xi)\geq 0
. Indeed, this follows immediately from
\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle/\lambda-\xi=\Theta(\sqrt{d})-o(1)=\omega(t)
and an application of
Corollary

4.8
.

∎

Claim 5.8
.

With
.96
probability,
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{2}\rangle)-\sum_{k\in(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j_{1}}\})}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)=o\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right)
.

Proof.

Fixing
q_{1}
and over the randomness of any
k\neq k_{j_{1}}
,
\langle k,q_{1}\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2})
for
\lambda=\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{\sqrt{d}}
.
Note that
\lambda=o(1)
as we are in the parameter regime where
d
is super-polynomial in
r
.
Let
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2})
. Since
T_{>t}
contains no degree-zero Hermite component, by the orthonormality of the Hermite basis
\{\varphi_{l,\lambda}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\mu)
(
Proposition

4.6
), we have that
\mathbb{E}[T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)]=\langle T_{>t},1\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0
.
Therefore,

\text{\bf Var}\Bigl[T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)^{2}\right]=\|T_{>t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

By the same orthonormality, this equals the sum of squares of the Hermite coefficients, i.e.

\text{\bf Var}\Bigl[T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)\Bigr]=\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\left(e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\frac{\lambda^{l}}{\sqrt{l!}}\right)^{2},

which is
O\left(\frac{\lambda^{2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right)
as
\lambda^{2}=o(1)
. Then by Chebyshev’s inequality, with
.99
chance,

\left|\sum_{k\in(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j_{1}}\})}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)\right|=O\left(\sqrt{m\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2t+2}}{(t+1)!}}\right).

The exact same argument applies for
q_{2}
and all keys
k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
.
Therefore,

\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{2}\rangle)-\sum_{k\in(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j_{1}}\})}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{m}\cdot r^{\prime 2t+2}}{\sqrt{d^{t+1}(t+1)!}}\right)

which is
o\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right)
as long as
m\frac{(t+1)!}{d^{t+1}}=o(1)
, which holds by the parameter assumptions in
Lemma

5.6
.
∎

Concluding
5.7
,
5.8
, and using the last parameter assumption of
Lemma

5.6
, we see that

T_{>t}(\langle k_{j_{1}},q_{1}\rangle)=\Omega(\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!})=\omega(\epsilon m)+\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{2}\rangle)-\sum_{k\in(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j_{1}}\})}T_{>t}(\langle k,q_{1}\rangle)

as desired. This completes the proof of
Lemma

5.6
.
∎

With the above ingredients, we are ready to prove our main lemma of this section.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

The total failure probability of the reduction described above is the sum of
.01
from the KDE data structure,
.02
from
5.4
, and
.02
from Chebyshev’s inequalities.

Wrapping up the reduction, we next show how to choose
m
with
\min\{(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}-o(1)},n\}\leq m\leq n
and an appropriate truncation threshold
t
that (1) satisfies the premise of
Lemma

5.6
and (2) gives
o(m)
storage space for
Lemma

5.5
.

We describe our choice of
m
and
t
in separate cases, depending on how
n
compares with
(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}}
. In the first case, when
n\geq(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}+o(1)}
, we choose
t=\left\lfloor\frac{\log(1/\eps)-3\log\log(1/\eps)}{\log(d/r'^2)}\right\rfloor
. In the setting of
Theorem

2.1
,
1/\epsilon\ll n^{100}
,
r^{\prime}=(\log n)^{o(1)}
, and
d\geq\log^{a}n
for
a>2
, so such
t
satisfies
t=o(\sqrt{d})
. It remains to show that for such
t
, there is a nonempty interval of admissible values of
m
that satisfies the premise of
Lemma

5.6
and gives
o(m)
storage space for
Lemma

5.5
. Equivalently, there exists
m
such that

\left\lparen\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\left\lparen t\log(d+t)+\log(n/\eps)\right\rparen\right\rparen\ll m\ll\min\left\{\frac{d^{t+1}}{(t+1)!},\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}\right\}.

(10)

The lower bound side is at most
\Theta(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\log n)=\Theta(\frac{d^{t}}{t!}\log n)
. This is asymptotically smaller than
\frac{d^{t+1}}{(t+1)!}
because

\frac{d^{t+1}/(t+1)!}{d^{t}/t!}=\frac{d}{t+1}=\omega(\sqrt{d})=\omega(\log n).

Also, this is asymptotically smaller than
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}
because

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{d}\right)^{t}\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{t+1}\cdot\frac{d^{t}}{t!}.

By our choice of
t
,
\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{d})^{t}=\Omega(\log^{3}(1/\epsilon))
, which, multiplied by
\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{t+1}
gives
\Omega(\log^{2}(1/\epsilon))\gg\log n
. Summarizing these, we see that the lower and upper bounds in
Equation

10
indeed induce a non-empty interval. Moreover, in this interval,
m\gg\frac{d^{t}}{t!}\log n=(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{\log\log(1/\eps)}{\log d}+o(1)}
by Stirling (see similar computation in
Equation

9
), which for
d\geq\log^{a}n
becomes
(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}+o(1)}
. Therefore, we successfully find
m
that satisfy all of our requirements.

In the second case, when
n\leq(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}-o(1)}
, we automatically need to set
m=n
. It remains to choose
t=o(\sqrt{d})
such that
m=n
satisfies
Equation

10
. As shown in the previous case, the lower bound side of
Equation

10
is at most
\Theta(\frac{d^{t}}{t!}\log n)
; also
\frac{d^{t+1}/(t+1)!}{d^{t}/t!}=\omega(\log n)
. Therefore, there is a non-empty interval between the lower bound and the first term of the upper bound. We choose
t
such that
n
lies in this interval, and is arbitrarily close to the upper bound. That is, we choose

t=\left(\frac{a}{a-1}+o(1)\right)\frac{\log n}{\log d}=o(\sqrt{d}).

Then
\frac{d^{t}}{t!}\log n\ll n\ll\frac{d^{t+1}}{(t+1)!}
, and the
o(1)
term in
t
can be set arbitrarily small at the cost of pushing
n
closer to this upper bound. Now we look at the ratio between the two upper bound terms in
Equation

10
, namely,

\frac{\frac{1}{\epsilon}\cdot\frac{r^{\prime 2t+2}}{(t+1)!}}{\frac{d^{t+1}}{(t+1)!}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{d}\right)^{t+1}.

This is
\omega(1)
as long as
\log(1/\eps)\geq\left(\frac{a}{a-1}+o(1)\right)\log n+\omega(1)
, equivalently,
n\leq(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}-o(1)}
, which is exactly our case assumption. In summary, all premises of
Lemma

5.6
hold, and the side information from
Lemma

5.5
has size
o(m)
.

Finally, when
(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}-o(1)}\leq n\leq(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}+o(1)}
, we choose a slightly larger error parameter
\epsilon^{\prime}\geq\epsilon
with
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon^{1-o(1)}
, so that
n\geq(1/\epsilon)^{1-\frac{1}{a}+o(1)}
. In this way, we are back in the first case, and now we are working with a slightly larger error parameter. Since any data structure that gives
\epsilon
-relative error also gives
\epsilon^{\prime}
-relative error, the lower bound for
\epsilon^{\prime}
also applies to the original data structure.

6.1
Upper Bound

Our starting point is the algorithm BalanceKV (

Lemma
(Restated from Lemma
4.3
)
Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive definite kernel, and let

be a predefined subset of the domain. There is a randomized algorithm

We address this limitation via a

– an approach pioneered in work on data-dependent data structures

4
;

On pseudo-random subsets, the original balancing procedure is near-optimal, as no direction is preferred. For low radius subsets, however, we exploit local structure by recentering and treating each region as contained in a smaller ball, allowing
BaseCompress
to operate at the appropriate scale.

In
Section

we present the algorithm

(

In

Lemma

To see why this helps, compare a dataset

with its recentered version
K-c\subset B(0,r)
. Finding coresets for
K
and
K-c
is essentially the same approximation problem, since

\exp(K,q)=\exp(\langle c,q\rangle)\cdot\exp(K-c,q),

so any weighted coreset for one yields the same multiplicative guarantee for the other. Yet,
BaseCompress
has a better error bound on the centered dataset. When
K
is far from the origin, the common offset
c
creates a large global trend in the kernel values, and
BaseCompress
(K)
must spend part of its error budget tracking this trend. After recentering, this shared bias disappears, and the algorithm only has to capture the local variation inside the cluster. Similarly, although approximating
\exp(K,q)
and
\exp(\alpha K,q/\alpha)
for
\alpha<1
is equally difficult at the level of the kernel values, since

\exp(\langle k,q\rangle)=\exp(\langle\alpha k,q/\alpha\rangle),

behaves differently on the two instances, as the error does not scale linearly with the diameters of the domains.

We exploit this observation in

6.4
. Any dense part

of

so that both the key and query domains become

\exp(\langle c',q\rangle)\cdot\exp(rr')\leq\exp\bigl(r(r^{\prime}+\|c^{\prime}\|_{2})\bigr),

whereas applying

For pseudo-random sets in

\text{argmin}_{k\in B(0,r)}\exp(\langle k,q\rangle),

whereas pseudo-randomness rules out such concentration.

Lemma 6.3

(Coresets for pseudo-random datasets)
.

Assume that

Proof.

Denote

.
We get the desired

\displaystyle\quantity|\sum_{k \in K'} e^{\<\alpha(k - c'), q'>} - 2\sum_{k \in K'} e^{\<\alpha(k - c'), q'>}|

\displaystyle\quantity|\expker(K', q) - 2\expker(\tilde{K}', q)|

\displaystyle=e^{\langle c^{\prime},q\rangle}\cdot\quantity|\sum_{k \in K'} e^{\<\alpha(k - c'), q'>} - 2\sum_{k \in K'} e^{\<\alpha(k - c'), q'>}|

Lemma 6.6

(Weighted Merge-and-Reduce)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a black-box offline algorithm which takes as input a dataset
K\subset B(0,r)
,
|K|\leq n
, and returns
\widetilde{K}\subset K
,
|\widetilde{K}|\leq 3|K|/4
, weight function
\alpha_{x}\in\{1,2\}
such that with probability
1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}n}
, for any fixed
q\in B(0,r)
,

\displaystyle\left|\exp(K, q)-\exp(\tilde{K}, \alpha, q)\right|\leq O\left(e^{r^{2}(1+o(1))}\cdot n^{o(1)}\cdot\frac{\exp(K, q)}{|K|}\right).

(18)

Let
T(m)
denote the runtime
\mathcal{A}
on inputs of size
m
. Assume that the space complexity of
\mathcal{A}
is
O(m)
.

Algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can be adapted to process
K
as a stream. At every step
j
of the stream, it maintains a weighted coreset
C_{j}
of the
j
first elements of the stream
K_{j}
. Let
w_{x}^{j}
denote the weight of a point
x\in C_{j}
and
w^{j}
– the weight function. We have with probability
1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
, for any fixed
q\in U
and desired error
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,

\left|\exp(K_j,q)-\exp(C_j, w^j, q)\right|\leq\epsilon\cdot\exp(K_j, q).

The size of the coreset, runtime and memory are defined in terms of the block size of the algorithm
b

|C_{j}|\leq O\left(b\log^{2}\left(\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)\right),\quad\text{ runtime}=O\left(T(b)\cdot\log^{2}\left(\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)\right),\quad\text{ space}=O\left(b\log^{2}\left(\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)\right),

and the block size
b
is defined by the desired error
\epsilon

b=\frac{e^{r^{2}(1+o(1))}\cdot n^{o(1)}}{\epsilon}.

Proof.

Let

\alpha:=e^{r^{2}(1+o(1))}\cdot n^{o(1)}.

Each level of the weighted Merge-and-Reduce algorithm consists of at most
O\left(\log\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)
weight classes but every point can belong to at most one stream per level. For every point
x
which has appeared at the level
i
at some point before the arrival of
k_{j+1}
, we use
\omega_{x}^{i}
to denote the weight class of
x
.

Since each invocation of
\mathcal{A}
on a dataset shrinks it by a factor of
3/4
, at every time step
j
there are at most
O(\log(|K_j|/b))
non-empty levels. Therefore, because at any time we store at most
b
data points per weight class at each level,

|C_{j}|=O\!\left(b\log^{2}\!\left(\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)\right),\qquad\text{space}=O\!\left(b\log^{2}\!\left(\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)\right).

A new arrival in the stream can trigger at most one invocation of
\mathcal{A}
per weight class at each level, so

\text{runtime}=O\!\left(T(b)\log^{2}\!\left(\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)\right).

It remains to bound the error. Fix
q\in B(0,r)
. Using the guarantees of the offline algorithm given by
Equation

18
, the total error produced by our algorithm at level
0
at time step
j
can be bounded by

O\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{\exp(K_j, q)}{b}\right).

Note that the error will be incurred block by block, but we use the fact that the softmax kernel is linear across partitions of
K
.

We can bound the total error at every subsequent level
i
similarly despite the fact that the guarantees of
\mathcal{A}
depend on error with the kernel of the prior layer, rather than the original kernel
\operatorname{exp}(K_{j},q)
.
At the end of the proof, by choosing
b
appropriately, we will ensure that the sum of the errors over all levels does not exceed
\epsilon\cdot\exp(K_j, q)
:

\left|\exp(K_j, q)-\sum_{k\in\text{level }1}\omega_{k}^{1}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}\right|+\sum_{i\geq 1}\left|\sum_{k\in\text{level }i}\omega^{i}_{k}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}-\sum_{k\in\text{level }(i+1)}\omega^{i+1}_{k}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}\right|\leq\epsilon\cdot\exp(K_j, q),

so in particular we may use

\left|\exp\left(K_{j},q\right)-\sum_{k\in\text{level }i}\omega_{k}^{i}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}\right|\leq\epsilon\cdot\exp(K_j, q).

The sum of the errors produced by invocations of
\mathcal{A}
on all blocks of all streams at level
i
is, therefore, bounded by

O\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{\sum_{k\in\text{level }i}\omega_{k}^{i}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}}{b}\right)\leq O\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{\exp\left(K_{j},q\right)}{b}\right).

Thus, we have bounded the error at every level by the same expression. Hence, the total error can be bounded by

\left|\exp(C_j, w, q)-\exp(K_j,q )\right|\leq O\left(\alpha\cdot\log\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\cdot\frac{\exp(K_j, q)}{b}\right).

In order to ensure that the right-hand side is bounded by
\epsilon\cdot\exp(K_j, q)
, it suffices to set

b=O\!\left(\frac{\alpha\log|K|}{\epsilon}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{e^{r^{2}(1+o(1))}\cdot n^{o(1)}}{\epsilon}\right).

Finally, note that our algorithm succeeds as long as all calls to
\mathcal{A}
succeed. We invoke
\mathcal{A}
at most
\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}
times at each level, and there are at most
O\left(\log\frac{|K_{j}|}{b}\right)
levels. Since every call to
\mathcal{A}
succeeds with probability
1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
, a union bound implies that the overall algorithm succeeds with probability
1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
.
∎

6.2
Lower Bound

In this section we are going to prove the lower bound from
Theorem

2.2
. The proof exploits again the idea from the lower bound in
Theorem

2.1
providing a reduction to
\texttt{INDEX}_{2n,n}
problem with sublinear amount of advice bits. But the analysis is going to be different, since we are now in
r=\Omega(\log n)
regime instead of a constant
r
. The signal term will not be dominated by a single term of the tail as in Hermite case. We will argue instead that the signal term is still of order
e^{r^{2}}
by using Taylor expansion. This also allows us to prove the bound for the whole range of
d=(\log n)^{1+\Omega(1)}
. Since we do not use orthogonal properties of polynomials anymore, we switch to uniform distribution on a sphere instead of Gaussian, so we do not need rescaling.

Below is our main lemma that together with
R^{\text{pub},\to}(\texttt{INDEX}_{2n,n})=\Omega(n)
from
Theorem

4.12
implies the lower bound of
Theorem

2.2
. Note that we can assume not only
r^{2}=\Omega(\log n)
, but
r^{2}=\Theta(\log n)
as otherwise we can consider smaller
r^{\prime}=O(\log n)
such that
n\ll\frac{e^{r^{\prime 2}}}{\epsilon}
and therefore show
\Omega(n)
lower bound for original
r
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
S_{n,d,r,\epsilon}
denote a deterministic upper bound on the space, measured in bits, of a (randomized) data structure for the Streaming KDE problem with parameters
n,d,r,\epsilon
and a failure probability
1/100
. Assume that
d=\omega(\log n),r^{2}=\Theta(\log n)
,
n<\frac{\exp(r^2 \p{1 - C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}}})}{16\epsilon}
for some constant
C
, there exists a communication protocol with a message size
S_{n,d,r,\epsilon}+o(n)
which solves the
\texttt{INDEX}_{2n,n}
problem with probability at least
9/10
.

The rest of the section is dedicated to proving
Lemma

6.7
.

The reduction.

Using the public randomness, Alice and Bob agree on a point set
K=\{k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,

k_{2n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that each
k_{i}\in K
is drawn i.i.d. uniformly at random from a
d
-dimensional Euclidean sphere of radius
r
. Given her input string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
from the
\texttt{INDEX}_{2n,n}
problem, Alice defines a corresponding dataset
K_{\mathbf{x}}=\{k_{i}\in K\mid\mathbf{x}_{i}=1\}
. Then she constructs her message to Bob, which contains two components: for the first component, Alice builds a KDE data structure on the dataset
K_{\mathbf{x}}
with parameters
n,d,r,\epsilon
. This takes
S_{n,d,r,\epsilon}
bits. For the second component, Alice constructs an additional sketch that approximates KDE for the same dataset
K_{\mathbf{x}}
but with polynomial kernel

\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(k,q)=\sum_{l=0}^{t}\frac{\left\langle k,q\right\rangle^{l}}{l!}.

This sketch is defined via a pair of feature maps
\psi_{A},\psi_{B}
such that
\langle\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(q)\rangle
approximates
\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(k,q)
. The precise guarantee is described in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
t<d
. There exist embeddings
\psi_{A},\psi_{B}
such that

\left\lvert\Bigl\langle\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(q)\Bigr\rangle-\operatorname{exp}_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q),

and that
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k)
can be stored in
O(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\log^{2}n)
bits of space.

Proof.

As shown by
Lemma

4.2
, tensor powers embedding with polynomial precision gives the desired data structure with memory consumption
{d+t\choose t}\cdot O(t\log(d+t)+\log n)=O(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\log^{2}n)
.
∎

Alice sends the KDE data structure of
K_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k)
to Bob. We now describe the second part of the protocol, which is how Bob uses these two information to reveal
\mathbf{x}_{j}
for his input index
j
from the
\texttt{INDEX}_{2n,n}
problem, with probability at least
9/10
. This task is equivalent to detecting whether
k_{j}\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
. For this, Bob first queries the data structure with
k_{j}
.
With
.99
chance, the KDE data structure outputs
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})
with

|\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})-\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})|\leq\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j}).

Bob also computes

\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j}):=\Bigl\langle\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k),\psi_{B}(k_{j})\Bigr\rangle

using the sketch
\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}\psi_{A}(k)
he receives.
After that, he can compute
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})-\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})
, which is a noisy evaluation of
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})
. Formally, with
.99
chance,

\Bigl|\Bigl(\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})-\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\Bigr)-\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\Bigr|\leq 2\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j}).

(19)

We next argue that Bob can decide whether
k_{j}\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
by thresholding the quantity
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})-\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})
. The idea is that when
k_{j}\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
,
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})
contains a “signal term”
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{j},k_{j})
. We will argue that the contribution of this signal term typically stands out from (1) the noise induced by the contribution of
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,k_{j})
for
k\neq k_{j}
in
K_{\mathbf{x}}
and (2) the approximation error from
Equation

19
. Concretely, we will show that
there exists an appropriate choice of
t
such that

•

\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{j},k_{j})\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{r^{2}}
,

•

\text{\bf Var}\Bigl[\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j}\},k_{j})\Bigr]=o(e^{2r^{2}})
, and

•

2\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\leq\frac{1}{8}e^{r^{2}}
.

Moreover, for this choice of
t
,
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})
can indeed be sent using
o(n)
bits. Once we have these, Bob can detect whether
k_{j}\in K_{\mathbf{x}}
by thresholding whether

\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})-\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{\leq t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\geq\mu+\frac{1}{4}e^{r^{2}}
for explicitly computable
\mu:=\mathbb{E}_{K}[\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\mid\mathbf{x}_{j}=0)]
. This succeeds with high constant probability by an application of Chebyshev’s inequality. This concludes the description of the communication protocol.

The rest of this section is organized as follows: we first bound the signal in
Lemma

6.9
. Then we bound the variance with respect to
t
in
Lemma

6.10
. Lastly, we state our choice of
t
, and show that it leads to the claimed final bounds for the variance, the approximation noise, and the bit complexity.

Lemma 6.9
.

For any
t<r^{2}
and any
k_{j}
from a sphere of radius
r
, we have
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{j},k_{j})\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{r^{2}}
.

Proof.

Since the cutoff point
t<r^{2}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{j},k_{j})=\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2l}}{l!}>\sum_{l=\left\lfloor r^{2}\right\rfloor}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2l}}{l!}>\frac{1}{2}e^{r^{2}}

where the last inequality uses the fact that the median of Poisson distribution is within distance
1
from its mean.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

For any
t>(\log n)^{1-o(1)}
, any fixed
k_{j}
from a sphere of radius
r
, and for uniformly random i.i.d. draws of
v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}
from a sphere of radius
r
, we have

\displaystyle\text{\bf Var}\Bigl[\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}\},k_{j})\Bigr]=O\quantity(n\frac{(2r)^{4t}}{d^t t!}).

Proof.

For each
v
,

\displaystyle\text{\bf Var}\Bigl[\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(v,k_{j})\Bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\operatorname{exp}^{2}_{>t}(v,k_{j})=\mathbb{E}\quantity(\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty} \frac{\<v, k_j>^{l}}{l!})^{2}

Terms in this sum are heavily correlated, but they decay rapidly. Therefore we can bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\quantity(\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty} \frac{\<v, k_j>^{l}}{l!})^{2}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\quantity(\frac{\<v, k_j>^{t+1}}{(t+1)!})^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\quantity(\sum_{l=t+2}^{\infty} \frac{\<v, k_j>^{l}}{l!})^{2}\leq\dots\leq\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{l-t}\mathbb{E}\left\langle v,k_{j}\right\rangle^{2l}}{(l!)^{2}}.

Using
Lemma

4.15
to bound moments of dot products and using
r^{2}=O(\log n),d=(\log n)^{1+\Omega(1)},t>(\log n)^{1-o(1)}
to bound tail of the sum, we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{l-t}\mathbb{E}\left\langle v,k_{j}\right\rangle^{2l}}{(l!)^{2}}\leq\sum_{l=t+1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{2l-t}r^{4l}}{d^{l}l!}\leq\frac{(2r)^{4t}}{d^{t}t!}.

Multiplying with
n
gives the variance of
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}\},k_{j})
by independence.
∎

Now we choose the cutoff parameter
t
:
let’s first pick
t_{max}=o(\log n)
such that
t_{\max}>\frac{\log n}{\log d/t_{max}}
. It already satisfies assumptions
t<r^{2}=\Theta(\log n)
and
t<d
(which are the premises of
Lemma

6.8
and
Lemma

6.10
), but
\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}=\omega(n)
, i.e. the bit complexity is too large. Thus, we start decreasing
t
from
t_{max}
until
\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}>\frac{n}{\log^{3}n}
holds. When we stop, it means that

\displaystyle\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\log^{2}n<\frac{n}{\log n}=o(n).

Since it is the moment we stopped and
t=o(d)
,

\displaystyle\frac{(d+t+1)^{t+1}}{{(t+1)}!}>\frac{n}{\log^{3}n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{d^{t}}{t!}>\frac{n}{2^{t}\cdot d\log^{3}n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\begin{cases}\frac{n}{d^{t}}=\frac{2^{t}(\log n)^{O(1)}}{t!}\\
t>\frac{\log n}{\log d}=(\log n)^{1-o(1)}\end{cases}

Therefore, the variance is

\displaystyle\text{\bf Var}\Bigl[\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(K_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{k_{j}\},k_{j})\Bigr]=O\quantity(n\frac{(2r)^{4t}}{d^t t!})=\frac{2^{5t}r^{4t}(\log n)^{O(1)}}{(t!)^{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{(\log n)^{2t(1+o(1))}}{(\log n)^{2t(1-o(1))}}=(\log n)^{o(t)}<e^{o(\frac{\log n}{\log d-\log t})\log\log n}=n^{o(\frac{\log\log n)}{\Omega(\log\log n)})}=n^{o(1)}=o(e^{2r^{2}}).

It remains to argue that the approximation error

2\epsilon\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\leq\frac{1}{8}e^{r^{2}}.

Since the dataset is
K_{\mathbf{x}}
sampled uniformly at random from a sphere, with high probability, we have
\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\leq e^{r^{2}}+ne^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}
by
Lemma

4.16
, therefore,

\epsilon\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},k_{j})\leq\epsilon(e^{r^{2}}+ne^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})})<\frac{1}{16}e^{r^{2}}

as desired, where for the second inequality we used

n<\frac{e^{r^{2}\left\lparen 1-C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}}\right\rparen}}{16\epsilon}.
Summarizing the above, we show that for our choice of
t
, the contribution of the signal term
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k_{j},k_{j})
typically stands out from the noise induced by the contribution of
\operatorname{exp}_{>t}(k,k_{j})
for
k\neq k_{j}
in
K_{\mathbf{x}}
and the approximation error. And the bit complexity of the sketch is
o(n)
. This concludes our proof of the reduction to the
INDEX
problem.

In this section we provide formal results for the Attention problem (

). First, we restate our main theorems.

Theorem 7.1

(Formal version of
Remark

)
.

Assume that
r=\log^{o(1)}n
,
\log n\leq d\leq\log^{a}n
for some constant
a>1
and
n^{-100}<\epsilon<n^{-c}
for any constant

. Then the Attention problem can be solved using

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\quantity(\qty(\frac{1}{\eps})^{1 - \frac{1}{a} + o(1)})

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}\quantity(\min\qty{n, \qty(\frac{1}{\eps})^{1 - \frac{1}{a} - o(1)}})

(Formal version of

2.2
)
.

, i.e.

. Then the Attention problem can be solved using

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\quantity(\frac{e^{r^2(1 + o(1))}}{\eps})

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}\quantity(\min\qty{n, \frac{e^{r^2(1 - o(1))}}{\eps}})

below. These results are put together in

to obtain proofs of

and

.

7.1
Upper bounds for Attention

In this section we will show how one can adapt our data structure that solves Softmax KDE problem to solve Attention problem. First, we slightly generalize the setup.

Definition 7.1

(Weighted Softmax KDE problem)
.

The algorithm receives key-weight pairs

(k_{1},w(k_{1}))\ldots,(k_{n},w(k_{n}))\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
in a stream and at every point
j=1,\ldots,n
,
given any fixed
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, outputs an estimate
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{j},w_{j},q)
of
\operatorname{exp}(K_{j},w_{j},q)
that satisfies

Proof (of
Lemma

7.3
).

As in the proof of
Lemma

4.2
, we let

\psi_{1}(k):=\quantity(a_l \cdot k^{{\otimes l}})_{l=\overline{0..t}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi_{2}(q):=\quantity(q^{{\otimes l}})_{l=\overline{0..t}}.

By linearity, in exact arithmetic we have:

\Bigl\langle\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\psi_{1}(k),\psi_{2}(q)\Bigr\rangle=\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\gamma(k,q).

Just as in
Lemma

4.2
, we first round

\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\psi_{1}(k)
to a finite number of bits and store the
result as
z
. Regarding the bit complexity of
z
, every coordinate of
\quantity(k^{{\otimes l}})_{l=\overline{0..t}}
and

\quantity(q^{{\otimes l}})_{l=\overline{0..t}}
is still bounded by
\max\{r,1\}^{t}
; thus every coordinate
of
\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\psi_{1}(k)
has absolute value at most

\sum_{k\in K}|w(k)|\max_{l}|a_{l}|\max\{r,1\}^{t}\leq|K|\max_{l}|a_{l}|\max\{r,1\}^{t}.

If
\|z-\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\psi_{1}(k)\|_{\infty}\leq\eta
, then

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Bigl\langle z,\psi_{2}(q)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\psi_{1}(k),\psi_{2}(q)\Bigr\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot\|z-\sum_{k\in K}w(k)\psi_{1}(k)\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\psi_{2}(q)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot\eta\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t}

where
m
is the target dimension of
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
, which is again
O\left(\frac{(d+t)^{t}}{t!}\right)
, as in
Lemma

4.2
. Therefore, to guarantee total error
\delta
, we can pick
\eta=\frac{\delta}{m\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t}}
. The bit complexity to store each coordinate to this precision is
O(\log\frac{|K|\max_{l}|a_{l}|\max\{r,1\}^{t}\cdot(m\cdot\max\{r,1\}^{t})}{\delta})
, and multiplying with
m
again gives the claimed space complexity.
∎

Proof (of
Lemma

7.4
).

As in the proof of
Lemma

4.3
, we again reduce the problem to
the inner-product setting, where we use a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}

for the inner-product kernel
\gamma_{0}(k,q):=\langle k,q\rangle

(see
Theorem

4.4
for details). Since
\operatorname{exp}
is positive definite, there exists a feature map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to H
into a Hilbert space
H
such that

\operatorname{exp}(x,y)=\langle\psi(x),\psi(y)\rangle_{H}\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We consider the subspace
\mathcal{S}
spanned by
\{\psi(k):k\in K\}
and
\psi(q)
. Choosing an orthonormal
basis of
\mathcal{S}
, we may identify
\mathcal{S}
isometrically with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
dimension
m\leq|K|+1
. Under this identification, each
\psi(k)
and
\psi(q)
becomes a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and all inner products among these vectors are preserved, i.e.,

\operatorname{exp}(k,q)=\langle\psi(k),\psi(q)\rangle_{H}=\langle\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}=\gamma_{0}(\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q))

where
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an isometric map defined by the chosen orthonormal basis. Furthermore,

w(k)\operatorname{exp}(k,q)=w(k)\langle\psi(k),\psi(q)\rangle_{H}=\langle w(k)\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}=\gamma_{0}(w(k)\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q)).

Therefore, we can apply
Theorem

4.4
to the inner-product kernel
\gamma_{0}
, with dataset
\{w(k)\pi\psi(k)\}_{k\in K}
, and query
\pi\psi(q)
. The algorithm returns a subset
\tilde{K}\subset K
of size at most
|K|/2
and with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{k\in K}\langle w(k)\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q)\rangle-2\sum_{k\in\tilde{K}}\langle w(k)\pi\psi(k),\pi\psi(q)\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\leq O\left(\sqrt{\max_{u\in U^{\prime}}\langle u,u\rangle\cdot\max_{v\in V^{\prime}}\langle v,v\rangle}\cdot\log(|K|/\delta)\right),

where
U^{\prime}:=\{w(u)\pi\psi(u):u\in U,|w(u)|\leq 1\}
and
V^{\prime}:=\{\pi\psi(v):v\in V\}
. The left-hand side is exactly
|\operatorname{exp}(K,w,q)-2\operatorname{exp}(\widetilde{K},w,q)|
. For the right-hand side, since
|w(u)|\leq 1
,

\max_{u\in U^{\prime}}\langle u,u\rangle=\max_{u\in U^{\prime}}w(u)^{2}\langle u,u\rangle\leq\operatorname{exp}(u,u)

and similarly for
v
. Hence the right-hand side is at most the claimed error bound. Finally,
Theorem

4.4
only requires oracle access to inner products in the transformed space. These can be computed using the weights together with oracle access to
\operatorname{exp}
. Therefore we do not need to compute the embedding explicitly, and the runtime remains
O(|K|^{2}\cdot T)
as in
Theorem

4.4
.
∎

Finally, we show a reduction from the Attention problem to the Weighted Softmax KDE problem.

Lemma 7.6
.

If there exists a streaming algorithm that solves the weighted Softmax KDE problem (
Definition

7.1
) with parameters
n,d,\epsilon,r
using
S(n,d,\epsilon,r)
space, then there exists a streaming algorithm that solves the Attention problem (
Definition

2.2
) with parameters
n,d,\tilde{O}(\epsilon),r
using
\tilde{O}(S(n,d,\epsilon,r))
space.

Proof.

Recall that in the Attention problem, at every time step of the stream
l
the algorithm needs to output
\hat{z}^{l}
such that

\|\hat{z}^{l}-\mathsf{Attn}(K_{l},q_{l},V_{l})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\cdot\|\operatorname{softmax}(K_{l},q_{l})\|_{2}\|V_{l}\|_{F}.

Our algorithm amounts to running several instances of streaming KDE, which has per-step approximation guarantees. Therefore, we can fix one time step
l\leq n
and drop the indexes
l
until the rest of the proof. Instead of bounding the
\ell_{2}
norm of the error we will bound its
\ell_{1}
norm:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\hat{z}_{j}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)_{j}|=\left\lVert\hat{z}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)\right\rVert_{1}.

Since we assume poly-log dimension
d=\tilde{O}(1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left\lVert\hat{z}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\hat{z}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\hat{z}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)\right\rVert_{1}.

Therefore, we lose at most a
\sqrt{d}=\tilde{O}(1)
factor by switching to

\left\lVert\hat{z}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)\right\rVert_{1}
. We also split RHS in the error guarantee by coordinates:

\displaystyle\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}=\tilde{\Omega}(1)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}.

Therefore, to solve Attention problem with
\tilde{O}(\epsilon)
precision it is enough to provide an estimator such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{z}-\mathsf{Attn}(K,q,V)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon\cdot\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|_{2}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}.

To do this we approximate each coordinate individually using
d=\tilde{O}(1)
separate data structures with guarantees

\displaystyle\quantity|\hat{z}_j - \frac{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}V_{i, j}}{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}}|\leq O(\epsilon)\cdot\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}.

(21)

Stacking them together will produce the required estimate
\hat{z}
. We fix one coordinate
j
in what follows. To provide the guarantee of (
21
) we will give two estimators –
\hat{N}
for the numerator and
\hat{D}
for the denominator:

\displaystyle\quantity|\hat{N} - \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}V_{i, j}|\leq\epsilon\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}|V_{i,j}|

(22)

and

\displaystyle\quantity|\hat{D} - \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}|\leq\epsilon\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}.

It is easy to see that we can construct
\hat{D}
the same way as
\hat{N}
by letting
V_{i,j}=1
, so we only focus on constructing
\hat{N}
. But first, let’s check that we do recover guarantee (
21
) for
\hat{z}=\frac{\hat{N}}{\hat{D}}
:

\displaystyle\quantity|\frac{\hat{N}}{\hat{D}} - \frac{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}V_{i, j}}{\expker(K, q)}|\leq\quantity|\frac{\hat{N}}{\hat{D}} - \frac{\hat{N}}{\expker(K, q)}|

\displaystyle+\quantity|\frac{\hat{N}}{\expker(K, q)} - \frac{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}V_{i, j}}{\expker(K, q)}|\leq

\displaystyle|\hat{N}|\cdot\quantity|\frac{\hat{D} - \expker(K, q)}{\hat{D} \cdot\expker(K, q)}|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\operatorname{exp}(K,q)}\cdot\quantity|\hat{N} - \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}V_{i, j}|\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}|V_{i,j}|\right)\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{(1-\epsilon)\operatorname{exp}(K,q)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}|V_{i,j}|}{\operatorname{exp}(K,q)}\leq

\displaystyle O(\epsilon)\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}|V_{i,j}|}{\operatorname{exp}(K,q)}

\displaystyle\leq O(\epsilon)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l}e^{2\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}}{\operatorname{exp}(K,q)}

(23)

\displaystyle=O(\epsilon)\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l}V_{i,j}^{2}}.

Now we only need to show how to build an estimator with guarantee
(
22
)
:

\displaystyle\quantity|\hat{N} - \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{l} e^{\<k_i, q>}V_{i, j}|\leq\epsilon\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}|V_{i,j}|.

Note that if all
|V_{i,j}|
have about the same value, i.e. if all
|V_{i,j}|
lie in
[\alpha;2\alpha]
for some scaling parameter
\alpha
, then this estimator is given by weighted KDE problem (
Definition

7.1
) with weights
w(p_{i})=\frac{V_{i}}{2\alpha}
. To see this assume that we are given
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}
such that (from
Definition

7.1
)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K,w,q)-\operatorname{exp}(K,w,q)|\leq\epsilon\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K,q)

Then, since
\operatorname{exp}(K,w,q)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}V_{i,j}
,

\displaystyle|2\alpha\cdot\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K,w,q)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}V_{i,j}|\leq\epsilon\cdot 2\alpha\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K,q)\leq 2\epsilon\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}e^{\left\langle k_{i},q\right\rangle}|V_{i,j}|.

Therefore, we can partition
P
into
O(\log n)
buckets according to the
magnitudes of the values
V_{i,j}
, and approximate each bucket separately,
incurring only a
\tilde{O}(1)
factor overhead in both error and space. It
suffices to use
O(\log n)
buckets because we assume that
e^{r^{2}}
and

1/\epsilon
are at most
\operatorname{poly}(n)
; hence buckets corresponding to

|V_{i,j}|<\frac{\max_{i\leq l}|V_{i,j}|}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}

can be ignored, as their contribution is negligible compared to
\exp(K, q)\geq e^{-r^{2}}\cdot\max_{i}|V_{i,j}|
. This concludes the
reduction.
∎

In this section, we show that our hard instances for Softmax KDE problem translate to hard instances for Attention problem with slight modifications.
We prove the reduction specifically to the type of random KDE instances used in our lower bound instances, which we generalize below.

Definition 7.2

(Random KDE Instance)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
be a binary vector with exactly
n
ones. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of
2n
points drawn i.i.d. from a rotationally invariant distribution, independently of
\mathbf{x}
. Let

be the set of points indexed by the ones of
\mathbf{x}
.
A

is an instance of the streaming KDE problem (
Definition

2.1
) in which the keys
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}
are the points in
K
and the query
q
is chosen from
K
. Vector
q
is chosen to be one of the keys

; the position in the dataset

is independent of the randomness of

.

Lemma 7.7
.

Assume
r^{2}<\frac{\log n}{2}
. If there exists a streaming algorithm that solves the attention problem (
Definition

2.2
) with parameters
2n,d,\epsilon,r
using

space, then there exists a streaming algorithm that solves the streaming KDE problem on random instances (

7.2
) with parameters

using

\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon\cdot O\left(e^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}\right)
.

Proof.

Given a random KDE instance (
Definition

7.2
) we choose key vectors for Attention instance to be
K
, i.e.
2n
vectors chosen independently of
\mathbf{x}
. Next we introduce value vectors.
For each
i\in[n]
, we define the corresponding value vector

(we treat
v_{i}
as
1
-dimensional, padding it with zeros if needed).

With our construction, the attention output is a scalar, whose numerator
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\langle k_{i},q\rangle}v_{i}
equals
\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)
, and the denominator
\sum_{i\in[n]}e^{\langle k_{i},q\rangle}
equals
\operatorname{exp}(K,q)
. The approximation guarantee from
Definition

2.2
ensures that the output
\hat{z}
satisfies

\quantity|\hat{z} - \frac{\expker(K_{\bx}, q)}{\expker(K, q)}|\leq\epsilon\cdot\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|_{2}\cdot\|V\|_{F}.

Note that

by construction.
We can bound
\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|_{2}
as
O\left(\frac{e^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
by

4.16
(using randomness of the KDE instance) and our assumption

:

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|^{2}_{2}=\frac{\sum e^{2\langle k_{i},q\rangle}}{(\sum e^{\langle k_{i},q\rangle})^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:rand_dataset_kde})}}{{=}}\frac{e^{2r^{2}}+n\cdot e^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}}{\Omega(n^{2})}=O\quantity(\frac{e^{O(r^2\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}}{n})

i.e.
\|\operatorname{softmax}(K,q)\|_{2}\cdot\|V\|_{F}=e^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}
. This guarantee means that
\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q)=\hat{z}\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K,q)

\quantity|\widehat{\expker}(K_{\bx}, q) - \expker(K_{\bx}, q)|\leq O(\epsilon)\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K,q)\cdot e^{O(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}})}=\epsilon^{\prime}\cdot\operatorname{exp}(K_{\mathbf{x}},q).

Here, the last step follows from
Lemma

4.16
. By
Lemma

4.16
, since
|K|=2n
and

\Omega(n)\leq\sum_{k\in K_{\mathbf{x}}}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}=\exp(K_{\bx}, q)\leq n\cdot e^{O\left(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}}\right)}+e^{r^{2}},

\Omega(n)\leq\sum_{k\in K}e^{\langle k,q\rangle}=\exp(K, q)\leq 2n\cdot e^{O\left(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}}\right)}+e^{r^{2}}.

Because

,

. Therefore,

\operatorname{exp}(K,q)\leq O\left(n\cdot e^{O\left(r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}}\right)}\right)\leq O\quantity(\expker(K_\bx, q) \cdot e^{O\left(r^2\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{d}}\right)}).

Therefore we can solve random KDE instance with parameters

using
S(2n,d,\epsilon,r)
space.

returns a point

Let

First of all, we assume without loss of generality that

Runtime of
Algorithm

1
.

At each level of recursion, the runtime of the algorithm is dominated by the
calls to
Certify
. We therefore bound only the total cost of these calls.
Fix one invocation of
Partition
on a dataset of size
|K|
. Each successful
call to
Certify
removes an
\Omega(\Delta^{2}\tau)
fraction of the current
dataset, which means the total work spent on this invocation is

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}O\!\left(\frac{d}{\Delta^{2}\tau}(1-\Delta^{2}\tau)\cdot|K|\log n\right)=O\!\left(\frac{d}{\Delta^{4}\tau^{2}}\cdot|K|\log n\right).

Now fix a recursion depth. The datasets processed at that depth are pairwise
disjoint subsets of the original input
K
, so the sum of their sizes is at
most
|K|
. Therefore the total runtime of all calls to
Certify
at any fixed depth is

O\!\left(\frac{d}{\Delta^{4}\tau^{2}}\cdot|K|\log n\right).

Finally, the recursion depth is

O\!\left(\frac{\log(r/r_0)}{\Delta^{4}}\right),

because each recursive call shrinks the radius by a factor
\sqrt{1-\Delta^{4}}
.
This yields the claimed runtime bound.

Size of
\mathcal{K}
.

Note that we only output a set of
\mathcal{K}
at a leaf of the recursion tree, so it suffices to bound the number of leaves. Because every time line (
9
) is triggered the size of
K

(1-\Omega(\Delta^{2}\tau))
,
Partition
makes
O\left(\frac{\log|K|}{\Delta^{2}\tau}\right)

O\left(\frac{\log(r/r_0)}{\Delta^{4}}\right)
because each recursive call targets a dataset enclosed in a ball of radius

|\mathcal{K}|\leq\quantity(\frac{\log|K|}{\Delta^2\cdot\tau})^{O\quantity(\frac{\log\frac{r}{r_0}}{\Delta^4})}

Probability of success of
Algorithm

1
.

The algorithm is successful if all runs of
Certify
are successful. Note that the number of calls to
Certify
at a node is at most one more than the number of its children. Therefore, we may bound the number of calls to
