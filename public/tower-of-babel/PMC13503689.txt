Preclinical characterization of a reversible XPO1 inhibitor for cancer therapy
Authors: Janne Van Hauwenhuyse, Felien Reniers, Leentje Persoons, Sam Noppen, Casey E. Wing, Ashley B. Niesman, Ho Yee Joyce Fung, Els Vanstreels, Maarten Jacquemyn, Eline Boel, Ann Vankerckhoven, Yani Berckmans, Bert Kwanten, An Coosemans, Guy Van den Mooter, Yuh Min Chook, Wim Dehaen, Dirk Daelemans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exportin 1 (XPO1/CRM1) is a clinically validated anticancer target whose inhibition blocks nuclear export and promotes cancer cell apoptosis. Current XPO1 inhibitors rely on covalent Michael addition to Cys528 in the nuclear export signal binding groove of XPO1. Here, we describe a novel XPO1 inhibitor, FR-027, that targets Cys528 through nucleophilic aromatic substitution. In contrast to clinical-stage XPO1 inhibitors selinexor and eltanexor, FR-027 acts reversibly and does not promote XPO1 protein degradation. Structural analysis of the XPO1-FR-027 complex reveals covalent modification of Cys528 and a closed-groove conformation that prevents degradation. FR-027 demonstrates potent on-target activity across multiple cancer cell types and delays disease progression while extending overall survival in xenograft and syngeneic models, including intracranial tumors. Notably, FR-027 does not induce significant thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, or neutropenia in heavily treated mice. These findings underscore the distinct molecular and pharmacological properties of FR-027 and support its further evaluation for clinical development in diseases with significant unmet medical needs.

Introduction

Market approval of covalent drugs has risen substantially in the past decade and coincides with an improved perception of their selectivity and safety1–3. Covalent inhibitors typically target a nucleophilic amino acid residue (e.g., cysteine, lysine, serine, tyrosine, threonine) within the protein target4. First, initial non-covalent interactions of the drug with the intended molecular target ensure target specificity and position the drug’s reactive chemical group, the warhead, in proximity to the amino acid. The subsequent chemical reaction and covalent bond ensure prolonged drug activity and improve potency. Most targeted covalent inhibitors have been designed to affect cysteine residues. Cysteine’s thiol(ate) side chain is often crucial for protein function and stability, and cysteine residues are relatively low in abundance in the proteome. This reduces the potential for off-target interactions of covalent drugs5.

Historically, cysteine residues are most often targeted via a Michael addition reaction of an electrophilic warhead, typically an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl, which is susceptible to a nucleophilic attack on the electron-deficient β-carbon1. The clinical success of this type of inhibition has led to an increased use of the Michael acceptor-type warheads and limited research into potentially improved electrophilic warheads4. Alternative reactions to establish the covalent bond to cysteine are nucleophilic addition, addition-elimination, oxidation and nucleophilic (aromatic) substitution reactions4,6. These alternative chemistries provide additional flexibility for designing targeted covalent inhibitors using a broader range of electrophilic reactive groups with distinct chemical and pharmacological properties but have been comparatively underexplored.

Many FDA approved drugs contain Michael addition-type warheads, including the anti-cancer drugs afatinib (2013), ibrutinib (2013), and osimertinib (2015) all carrying the predominant acrylamide functional group1,7. Another successful covalent drug is the first-in-class inhibitor of nuclear export selinexor (KPT-330, XPOVIO®). Selinexor targets Exportin 1 (XPO1; also named chromosomal region maintenance 1 or CRM1), a nuclear export receptor that mediates transport of proteins from the nucleus to the cytoplasm8–12. The drug originates from a series of drug-like N-azolylacrylate XPO1 inhibitors that were discovered in the context of inhibition of the nuclear export of human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) viral RNA13,14. These molecules covalently bind to the Cys528 residue within the hydrophobic cargo-binding site of XPO1 through a Michael addition-type reaction to the acrylate’s α,β-unsaturated carbonyl, thereby blocking the interaction of XPO1 with its nuclear export signal (NES)-containing protein cargos13,14. Like the parent compounds, selinexor and its second generation analog eltanexor (KPT-8602) covalently conjugate Cys528 in the NES-binding groove of XPO1. By occupying the groove, they sterically block NES binding and nuclear export while stabilizing the groove in an open conformation15–20. These inhibitors also have been reported to induce proteasomal degradation of XPO118,21. Recently, the conformational change induced by inhibitor binding was found to expose a cryptic degron that recruits the Cullin-RING E3 ligase substrate receptor ASB8, promoting ubiquitination and degradation of XPO122,23.

XPO1 is the best characterized nuclear export receptor and has a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by preserving the delicate balance between the nuclear and cytoplasmic localization of over 400 cargo proteins, including tumor suppressors and cell cycle regulators (e.g., p53, p21, p27 and iκB)24–27. However, XPO1 is frequently overexpressed in both solid tumors and hematological malignancies, and this overexpression correlates with poor patient prognosis26,28–31. Elevated XPO1 levels drive cytoplasmic mislocalization of numerous cargos, including tumor suppressor proteins, leading to their functional inactivation. In contrast, inhibition of XPO1-mediated nuclear export causes these cargos to accumulate in the nucleus, where they promote cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in DNA-damaged cancer cells25,32. In non-malignant cells, XPO1 inhibition also triggers cell cycle arrest but does not induce apoptosis, and normal cell cycling resumes once XPO1 function is restored21,25.

Thus, targeting XPO1-dependent nuclear export provides a compelling therapeutic strategy across diverse cancer types that depend on the cytoplasmic sequestration of XPO1 cargos for survival. Indeed, XPO1 has been validated as a clinically actionable target, with selinexor (XPOVIO®) receiving regulatory approval from the FDA, EMA, and NMPA for the treatment of patients with multiple myeloma or diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) across the US, Europe, China, and other global markets33,34. Although selinexor has been evaluated primarily in hematological malignancies, it has also been evaluated in gynecologic cancers, lung cancer, melanoma, and breast cancer, with outcomes varying across indications35–40. Many clinical studies report improvements in progression-free or overall survival but also note the occurrence of significant toxicities, including grade 3-4 thrombocytopenia, anemia, and fatigue, which constrain dosing frequency. For instance, a phase 1/2 trial in newly diagnosed or recurrent glioblastoma was terminated prematurely, and subsequent development efforts in glioblastoma appear to have stalled41. Eltanexor, which incorporates a modified warhead designed to increase the reversibility of binding, showed improved tolerability in preclinical studies, but hematological toxicity remains a major limitation in clinical settings18,42,43.

All established potent covalent XPO1 inhibitors, including leptomycin B (LMB) analogues and N-azolylacrylate derivatives such as selinexor and eltanexor, use electrophilic warheads that react with Cys528 through Michael addition26.

Here, we introduce a novel compound, FR-027, bearing a nitroimidazole-based electrophilic warhead designed to target Cys528 in XPO1. Replacing selinexor’s acrylamide warhead with this new functional group produces an XPO1 inhibitor with chemical, molecular, and pharmacokinetic properties distinct from those of selinexor or eltanexor. Biochemical and structural analyses confirm that, unlike selinexor and other Michael acceptor-based inhibitors, the FR-027 warhead reacts with Cys528 through nucleophilic aromatic substitution, does not induce degradation of XPO1, and reversibly inhibits XPO1-mediated nuclear export. Consistent with its ability to block the NES-binding groove and inhibit XPO1 activity, FR-027 shows cytotoxic potency in tumor cell lines comparable to selinexor and eltanexor. We demonstrate its in vivo therapeutic potential in mouse models of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL), ovarian cancer, and orthotropic intracranial models such as U87-MG brain tumor and primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL). Finally, we show that FR-027 does not induce thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia or neutropenia in murine models.

Results

FR-027 is a potent inhibitor of XPO1 and inhibits XPO1-NES binding

During a phenotypic screen of a structurally diverse compound library aimed at identifying novel XPO1 inhibitors we discovered a new small molecule, FR-027 (Fig. 1A), that inhibited the XPO1-mediated nuclear export of a reporter cargo protein in our XPO1-dependent reporter assay (Fig. 1B). The assay uses a HeLa cell line stably expressing an SV40NLS-AcGFP-PKINES reporter protein44,45. Under steady state conditions, this reporter localizes predominantly to the cytoplasm because of its strong nuclear export signal (NES), despite continuous nuclear import driven by its nuclear localization signal (NLS)8,46. Consequently, genetic disruption of XPO1 results in active nuclear accumulation of the reporter44, a phenotype mimicked by pharmacological XPO1 inhibition with selinexor or eltanexor (Fig. 1B). Treatment of these reporter cells with FR-027 similarly induced nuclear accumulation of the reporter, demonstrating that FR-027 effectively inhibits XPO1 (Fig. 1B). Inhibition of XPO1-cargo NES binding was confirmed by in vitro pulldown assays using resin-immobilized GST-PKINES. In the absence of inhibitor, XPO1 bound GST-PKINES in a RanGTP-dependent manner (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 8). FR-027 covalently modifies XPO1 Cys528

The chemical structure of FR-027 is similar to that of selinexor except for its distinct and innovative warhead, as confirmed via 1H, 13C and 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Figs. 1–3). The 1-methyl-4-nitro-1H-imidazole moiety serves as an electrophilic warhead that targets XPO1 Cys528 via nucleophilic aromatic substitution (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this mechanism, incubation of FR-027 with methyl(tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-cysteinate (1), followed by purification and 1H and 13C NMR analysis, yielded a stable substitution product 2, isolated as a white solid in 72% yield, resulting from nucleophilic displacement of the triazole moiety by the cysteinate. Product 3 was isolated as a yellow oil in 50% yield (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Figs. 4–7).

The 1,2,4-triazole ring and 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl group of FR-027, also present in selinexor and eltanexor, are expected to mediate initial non-covalent hydrophobic interactions with residues within the XPO1 NES-binding groove15,32. These interactions likely provide sufficient binding energy to position the compound for covalent modification of Cys528. In this reaction, the 1,2,4-triazole ring serves as a leaving group during covalent bond formation, resulting in retention of the 1-methyl-4-nitro-1H-imidazole adduct on Cys528 (Fig. 1F). When FR-027 was incubated with purified human XPO1 protein, mass spectrometry confirmed covalent conjugation of a 122.9 Da adduct (Fig. 1E), corresponding to the nitro-imidazole moiety (hereafter referred to as FR-027*) following loss of the triazole-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl leaving group during nucleophilic aromatic substitution (Fig. 1F). These results confirm that FR-027 covalently modifies XPO1 at Cys528 through its nitroimidazole warhead.

Cryo-EM structure of XPO1 in complex with FR-027

To investigate how covalent modification of Cys528 by FR-027 reshapes the NES-binding groove, we determined the 2.9 Å cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the XPO1-FR-027* complex formed after treatment of XPO1 with FR-027 (Fig. 2A–F and Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 9). In this structure, XPO1 retains its characteristic ring-shaped superhelical architecture composed of 21 HEAT repeats (h1-h21). Consistent with previous cryo-EM structures of XPO1-inhibitor complexes23, the cryo-EM map for FR-027*-bound XPO1 is well resolved across most of the protein, except for the dynamic N-terminal h1–h4 repeats (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Clear density corresponding to FR-027* is observed covalently attached to Cys528 within the NES-binding groove formed by repeats h11 and h12 (Supplementary Fig. 10B). The nitroimidazole ring of FR-027* fits well within this density, although the methyl and nitro substituents appear less well defined, likely reflecting rotational flexibility of the nitroimidazole ring around the Cys528-FR-027* thioether linkage. To assess conformational changes that may underlie nuclear export inhibition and ligand-induced XPO1 degradation, we compared the XPO1-FR-027* structure with previously determined cryo-EM structures of unliganded and selinexor-bound XPO1 (Fig. 2A–F). Structural alignments revealed low overall structural deviation (~ 0.6 Å r.m.s.d. over 848 Cα atoms), indicating that the global XPO1 architecture is preserved across all three structures (Fig. 2A–F). The NES-binding groove of XPO1 is known to adopt either open or closed conformations depending on ligand binding and local structural rearrangements. In the FR-027*-bound structure, the XPO1 groove appears largely closed, except for a localized opening around the conjugated nitroimidazole (Fig. 2A, D and Supplementary Fig. 10C). By comparison, the unliganded XPO1 groove (PDB 9OG9) is closed (Fig. 2B, E), whereas the groove in the XPO1-selinexor complex (PDB 9OGA) adopts an open conformation (Fig. 2C, F).

Comparison of the FR-027*-bound, unliganded, and selinexor-bound grooves reveals key differences that stabilize either the closed or open conformations (Fig. 2A–F). XPO1 side chain configurations in the FR-027*-bound groove closely resemble those observed in unliganded XPO1 (Fig. 2A, B). In contrast, binding of the larger selinexor compound induces conformational changes in three groove residues: Leu525 on helix h11A, and Phe561 and Lys568 on helix h12A (Fig. 2C). In unliganded XPO1, these side chains extend across the groove, effectively closing it (Fig. 2B, E). Upon selinexor binding, however, the Leu525, Phe561 and Lys568 side chains rearrange to accommodate and interact with the inhibitor (Fig. 2C). The Leu525 and Lys568 side chains move out of the groove toward solvent while forming contacts with selinexor, whereas the Phe561 side chain rotates toward the back of the groove. This repositioning of Phe561 enables hydrophobic interactions with one of selinexor’s trifluoro-methyl groups and this Phe561 configuration also exposes the P0 and P1 pockets of the XPO1 NES-binding groove (Fig. 2F). In the FR-027*-bound structure, Lys568 shifts toward solvent to accommodate and contact the compound’s nitroimidazole ring (Fig. 2A). However, the much smaller covalent adduct is compatible with the Leu525 and Phe561 side chain configurations observed in the unliganded structure. As a result, the NES-binding groove remains closed despite inhibitor binding (Fig. 2A, B, D, E). Together, these structural differences explain how FR-027 blocks NES binding and preserves the closed conformation of the XPO1 groove.

FR-027 does not induce degradation of XPO1

It has previously been demonstrated that selinexor induces proteasomal degradation of XPO1 protein in cells21,44. We recently elucidated the underlying mechanism: selinexor binds to XPO1 and stabilizes its NES-binding groove in an open conformation, which allows recruitment of Cullin-RING E3 ligase (CRL) substrate receptor ASB8. This recruitment leads to XPO1 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation22,23. Because our structural data show that the NES-binding groove remains closed in the FR-027*-bound XPO1 structure (Fig. 2A, D), it would not be accessible to ASB8, precluding degradation.

We aimed to confirm this in cells and treated wild-type Jurkat, MOLT-4, U87-MG, OCI-LY7, and HAP1 cells with FR-027, selinexor, or eltanexor and analyzed XPO1 protein levels by Simple Western. As expected, selinexor and eltanexor markedly decreased XPO1 levels relative to DMSO controls (Fig. 3A). In contrast, FR-027 did not reduce XPO1 protein abundance in any of the tested cell lines. Because XPO1 degradation requires recruitment of ASB822, we asked whether FR-027 promotes formation of the XPO1-ASB8 complex. We examined XPO1-ASB8 binding in vitro using pulldown assays with purified XPO1 and ASB8. XPO1 treated with selinexor bound strongly to immobilized GST-ASB8, whereas FR-027-treated XPO1 did not, consistent with both our structural and cellular data (Figs. 2D and 3B). To explain this difference, we compared the previously determined selinexor-XPO1-ASB8-ELOB/C cryo-EM structure (PDB 9OG9) with the XPO1-FR-027* structure. In the selinexor-XPO1-ASB8-ELOB/C structure, ankyrin (ANK) repeats 3-7 of ASB8 interact with the convex surface of XPO1 across HEAT repeats h10-h12 (Fig. 3C), a region that includes the portion of the NES-binding groove where the P0 and P1 pockets become exposed upon SINE binding. By contrast, in the XPO1-FR-027* structure, the P0 and P1 pockets remain occluded because the Leu525 and Phe561 side chains adopt their groove-closing conformations, rendering the ASB8-binding surface inaccessible (Figs. 2A, D and 3D). When ASB8 is overlaid onto the XPO1-FR-027* structure, it sterically clashes both with the closed groove and adjacent surface spanning repeats h12-h13 (Fig. 3C, D). These steric conflicts explain why ASB8 cannot bind with FR-027*-modified XPO1 and why FR-027 fails to induce XPO1 protein degradation (Fig. 3D).

Together, these findings show that FR-027 represents a mechanistically distinct class of covalent XPO1 inhibitors that inhibit nuclear export by covalently modifying Cys528 while maintaining the NES-binding groove in a closed configuration, thereby preventing ASB8 recruitment and subsequent XPO1 degradation.

XPO1-FR-027* conjugation and inhibition of nuclear export by FR-027 are reversible

Covalent inhibitors often yield sustained target inhibition because the covalent adduct they form with the target protein exhibits limited reversibility. However, both the intrinsic chemical properties of the inhibitor warhead and the local protein environment can influence the stability and reversibility of this bond. For example, the reversibility of the XPO1 Cys528-LMB adduct is greater when the inhibitor’s lactone ring remains intact than after its hydrolysis17. Lactone hydrolysis catalyzed by XPO1 reduces reversibility of the Cys528-LMB adduct and renders it effectively irreversible, whereas conjugation of other inhibitors such as selinexor has been shown to be slowly reversible. Because FR-027 uses a distinct warhead to covalently modify Cys528, we examined whether this chemistry affects the reversibility of the Cys528-FR-027* adduct and, consequently, the durability of target engagement and inhibition.

We first assessed reversibility of XPO1 inhibition by FR-027 using a pulldown binding assay. XPO1 pre-incubated with either FR-027, selinexor, or LMB was transferred into inhibitor-free buffers containing competing thiols and/or varying pH conditions. Unconjugated inhibitor was removed at regular intervals, after which the XPO1 samples were subjected to binding with immobilized GST-PKINES. Relative to samples analyzed immediately after FR-027 treatment, XPO1 incubated for 24 h showed a modest recovery of GST-PKINES binding, consistent with partial reversal of FR-027-mediated inhibition over time (Fig. 4A). Incubation of XPO1-FR-027* with DTT further increased NES binding, particularly at pH 8.5, indicating removal of the FR-027* adduct, whereas incubation with GSH did not produce statistically significant effects (Fig. 4A). We next performed complementary cellular experiments to evaluate the reversibility and temporal dynamics of XPO1 inhibition by FR-027 in a biological context. To characterize target engagement kinetics in cells, we quantified XPO1 occupancy in U87-MG cells treated with FR-027, selinexor, or eltanexor, using a protocol adapted from Crochiere et al.47. To prevent the selinexor- and eltanexor-induced XPO1 degradation from confounding the analysis, we generated ASB8-knockout cells (Supplementary Fig. 11C), as ASB8 KO has been demonstrated to prevent compound-induced degradation22, and incubated them with each test compound in the presence of cycloheximide (CHX) for 1.5 h. Inclusion of CHX is critical as it prevents de novo protein synthesis, ensuring that newly synthesized, compound-free XPO1 does not interfere with occupancy measurements. After compound washout, cells were treated with biotinylated LMB (b-LMB) for 1.5 h, either immediately (day 0) or after an additional 24 h CHX incubation (day 1). Following b-LMB exposure, whole-cell lysates (WCL) were analyzed directly by Simple Western to measure total protein or incubated with streptavidin beads to isolate b-LMB-bound XPO1 before Simple Western analysis (Fig. 4B). Free-fraction XPO1 was quantified as the streptavidin-captured XPO1 signal normalized to total XPO1 in the WCL and expressed relative to DMSO controls at each time point (Fig. 4C). Higher free-fraction values correspond to lower occupancy by the test compound. FR-027 produced a substantially greater, dose-dependent increase in free-fraction XPO1 at day 1 compared with selinexor and eltanexor (Fig. 4C, D), demonstrating that FR-027 dissociates from XPO1 significantly more rapidly and reversibly than the benchmark inhibitors.

To further compare the duration of XPO1 inhibition by FR-027 with that of selinexor and eltanexor, the XPO1 reporter cell line was treated with increasing concentrations of each compound for two h (t0; Fig. 4E). Cells were imaged using a high content imager, and nuclear accumulation of the SV40NLS-AcGFP-PKINES reporter cargo was quantified relative to the DMSO control. All three compounds strongly inhibited XPO1-mediated nuclear export of cargo, as indicated by robust nuclear reporter accumulation (Fig. 4F). Next, cells were extensively washed to remove residual compound and incubated for an additional 24 h. To ensure that any recovery of cytoplasmic cargo localization reflected dissociation of the inhibitor from XPO1 rather than newly synthesized protein, ongoing protein turnover was blocked by continuous treatment with CHX and the proteasome inhibitor MG-132. After 24 h (t24, Fig. 4E), cells were fixed and imaged again. Cells previously treated with FR-027 showed substantial restoration of cytoplasmic reporter localization, whereas those treated with selinexor or eltanexor exhibited markedly less recovery (Fig. 4F). An independent experiment using RanBP1, a known endogenous cellular XPO1 cargo that is actively exported from the nucleus and predominantly cytoplasmic under basal conditions48, yielded the same result (Fig. 4G). Notably, selinexor reversibility appears greater under in vitro conditions than in cellular assays, likely reflecting differences in thiol availability, inhibitor retention, and intracellular environment compared to defined biochemical conditions in vitro.

Together, these data demonstrate that FR-027 is a highly reversible XPO1 inhibitor, whereas selinexor and eltanexor dissociate more slowly from XPO1. Consistent with previous studies, eltanexor displayed greater reversibility than selinexor16,18.

We further validated the inhibition of XPO1-dependent nuclear export using endogenous RanBP1. Treatment of wild-type Jurkat cells with FR-027, selinexor, or eltanexor induced pronounced nuclear accumulation of RanBP1 (Fig. 5C). To confirm that FR-027 selectively targets XPO1, we repeated these experiments in Jurkat cells expressing mutant XPO1 in which cysteine 528 is replaced by serine. We previously demonstrated that substituting this residue with serine or alanine disrupts covalent engagement of XPO1 inhibitors and confers resistance49. Consistent with this mechanism, FR-027 did not alter the subcellular localization of RanBP1 in XPO1C528S mutant cells (Fig. 5C), confirming selective engagement of XPO1. These findings independently verify that FR-027, also in a cellular context, targets Cys528 in XPO1, analogous to selinexor and eltanexor49 with comparable potencies.

FR-027 potently inhibits cancer cell proliferation through selective targeting of XPO1

We next examined whether the nuclear export inhibition observed in cells also impacts tumor cell proliferation and compared FR-027 with the benchmark inhibitors. To determine the anti-proliferative potential, we exposed a diverse panel of human cancer cell lines to increasing concentrations of each compound and measured cell viability after 72 h. FR-027 reduced tumor cell viability across all tested cell lines with EC50 values ranging from 0.04 to 0.28 µM (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 2), demonstrating potency comparable to selinexor and eltanexor. To further confirm that this anti-cancer activity results from the selective inhibition of XPO1, we assessed FR-027 in cancer cell lines engineered to express XPO1 containing the Cys528 to Ser528 substitution. Consistent with XPO1-dependent activity, FR-027 potently inhibited proliferation of wild-type cells but showed no activity in XPO1C528S mutant cells, even at micromolar concentrations (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 12). Because induction or restoration of nuclear p53 activity promotes tumor suppression50, and considering p53 is a well-characterized XPO1 cargo51, we next investigated whether FR-027 enhances p53 nuclear localization and induces apoptosis, as previously described for selinexor44. In wild-type MOLT-4 cells, FR-027 increased both nuclear p53 levels and total cellular p53 abundance (Fig. 6B–D). In contrast, FR-027 failed to increase p53 levels in XPO1C528S mutant MOLT-4 cells, mirroring the behavior of selinexor and eltanexor, and confirming XPO1-selective activity. FR-027 is orally bioavailable and demonstrates excellent blood-brain barrier penetration

After completing our in vitro evaluations, we next examined the in vivo characteristics of FR-027 using small-animal cancer models, beginning with pharmacokinetic (PK) profiling. Male CD-1 mice received FR-027 either intravenously (IV, 5 mg/kg) or by oral gavage (PO, 10 mg/kg). Plasma samples were collected at eight time points ranging from five min to 24 h after administration to determine plasma drug concentrations over time (Supplementary Fig. 13A). FR-027 exhibited high oral bioavailability (F = 74.4%), supporting the feasibility of oral dosing in subsequent studies. The plasma half-life (T1/2) ranges from 1 to 3 h depending on the route of administration. Dose escalation resulted in proportionally higher plasma concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 13B), indicating dose-linear pharmacokinetics that facilitate predictable exposure, simplified dosing regimens, and reliable therapeutic drug monitoring. Strikingly, FR-027 accumulated in the brain to levels substantially higher than in the plasma, with a brain-plasma ratio of 4.31 (Supplementary Fig. 13B). For comparison, previously reported brain-plasma ratios in mice for the first-generation XPO1 inhibitor selinexor and the second-generation inhibitor eltanexor are 0.71 and 0.19 respectively18, underscoring the superior central nervous system (CNS) penetration of FR-027. This enhanced brain exposure suggests a potential therapeutic advantage for FR-027 in the treatment of CNS malignancies and other neurological disorders involving dysregulated nuclear export.

FR-027 increases overall survival in a MOLT-4 T-ALL xenograft model

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of FR-027, we first employed an orthotopic T-ALL mouse model previously used to demonstrate the potent activity of the clinical XPO1 inhibitor selinexor52. NSG mice were intravenously injected with MOLT-4-Luc2 cells and subsequently treated by oral gavage with vehicle (soluplus) or FR-027 at 100 mg/kg, three times per week, beginning five days after tumor inoculation (Fig. 7A). This dosing schedule was chosen to parallel the maximum tolerable frequency of selinexor administration. FR-027 was well tolerated. During the first week of treatment, mice experienced up to 5% reduction in body weight but rapidly regained weight during a two-day drug holiday (Fig. 7B). In subsequent weeks, body weight remained stable until tumor progression itself led to weight loss. Tumor burden was monitored twice weekly by bioluminescence imaging (BLI). FR-027 significantly delayed tumor growth, as evidenced by BLI measurements on day 23 after inoculation (Fig. 7C–E). At this time point, tumor progression in the vehicle-treated control group necessitated euthanasia due to reaching humane endpoints. FR-027 treatment resulted in a significant extension of overall survival (OS) compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 7F). At necropsy, tumor-bearing control animals exhibited markedly enlarged spleens, consistent with elevated levels of circulating leukemic cells. In contrast, spleens from FR-027-treated animals were not significantly enlarged and comparable in size to those from non-tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 7G), suggesting reduced leukemic cell dissemination in peripheral tissues. The magnitude of therapeutic benefit achieved with FR-027 was similar to that previously reported for selinexor (KPT-330) in this model52. In advanced disease stages, untreated animals showed progressive neurologic decline, characterized by reduced mobility followed by paralysis of one or both hind limbs, indicating CNS involvement. FR-027 improves overall survival in a syngeneic ID8-fLuc ovarian cancer model

After demonstrating activity in a hematological cancer model in immunocompromised mice, we next evaluated FR-027 in a solid tumor setting with an intact immune system. This allowed a more comprehensive assessment of the compound's efficacy and tolerability in a physiologically relevant context. We employed the syngeneic ID8-fLuc ovarian cancer model in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 7H)53,54. Treatment began 21 days after tumor cell inoculation, when tumors were well established, to mimic advanced stage (FIGO stage III-IV) ovarian cancer in patients54,55. Mice received either oral vehicle (soluplus) or 100 mg/kg FR-027 administered three times weekly for five weeks, or a single intraperitoneal injection on day 21 of carboplatin (100 mg/kg) plus paclitaxel (10 mg/kg), representing the standard-of-care56,57. While FR-027 was well tolerated, mice treated with carboplatin plus paclitaxel rapidly lost weight following dosing (Fig. 7I), and three of six animals in this group reached humane endpoints from chemotoxicity by day 31.

Control mice developed tumor-induced ascites from day 40 onward, and ascites fluid was drained whenever body weight reached 32 grams to prolong survival54. Ascites onset and first drainage were delayed in both FR-027- and carboplatin-paclitaxel-treated groups (Fig. 7J). Continued treatment with FR-027 after ascites drainage resulted in a significant extension of overall survival (median 86 days) compared with control animals (58 days) and carboplatin-paclitaxel-treated mice (73 days, Fig. 7K). Together, these results show that FR-027 demonstrates in vivo efficacy not only in early-stage hematological cancers but also in advanced disease within an immunocompetent solid tumor model.

FR-027 treatment does not affect platelet, neutrophil, or lymphocyte counts

After establishing the antitumor efficacy of FR-027 across multiple orthotopic cancer models, we next compared its hematologic safety profile with that of selinexor and eltanexor. Thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, and anemia are among the most common grade 3-4 treatment-related adverse events for both XPO1 inhibitors in patients40,43. Female CD-1 mice were orally treated five times per week with vehicle control (soluplus or DMSO), 100 mg/kg FR-027, or 15 mg/kg eltanexor, or three times per week with 10, 15, or 20 mg/kg selinexor (Fig. 10A). FR-027 treatment did not result in weight loss compared to the vehicle control condition, whereas selinexor treatment was associated with substantial weight loss on the day after administration, which intensified at higher doses (Fig. 10B). Blood counts were performed on peripheral blood collected before treatment initiation and throughout three weeks of dosing (Fig. 10A). In FR-027-treated mice, platelet counts increased by up to 24% relative to vehicle-treated controls, whereas high-dose selinexor and eltanexor caused reductions in platelet numbers of 23% and 19%, respectively (Fig. 10C). In contrast, selinexor and eltanexor induced significant leukopenia, with dose-dependent reductions in lymphocytes (−48% to −81%) and neutrophils (−29% to −63%) compared with their vehicle controls. Discussion

Targeted covalent inhibitors (TCIs) have become a mainstay of clinical pharmacology due to advances in selectivity profiling and on-target mechanistic understanding. Although most widely used in oncology, TCIs also have applications in neurodegenerative, cardiovascular, and gastrointestinal diseases, as well as in anti-infective therapy1. The reactive functional groups, or warheads, govern reactivity and duration of action. The predominant strategy for covalently engaging cysteines is the Michael addition-type reaction. Notably, 9 out of 14 covalent drugs approved between 2011 and 2019 feature an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl warhead that forms such adducts1. While successful, confirmed by the number of FDA-approved or clinical trial stage drugs in this category, some targets may benefit from warheads rationally tailored to the specific protein nucleophile, most commonly a cysteine, to optimize efficacy and safety4.

Potent XPO1 inhibitors covalently engage cysteine residue 528 within the hydrophobic NES-binding groove of XPO1 via Michael addition-type chemistry15,58,59. Despite extensive efforts by us and others, non-covalent XPO1 inhibitors have not yet achieved sufficient potency60,61. Clinically, XPO1 is validated, and the first-in-class inhibitor selinexor (XPOVIO®) is approved for treatment of patients with multiple myeloma or DLBCL33,34. However, across indications, selinexor’s acceptable efficacy is often offset by grade 3-4 adverse events, including thrombocytopenia, anemia, neutropenia, nausea, and fatigue, observed in clinical trials35,40,62. The second-generation inhibitor eltanexor has shown improved tolerability in early-stage clinical trials but has not progressed to approval18,42. Although XPO1 inhibition exhibits anticancer activity across diverse tumor types, its translation into widespread clinical benefit, particularly in solid tumors, has been challenging. In multiple trials, selinexor’s therapeutic potential has been insufficient and limited by dose-limiting toxicities, despite clear efficacy in selected hematological malignancies. Thus, there remains a clear opportunity for next-generation XPO1 inhibitors.

Here, we introduce FR-027, a potent and reversible XPO1 inhibitor that employs a novel warhead to engage cysteine 528 via nucleophilic aromatic substitution (Fig. 1C). FR-027 selectively inhibits XPO1 through covalent interaction with Cys528 (Figs. 2 and 5C; 6B) and reduces cancer cell viability across multiple tumor types in vitro (EC50 0.04-0.28 µM) (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 12).

Importantly, in contrast to selinexor and eltanexor, FR-027 does not induce proteasomal degradation of XPO1 protein (Fig. 3). We recently demonstrated that selinexor induces an allosteric conformational change in XPO1 that exposes a cryptic degron for ASB8 recruitment, thereby driving ubiquitination and degradation22,23. Here, we show that the conjugated FR-027* adduct is too small to stabilize the open conformation of the XPO1 groove required to expose the degron that recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase ASB8 (Figs. 2A, D, 3D). Consequently, proteasomal turnover is not triggered after FR-027 treatment.

To our knowledge, ligand engagement of the XPO1 groove without stabilization of the open conformation has not previously been observed. In the absence of ligand, XPO1 adopts a closed NES-binding groove, and ligands that stabilize the open state must overcome this intrinsic structural preference. This implies that a threshold level of ligand occupancy within the groove is required to stabilize the open state. FR-027 likely engages the XPO1 groove similarly to selinexor, transiently stabilizing the open conformation and positioning its electrophilic warhead for reaction with Cys528. However, the subsequent nucleophilic aromatic substitution, removing the triazole-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl group and leaving the 1-methyl-4-nitro-1H-imidazole adduct (FR-027*) on Cys528, simultaneously abolishes the inhibitor-XPO1 contacts needed to keep the groove open. Once these interactions are lost, the NES-binding groove relaxes back to its closed conformation, preventing exposure of the ASB8-recognition surface and thereby decoupling covalent inhibition of nuclear export from ligand-induced XPO1 degradation.

This mechanism differs from that of other XPO1 inhibitors that also fail to induce degradation, such as LMB and SINE analogs KPT-396 and KPT-404. In those cases, the inhibitors are bulky enough to occupy the groove and sterically block the ASB8-binding surface, directly competing with ASB823. In contrast, FR-027 prevents ASB8 recruitment not through steric occlusion but by failing to stabilize the conformational opening required to expose the degron.

Although the detailed kinetic sequence requires further biophysical study, prior work demonstrates that SINE analogs with substituents smaller than the bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl group of selinexor (and FR-027) show progressively reduced conjugation to Cys52823. These observations indicate that binding energy contributed by the triazole-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl moiety is critical for maintaining sufficient residence time to enable covalent bond formation with Cys528.

Compared with selinexor and eltanexor, FR-027 also exhibits increased reversibility of conjugation to XPO1’s Cys528 and consequent nuclear export inhibition (Fig. 4). This reversibility likely arises from the nucleophilic aromatic substitution reaction used to form the covalent adduct. In this chemistry, the cysteine thiolate of XPO1 functions both as an effective nucleophile and a competent leaving group, allowing displacement of the nitroimidazole by cellular nucleophiles and regeneration of free cysteine. Consistent with this mechanism, the covalent FR-027* adduct can be displaced by a thiol such as DTT (Fig. 4A), yielding a nitroimidazole-DTT product that does not resemble the original FR-027 and therefore is unlikely to re-engage the XPO1 groove. As a result, re-conjugation to Cys528 is improbable. In contrast, selinexor and related analogs form Michael addition adducts with Cys528 that can, dependent on the local environment, reverse through slow retro-Michael elimination, regenerating both the original electrophilic inhibitor and the free cysteine. Because the released inhibitor is intact and retains the structural features necessary for groove binding, released selinexor can re-engage XPO1 and subsequently re-react with Cys528. Nevertheless, FR-027’s reversibility of engagement to XPO1’s Cys528 does not impact its potency in terms of inhibition of XPO1 function nor anti-cancer activity as compared to the benchmark inhibitors.

We hypothesize that the combination of reversible target engagement and preserved XPO1 protein levels after FR-027 treatment may enable better tolerability than selinexor or eltanexor by limiting the impact on normal cells, as already reflected in the hematologic safety profile we observed in mice (Fig. 10). We further hypothesize that in mice the improved tolerability of FR-027 compared with selinexor is primarily due to its reversibility rather than the absence of XPO1 protein degradation, since selinexor itself does not appear to reduce XPO1 levels in murine cells (Supplementary Fig. 11A). This suggests that in humans, the safety advantage of FR-027 over selinexor is expected to be even greater. The exact target residence time and potential off-target interactions of FR-027 have not been elucidated. However, hematologic toxicity observed with selinexor has been predominantly attributed to on-target effects of sustained XPO1 inhibition rather than off-target activity63. In this context, the time-limited engagement of XPO1 by FR-027, together with the absence of XPO1 degradation, may contribute to improved hematologic tolerability by reducing prolonged suppression of nuclear export in sensitive cell populations15,64. This feature distinguishes FR-027 mechanistically from slowly reversible or degradation-inducing covalent inhibitors, which can maintain extended target inhibition even after systemic drug clearance.

Pharmacokinetic data indicate good oral bioavailability of FR-027. Despite a relatively short plasma half-life (Supplementary Fig. 13A), it demonstrates strong antitumor efficacy, consistent with the pharmacology of covalent inhibitors whose durable target engagement mitigates dependence on sustained plasma exposure. Although FR-027’s adduct is reversible, continuous drug presence may not be necessary for a durable biological effect. Once XPO1 inhibition restores nuclear localization of tumor suppressors and initiates apoptosis, downstream commitment to cell death is irreversible, thereby limiting cancer cell escape. Accordingly, FR-027 demonstrates potent anti-leukemic efficacy in an aggressive MOLT-4 T-ALL xenograft model52 and confers significant survival benefit as monotherapy in a metastatic syngeneic ovarian cancer model (Fig. 7). FR-027 also decreased spleen weight relative to vehicle-treated tumor-bearing controls (Fig. 7G). Similar reductions in spleen size have been reported for selinexor and eltanexor in patient-derived xenograft models of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and B- and T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia44,65. Notably, selinexor is currently being evaluated in JAK inhibitor-naïve myelofibrosis in the Phase 1/3 SENTRY trial (NCT04562389), in which ≥35% spleen-volume reduction is a co-primary endpoint and has already been achieved in the Phase 1 portion of the trial66,67.

Importantly, FR-027 crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) (Supplementary Fig. 13B), broadening the applicability of XPO1 inhibition to CNS tumors, where selinexor has not yielded favorable outcomes41,68. Therapeutic options for aggressive CNS malignancies such as glioblastoma (GBM, WHO grade IV) and primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) remain limited and non-curative, with 5-year survival rates of approximately 5% and 33%, respectively69–72. GBM invasiveness, inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and cell plasticity, compounded by the blood-brain barrier, render treatment especially challenging73,74. XPO1 represents a particularly compelling therapeutic target in GBM because it operates as a core, nonredundant cellular mechanism rather than a lineage- or state-specific pathway. Moreover, because it is an essential and centrally integrated process required across diverse cellular states, XPO1 inhibition is inherently less vulnerable to intratumoral heterogeneity and adaptive plasticity, which are hallmarks of GBM resistance to targeted therapies. Given the breadth of preclinical sensitivity to XPO1 inhibition across heterogeneous tumors, BBB-penetrant XPO1 inhibitors may be particularly valuable. Indeed, FR-027 significantly extended overall survival in two orthotopic brain tumor models (Figs. 8, 9). Eltanexor’s limited BBB penetration has been hypothesized to contribute to its gastro-intestinal tolerability relative to selinexor, though this remains unproven. If true, the favorable tolerability observed for BBB-penetrant FR-027 would be unexpected, unless explained by its distinct chemistry and mechanistic profile. In our studies, FR-027 did not produce notable adverse effects compared with selinexor or eltanexor.

Although the U87-MG xenograft model (Fig. 8) provides a well-established and reproducible system to assess in vivo antitumor activity, it is limited in its ability to fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of human glioblastomas, including their infiltrative nature and partially protective blood-brain barrier, particularly at the invasive tumor margins. As such, the observed efficacy of FR-027 in this model may overestimate drug penetration, availability, and therapeutic impact relative to more restrictive tumor regions in patients. Therefore, follow-up studies in more clinically representative and heterogeneous glioblastoma models will be essential to further validate these findings and strengthen their translational relevance.

As the lead compound of a new XPO1 inhibitor class, FR-027 remains amenable to further chemical optimization. The nitro group, often considered a structural alert because of potential bioactivation and genotoxicity, could be replaced with alternative electron-withdrawing substituents75. Despite such concerns, several nitro-containing drugs (e.g., metronidazole, a nitroimidazole containing cornerstone antibiotic systemically applied for treating a wide variety of infections) are safely and widely used.

The encouraging efficacy and tolerability of FR-027 in our in vivo models, together with the absence of severe hematologic toxicities, support continued development. Whereas selinexor and eltanexor frequently induce severe thrombocytopenia, anemia, lymphopenia, and neutropenia that limit dosing and may compromise patient outcomes, FR-027 did not induce thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, or neutropenia in heavily treated mice (Fig. 10), suggesting a more favorable safety profile. Importantly, the 100 mg/kg dose used for FR-027 in our experiments is not an empirically determined maximally tolerated dose. Higher doses may be tolerated, but the current formulation does not allow administration at concentrations above this level. Evaluation of higher doses would require further optimization of the formulation. We note that although FR-027 is administered at a much dose than selinexor or eltanexor, direct comparison of the administered doses between FR-027, selinexor, and eltanexor is not informative with respect to intrinsic in vivo potency, since administered dose does not inherently reflect pharmacologically relevant exposure at the site of action. FR-027 represents an early-stage compound from an ongoing drug discovery program, whereas selinexor is a clinically approved agent that has undergone extensive multi-parameter optimization over many years, including optimization of potency, exposure, formulation, tolerability, and pharmaceutical properties. Advancement towards clinical translation will require further comprehensive formulation, pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and toxicology studies in larger animal species to further compare FR-027 to clinical-stage compounds selinexor and eltanexor.

In summary, the unique chemical, structural, biological, and pharmacological properties arising from FR-027’s novel cysteine-targeting warhead confer properties that may allow it to complement, or even serve as an alternative to, selinexor. Its reversible covalent mechanism and lack of compound-induced XPO1 degradation may enhance tolerability. FR-027 delays tumor growth and extends survival across hematological and solid tumor models, in peripheral and CNS settings, and in both immunosuppressed and immunocompetent mice. With strong BBB penetration, FR-027 matches selinexor’s efficacy in hematological models and exceeds it in CNS models, while permitting frequent dosing without high-grade adverse effects in our models. Collectively, these data establish FR-027’s promising molecular and pharmacological profile and justify further investigation toward clinical development. More broadly, the discovery of this novel nucleophilic aromatic substitution-based warhead opens additional avenues for the design of next-generation covalent drugs, as illustrated here for XPO1 inhibitors.

Compounds

Stock solutions of selinexor (S7252, SelleckChem), eltanexor (S8397, SelleckChem), MG-132 (2194, Cell Signaling Technology), cycloheximide (C7698, Sigma-Aldrich), and biotinylated leptomycin B (HY-164710, MedChemExpress) were prepared in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich).

FR-027 was synthesized in-house in a two-step process. The reaction scheme is shown in Supplementary Fig. 14. Chemicals obtained from commercial sources (Merck, Acros Organics, J&K Scientific, Alfa Aesar, TCI Chemicals, Fluorochem or BLDPharm) were used without further purification. All reactions were performed in oven-dried screw-capped reaction tubes or round-bottom flasks. If heating was required, the reactions were performed in oven-dried screw-capped reaction tubes placed in an aluminum heating block on a hot plate with magnetic stirring or in round-bottom flasks placed in a heated oil bath. No special precautions were taken for the exclusion of moisture. All reactions were conducted under an inert atmosphere (argon or nitrogen). Dry reaction solvents were purchased from commercial sources. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on silica gel 0.20 mm 60 with fluorescent indicator UV254 (pre-coated aluminum sheets) from Merck. Compounds were visualized under visible light, UV irradiation (254 nm) or with iodine-coated silica. Column chromatography was performed with a CombiFlash EZ prep apparatus using BGB Scorpius Silica 60 Å Irregular − 50 mm cartridges or via standard column chromatography with silica 60, 70-230 mesh S4 (Acros) as the stationary phase. Solvents were concentrated with a rotary evaporator at 50 °C. NMR spectra were acquired on commercial instruments (Bruker Avance III HD 400 or Bruker Avance II + 600 MHz with OPUS software) and chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) referenced to tetramethylsilane (1H), or the internal (NMR) solvent signal (13C). The following abbreviations were used to explain multiplicities: s = singlet, q = quartet, m = multiplet. Coupling constants (J) were reported in Hertz (Hz). High-resolution mass spectra were acquired on a quadrupole orthogonal acceleration time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Synapt G2 HDMS, Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Samples were infused at 3 μL/min and spectra were obtained in positive mode with a resolution of 15 000 (FWHM) using leucine enkephalin as lock mass. Melting points were determined on a Reichert-Jung Thermovar system and are uncorrected. The synthesis of FR-027 starts from amide 1 which is transformed to NH−1,2,4-triazole 2 via a stepwise condensation and cyclisation reaction. First, a N,N-dimethylformimidamide intermediate is formed starting from amide 1 with treatment of N,N-dimethylformamide dimethyl acetal (DMF-DMA). Subsequent treatment with hydrazine hydrate under acidic medium yields NH−1,2,4-triazole 2 in excellent yield (99%). Next, a nucleophilic aromatic substitution is performed on 5-chloro-nitroimidazole 3 with NH−1,2,4-triazole 2 resulting in FR-027 in a very good yield of 79%. The overall yield of the reaction pathway toward FR-027 is 78%. The synthesis was easily scaled up from milligram scale to multi-gram scale ( > 10 gram, described below) without significant loss of yield.

STEP 1: Synthesis of 3-(3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl)−1H−1,2,4-triazole 2: 3,5-Bis(trifluoromethyl)benzamide 1 (20.0 g, 0.08 mol) was mixed with N,N-dimethylformamide dimethyl acetal (DMF-DMA) (53.1 mL, 0.40 mol) and heated to 120 °C for two h. In vitro pull-down binding assays

For the binding assay in Figs. 1D and 3B, 0.5 µM XPO1 was preincubated with either DMSO, 1 µM selinexor, or 1 or 2.5 µM FR-027, with or without 1.5 μM RanGTP for 15 min at room temperature, before addition to 0.5 µM GST, GST-PKINES or GST-ASB8•ELOB/C, and 20 µL of GSH bead slurry in a total volume of 200 µL in the aforementioned TB buffer. Reactions were rotated at room temperature for 30 min and spun down at 6000 × g for 1 min from which the supernatant sample was obtained. Supernatant was discarded, and the beads were further washed with 500 µL of TB buffer three times, before the bound samples on beads were boiled in sample buffer and visualized by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie staining.

For the in vitro reversibility assay in Fig. 4A, 11 μM XPO1 was incubated with 33 μM FR-027, selinexor, leptomycin B (LMB) or DMSO for 1 h at 4 °C in TB buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 110 K(OAc), 2 mM Mg(OAc)2, 10% glycerol). Samples were then diafiltrated to remove excess inhibitor and exchanged into pH-adjusted buffer (100 mM MES pH 6.5, HEPES pH 7.5 or Tris pH 8.5; 110 mM K(OAc), 2 mM Mg(OAc)2, 10% glycerol) either without reducing agent or supplemented with 10 mM GSH or 2 mM DTT. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C, then diafiltrated again to remove deconjugated inhibitor and exchanged back into TB buffer. Binding assays were performed by incubating 0.5 µM GST or GST-PKINES, 0.5 µM XPO1 and 1.5 μM RanGTP with 20 µL GSH-Sepharose beads (bead bed volume; Cytiva) in a 200 µL reaction in TB buffer. For time 0 samples, an additional 1.5 µM inhibitor was added immediately prior to the binding assay. After 1 h incubation with rotation at 4 °C, all samples were washed three times with 500 µL TB buffer, and bound proteins were eluted, separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie staining. Binding assays were performed in triplicate. For quantification, the ratio of XPO1/GST-NES peak area was determined using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and normalized to the XPO1 lane without inhibitor at pH 7.5 in each assay.

Intact mass spectrometry

Intact mass analysis of XPO1 samples was performed by the UT Southwestern Proteomics Core Facility. Two samples were analyzed: purified XPO1 treated with DMSO (control) and purified XPO1 treated with FR-027. One sample of each condition was analyzed (n = 1 per condition). Samples were prepared in TB buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 110 mM KOAc, 2 mM Mg(OAc)2, 10% glycerol), at a concentration of 16 µM and treated with DMSO or 80 µM FR-027 prior to analysis. Samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), using a Sciex X500B QTOF mass spectrometer operating in positive ion mode, coupled to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II HPLC. Samples were injected onto a POROS R1 reverse-phase column (2.1 ×30 mm, 20 µm particle size, 4000 Å pore size) and desalted. The mobile phase flow rate was 300 μL/min and the gradient was as follows: 0-3 min, 0% B; 3-4 min, 0-15% B; 4-16 min, 15-55% B; 16-16.1 min, 55-80% B; 16.1-18 min, 80% B. The column was then re-equilibrated at initial conditions prior to the subsequent injection. Buffer A contained 0.1% formic acid in water, and buffer B contained 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The mass spectrometer was controlled by Sciex OS v3.3 with the following parameters: ion source gas 1, 30 psi; ion source gas 2, 30 psi; curtain gas, 35; CAD gas, 7; temperature, 300 °C; spray voltage, 5500 V; declustering potential, 125 V; collision energy, 10 V. Data were acquired from 400-2000 m/z with a 0.5 s accumulation time and 4 time bins summed. The acquired mass spectra were deconvoluted using BioPharmaView v. 3.0.1 to determine intact molecular masses. The experimentally determined molecular masses were compared with the expected masses of unmodified XPO1 and FR-027-modified XPO1 to confirm protein identity and covalent ligand modification.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

3 mg/mL XPO1 was mixed with 5 times molar excess of FR-027 and 0.025% Tyloxapol (BioVision) in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM Mg(OAc)2 and 2 mM TCEP. A 300 mesh R1.2/1.3 copper grid (Quantifoil) was first glow-discharged for 80 s at 30 mA using the PELCO easiGlow (Ted Pella). 3.5 µL of the XPO1 sample was applied to the grid, blotted with a filter paper for 3.5 s at blot force of 0 and plunge frozen in liquid ethane using the Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) at 7 °C with 95% humidity. The grid was first pre-screened at the UT Southwestern Cryo-Electron Microscopy Facility and then sent to the Pacific Northwest Center for Cryo-EM (PNCC) for data collection for 48 hrs. A total of 25,729 movies were collected on a Titan Krios 300 kV microscope equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and a 10 eV slit-width Selectris X energy filter using EPU software (Thermo Fisher) at 165,000X magnification, 50 e-/Å2 total dose and defocus values of −2.4 to −0.9 µm at 0.1 increments.

Cryo-EM data processing and modeling

Cryo-EM data were processed in CryoSPARC. Movies from the full dataset (25,700 movies) were processed in CryoSPARC Live for motion correction, CTF estimation, and initial particle picking using blob picking, yielding ~9.2 million particles. After three rounds of 2D classification, ~2.9 million particles remained. These particles were subjected to heterogeneous refinement using three reference classes derived from a previously determined unliganded XPO1 structure23, which separated the dataset into three classes. One class containing ~1.1 million particles yielded a ~ 4 Å map resembling full-length XPO1 and was used to train Topaz for particle repicking, resulting in 1.8 million particles. Following one additional round of 2D classification, 1.7 million particles were retained and subjected to six rounds of heterogeneous refinement using two classes (one representing the expected XPO1 structure and one junk class). This procedure yielded a final set of 630,291 particles, which were used for non-uniform refinement, producing a reconstruction at 2.95 Å resolution.

Cryo-EM model building, refinement, and analysis

Unliganded XPO1 (PDB 9OG9) was used as an initial model and was roughly docked into unsharpened maps using UCSF Chimera or ChimeraX79 and then subjected to real-space refinement with global minimization and rigid body restraints in PHENIX80 and manual model building using ISOLDE81 and Coot82. Model validation was performed in PHENIX. Structures were analyzed using PyMOL (version 3.1; Schrödinger). All figures were generated using PyMOL or ChimeraX.

XPO1 occupancy assay47

U87-MG ASB8 knockout cells were seeded at 500 000 cells per well in 6‑well plates one day prior to compound treatment. On the treatment day, 1 mL fresh medium containing 5 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX) and DMSO or different concentrations of FR-027, selinexor or eltanexor (final concentrations: 0.1, 0.25 µM and 1 µM) were added and cells were incubated for 1.5 h. Medium was then removed, cells were washed once with PBS, and plates corresponding to the 24 h washout time points received fresh medium containing CHX. The animals were immobilized using a stereotactic frame, and the cells, suspended in 2 μl DPBS, were inoculated 0.5 mm anterior to the bregma, 2.5 mm lateral from the midline and 2.5 mm below the dura mater with a 26-gauge syringe (Hamilton).

Bioluminescence imaging

Bioluminescence imaging was used to measure tumor burden in an IVIS Lumina S5 system (Revvity) with Living Image Software (v. 4.8.0, Revvity) twice a week. Mice were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane (Iso-vet®, Dechra) in 100% oxygen and injected subcutaneously in the scruff with 3 mg D-luciferin (Revvity) in DPBS. Bioluminescence was recorded when total flux (photons/s), the sum of all radiance values (photons/sec/cm²/sr) within a region of interest, reached its maximum at 10–20 min after luciferin injection.

Treatment

Treatment started on day five after inoculation unless otherwise specified. Mice received either vehicle control, FR-027 (100 mg/kg), selinexor (10 mg/kg, S7252, SelleckChem), or eltanexor (10 mg/kg, S8397, SelleckChem) three to five times per week via oral gavage until humane endpoints were reached. The vehicle consisted of 90% soluplus dissolved in a 0.01 M phosphate buffer containing 0.5% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) to compare with treatment with FR-027 or 5% DMSO, 40% PEG300 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 5% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) in water to compare with treatment with selinexor and eltanexor. FR-027 was formulated in 90% soluplus and dissolved in a 0.01 M phosphate buffer containing 0.5% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich). Selinexor and eltanexor were dissolved according to the manufacturer’s instructions (SelleckChem): 2% (selinexor) or 5% (eltanexor) DMSO, 40% PEG300 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 5% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) in water. FR-027 was dissolved at 10 mg/mL prior to administration; selinexor and eltanexor were dissolved at 1 mg/mL. Animals were weighed daily and visually inspected for treatment-induced and tumor-related symptoms.

In the ID8-fLuc ovarian cancer model, carboplatin (100 mg/kg) and paclitaxel (10 mg/kg) were administered through intraperitoneal injection via a single dose on day 21. FR-027 was administered via oral gavage three times per week for a period of five weeks, starting from day 21 after inoculation. All control mice received vehicle control administrations similar to the FR-027 treatment scheme. Mice were monitored and weighed daily once ascites accumulation appeared. Ascites was drained when mice reached 32 grams in body weight.

Humane endpoints

T-ALL-induced mice were euthanized when they lost 20% of their initial body weight or displayed symptoms of end-stage disease (paralysis, ascites, failure to thrive). Ovarian cancer-induced mice were evaluated for survival based on criteria published previously by Baert et al54. Briefly, mice were euthanized when they lost over 2 g of body weight in 48 h or 3 g in 7 days, or displayed increased respiratory frequency, a hunched posture with tremor, or no spontaneous movement when nudged. Mice with brain tumors were euthanized when they lost 20% of their initial bodyweight or reached grade 3–4 symptoms on a scoring system described by Riva et al.85. The scoring system consists of five grades: grade 0, no symptoms; grade 1, mild hemi-paresis (mouse moving slower than normal, with no circling behavior); grade 2, moderate hemi-paresis (mouse moving slower, unstable gait with some oscillations or drops, no circling behavior); grade 3, hunched posture, severe hemi-paresis (constant circling behavior, frequent drops, inability to move) or both; grade 4, moribund mouse. Survival analysis was based on reaching these humane endpoints in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. No predefined ethical limits for distress were exceeded. Animals that developed an immediate (within three min) procedure-related adverse event following oral gavage and did not survive were excluded from survival analysis, but data collected up to that point were retained for all other analyses. Animals that failed to achieve adequate tumor engraftment were excluded from all analyses. In hematology analysis, animals were excluded from the experiment when any of the baseline blood count parameters fell outside two standard deviations of the cohort mean.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 2% isoflurane (Iso-vet®, Dechra) in 100% oxygen. Breathing was monitored continuously, and body temperature was maintained during acquisition through a water heating system. MRI images were obtained at the KU Leuven Department of Imaging and Pathology MicroImaging facility on a Biospec 94/20 scanner (9.4 T, Bruker) equipped with actively shielded gradients, a quadrature volume coil for transmission (Tx), and a mouse brain surface coil for reception (Rx, Bruker). Coronal T2-weighted images were acquired using a 2D Rapid Acquisition with Relaxation Enhancement (RARE) sequence (repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, effective echo time (TE) = 33.9 ms, RARE factor = 8, field of view (FOV) = 20 × 20 mm, matrix = 200 × 200, in-plane resolution = 100 µm, 24 contiguous slices of 0.5 mm thickness, averages = 4, acquisition time = 180 s). Images were collected using ParaVision Acquisition software (v. 6.0.1) and analyzed using ImageJ software for visualization.

Hematologic analysis

Eight- to twelve-week-old female CD-1 mice (Charles River Laboratories) received either vehicle control, FR-027 (100 mg/kg), selinexor (10, 15, or 20 mg/kg), or eltanexor (15 mg/kg) three to five times a week via oral gavage. Compounds were formulated as previously described. Animals were weighed daily and visually inspected for treatment-induced symptoms. Whole blood was collected from the submandibular vein three days prior to treatment (baseline) and at days 5 (week 1), 12 (week 2), and 19 (week 3) of treatment in EDTA-coated capillary tubes (Microvette®, Sarstedt). Blood counts were performed on an Element HT5 hematology analyzer (Antech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were analyzed within 1.5 h of collection. Blood count values at each time point were first normalized to the baseline value of the animal to control for individual baseline variability. Mice with a baseline blood sample that deviated more than two standard deviations from the mean in any parameter were excluded from the analysis. The resulting ratios were then compared with the mean value of the vehicle control group at the corresponding time point and expressed as percentages.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (v.10.6.1) or R software (package survival, v.2026.01.1). All analyses were performed at a threshold α level of 0.05. Information on the number of replicates and specific statistical tests used for each dataset is described in the respective Figure caption. In vitro experiments were reproduced at least three times. Details on all statistical analyses are available in the Source Data file.
