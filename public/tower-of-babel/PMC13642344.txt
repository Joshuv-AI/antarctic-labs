TRIM37 interacts with TRIM28 to maintain primordial germ cell identity during migration
Authors: Qing Li, Xidi Yin, Chuanyin Li, Li Guo, Yingyi Wang, Yanbo Cheng, Nan Liu, Qi Yin, Yifu Ding, Meng Yan, Zihan Liu, Junyu Chen, Jin Zhang, Shuo Huang, Wangpeng Gu, Anjing Guo, Huanhuan Deng, Yuanyuan Li, Di Chen, Ian Chambers, Hui Zheng, Ronggui Hu, Man Zhang, Jinsong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During primordial germ cell (PGC) specification, repression of somatic programs is essential for the establishment of germline identity. However, mechanisms that safeguard PGC fate thereafter remain unknown. Here, we identify the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM37 as a critical safeguard of PGC fate during migration. Trim37 deficiency causes severe PGC defects beginning at embryonic day 9.5 (E9.5) with complete PGC depletion by E12.5, and leads to an aberrant transition toward somatic cell states. Mechanistically, TRIM37 binds TRIM28 through its MATH domain and ubiquitinates TRIM28 via its RING domain, enhancing the TRIM37–TRIM28 interaction and promoting the nuclear retention of TRIM37. Forced nuclear export of TRIM37 results in PGC loss. Moreover, disruption of TRIM37 ligase activity or mutation of TRIM28 ubiquitination sites compromises PGC maintenance. We further show that the TRIM37–TRIM28 complex, likely acting in cooperation with AP2γ, restricts chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac levels at somatic gene loci, thereby repressing somatic transcriptional programs in PGCs. Together, our findings uncover a TRIM37–TRIM28–AP2γ regulatory complex that safeguards germ cell fate by preventing the activation of somatic transcriptional programs during PGC migration.

Introduction

The germline and soma are the two essential cell types within an individual embryo proper. During embryonic development, primordial germ cells (PGCs), the precursors of gametes, are vital for gametogenesis and species continuity.1,2 In mice, PGCs form from pluripotent epiblast cells at ~E6.25 and are induced by bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling from extraembryonic tissues.3–6 BMP, together with WNT signaling, downregulates OTX2 and upregulates germline-specific factors to segregate germ cells from somatic cells (SCs).3–5,7 Three master transcription factors, BLIMP1 (also known as PRDM1), PRDM14, and AP2γ (also known as TFAP2C), are essential for mouse PGC specification8–10 and coordinately downregulate somatic genes while activating germline-specific and pluripotency-associated regulators.11,12 Following specification, PGCs proliferate and migrate through the dorsal mesentery to the genital ridges.3,13,14 During migration, extensive epigenetic reprogramming occurs, including genome-wide DNA demethylation and alterations in histone modifications, which prepare the epigenome for meiosis and eventual fertilization.1–3,15 However, the mechanisms that maintain PGC identity during migration and epigenetic reprogramming remain largely unknown.

Here, we identify TRIM37, a member of the TRIM E3 ubiquitin ligase family,16 as indispensable for maintaining PGC identity during migration. TRIM37 consists of a Really Interesting New Gene (RING) domain, B-box motifs, a coiled-coil region, and a meprin and TRAF homology (MATH) domain at its C-terminus.16 TRIM37 mutation causes Mulibrey (muscle-liver-brain-eye) nanism in humans, a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by severe growth failure due to defects in mesoderm-derived tissues, as well as infertility.16–18 We show that TRIM37 interacts with TRIM28, a corepressor of Krüppel-associated box zinc finger proteins (KRAB-ZFPs).19 The interaction between TRIM37 and TRIM28 represses somatic gene expression and maintains PGC fate. TRIM37 ubiquitinates TRIM28 at lysine residues K774 and K779, strengthening the interaction between TRIM37 and the TRIM28–NuRD repressor complex at somatic cell-specific genomic loci. Our results show that repression of somatic developmental genes by the TRIM37–TRIM28 complex is essential for maintaining PGC fate.

Results

Genetic screening in mice identifies Trim37 that is essential for PGC development

We previously described a system to screen for functional genes in mice.20,21 In this approach, androgenetic haploid embryonic stem cells (AG-haESCs) with deletions in the differentially methylated regions of the imprinted loci H19-DMR and IG-DMR (termed DKO-AG-haESCs) are injected into the oocyte cytoplasm to efficiently produce gene-modified animals, referred to as semi-cloned mice.20 Combining DKO-AG-haESCs with an sgRNA library allows mice with specific gene mutations to be produced in a single step.21 However, the overall efficiency of the method remained suboptimal. Recently, through combined application with the CRISPR-Cas9-based base editor, this approach allowed us to characterize individual residues critical for the function of DND1 that is an essential gene in PGC development,22 in which, a CAG promoter showed to have a high mutagenesis efficiency in vivo.23 Therefore, we first aimed to improve and optimize the approach for functional gene screen by replacing the EFS promoter21 with the CAG promoter in the DKO-AG-haESC line carrying an Oct4-EGFP transgene (Supplementary information, Fig. S1a). We generated cell lines referred to as EFS-C9 and CAG-C9. Comparison of the CAG-C9 and EFS-C9 lines showed that the CAG-C9 lines had higher expression of Cas9 mRNA and protein (compare Supplementary information, Fig. S1b, c with S1g, h) and superior GFP mutagenesis efficiency (Supplementary information, Fig. S1d−f, i−k). We selected the CAG-C9-3 line for subsequent experiments. As expected, the three designed sgRNAs efficiently induced indels in Dnd1 in CAG-C9-3 cells (Supplementary information, Fig. S2a, b). Next, the ability of these cells to mutate Dnd1 in vivo was tested by injecting CAG-C9-3 cells infected with a lentivirus carrying Dnd1 sgRNA1 into oocytes to produce semi-cloned embryos (Supplementary information, Fig. S2c). Encouragingly, 13 of 15 semi-cloned embryos derived from CAG-C9-3 cells showed biallelic mutations in Dnd1, leading to PGC developmental failure at E12.5 (Supplementary information, Fig. S2d).

Using the CAG-C9-3 cell-mediated in vivo mutagenesis system, we performed genetic screening for genes crucial for PGC development using sgRNA libraries targeting different proteins (Fig. 1a). In one library, we targeted the TRIM gene family, one of the largest E3 ubiquitin ligase families.24 TRIM proteins possess a tripartite motif (TRIM) composed of a RING domain, one or two B-box domains, and a coiled-coil region (RBCC)24,25 and may contain additional domains. TRIM proteins participate in various developmental processes, including innate immunity, carcinogenesis, and germ cell development, through ubiquitination-mediated protein degradation or stabilization.25–27 We first performed RNA-seq of wild-type PGCs at E9.5 and analyzed the FPKM values of TRIM family genes (Supplementary information, Table S1). Among the 68 members,28 33 TRIM genes were expressed (average FPKM > 0.5) in PGCs and were selected for further analysis (Fig. 1b). These genes were divided into two groups containing 15 and 18 genes according to their transcript levels, and three sgRNAs per gene were designed for sgRNA library construction (Fig. 1b; Supplementary information, Table S2). After transfection of the sgRNA libraries into CAG-C9-3 cells, we obtained two stable cell populations carrying constitutively expressed Cas9 and sgRNAs (Trim-library-1 and Trim-library-2). Deep-sequencing analysis revealed that all sgRNAs were present in the cells (Supplementary information, Fig. S3a). We obtained E12.5 semi-cloned embryos using intracytoplasmic AG-haESC injection (ICAHCI) and dissected the gonads for phenotypic analysis (Fig. 1a). We chose embryos that exhibited impaired or failed PGC development for genotypic analysis. In our initial phenotype-driven screen, 238 semi-cloned embryos were produced from Trim-library-1 and Trim-library-2, among which two embryos exhibited PGC failure, and two others showed reduced PGC numbers (Supplementary information, Fig. S3b, c). To evaluate overall mutagenesis efficiency and library coverage, we genotyped 56 embryos, including all four phenotypic embryos and 52 embryos with apparently normal PGC development (Supplementary information, Fig. S3c and Table S3). Of these, 46 embryos carried biallelic mutations that collectively covered 24 Trim genes, indicating high editing efficiency and broad representation of the library in vivo (Supplementary information, Fig. S3c and Table S3). The two embryos with reduced PGC numbers were independently targeted at distinct genes, yet other embryos targeted at the same genes exhibited normal PGC numbers (Supplementary information, Table S3), suggesting potential off-target effects. In contrast, two independent embryos harboring biallelic Trim37 mutations displayed severe PGC loss, fulfilling our predefined screening criteria for a positive hit and identifying Trim37 as a positive candidate gene in this screen (Fig. 1c; Supplementary information, Fig. S3c). To validate its function in PGC development, we generated two independent Trim37 knockout models (Trim37ko1, 7-bp insertion in exon 4; Trim37ko2, 1-bp deletion in exon 2) by zygote injection of CRISPR-Cas9 with different sgRNAs (Supplementary information, Fig. S3d, e). Both knockout models lacked detectable TRIM37 protein (Supplementary information, Fig. S3f, g). Notably, PGCs were completely absent in the E12.5 gonads of both models (Fig. 1d; Supplementary information, Fig. S3h). Together, these results confirm Trim37 as an essential factor for mouse PGC development.

TRIM37 protects PGC development in a predominantly PGC-intrinsic manner

Trim37 was expressed in both PGCs and SCs during PGC development (Supplementary information, Fig. S4a, b). To determine whether Trim37-deletion-induced PGC depletion is germ cell autonomous, we first constructed a Trim37 knockout embryonic stem cell line (Trim37-del) based on an E14-derived ESC line in which 3×Flag was knocked in at the C-terminus of Trim37 (Trim37-3×Flag) (Supplementary information, Fig. S4c, d). We then induced Trim37-3×Flag and Trim37-del ESCs into PGC-like cells (PGCLCs) in vitro according to a published protocol.7 The results showed that Trim37 deletion significantly decreased the number of PGCLCs on day 6 but not on day 4 (Supplementary information, Fig. S4e−i), suggesting that Trim37 is crucial for PGC development rather than PGC specification.

Next, to further investigate the role of Trim37 in PGCs in vivo, we first generated Blimp1-T2A-Cre knock-in mice and validated Cre-mediated loxP recombination in gonads using Rainbow mice29 (Supplementary information, Fig. S5a−c). We next produced a Trim37flox mouse model using semi-cloning technology (Supplementary information, Fig. S5d). By crossing the two models, we generated PGC-specific Trim37 knockout mice, which displayed normal growth (Fig. 1e; Supplementary information, Fig. S5d−g). However, these mice completely lacked germ cells in both testes and ovaries (Supplementary information, Fig. S5h, i). In situ hybridization (ISH) for Stella showed a complete absence of PGCs in E12.5 Trim37cko embryos (Fig. 1f). These results demonstrate that PGC depletion in Trim37 knockout mice is most likely PGC autonomous.

Trim37 is involved in PGC migration

Phenotypic analysis of Trim37ko1 and Trim37ko2showed that adult mice from both lines displayed no overt abnormalities in body weight or morphology (Supplementary information, Fig. S5j−m), failing to phenocopy the prenatal-onset growth failure observed in Mulibrey nanism (MUL) patients.16,30 Interestingly, however, their testes and ovaries were atrophic and completely devoid of germ cells (Supplementary information, Fig. S5n−q), effectively recapitulating the infertility phenotype associated with MUL.31 Previous studies have shown that Finnish MUL patients carrying the FINmajor mutation (c.493-2A>G) in TRIM37 are infertile, as this mutation causes aberrant splicing at the next AG site, resulting in a truncated protein lacking 174 amino acids.17,18 We therefore created a homozygous FINmajor mouse model carrying this mutation (Supplementary information, Fig. S6a) and found normal growth but no testicular or ovarian germ cells in adults (Supplementary information, Fig. S6b−e). In addition, both male and female genital ridges lacked PGCs at E12.5 (Fig. 1g). These results suggest a conserved function of TRIM37 in germ cell development in both mice and humans.

To investigate the stage at which PGC development fails in Trim37 knockout mice, embryos were assessed at different embryonic days. Alkaline phosphatase staining revealed comparable numbers of PGCs at the base of the allantois in wild-type and Trim37ko1 embryos at E8.5 (Fig. 1h, i). However, whole-mount EGFP immunostaining in Trim37ko1 embryos showed a 70% reduction in PGC numbers 1 day later at E9.5 (Fig. 1j, k). This number further declined to less than 10% at E11.5, as quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 1l−n). Consistently, similar results were obtained in Trim37cko embryos. At E8.5, comparable numbers of PGCs were found in control and Trim37cko embryos (Fig. 1o; Supplementary information, Fig. S6f). However, by E9.5, PGC numbers were specifically decreased in Trim37cko embryos (Fig. 1p; Supplementary information, Fig. S6g). These results establish that TRIM37 is not required for PGC specification but plays an essential role in PGC migration.

Trim37 maintains PGC fate by inhibiting somatic transition

To investigate the fate of PGCs in Trim37 knockout embryos, we first evaluated whether Trim37 deficiency causes apoptosis in PGCs by cleaved caspase-3 staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay. However, no apoptotic signal was observed in PGCs from Trim37ko1 embryos at E9.5 (Fig. 2a, b). To explore the mechanisms underlying PGC developmental failure, we carried out single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of wild-type and Trim37-deficient PGCs and SCs at E8.5 and E9.5 (Fig. 2c; Supplementary information, Fig. S7a). Because Oct4-EGFP does not reliably label early PGCs, we used the Nanog-EGFP transgenic model at E8.5 to distinguish PGCs from SCs based on EGFP expression32 (Supplementary information, Fig. S7a). To generate Trim37 knockout PGCs in Nanog-EGFP transgenic embryos, we designed an sgRNA targeting exon 5 of Trim37 (Supplementary information, Fig. S7b). This sgRNA effectively introduced a stop codon at the glutamine residue corresponding to amino acid 116 of TRIM37 (termed Trim37ko3) by intracytoplasmic co-injection with the hA3A-eBE3-Y130F base editor,33 leading to complete PGC deficiency at E12.5 (33 homozygotes out of 36 embryos) (Supplementary information, Fig. S7b−d). Interestingly, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis showed that Trim37ko3 PGCs clustered with their wild-type counterparts at E8.5 (Supplementary information, Fig. S7e−h). However, at E9.5, 20% (6 of 30) of Trim37-depleted PGCs (termed atypical Trim37ko1 PGCs) clustered with SCs (Fig. 2d; Supplementary information, Fig. S7i, j). Moreover, gene expression in atypical PGCs displayed characteristics of somatic development (Fig. 2e, f). We next integrated our scRNA-seq data with published E9.5 embryo datasets.34 While wild-type and normal Trim37 knockout PGCs clustered with previously reported PGC populations, atypical E9.5 Trim37 knockout PGCs instead grouped with mesoderm-associated lineages (Supplementary information, Fig. S8a). To test the hypothesis that Trim37 deficiency leads to loss of PGC fate, we established a lineage tracing system. Blimp1-T2A-Cre sperm were injected into mTmG oocytes to trace PGCs, followed by injection of a base editor mixture to introduce Trim37ko3 into the resulting embryos (Fig. 2g). In these embryos, PGCs switch from red fluorescence (membrane-targeted tdTomato, mT) to green fluorescence (membrane-targeted enhanced green fluorescent protein, mG) due to Blimp1-T2A-Cre expression35 (Fig. 2g). GFP was detectable in control gonads but not in mTmG-Trim37ko3 gonads at E12.5 (Supplementary information, Fig. S9a), suggesting that Trim37 deficiency leads to the loss of PGCs prior to E12.5. To further investigate the timing of PGC loss, we collected control and mTmG-Trim37ko3 embryos at E9.5 and analyzed OCT4 protein (a PGC marker) and GFP (traced PGCs) signals (Fig. 2h). The results showed that the majority of GFP-positive cells (237 cells from 9 embryos) were PGCs in controls ( ~93.3%), whereas the proportion was ~32% in mTmG-Trim37ko3 embryos (185 cells from 11 embryos), with the remaining GFP+ population lacking OCT4 expression (Fig. 2h, i). These results indicate that Trim37 is essential for maintaining PGC fate during migration and suggest that TRIM37 depletion may induce a switch from PGC to SC identity beginning at E9.5 (Fig. 2j).

TRIM37 interacts with TRIM28 to maintain PGC fate

To further assess how TRIM37 maintains PGC identity, we examined gonadal extracts for TRIM37-interacting proteins by immunoprecipitation (IP) followed by mass spectrometric analysis (Fig. 3a). We first generated an AG-haESC line carrying 3×Flag at the N-terminus of TRIM37 (Trim373×Flag AG-haESCs) and produced a mouse model by injecting these cells into oocytes (Supplementary information, Fig. S9b). IP-mass spectrometry (MS) of Trim373×Flag and wild-type gonadal lysates identified 39 proteins (Fig. 3b; Supplementary information, Table S4). Among these candidates, TRIM28 ranked second and was prioritized because of its known role in heterochromatin maintenance and repression of differentiation genes,36,37 although its early embryonic lethality prevented its inclusion in the initial screening.38,39 To confirm the interaction between TRIM37 and TRIM28, co-IP assays were performed in HEK293T cells overexpressing tagged TRIM37 and TRIM28 proteins. The results revealed a strong interaction between TRIM37 and TRIM28 (Fig. 3c). To identify the functional domain of TRIM37 responsible for TRIM28 binding, we co-overexpressed TRIM28 with full-length or various truncated TRIM37 constructs in HEK293T cells (Fig. 3e). These analyzes showed that deletion of the MATH domain abolished TRIM37 binding to TRIM28 (Fig. 3f). Among TRIM family members, only TRIM37 contains a MATH domain, which facilitates protein–protein interactions.16 To investigate whether the TRIM37–TRIM28 interaction is required for PGC development, we constructed an AG-haESC line carrying a deletion of the MATH and injected it into Trim37ko1 oocytes (Supplementary information, Fig. S9c, d). The results showed that deletion of the TRIM37 MATH domain caused almost complete depletion of PGCs at E12.5 (Supplementary information, Fig. S9e).

A previous study reported that MUL patients carry a G322V mutation in the MATH domain.40 To determine whether mutation of G322 alone was sufficient to disrupt PGC development, we generated mice carrying the G322V mutation (Trim37G322V/G322V model). Notably, these mice recapitulated the infertility phenotype observed in Trim37ko1 mice, exhibiting complete loss of PGCs and mature germ cells in both males and females (Fig. 3g, h). Co-IP showed that the G322V mutation dramatically decreased the interaction between TRIM37 and TRIM28 (Fig. 3i). These results establish that the TRIM37–TRIM28 interaction is essential for PGC development and suggest that the MUL patient-associated mutation causes infertility by disrupting the TRIM37–TRIM28 interaction.

TRIM28-mediated nuclear retention of TRIM37 is essential for PGC development

To further investigate the interaction between TRIM37 and TRIM28, we overexpressed TRIM37 in HeLa cells and observed that the majority of TRIM37 localized to the cytoplasm (Fig. 4f). However, co-expression of TRIM28 with TRIM37 prompted the colocalization of TRIM37 and TRIM28 within the nucleus (Fig. 4f). This indicates that TRIM28 promotes nuclear accumulation of TRIM37. The G322V mutation in the TRIM37 MATH domain, which disrupts the interaction with TRIM28, dramatically decreased the nuclear accumulation of TRIM37 (Fig. 4f, g). Furthermore, compound R487E + V488E mutations, which interfere with the nuclear import of TRIM28,41 also disrupted nuclear accumulation of TRIM37 (Fig. 4f, g). These results indicate that TRIM28 facilitates the nuclear localization of TRIM37, suggesting that TRIM37 may play an essential role in the nucleus.

To determine whether nuclear localization is critical for TRIM37 function, we deleted a predicted nuclear localization sequence (NLS)42 in Trim373×Flag AG-haESCs (Fig. 4h). Western blot analysis showed that TRIM37 was reduced in the nuclei of Trim37ΔNLS AG-haESCs (Fig. 4i). However, gonads derived from embryos generated by injection of Trim37ΔNLS AG-haESCs into Trim37ko1 oocytes showed normal PGC development, possibly due to residual nuclear localization of TRIM37 (Fig. 4i, j). Therefore, to force TRIM37 to exit the nucleus, the NLS was replaced with a nuclear export signal (NES) sequence (Trim37NES) (Fig. 4h). Generation of AG-haESCs carrying Trim37NES showed that nuclear TRIM37 was almost completely lost in Trim37NES cells (Fig. 4k). In this case, and in contrast to the findings with Trim37ΔNLS, PGCs were nearly eliminated from Trim37NES/ko1 gonads at E12.5 (Fig. 4l). These changes occurred without affecting the TRIM37–TRIM28 interaction (Fig. 4m), suggesting that TRIM28 facilitates the nuclear function of TRIM37 during mouse PGC development.

Ubiquitination of TRIM28 by TRIM37 protects PGC development

Previous studies indicate that TRIM37 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase.43,44 To assess whether TRIM37 directly ubiquitinates TRIM28, we performed in vitro ubiquitination reactions. The results showed that TRIM37 conjugated mono- and poly-Ub chains to TRIM28 as an E3 ligase (Fig. 5a). Simultaneous overexpression of TRIM28 and TRIM37 in E. coli resulted in robust ubiquitination of TRIM28 (Fig. 5b). Ubiquitination activity was dependent on a critical cysteine residue (C18) within the TRIM37 RING domain,43 as TRIM37C18R failed to catalyze TRIM28 ubiquitination in E. coli (Fig. 5b). Consistently, in mammalian cells, ubiquitination of TRIM28 also required an intact C18 residue in TRIM37 (Fig. 5c). Trim37 deletion in mice reduced ubiquitination of TRIM28 in tissues with high Trim37 expression (brain and testis)18 (Supplementary information, Fig. S10a, b). Notably, the G322V mutation of TRIM37, which disrupts the TRIM37–TRIM28 interaction, abolished TRIM28 ubiquitination by TRIM37 (Supplementary information, Fig. S10c). To explore the role of the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRIM37 in PGC development, we deleted the RING domain in AG-haESCs (Trim37ΔRING) (Supplementary information, Fig. S10d). Injection of these AG-haESCs into Trim37ko1 oocytes disrupted PGC development at E12.5 (Fig. 5d; Supplementary information, Fig. S10e, f). Consistent with this, Trim37C18R/C18R mice exhibited reduced PGC numbers at E12.5 (Fig. 5e−g) and significantly impaired germ cell development in both testes and ovaries (Supplementary information, Fig. S10g, h). Together, these data indicate that the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRIM37 is important for PGC development.

Canonical ubiquitination sites in substrates are characterized by Gly-Gly remnants on ubiquitinated lysine (K) residues.45 To identify the lysine residues in TRIM28 modified by ubiquitin, ubiquitinated TRIM28 proteins were immunopurified from E. coli lysates for MS analysis (Fig. 5h). K774 and K779 in the bromodomain of TRIM28 were identified as residues ubiquitinated by TRIM37 (Fig. 5h). Mutation of these lysine residues to arginine (R) reduced TRIM28 ubiquitination in HEK293T cells, especially when the mutations were combined (K774R + K779R) (Fig. 5i). The K774R + K779R mutations in TRIM28 also impaired the interaction between TRIM28 and TRIM37 (Supplementary information, Fig. S10i). Moreover, the C18R mutation in TRIM37 hindered its interaction with TRIM28 and its nuclear accumulation (Supplementary information, Fig. S10j, k), suggesting that ubiquitination of TRIM28 enhances its interaction with TRIM37.

To explore the effect of the TRIM28 double-KR mutation on PGC development, we first generated a K774R + K779R AG-haESC line based on Blimp1-T2A-Cre cells (Fig. 5j; Supplementary information, Fig. S10l). We then injected Trim28K774R+K779R AG-haESCs into Trim28flox oocytes to obtain conditional point-mutant embryos (Fig. 5k). ISH for Stella and flow cytometry revealed that TRIM28K774R+K779R reduced PGC numbers at E12.5, partially mimicking the Trim37 knockout phenotype (Fig. 5l−n). These results indicate that TRIM37-mediated ubiquitination of TRIM28 at K774 and K779 facilitates the nuclear accumulation of TRIM37, thereby playing an important role in PGC development.

TRIM28 is a crucial co-repressor involved in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional repression through the recruitment of repressor complexes, including SETDB1 and the NuRD complex.19,36,38 To explore which co-repressor may be recruited by the TRIM28 complex, H3K27ac and H3K9me3 profiles were analyzed in both control and Trim37ko3 PGCs using CUT&Tag (Supplementary information, Fig. S11a). Globally, Trim37 deficiency led to increased H3K27ac levels at genomic regions associated with developmental processes (Fig. 6c; Supplementary information, Fig. S12a−c). In contrast, a subset of pattern specification-related genes exhibited altered H3K9me3 signals in Trim37-deficient PGCs, with both gains and losses detected (Supplementary information, Fig. S12d–f). However, the overall enrichment of H3K9me3 at these differential peaks remained very low (Supplementary information, Fig. S13a), suggesting that Trim37 loss does not induce substantial changes in H3K9me3-mediated heterochromatin. Consistently, H3K9me3 displayed relatively low enrichment at promoters across the three gene groups analyzed (Supplementary information, Fig. S13b). By comparison, H3K27ac signals exhibited a modest but consistent increase at transcription start sites (TSSs) following Trim37 knockout (Supplementary information, Fig. S13c), particularly in genes that became transcriptionally activated (FPKM ≤ 1 in control and FPKM ≥ 5 in Trim37ko3 PGCs) (Supplementary information, Fig. S13d). In addition, distal H3K27ac signals were clearly enriched at regulatory regions associated with genes upregulated in Trim37ko3 PGCs (Supplementary information, Fig. S13e), suggesting that enhancer activation also contributes to the transcriptional derepression observed in Trim37-deficient PGCs.

H3K27ac is regulated by the NuRD histone deacetylase complex and is closely associated with chromatin accessibility.46,47 Notably, regions showing enhanced chromatin accessibility in ATAC-seq data were also marked by elevated H3K27ac levels but low H3K9me3 across promoters, intergenic regions, and gene bodies in Trim37ko3 PGCs (Fig. 6d; Supplementary information, Fig. S14a). Approximately 454 of 1431 upregulated genes in Trim37ko3 PGCs exhibited open chromatin regions and increased H3K27ac signals in their regulatory regions (Fig. 6e). These genes, such as Ccnd1 and Btg2, were enriched in axonogenesis, mesenchyme development, and embryonic organ morphogenesis (Fig. 6e, f). To further investigate the relationship between open chromatin regions and TRIM28 complex binding sites in Trim37-deficient PGCs, we performed CUT&RUN for TRIM28 in control and Trim37ko3 PGCs (Fig. 6g; Supplementary information, Fig. S14b). Among the differential TRIM28-binding peaks, 95.8% (12,381/12,922) were downregulated in Trim37ko3 PGCs and were associated with developmental genes (Supplementary information, Fig. S14b−d). Furthermore, ~324 of the 1431 upregulated genes in Trim37ko3 PGCs, including Chpf, Obsl1, and Rnf122, exhibited open chromatin regions and loss of TRIM28 binding in their regulatory regions (Fig. 6h, i; Supplementary information, Fig. S14e). These findings collectively indicate that TRIM37 deficiency compromises the ability of the TRIM28–NuRD complex to repress developmental genes through chromatin remodeling.

Neither TRIM37 nor TRIM28 is a DNA-binding protein, and they are thus likely to act through intermediary sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins.48,49 We therefore performed an unbiased motif analysis of the TRIM28 downregulated peaks in Trim37ko3 PGCs. This analysis showed strong enrichment of AP2γ binding sites within the downregulated TRIM28 binding peaks (Fig. 6j). Previous studies have reported that AP2γ-null PGCs exhibit derepression of somatic genes in specified PGCs .10 AP2γ expression persists until E12.5 during PGC development,10 suggesting that AP2γ may function after germ cell specification. In line with this notion, conditional deletion of Ap2γ in PGCs after specification using Blimp1-Cre resulted in severe germ cell depletion, with only a few residual PGCs detectable at E9.5 and complete loss of PGCs by E12.5 (Supplementary information, Fig. S15a−f). We next performed a co-IP assay to assess a possible interaction between TRIM37 and AP2γ, which revealed interaction between these proteins in HEK293T cells (Fig. 6k). To examine the relationship between AP2γ occupancy and TRIM37–TRIM28-associated chromatin sites, we performed CUT&RUN for AP2γ using E9.25 PGCs (Supplementary information, Fig. S11a). Notably, 4089 of the 12,381 downregulated TRIM28 binding peaks in Trim37ko3 PGCs overlapped with AP2γ binding sites (Fig. 6l). Additionally, the downregulated TRIM28 binding peaks showed stronger AP2γ binding, which was also associated with developmental genes (Supplementary information, Fig. S16a, b). Together, the enrichment of AP2γ motifs at TRIM28-occupied loci, the physical interaction between TRIM37 and AP2γ, and their genomic co-occupancy support a model in which AP2γ may cooperate with, or help stabilize, the TRIM37–TRIM28 repressor complex at a subset of somatic gene loci. Discussion

The somatic program must be repressed to specify germ cells from the pluripotent epiblast.9,50,51 However, it remains uncertain whether the conflict between the germline and soma concludes after specification. Previous work showed that pluripotency transcription factors play important roles in PGC development, with conditional inactivation of either Oct4 or Sox2 resulting in loss of PGCs due to apoptosis or reduced proliferation, respectively.52,53 In addition, deletion of Nanog or Esrrb from the germline reduces PGC numbers by 50%−80% between E12.5 and E15.54–56 These studies have supported the prevailing hypothesis that, during migration, the PGC population is maintained through a balance between proliferation and apoptosis, with apoptosis representing the exclusive fate of mammalian PGCs that exit the germline due to genetic mutations or lack of somatic environmental cues.52,57 Moreover, developmentally compromised PGCs that evade apoptosis may serve as the origin of non-gonadal pediatric germ cell tumors.58 Our findings demonstrate that migrating germ cells rely on the TRIM37–TRIM28 repressor complex to actively repress somatic fate following specification.

A long-standing question in developmental biology is how germ cell fate, characterized by pluripotency and developmental immortality, is stably maintained during progressive somatic differentiation.59 In C. elegans, PGCs exhibit a pronounced propensity to adopt somatic cell identities upon loss of key epigenetic regulators, such as the histone chaperone LIN-53 or components of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2).60,61 Similarly, in zebrafish, depletion of Dnd1 causes PGCs to undergo fate transformation, accompanied by marked alterations in cell morphology and shifts in transcriptional programs toward those of somatic lineages.62 However, whether these reprogrammed PGCs are capable of fully differentiating into functional somatic cell types remains unresolved.62 In this context, our results support a model in which Trim37-deficient PGCs exit the germline program and enter an aberrant somatic-like state marked by partial and heterogeneous activation of multiple somatic gene programs, rather than bona fide lineage-restricted transdifferentiation. As a future direction, we propose testing whether, in the absence of Trim37, PGCs progressively lose intrinsic germline determinants and fail to stabilize a coherent somatic identity because they do not receive appropriate lineage-specifying cues, ultimately resulting in elimination through cell death.

Mechanistically, mono- and poly-ubiquitination of TRIM28 by the RING domain of TRIM37 facilitates the interaction between TRIM28 and TRIM37 and promotes the nuclear accumulation of TRIM37, thereby enabling the TRIM37–TRIM28 complex to exert its repressive function. TRIM37 deficiency disrupted the binding profile of the TRIM28 repressor complex, leading to increased chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac modification at soma-specific loci and the upregulation of associated genes. Intriguingly, our data suggest that AP2γ, a critical transcription factor in PGC specification, might be responsible for the recruitment of the TRIM37–TRIM28 repressor complex to downstream target loci in migrating PGCs. Previous studies have shown that AP2γ enhances somatic gene repression and germline program activation during PGC specification.10,12 Considering that AP2γ is expressed in PGCs from E7.5 to E12.5, a period encompassing the entire germ cell migration period, our work supports the notion that AP2γ is not only important for PGC specification but also critical for the maintenance of germ cell fate in migrating PGCs. However, TRIM28 peaks only partially and inconsistently overlapped with regions of increased ATAC-seq signal or AP2γ binding sites, suggesting that additional mechanisms beyond the currently proposed TRIM37–TRIM28–AP2γ model may contribute to the regulation of somatic gene repression in PGCs. It is also possible that technical limitations, such as low cell number, epitope accessibility, or transient protein–DNA interactions, hinder accurate detection of TRIM28 or AP2γ chromatin binding in PGCs. Taken together, these observations support a model in which AP2γ likely cooperates with the TRIM37–TRIM28 complex at a subset of loci, while additional yet-to-be-identified factors may also contribute to targeting or stabilizing the complex at other sites.

Interestingly, TRIM37 mutants identified in MUL patients also lead to infertility in mouse models (FINmajor and G322V). Moreover, AP2γ and TRIM28 are expressed together with TRIM37 during human PGC development (Supplementary information, Fig. S16c), implying a conserved mechanism of TRIM37-mediated PGC regulation in humans and mice. Future studies using human PGCLC systems will be important to directly test this conservation, particularly through targeted disruption of TRIM37 or modeling of patient-associated mutations. In summary, our work outlines a comprehensive mechanism that protects germ cell fate from somatic differentiation in specified PGCs, providing a deeper understanding of germ cell biology and potentially offering implications for reproductive health and developmental biology.

Materials and methods

Mouse studies

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Center for Excellence in Molecular Cell Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) in an accredited specific pathogen-free facility. The room was maintained at a controlled temperature (20−25 °C) and humidity (30%−70%) under a 12 h light-dark cycle. B6D2F1 (C57BL/6J × DBA2) female mice were used as oocyte donors. ICR female mice were used as pseudopregnant foster mothers. Oct4-EGFP and Nanog-EGFP transgenic mice have been described previously and were used for labeling PGCs.23,32 Ap2γflox/flox mice (RRID: IMSR-JAX:023408) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory.63 mTmG mice (full name: B6.129(Cg)-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm4(ACTB-tdTomato,-EGFP)Luo/J; JAX strain 007676) were used to trace PGCs. Trim37ko1, Trim373×Flag, Trim37flox, Trim37C18R, Trim37G322V, and FINmajor mice were described and used in our recent work,64 which was conducted in parallel with the current study. In this study, we further generated additional mouse models, including Trim37ko2, Trim37ko3, Trim37ΔRING, Trim37ΔMATH, Trim37ΔNLS, Trim37NES, Blimp1-T2A-Cre, Trim28flox, and Trim28K774R+K779R. Embryos were collected from recipient mice and analyzed at specific developmental stages, including E8.5, E9.25, E9.5, E11.5, and E12.5, to assess PGCs.

Plasmid construction

To generate CRISPR-Cas9 plasmids for gene manipulation in AG-haESCs, specific sgRNAs were synthesized, annealed, and inserted into the BpiI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA)-linearized pX330-mCherry plasmid (Cat# 98750; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA). To generate donor plasmids, the left and right homologous arm sequences were amplified from genomic DNA using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Cat# P505; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and then ligated into the PMD19T vector together with the knock-in sequence. To generate expression plasmids, wild-type and mutant CDSs of Cas9, TRIM37, TRIM28, and AP2γ were ligated into PiggyBac-CAG, pcDNA-CMV, or pACYC-Ub-HA plasmids. All inserted sequences were validated by Sanger sequencing.

AG-haESC culture and transfection

AG-haESCs carrying deletions in the H19-DMR and IG-DMR regions, as well as an Oct4-EGFP transgene (termed H19ΔDMR-IGΔDMR-AGH-OG3 or 408), were used to generate EFS-C9 and CAG-C9 cells for genetic screening. Enhanced AG-haESCs carrying H19, H19-DMR, and IG-DMR deletions together with an Oct4-EGFP transgene (termed H19Δ13kb-IGΔDMR-AGH-OG3-2 or 408-TKO-265), were used for the generation of Trim373×Flag, Trim37ΔNLS, and Trim37NES cells. AG-haESCs (named IGΔDMR-H19ΔDMR-AGH-2 or O4820) were used for the generation of Blimp1-T2A-Cre, Trim28flox, Trim37ΔMATH, Trim37ΔRING, and Trim28K774R+K779R cells. These AG-haESCs were cultured in 2i medium consisting of DMEM (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) supplemented with 15% FBS (ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China), penicillin–streptomycin, non-essential amino acids, nucleosides, L-glutamine, β-mercaptoethanol, 1000 U/mL LIF, 1 μM PD0325901 (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA), and 3 μM CHIR99021 (Selleck Chemicals). Cells were passaged into a new 6-well plate at a density of 1.2 × 106 cells per well, ~30 min before transfection in 2 mL of 2i medium and transfected with 250 µL of Opti-MEM containing 8 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 and 4−6 µg of plasmid DNA. After 18−24 h, mCherry-positive haploid cells were enriched by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using an Aria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and seeded into a new well of a 6-well plate at a density of 6000−10,000 cells per well. After 6−8 days, single-cell-derived clones were picked and genotyped using PCR and Sanger sequencing.

PGCLC differentiation

Differentiation was performed as previously described with minor modifications.7,66 Briefly, Trim37-3×Flag and Trim37-del ESCs were cultured in N2B27 medium supplemented with 2i/LIF for at least four passages. Subsequently, cells were seeded into 12-well plates coated with 10 ng/mL fibronectin at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in N2B27 medium supplemented with 1% KSR, bFGF, and activin A to generate epiblast-like cells (EpiLCs). After 2 days, EpiLCs were collected and aggregated at a density of 2000 cells per 25 μL drop in PGCLC medium containing 50 ng/mL BMP4, 50 ng/mL BMP8a, 10 ng/mL SCF, 10 ng/mL EGF, and 1000 U/mL LIF. The drops were plated onto the lids of tissue culture dishes and incubated over a PBS reservoir for 2 days. Subsequently, the hanging drops were transferred to untreated culture dishes and cultured in PGCLC medium with rotation at 72 rpm. After 2 days (PGCLC day 4) and 4 days (PGCLC day 6), aggregates were collected and dissociated into single cells using 0.05% trypsin for 10 min. The dissociated cells were stained with anti-SSEA1 eFluor™ 660 (1:20 dilution; Cat# 50-8813-41; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and anti-CD61 PE (1:50 dilution; Cat# 104307; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and then analyzed by flow cytometry.

Construction of the lentiviral sgRNA library

sgRNA sequences targeting each gene were selected based on previous work67 and synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China. All sgRNA sequences were annealed, mixed thoroughly, and then ligated into the BbsI-linearized pKLV-U6(BbsI)-PGKpuro2ABFP vector (Cat# 50946; Addgene) with T4 DNA ligase (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). To assess library coverage, we randomly picked more than ten E. coli clones from each dish to ensure that different clones contained different sgRNAs. Bacteria from 5 × 10-cm dishes were scraped and combined for purification of sgRNA library plasmids using an EndoFree Maxi Plasmid kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The method used to produce lentivirus from the sgRNA library plasmids and the lentiCas9-Blast plasmid (Cat# 52962; Addgene) was similar to that described in our previous work.23 For the lentiviral sgRNA library, virus volumes were optimized to achieve a multiplicity of infection of 0.3, ensuring that most cells received a single copy of the lentiviral vector. EFS-C9 or CAG-C9 AG-haESCs were infected with lentivirus for ~48 h and then selected with puromycin for 48 h. The infected cells were passaged and sorted for haploid cells by flow cytometry for ICAHCI and subsequently collected for genomic DNA extraction.

Generation of embryos and mice via ICAHCI

To generate semi-cloned embryos and mice via ICAHCI, haploid cells carrying different gene modifications were arrested in M phase by culture in 2i medium containing 0.05 μg/mL demecolcine for 12 h. Arrested haploid cells were trypsinized and suspended in HCZB medium for ICAHCI. MII oocytes were collected from the oviducts of superovulated B6D2F1 females (8 weeks old), Trim37ko1 heterozygotes (6−10 weeks old), or Trim28flox homozygotes (6−10 weeks old). A single haploid cell was then injected into the cytoplasm of an MII oocyte using a Piezo-drill micromanipulator. The reconstructed zygotes were cultured in CZB medium for 30 min and then activated for 5−6 h in Ca2+-free CZB with SrCl2. After activation, the reconstructed embryos were cultured in AA-KSOM medium (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After 18−24 h, two-cell embryos were transferred into the oviducts of 0.5 days post coitum (dpc) pseudopregnant ICR females (18−23 embryos per oviduct). Embryos were dissected from recipient mice at specific stages after transplantation, and gene-modified pups were obtained from recipient mice at E19.5.

Deep sequencing and analysis

To analyze the distribution of sgRNAs in Trim-library-1 and Trim-library-2 haploid cells, genomic DNA was extracted from the cells. sgRNA inserts were amplified from the extracted DNA using Phanta® Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme). Libraries were prepared using adapter-modified PCR and sequenced on a HiSeq X Ten platform (2 × 150 bp PE; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Annoroad Gene Technology Corporation, Beijing, China. The frequencies of different sgRNAs were determined by mapping clean reads to the reference sequence.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from AG-haESCs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Approximately 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a Wiper kit (Vazyme). Real-time PCR (RT-PCR) was performed on a Mastercycler Nexus Gradient GSX1 thermal cycler (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) using Phanta® Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. Approximately 10 PGCs were isolated from E9.5 embryos using a Piezo-drill micromanipulator and transferred into a 200-µL tube containing 3.5 µL of lysis buffer. The lysate was then used for cDNA amplification with a Single Cell Full-Length mRNA Amplification Kit (Cat# N712; Vazyme). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed in triplicate on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using SYBR Green Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The expression level of Trim37 was normalized to that of the internal control gene Gapdh.

Base editing-mediated gene knockout in the embryo

To trace PGC fate after Trim37 knockout, we adopted a base editor previously reported in our recent work33 to efficiently inactivate genes in zygotes. The mRNA of an efficient base editor, hA3A-eBE3-Y130F, and Trim37-sgRNA-stop were transcribed using an mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 ULTRA Transcription Kit (Cat# AM1345; Invitrogen) and a MEGAshortscript T7 Kit (Cat# AM1354; Invitrogen), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. mRNAs and sgRNAs were subsequently purified using a MEGAclear Transcription Clean-Up Kit (Cat# AM1908; Invitrogen), resuspended in hot (95 °C) RNase-free water, and stored at −80 °C. The mixture of hA3A-eBE3-Y130F mRNA (100 ng/μL) and Trim37-sgRNA-stop (100 ng/μL) was diluted in RNase-free water, centrifuged at 13,400 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and injected into the cytoplasm of zygotes (mTmG♀ × Blimp1-T2A-Cre♂ or B6D2F1♀ × Nanog-EGFP♂) using a micromanipulator (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a FemtoJet microinjector (Eppendorf). The resulting embryos were transferred into recipient mice and collected at specific stages for analysis of PGC development.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining

Testes, epididymides, and ovaries were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at room temperature, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%; diluted with distilled water), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Serial 4-μm cross sections were mounted on slides, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol concentrations (100%, 95%, 70%, and 50%; diluted with distilled water), followed by staining with hematoxylin and eosin. Images were captured using a BX51 microscope (Olympus).

Isolation of PGCs by FACS

Dissected gonads (E11.5 and E12.5) and E9.25 embryos were enzymatically dissociated using 100 µL of 0.25% trypsin (Cat# 25200072; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 8 min. Trypsin activity was quenched by adding 200 μL of MEF medium (10% FBS in DMEM), and the cell suspensions were centrifuged at 300× g. Cells from Trim37ko1 gonads were directly resuspended in MEF medium, passed through a 40-µm cell strainer into a 5-mL Falcon Corning FACS tube, and subjected to FACS. Oct4-EGFP- or Nanog-EGFP-positive PGCs were sorted using the FITC channel and used for further analysis. Cells from Trim37C18R and Trim28K774R+K779R gonads were resuspended in FACS buffer (0.1% BSA in PBS), stained with anti-SSEA1 eFluor™ 660 (1:20 dilution; Cat# 50-8813-41; Invitrogen), and then subjected to FACS. SSEA1-positive PGCs were sorted and collected for further analysis.

Whole-mount embryo alkaline phosphatase staining and ISH

For alkaline phosphatase staining, E8.5 embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C and then washed twice with TBST. The embryos were then incubated in 70% ethanol for 8 h at 4 °C, stained in freshly prepared alkaline phosphatase staining solution for 5−7 min at room temperature, and submerged in 80% glycerin until they sank to the bottom.

For ISH, gonads and E8.5 embryos were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, samples were dehydrated through a graded methanol series (25%, 50%, and 75%; diluted with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST)) and then stored in 100% methanol at −20 °C. The dehydrated embryos were rehydrated and subjected to prehybridization in prehybridization solution at 68 °C for 5 h. Embryos were hybridized with a digoxigenin-labeled Stella riboprobe generated by in vitro transcription from a linearized template using a DIG RNA Labelling Kit (Cat# 11175025910; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and hybridization was carried out overnight at 68 °C. Next, embryos were blocked overnight at 4 °C in blocking solution containing an anti-DIG antibody conjugated to alkaline phosphatase (Cat# 11093274910; Roche). Finally, embryos were stained with 20 μL/mL NBT/BCIP (Cat# 11681451001; Roche) at room temperature and submerged in 80% glycerin until they sank to the bottom.

Whole-mount embryo immunostaining

E9.5 embryos (Trim37ko1 and Trim37cko) were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h, and washed twice with PBS. Next, embryos were blocked overnight at 4 °C in blocking buffer (5% donkey serum, 1% BSA, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) before primary antibody incubation. Trim37ko1 and Trim37cko embryos were incubated for 48 h at 4 °C with anti-GFP (1:2000 dilution; Cat# ab13970; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-STELLA (1:200 dilution; Cat# ab19878; Abcam), respectively, diluted in blocking buffer. Samples were then incubated at room temperature for 24 h with goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000 dilution; Cat# ab150169; Abcam), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000 dilution; Cat# A-21206; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and DAPI (1 μg/mL; Cat# D8417; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) diluted in PBST. Subsequently, samples were washed overnight at room temperature with washing buffer (0.2% Triton X-100 and 0.5% 1-thioglycerol in PBS) and then cleared for 36 h at room temperature in clearing medium (40% N-methylacetamide, 1.455 g/mL Histodenz, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.5% 1-thioglycerol in PBS). Samples were immersed in fresh clearing medium and sealed with vacuum grease for imaging.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic extract isolation

Control and Trim373×Flag AG-haESCs were cultured in 10-cm dishes, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, trypsinized, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Cell pellets were gently resuspended in 400 µL of lysis buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 140 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.5% Igepal) containing 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat# 11697498001; Sigma) and incubated on ice for 3 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 3 min at 4 °C to separate the nuclei from the cytoplasmic fraction. To obtain pure nuclear protein, nuclear pellets were washed twice with 350 µL of lysis buffer and once with lysis buffer containing 0.5% deoxycholic acid. To obtain pure cytoplasmic protein, the supernatant fraction was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was carefully collected into a new tube. Finally, the purified nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were boiled in 60 µL of 2× SDS loading buffer (Cat# P0015L; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 15 min at 100 °C. Nuclear and cytoplasmic extracts were analyzed by western blot.

Co-IP

Briefly, HEK293T cells were cultured in 6-cm dishes and transfected with various combinations of expression plasmids using polyethyleneimine (Cat# 23966-1; Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h, cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed in immunoprecipitation buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, and 1% NP-40, pH 7.8) containing 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma) on ice for 1 h. The lysates were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 30 min, and the resulting supernatants were used for immunoprecipitation with Anti-Flag Affinity Gel (Cat# A2220; Sigma) or Anti-HA Affinity Gel (Cat# GNI4510-HA; GNI, Shanghai, China) overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times with low- and high-salt lysis buffers, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 1× SDS loading buffer for 15 min at 100 °C, followed by immunoblotting analysis.

MS analysis of TRIM37 IP

Gonads (30−40 per sample) were collected from wild-type and Trim373×-Flag embryos and lysed in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, and 1% NP-40. The lysates were subjected to IP using Anti-Flag Affinity Gel. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 1× SDS loading buffer for 15 min at 100 °C, followed by electrophoresis. The gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) and divided into three sections based on protein size: band A (≥ 60 kDa), band B (15−35 kDa and 45−65 kDa), and band C (< 15 kDa or 30−50 kDa). MS analysis of the gel sections was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Subsequently, protein identification and quantification were performed using Proteome Discoverer software (v1.4; Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the original spectra. A value of “0” indicates that although the protein was identified, its peak area was too small for quantification and was therefore reported as 0. Candidate proteins were selected based on size and presence in all three Trim373×Flag replicates but were nearly absent from control samples.

E. coli ubiquitination system reconstitution and MS

All components of the ubiquitination system were cloned into the pACYCDuet-1 vector (Novagen, Madison, WI, USA) using standard cloning techniques. HA-tagged ubiquitin (HA-Ub), UBCH5A, and UBA1 coding sequences were inserted into the first multiple cloning site (MCS) under the control of the T7 promoter/lac operator and ribosome-binding sites. These elements were separated by Shine-Dalgarno sequences to ensure proper expression. The mouse TRIM37 coding sequence was inserted into the second MCS, generating the plasmid pACYC-HA-Ub-UBCH5A-TRIM37. pACYC-HA-Ub-UBCH5A-TRIM37 and pET22b-TRIM28 plasmids were co-transformed into E. coli BL21 cells and were selected with ampicillin and chloramphenicol. Protein expression in the BL21 strain was induced with 0.3 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Sigma) at an OD600 of 0.6, followed by incubation for 20 h at 16 °C. Cells were harvested and lysed in lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 300 mM NaCl, 50 mM Na2HPO4, 50 mM Tris-HCl, and 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0. Lysis was performed using an ultrasonic homogenizer (JY92-1IN; Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Ningbo, China). Ubiquitinated proteins were enriched using Ni-NTA agarose beads (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting or MS. TRIM28 protein was purified from the reconstituted E. coli system and dissolved in elution buffer containing 8 M urea and 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0. The dissolved protein was subjected to trypsin digestion, and the resulting peptides were separated using an EASY-nLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MS analysis was performed on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein and ubiquitination data were analyzed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer 2.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with searches conducted against the UniProt Human database (http://www.uniprot.org/).

Ubiquitination assay in cells

HEK293T cells were transfected with the specified vector combinations. After 48 h, cells were harvested and lysed in buffer A (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% SDS, pH 7.6). The lysates were boiled for 10 min at 95 °C, diluted tenfold with buffer B (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.6), and sonicated. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C using an anti-TRIM28 antibody coupled to agarose beads (Cat# 16-266; Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) or Anti-Flag Affinity Gel (Cat# A4596; Sigma). The immunoprecipitates were washed three times with RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, pH 7.6), boiled in 2× SDS-PAGE sample buffer, and analyzed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies. Tissue samples were homogenized in buffer A using a Touch Homogenizer (Precellys 24; Bertin, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France), followed by processing as described above.

In vitro ubiquitination assay

In vitro ubiquitination assays were performed as previously described.68 Briefly, the reaction mixture (final volume: 50 μL) contained 50 ng of His6-Ub, 20 ng of UBA1-His6 (E1), 50 ng of UBCH5A-His6 (E2), 100 ng of E3 (TRIM37 or its mutant)-SUMO-His6, and 100 ng of TRIM28-Flag in ubiquitination buffer (25 mM Tris-Cl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 5 mM MgCl2, pH 7.6) supplemented with freshly added 2 mM ATP. The reaction was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and then denatured by heating for 10 min at 100 °C in 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer. The ubiquitination level of TRIM28 was analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti-Flag antibody.

Protein expression and purification

The codons of TRIM37 and TRIM28 were optimized for expression in E. coli. Full-length mouse TRIM37 (residues 1–961) and its mutants were cloned into a modified pET28a vector featuring a SUMO protein fused at the N-terminus and a 6×His tag at the C-terminus. Full-length mouse TRIM28 (residues 1–1067) and its mutants were cloned into the pET-41a vector, with a GST tag fused at the N-terminus. The optimized gene sequences were synthesized and ligated into the vectors by Tsingke Biological Technology (Beijing, China), where the proteins were also purified. In brief, E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells carrying expression plasmids were induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 18 h at 16 °C, after which the cells were collected by centrifugation. The cell pellets were resuspended in TBS with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and disrupted by sonication on ice. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA resin, washed with PBS, pH 7.4, and sequentially eluted with PBS buffer, pH 7.4, containing 25 mM, 50 mM, 100 mM, and 200 mM imidazole. The collected protein was dialyzed against PBS at a 1:50 ratio overnight, followed by replacement of the dialysis solution and further dialysis for an additional 8 h. Proteins were concentrated using PEG 20,000. Purified proteins were used immediately or stored at −80 °C.

GST pull-down assay

Purified GST-TRIM28 or its mutant (20 μg) was incubated with TRIM37-SUMO-His6 (20 μg) overnight at 4 °C with Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) in 1 mL pull-down buffer (20 mM Tris-Cl, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.5% NP-40, 10 μg/mL BSA, pH 7.5). The beads were pelleted and washed five times with the same pull-down buffer. Bound proteins were denatured by heating the beads in 2× SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 10 min at 100 °C and analyzed by immunoblotting.

Immunoblotting analysis

Cells and tissues were lysed in 1× RIPA buffer (Cat# 9806S; Cell Signaling Technology) containing 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium pyrophosphate, and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate and supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat# P8340; Sigma) on ice for 1 h. Before gel electrophoresis, protein lysates were boiled in 5× loading buffer for 10 min at 100 °C. Protein lysates from different assays were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, blotted onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane, blocked in blocking solution (10% non-fat dry milk in TBST buffer) for several hours, and incubated overnight at 4 °C in TBST buffer with the appropriate primary antibody (anti-Flag, 1:5000 dilution, Cat# M20008M (Abmart, Shanghai, China); anti-HA, 1:1000 dilution, Cat# 3724S (Cell Signaling Technology); anti-TRIM37, 1:1000 dilution, Cat# 96167 (Cell Signaling Technology); anti-KAP1, 1:2000 dilution, Cat# ab10483 (Abcam); anti-Lamin B1, 1:5000 dilution, Cat# ab16048 (Abcam); anti-HRP-conjugated His, 1:20,000 dilution, Cat# HRP-66005 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA); anti-HRP-conjugated GST, 1:20,000 dilution, Cat# HRP-66001 (Proteintech); anti-Ub, 1:5000 dilution, Cat# 10201-2-AP (Proteintech); and anti-GAPDH, 1:5000 dilution, Cat# 60004-1-Ig (Proteintech)). The membranes were washed three times for 10 min each with TBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in TBST with the appropriate secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:5000 dilution, Cat# D110058-0001 (BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China) or goat anti-mouse IgG, 1:10,000 dilution, Cat# SA00001-1 (Proteintech)). Chemiluminescence detection was performed using an ECL reagent solution kit (ShareBio, Shanghai, China).

scRNA-seq and data analysis

E8.5 Nanog-EGFP transgenic embryos and E9.5 Oct4-EGFP transgenic embryos were dissected from recipient mice and digested with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Cat# 25200072; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C. After digestion, embryos were gently pipetted and resuspended in HCZB medium. E8.5 Trim37ko3 embryos were generated by injecting hA3A-eBE3-Y130F mRNA and Trim37-sgRNA-stop into the cytoplasm of zygotes from Nanog-EGFP males mated with BDF1 females. E9.5 Trim37ko1 embryos were obtained by mating Oct4-EGFP/Trim37ko1 heterozygous males with Trim37ko1 heterozygous females. Single PGCs from wild-type and Trim37 knockout embryos were isolated using a Piezo-drill micromanipulator and transferred into a 200-µL-tube containing 3.5 µL of lysis buffer. The lysate was used for cDNA amplification with a Single Cell Full-Length mRNA Amplification Kit (Cat# N712; Vazyme). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using a TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 (Cat# TD503; Vazyme) for Illumina. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads by Genergy Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality control of the raw FASTQ data was conducted using Trim Galore (v0.6.4), followed by alignment to the mm10 genome using STAR (v2.7.3a) with default settings. Only uniquely mapped reads were retained and filtered using Sambamba (v1.0.0). Mapping quality was assessed using RSeQC (v3.0.0). Raw counts were obtained using featureCounts (v2.0.6), whereas TPM and FPKM values were calculated using StringTie (v2.0). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.42.0), and GO analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0).

Processed RNA-seq expression matrices and cell-type annotations were obtained from a published dataset. To reduce computational burden, up to 500 cells were randomly sampled from each annotated cell type, whereas all PGCs were retained. The published sci-RNA-seq dataset and Smart-seq2 data generated in this study were analyzed using Seurat (v4.3.0).69 Datasets from different platforms were integrated using an anchor-based strategy with reciprocal PCA (RPCA) following independent normalization and identification of highly variable genes. The integrated batch-corrected expression matrix was used for dimensionality reduction by PCA, followed by UMAP visualization based on the first 30 principal components. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle3 (v1.0).70 Germ cell populations were extracted from the integrated dataset, and a Monocle3 object was constructed using the raw count matrix and corresponding metadata. Trajectory inference was performed following the standard Monocle3 workflow, with E8.5 wild-type PGCs specified as the root population representing the earliest developmental stage.

Bulk RNA-seq and data analysis

E9.25 control and Trim37ko3 embryos carrying the Nanog-EGFP transgene were dissected from recipient mice and digested with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Cat# 25200072; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C. Following digestion, Nanog-EGFP-positive PGCs were isolated from the cell suspension by FACS. Control embryos were produced by injecting hA3A-eBE3-Y130F mRNA into the cytoplasm of zygotes from Nanog-EGFP males mated with BDF1 females. Trim37ko3 embryos were generated by co-injecting hA3A-eBE3-Y130F mRNA and Trim37-sgRNA-stop. Approximately 80 PGCs per sample were transferred into a 200-µL tube containing 3.5 µL of lysis buffer. The lysate was subjected to cDNA amplification using a Single Cell Full Length mRNA Amplification Kit (Cat# N712; Vazyme). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 (Cat# TD503; Vazyme) for Illumina sequencing. Sequencing was conducted on a NovaSeq 6000 at Genergy Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The raw RNA-seq data were first trimmed using TrimGalore (v0.6.10) to remove low-quality sequences and adapter contamination. Clean reads were then aligned to the mm10 reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene-level counts were obtained using featureCounts (v2.0.1) based on UCSC GTF annotation, and FPKM values were calculated using Cufflinks (v2.2.1). Differential expression analysis was conducted in R using DESeq2 (v1.40.0), with genes considered differentially expressed if they had an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and a fold change > 2.

ATAC-seq

Approximately 1500 PGCs per sample, isolated from E9.25 embryos following the aforementioned procedure, were used for miniATAC-seq as previously described.71 Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform at Genergy Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China).

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag was performed to detect H3K27ac (anti-H3K27ac antibody, Cat# ab4729; Abcam) and H3K9me3 (anti-H3K9me3 antibody, Cat# 39062; Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA, USA) modifications in the genomes of E9.25 control and Trim37ko3 PGCs. Approximately 1000 PGCs per sample were transferred into a 1.5-mL tube containing 500 µL of wash buffer. Samples were then processed, and libraries were constructed using a Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Cat# TD904; Vazyme). Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 system at Genergy Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

CUT&RUN

CUT&RUN was performed to detect TRIM28 or AP2γ binding in the genomes of E9.25 control and Trim37ko3 PGCs using anti-TRIM28 antibody (Cat# ab10483; Abcam) or an anti-AP2γ antibody (Cat# A7739; Abclonal, Wuhan, China), respectively. Approximately 3000 PGCs per sample were transferred into 1.5-mL tubes and resuspended in 60 µL of wash buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, and protease inhibitors). Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads (10 µL; Cat# 86057; Polysciences) were washed twice with binding buffer, resuspended, and added to the samples for 10 min at 23 °C with shaking. After placement on a magnetic stand to remove the buffer, samples were resuspended in antibody buffer (wash buffer with 0.02% digitonin and 2 mM EDTA) and incubated with the TRIM28 or AP2γ antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, samples were washed in dig wash buffer (wash buffer with 0.02% digitonin) and incubated in dig wash buffer containing pA-MNase (1:1000 dilution) for 1.5 h at 4 °C. After two washes, samples were resuspended in dig wash buffer, and 2 µL of 100 mM CaCl2 was added for digestion on ice for 25 min. To stop the reaction, stop buffer (340 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 4 mM EGTA, 50 μg/mL RNase A, 100 μg/mL glycogen, and 3 pg/mL Drosophila DNA) was added, followed by incubation for 3 min at 4 °C and for 39 min at 37 °C.

Following digestion, samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred into new tubes and treated with 2 μL of 10% SDS and proteinase K (20 mg/mL). Samples were vortexed, incubated for 10 min at 70 °C, and purified by phenol-chloroform extraction. DNA was precipitated with ethanol, washed, dried, and dissolved in 25 µL of water. For library construction, 3.5 µL of End Prep buffer, 1.5 µL of enzyme mix, and diluted adaptors were added, followed by ligation using reagents from the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit. After ligation and USER enzyme treatment, samples were purified using AMPure beads, PCR amplified, and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina).

Data analysis of ATAC-seq, CUT&Tag, and CUT&RUN

Raw sequencing reads were trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.10) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the mm10 reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.5.1) with the following parameters: -N 1 -X 1000 --no-mixed --no-discordant. Unmapped reads, multiply mapped reads, and PCR duplicates were subsequently filtered out using Sambamba (v0.8.2). Peaks were called using MACS2 (v2.2.6) with the parameters -q 0.05 --nomodel --nolambda. To normalize read counts, RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) values were calculated in 100-bp bins using bamCoverage from deepTools (v3.5.1). Differential binding peaks between control and Trim37ko3 samples were identified by pooling peaks across both groups and calculating the average Z-score-normalized RPKM values, with peaks showing a fold change ≥2 considered differentially bound. Finally, peaks were annotated to the nearest genes within 50 kb using the R package ChIPseeker.
