The youngest reported oropharyngeal leech infestation: severe anemia without airway obstruction in a 6-month-old Ethiopian infant: a case report
Authors: Amanuel Mesfin Oljira, Samuel Kefiyalew Kelbessa, Boka Imiru Shorro, Bekele Abera Mekonen, Dereje Gurmessa Geleta, Biniyam Nigussie Burussa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfestation of the oropharynx by a leech is uncommon, especially in young children, and can cause major bleeding because leech saliva contains the anticoagulant hirudin. This case, which presents oropharyngeal leech infestation causing severe anemia without obstruction of the airway in a 6-month-old infant, is the youngest ever documented.Case presentationParents from rural Ambo brought in an Oromo male infant of 6 months of age, complaining of oral bleeding, decreased oral intake, hematemesis, dark stool, and agitation that had occurred throughout the previous two days. Although there was no respiratory distress, a physical examination revealed blood-stained oral mucosa and a dark brown substance in the oropharynx; a complete blood test showed severe anemia (Hb 7.3 g/dL). The bleeding immediately stopped after removal of a 2.5 cm long leech by Magill forceps from the oropharyngeal wall under direct visualization with a rigid laryngoscope. Blood transfusions, antibiotics, and an iron suspension were given, and the patient's complete recovery was confirmed by an outpatient follow-up after discharge.ConclusionIn environments with limited resources, this case highlights the importance of preventing such rare but dangerous infestations by improving water sanitation, adhering to safe feeding practices, and quickly suspecting parasitic causes in infants exhibiting unexplained bleeding.Keywords: Infant, Leech infestation, Anemia, Hematemesis, Case report

Introduction

Leeches can adhere to the upper aerodigestive tract when ingesting contaminated water, making leech infestations of the oropharynx harmful even though they are rare, particularly in places with unclean water [1, 2], and particularly in infants given their little blood volume. The anticoagulant hirudin, which is present in leech saliva, can cause severe anemia and ongoing bleeding [3, 4]. This case-the youngest reported oropharyngeal leech infestation in a 6-month-old infant, is unique because of the patient's early exposure to contaminated water through premature complementary feeding, severe anemia without airway obstruction, and efficient management in a resource-constrained environment. It adds to the body of literature by highlighting the importance of early diagnosis, the vulnerability of young infants, the need for improved water sanitation, and adherence to WHO feeding guidelines [5].

Case presentation

A 6-month-old Oromo boy from a rural part of Ambo, Ethiopia, came to our emergency room with a 2-day history of bleeding from the mouth, vomiting of ingested matter mixed with blood, tarry stool, agitation, and decreased oral intake. In violation of WHO guidelines for exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months, the infant had been given contaminated water for supplemental feeding since 3 months of age, and the household used untreated spring water [5].There was no reported family history of trauma or bleeding disorders. The infant was born at term, partially vaccinated, and developing normally (babbling, crawling, and sitting unsupported). The mother also attended four prenatal care visits. On examination, he weighed 6.2 kg, was irritable, with a temperature of 36.9 °C, a respiratory rate of 32 breaths per minute, a pulse rate of 148 beats per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Physical findings included pale conjunctiva, moist mucosa, a blood-stained oral cavity, and a dark brown structure in the posterior oropharynx visualized with difficulty. No stridor or respiratory distress was noted, despite the leech’s location and the infant’s small airway. The capillary refill was normal, and heart sounds were normal; the breath sounds were vesicular, and there was no hepatosplenomegaly. The timeline of care

Two days prior to the presentation: the onset of the patients’ symptoms.

On day 0 (presentation): an intravenous line was secured, and a whole blood transfusion was administered. Rigid laryngoscopy under ketamine sedation confirmed a 2.5 cm × 0.5 cm leech attached to the posterior oropharynx (Fig. 1), which was removed using Magill forceps.

The extracted oropharyngeal leech measuring approximately 2.5 × 0.5 cm in length

Day 0–2 post-procedure: in-hospital monitoring and initiation of breastfeeding and second transfusion.

Day 2 post-procedure: he was discharged with amoxicillin, iron syrup, and dietary and water sanitation education.

At one, three, and six months post-discharge, the patient was monitored at the outpatient department by physical examinations and laboratory tests.

Management

After the intravenous line was put up, 10 mL/kg of whole blood was given, followed by a rigid laryngoscopy under ketamine sedation, which showed a leech attached to the posterior oropharynx. We used Magill forceps to extract the leech, which measured 2.5 cm by 0.5 cm (Fig. 1), and the bleeding from the attachment site stopped immediately with negligible intra-procedural loss (< 5 ml). Following the surgery, a second transfusion (10 ml/kg) was given. Until the patient began oral intake, calculated maintenance fluid was administered. After the patient was transferred to the surgical ward, he was monitored for bleeding incidents and was started on feeding gradually. Following treatment, oral intake was gradually introduced. The infant was discharged on day two with parent education on water purification, proper child nutrition, and seeking medical help if symptoms resurfaced. He was also given iron syrup (3 mg/kg per day) and amoxicillin syrup 50 mg/kg in two separate doses for seven days. Follow-up visits at 1, 3, and 6 months showed normal growth and correction of anemia (hemoglobin readings of 10.8 g/dL, 12.4 g/dL, and 13.1 g/dL, respectively). Parental reports attested to compliance with medication and hygiene guidelines without experiencing any negative consequences.

Discussion

Relevance and originality

This case represents the earliest documented instance of oropharyngeal leech infestation, emphasizing its rarity and clinical significance in infants, who are especially at risk because of their low blood volume [3, 6]. This infant manifested severe anemia (Hb 7.3 g/dL) without respiratory distress, in contrast to usual presentations featuring airway obstruction or hemoptysis [7, 8], despite the leech’s attachment at the posterior oropharynx. The lack of airway compromise, despite the infant’s narrow airway and the leech’s dimensions (2.5 cm × 0.5 cm), is unusual and probably results from the leech’s specific point of attachment, which did not block the laryngeal inlet [9]. This case adds to the current literature by pinpointing the youngest age for this specific presentation and providing an instance of effective management with few resources, supported by thorough follow-up showing anemia resolution (13.1 g/dL at 6 months).

Dietary habits and the use of contaminated water

In violation of WHO recommendations [5], the infant’s early supplementary feeding at 3 months, which exposed him to contaminated water, most likely contributed to the leech infestation. In Ethiopia, 65% of rural families do not have access to clean water [10], and these practices heighten the threat of parasitic infections. This situation highlights the importance of exclusive breastfeeding for up to 6 months and having access to safe water to avoid such occurrences.

Mechanism of anemia and the absence of airway blockage

Significant blood loss results from the inhibition of thrombin by hirudin, which is found in leech saliva [3, 4]. In this instance, the leech’s adherence to the back of the oropharynx led to hematemesis and melena, causing significant anemia. The absence of airway blockage may be ascribed to the leech’s size and location, which did not invade the larynx or trachea, in contrast to laryngeal infestations linked with stridor [7, 8]. This was corroborated by the baby’s lack of respiratory distress.

Common vs. unusual presentation

Common oropharyngeal leech infestations typically manifest as hematemesis, hemoptysis, or airway blockage, frequently seen in older children or adults [1, 6, 8]. Nasal infestations result in epistaxis [11], while laryngeal instances can cause stridor or dyspnea [7]. Since the patient was 6 months old, had severe anemia without airway blockage, and began supplemental nourishment (including dirty water) too early at 3 months of age, our case differed from reports of older individuals or various anatomical attachment sites [1, 6, 12].

Diagnostic approaches

Similar to other studies, flexible endoscopy was not accessible in our resource-limited setup, so rigid laryngoscopy was used [3, 13]. Because of the infant's age, ketamine sedation-which is safe and effective for brief procedures in children-was necessary [14]. Although flexible endoscopy is ideal for detailed examination, our approach is validated by the fact that it is commonly unavailable in remote facilities. Since there was no family history of bleeding disorders, no mass lesions, and normal coagulation parameters (PT, APTT, INR), other conditions (hemophilia, infections, and tumors) were ruled out [3, 13].

Treatment justification

Leech extraction using Magill forceps and direct viewing with a rigid laryngoscope is a popular and effective method in areas with limited resources [1, 7, 8]. Ketamine was used for sedation to reduce the risk of mucosal damage, ensuring safety. Alternative techniques, such as immobilizing the leech with lidocaine or hypertonic saline, were not used due to the effectiveness of forceps extraction and restricted accessibility of the patient's upper aerodigestive tract [9, 15]. As delayed action may result in leech migration or increased bleeding, prompt removal immediately stopped the bleeding, preventing further blood loss or potential airway complications [3, 9].

Intervention and rehabilitation

The prompt halting of bleeding after removal, along with transfusions and iron supplementation, significantly aided the infant’s recovery, demonstrated by hemoglobin levels returning to normal (13.1 g/dL at 6 months). Rapid management minimized problems such as airway blockage and hemorrhagic shock, which can be deadly if left untreated [3, 6]. Follow-up verified ongoing improvement, highlighting the importance of consistent monitoring.

Public health implications

This case highlights the need for public health measures to avoid leech infestations, including educating communities on adequate water cleaning methods and following dietary guidelines [10]. Particularly in rural Ethiopia, clean water availability should be prioritized.

Constraints and broader applicability

The extrapolation of the results of our report is limited, as it is a single case report. It is hypothesized that early feeding and infestation are related, and relying on parental explanations for compliance could potentially introduce bias. The lack of flexible endoscopy serves as an example of resource constraints that would not exist in settings with advanced equipment. This case specifically offers important insights on leech infestations in infants and the application of rigid laryngoscopy and forceps, which can be adapted in environments with limited resources [3, 6].

Conclusion

This case, which is the earliest oropharyngeal leech infestation ever recorded, serves as a reminder of the risks associated with contaminated water and early initiation of supplemental feeding in resource-constrained environments. Prompt diagnosis through rigid laryngoscopy and leech extraction with Magill forceps, aided by transfusions and monitoring, guaranteed complete recovery. Public health initiatives should focus on water sanitation and exclusive breastfeeding to avert these uncommon yet life-threatening infections.
