Discovery of potential AXL inhibitors using virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics, molecular mechanics, and in vitro validation
Authors: Nebahat Sahin, Alper Onder, Nermin Kahraman, Sayra Dilmac, Bulent Ozpolat, Ferah Comert Onder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAXL is overexpressed in many cancers, including breast, lung, colon, osteosarcoma, kidney, stomach, and gliomas, and associated with poor patient survival and plays an important role in cancer cell proliferation, survival, tumor growth, progression and metastasis. In vivo studies involving genetic targeting of AXL validated it as a promising therapeutic target. In this study, we performed integrated in silico analyses, including virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and MM/GBSA calculations to search the compound libraries from various small molecule databases to identify potential inhibitors targeting AXL. We identified key amino acid residues such as Met623, Pro621, Asp627, and Asp690 located in the binding pocket of AXL interacting with the potential inhibitors and positive control compounds through with hydrogen and pi-pi bonds. In vitro studies with the three lead compounds (STOCK1N-80636, STOCK1N-66436 and Nebivolol) demonstrated significant antiproliferative effects in four different breast cancer cell lines STOCK1N-80636 was being the most potent. Introduction

Breast cancer is a significant global health problem, being the most commonly diagnosed type of cancer in women and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths1. Approximately 1 in 8 women is diagnosed with breast cancer. In 2026, it is estimated that approximately a quarter million new breast cancer cases will be diagnosed in the United States2. Breast cancer has three main pathological subtypes: estrogen receptor-positive (ER +), HER receptor-positive (HER2 +), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a highly aggressive and metastatic subtype of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancers. While ER + and HER + BC are treated with anti-estrogens (e.g., tamoxifen) and anti-HER2 antibodies, respectively. Because TNBC tumors do not express ER + , PR, and HER + receptors and targetable receptors there is no effective or curative targeted therapy, leading to the lowest survival rates among all BC subtypes due to the lack of effective targeted therapies. Therefore, current treatment includes surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. The mean survival time for TNBC patients with metastatic disease is only ~ 12 months, and the 5-year survival rate is 50%. Therefore, there is a critical need for new molecularly targeted treatment strategies to improve patient survival3,4.

The tyrosine kinase protein family consists of two groups, including non-receptor tyrosine kinases (NRTKs) and receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). RTKs play a crucial role in carcinogenesis and serve as actionable targets for cancer therapy for cancer patients5. Tyrosine kinases (TKs), which constitute a significant portion of all oncoproteins, are fundamental mediators of the signal transduction process and play a critical role in cell differentiation, migration, proliferation, apoptosis and metabolism6–8. Currently, there are approximately 94 FDA-approved small-molecule protein kinase inhibitors, 80 of which are used in cancer treatment. AXL, derived from the Greek word ‘anexelekto’ meaning ‘uncontrolled’, is a receptor tyrosine kinase belonging to the Tyro3-AXL-Mer (TAM) family9. Growth arrest-specific gene 6 (GAS6), a natural ligand, binds to the AXL receptor and activates it, driving cell signalling pathways that regulate cell proliferation, tumor growth and metastasis10–12. AXL signaling is mediated by several downstream pathways, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK, SRC/FAK and PKC13–15. AXL overactivation is associated with drug resistance, immune suppression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis, resistance to apoptosis, and cancer stem cell phenotype15–17. AXL is typically overexpressed through gene amplification or stimulation by the factors in the tumor microenvironment.9 AXL is overexpressed in various human tumors18 such as prostate19, TNBC20, lung21, pancreas22, colorectal23,24, esophagus25, ovarian26,27, stomach28, renal cell carcinoma29, myeloid leukemia14, hepatocellular carcinoma30, and thyroid cancer31, and this overexpression has been found to be associated with cancer recurrence and poor prognosis4.

Although, recently, small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors targeting AXL have been identified, only BGB324 reached to human clinical trials32. Other selective AXL inhibitors including, foretinib33, gilteritinib34, BMS-77760735, cabozantinib36, crizotinib, and dubermatinib37, are multitarget inhibitors and all have been found to be nonselective; highlighting the need for compounds with high selectivity (Fig. 1)7. Rational design and development of novel effective and safer drug candidates often requires in silico studies38. In silico approaches are generally cheaper and faster than experimental drug discovery methods. These computational strategies include high-throughput and structure-based virtual screening, molecular docking, MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations, and molecular dynamics simulations. In modern anticancer drug discovery, structure-based virtual screening and molecular docking enable the rapid evaluation of large compound libraries against selected molecular targets, thereby facilitating the prioritization of promising molecules for further investigation. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations provide critical insights at molecular level into the time-dependent stability of protein–ligand complexes and conformational changes in proteins by mimicking molecular interactions under physiological conditions. MM/GBSA calculations, on the other hand, combine molecular mechanics-based approaches with solvation free energy estimation, thereby providing a more refined estimation of the binding affinities of compounds toward target proteins and supporting the selection of the most promising hit compounds39–42.

Structures of some known AXL inhibitors and candidates selected for in vitro analyses in this study.

In this study, we utilized in silico studies to screen various compound libraries to identify novel potential AXL inhibitors and evaluated their effects using in vitro breast cancer cell lines. This study has enabled the identification of new chromen ring containing compounds like reference inhibitor Y041-5921, and the results are promising for future research.

Result and discussion

In silico screening, molecular docking and selection of candidate compounds

First, we screened the compound library from various small molecule databases targeting AXL. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and MM/GBSA calculations were employed to select potential compounds based on three criteria: 1) high binding affinity with AXL; 2) relative binding potential compared to the reference compounds listed in Table 1; 3) comparative analysis with similar chemical structures including the reference molecule YO41-5921 (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 1, ten molecules with the best binding affinity were identified, including STOCK1N-80636, STOCK1N-66436, STOCK1S-21850, STOCK2S-18782, STOCK5S-30052, STOCK5S-46859, and approved drugs. The following reference molecules were used to investigate the detailed binding activity of 7 identified compounds targeting AXL: 7YS, a co-crystallized ligand showing good binding to AXL; crizotinib, a small molecule RTK inhibitor that also inhibits AXL; and Y041-5921 a reference compound reported to be effective against AXL in TNBC cell lines (Table 1). The compounds interacted with the Pro621, Phe622, and Met623 residues, which have been reported to mediate the interaction with AXL and provide the crucial specific interactions with the target AXL binding pocket42.

Regarding the AXL-STOCK1N-66436 complex, hydrogen bond interactions were observed between the ligand and residues of the protein target, including, Met623, Asp690, Lys567, Arg676, and Asn677. This complex also exhibited alkyl interactions with Ala565 and Leu542, C-H bond interactions with Glu546 and Phe622, and pi-pi interactions with Phe622 (Fig. 2). Molecular docking analysis revealed that Nebivolol is stably bound to the active site of AXL kinase via multiple noncovalent interactions. While conventional hydrogen bonds formed with Asp690, Arg676, and Asn677, a C-H bond interaction was observed with Phe622. In addition, various hydrophobic interactions, including alkyl interactions with Val550 and Leu542, and π-alkyl interactions with Ala565, Met679, Leu620, and Met623 residues, contributed to the complex stabilization (Fig. 2). Due to the structural similarity of the chromen ring of STOCK1N-80636, STOCK1N-66436, and Nebivolol to the reference Y041-5921, these ligands were selected for use in in vitro analysis (Fig. 1).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for 300 ns were performed to evaluate the stability of protein–ligand complexes and to elucidate the behavior of ligands at the active site of the AXL protein target. Protein–ligand interactions and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) results are presented for the highest ranking ligands, while conformational changes in both protein and ligand during the simulation were quantified by root mean square deviation (RMSD). An RMSF analysis was performed to characterize the effect of Nebivolol on the mobility of individual amino acid residues along the protein backbone; the resulting peaks highlight the most mobile regions of the protein chain.

The RMSD profiles for the AXL-ligand complexes are presented in Fig. 3A, where the average RMSD values of the proteins Cα was observed to remain in the range of 2.13 ± 0.27 Å and 2.95 ± 0.58 Å for 7YS and STOCK1N-80636, respectively. The average RMSD value of the protein Cα for the AXL-STOCK1N-66436 complex was found to be 2.62 ± 0.28 Å. The mean RMSD values for Nebivolol (2.54 ± 0.34 Å) and Y041-5921 (2.53 ± 0.33 Å) were found to be very close to each other (Fig. 3A). A comparative evaluation of the trajectories reveals that the binding of the Y041-5921 demonstrates a notable rigidification effect on several dynamic regions of the receptor. Specifically, around the prominent fluctuation peaks located near residue regions 45–60, 95–105, and 125–135, the Y041-5921 complex successfully dampens the residual fluctuations compared to more flexible complexes like 7YS and STOCK1N-80636. This stabilization highlights that Y041-5921 establishes a well-optimized binding posture, locking critical binding pocket residues and contiguous loop segments into a restricted, stable conformation. In contrast, STOCK1N-80636 consistently promotes the highest residual fluctuations, particularly dominating the major peaks at residue indices 125–135 and 190–205, where its RMSF values approach or exceed 5.5 Å. Conversely, the drug Nebivolol and STOCK1N-66436 display highly coordinated rigidification profiles, mimicking the rigid core behavior of the apo protein baseline. Taken together, the RMSF analysis underscores that Y041-5921 effectively constrains the dynamic domains of the target protein, providing a strong structural rationale for its robust thermodynamic stability and favorable binding affinity (Fig. 3B). The strength of the interaction between STOCK1N-80636 and AXL was monitored throughout the 300 ns MD simulation. Notably, Met623 and Pro621 consistently exhibited strong binding to the ligand. Furthermore, the ligand formed significant hydrogen bonds with a crucial residue Met623. The stability of the complex was further enhanced by water bridges involving Arg676, Lys567 as well as hydrophobic interactions with the residues such as Leu542, Phe622, and Met679 (Figure S1). Interactions for STOCK1N-80636 in the binding pocket of AXL revealed that the strong hydrogen bonds with the amino acid residues Met623 and Asp672. Additionally, the protein–ligand stability is supported by water bridges involving Glu546 and Asp690, and hydrophobic interactions with Phe622, Met679, and Leu542 (Figure S2). Protein–ligand interactions within the binding pocket were shown in Figure S3, and it has been revealed that Nebivolol formed hydrogen bonds and pi-cation. Specifically, the ligand establishes hydrogen bonds with Asp690, Asn677, and Arg676 (Figure S3).

To evaluate the structural stability, compactness, and tertiary fold integrity of the protein–ligand complexes, the Radius of Gyration (rGyr) and Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) trajectories were analyzed throughout the MD simulations. The apo protein served as a reference baseline, exhibiting the lowest values across both structural metrics with an average rGyr of 20.09 ± 0.15 Å2 and a SASA of 15126 ± 313 Å2. These minimal values, coupled with remarkably low standard deviations (SD), signify that the standalone receptor maintains a highly consolidated, tightly packed, and rigid native conformation throughout the simulation timeline. Upon binding of the prioritized candidate Y041-5921, the protein complex experienced a minor structural expansion, as evidenced by a slight elevation in the rGyr value (20.57 ± 0.16 Å2) relative to the apo protein state. The corresponding SASA value for the Y041-5921 complex was recorded at 15436 ± 237 Å2. Although this represents a slight increase in solvent exposure suggesting a minor conformational reorientation or a localized opening of surface loops that renders certain pockets marginally more accessible to the aqueous environment the exceptionally low SD underscores that the complex reaches a highly stable and rigid state without experiencing continuous structural drift. It displays a structural trajectory nearly identical to STOCK1N-66436 (20.57 ± 0.15 Å2 and 15386 ± 246 Å2), which, when coupled with their comparable and highly favorable binding affinities (MM/GBSA values of -60.31 ± 12.55 and -62.03 ± 8.78 kcal/mol, respectively), implies a similar geometric influences in the binding pocket (Table 3). In contrast, Nebivolol preserves the native structure, yielding values closest to the apo protein state (20.36 ± 0.17 Å2 and 15318 ± 298 Å2), For 7YS, the values found were for rGyr 20.58 ± 0.17 Å2 and for SASA 15224 ± 253 Å2. Conversely, STOCK1N-80636 demonstrated the highest overall structural expansion and solvent exposure (20.78 ± 0.24 Å2 and 15451 ± 247 Å2). The elevated fluctuations of STOCK1N-80636 indicate a failure to establish tight complementary interactions, which directly correlates with its weak binding free energy (-43.32 ± 8.57 kcal/mol) (Fig. 3C,D). Moreover, the average numbers of hydrogen bonds for the selected ligands and references were calculated in this study and is shown in Fig. 4 and Table 3. The average number of hydrogen bonds were determined to be higher and lower for Nebivolol (3.49) and Y041-5921 (0.76), respectively (Fig. 4).

Molecular mechanics/generalized born surface area (MM/GBSA) calculations

The MM/GBSA (Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area) method was employed to evaluate the binding affinities of STOCK1N-66436, STOCK1N-80636, Nebivolol, Y041-5921 and 7YS to the AXL protein. As shown in Fig. 5, when compared with the reference inhibitor Y041-5921 and the protein’s native inhibitor 7YS, the binding free energies (ΔGbind) of STOCK1N-66436 and Nebivolol were found to be close each other, except for STOCK1N-80636. As shown in Fig. 6, these snapshots confirm the structural stability of the protein–ligand complexes, with each ligand remaining securely bound to the protein’s active site along its trajectories. The binding free energies for the complexes STOCK1N-66436, STOCK1N-80636, Nebivolol and reference inhibitor Y041-5921 and the protein’s native inhibitor 7YS were calculated as -62.03 ± 8.78 kcal/mol, −43.32 ± 8.57 kcal/mol, −62.64 ± 6.46 kcal/mol, −60.31 ± 12.55, and −64.92 ± 5.68, respectively (Table 3). Consequently, STOCK1N-66436 exhibited the highest binding affinity, while STOCK1N-80636 showed the lowest relative energy. These studies used Nebivolol, an FDA-approved beta-blocker traditionally used to treat hypertension and heart failure, as well as the newly identified precursor compounds STOCK1N-80636 and STOCK1N-66436.

In vitro results

Colony formation, cell proliferation in breast cancer cells

To evaluate the in vitro cytotoxic effects of the compounds STOCK1N-80636 and Nebivolol on the cell proliferation and colony formation in TNBC (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, BT-549, BT-20) and ER + MCF-7 cell lines (Fig. 7A–D), treatment with STOCK1N-80636 significantly reduced cell proliferation and colony formation in breast cancer lines in dose- and time-dependent manner studies using chlonogenic analysis and MTT assay (Fig. 7A,B). Figure 7A shows that STOCK1N-80636 significantly suppresses colonies in MDA-MB-231, BT-20, and MCF-7 cell lines at concentrations of 20, 25, and 10 μM, respectively. Cell viability was assessed as a time- and dose-dependent studies using the MTT assay, and STOCK1N-80636 exhibited an IC50 value that reduced cell viability in MCF-7 cells at 0.64 ± 0.17 µM for 48 h. Moreover, STOCK1N-80636 showed cytotoxic effects in MDA-MB-231 cells at a concentration of 23.72 ± 0.92 μM after 72 h, while a less pronounced effect was observed in BT-549 cells at 44.75 ± 2.83 μM after 48 h and in BT-20 cells at 45.13 ± 1.43 μM after 72 h (Fig. 7B). Comparative in vitro analyses were performed using Nebivolol, the drug determined by in silico studies. The MTT assay revealed that Nebivolol inhibited BT-549 cells at 31.15 ± 1.84 µM for 72 h. Nebivolol exhibited significant inhibitory effects in MDA-MB-231 and BT-20 cells at 71.19 ± 4.04 μM and 74.99 ± 1.32 μM after 72 h and in MCF-7 cells at 53.00 ± 1.97 μM (Table 4, Fig. 7C). Nebivolol inhibited colony formation in MDA-MB-231 cells at concentrations of approximetly 30 μM, in BT-20 cells below 50 μM, in BT-549 cells at 10 μM, and in ER + MCF-7 cells at 50 μM. Specifically, it completely suppressed colony formation in BT-549 and BT-20 cells at at a concentration of 20 and 50 μM, respectively (Fig. 7D). To facilitate comparison of compound activities across different cell lines, the IC50 summary table is provided in Table 4.

Western blot analysis

Following in vitro cytotoxicity screening, STOCK1N-66436 and Nebivolol, which had the highest binding free energy values, showed cytotoxic activity in breast cancer cells at higher doses; therefore, STOCK1N-80636, which showed a lower RMSD profile and binding free energy value, was tested using Western blot analysis. To evaluate the effect of STOCK1N-80636 on total AXL protein expression was investigated in MDA-MB-231 cells via Western blot analysis after a 24 h treatment period (Fig. 7E). STOCK1N-80636 induced a clear, dose-dependent downregulation of total AXL protein levels at concentrations of 1, 2.5, and 5 µM compared to the non-treated (NT) control cells. Notably, a visible reduction in AXL expression was initiated at a low concentration of 1 µM, with the most pronounced suppression observed at 5 µM. GAPDH remained stable across all groups, confirming equal protein loading and verifying. Quantification of protein expression levels is presented in the Fig. 7E after normalization to GAPDH. These findings demonstrate that STOCK1N-80636 effectively modulates total AXL protein levels and are consistent with previously established molecular docking data for this compound.

Combinatorial study

While STOCK1N-66436 alone exhibited inhibitory effect at 100 µM in the MDA-MB-231 cell line, its efficacy was significantly increased when administered in combination with fixed doses of Doxorubicin (Dox) and Quercetin. Specifically, STOCK1N-66436 was tested in MDA-MB-231 cells at increasing concentrations in combination with a fixed dose of Dox (in a 1:1 ratio) at 0.1 µM and a dose of Quercetin (in a 1:2 ratio) at 10 µM. The combination of Dox and STOCK1N-66436 suppressed the colonies at a concentration of 10 µM. The combination of STOCK1N-66436 and Quercetin initiated colony inhibition at 10 µM, and the inhibitory effects progressively increased at higher concentration at 20 µM. In contrast, in MCF-7 cells, combination of 66436 with Dox resulted in a decrease in the number of colonies at 5 µM and 10 µM. Similarly, MTT graphs have shown that STOCK1N-66436, in combination with Dox and Quercetin, reduces cell viability for 48 h treatments. Compared to its combination with Quercetin, its combination with Dox showed an effect at almost 10 µM, supporting the clonogenic analysis result. In MTT tests conducted on the MCF-7 cell line, the combination of STOCK1N-66436 with Dox was observed to be more effective than its combination with Quercetin. Although the combination of Abacavir with Dox inhibited colony formation in MCF-7 cells at 25 µM, the same effect was not observed with the combination of Quercetin (Fig. 8). Using the MTT analysis, we reported an IC50 value of approximately 100 nM for Dox in MDA-MB-231 cells43. These findings highlight the potential of STOCK1N-66436 for use in combination therapy, demonstrating its value as a promising candidate for further investigation in cancer treatment.

Overall, in this study, potential compounds were selected using a structure-based virtual screening method. MD simulation was used to analyze the interactions of the identified compounds with AXL. The virtual screening also identified the known AXL inhibitor Crizotinib, which served as a reference point in the identification of Nebivolol. In addition to being reported as an AXL inhibitor, crizotinib was also identified in silico as an EGFR inhibitor in another study44. Consequently, in vitro anticancer activity studies were conducted to investigate the potential reuse of Nebivolol for cancer treatment. In addition, in vitro analyses were performed for the precursor compounds STOCK1N-80636 and STOCK1N-66436. In the binding pocket, Pro621, Phe622, and Met623 residues mediate critical interactions between the target and ligand. Furthermore, Asp627 acts as an acceptor in hydrogen bond interactions with the hydroxyl group of the ligand and backbone peptide donor, acting on the ligand oxygen42. ATP binding is facilitated by crucial residues such as Pro621, Phe622, Met623, and Lys567, while the activation loop includes the DFG motif formed by Asp690, Phe691, and Gly69245. Specific hydrogen bond interactions between STOCK1N-80636 and AXL have been identified at residues Arg676, Met623, Lys567, and Pro621. In vitro experiments have shown that STOCK1N-80636 inhibites colony formation at concentrations of 20 μM in the MDA-MB-231 cell line, 25 μM in the BT-20 cell line, 10 μM in the BT-549 cell line, and 10 μM in the MCF-7 cell line. In the MCF-7 cell line, cytotoxic effects were observed after 48 h of MTT treatment at concentrations of 1 μM and above. The binding affinity of STOCK1N-80636 to AXL was found to be higher than that of the 7YS, with a binding score of -9.042 kcal/mol. These findings suggest that STOCK1N-80636 exhibits strong binding and inhibitory effects against AXL and should be further evaluated as a potential therapeutic agent. Hydrogen bonding interactions were observed between the STOCK1N-66436 and AXL at residues Ala565, Phe622, Asp690, Arg677, Lys567, Phe547, and Asp672, while a π-π interaction was detected with Phe622. The binding score was determined as −8.840 kcal/mol. The binding affinity of STOCK1N-66436 with AXL was found to be higher than that of compound Y041-5921. In studies conducted on the MCF-7 cell line, it was observed that the combination of 0.1 μM Dox and 10 μM Quercetin reduced the doses of the active drug components.

Nebivolol, an FDA-approved β1-selective blocker for the treatment of hypertension, has recently gained attention due to its inhibitory effects on various tumors, including breast, lung, and oral squamous cell carcinomas46. It has been reported to inhibit Complex I and ATP synthase activities in cancer cells, thereby limiting the growth of colon and breast tumors while suppressing angiogenesis47. Furthermore, Nebivolol induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, suppress oral squamous cell carcinoma growth, and exhibits specific anti-tumor activity in both 2D cultures and 3D tumor spheroid models of uveal melanoma (UM). Despite these promising results, further research is required to fully elucidate the effects of β1-selective blockers in oncology46.

Furthermore, another study indicates that Nebivolol did not inhibit the growth of the normal human breast epithelial cell line MCF10A, but significantly reduced the invasion and migration of TNBC cells in a concentration-dependent manner47. Specifically, Nebivolol demonstrated growth inhibition in various breast cancer cell lines with IC50 values of 6.57 µM for MDA-MB-231, 4.60 µM for MDA-MB-468, and 3.47 µM for BT474 LTR48. Moreover, it effectively reduced invasion (IC50 = 208.3 nM) and migration (IC50 = 9.39 nM) in MDA-MB-231 cells48. We identified Nebivolol, through the in silico screenings as a potential lead in anticancer studies, warrants further investigation as an AXL inhibitor in breast cancer to support its development as a therapeutic agent.

In the search for new targets and drugs to prevent tumor progression and metastasis, repurposed drugs are gaining increasing attention. In this context, since the development and approval of new drugs are often time-consuming and costly, β-blockers have emerged as readily available candidates. Furthermore, β-blockers may offer an additional advantage because their common contraindications and side effects are already well characterized48. In this study, Nebivolol was identified as a promising repurposed candidate based on its binding score, favorable interaction profile with the AXL kinase domain, molecular dynamics stability, MM/GBSA binding free energy results, and literature-supported broad anticancer potential. Therefore, Nebivolol should be investigated in more detail in experimental studies as a potential AXL-targeting candidate compound in breast cancer, and its therapeutic development potential should be evaluated.

Conclusion and future remarks

In conclusion, our study establishes a workflow that successfully bridges in silico approaches with in vitro experimental validation. These computational tools are highly predictive for prioritizing top-tier, target-directed leads. In this study, we selected the chromen ring containing compounds to compare reference inhibitor Y041-5921. Compared to the structure of STOCK1N-80636 with other compounds, the molecular docking value for this compound is high, but it exhibited lower average RMSD and a binding free energy values. It is predicted that this may be due to the adjacent triple ring structure and geometry of the compound. However, in in vitro analysis in breast cancer cell lines, STOCK1N-80636 showed high performance at low doses in terms of both reducing cell proliferation and inhibiting protein expression levels. Furthermore, evaluating candidates (STOCK1N-66436 and Abacavir) with weaker cytotoxic activity at low concentrations in combination with Doxorubicin (DOX) revealed promising synergistic pathways. This showcases how compounds or approved drugs with moderate cytotoxic activity in the tested cell lines can still be strategically repurposed for chemosensitization.

The findings from both our drug repurposing efforts and the structural evaluation of these previously uncharacterized molecules provide a foundational blueprint for the future development of AXL inhibitors. These compounds show considerable potential for the treatment of TNBC and ER + breast cancers, where AXL signaling plays a critical role in disease progression. Overall, these results indicate that the identified novel potential inhibitor candidates warrant further investigation in other malignancies characterized by AXL overexpression, as well as rigorous in vivo evaluation in breast cancer models, offering a broader and more realistic therapeutic potential for future oncology research.

Beyond breast cancer, AXL overexpression is a well-known driver of tumor progression, metastasis, and drug resistance in many other cancers such as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), pancreatic cancer, glioblastoma, colorectal cancer, and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Therefore, the novel potential inhibitor candidates identified and studied in vitro in this study hold a broader therapeutic promise. Future research should expand into evaluating these chemical scaffolds in other AXL-driven malignancies. Therefore, we recommend further in vitro and in vivo research of these compounds in these diverse cancer models. Future studies may focus on testing these leads both as single agents and in combinatorial studies. Similar to our successful results with Doxorubicin, combining these novel inhibitor candidates with standard chemotherapeutics could provide a powerful strategy to overcome drug resistance and block metastasis.

Experimental

Structure-based virtual screening study

In silico analyses were performed using Maestro (Schrödinger Release 2021–4 and 2023). BIOVIA Discovery Studio (DS) 2021 software was used to filter the ligands before virtual screening study49. For a structure-based virtual screening study, various compound databases (DrugBank, ChEMBL, ApexBio, Otava, Enamine, IBS, ChemDiv, Selleckchem) including approximately 800,000 compounds were screened against the AXL target (PDB ID: 5U6B)42. Firstly, non-drug likeness compounds were filtered by DS according to their pharmacokinetic properties as well as ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, Toxicity) and Lipinski and Veber (Ro5) rule. The filtered compounds were used for molecular docking study.

Molecular docking

For molecular docking study, the crystal structure for AXL from the Protein Data Bank (PDB: 5U6B) (https://www.rcsb.org/) was utilized42. The protein was prepared at physiological pH 7.4 by removing water molecules and adding missing hydrogen atoms using the software Protein Preperation Workflow of Maestro (Schrödinger Release 2021-4 and 2023)50. To prepare the ligands, LigPrep module of Maestro was used48. Protein and the ligands were minimized for their energies and optimized to determine their most stable conformers51. The binding site of AXL was defined by a receptor grid centered on the centroid of the co-crystallized ligand within the 5U6B crystal structure. The Glide/SP method was used for the structure-based virtual scanning study52. Ionization and protonation states at a physiological pH of 7.0 ± 2.0 were calculated using Epik53. No constraints were applied during docking and flexible ligand sampling was permitted. Interaction and binding energies were calculated in kcal/mol49.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Desmond54,55. The OPLS4 force field56 and the simple point charge (TIP3P) water model were employed. To neutralize the system and mimic physiological conditions, Na + counterion was added to neutralize the system to achieve the condition close to physiological state, and NaCl solution (0.15 M) was added to maintain the salt environment. Following the minimization and equilibration steps, MD simulation analysis was carried out under NPT ensemble conditions at 310 K and 1.013 bar. The Nose–Hoover chain thermostat and Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat were used to maintain constant temperature and pressure, respectively. For the ligands selected for use in in vitro studies, 300 ns MD simulation studies were performed, while for the other ligands, 100 ns MD simulations were conducted.

Molecular mechanics-generalized born surface area (MM/GBSA) calculations

Maestro’s Prime module was used to calculate the binding free energies of potential AXL inhibitors. Default settings were applied to determine the energy values and the related parameters of the minimized complexes in the Prime MM/GBSA57. The VSGB solvent model were used.

Cell culture studies

Various human triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell lines (MDA-MB-231, BT-549, and BT-20) and the estrogen receptor-positive ER + BC cell line (MCF-7) were used in this study. The cell lines were gift from Erciyes University. The cell line (MDA-MB-231) was provided from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The culture medium (DMEM/F12) was supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS) at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO₂. Cell passage was performed at appropriate time intervals using 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA. The cells were re-seeded into culture dishes containing fresh medium and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂ until the next passage58–60. The ligands (STOCK1N-88636 and STOCK1N-66436) from IBS were purchased to be used in the treatments.

Inhibition of colony formation

To inhibit colony formation, treatments were performed with the compounds at different concentrations. Compounds that inhibited cell colony formation were identified (8–12 days). Crystal violet staining was performed to visualize cell colonies. DMSO was used as a positive control58–60.

Cytotoxicity analysis

TNBC cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. After the incubation, various concentrations of the compounds were applied to the cells. To evaluate the 72-h cytotoxic effects of the compounds, MTT solution (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 2–4 h. After the incubation, the medium in each well was carefully removed, and DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a 96-well plate ELISA reader (Thermo). DMSO was used as the control vehicle. The drug concentrations that resulted in a 50% reduction in cell viability (IC50) were calculated58–60. Statistical analyses for cell culture studies were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2.

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was applied according to our previously reported studies58,59. The MDA-MB-231 cell line was treated with increasing concentrations of STOCK1N-80636 (1, 2.5, 5 μM) for 2 h. The lysates were obtained from the samples by adding protease inhibitor (10 μL), phosphatase inhibitor (10 μL) and NaF (1%) (4 μL) in lysis buffer solution (1 mL) on the sample pellet. The total protein content of the lysates was quantified using the BCA (Bicinchoninic acid) protein assay. Absorption values of the samples were measured at 570 nm against standards, and total protein amounts were calculated from the obtained absorption values. PVDF (polyvinylidene difluoride) membrane was used for protein transfer. After the electrophoresis, the expression levels were identified with the primary antibody (AXL (R&D AF154)). DMSO was used as a positive control. GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) (CST5174) was used as a loading control.

Statistical analysis

The IC50 values were calculated from MTT cell viability data using nonlinear regression analysis with a variable slope dose–response model in GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. The results are expressed as the mean ± SD from the replicated of experiments. Experimental group differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 or p < 0.01. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For MTT cell viability assay, each treatment condition was measured in six technical replicates per treatment group. Two independent studies were conducted. Statistical comparisons were performed between each treatment group and the untreated control group (CT) using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical annotations were defined as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; and ****p < 0.0001.
