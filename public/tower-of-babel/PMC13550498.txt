Electrospun poly(lactic acid) biopolymer fibers functionalized with Y-sperm-specific scFv enable immunoaffinity-driven bovine sperm sexing
Authors: Phanuwit Paitoon, Marninphan Thongkham, Kittisak Jantanasakulwong, Aphisit Saenjaiban, Apinya Sartsook, Surat Hongsibsong, Nuttapol Tanadchangsaeng, Pornchai Rachtanapun, Korawan Sringarm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Assisted reproductive technologies increasingly demand efficient approaches for sperm sexing in livestock, ideally compatible with scalable manufacturing and reduced resource use. This study serves as a proof-of-concept for a biocompatible electrospun poly (lactic acid) (PLA) biomaterial platform functionalized with a recombinant single-chain variable fragment antibody specific to bovine Y-sperm (BY-scFv), enabling selective separation of bovine Y-sperm from X-sperm populations. Electrospun mats were prepared by selection PLA concentration (6–10% w/w) and deposited solution volume (10–20 mL), and were evaluated based on electrospinning performance, fiber morphology, thickness, water-accessible porosity and water uptake capacity. A mat fabricated at 8% PLA with a 15 mL deposited volume was selected for subsequent functionalization and separation experiments. The presence and retention of immobilized BY-scFv after EDC/NHS coupling were supported by ELISA, FT‑IR, SEM, and fluorescence labeling. Using a syringe‑packed format, the functionalized mats enabled selective sperm capture and produced sex sperm ratio in different fractions, with maximum values of 71.4% X‑sperm in the supernatant fraction and 76.1% Y‑sperm in the eluted fraction. These results indicate that antibody‑functionalized electrospun PLA scaffolds can serve as a biopolymer-based platform for sperm immunoaffinity processing, with potential for further optimization and translational evaluation.

Introduction

Assisted reproductive technologies (ART) have become essential tools in modern livestock breeding, enabling accelerated genetic improvement, enhanced reproductive efficiency, and better control of offspring sex to meet production demands1,2. The use of sexed semen allows producers to predetermine progeny sex, supporting the production of female calves for dairy systems and male calves for beef production with superior growth performance3,4. The adoption of sex-sorted semen has steadily increased, accounting for nearly one-third of artificial insemination (AI) doses in North America, with more than 20% of heifers and ~ 2% of cows inseminated, similar to trends observed in Japan5,6. This technology contributes to improved herd management, production efficiency, and resource utilization in livestock systems7,8. Currently, the most widely established method for sperm sexing is fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), particularly flow cytometry cell sorting. This technique exploits the ~ 3–4% difference in DNA content between X- and Y-bearing sperm by fluorescent staining and sorting based on signal intensity9,10. FACS is the only widely commercialized technology in the livestock industry and can achieve enrichment accuracy exceeding 90%. However, its reliance on complex instrumentation, fluorescent labeling, and high-energy sorting processes limits its routine and large-scale application. High operational costs, low throughput, specialized infrastructure, and potential negative effects on sperm viability and fertility—resulting in ~ 20–25% lower pregnancy rates—remain major constraints11,12. In addition to FACS, alternative approaches such as density gradient centrifugation, swim-up/down, and microfluidic techniques have been explored. Although these methods offer improved simplicity and lower cost, they generally suffer from reduced accuracy or limited scalability, highlighting the need for alternative platforms that balance efficiency, simplicity, and practical applicability13.

Among these emerging alternatives, immunological sperm sexing has attracted increasing attention as a promising strategy, although several challenges remain. This approach utilizes antibody–antigen interactions to selectively recognize X- or Y-bearing sperm based on surface markers10,14. Two main strategies have been reported: immunoadsorption, where antibodies are immobilized on carriers such as magnetic microbeads to capture target sperm12,15, and complement-mediated cytotoxicity, which selectively impairs unwanted sperm populations via antibody-triggered complement activation16,17. Immunoadsorption systems have achieved enrichment rates of up to 80% while maintaining sperm viability, demonstrating their potential as gentler alternatives to high-energy sorting techniques11,18. However, current platforms still face challenges related to material complexity, high consumption, and limited reusability, particularly for microbead-based carriers, which complicate handling and raise concerns regarding sustainability19. Advances in recombinant antibody engineering have further improved the specificity of immunological approaches. Single-chain variable fragment (scFv) antibodies derived from monoclonal IgG against bovine Y-sperm offer advantages including smaller size, improved surface orientation, reduced steric hindrance, and more controlled conjugation, enabling efficient presentation on biomaterial surfaces20,21. Previous studies using scFv-functionalized microbeads demonstrated effective sperm sorting, while PLA film-based platforms achieved X-sperm purity of up to ~ 76.1%11,18,19. Nevertheless, these systems remain limited by their physical formats. Microbead-based systems require additional separation steps, while film-based platforms are constrained by their two-dimensional (2D) geometry, requiring large surface areas to achieve sufficient sperm–antibody interactions. These limitations are not well aligned with the volume and concentration of semen used in livestock production, emphasizing the need for compact, high-surface-area three-dimensional (3D) platforms. Biopolymer materials, particularly poly(lactic acid) (PLA), have gained attention due to their biodegradability, biocompatibility, and sustainable origin22–24. Electrospinning enables the fabrication of PLA into micro–nanoscale fibrous scaffolds with high surface area-to-volume ratios, tunable morphology, and interconnected porosity25,26. These structures provide abundant active sites for antibody immobilization and facilitate fluid penetration and reagent diffusion, which are critical for uniform bioconjugation within 3D matrices. In addition, electrospun mats can be produced using scalable fabrication processes, supporting their potential for practical applications. In contrast, previously reported PLA film-based platforms provide a simple and stable surface for antibody immobilization; however, their inherently 2D geometry limits surface accessibility and requires relatively large material areas to achieve sufficient sperm–antibody interactions. Despite these advantages, the integration of electrospun biopolymer fibers with recombinant scFv antibodies for sperm sexing remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we address this limitation by developing a 3D electrospun PLA fibrous scaffold with an interconnected porous structure, designed to enhance surface accessibility and reduce material usage for sperm sexing process. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are limited reports integrating electrospun biopolymer fibers with scFv antibody engineering for targeted sperm sexing. This approach leverages the 3D platform and micro-nanoscale topography of electrospun fibers to enhance antibody accessibility and binding efficiency, with the aim of enabling spatially distributed antibody presentation for selective Y-sperm capture. Accordingly, the present study addressed this unmet need by developing, characterizing, and evaluating a proof-of-concept platform of electrospun PLA biopolymer micro-nanoscale fibers functionalized with Y-chromosome-specific scFv antibodies engineered for sex-targeted separation of bovine sperm. This study is intended as a proof-of-concept investigation focusing on the feasibility of antibody immobilization on electrospun PLA fibers and preliminary sperm-binding capability. The primary objectives of this work were to (1) design and select the fabrication of electrospun PLA fibers for maximal antibody conjugation and mechanical integrity; (2) establish stable BY-scFv immobilization using EDC/NHS-mediated coupling and quantify antibody loading and binding activity; and (3) evaluate the specificity and efficiency of Y-sperm capture from conventional bovine sperm populations. Through this multidisciplinary approach, we aimed to explore the feasibility of a materials-based platform for antibody-mediated sperm sex separation with potential relevance to scalable livestock reproductive technologies.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Granule poly (lactic acid) (PLA) (4043D, density 1.24 g/cc, MFI = 7 g/10 min at 210 °C, MW = 100,000 g/mol; NatureWorks LLC, Minnetonka, MN, USA) was acquired from PTT Global Chemical PubCo., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand. The scFv antibody specific to plasma membrane epitopes of bovine Y-sperm (BY-scFv) was produced in our laboratory following the protocol of Thaworn et al.26; this antibody demonstrated a high binding capacity for Y-sperm and low cross-reactivity (4.25%) with X-sperm. 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), and 3,30,5,50-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA). Goat anti-HA tag HRP conjugated (BETHYL, Montgomery, TX, USA) was used. FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG and anti-HA tag mouse monoclonal antibody (4F6) were purchased from Abbkine (Wuhan, China). Hoechst 33,342 was obtained from Invitrogen (Eugene, OR, USA).

Preparation of the PLA solution and fabrication of the electrospun PLA mats

The PLA solution was divided into nine conditions based on concentration (6, 8, and 10% w/w) and volume (10, 15, and 20 mL). The PLA solution was prepared by dissolving various amounts of granular PLA in a mixture of dimethylformamide (DMF) and acetone at a ratio of 1:1. Afterward, each solution was heated at 160 °C until the PLA completely dissolved, and the solution was cooled to approximately 50 °C in a fume hood for 10 min. After that, the samples were loaded into syringes for the electrospinning process (Fig. 1a).

Electrospinning parameters (voltage, tip-to-collector distance, and flow rate) were fixed based on prior system calibration to ensure stable and reproducible fiber fabrication. Therefore, only PLA concentration and solution volume (spinning duration) were varied in this study. The various conditions of the PLA solution were spun at ambient temperature and humidity using a lab-scale electrospinning machine consisting of a single-nozzle electrospinning setup with a customized rotating drum collector (rolling electrospinning; RE) with the speed set at 400 rpm. A high-voltage power supply (ES60P-10 W, Gamma High Voltage, Ormond Beach, FL, USA) was attached to a metal nozzle to create a voltage between the metal nozzle and a collector of 22 kilovolts (kV). A charged metal nozzle with a 0.57 mm outer diameter was placed 22 cm from the collector and covered with aluminum foil. A mechanical pump (NE-300 Just Infusion™ Syringe Pump, New Era Pump Systems, Inc., Farmingdale, NY, USA) was used to deliver the solution from a plastic syringe to the metal nozzle at a rate of 3 mL/h, with the feeding time-dependent on the volume of solution. The produced electrospun PLA mats were covered with aluminum foil and kept at room temperature and low humidity until use in the next step (Fig. 1b).

Measurement of the electrospun PLA mat thickness

The thickness of each sample was measured at 10 locations using a thickness gauge (Mitutoyo, Kanagawa, Japan) following Sathirapongsasuti et al.27. The thickness of the produced electrospun PLA mats was examined to determine the effects of the volume of solution utilized on the thickness.

Measurement of the diameter of the electrospun PLA fibers

The diameter of the selected electrospun PLA fibers was measured. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JSM-IT300LV model, Tokyo, Japan) was used to analyze the morphology and diameter of the electrospun PLA fibers. Samples were prepared in 1 cm2 dimensions, and the surface was coated with gold (Au) for 1 min using a fully automated sputter coater (DII-29090 SCTR Smart Coater, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) before analysis. The electrospun PLA fiber samples were observed at magnifications of 1,000 and 4,000 ×. The average fiber diameter and standard deviation (SD) were measured using ImageJ software (www.imagej.nih.gov, accessed on 11 June 2024), as described by Sathirapongsasuti et al.28.

The water uptake capacity was calculated based on the weight difference before and after swelling in distilled water and the water-accessible porosity of the electrospun PLA mats was determined using the liquid displacement method28,29. The samples were then immersed in distilled water at RT for 24 h to ensure equilibrium swelling. Preparation of electrospun PLA fibers coupled with BY-scFv

Electrospun PLA fibers were coupled with BY-scFv antibodies. Each sample was trimmed to a dimension of 1 × 1 cm (1 cm2), wetted with DPBS, and placed in a 15-mL glass tube. The carboxylic acid groups on the electrospun PLA surface were then activated by adding EDC and NHS solutions at a final concentration of 0.1 mM, followed by a 4 h incubation at 4 °C. After that, BY-scFv antibody solution was added to the activated samples at five concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL), and each sample was completely mixed in a shaker incubator at 4 °C overnight (Fig. 2.). After incubation, each electrospun PLA fiber coupled with BY-scFv antibodies was rinsed with 1 mL of DPBS. After that, the unbound carboxylic acid groups on the electrospun PLA fibers were enclosed by Tris (50 mM/L, pH 7.4) at room temperature for 15 min, and the electrospun PLA fibers conjugated with BY-scFv (BY-fibers) were then washed three times with DPBS and maintained in DPBS at 4 °C.

Evaluation of the coupling capacity of BY-scFv on electrospun PLA fibers

The coupling efficiency of BY-scFv to electrospun PLA fibers was assessed by ELISA according to the methods of Thongkham et al.19. Each BY-fiber sample was washed three times with 1 mL of DPBS, followed by the addition of 100 µL of goat anti-HA tag HRP conjugated (1:100,000) and incubation at room temperature for 1 h. The samples were rinsed three times with 200 µL of PBST (0.1% v/v Tween-20 in PBS, pH 7.4, without Ca2+/Mg2+) and subsequently incubated with 100 µL of TMB substrate solution for 15 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped with 2 M sulfuric acid, and the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M3; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Optical density (OD) readings were used to compare immunoreactivities.

Surface morphology examination of the BY-fibers

At each conjugation stage, the surface morphology of the BY-fiber was observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JSM-IT300LV model, Tokyo, Japan) at magnifications of 1,000×, 2,000×, and 4,000×, following Thongkham et al.19. Wet samples were freeze-dried prior to SEM analysis, and the surfaces were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold (Au) for 1 min using a DII-29,090 SCTR Smart Coater (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Functional and structural analysis of the BY-fibers

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were used to analyze the functional groups, interactions, and reactions of BY-fibers. In accordance with the methods of Thongkham et al.19, wet samples were freeze-dried before being analyzed using a Jasco FT-IR-4700 (Easton, MD, USA) in transmittance mode. The recorded IR spectra captured the characteristic peaks.

Fluorescence labeling of BY-fibers

To provide additional evidence for the presence of immobilized BY-scFv on electrospun PLA fibers, fluorescence-based immunolabeling was performed. Three sample groups were evaluated: (i) pristine electrospun PLA fibers, (ii) electrospun PLA fibers incubated with BY-scFv in the absence of EDC/NHS activation, and (iii) electrospun PLA fibers activated with EDC/NHS prior to BY-scFv conjugation (BY-fibers). After the conjugation procedure, each sample was washed five times with PBST to remove unbound proteins. The fibers were then incubated with Anti-HA Tag Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (4F6; Abbkine) diluted 1:1000 in PBST at 37 °C for 1 h. After five washes with PBST, the samples were incubated with FITC-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (Abbkine) diluted 1:100 in PBST at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The samples were subsequently washed five times with PBST, and fluorescence images were captured using a conventional fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71) equipped with an Olympus DP73 digital camera30.

Sperm sex sorting using BY-fibers

The 1 cm² BY-fibers were packed into 5-mL glass syringes, and 5.0 × 106 cells of fresh bull semen (X: Y-sperm = 50:50%) obtained from the Inthanon Royal Project Livestock Semen Production Center, Chiang Mai, Thailand (Protocol no. RAGIACUC006/2564), diluted in a Tris–citric acid-based extender, were loaded onto the fibers. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min with gentle agitation every 5 min. After incubation, the unbound sperm were collected into new tubes and designated the “supernatant fraction” (SF). Sperm entrapped on the BY fibers were released by incubation with elution buffer (Tris–citric acid extender containing 50 mM imidazole, pH 7.4) for 5 min at 37 °C, and the eluted sperm were transferred to new tubes and defined as the “eluted fraction” (EF), as shown in Fig. 3. Discrimination of X/Y-sperm in the SF and EF

The X-/Y-sperm proportions in the SF and EF obtained from each BY-fiber separation were determined as described by Thongkham et al.19. The sperm samples were diluted to 1.0 × 106 cells/mL in PBS and stained with 2 µL of Hoechst 33342 (50 µg/mL), followed by incubation for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. Imaging flow cytometry (FlowSight, Seattle, WA, USA) was performed using a 15-mW 405-nm laser to excite Hoechst fluorescence. Histograms were generated to distinguish the X-sperm, which exhibited higher fluorescence intensity due to greater DNA content, from the Y-sperm.

Evaluation of sexed semen by SYBR® green RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from sperm after sex separation using electrospun PLA fibers conjugated with BY-scFv at different concentrations (0–8 mg/mL) via a PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quantity and purity were evaluated by measuring the absorbance at 260 and 280 nm. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using oligo (dT) 20 primers and the SuperScript™ III kit (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sexed sperm was analyzed by SYBR® Green RT–qPCR following the method described by Sringarm et al.12. The housekeeping gene GAPDH was used as a positive control (Forward 5´-GGCGCCAAGAGGGTCAT-3´, Reverse 5´-GGTGGTGCAGGAGGCATT-3´), yielding an amplicon of 120 bp (GenBank accession no. NM_001034034.2). A pair of Y-specific primers was designed to amplify a conserved region of the SRY gene located on the bovine Y chromosome (Forward 5´-GAAAATAAGCACAAGAAAGTCCAGG-3´, Reverse 5´-CAAAAGGAGCATCACAGCAGC-3´), producing a 124-bp product (GenBank accession no. EU581861.1). For the bovine proteolipid protein (PLP) gene, primers specific to the X chromosome were designed (Forward 5´-GGTGTGTTAGTTTCTGCTGTACAATAAATGG-3´, Reverse 5´-GATGGCAGGTGAGGGTAGGA-3´), generating a 96-bp fragment (GenBank accession no. AJ009913.1). RT–qPCR was performed using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (2X) (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA) on a qTOWER 2.2 System (Jena, Germany). Each reaction was carried out in triplicate using 50 ng of cDNA along with the appropriate controls. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method31. RT–qPCR approach was not intended to assess gene expression in a functional sense, but rather to use the relative abundance of sex-specific transcripts (SRY for Y-sperm and PLP for X-sperm) as molecular indicators of sperm population composition following separation.

Statistical analysis

Plots are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. Statistical differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with the statistical software program SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Duncan’s new multiple range test was used to compare values between individual groups when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Results and discussion

Fabrication of electrospun PLA fibers

The PLA solutions were prepared under fixed electrospinning parameters (voltage, tip-to-collector distance, and flow rate) in nine different concentrations of PLA (6, 8, and 10% w/w) and different solution volumes (10, 15, and 20 mL), as shown in Fig. 1a. To ensure consistency across these conditions, all the fibers were subsequently produced by rolling-electrospinning (Fig. 1b) using identical machine settings in a controlled air-conditioned room, with ambient temperature (25–30 °C) and humidity (30–50%) (single-nozzle setup with a rotating drum collector at 400 rpm, 22 kV, 22 cm nozzle-to-collector distance, and a solution feed rate of 3 mL/h). Under fixed electrospinning parameters, the primary variables governing jet behavior were the properties of the polymer solution and the total deposition time. In particular, increasing PLA concentration is expected to increase solution viscosity and polymer chain entanglement, which typically stabilizes the jet and alters the extent of jet stretching during flight, resulting in systematic changes in fiber diameter. In contrast, varying the dispensed solution volume (10–20 mL) at a constant feed rate mainly changes the spinning/deposition duration, thereby increasing the collected mass per unit area (areal density) and mat thickness. These thickness and packing changes can, in turn, influence the scaffold pore structure and liquid infiltration, which are reflected in the measured porosity and water uptake of the electrospun mats.

During this study, some electrospinning issues were observed depending on the PLA concentration. With respect to the 6% PLA solution, occasional solution dripping at the needle tip was observed, likely due to the low polymer concentration (low viscosity). In contrast, the 10% PLA solution, which had a relatively high concentration (high viscosity), occasionally solidified during electrospinning, causing needle clogging and interrupted flow27,28. However, no abnormalities were observed for the 8% PLA solution, suggesting that the fixed electrospinning conditions were more favorable for producing uniform PLA electrospun fibers at this concentration. Moreover, all the produced electrospun PLA mats formed white, uniform sheets of 30 × 30 cm (900 cm2) with a smooth, dry, paper-like surface and were collected on aluminum.

SEM images of the electrospun PLA fibers produced by the electrospinning technique from PLA solutions at concentrations of 6%, 8%, and 10% (w/w) are shown at a magnification of 4,000× in Fig. 4a. The resulting electrospun PLA fibers exhibited smooth, nonporous surfaces, as the solvent fully evaporated during the instability of the whipping before it reached the collector, preventing the deposition of solvent droplets on the fibers. This finding indicates that the acetone: DMF (1:1) solvent system was appropriate for producing uniform, nonporous PLA fibers27, although droplets (size ≥ 2 μm) were still observed under some conditions (Fig. 5a). At 6% PLA, numerous droplets with an average size of 3.62 ± 1.76 μm were observed. Similarly, the average sizes of the fibers from the 8% and 10% PLA solutions were 3.25 ± 1.05 μm and 3.39 ± 0.88 μm, respectively. However, the droplet density markedly differed among the concentrations: the 6% PLA fibers contained the highest droplet density (3,662.04 ± 508.25 droplets/mm2), which decreased substantially at higher PLA concentrations (8% = 2,061.63 ± 188.36; 10% = 623.91 ± 204.65 droplets/mm2). This trend is consistent with the well-established phenomenon that droplet formation is predominant at low PLA concentrations, where reduced viscoelastic forces and insufficient solution viscosity cannot counterbalance the applied electrospinning voltage, resulting in jet instability and fiber contraction32. Conversely, increasing the PLA concentration enhances the jet stability and promotes the production of continuous fibers with minimal droplet formation. Since excessive droplets can reduce the effective surface area available for antibody immobilization, electrospun PLA fibers with lower droplet densities are more suitable for subsequent functionalization33,34. These results suggest that increasing the PLA concentration is associated with reduced droplet formation on fibers and promotes the production of uniform, droplet-free fibers. In addition, the average fiber diameter increased significantly with increasing PLA concentration (p < 0.05). The mean diameters were 253.45 ± 57.26 nm for 6% PLA, 339.45 ± 94.98 nm for 8% PLA, and 418.00 ± 102.15 nm for 10% PLA, with corresponding diameters ranging from 117 to 491 nm, 177–648 nm, and 200–750 nm, respectively. No significant differences were observed between different solution volumes (10, 15, and 20 mL) within the same concentration group (p > 0.05). The increase in the diameter of the PLA fibers with increasing PLA concentration may be attributed to the higher solution viscosity, which reduces the stretching of the polymer jet during electrospinning. At low concentrations, the polymer chains are less entangled, allowing for the jet to stretch more and produce thinner fibers. In contrast, higher concentrations result in more entangled polymer chains, limiting jet elongation and generating thicker fibers32,35. Similarly, a previous study reported that the grafting of 10% wt PLA onto CNC (CNC-g-PLA) reduced the solution surface tension and increased the viscosity, resulting in the production of smooth, uniform nanofibers with larger diameters21. Significant differences among the concentration groups were evident, as indicated by the different superscript letters in Fig. 4a.

The thickness of the electrospun PLA mats prepared under these varying PLA concentrations and solution volumes was subsequently measured using a thickness gauge (Mitutoyo, Kanagawa, Japan), as shown in Fig. 4b. The thickness of the electrospun PLA mats increased significantly with increasing solution volume at all the tested PLA concentrations (p < 0.05). With respect to 6% PLA, the thickness increased from 34.30 ± 7.58 μm at 10 mL to 62.40 ± 14.29 μm at 20 mL. Similarly, the thickness of the mats from 8% PLA increased from 34.50 ± 7.96 μm (10 mL) to 100.80 ± 19.34 μm (20 mL), and that of the 10% PLA mats increased from 75.30 ± 30.51 μm (10 mL) to 144.80 ± 34.53 μm (20 mL). The mat thickness increased with increasing solution volume due to the greater deposition of the polymer jet on the collector during electrospinning, where more fibers accumulated and layered to form a thicker nonwoven mat, resulting in markedly thicker electrospun PLA mats36. The solution volume corresponds to the spinning duration at a constant flow rate and primarily affects the amount of deposited material (mat thickness), rather than intrinsic fiber morphology. These results indicate that both the PLA concentration and solution volume significantly influence mat thickness.

The water-accessible porosity and water uptake capacity of the electrospun PLA fibers were strongly influenced by both the PLA concentration and the solution volume used during electrospinning (Fig. 5b–c). The porosity increased from 6% to 8% PLA, reaching the highest values for fibers prepared from 8% PLA at 10 mL (26.74 ± 3.47%) and 15 mL (27.25 ± 4.07%), likely due to the balanced viscosity and stable jet formation, which resulted in the formation of well-interconnected pores. With 10% PLA, the porosity decreased to 24.71 ± 2.33% at 10 mL, reflecting that higher viscosity restricted jet splitting and pore generation. Increasing the solution volume to 20 mL further reduced the porosity to 20.89 ± 0.95% owing to fiber stacking and pore collapse. Similarly, the water uptake followed a similar trend. The highest value was observed for fibers produced from 8% PLA at 15 mL (17.72 ± 2.69×), attributed to their moderate packing density and open pore channels, which facilitated water infiltration. In contrast, both the 6% and the 10% PLA fibers exhibited restricted water absorption, with water uptake values ranging from 13.95–16.02× and 13.57–15.70×, respectively. These trends are consistent with variations in fiber diameter across the fabrication conditions, where changes in diameter influence packing density and pore architecture of the electrospun network, thereby affecting water-accessible porosity and water uptake capacity. As these properties govern fluid penetration and structural accessibility within the fibrous scaffold, they are directly relevant to sperm accessibility and interaction during the separation process. These results collectively indicate that 8% PLA with a 15 mL collection volume showed a more favorable balance under the tested conditions between water-accessible porosity and water uptake capacity for subsequent functionalization. The porosity values obtained in this study reflect water-accessible void volume rather than intrinsic total porosity, as they are derived from swelling-based and liquid displacement measurements. Therefore, these values should be interpreted as relative indicators of structural accessibility rather than absolute measures of pore structure.

Therefore, one representative condition was selected based on the combined physicochemical and structural performance for subsequent evaluation. This approach represents a structured material selection rather than a full functional selection across all variables. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the physicochemical and morphological properties of electrospun PLA fibers are strongly governed by both PLA concentration and solution volume, which together determine their suitability for downstream bioconjugation. The 6% PLA formulations were less suitable due to low viscosity, resulting in unstable jet formation, droplet generation, and fragile mats with insufficient thickness and porosity. In contrast, 10% PLA solutions exhibited high viscosity, leading to needle clogging, increased fiber diameter, and dense, low-porosity mats with limited liquid permeability. Increasing the electrospinning volume beyond 15 mL further exacerbated these effects through fiber stacking and structural compaction. Among the tested conditions, 8% PLA at 15 mL provided a balanced combination of uniform morphology, moderate fiber diameter, and interconnected porosity, yielding mats with sufficient thickness, mechanical integrity, and favorable water permeability. These characteristics support effective diffusion of activation and coupling reagents within the fibrous network, facilitating uniform EDC/NHS activation and antibody immobilization19. This selection condition was supported by quantitative comparison of key scaffold parameters (fiber diameter, thickness, droplet density, water-accessible porosity, and water uptake) across all conditions. Among these, mat thickness showed a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05), whereas other parameters were not significantly different between 8% PLA at 10 and 15 mL (p > 0.05). Despite comparable values in most parameters, the 10 mL condition produced thinner mats that were mechanically fragile and prone to tearing during handling. Therefore, the 15 mL condition was selected due to its improved structural robustness and practical handling characteristics. These findings are further supported by a semi-quantitative evaluation of electrospinning performance (Table 1) and a quantitative comparison of scaffold descriptors (Table 2), which together form the basis for condition selection. Importantly, these material properties are directly relevant to functional performance, as they influence reagent accessibility and potential sperm–surface interactions within the 3D scaffold. Accordingly, the selected condition provides a suitable architecture for subsequent antibody functionalization, combining process stability with favorable physicochemical characteristics. However, direct functional comparison across all fabrication conditions was not performed and should be addressed in future studies.

Coupling ability of BY-scFv on electrospun PLA fibers

To enable a bioactive interface capable of selective cell recognition, electrospun PLA fibers were functionalized with a recombinant single-chain variable fragment (scFv) antibody specific to bovine Y-sperm (BY-scFv). The BY-scFv antibody (clone N1:1/03) was produced in our laboratory. This scFv consists of a 350-bp variable heavy (VH) gene and a 318-bp variable light (VL) gene, which were successfully assembled into a complete scFv construct of 650 bp. Functional characterization demonstrated that BY-scFv exhibited a high binding capacity for Y-sperm (100%) but low cross-reactivity with X-sperm (4.25%), indicating its specificity and potential for selective sperm targeting26. The conjugation of BY-scFv antibodies onto electrospun PLA fibers was evaluated by ELISA (Fig. 6a). The schematic illustrates that BY-scFv immobilized on fiber surfaces can be specifically detected using an anti-HA tag conjugated with HRP specific to the HA site of the BY-scFv antibody, with subsequent TMB oxidation producing a measurable colorimetric signal. The conjugation of BY-scFv antibodies onto electrospun PLA fibers was performed at five concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL). Representative images of the electrospun PLA mats after the conjugation process are shown in Fig. 6b. No obvious visual changes were observed among the fibers at different antibody concentrations, as this step confirms only that the conjugation process was carried out prior to ELISA analysis. These conjugated mats were subsequently used in the ELISA to evaluate the actual antibody binding efficiency. ELISA primarily confirms the presence of antibody on the fiber surface. As shown in Fig. 6c, the ELISA results revealed clear colorimetric changes that correlated with the concentration of BY-scFv used during conjugation. Fibers conjugated with higher antibody concentrations produced stronger blue color development upon TMB oxidation, subsequently turned yellow after the addition of stop solution (2 M sulfuric acid) and were quantitatively supported by increased absorbance values at 450 nm. The signal increased sharply between 0 and 1 mg/mL and gradually reached a plateau at 2–8 mg/mL, indicating an apparent saturation of the ELISA-detectable anti‑HA signal under the current assay conditions. These results are consistent with previous studies showing that antibodies can be effectively conjugated on carboxyl-rich surfaces through the EDC/NHS method, as verified by ELISA11,12,19. This concentration-dependent immobilization highlights the efficient EDC–NHS–mediated coupling of BY-scFv to the electrospun PLA matrix and demonstrates the high binding capacity of the scaffold. Such antibody attachment is important for downstream applications; however, the present results do not distinguish immobilization from strong physical adsorption, where the antibody density on fibers directly influences the specificity and functional performance. In addition, it is important to note that EDC/NHS-mediated coupling results in non-site-specific attachment via available amine groups on the scFv, and therefore does not ensure controlled antibody orientation. As such, the present study does not claim defined molecular orientation but instead evaluates the retention of functional binding activity after immobilization. Importantly, the high surface-area-to-volume ratio of the electrospun fibrous network may enable spatial distribution of scFv on the fiber surface, which could contribute to improved accessibility for antigen recognition at the solid–liquid interface. The relatively low EDC/NHS concentration (0.1 mM) used in this study was selected to maintain mild reaction conditions and minimize potential structural alteration of the electrospun PLA fibers. Although higher concentrations are commonly reported, the present conditions were sufficient to achieve detectable antibody attachment as indicated by ELISA. Notably, the ELISA signal (anti‑HA detection) approached a plateau at higher BY‑scFv concentrations (2–8 mg/mL), whereas separation performance continued to improve with increasing BY‑scFv concentration. In this context, ELISA is interpreted as supportive evidence for the presence/retention of HA‑tagged BY‑scFv on the scaffold under the assay conditions rather than a direct quantitative measure of the number of functionally available antigen‑binding sites for sperm capture in the syringe‑packed format. Therefore, an apparent ELISA plateau does not necessarily coincide with saturation of functional capture capacity. We emphasize that the mechanistic origin of this discrepancy cannot be resolved from the current dataset. Dedicated measurements (e.g., binding-capacity/kinetics assays using Y‑sperm or relevant antigens, and transport-related characterization of the packed mat) will be required to determine whether the plateau arises from ELISA detection limits, epitope masking, or differences between assay conditions and the separation format. While the antibody and coupling chemistry are consistent with previous work, the key advancement lies in the transition from a planar film (2D) to a 3D fibrous architecture.

Surface morphology, functional, and structural analyses of BY-fibers

The conjugation of antibodies to biomaterials bearing surface carboxyl groups was performed using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). EDC activates the carboxyl groups to form an O-acylisourea ester intermediate, which is unstable under aqueous conditions. NHS stabilizes this intermediate by converting it into an NHS ester, which readily reacts with amino groups on the antibody to form a covalent amide bond19,37, thereby linking the BY-scFv antibody to the electrospun PLA fiber surface, as shown in Fig. 2. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were performed to monitor the chemical and morphological changes in the electrospun PLA fibers during the conjugation process36. FT-IR spectroscopy (Jasco FT-IR-4700, Easton, MD, USA) was performed to monitor chemical changes during the bioconjugation process, as shown in Fig. 7a. The FT-IR spectrum of the pristine electrospun PLA fibers exhibited a strong absorption peak at 1752.31 cm-1, corresponding to the C = O stretching vibration of the ester functional groups in the polymer backbone. This characteristic peak confirms the structural integrity of the ester-based electrospun PLA fibers. Following activation with EDC/NHS, the spectrum of the electrospun PLA fiber/EDC-NHS complex displayed two additional peaks: the amide I band at 1640.32 cm-1 and the amide II band at 1542.82 cm-1. These peaks are indicative of the presence of activated carboxylic acid groups, suggesting successful chemical modification in the preparation for antibody conjugation. After the BY-scFv was immobilized, the spectrum of the electrospun PLA fiber/EDC-NHS/BY-scFv construct revealed slight shifts in the amide I and amide II bands to 1636.36 cm-1 and 1538.87 cm-1, respectively. These spectral shifts are consistent with amide bond formation; however, they should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of immobilization between the carboxyl groups of the electrospun PLA fibers and the amine groups of the antibody. The consistent presence of amide I and II bands in the final construct strongly supports the successful conjugation of the BY-scFv antibody onto the electrospun PLA fiber surface. Consistently, the SEM (JSM-IT300LV model, Tokyo, Japan) results illustrate the surface morphology of electrospun PLA fibers at different stages of the conjugation process and interaction with sperm. Nevertheless, because FT‑IR cannot definitively distinguish covalent attachment from strongly adsorbed protein, these spectral changes are best considered supportive of successful BY‑scFv immobilization when interpreted together with the complementary retention and surface-accessibility evidence obtained from ELISA and fluorescence immunolabeling after washing (Figs. 6 and 7 d).

To complement the FT‑IR findings, SEM was used to qualitatively examine morphology changes across each modification step; however, SEM observations were interpreted cautiously because apparent diameter differences can also arise from surface deposition and sample preparation effects rather than from a true change in the intrinsic fiber diameter. The untreated electrospun PLA fibers (average diameter 353.73 ± 46.69 nm) exhibited a smooth surface morphology. Following activation with the EDC/NHS solution, an apparent increase in fiber diameter was observed by SEM (average diameter of 2008.17 ± 189.75 nm), accompanied by increased surface roughness. However, this change is unlikely to reflect a true increase in the intrinsic fiber diameter. Instead, it is more plausibly attributed to surface deposition of reaction intermediates, residual reagents, or sample preparation artifacts (e.g., freeze-drying effects). Upon conjugation with BY-scFv antibodies, the fibers demonstrated significant surface roughness along with the appearance of crystalline-like plate structures in certain regions. The fiber diameter could not be measured in this group because of the extensive surface modifications, which are attributed to antibody attachment (Fig. 7b). Finally, incubation of the BY-fibers with sperm revealed clear sperm binding to the fiber surfaces, as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 7c. Therefore, SEM observations should be interpreted with caution and considered as qualitative evidence of surface changes rather than definitive proof of successful chemical activation. Complementary analytical techniques are required to confirm the underlying chemical processes. These observations provide qualitative evidence of surface modification following the conjugation process but should not be interpreted as direct proof of successful immobilization onto electrospun PLA fibers, although they do not independently confirm immobilization. To further support the presence and surface accessibility of BY-scFv, a fluorescence-based detection assay was performed. As shown in Fig. 7d, strong fluorescence signals were observed on the surface of BY-scFv-conjugated fibers after labeling with anti-HA antibody and FITC-conjugated secondary antibody, whereas negligible signal was detected in control samples without BY-scFv. The fluorescence signal remained detectable after washing steps, suggesting relatively stable association of the antibody with the fiber surface. While this result provides additional supporting evidence for stable antibody immobilization, it does not conclusively distinguish covalent bonding from strong physical adsorption. The SEM and FT-IR results of this study are consistent with those of Thongkham et al.19, who reported that PLA films conjugated with scFv antibodies (PF-scFv films) exhibited functional group changes similar to those observed here, resulting from EDC/NHS-mediated activation of surface carboxyl groups and subsequent antibody coupling. Moreover, SEM analysis further revealed that the functionalized PF-scFv films could bind to sperm, which is in line with the surface roughening and structural modifications observed on BY-fibers. These results demonstrate the successful conjugation of BY-scFv antibodies onto electrospun PLA fibers, preserving their functional activity and enabling specific sperm binding, which was ready for subsequent sperm sex-sorting tests. Together, these structural and chemical characterizations confirm the formation of a stable, bioactive fibrous interface capable of mediating selective cell–material interactions, a key requirement for immunoaffinity-based separation systems.

Efficiency of sperm sex sorting using BY-fibers

This study provides a materials-engineering perspective by linking electrospinning parameters to scaffold properties (fiber diameter, water-accessible porosity, and water uptake) and their implications for antibody immobilization and sperm interaction within a 3D matrix. After confirming the successful conjugation of BY-scFv to the electrospun PLA fibers (BY-fibers) and their binding affinity to sperm, the BY-fibers were subsequently employed for sex separation. The overall workflow is depicted in Fig. 3. Fresh semen samples were incubated in a syringe packed with BY-fibers, where Y-sperm were selectively bound through antigen–antibody interactions. During this process, the unbound sperm passed through the column and were collected as the “supernatant fractions (SF)”, whereas the sperm retained on the BY fibers were released by elution buffer, yielding the “eluted fractions (EF)”. This separation resulted in two distinct populations of sperm, enabling subsequent evaluation of the sperm sex ratio.The efficiency of BY fibers in sperm sex separation was evaluated at different BY-scFv antibody conjugation concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL). As shown in Fig. 8a., the number of sperm present in the SF decreased with increasing BY-scFv concentration, whereas the number of sperm in the eluted fraction (EF) increased correspondingly. The concentration of BY-scFv antibodies significantly influenced the binding capacity of the BY-fibers, with higher antibody concentrations resulting in greater sperm binding efficiency, reaching approximately 33.90% at 8 mg/mL. Moreover, imaging flow cytometry further confirmed the distribution of X- and Y-sperm populations in the SF and EF (Fig. 8b). In the absence of BY-scFv conjugation (0 mg/mL), the X- and Y-sperm ratios remained close to the natural 1:1 distribution (49.9:50.1%). After separation, the supernatant fraction (SF) was enriched in X-sperm, with proportions increasing from approximately 60% at lower BY-scFv concentrations to more than 71% at the highest concentration. Conversely, the eluted fraction (EF) became progressively enriched in Y-sperm, reaching approximately 76% at the highest antibody concentration. Collectively, the results in Fig. 8c. confirm, through both quantitative analysis and imaging flow cytometry, the specific binding of Y-sperm by BY-fibers. These results clearly demonstrate that BY-fibers are capable of selectively capturing Y-sperm through antigen–antibody interactions, leading to X-sperm enrichment in the unbound SF. The observed dose-dependent trend indicates that higher BY-scFv conjugation enhances Y-sperm binding, thereby increasing the yield of X-sperm in the SF. Notably, the maximum enrichment of X- and Y-sperm reached 71.40 ± 2.17% in SF and 76.10 ± 3.45% in EF, respectively. All experiments were performed with independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, supporting the reproducibility of the observed trends. Although, ELISA signal suggested near-saturation of anti‑HA detectability at higher BY‑scFv concentrations, separation performance continued to increase with antibody concentration. Because ELISA in this study reports HA‑tag accessibility rather than functional binding-site availability for sperm capture, the mechanistic basis of this divergence cannot be concluded from the present data. Future work should directly quantify functional binding capacity/kinetics and assess whether transport and encounter effects in the packed 3D mat contribute to the observed dose-dependent separation trend. Additionally, RT–qPCR analysis revealed differential expression patterns of X- and Y-specific genes following sex separation using BY-fiber conjugates at different concentrations of the scFv antibody (0–8 mg/mL) (Fig. 9). The observed differences in SRY and PLP signals between fractions are interpreted as reflecting changes in the relative proportions of Y- and X-bearing sperm rather than transcriptional regulation, as mature spermatozoa are transcriptionally inactive. Therefore, RT–qPCR in this context serves as an indirect method to assess sperm sex composition. GAPDH was used as a reference gene to normalize variations in RNA input and cDNA synthesis efficiency across samples. Although the use of housekeeping genes in sperm is subject to limitations due to minimal transcriptional activity, this approach provides a consistent basis for relative comparison under controlled experimental conditions. The relative expression level at 0 mg/mL was designated as the baseline control. In the SF fraction, the mean relative expression of the X-specific primer (PLP) was significantly greater (p < 0.05) in all the treated groups (1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL) than in the control group, with expression levels increasing in a dose-dependent manner. This elevation in PLP expression indicates a progressive enrichment of X-sperm within the SF fraction. In contrast, compared with the control, the Y-specific primer (SRY) did not significantly affect the SF fraction, supporting the reduced proportion of Y-sperm in this fraction. With respect to the EF fraction, compared with the control, the Y-specific primer (SRY) significantly increased (p < 0.05) the mean relative expression across all treatment concentrations (1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL), with the highest expression observed at 8 mg/mL (3.09-fold). Conversely, no significant difference was detected for the X-specific primer (PLP) in the EF fraction, where expression levels remained relatively low, which is consistent with the reduced proportion of X-sperm present in EF. RT–qPCR-based detection of sex-specific gene expression (PLP for X-sperm and SRY for Y-sperm) clearly demonstrated that sex separation using BY-fiber conjugates at different concentrations of the scFv antibody effectively altered the sperm population ratio. The enrichment of X-sperm in the SF fraction and Y-sperm in the EF fraction confirmed the efficiency of this method in shifting the proportion of X- and Y-sperm following separation. Taken together, the enrichment results obtained from imaging flow cytometry and RT–qPCR analyses demonstrate that BY-fibers enable effective and selective modulation of X- and Y-sperm populations through antibody-mediated interactions. Although Hoechst staining and RT–qPCR were used to validate sperm sex separation, additional complementary methods, such as alternative quantitative approaches or independent validation using different semen samples, would further strengthen the robustness of the conclusions and should be considered in future studies. Importantly, rather than prioritizing maximum sorting accuracy alone, the electrospun PLA fiber platform represents a distinct materials-architecture strategy that emphasizes a 3D structure and efficient fluid penetration and reagent diffusion within the fibrous network. These material attributes facilitate the formation of a more accessible biointerface and spatially distributed antibody presentation, while reducing material usage compared with planar formats, thereby highlighting the potential of fibrous material architectures for practical and efficient immunoaffinity-based sperm separation. To clarify capacity on an equal-area basis, Thongkham et al.19 2D PLA film format, 4.0 × 106 sperm were processed using a 2 × 2 cm film (4 cm2) (i.e., 1.0 × 106 sperm/cm2). In the present 3D electrospun mat, 5.0 × 106 sperm were processed using a 1 cm2 footprint (i.e., 5.0 × 106 sperm/cm2), which suggests an approximately fivefold higher processing capacity per unit area under our conditions. This comparison reflects the higher accessible interfacial area provided by the 3D fibrous architecture within the same footprint, this 3D electrospun mat format is practically relevant for bovine semen handling, because fresh ejaculate volumes are limited (typically ~ 5–7 mL), and a platform that operates effectively at small working volumes is advantageous for implementation in field-relevant workflows.

Although selective binding originates from antigen–antibody complex, the separation outcome in a fibrous scaffold is also governed by transport and contact phenomena dictated by the mat architecture. The interconnected porosity of the electrospun PLA network, together with its water-uptake capacity, is expected to promote fluid infiltration and maintain continuous aqueous pathways through the mat, thereby reducing transport limitations and enabling sperm to access antibody-presenting fibers throughout the 3D volume rather than only at the outer surface. At the microstructural level, variations in fiber diameter and packing density can shift the effective pore architecture and tortuosity, which may alter sperm residence time and the frequency of sperm–fiber encounters, ultimately affecting the probability of specific capture. In addition, hydration of the scaffold in aqueous media (as reflected by water uptake) may influence the balance between specific binding and non-specific retention by changing local lubrication and contact conditions within the fibrous network. Therefore, the observed enrichment likely reflects a combined effect of BY‑scFv recognition and a scaffold architecture that facilitates transport and contact within a compact footprint. Future work needs to quantify pore size distribution and permeability and relate these parameters to binding kinetics and non-specific retention to further rationalize and optimize the separation mechanism.

The fabrication parameters, specifically PLA concentration and solution volume, strongly influenced the electrospun scaffold’s fiber diameter, mat thickness, porosity, and water uptake. The optimized condition (8% PLA at 15 mL) yielded a fibrous mat with balanced micro–nanoscale architecture, interconnected porosity, and high-water uptake, facilitating fluid infiltration and sperm transport within the 3D network. These structural features likely enhance sperm access to immobilized BY-scFv antibodies by increasing contact frequency and residence time, thereby improving selective binding and sex separation efficiency. Although wettability was not directly measured, water uptake suggests scaffold hydration that may reduce transport limitations and promote uniform sperm–surface interactions. The observed sperm sex sperm ratio results align with this materials-architecture rationale, demonstrating the advantage of a porous 3D scaffold over planar formats in practical semen processing.

While SEM, porosity/water-uptake measurements, FT-IR, ELISA, and fluorescence immunolabeling support the formation of a porous 3D electrospun scaffold and stable BY-scFv retention after PBST washing, several limitations should be noted. First, we did not quantify key architecture descriptors that are highly relevant to binding-based separation, including specific surface area (e.g., BET), pore size distribution/permeability, or mechanical stability; these measurements are needed to directly link scaffold structure (fiber diameter, packing density, and mat thickness/areal density) to transport behavior and to demonstrate robustness for scaled and field-relevant use. Second, although physicochemical features of the mat (interconnected porosity, water uptake, and wettability) are expected to influence fluid penetration and sperm migration and thus binding probability these effects were inferred rather than directly isolated or modeled and require systematic investigation. Third, reproductive properties of the sex-enriched fractions were not measured or compared with unsorted semen under matched handling conditions; therefore, conclusions regarding biocompatibility and sexed sperm processing remain preliminary. Future work needs to include standardized post-processing sperm quality assessment (motility, viability, membrane/acrosome integrity) with unsorted and process-control groups and extension to fertility-related endpoints. Despite these limitations, the electrospun 3D architecture enables higher sperm processing capacity per unit footprint area than our prior 2D PLA film format under comparable conditions, highlighting the value of structural design for improving material efficiency alongside sex sperm ratio performance. A formal cost-of-goods/techno-economic analysis was not performed in this study and will be required to substantiate cost-effectiveness claims.

Conclusion

Electrospun poly(lactic acid) (PLA) fibers were developed as a biofunctional fibrous scaffold and functionalized with a bovine Y-sperm–specific single-chain variable fragment (BY-scFv) antibody for immunoaffinity-based sperm sex separation. The electrospinning conditions were systematically investigated, and the use of 8% PLA at a 15 mL collection volume provided a uniform micro–nanoscale architecture with balanced fiber diameter, mat thickness, interconnected porosity, and wettability. These structural features may support surface activation and antibody attachment within the fibrous network. The presence of BY-scFv on the fiber surface was supported by ELISA, FT-IR, SEM, and fluorescence analyses, although these techniques provide indirect evidence and do not definitively confirm immobilization. Functional evaluation suggested that the BY-fibers are capable of selectively interacting with Y-sperm through antibody–antigen recognition, resulting in enrichment of X-sperm to approximately 71.4% in the supernatant fraction, which is comparable to previously reported immunological approaches. Collectively, the findings of this study should be considered as a proof-of-concept, demonstrating the feasibility of using electrospun antibody-functionalized fibrous systems for sperm separation. This material efficiency indicates potential scalability, may reduce material usage and support further development toward practical applications in livestock semen sexing. However, additional studies are required to evaluate sperm quality, validate reproducibility using independent samples, and further investigate functional performance under practical conditions.
