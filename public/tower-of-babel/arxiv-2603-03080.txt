Beyond Factual Correctness: Mitigating Preference-Inconsistent Explanations in Explainable Recommendation DOI: XXXXXXX.XXXXXXX Conference: ; ; CCS: Information systems Recommender systems
Chengkai Wang, Baisong Liu

Abstract
. Explainable recommendation systems increasingly employ large language models (LLMs) to generate fluent and persuasive natural-language explanations for recommended items. Prior work has largely focused on improving factual correctness, implicitly assuming that factually grounded explanations are sufficient to establish user trust. However, in practice, explanations may remain factually correct yet justify recommendations using attributes that are weakly supported or even contradicted by a user’s historical preferences, resulting in reasoning that is logically valid but normatively unconvincing to the user. This failure mode is not explicitly addressed by existing explainable recommender systems and remains invisible to standard hallucination or model-faithfulness-based evaluation metrics. In this work, we identify this phenomenon as preference-inconsistent explanations, a distinct and previously under-examined failure mode in explainable recommendation, and propose PURE, a preference-aware reasoning framework that follows a select-then-generate paradigm. PURE intervenes at the evidence selection stage rather than generation, identifying a compact set of reasoning paths that are both factually grounded and aligned with user preference structure. Concretely, it performs preference-aware reasoning path selection over multi-hop item-centric structures, guided by user intent, specificity, and diversity, thereby suppressing generic but weakly personalized evidence. The selected evidence is then incorporated into LLM-based generation via structure-aware prompting that preserves relational constraints. To make this failure mode measurable, we introduce a feature-level evaluation metric that serves as a complementary, user-centric lens for quantifying preference inconsistency, revealing systematic misalignment that factuality-based measures fail to detect. Experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate that PURE consistently reduces preference-inconsistent explanations and factual hallucinations, while maintaining competitive recommendation accuracy, explanation quality, and inference efficiency. Taken together, these results suggest that explanation faithfulness must be understood not only as factual correctness, but also as justification aligned with user preferences, highlighting a previously under-evaluated, user-centric dimension of trustworthy explainable recommendation.

However, increased linguistic fluency does not necessarily imply increased trustworthiness. Most existing explainable recommender systems implicitly assume that an explanation is trustworthy as long as it is factually correct, i.e., it mentions item attributes that are objectively true

Yet factual correctness alone is insufficient to explain why an item is suitable for a particular user. Consider a user who has consistently engaged with light-hearted, comforting movies characterized by warmth, humor, and emotional healing. The recommender system suggests a critically acclaimed prison drama that indeed contains themes of hope and redemption. When asked for an explanation, the system highlights the film’s realistic depiction of suffering and psychological struggle—attributes that are factually correct and well supported by external knowledge sources.

From the user’s perspective, however, the explanation feels unconvincing. Although the recommended item itself may still be reasonable, the justification emphasizes precisely the aspects the user has historically avoided. The system does not fabricate facts, nor does it provide irrelevant information; instead, it offers the wrong reasons for the recommendation. Over time, such explanations can erode user trust, as users perceive the system as misunderstanding why items are recommended, even when recommendation accuracy appears acceptable.

This example illustrates a subtle yet critical failure mode in explainable recommendation: explanations that remain factually correct but are misaligned with a user’s preference structure. In this work, we refer to this phenomenon as preference-inconsistent explanations. As categorized in Table
1
, we characterize this as a logically valid but normatively invalid explanation error: while the reasoning is factually sound, it violates the normative expectation that a recommendation must align with the user’s specific interests. Unlike generic or low-informative explanations, preference-inconsistent explanations actively present factual item features as positive reasons despite lacking support from the user’s established preferences. As illustrated in Figure
1
, this failure mode is fundamentally different from factual hallucination: the explanation is grounded in true attributes, yet its reasoning contradicts what the user values.

Existing explainable recommender systems rarely address this issue explicitly. Retrieval-augmented and knowledge-grounded approaches effectively reduce blatant factual errors, but they typically prioritize globally salient or high-frequency concepts when selecting evidence. Thus, explanations may be factually grounded yet preference-misaligned, especially when multi-hop reasoning paths emphasize popular but weakly personalized attributes. Because standard faithfulness metrics focus primarily on factual correctness, preference-inconsistent explanations often remain undetected and unpenalized.

These observations suggest that improving trustworthiness in explainable recommendation requires more than factual grounding. It calls for a principled mechanism that explicitly incorporates user preference structure into the reasoning process underlying explanation generation, ensuring that explanations justify recommendations using preference-aligned evidence rather than merely correct facts.

In this paper, we propose
PURE
(Preference-aligned Unhallucinated Reasoning for Explanation), a preference-aware reasoning framework for explainable recommendation. The key insight behind PURE is that preference inconsistency primarily arises from generic, high-frequency reasoning paths, rather than from missing factual information. Accordingly, PURE focuses on identifying a compact set of factually grounded and preference-aligned reasoning paths instead of maximizing knowledge coverage. PURE constructs multi-hop item-centric subgraphs, applies preference-aware path pruning based on target-aware user intent, node specificity, and diversity-aware reranking, and conditions LLM-based explanation generation on the resulting preference-consistent evidence through a hybrid structure-aware prompting mechanism.
Our contributions are threefold:

•

We identify preference-inconsistent explanations as a previously under-characterized failure mode in LLM-based explainable recommendation, where explanations remain factually correct yet violate the user’s intrinsic interests, a limitation not captured by existing faithfulness metrics.

•

We propose
PURE
, a preference-aware reasoning framework that aligns explanation generation with user preferences via KG-based path construction, specificity and intent-aware path pruning, and structure-preserving prompting.

•

We introduce feature-level metrics to quantify both factual hallucinations and preference inconsistency, and demonstrate substantial improvements on three real-world datasets.

Related Work

2.1.
Explainable Recommendation with LLMs

LLM-driven explainable recommendation has advanced rapidly, moving beyond template-based methods to generate fluent, human-like justifications. Representative methods, such as PEPLER

Despite these advances, most existing LLM-based explainers primarily optimize surface-level properties of explanations, such as linguistic plausibility, topical relevance, or semantic coherence with the recommended item. Explanation faithfulness is often implicitly reduced to factual correctness, assuming that mentioning objectively true item attributes suffices to justify recommendations. As a result, explanations that are fluent and factually accurate are typically regarded as trustworthy, without explicitly considering whether the explanation logic aligns with the user’s preference structure.

Departing from these generation-centric approaches, we argue that factual correctness alone is insufficient. We identify preference inconsistency—where explanations remain factually correct yet cite misaligned features—as a critical failure mode. Unlike prior explainers prioritizing plausibility, we treat preference consistency as a first-class objective, explicitly aligning explanation reasoning with user interests rather than treating it as a byproduct of fluency.

Retrieval-Augmented Recommendation

However, existing retrieval-based approaches exhibit key limitations regarding preference alignment. First, retrieved evidence is typically linearized into plain textual context, discarding graph topology and multi-hop structures that may encode preference-relevant reasoning patterns. Second, retrieval strategies often favor high-degree concepts that are globally salient but weakly personalized, leading to explanations that are factually grounded yet generic.

In contrast, PURE performs preference-aware structural pruning. Rather than maximizing evidence coverage, we select a compact set of multi-hop reasoning paths that are both faithful and strictly aligned with the user’s preference structure, preserving these structural constraints during LLM conditioning to avoid misaligned justifications.

Faithfulness is a prerequisite for user trust in explainable recommendation. Prior approaches, such as FIRE

Yet, a critical gap remains regarding generative alignment: whether the generated explanation strictly adheres to the user’s established preference structure. We argue that in user-centric systems, merely analyzing preferences is ineffective if the final justification contradicts them.

To bridge this gap, we identify preference inconsistency as a distinct failure mode. While prior art treats all factual features or identified concepts as valid candidates, we propose feature-level metrics to explicitly quantify preference misalignment. By extending faithfulness from model parameters to user preferences, we demonstrate that preference consistency captures an essential dimension of trustworthiness that current protocols fail to address.

3.
Preliminaries

We assume a shared feature space
\mathcal{F}
for items and explanations.
Conceptually, each user
u
is associated with a set of historical positive features
\mathcal{F}_{u}^{\text{hist}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, a latent preference function
g_{u}:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and a decision threshold
\tau
.
We define a feature
f
to be
user-aligned
if it is a verified historical preference (
f\in\mathcal{F}_{u}^{\text{hist}}
) or a valid serendipitous interest (
g_{u}(f)\geq\tau
).
For a recommendation instance
e=(u,i)
, let
\mathcal{F}_{\text{item}}(i)\subseteq\mathcal{F}
denote the ground-truth attributes of item
i
. Given a generated explanation
Y
, a feature extractor derives the set of generated features
\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.

Factual Hallucination.

An explanation is considered factually hallucinated if it contains features absent from the item’s ground-truth attributes:

\exists f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)\setminus\mathcal{F}_{\text{item}}(i).

Preference Inconsistency.

While factual hallucination concerns the item, preference inconsistency pertains to the user. Ideally, a feature
f
serves as a valid justification only if it aligns with user interests. A preference inconsistency occurs when the explanation promotes a feature that is neither historically verified nor supported by the user’s latent preferences:

\exists f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad f\notin\mathcal{F}_{u}^{\text{hist}}\land g_{u}(f)<\tau.

Since the true latent preference
g_{u}
is unobservable, we approximate it using an
estimated preference proxy

\hat{g}_{u}
, constructed from the user’s historical interactions and semantic reasoning (detailed in Section
5.1.3
).

4.
Methodology

Before detailing the technical components, we clarify the design rationale of PURE. The central goal is neither to maximize the volume of retrieved knowledge nor to overload explanations with attributes. Instead, PURE aims to identify a concise set of factually grounded yet preference-aligned reasoning paths that faithfully justify the recommendation for a specific user.

We observe that preference inconsistency primarily arises from generic and high-degree reasoning paths that dominate conventional retrieval pipelines. These paths, while factually correct, often emphasize globally salient attributes that may contradict a user’s specific interests. Crucially, generation-level alignment is insufficient; even a well-instructed LLM will hallucinate preferences if the upstream evidence is generic. Therefore, PURE intervenes before generation, enforcing intent relevance at the reasoning path selection stage. This design shifts the focus from how to generate explanations to what evidence should justify them.

4.1.
Structure-Enhanced Semantic Indexing

To enable preference-aware reasoning, PURE constructs a structure-enhanced semantic index that stratifies reasoning paths by semantic depth. Unlike conventional multi-hop retrieval methods that treat all hops as homogeneous context, we explicitly assign distinct semantic roles to varying hop depths. This design simultaneously reinforces factual grounding and deepens personalization:

•

1-hop (Explicit Features).
Grounds explanations in directly verifiable item attributes, thereby mitigating factual hallucination.

•

2-hop (Relational Reasoning).
Captures proximal associations to support analogical reasoning across related items.

•

3-hop (Implicit Preference).
Exposes high-order relational patterns that reflect latent user interests, addressing preference inconsistency beyond surface-level matching.

We restrict the retrieval depth to 3 hops, as empirical evidence indicates that deeper traversals introduce semantic drift and noise, which tend to exacerbate rather than resolve preference inconsistency.

To materialize this index, we first initialize entity and relation representations using a frozen pre-trained language model (PLM), ensuring a robust semantic foundation. Building on these frozen embeddings, we employ a Relational Graph Attention Network (RGAT)
(
32
)
as the offline encoder to capture high-order structural dependencies. Instead of performing complex gradient updates for downstream tasks, the RGAT focuses on iteratively aggregating multi-hop neighbor information to synthesize structure-aware item representations. These embeddings are pre-computed and stored in a vector database, facilitating efficient retrieval without incurring online inference overhead.

4.2.
Preference-Aware Path Retrieval

While the indexed subgraphs encapsulate rich factual and relational evidence, naively ingesting all paths into the LLM leads to
specificity dilution
: high-degree hub nodes often dominate the context, resulting in generic and non-informative explanations. To address this, we design a Preference-Aware Retrieval module to selectively extract paths that are strictly factually grounded yet deeply aligned with personalized preferences.

4.2.1.
Target-Aware User Intent Modeling

User preferences are inherently context-dependent, varying significantly based on the candidate item. Unlike static approaches that represent users by averaging historical interactions, we construct a
target-aware
intent vector that dynamically prioritizes history items relevant to the target item
i_{t}
.

Formally, given the user history
\mathcal{H}_{u}
and the set of pre-computed graph embeddings
\{\mathbf{h}^{(K)}_{v}\}
from the RGAT encoder, we employ an attention mechanism where the target
i_{t}
serves as the query, and history items
i_{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}
serve as keys and values. The user intent representation
\mathbf{h}_{u}(i_{t})
is computed as:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{u}(i_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}}\alpha_{i_{h}}\,(\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{h}^{(K)}_{i_{h}}),

where
\mathbf{W}_{V}
is a learnable projection matrix, and
\alpha_{i_{h}}
denotes the normalized attention weight derived from the similarity between the target and history embeddings.

By conditioning user intent on the specific target, this mechanism avoids collapsing diverse historical interests into a single static centroid—a limitation that would otherwise amplify generic reasoning paths and exacerbate preference inconsistency.

4.2.2.
Node Specificity

We posit that a node’s explanatory value hinges on three dimensions: structural discriminativeness, semantic specificity, and contextual relevance. Relying solely on structural degree metrics is inadequate, as it fails to distinguish between
popular entities
(which are informative) and
generic concepts
(which induce noise).

To address this, we quantify node informativeness via a multi-view specificity metric that synergizes three complementary signals. Formally, the specificity score
I(v,u)
for node
v
and user
u
is defined as:

(2)

I(v,u)=\lambda_{s}I_{\text{struct}}(v)+\lambda_{m}I_{\text{sem}}(v)+\lambda_{p}I_{\text{pref}}(v,u).

Structural Specificity.

To penalize global hubs while preserving locally informative nodes, we adopt an inverse degree-based scoring function:

(3)

I_{\text{struct}}(v)=\exp\!\big(-\alpha\log(\deg(v)+\epsilon)\big),

where
\deg(v)
denotes the node degree in the knowledge graph,
\epsilon
is a smoothing term, and
\alpha>0
controls the penalty strength.

Semantic Specificity.

To identify semantically generic concepts that may act as "bridges" across unrelated clusters, we measure the entropy of a node’s neighborhood distribution. We employ
K
-means clustering on the pre-trained node embeddings to obtain
K
semantic clusters. The semantic entropy is computed as:

(4)

H_{\text{sem}}(v)=-\sum_{c=1}^{K}p(c\mid v)\,\log p(c\mid v),

where
p(c\mid v)
represents the empirical distribution of
v
’s neighbors over the clusters. The specificity score is then derived as:

(5)

I_{\text{sem}}(v)=1-\frac{H_{\text{sem}}(v)}{\log K}.

A higher
I_{\text{sem}}(v)
indicates that the node’s neighbors are concentrated in specific semantic domains, rather than spanning diverse, unrelated topics.

Preference-Aware Specificity.

To contextualize specificity within the user’s latent interests, we incorporate a relevance score based on the target-aware intent. Using the node embedding
\mathbf{h}^{(K)}_{v}
and the user intent vector
\mathbf{h}_{u}(i_{t})
(Eq.
1
), we compute:

(6)

I_{\text{pref}}(v,u)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos\big(\mathbf{h}^{(K)}_{v},\mathbf{h}_{u}(i_{t})\big)\right).

This term ensures that the retrieval process prioritizes nodes that are not only specific but also aligned with the user’s current decision-making context.

4.2.3.
Specificity-Aware Path Pruning

Given the target-aware user intent, we score each path
p=i_{h}\xrightarrow{r_{1}}e\xrightarrow{r_{2}}i_{t}
that connects a history item
i_{h}
and the target item
i_{t}
through an intermediate entity
e
. We employ a joint scoring function that integrates global path semantics with aggregated node specificity to mitigate preference inconsistency:

(7)

\displaystyle S(p)

\displaystyle=\cos\big(\mathbf{h}_{u}(i_{t}),\mathbf{h}_{p}\big)\cdot\frac{1}{|V(p)|}\sum_{v\in V(p)}I(v,u),

where
\mathbf{h}_{p}
is the semantic representation of path
p
, obtained by encoding the linearized path sequence via BERT to capture deep semantic interactions. Unlike conventional relevance-based retrieval, this objective explicitly penalizes paths dominated by generic or preference-unsupported nodes, even if they are semantically related to the target item.

4.2.4.
Diversity-Aware Reranking

Even after pruning, a naive top-
K
selection based solely on scores may yield semantically redundant paths (e.g., multiple paths focusing on the same attribute). To ensure comprehensive reasoning that covers complementary aspects—such as director, genre, and style—we employ Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) to balance relevance with diversity. At each step, the next path
p^{*}
is selected via:

(8)

p^{*}=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rem}}}\Big[\gamma\cdot S(p)-(1-\gamma)\cdot\max_{p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sel}}}\cos(\mathbf{h}_{p},\mathbf{h}_{p^{\prime}})\Big],

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rem}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sel}}
denote the sets of candidate and selected paths, respectively. This mechanism explicitly minimizes semantic overlap, ensuring the generated explanations cover diverse reasoning angles.

4.3.
Preference-Aware Explanation Generation

Retrieving high-quality paths is necessary but insufficient for generating convincing explanations. Direct linearization of these paths often discards critical topological signals, causing LLMs to overlook relational dependencies within the graph. To address this, we propose a structure-aware hybrid prompting strategy. We integrate continuous soft prompts—which inject latent structural priors—with discrete hard prompts that serve as factual anchors. This combination ensures that the generated explanations maintain both logical coherence and factual faithfulness.

4.3.1.
Structure-Aware Subgraph Encoding

We encode the explanation subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{\text{exp}}
, composed of the selected reasoning paths, into a dense vector space. To capture global dependencies and long-range interactions beyond local neighborhoods, we employ a Graph Transformer
(
39
)
. To preserve the topological structure and edge semantics, we incorporate relation-aware structural encodings directly into the self-attention mechanism. Let
\mathbf{h}_{v}^{(L)}
denote the representation of node
v
after
L
Transformer layers. We derive the global subgraph representation via a readout operation:

(9)

\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathrm{Pooling}\big(\{\,\mathbf{h}_{v}^{(L)}\mid v\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{exp}}\,\}\big),

which compresses the discrete path information into a structure-aware global embedding.

4.3.2.
Heterogeneous Space Alignment

The graph representation
\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{G}}
resides in a topological graph space, whereas the LLM operates in a sequential textual embedding space. To bridge this gap and enable a hybrid prompting scheme, we employ a lightweight projector to map
\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{G}}
into a sequence of continuous soft prompts:

(10)

\mathbf{P}_{\text{kg}}=\mathrm{Reshape}\big(\mathrm{MLP}_{\text{proj}}(\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{G}})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times D_{\text{llm}}},

where
\mathbf{P}_{\text{kg}}
consists of
N_{p}
virtual tokens aligned with the LLM’s embedding dimension
D_{\text{llm}}
. These soft prompts are prepended to the discrete hard prompts. This design creates a synergy:
\mathbf{P}_{\text{kg}}
efficiently injects latent structural priors that are often lost during text linearization, while the hard prompts provide the explicit factual context necessary for the LLM to generate coherent and readable explanations.

4.3.3.
Instruction Tuning and Explanation Generation

We construct the LLM input sequence by concatenating the structure-aware soft prompts with the embeddings of the system instruction
I_{\text{sys}}
, user history
\mathcal{H}_{\text{text}}
, and target item
t_{\text{item}}
:

(11)

\mathbf{E}_{\text{input}}=\big[\,\mathbf{P}_{\text{kg}},\;\mathbf{E}(I_{\text{sys}}),\;\mathbf{E}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{text}}),\;\mathbf{E}(t_{\text{item}})\,\big].

To ensure modality alignment, we employ a joint optimization objective
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
. Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}
denotes the causal language modeling loss where the LLM is fine-tuned via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
8
)
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
enforces semantic consistency between the graph representation
\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{G}}
and the ground-truth explanation embedding
\mathbf{h}_{Y}
:

(12)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}=1-\cos\big(\mathrm{Proj}(\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{G}}),\mathbf{h}_{Y}\big).

This end-to-end strategy aligns graph-level signals with the LLM’s semantic space, mitigating the gradient vanishing issue for the Graph Transformer while tightly coupling structural reasoning with explanation generation.

Fact-based Hallucination (F-EHR).

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the test set. For each instance
e\in\mathcal{D}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)
be the generated feature set and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{item}}(e)
be the ground-truth attribute set. F-EHR measures the ratio of non-factual features:

(13)

\text{F-EHR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{\big|\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)\setminus\mathcal{F}_{\text{item}}(e)\big|}{\big|\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)\big|}.

Preference-based Hallucination (P-EHR).

P-EHR quantifies the degree of preference misalignment by evaluating features against the estimated user preference proxy
\hat{g}_{u}
. We compute it via a three-step process:

(1)
History Extraction:
We employ Sentires-Guide
(
15
)
to extract phrase-level quadruples (feature, opinion, sentence, score). Features from user history with aggregated positive sentiment scores constitute the explicit preference set
\mathcal{F}_{u}^{\mathrm{hist}+}
.

(2)
Semantic Proxy:
To avoid penalizing valid novelty, we introduce a model-agnostic semantic proxy. We encode each feature
f
into a vector
\phi(f)
using a pretrained Sentence-BERT. For each user
u
, we compute a history intent vector
\mathbf{h}_{u}
by averaging the embeddings of their historical positive features. The cosine similarity between
\phi(f)
and
\mathbf{h}_{u}
serves as the proxy score
\hat{g}_{u}(f)
. A feature is considered valid if it appears in
\mathcal{F}_{u}^{\mathrm{hist}+}
or its proxy score exceeds
\tau=0.4
(fixed based on sensitivity analysis in Section
5.4
).

(3)
Metric Calculation:
We extract the feature set
\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)
from the generated explanation. A feature
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)
incurs a penalty (
\mathcal{C}(f,u)=1
) only if it fails both validity conditions mentioned above. P-EHR reports the average penalty rate:

(14)

\text{P-EHR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)}\mathcal{C}(f,u)}{|\mathcal{F}_{\text{gen}}(e)|}.

We acknowledge that P-EHR is a strict metric. However, short explanations mean that every mentioned attribute serves as a direct justification signal. Even a seemingly peripheral but contradictory feature can undermine user trust, so strictly penalizing any misalignment helps guarantee explanation “safety”. P-EHR is used solely for evaluation and is not involved in model training.

Efficiency Analysis (RQ4)

Figure

Compared with MAPLE, PURE reduces P-EHR by approximately 60% with only a modest 1.2

Compared with G-Refer, PURE more than halves the P-EHR while reducing average latency from 2,030 ms to 1,630 ms.

Theoretically, PURE circumvents the computational bottleneck of real-time graph traversal, which typically scales exponentially as
O(HB^{K})
.
Instead of expensive on-the-fly enumeration, we shift the structural reasoning burden to the offline phase via Structure-Enhanced Semantic Indexing.
By pre-computing multi-hop embeddings using RGAT and storing them in a vector database, PURE decouples complex graph aggregation from online inference.
Consequently, the online process is reduced to efficient vector retrieval, ensuring that explanation quality is enhanced through deep reasoning without incurring prohibitive inference costs.

5.6.
Human Alignment of P-EHR (RQ5)

To validate the robustness of P-EHR, we conducted a blind human evaluation comparing PURE with G-Refer on 450 pairs.
Annotators were presented with a summary of the user’s past reviews and the target item, and were asked to identify which of two anonymized explanations better aligned with user preferences (or indicate a tie).
We recruited 15 graduate students with backgrounds in recommender systems; each pair was independently evaluated by three annotators blinded to model identities, whose majority-vote preferences were compared against pairwise P-EHR scores.
A pair was considered consistent if the model preferred by humans achieved a lower P-EHR score.

6.
Conclusion

This paper investigates preference-inconsistent explanations where generated descriptions are factually correct yet misrepresent the rationale behind a user-item match—a failure mode largely overlooked by existing metrics.
We propose PURE, a preference-aware reasoning framework that integrates multi-hop subgraph construction, intent modeling, and specificity-based pruning to select factually grounded, preference-consistent reasoning paths.
Experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate that PURE reduces both factual hallucination and preference inconsistency while maintaining competitive accuracy and efficiency.
Crucially, PURE reconciles the tension between serendipity and consistency: rather than restricting item novelty, it ensures that even novel recommendations are grounded in familiar preference anchors, thereby preventing “reasoning filter bubbles.”
While extending this paradigm to implicit feedback, multimodal contexts, and dynamic environments remains a key frontier, we position preference alignment as a critical dimension of faithfulness beyond surface-level correctness.
