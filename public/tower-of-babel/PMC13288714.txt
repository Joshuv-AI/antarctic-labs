Health literacy, medication adherence, and quality-of-life scores in Jordanian adults with chronic diseases: a dual analytical study using the long-term conditions questionnaire and the EuroQol five-dimension three-level questionnaire
Authors: Walid Al-Qerem, Anan Jarab, Judith Eberhardt, Salwa Abdo, Lujain Al-Saa’di, Razan Al-Shehadeh, Dana Khasim, Ruba Zumot, Sarah Khalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study examined the association of health literacy with quality-of-life outcomes among Jordanian adults with chronic diseases using two patient-reported outcome measures, the Long-Term Conditions Questionnaire (LTCQ) and the EuroQol five-dimension, three-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L). It also compared conventional regression and machine-learning (ML) approaches for modelling these relationships.Materials and methodsIn this cross-sectional online study, Arabic-language questionnaires were completed by Jordanian adults aged 18 years or older with at least one chronic physical, mental, or neurological condition. The survey, designed to take 10–12 minutes, included the LTCQ, EQ-5D-3L, Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5), and 12-item Health Literacy Scale (HLS-Q12). A total of 901 complete responses were analyzed. Data were split into training (n = 633) and test (n = 268) sets. Multiple linear regression was used for LTCQ, Tobit regression for EQ-5D-3L index scores, and ML models were trained to explore nonlinear associations.ResultsHealth literacy and medication adherence were positively associated with both LTCQ (beta = 3.923 and 2.219, respectively; both p < 0.001) and EQ-5D-3L index scores (beta = 0.025, p = 0.001; beta = 0.020, p = 0.009). Number of medications was inversely associated with both outcomes. In 10-fold cross-validation, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) showed the lowest average root mean square error (RMSE) for LTCQ (9.18; coefficient of determination [R²] = 0.384) and EQ-5D-3L (0.172; R² = 0.193), although differences between the leading models were modest.ConclusionLTCQ and EQ-5D-3L showed overlapping but distinct association patterns. LTCQ was more strongly linked with psychosocial and self-management variables, whereas EQ-5D-3L was more closely aligned with medication burden and clinical comorbidity. These findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal and as differences in captured dimensions rather than evidence of instrument superiority.

Introduction

Chronic diseases, defined as conditions that last for at least 3 to 6 months and can be managed but not cured, are the leading cause of death worldwide [1]. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), cardiovascular diseases account for the highest number of chronic disease-related deaths, followed by cancer, respiratory diseases, and diabetes mellitus [2]. These conditions place a significant burden on patients, their families, and healthcare systems [3]. In Jordan, chronic diseases are the leading cause of both morbidity and mortality, accounting for 78% of all deaths [4].

Because chronic diseases require ongoing medical care and significantly affect patients’ daily lives, it is important to accurately assess their health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Several questionnaires have been developed for this purpose, including the Long-Term Conditions Questionnaire (LTCQ) and the EuroQol five-dimension, three-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L). The LTCQ is a 20-item patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) that has been validated to assess the physical, social, and emotional impact of living with one or more chronic conditions [5]. It helps clinicians evaluate the burden of chronic illness on patients and identify areas where intervention may be needed. The EQ-5D-3L is another widely used and validated tool for assessing HRQoL in individuals with chronic diseases [6]. It provides a general framework for evaluating five key dimensions of health: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression [7].

Although both instruments are widely used, Arabic-language evidence for the LTCQ remains recent, and broader patient-reported outcome measurement evidence in Arabic-speaking populations is still incomplete [8,9]. In Jordan, where the burden of chronic disease is rising [4], no research has yet examined how well these tools reflect the lived experiences of affected individuals.

Medication adherence is similarly relevant because complex treatment regimens and polypharmacy often shape both symptom burden and perceived well-being. Despite the growing importance of these variables, integrated analyses examining how health literacy and adherence relate to quality-of-life (QoL) scores in chronic disease remain limited, particularly in Middle Eastern settings.

Health literacy, defined as “the degree to which individuals can obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions” [10], has emerged as a crucial modifiable risk factor influencing disease outcomes. It plays a mediating role between educational and socioeconomic status and overall health outcomes [11]. Low health literacy has been associated with poor medication adherence, limited disease knowledge, and lower HRQoL across a range of chronic conditions [12,13].

This study aimed to examine and compare the associations of health literacy and other relevant factors with LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L scores in a sample of Jordanian adults with chronic diseases using a dual-analytical approach that combined traditional regression models and machine-learning (ML) algorithms. Regression models were used to estimate linear associations and identify significant predictors of questionnaire scores, whereas ML models were used to explore additional nonlinear patterns that might not be captured by conventional analyses. Together, these approaches were intended to provide complementary insights into how health literacy and other relevant factors relate to LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L scores. Accordingly, the study hypothesised that health literacy would be independently associated with both LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L scores. It was further hypothesised that the magnitude and pattern of these associations would differ between the two instruments, and that ML models would identify nonlinear relationships not fully captured by conventional regression analyses.

Methods

This cross-sectional study used convenience sampling and an online questionnaire distributed between September 2024 and April 2025. Eligible participants were Jordanian adults aged 18 years or older who reported at least one chronic physical, mental, or neurological condition. The questionnaire link, participant information, and consent form were disseminated through community centres, healthcare settings, and Jordanian social media platforms. To minimise missing data, the electronic form was designed so it could not be submitted unless all mandatory items were completed; however, respondents could leave the questionnaire at any time without submitting their responses. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board and the Deanship of Research at Al-Zaytoonah University of Jordan (Reference No.: 2025-2024/07/42). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This cross-sectional study was reported primarily in line with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) principles [14], and relevant Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) [15] reporting items were used to improve the transparency of the predictive modelling procedures.

Study instruments

The questionnaire was administered in Arabic, was designed to take approximately 10-12 minutes to complete, and included four validated scales. The first was the validated Arabic version of the LTCQ [16], which includes 20 Likert-scale items with five response options ranging from 0 (“Never”) to 4 (“Always”). Items 9 to 15 were negatively phrased and reverse-scored. Item scores were summed up for analysis, with higher values indicating a greater ability to “live well” with long-term conditions. The second instrument was the validated Arabic version of EQ-5D-3L [17], a widely used measure of HRQoL in individuals with chronic conditions. It assesses five dimensions of health: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension includes three response levels: no problems, some problems, and extreme problems. The third instrument was the Arabic version of the Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5) [18], which evaluates medication adherence by assessing behaviours such as altering, forgetting, discontinuing, or skipping doses. It consists of five Likert-scale items, with responses ranging from 1 (“Always”) to 5 (“Never”), where higher scores indicate better adherence. The final instrument was the Arabic version of the Health Literacy Scale (HLS-Q12) [19]. This concise, validated tool is the short form of the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire (HLS-EU-Q47) and assesses the ability to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information in daily life. It includes 12 four-point Likert-scale items ranging from “Very difficult” to “Very easy.” Higher scores reflect better health literacy. The scale covers key domains of health literacy, including healthcare, disease prevention, and health promotion, making it useful for guiding targeted interventions to improve patient outcomes and healthcare system performance.

The survey also collected socio-demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, education level, monthly income, marital status, insurance status, employment in the health sector, disease duration, number of medications, number of chronic conditions, and self-reported chronic diseases. Marital status was coded as married versus not married, with the latter category comprising single, divorced, and widowed participants.

Analytical framework and modelling objectives

The study had two linked analytical objectives. First, regression models were used as explanatory models to estimate associations between candidate predictors and the two quality-of-life outcomes. Second, ML models were used as predictive models to examine whether the same predictors captured additional nonlinear patterns in LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L index scores. Accordingly, LTCQ total score and EQ-5D-3L index score were the two primary outcomes. Candidate predictors were HLS-Q12 score, MARS-5 score, and the socio-demographic and clinical variables listed above. The target population was Jordanian adults with chronic diseases completing the Arabic online questionnaire. The intended use of the models was exploratory and comparative within this study to better understand how the two PROM scores related to health literacy and other relevant factors; the models were not developed for immediate individual-level clinical deployment. Internal validation was performed using 10-fold cross-validation within the training set and a held-out test set, whereas no external validation dataset was available.

Sample size and model adequacy

Sample suitability was assessed separately for the regression and ML components using the training sample employed for model estimation (n = 633; 24 predictors). For the LTCQ multiple linear regression, adequacy was evaluated using the overall F-test framework for multiple regression at a two-sided alpha of 0.05. This showed that the available sample provided 80% power to detect an overall effect size of f² = 0.037 (equivalent coefficient of determination [R²] ≈ 0.035) and 90% power to detect f² = 0.045 (equivalent R² ≈ 0.043) (Supplementary Table S1). Because an equivalent Cohen’s f² framework does not apply directly to Tobit regression, adequacy of the EQ-5D-3L Tobit model was assessed by simulation using the fitted model structure, the empirical training-sample predictor matrix, and the fitted error scale. Across 1,000 simulated datasets generated under the fitted Tobit specification and censored at 0 and 1, model fitting was successful in 100% of runs, with empirical power of 0.913 for health literacy, 0.749 for medication adherence, and 0.995 for number of medications (Supplementary Table S2). For the ML analyses, because no standard a priori power framework exists for continuous-outcome prediction models, adequacy was assessed empirically using the training-sample-to-predictor ratio, fixed 10-fold cross-validation, and consistency between cross-validated and held-out test performance of the leading algorithms. The training sample corresponded to 26.4 cases per predictor overall and approximately 569.7 observations in each cross-validation development split. The leading algorithms also showed minimal drift between cross-validated and held-out test performance, with Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) showing root mean square error (RMSE) values of 9.183 versus 9.170 for LTCQ and 0.172 versus 0.171 for EQ-5D-3L index, respectively (Supplementary Table S3).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R. The archived analytic dataset contained 901 complete cases; therefore, no imputation was required. A random 70/30 split created a training dataset (n = 633) and a held-out test dataset (n = 268). Descriptive psychometric indicators were calculated in the 901-participant analytic subset, including Cronbach's alpha and floor/ceiling frequencies for LTCQ, MARS-5, EQ-5D-3L and HLS-Q12.

ML models were fitted separately for LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L index scores. Algorithms included Random Forest, support vector machine (SVM) with radial kernel, k-nearest neighbours (kNN), gradient boosting machine (GBM), XGBoost, decision tree, and a generalised linear model (GLM) benchmark. Hyperparameters were fixed at their selected values, and all seven predictive models were rerun using the same 10-fold cross-validation splits within the training set before evaluation on the same held-out test set (for more details on model settings, see Table S4). To improve transparency, model performance was reported at three stages: in-sample training fit, 10-fold cross-validated performance within the training set, and performance in the held-out test set. Because apparent training performance is typically optimistic, interpretation focused primarily on the cross-validated and test-set results.

To assess whether apparent performance differences between algorithms exceeded fold-to-fold sampling variability, fold-level RMSE values from the fixed 10-fold cross-validation splits were formally compared. An omnibus Friedman test was first applied separately for the LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L index models. When overall differences were present, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare the lowest-RMSE cross-validated model with each competing algorithm, with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. Model performance was summarised using RMSE, mean absolute error (MAE), and R². Calibration plots for the best-performing held-out GBM models are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Additional performance diagnostics for the best-performing held-out models were examined using residual-versus-predicted plots and residual-distribution plots in the held-out test set (Supplementary Figure S2). For the cross-validated best-performing ML model, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were used to summarise predictor importance and the direction of predictor contributions.

For regression models, LTCQ was treated as a continuous outcome and analysed using multiple linear regression. EQ-5D-3L index scores showed strong upper-end concentration and included negative values, which made standard beta regression on the natural scale inappropriate. Therefore, Tobit regression was used to model the bounded distribution while retaining the original utility scale. In the training data, 5 observations (0.8%) were left-censored and none were right-censored. Multicollinearity was examined using variance inflation factors (VIFs); number of chronic conditions was removed from the final regression models because of collinearity with number of medications. As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, post-stratification weights were constructed using available Jordanian population benchmarks for sex and educational attainment to examine whether the main regression findings were robust to sample imbalance. Because the sample was derived from a nonprobability online survey and the benchmark distributions were obtained from the general Jordanian population rather than a chronic-disease-specific sampling frame, the weighted analyses were interpreted cautiously.

Results

A total of 901 complete responses were included in the analysis, with 633 assigned to the training set and 268 to the held-out test set. Table 1 shows that there were no significant differences across demographic characteristics after splitting. Overall, participants had a median age of 42 years (interquartile range [IQR], 29–55), 63.3% were female, 68.5% had a college or university degree, and 52.5% reported a monthly income below 500 Jordanian dinars (JOD). The median number of medications was 2 (IQR 1-4), and 26.7% reported working in the health sector. Military insurance represented 12.4% of the overall sample rather than a dominant majority.

Psychometric indicators derived from the item-level dataset are summarised in Supplementary Table S5. Internal consistency was excellent/good for LTCQ (Cronbach's alpha = 0.875), EQ-5D-3L (Cronbach's alpha = 0.730), MARS-5 (alpha = 0.874), and HLS-Q12 (alpha = 0.889). LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L scores were moderately correlated (Pearson r = 0.389; Spearman rho = 0.378), supporting convergent validity. Floor and ceiling effects were limited for LTCQ (0.1% at the lowest observed score and 1.4% at the highest observed score), MARS-5 (1.9% and 14.8%, respectively), and HLS-Q12 (0.8% and 4.3%, respectively). For the EQ-5D-3L descriptive system, 30.6% of participants reported full health (11111), whereas 0.7% reported the worst health state (33333), indicating a notable ceiling effect but minimal floor effect. VIF values are presented in Supplementary Table S6. Before removing number of chronic conditions, the maximum VIF was 5.59; after its exclusion because of collinearity with number of medications, the maximum VIF decreased to 2.27.

ML models are presented in Table 2. Across the modelling approaches, a clear contrast was observed between apparent in-sample fit and out-of-sample performance. In the development sample, random forest showed the most favourable apparent fit for both LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L index (LTCQ: RMSE = 4.239, MAE = 3.323, R² = 0.909; EQ-5D-3L index: RMSE = 0.132, MAE = 0.097, R² = 0.698). However, this apparent advantage was not maintained after validation. Under 10-fold cross-validation, performance differences narrowed substantially, with XGBoost showing the lowest mean RMSE for both outcomes, although the differences relative to GBM, SVM, and random forest were modest. For LTCQ, XGBoost showed RMSE = 9.18 and R² = 0.384, followed closely by GBM (RMSE = 9.26), SVM (RMSE = 9.26), and random forest (RMSE = 9.27). For EQ-5D-3L index, XGBoost showed RMSE = 0.172 and R² = 0.193, closely followed by GBM (RMSE = 0.173). Omnibus Friedman tests applied to fold-level RMSE values indicated overall differences among the candidate algorithms for both LTCQ (p = 0.00029) and EQ-5D-3L index (p = 0.00187). However, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank comparisons of the best cross-validated model (XGBoost) with competing algorithms showed that most differences were modest after Holm correction. For LTCQ, the only pairwise difference that remained statistically significant was better performance of XGBoost than the decision tree (adjusted p = 0.036). For EQ-5D-3L index, none of the pairwise differences remained statistically significant after Holm correction. On the held-out test set, GBM showed the lowest RMSE for both LTCQ (RMSE = 9.05, MAE = 7.18, R² = 0.422) and EQ-5D-3L index (RMSE = 0.165, MAE = 0.117, R² = 0.311), although in exploratory paired test-set comparisons these differences were not statistically significant relative to XGBoost after Holm correction. Calibration plots and residual diagnostics for the best-performing held-out GBM models are provided in Supplementary Figures S1 & S2. For LTCQ, residuals were centred close to zero and were approximately symmetric, with no strong evidence of systematic bias across the prediction range. For EQ-5D-3L index scores, residuals were also centred near zero overall, but the distribution was left-skewed and the residual-versus-predicted plot suggested some curvature near the upper end of the scale, consistent with the observed ceiling concentration.

SHAP analysis (Figures 1 and 2) showed distinct predictor patterns for the two outcomes. For LTCQ, health literacy (HLS-Q12 score) was the dominant predictor, followed by medication adherence (MARS-5 score), number of medications, income, disease duration, and depression/anxiety. For the EQ-5D-3L index, number of medications was the leading predictor, followed by health literacy (HLS-Q12 score), marital status, medication adherence (MARS-5 score), income, and disease duration. Across both outcomes, higher health literacy and better medication adherence tended to contribute positively, whereas greater medication burden and depression/anxiety tended to contribute negatively.

In the Tobit model for EQ-5D-3L index scores (Table 3), the global Wald statistic indicated that the set of predictors was jointly associated with the outcome (Wald chi-square = 206.5, p < 0.001; scale parameter = 0.1622). Higher health literacy (HLS-Q12 score), medication adherence (MARS-5 score), education level, income, and being married were associated with higher EQ-5D-3L index scores, whereas higher number of medications and several chronic conditions were associated with lower scores. The observed-minus-fitted residuals from the model were centred near zero overall but remained left-skewed, consistent with censoring at the lower bound (Supplementary Table S7).

The overall multiple linear regression model for LTCQ scores was statistically significant (F (24, 608) = 19.30, p < 0.001), explaining 43.2% of the variance in LTCQ scores (adjusted R² = 0.410; residual standard error = 8.96) (Table 4). The model indicated that higher medication adherence (MARS-5 score), higher health literacy (HLS-Q12 score), and higher income were associated with higher LTCQ scores. Higher number of medications was associated with lower LTCQ scores. Osteoarthritis, multiple sclerosis, cancer, bipolar disorder, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and depression/anxiety were also associated with lower LTCQ scores, with depression/anxiety showing the largest negative coefficient among the chronic conditions. Residual diagnostics for the LTCQ linear regression showed residuals centred near zero, no evidence of heteroscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan p = .626), and limited influence from individual observations (Supplementary Table S7).

In an exploratory weighted sensitivity analysis using sex- and education-based post-stratification weights, the overall pattern of the regression findings was largely unchanged. Health literacy remained positively associated with both LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L index scores, and number of medications remained inversely associated with both outcomes. The positive association between medication adherence and LTCQ scores also remained evident. However, the association between medication adherence and EQ-5D-3L index scores was attenuated and was no longer statistically significant after weighting. (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional sample of Jordanian adults with chronic diseases, LTCQ scores were more strongly associated with health literacy and medication adherence, as measured by HLS-Q12 and MARS-5, whereas EQ-5D-3L index scores were more strongly associated with medication burden and selected clinical comorbidities. These results suggest that the two PROMs captured overlapping but distinct dimensions of chronic illness rather than demonstrating that one instrument was universally superior. The LTCQ was designed to assess the broader psychosocial and functional impact of living with chronic conditions [20], and the present results align with that broader orientation. Health literacy and medication adherence remained prominent correlates of LTCQ score, which is consistent with the instrument's emphasis on autonomy, control, and self-management in everyday life [20]. These results should be interpreted as differences in captured dimensions rather than evidence of instrument superiority. Although the impact of health literacy [21–25] and adherence [26–29] on HRQoL is well documented, this study adds evidence that these factors are associated with LTCQ scores in this setting.

By contrast, the predictors of EQ-5D-3L index scores, although overlapping with those for LTCQ, showed a stronger emphasis on physical disease burden, as evidenced by the leading influence of Number of Medications. This finding is consistent with multiple studies that have demonstrated a consistent association between polypharmacy and poorer HRQoL when measured using the EQ-5D instrument. Patients taking five or more medications, particularly those with chronic conditions or multimorbidity, often report lower EQ-5D-3L index scores [30–34]. This negative impact appears even more pronounced in older adults [33] and those managing complex medication regimens [32], underscoring the need for careful medication review in these populations. Although health literacy and adherence remained relevant predictors for EQ-5D-3L index score, their relative influence was more limited compared to that of physical indicators. The EQ-5D-3L, which is inherently focused on physical dimensions such as mobility, self-care, and pain or discomfort, tends to be more responsive to clinical variables that directly affect functional status [35,36].

The relative importance of socioeconomic indicators such as income status and marital status across both models is consistent with findings from previous research [37] These factors likely exert an indirect influence on QoL through mechanisms such as access to healthcare resources, social support, and psychological resilience [38]. However, the specific pathways through which these influences operate may vary, depending on whether the outcome reflects broader life impact (as captured by the LTCQ) or more narrowly defined health states (as measured by the EQ-5D-3L index score).

Interestingly, comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus, while clinically important, ranked lower in importance for both LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L index score outcomes. One possible explanation is that after accounting for functional limitations and patient engagement factors, the presence of specific diseases does not independently account for a substantial portion of the variance in subjective QoL. This interpretation aligns with prior literature, which has suggested that functional capacity, health literacy, and social context often shape the lived experience of illness more significantly than disease count alone. A meta-analysis of 56 clinical trials found that treatment outcomes related to QoL did not significantly differ based on comorbidity count at baseline, pointing instead to the greater influence of factors such as functional abilities and cognitive health [39]. These findings suggest that medical interventions focused solely on reducing disease burden may fall short if they do not also support self-management and health literacy, which are critical for achieving meaningful improvements in patient-centred outcomes.

A notable ceiling effect was observed for the EQ-5D-3L descriptive system, with 30.6% of participants reporting full health (11111). This pattern is consistent with the known tendency of the EQ-5D-3L to concentrate responses at the healthier end of the scale because of its limited three-level response structure [40]. Importantly, the observed ceiling in the current study chronic disease sample was higher than the pooled estimate reported for patient samples in a systematic review of EQ-5D-3L/5L comparisons (approximately 23%), but remained substantially lower than the pooled ceiling reported for general adult population samples (approximately 53%–56%) [41–43]. The dimension-level pattern was also broadly consistent with previous work, with the highest proportion of “no problems” observed in self-care and the lowest in pain/discomfort, suggesting that the instrument may capture physical independence better than more subtle symptom burden. Taken together, these findings suggest that EQ-5D-3L was feasible and informative in this cohort, but its ceiling effect should be considered when interpreting comparatively high health states, and future studies may benefit from evaluating the five-level version in similar populations.

The SHAP rankings and regression results identify several clinically actionable variables. First, routine chronic disease reviews could incorporate a brief health literacy screen and an adherence screen, followed by targeted education or counselling for patients with lower scores. Second, medication count emerged as an important marker in both instruments, supporting regular medication review and deprescribing discussions where appropriate. Third, the two PROMs may serve different purposes in routine care: LTCQ can add psychosocial and self-management context, whereas EQ-5D-3L can provide a concise summary of health-state burden. Used together, they may help teams distinguish between clinical deterioration, treatment burden, and difficulties in navigating care.

The ML findings should be interpreted cautiously. In the rerun with fixed 10-fold cross-validation, XGBoost had the lowest average RMSE for both outcomes, but the margin over GBM, SVM, and Random Forest was small. After Holm correction, the only clear pairwise difference was better LTCQ performance than the decision tree. On the held-out test set, GBM produced the lowest RMSE for both outcomes. Taken together, these results suggest that leading tree-based and kernel-based models were broadly comparable, and no single algorithm should be treated as definitively optimal. The ML models were valuable for ranking predictors and exploring nonlinear associations, whereas the regression models provided interpretable effect estimates.

Strengths and limitations

This study adds evidence from a Middle Eastern setting and used both regression and ML approaches to characterise two widely used PROMs and assess the impact of health literacy on QoL. However, several limitations should shape interpretation. The cross-sectional design does not support causal inference. A key limitation is the use of convenience-based, self-administered online survey, which may have introduced selection bias by favoring participants with internet access, stronger digital skills, and higher educational attainment. However, the likely impact of digital exclusion may have been less pronounced than in settings with lower connectivity, as the 2024 Household Information and Communications Technology (ICT) Usage and Access Survey in Jordan reported home internet access in 96.5% of households and internet use in 95.6% of individuals [44]. In addition, self-administered online questionnaires may increase perceived privacy and reduce interviewer-related pressure when answering sensitive questions, which may support more candid reporting and decrease social desirability bias [45]. The sample nevertheless appeared more educated than the broader Jordanian population, with 68.5% of participants reporting a college/university degree, compared with 27.1% of Jordanians aged 15 years or older reported by the Department of Statistics in 2024 [46]. Which would be much higher in 22 + age group; however, exact values are lacking. Although an exploratory post-stratification sensitivity analysis was conducted, weighting cannot fully correct for selection bias in a nonprobability online sample. In addition, the weighting benchmarks were derived from the general Jordanian population rather than adults with chronic diseases, so the weighted findings should be interpreted as a robustness check rather than as nationally representative estimates. Furthermore, the online study design only archived complete questionnaires, thus, formal participant recruitment flowchart and response rate could not be reconstructed. Although all candidate predictive models were rerun with matched cross-validation and held-out test evaluation, model comparisons remained internal, and the ranking shifted between cross-validation and held-out testing. External validation in an independent Jordanian cohort remains necessary.

Conclusion

Among Jordanian adults with chronic diseases, LTCQ and EQ-5D-3L scores showed different but complementary association patterns. LTCQ scores were more strongly linked with health literacy and medication adherence, whereas EQ-5D-3L scores were more closely linked with medication burden and selected clinical comorbidities. These findings support using careful, context-dependent PROM selection in chronic disease care and suggest that combining instruments may offer a broader view of patient experience. The observed associations should be interpreted cautiously because of the study's cross-sectional design, convenience sampling, and internal-only validation.
