Identifying and designing altermagnetic crystals in real space
Ying Chen, Qiushi Huang, Yu Wu, Xiaolan Yan, Su-Huai Wei

Abstract
Altermagnetism is a compensated magnetic phase characterized by zero net magnetization and exchange-driven spin splitting. However, identifying altermagnets among collinear antiferromagnets usually requires full magnetic-space-group or spin-group analysis, which is not intuitive. Here we formulate a simple real-space criterion based on how the crystallographic operations of the host nonmagnetic structure permute the two opposite-spin sublattices. For simplicity, we focus on collinear compensated antiferromagnets whose magnetic primitive cell coincides with the host nonmagnetic crystallographic primitive cell. In this class, altermagnetic spin splitting is generally allowed unless an inversion-type operation exists that exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices. First-principles calculations on representative noncentrosymmetric and centrosymmetric materials demonstrate this criterion. Similar rules can also be applied to low-dimensional crystals or quasicrystals. Our work reduces the identification of altermagnetism to a transparent real-space symmetry test and provides a practical route for discovering altermagnetic crystals.

Here we formulate such a criterion for collinear fully compensated antiferromagnets by examining how the crystallographic operations of the host nonmagnetic structure (i.e., when the spin character is removed) act on the two opposite-spin sublattice sites. In the absence of SOC, spin is a good quantum number, i.e., the band energies can be labeled as
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)
and
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow)
, where
\mathbf{k}
is the momentum. Because the system is globally antiferromagnetic, the system must have an equal number of crystallographically equivalent spin-up and spin-down sites, forming two spin-sublattices. If the spin-cell is a supercell of the host nonmagnetic cell and the spin-up and spin-down sublattices are connected simply by the host translational vector
T
, then
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow)
, because
R_{T}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}
, i.e., the spin is degenerate for all
\mathbf{k}
vectors
Cheong and Huang 2025
;
Yuan et al. 2020
(see more detailed discussion below). Therefore, in this study, we only focus on systems whose magnetic primitive cell coincides with the nonmagnetic crystallographic primitive cell. For brevity, we refer to them as equal magnetic and nonmagnetic unit cell (EMNUC) below.

Within this class, we show that the generic spin splitting can be classified by inspecting inversion-type operations and their action on the two spin sublattices. If inversion is absent, or if inversion exists but preserves each spin sublattice, exchange-driven spin splitting is symmetry-allowed at generic momenta. If inversion exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices, same-
\mathbf{k}
spin degeneracy is enforced throughout the Brillouin zone. This simple real-space criterion turns the identification of altermagnetism into a transparent symmetry test based on real-space sublattice permutation. We demonstrate it using representative centrosymmetric and noncentrosymmetric EMNUC antiferromagnetic systems and discuss how little-group operations restore spin degeneracy at selected high-symmetry momenta.

Real-space criterion for spin degeneracy
—The criterion is most transparently formulated by tracking how crystallographic operations of the host nonmagnetic structure act on the two opposite-spin sublattices. As stated above, for a collinear antiferromagnet in the absence of SOC, spin remains a good quantum number, allowing the band energies to be labeled as
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)
and
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow)
.

Let
G
be the space group of the nonmagnetic crystallographic structure and let
\mathcal{L}_{\uparrow}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\downarrow}
denote the two sets of magnetic sites carrying opposite collinear moments. Once the antiferromagnetic spin pattern is specified, each operation
g=\{R_{g}|\tau_{g}\}
in
G
can be classified according to its action on the two magnetic sublattices:

G^{+}:\quad g\mathcal{L}_{\uparrow}=\mathcal{L}_{\uparrow},\quad g\mathcal{L}_{\downarrow}=\mathcal{L}_{\downarrow},

G^{-}:\quad g\mathcal{L}_{\uparrow}=\mathcal{L}_{\downarrow},\quad g\mathcal{L}_{\downarrow}=\mathcal{L}_{\uparrow}.

Here
G^{+}
contains operations that preserve each spin sublattice, whereas
G^{-}
contains operations that exchange the two opposite-spin sublattices.

Following Neumann’s principle, for
g
in
G^{+}
, the operation imposes an equivalence within the same spin channel,

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\sigma)=\varepsilon_{n}(R_{g}\mathbf{k},\sigma),\quad\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow.

For
g
in
G^{-}
, the operation maps the spin-up sublattice onto the spin-down sublattice and imposes

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(R_{g}\mathbf{k},\downarrow).

This is the central relation of the present real-space criterion. It shows that, in general, a crystallographic symmetry operation relates opposite-spin states at different momenta. Same-
\mathbf{k}
spin degeneracy is enforced only when there exists a sublattice-exchanging operation
g\in G^{-}
whose point operation maps
\mathbf{k}
back to the same crystal momentum,

R_{g}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K},

where
\mathbf{K}
is a reciprocal lattice vector.

A primitive translation
T
in a magnetic supercell is a special case because a translation
R_{T}
leaves
\mathbf{k}
unchanged. If
T
maps one spin sublattice onto the other, then
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow)
throughout the Brillouin zone. Such systems are ordinary spin-degenerate antiferromagnets. We therefore focus on EMNUC systems, where the magnetic primitive cell coincides with the nonmagnetic crystallographic primitive cell and no primitive translation of the nonmagnetic structure exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices.

For EMNUC systems, inversion-type operations provide the remaining global diagnostic. An inversion-type operation
\widetilde{I}
denotes either pure inversion
I
or a translated inversion
\{I|\tau\}
. Its point part transforms
\mathbf{k}
into
-\mathbf{k}
. If
\widetilde{I}
exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices,

\widetilde{I}\in G^{-},

then the general
G^{-}
relation gives

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(-\mathbf{k},\downarrow).

However, in the absence of SOC, each spin channel is described by a scalar relativistic Hamiltonian with a real periodic potential, so the band energy in a fixed spin channel satisfies

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\sigma)=\varepsilon_{n}(-\mathbf{k},\sigma),\quad\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow.

Combining the two equalities gives

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow)

for all
\mathbf{k}
. Therefore, when an inversion-type operation exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices, same-
\mathbf{k}
spin degeneracy is enforced throughout the Brillouin zone. This case corresponds to an ordinary spin-degenerate antiferromagnet rather than an altermagnet.

This observation leads to a simple real-space classification of EMNUC antiferromagnetic systems according to whether an inversion-type operation exists and how it acts on the two opposite-spin sublattices.

Real-space classification
—According to the above criterion, EMNUC antiferromagnetic systems can be divided into three cases.

Case I: no inversion-type operation
—The first case is that no inversion-type operation exists in the nonmagnetic crystallographic structure. In this noncentrosymmetric case, no global inversion-based operation can enforce same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy throughout the Brillouin zone. Although other sublattice-exchanging operations
g^{-}\in G^{-}
may exist, Eq. (4) shows that they generally map a state at
\mathbf{k}
to an opposite-spin state at a symmetry-transformed momentum
R_{g^{-}}\mathbf{k}
, which is not equivalent to
\mathbf{k}
at a generic momentum. Therefore, same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy is not globally enforced, and exchange-driven spin splitting is symmetry-allowed at generic momenta. This case corresponds to noncentrosymmetric altermagnets. Same-
\mathbf{k}
spin degeneracy can still be restored at special momenta where a sublattice-exchanging operation
g^{-}\in G^{-}
satisfies
R_{g^{-}}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}
, typically at high-symmetry points, lines, or planes, as discussed below in terms of the little-group constraint.

Case II: sublattice-preserving inversion
—The second case is that an inversion-type operation exists but preserves each spin sublattice,

\widetilde{I}\in G^{+}.

In this case, inversion belongs to the same-sublattice operation set defined in Eq. (1). According to Eq. (3), it only imposes an equivalence within the same spin channel,

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\sigma)=\varepsilon_{n}(-\mathbf{k},\sigma),\quad\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow.

Because
\widetilde{I}
does not exchange the two spin sublattices, it does not impose the opposite-spin relation in Eq. (7) and therefore cannot enforce same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy. Thus, same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy is not globally enforced, and exchange-driven spin splitting is symmetry-allowed at generic momenta. This case provides a centrosymmetric route to altermagnetism. As in Case I, spin degeneracy may still be restored at special momenta if a sublattice-exchanging operation
g^{-}\in G^{-}
also satisfies the little-group condition in Eq. (5).

Case III: sublattice-exchanging inversion
—The third case is that an inversion-type operation exists and exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices,

\widetilde{I}\in G^{-}.

This is precisely the situation described by Eqs. (6)–(9). In this case, the inversion-type operation first maps a spin-up state at
\mathbf{k}
to a spin-down state at
-\mathbf{k}
, as expressed in Eq. (7). Together with the fixed-spin-channel equivalence in Eq. (8), this gives the same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy in Eq. (9) for all
\mathbf{k}
. Therefore, same-
\mathbf{k}
spin degeneracy is globally enforced throughout the Brillouin zone, and exchange-driven altermagnetic splitting is forbidden in the absence of SOC. This case corresponds to an ordinary spin-degenerate antiferromagnet rather than an altermagnet.

Little-group constraint
—The classification above determines whether opposite-spin degeneracy is enforced at generic momenta. In the altermagnetic cases, namely Case I and Case II, no global operation enforces such a degeneracy at generic momenta. However, spin splitting does not necessarily survive at every momentum. At high-symmetry points, lines, or planes, the little group of
\mathbf{k}
may contain a spin sublattice-exchanging operation
g^{\prime}\in G_{\mathbf{k}}
, and satisfies the following condition:

g^{\prime}\in G^{-}\text{ and }G_{\mathbf{k}}=\{g^{\prime}\in G\,|\,R_{g^{\prime}}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}\}.

According to Eq. (4), this operation imposes

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(R_{g^{\prime}}\mathbf{k},\downarrow).

Because
g^{\prime}
also belongs to the little group of
\mathbf{k}
, it satisfies
R_{g^{\prime}}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{K}
. Thus, the above relation reduces to the same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy,

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow).

Therefore, even when altermagnetic spin splitting is allowed at generic momenta in Case I and Case II, the degeneracy can be locally restored at special momenta if the little group contains a spin sublattice-exchanging operation.

In contrast, if Eq. (10) is not satisfied, namely
G_{\mathbf{k}}\cap G^{-}=\varnothing
, then no little-group operation at
\mathbf{k}
exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices. All little-group operations preserve each spin sublattice and impose only same-spin equivalences. Therefore, they cannot enforce

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\downarrow),

and same-
\mathbf{k}
spin splitting remains symmetry-allowed at this momentum.

A sublattice-exchanging operation outside the little group may still relate the two spin channels through Eq. (4). In that case, for
g^{\prime\prime}\in G^{-}
but
g^{\prime\prime}\notin G_{\mathbf{k}}
, one obtains

\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_{n}(R_{g^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{k},\downarrow),

where
R_{g^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{k}
is not equivalent to
\mathbf{k}
. The opposite-spin counterpart of a state at
\mathbf{k}
therefore appears at a symmetry-transformed momentum rather than at the same
\mathbf{k}
. This momentum-separated opposite-spin pairing is the characteristic band-structure feature of altermagnets.

Conclusion
—We have established a real-space symmetry criterion for altermagnetism in EMNUC collinear compensated antiferromagnets following Neumann’s principle,
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k},\sigma)=\varepsilon_{n}(R_{g}\mathbf{k},g\sigma)
. Since the primitive spin-translation operation is absent in this class, global same-
\mathbf{k}
opposite-spin degeneracy is normally absent unless an inversion-type operation exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices. In this case, the combined spin-inversion operation restores the equivalence between the two spin channels and forbids altermagnetic splitting in the absence of SOC. At high-symmetry momenta, the little group further determines whether the splitting survives locally. Using MnS, Mn
2
SSe, Fe
2
B, and CrSb as examples, we show both noncentrosymmetric and centrosymmetric routes to altermagnetism. Our analysis clarifies that altermagnetic spin splitting is governed by real-space sublattice permutation rather than by centrosymmetry alone, providing a simple physically transparent and practically useful symmetry principle for identifying, classifying, and designing altermagnetic crystals. Such a criterion can be easily extended to other systems such as low-dimensional materials and quasicrystals.
