1.0 Summary
 Aerosol is the largest individual source of uncertainty in assessing the Earth’s radiative balance due to
 poorly understood aerosol processes and aerosol-cloud interactions. Thus, to constrain aerosol’s climate
 impacts, it is critical to understand aerosol compositions at high elevations. Organic material accounts for
 about 20-80% of total aerosol mass in the atmosphere and is known to play a key role in aerosol processes
 and cloud formation. In prior research, characterization of aerosol molecular composition has been mostly
 carried out at ground level. In these ground-level measurements, total speciated organosulfates and
 organonitrates may contribute significantly to organic aerosol mass on the order of 10-40%, most of
 which are from abundant biogenic volatile organic compounds such as isoprene and monoterpenes.
 Organosulfates and organonitrates have also been shown to be present in substantial amounts in cloud
 water, suggesting that they can be transported and/or directly formed at high elevations. On the other
 hand, the less common and more expensive aircraft studies have performed high-altitude measurements,
 but were often limited in the number of different altitudes sampled and in sample collection under
 consistent conditions. Therefore, the vertical distributions of key aerosol components such as
 organosulfates and organonitrates are not well understood and have not been extensively studied.
 Nevertheless, it is important to understand how organosulfates and organonitrates in aerosols are
 distributed, transformed, participate in aqueous-phase processes, and interact with high-altitude clouds.

 In this project, we used the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Atmospheric Radiation Measurement
 (ARM) User Facility’s tethered balloon systems (TBS) to collect vertically resolved (up to ~ 800 m above
 ground) aerosol samples at DOE’s Southern Great Plains (SGP) ARM observatory during flights in the
 winter, summer, and fall of 2024. Complementary aerosol samples were also collected at the ground level
 during the same periods for comparison. The primary goal of this project is to study vertically resolved
 aerosol composition with a focus on organosulfates and organonitrates. We collaborated with the
 Environmental Molecular Sciences Laboratory (EMSL) at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory
 (PNNL) through a Facilities Integrating Collaborations for User Science (FICUS) award to perform detail
 chemical characterization of the aerosol samples using several complementary state-of-the-art mass
 spectrometry techniques. Through these analyses, we accurately determined the comprehensive molecular
 formulas of over 7,000 unique organic species in the collected aerosols and identified the structure or
 source of major species.

 Our analysis suggested that ~11.3% of the organic species are sulfur-containing compounds. Through ion
 mobility mass spectrometry and tandem mass spectrometry, we found that the major sulfur-containing
 species (CHOS) are organosulfates, dominated by isoprene-derived organosulfates during summertime,
 but monoterpene-derived organosulfates overall. For the isoprene-derived organosulfates, the vertical
 distribution suggests that they are more abundant at ground level and at high altitude, but less abundant in
 between. This implies their substantial presence in clouds. The majority of the observed organosulfates
 correlate strongly, indicating identical formation mechanisms. On the other hand, nitrogen-containing
 organic compounds (CHNO) contribute ~ 35.6% of total organics, but they do not correlate strongly
 within their group, indicating different formation mechanisms and sources. The majority of CHNO
 compounds appear to be nitroaromatics and are more abundant during winter, corresponding to wildfire
 impacts. Compounds containing both nitrogen and sulfur (CHNOS) were also observed, but more
 prominently at ground level (~6.1%). This may suggest unique formation mechanisms associated with
 ground-level processes. But it could also be due to the relatively lower aerosol mass aloft and CHNOS
 aloft may be present below detection limit.

 The major limitation of this project is the limited number and times of samples collected aloft and the
 diverse environmental conditions during collections. This led to ambiguity in understanding the molecular
 vertical trends. However, more comprehensive statistical analyses are underway to better understand the
 variations in aerosol compositions and their relationships with environmental conditions.

2.0 Results
 All the collected aerosol samples from this project (n=157, including 52 aerial and 105 ground samples)
 have been analyzed by the nanospray desorption electrospray ionization coupled to high-resolution mass
 spectrometry (nano-DESI-HRMS) available at the EMSL facility at PNNL. Selected samples have also
 been analyzed by an ion mobility spectrometry time-of-flight mass spectrometer (IMSMS) and
 quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOFMS) with tandem mass spectrometry at the
 University of California, Riverside. All these analyses were performed in the negative ion mode.

 These complementary analyses allowed us to: (1) accurately determine the comprehensive molecular
 formulas of over 7,000 unique organic species in the aerosols; and (2) identify the structure or source of
 major species. These identified molecular formulas are categorized by their elemental composition,
 including CHO, CHNO, CHOS, and CHNOS species. Our analysis focuses on molecular formulas that
 are present in over 50% of samples (~ 2,600 species). Figure 1 maps the comprehensive aerosol molecular
 composition in a van Krevelen diagram, which is colored by different types of compounds, including
 lipid-like compounds, aliphatic/peptides, carboxyl-rich alicyclic molecules (CRAM), carbohydrates,
 aromatics, and highly oxidized compounds (HOC). Several statistical analyses were performed for this
 complex data set.

 Figure 1. Van Krevelen diagram of SGP aerosol species, grouped by the types of compounds.

 For the ground samples, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the elemental composition (nC, O/C,
 H/C, N/C, S/C, DBE/C) revealed strong compositional patterns, with PC1 (39.8% variance) separating
 oxidized, hydrated compounds (positive loadings: H/C, S/C, O/C) from aromatic, condensed species

(negative loadings: DBE/C, nC), and PC2 (32.4% variance) distinguishing aliphatic hydrocarbons
(positive H/C, nC) from nitrogen-containing aromatics (negative DBE/C, N/C). ANOVA on PC scores
identified significant seasonal differences in PC2 (F=4.10, p=0.019) and PC4 (F=4.61, p=0.012),
indicating systematic seasonal variation in aerosol composition. PERMANOVA analysis on sample-level
PC scores demonstrated highly significant seasonal compositional differences overall (F=28.49,
p<0.0001, R²=0.358, indicating season explains 35.8% of compositional variance), with all pairwise
seasonal comparisons showing strong significance: Winter versus Summer (F=47.24, p<0.0001,
R²=0.355), Winter versus Fall (F=4.40, p=0.0082, R²=0.063), and Summer versus Fall (F=29.46,
p<0.0001, R²=0.362).

Analysis of 52 aerial samples collected at altitudes ranging from approximately 100 to 900 meters
revealed systematic vertical stratification of atmospheric chemistry. Mann-Whitney U tests comparing
within-boundary-layer height (BLH) and above-BLH samples revealed significant differences in CHNO
compound abundance (p=0.034), with trends toward higher CHO and CHOS abundances within the
boundary layer though not reaching statistical significance. Comprehensive vertical profiles were
constructed for the top 20 most abundant formulas and for each chemical group separately, with
correlation analyses examining relationships between chemical composition and meteorological
parameters including altitude, temperature, relative humidity, particle number concentration (measured by
condensation particle counter), BLH, and particle size distributions from aerodynamic particle sizer and
scanning mobility particle sizer instruments. These analyses revealed that chemical group abundances
show variable correlations with altitude and BLH position, indicating that vertical mixing and
atmospheric processing affect different compound classes through distinct mechanisms.

To minimize temporal artifacts in ground-aloft comparisons, which is a critical challenge when
simultaneous sampling is impossible, a matched-pairs analysis framework was developed based on
temporal overlap criteria containing 52 aloft-ground pairs with temporal overlaps ranging from 0.25 to
6 hours. Three progressively rigorous sample-matching strategies were implemented for sensitivity
analysis: (1) all samples approach providing maximum statistical power (105 ground versus 52 aloft),
(2) matched-day samples controlling for daily meteorological variation, and (3) overlap-time samples
with approximately 4-hour collection periods providing the most stringent temporal matching. The
analysis implements multiple compositional divergence metrics including Jaccard similarity (measuring
compositional overlap), Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (quantifying abundance-weighted compositional
differences), PCA distance (Euclidean distance in principal component space), and diversity metrics
(Shannon entropy and species richness comparisons). A critical finding from this analysis is that higher
sample similarities are present within the ground samples and likewise within the aerial samples, but less
similarity is found between ground and aerial samples, highlighting unique processes for aerosols aloft
(Figure 2).

 The analysis carried out so far establishes several fundamental insights about atmospheric aerosol
 chemistry at the SGP site. First, ground-level aerosol composition exhibits strong, statistically robust
 seasonal variations that exceed variability within seasons, with season explaining 35.8% of overall
 compositional variance, which is a substantial effect size indicating that seasonal atmospheric processes
 fundamentally alter aerosol molecular composition through changes in sources, transport patterns,
 photochemical processing, and meteorological conditions. Second, vertical atmospheric chemistry differs
 systematically between ground and aerial samples, with altitude-dependent patterns particularly evident
 for nitrogen-containing compounds that show significant depletion above the boundary layer, suggesting
 either preferential surface sources for these species or enhanced removal processes aloft. Third, the
 combination of high-abundance formulas (which drive bulk compositional patterns) with high-PCA
 loading formulas (which serve as sensitive atmospheric tracers showing maximum variance across
 samples) reveals that compositional similarities in abundant species can mask important chemical
 differences in specific discriminating compounds. This finding has profound implications for atmospheric
 chemistry studies, as it demonstrates that relying solely on the most abundant compounds may overlook
 critical tracers of atmospheric processes, source attribution, or transformation pathways. The analytical
 framework combining PCA dimensionality reduction, PERMANOVA multivariate testing, traditional
 chemical metrics (O/C, H/C, OSc), and comprehensive meteorological correlations provides a robust
 foundation for understanding complex atmospheric aerosol chemistry patterns while properly controlling
 for temporal variability and instrumental biases, representing a significant methodological advancement
 for field campaign data interpretation where logistical constraints prevent simultaneous ground and aerial
 sampling.
