Pulmonary Tumor Thrombotic Microangiopathy Diagnosed by Right Heart Catheterization in a Patient With Breast Cancer: A Case Report
Authors: Shonosuke Tajima, Yugo Nakata, Kazuhiro Sanda, Yoshihiko Koshimo, Michio Shigematsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy (PTTM) is a severe complication of malignancy, characterized by rapidly progressive pulmonary hypertension and a dismal prognosis. This report describes the case of a 71-year-old woman with a history of estrogen receptor (ER)-positive left breast cancer who developed PTTM. She presented with exertional dyspnea and a cough. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed hypoxemia with an elevated alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient. Chest computed tomography (CT) showed diffuse ground-glass and nodular opacities without obvious pulmonary embolism (PE). Echocardiography revealed right ventricular enlargement and moderate pulmonary hypertension. Right heart catheterization confirmed severe pulmonary hypertension with a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 46 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance of 844 dynes/sec/cm⁵. After transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) failed to yield a diagnosis, PTTM was confirmed by detecting malignant cells via cytology of blood aspirated from the pulmonary artery during catheterization. The administration of systemic chemotherapy resulted in a marked improvement in hemodynamics, radiological findings, and symptoms, allowing her to be weaned from home oxygen therapy.

Introduction

Pulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy (PTTM) is a fatal disease characterized by rapidly progressive pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [1]. It is caused by the hematogenous dissemination of malignant tumor cells into the pulmonary microvasculature, leading to microthrombus formation and fibrocellular intimal proliferation [1,2].

Epidemiologically, it is often associated with mucin-producing adenocarcinomas, such as gastric cancer, but reports of PTTM associated with breast cancer are also scattered. Although the exact prevalence remains largely unclear, it has been reported to occur in approximately 1.4% to 3.3% of autopsy cases of malignant tumors. Clinically and radiologically, PTTM can mimic chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) or macroscopic pulmonary tumor embolism; however, PTTM is distinct in its microscopic pathophysiology, which is characterized by severe fibrocellular vascular remodeling rather than large-vessel mechanical occlusion. Most cases are diagnosed postmortem (approximately 80%), and reports of patients surviving long enough to receive targeted treatment are exceedingly rare [2,3]. Premortem diagnosis is challenging, often requiring highly invasive surgical lung biopsies in hemodynamically unstable patients [4]. To overcome this clinical dilemma, a diagnostic algorithm utilizing right heart catheterization and pulmonary artery blood sampling has been proposed as a safer and less invasive alternative. Here, a rare case of PTTM associated with breast cancer recurrence is reported, which was successfully diagnosed premortem via pulmonary artery blood cytology during right heart catheterization. The patient demonstrated remarkable hemodynamic and symptomatic improvement following the introduction of systemic chemotherapy.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old woman presented to the hospital with a chief complaint of exertional dyspnea and persistent cough. Seven years prior, she had been diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma (scirrhous type) of the left breast. Immunohistochemistry of the primary tumor showed it was strongly positive for estrogen receptor (ER, 100%) and positive for progesterone receptor (PgR, 60%), but negative for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). She underwent a partial left mastectomy followed by adjuvant chemotherapy with docetaxel and cyclophosphamide, local radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy with letrozole. Despite these treatments, she developed multiple bone metastases three years prior to the current admission, leading to multiple changes in her treatment regimen. Approximately one month prior to her current admission, a routine follow-up computed tomography (CT) scan revealed the further progression of her multiple bone metastases, and her disease was judged to be refractory to the ongoing endocrine therapy. Concurrently with this documented oncological exacerbation, she began experiencing a new onset of dry cough and progressively worsening exertional dyspnea, which eventually prompted her hospital admission. Because the recurrence was initially exclusively bone-related, a re-biopsy to reassess eligibility for targeted therapies was not performed, and subsequent treatment decisions continued to rely on the receptor status of the primary tumor. Her metastatic disease was sequentially managed with multiple lines of endocrine therapy, including fulvestrant, exemestane, and tamoxifen, in response to disease progression.

Upon admission, her percutaneous oxygen saturation was 96% on 2 L/min of supplemental oxygen. Auscultation revealed fine crackles in the bilateral lower lung fields, and a prominent pulmonary component of the second heart sound (IIp) was noted. Arterial blood gas analysis on room air showed hypoxemia (PaO2 55.1 mmHg) with an elevated alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient (Aa-DO2 44 mmHg).

Blood tests revealed a mildly elevated brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level (135.5 pg/mL). Contrast-enhanced chest CT revealed diffuse ground-glass and nodular opacities in both lungs (Figure 1).

Based on these clinical and radiological findings, several differential diagnoses were considered for her rapidly progressive pulmonary hypertension and respiratory failure. These primarily included drug-induced interstitial pneumonia, pulmonary infections, and macroscopic thromboembolic diseases such as pulmonary embolism (PE) and CTEPH.

To systematically rule out these possibilities, comprehensive investigations were performed. First, blood tests showed a normal D-dimer level (0.66 μg/mL), and both the contrast-enhanced CT and lower extremity ultrasonography revealed no evidence of macroscopic pulmonary artery filling defects (Figure 2) or deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) at diagnosis.

The mediastinal window shows no filling defects in the pulmonary artery, indicating the absence of a macroscopic thromboembolic event.

These findings effectively ruled out PE and CTEPH, strongly supporting our argument that the severe pulmonary hypertension arose from a microvascular tumor thrombotic disease rather than a typical large-vessel thromboembolism. Second, a normal Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) level (377 U/mL) clinically argued against progressive interstitial lung disease as the main driver of her respiratory failure. Finally, to definitively rule out pulmonary infections and further investigate the ground-glass opacities, a bronchoscopy was performed. A transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) from the right upper lobe and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) from the left lower lobe showed no evidence of infection or malignant cells.

At this point, it was concluded that the patient's severe pulmonary hypertension could not be explained by interstitial or alveolar diseases alone. Ventilation scintigraphy shows preserved ventilation with no obvious defects (Figure 3). Perfusion scintigraphy demonstrates heterogeneous tracer accumulation with multiple peripheral perfusion defects (Figure 4). This mismatched pattern (preserved ventilation and heterogeneous peripheral perfusion defects) fits the differential diagnosis by supporting an underlying microvascular disease such as PTTM while arguing against typical macroscopic pulmonary thromboembolism.

Echocardiography revealed right ventricular enlargement, right ventricular systolic dysfunction, and left ventricular compression, with a baseline tricuspid regurgitation peak gradient (TR-PG) of 53 mmHg (normal value: < 28 mmHg).

After systematically ruling out common etiologies, a pulmonary vascular disease, specifically PTTM, was strongly suspected. Given the non-diagnostic bronchoscopy and the patient's deteriorating hemodynamics, the case was discussed by a multidisciplinary team comprising pulmonologists, oncologists, and cardiologists. A conventional surgical lung biopsy was deemed too high-risk for this hemodynamically unstable patient. Therefore, a multidisciplinary decision was made to proceed with a right heart catheterization. The procedure was primarily intended for a precise hemodynamic assessment of severe pulmonary hypertension, with pulmonary artery blood cytology added as an adjunct diagnostic tool for the suspected PTTM. It demonstrated markedly elevated pulmonary artery pressures of 77/26 (mean 46) mmHg and an increased pulmonary vascular resistance of 844 dynes/sec/cm⁵. Cytological examination of the blood aspirated from the proximal pulmonary artery using Papanicolaou staining revealed clusters of atypical cells, which were positive for ER on immunostaining (Figures 5, 6). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of PTTM secondary to breast cancer.

Small clusters exhibiting cell pairing (cell-in-cell arrangements) are also observed, suggesting malignant cells of adenocarcinoma origin.

Initially, an endothelin receptor antagonist (macitentan) was administered orally at a dose of 5 mg once daily, but it showed no efficacy (with the TR-PG worsening to 64 mmHg) and was therefore discontinued after seven days. Following the definitive diagnosis, because her ER-positive, HER2-negative tumor had become refractory to multiple lines of endocrine therapy, a transition to cytotoxic chemotherapy was indicated. Systemic chemotherapy comprising paclitaxel (130 mg) and bevacizumab (640 mg) administered intravenously was initiated. This regimen was selected primarily as the standard oncologic treatment strategy for her progressive metastatic disease. Furthermore, bevacizumab was considered particularly advantageous for the concurrent management of PTTM, as its anti-angiogenic properties theoretically target the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-driven fibrocellular intimal proliferation characteristic of this condition. The treatment led to a dramatic decrease in TR-PG, which dropped to 43 mmHg after approximately one month of treatment and eventually to 32 mmHg at four months. Chest CT also showed the shrinkage and disappearance of the diffuse nodular and ground-glass opacities. The patient's respiratory symptoms gradually resolved, allowing her to be successfully weaned from home oxygen therapy four months after the initiation of the chemotherapy regimen. Six months after the initiation of treatment, the regimen was switched to bevacizumab monotherapy, which led to a worsening of pulmonary hypertension (TR-PG elevation). However, the reintroduction of paclitaxel in combination with bevacizumab resulted in a renewed reduction in TR-PG, confirming the chemotherapy's direct role in controlling the hemodynamics of PTTM.

Following the reintroduction of the combination therapy, the patient was closely followed by a multidisciplinary cardio-oncology team comprising pulmonologists, medical oncologists, and cardiologists. Her PTTM was serially monitored through transthoracic echocardiography performed approximately every one to two months, which confirmed sustained improvements in TR-PG, right ventricular enlargement, and systolic function. Oncologically, the patient achieved sustained disease stability with the paclitaxel and bevacizumab regimen. This effective systemic control of her advanced metastatic breast cancer was the key reason she maintained such a relatively long progression-free survival. She was able to continue the chemotherapy for 13 months after the initial diagnosis of PTTM. However, progressive disease (PD) eventually ensued, and the patient developed jaundice due to liver metastases and pancreatic head lymph node metastases. She died of hepatic failure 15 months after the diagnosis.

Discussion

This case highlights the utility of pulmonary artery blood cytology during right heart catheterization for the premortem diagnosis of PTTM, and demonstrates that aggressive control of the underlying malignancy is directly linked to improving the hemodynamics of PTTM.

Unlike macroscopic pulmonary thromboembolism, PTTM is a severe complication in which malignant tumor cells proliferate within the pulmonary microvasculature, inducing microthrombus formation and fibrocellular intimal proliferation (primarily involving myofibroblasts and endothelial cells), which leads to rapidly progressive pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure (acute cor pulmonale) [1]. It is clinically important to distinguish PTTM from a macroscopic tumor embolism. While a conventional tumor embolism is caused by large tumor fragments physically occluding the main or proximal pulmonary arteries, PTTM is a microscopic process. In PTTM, tumor cells disseminate into the pulmonary arterioles and capillaries, where they not only cause obstruction but also induce local activation of the coagulation cascade and subsequent fibrocellular intimal proliferation. This unique vascular remodeling, rather than simple mechanical occlusion, is considered the primary driver of the severe increase in pulmonary vascular resistance.

In the present case, rapid respiratory failure progressed alongside the exacerbation of multiple bone metastases, suggesting that the deterioration of the underlying disease led to the hematogenous dissemination of tumor cells to the pulmonary microvasculature, thereby forming this pathology. Although a surgical lung biopsy is often required for a definitive diagnosis, performing a lung biopsy under general anesthesia in patients with hypoxemia accompanied by severe pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure is extremely high-risk and often practically difficult [2,5]. In this case, marked pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary artery pressure of 77/26 mmHg) was observed. This severity was disproportionate to the suspected interstitial pneumonia noted on the initial CT, leading to the strong suspicion of PTTM after ruling out obvious PE and DVT on imaging. By demonstrating malignant cells through pulmonary artery blood aspiration cytology during right heart catheterization, which is less invasive compared to a lung biopsy, we were able to obtain a definitive diagnosis safely and rapidly [6,7]. This approach is considered a useful method for obtaining a definitive diagnosis of PTTM while avoiding highly invasive lung biopsies. Indeed, a diagnostic algorithm for PTTM utilizing right heart catheterization and pulmonary artery blood sampling has already been proposed [2]. The present case successfully demonstrates the clinical utility of this established algorithm, leading to early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

The insights gained regarding the causal relationship from the treatment course are also crucial. In this case, pulmonary vasodilator therapy (macitentan) was initially introduced for pulmonary hypertension but proved ineffective [4,8]. This lack of efficacy can be explained by the specific pathophysiology of PTTM. Unlike typical pulmonary arterial hypertension, where functional vasoconstriction plays a major role, the elevated pulmonary vascular resistance in PTTM is primarily driven by organic and mechanical obstruction caused by microthrombi and severe fibrocellular intimal proliferation. Therefore, merely inducing vasodilation is insufficient to overcome this profound structural narrowing. Conversely, the initiation of systemic chemotherapy for the primary tumor resulted in a reduction in TR-PG and a clear improvement in symptoms [9,10]. Furthermore, the subsequent worsening of pulmonary hypertension upon switching to monotherapy, followed by re-improvement upon re-adding the combination regimen, clinically demonstrates that controlling the primary tumor itself is directly linked to reversing or halting the hemodynamic deterioration of PTTM [11,12].

In future clinical practice, PTTM should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cancer patients presenting with unexplained, rapidly progressive pulmonary hypertension. Furthermore, the utility of pulmonary artery blood cytology using right heart catheterization, as demonstrated in this case, should be further evaluated.

Conclusions

We successfully diagnosed PTTM secondary to recurrent breast cancer using relatively low-risk pulmonary artery blood cytology during right heart catheterization, overcoming the limitations of conventional transbronchial biopsies. Systemic chemotherapy yielded an improvement in hemodynamics and survival. In cancer patients presenting with sudden, unexplained pulmonary hypertension, this diagnostic approach may be a useful option for definitive diagnosis and early therapeutic intervention.
