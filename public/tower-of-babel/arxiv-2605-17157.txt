Gaps of Binary Numerical Semigroups and of Binary Inclusion-Exclusion Polynomials
Gennady Bachman

Abstract
. Let p p be a given modulus, let u u be prime to p p , and consider the linear permutation u ⋅ n ( mod p ) u\cdot n\pmod{p} of the residue system modulo p p . Writing ⟨ x ⟩ p \langle x\rangle_{p} to denote the least nonnegative residue of x x modulo p p , we say that a pair of integers ( a , b ) (a,b) is a dominant pair of this permutation if either the inequality max ⁡ ( ⟨ u ​ a ⟩ p , ⟨ u ​ b ⟩ p ) < min a < n < b ⁡ ⟨ u ​ n ⟩ p \max(\langle ua\rangle_{p},\langle ub\rangle_{p})<\min_{a<n<b}\langle un\rangle_{p} , or the inequality min ⁡ ( ⟨ u ​ a ⟩ p , ⟨ u ​ b ⟩ p ) > max a < n < b ⁡ ⟨ u ​ n ⟩ p \min(\langle ua\rangle_{p},\langle ub\rangle_{p})>\max_{a<n<b}\langle un\rangle_{p} hold. The main technical part of this work gives analysis of this property of linear permutations of residue systems. We then apply this analysis to the problems that motivated it, and give (i) complete description of the gapsets of binary inclusion-exclusion polynomials Q { p , q } Q_{\{p,q\}} (which include binary cyclotomic polynomials Φ p ​ q \Phi_{pq} as its principal special case), and (ii) complete description of all possible distances between consecutive elements of a numerical semigroup ⟨ p , q ⟩ \langle p,q\rangle .

1.
Introduction

In their recent work
[
5
]
, Hong, et al. introduced a new and interesting puzzle about the behavior of coefficients of cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{pq}(x)
, where
p<q
are distinct odd primes. To state their problem, we need to introduce the notion of the
gapset
of a polynomial. Given a polynomial
f(x)
of degree
d
, write it down omitting its zero coefficients
f(x)=c_{1}x^{e_{1}}+c_{2}x^{e_{2}}+\dots+c_{k}x^{e_{k}}
, where
0\leq e_{1}<e_{2}<\dots<e_{k}=d
and
c_{i}\neq 0
. Then, if
k>1
, the gapset of
f
is given by
G(f)\coloneq\{e_{i+1}-e_{i}\}
, and the individual elements are the
gaps
of
f
. Motivated by a certain application, Hong, et al, were particularly interested in the largest gap of
\Phi_{pq}
, denoted by
g_{1}(\Phi_{pq})
. They showed that
g_{1}(\Phi_{pq})=p-1
, and in the process initiated the problem of studying the gaps of
\Phi_{pq}
.

This was followed by several new contributions investigating this and related problems. Below we describe those among them that are most relevant to the objectives of this paper. For a more comprehensive discussion and references to these contributions we refer the reader to the papers
[
1
]
and
[
8
]
. New proofs of the
g_{1}(\Phi_{pq})=p-1
result were given in
[
6
,
9
,
4
]
. Zhang
[
9
]
showed that the gap
p-1
occurs exactly
\lfloor q/p\rfloor
times. The simplest special case of the gaps problem for
\Phi_{pq}
occurs when
q\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
. The analysis of this case was given, among other results, by Camburu, et al, in
[
4
]
. They showed that the relation
q\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
is equivalent to the evaluation
G(\Phi_{pq})=\{1,2,\dots,p-1\}
. In addition to the largest gap, we now also know the second largest gap of
\Phi_{pq}
, denoted by
g_{2}(\Phi_{pq})
. This result is due to Cafure and Cesaratto
[
3
]
and it says that
g_{2}(\Phi_{pq})=\max(r-1,p-r-1)
, where
r
is the remainder of
q
on division by
p
. Finally, we mention what is known about the gaps of general cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{n}
. It is clear the the situation here is much more challenging than in the binary case we have been discussing above. Nevertheless, Al-Kateeb, et al,
[
1
]
were able to determine the largest gap of
\Phi_{n}
for
n
satisfying certain constraints. First, they note that if
n_{0}=\prod_{p\mid n,p\geq 3}p
is an “odd squarefree kernel” of
n
, then
g_{1}(\Phi_{n})=\frac{n}{n_{0}}g_{1}(\Phi_{n_{0}})
. (Note that this applies to our case
n_{0}=pq
.) So the problem reduces to the study of gaps of
\Phi_{n}
for squarefree odd integers
n
. For such
n
, they showed that if the largest prime factor
q
of
n
satisfies
q>\sqrt{n}
, then
g_{1}(\Phi_{n})=\varphi(n/q)
, where
\varphi
is the Euler’s totient function.

Henceforth we shall assume that integers
q>p\geq 3
are coprime, but not necessarily prime numbers. We will work in the more general setting of binary inclusion-exclusion polynomials which are defined as quotients

(1)

Q_{\{p,q\}}(x)\coloneq\frac{(1-x^{pq})(1-x)}{(1-x^{p})(1-x^{q})}.

It is easy to verify (see
[
2
]
for an introduction to inclusion-exclusion polynomials) that
Q_{\{p,q\}}(x)
reduces to a polynomial of degree
\varphi(p,q)\coloneq(p-1)(q-1)
, and we write

Q_{\{p,q\}}(x)=\sum_{0\leq m\leq\varphi(p,q)}a_{m}x^{m}\qquad[a_{m}=a_{m}(p,q)].

When the pair
\{p,q\}
is a pair of prime numbers, polynomial
Q_{\{p,q\}}
is better known as the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{pq}
. In particular, our gap results for this larger class of polynomials apply to the subclass of cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{pq}
.

From the definition (
1
) and the fact that the degree of
Q_{\{p,q\}}
is
\varphi(p,q)
, it follows that

(2)

Q_{\{p,q\}}(x)\equiv(1-x)\sum_{i,j\geq 0}x^{iq+jp}\pmod{x^{\varphi(p,q)+1}}.

Two things are now clear. First, is that each coefficient
a_{m}
takes on one of three possible values, 0 or
\pm 1
. Second, is that it is all about integers representable as linear combinations of
p
and
q
with nonnegative integer coefficients,

(3)

\langle p,q\rangle\coloneq\{\,iq+jp\mid i,j\geq 0\,\}.

Such a structure is called a numerical semigroup of embedding dimension two (see, for example
[
7
, §7]
)—we took the liberty of abbreviating this to “binary semigroup” in our title. In view of (
2
), of crucial importance to us is the interesting part of
\langle p,q\rangle
, namely

S(p,q)\coloneq\langle p,q\rangle\cap[0,(p-1)(q-1)].

We say interesting, because the largest integer not in
\langle p,q\rangle
is
pq-p-q
(see
[
7
, §2.1]
). The total number of integers in
[0,(p-1)(q-1)]
missing from
S(p,q)
is
\vartheta\coloneq\varphi(p,q)/2
(see
[
7
, §5.1]
), the largest of which is
pq-p-q
, and the number of integers in
S(p,q)
is
\vartheta+1
. We ought to comment that, unfortunately, we encounter here a bit of a notational conflict. The terms “gap” as well as “nongap” are also used in the context of these semigroups—the elements of
\langle p,q\rangle
are the nongaps and the missing integers are the gaps. We avoid this conflict by using the terms representable and nonrepresentable instead.

It readily follows from (
2
) that the gaps of
Q_{\{p,q\}}
correspond to blocks of consecutive representable integers and blocks of consecutive nonrepresentable integers in
[0,\varphi(p,q)]
. More precisely (see Section 4 for details), by a block we mean two or more of consecutive integers of the same type, and the corresponding gap equals to the length of the block. Let us list the elements of
S(p,q)
in the increasing order:
\ell_{0}<\ell_{1}<\dots<\ell_{\vartheta}
, so that
\ell_{0}=0,\,\ell_{1}=p,\,\dots,\,\ell_{\vartheta}=(p-1)(q-1)
, and do the same for nonrepresentables:
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{\vartheta}
, so that
n_{1}=1,\,n_{2}=2,\,\dots,\,n_{\vartheta}=pq-p-q
. Then block lengths are given by various values of
\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}-1>0
and
n_{j+1}-n_{j}-1>0
, and the preceding assertion is that

G(Q_{\{p,q\}})=\{\,\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}-1>0\,\}\cup\{\,n_{j+1}-n_{j}-1>0\,\}.

So the problem of understanding the gapset of polynomials
Q_{\{p,q\}}
comes to the same thing as the problem of understanding the sets

(4)

S_{\Delta}(p,q)\coloneq\{\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}\}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{\Delta}(p,q)\coloneq\{n_{j+1}-n_{j}\},

an interesting problem in its own right. We give a complete solution in Section 3.

Now consider
p
as a given modulus and let
u
be a fixed integer coprime with
p
. (In our applications to
Q_{\{p,q\}}
and to
\langle p,q\rangle
,
u
will be a multiplicative inverse of
q
modulo
p
.) Linear congruence
u\cdot n\pmod{p}
gives a permutation of the residue system modulo
p
. We shall show that, for the problems discussed above, the crux of the matter is to understand the following property of such permutations. We use the notation
\langle x\rangle_{p}
to denote the least nonnegative residue of
x
modulo
p
, that is,

\langle x\rangle_{p}\equiv x\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\langle x\rangle_{p}<p.

We wish to understand the
dominant pairs
of integers
a
and
b
: these are integers
a<b
such that either

(5)

\max(\langle ua\rangle_{p},\langle ub\rangle_{p})<\min_{a<n<b}\langle un\rangle_{p}

or

(6)

\min(\langle ua\rangle_{p},\langle ub\rangle_{p})>\max_{a<n<b}\langle un\rangle_{p}.

To give the reader a quick idea of the relevance of such pairs, we point out that it is plain that the maximum difference
b-a
for pairs
(a,b)
satisfying (
5
) is
p
, for example
a=0
and
b=p
, and the maximum gap of
Q_{\{p,q\}}
is
p-1=b-a-1
.

The next section, the main part of this paper, studies (
5
) and (
6
) and, in particular, gives the complete description of the set of values
\{b-a\}
for the dominant pairs. To carry out our analysis we use what we shall call the
Euclidean algorithm for residue systems
. This gives a representation of a residue system modulo
p
that renders conditions (
5
) and (
6
) quite transparent. We hope that this representation is of independent interest and might see other uses in the future.

2.
Linear Permutations

Let a modulus
p
(
\geq 3
) and an integer
u
prime to
p
be given. In this section we study the dominant pairs
(a,b)
, that is, the solutions of the inequalities (
5
) and (
6
). Let
r_{1}
,
0<r_{1}<p
, be the multiplicative inverse of
u
modulo
p
, so that
\langle ur_{1}\rangle_{p}=1
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
D=D(p,u)
be the collection of all pairs
(a,b)
satisfying (
5
) and let
D^{\prime}=D^{\prime}(p,u)
be the collection of all pairs
(a,b)
satisfying (
6
). Then the involution

(x,y)\longmapsto(-r_{1}-y,-r_{1}-x)

is a bijection on the sets
D
and
D^{\prime}
, whence

\{\,b-a\mid(a,b)\in D\,\}=\{\,b-a\mid(a,b)\in D^{\prime}\,\}.

Proof.

Observe that
p-1-\langle ux\rangle_{p}=\langle u(-r_{1}-x)\rangle_{p}
, since
0\leq p-1-\langle ux\rangle_{p}\leq p-1
. It follows that if (
5
) holds for the pair
(a,b)
, then (
6
) holds with
(a,b)
replaced by
(-r_{1}-b,-r_{1}-a)
. In the opposite direction, if (
6
) holds for the pair
(a,b)
, then (
5
) holds with
(a,b)
replaced by
(-r_{1}-b,-r_{1}-a)
.
∎

In view of Lemma
1
, it suffices to examine the inequality (
5
). The key objective of this section is to show that this inequality is governed by the outcome of the Euclidean algorithm applied to integers
p
and
r_{1}
. We will find it convenient to use the following slightly nonstandard notation for the Euclidean algorithm. Put
r_{0}=p
and define the sequences
\bigl(r_{i}\bigr)_{i=0}^{t+1}
and
\bigl(Z_{i}\bigr)_{i=1}^{t}
by the formula

(7)

r_{i-1}=Z_{i}r_{i}+r_{i+1},\quad 0\leq r_{i+1}<r_{i},

for
1\leq i\leq t
, where
r_{t}=1
, so that
r_{t+1}=0
and
Z_{t}=r_{t-1}
.

Main Lemma
.

Let
D_{\Delta}=D_{\Delta}(p,u)\coloneq\{\,b-a\mid(a,b)\in D\,\}
. Then

D_{\Delta}=\{\,r_{i-1}-zr_{i}\mid 0\leq z\leq Z_{i}-1\text{ and }1\leq i\leq t\,\}.

Remark
.

In view of the process that generates solutions to (
5
),
D_{\Delta}(p,u)
naturally partitions into
t
parts
D_{i}(p,u)
:

(8)

D_{\Delta}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}D_{i}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\{\,r_{i-1}-zr_{i}\mid 0\leq z\leq Z_{i}-1\,\}.

In this scheme the “larger differences” go into
D_{1}
, “next larger differences” go into
D_{2}
, etc.

Our proof of the Main Lemma uses a representation of the residue system modulo
p
that is a byproduct of the Euclidean algorithm for
p
and
r_{1}
. We carry out this construction next.

2.1.
The Euclidean Algorithm for Residue Systems

Lemma 2
.

For
1\leq i\leq t
, write
t=i+2k+1
or
t=i+2k
. Then we have

\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}Z_{i+2j}r_{i+2j}=\begin{cases}r_{i-1}-1,&\text{if }i=t-2k-1\\
r_{i-1},&\text{if }i=t-2k.\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall that, by (
7
),
Z_{i}r_{i}=r_{i-1}-r_{i+1}
. Therefore,

\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}Z_{i+2j}r_{i+2j}=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}(r_{i-1+2j}-r_{i+1+2j})=r_{i-1}-r_{i+1+2k},

and the claim follows since
r_{t}=1
and
r_{t+1}=0
.
∎

Next, we introduce several conventions and definitions. For each
1\leq i\leq t
, variable
z_{i}
will always denote an integer in the range
0\leq z_{i}\leq Z_{i}
. The following generalizations of the sum appearing in Lemma
2
will prove to be useful. Put, for
0\leq j_{1}\leq j_{2}\leq(t-1)/2
,

(9)

\sigma_{1}(j_{1},j_{2};z_{2j_{2}+1})\coloneq\sum_{j_{1}\leq j\leq j_{2}-1}Z_{2j+1}r_{2j+1}+z_{2j_{2}+1}r_{2j_{2}+1},

note that this is just
z_{2j_{2}+1}r_{2j_{2}+1}
if
j_{2}=j_{1}
, and, for
0\leq j_{1}\leq j_{2}\leq t/2
,

(10)

\sigma_{0}(j_{1},j_{2};z_{2j_{2}})\coloneq\sum_{j_{1}\leq j\leq j_{2}-1}Z_{2j}r_{2j}+z_{2j_{2}}r_{2j_{2}}.

We let
\mathbf{z}
denote a
t
-tuple
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{t})
. Not all such
t
-tuples will play a role in our construction. In addition to
\mathbf{0}=(0,0,\dots,0)
, we will use
\mathbf{z}
with the properties: (i) the smallest index
i
such that
z_{i}>0
is odd, and (ii)
0\leq z_{t}\leq Z_{t}-1
. Let us denote the subset of such
t
-tuples by
\mathcal{Z}
, and let
\bar{\iota}=\bar{\iota}(\mathbf{z})
denote the special index of property (i). Finally, for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
, we put

(11)

R(\mathbf{z})\coloneq\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}z_{i}(-1)^{i-1}r_{i}.

Lemma 3
.

For
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
, we have
0<R(\mathbf{z})<p
, unless
\mathbf{z}=\textbf{0}
, and
R(\textbf{0})=0
.

Proof.

For the upper bound, we have

R(\mathbf{z})\leq z_{1}r_{1}+z_{3}r_{3}+\dots\leq p-1,

by Lemma
2
with
i=1
. Note that we use the fact that
z_{t}\leq Z_{t}-1
, to get the last inequality for odd values of
t
. For the lower bound, since
z_{i}=0
for all
i<\bar{\iota}
, we have

R(\mathbf{z})\geq z_{\bar{\iota}}r_{\bar{\iota}}-(z_{\bar{\iota}+1}r_{\bar{\iota}+1}+z_{\bar{\iota}+3}r_{\bar{\iota}+3}+\dots).

Another application of Lemma
2
(adjusted for
z_{t}\leq Z_{t}-1
), with
i=\bar{\iota}+1
, gives
R(\mathbf{z})\geq 1
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Each integer
n\in[0,p)
has a representation in the form
n=R(\mathbf{z})
, for some
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Remark
.

The example
n=r_{3}=r_{1}-Z_{2}r_{2}
shows that a representation
n=R(\mathbf{z})
need not be unique. This ambiguity is of no moment in the present paper. However, it might be worth noting that the algorithm given in the proof of the lemma does give an injection
n\mapsto\mathbf{z}(n)
from
[0,p)
to
\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

0=R(\mathbf{0})
. Now fix an arbitrary integer
0<n<p
. By Lemma
2
with
i=1
and definition (
9
), there is the smallest index
i=2k_{1}+1
such that
\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};Z_{2k_{1}+1})\geq n
. Let
z_{2k_{1}+1}
be the smallest value (it must be
\geq 1
) such that
\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})\geq n
. If this holds with equality, we stop. If not, observe that

0<n_{1}=\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})-n<r_{2k_{1}+1},

whence
2k_{1}+1<t
. Another application of Lemma
2
, this time with
i=2k_{1}+2
, gives, using (
10
),
\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})\geq n_{1}
, where the index
i=2k_{2}
and the value
z_{2k_{2}}\ (\geq 1)
are chosen to be minimal, so that
\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})<n_{1}+r_{2k_{2}}
. In the case that the equality holds, we get

n=\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})-\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})

and we stop. Otherwise, we have

(12)

\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})-\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})<n<\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1}),

and

n_{2}=n-\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})+\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})<r_{2k_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad 2k_{2}<t.

Our proof strategy and technique are now clear. Thus the next step in our argument is to further zoom in on
n
from above and improve the upper bound in (
12
) to

n\leq\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})-\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})+\sigma_{1}(k_{2},k_{3};z_{2k_{3}+1}),

and note that if this upper bound is strict, then we have

0<\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})-\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})+\sigma_{1}(k_{2},k_{3};z_{2k_{3}+1})-n<r_{2k_{3}+1},

and
2k_{3}+1<t
. It is also clear that this zooming in algorithm must terminate after a finite number of steps, and that the termination means that we arrive, after
s
steps, at a representation

(13)

n=\sigma_{1}(0,k_{1};z_{2k_{1}+1})-\sigma_{0}(k_{1}+1,k_{2};z_{2k_{2}})+-\dots,

where the last term is either
+\sigma_{1}(k_{s-1},k_{s};z_{2k_{s}+1})
, if
s
is odd, or
-\sigma_{0}(k_{s-1}+1,k_{s};z_{2k_{s}})
, if
s
is even.

Because there is no overlapping in the arguments of any of the
\sigma
s on the right side of (
13
), it is now just a formality to show that this sum is, in fact,
R(\mathbf{z})
. To that end, we define
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{s}
as:

\mathbf{z}_{1}=(Z_{1},0,Z_{3},0,\dots,z_{2k_{1}+1},\textrm{O}),

where O denotes a block of 0s of the appropriate size and
z_{2k_{1}+1}
is the first entry if
k_{1}=0
,

\mathbf{z}_{2}=(\textrm{O},Z_{2k_{1}+2},0,Z_{2k_{1}+4},0,\dots,z_{2k_{2}},\textrm{O}),\ \mathbf{z}_{3}=(\textrm{O},Z_{2k_{2}+1},0,\dots,z_{2k_{3}+1},\textrm{O}),

etc, and put
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}_{1}+\mathbf{z}_{2}+\dots+\mathbf{z}_{s}
. By the definition (
11
) of
R
, it is now plain that the right side of (
13
) equals to
R(\mathbf{z})
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Next, we show how a representation
n=R(\mathbf{z})
will be used to evaluate
\langle un\rangle_{p}
. We begin by defining the sequence
s_{0}=0,\ s_{1}=1
, and, for
1\leq i\leq t
,

(14)

s_{i+1}=Z_{i}s_{i}+s_{i-1}.

Lemma 5
.

The identity
p=s_{i}r_{i-1}+s_{i-1}r_{i}
holds for all
1\leq i\leq t+1
. In particular, we conclude that
s_{t+1}=p
, since
r_{t}=1
and
r_{t+1}=0
, and that
s_{i}
is strictly increasing to
p
.

Proof.

By (
7
) and (
14
), we have

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=1\cdot r_{0}+0\cdot r_{1}=s_{1}r_{0}+s_{0}r_{1}=s_{1}(Z_{1}r_{1}+r_{2})+s_{0}r_{1}

\displaystyle=(Z_{1}s_{1}+s_{0})r_{1}+s_{1}r_{2}=s_{2}r_{1}+s_{1}r_{2}.

Proceeding inductively:

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=s_{i}r_{i-1}+s_{i-1}r_{i}=s_{i}(Z_{i}r_{i}+r_{i+1})+s_{i-1}r_{i}

\displaystyle=(Z_{i}s_{i}+s_{i-1})r_{i}+s_{i}r_{i+1}=s_{i+1}r_{i}+s_{i}r_{i+1},

completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6
.

\langle u(-1)^{i-1}r_{i}\rangle_{p}=s_{i}
, for
0\leq i\leq t
.

Proof.

To start an induction argument, we verify that
\langle ur_{0}\rangle_{p}=0=s_{0}
,
\langle ur_{1}\rangle_{p}=1=s_{1}
, and, by (
7
) and (
14
),

\langle u(-r_{2})\rangle_{p}=\langle uZ_{1}r_{1}\rangle_{p}=Z_{1}=s_{2}.

We complete the proof by induction. By Lemma
5
, for
2\leq i\leq t
, we have

s_{i}r_{i-1}+s_{i-1}r_{i}=p\quad\text{and}\quad s_{i}r_{i-1},\,s_{i-1}r_{i}>0.

Therefore,
\langle s_{i}ur_{i-1}\rangle_{p}+\langle s_{i-1}ur_{i}\rangle_{p}=p
. To apply the induction hypothesis, we must distinguish two cases according to the parity of
i
. If
i
is odd, we get

\langle s_{i-1}ur_{i}\rangle_{p}=p-\langle s_{i}ur_{i-1}\rangle_{p}=\langle s_{i}u(-r_{i-1})\rangle_{p}=\langle s_{i}s_{i-1}\rangle_{p},

whence
\langle ur_{i}\rangle_{p}=s_{i}
. Similarly, if
i
is even, we get

p-\langle s_{i-1}ur_{i}\rangle_{p}=\langle s_{i-1}u(-r_{i})\rangle_{p}=\langle s_{i}ur_{i-1}\rangle_{p}=\langle s_{i}s_{i-1}\rangle_{p},

whence
\langle u(-r_{i})\rangle_{p}=s_{i}
, and the claim follows.
∎

We are now ready to evaluate

Lemma 7
.

For

\langle uR(\mathbf{z})\rangle_{p}=\langle u\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}z_{i}(-1)^{i-1}r_{i}\rangle_{p}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}z_{i}s_{i}.

Proof of the Main Lemma

3.
The Sets
S_{\Delta}(p,q)
and
N_{\Delta}(p,q)

In Section 2, we analyzed dominant pairs of linear permutations
u\cdot n\pmod{p}
. In the present section we apply this analysis to give complete characterization of the sets
S_{\Delta}(p,q)
and
N_{\Delta}(p,q)
defined in (
4
). For this application, we take the parameter
u
in Section 2 to be the multiplicative inverse of
q
modulo
p
. Note that with
u
so defined, the corresponding parameter
r_{1}
,
ur_{1}\equiv 1\equiv uq\pmod{p}
, also satisfies
r_{1}\equiv q\pmod{p}
. With these conventions in place, we may now interpret (
7
) and the sequences
(r_{i})
and
(Z_{i})
as being defined in terms of the given pair
p
and
q
. Parenthetically, for an alternative initial conditions of (
7
) we may now take
r_{-1}=q
and
r_{0}=p
.

Theorem 1
.

(i)
Unless
q=p+1
, we have
S_{\Delta}(p,q)=N_{\Delta}(p,q)
. For
q=p+1
, we have
S_{\Delta}(p,q)=N_{\Delta}(p,q)\cup\{2\}
, and
2\notin N_{\Delta}(p,q)
.

(ii)
We have

(24)

S_{\Delta}(p,q)=\{\,r_{i-1}-zr_{i}\mid 0\leq z\leq Z_{i}-1\text{\ and\ }1\leq i\leq t\,\}.

Let us recall some of the terminology introduced in Section 1. The set

S=S(p,q)=\{\,\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{\vartheta}\,\}\qquad[\ell_{j}<\ell_{j+1}],

is the set of representable integers
\leq\varphi(p,q)
, where
\vartheta=\varphi(p,q)/2
. Nonrepresentable integers
>0
are

N=N(p,q)=\{\,n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{\vartheta}\,\}.

Thus
S_{\Delta}=\{\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}\}
and
N_{\Delta}=\{n_{j+1}-n_{j}\}
give all possible distances between consecutive integers of each type. Perhaps it is worth making it explicit that, for instance, the distance
n_{j+1}-n_{j}
is one greater that the length of the block of consecutive representable integers between
n_{j}
and
n_{j+1}
. We now introduce the characteristic function of the representable integers
\langle p,q\rangle
(
3
),

\chi(n)\coloneq\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n\in\langle p,q\rangle\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, observe that, by the Chinese remainder theorem, each integer
n
can be written uniquely in the form

(25)

n=x_{n}q+y_{n}p+\delta_{n}pq,

where
0\leq x_{n}<p
,
0\leq y_{n}<q
and
\delta_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, so that each
n
may be identified
n\longleftrightarrow(x_{n},y_{n},\delta_{n})
with a triple of such integers. Representable integers are
n
with
\delta_{n}\geq 0
:

(26)

\chi(n)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\delta_{n}\geq 0.

Finally, coefficients
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
satisfy the congruences

(27)

n\equiv x_{n}q\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad n\equiv y_{n}p\pmod{q}.

The semigroup
\langle p,q\rangle
is said to be symmetric in view of the fact that
n\in\langle p,q\rangle
if and only if
pq-p-q-n\notin\langle p,q\rangle
. This readily follows from (
25
). (As are the facts that
\chi(pq-p-q)=0
and
\chi(pq-p-q+1)=1
mentioned earlier.) This symmetry implies the identity
n_{j}+\ell_{\vartheta-j}=pq-p-q
and shows that

(28)

\{\,n_{j+1}-n_{j}\,\}=\{\,\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}\mid 1\leq j\leq\vartheta-1\,\}.

This takes us most of the way of comparing the sets
N_{\Delta}
and
S_{\Delta}
, but leaves the question of whether
\ell_{\vartheta}-\ell_{\vartheta-1}=\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}
for some
j<\vartheta
, and thereby
S_{\Delta}=N_{\Delta}
.

Lemma 12
.

The following three assertion hold.

(i)

\ell_{\vartheta}=pq-p-q+1
and
\ell_{\vartheta-1}=pq-p-q-1
, so
\ell_{\vartheta}-\ell_{\vartheta-1}=2
.

(ii)

There exists
\ell_{j}
such that
\ell_{j}+1=\ell_{j+1}
.

(iii)

The equation
\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}=2
is soluble for
j<\vartheta
if and only if
q>p+1
.

Proof.

We will repeatedly apply (
25
) and (
26
). Thus, the aforementioned fact that
\chi(pq-p-q)=0
is immediate. Now write

1=x_{1}q+y_{1}p+\delta_{1}pq=x_{1}q+y_{1}p-pq,

and observe that, since
x_{1},y_{1}>0
,

pq-p-q+1=(x_{1}-1)q+(y_{1}-1)p\implies\chi(pq-p-q+1)=1

and

pq-p-q-1=(p-1-x_{1})q+(q-1-y_{1})p\implies\chi(pq-p-q-1)=1.

This proves our first assertion.

The second assertion is immediate if
p+1=q
. For
q\geq p+2
, we note that

(p-x_{1})q+1=y_{1}p\quad\text{and}\quad(q-y_{1})p+1=x_{1}q,

from which the assertion follows, provided that one of the terms
y_{1}p
or
x_{1}q
is
\leq\varphi(p,q)
. One readily verifies that if
q\geq p+2
, then
2\varphi(p,q)\geq pq+1
, and we see that the smaller of these two terms is
<\varphi(p,q)
, as required.

For the last assertion, observe that
\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}=2
is soluble for
j<\vartheta
if and only if there is
0<n<pq-p-q
such that
\chi(n)=0
and
\chi(n\pm 1)=1
. Note that

pq-p-q=(p-1)q+(q-1)p-pq,

so any such
n=x_{n}q+y_{n}p-pq
must have either
x_{n}<p-1
or
y_{n}<q-1
. Now, if
q=p+1
, then

n\pm 1=(x_{n}\pm 1)q+(y_{n}\mp 1)p-pq,

and we see that either
\chi(n+1)=0
, if
x_{n}<p-1
, or
\chi(n-1)=0
, if
y_{n}<q-1
. This takes care of half of the assertion (iii).

To complete the proof, assume that
q>p+1
, write
1=x_{1}q+y_{1}p-pq
and observe that
y_{1}
must satisfy
1<y_{1}<q-1
. Now take
n=pq-q-2p
, so that
\chi(n)=0
(

), and observe that

\chi(n+1)=\chi((x_{1}-1)q+(y_{1}-2)p)=1

and

\chi(n-1)=\chi((p-1-x_{1})q+(q-2-y_{1})p)=1.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem 1(i).

This is immediate from (

Lemma 14

S_{\Delta}(p,q)\subseteq D_{\Delta}(p,x_{1})

Proof.

We prove the claim by showing that every pair

S_{\Delta}(p,q)\supseteq D_{\Delta}(p,x_{1})

It is more convenient to treat trivial cases separately, and we do this first. Recall that

We now deal with the remaining cases. We begin by recalling that the remaining elements of

\langle ub\rangle_{p}<\langle ua\rangle_{p}<\min_{a<n<b}\langle un\rangle_{p}.

\ell^{\prime}-\ell=b-a
, as required.

We must also address the case where, in addition to satisfying (
15
), a dominant pair
(a,b)
is such that
\langle ua\rangle_{p}<\langle ub\rangle_{p}
. One readily verifies that an obvious adaptation of the preceding argument completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem 1(ii).

Lemmas 14 and 15 show that
S_{\Delta}(p,q)=D_{\Delta}(p,u)
, where
uq\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. The result now follows from the Main Lemma.
∎

4.
The Set
G(Q_{\{p,q\}})

We now return to the gaps of polynomials
Q_{\{p,q\}}
. Recall that we denote the coefficients of
Q_{\{p,q\}}
by
a_{m}
,
Q_{\{p,q\}}(x)=\sum_{0\leq m\leq\varphi(p,q)}a_{m}x^{m}
, and that (
2
) hols. Using the characteristic function
\chi
of
\langle p,q\rangle
, we now rewrite (
2
) in the form

Q_{\{p,q\}}(x)\equiv(1-x)\sum_{n}\chi(n)x^{n}\pmod{x^{\varphi(p,q)+1}}

and conclude that the coefficients
a_{m}
are given by

(32)

a_{m}=\chi(m)-\chi(m-1).

In particular, it is immediate that
a_{m}=0
if and only if
\chi(m)=\chi(m-1)
. Let us also note that utilizing the natural interpretation
a_{m}=0
for
m<0
or
m>\varphi(p,q)
, formula (
32
) extends to all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

It is clear from (
32
) that the gaps of
Q_{\{p,q\}}
arise in two ways. We have either a string of consecutive coefficients
a_{m}
of the form

(i)

(1,0,\dots,0,-1)=(a_{m})_{m=n_{j}+1}^{n_{j+1}}
, with
\chi(n_{j}+1)=1
, so that
n_{j+1}>n_{j}+1
, or of the form

(ii)

(-1,0\dots,0,1)=(a_{m})_{m=\ell_{j}+1}^{\ell_{j+1}}
, with
\chi(\ell_{j}+1)=0
, so that
\ell_{j+1}>\ell_{j}+1
.

This description includes strings
(1,-1)
and
(-1,1)
with no 0s in between. The gaps associated with strings of type (i)/(ii) are positive numbers of the form
n_{j+1}-n_{j}-1
or
\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}-1
, respectively. But the “gaps of type (i)” are always included amongst the “gaps of type (ii)”, since, by Theorem
1
(i),
N_{\Delta}(p,q)\subseteq S_{\Delta}(p,q)
. Therefore, using a convenient abbreviation
G(p,q)
for
G(Q_{\{p,q\}})
, we have

(33)

G(p,q)=\{\,\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}-1\mid\ell_{j+1}>\ell_{j}+1\,\}.

Theorem 2
.

Let a pair of coprime integers
q\geq p\geq 3
be given, and let the sequences
(r_{i})
and
(Z_{i})
be given by (
7
), with the initial conditions
r_{0}=p
and
r_{1}=\langle q\rangle_{p}
(or
r_{-1}=q
). Put

(34)

\displaystyle G_{i}=G_{i}(p,q)

\displaystyle\coloneq\{\,r_{i-1}-zr_{i}-1\mid 0\leq z\leq Z_{i}-1\,\}\qquad[1\leq i\leq t-1],

\displaystyle G_{t}=G_{t}(p,q)

\displaystyle\coloneq\{\,r_{t-1}-zr_{t}-1\mid 0\leq z\leq Z_{t}-2\,\}=\{\,1,2,\dots,r_{t-1}-1\,\}.

We have

(35)

G(p,q)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}G_{i}(p,q)

and, noting that
Z_{i}=\lfloor r_{i-1}/r_{i}\rfloor
,

(36)

\#G(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lfloor r_{i-1}/r_{i}\rfloor-1.

Proof.

This is immediate from (
33
) and Theorem
1
(ii).
∎

Essentially reiterating the remark following the Main Lemma, we point out that (
35
) and (
34
) give a partition of the gap set
G(p,q)
, with the part
G_{1}
containing the “larger gaps”,
G_{2}
containing the “next larger gaps”, etc. The following two examples capture the two extremes of possible sizes of gapsets
G(p,q)
.

Example 1
.

If
r_{1}=\langle q\rangle_{p}=1
, so that
t=1
and
Z_{t}=r_{0}=p
, we get

G(p,q)=G_{1}=\{\,p-1,p-2,\dots,1\,\}.

If
r_{1}=p-1
, so that
Z_{1}=1=r_{2}
and
Z_{2}=p-1
, we get

G(p,q)=G_{1}\cup G_{2}=\{p-1\}\cup\{\,p-2,p-3,\dots,1\,\}.

Recall that the fact that
G(p,q)=\{1,\dots,p-1\}
in these cases was already established in
[
4
]
.

Example 2
.

Let
F_{1}=1
,
F_{2}=2
, …, be the Fibonacci numbers and take
p=F_{k}
and
q=F_{k+1}
. One readily verifies that in this case (
7
) yields
t=k-1
and

Z_{i}\equiv 1\quad\text{and}\quad r_{i}=F_{k-i}\ [0\leq i\leq k-1].

Then the sets
G_{i}
are just the singletons
\{F_{k-i}-1\}
and we get

G(F_{k},F_{k+1})=\{\,F_{2}-1,F_{3}-1,\dots,F_{k}-1\,\}.

Theorem 3
.

In addition to the trivial upper bound
\#G(p,q)\leq p-1
, we have the lower bound
\#G(p,q)\geq k
, where
k
is given by the condition
F_{k}<p\leq F_{k+1}
. Both estimates are sharp.

Remark
.

In view of the well-known fact that
F_{k}
is the nearest integer to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2})^{k+1}
, we see that there is a constant
c>0
such that
\#G(p,q)>c\log p
.

In the proof of Theorem
3
we will use the following simple inequality.

Lemma 16
.

The inequality
nF_{k}+F_{k-1}\leq F_{k+n}
holds for all
n,k\geq 1
.

Proof.

This inequality holds with equality for
n=1
and 2. The rest follows by induction:

(n+1)F_{k}+F_{k-1}=F_{k}+nF_{k}+F_{k-1}<F_{k+n-1}+F_{k+n}=F_{k+n+1}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

The upper bound is obvious since, by Theorem
2
, the largest gap is
r_{0}-1=p-1
.

To prove the lower bound we argue by induction on the parameter
t
. For
t=1
, we have seen in Example 1 that

\#G(p,q)=p-1\geq F_{k}\geq k.

Now, the parameter
t
corresponds to the pair
(p,q)=(r_{0},r_{-1})
. To the pairs
(r_{1},r_{0})
and
(r_{2},r_{1})
there correspond the parameters
t-1\ (\geq 1)
and
t-2\ (\geq 1)
, respectively. It is for these pairs and to the corresponding quantities
\#G(r_{1},r_{0})
and
\#G(r_{2},r_{1})
that our induction hypothesis will be applied.

We treat
t=2
separately. In this case
r_{2}=1
and
r_{0}=Z_{1}r_{1}+1
, so that
Z_{1}r_{1}\geq F_{k}
. Now, by (
36
), we have

(37)

\#G(p,q)=Z_{1}+\#G(r_{1},r_{0}),

and we need only to show that
\#G(r_{1},r_{0})\geq k-Z_{1}
. So let us consider the possibility that
\#G(r_{1},r_{0})<k-Z_{1}
. Then, by the induction hypothesis, it must be that
r_{1}\leq F_{k-Z_{1}}
. But then, by Lemma
16
,

Z_{1}r_{1}\leq Z_{1}F_{k-Z_{1}}<F_{k},

a contradiction. Thus
\#G(p,q)\geq k
in this case.

Since
Z_{1}r_{1}
could be
<F_{k}
if
r_{2}>1
, we need to work a little harder when
t>2
. We start with (
37
) and note that if
\#G(r_{1},r_{0})\geq k-Z_{1}
, then we are done. Assume that
\#G(r_{1},r_{0})<k-Z_{1}
and write, by another application of (
36
),

\#G(p,q)=Z_{1}+Z_{2}+\#G(r_{2},r_{1}).

Again, if
\#G(r_{2},r_{1})\geq k-Z_{1}-Z_{2}
, then we are done. So consider the possibility that
\#G(r_{2},r_{1})<k-Z_{1}-Z_{2}
. Our current assumption tells us, by the induction hypothesis, that
r_{1}\leq F_{k-Z_{1}}
and
r_{2}\leq F_{k-Z_{1}-Z_{2}}
. But then, by Lemma
16
,

r_{0}=Z_{1}r_{1}+r_{2}\leq Z_{1}F_{k-Z_{1}}+F_{k-Z_{1}-Z_{2}}\leq F_{k},

a contradiction. This completes the proof that
\#G(p,q)\geq k
.

Finally, Examples 1 and 2 show that the upper and the lower bounds of the theorem are sharp.
∎
