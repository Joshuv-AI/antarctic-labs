Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 prevents oxidative DNA damage in intestinal epithelial cells induced by genotoxic Escherichia coli NC101
Authors: Lindsay D. Butcher, Gerco den Hartog, Amber L. Ablack, Mason Matsubara, Leonid M. Rovner, David Rios-Covian, Taren Thron, Sarkis K. Mazmanian, Jailal N.G. Ablack, Thaddeus Stappenbeck, Lars Eckmann, Tadahide Izumi, Alina Tirla, Kevin M. Wernke, Seth B. Herzon, Michael W. Dougherty, Christian Jobin, Hiroshi Kiyono, Peter B. Ernst, Sheila E. Crowe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1) is a multifunctional protein that regulates host responses to oxidative stress. We investigated its role in controlling DNA damage following infection with a genotoxic intestinal microbe.MethodsMice rendered hypomorphic for APE1 were crossed with tamoxifen (TMX)-inducible or constitutive Vil1ACre mice to yield intestinal epithelial cell-targeted knockout mice (Apex1iΔIEC or Apex1cΔIEC). Mice were infected with a genotoxic strain of E. coli (pks + E. coli NC101) to determine the APE1-sensitive effects on the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative DNA damage. Murine and human primary colonic epithelial cell lines genetically altered to reduce APE1 expression were infected with a pks-deficient, the genotoxic E. coli strain or its synthetic colibactin genotoxin.ResultsAPE1 expression was markedly diminished in enterocytes of Apex1iΔIEC mice, which was accompanied by increased constitutive epithelial cell DNA damage comparable to that observed in APE1 hypomorphic mice. The DNA damage, as indicated by γH2AX levels, was attenuated in germ-free Apex1iΔIEC mice and in APE1-hypomorphic mice treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, indicating that commensal bacteria exert genotoxic effects that are unmasked by the loss of APE1 function. Levels of ROS and oxidative DNA damage in APE1-deficient primary colonic epithelial cells were increased significantly by pks + E. coli NC101 or its synthetic colibactin 742 genotoxin and this damage was prevented by prior treatment with an antioxidant.ConclusionsAPE1 protects epithelial cells by inhibiting the accumulation of ROS and oxidative DNA damage induced by intestinal bacteria, including the colibactin produced by E. coli NC101.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced in response to many infections as well as during chronic inflammation [1,2]. ROS contribute to homeostasis of gastrointestinal epithelial cells following colonization with commensals [3], but when environmental factors are unfavorable, normal resident microbiota can give rise to DNA damage and the development of disease including cancer [2,4,5]. For example, Helicobacter pylori cause chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [6,7] and was the first bacterial pathogen to be recognized as a carcinogen [8]. While H. pylori dominate the gastric microbiota, the diverse colonic microbial communities include multiple species that have been implicated in the pathogenesis of colorectal cancers [5,[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]].

Many bacterial species produce virulence factors causing DNA damage leading to cancer. Colibactin is a peptide-polyketide in commensal and pathogenic microorganisms [16,17] that has genotoxic activity [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. E. coli elaborating colibactin (pks+) are associated with chronic inflammation [22,23] and the pathogenesis of colorectal cancer [5].

Colibactin causes double-strand DNA breaks secondary to DNA crossing linking [15,18,[23], [24], [25], [26]], DNA alkylation [27] or DNA mismatch [28]. This damage leads to mutations in genes associated with colorectal cancer including APC [29] as well as having effects on gene transcription that could favor tumorigenesis [28]. Further, the mutational signature of colorectal cancers can be found in primary cell cultures chronically exposed to genotoxic pks + E. coli [27].

In studying bacterial-induced DNA damage, reports often rely on the detection of common events such as double strand breaks that, when not repaired properly, lead to mutations [30]. However, double strand breaks may emerge due to multiple molecular events that occur upstream. Thus, the identification of mechanisms preceding genotoxin-induced DNA damage may identify strategies for preventing cancer-promoting outcomes. For example, colibactin has been associated with changes in gene expression that impact oxidative stress [28] and contribute to oxidative stress impacting immunogenicity and tumor survival [31]. However, the effect of colibactin on oxidative DNA damage has not been examined directly.

Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease (APE1, encoded by APEX1) is a multifunctional protein that is a central regulator of cellular responses to oxidative stress [2,32]. While the N-terminus of APE1 prevents Rac1 activation [33] and the accumulation of ROS [6], the C-terminus acts as the rate-limiting enzyme in single-strand DNA base excision repair of oxidative lesions [4,34]. Its name reflects its ability to repair abasic sites lacking a purine or a pyrimidine that may occur spontaneously or during the repair of oxidative DNA damage [[35], [36], [37]]. Mutations in APEX1 have been associated with altered APE1 expression in patients with colorectal cancer [36,38,39]. While the importance of APE1 in DNA repair is established, the role of APE1 in daily interactions with oxidative stress induced by host microbiota is less well understood. By exploring these functions of APE1, oxidative DNA damage as an upstream event leading to double strand breaks may be identified thereby providing novel insights that may lead to new strategies for mitigating the genotoxic effects attributed to bacterial virulence factors including disease.

In hypomorphic (HM) mice, we observed that DNA damage was widespread throughout the epithelium of the digestive tract [40]. This led to the hypothesis that the local microbial burden creates an oxidative stress that increases the risk of epithelial DNA damage when APE1 function is compromised. We now show that the presence of APE1 is essential to prevent the onset and progression of oxidative DNA damage caused by commensal and genotoxic bacteria, including E. coli NC101 or its synthetic genotoxin.

Methods

Mice

All animal experiments received IACUC approval by the University of California San Diego (S00205) and/or California Institute of Technology. Derivation of the mouse strains is described below and in Supplemental Fig. 1 and Supplemental Table 1.

Animals and breeding strategies

Generation of Apex1 hypomorph (HM) mice

Mice harboring a floxed Apex1 allele along with a neomycin resistance cassette (neo-Apex1fl/fl) were utilized as a whole-body hypomorph (HM) model. The presence of the neo cassette in these mice results in a systemic knockdown of Apex1 expression. Neo-Apex1fl/fl mice were maintained as heterozygotes and crossed to produce homozygous Neo-Apex1 fl/fl mice which exhibit the HM phenotype including a kinked tail [40].

Generation of Apex1 floxed mice

To generate mice with normal baseline Apex1 expression for conditional knockout studies, neo-Apex1fl/fl mice were crossed with transgenic mice expressing Flp recombinase. Flp-mediated recombination resulted in the excision of the FRT-flanked neo cassette, yielding functionally normal Apex1fl/fl mice.

Generation of intestinal epithelial-specific Apex1 knockout mice

To investigate the intestinal-specific roles of Apex1, the restored 8 week old Apex1fl/fl female mice were crossed with Cre + Apex1 fl/fl males to generate two distinct conditional knockout lines.1.Inducible Knockout (Apex1iΔIEC): Apex1fl/fl mice were crossed with transgenic mice expressing a tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase fusion protein driven by the villin promoter (Vil-Cre-ERT2). In the resulting Apex1iΔIEC offspring, Apex1 deletion is specific to the intestinal epithelium and is temporally controlled via the administration of tamoxifen. TMX was administered once daily by oral gavage of 50 mg/kg dissolved in corn oil for 2 days followed by a rest day and repeat the TMX dose on the 4th and 5th day. Typically, mice were weaned at 3 weeks then treated with TMX.2.Constitutive Knockout (Apex1cΔIEC): Apex1fl/fl mice were crossed with Vil-Cre transgenic mice, which constitutively express Cre recombinase under the control of the villin promoter. This breeding scheme generated Apex1cΔIEC mice featuring a constitutive, tissue-specific deletion of Apex1 localized exclusively to the intestinal epithelium.

All mice were maintained on a standard chow diet under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle unless otherwise indicated. Genotyping was performed using PCR analysis of genomic DNA extracted from tail snips or ear punches using established genotyping procedures [40]

In some cases, control C57BL/6 (WT) and HM mice were given broad-spectrum antibiotics (ampicillin 50 mg/ml, gentamicin 50 mg/ml, metronidazole 5 mg/ml, neomycin 50 mg/ml and vancomycin 50 mg/ml) orally to reduce gastroenteric bacteria [41]. Apex1iΔIEC and C57BL/6 mice were re-derived into germ-free (GF) status and housed at CalTech or UCSD.

Antibodies

The following mouse monoclonals were used: anti-APE 13B8E5C2 (Novus Biologicals); anti-α-Tubulin clone DM1A (Abcam); [N45.1] to 8-Hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (Abcam); anti-β-Actin (Sigma Aldrich); anti-phospho-histone γH2AX (Biolegend) for western blotting; and rabbit anti-phospho-histone γH2AX for immunofluorescence (Cell Signaling Technology). For western blots, the secondary antibodies were HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG (Cell Signaling Technology) and for confocal microscopy, AlexaFlour™ 488 and 568-conjugated goat anti-mouse, and goat anti-rabbit (Life Technologies) were used (Supplemental Table 3).

Bacteria

E. coli NC101 and the E. coli NC101 pks-deficient mutant [22] were maintained on LB agar plates and expanded in LB broth at 37°C with shaking for 6h. After 6h, 100 μl of culture was placed into 10 ml of LB broth overnight at 37°C under static conditions [22]. Cells were infected at a 100 MOI for varying periods of time (1h for ROS experiments and 6h + overnight for DNA damage experiments). An MOI of 100 was determined to be optimal and thus used for all NC101 infection experiments after testing multiple values (data not shown).

Colibactin

Colibactin 742 was purified as previously described [28]. IEC 6 (ATCC) or mouse primary colon Apex1fl/fl and Apex1cΔIEC epithelial cells were incubated with varying concentrations of colibactin 742 for 48h. Cells were washed with room temperature PBS and fixed in 4% methanol-free formaldehyde (Fisher Scientific) for 30 min before washing with PBS. Cells were then assayed for γH2AX by immunofluorescence.

Epithelial cell lines

Primary epithelial cell cultures from mouse and human colon (IRB UCSD HRPP 150476) were generated as previously described [40,42]. Cultures were maintained in medium containing Wnt3a, R-spondin, and Noggin in Matrigel (spheroids or undifferentiated). Lentiviral transduction was used to suppress APE1 (shAPE) in isolated human primary colonic spheroids (undifferentiated cells). Cells were sorted based on GFP fluorescence and tested for APE1 suppression by Western blot (Supplemental Fig. 3A and 3B). MTT assays (Sigma) were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocol to assess proliferation and viability (Supplemental Fig. 3C). Monolayers (differentiated cells) were grown on plastic or glass surfaces pretreated with a 1:30 diluted Matrigel solution incubated with differentiation media refreshed each day. IEC cells were used to enable responses to be related to previously obtained RNAseq data [28].

RNAseq and GSEA analysis

RNA-seq data was re-analyzed from a previous study and available at NCBI SRA Accession: PRJNA849184 [28]. Briefly, FHC cells (ATCC) were seeded at 2 × 105 per well in 6-well plates and collected after 12h treatment with a 10 or 100 μM of synthetic colibactin analog (742) or an inactive synthetic analog with a modified putative DNA-binding site (746) [28]. Breifly, 250 ng of total RNA (RIN >7) was used for library construction using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs) along with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer's, user guide. Using a High Sensitivity DNA1000 Screen Tape by Agilent TapeStation, the library size and mass were assessed. The normalized libraries were treated with Free Adapter Blocking Reagent (Illumina). The library pool was then diluted to 0.8 nM and and 120 pM with 1% PhiX spike-in control and was sequenced on one S4 flow cell lane (2 × 150 cycles). As previously described [39,43] barcoded libraries were pooled for sequencing equimolarly on a single 0.52 lane of NovaSeq 6000 S4 (2 × 150 cycles). Reads were quality trimmed at sequencing quality score of 20 and filtered to remove sequencing adaptors using Trimmomatic (v.0.39). Alignment and gene expression quantification were done through the program STAR (v.2.7.9a) using the human reference genome (GRCh38). The corresponding annotation file was obtained from the European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI, http://ftp.ebi.ac.uk). More than 90% of the input reads for each sample were successfully aligned to GRCh38. For normalization and differential expression analysis, gene counts were then imported into edgeR (v.3.26, R v.4.1.2). To be considered differentially expressed, a transcript needed to have an edgeR FDR adjusted P-value less than 0.05. Genes were ranked by normalized fold change (742/746). Pre-ranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using an unweighted scoring scheme, 1000 permutations, and enrichment scores were calculated to reflect the degree to which signaling pathways defined by the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were over- or under-represented in colibactin-treated cells as previously described [43].

Evaluation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation

Cytosolic superoxide was estimated using dihydroethidium (DHE) staining according to the manufacturer's protocol (Sigma). Monolayers were washed twice with PBS prior to treatment with 10 μM DHE and infection with E. coli NC101 (MOI 100) in conditioned media containing Y27632 (selective ROCK inhibitor to maintain viability, Tocris) without antibiotics for 1 to 24h. Cells were imaged with an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope and analyzed using ImageJ (NIH).

ROS in IEC-6 cells was assessed using the H2DCFDA probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific D399) [7]. IEC-6 cells were seeded in 24-well tissue culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well overnight before treatment with 50 μM of H2DCFDA for 1h. H2O2 was used as a positive control for ROS. Next, cells were visualized and immunofluorescence assessed.

Inhibition of ROS

DHE fluorescence and confocal microscopy were used to detect intracellular ROS levels. 5 mM N-acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC) was used for short-term inhibition of ROS. Diphenyleneiodonium (DPI), a NADPH specific inhibitor [44], was used for longer time points during E. coli NC101 infection. After 6h, bacteria were removed and media was replaced with 0% CM + Y27632 media with 0.1 mg/ml gentamicin for 1.5 h, then replaced with fresh 0% CM + Y27632 media with 0.01 mg/ml gentamycin and DPI (5 μM, and 10 μM). The Nox1 specific inhibitor, ML171, was used during E. coli NC101 infection at 10 μM and assessed for ROS inhibition by DHE.

Western blot analysis

Protein was isolated from cells and tissues with RIPA containing protease cocktail inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor, run on Tris-glycine gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Western blot analyses for APE1 (Novus Biologicals, cat# NB100-116) or γH2AX (Biolegend, cat# 613402) were done as described previously [40]. APE1 and γH2AX mouse monoclonal Abs and were used at 1:2500 and 1:1000 respectively. 1:3000 β-Actin (Sigma Aldrich, cat# A1978) and α-Tubulin antibody [DM1A] (Abcam, cat# ab7291) served as loading controls.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Human tissue samples were stored in RNAlater and in vitro cells were lysed in TRI-reagent. RNA was extracted using Zymo's minikit, according to the manufacturer's protocol. Total cDNA was made using qScript (Quantabio) and used for qPCR on an ABI StepOne Plus thermocycler as done previously [40]. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Induction and detection of DNA damage

Oxidative DNA damage progresses through a series of steps, beginning with oxidation of bases, excision of the oxidized bases to create abasic sites, single strand breaks (SSB) and repair; or when damage exceeds repair, progression to double strand breaks (DSB) [37,[45], [46], [47]]. A DNA alkylating agent, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS), served as a positive control to induce DNA damage in primary cells [40]. Human primary colon monolayers were treated with 0.5 mM MMS for 2h and assessed for DNA damage (Supplemental Fig. 4). In other experiments, cells were exposed to genotoxic bacteria or synthetic genotoxin 742 as described below.

DNA damage was detected by the long-amplicon PCR (LA-PCR) assay that detects SSB or DSB and was carried out as described previously [40,48,49]. Oxidative DNA damage was assessed with 8-OHdG (1:200, Abcam, cat# ab48508) staining [50]. Double strand breaks were detected by immunofluorescence or Western blot to detect γH2AX (1:100, cell signaling, 9718S).

Immunofluorescence detection of APE1, 8-OHdG, and γH2AX

Cells were fixed in 2% formaldehyde for 15 min at 37°C, washed and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. After washing the cells twice with PBS, slides were blocked (1.5% BSA and 5% goat serum in PBS) for 1h at 37°C. Primary antibodies were used at a 1:200 dilution and allowed to bind overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer. Samples were washed three times and incubated with Alexa Fluor™ labeled secondary antibodies (1:400 Invitrogen) in blocking buffer for 1h at room temperature. After washing, cells were incubated with Hoechst (1:1000 in PBS) for 10 min to stain nuclei. After 3 washes in PBS, cells were mounted overnight in ProLong diamond antifade mounting medium. Images were acquired on Olympus FV1000 confocal or Leica SP8 confocal microscopes [40].

Immunofluorescent detection of γH2AX in murine primary colonic epithelial cell lines was carried out as previously described [28]. The fraction of nuclei containing γH2AX foci relative to the total number of nuclei is shown as one data point per organoid.

Tissues were prepared for staining using standard techniques. Antigen retrieval was accomplished with an antigen unmasking solution comprised of 10 mM sodium citrate buffer pH 6.0 (Vector-H-3300-250) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Sections were stained as described above for APE1, 8-OHdG, and γH2AX and imaged on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope. Tissue sections were also stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and scanned on a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer Slide Scanner.

Statistical methods/analysis

LAS X software (Leica) and Image J were used for image analysis. NDP viewer software was used for H&E scanned slides. GraphPad Prism version 9 was used to perform statistical analysis using either the Student's t-test or one-way, or two-way ANOVA and posthoc testing including Tukey's, Bonferroni and Sidak tests as described in the figure legends.

Results

Genetically engineered mice

APE1 regulates multiple ROS-sensitive responses to gastroenteric infections [2] and inflammation [4], but detailed exploration of its functions in genetic models is complicated by embryonic lethality of the whole body knock-out [51,52]. To overcome this limitation, we used a previously generated whole-body hypomorphic (HM) mouse by inserting a neoR cassette into intron 3 and 4 of the APE1 gene, which led to markedly reduced APE1 expression but retained 10-20% expression which was sufficient for survival [40]. In a new approach, we constructed an inducible targeted APE1 knock-out mice using the Cre-LoxP system (Supplemental Fig. 1A–C) in which APE1 could be deleted in intestinal epithelial cells by TMX treatment. The floxed mice developed normally before TMX administration and displayed no gross abnormalities after treatment (Apex1iΔIEC). Furthermore, H&E staining of the ileum and colon showed no obvious differences in these mice (Supplemental Fig. 1D)

APE1 was significantly decreased in the epithelium of the inducible epithelial cell knockout while normal levels remained in the spleen and underlying tissue including the lamina propria, lymphatics, and mesentery indicating the specificity of the mouse model (Fig. 1A and B). The reduction of APE1 in intestinal epithelial cells from Apex1iΔIEC mice was confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 1C and D). The ileum and colon epithelial cells were examined for DNA integrity using LA-PCR (Fig. 1E). Gut microbiota contribute to epithelial DNA damage

Since epithelial cells displayed an increase in DNA damage in HM [40] and Apex1iΔIEC mice under baseline conditions, we explored whether commensal bacteria promoted DNA damage. First, WT C57BL/6 and HM mice were given broad-spectrum antibiotics which markedly reduced the fecal bacteria cultured on BHI agar (Fig. 2A) and tended to improved DNA integrity (Fig. 2B) which was significant when the mice were housed in germ-free (GF) conditions (Fig. 2C). To investigate the relationship between the microbiota and DNA damage, Apex1fl/fl mice were re-derived into GF conditions and compared to conventional C57BL/6 mice as well as Apex1iΔIEC mice housed under conventional SPF conditions. There was a decrease in epithelial APE1 expression in Apex1iΔIEC mice under both GF and SPF conditions (Fig. 2D, E, and 2H). Also, APE1 expression in Apex1fl/fl intestinal epithelial cells was significantly increased in GF conditions. Levels of γH2AX (double strand breaks-DSB) were significantly elevated in Apex1iΔIEC mice under SPF conditions and reduced in intestinal epithelial cells from both Apex1fl/fl and Apex1iΔIEC housed under GF conditions (2D, 2F, 2G, and 2I). Furthermore, immunofluorescence of colon and ileum sections showed an increase in 8-OHdG and γH2AX levels (Fig. 2J, K, 2L). The pattern of staining for oxidative DNA damage 8-OHdG revealed significant increases throughout Apex1iΔIEC glands (crypts). Cells expressing increased levels of γH2AX from Apex1iΔIEC mice were enriched towards the distal end of the glands adjacent to the lumen (Fig. 2J, white arrows). APE1 limits constitutive DNA damage in human primary colonic epithelial cells

The consequences of DNA damage are predictably most severe if they occur in self-renewing stem cells, whereas the risk of accumulating DNA damage in terminally differentiated cells could be less relevant since these are normally shed within days. To determine if APE1 is differentially expressed in epithelial stem cells (spheroids) compared to differentiated cells (monolayers), human primary colonic epithelial cells were grown as either stem cells or differentiated cells. In the undifferentiated stem cells, increased expression of LGR5 and MYC was observed compared to differentiated cells (Fig. 3A) while the differentiated cells expressed higher levels of colon-specific differentiation markers, MUC2 and CA1 (Fig. 3A). mRNA expression for APEX1 was markedly (∼6 fold) higher in undifferentiated stem cells (Fig. 3A) which was paralleled by higher proteins levels (Fig. 3B and C). To assess APE1 function in the primary epithelial cultures, we constructed stable knock-down cells using shRNA targeting APEX1. Depletion was >90% effective in both undifferentiated and differentiated epithelial cells (Fig. 3B and C). To determine if colibactin 742-induced ROS caused DNA damage that is prevented by an APE1-associated mechanism, we quantified γH2AX accumulation after colibactin 742 treatment in primary colonic epithelial cells that differed in APE1 expression. APE1-deficient primary colonic epithelial cell lines had a significantly higher proportion of γH2AX positive cells following 5 and 10 μM colibactin 742 treatment (Fig. 7C and D). There was no significant difference in stem cell cluster diameter during colibactin exposure when APE1 was lacking (Fig. 7E). Thus, colibactin 742 was sufficient to induce the accumulation of ROS and DNA damage when APE1 expression was impaired. These findings suggest that genotoxic stimuli, including exposure to colibactin from E. coli NC101, generate ROS and account for oxidative DNA damage that is counteracted by APE1.

Discussion

The data demonstrated that APE1 protects colonic epithelium from DNA damage induced by genotoxic bacteria through the inhibition of ROS production and the repair of oxidative DNA damage. Using APE1 hypomorphic mice and intestinal epithelial cell-targeted APE1 knockout mice, we demonstrated that commensal microbiota contributed to DNA damage in the epithelium. Oxidative DNA damage is an early pathogenic mechanism since blocking ROS accumulation in microbially exposed cells inhibited DNA damage. Further, E. coli strains expressing the genotoxin colibactin, or the synthetic genotoxin alone, induced ROS and DNA damage. The accumulation of ROS and the DNA damage induced by NC101 was prevented by functional APE1 and antioxidants. These findings support the hypothesis that the microbial burden, and even specific species and molecular entities, create oxidative stress that increases the risk of epithelial DNA damage counteracted by the actions of APE1.

APE1 is a multifunctional protein that prevents and repairs oxidative DNA damage [2,4,47]. APE1 expression is increased in association with infections, such as H. pylori, as well as in response to ROS [53]. APE1 regulates the activation of transcription factors [4] that enhance IL-8 production in the context of gastrointestinal inflammation [54,55] and in so doing, promotes the accumulation of inflammatory cells that contribute to oxidative stress [2,32] and oxidative DNA damage [4,5,32,40,47,[56], [57], [58], [59]]. The N-terminus of APE1 has been shown to attenuate the accumulation of ROS in response to infection by inhibiting Rac1 activation [6]. This may be a partial explanation for the inhibition of ROS and the potential protective effects of APE1. APE1 also plays a key role in the base excision repair of oxidative DNA damage, thus, providing the host with additional protection against oxidative DNA damage [4,47]. The novel animal models and cell lines described here provided an experimental approach demonstrating that the genotoxic bacteria E. coli NC101 increases the accumulation of ROS and oxidative DNA damage. The fact that the “normal” microbiota in SPF mice increased DNA damage in APE-1-deficient mice suggests multiple species impart a stress on the integrity of the DNA. These findings provide support for future experimentation identifying other species of bacteria that may invoke oxidative stress and heighten the risk of DNA damage and colorectal cancer. It remains to be determined if manipulating our microbiota or modifying diets to include more antioxidants could ameliorate our risk of developing these diseases.

In the presence of ROS, nucleobases, particularly guanine, are oxidized as evidenced by the increase in 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine. Subsequently, DNA glycosylases remove oxidized bases creating an abasic site that is recognized by APE1 and enables the modification of the single strand break so that repair by DNA ligases can occur [4,35,47]. The lack of APE1 would allow single strand breaks to accumulate, progress to double strand breaks and likely increase the incidence of mutations in key sites that portend malignant transformation. The importance of APE1 in regulating the outcome of bacterial-epithelial cell interactions was illustrated by: 1) the fact that only epithelial cells had detectable DNA damage in the gut in Apex1 hypomorphic mice (data not shown); 2) logically, stem cells had increased levels of APE1 as they require the highest degree of protection; 3) decreasing bacteria by the administration of antibiotics reduced DNA damage; 4) DNA damage was reduced by rederiving APE1-deficient mice into germ-free conditions; and 5) primary epithelial cell lines in which APE1 expression was inhibited were more susceptible to DNA damage after infection with genotoxic bacteria or exposure to a synthetic genotoxin in vitro. Although epithelial cells in the small intestine of the APE1-deficient mice were also susceptible to DNA damage, this may be less significant in humans as the bacterial burden is dramatically lower in this site. In contrast, colonic epithelial cells are exposed to many microorganisms that have been implicated in DNA damage and colorectal cancer [9,12,13,21,60].

E. coli NC101 has been described as a genotoxic strain [22] that leads to double strand breaks in the DNA [47]. These DNA breaks have been reported to arise from crosslinking [18,19,23,24], alkylation [19,26,27] or DNA mismatch [28] leading to mutations in genes associated with colorectal cancer including APC [29] as well as having effects on gene transcription that could favor tumorigenesis [28]. The synthesis of the active colibactin is complex and has been reported to involve oxidation steps to yield a macrocyclic colibactin that induces double strand breaks [61]. This in part, might explain the contribution of ROS to multiple mechanisms of DNA damage and account for the observation that APE1 may be protective since it can decrease ROS production through its ability to inhibit the activation of Rac1 [6,33].

To date, there is limited evidence to show that E. coli NC101 induces oxidative DNA damage prior to the emergence of DNA breaks. ROS induction has a rapid onset suggesting it might be an early or a coincident event in DNA damage that can arise from multiple mechanisms. Since strains of E. coli NC101 lacking the genotoxic island (Δpks) did not induce significant ROS accumulation or DNA damage, one interpretation could be that ROS accumulation is a necessary event upstream of multiple mechanisms that account for DNA damage. This is supported by the data showing that treatment of human primary colonic epithelial cells – expressing normal or reduced levels of APE1 - with the antioxidant DPI blocked both the accumulation of ROS and the increase in γH2AX following infection with E. coli NC101. However, ML171 had little effect on DNA damage, perhaps due to the fact it is an isoform-selective (Nox1) inhibitor, although the target of these agents was not resolved in the current studies. Perhaps mitochondrial ROS or other isoforms of Nox are responsible for the genotoxic damage. Alternatively, ML171 may be toxic to the cell over the 24-h period during which DNA damage was assayed. The activation of numerous metabolic pathways in response to colibactin would be consistent with several mechanisms of DNA damage being in place, some of which may be facilitated by the accumulation of ROS [61]. Further, the increase in DNA damage may be at least partially attributable to the effect APE1 has on gene transcription such that its absence alters the availability of other DNA repair machinery. Thus, the multiple targets of drugs, ROS and APE1 itself make it challenging to define the specific mechanisms involved and it is most likely, multiple genotoxic events are in play.

Conclusion

The data presented in this paper support the notion that APE1 is a key molecule in controlling premalignant events, including the induction of oxidative DNA damage, by genotoxic E. coli NC101. Inhibition of these events by an antioxidant implies they have the potential to block at least some of the genotoxic effects caused by the oxidative stress imparted by this infection. Genotoxic E. coli have been recognized for their association with colorectal cancers [14,28,29,31], including disease in younger subjects [29] and the data presented here suggest that oxidative stress contributes to the DNA damage that may lead to mutations favoring the development of cancer. The association of polymorphisms in the APEX1 gene with several cancers has been associated primarily with mutations in the carboxy terminus of APE1 that is largely responsible for DNA repair [36,39]. However, some SNPs are implicated in the amino terminus and may partially explain how APE1 regulates the accumulation of ROS leading to oxidative DNA damage. The polymorphisms in the gene [37], or other regions upstream, could also impact the transcriptional properties of APE1 [35,59] with, as of yet, unknown impact on other gene products that are protective in the host. It should be noted that the near total ablation of APE1 expression in the epithelial targets described here are likely more complete than may be associated with natural polymorphism. However, the use of the novel mouse models may accelerate attempts to identify a role for APE1 in molecular pathways associated with a variety of gastrointestinal cancers and could enable the testing of pharmacological candidates that reduce the risk of these malignancies.
