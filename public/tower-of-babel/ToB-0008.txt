# Tripitaka (Pali Canon) — Buddhist Scripture

## Overview

The Tripitaka (Pali: Tipitaka; Sanskrit: Tripiṭaka), meaning "Three Baskets," is the canonical literature of Theravada Buddhism. It comprises approximately 40 volumes in the Pali language and represents the earliest extant collection of Buddhist teachings. According to tradition, these texts were codified at the Fourth Buddhist Council in Sri Lanka around the 1st century BCE, though the core teachings derive from the historical Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama, c. 563–483 BCE) and his early disciples.

The three baskets are:
- **Vinaya Pitaka** (Basket of Discipline) — Rules for monastic life
- **Sutta Pitaka** (Basket of Discourses) — Discourses and teachings

This sutta addresses two young Brahmins, Vassehtha and Bharadvaja, who dispute which of their teachers knows the true path to union with Brahma. The Buddha criticizes the Brahmins for claiming to know the way to Brahma when none of them — nor any of their teachers going back seven generations — has ever actually seen Brahma face to face.

The Buddha then teaches the Brahma-viharas (Divine Abidings):
- **Metta** (Loving-kindness) — unbounded goodwill toward all beings
- **Karuna** (Compassion) — sympathy for the suffering of others
- **Mudita** (Appreciative joy) — delight in the happiness of others
- **Upekkha** (Equanimity) — balanced patience toward all

These four practices, the Buddha teaches, are the true way to reach Brahma's world, far superior to Vedic ritual sacrifice.

The Abhidhamma Pitaka contains seven treatises on Buddhist psychology and philosophy:

1. **Dhammasangani** (Enumeration of Dhammas) — Classification of all phenomena
2. **Vibhanga** (The Book of Analysis) — Systematic analysis ofdhammas
3. **Dhatukatha** (Discussion of Elements)
4. **Puggalapannatti** (Fictions of Persons)
5. **Kathavatthu** (Points of Controversy) — Refutation of rival theories
6. **Yamaka** (The Pairs) — Analytical treatment of doctrinal points

## Key Teachings of the Tripitaka

### The Four Noble Truths (Ariya Sacca)
1. **Dukkha** — Life is suffering (birth, aging, sickness, death, grief, etc.)
2. **Samudaya** — The cause of suffering is craving (tanha) and attachment
3. **Nirodha** — The cessation of suffering is possible
4. **Magga** — The path to the cessation of suffering is the Noble Eightfold Path

**Magga** — The path to the cessation of suffering is the Noble Eightfold Path

### The Noble Eightfold Path (Ariya Atthangika Magga)
1. Right View (Samma ditthi)
2. Right Intention (Samma sankappa)
3. Right Speech (Samma vayama)
4. Right Action (Samma kammanta)
5. Right Livelihood (Samma ajiva)
6. Right Effort (Samma vayama)
7. Right Mindfulness (Samma sati)
8. Right Concentration (Samma samadhi)

### The Five Precepts (Pancasila)
1. Refrain from killing
2. Refrain from stealing
3. Refrain from sexual misconduct
4. Refrain from lying
5. Refrain from intoxicants

### The Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipatthana)
1. Mindfulness of body (kayanupassana)
2. Mindfulness of feelings (vedananupassana)
3. Mindfulness of mind (cittanupassana)
4. Mindfulness of phenomena (dhammanupassana)

fulness of mind (cittanupassana)
4. Mindfulness of phenomena (dhammanupassana)

### The Four Brahma-Viharas (Divine Abidings)
1. Loving-kindness (metta) — May all beings be happy
2. Compassion (karuna) — May all beings be free from suffering
3. Appreciative joy (mudita) — May all beings be happy in their happiness
4. Equanimity (upekkha) — May all beings have equanimity toward all

### The Five Aggregates (Khandhas)
1. Material form (rupa)
2. Feeling/sensation (vedana)
3. Perception/recognition (sañña)
4. Mental formations/volitions (sankhara)
5. Consciousness (viññana)
