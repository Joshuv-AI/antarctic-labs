1.
Introduction

A partition of a positive integer
n
is a nonincreasing sequence of positive integers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r}
such that
n=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{r}
. We say that each
\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq r
, is called a part of the partition. We denote the number of partitions of
n
by
p(n)
.

In the late 1910’s, Ramanujan
[
5
]
proved the following congruences satisfied by
p(n)
for the moduli
5,7,
and
11
using generating function manipulations.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle p(5n+4)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5},

\displaystyle p(7n+5)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{7},\text{ and}

\displaystyle p(11n+6)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{11}.

These congruences, and others that have since been proven for numerous restricted partition functions, serve as the motivation for this work.

In recent work of Dombos
[
4
]
, the set of integer partitions of
n
wherein the parts are either divisible by 4 or congruent to
\pm 1\pmod{6}
arose in a natural way. (See
[
4
, Theorem 1.6]
for more details.) In this work, we will denote the function which counts the number of such partitions of
n
by
dp(n)
. In light of this definition, it is clear that the generating function for
dp(n)
is given by the following:

(1)

G(q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(n)q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{5};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}},

where
(a;q)_{\infty}:=(1-a)(1-aq)(1-aq^{2})\cdots

is the usual
q
-Pochhammer symbol.

The primary goal of this brief note is to prove a number of Ramanujan–like congruences satisfied by the function
dp(n)
in certain arithmetic progressions. In the work below, we will prove the following results.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

(2)

\displaystyle dp(6n+4)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{2},

(3)

\displaystyle dp(18n+10)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(4)

\displaystyle dp(54n+52)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p
be a prime such that
p\equiv 17\mbox{ or }23\pmod{24}
. Then for all
k,m\geq 0
with
p\nmid m
, we have

dp\left(6p^{2k+1}m+\frac{15p^{2k+2}+1}{4}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

(5)

\displaystyle dp(27n+16)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

Theorem 1.5
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

dp(27n+7)\equiv dp(3n+1)\pmod{3}.

Theorem 1.6
.

For all
\alpha\geq 1
and all
n\geq 0
,

dp\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{7\cdot 9^{\alpha}+1}{4}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

Throughout the remainder of this paper, we define

We begin by rewriting the generating function (

Lemma 2.1

(6)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{2}f_{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}}.

Proof.

Initially, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q;q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{5};q^{6})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{6};q^{6})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{5};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{6};q^{6})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{6})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{6})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{3})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{3})_{\infty}}

Thus, it follows from (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}},

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let

H(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(n)q^{n}=G(-q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(n)(-q)^{n}.

Thus,
h(n)=(-1)^{n}dp(n)
for all
n
. We note that

(-q;-q)_{\infty}=\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}}.

Using this identity and (
6
) we find that

H(q)=\frac{f_{1}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{3}f_{12}}=\frac{f_{6}^{2}}{f_{3}f_{12}}\frac{1}{\psi(q)}.

Thanks to (
13
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(3n+1)q^{3n+1}

\displaystyle=-q\frac{f_{3}^{2}f_{18}^{3}}{f_{6}^{5}f_{12}},

which, after dividing by
q
and replacing
q^{3}
by
q
, yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(3n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{f_{1}^{2}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{2}^{5}f_{4}}.

Using (
7
), we can 2-dissect the expression above to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(6n+4)q^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=2q\frac{f_{6}^{3}f_{16}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{4}f_{8}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(6n+4)q^{n}

\displaystyle=2\frac{f_{3}^{3}f_{8}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{2}f_{4}},

which proves (
2
) since
dp(6n+4)=h(6n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

Working modulo 8, we have

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(6n+4)q^{n}

\displaystyle=2\frac{f_{3}^{3}f_{8}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{2}f_{4}}\equiv 2f_{3}^{3}\frac{f_{4}^{3}}{f_{2}^{3}}\pmod{8}.

Thanks to (
12

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(18n+10)q^{3n+1}

\displaystyle\equiv 4q^{4}\frac{f_{3}^{3}f_{12}^{3}f_{18}^{2}f_{36}^{2}}{f_{6}^{7}}\pmod{8},

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(18n+10)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv 4q\frac{f_{1}^{3}f_{4}^{3}f_{6}^{2}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{2}^{7}}\pmod{8}.

It follows that
h(18n+10)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, which completes the proof of (
3
) since
h(n)=(-1)^{n}dp(n)
.

In order to prove (
4
), we use (
12
) to extract the terms of the form

from (

). The resulting congruence is

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(18n+16)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv 6\frac{f_{1}^{3}f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{5}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{12}}\pmod{8}

\displaystyle\equiv 6\frac{f_{6}^{5}}{f_{12}}f_{1}^{3}\pmod{8}

\displaystyle\equiv 6\frac{f_{6}^{5}}{f_{12}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}(2n+1)q^{n(n+1)/2}\pmod{8}

by (

is a function of

and

is never congruent to 2 modulo 3. Hence, once we expand (

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Initially, we use (
9

q^{3n+1}
from (
6

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(3n+1)q^{3n+1}=q\frac{f_{6}f_{18}^{3}}{f_{3}^{2}f_{12}^{3}}.

After dividing by
q
and replacing
q^{3}
by
q
, we are left with

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(3n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{6}^{3}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{4}^{3}}.

Now we use (
8
) to extract the odd parts of (
17
), which gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(6n+4)q^{n}

\displaystyle=2\frac{f_{3}^{3}f_{8}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{2}f_{4}}

(18)

\displaystyle\equiv 2f_{2}^{3}f_{3}^{3}\pmod{4}.

Using (

), it follows from (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(6n+4)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv 2f_{2}^{3}f_{3}^{3}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k,l=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k+l}(2k+1)(2l+1)q^{k(k+1)+3l(l+1)/2}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\sum_{k,l=1}^{\infty}q^{k(k+1)+3l(l+1)/2}\pmod{4}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}dp(6n+4)q^{24n+15}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\sum_{k,l=1}^{\infty}q^{6(2k+1)^{2}+(6l+3)^{2}}\pmod{4}.

It follows that

if

is not of the form

.

Since

, it follows that
\displaystyle\left(\frac{-6}{p}\right)=-1
, which implies that
\nu_{p}(N)
is even if

. Taking

Therefore,
\nu_{p}(24n+15)
is odd and the result follows by the fact that

6\left(p^{2k+1}m+\frac{5(p^{2k+2}-1)}{8}\right)+4=6p^{2k+1}m+\frac{15p^{2k+2}+1}{4}.

Lastly, we use the previous two theorems to prove Theorem

We prove this theorem by induction on

Thus, we assume that, for some

We then want to prove that

We note that

\displaystyle dp\left(3\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{3\cdot 7\cdot 9^{\alpha-1}-1}{4}\right)+1\right)

\displaystyle=dp\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{7\cdot 9^{\alpha}-3}{4}+1\right)

\displaystyle=dp\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{7\cdot 9^{\alpha}+1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},

by the induction hypothesis. Finally, by Theorem

\displaystyle dp\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{7\cdot 9^{\alpha+1}+1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=dp\left(3^{2\alpha+3}n+\frac{7\cdot 9^{\alpha+1}-27}{4}+7\right)

\displaystyle=dp\left(27\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{3\cdot 7\cdot 9^{\alpha-1}-1}{4}\right)+7\right)

\displaystyle\equiv dp\left(3\left(3^{2\alpha}n+\frac{3\cdot 7\cdot 9^{\alpha-1}-1}{4}\right)+1\right)\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},

which concludes the inductive proof.
