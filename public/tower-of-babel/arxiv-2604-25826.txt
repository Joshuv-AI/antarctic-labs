General-Purpose Technology and Speculative Bubble Detection

Abstract

We show that the leading bubble test suffers severe size distortion when fundamentals incorporate general-purpose technology adoption. Embedding a hump-shaped technology shock in the Campbell-Shiller present-value model, we prove that the fundamental price becomes locally explosive during adoption, contaminating the test’s limit distribution with a non-centrality parameter proportional to the shock’s peak. We propose a fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition that projects prices onto observable technology proxies and applies the test to the residual. Empirically, the decomposition eliminates evidence of speculation in the 2020–2025 AI rally while confirming a speculative peak confined to December 1999–March 2000 in the dot-com episode.

Keywords
: general-purpose technology, speculative bubbles, bubble detection, artificial intelligence, present-value model

JEL classification
: C12, C22, G12, O33

Introduction

Since the release of large language models in late 2022, equity markets have experienced a dramatic rally concentrated in artificial-intelligence (AI)-related firms. The “Magnificent Seven” (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA, and Tesla) accounted for a majority of the S&P 500’s gains during 2023–2024
(
3
)
, and the NASDAQ Composite nearly doubled from its October 2022 trough. Corporate AI investment has surged in parallel, with aggregate compute expenditure, related patenting, and research and development (R&D) intensity all rising rapidly
(
21
;
23
;
2
)
, while productivity effects of generative AI are beginning to materialize in firm-level data
(
7
)
. The pattern of a concentrated technology rally amid rapid but uncertain technological change echoes the late-1990s NASDAQ run-up, whose subsequent crash destroyed trillions of dollars in market value. The central question for researchers, regulators, and investors is the same now as it was then: are these price dynamics a speculative bubble, or a rational repricing of the economy’s productive capacity?

This paper asks a question that recurs with every technological revolution: are standard bubble tests systematically mistaking fundamental repricing for speculative excess? The most widely used real-time bubble detector is the seminal right-tailed unit root test of
28
;
29
(hereafter PSY), now embedded in the surveillance frameworks of central banks worldwide.
1

1

See, for example, the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas International House Price Database, which routinely applies the PSY procedure
(

Applied to the current rally, PSY detects explosive dynamics. Yet its null hypothesis is a pure random walk and abstracts entirely from structural change in fundamentals. Precisely during periods of general-purpose technology (GPT) adoption, when the bubble question is most pressing, technology-driven fundamentals themselves generate locally explosive price dynamics that are observationally indistinguishable from speculation. The risk is not merely a statistical artifact: if policymakers treat a technological repricing as a bubble, premature intervention can stifle the very adoption process that drives long-run growth.

Our first contribution is new empirical evidence on the AI rally and the late-1990s dot-com episode. We show that the 2020–2025 AI rally leaves no evidence of speculative behavior at any conventional significance level once technology fundamentals are accounted for. For the dot-com episode, the decomposition eliminates an early spurious signal but confirms a genuine speculative peak from December 1999 to March 2000: the technology-driven run-up was fundamental, and only the terminal phase was speculative. These findings speak to the long-standing debate over whether asset price booms during technological revolutions reflect rational repricing or irrational exuberance
(
25
;
24
)
, and provide the first formal evidence that the two episodes, often treated as analogous, are economically distinct.

Our second contribution is methodological. The size distortion we document is a specific instance of a broader identification problem: deterministic structural change in fundamentals can lead the PSY test to reject the null of no bubble even when no speculative component is present. Existing work has examined related sources of misspecification, including time-varying discount rates
(
11
)
, rational bubble dynamics
(
5
;
12
;
13
)
, and structural breaks in unit root inference
(
30
;
29
;
17
;
16
;
22
)
. The technology channel is distinctive: the hump-shaped, nonlinear adoption profile of GPTs produces locally explosive dynamics that standard linear detrending cannot remove. Formally, the absence of a bubble does not guarantee the absence of explosive dynamics once fundamentals incorporate technology-driven structural change.

Our third contribution is a fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition that removes the estimated technology component before applying PSY, restoring correct size while preserving power against genuine bubbles. The decomposition uses three observable technology proxies—total factor productivity (TFP), information technology (IT) investment, and patent grants—each grounded in a production-side microfoundation and each separable from the speculative component of prices. A stochastic technology extension nests a simplified version of the
25
uncertainty-resolution framework and shows that the deterministic results are a conservative lower bound on the true size distortion.

The analysis is embedded in the
8
log-linear present-value framework. A GPT shock, defined as a hump-shaped, deterministic component in dividend growth that reflects technology adoption and maturation
(
6
;
19
)
, generates a technology present-value term in the fundamental price. During the adoption phase, this term rises sharply as the market prices in future technology-driven dividend growth, producing locally explosive dynamics in the price-dividend ratio that are entirely fundamental. Empirically, we estimate the technology component via a cointegrating regression of prices on observable technology proxies during a pre-bubble training period, following the counterfactual approach of
18
, and apply PSY to the residual, which under the null of no bubble is stationary.

The quantitative magnitude of the distortion is large. In Monte Carlo simulations calibrated to postwar United States equity data and containing only technology-driven fundamentals with no speculative component, PSY rejects the null of no bubble in 93 percent of samples for detrended log prices and 100 percent for the price-dividend ratio at the 5 percent nominal level. Removing the technology component restores the rejection rate to its nominal level. In a controlled overlap experiment in which a genuine bubble coexists with a technology shock, the unadjusted test fires prematurely during the technology-only phase, while the technology-adjusted procedure correctly delays detection until genuine speculative activity begins. For the NASDAQ dot-com episode, the unadjusted test detects two explosive episodes: a brief signal in May 1996 and the main episode from November 1999 to March 2000. Our decomposition eliminates the early signal and narrows the genuine speculative peak to December 1999 through March 2000. For the 2020–2025 AI rally, the technology adjustment eliminates the explosive signal entirely: conditional on the technology-adjusted framework, there is no evidence of speculation at any conventional significance level. Firm-level analysis of the Magnificent Seven confirms this pattern: explosive signals in Microsoft, Alphabet, and NVIDIA disappear after technology adjustment.

The results are robust across four functional forms for the technology shock (triangular, Gaussian, Beta, and Gamma-like profiles); across dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS), fully modified ordinary least squares (FMOLS), and first-difference specifications of the cointegrating regression; and across 121 alternative training windows for the AI era. A battery of identification diagnostics—Granger causality tests, placebo tests with non-technology covariates, principal component analysis (PCA) of firm-level price gaps, and training-window stability analysis—supports the separability of the technology proxies from the speculative component.

Theoretical Framework

2.1
Present-Value Decomposition

Our theoretical framework builds on the standard present-value identity. This subsection isolates the object
f_{t}
—the fundamental log price—whose behavior we will subsequently show can become locally explosive even in the absence of speculative bubbles.

We consider an economy in which a single risky asset pays a stochastic dividend stream
\{D_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
. Let
P_{t}
denote the ex-dividend price and define
p_{t}=\log P_{t}
and
d_{t}=\log D_{t}
. The gross log return is

r_{t+1}=\log(P_{t+1}+D_{t+1})-\log P_{t}.

(1)

Following
8
, a first-order Taylor expansion of (
1
) around the mean log dividend-price ratio
\overline{d-p}
yields the log-linear approximation

r_{t+1}\approx\kappa+\rho\,p_{t+1}+(1-\rho)\,d_{t+1}-p_{t},

(2)

where

\rho=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\overline{d-p}\,)},\qquad\kappa=\log(1+\exp(\overline{d-p}\,))-(1-\rho)\,\overline{d-p}.

The parameter
\rho\in(0,1)
is the log-linearization discount factor.
2
2

2

For the postwar U.S. aggregate stock market, the annual log dividend-price ratio averages roughly
-3.4
, yielding
\rho\approx 0.97
.

Iterating (
2
) forward and taking conditional expectations gives the standard present-value identity:

p_{t}=\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+d_{t}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\Delta d_{t+1+j}-r_{t+1+j}\right]+\lim_{j\to\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{t+j}].

(3)

The identity (
3
) cleanly separates the log price into three components: a constant, the current dividend, an infinite-horizon sum of expected future dividend growth net of expected returns, and a terminal term that captures self-fulfilling speculative dynamics. We use this separation to define fundamental and bubble components.

Definition 1

(Fundamental and Bubble Components)
.

The fundamental component is

f_{t}=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\Delta d_{t+1+j}-r_{t+1+j}\right],

and the bubble component is
b_{t}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{t+j}]
, satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{t}[b_{t+1}]=\rho^{-1}b_{t}
. The log price admits the decomposition
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
.

In the standard rational-expectations no-bubble equilibrium,
b_{t}\equiv 0
and the price coincides with the fundamental component. Conventional intuition associates a stationary dividend-growth process with a random-walk-with-drift
f_{t}
, so that any locally explosive behavior in observed prices must reflect a non-zero bubble
b_{t}
. The next subsection relaxes this intuition: we introduce a transient, hump-shaped technology shock into the dividend-growth process and show that the induced
f_{t}
departs from a pure random walk during the adoption phase, mimicking the behavior usually attributed to a bubble. Imposing the no-bubble condition (
b_{t}=0
for all
t
), this raises our central question:
does
f_{t}
itself exhibit locally explosive dynamics that mimic the behavior of
b_{t}>0
?

2.2
Technology-Augmented Dividend Growth

This subsection introduces the structural model of dividend growth that drives our main results. The key innovation is a transient, hump-shaped technology component
\delta_{t}
superimposed on an otherwise standard stochastic trend. Economically,
\delta_{t}
represents the transient contribution of a general-purpose technology—the steam engine, electrification, or artificial intelligence—that temporarily lifts aggregate productivity while adoption diffuses through the economy and then fades as the new technology is fully absorbed. Formally,
\delta_{t}
is deterministic and bounded, which preserves cointegration of the dividend process with its mean growth rate while allowing rich non-stationary dynamics over the adoption window. Assumption
1
specifies the dividend-growth process; Assumption
2
the hump shape; Assumption
3
constant required returns (relaxed later); and Assumption
4
a mild convexity property of the present-value object
\mathcal{T}_{t}
on which our explosiveness results rely.

Assumption 1

(Dividend Growth)
.

Log dividend growth follows

\Delta d_{t}=c+\delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad\varepsilon_{t}\sim\text{i.i.d.}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}).

where
c>0
is the steady-state growth rate.

The decomposition
\Delta d_{t}=c+\delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
is deliberately minimal: it embeds the technology shock
\delta_{t}
as an additive, transient deviation from steady-state growth
c
, while
\varepsilon_{t}
captures idiosyncratic dividend innovations. When
\delta_{t}\equiv 0
, the process reduces to the standard random-walk-with-drift specification in which
f_{t}
is itself a random walk with drift
c
under constant required returns.

Assumption 2

(Technology Shock)
.

The technology component
\delta_{t}
is a deterministic, hump-shaped process with support on
[T_{1},T_{2}]
:

\delta_{t}=\begin{cases}g(t-T_{1})&\text{if }t\in[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau],\\[4.0pt]
g(\tau)\,h(t-T_{1}-\tau)&\text{if }t\in(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2}],\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
T_{1}
is the adoption date,
T_{2}>T_{1}
is the maturation date,
\tau\in(0,T_{2}-T_{1})
is the peak lag,
g:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is strictly increasing with
g(0)=0
, and
h:[0,T_{2}-T_{1}-\tau]\to[0,1]
is strictly decreasing with
h(0)=1
and
h(T_{2}-T_{1}-\tau)=0
.

The hump shape captures the stylized empirical pattern of technological adoption: a ramp-up phase
(T_{1},T_{1}+\tau)
during which the productivity contribution rises to a peak
g(\tau)
, followed by a decline phase
(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2})
during which the contribution dissipates as the technology matures. The process is bounded and has compact support, so
\delta_{t}
never destabilizes the long-run growth rate of dividends; this preserves the standard cointegration structure outside the adoption window. The monotonicity and boundary conditions on
g
and
h
are the minimal primitives needed for our analysis of local explosiveness in Section
2.5
.

Remark 1
.

A simple parametric example is the triangular specification

\delta_{t}=\delta_{\max}\cdot\begin{cases}(t-T_{1})/\tau&\text{if }t\in[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau],\\[4.0pt]
(T_{2}-t)/(T_{2}-T_{1}-\tau)&\text{if }t\in(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2}],\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

which we use in our Monte Carlo experiments. The qualitative results hold for any hump-shaped specification satisfying Assumption
2
, including smooth bell-shaped functions, Beta densities rescaled to
[T_{1},T_{2}]
, or Gaussian kernels.

Assumption 3

(Constant Expected Returns)
.

\mathbb{E}_{t}[r_{t+1+j}]=\bar{r}
for all
j\geq 0
.

Remark 2
.

Assumption
3
is made for tractability. We relax it in Section
2.6
, where Proposition
8
shows that, when expected returns respond to the technology state as
\mathbb{E}_{t}[r_{t+1}]=\bar{r}+\phi\,\delta_{t}
, the qualitative explosive pattern is preserved for all
\phi<1
and is strictly
amplified
when
\phi<0
(technology lowers required returns).

With the dividend-growth structure in place, we can compute the fundamental log price in closed form. Substituting Assumption
1
and the constant-returns condition into the present-value identity from §
2.1
yields a clean separation of
f_{t}
into a level component tied to current dividends, a constant, and a discounted present-value term that isolates the technology contribution.

Proposition 1

(Fundamental Price Decomposition)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
and
b_{t}=0
,

f_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t},

where
C=\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\frac{c-\bar{r}}{1-\rho}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}
is the technology present-value term.

2.3
Microfoundation: A Production Economy with Innovation

The technology-augmented dividend specification used above admits a rigorous production-side foundation. Consider a standard Cobb–Douglas production economy with physical and information-technology (IT) capital, disembodied total factor productivity driven by patent quality and IT diffusion, and a representative firm that chooses labor, investment, and innovation to maximize the present value of dividends under a stochastic discount factor. Log-linearization around the balanced-growth path delivers
\Delta d_{t}=c+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
with
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(TFP_{t},\,\log IT_{t},\,\log Pat_{t})^{\prime}
, and a first-order VAR for
\mathbf{X}_{t}
yields the closed-form decomposition
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\eta_{t}
with
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\mathbf{I}-\rho\,\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-\top}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\eta_{t}
a mean-zero stationary innovation orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. Three identification conditions—block-separability of the production and valuation blocks, a predetermined patent index (grant lag
\ell\geq 1
), and the real IT Euler equation—ensure that
\mathbf{X}_{t}
spans the technology-driven component of fundamentals while the bubble
b_{t}
is excluded from the cointegrating vector on economic grounds. Proposition
2
below summarizes the result; the full derivation, including the seven-step construction (environment and preferences, production technology, firm problem, balanced-growth path, log-linearization, the technology-state VAR with assumption details, and identification), is relegated to Appendix
A
.

Proposition 2

(Observable Technology Proxies and Fundamental Value)
.

Under the production economy and identification conditions of Appendix
A
, to a first-order approximation around the balanced-growth path:

(i)

Log dividend growth satisfies
\Delta d_{t}=c+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
, with
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathbf{X}_{t}]=0
.

(ii)

The technology present-value term admits the decomposition
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\eta_{t}
, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\mathbf{I}-\rho\,\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-\top}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is the predictable loading on the observable state and
\eta_{t}
is a mean-zero, stationary, weighted sum of future VAR innovations that is orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.

(iii)

The fundamental price satisfies
f_{t}=d_{t}+\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\eta_{t}
, where
\beta_{0}=C
and
\eta_{t}
is the orthogonal innovation term from Part (ii).

Hence
\mathbf{X}_{t}
spans the technology-driven component of fundamentals, and the bubble
b_{t}
is identified from the residual of the structural cointegrating regression (
A.13
).

Two remarks complete the picture. First, the deterministic hump
\delta_{t}
of Assumption
2
is the conditional-mean path of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}
that would obtain under a technology-diffusion regime; the stochastic component
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}-\delta_{t}
is absorbed into the residual noise in the empirical specification (Remark
8
in Appendix
A
). Second, the bubble enters as an additive component in secondary-market valuation but is excluded from the production block determining
\mathbf{X}_{t}
on identifiable economic grounds (Remark
9
in Appendix
A
); even if stock valuations affect future innovation through financing constraints, the resulting variation in
\mathbf{X}_{t+j}
for
j\geq 1
operates through the fundamental mapping, not through the residual at date
t
.

2.4
Dynamics of the Fundamental Process

With the fundamental price characterized in levels by Proposition
1
and with the microfoundation of Section
2.3
ensuring that
\mathcal{T}_{t}
is spanned to first order by the observable state, we now turn to the
dynamic
implications of the technology component. The central question is how the hump-shaped path of
\delta_{t}
translates into the time series of
f_{t}
—specifically, what drift and what curvature the fundamental process inherits from the adoption–maturation cycle. The following lemma records a simple recursion for
\mathcal{T}_{t}
that proves repeatedly useful in the sequel, and the subsequent proposition uses it to express the conditional drift of
f_{t}
as an affine function of the current technology shock
\delta_{t}
and the lagged present value
\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
. This representation drives the local explosiveness result of Section
2.5
: it makes transparent how a bounded, deterministic hump in
\delta_{t}
can induce a super-unit-root pattern in
f_{t}
over a finite window.

Lemma 1

(Recursion of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
)
.

\mathcal{T}_{t}=\rho^{-1}\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-\rho^{-1}\,\delta_{t}
.

Proposition 3

(Fundamental Dynamics)
.

The fundamental price satisfies
f_{t}=f_{t-1}+\mu_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
, where

\mu_{t}=c+\delta_{t}+\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}

is a time-varying drift. Using the recursion of Lemma
1
:

\mu_{t}=c+(1-\rho^{-1})\delta_{t}+(\rho^{-1}-1)\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}.

Proposition
3
shows that the drift
\mu_{t}
is governed by the
level
of
\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
and the current shock
\delta_{t}
. The qualitative shape of the resulting dynamics—and in particular whether the log price–dividend ratio is merely increasing or is
convex
in
t
on an initial subinterval of the adoption window—depends on the curvature of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, not on the level. To isolate the curvature channel cleanly we impose a direct, high-level condition on the discounted present-value term itself.

Assumption 4

(Convexity of the Technology Present-Value Term)
.

There exists
\tau^{\star}\in(0,\tau]
such that the technology present-value term
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}
is strictly convex in
t
on the initial ramp-up subinterval
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau^{\star})
; equivalently,
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}>0
for all integer
t
with
T_{1}\leq t\leq T_{1}+\tau^{\star}-1
.

Remark 3

(Motivation and Scope of Assumption
4
)
.

This assumption replaces the stronger requirement “
g
strictly convex” from earlier drafts and is a direct, high-level restriction on the discounted present-value term
\mathcal{T}_{t}
rather than on the adoption shape
g
itself. Two facts make it natural. First, the identity

\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j-1}\,\Delta^{2}\delta_{t+j},

derived in the proof of Proposition
4
(Appendix
D
), shows that
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}
is a
\rho
-discounted weighted sum of future second differences of
\delta
. Second, the weaker primitive “
g
is strictly increasing with
g(0)=0
” in Assumption
2
is sufficient for the strict monotonicity
\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}>0
on
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau)
(which drives
\mu_{t}>c
) but is not sufficient for convexity. Lemma
2
below exhibits two primitive regimes—(i) strictly convex
g
, (ii) smooth hump profiles that are
C^{2}
with a strictly positive second derivative at the ramp-up origin—under which Assumption
4
is satisfied for an explicit, non-empty
\tau^{\star}
. The piecewise-linear triangular profile is a counterexample whose kink at the peak violates Assumption
4
; its treatment is therefore routed through the detrended-price theory of Theorem
2
, which does not require convexity of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

2.5
Local Explosiveness of Fundamentals

The drift representation of Proposition
3
shows that the sign and magnitude of
\mu_{t}-c
are governed by
(1-\rho^{-1})\delta_{t}+(\rho^{-1}-1)\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
. Because
\rho^{-1}>1
, the first term is negative in
\delta_{t}
while the second is positive in
\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
; during the early adoption phase,
\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
rises faster than
\delta_{t}
(forward-looking agents price in all still-unrealized future shocks), so the positive second term dominates and the fundamental process exhibits a drift that is strictly above its unconditional mean
c
. The next proposition records this heuristic formally: along the adoption–maturation cycle, the fundamental price passes through four qualitatively distinct regimes—a near-random-walk regime before adoption, a super-normal-growth regime early in adoption, a deceleration regime late in adoption, and a new-level random-walk regime after maturation. The key statistical implication is that the price-dividend ratio is strictly convex on an initial subinterval of the adoption window, which is precisely the property that drives the spurious-explosiveness result of Theorem
1
.

Proposition 4

(Local Explosiveness)
.

Suppose
b_{t}=0
for all
t
. Under Assumptions
1
–
3
and Assumption
4
:

(i)

Pre-adoption (
t<T_{1}
): When
t
is sufficiently far below
T_{1}
that
\rho^{T_{1}-t-1}
is numerically negligible,
\mathcal{T}_{t}
is negligibly small and
f_{t}
behaves, to that order, as a random walk with drift
c
. As
t
approaches
T_{1}
from below,
\mathcal{T}_{t}
rises monotonically above zero—remaining strictly smaller than its early-adoption values but no longer negligible—because forward-looking agents begin pricing in the approaching technology shock.

(ii)

Early adoption (
t\in[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau)
):
\mu_{t}>c
, and
f_{t}
exhibits super-normal growth. The log price-dividend ratio
f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
and, under Assumption
4
, is strictly convex in the integer time index
t
on
\{T_{1},T_{1}+1,\dots,T_{1}+\tau^{\star}-1\}
(equivalently,
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}>0
on this index set).

(iii)

Late adoption (
t\in(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2}]
): The drift
\mu_{t}
declines toward
c
.

(iv)

Post-maturation (
t>T_{2}
):
\mathcal{T}_{t}=0
and
f_{t}
reverts to a random walk with drift
c
at a permanently elevated level.

Remark.
The convexity claim in Part (ii) is what is subsequently invoked in Proposition
5
and Theorem

(v) to deliver the right-tail size distortion of the PSY statistic. The motivation for Assumption
4
and its relation to primitives on

are discussed after Proposition

\mathcal{T}_{t}
requires further conditions on the discounted future-shock profile and is not asserted here.

Lemma 2

(Sufficient Conditions for Assumption
4
)
.

Let
\delta_{t}
satisfy Assumption
2
. Then Assumption
4
holds (for some
\tau^{\star}\in(0,\tau]
) in each of the following cases:

(i)

Convex ramp-up: If

on
[0,\tau]
with
\inf_{x\in[0,\tau]}g^{\prime\prime}(x)>0

(ii)

The piecewise-linear triangular profile (Remark
1
) does
not
satisfy Assumption
4
on the ramp-up: its second-difference atoms at the kinks, weighted by the
\rho
-discounted future, produce
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}\leq 0
on interior ramp-up dates. This is a counterexample rather than an example, and is treated separately in the proof.

The intuition for each part is as follows. Well before adoption (
t\ll T_{1}
), no technology shocks are present and future shocks are heavily discounted, so
\mathcal{T}_{t}\approx 0
and the price follows approximately a standard random walk with drift. As
t
approaches
T_{1}
, forward-looking agents begin to price in the approaching technology shock, causing
\mathcal{T}_{t}
to rise above zero even before
T_{1}
. During the early adoption phase, the present value
\mathcal{T}_{t}
rises further because each additional period brings new positive future
\delta_{s}
values into the discounting window, while the current
\delta_{t}
is still small relative to the accumulated future shocks, so that the market “prices in” the technology boom. After the peak, remaining future shocks diminish and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
declines. After maturation, the technology component vanishes from the present value but its cumulative effect remains permanently embedded in the dividend level. The formal proof is provided in Appendix
D
.

Corollary 1

(Bubble-Like Pattern without a Bubble)
.

Under
b_{t}=0
, the log price-dividend ratio
f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
follows a hump-shaped deterministic path over
[T_{1},T_{2}]
, rising during the adoption phase
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau]
and declining during the maturation phase
(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2}]
. This trajectory is observationally similar to a “bubble and collapse” in the price-dividend ratio, despite being entirely fundamental.

2.6
Extensions

The baseline theory of Sections
2.1
–
2.5
rests on three simplifying restrictions: (i) constant expected log-return
\bar{r}
(Assumption
3
); (ii) a deterministic hump-shaped technology shock (Assumption
2
); and (iii) representative-asset analysis. Three extensions in Appendix
B
relax each in turn and establish that the spurious-explosiveness mechanism is preserved or strengthened in every case. Proposition
8
shows that under time-varying expected returns of the form
\mathbb{E}_{t}[r_{t+1+j}]=\bar{r}+\phi\,\delta_{t+1+j}
, the fundamental price becomes
f_{t}=d_{t}+C+(1-\phi)\,\mathcal{T}_{t}
, so a technology-induced decline in expected returns (
\phi<0
)
amplifies
the locally explosive pattern by the factor
1-\phi>1
. Proposition
9
formalizes Bayesian learning under stochastic technology uncertainty (Assumption
9
): positive technology news generates discrete upward jumps in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
that compound the deterministic hump; the asymptotic size distortion is unchanged at first order, while finite-sample rejection rates can rise substantially (e.g., from 10.4% to 29.0% in the P/D-ratio implementation, documented in Appendix
E.5
). Cross-sectional heterogeneity (
\delta_{i,t}=\lambda_{i}\delta_{t}
) implies that the aggregate market fundamental inherits the technology effect weighted by
\sum_{i}w_{i}\lambda_{i}
, so high-exposure, high-capitalization industries (e.g., late-1990s NASDAQ) are most susceptible to spurious detection. Remark
10
in Appendix
B
connects the stochastic extension to
25
: high prices during a learning phase reflect the conditional expectation
\hat{\delta}_{t}
entering
f_{t}
, not a speculative component
b_{t}
, and the technology-adjusted test correctly attributes these dynamics to fundamentals.

3
The PSY Test and Size Distortion

3.1
Review of the PSY Procedure

The PSY procedure
(
28
;
29
)
detects explosive behavior via recursive right-tailed augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) regressions. For subsample
[r_{1},r_{2}]
with
r_{2}-r_{1}\geq r_{0}
, define the BSADF statistic
\mathrm{BSADF}(r_{2})=\sup_{r_{1}\in[0,\,r_{2}-r_{0}]}\mathrm{ADF}_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}
and the GSADF statistic
\mathrm{GSADF}=\sup_{r_{2}\in[r_{0},1]}\mathrm{BSADF}(r_{2})
, where
\mathrm{ADF}_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}
is the right-tailed
t
-statistic on
\hat{\beta}
in

\Delta y_{t}=\hat{\alpha}_{r_{1},r_{2}}+\hat{\beta}_{r_{1},r_{2}}\,y_{t-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\gamma}_{k,r_{1},r_{2}}\,\Delta y_{t-k}+\hat{u}_{t}.

(5)

Under the null
H_{0}:y_{t}=y_{t-1}+\eta_{t}
, critical values are obtained by simulation. Date-stamping identifies bubble origination where
\mathrm{BSADF}(r_{2})
first exceeds its critical value and collapse where it falls back below.

3.2
The Size Distortion Problem

The null hypothesis of the PSY test is a pure random walk. Under our technology-augmented data-generating process, however, the observables do not follow a random walk even in the absence of a bubble. We consider two common empirical implementations.

Implementation 1: Detrended log prices.

Since
f_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
and
d_{t}=d_{0}+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\delta_{s}+\varepsilon_{s})
, the fundamental price is

f_{t}=d_{0}+C+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\delta_{s}+\varepsilon_{s})+\mathcal{T}_{t}.

After removing a linear trend (as is common practice before applying unit root tests), the detrended process is

\tilde{f}_{t}=f_{t}-(\hat{a}+\hat{b}\,t)\approx\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+\underbrace{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\delta_{s}+\mathcal{T}_{t}-(\text{linear projection of these on }t)}_{\text{hump-shaped residual}}.

The cumulative technology term
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\delta_{s}+\mathcal{T}_{t}
is a hump-shaped function of
t
that is not well approximated by a linear trend. The residual after detrending retains a convex, locally explosive component during
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau]
.

Implementation 2: Log price-dividend ratio.

From Proposition
1
,
f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
. In the absence of a technology shock, the log price-dividend ratio is the constant
C
. With a technology shock, it follows the hump-shaped path
C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
. PSY applied to
\{f_{t}-d_{t}\}
will detect the rising phase of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
as explosive behavior.

3.3
Formal Size Distortion Result

We now state the main econometric result of the paper.

Theorem 1

(Size Distortion of the PSY Test under Technology-Augmented Fundamentals)
.

Let
\{f_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be generated by the data-generating process of Propositions
1
–
3
with
b_{t}=0
and a technology shock satisfying Assumption
2
with
\delta_{\max}>0
. Let
\psi_{T}
denote the rejection probability of the
\mathrm{GSADF}
test at nominal level
\alpha
, where critical values are computed under the driftless random walk null
H_{0}:y_{t}=y_{t-1}+\eta_{t}
. Then:

(i)

\psi_{T}>\alpha
for all
T
sufficiently large, provided the technology window
[T_{1},T_{2}]
is contained in the sample.

(ii)

Larger peak technology shocks magnify the deterministic component driving the GSADF statistic. In particular, in both implementations
\psi_{T}\to 1
along any sequence with
\sqrt{T}\,\delta_{\max}(T)\to\infty
.

(v)

In the case of the log price-dividend ratio
f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
, the rejection probability
\psi_{T}\to 1
as
\delta_{\max}\to\infty
for any fixed nominal level
\alpha
.

Remark 4

(Comparative Statics in Discount Factor)
.

In the case of the log price-dividend ratio
f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
, a higher
\rho
raises the deterministic profile
\mathcal{T}_{t}
pointwise during the adoption phase and therefore strengthens the local explosive signal on ramp-up windows. For detrended log prices, the relationship need not be monotone: lower
\rho
can sharpen the hump sufficiently to offset the lower level.

Remark 5

(Comparative Statics in Adoption Duration)
.

Under the self-similar duration family of Assumption
7
, lengthening the adoption window increases the local deterministic signal on ramp-up windows. Consequently, once that signal is sufficiently strong, the induced rejection probability rises with
T_{2}-T_{1}
.

The formal proof is given in Appendix
D
. The key mechanism is that the technology shock introduces a locally deterministic trending component into the process under the null, causing spurious rejections for both detrended log prices and the log price-dividend ratio. The formal asymptotic treatment is developed in Theorem
2
and Proposition
5
below.

Remark 6
.

Theorem
1
is stated for the GSADF test, but identical conclusions apply to the BSADF date-stamping procedure: the BSADF statistic will cross the critical value sequence near
T_{1}
(origination of the spurious “bubble”) and fall back near
T_{1}+\tau
(spurious “collapse”), precisely the pattern one would observe if there were a genuine bubble during the technology adoption phase.

Remark 7
.

The source of size distortion we identify is fundamentally different from the drift-induced distortion discussed in the unit root literature
(
34
, e.g.,)
. A constant drift in log prices can be removed by detrending. Our result arises from a
hump-shaped, nonlinear
deterministic component that cannot be removed by standard linear detrending.

To formally establish the size distortion properties described in Theorem
1
, we embed the technology shock in a local asymptotic framework. This embedding, standard in the analysis of unit root tests against local alternatives
(
31
;
27
)
, yields non-degenerate continuous-time limits of the test statistics under the technology-augmented data-generating process.

Assumption 5

(Local Asymptotic Embedding)
.

The technology shock from Assumption
2
is embedded as a triangular array

\delta_{t,T}=\delta_{\max}(T)\,h(t/T),\qquad\delta_{\max}(T)=c_{\delta}/\sqrt{T},\qquad c_{\delta}>0,

where the adoption window is proportional to the sample size, with fixed fractions

\lambda_{1}=T_{1}/T,\qquad\lambda_{2}=T_{2}/T,\qquad\kappa=\tau/(T_{2}-T_{1}),

and where
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous, supported on
[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
, normalized by
\max_{r\in[0,1]}h(r)=1
, strictly increasing on
[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}+\kappa(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})]
, and strictly decreasing on
[\lambda_{1}+\kappa(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}]
.

Assumption 6

(Local Stochastic Technology Scaling)
.

Under the local framework of Assumption
5
, the stochastic technology process from Assumption
9
satisfies the triangular-array scaling

g_{\star,T}(s)=T^{-1}h_{\star}(s/T)+o(T^{-1})

for some bounded continuous

[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]

\mu_{\delta}=O(\sqrt{T})
, prior standard deviation
\sigma_{\delta}=O(\sqrt{T})
, and prior variance
P_{0}=T\tau_{\delta}^{2}
for some
\tau_{\delta}>0
.

Assumption 7

(Self-Similar Duration Family)
.

For the duration comparative static, the normalized local shape belongs to a dilation family

h_{L}(r)=h_{0}\!\left(\frac{r-\lambda_{1}}{L}\right)\mathbf{1}\{\lambda_{1}\leq r\leq\lambda_{1}+L\},\qquad L\in(0,1-\lambda_{1}],

where
h_{0}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous,
\max_{u\in[0,1]}h_{0}(u)=1
, strictly increasing on
[0,\kappa]
, and strictly decreasing on
[\kappa,1]
.

The rate
T^{-1/2}
is dictated by the requirement that the cumulated technology shock
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\delta_{s,T}
(which enters the detrended log price at magnitude
O(T\cdot\delta_{\max}(T))
) be commensurate with the
O(\sqrt{T})
stochastic trend from the random walk component of dividends.

Theorem 2

(Contaminated Brownian Motion Limit)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
5
with
b_{t}=0
, define the integrated hump
G(r)=\int_{0}^{r}h(s)\mathbf{1}\{\lambda_{1}\leq s\leq\lambda_{2}\}\,ds
and its linearly detrended version
g(r)=G(r)-\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{1}r
, where
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})=\arg\min\int_{0}^{1}[G(r)-a-br]^{2}dr
. Then, as
T\to\infty
:

(a)

The standardized detrended log price converges weakly:

T^{-1/2}\tilde{f}_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}\Rightarrow Z_{c_{\delta}}(r):=\sigma W(r)+c_{\delta}\,g(r),

where

is a standard Brownian motion and

.

(b)

For any subsample window

with

\mathrm{ADF}_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}(r_{1},r_{2};\,c_{\delta}),

where the functional

\mathcal{F}(r_{1},r_{2};\,c_{\delta})=\frac{\frac{1}{2}r_{w}\big(Z_{c_{\delta}}(r_{2})^{2}-Z_{c_{\delta}}(r_{1})^{2}-\sigma^{2}r_{w}\big)-\big(\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}Z_{c_{\delta}}\,dr\big)\big(Z_{c_{\delta}}(r_{2})-Z_{c_{\delta}}(r_{1})\big)}{\Big[\sigma^{2}r_{w}\Big\{r_{w}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}Z_{c_{\delta}}^{2}\,dr-\big(\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}Z_{c_{\delta}}\,dr\big)^{2}\Big\}\Big]^{1/2}}.

(6)

When

(

.

(c)

The

\mathrm{GSADF}_{T}\Rightarrow\sup_{(r_{1},r_{2})\,\in\,\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{F}(r_{1},r_{2};\,c_{\delta}),

where

(d)

Size distortion for sufficiently large local contamination.
Let

enters the continuous-time limit of the detrended price and shifts the distribution of the ADF functional to the right on any subsample where

We next consider the log price-dividend ratio, where the technology component enters as a level effect rather than through cumulation.

Proposition 5

(Deterministic Limit for the Price-Dividend Ratio)
.

Suppose the log price-dividend ratio is

y_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t,T}+u_{t},

where
u_{t}
is a stationary process with
\mathbb{E}[u_{t}]=0
,
\mathrm{Var}(u_{t})=\sigma_{u}^{2}>0
, and in fact
u_{t}
is white noise; correspondingly, the ADF regression is estimated with
K=0
. Let
q(r)
denote the continuous-time limit of
\mathcal{T}_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor,T}/\delta_{\max}(T)
, and define the window-demeaned shape

\tilde{q}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(r)=q(r)-\frac{1}{r_{w}}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}q(s)\,ds,\qquad r_{w}:=r_{2}-r_{1}.

Fix a subsample window
[r_{1},r_{2}]\subseteq[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}+\kappa(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})]
with
r_{w}\geq r_{0}>0
, so the window lies strictly inside the ramp-up phase. Assume that
q\in C^{1}
is strictly increasing and strictly convex on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
, and that

\frac{\delta_{\max}(T)^{2}}{T\sigma_{u}^{2}}\longrightarrow\infty.

Then

T\,\hat{\beta}_{r_{1},r_{2}}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}B(r_{1},r_{2})=\frac{\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(r)\,q^{\prime}(r)\,dr}{\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(r)^{2}\,dr},\qquad B(r_{1},r_{2})>0.

Moreover,

t_{\hat{\beta}}=\frac{B(r_{1},r_{2})\,\delta_{\max}(T)}{\sigma_{u}\sqrt{T}}\{1+o_{p}(1)\},

so the ADF
t
-statistic on that window diverges to
+\infty
whenever
\delta_{\max}(T)/(\sigma_{u}\sqrt{T})\to\infty
, equivalently under the display above. Consequently, if the BSADF/GSADF admissible class contains such a ramp-up window, the corresponding supremum statistic also diverges in probability.

The intuition for Proposition
5
is that the augmented Dickey–Fuller regression fits a local first-order autoregression to a deterministic, convex path. The ordinary least squares coefficient
\hat{\beta}
captures the accelerating growth rate during the ramp-up, producing a positive (explosive) estimate. Crucially, the signal arises from the
curvature
of
q(r)
, not merely its slope: were
q
locally affine, demeaning within the window would eliminate the trend and
B(r_{1},r_{2})=0
. The convexity of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
during the adoption phase, a direct consequence of the hump-shaped technology profile, is therefore the key mechanism.

4
A Fundamental-versus-Speculative Decomposition Procedure

4.1
The Adjustment Procedure

The size distortion documented in Appendix
E
arises because the standard PSY test does not account for the technology-driven component of fundamentals. Our remedy is to estimate this component and remove it before applying PSY.

Definition 2

(Technology-Adjusted PSY Test)
.

Let
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
denote the series to which the PSY procedure is applied.

(i)

If
y_{t}=f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}+u_{t}
is the log price-dividend ratio, define

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=y_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}.

(ii)

If
y_{t}=f_{t}
(or
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
) is the log price level, define

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=y_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\delta}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}.

The adjusted series
y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}
removes the deterministic technology component while preserving any genuine speculative bubble component
b_{t}
.

4.2
Estimation of
\mathcal{T}_{t}

In practice,
\mathcal{T}_{t}
is not directly observable and must be estimated. We outline three approaches, in order of increasing data requirements:

Approach 1: Direct construction from technology proxies.

If a time series of technology-driven dividend growth increments
\hat{\delta}_{t}
is available (e.g., from patent flows, research and development expenditure growth, or productivity measures), one can construct

\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\hat{\rho}^{\,j}\,\hat{\delta}_{t+1+j},

where
\hat{\rho}
is estimated from the historical price-dividend ratio and the sum is truncated at a finite horizon
J
(contributions beyond
J
are negligible for
\hat{\rho}<1
). If future values of
\hat{\delta}
are not available, one can use
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\hat{\rho}^{\,j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\hat{\delta}_{t+1+j}]
with forecasts from an auxiliary model (e.g., an AR process fitted to
\hat{\delta}_{t}
).

Approach 2: Semiparametric extraction.

One can estimate the deterministic component of the price-dividend ratio nonparametrically, e.g., by fitting a smooth function of time:

f_{t}-d_{t}=m(t/T)+u_{t},

where
m(\cdot)
is estimated by local polynomial regression or a penalized spline. The estimated
\hat{m}(t/T)
serves as a proxy for
C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

Approach 3: Regime-based estimation.

During periods identified as non-bubble by PSY (“training periods”), estimate the long-run relationship between prices, dividends, and technology proxies via a cointegrating regression. Use the estimated model to construct the technology component during the periods flagged as bubbles.

4.3
Properties of the Adjusted Test

Proposition 6

(Size of the Adjusted Test)
.

Suppose the technology adjustment is implemented as in Definition
2
. For the log-price implementation, assume

\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\hat{\delta}_{s}-\delta_{s})\right|\xrightarrow{p}0,\qquad\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}|\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}-\mathcal{T}_{t}|\xrightarrow{p}0.

Then, under the data-generating process of Assumptions
1
–
3
with
b_{t}=0
, the technology-adjusted PSY test applied to detrended log prices has asymptotic size equal to the nominal level
\alpha
. In the log price-dividend-ratio implementation (under the weaker assumption
\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}|\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}-\mathcal{T}_{t}|\xrightarrow{p}0
), the same adjustment removes the technology component up to
o_{p}(1)
, leaving
C+u_{t}+o_{p}(1)
and hence no technology-driven spurious explosiveness.

Proposition 7

(Power of the Adjusted Test)
.

Suppose the technology adjustment is implemented as in Definition
2
, and that the corresponding estimation errors satisfy the uniform-consistency conditions of Proposition
6
. Under the alternative
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
with
b_{t}>0
exhibiting explosive behavior, the technology-adjusted PSY test applied to detrended log prices retains the same asymptotic power as the standard PSY test applied to the technology-free series. In the log price-dividend-ratio implementation, the adjustment leaves
C+u_{t}+b_{t}+o_{p}(1)
, so the explosive component
b_{t}
is preserved.

Our Monte Carlo evidence confirms these theoretical predictions: the “no technology” rejection rates in Table
E.2
correspond to perfect knowledge of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
(the “oracle” adjusted test), and the rejection rates are uniformly at or below the nominal level.

5
Empirical Applications

The preceding sections establish theoretically and via simulation that technology-driven fundamentals can generate locally explosive price dynamics that are observationally indistinguishable from speculative bubbles. We now apply the fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition alongside the standard PSY test to two episodes that bookend the digital age: the late-1990s NASDAQ dot-com run-up and the AI-driven rally of the 2020s.

5.1
The Identification Challenge

A fundamental obstacle in empirical bubble detection is that the econometrician observes only the market price
p_{t}
, the sum of the fundamental value
f_{t}
and any bubble component
b_{t}
:

p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}.

During a potential bubble period,
f_{t}
is not directly observable: it cannot be “read off” from prices, because prices themselves are potentially contaminated by the bubble. The result is a circularity—testing for a bubble requires knowing the fundamental, but knowing the fundamental requires knowing whether there is a bubble.

Our theoretical framework (Section
2
) shows that the fundamental price is a function of dividends, technology-driven growth, and discount rates:

f_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t},

where
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+1+j}
aggregates the future path of technology-driven dividend growth. In principle, if we could observe the technology component
\delta_{t}
and the discount factor
\rho
, we could construct
\mathcal{T}_{t}
directly. In practice, the technology path is uncertain, agents’ information sets are richer than the econometrician’s, and the mapping from observable technology indicators to
\delta_{t}
is imprecise.

We address this challenge by adapting the counterfactual estimation approach of
18
. The key insight is that during
non-bubble periods
, the observed price equals the fundamental (up to stationary noise), so one can use these periods to estimate the relationship between the fundamental and observable covariates. During the potential bubble period, the estimated relationship is used to construct a counterfactual fundamental, and PSY is applied to the residual.

5.2
Counterfactual Fundamental Estimation

5.2.1  Setup

Let
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(x_{1t},x_{2t},\ldots,x_{kt})^{\prime}
denote a
k
-vector of observable covariates that are correlated with the fundamental value but are not directly affected by a speculative bubble in the stock price. The sample is divided into two subperiods:

•

Training period
\mathcal{T}_{0}=\{1,\ldots,T_{0}\}
: A period during which no bubble is present, so
b_{t}=0
and
p_{t}=f_{t}+u_{t}
, where
u_{t}
is a stationary noise term (e.g., microstructure noise, transitory discount rate variation).

•

Evaluation period
\mathcal{T}_{1}=\{T_{0}+1,\ldots,T\}
: The period during which a bubble may be present.

During the training period, the fundamental satisfies the long-run equilibrium relationship:

p_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}+u_{t},\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}_{0},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
captures the mapping from technology and financial covariates to the fundamental price. This regression is well-defined because
b_{t}=0
during
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, so
p_{t}=f_{t}+u_{t}
and the covariates explain fundamental variation.

During the evaluation period, the counterfactual fundamental is constructed as:

\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t},\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}_{1},

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is estimated from the training period. The technology-adjusted price is then:

p_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=p_{t}-\hat{f}_{t}=(f_{t}-\hat{f}_{t})+b_{t}=\hat{u}_{t}+b_{t},

where
\hat{u}_{t}=f_{t}-\hat{f}_{t}
is the estimation error. If
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is consistent and
\mathbf{X}_{t}
spans the fundamental, then
\hat{u}_{t}
is stationary and the adjusted series inherits the explosive dynamics of
b_{t}
(if any) without the confounding technology component.

PSY is then applied to
\{p_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Under the null of no bubble (
b_{t}=0
), the adjusted series is approximately stationary (the estimation error
\hat{u}_{t}
from a well-specified regression), and the test should not reject. Under the alternative of a genuine bubble (
b_{t}>0
with explosive dynamics), the bubble component dominates
\hat{u}_{t}
and the test retains power.

5.2.2  Covariate Selection

The covariates
\mathbf{X}_{t}
must satisfy two requirements: (a) they are informative about the fundamental value (relevance), and (b) they are not directly affected by the speculative bubble in stock prices (exogeneity to the bubble). The production-side microfoundation of Section
2.3
provides a structural justification for both requirements: Proposition
2
shows that
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(TFP_{t},\,\log IT_{t},\,\log Pat_{t})^{\prime}
spans the technology-driven component of fundamentals, while Remark
9
establishes the separability of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
from the bubble
b_{t}
. Our baseline specification focuses on these three technology covariates.

Baseline.

These variables capture the technology-driven component
\mathcal{T}_{t}
of the fundamental, each corresponding to a distinct structural margin in the production model of Section
2.3
.
Real IT investment
(BEA NIPA, deflated by the Consumer Price Index (CPI)) captures the diffusion and adoption margin through economy-wide IT capital deepening (
G_{t}
in the model), driven by the expected marginal product of IT capital (equation
A.7
) rather than speculative dynamics.
TFP
(Fernald’s utilization-adjusted series from the San Francisco Fed) captures the realized productivity margin (
a_{t}
) and is determined by the production function, not by financial-market speculation.
Patent grants
from the USPTO measure the invention margin (
q_{t-\ell}
); the examination process introduces a lag
\ell\geq 1
between application and grant, rendering grants largely predetermined with respect to contemporaneous speculative dynamics.

Robustness.

We also consider extended specifications that include financial fundamentals and macroeconomic controls as robustness checks. These include aggregate revenue, aggregate book equity, and industrial production to capture the non-technology component of fundamentals, as well as the 10-year Treasury yield, credit spreads, and CPI to capture discount rate and inflationary effects. We also consider aggregate R&D expenditure for NASDAQ-listed firms, though it is excluded from the baseline due to data availability constraints in the earlier training period.

5.2.3  Econometric Specification

Since log stock prices and several covariates (R&D expenditure, earnings, industrial production) are likely integrated of order one, direct ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation of (
7
) in levels may produce spurious results. We address this through two approaches.

Approach 1: Cointegrating regression.

If
p_{t}
and
\mathbf{X}_{t}
are cointegrated, as the present-value model predicts since the log price should share a common stochastic trend with log fundamentals, then (
7
) can be estimated by DOLS, which provides asymptotically efficient and median-unbiased estimates of the cointegrating vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. We use a parsimonious specification with three technology covariates and include leads and lags of
\Delta\mathbf{X}_{t}
in the DOLS specification to correct for endogeneity of the regressors:

p_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}+\sum_{j=-q}^{q}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}^{\prime}\Delta\mathbf{X}_{t-j}+u_{t}.

(8)

We set
q=1
in the baseline and test for cointegration using the Engle–Granger residual-based test.

Approach 2: First-difference regression.

As a robustness check, we estimate the relationship in first differences:

\Delta p_{t}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}\Delta\mathbf{X}_{t}+v_{t},\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}_{0}.

The counterfactual is then constructed by cumulating the fitted values:
\hat{f}_{t}=p_{T_{0}}+\sum_{s=T_{0}+1}^{t}\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}\Delta\mathbf{X}_{s}
. This approach avoids the cointegration assumption but may be less efficient.

5.3
Endogeneity and Identification

The key identification assumption is that
\mathbf{X}_{t}
is not directly affected by the speculative bubble
b_{t}
. The production-side microfoundation of Section
2.3
provides the theoretical basis for this assumption: the bubble enters as an additive component in the secondary-market valuation (
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
) but does not appear in the production block that determines
\mathbf{X}_{t}
(Remark
9
). This
separability
implies that the technology variables load on the fundamental
f_{t}
but not on
b_{t}
. We reinforce this structural argument with four empirical strategies.

Structural separability.

The production economy of Section
2.3
implies that the technology observables
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(TFP_{t},\,\log IT_{t},\,\log Pat_{t})^{\prime}
are determined by the real side of the economy (productivity, capital accumulation, and innovation), not by speculative dynamics in equity markets. In particular: (i) patent grants reflect the latent innovation quality
q_{t-\ell}
with
\ell\geq 1
, making them predetermined with respect to contemporaneous market conditions because successful R&D must be undertaken, filed, examined, and only later granted; (ii) TFP is a production-side measure of technology and factor utilization; and (iii) IT investment is pinned down by the optimality condition (
A.7
), which equates the cost of IT capital to its expected marginal product, a fundamental object, not to speculative resale value. Even if stock-market valuations affect future innovation or IT adoption through financing constraints, the resulting variation operates through future productivity and dividends, and is therefore absorbed by the fundamental mapping
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}
in (
A.13
), not by the residual bubble
b_{t}
.

Training-period estimation.

Following
18
, the regression is estimated exclusively during the training period
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, when
b_{t}=0
by assumption. Even if the bubble subsequently affects some covariates (e.g., a stock-price bubble may induce additional R&D spending via relaxed financing constraints), this feedback does not contaminate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
because no bubble is present during estimation. The counterfactual
\hat{f}_{t}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}
during the evaluation period may incorporate some bubble-induced variation in
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, but this operates through the
fundamental relationship
(the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
mapping), not through the bubble itself. The resulting bias is conservative: if the bubble inflates
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, the counterfactual
\hat{f}_{t}
is biased upward, making the adjusted series
p_{t}-\hat{f}_{t}

smaller
and PSY
less
likely to reject. Hence any bubble detected in the adjusted series is robust to this source of contamination.

Lagged covariates.

To further mitigate contemporaneous feedback, we use lagged values of the covariates:
\mathbf{X}_{t-h}
with
h\geq 1
(e.g.,
h=3
months). This ensures that even if a bubble at time
t
affects
\mathbf{X}_{t}
contemporaneously, the regression uses pre-determined values that cannot reflect the current bubble.

Placebo and robustness tests.

We conduct several diagnostic checks: (i) a
pre-bubble placebo test
that applies PSY to the adjusted series during a known non-bubble subperiod to verify correct size; (ii)
leave-one-out analysis
to assess sensitivity to individual covariates; and (iii) comparison of results across DOLS, FMOLS, and first-difference specifications.

5.6.7  Discussion

The AI-era results, combined with the dot-com findings, yield three conclusions. (1) The technology-adjustment methodology is structurally identical across both episodes, yet it produces asymmetric outcomes. In the dot-com case, both tests reject: the adjustment sharpens dating but confirms speculation. In the AI era, the standard test rejects at 10% while the adjusted test eliminates the signal entirely. This divergence addresses the identification problem: within the technology-adjusted framework, the explosive dynamics in the AI-era price are consistent with fundamental repricing rather than speculation. (2) The AI rally differs from dot-com: (i) substantial IT investment and patenting growth; (ii) less extreme price-to-fundamental acceleration; (iii) concentration in profitable incumbents. (3) The contrasting results across the two episodes suggest a practical surveillance protocol. Central banks and regulators applying PSY should routinely apply the technology-adjusted procedure in parallel to obtain a more granular diagnosis of explosive dynamics. If both tests reject, as in the dot-com case, there is strong evidence of a speculative component above and beyond technology-driven fundamentals. If the standard test rejects but the technology-adjusted test does not, as in our AI-era application, the explosive dynamics are attributable to technology-driven fundamental repricing rather than speculation. This two-test protocol directly addresses the identification problem that arises during periods of rapid technological change.

5.8
Robustness of the Identification Strategy

The identification strategy rests on the separability of technology proxies from the speculative bubble component. Appendix
F

Second, placebo tests using non-technology covariates (consumer sentiment, VIX, industrial production) fail to replicate the bubble-elimination effect, confirming that the technology proxies capture specific fundamental content rather than generic macro variation. Third, PCA of the Magnificent Seven price gaps reveals a dominant common factor (60.0% of variance) with no explosive behavior in any linear combination, providing cross-sectional evidence consistent with fundamental repricing rather than speculation. Fourth, training-window stability analysis shows that the AI-era non-rejection finding holds across all 121 training windows (
\text{GSADF}\in[-0.30,0.84]
, all below
\text{CV}_{0.10}=1.176
); the dot-com results are more sensitive, driven primarily by the patent coefficient. Fifth, the
15

L_{c}
test rejects parameter constancy in both eras, qualifying the DOLS estimates as reflecting an average equilibrium, a limitation that the training-window robustness, particularly for the AI era, mitigates.

6
Conclusion

This paper develops a fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition framework for asset prices during general-purpose technology adoption. The central finding is that technology-driven structural change in fundamentals generates locally explosive price dynamics that the standard PSY bubble test systematically misclassifies as speculative, a misidentification that matters most precisely when the bubble question is most pressing. Applying the framework to two bookend episodes of the digital age, the 2020–2025 AI-driven rally and the late-1990s dot-com run-up, we reach sharply different conclusions. Conditional on the technology-adjusted framework, no evidence of speculative behavior remains in the AI era at any conventional significance level once technology fundamentals, including total factor productivity, information technology investment, and patent grants, are accounted for. In contrast, the dot-com episode was partially speculative: while the technology adjustment eliminates an early spurious signal, it confirms the presence of genuine speculation from December 1999 to March 2000. Firm-level evidence from the Magnificent Seven largely reinforces these aggregate findings: five of the seven firms show no explosive behavior after adjusting for firm-specific fundamentals, although Tesla and Apple retain residual signals that may reflect idiosyncratic speculative dynamics. These results support the view that the current AI era reflects a structural shift in productivity rather than a repeat of the late 1990s exuberance.

The framework makes a broader methodological point: standard bubble tests applied during technological transitions risk a paradigm mistake, confusing fundamental repricing for speculative excess. The toolkit for bubble detection has remained largely statistical. Just as risk management evolved from the static Value-at-Risk (VaR) threshold to the more informative tail-risk measures of Conditional VaR (CVaR) and the forward-looking, market-consistent Mark-to-Market VaR (MMVaR) of
9
, the econometrics of bubble detection must evolve from detecting statistical explosiveness to diagnosing its economic source. Our framework is a step in that direction: technology-driven structural change in fundamentals can generate locally explosive dynamics that satisfy all the statistical signatures of a bubble without any speculative component being present, and the decomposition restores correct test size while preserving power against genuine bubbles. The results are robust across alternative shock shapes (triangular, Gaussian, Beta, and Gamma-like), estimation methods (DOLS, FMOLS, and first-differencing), and 121 alternative training windows for the AI era.

These findings suggest a two-test surveillance protocol for central banks and financial regulators conducting real-time bubble monitoring. During periods of rapid technological transition, regulators should run the standard and technology-adjusted PSY tests in parallel. If both reject the null of no bubble, as in the dot-com case, there is strong evidence of a speculative component. If the standard test rejects but the adjusted test does not, as in the recent AI case, the explosive dynamics are more consistent with fundamental repricing than with speculation. This protocol offers a more nuanced diagnostic for policy, helping avoid premature interventions that could stifle technological adoption and growth.

Appendix A
Microfoundation: A Production Economy with Innovation

This appendix provides the production-economy microfoundation for the technology-adjusted PSY test summarized in Section
2.3
. We derive the dividend-growth representation
\Delta d_{t}=c+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
, the closed-form expression for the technology present-value term
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}+\eta_{t}
, and the identification conditions (R1)–(R3) that justify treating the bubble component as orthogonal to the observable technology state
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(TFP_{t},\log IT_{t},\log Pat_{t})^{\prime}
.

A.1
Environment and Preferences

Time is discrete,
t=0,1,2,\dots
. The economy is populated by a unit mass of ex ante identical, infinitely lived households who consume a single final good, supply labor inelastically, and trade shares in a representative firm and a one-period riskless bond. Let
C_{t}
denote aggregate consumption and
\mathcal{F}_{t}
the information set available at date
t
. Households have preferences over consumption streams represented by the stochastic discount factor (SDF)
\{M_{t,t+j}\}_{j\geq 0}
, with one-period SDF
M_{t+1}\equiv M_{t,t+1}
satisfying the Euler equations

\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[M_{t+1}\,R_{t+1}^{i}\right]=1,\qquad i\in\{\text{equity},\text{bond}\},

(A.1)

for each traded asset with gross return
R_{t+1}^{i}
. We assume throughout this subsection that
M_{t+1}>0
almost surely and that
\mathbb{E}_{t}[M_{t+1}]
is a strictly positive,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variable bounded away from
0
and
\infty
. Under the log-linear approximation of Campbell and Shiller (1988) that we invoke in Section
A.5
, (
A.1
) collapses to the constant expected log-return condition
\mathbb{E}_{t}[r_{t+1}]=\bar{r}
used in Assumption
3
, so the two layers are consistent. The standard transversality condition

\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[M_{t,t+j}\,P_{t+j}\right]=0

is imposed on the fundamental component; the bubble component
b_{t}
, by definition, is the solution of the martingale equation
\mathbb{E}_{t}[M_{t+1}b_{t+1}]=b_{t}
that violates transversality when
b_{t}\neq 0
.

A.2
Production Technology

A representative firm produces output using a constant-returns Cobb–Douglas technology that combines physical capital
K_{t}
, information-technology (IT) capital
G_{t}
, and labor
L_{t}
with disembodied total factor productivity
A_{t}
:

Y_{t}\;=\;A_{t}\,K_{t}^{\alpha}\,G_{t}^{\eta}\,L_{t}^{1-\alpha-\eta},\qquad\alpha,\eta\in(0,1),\quad\alpha+\eta<1.

(A.2)

The two capital stocks evolve according to

K_{t+1}=(1-\delta_{K})\,K_{t}+I_{t}^{K},\qquad G_{t+1}=(1-\delta_{G})\,G_{t}+I_{t}^{IT},

(A.3)

where
\delta_{K},\delta_{G}\in(0,1)
are depreciation rates and
I_{t}^{K},I_{t}^{IT}\geq 0
are gross investments. Disembodied productivity follows the process

\Delta a_{t+1}\;=\;\mu_{A}\;+\;\alpha_{q}\,q_{t}\;+\;\alpha_{I}\,i_{t}^{IT}\;+\;u_{t+1}^{A},\qquad\alpha_{q},\alpha_{I}>0,

(A.4)

where
a_{t}\equiv\log A_{t}
,
i_{t}^{IT}\equiv\log I_{t}^{IT}
,
q_{t}
is a patent-quality index (defined below), and
u_{t+1}^{A}
is a mean-zero innovation orthogonal to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
with finite variance. The coefficients
(\alpha_{q},\alpha_{I})
parameterize the two complementary channels through which innovation lifts TFP: the
invention
margin (quality of newly granted patents) and the
diffusion
margin (adoption and use of IT capital).

We assume a patent-grant lag
\ell\geq 1
: if
Q_{t}
denotes the true (unobserved) innovation-quality shock at date
t
, the granted patent index
Pat_{t+\ell}
observed at date
t+\ell
is a deterministic function of
\{Q_{s}\}_{s\leq t}
and of pre-
t
variables only. This makes
Pat_{t}
a predetermined variable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, a fact we use in Section
A.7
to rule out contemporaneous feedback from the bubble into the patent measure. The
observable technology vector
is

\mathbf{X}_{t}\;\equiv\;(TFP_{t},\;\log IT_{t},\;\log Pat_{t})^{\prime},

with
TFP_{t}
the Solow residual constructed from (
A.2
),
IT_{t}=I_{t}^{IT}
, and
Pat_{t}
the granted-patent index.

A.3
Firm Problem and First-Order Conditions

The firm chooses contingent plans for labor, physical investment, IT investment, and innovation spending to maximize the present value of dividends paid to shareholders under the SDF
M_{t,t+j}
:

\max_{\{L_{t},I_{t}^{K},I_{t}^{IT},q_{t}\}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{0}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}M_{0,t}\,D_{t},

(A.5)

subject to (
A.2
)–(
A.4
), where the dividend (residual cash flow) is

D_{t}\;=\;Y_{t}\;-\;w_{t}\,L_{t}\;-\;I_{t}^{K}\;-\;I_{t}^{IT}\;-\;\Phi\!\left(I_{t}^{IT}/G_{t}\right)\,G_{t}\;-\;\Xi(q_{t}).

(A.6)

Here
w_{t}
is the real wage;
\Phi(\cdot)
is a
C^{2}
, strictly convex IT-investment adjustment-cost function normalized so that
\Phi(\delta_{G})=0
and
\Phi^{\prime}(\delta_{G})=0
at the steady-state rate
\delta_{G}
; and
\Xi(\cdot)
is a
C^{2}
, strictly convex innovation cost. Attaching Lagrange multipliers to the two capital laws in (
A.3
) and differentiating, the firm’s first-order conditions are:

Labor.

\;(1-\alpha-\eta)\,Y_{t}/L_{t}=w_{t}
.

Physical capital (standard
Q
-equation).

\;1=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[M_{t+1}\left(\alpha\,Y_{t+1}/K_{t+1}+1-\delta_{K}\right)\right]
.

IT capital (the key Euler equation).

1\;+\;\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(I_{t}^{IT}/G_{t}\right)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[M_{t+1}\left(\eta\,Y_{t+1}/G_{t+1}\;+\;1-\delta_{G}\right)\right].

(A.7)

Innovation quality.

\;\Xi^{\prime}(q_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M_{t,t+j}\,\alpha_{q}\,Y_{t+j}/A_{t+j}\right]
, i.e., the marginal cost of innovation equals the expected discounted marginal product of the resulting patent quality operating through (
A.4
).

Equation (
A.7
) is the central identifying restriction of the microfoundation: it ties IT investment to the
real
marginal product of IT capital,
\eta Y_{t+1}/G_{t+1}
, and to the SDF—not to the secondary-market price of equity, and hence not to the bubble
b_{t}
. This separability is what permits us in Section
A.7
to exclude
b_{t}
from the structural regression of
f_{t}
on
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.

A.4
Balanced-Growth Path

A deterministic balanced-growth path (BGP) is a sequence
\{A_{t}^{\ast},K_{t}^{\ast},G_{t}^{\ast},L_{t}^{\ast},Y_{t}^{\ast},D_{t}^{\ast},w_{t}^{\ast}\}
along which
A_{t}^{\ast},K_{t}^{\ast},G_{t}^{\ast},Y_{t}^{\ast},D_{t}^{\ast},w_{t}^{\ast}
grow at a common gross rate
e^{\mu_{A}/(1-\alpha-\eta)}
and
L_{t}^{\ast}
is constant, with innovation quality
q_{t}^{\ast}
and IT-investment rate
I_{t}^{IT,\ast}/G_{t}^{\ast}=\delta_{G}
both constant. Existence and uniqueness of such a BGP under (
A.2
)–(
A.4
) and standard Inada-type conditions on
\Phi
and
\Xi
is a routine application of the arguments in King, Plosser, and Rebelo (1988); we take it as given. The BGP pins down the steady-state expenditure shares

s_{Y}\equiv\frac{Y^{\ast}}{D^{\ast}},\quad s_{w}\equiv\frac{w^{\ast}L^{\ast}}{D^{\ast}},\quad s_{K}\equiv\frac{I^{K,\ast}}{D^{\ast}},\quad s_{IT}\equiv\frac{I^{IT,\ast}}{D^{\ast}},\quad s_{\Phi}\equiv\frac{\Phi(\delta_{G})\,G^{\ast}}{D^{\ast}},\quad s_{\Xi}\equiv\frac{\Xi(q^{\ast})}{D^{\ast}},

which satisfy the accounting identity
s_{Y}-s_{w}-s_{K}-s_{IT}-s_{\Phi}-s_{\Xi}=1
(with
s_{\Phi}=0
because
\Phi(\delta_{G})=0
by normalization). Logarithmic deviations from trend are denoted
\widehat{x}_{t}\equiv\log X_{t}-\log X_{t}^{\ast}
for any variable
X_{t}
.

A.5
Log-Linearization of the Dividend Process

We now derive the dividend representation rigorously by log-linearizing (
A.6
) around the BGP. Step 1. Take logarithms of (
A.2
):

y_{t}\;=\;a_{t}\;+\;\alpha\,k_{t}\;+\;\eta\,g_{t}\;+\;(1-\alpha-\eta)\,\ell_{t},

(A.8)

so that
\widehat{y}_{t}=\widehat{a}_{t}+\alpha\widehat{k}_{t}+\eta\widehat{g}_{t}+(1-\alpha-\eta)\widehat{\ell}_{t}
. Step 2. First-order Taylor expansion of (
A.6
) around the BGP yields

\widehat{d}_{t}\;=\;s_{Y}\,\widehat{y}_{t}\;-\;s_{w}\,(\widehat{w}_{t}+\widehat{\ell}_{t})\;-\;s_{K}\,\widehat{I}_{t}^{K}\;-\;s_{IT}\,\widehat{I}_{t}^{IT}\;-\;s_{\Phi}\,(\widehat{I}_{t}^{IT}-\widehat{g}_{t})\;-\;s_{\Xi}\,\widehat{q}_{t}.

(A.9)

Here the
s_{\Phi}
term arises from differentiating
\Phi(I_{t}^{IT}/G_{t})\,G_{t}
and using the BGP normalization
\Phi^{\prime}(\delta_{G})=0
(so the coefficient on
\widehat{I}_{t}^{IT}-\widehat{g}_{t}
enters only through the second-order term, which we collect into the residual if
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\delta_{G})>0
; under the first-order approximation
s_{\Phi}
vanishes at the BGP and we retain the placeholder for bookkeeping). Step 3. First-difference (
A.9
) and substitute: (a) (
A.8
) for
\Delta\widehat{y}_{t}
; (b) the TFP law (
A.4
) for
\Delta a_{t+1}
, which expresses
\Delta a_{t}
as an affine function of
q_{t-1}
and
i_{t-1}^{IT}
; (c) the IT-investment Euler equation (
A.7
), which under log-linearization ties
\widehat{I}_{t}^{IT}-\widehat{g}_{t}
to the expected real marginal product of IT,
\eta\,\widehat{y}_{t+1}-\widehat{g}_{t+1}
, and hence to the TFP component
TFP_{t+1}
; and (d) the labor FOC
(1-\alpha-\eta)Y_{t}/L_{t}=w_{t}
, which after log-linearization eliminates
\widehat{w}_{t}+\widehat{\ell}_{t}
in favor of
\widehat{y}_{t}
. Step 4. Collecting all resulting terms and projecting onto the span of
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(TFP_{t},\log IT_{t},\log Pat_{t})^{\prime}
yields the affine representation

\Delta d_{t}\;=\;c\;+\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;\varepsilon_{t},

(A.10)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\equiv(\gamma_{TFP},\gamma_{IT},\gamma_{Pat})^{\prime}
is a vector of reduced-form elasticities with
\gamma_{TFP},\gamma_{IT},\gamma_{Pat}>0
under the sign conventions
\alpha_{q},\alpha_{I}>0
and
\alpha,\eta\in(0,1)
, the constant
c
absorbs the deterministic BGP growth
\mu_{A}/(1-\alpha-\eta)
and any steady-state contributions from
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]
(so
\mathbf{X}_{t}
in (
A.10
) is understood as demeaned), and
\varepsilon_{t}
collects (i) the TFP innovation
u_{t}^{A}
, (ii) the log-linearization remainder of order
O_{p}(\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\|^{2})
, and (iii) any projection residual orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. By construction,
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathbf{X}_{t}]=0
.

A.6
Technology-State VAR and Closed-Form
\mathcal{T}_{t}

To obtain a closed form for the technology present-value term
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}
, we impose the following reduced-form dynamics on the observable state.

Assumption 8

(Technology-State VAR)
.

The observable technology vector
\mathbf{X}_{t}
follows a first-order VAR,

\mathbf{X}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\mathbf{v}_{t+1},

where
\{\mathbf{v}_{t+1},\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
is a strictly stationary, square-integrable martingale-difference sequence satisfying

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{v}_{t+1}\|^{2}<\infty,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}_{t+1}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}^{\prime}]=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{v},

with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{v}
finite, positive semi-definite, and time-invariant. The spectral radius of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
satisfies
\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<\rho^{-1}
, where
\rho\in(0,1)
is the Campbell–Shiller log-linearization constant.

Under Assumption
8
, we derive a closed-form expression for
\mathcal{T}_{t}
by decomposing each future
\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}
into its conditional mean and the cumulated innovation noise, and then summing the two components separately. Substituting (
A.10
) (which gives
\delta_{t+1+j}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}
) into the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
and adding and subtracting the conditional expectation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\Big(\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]\;+\;\big(\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]}_{\text{predictable part}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\big(\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]\big)}_{\text{innovation noise}}.

Iterating the VAR gives
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\mathbf{X}_{t}
for each
j\geq 0
, while iterated substitution of
\mathbf{X}_{s+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{X}_{s}+\mathbf{v}_{s+1}
yields the moving-average representation
\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}
. Substituting both into the display above,

\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\Bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}\Bigg).

The first (predictable) sum is a matrix geometric series that converges because
\rho(\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})=\rho\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<1
by Assumption
8
, and evaluates to

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{\,j}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}.

Hence we obtain the decomposition

\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;\eta_{t},\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}\;\equiv\;(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma},

(A.11)

where the stationary innovation term

\eta_{t}\;\equiv\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\Bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}\Bigg)

(A.12)

is a mean-zero,
\rho
-discounted weighted sum of the VAR innovations
\{\mathbf{v}_{s}\}_{s\geq t+1}
. Under Assumption
8
the process
\{\eta_{t}\}
is stationary and has finite variance: applying the submultiplicative matrix norm
\|\cdot\|
and the spectral-radius bound
\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<\rho^{-1}
gives
\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\|\leq C_{0}\,r^{\,l}
for some
r<\rho^{-1}
, so the double sum in (
A.12
) is absolutely convergent in mean square.

The predictable part
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}
is the economically meaningful component of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
: it is the projection of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
onto the information set spanned by the observable state
\mathbf{X}_{t}
and is what the empirical cointegrating regression identifies. The innovation component
\eta_{t}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
(by construction, as a sum of future innovations
\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}
that are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-unpredictable) and is therefore absorbed into the regression residual when (
A.11
) is combined with Proposition
1
.

Adding the bubble
b_{t}
to the fundamental price and using Proposition
1
delivers the structural cointegrating regression

p_{t}\;=\;f_{t}+b_{t}\;=\;\beta_{0}\;+\;\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;u_{t},\qquad u_{t}\;=\;b_{t}\;+\;\eta_{t}\;+\;\zeta_{t},

(A.13)

where
\beta_{0}=C
is the Campbell–Shiller constant,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}
collects
\boldsymbol{\beta}
from (
A.11
) together with the contemporaneous loading of
d_{t}
on
\mathbf{X}_{t}
implied by (
A.10
), and the residual
u_{t}
pools three orthogonal components: the bubble
b_{t}
, the stationary VAR-innovation term
\eta_{t}
from (
A.12
), and a further
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable noise
\zeta_{t}\perp\mathbf{X}_{t}
that collects the dividend-growth residual
\varepsilon_{t}
from (
A.10
) and higher-order log-linearization terms. Equation (
A.13
) is the structural counterpart of the empirical cointegrating regression estimated in the technology-adjusted PSY test; the explosive content of
b_{t}
is therefore identified from the residual
\widehat{u}_{t}
when OLS is applied under identification conditions (R1)–(R3) below.

A.7
Identification of the Cointegrating Regression

Three identification requirements are needed for (
A.13
) to deliver a consistent estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}
despite the presence of the bubble
b_{t}
in the residual
u_{t}
.

(R1) Separability of the production and valuation blocks.
The bubble enters the share price
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
additively and satisfies the martingale equation
\mathbb{E}_{t}[M_{t+1}b_{t+1}]=b_{t}
, but does not enter the production function (
A.2
), the capital laws (
A.3
), the TFP law (
A.4
), or any of the firm’s first-order conditions in Section
A.3
. Consequently,
\mathbf{X}_{t}
is generated by a dynamic system that is
block-separable
from
b_{t}
, so
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_{t},b_{t})=0
in population.

(R2) Predetermined patent index.
The patent-grant lag
\ell\geq 1
implies that
Pat_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-\ell}
-measurable. Even if shareholders’ speculative exuberance affects innovation
financing
contemporaneously, the resulting change in realized patent grants is observed only at date
t+\ell
and enters
\mathbf{X}_{t+\ell}
, not
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. Thus
Pat_{t}
is strictly exogenous with respect to
b_{t}
at any horizon.

(R3) Real IT Euler equation.
The IT-investment FOC (
A.7
) ties
I_{t}^{IT}
to the expected real marginal product of IT capital under the SDF, not to the market price of the firm’s equity. Formally, (
A.7
) depends on
(Y_{t+1},G_{t+1},M_{t+1})
only; since none of these objects is a function of
b_{t}
under (R1), contemporaneous IT investment is orthogonal to the bubble.

Under (R1)–(R3), OLS applied to (
A.13
) consistently estimates
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}
in the absence of an explosive bubble, and the regression residual
\widehat{u}_{t}
inherits any explosive component of
b_{t}
—the feature that the technology-adjusted PSY test is designed to detect.

Remark 8

(Theoretical vs. Empirical Technology Components)
.

The theoretical analysis in Sections
2.2
–
2.5
uses a deterministic technology component
\delta_{t}
(Assumption
2
) for analytical tractability: the hump-shaped, bounded-support structure enables closed-form characterization of the fundamental dynamics and sharp statements about local explosiveness. The microfoundation of this appendix derives
\Delta d_{t}=c+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
, where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}
is the
empirical
counterpart of
\delta_{t}
—a stochastic, observable proxy driven by the VAR dynamics of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. Formally, the two are linked by the identification
\delta_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}\mid\text{regime}]
: the deterministic hump
\delta_{t}
of Assumption
2
is the conditional-mean path of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}
that would obtain under a technology-diffusion regime with adoption at
T_{1}
and maturation at
T_{2}
, while the stochastic component
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t}-\delta_{t}
is absorbed into the residual noise in the empirical specification. Under this mapping, Propositions
4
and
5
describe the signal component of the data-generating process that the PSY test erroneously attributes to explosive dynamics, and the technology-adjusted PSY test removes exactly this signal component by projecting on
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.

Remark 9

(Separability and Exclusion of Bubbles)
.

The identification conditions (R1)–(R3) above formalize what was informally called “separability” in earlier drafts. The bubble enters as an additive component in secondary-market valuation (
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
,
\mathbb{E}_{t}[M_{t+1}b_{t+1}]=b_{t}
) but is excluded from the production block determining
\mathbf{X}_{t}
by the structure of (
A.2
)–(
A.4
) and the FOCs (
A.7
). Even if stock valuations affect future innovation through financing constraints, the resulting variation in
\mathbf{X}_{t+j}
for
j\geq 1
operates through the fundamental mapping
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{t+j}
, not through the residual
u_{t}
at date
t
. Patents are predetermined (grant lag
\ell\geq 1
), TFP is a production-side measure, and IT investment satisfies (
A.7
); these three facts are the backbone of the exclusion restriction.

Appendix B
Extensions

This appendix presents the three extensions summarized in Section
2.6
: time-varying expected returns, stochastic technology shocks under Bayesian learning, and cross-sectional heterogeneity. Each extension relaxes one of the simplifying restrictions of the baseline theory and shows that the spurious-explosiveness mechanism is preserved or strengthened.

B.1
Time-Varying Expected Returns

The first extension asks whether the explosive pattern survives when expected returns respond to the technology state. High-valuation regimes are typically associated with low expected returns
(
8
;
10
)
, so one might worry that a technology-induced decline in
\bar{r}
could offset the rise in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
and mute the spurious explosiveness. The following result shows the opposite under the natural parameter region
\phi<1
: a technology-induced decline in expected returns
amplifies
, rather than attenuates, the locally explosive pattern, because a lower discount rate magnifies the present value of future technology payoffs.

Relaxing Assumption
3
, suppose expected returns respond to the technology shock:

\mathbb{E}_{t}[r_{t+1+j}]=\bar{r}+\phi\,\delta_{t+1+j}\qquad\text{for all }j\geq 0,\qquad\phi\in\mathbb{R}.

(B.1)

This horizon-by-horizon specification is consistent with consumption-based asset-pricing environments in which the technology state
\delta_{t}
enters the stochastic discount factor.

Proposition 8
.

Under (
B.1
),

f_{t}=d_{t}+C+(1-\phi)\,\mathcal{T}_{t}.

If
\phi<1
, the technology component of the log price-dividend ratio,
f_{t}-d_{t}-C=(1-\phi)\mathcal{T}_{t}
, has the same sign, monotonicity, and convexity properties as
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. If
\phi<0
, this component is amplified by the factor
1-\phi>1
. The drift of
f_{t}
becomes
c+\delta_{t}+(1-\phi)\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}
, so any statement about drift relative to
c
requires the corresponding sign condition on
\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}
on the window of interest.

B.2
Stochastic Technology Shocks

The second extension relaxes determinism: in practice the ultimate cumulative impact of a new general-purpose technology (the eventual productivity gains from electrification, the internet, or artificial intelligence) is not known ex ante, and agents learn about it gradually through observation—the mechanism
25
identify as central to technology-related asset price dynamics. We now formalize this uncertainty. Under the local parametrization of Assumption
5
with a fixed discount factor
\rho\in[0,1)
, Bayesian learning affects the statistic only through its pathwise contribution at finite
T
: asymptotically, the learning-innovation term is negligible at PSY scale, so the first-order limit theory is governed by the deterministic channel of Assumption
2
. The news-variance parameter
\sigma_{\xi}
therefore enters the rejection probability as a
finite-sample
refinement; its quantitative effect is documented empirically via Monte Carlo in Appendix
E.5
.

Assumption 9

(Stochastic Technology Uncertainty)
.

The technology shock is

\delta_{t}=\bar{\delta}\cdot g_{\star}(t)+\sigma_{\xi}\,\xi_{t},\qquad t\in[T_{1},T_{2}],

where
\bar{\delta}\sim N(\mu_{\delta},\sigma_{\delta}^{2})
is the uncertain total technology impact,
g_{\star}:[T_{1},T_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the full normalized hump shape obtained from the deterministic profile of Assumption
2
by rescaling so that
\sum_{t=T_{1}}^{T_{2}}g_{\star}(t)=1
(note:
g_{\star}
extends over the entire adoption–maturation window
[T_{1},T_{2}]
and should not be confused with the ramp-only function
g:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
of Assumption
2
),
\xi_{t}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
represents period-by-period technology news, and
\sigma_{\xi}>0
scales the news variance. The prior mean
\mu_{\delta}
and variance
\sigma_{\delta}^{2}
are common knowledge at
t=T_{1}
.

Under Bayesian updating, agents observe
\delta_{t}
and update their beliefs about
\bar{\delta}
. The signal
\delta_{t}-\sigma_{\xi}\xi_{t}=\bar{\delta}\,g_{\star}(t)
is contaminated by the news shock
\xi_{t}
, so the conditional expectation
\hat{\delta}_{t}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{t}[\bar{\delta}]
evolves as a martingale:

\hat{\delta}_{t}=\hat{\delta}_{t-1}+K_{t}\,\nu_{t},\qquad\nu_{t}=\delta_{t}-\hat{\delta}_{t-1}\,g_{\star}(t),

where
\nu_{t}
denotes the Kalman innovation (to avoid collision with the VAR innovation
\eta_{t}
of (
A.12
)),
K_{t}=P_{t-1}\,g_{\star}(t)\,[g_{\star}(t)^{2}\,P_{t-1}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}]^{-1}
is the Kalman gain and
P_{t}=P_{t-1}-K_{t}\,g_{\star}(t)\,P_{t-1}
is the posterior variance, initialized at
P_{0}=\sigma_{\delta}^{2}
. The technology present-value term becomes

\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\delta_{t+1+j}]=\hat{\delta}_{t}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,g_{\star}(t+1+j)+\sigma_{\xi}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\xi_{t+1+j}].

Since
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\xi_{t+1+j}]=0
, the second sum vanishes and
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\hat{\delta}_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t)
, where
G_{\rho}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,g_{\star}(t+1+j)
is a deterministic weight. Revisions to beliefs about
\bar{\delta}
propagate directly into
\mathcal{T}_{t}
:

\mathcal{T}_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\mathcal{T}_{t}]=K_{t}\,\nu_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t).

Positive news (
\nu_{t}>0
) generates a discrete upward jump in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
scaled by
G_{\rho}(t)
, which is largest during the early adoption phase when the remaining future technology path is long. These jumps compound the deterministic hump-shaped rise, producing price dynamics more explosive than the deterministic baseline.

Proposition 9

(Stochastic Technology Amplification)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
3
,
9
,
5
, and
6
with
b_{t}=0
:

(i)

Jumps compound explosive dynamics. Positive news about
\bar{\delta}
generates upward jumps in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
of magnitude
K_{t}\,\nu_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t)
. During the early adoption phase
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau]
,
K_{t}
is high (prior uncertainty is greatest) and
G_{\rho}(t)>0
throughout the adoption window (indeed bounded away from zero on a non-empty subinterval near the peak). The news innovations
\nu_{t}
form a martingale difference sequence (hence are serially uncorrelated), but the posterior mean
\hat{\delta}_{t}
is a martingale whose levels are highly persistent. It is this persistence in levels—not serial correlation of innovations—that propagates each positive jump into all future values of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, amplifying the locally explosive pattern relative to the deterministic baseline.

(ii)

Asymptotic negligibility of the learning channel; finite-sample dependence on
\sigma_{\xi}
. Under the local parametrization of Assumption
5
with fixed discount factor
\rho\in[0,1)
, the partial-sum process of Bayesian learning innovations is asymptotically negligible at PSY scale:
T^{-1/2}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor Tr\rfloor}K_{s}\,\nu_{s}\,G_{\rho}(s)\Rightarrow 0
in
D[0,1]
. Consequently the gsADF limit law coincides with the deterministic-
\delta
limit law of Theorem
2
, and the asymptotic size distortion is
not
altered by
\sigma_{\xi}
at first order. Nontrivial dependence of the test’s rejection probability on
\sigma_{\xi}
is therefore a
finite-sample
phenomenon—operating through the pathwise contribution of
K_{t}\,\nu_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t)
to
\tilde{y}_{t}
at empirically relevant sample sizes—and its sign and magnitude are documented numerically in Appendix
E.5
, where Panel A of Table
E.3
shows substantial amplification in the price-dividend implementation and Panel B shows near-invariance in the detrended log-price implementation.

(iii)

Adjusted test remains valid (implementation-specific). If the econometrician uniformly consistently estimates the implementation-specific technology component required by Definition
2
—namely
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in the log price-dividend-ratio implementation and
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\delta_{s}+\mathcal{T}_{t}
in the detrended log-price implementation—then the technology-adjusted PSY statistic has the same first-order asymptotics as in the corresponding technology-free benchmark. In particular, for detrended log prices its asymptotic size remains
\alpha
, and for the price-dividend-ratio implementation the stochastic technology component is removed along with the deterministic one (so any residual rejection cannot be attributed to the hump-shaped technology term).

Remark 10

(Connection to
25
)
.

The stochastic extension nests a simplified version of the
25
framework. In their model, uncertainty about a new technology’s productivity generates high prices during the adoption phase (the “bubble”) and price declines when uncertainty resolves unfavorably (the “crash”). Our decomposition identifies precisely what PSY misattributes: the high prices during the learning phase reflect the conditional expectation
\hat{\delta}_{t}
entering the fundamental
f_{t}=d_{t}+C+\hat{\delta}_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t)
, not a speculative component
b_{t}
. When uncertainty resolves favorably (
\hat{\delta}_{t}
rises), prices jump, a rational Bayesian response. When it resolves unfavorably, prices decline. Both outcomes are fundamental, yet PSY may classify them as bubble origination and collapse. The technology-adjusted test correctly attributes these dynamics to
f_{t}
.

B.3
Cross-Sectional Heterogeneity

The third extension moves from a representative asset to a cross-section. General-purpose technologies affect firms asymmetrically: technology-intensive industries load heavily on
\delta_{t}
while traditional industries load lightly, so the bubble-like pattern that the spurious-explosiveness mechanism generates at the asset level aggregates into a
portfolio-level
pattern whose magnitude depends on the concentration of high-exposure firms in the index. This observation is empirically important because PSY tests are commonly applied to sector indices and to the broad market aggregate, where cross-sectional concentration matters directly.

When firms differ in technology exposure (
\delta_{i,t}=\lambda_{i}\delta_{t}
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
), the aggregate market fundamental
f_{t}^{\mathrm{mkt}}=\sum_{i}w_{i}f_{i,t}
inherits the technology effect weighted by
\sum_{i}w_{i}\lambda_{i}
. Industries with high technology exposure and large market capitalization weight (e.g., the late-1990s NASDAQ) are most susceptible to spurious bubble detection.

Appendix C
Firm-Level Analysis: The Magnificent Seven

This appendix reports the full firm-level analysis of the Magnificent Seven stocks summarized in Section
5.7
.

The aggregate NASDAQ index masks substantial heterogeneity across individual stocks.
3
applied PSY to the “Magnificent Seven” technology stocks to evaluate firm-level speculative dynamics; we extend this analysis by applying our technology-adjusted test at the firm level. We use log dividend-adjusted monthly closing prices from Yahoo Finance, applying the same PSY specification as the index-level analysis: a minimum window
r_{0}
defined by the formula
0.01+1.8/\sqrt{T}
, lag length of 1, and Monte Carlo critical values with 2,000 replications. Sample periods vary by initial public offering date, extending through early 2026.

Table
C.1
and Figure
C.1
present the results. We find that four of the seven stocks reject the null hypothesis of no explosive behavior at the 10% level: MSFT, GOOGL, TSLA, and NVDA. Three of these (MSFT, TSLA, and NVDA) also reject at the 5% level, and two (TSLA and NVDA) reject at the stringent 1% level. NVDA exhibits the strongest signal by far, with a GSADF statistic of 4.155 (well above the 1% critical value of
2.657
) and extensive explosive episodes from January 2024 through the end of the sample. TSLA shows a prolonged explosive episode from July 2020 to December 2021, coinciding with the broader electric-vehicle and technology euphoria. MSFT shows nearly continuous explosive behavior from February 2019 through January 2026, spanning both the pre- and post-large-language-model periods.

The remaining three stocks (AMZN, META, and AAPL) do not show statistically significant explosive behavior despite their status as market leaders. This cross-sectional heterogeneity is a key finding: even within the Magnificent Seven, explosive price dynamics are not universal.

C.1
Technology-Adjusted Firm-Level Tests

To assess whether the detected explosive episodes reflect genuine speculation or technology-driven fundamental repricing, we apply the technology-adjusted PSY test to each Magnificent Seven stock. Unlike the index-level analysis, which uses aggregate technology covariates (log IT investment, TFP, and log patent grants), the firm-level analysis constructs individual counterfactuals from each firm’s own Compustat fundamentals. Specifically, for each stock
i
we estimate the OLS regression

\log P_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{1i}\,\log\!\bigl(\text{RevPS}_{i,t}^{\text{real}}\bigr)+\beta_{2i}\,\log\!\bigl(1+\text{RDPS}_{i,t}^{\text{real}}\bigr)+u_{i,t},

where
\text{RevPS}_{i,t}^{\text{real}}=\text{revtq}_{i,t}/(\text{CPI}_{t}\times\text{cshoq}_{i,t})
is CPI-deflated revenue per share and
\text{RDPS}_{i,t}^{\text{real}}=\text{xrdq}_{i,t}/(\text{CPI}_{t}\times\text{cshoq}_{i,t})
is CPI-deflated R&D expenditure per share, both constructed from quarterly Compustat data interpolated to monthly frequency via last observation carried forward (LOCF). Revenue per share captures realized commercialization and firm scale, while R&D expenditure per share proxies firm-specific technology investment. The training window is the 2006–2019 period, consistent with the index-level AI-era analysis of Section
5.6.4
, which postdates the dot-com bubble and predates the AI-driven rally. Crucially, because several Magnificent Seven stocks exhibit PSY-detected explosive episodes within the 2006–2019 window (Figure
C.1
), we exclude each stock’s own bubble months from its training sample. This ensures that the counterfactual relationship is estimated exclusively from non-bubble observations, consistent with the requirement that the training period should not be contaminated by speculative dynamics. We then compute the price gap
\hat{g}_{i,t}=p_{i,t}-\hat{f}_{i,t}
and apply PSY to the residual.

Table
C.2
and Figure
C.2
present the technology-adjusted results for all seven stocks. The results are consistent with our central thesis. After technology adjustment, the explosive signals weaken substantially for most stocks. Of the four stocks that rejected the null in the unadjusted test, three (MSFT (test statistic
=0.605
), GOOGL (test statistic
=0.633
), and NVDA (test statistic
=1.531
)) no longer reject at any standard significance level after technology adjustment. Notably, the extensive NVDA explosive episodes spanning 2016–2018 and 2024–2026 largely disappear once the counterfactual is estimated from firm-level fundamentals on bubble-free training data (134 of 168 months retained after excluding 34 bubble months), leaving only minor episodes in 2016–2017. TSLA remains highly significant after adjustment (test statistic
=4.696
, rejecting at the 1% level), with episodes concentrated in the 2020–2021 period coinciding with electric-vehicle euphoria; its relatively high
R^{2}
of 0.898 indicates that the firm-level fundamentals capture a substantial share of price variation even for Tesla, and the residual explosive behavior is plausibly speculative. AAPL, which did not reject in the unadjusted test, shows a significant adjusted test statistic (
2.317
), rejecting at the 10% and 5% levels but not at the 1% level (CV
{}_{1\%}=2.660
), with detected episodes in the early-to-mid 2000s, a period outside the 2006–2019 training window where the counterfactual extrapolates less reliably; its lower
R^{2}
of 0.407 suggests that the two-covariate specification captures less of Apple’s price dynamics, possibly because Apple’s transformation from a niche computer maker to a consumer electronics and services platform involved drivers beyond revenue and R&D growth. The remaining stocks (MSFT, AMZN, META, GOOGL) show no explosive episodes after adjustment, with
R^{2}
values ranging from 0.741 (MSFT) to 0.961 (AMZN). These firm-level results parallel the index-level finding: the technology adjustment removes the dominant source of apparent explosiveness in prices that are fundamentally driven by AI-era technology growth.

These firm-level results reinforce the aggregate finding: the technology-adjusted test, used alongside the standard PSY test, reveals that much of the apparent speculative behavior in Magnificent Seven stocks is technology-driven fundamental repricing, a diagnostic decomposition that neither test offers in isolation.

Appendix D
Proofs

This appendix collects the formal proofs of all propositions, lemmas, and theorems stated in the main text.

Proof of Proposition
1
(Fundamental Price Decomposition)

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
and
b_{t}=0
, the present-value identity (
3
) becomes

f_{t}=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta d_{t+1+j}-r_{t+1+j}].

Substituting the dividend growth process
\Delta d_{t+1+j}=c+\delta_{t+1+j}+\varepsilon_{t+1+j}
from Assumption
1
and the constant expected return
\mathbb{E}_{t}[r_{t+1+j}]=\bar{r}
from Assumption
3
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta d_{t+1+j}-r_{t+1+j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}[c+\delta_{t+1+j}+\varepsilon_{t+1+j}]-\bar{r}

\displaystyle=(c-\bar{r})+\delta_{t+1+j},

(D.1)

where we used the fact that
\delta_{t+1+j}
is deterministic (Assumption
2
) and
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\varepsilon_{t+1+j}]=0
. Substituting:

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\big[(c-\bar{r})+\delta_{t+1+j}\big]

\displaystyle=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\frac{c-\bar{r}}{1-\rho}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}

\displaystyle=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t},

(D.2)

where
C=\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\frac{c-\bar{r}}{1-\rho}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}
. The geometric sum
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}=(1-\rho)^{-1}
converges since
\rho\in(0,1)
, and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
converges since
\delta_{t}
has bounded support
[T_{1},T_{2}]
. ∎

Proof of Proposition
2
(Observable Technology Proxies and Fundamental Value)

Denote the technology state vector
\mathbf{X}_{t}\equiv(TFP_{t},\,\log IT_{t},\,\log Pat_{t})^{\prime}
and suppose it follows a first-order vector autoregressive process
\mathbf{X}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\mathbf{v}_{t+1}
, where the spectral radius of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
satisfies
\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<\rho^{-1}
(the persistence of the technology state is dominated by the discount factor). We prove each part in turn.

Part (i): Log dividend growth.

The production function is
Y_{t}=A_{t}\,K_{t}^{\alpha}\,G_{t}^{\eta}\,L_{t}^{1-\alpha-\eta}
. Log-linearizing output around the balanced-growth path and using the optimality conditions for labor, physical capital, and information technology capital yields
\Delta\log Y_{t}=\Delta a_{t}+\alpha\,\Delta\log K_{t}+\eta\,\Delta\log G_{t}+(1-\alpha-\eta)\,\Delta\log L_{t}
. Dividends are residual cash flow:
D_{t}=Y_{t}-w_{t}L_{t}-I_{t}^{K}-I_{t}^{IT}-\Phi(I_{t}^{IT}/G_{t})G_{t}-\Xi(q_{t})
. To a first-order approximation around the balanced-growth path, the log-linearized dividend growth is affine in the technology state:

\Delta d_{t}=c+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t},

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{TFP},\,\gamma_{IT},\,\gamma_{Pat})^{\prime}
collects the elasticities of dividend growth with respect to each technology proxy and
\varepsilon_{t}
captures idiosyncratic shocks orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.

The key structural ingredients are: (a) total factor productivity enters through the disembodied productivity channel
\Delta a_{t+1}=\mu_{A}+\alpha_{q}\,q_{t}+\alpha_{I}\,i_{t}^{IT}+u_{t+1}^{A}
; (b) information technology investment enters through the information technology capital accumulation
G_{t+1}=(1-\delta_{G})\,G_{t}+I_{t}^{IT}
and the optimality condition (
A.7
); (c) patent grants enter through the innovation quality channel with grant lag
\ell\geq 1
.

Part (ii):
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\eta_{t}
.

From Proposition
1
, the technology present-value term is
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}
, and from Part (i) the identification
\delta_{s}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}_{s}
(to first order, after absorbing the zero-mean dividend-growth residual
\varepsilon_{s}
into the noise) yields

\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}.

(D.3)

Step 1: Decompose each
\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}
into predictable part and innovation noise.
Add and subtract the conditional expectation to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\Big(\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]\;+\;\big(\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]}_{\text{(A) predictable part}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\big(\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]\big)}_{\text{(B) innovation noise}}.

(D.4)

Step 2: Evaluate the predictable part (A).
Iterating the VAR
\mathbf{X}_{s+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{X}_{s}+\mathbf{v}_{s+1}
forward from date
t
and taking conditional expectations,
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\mathbf{X}_{t}
for each
j\geq 0
. (The unconditional mean is zero by demeaning, so no constant term arises.) Substituting,

\text{(A)}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(\rho\,\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{\,j}\Bigg)\,\mathbf{X}_{t}.

The matrix geometric series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{\,j}
converges in operator norm because the spectral radius satisfies
\rho(\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})=\rho\cdot\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<\rho\cdot\rho^{-1}=1
by Assumption
8
, and the standard Neumann-series identity gives
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{\,j}=(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}
. Therefore

\text{(A)}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;=\;\big[(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-\top}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}\big]^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;\equiv\;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t},

(D.5)

where we have used the identity
(\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{B})^{\prime}=\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{A}
applied to
\mathbf{A}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{B}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}
, and we have defined the coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\equiv(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

Step 3: Evaluate the innovation term (B).
Iterating the VAR forward from date
t
via
\mathbf{X}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{X}_{t}+\mathbf{v}_{t+1}
,
\mathbf{X}_{t+2}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{2}\mathbf{X}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{v}_{t+1}+\mathbf{v}_{t+2}
, and more generally,

\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;\sum_{l=0}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}.

Subtracting
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,j+1}\mathbf{X}_{t}
isolates the innovation component
\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1+j}]=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}
. Substituting into (
D.4
) and defining

\eta_{t}\;\equiv\;\text{(B)}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\Bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}\Bigg),

(D.6)

we must verify: (a) absolute convergence in mean square; (b) mean zero; (c) stationarity; and (d) orthogonality to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.

Convergence.
Because
\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<\rho^{-1}
, by Gelfand’s formula there exist constants
C_{0}>0
and
r\in(\rho(\boldsymbol{\Phi}),\rho^{-1})
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}\|\leq C_{0}\,r^{\,l}
for all
l\geq 0
(in any submultiplicative matrix norm). Using
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{v}_{s}\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{v}^{2}<\infty
and uncorrelatedness of the
\mathbf{v}_{s}
across
s
, the double sum defining
\eta_{t}
is bounded in
L^{2}
by exchanging orders of summation:

\mathbb{E}\|\eta_{t}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|^{2}\,C_{0}^{2}\,\sigma_{v}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\rho^{\,j}\,r^{\,l}\;=\;\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|^{2}\,C_{0}^{2}\,\sigma_{v}^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}r^{\,l}\sum_{j=l}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\;=\;\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|^{2}\,C_{0}^{2}\,\sigma_{v}^{2}}{(1-\rho)(1-\rho r)},

where the final equality uses
\sum_{j=l}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}=\rho^{\,l}/(1-\rho)
and
\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\rho r)^{\,l}=1/(1-\rho r)
, both finite because
\rho r<\rho\cdot\rho^{-1}=1
. Hence
\eta_{t}
converges absolutely in
L^{2}
.

Mean zero.
Each
\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+j-l}
-measurable and satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}_{s}]=0
, hence
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}]=0
.

Stationarity.
The joint distribution of
\{\mathbf{v}_{s}\}_{s\geq t+1}
depends on
t
only through the index shift
s\mapsto s-t
, and the deterministic weights
\rho^{\,j}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\,l}
depend only on
(j,l)
. Hence
\eta_{t}
is strictly stationary when
\{\mathbf{v}_{s}\}
is strictly stationary (and covariance-stationary otherwise), with finite second moment by the convergence argument above.

Orthogonality to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.
Each innovation
\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}
(for
j\geq 0
,
0\leq l\leq j
, which ranges over indices
t+1-l+j\geq t+1
) is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-unpredictable, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}_{t+1+j-l}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0
. Since
\mathbf{X}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, the conditional covariance
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_{t},\eta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})=0
, and hence
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}_{t},\eta_{t})=0
in population.

Step 4: Assemble.
Combining (
D.4
) with (
D.5
) and (
D.6
), we obtain the decomposition

\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;\eta_{t},\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}\;=\;(\mathbf{I}-\rho\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma},

with
\eta_{t}
mean-zero, stationary, and orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, as claimed.

Part (iii):
f_{t}=d_{t}+\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}+\eta_{t}
.

From Proposition
1
,
f_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
. Substituting Part (ii) yields

f_{t}\;=\;d_{t}\;+\;C\;+\;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\;+\;\eta_{t}.

Defining
\beta_{0}\equiv C
gives the stated representation. By Part (ii),
\eta_{t}
is mean-zero, stationary, and orthogonal to
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. This argument keeps
d_{t}
explicit; the reduced-form coefficient
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}
in (
A.13
) arises only after absorbing the contemporaneous loading of
d_{t}
on
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. Hence
\mathbf{X}_{t}
spans the technology-driven present-value component of fundamentals. ∎

Proof of Proposition
3
(Fundamental Dynamics)

From Proposition
1
,
f_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
. First-differencing:

\displaystyle f_{t}-f_{t-1}

\displaystyle=(d_{t}-d_{t-1})+(\mathcal{T}_{t}-\mathcal{T}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=\Delta d_{t}+\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}

\displaystyle=c+\delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}+\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}.

(D.7)

Using the recursion from Lemma
1
:

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{t}-\mathcal{T}_{t-1}=(\rho^{-1}\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-\rho^{-1}\,\delta_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=(\rho^{-1}-1)\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-\rho^{-1}\,\delta_{t}.

(D.8)

Substituting back:

\displaystyle f_{t}-f_{t-1}

\displaystyle=c+\delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}+(\rho^{-1}-1)\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-\rho^{-1}\,\delta_{t}

\displaystyle=c+(1-\rho^{-1})\,\delta_{t}+(\rho^{-1}-1)\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}+\varepsilon_{t},

(D.9)

where
\mu_{t}=c+(1-\rho^{-1})\,\delta_{t}+(\rho^{-1}-1)\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
. ∎

Proof of Proposition
4
(Local Explosiveness)

We verify each part of the proposition.

Part (i): Pre-adoption (
t<T_{1}
).
For
t<T_{1}
,
\delta_{s}=0
for all
s\leq t
. Writing
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+1+j}
and noting that
\delta_{t+1+j}=0
for
j=0,\ldots,T_{1}-t-2
(when
t+1<T_{1}
), only indices
j\geq T_{1}-t-1
contribute. Using the uniform bound
0\leq\delta_{s}\leq\delta_{\max}
on the support of
\delta
(Assumption
2
), we obtain the explicit inequality

0\;\leq\;\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{j=T_{1}-t-1}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}\;\leq\;\delta_{\max}\sum_{j=T_{1}-t-1}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\;=\;\delta_{\max}\,\frac{\rho^{T_{1}-t-1}}{1-\rho}.

Hence
\mathcal{T}_{t}
decays geometrically in the gap
T_{1}-t
at rate
\rho
. Combined with the drift identity
\mu_{t}=c+(\rho^{-1}-1)\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-(\rho^{-1}-1)\delta_{t}
(Proposition
3
) and
\delta_{t}=0
, we obtain
\mu_{t}-c=(\rho^{-1}-1)\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
, so

|\mu_{t}-c|\;=\;(\rho^{-1}-1)\,\mathcal{T}_{t-1}\;\leq\;(\rho^{-1}-1)\cdot\frac{\delta_{\max}\,\rho^{\,T_{1}-t}}{1-\rho}\;=\;\frac{\delta_{\max}}{\rho}\cdot\rho^{\,T_{1}-t},

using
(\rho^{-1}-1)=(1-\rho)/\rho
. Thus
\mu_{t}\to c
geometrically at rate
\rho
as
T_{1}-t\to\infty
, so
f_{t}
is asymptotically a random walk with drift
c
. In many applications (and in our Monte Carlo design), we set
\delta_{t}=0
for
t<T_{1}
exactly and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
begins rising from zero only as
t
approaches
T_{1}-1
from below.

Part (ii): Early adoption (
t\in[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau)
).
We use the compact identity
\mu_{t}-c=(\rho^{-1}-1)(\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-\delta_{t})
, which follows from the drift formula
\mu_{t}=c+(1-\rho^{-1})\delta_{t}+(\rho^{-1}-1)\mathcal{T}_{t-1}
in Proposition
3
. Substituting the definition
\mathcal{T}_{t-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+j}=\delta_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+j}
gives

\mu_{t}-c\;=\;(\rho^{-1}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+j}.

Since
\rho^{-1}>1
,
\rho^{j}>0
, and
\delta_{t+j}\geq 0
with at least one strictly positive term for
t\in[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau)
(because future shocks in the adoption window are strictly positive by Assumption
2
), we conclude
\mu_{t}>c
strictly throughout this phase. This proves the “above-drift-
c
” claim without any sign restriction on
\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

Convexity of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
on
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau^{\star})
under Assumption
4
.
We derive a useful identity for
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}
in terms of second differences of
\delta
, which both motivates Assumption
4
and underpins the verification Lemma
2
. By Lemma
1
,
\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}=(\rho^{-1}-1)\mathcal{T}_{t-1}-\rho^{-1}\Delta\delta_{t}
. Applying the same recursion once more and rearranging,

\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j-1}\,\Delta^{2}\delta_{t+j},

which one verifies by substituting the definition
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+1+j}
and telescoping. Thus
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}
is a
\rho
-discounted weighted average of future second differences of
\delta
. Assumption
4
(
\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}>0
on
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau^{\star})
) is therefore an endogenous property of the hump shape that one checks by direct calculation; it holds for strictly-convex
g
and standard smooth-hump profiles, but fails for the piecewise-linear triangular profile (Lemma
2
). Granting Assumption
4
,
\mathcal{T}_{t}
is strictly convex on
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau^{\star})
, and the log price–dividend ratio
f_{t}-d_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}
inherits strict convexity. This is the convexity invoked in the proof of Proposition
5
. No monotonicity claim for
\mathcal{T}_{t}
follows from
\mu_{t}>c
alone; such a claim would require additional restrictions on the discounted future-shock profile.

Part (iii): Late adoption (
t\in(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2}]
).
After the peak,
\delta_{t}
is positive but decreasing (
h
is strictly decreasing on
[\tau,T_{2}-T_{1}]
by Assumption
2
). The identity
\mu_{t}-c=(\rho^{-1}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+j}
derived in Part (ii) remains valid. Its right-hand side is a
\rho
-weighted sum of non-negative future
\delta
values with at least one strictly positive term whenever
t<T_{2}
, so
\mu_{t}>c
strictly on
(T_{1}+\tau,T_{2}]
. To show monotone decline of
\mu_{t}
to
c
, compute

(\mu_{t+1}-c)-(\mu_{t}-c)\;=\;(\rho^{-1}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\big[\delta_{t+1+j}-\delta_{t+j}\big].

For
t\geq T_{1}+\tau
and each
j\geq 1
, the index
t+j
lies in the descending branch of
h
(until it exits the support
[T_{1},T_{2}]
, after which the term is identically zero), so
\delta_{t+1+j}-\delta_{t+j}\leq 0
with strict inequality for at least one
j
as long as
t<T_{2}
. Hence
\mu_{t+1}<\mu_{t}
strictly, and as
t\to T_{2}
the sum shrinks to zero, giving
\mu_{t}\downarrow c
monotonically on this phase.

Part (iv): Post-maturation (
t>T_{2}
).
For
t>T_{2}
,
\delta_{s}=0
for all
s>T_{2}
, so
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\delta_{t+1+j}=0
, and the drift
\mu_{t}=c
. The fundamental price resumes a random walk with drift
c
, but at a permanently elevated level reflecting the cumulative technology-driven dividend growth
\sum_{s=T_{1}}^{T_{2}}\delta_{s}
. ∎

Proof of Proposition
5
(Deterministic Price-Dividend Ratio Limit)

Under the price-dividend implementation,
y_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t,T}+u_{t}
. We restrict attention to subsample windows
[\lfloor r_{1}T\rfloor,\lfloor r_{2}T\rfloor]
with
(r_{1},r_{2})\subseteq(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}+\kappa(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}))
(i.e. strictly inside the ramp-up phase where
q
is strictly increasing and strictly convex), and we impose the signal-dominance condition

\frac{\delta_{\max}(T)^{2}}{T\,\sigma_{u}^{2}}\;\longrightarrow\;\infty\qquad\text{as }T\to\infty,

equivalently
\delta_{\max}(T)/(\sigma_{u}\sqrt{T})\to\infty
. This requires
\delta_{\max}(T)
to grow sufficiently quickly relative to
\sqrt{T}
; in particular, neither the fixed-
\delta_{\max}
regime nor the local parametrization
\delta_{\max}(T)=c_{\delta}/\sqrt{T}
satisfies this condition. Those non-divergent cases are outside the scope of this proposition. Consider the augmented Dickey–Fuller regression
\Delta y_{t}=\hat{\alpha}+\hat{\beta}\,y_{t-1}+\hat{u}_{t}
on this subsample.

Step 1: Deterministic limit of
\hat{\beta}
.

Under the continuous-time embedding,
\mathcal{T}_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor,T}=\delta_{\max}(T)\,q(r)+o(\delta_{\max}(T))
, so
\Delta y_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}\approx\delta_{\max}(T)\,q^{\prime}(r)/T+\Delta u_{t}
. The OLS formula with demeaning gives

\hat{\beta}_{r_{1},r_{2}}=\frac{\sum_{t}(y_{t-1}-\bar{y}_{-1})(\Delta y_{t}-\overline{\Delta y})}{\sum_{t}(y_{t-1}-\bar{y}_{-1})^{2}}.

Under the signal-dominance condition, the deterministic component dominates both sample moments. Denote
\tilde{q}(r)=q(r)-\frac{1}{r_{2}-r_{1}}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}q(s)ds
. Then:

\displaystyle\sum_{t}(y_{t-1}-\bar{y}_{-1})^{2}

\displaystyle=T\,\delta_{\max}(T)^{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}(r)^{2}\,dr\;+\;O_{p}(T\sigma_{u}^{2})\;+\;O_{p}(\sqrt{T}\,\delta_{\max}(T)\sigma_{u}),

\displaystyle\sum_{t}(y_{t-1}-\bar{y}_{-1})(\Delta y_{t}-\overline{\Delta y})

\displaystyle=\delta_{\max}(T)^{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}(r)\,q^{\prime}(r)\,dr\;+\;O_{p}(\delta_{\max}(T)\sigma_{u})\;+\;O_{p}(\sigma_{u}^{2}).

Under the imposed signal-dominance condition
\delta_{\max}(T)^{2}/(T\sigma_{u}^{2})\to\infty
, these stochastic remainder terms are negligible for the probability limit of
T\,\hat{\beta}_{r_{1},r_{2}}
, so the deterministic component governs the first-order behavior. Multiplying through by
T
yields

T\,\hat{\beta}_{r_{1},r_{2}}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}B(r_{1},r_{2})=\frac{\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(r)\,q^{\prime}(r)\,dr}{\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(r)^{2}\,dr}.

Step 2: Sign of
B(r_{1},r_{2})
.

The denominator is always positive. For the numerator, integration by parts gives

\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}(r)\,q^{\prime}(r)\,dr=\Big[\tilde{q}(r)\,q(r)\Big]_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}-\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}q(r)\,\tilde{q}^{\prime}(r)\,dr.

Since
\tilde{q}^{\prime}=q^{\prime}
(the mean subtraction is a constant) and using
\tilde{q}(r)=q(r)-\bar{q}
:

\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}(r)\,q^{\prime}(r)\,dr=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\tilde{q}(r_{2})^{2}-\tilde{q}(r_{1})^{2}\Big].

It remains to sign
\tilde{q}(r_{2})^{2}-\tilde{q}(r_{1})^{2}
. Because
q
is strictly increasing and strictly convex on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
(by Proposition
4
(ii) under Assumption
4
; see Lemma
2
for primitive sufficient conditions), the following observation suffices.

Sub-Lemma (revised).
Let
q
be strictly convex and strictly increasing on
[r_{1},r_{2}]
, with
\bar{q}=(r_{2}-r_{1})^{-1}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}q(s)\,ds
and
\tilde{q}(r)=q(r)-\bar{q}
. Then: (i) by strict convexity,
\bar{q}<\tfrac{1}{2}(q(r_{1})+q(r_{2}))
, hence
\tilde{q}(r_{1})+\tilde{q}(r_{2})>0
; (ii) since
q
is strictly increasing,
\tilde{q}(r_{1})<\tilde{q}(r_{2})
, so together with (i) we obtain
\tilde{q}(r_{1})<0<\tilde{q}(r_{2})
and
\tilde{q}(r_{2})>|\tilde{q}(r_{1})|
; (iii) therefore

\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\tilde{q}(r)\,q^{\prime}(r)\,dr=\frac{1}{2}\big[\tilde{q}(r_{2})^{2}-\tilde{q}(r_{1})^{2}\big]>0,

and hence
B(r_{1},r_{2})>0
.

Step 3: Divergence of the
t
-statistic.

Under the signal-dominance condition
\delta_{\max}(T)^{2}/(T\sigma_{u}^{2})\to\infty
, the standard error of
\hat{\beta}
is
\mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta})=O_{p}(T^{-1/2}\,\sigma_{u}/\delta_{\max}(T))
(derived from the leading deterministic denominator and the residual variance
\sigma_{u}^{2}+o_{p}(1)
). Since
\hat{\beta}=B(r_{1},r_{2})/T+o_{p}(1/T)
with
B>0
:

t_{\hat{\beta}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}}{\mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta})}=O_{p}\!\left(\frac{B/T}{T^{-1/2}\,\sigma_{u}/\delta_{\max}(T)}\right)=O_{p}\!\left(\frac{B\,\delta_{\max}(T)}{\sigma_{u}\sqrt{T}}\right).

Therefore
t_{\hat{\beta}}\to+\infty
whenever
\delta_{\max}(T)/(\sigma_{u}\sqrt{T})\to\infty
, equivalently under the imposed signal-dominance condition. In particular, fixed
\delta_{\max}>0
does
not
yield divergence as
T\to\infty
; divergence requires
\delta_{\max}(T)
to grow sufficiently fast relative to
\sqrt{T}
. Hence the generalized supremum augmented Dickey–Fuller test rejects with probability approaching one whenever the admissible class contains such a ramp-up window. ∎

Proof of Proposition
6
(Size of the Adjusted Test)

Under
b_{t}=0
, consider first the detrended log-price implementation. By Definition
2
,

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=y_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\delta}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\delta}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}.

Using
d_{t}=d_{0}+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\delta_{s}+\varepsilon_{s})
, this becomes

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=d_{0}+C+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\delta_{s}-\hat{\delta}_{s})+(\mathcal{T}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}).

Hence, under the uniform-consistency assumptions of the proposition,

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=d_{0}+C+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+o_{p}(1)

uniformly in
t
. After detrending,

\tilde{y}_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+r_{t,T},\qquad\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}|r_{t,T}|=o_{p}(1).

If
Y_{t}^{(0)}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}
, then by continuous mapping for the GSADF functional applied uniformly over admissible windows, the GSADF statistic computed from
Y^{(0)}+r
converges in distribution to the GSADF statistic computed from
Y^{(0)}
. Hence the limit distribution coincides with the standard PSY null, and the adjusted detrended log-price test has asymptotic size
\alpha
.

For the price-dividend implementation,

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj,PD}}=(p_{t}-d_{t})-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=C+\mathcal{T}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=C+o_{p}(1)

uniformly under
b_{t}=0
. Therefore GSADF applied to a constant plus
o_{p}(1)
has the same limit as on a constant, so no technology-driven explosive behavior remains detectable after adjustment. ∎

Proof of Proposition
7
(Power of the Adjusted Test)

Under the alternative
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}
, consider first the detrended log-price implementation. By Definition
2
,

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=p_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\delta}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=d_{t}+C+\mathcal{T}_{t}+b_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\delta}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}.

Using
d_{t}=d_{0}+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\delta_{s}+\varepsilon_{s})
and the uniform-consistency conditions,

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=d_{0}+C+ct+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+b_{t}+o_{p}(1)

uniformly in
t
. Detrending removes
d_{0}+ct
, leaving

\tilde{y}_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+b_{t}+r_{t,T},\qquad\sup_{1\leq t\leq T}|r_{t,T}|=o_{p}(1).

Thus the explosive component
b_{t}
remains. If the standard PSY statistic for the technology-free benchmark
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}+b_{t}
rejects at rate
\omega_{T}\to\infty
, then any remainder with
\sup_{t}|r_{t,T}|=o_{p}(\omega_{T})
is asymptotically negligible for the GSADF functional, so the adjusted test has the same rejection probability limit, namely one.

For the price-dividend implementation,

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj,PD}}=(p_{t}-d_{t})-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=C+u_{t}+b_{t}+(\mathcal{T}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t})=C+u_{t}+b_{t}+o_{p}(1).

Hence the adjustment removes only the technology component and leaves the explosive bubble term
b_{t}
intact. The adjusted price-dividend-ratio test therefore has the same first-order power as the standard PSY test applied to the corresponding technology-free series. ∎

Proof of Proposition
8
(Time-Varying Expected Returns)

Under the horizon-by-horizon specification (
B.1
), the present-value identity becomes

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta d_{t+1+j}-r_{t+1+j}]

\displaystyle=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\big[(c+\delta_{t+1+j})-(\bar{r}+\phi\,\delta_{t+1+j})\big]

\displaystyle=d_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{1-\rho}+\frac{c-\bar{r}}{1-\rho}+(1-\phi)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\delta_{t+1+j}

\displaystyle=d_{t}+C+(1-\phi)\,\mathcal{T}_{t}.

(D.10)

Hence

f_{t}-d_{t}=C+(1-\phi)\,\mathcal{T}_{t}.

If
\phi<1
, then
1-\phi>0
, so the technology component of the log price-dividend ratio has exactly the same sign, monotonicity, and convexity properties as
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. Indeed,

\Delta(f_{t}-d_{t})=(1-\phi)\,\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t},\qquad\Delta^{2}(f_{t}-d_{t})=(1-\phi)\,\Delta^{2}\mathcal{T}_{t}.

If
\phi<0
, then
1-\phi>1
, so the level, slope, and curvature of the price-dividend-ratio technology component are amplified by the multiplicative factor
1-\phi
.

For the drift of the log price itself,

\Delta f_{t}=c+\delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}+(1-\phi)\,\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}.

Thus any comparison of the drift with

depends on the sign of
\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}
on the window under consideration; no stronger global drift claim follows from
\phi<1
alone. ∎

Proof of Proposition
9
(Stochastic Technology Amplification)

Part (i): Jumps compound explosive dynamics via persistent posterior levels.

Under Assumption
9
, agents observe

\delta_{t}=g_{\star}(t)\,\bar{\delta}+\sigma_{\xi}\,\xi_{t},\qquad\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

during the learning window, and form the posterior mean
\hat{\delta}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
. The Kalman filter therefore gives

\hat{\delta}_{t}\;=\;\hat{\delta}_{t-1}+K_{t}\,\nu_{t},\qquad\nu_{t}:=\delta_{t}-g_{\star}(t)\,\hat{\delta}_{t-1},\qquad K_{t}:=\frac{P_{t-1}\,g_{\star}(t)}{g_{\star}(t)^{2}\,P_{t-1}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}},

with posterior variance recursion

P_{t}\;=\;P_{t-1}-K_{t}\,g_{\star}(t)\,P_{t-1}\;=\;\frac{P_{t-1}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{g_{\star}(t)^{2}\,P_{t-1}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}},

where
P_{t-1}=\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
. The innovation
\nu_{t}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\nu_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\;=\;g_{\star}(t)\,\mathbb{E}[\bar{\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-g_{\star}(t)\,\hat{\delta}_{t-1}+\sigma_{\xi}\,\mathbb{E}[\xi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\;=\;0,

so
\{\nu_{t}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
and is therefore serially uncorrelated.

However, the posterior mean
\hat{\delta}_{t}
is itself a martingale (by the tower property applied to
\hat{\delta}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
), and hence its
levels
are maximally persistent:
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\delta}_{t},\hat{\delta}_{t+h})=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\delta}_{t})=P_{0}-P_{t}
, which is nondecreasing in
t
and bounded by
P_{0}
. Substituting the posterior mean into the technology present-value operator,

\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\delta_{t+1+j}]\;=\;\hat{\delta}_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t),\qquad G_{\rho}(t):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{j}\,g_{\star}(t+1+j),

so the innovation in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
relative to
\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\mathcal{T}_{t}]
is
G_{\rho}(t)\,K_{t}\,\nu_{t}
, while the total change satisfies

\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}\;=\;G_{\rho}(t)\,K_{t}\,\nu_{t}+\hat{\delta}_{t-1}\,\Delta G_{\rho}(t),

where
G_{\rho}(t)\,K_{t}\,\nu_{t}
is the surprise component (an MDS) and
\hat{\delta}_{t-1}\,\Delta G_{\rho}(t)
is the predictable drift. Because
\hat{\delta}_{s}
for
s>t
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\hat{\delta}_{s}]=\hat{\delta}_{t}
(martingale property), the positive surprise component propagates into
all
future values of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in expectation: a single
\nu_{t}>0
raises
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\mathcal{T}_{t}]
for every subsequent
t^{\prime}>t
within the adoption window, because
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\hat{\delta}_{t^{\prime}}]G_{\rho}(t^{\prime})=\hat{\delta}_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t^{\prime})
and
\hat{\delta}_{t}
has been permanently revised upward.

During the early adoption phase
[T_{1},T_{1}+\tau]
,
K_{t}
is relatively high because
P_{t-1}
is close to
P_{0}
when little learning has yet taken place. Moreover,
G_{\rho}(t)>0
throughout the adoption window and is bounded away from zero on a non-empty subinterval near the peak. Hence the early-adoption innovation term is non-trivially amplified relative to the post-maturation regime where
G_{\rho}(t)\to 0
. The distinction between innovation-level serial uncorrelation and posterior-level persistence is the precise economic content: PSY’s rejection probability is driven by the
level path
of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, not by autocorrelation of
\Delta\mathcal{T}_{t}
, so a martingale-difference innovation sequence that raises the level persistently is sufficient to amplify spurious explosiveness. The finite-sample quantitative effect is documented in Panel A of Table
E.3
.

Part (ii): Asymptotic negligibility of the learning channel at PSY scale.

Under Assumption
9
, the fundamental price innovation decomposes as
\Delta f_{t}=\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{det}}+\varepsilon_{t}+K_{t}\,\nu_{t}\,G_{\rho}(t)
, where
\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{det}}
is the deterministic drift from Proposition
3
and the last term captures the Bayesian learning innovation (
\nu_{t}
is the Kalman innovation, a martingale difference with conditional variance
g_{\star}(t)^{2}P_{t-1}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}
, mutually independent of
\varepsilon_{t}
). We establish that under the local parametrization of Assumption
5
with a fixed discount factor
\rho\in[0,1)
, the partial-sum martingale
S_{t}\equiv\sum_{s\leq t}K_{s}\,\nu_{s}\,G_{\rho}(s)
is asymptotically negligible at PSY scale, so the gsADF limit law coincides with the deterministic-
\delta
limit law of Theorem
2
. Any
\sigma_{\xi}
-dependence of the test’s rejection probability is therefore a finite-sample, not a first-order asymptotic, phenomenon.

Step (ii.a): Scaling of
g_{\star}
,
K_{s}
, and
G_{\rho}
.
Assumption
9
normalizes
g_{\star}
as a discrete density on the adoption–maturation window
[T_{1},T_{2}]
. Under Assumption
5
this window has length
\Theta(T)
, so the natural local specification is

g_{\star,T}(s)\;=\;T^{-1}\,h_{\star}(s/T)\;+\;o(T^{-1})\qquad\text{uniformly in }s\in[T_{1},T_{2}],

for a continuous shape function
h_{\star}:[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\int_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{2}}h_{\star}(u)\,du=1
. To keep the deterministic local channel
\delta_{\max}(T)\,q(\cdot)=c_{\delta}T^{-1/2}\,q(\cdot)
nondegenerate and on the same scale, prior moments are nested as
\mu_{\delta}=O(\sqrt{T})
and
\sigma_{\delta}=O(\sqrt{T})
, i.e.
P_{0}=T\tau_{\delta}^{2}
for some
\tau_{\delta}\in(0,\infty)
; the innovation variance
\sigma_{\xi}
is kept fixed in
(0,\infty)
.

The Kalman precision recursion
P_{s}^{-1}=P_{0}^{-1}+\sigma_{\xi}^{-2}\sum_{j\leq s}g_{\star,T}(j)^{2}
then gives, uniformly in

,

P_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor,T}\;=\;T\,p(r)+o(T),\qquad p(r)=\Big[\tau_{\delta}^{-2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{-2}\!\int_{\lambda_{1}}^{r\wedge\lambda_{2}}h_{\star}(u)^{2}\,du\Big]^{-1},

and hence
K_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor,T}=p(r)\,h_{\star}(r)/\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+o(1)=O(1)
. For fixed
\rho\in[0,1)
, the geometrically weighted kernel satisfies

G_{\rho,T}(s)\;=\;\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho^{j}\,g_{\star,T}(s+1+j)\;=\;T^{-1}\,\frac{h_{\star}(s/T)}{1-\rho}\;+\;o(T^{-1}),

uniformly on
[T\lambda_{1},T\lambda_{2}]
. Consequently

K_{s}\,G_{\rho,T}(s)\;=\;T^{-1}\,\kappa_{\rho}(s/T)+o(T^{-1}),\qquad\kappa_{\rho}(u)=\frac{p(u)\,h_{\star}(u)^{2}}{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\,(1-\rho)}.

Step (ii.b): Negligibility of
T^{-1/2}S_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}
.
Because
\{\nu_{s}\}
is a martingale difference array with conditional variance
g_{\star,T}(s)^{2}P_{s-1,T}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}=\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+o(1)
(the first term is
O(T^{-1})
), the predictable quadratic variation of
T^{-1/2}S_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}
satisfies

\displaystyle T^{-1}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor Tr\rfloor}\!\big(K_{s}\,G_{\rho,T}(s)\big)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\nu_{s}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}]

\displaystyle=T^{-1}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor Tr\rfloor}\!\big(T^{-1}\kappa_{\rho}(s/T)\big)^{2}\!\big(\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+o(1)\big)

\displaystyle=O(T^{-2})\cdot T\cdot O(1)\;=\;O(T^{-2})\;\to\;0.

By Chebyshev’s inequality applied to the martingale
T^{-1/2}S_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}
,
T^{-1/2}S_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}\xrightarrow{L^{2}}0
, and tightness of the martingale
(
31
)
yields

T^{-1/2}\,S_{\lfloor Tr\rfloor}\;\Rightarrow\;0\qquad\text{in }D[0,1].

Step (ii.c): gsADF limit unchanged.
Decomposing the detrended process as
\tilde{y}_{t}=\tilde{y}_{t}^{\mathrm{det}}+S_{t}
on any subsample window, Step (ii.b) and the Continuous Mapping Theorem applied to the sample-ratio functional
\mathcal{F}
of (
6
) imply that
\mathrm{GSADF}_{T}
converges to the same limit as the deterministic-
\delta
case, namely the limit of Theorem
2
. Hence
\sigma_{\xi}
does
not
appear in the first-order limit law.

Remark on finite-sample behavior.
The negligibility result holds at PSY scale
T^{-1/2}
under fixed
\rho
. At finite
T
, the learning-innovation contribution
S_{t}
is of root-mean-square order
T^{-1}\,\big(\int_{0}^{r}\kappa_{\rho}(u)^{2}\,du\big)^{1/2}\,\sigma_{\xi}
, which is nonzero and depends on
\sigma_{\xi}
through the weight
\kappa_{\rho}
. This pathwise contribution can shift the rejection probability in either direction depending on the drift

, the window
\mathcal{R}
, and the specific empirical implementation. Accordingly we do not state a monotone size result as a theorem; the empirical direction is documented in Table
E.3
, where Panel A (price-dividend ratio) exhibits a monotonic increase in rejection rate with

and Panel B (detrended log price) shows near-invariance. A nondegenerate Brownian-functional limit featuring

arises only under a local-to-unity parametrization
\rho_{T}=1-c_{\rho}/T
; that extension is outside the scope of this paper, which takes

as the fixed steady-state log-linearization constant of

.

Part (iii): Adjusted test validity.

For the detrended log-price implementation, Definition
2
uses the full adjustment

y_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=y_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\delta}_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{t}.

Hence the conclusion follows from Proposition

: after subtracting both the cumulative technology shocks and the present-value term, the adjusted series has the same first-order asymptotics as the technology-free benchmark. For the log price-dividend-ratio implementation, Definition

subtracts only

Lemma 3

The baseline experiments above treat the technology shock as deterministic. We now assess the quantitative implications of the stochastic extension developed in Section

We simulate the stochastic data-generating process of Assumption

\hat{\delta}_{t}

\sigma_{\xi}=0.005

\varepsilon_{t}

\xi_{t}

\bar{\delta}

displays the rejection rate as a function of CV for both implementations, with the deterministic baseline and technology-adjusted rates shown for reference. The monotonic increase in Panel A and the flat profile in Panel B together provide a quantitative, implementation-dependent refinement of Proposition
9
(ii): the theorem guarantees only that the limit law depends nontrivially on
\sigma_{\xi}
, and the simulations show that this dependence translates into substantial size amplification through the price-dividend ratio channel—where the learning-driven martingale component enters as a direct level effect—while being largely absorbed by cumulation and linear detrending in the detrended log-price channel. The deterministic results of Section
E.3
therefore represent a conservative lower bound on the size distortion for the price-dividend implementation when agents face genuine uncertainty about the technology’s ultimate impact, precisely the empirically relevant case during general-purpose technology adoption episodes.

E.6
Distinguishing Technology from Speculation: An Overlap Experiment

The preceding experiments establish that PSY produces spurious rejections when fundamentals incorporate technology-driven growth, even in the complete absence of a speculative bubble. A natural follow-up question is whether the technology-adjusted PSY test identifies a genuine speculative bubble while avoiding the spurious detection generated by the technology component when both are present. We address this question through a controlled overlap experiment.

E.6.1  Design

We construct a data-generating process in which a technology shock and a genuine speculative bubble overlap in time:

•

Technology window:
[T_{1},T_{2}]=[50,150]
with a Gamma-like
\delta_{t}
profile,
\delta_{\max}=0.25
(a strong technology shock chosen to ensure robust spurious detection by the unadjusted PSY test).

•

Bubble window:
[t_{2},t_{3}]=[100,200]
. The speculative bubble component
b_{t}
is initialized at
b_{t_{2}}=0.3
and follows an explosive AR(1) process
b_{t}=\rho_{\mathrm{bub}}\,b_{t-1}+\eta_{t}
with
\rho_{\mathrm{bub}}=1.035
and
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.10^{2})
during
[t_{2},t_{3}]
. After
t_{3}
, the bubble collapses via
b_{t}=0.5\,b_{t-1}+\eta_{t}
.

The observed price is
p_{t}=f_{t}+b_{t}+e_{t}
, where
f_{t}
is the technology-augmented fundamental and
e_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.30^{2})
is observation noise. The technology-adjusted price removes both the present-value term
\mathcal{T}_{t}
and the cumulative technology contribution to dividends:
p_{t}^{\mathrm{adj}}=p_{t}-\mathcal{T}_{t}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\delta_{s}
. We apply PSY to the detrended log price, which is the transformation used in our empirical analysis.

The key prediction is that the unadjusted PSY test should first trigger during the technology-only phase (between
t=50
, the start of technology window, and
t=100
, the start of bubble window), constituting a spurious early detection, while the technology-adjusted PSY test should first trigger during or after the bubble window (a genuine detection).

This experiment illustrates the practical consequences of ignoring technology-driven fundamentals in bubble detection. The unadjusted PSY test produces a spurious early warning 41 periods before any speculative activity begins, with the first detection occurring during the technology-only phase (

[100,150]

Appendix F
Robustness Tests: Identification and Specification

This appendix reports five complementary diagnostics that assess the separability of technology proxies from the speculative bubble component, which is the paper’s main identification assumption. While Proposition
2
provides a structural justification for this separability, the tests below evaluate its empirical validity.

F.1
Granger Causality Tests

Under the separability assumption, stock returns should not Granger-cause the technology proxies during speculative periods: if the bubble component
b_{t}
is orthogonal to fundamentals, explosive price dynamics should not feed back into real technology variables. During non-speculative periods, however, bidirectional causality between technology and returns is expected, since technology fundamentals drive prices and stock market valuations affect corporate investment.

We test this prediction by estimating bivariate Granger causality regressions between NASDAQ returns and each of the three technology proxies (IT-investment growth, TFP growth, and patent growth), separately for speculative and non-speculative regimes. The PSY-detected bubble periods are too narrow (5–6 months for the dot-com episode) to support meaningful inference, so we define broader speculative periods based on the run-up phase: January 1997 to March 2000 for the dot-com era (
n=39
) and January 2023 to December 2025 for the AI era (
n=33
–
35
). Lag lengths are selected by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust
p
-values could not be computed because of near-singular covariance matrices in the small speculative-period samples; we therefore report both standard
F
-test and wild bootstrap
p
-values, which yield consistent conclusions throughout.

Table
F.1
reports the results. During both speculative periods, no technology proxy is predicted by lagged returns in the Granger sense: all
p
-values exceed 0.70 for the dot-com episode and 0.54 for the AI era. The reverse direction is also insignificant: technology variables do not predict returns in the Granger sense during speculative periods, which is consistent with speculative dynamics reflecting sentiment rather than fundamentals. This pattern holds uniformly across all three technology proxies and both eras, providing direct support for the separability assumption.

The non-speculative periods tell a complementary story. In the dot-com non-speculative sample (
n=136
–
141
), only one marginally significant relationship emerges: returns Granger-cause IT investment at the 10% level (
F=2.476
,
p=0.062
), consistent with an accelerator channel whereby equity market valuations affect IT spending with a lag. No other direction is significant. In the AI non-speculative sample (
n=36
), the results are richer: IT investment, TFP, and patent grants all Granger-cause returns at the 5% level or better (
p=0.019
,
0.015
, and
0.005
, respectively), while returns also Granger-cause TFP (
p=0.016
). This bidirectional causality during normal periods is expected and economically sensible: technology fundamentals drive asset prices, while stock market wealth effects and Tobin’s
q
channels feed back into productivity. Crucially, this bidirectional link is absent during the speculative regime, which is precisely what the separability assumption requires.

F.2
Placebo Tests: Non-Technology Covariates

If the technology-adjusted test eliminates explosive signals specifically because the technology proxies capture genuine fundamentals, then replacing these proxies with economically irrelevant covariates should fail to eliminate the signal. We construct “placebo” counterfactuals by regressing log NASDAQ prices on three non-technology variables (consumer sentiment (University of Michigan Index), the VIX implied volatility index, and industrial production (INDPRO)) via DOLS over the same training windows, then apply PSY to the resulting price gaps. Table
F.2
reports the results alongside the technology baseline.

For the AI era, all three placebos yield GSADF statistics well below their respective 10 percent critical values (
-0.223
,
0.712
, and
0.869
against
\text{CV}_{0.10}=1.334
), consistent with the baseline technology adjustment (test statistic
=-0.275
). This is expected: when no explosive dynamic exists in the data, neither the correct nor incorrect covariates will produce a rejection.

The dot-com results are more nuanced. Industrial production does not reject (
\text{test statistic}=1.460<\text{CV}_{0.10}=1.768
;
R^{2}=0.743
), confirming that a generic macroeconomic variable cannot replicate the technology adjustment’s bubble-elimination effect. The VIX could not be tested for the dot-com era because the CBOE VIX series begins in January 1990, providing only 12 training observations, which is insufficient for reliable DOLS estimation. However, consumer sentiment rejects at both the 10 and 5 percent levels (
\text{test statistic}=2.283
,
\text{CV}_{0.10}=1.768
,
\text{CV}_{0.05}=2.025
). The result requires careful interpretation. The sentiment-based DOLS specification achieves a low
R^{2}
of only 0.308, indicating that sentiment is a poor predictor of log price levels; the resulting price gap retains most of the original price variation, including both the fundamental and speculative components. Moreover, during the dot-com boom, consumer sentiment was itself heavily influenced by the technology-driven equity rally through wealth effects
(
32
)
, making it endogenous to the very dynamics it is intended to “placebo” against. The sentiment rejection therefore does not undermine the identification strategy; it reflects the fact that sentiment is not exogenous to the bubble.

F.3
Cross-Sectional Identification: PCA of Magnificent Seven Price Gaps

The Magnificent Seven panel provides a cross-sectional test of the technology-adjustment methodology. We apply PCA to the technology-adjusted price gaps
\hat{g}_{i,t}
of the seven firms, computed from OLS regressions of each firm’s log price on firm-specific Compustat fundamentals (real revenue per share and real R&D expenditure per share) using the 2006–2019 training window (excluding each firm’s own bubble months). The analysis uses 72 months of common post-training observations.

Table
F.3
reports the variance decomposition and PSY results for each principal component. The first principal component (PC1) explains 60.0% of the total variation, revealing a dominant common factor in the adjusted price gaps. The loadings on PC1 are broadly similar across six of the seven firms (GOOGL (
-0.472
), TSLA (
-0.437
), NVDA (
-0.423
), AMZN (
-0.374
), META (
-0.371
), and AAPL (
-0.358
)), but conspicuously low for MSFT (
-0.054
). Microsoft’s near-zero loading indicates that its price-gap dynamics are driven by idiosyncratic factors rather than the common AI-driven technology repricing that affects the other six firms. PC2 accounts for 20.6% of variance and is loaded most heavily on MSFT (0.754) and AMZN (
-0.435

The key result is that none of the seven principal components exhibits explosive behavior. All GSADF statistics fall below the 10 percent critical value of 1.334: PC1 yields
\text{GSADF}=0.844

Even the dominant common factor, which loads on all firms except Microsoft, is stationary, consistent with the interpretation that the firm-level technology adjustment has successfully removed the fundamental repricing component, leaving residuals with no evidence of speculative excess. F.4
Training-Window Stability Analysis

A potential concern with the technology-adjusted test is that results depend on the training period used to estimate the DOLS coefficients. We systematically vary the training window end-date: from January 1985 to December 1994 for the dot-com application (with a fixed start of January 1975) and from January 2010 to December 2019 for the AI application (with a fixed start of January 2006). We reestimate the DOLS coefficients on each subsample and apply PSY to the resulting price gaps. Table
F.4
reports representative windows; Figure
F.2
displays the complete trajectories.

The two eras present a clear contrast. For the AI era, the GSADF statistic ranges from
-0.30
to
0.84
across 121 training windows, never approaching the 10 percent critical value of 1.176. The “no AI bubble” conclusion is entirely insensitive to training window choice. This robustness holds despite substantial variation in the DOLS coefficients: the IT-investment coefficient
\hat{\beta}_{\text{IT}}
ranges from 0.35 to 2.65, and the patent coefficient
\hat{\beta}_{\text{Pat}}
spans
-0.16
to 1.19, yet the adjusted price gap never produces a rejection. The
R^{2}
remains high throughout (0.82–0.91), indicating that the technology proxies explain a large and stable share of price variation regardless of the specific training window.

For the dot-com era, the picture is more complex. The test statistic varies from approximately 1.30 (training ending September 1987) to 2.76 (training ending June 1985), a range of 1.46. With the 10 percent critical value at 1.745, early training windows (ending before mid-1987) yield rejections, a gap from approximately mid-1987 through late 1988 yields non-rejections, then windows ending from late 1988 through early 1992 return to rejection, before settling below the critical value for windows ending after mid-1992. The source of this instability is primarily the patent coefficient, which varies from
-0.39
(January 1985) to
+1.04
(December 1994), while the IT coefficient is comparatively stable at
0.42
–
0.49
. This sensitivity reflects the changing role of patenting in the technology-growth relationship: in the early 1980s, patent grants were a poor proxy for innovation
(
20
)
, while their informational content improved substantially through the 1990s.

F.5
Hansen (1992) Parameter Instability Test

The training-window analysis reveals coefficient variation, but does not formally test whether the cointegrating relationship is stable within each training period. We apply the
15

L_{c}
test for parameter instability in cointegrating regressions, which tests the null hypothesis that the DOLS coefficients are constant against the alternative of a martingale parameter process. Table
F.5
reports the results.

The
L_{c}
test rejects parameter stability in both eras and both specifications. For the dot-com baseline (technology-only),
L_{c}=1.845
(
p=0.009
); for the AI-era baseline,
L_{c}=3.994
(
p<0.001
). The extended specifications including the term spread yield similar conclusions (
L_{c}=1.780
,
p=0.018
for dot-com;
L_{c}=3.941
,
p<0.001
for the AI era). All statistics far exceed the 1% critical values (1.160 for baseline, 1.278 for extended), indicating strong evidence of parameter instability.

This finding has important implications. The cointegrating relationship between log prices and technology proxies is not stable over the training periods. For the dot-com era (1975–1990), this is unsurprising: the technology-growth nexus evolved dramatically as IT transitioned from mainframes to personal computers to networked systems. For the AI era (2006–2019), instability reflects the transition from mobile and cloud computing to the nascent AI paradigm, with the patent coefficient (capturing innovation composition) absorbing most of the structural change, as the training-window analysis of Section
F.4
confirms.

The Hansen rejection does not invalidate the technology-adjusted test, but it qualifies the interpretation. The DOLS estimates represent an average technology-price relationship over the training period, and the adjustment is valid insofar as this average captures the typical equilibrium. The training-window analysis provides reassurance: despite parameter instability, the qualitative conclusions are robust. In particular, the AI-era non-rejection finding holds across all 121 training windows, even as individual coefficients vary substantially. The dot-com results are more fragile, with the GSADF statistic crossing the critical value as the training window shifts, though the qualitative interpretation (a genuine speculative peak in 1999–2000) is supported across the majority of specifications. Future work could address parameter instability more formally through time-varying coefficient approaches, such as the
4
time-varying cointegration framework or state-space models with Kalman-filtered parameters.
