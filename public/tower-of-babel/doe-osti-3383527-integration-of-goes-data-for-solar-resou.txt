Executive Summary
The National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB), produced by the National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR), provides high-resolution solar resource data for the contiguous United States
(CONUS) using Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite (GOES) East and West
observations. This study evaluates the integration of multi-satellite data within the GOES-
East/West overlap regions, where conventional longitude-based selection methods often produce
an artificial boundary seam. Our results demonstrate that an advanced blending algorithm, which
incorporates sun-satellite scattering angles and satellite viewing zenith angles, improves NSRDB
accuracy and creates a spatially continuous dataset. Validation against ground-based irradiance
measurements reveals reductions in both percentage error and normalized root mean square
error, particularly in the central United States. The dynamical integration of multi-satellite data
provides a robust foundation for more precise modeling of the solar resource and improved
spatiotemporal analysis of solar ramps across CONUS.

Introduction
Geostationary satellites play an important role in modern solar resource assessments by
providing a continuous, global-scale perspective that is unattainable through surface-based
measurements. These observations capture the evolution of clouds, which represents the primary
source of uncertainty in the modeling of surface solar radiation. Consequently, data from
geostationary satellites are often used to quantify key metrics, such as global horizontal
irradiance (GHI) and direct normal irradiance (DNI), that are required for assessing solar energy
potential and managing the variability of large-scale photovoltaic integration.

The National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB) (Sengupta et al., 2018) delivers high-spatial-
resolution and high-temporal-resolution estimates of GHI and DNI by integrating satellite
observations with advanced physical models (Xie and Sengupta, 2018; Xie et al., 2018; Xie et
al., 2016; Xie et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). Over the contiguous United States
(CONUS), this approach relies on data from the Geostationary Operational Environmental
Satellites (GOES) series. Cloud property retrievals from the Pathfinder Atmospheres Extended
(PATMOS-x) system (Foster et al., 2023) are incorporated into the Fast All-sky Radiation Model
for Solar applications (FARMS) (Xie et al., 2016) to process the data. This computational
framework is employed to compute irradiance data at spatial resolutions of 2–4 km and temporal
intervals as frequent as 5–30 minutes.

The NSRDB data over the CONUS domain are derived from two geostationary platforms,
GOES-West and GOES-East, each covering distinct portions of the domain. Although this dual-
satellite configuration provides complete geographic coverage, it introduces challenges in their
overlapping regions. For example, inconsistencies between GOES-West and GOES-East
retrievals are commonly observed due to differences in viewing geometry, sensor calibration,
and atmospheric path length. The NSRDB has applied a strict longitudinal boundary at 105°W to
partition data between the two satellites. This binary approach can lead to artificial seams or
discontinuities in the dataset, especially near the boundary. In more recent NSRDB data,
however, this difference is less pronounced, possibly reflecting advancements in the calibration
of satellite instruments.

Satellite-derived estimation often has biases depending on the terrain complexity and the local
atmospheric conditions. To address these limitations, high-quality surface measurements are
often employed as a critical benchmark for validating retrieval accuracy. Relevant studies
emphasized that cross-validation of satellite data with surface observations is critical for
identifying model sensitivities to variables, such as aerosol optical depth and cloud optical
thickness (Habte et al., 2017; Yang and Bright, 2020).

This study aims to investigate the integration of GOES-West and GOES-East data to improve the
consistency of solar radiation products. To achieve this goal, we analyze GHI and DNI values
across 17 surface sites, covering a wide range of climatic and terrain conditions. The satellite-
derived estimates are validated against high-quality surface measurements from several radiation
networks, including the Surface Radiation Budget (SURFRAD), Solar Radiation (SOLRAD), the
University of Oregon, the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement
(ARM) program, and the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR). By benchmarking satellite

1 Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellites
  Data for the National Solar Radiation Database
The NSRDB data are available from 1998 to 2024. Data for 2025 will become available by
September 2026. Figure 1 illustrates the satellite data used for the NSRDB. It uses dual
geostationary satellites positioned at GOES-West and GOES-East to monitor CONUS.
Historically, individual satellites maintained operational lifespans of 6–10 years. Significant
architectural upgrades in 2019 transitioned the system from legacy five-channel imagers to the
Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI). This transition enhanced the temporal resolution from 30
minutes to 5 minutes over CONUS and 10 minutes for the full disk. The spatial resolution
increased from 4 km to 2 km.

 Figure 1. Satellite data used for the NSRDB

A key challenge in earlier GOES observations was that a single satellite could not provide
complete coverage of CONUS. Prior to 2019, individual satellites had a limited field of view that
prevented full-domain coverage. For example, GOES-West and GOES-East failed to capture
portions of the northeastern and northwestern United States, respectively. As a result, multiple
satellites are required to achieve complete spatial coverage for the period. Since 2019, the ABI’s
superior performance at large viewing zenith angles has expanded the domain of high-quality
cloud products. The expanded field of view allows either orbital position to provide full CONUS
coverage independently (Figure 2).

 3

 Figure 2. Spatial coverage of GOES

A more significant challenge with the GOES data is the temporal variation in the availability of
cloud property retrievals. Figure 3 shows sample cloud optical thickness from GOES-West and
GOES-East with inconsistent spatial and temporal availability. During morning hours, GOES-
East begins producing retrievals along the East Coast, whereas GOES-West provides no
coverage due to unfavorable sun-satellite scattering geometry. In both morning and afternoon
periods, GOES-East generally yields more daytime cloud retrievals than GOES-West, especially
over the eastern United States; however, GOES-West provides critical coverage over Hawaii and
parts of Alaska, and it provides improved data availability in the northwestern United States.

 4

  Figure 3. Sample data for cloud optical thickness from GOES-West (left) and GOES-East (right)

Because of satellite geometry, GOES-West systematically observes CONUS at larger satellite
viewing zenith angles than GOES-East (Figure 4). The larger slant paths increase the
atmospheric scattering and lead to higher uncertainty in cloud retrievals. As a result, cloud
products derived from GOES-West are more susceptible to lower data quality. When the satellite
viewing zenith angles or associated scattering geometry exceeds the retrieval algorithm’s
thresholds, retrievals can be omitted from the output product. Consequently, GOES-West shows
a higher frequency of missing cloud retrievals over CONUS than GOES-East.

2 Current Blending Algorithm in the National Solar
  Radiation Database
In the current NSRDB, a fixed longitude-based partition at 105°W is used to merge geostationary
satellite observations. Specifically, GOES-West data are exclusively used west of this meridian,
while GOES-East data are exclusively used to the east (Figure 5). Although computationally
straightforward, this hard split introduces a discontinuity in the treatment of satellite observations
across the domain.

 Figure 5. Algorithm to integrate GOES-West and GOES-East data into the current NSRDB

For example, a boundary seam can often be observed in the merged products at the interface
between the GOES-West and GOES-East coverage (Figure 6). This artifact manifests as a spatial
shift in retrieved cloud properties and surface irradiance. The discontinuity primarily reflects
differences in satellite calibration, sensor characteristics, and viewing geometry between the two
orbital platforms. In an ideal blending algorithm, the transition between adjacent satellite
domains should be continuous and physically consistent, which requires an advanced blending
strategy.

 7

 Figure 6. Mean GHI in 2017 using the current NSRDB data

In the latest-generation GOES observations, this boundary artifact is substantially reduced due to
improved sensor calibration, enhanced radiometric consistency, and improved retrieval
algorithms that better account for large-angle viewing effects. As a result, the seam becomes
nearly imperceptible in modern datasets; however, it remains evident in earlier NSRDB datasets,
where differences in calibration maturity and retrieval methodology are more pronounced.

3 New Blending Algorithm for the National Solar
  Radiation Database
3.1 Data
To develop a new blending algorithm for the NSRDB, we selected 17 surface sites from several
radiation networks, including SURFRAD, SOLRAD, the University of Oregon, the U.S.
Department of Energy ARM, and NLR. They are distributed across the United States to ensure
representative coverage of different climatic and solar regimes (Figure 7). To reduce the
influence of pixel-scale noise and geolocation mismatches between satellite retrievals and
surface measurements, we applied a spatial aggregation approach that averages satellite-derived
variables over 50-km × 50-km domains centered on each site. This spatial averaging also
provides an estimate of satellite-derived cloud fraction, computed as an average of cloud mask
data within each aggregation window.

 Figure 7. Surface sites used in the satellite data validation

Given the improved radiometric calibration and retrieval algorithm of the latest GOES data, we
use data from 2023 as the basis for developing a new satellite blending algorithm. This period
provides reduced inter-satellite bias for studying the characterization of geometry-dependent
differences between GOES-East and GOES-West retrievals. To assess the performance of the
proposed approach, the developed model is independently applied to the 2017 dataset, which
represents an earlier generation of GOES observations with larger inter-satellite inconsistency.

3.2 Dependence of the Bias in Global Horizontal Irradiance
When comparing satellite-derived products against surface observations, we find that the bias is
dependent on multiple observational and geometric factors. Among these, the scattering
geometry associated with GOES observations is a dominant factor affecting the systematic error.

The analysis of the mean bias error and the normalized root mean square error (nRMSE) for GHI
indicates that most observations occur under backward-scattering conditions for GOES-East
(Figure 8). In this regime, GOES-East retrievals generally show smaller biases than GOES-West.
This improved performance is associated with favorable viewing geometry and increased
sensitivity to cloud optical properties under backward-scattering configurations. In forward-
scattering directions for GOES-East, however, GOES-West retrievals frequently demonstrate
improved error statistics compared to GOES-East. These results indicate that the integration of
GOES-West and GOES-East data following their viewing geometries could improve the overall
performance by a single satellite.

  Figure 8. Bias dependence on the scattering angle for GOES-West (left) and GOES-East (right)

To examine the large-scale behavior of the data, we analyzed the spatial and temporal patterns of
the solar geometry and the satellite viewing angles. Figure 9 illustrates the solar elevation angles
where progressively warmer colors (yellow to red) indicate increasing solar elevation and,
consequently, generally higher solar radiation. The right panels of Figure 9 present the
corresponding scattering angles for GOES-East and GOES-West. Following sunrise, the
scattering angle associated with GOES-East rapidly increases, exceeding 100° and reaching a
maximum around 19 UTC, before gradually declining toward sunset. Because GOES-East
demonstrates improved performance at larger scattering angles, its observations are generally
more reliable from morning through early afternoon. As the scattering angle decreases later in
the day, transitioning to GOES-West becomes advantageous.

 Figure 9. Solar elevation angles (left) and scattering angles for GOES-West (middle) and GOES-
 East (right)

Observations in the backward-scattering regime tend to produce more accurate results than those
in the forward-scattering regime, which can be physically explained by the angular
characteristics of cloud scattering. In the forward-scattering directions, the cloud phase function
shows a pronounced peak, where radiance can vary by orders of magnitude over small angular
ranges. As a result, minor uncertainties in viewing geometry, radiometric calibration, or model
assumptions can lead to substantial biases in retrieved cloud properties and derived irradiance. In
contrast, radiance variations in the backward-scattering direction are smoother, leading to more
stable retrievals and a reduced sensitivity to small errors.

We further examined the dependence of retrieval bias on satellite viewing zenith angle (Figure
10). Specifically, GOES-West exhibits slightly improved accuracy at larger viewing zenith
angles (greater than approximately 65°). This behavior combined with the scattering-angle
dependence forms the foundation of a new blending model.

Overall, the new blending approach leads to a systematic reduction in bias, with the most
substantial improvements observed in the mountain and Midwestern regions. The improvements
are consistent with the expected reduction of bias with improved satellite selection according to
viewing geometries. In contrast, performance improvements in the eastern United States are
limited because GOES-East already provides the dominant data source in both the current and
updated methods. These results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm effectively reduces the
bias of the data while preserving strong performance in areas where the existing methodology is
optimal.

In terms of annual mean GHI, the artificial boundary seam present in the current NSRDB is
effectively eliminated by the new blending approach (Figure 15). The spatial distribution of GHI
becomes smoother, particularly across the previous transition area around 105°W longitude. This
improvement reflects a more gradual and realistic shift in satellite data usage, avoiding the
abrupt changes that are tied to fixed geographic boundaries. The reduction of such discontinuities
is critical for downstream applications.

4 Conclusions
The NSRDB leverages observations from multiple GOES satellites to provide high-resolution
solar radiation products over CONUS. Due to incomplete spatial coverage by a single satellite
and time-varying gaps in cloud retrievals, artifacts such as the boundary seam in the current
dataset exist, which can undermine user confidence, particularly for applications requiring spatial
consistency.

Through systematic evaluation against surface observations, we identified a strong bias
dependence on light scattering geometry, with substantially lower errors under backward-
scattering conditions. This physical relationship provides a robust basis for improving satellite
data integration. Following the analysis, we developed a new blending algorithm that adaptively
selects satellite observations based on scattering angle and viewing geometry. The new approach
reduces biases in GHI and DNI, and it effectively removes the artificial seam present in the
current NSRDB.

For future studies, artificial intelligence (AI) offers a potentially powerful framework for
optimizing satellite blending by identifying complex, condition-dependent performance patterns;
however, our preliminary investigations indicate important limitations within this context. The
availability of high-quality surface observations for training remains limited, which constrains
model generalization. Moreover, although AI methods excel in high-dimensional classification
tasks, they often lack interpretability and struggle to yield compact, physically meaningful
parameterizations that are suitable for operational implementation. A more promising pathway is
therefore a hybrid approach that integrates physical understanding with data-driven methods.
Within this framework, physically informed constraints—such as the scattering-angle
dependence identified in this study—can guide AI models, refine parameter choices, improve
generalization, and explore additional predictive features without discarding established physical
principles. Advancing such physics-informed AI methodologies is probably the next step toward
further enhancing the accuracy and robustness of satellite-derived solar resource datasets.
