Development and evaluation of sustainable ionic liquid-based surfactant formulations for EOR in high-temperature, high-salinity, low-permeability carbonate reservoirs
Authors: Amin Sharifi, Rafat Parsaei, Masoud Riazi, Shirin Baghersaei, Amir Abdolmaleki, Farid B. Cortes, Seyed Mohammad Hashem Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing robust surfactant-based EOR formulations for carbonate reservoirs under high temperature, high salinity, and low permeability (< 10 md) conditions is particularly complex. This study focuses on formulating and evaluating an ionic liquid (IL)-based surfactant system tailored for such environments. A systematic screening workflow was employed to identify the optimal composition. This workflow included stability analysis, emulsification behavior, contact angle measurements, phase behavior evaluation, adsorption critical study, and coreflooding experiments. A newly synthesized imidazolium-based ionic liquid (MDIMO) was blended with amphoteric surfactants and IOS to leverage the tunable and stability characteristics of ILs. The optimized formulation achieved IFT of 0.003 mN/m and contact angles of 50°, while maintaining stability at salinity levels up to 230,000 mg/L and ultrahigh hardness (> 10,000 mg/L). Injection of this IL-based formulation into a low-permeability carbonate core yielded a 19% incremental oil recovery beyond seawater flooding. Moreover, incorporating sodium polyacrylate significantly reduced the adsorption density of MDIMO to less than 1 mg/g on the carbonate rock. Compared to a conventional surfactant blend, the IL-based formulation demonstrated markedly superior performance in IFT reduction, emulsification, and oil recovery. The combined Amott–USBM, contact angle, FTIR analyses, and ζ-potential validated the wettability state and alteration mechanisms. These findings highlight the structure-performance technique and its collaboration with blending approach to develop a formulation for EOR in low permeable carbonate reservoir. This study benchmarks the performance of a bulky synthesized ionic liquid against traditional surfactants for the development of more sustainable and efficient EOR strategies under harsh conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59594-5.

Introduction

Almost two-thirds of oil is trapped in carbonate reservoirs after waterflooding because of macroscopic heterogeneity, bypassing, microscopic trapping, and rock wetting state. This becomes even worse in the low permeable reservoirs where they usually suffers from poor ultimate oil recovery (< 10%)1. Fluid mobility in the porous media is generally controlled by the complex interplay of viscous-capillary forces, namely, capillary number2–4. Capillary forces, which retain a significant fraction of the oil phase within rock pores, are several orders of magnitude stronger than viscous forces applied by the conventional waterflood5,6. Capillary number for mature waterflooding is reported to be in the range of 10–7–10–6 and hence left significant oil7. To unleash residual oil saturation, capillary number must be increased, accomplished by significant IFT reduction or increasing displacing phase viscosity. Because of limitation for viscosity enhancement, IFT reduction is the key mechanism for chemical enhanced oil recovery (EOR) approaches8. Thereby, scaling of capillary number can help to determine individual contribution of mechanisms like IFT reduction, emulsification and so forth.

Surface active materials, commonly surfactants, have been used to reduce oil–water IFT and improve oil displacement efficiency. However, strong electrolyte, high temperature, pore scale heterogeneity and dense rock can suppress the surfactant9,10. These pertinent properties could impose stability issue, poor solubility, injectability, and high retention of surfactants in the porous media9. Surfactant performance is totally controlled by salinity, temperature, molecular structure of surfactant, oil type and rock mineralogy. High brine salinity especially Ca2+ and Mg2+ can accelerate surfactant precipitation, adsorption on rock surface, and partitioning into oil phase while temperature results into surfactant degradation11. On the other hand, most carbonate oil reservoirs have high temperature (70–130 °C) and high salinity (up to 200,000 TDS) conditions, detrimental to surfactant application3,10. Hence, to provide surfactant formulation for such harsh conditions, multiple schemes are proposed like modification of surfactant structure (hydrophobe chain, branches, alkoxylation, hydrophilic group change), mixing with cosurfactants, and new surfactant classes9.

Regradless of maturity of conventional surfactnt science, there is still room for innovative molecules tailored for special conditions and applications. Ionic liquids are offered as a novel surfactants with inherited excellent characteristics composed of various anions and cations. Surface-active ionic liquids (SAILs) with long alkyl chains are emerging as a promising alternative to surfactant EOR5,12. Furthermore, SAILs have lower critical micelle concentration (CMC), greener solvent and recyclable than conventional surfactants of the same family13. Despite the unique properties of SAILs like low vapor pressure, high solvation potential, they can be tailored for task specific functions14. SAILs can be easily manufactured and optimized to be stable at the harsh salinity conditions while keeping the surface activities5,15.

Numerous studies underscore the EOR effectiveness of SAILs in carbonates16. However, performance analysis of SAILs under high salinity and temperature conditions of carbonates is still under investigation. On the other hand, most studies have investigated single SAILs for EOR and mixtures of surfactants based on SAIL is scarce. Actually, logistic simplicity and low compositional gradient in the porous media are advantages of stand-alone surfactant formulation. Nevertheless, a sopheticated procedure is needed to design a single surfactant or ionic liquid structure for the tradeoff between high performance and sustained stability. The synergic effect generated by surfactant mixtures usually outperfroms than individual ones by lowering IFT, better emulsification, and higher salinity and temperature toleranc17,18. To our best knowledge, individual SAILs may not be able to significantly reduce the IFT between crude oil and maintain maximum capability of EOR in harsh conditions. On the other hand, a few comprehensive studies exists wherein the blending formulations of SAILS and conventional surfactants have been made for the EOR process19.

Imadazolium-based SAILs, were employed by Pillai et al15. They demonstrated stability of [C8mimBF4], [C10mimBF4], [C12mimBF4], in salinity levels up to 200,000 mg/L (NaCl) and at temperature of 90 °C. This formulation effectively reducing IFT to 0.04 mN/m and altering wettability to wet water conditions. Polymer and alkali were added to SAILs and high recovery (32% original oil in place or OOIP) was achieved for high permeability sand pack. Moreover, IFT reduction and wettability was proposed for active mechanisms. The same SAILs were used in carbonate rock (calcite) to mechanistically evaluate wettability change and adsorption process and effect of parameters like salinity and alkali16. This study reported the adsorption of SAILs on the carbonate rock was best investigated by the Langmuir isotherm.

Nabipour et al. investigated EOR potential of single SAILs, namely [C12mimCl] and [C18mimCl], in high salinity brine (> 200,000 mg/L) and low temperature. Incremental recovery factors of 8–22% were achieved by EOR coreflooding at low permeability rocks. Despite the promising results (IFT was 0.07 mN/m), further phase behavior and adsorption study was needed to undercover molecular mechanism and optimize formulation20. According to this study, IFT reduction and wettability change played the key roles in EOR production in the SAILs flooding experiments.

The EOR potential by blending strategy for a SAILs, namely, (Tributylhexadecylphosphonium bromide) and a nonionic surfactant (TERGITOL 15-S-9) was investigated by Nandwani et al.19 The formulation with equimolar ratio of phosphonium based SAILs and nonionic surfactant resulted in Winsor type III under high salinity condition (> 92,000 mg/L) while produced additional oil (16.69% OOIP) compared to waterflooding. They fully investigated mixed micelle properties like size, shapes, and stabilities of the micelles. Regardless, further assessment in high temperature, high hardness brine, and low permeability rock could be insightful. Moreover, other mechanisms like wettability change may contribute to oil production.

In a mechanistic study, Sakthival evaluated effect of salinity, temperature and pressure on singles imidazolium based SAILs performance to change wettability in carbonate rocks. The tested ionic liquids altered wettability from strongly oil wet to neutral condition with small effect on oil–water IFT21.

Zabihi et al. carried out performance analysis like Amot-Harvey Index, IFT and core flooding to evaluate capability of [C12mimCl] and [C18mimCl] respect to an anionic surfactant (sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate-SDBS) in the formation brine14. They observed wettability alteration to mixed wet condition as the dominant mechanism while incremental recovery of 13–16.5% over waterflooding was resulted. However, SAILs were tested under ambient temperature and no chemicals were added to optimize for IFT, emulsification and adsorption.

In 2017, one of the pioneering studies examined the performance of SAILs blends with conventional surfactants under various laboratory conditions22. They showed that an blend of cationic/anionic SAILs and IOS produced an ultralow IFT in hard brine at 25 °C, opposed to individual SAILs22. Higher length imidazolium-based SAILs was reported to enhance oil recovery during water flooding by 10–26% at 100 °C and salinity of 240,000 mg/L.

Somoza et al. offered an optimized formulation including SDBS and cocosalkylpentaethoximethylammonium methylsulfate ([C1EG][MeSO4]) at seawater and temperature of 75 °C5. At this study, SAILs was added to tune SDBS performance in high salinity carbonate media. The optimized blend produced ultralow IFT at 10,000 mg/L, altered wettability into intermediate-wet and reduced dynamic adsorption down to 0.37 mg/g. The dominant mechanism associated with the oil recovery was IFT reduction. The viscous forces or wettability alteration was lesser effective. The main limitation of the proposed formulation was that oil recovery decreased with temperature. Moreover, the conventional surfactant SDBS with large adsorption on carbonate rock was still the target surfactant, optimized with SAILs.

In another recent mixing strategy, Tafur et al. assessed addition of [C12mimBr] on the efficiency of the blend including dioctyl sulfosuccinate sodium (AOT) and polyoxyethylene octyl ether carboxylic acid (LF2)23. According to phase behavior study, blend of AOT and LF2 produced Winsor type III (IFT = 0.015 mN/m) in synthetic seawater under maximum temperature of 75 °C. A moderate incremental recovery (7.3% of OOIP) and dynamic adsorption capacity of 0.42 mg/g rock was obtained through core flooding. By addition [C12mimBr] to this conventional blend, lower concentration of formulation, reduced IFT and dynamic adsorption to 0.011 mN/m and 0.21 mg/g, respectively while improved additional recovery to 11.5%. Despite, the stability of formulation was not evaluated at harsh salinity and temperature and significant further analysis were needed to understand effect of [C12mimBr] on wettability change process.

Recently, Qi et al. carried out an experimental investigation to improve performance an anionic-nonionic surfactant blend (ANS-1) by adding a SAILs24. After testing four SAILs, they found that 1-Ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate (EMIMAc) can improve wettability alteration capability of ANS-1 confirmed by SEM analysis. They also conducted Amott-imbibition tests and observed that EMIMAc improved final recovery factor of ANS-1 from 34.8 to 40.8% under temperature of 95 °C. Nonetheless, no significant effect on IFT reduction was observed and stability of formulation in high salinity brine was not investigated. Furthermore, performance of formulation was assessed in a high permeability core where no analysis conducted for adsorption capacity.

Unlike many studies focused on tuning conventional surfactants by SAILs, the present study targets the modification of SAILs by co-surfactants to optimize formulation for harsh carbonate reservoir. Accordingly, this study aims to advance the understanding of SAILs based-surfactant EOR in carbonate reservoir subjected to harsh conditions.

Building on previous research, we evaluate the performance of a imidazolium-based SAILs blended with conventional surfactants to address IFT reduction and wettability alteration. The novelty of this work lies in the development and assessment of a bulky and newly synthesized SAILs which optimized by blending technique and compared to conventional surfactants under harsh reservoir conditions. We systematically investigate the chemical, thermal, and interfacial stability, complemented by emulsification and phase behavior studies. Then we elucidate the governing mechanisms and adsorption heterogeneity on carbonate tight rock through critical isotherm modeling. We also assess the effect of inhibitors like sodium polyacrylate and salinity on minimizing surfactant loss and economic viability. The wettability alteration is further investigated via contact angle measurements, FTIR spectroscopy, USBM-Amott analysis, and ζ-potential tests. Ultimately, we validate the effectiveness of optimized formulation by coreflooding tests while demonstrating each production mechanism by scaling capillary number. This research provides a robust scientific foundation for the application of eco-friendly, high-performance SAILs under harsh reservoir conditions.

Cationic SAILs were synthesized according to the following procedure25,26: A mixture of 1-methylimidazole (0.025 mol, 1.99 mL) and 1-bromododecane (0.030 mol, 7.2 mL) was added to a reaction vessel containing 15 mL of chloroform. The reaction mixture was refluxed for 15 h under continuous stirring and temperature of 70 °C. Upon completion, the crude product was washed with a 4:1 (v/v) mixture of diethyl ether and hexane, followed by drying in a freeze-dryer for 24 h. The desired product, 3-dodecyl-1-methyl-1H-imidazolium-3 bromide (MDIMB), was obtained as a white solid (Figure S1a). 3-Dodecyl-1-methyl-1H-imidazolium-3 bromide (0.005 mol, 1.34 g) was dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol, followed by the addition of sodium oleate (0.005 mol, 1.52 g). The reaction mixture was stirred magnetically at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting product was washed with diethyl ether and dried using a freeze-dryer rather than solvent evaporation. A viscous yellow liquid of 3-dodecyl-1-methyl-1H-imidazolium-3 oleate (MDIMO) was final product (Figure S1b). MDIMO is an imidazolium-based ionic liquid which could be regarded as a green solvent due to low inflammability, low vapor pressure and negligible impact on the environment13,27,28. Moreover, ionic liquids with more hydrolyzable anions like oleate and acetate may break down faster than inert ones28.

Laboratory-grade salts, including sodium chloride, calcium chloride dihydrate, magnesium chloride hexahydrate, sodium sulfate, and sodium polyacrylate (NaPA, molecular weight of 2500), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (99.5% purity). These salts were dissolved in twice-deionized water to prepare synthetic seawater and formation brines, and filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane. Methods

Stability tests

Stability is a critical step in the development of a surfactant formulation for EOR. To conduct the stability test, 2000 mg/L of each SAILs and surfactants was dissolved in 10 mL of water at the target salinity using a magnetic stirrer, then stored in an oven for at least 60 days. The stability was monitored under different salinities and temperatures. It is aimed to address the potential risk of surfactant instability during injection, from surface preparation to reservoir conditions. Therefore, the stability evaluation was performed under three salinity conditions: NaCl solutions with salinity ranging from 50,000 to 200,000 mg/L, seawater (SW), and formation water (FW). Tests were conducted at 25 °C and 85 °C. Any occurrence of precipitation, cloudiness, or significant color changes was considered an indicator of instability, whereas clear solutions were deemed stable.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA): To determine degradation temperature for SAILs, TGA analysis was performed using an analyzer (SDT Q600 V20.9 Build 20). For this test, 14 mg of the SAILs was heated from 25 to 600 °C at rate 10 °C/min under nitrogen gas (40 mL/min).

Interfacial properties evaluation

Critical micelle concentration (CMC) measurement: Lower CMC values provides a practical criterion for preliminary screening of surfactant EOR application. In the subsequent step, the CMC was determined by conductivity measurements, with particular emphasis on SAILs, cationic and zwitterionic surfactants. Electrical conductivity measurements were performed using a Meterohm AZ8306 in DI water. This instrument is a handheld EC/TDS/Temperature meter commonly used for water quality measurements over a range of 0.0 to 200.0 mS. Before analysis, the meter was automatically calibrated using a 0.01 N KCl standard. Conductivity measurements for each sample were performed in triplicate, and the mean value of the three readings was calculated and reported. The CMC values were estimated by applying segmented linear fitting to the conductivity–concentration data. Two linear regions were matched to the experimental points, and the intersection of these fitted lines was taken as the CMC. The CMC can also be estimated from the breakpoint observed in IFT plot as a function of surfactant concentration29.

Emulsification test procedure: Qualitative emulsification experiments were performed to rapidly evaluate the emulsion-forming potential of surfactant. This step is prior to more time-intensive phase behavior studies. Constant concentration of surfactants and SAILs were added to 5 mL of DI water, mixed with an equal volume of crude oil, and hand-shaken for 5 minutes30. The resulting mixtures were monitored at reservoir temperature for 60 days. The equilibrium time was assumed to be obtained when there is no significant change in the emulsion state31. The presence of individually dispersed oil droplets with large size indicated high interfacial tension32. Conversely, the formation of a single homogeneous phase suggested the potential to achieve low IFT.

Combined Amott-USBM method for wettability measurement: This method integrates both the Amott and USBM techniques to quantitatively assess the wettability of core plugs33. In this experiment, spontaneous imbibition was conducted using an Amott cell, while forced imbibition was carried out in a high-speed centrifuge. ζ-potentian measurement: The ζ-potential of the colloidal dispersions in various aqueous solutions was determined via electrophoretic mobility measurements utilizing a Malvern Zetasizer. Measurements were conducted on the carbonate rock powder, the rock-soaked in oil, and surfactant-aged carbonate rock. Initially, the crushed rock was sieved through a 400-mesh screen to isolate fine particles with a diameter (75–150 μm). Subsequently, 10 g of the sieved powder was dispersed in 25 mL of the target solution. To achieve a homogeneous dispersion, the suspension was subjected to probe ultrasonication (Misonix Sonicator 3000) for 30 min, followed by a 12 h period to allow for phase equilibration. Finally, aliquots were carefully extracted from the supernatant for ζ-potential analysis. To ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility, all measurements were performed in triplicate. The standard deviation for all measurements in this study were within of ± 1.2 mV.

Contact angle (CA) and IFT measurement: The captive drop method was used to measure the static CA of oil droplets on carbonate thin sections in various solutions. This method can evaluate both the initial wettability and altered wettability states of the rock samples. In this procedure thin discs of 0.15 cm in thickness were cut from 1.5-in diameter carbonate core plug. The discs were polished using fine-grit sandpaper to minimize surface roughness. This step is critical, as surface irregularities can significantly mislead wettability interpretations in the contact angle method34–37. The polished discs were cleaned using a soxhlet extractor with methanol and ethanol to remove residual contaminants. After cleaning, the samples were oven-dried at 90 °C. Most carbonate reservoirs are categorized as oil-wet. Accordingly, an aging process was performed to adjust the native wettability of the rock and alter to an oil–wet condition. For that aim, after 24 h equilibrium in FW under ambient temperature, the dried discs were left to equilibrate in crude oil for a period exceeding two weeks at reservoir temperature. This aging time was assumed to be sufficient to attain homogenous oil wetting state, as reported by various authors5. After aging, a non-absorbent paper was used to carefully remove excess oil from the discs surface. Afterward, the discs were placed in high-temperature, blue cap glass cells containing the target solutions. The samples aged for 7 days and surfactant candidates were visually selected based on their ability to alter the rock surface wettability. The cleanliness of the rock surface could be an indirect indicator of wettability alteration. Subsequently, the selected discs were carefully transferred to a transparent chamber filled with the same solutions, and the contact angles were measured using a goniometer.

IFT measurement procedure: The pendant drop method was also used to measure the IFT of the oil and aqueous solutions as the drop phase and the bulk phase, respectively31,38. We prepared the aqueous solutions by adding the chemicals at concentrations of 100–1000 mg/L to water. After 15 min of equilibration time for each droplet, where no change was detected in droplet shape, the IFT data were measured. Oil and aqueous phase densities were imported into the software and the IFT was determined by Laplace-Young equation on each droplet of complete shape10. Moreover, we used ImageJ software to analyze and estimate the IFT data. Before any droplet formation, the detection limit of camera was adjusted. Once the droplet formed, its detection level was more enhanced by controlling the light and the contrast level10. After each measurement cycle, the tensiometer was calibrated to ensure accurate IFT data. Similarly, the instrument was also cross-checked by measuring the IFT of a normal heptane−DI water system at 25 °C where the reported literature value of 50.71 mN/m was well confirmed by measured IFT of 51.01 mN/m39. The uncertainty of measurements for IFT data was estimated to be approximately ± 0.05-0.65 mN/m. Each measurement was repeated at least three times to ensure repeatability.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy: To better understand the effects of fluids and their functional groups on carbonate rock, we used a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrophotometer (range of 400 to 4000 cm−1). Measurements were conducted on crude oil, carbonate rock soaked in oil, and surfactant-aged carbonate rock. The FTIR analysis was also employed to confirm the desired chemical structure and functional performance of the synthesized surface-active ionic liquids (SAILs).

Phase behavior procedure: Phase behavior evaluation is a fundamental step in surfactant flooding studies, as it elucidates the interactions among surfactant, oil, and brine systems. The microemulsion phase behavior determines optimum salinity and interfacial tension reduction to asses the efficiency of a surfactant formulation. The standard procedures were followed for the phase behavior tests40–42. According to this procedure, borosilicate pipettes were filled with 5 mL of DI water and crude oil, where aqueous phase salinity was varied from 0 to 100,000 mg/L. The total surfactants concentration remained constant at range of 8000–10,000 mg/L across all samples. Each pipette was sealed and maintained at 85 °C. Over the initial days, the samples were periodically agitated to accelerate phase equilibrium and better simulate conditions in porous media. Equilibrium was considered to be achieved 24 h after no significant changes in phase levels were observed. We investigated phase behavior in three parts: effect of salt type where no surfactant added and only NaCl were compared with equivalent seawater in aqueous phase. In second part, SAILs (or surfactant) and co-surfactant blend ratio is optimized at a fixed salinity. SAILs and surfactants were selected from the candidate chemicals from emulsification step. Finally, main salinity scan tests were conducted to address the optimum formulation.

Effect of salt type: In most of salinity scan procedures, NaCl is added to DI water to create the aqueous phase. However, in practical applications, NaCl-based water may not be feasible, as in the field cases seawater is usually the candidate source. To account for this, two sets of salinity scan tests were designed to examine the influence of salt selection on phase behavior (without adding surfactants). In the first set of experiments, NaCl and in the second one, seawater-based solutions with equivalent ionic strengths to NaCl were utilized as the aqueous phase.

Blend scan test: An important step is to find the optimized blending ratio of two promising SAILs and surfactants. A blend scan was performed at constant aqueous phase salinity with varying ratios of primary SAILs (or surfactant) and co-surfactant. The total surfactant concentration was maintained at 8000 mg/L. The objective was to determine the optimal surfactants blend ratio for subsequent salinity scans.

Salinity scan test: After finding the optimized blend ratio of SAILs (or surfactant) and co-surfactants, other surfactants were added to tune phase behavior in term of optimal salinity and microemulsion phase volume. In certain formulations, a co-solvent was included to reduce microemulsion viscosity, prevent crystallization, and improve stability. Coreflood tests

Coreflood experiments were conducted to evaluate the enhanced displacement efficiency of the optimized formulation under reservoir-representative conditions. Prior to flooding, the carbonate cores were cleaned, dried, and reconditioned, ensuring consistent initial wettability and pore-fluid conditions. Each core plug was mounted vertically inside a Hassler-type core holder equipped with an external confining system. A constant confining pressure of 700 psi was applied while injecting fluids at a fixed flow rate of 0.1 mL/min. The entire assembly was placed inside an oven maintained at 85 °C to replicate reservoir temperature. We saturated the core plugs with crude oil and aged them for more than a month under 85 °C. Then, the core plugs were flooded with SW to establish the baseline secondary-recovery performance. The injection was continued until no appreciable oil production was observed. Subsequently, the candidate chemical formulation was introduced at the same injection rate. Throughout the test, the differential pressure and cumulative oil recovery were continuously monitored.

Characterization of rock and fluid

XRD analysis was conducted on representative samples from the reservoir core (C#1) to determine the mineralogical composition. The results revealed that calcite was the predominant mineral, comprising more than 98%, as shown in Fig. 3. A sharp and intense peak at 2θ = 29.4, along with several medium peaks, indicated that the rock was mostly composed of calcite. No significant amounts of anhydrite or effective clay minerals were detected in the XRD analysis. The absence of effective clay minerals suggests that clay-related adsorption effects can be considered negligible in this study. The CII was calculated to be 1.23 based on values from Table 5, indicating that the crude oil falls within the asphaltene instability range57. This result supports the potential activity of polar components and their likelihood of being adsorbed onto rock surfaces. The main driving forces behind asphaltene adsorption on carbonate rocks include electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and precipitation. Asphaltene molecules are prone to aggregation due to their polar peripheral groups, while resins serve to stabilize asphaltenes. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to analyze the functional groups present in the crude oil to better understand the initial wetting conditions. The FTIR spectrum of the crude oil exhibited distinct absorption bands corresponding to surface-active chemical functionalities (Fig. 6). Prominent absorption peaks at 2925 cm−1 and 2848 cm−1 correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of aliphatic C–H bonds. These peaks indicate a substantial presence of saturated hydrocarbons commonly found in paraffinic or naphthenic crude oils. Additional peaks observed at 727, 1377, and 145 cm−1 are attributed to CH2 bending (paraffinic or waxy crude), symmetric CH₃, and CH bending vibrations, respectively. A broad and moderately intense band observed near 3417 cm−1 is attributed to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations. This peak suggests hydroxyl- or amine-containing polar compounds (alcoholic content), typically associated with resins and asphaltenes. The peak at 1085 cm−1 is associated with sulfur-containing compounds, which are often found in resins. The peak at 1310 cm−1 is likely due to esters and ethers, which are typically found in asphaltene molecules. The spectrum also displays peaks around 1610 and 811 cm−1, indicative of C=C stretching and CH bonding in aromatic rings (asphaltenes). Furthermore, a weaker absorption near 1700 cm−1 indicates carbonyl (C=O) stretching, a signature of carboxylic acid groups associated with resin components. These polar functional groups are known for their strong affinity toward carbonate surfaces due to electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and surface precipitation. The presence of polar and aromatic groups in crude oil implies a significant concentration of surface-active molecules, particularly resins and asphaltenes. These components have a high tendency to adsorb onto positively charged rock surfaces, rendering them oil-wet. In addition, fluid composition analysis (micro-distillation test) indicated that more than 44 mol% of crude oil consists of hydrocarbons with a carbon number greater than C12+. This is attributed to the presence of heavy components and asphaltene constituents, which contribute to the oil-wet condition of the rock surface.Fig. 6FTIR spectrum of crude oil samples.

CMC results: The critical micelle concentration (CMC) is the minimum concentration at which surfactant molecules begin to form micelles. Electrical conductivity measurements were used to estimate the CMC of the SAILs and ionic surfactants in DI water (Fig. 10 and Table S1). The CMC values were determined using mathematical fitting of the conductivity data, where the slope of the fitted line changes. Hence, two fitted lines were applied to the conductivity data of each surfactant to identify the point of intersection, defined as the CMC. A fixed concentration sufficiently higher than the CMC was selected for further analysis to ensure the best chemical performance. As shown in Fig. 10, the CMCs of MDIMO, AMF1, AMF2, and T-50 were 330 mg/L, 1150 mg/L, 1243 mg/L, and 573 mg/L, respectively. Moreover, the CMCs for MDIMB and C-25 were measured as 2700 mg/L and 795 mg/L, respectively. Due to data density and figure clarity considerations, not all data are shown. Both conductivity and interfacial tension measurements yielded comparable CMC values, as will be shown in the emulsification behavior section. It is noted that the CMC of MDIMO is significantly lower than that of MDIMB because the oleate group increases the hydrophobicity of MDIMO. This enhances hydrophobic interactions between the long alkyl chains, increases the aggregation number, and leads to earlier micelle formation60. It should be noted that higher salinity, particularly hardness, can potentially decrease the CMC61. Since all the CMC values were less than 2000 mg/L, this concentration was used for subsequent emulsification and wettability tests.Fig. 10Electrical conductivity versus surfactant concentration (in the range of 100–4000 mg/L) in DI water under ambient temperature to define CMC. Emulsification behavior of various chemicals: Emulsification testing was conducted as a qualitative decision-gate to assess the potential of chemicals for phase-behavior studies. MDIMO demonstrated the highest emulsification efficiency, producing a uniform brown emulsion phase. This can be a clear sign that MDIMO interacts significantly with the crude oil. Despite the presence of small oil droplets in the aqueous phase, no significant emulsion was produced for MDIMB (Fig. 11a). According to this test result, no observable emulsification was detected for C-25, EC-25, AMF2, and MCN (Fig. 11a). This was evidenced by the presence of a clear aqueous phase or the presence of discrete, large oil droplets dispersed in the aqueous phase (Fig. 11b). As such, the formation of very low IFTs was not anticipated for C-25, EC-25, AMF2, and MCN in phase behavior tests. The surfactant IOS effectively solubilized the oil phase, producing a brown, emulsified solution that demonstrated its potential as a cosurfactant for phase-behavior evaluation. According to the results of Fig. 11, MDIMO, IOS and AMF1 created oil-in-water emulsion (O/W) type which is the predictable as the base aqueous solutions was prepared in DI water. Surface active materials like surfactants and ionic liquids can facilitate migration of oil droplets into water or microemulsion interface. This phenomena and emulsion type was predominantly controlled by surfactant molecular structure, salinity and temperature conditions.

Low salinity commonly produces the oil-in-water emulsion while emulsion becomes water-in-oil at higher salinity. The microemulsion of Winsor type III is the best emulsion type which usually obtained through phase behavior study62. However, at high salinity, surfactant molecules migrates into the oil phase through salting-out-effect and creates Winsor type II microemulsion. Despite the high salinity or hardness, high temperature condition can also violate the effectiveness of emulsification. High temperatures usually decrease performance of thermodynamically stable emulsion by increasing the droplet size, collision rates and phase separation. In other words, elevated temperatures can increase thermal kinetic energy, impair surfactant performance, thereby inducing phase separation. According to the emulsification section results, no phase separation was observed at reservoir temperature. The candidate SAILs and surfactants in this section withstand high temperature of 85 °C while keeping emulsification behavior. Hence, to get the best stable and optimized microemulsion, we further evaluated various compositions of candidate SAILs/surfactant at the phase behavior section under the reservoir temperature.

This can mitigate mobility ratio challenge while keeping the optimized interfacial properties. Therefore, optimized emulsion can improve EOR performance by synergic effect of low IFT and better sweep efficiency which collectively promotes recovery of trapped oil droplet in the porous media63.

Phase behavior analysis: Seawater is the primary source for injection into South-Western carbonate reservoirs in Iran. Therefore, we evaluated the effect of different water types prior to comprehensive salinity scan tests. The effect of NaCl was assessed over a concentration range of 0–100,000 mg/L and compared with a seawater phase adjusted to the same ionic strength. In the both water types, no surfactant was added. The test outcomes were checked by visually comparing the initial state (Fig. 18a,b) with the final state after mixing the oil and aqueous phases (Fig. 18c,d). Notably, a higher number of oil droplets-ranging from small to medium size were observed in NaCl solutions. This is particularly evident in the 0–30,000 mg/L salinity range (Fig. 18c). In this salinity range, the oil–water interface in seawater solutions appeared lower than its original position, and visible oil dispersed/emulsification occurred (Fig. 18d). This behavior can be attributed to the critical role of divalent cations such as Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺. Previous studies have confirmed that divalent cations, particularly Mg2⁺ and Ca2⁺, exert a stronger effect on interfacial behavior than monovalent Na⁺ ions64. These multivalent ions enhance electrostatic and ion–dipole interactions with polar functional groups. The presence of Mg2⁺, with higher charge density compared to Ca2⁺, contributes to strong affinity adsorption kinetics at the interface. This promotes tighter surfactant packing and the formation of more viscoelastic interfacial layers. At a salinity close to that of seawater, more oil droplets were observed in the SW-based solution compared to the NaCl solution of equivalent salinity. At lower salinities, oil droplets remained mostly dispersed within the aqueous phase. The low ionic strength in these conditions leads to strong electrostatic repulsion between droplets, inhibiting coalescence and settling. The resulting dispersion is stable, and the oil–water interface is not sharply defined. At intermediate salinities (20,000–30,000 mg/L), partial screening of repulsive forces takes effect, promoting droplet coalescence and phase separation. The crude oil contains a substantial amount of asphaltenes and resins. These heavy components readily adhere to glass surfaces, which explains the droplets observed along the tube walls. In NaCl brine, coarse droplets formed initially and gradually coalesced, leading to a relatively sharp oil–water interface. In contrast, seawater produced much finer and more uniformly distributed droplets from the outset. This is largely due to the presence of Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions, which are known to interact with polar components of oil and promote the formation of smaller, more stable aggregates. To cross-check this behavior, we measured interfacial tensions between oil and aqueous phases in seawater and NaCl brine. The IFT between crude oil and seawater was 18 mN/m, compared to 27 mN/m for NaCl brine. The lower IFT in seawater reduces the energy required to generate new interfaces, resulting in a larger number of small droplets. Consequently, a greater portion of the oil becomes dispersed into the aqueous phase, lowering the apparent oil–water interface height in seawater.Fig. 18Effect of salt type on phase behavior without surfactant: (a) NaCl brine at initial state, (b) seawater at initial state, (c) NaCl brine at final state, (d) seawater at final state. Adsorption and inhibitor studies

To quantify the potential chemical loss, static adsorption tests were conducted on the carbonate rock surface. Due to the multicomponent nature of the formulation #4, analytical limitations and the central role of its ionic liquid, MDIMO was selected for detailed analysis. MDIMO inherently possesses a distinctly dark brown and yields strong UV–Vis absorbance. Therefore, it is easily quantified in isolation, while mixing with the other surfactants causes overlapping spectral interference. This chromatic interference precludes accurate effluent concentration tracking via UV–Vis spectroscopy during dynamic coreflooding. Consequently, the retention was estimated via static adsorption on crushed rock. Because crushed samples expose artificially high surface areas, this method effectively captures the maximum possible adsorption capacity, serving as a ‘worst-case scenario’ for reservoir retention. This focus also enabled comparison with the conventional cationic surfactant T-50.

The adsorption behavior is influenced by factors such as surface charge, mineral impurities (e.g., silicates), ionic strength, and temperature. Negatively charged sites on silica and clay enhance the adsorption of the cationic species on carbonate surfaces. High ionic strength, as in seawater, screens electrostatic charges, reducing repulsion between similarly charged molecules. This promotes aggregation or micelle formation even at low chemical concentrations. Such aggregation affects UV–Vis absorbance and light scattering, requiring measurements in the both DI and SW environments to evaluate the salinity effects. Based on UV–Vis spectrophotometric analysis (Table S6), MDIMO showed an adsorption capacity of 1.573 mg/g of rock (Fig. 22 and Table S7).Fig. 22Static adsorption data and fitted isotherms of Langmuir-solid blue line, Feundlich-solid green line and Temkin-solid yellow line for MDIMO (a) and T-50 (b).

Static adsorption data and fitted isotherms of Langmuir-solid blue line, Feundlich-solid green line and Temkin-solid yellow line for MDIMO (a) and T-50 (b).

Under our experimental conditions, the adsorption behavior of MDIMO was initially assumed to be governed by electrostatic interactions16. This is interaction between the positively charged imidazolium headgroup and negatively charged carbonate sites. Several studies report that this classical Coulombic interaction governs both high and low adsorption regimes for imidazolium ionic liquids on carbonate minerals65,66. Our selection of primary SAILs structure, particularly alkyl-chain length and anion chemistry, was designed to reduce chemical loss while optimizing EOR performance28. This behavior aligns with observations by Nandwani et al., who reported that imidazolium cations accumulate readily at negatively charged carbonate interfaces through dominant electrostatic forces67. However, hydrophobic and mechanical hindrance, imposed by cationic and anionic head groups, further limited its adsorption capacity on the carbonate surface67,68. The presence of a long hydrophobic alkyl tail can, slow the initial approach of the cation to the surface due to steric and diffusional constraints16. Furthermore, the bulky oleate anion introduces additional steric hindrance and promotes aggregation in the bulk phase, thereby decreasing direct adsorption27. It is worlthy note that imidazolium based-ILs show weaker electrostatic binding compared to cations such as pyridinium-based ILs67. Hydrophobic interactions also play a key role in the adsorption process. The long dodecyl chain of the MDIMO cation and the hydrocarbon chain of the oleate anion enhance lateral association once the molecule is anchored to the surface. At the same time, hydrophobic bulkiness introduces steric limitations that reduce suppress adsorption at higher loadings.

Because of mineral heterogeneity, variable surface energy sites, and pore-scale accessibility, monolayer and ideal adsorption of Langmuir isotherm could be misleading. Effect of aqueous phase salinity on MDIMO adsorption.

Effect of inhibitors: Sodium polyacrylate (NaPA) was used as an inorganic inhibitor to reduce adsorption capacity of MDIMO on the rock surface. NaPA at two concentrations (1000 mg/L and 2000 mg/L) were added to a 2000 mg/L MDIMO solution, and adsorption values were recorded. The solution at 2000 mg/L NaPA exhibited gel-like behavior and poor solubility in SW with MDIMO, making it unsuitable for further analysis. However, 1000 mg/L NaPA effectively reduced MDIMO adsorption for 40%. This inhibitory effect is attributed to carboxylated polymer chains of NaPA. These chains complex with surface Ca2⁺ ions and strongly bind to calcite, forming a preadsorbed and highly hydrated layer. This layer physically blocks adsorption sites that would otherwise accommodate imidazolium cations or facilitate Ca–oleate complex formation. The Ca2⁺ sites on the calcite surface form strong ionic bridges with the carboxylate groups of NaPA. Therefore, this phenomenea can produce dense and irreversible adsorption layers under static conditions. Furthurmore, the large coil structure of NaPA generates a thick, water-rich steric barrier that hinders MDIMO penetration. Therefore, it reduces both the affinity constant and the maximum adsorption capacity (surface excess). In essence, NaPA functions as a sacrificial and competitive inhibitor, occupying key surface sites of MDIMO binding. Additionally, the effect of carbon-based inhibitors was evaluated. No significant change was observed in MDIMO adsorption by adding 500 mg/L and 1000 mg/L of MCN carbon nanotubes. According to this section, MDIMO exhibits a lower adsorption capacity than the typical cationic surfactant T-50. Furthermore, the adsorption density of MDIMO falls within the moderate range expected for SAILs and other ionic surfactants. The addition of the inhibitor also reduced MDIMO adsorption by 40%, demonstrating that adsorption can be effectively mitigated. These findings highlight a promising direction for optimizing and partially controlling adsorption through formulation design.

Coreflooding

Coreflooding experiments were carried out to validate the displacement efficiency of Formulation #4 under reservoir-representative conditions. The carbonate core samples used had similar petrophysical properties, including porosity of 25.7–26.3% and permeability of 5.8–7 mD. Core sample C#7 was assigned to the Formulation #4 system emphasized in this study. The conventional surfactant blend (1000 mg/L of T-50 and 1000 mg/L of EC-25) was injected into core sample C#8 as the reference case following SW injection. Formulation #4, composed of MDIMO blended with AMF1, IOS, and LE7, was evaluated in core sample C#7 to assess its ability to mobilize residual oil after SW flooding. The coreflooding results, including cumulative oil recovery and differential pressures, are shown in Fig. 24 and Table S8. Subsequent injection of Formulation #4, for approximately 3 PV, achieved a final residual oil saturation (Sorf) of 12.5%. Furthermore, we found that the breakthrough time validated our wettability analysis. In C#7, the breakthrough time occurred when 0.42 PV of SW injected while it happened in core #8 after injection of 0.37 PV. For the C#7 coreflooding, the differential pressure initially elevated up to 36 psi until the point of SW breakthrough. Following this point, pressure drops decreased and stabilized around 26 psi at the end of this stage. This behavior is commonly observed in coreflooding since viscous force should overcome capillary initially but after breakthrough, SW channels through the fingering effect. However, after injecting Formulation #4, differential pressure increased up to 30 psi providing more piston like movement of emulsion.

It is worthy note that the early breakthrough times are a classic macroscopic indicator of strongly oil-wet porous media. Because the injected seawater is the non-wetting phase, it preferentially flows through the centers of the largest pore channels, bypassing the smaller oil-filled pores and the oil films adhering to the rock surface. This channeling leads to rapid water breakthrough and the relatively high residual oil saturation observed at the end of the SW flood. The minor variance in breakthrough times between the two cores is attributed to inherent, natural rock heterogeneities, such as slight variations in pore size distribution and local permeability variations.

Economic feasibility of ionic liquids especially the one synthesized in this study, results from trade-off between technical performance and manufacturing costs. Imidazolium-based SAILs have moderate production costs due to the chemical complexity of synthesizing alkyl chain-functionalized cations and compatible anions. Still, their production is increasingly justified by their superior performance in harsh reservoir conditions. The diversity and tuning of imidazolium-bsed SAILs allow to customize it for EOR and optimize their cost-effectiveness.

Despite the high price of imidazolium SAILs at early 2000s, significant progress has been made in reducing SAILs costs through process innovations and bulk manufacturing28. Some simple SAILs are already cost-competitive with traditional chemicals. For example some techno-economic studies have shown that certain type of SAILs including imidazoium-based one could be manufactured at industrial scale for less than 6$ per kg28. Hence, synthesis-purification development could help making these SAILs even feasible and more competitive with conventional surfactants. Finally, insights gained from this work can guide the design of next-generation, cost-optimized ionic-liquid-based or deep eutectic surfactant systems with improved scalability and economic viability. On the other hand, this SAILs has very low CMC, compared to traditional and commercial surfactants which could potentially reduce overall chemical costs. Furthermore, SAILs has impressive stability, low degradability and precipitation at harsh conditions could be promising for reuse and cost-effectiveness (green chemistry).

Conclusions

A synthetic SAILs, blended with a betaine surfactant and IOS, was selected through a systematic screening conducted under the harsh conditions of carbonate reservoirs. The screening procedure involved assessments of thermal and chemical stability, oil–water IFT, wettability, adsorption behavior, and coreflooding performance. Furthermore, the dynamic performance of this SAILs-based formulation was compared to that of conventional surfactants.
