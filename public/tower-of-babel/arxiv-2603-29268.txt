From Physics to Surrogate Intelligence: A Unified Electro-Thermo-Optimization Framework for TSV Networks
Mohamed Gharib, Leonid Popryho, and Inna Partin-Vaisband

Abstract
High-density through-substrate vias (TSVs) enable 2.5D/3D heterogeneous integration but introduce significant signal-integrity and thermal-reliability challenges due to electrical coupling, insertion loss, and self-heating. Conventional full-wave finite-element method (FEM) simulations provide high accuracy but become computationally prohibitive for large design-space exploration. This work presents a scalable electro–thermal modeling and optimization framework that combines physics-informed analytical modeling, graph neural network (GNN) surrogates, and full-wave sign-off validation. A multi-conductor analytical model computes broadband S-parameters and effective anisotropic thermal conductivities of TSV arrays, achieving 5 % 5\% – 10 % 10\% relative Frobenius error (RFE) across array sizes up to 15 × 15 15\times 15 . A physics-informed GNN surrogate (TSV-PhGNN), trained on analytical data and fine-tuned with HFSS simulations, generalizes to larger arrays with RFE below 2 % 2\% and nearly constant variance. The surrogate is integrated into a multi-objective Pareto optimization framework targeting reflection coefficient, insertion loss, worst-case crosstalk (NEXT/FEXT), and effective thermal conductivity. Millions of TSV configurations can be explored within minutes, enabling exhaustive layout and geometric optimization that would be infeasible using FEM alone. Final designs are validated with Ansys HFSS and Mechanical, showing strong agreement. The proposed framework enables rapid electro–thermal co-design of TSV arrays while reducing per-design evaluation time by more than six orders of magnitude.

II
Electrothermal Analytical Model

The electrothermal modeling framework for cylindrical vertical interconnects in
[
6
]
is adopted as the basis of this work and extended to dense, irregular TSV arrays. Electrically, the original metal-insulator-metal (MIM) formulation for a single-reference ground is generalized to multi-ground signal-ground configurations, enabling accurate modeling of practical TSV arrays with arbitrary signal assignments and multiple return paths.

Thermally, the steady-state ETC formulation in
[
6
]
is extended with sparsity-aware corrections for partially populated and irregular layouts. This anisotropic treatment preserves physical consistency and avoids overestimating lateral heat spreading under uniform homogenization assumptions.

II-B

Electrothermal Framework Using Equivalent Thermal Conductivity

In practical TSV-based interconnect systems, metallic conductors exhibit finite electrical resistance, resulting in ohmic power dissipation and the generation of Joule heat. Temperature variations, in turn, directly affect electrical behavior by increasing the resistivity of conductive materials, thereby further amplifying power loss. The temperature dependence of copper resistivity is modeled as
[
22
]

\rho_{\text{Cu}}=\rho_{0}\bigl[1+\alpha(T-T_{0})\bigr],

(3)

where
\rho_{0}=1.7\times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}
denotes the resistivity at the reference temperature
T_{0}=300\,\text{K}
, and
\alpha=3.9\times 10^{-3}\,\text{K}^{-1}
is the temperature coefficient of resistivity. As evident from (
3
), an increase in temperature raises conductor resistance, which increases ohmic losses and further elevates temperature. This electrothermal interaction forms a positive feedback loop that must converge to a steady-state operating point.

The proposed framework captures this interaction through a coupled electrothermal modeling flow, in which electrical losses extracted from S-parameters are mapped to thermal heat sources, and the resulting temperature rise is used to update temperature-dependent electrical parameters. While the framework supports transient electrothermal analysis, the focus of this work is on steady-state temperature estimation. The overall electrothermal simulation flow is illustrated in Fig.
3
.

1
Equivalent Thermal Conductivity Formulation

Each TSV is modeled using a square thermal unit cell of side length

2(r_{\mathrm{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}})
, comprising three distinct regions:
the copper via, the surrounding oxide liner, and the substrate.
The corresponding cross-sectional areas are given by

\displaystyle s_{v}^{z}

\displaystyle=\pi r_{\mathrm{cond}}^{2},

(4)

\displaystyle s_{l}^{z}

\displaystyle=\pi\bigl[(r_{\mathrm{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}})^{2}-r_{\mathrm{cond}}^{2}\bigr],

(5)

\displaystyle s_{s}^{z}

\displaystyle=(r_{\mathrm{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}})^{2}(4-\pi).

(6)

Using these area fractions, the vertical ETC
of a single TSV unit cell is computed as

k^{z}_{\text{cond}}=\frac{k_{v}s_{v}^{z}+k_{l}s_{l}^{z}+k_{s}s_{s}^{z}}{4(r_{\mathrm{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}})^{2}},

(7)

where
k_{v}
,
k_{l}
, and
k_{s}
denote the thermal conductivities of the via metal, insulating liner, and substrate, respectively, and
s^{z}
is the cross-sectional area (along the
z
-direction) of the corresponding material region.

Unlike vertical heat flow, lateral heat conduction encounters spatially
varying material composition across the TSV cross section. To accurately
capture this behavior, the lateral ETC is
computed using numerical integration

k^{x,y}_{\text{cond}}=\int_{0}^{r_{\text{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}}}\frac{dx}{\frac{W_{v}(x)}{k_{v}}+\frac{W_{l}(x)}{k_{l}}+\frac{W_{s}(x)}{k_{s}}},

(8)

where
W_{v}(x)
,
W_{l}(x)
, and
W_{s}(x)
represent the local material widths
at position
x
. This formulation captures nonuniform lateral heat-flow
paths without relying on simplified series or parallel resistance
approximations.

II-B
2
Equivalent Volumetric Heat Capacity

In addition to ETC, the framework computes an equivalent volumetric heat
capacity for completeness and future use in transient electrothermal
simulations. For a single TSV unit cell, the equivalent volumetric heat
capacity is given by

(\rho C_{p})_{\text{eq}}=\frac{\rho_{v}C_{p,v}s_{v}^{z}+\rho_{l}C_{p,l}s_{l}^{z}+\rho_{s}C_{p,s}s_{s}^{z}}{4(r_{\text{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}})^{2}},

(9)

where
\rho
and
C_{p}
denote, respectively, the mass density and specific heat capacity of the corresponding material region.

To account for the surrounding substrate beyond the TSV unit cells,
an extended equivalent volumetric heat capacity is computed over the full
substrate area. Under steady-state conditions, the
\rho C_{p}
term
multiplies the transient temperature derivative and therefore drops out
from the governing heat equation. Nevertheless, it is reported to enable
direct extension of the framework to transient electrothermal analysis.

II-B
3
Array-Level Anisotropic Conductivities

Prior array-level ETC formulations, including
[
25
]
,
implicitly assume that TSVs form a
fully populated rectangular array

with uniform spacing, such that all
M\times N
unit cells contain TSVs.
While this assumption simplifies analysis, it does not generally hold for practical TSV layouts, which often include reserved regions for routing, power delivery, or mechanical constraints.

To address this limitation, the proposed framework explicitly accounts
for
sparse TSV arrays
. Let
M
and
N
denote the number of
rows and columns, respectively, and let
n_{\text{TSV}}
denote the total
number of TSVs within the
M\times N
array. A sparsity factor is defined as

f_{\text{occ}}=\frac{n_{\text{TSV}}}{MN},\qquad 0<f_{\text{occ}}\leq 1.

(10)

The effective TSV footprint used in lateral conduction is scaled by

\sqrt{f_{\text{occ}}}
to preserve the correct total TSV area contribution,
yielding

l_{\text{cond,eff}}=w_{\text{cond,eff}}=2(r_{\text{cond}}+t_{\text{ins}})\sqrt{f_{\text{occ}}},

(11)

where
l_{\text{cond,eff}}
and
w_{\text{cond,eff}}
are the side lengths of a square thermal TSV unit cell.

Using these effective dimensions, the lateral array-level ETCs are
expressed as

k^{x}_{\text{eq}}=\frac{k_{s}(w_{s}-Nw_{\text{cond,eff}})}{w_{s}}\;\Bigg|\Bigg|\;\frac{k^{x}_{\text{cond}}\cdot Nl_{s}w_{\text{cond,eff}}}{Ml_{\text{cond,eff}}w_{s}},

(12)

k^{y}_{\text{eq}}=\frac{k_{s}(l_{s}-Ml_{\text{cond,eff}})}{l_{s}}\;\Bigg|\Bigg|\;\frac{k^{y}_{\text{cond}}\cdot Mw_{s}l_{\text{cond,eff}}}{Nl_{s}w_{\text{cond,eff}}},

(13)

where
l_{s}
and
w_{s}
denote total lateral length and width of the substrate region, respectively.
The vertical array-level ETC is computed using the
actual TSV area
fraction
rather than assuming a fully populated rectangle

k^{z}_{\text{eq}}=k_{s}+\frac{l_{\text{cond}}w_{\text{cond}}}{l_{s}w_{s}}n_{\text{TSV}}\bigl(k^{z}_{\text{cond}}-k_{s}\bigr).

(14)

This sparsity-aware formulation ensures that the effective anisotropic
thermal conductivities remain physically consistent for irregular TSV
layouts and avoids systematic overestimation of heat-spreading capability
in partially populated arrays.

4
Heat Source Extraction from S-Parameters

The volumetric heat generation rate
G
is derived directly from the electrical S-parameters. Since TSV arrays exhibit conductive and dielectric losses, the sum of the squared magnitudes of the scattering parameters is less than unity, and the deficit corresponds to dissipated power. For excitation of the
j^{\text{th}}
conductor, the dissipated power is computed as

P_{\text{loss}}=P_{\text{in}}\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{q}|S_{ij}|^{2}\right),

(15)

where
q=(N\times M-1)\times 2
denotes the total number of ports in the array with each TSV modeled as a two-port network.

:

The corresponding volumetric heat generation rate within the
j^{\text{th}}
TSV is obtained by normalizing the dissipated power over the conductor volume,

G_{\text{cond},j}=\frac{P_{\text{loss}}}{\pi r_{\text{cond}}^{2}h}.

(16)

This procedure is applied to all conductors to construct the full volumetric heat generation field
G
as shown in Fig.

5
Steady-State Thermal Solution Using Ansys Mechanical

Instead of explicitly solving the heat conduction equation on TSV-resolved geometries, the extracted anisotropic ETCs
(k^{x}_{eq},k^{y}_{eq},k^{z}_{eq})
are imported into
Ansys Mechanical
as an equivalent anisotropic thermal block. The volumetric heat sources derived from the S-parameters are applied to this block under the same boundary conditions used in full 3D simulations.

This homogenized ETC-based approach enables rapid steady-state temperature extraction while maintaining close agreement with TSV-resolved finite-element simulations, achieving speedups from
\sim 35\times
for
5\times 5
sparse arrays to
\sim 1300\times
for fully populated
10\times 10
arrays.

III
ML-Assisted Optimization Framework

The proposed framework integrates analytical physics modeling, GNNs, and transfer learning to create a surrogate model capable of rapid and accurate S-parameter prediction for TSV arrays.

III-A

Analytical Dataset Generation

To mitigate the prohibitive computational cost of three-dimensional FEM simulations (e.g., Ansys HFSS) during the initial model training stage, the physics-based analytical framework described in Section
II
is used to generate a large-scale pretraining dataset. Using this framework, 100,000 data samples are generated analytically for TSV arrays with dimensions ranging from
3\times 3
to
20\times 20
, and the corresponding RLCG-based netlists are simulated using the HSPICE circuit simulator. The resulting dataset is partitioned into 80,000 samples for training and 20,000 samples for validation.

III-B

Graph Representation

The TSV array is modeled as a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, enabling a flexible representation that naturally supports arbitrary TSV arrangement patterns and variable array sizes as shown in Fig.
5
. Graph-based modeling is particularly well suited for this problem, as it captures complex spatial interactions while remaining inherently scalable. As a result, the proposed framework can generalize to TSV arrays larger than those encountered during training without requiring architectural modifications.

III-B
1
Node Representation

The node set
\mathcal{V}
corresponds to the TSVs in the array, where each TSV is represented by a node
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
. The node feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
encodes both the physical dimensions and the functional role of the TSV:

\mathbf{x}_{i}=\big[\text{Type},\,r_{\text{via}},\,p,\,h_{\text{via}},\,t_{\text{ox}},\,f\big].

(17)

Here,
\text{Type}\in\{1,0,-1\}
denotes signal TSVs, empty locations, and ground TSVs, respectively. The remaining features represent the TSV radius
r_{\text{via}}
, pitch
p
, via height
h_{\text{via}}
, oxide thickness
t_{\text{ox}}
, and operating frequency
f
. This formulation enables the model to jointly reason about geometric properties, material configuration, and electrical excitation.

III-B
2
Edge Representation

The edge set
\mathcal{E}
captures electromagnetic interactions between TSVs. To account for long-range coupling effects, the graph is constructed as fully connected, where each edge
e_{ij}\in\mathcal{E}
represents the interaction between TSVs
i
and
j
. The corresponding edge feature vector
\mathbf{e}_{ij}
encodes their spatial relationship through distance-based descriptors:

\mathbf{e}_{ij}=\big[d_{ij},\,d_{ij}^{-1},\,d_{ij}^{-2}\big],

(18)

where
d_{ij}
is the Euclidean distance between TSVs
i
and
j
. Explicit inclusion of inverse-distance terms facilitates learning of electromagnetic field decay behavior and mutual coupling characteristics, which are critical for accurate wideband S-parameter prediction.

III-C

Physics-Informed Graph Neural Network

The proposed learning architecture, referred to as
TSV-PhGNN
, is explicitly designed to embed key physical principles governing electromagnetic propagation and coupling in TSV arrays.

III-C
1
Feature-Wise Linear Modulation

Electromagnetic behavior in TSV interconnects exhibits distinct operating regimes as a function of frequency (e.g., transition from quasi-static to skin-effect-dominated behavior). To capture this dependence, the operating frequency
f
is treated as a conditioning variable rather than a conventional input feature. Specifically, a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) mechanism is employed to adapt the geometry-dependent latent representation based on frequency
[
29
]
.

Let
\mathbf{h}_{\text{geom}}
denote the geometry embedding and
\mathbf{h}_{\text{freq}}
denote the frequency embedding. The modulated representation is

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=\gamma(\mathbf{h}_{\text{freq}})\odot\mathbf{h}_{\text{geom}}+\beta(\mathbf{h}_{\text{freq}}),

(19)

where
\gamma(\cdot)
and
\beta(\cdot)
are learnable functions producing feature-wise scaling and bias terms, respectively, and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication. This formulation enables the network to dynamically adapt its internal representations across frequency regimes.

III-C
2
Message Passing via Graph Transformer

Message passing is performed using a Graph Transformer architecture. Unlike uniform aggregation schemes, the Transformer computes attention coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
for each edge, allowing the model to learn direction-dependent and context-aware coupling strengths between TSVs. This mechanism is particularly effective for capturing electromagnetic shielding and screening effects, for example by assigning reduced attention weights to interactions obstructed by ground TSVs.

III-C
3
Symmetry-Enforced Output Heads

The network predicts complex-valued S-parameters and employs two specialized output heads:

•

Node Head:
Predicts return loss (
S_{11}
) and insertion loss (
S_{21}
) for each signal TSV.

•

Edge Head:
Predicts near-end crosstalk (NEXT) and far-end crosstalk (FEXT) between pairs of signal TSVs.

A fundamental physical constraint is reciprocity, which requires
S_{ij}=S_{ji}
. Since standard neural network architectures do not inherently satisfy this condition, reciprocity is enforced explicitly by symmetrizing the edge-level predictions:

\hat{y}_{\text{edge}}^{(i,j)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{MLP}(\mathbf{h}_{i},\mathbf{h}_{j})+\text{MLP}(\mathbf{h}_{j},\mathbf{h}_{i})\right).

(20)

The TSV array is represented as a fully connected graph with geometry-aware node features and distance-based edge features. The architecture integrates feature-wise linear modulation, Graph Transformer message passing, symmetry-enforced output heads, and a homoscedastic physics-aware loss function. The pre-trained model is subsequently fine-tuned using high-fidelity FEM data (10,000 samples) generated by Ansys HFSS through a simulation-to-simulation (Sim-to-Sim) transfer learning strategy.

III-D

Homoscedastic Physics-Aware Loss Function

The training objective jointly enforces regression accuracy and physical consistency. Due to the large dynamic range between transmission-related terms (e.g.,
S_{21}
) and crosstalk terms (e.g.,
S_{ij}
), direct optimization using a single loss leads to task imbalance. To address this issue, a homoscedastic uncertainty-based weighting strategy is adopted.
The regression loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE},k}+\log\sigma_{k}\right),

(21)

where
\mathcal{K}=\{\mathrm{IL},\mathrm{RL},\mathrm{NEXT},\mathrm{FEXT}\}
denotes the set of predicted S-parameter quantities. Specifically,
\mathrm{IL}
corresponds to insertion loss (e.g.,
|S_{21}|
in dB),
\mathrm{RL}
to return loss (e.g.,
|S_{11}|
in dB), and
\mathrm{NEXT}
/
\mathrm{FEXT}
to near- and far-end crosstalk magnitudes (e.g.,
|S_{31}|
and
|S_{41}|
in dB, depending on port assignment).

The mean-squared error for task
k
is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE},k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\hat{y}_{k,n}-y_{k,n}\right)^{2},

(22)

where
\hat{y}_{k,n}
and
y_{k,n}
denote the predicted and reference values, respectively. The parameters
\sigma_{k}
are learnable task-dependent variances that adaptively balance the contributions of the individual regression targets during training.

In addition, a
passivity constraint
is imposed to enforce energy conservation. For a passive multiport network, the sum of squared magnitudes in any row of the S-matrix must not exceed unity. Violations are penalized using

\mathcal{L}_{\text{passivity}}=\operatorname{mean}\left(\operatorname{ReLU}\left(\sum_{j}|S_{ij}|^{2}-1\right)\right).

(23)

The total training objective is given by

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{passivity}},

(24)

where
\lambda
controls the strength of the passivity regularization.

III-E

Transfer Learning Strategy

While the implemented physics-based analytical model enables rapid generation of large-scale training data, it relies on simplifying assumptions (e.g., quasi-TEM behavior) that do not fully capture three-dimensional electromagnetic effects. To achieve analytical scalability with full-wave fidelity, a multi-fidelity simulation-to-simulation (Sim-to-Sim) transfer learning framework is adopted
[
27
]
. The approach exploits the strong correlation between low-fidelity (analytical) and high-fidelity (full-wave) physical manifolds. Although the analytical model may exhibit magnitude deviations, it preserves the dominant geometric and frequency-dependent trends of the S-parameters. Accordingly, training is performed in two stages.

1) Pre-training (Low Fidelity):
The proposed TSV-PhGNN is first trained on a large analytically generated dataset (
N=80{,}000
) obtained from RLCG-based HSPICE simulations. This stage enables the network to learn topology-aware relationships between TSV geometry, spatial arrangement, and broadband S-parameter behavior, providing a physics-consistent weight initialization.

2) Fine-tuning (High Fidelity):
The pre-trained model is subsequently fine-tuned using a smaller full-wave dataset (
N=10{,}000
) generated from 3D FEM simulations in Ansys HFSS for compact arrays (
3\times 3
to
7\times 7
). With an identical input feature space and a reduced learning rate (
10^{-4}
), the network adapts to higher-order 3D effects (e.g., skin effect and complex scattering) while retaining the structural knowledge acquired during pre-training.

This multi-fidelity strategy substantially reduces reliance on computationally intensive full-wave simulations while maintaining FEM-level predictive accuracy (Fig.
6
). Thermal parameters, including the equivalent thermal conductivities, are computed directly from the analytical electrothermal model (Section
II-B
) since their evaluation is computationally inexpensive and does not require surrogate modeling.

IV
Experiments and Results

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed framework. First, the analytical electrothermal model described in Section
II
is validated against full-wave electromagnetic simulations in Ansys HFSS and thermal simulations in Ansys Mechanical. Second, the predictive performance of the proposed ML model is evaluated by comparing its S-parameter predictions against Ansys simulation results.
Finally, optimization case studies are conducted using the trained ML surrogate model, and the resulting designs are benchmarked against SOTA TSV architectures to demonstrate performance improvements.

IV-D

Relative Frobenius Error (RFE) Evaluation

To quantitatively assess the agreement between the ML model, the analytical model, and the full-wave HFSS reference, we compute the RFE in the complex domain. Unlike magnitude-only or dB-based comparisons, the RFE captures both magnitude and phase discrepancies simultaneously. For a given S-parameter matrix,
\mathbf{S}(f)
, evaluated over all frequencies and port pairs, the RFE is defined as

\mathrm{RFE}=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{S}_{\text{model}}-\mathbf{S}_{\text{HFSS}}\right\|_{F}}{\left\|\mathbf{S}_{\text{HFSS}}\right\|_{F}},

(25)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm, defined as

\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{f}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\left|A_{ij}(f)\right|^{2}}.

(26)

Here,
\mathbf{S}_{\text{model}}
represents either the ML-predicted or analytical S-parameter matrix, while
\mathbf{S}_{\text{HFSS}}
corresponds to the full-wave reference. The normalization by the HFSS Frobenius norm ensures scale invariance, enabling fair comparison across different array sizes and frequency points. This metric provides a compact global measure of complex-domain error and is used consistently to benchmark both the ML and analytical models against the full-wave electromagnetic solver.

X_{\mathrm{talk}}=\frac{1}{s(s-1)}\sum_{v=1}^{s}\left|\mathrm{dB}\!\left(X_{v}^{\mathrm{tot}}\right)\right|,

IV-F

Scalability and Computational Considerations

The computational scalability of the proposed graph-based ML framework is discussed in this section.
For an
N\times N
TSV grid, the number of TSVs scales as
V=N^{2}
. Since the graph representation models coupling interactions between all TSV pairs, it is treated as fully connected, leading to
E=V(V-1)=\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
edges.
This quadratic growth in vertices and quartic growth in edge interactions increases memory and computational requirements.
Because inference is primarily GPU-accelerated, the framework becomes limited by available VRAM for very large arrays. While CPU-based inference can support larger graphs, it incurs higher runtime.
Consequently, a tradeoff exists between array scalability, runtime, and hardware memory constraints. Future work will investigate sparse graph representations and locality-aware edge pruning to reduce memory complexity while preserving modeling accuracy.

V
Conclusion

A scalable electro–thermal co-design framework for dense TSV arrays is presented, integrating physics-based analytical modeling, machine-learning acceleration, and FEM sign-off. The analytical solver computes wideband multi-port S-parameters and anisotropic thermal conductivities for arbitrary TSV layouts. Validation against HFSS using complex-domain RFE shows
5\%
–
10.6\%
error for moderate array sizes while reducing evaluation time from
\sim 10^{3}
seconds (FEM) to sub-second levels.
A GNN surrogate, fine-tuned with a limited HFSS dataset, further improves accuracy to below
\sim
2% RFE with millisecond inference, enabling over
10^{6}\times
acceleration for medium-size (
