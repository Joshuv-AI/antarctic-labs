Warm staple-line irrigation (37–40 °C) before omentopexy reduces early bleeding after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy: a prospective, randomized trial
Authors: Gamal M Abdalla, Ahmed M Farrag, George M Halim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly bleeding remains a common concern after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), despite improvements in stapling technology and reinforcement techniques. This study examined whether using warm saline to irrigate the staple line before performing omentopexy could enhance intra-operative hemostasis and reduce early postoperative bleeding.MethodsIn this prospective randomized trial conducted between December 2024 and June 2025, 200 adults undergoing primary LSG were allocated equally to warm saline irrigation (37–40 °C) or conventional room-temperature irrigation (22–24 °C), reflecting two clinically relevant intraoperative strategies in routine laparoscopic practice. Irrigation was applied directly to the staple line, followed by complete suctioning, careful inspection, and standardized omentopexy using 2–0 PDS sutures spaced 2 cm apart. The primary endpoint was a 48-h composite of early postoperative bleeding, defined as clinical bleeding, need for endoscopic or surgical control, hemoglobin drop (ΔHb) ≥ 2 g/dL on postoperative day 1, or drain output ≥ 150 mL in the first 24 h. Secondary outcomes included hemostasis time, ΔHb, drain volume, pain scores, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), length of stay (LOS), and 30-day complications. This trial was powered for a composite bleeding endpoint rather than rare major bleeding events.ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar between groups. The composite bleeding endpoint occurred in 5% of patients receiving warm irrigation versus 15% in the control group (p = 0.02). Warm irrigation also resulted in lower drain output (82 ± 31 vs 128 ± 46 mL; p < 0.001) and a smaller ΔHb drop (1.28 ± 0.52 vs 2.06 ± 0.61 g/dL; p < 0.001). Pain scores were modestly lower at 24 h. LOS, PONV, readmission rates, and leak rates were comparable.ConclusionWarm saline irrigation may improve hemostasis and early bleeding-related outcomes after LSG. However, given the composite nature of the primary endpoint and the limited sample size for rare complications, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and larger multicenter studies are needed for confirmation.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07475169), retrospectively registered on 12 February 2026.Keywords: Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, Staple-line bleeding, Omentopexy, Warm saline irrigation, Hemostasis, Bariatric surgery

Introduction

Obesity has become one of the most important global health challenges, with prevalence continuing to rise across all regions. Current estimates from the World Health Organization indicate that more than one billion adults are living with obesity, a number expected to increase further as lifestyle habits shift and risk factors accumulate worldwide [1]. Beyond excess weight, obesity contributes directly to serious cardiometabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, and coronary artery disease, all of which increase overall mortality and reduce life expectancy [2]. While lifestyle interventions and medications remain essential in early management, long-term data consistently show that bariatric and metabolic surgery provides the most durable weight reduction and the greatest likelihood of achieving remission of metabolic comorbidities [3, 4].

Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) has become the most widely performed metabolic bariatric procedure globally. International registry data from the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) indicate that LSG represents more than half of all metabolic bariatric operations in many regions [5]. Its popularity reflects several advantages: the procedure is technically straightforward, preserves normal gastrointestinal continuity, avoids intestinal bypass, and achieves substantial excess weight loss with a relatively low risk of long-term nutritional deficiencies [6]. Despite these benefits, early complications such as staple-line bleeding and gastric leak can occur and may prolong recovery or require additional interventions [7, 8].

Postoperative bleeding after LSG typically arises from short gastric vessels or the raw staple line. Incidence rates vary between 1–6% depending on institutional practices and definitions used to classify bleeding [7–9]. Although many cases are self-limiting, significant bleeding may lead to tachycardia, hypotension, the need for transfusion, or re-intervention. Therefore, improving intraoperative hemostasis and reducing staple-line bleeding remain key goals during LSG.

Various techniques have been proposed to minimize bleeding risk. Oversewing the staple line is a widely used approach but may increase operative time and carries a risk of sleeve narrowing if performed too tightly [10]. Buttressing materials can effectively reduce bleeding but add cost and, in some cases, have been associated with device-related complications [11]. Topical sealants may control localized bleeding but have limited effect on diffuse microvascular oozing [12]. Omentopexy has also been suggested to reduce bleeding and stabilize the newly formed sleeve, although its effect on hemorrhage remains inconsistent across studies [13].

Given these limitations, attention has shifted toward simple physiologic measures that might improve hemostasis. Warm saline irrigation is one such measure and has shown benefit in other surgical specialties. In functional endoscopic sinus surgery, warm irrigation improves visualization and reduces mucosal oozing compared with room-temperature irrigation [14–16]. In general laparoscopic surgery, warm irrigation has been associated with reduced postoperative pain, improved maintenance of tissue temperature, and preservation of normothermia, all of which may support early recovery [17–20].

Despite its known advantages elsewhere, the role of irrigation temperature has not been evaluated in the context of LSG. In contemporary laparoscopic practice, irrigation of the operative field is routinely performed to clear blood, improve visualization, and allow precise identification of bleeding points; therefore, the relevant clinical question is not whether irrigation should be used, but whether the temperature of the irrigating fluid influences hemostasis. A technique that enhances visualization of the staple line and facilitates recognition of subtle oozing could meaningfully improve intraoperative control, particularly before performing omentopexy, when the staple line will be partially obscured. Warm saline is inexpensive, readily available, and easily administered using standard laparoscopic irrigation systems, making it a practical adjunct if proven effective.

To address this gap, we designed a prospective randomized clinical trial to determine whether warm saline irrigation (37–40 °C) applied along the staple line before omentopexy can reduce early postoperative bleeding compared with conventional room-temperature irrigation. We hypothesized that warm irrigation would enhance visualization, facilitate more effective hemostasis, reduce drain output and hemoglobin decline, and potentially improve early postoperative recovery without increasing complications. In the present study, omentopexy was intentionally applied as a standardized background technique in both groups to isolate the independent effect of irrigation temperature.

Methodology

This study was conducted as a prospective, randomized, single-blind clinical trial designed to evaluate whether warm saline irrigation applied to the gastric staple line before omentopexy could reduce early postoperative bleeding following laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG). The trial took place at two high-volume bariatric centers in Cairo, Egypt, between December 2024 and June 2025. Both institutions follow standardized metabolic bariatric surgery protocols and each performs more than 600 cases annually, providing a consistent operative environment and minimizing variability in surgical technique. Both participating centers followed a highly standardized protocol and were selected specifically because of comparable case volume, surgeon experience, and shared operative routine. The same operative sequence, irrigation device, inspection method, criteria for focal hemostatic control, and omentopexy technique were used in both centers to maximize procedural reproducibility. Operations were performed by experienced bariatric surgeons familiar with the standardized study protocol.

Regarding ethics approval and informed consent, the study protocol was reviewed and prospectively approved by the Ain Shams University Faculty of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (IRB No. 00006379). The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed CONSORT 2010 guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07475169 (retrospectively registered).

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 60 years who met accepted indications for primary LSG, specifically a body mass index (BMI) of 40 kg/m2 or greater, or 35 kg/m2 with a significant obesity-related comorbidity such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or obstructive sleep apnea. Restricting the study to primary LSG patients ensured uniform anatomy and eliminated confounding factors associated with revisional or bypass procedures. Patients were excluded if they had undergone prior major upper-abdominal surgery with expected adhesions, had known bleeding disorders, or had received anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy within seven days of surgery. Additional exclusions included advanced hepatic or renal disease, unstable cardiac conditions, pregnancy, immunosuppression, active systemic infection, or inability to adhere to the follow-up schedule. All candidates underwent a standardized preoperative assessment that included laboratory investigations, coagulation studies, liver and renal function tests, electrocardiography, and anesthetic evaluation.

Randomization followed a computer-generated sequence created by an independent statistician not involved in patient care. Patients were allocated in a 1:1 ratio to receive either warm saline irrigation at 37–40 °C or room-temperature saline at 22–24 °C. To maintain allocation concealment, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes were prepared and opened only after gastric stapling had been completed. Although it was not possible to blind the operating surgeon to the intervention being applied, all postoperative outcome assessors, ward nurses, and research staff remained blinded to treatment allocation. Patients were similarly unaware of their assigned group until completion of the study.

Intraoperative blood pressure was maintained within a standardized range under anesthetic control to minimize its effect on bleeding outcomes. All operations were performed laparoscopically under general anesthesia using a standardized five-port approach with the patient in the French position. All procedures were performed using standardized surgical techniques and similar stapling devices (Ethicon Echelon Flex Johnson and Johnson power plus stapler with gripping surface technology) across participating centers to minimize variability. Mobilization of the greater curvature was initiated 4–6 cm proximal to the pylorus and carried cephalad to the angle of His, ensuring complete division of the short gastric vessels. Calibration of the gastric sleeve was performed using a 36-French bougie. Gastric transection was undertaken using linear staplers of appropriate height depending on tissue thickness. No reinforcement materials, serosal sutures, fibrin sealants, or topical hemostatic agents were applied to the staple line in any patient, ensuring that irrigation temperature remained the sole intervention under investigation. At the conclusion of gastric transection, the staple line was exposed fully and inspected, and the interval from completion of stapling to confirmation of a dry staple line was defined as the hemostasis phase.

In contemporary laparoscopic practice, irrigation of the operative field is routinely performed to clear blood, improve visualization, and allow accurate identification of bleeding points. Omitting irrigation altogether was therefore considered neither practical nor ethically appropriate in our operative environment. Accordingly, the study was designed to compare two clinically relevant and widely applicable strategies: conventional room-temperature irrigation as the control condition and warm irrigation as the investigational intervention. In the warm-irrigation group, 400 mL of sterile saline heated to 37–40 °C was applied along the entire staple line using a laparoscopic suction–irrigation device. The fluid remained in contact with the staple line for approximately 30 s before being completely suctioned. The control group received an identical technique and volume of saline, with the only difference being that the solution was at room temperature (22–24 °C). The volume of 400 mL was selected based on previous laparoscopic data showing that 300–500 mL provides sufficient irrigation coverage without leaving residual fluid. After suctioning, the staple line was re-examined, and any uncovered oozing points were treated using gentle bipolar coagulation or clips. Additional hemostatic maneuvers (clip application and/or gentle bipolar coagulation) were recorded as binary variables (presence or absence), rather than as absolute counts, to reflect clinically relevant decision-making.

Following confirmation of complete hemostasis, omentopexy was performed in all patients using a standardized technique. Omentopexy represents routine institutional practice in both participating centers and was applied uniformly in the two study arms to avoid confounding and to isolate the effect of irrigation temperature on staple-line hemostasis. In another words, omentopexy was not considered a study variable but rather a fixed component of the standardized surgical protocol to minimize variability and avoid confounding due to different reinforcement practices. The greater omentum was sutured to the staple line with interrupted 2–0 polydioxanone (PDS) sutures placed 2 cm apart with a tissue depth of approximately 0.5 cm. Care was taken to avoid excessive tension that might compromise blood supply. A closed-suction drain (18-French) was placed along the staple line and removed after 24–48 h when drainage became serous and less than 50 mL per day. The drain was used as part of the study protocol to allow objective assessment of early postoperative bleeding parameters.

Postoperative care adhered to enhanced recovery after surgery principles. Early ambulation was encouraged six hours postoperatively, and clear fluids were introduced the next morning. Analgesia included paracetamol with or without NSAIDs, and antiemetics were administered as necessary. Hemoglobin was measured preoperatively and again on postoperative day one to calculate the hemoglobin drop (ΔHb). Drain output was measured every eight hours and evaluated for both volume and appearance. Drain output ≥ 150 mL of predominantly sanguineous fluid was incorporated as one component of a prespecified composite endpoint designed to capture early bleeding-related signals, including subclinical or evolving staple-line oozing rather than as equivalent to major hemorrhage. Daily clinical assessment focused on detecting signs of bleeding, tachycardia, hypotension, or abdominal discomfort.

Patients were followed at one week, one month, and three months postoperatively. Complications were classified using the Clavien–Dindo system. Any readmission, re-intervention, endoscopy, or radiologic evaluation within 30 days was recorded. The primary endpoint was early postoperative bleeding within 48 h, defined as any of the following: clinically visible bleeding, a hemoglobin drop of 2 g/dL or more, drain output of at least 150 mL of sanguineous fluid in 24 h, or the need for endoscopic or surgical hemostasis. Secondary outcomes included hemostasis-phase time, need for additional intraoperative hemostatic maneuvers, hemoglobin drop on postoperative day 1, 24-h drain output, persistent sanguineous drainage beyond 12 h, postoperative pain scores, postoperative nausea and vomiting, length of stay, 30-day complications, readmission, and leak. Clinical bleeding events (e.g., hematemesis, melena, reoperation for bleeding) were additionally analyzed separately from surrogate bleeding markers. Postoperative adverse events were predefined, systematically collected, and categorized as bleeding-related and non–bleeding-related complications.

Sample-size calculations were based on reducing bleeding from 15 to 5%, yielding a requirement of 90 patients per group; 100 were enrolled to allow for attrition. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 28, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for all primary and key secondary outcomes.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for key binary outcomes, while all effect estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals to reflect both magnitude and precision of observed differences. All analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle. No interim analysis was performed.

Finally, the study was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement, and all relevant checklist items were addressed in the manuscript. A total of 226 individuals were assessed for eligibility; 26 were excluded due to not meeting inclusion criteria or declining participation. The remaining 200 patients were randomized equally between the two study arms. All randomized patients completed follow-up and were included in the final analysis without protocol violations or loss to follow-up (Fig. 1).

Results

A total of 226 patients were evaluated for eligibility during the study period. Twenty-six were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria or declined participation. The remaining 200 patients were randomized equally into the warm-irrigation group (n = 100) and the room-temperature control group (n = 100). All randomized patients completed the allocated intervention and the scheduled follow-up visits, with no losses to follow-up or withdrawals. This yielded a complete and robust dataset suitable for intention-to-treat analysis.

Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two groups, confirming the success of randomization. The mean age was 38.9 ± 9.1 years in the warm-irrigation group compared with 39.5 ± 8.8 years in the control group (p = 0.67). Women constituted nearly identical proportions of both groups (69% vs 70%; p = 0.81). Preoperative BMI values were also comparable (45.9 ± 4.8 vs 46.2 ± 4.6 kg/m2; p = 0.73). There were no meaningful differences in the prevalence of major comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes (34% vs 36%; p = 0.77), hypertension (42% vs 39%; p = 0.62), or obstructive sleep apnea (18% vs 21%; p = 0.58). Mean operative time was similar as well (65.1 ± 9.6 vs 66.0 ± 9.9 min; p = 0.48). These findings indicate that both study groups were comparable at baseline, allowing a reliable assessment of the effect of irrigation temperature (Table 1).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

Warm saline irrigation demonstrated a clear intraoperative advantage. The hemostasis phase was significantly shorter in the warm-irrigation group, averaging 3.9 ± 1.3 min versus 6.1 ± 2.0 min in the control group (p < 0.001), reflecting a notable 36% reduction. In line with this, fewer patients in the warm-irrigation group required additional hemostatic maneuvers such as bipolar coagulation or clipping (13% vs 29%; p = 0.01), corresponding to a relative risk of 0.45 (95% CI 0.23–0.89). In the warm irrigation group, 13 patients (13%) required additional hemostatic maneuvers, including clip application in 9 patients (9%) and bipolar coagulation in 6 patients (6%), with some patients requiring both. In the control group, 29 patients (29%) required additional hemostatic maneuvers, including clip application in 18 patients (18%) and bipolar coagulation in 14 patients (14%). These findings confirm that warm irrigation enhanced intraoperative visibility and efficiency (Table 2).

Intraoperative hemostasis outcomes

The primary outcome—early postoperative bleeding within 48 h—favored the warm-irrigation group as well. The composite bleeding endpoint occurred in only 5% of patients receiving warm irrigation compared with 15% in the control group (p = 0.02), representing a relative risk reduction of 64% (RR = 0.36; 95% CI 0.14–0.90). Although the absolute number of clinical bleeding events was small, fewer patients in the warm-irrigation group developed hematemesis or melena. No patient in the warm-irrigation arm required surgical re-intervention, whereas two patients in the control group underwent early re-laparoscopy for persistent bleeding. Although not statistically significant due to the small number of events (p = 0.15), the absence of reoperation in the warm-irrigation group is clinically relevant in metabolic bariatric practice. Markers of bleeding severity supported the primary outcome. The mean hemoglobin drop (ΔHb) from baseline to postoperative day one was significantly lower in the warm-irrigation group (1.28 ± 0.52 g/dL) than in controls (2.06 ± 0.61 g/dL) (95% CI − 1.01 to − 0.55;p < 0.001). Similarly, 24-h drain output was substantially reduced in the warm-irrigation arm (82 ± 31 mL vs 128 ± 46 mL; 95% CI − 58 to − 34; p < 0.001). Persistent sanguineous drainage beyond 12 h was also less common with warm irrigation (7% vs 20%; RR = 0.35; 95% CI 0.15–0.80 p = 0.01), indicating faster stabilization of the staple line (Table 3).

Postoperative bleeding outcomes

Warm irrigation also appeared to enhance early postoperative recovery. Pain scores were significantly lower at both 6 h (3.3 ± 1.0 vs 4.6 ± 1.2; p < 0.001) and 24 h (2.2 ± 0.9 vs 3.2 ± 1.0; p < 0.001). Although postoperative nausea and vomiting were slightly less frequent in the warm-irrigation group (10% vs 16%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.18). Length of stay was modestly but significantly shorter with warm irrigation (1.3 ± 0.4 vs 1.6 ± 0.5 days; p = 0.006). Overall complication rates were low and comparable, no leaks or abscesses were observed in either group. Two patients in the control group experienced major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III), while no such events occurred in the warm-irrigation group. Minor events (Clavien–Dindo I–II) occurred in 8% of warm-irrigation patients versus 16% in the control group (p = 0.09). Thirty-day readmissions were uncommon, occurring in 2% of control patients and none of those receiving warm irrigation (p = 0.15). Readmissions in the control group were mainly related to a case of vague abdominal pain for investigations (gastritis due to non-compliant patient with the postoperative nutritional instructions) and another case with uncontrolled diabetes which was managed successfully, with no significant difference between groups (Table 4).

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrates that warm saline irrigation at physiologic temperatures (37–40 °C), applied along the staple line before performing omentopexy in laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), offers a clear hemostatic advantage over standard room-temperature irrigation. The intervention consistently improved several intraoperative and early postoperative outcomes, including a shorter hemostasis phase, reduced need for additional hemostatic maneuvers, lower hemoglobin drop, diminished drain output, and fewer early bleeding events. Although blood pressure is a known determinant of staple-line bleeding, intraoperative hemodynamics were controlled in both groups, reducing its confounding impact. These benefits were achieved without prolonging operative time or increasing postoperative morbidity, suggesting that warm irrigation may serve as a simple, low-cost physiologic adjunct that can enhance staple-line hemostasis.

Early postoperative bleeding remains one of the most relevant complications after sleeve gastrectomy, even as stapling technology and operative standardization continue to evolve. Although the incidence of clinically apparent hemorrhage is frequently reported as 1–6% [7–9], the real burden is likely higher when incorporating subclinical indicators such as ΔHb and drain output. In our study, the 15% bleeding rate observed in the control group—defined by a comprehensive composite endpoint—aligns with concerns raised by Parmar and Mahawar regarding under-recognition of subtle but clinically meaningful bleeding episodes [9]. Therefore, the higher observed bleeding rate in the control group in our study reflects the use of a sensitive composite endpoint that includes subclinical bleeding markers such as hemoglobin drop and drain output, rather than only clinically overt hemorrhage. Against this baseline, the 64% relative risk reduction achieved with warm irrigation is clinically important and compares favorably with the effect sizes attributed to mechanical reinforcement techniques.

Current reinforcement strategies each have limitations. Oversewing may reduce bleeding but can add operative time and carries potential risk of sleeve narrowing if not executed with precision [10]. Buttressing materials can improve staple-line performance but increase cost and may introduce technical challenges such as partial mis-fires or tissue thickness variability [11]. Topical sealants offer localized hemostatic benefit but have inconsistent impact on diffuse bleeding and also increase resource utilization [12]. Omentopexy, despite being widely adopted for stabilization of the staple line, has shown mixed results regarding bleeding reduction [13]. Unlike these mechanical interventions, warm saline irrigation is cost-free, does not modify the staple line itself, and adds negligible time to the operation, making it an appealing adjunct for both high-resource and resource-limited environments.

The physiologic rationale for using warmed irrigation fluid is well supported by evidence from other surgical fields. In endoscopic sinus surgery, multiple trials and systematic reviews have demonstrated that warm or hot saline irrigation can improve visualization, decrease blood loss, and shorten operative duration [14–16, 21]. Studies in this domain support the observation that temperature influences microvascular hemostasis through improved clot dissolution, more efficient clearing of blood from the operative field, and a temperature-mediated effect on local circulation. Experimental work in animal models further strengthens the biological plausibility, showing that saline warmed to physiologic or near-physiologic temperatures significantly reduces bleeding times compared with room-temperature fluid [22]. These principles translate well into the laparoscopic environment, where clear visualization and control of micro-oozing are essential for safe and efficient surgery. Therefore, the benefit of warm irrigation is unlikely to be solely related to transient vasoconstriction, but rather to improved visualization and identification of bleeding points, enabling more effective intraoperative hemostasis.

Our findings strongly align with this mechanistic explanation. Warm irrigation sharply reduced the hemostasis phase—from 6.1 to 3.9 min—and halved the need for additional hemostatic steps. These intraoperative improvements were echoed by markedly lower ΔHb values and drain output in the warm-irrigation group, indicating more stable early clot formation and fewer persistently oozing points after surgery. Taken together, these outcomes point to a direct temperature-driven enhancement of staple-line hemostasis.

In addition to improving bleeding-related outcomes, warm irrigation also positively influenced early recovery. Patients in the warm-irrigation group experienced significantly lower postoperative pain at both 6 and 24 h. This observation is consistent with prior reports from laparoscopic cholecystectomy and other intraperitoneal procedures, where using warmed irrigation fluid has been associated with reduced peritoneal irritation and improved postoperative comfort [17, 18, 20]. Although postoperative nausea and vomiting occurred slightly less frequently with warm irrigation, the difference was not statistically significant; however, the trend parallels the observed reduction in early discomfort. The shorter hospital stay in the warm-irrigation group reflects smoother postoperative stabilization and may have meaningful implications for centers handling large caseloads or practicing enhanced-recovery protocols.

There were no statistically significant differences in major complications, although two cases (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III) were observed in the control group and none in the warm-irrigation group, and no patient in either group experienced a staple-line leak—consistent with earlier studies indicating that warming irrigation fluids does not adversely affect tissue integrity or healing [19, 20, 23–25]. Minor complications were numerically lower in the warm-irrigation group, and while the study was not powered to analyze rare events, the overall pattern suggests no apparent early safety signal within the limitations of the present sample size; however, the study was not powered to detect rare complications.

Additional hemostatic maneuvers such as clipping or bipolar coagulation were applied selectively and only after standardized irrigation and inspection. Because the same decision framework was used in both groups, these interventions are more appropriately interpreted as a consequence of intraoperative visibility and bleeding control rather than independent parallel interventions. Nevertheless, operator-triggered focal hemostasis remains a potential source of residual variability.

The study has several limitations. First, the sample size was powered to detect differences in composite bleeding outcomes but not for rare clinically significant bleeding events. Thus, even if the sample size was sufficient to detect differences in composite bleeding outcome, it was not large enough to evaluate very rare outcomes such as leaks or mortality. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and supportive of further larger-scale validation and further larger multicenter trials. A “no-irrigation” arm was not included, as irrigation of the operative field represents routine laparoscopic practice in our setting and was considered ethically and practically inappropriate to omit; accordingly, the trial was designed to compare two clinically relevant strategies—room-temperature versus warm irrigation. All patients underwent omentopexy, which reflects institutional standard practice and may limit the direct applicability of results to centers that do not routinely perform this step. Therefore, the absence of a non-omentopexy arm limits the ability to assess the independent or synergistic effect of omentopexy on bleeding outcomes. In this regard, the findings are most generalizable to units in which irrigation is part of daily operative workflow. Additionally, temperature control—while standardized—may vary slightly in real-world settings. Long-term outcomes, including adhesion formation and weight-loss trajectories, were not assessed. Moreover, although routine drainage is not universally recommended, it was used in this study to provide objective quantitative assessment of postoperative bleeding. Although both centers followed a strictly standardized protocol, we did not perform center-adjusted or center-stratified analyses. Therefore, potential center-related effects cannot be fully excluded, and this should be considered when interpreting the observed treatment effect and consequently, the observed treatment effect should be interpreted as arising within a standardized two-center protocol rather than as definitive evidence independent of possible center- or operator-related influences. Finally, although physiologic mechanisms for temperature-mediated hemostasis are supported by experimental data, the precise pathways were not evaluated directly in this study and warrant further research.

Despite these limitations, the strengths of this trial are considerable. It represents the first randomized clinical evaluation of irrigation temperature in metabolic bariatric surgery, employed rigorous CONSORT methodology, used standardized surgical technique, and incorporated a clinically meaningful composite bleeding outcome. Because warm irrigation adds no cost, requires no specialized equipment, and does not extend operative time, its potential for widespread adoption is high.

Conclusion

Warm saline irrigation may improve intraoperative hemostasis and reduce early bleeding-related outcomes after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. However, given the sample size and the composite nature of the primary endpoint, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and cannot establish definitive conclusions regarding rare or major bleeding events. Further large-scale multicenter studies are warranted.
