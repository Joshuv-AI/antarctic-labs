Effect of preoperative β-blocker use on Analgesia–Nociception Index during laparoscopic cholecystectomy
Authors: Joon-suk Bom, Seongheon Lee, Ye-chan Yoo, Joungmin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background The Analgesia–Nociception Index (ANI), derived from heart rate variability, reflects parasympathetic activity and is used to assess intraoperative nociception. β-blockers modulate autonomic tone and heart rate variability and may influence responses to nociceptive stimuli. This study investigated whether preoperative β-blocker use was associated with altered intraoperative ANI dynamics during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Method The data of 49 patients with hypertension who underwent elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy under a standardized propofol–remifentanil anesthetic protocol were analyzed in this prospective observational study. Patients were classified according to chronic β-blocker use (β-blocker group, n = 24; non-β-blocker group, n = 25). Four predefined nociceptive stimuli were applied under stable anesthetic conditions. The minimum ANI value within 2 min after each stimulus (ANI_min) and the change from the pre-stimulus value (ΔANI) were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance. Results Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of time, indicating a consistent decrease in ANI in response to standardized nociceptive stimuli. No significant time × group interaction or main effect of group was observed for ANI_min or ΔANI. The temporal patterns and magnitudes of ANI changes were comparable between groups across all predefined time points. Conclusions Preoperative β-blocker therapy did not substantially alter stimulus-related ANI dynamics during laparoscopic cholecystectomy under propofol–remifentanil anesthesia. These findings suggest that ANI may remain interpretable as a dynamic within-patient autonomic response in chronically β-blocked patients under these anesthetic conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10877-026-01461-9.

Introduction

Accurate assessment of intraoperative nociception is essential for optimal anesthetic management. However, conventional hemodynamic variables such as heart rate and mean arterial pressure are limited surrogate markers because they are influenced by anesthetic depth, vasoactive drugs, and autonomic tone [1–4].

The Analgesia–Nociception Index (ANI) is a heart rate variability (HRV)-derived parameter that reflects parasympathetic nervous system activity and is used to assess the nociception-antinociception balance. It provides a continuous numerical scale ranging from 0 to 100 [5]. ANI-guided analgesia has been associated with reduced intraoperative opioid consumption in some clinical studies and demonstrates a significant inverse correlation with postoperative pain intensity [6, 7]. Nevertheless, its overall clinical validity as a nociception monitor remains controversial because HRV-derived indices may be affected by factors unrelated to nociceptive stimuli, including medications that alter autonomic tone.

Among medications that modulate autonomic tone, β-blockers are relevant in the perioperative setting, as they are widely prescribed for hypertension and cardiovascular disease and are known to influence sympathovagal balance and modify HRV [8, 9]. Given that ANI reflects parasympathetic responses to nociceptive stimuli, β-blocker therapy may modify stimulus-evoked ANI dynamics. However, the impact of preoperative β-blocker use on intraoperative ANI dynamics has not been systematically evaluated.

Therefore, this prospective observational study aimed to examine the association between preoperative β-blocker use and intraoperative ANI dynamics during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. We hypothesized that chronic β-blocker therapy might be associated with altered stimulus-evoked ANI dynamics during surgery.

Materials and methods

This single-center prospective observational study was conducted between January 2023 and August 2025 after approval by the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Adult patients with hypertension who were aged 18–65 years and scheduled for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy under general anesthesia were enrolled in this study. Eligible patients were screened consecutively during the study period and classified according to preoperative β-blocker use. Recruitment was continued until a predefined target sample size was achieved for each group.

Patients were categorized into two groups: the β-blocker group comprising patients receiving β-blockers as part of their antihypertensive regimen; and the non-β-blocker group comprising patients not receiving β-blockers. Concomitant antihypertensive medications were recorded and categorized as calcium channel blockers (CCBs), renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi), or diuretics. Detailed distributions of concomitant antihypertensive medications and β-blocker subtypes are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Patients were excluded if they had cardiac arrhythmias, implanted pacemakers, chronic pain requiring ongoing treatment, or known autonomic dysfunction. In addition, patients were excluded from the final analysis if medications known to significantly alter autonomic tone were administered before completion of the predefined nociceptive stimulus protocol (T1–T4) or if complete ANI data could not be obtained during this observation period. All patients continued their routine antihypertensive medications, including β-blockers, on the day of surgery. Standard monitoring, including electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure measurement, pulse oximetry, and capnography, was performed before anesthetic induction.

Anesthesia was induced using target-controlled infusions (TCI; Orchestra Base Primea, Fresenius Kabi, France) of propofol (effect-site concentration [Ce] 4.0 µg/mL) and remifentanil (Ce 3.0 ng/mL). Rocuronium (0.8–1.0 mg/kg of ideal body weight) was administered to facilitate tracheal intubation, which was performed using a video laryngoscope (GlideScope™, Verathon Inc., USA) once adequate neuromuscular blockade was achieved (train-of-four count of zero).

Following induction, the propofol effect-site concentration was reduced to approximately 2.5 µg/mL, and remifentanil was adjusted to approximately 2.0 ng/mL. Both agents were titrated to maintain a bispectral index (BIS) of 40–60. Increases in heart rate or mean arterial pressure exceeding 20% of the baseline were managed by stepwise increases in remifentanil, whereas decreases exceeding 20% were treated according to the predefined protocol. During the predefined ANI assessment periods following each nociceptive stimulus, anesthetic management generally followed the standardized study protocol, and major anesthetic adjustments were avoided whenever clinically feasible. Patients requiring vasoactive agents were excluded from the final analysis. Neuromuscular transmission was monitored, end-tidal CO₂ was maintained at 35–40 mmHg, and normothermia was maintained throughout the study period. Remifentanil effect-site concentrations were generally maintained during the predefined 2-minute ANI assessment window following each stimulus, unless acute hemodynamic instability required immediate clinical intervention.

Analgesia Nociception Index (ANI) was continuously monitored using a commercial device (ANI® Monitor, MDoloris Medical Systems, Lille, France). Electrocardiographic electrodes were positioned on the upper thorax according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and the instantaneous (short-term averaged) ANI value was used for the analysis. ANI monitoring was performed using the device’s proprietary signal processing and artifact filtering system. Patients with incomplete ANI recordings during the predefined observation period were excluded from the final analysis. The experimental timeline and sequence of the nociceptive stimuli are illustrated in Fig. 1. Baseline ANI (T0) was defined as the lowest ANI value measured over 2 min immediately before anesthetic induction, while the patients were awake and breathing spontaneously. During anesthesia, four standardized nociceptive stimuli were applied at predefined time points under stable anesthetic depth (BIS 40–60) and hemodynamic conditions: tetanic stimulation (T1; 70 mA, 50 Hz, 5 s), laryngoscopic insertion (T2), skin incision (T3), and initiation of carbon dioxide insufflation (T4).

For each predefined time point (T1–T4), the ANI value at the stimulus onset was defined as the pre-stimulus reference (ANI_pre), and the lowest ANI value observed within the subsequent 2-min window (ANI_min) was used to represent the peak autonomic response to nociceptive stimulation. Hemodynamic parameters including heart rate and mean arterial pressure were recorded concurrently at each time point.

The primary outcome was the inter-group difference in ANI_min in response to standardized nociceptive stimuli across predefined time points (T1–T4). This outcome was evaluated using repeated-measures analysis of variance. ANI_min was defined as the lowest ANI value observed within 2 min following stimulus onset. ΔANI was defined as the difference between the ANI value at stimulus onset (ANI_pre) and the corresponding ANI_min at each time point (ΔANI = ANI_pre − ANI_min).

The sample size estimation was based on preliminary pilot data obtained at T3 (skin incision), which was selected for power calculation because it represents a consistent and clinically relevant surgical nociceptive stimulus under stable anesthetic conditions. To achieve 90% power with a two-sided α of 0.05, 21 patients per group were required. Therefore, a total sample size of 50 patients was used to account for potential dropouts.

Time-dependent changes in ANI_min, heart rate, and mean arterial pressure were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), with time (T1–T4) as the intra-subject factor and β-blocker use as the inter-subject factor. The Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated (Mauchly’s test).

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Inter-group comparisons were performed using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Fifty-two patients receiving antihypertensive therapy were screened for eligibility. Two patients were excluded before enrollment due to incomplete antihypertensive medication records. The remaining 50 patients were enrolled and classified according to preoperative β-blocker use, with 25 patients in the β-blocker and 25 in the non-β-blocker groups. One patient in the β-blocker group was excluded from the final analysis because of intraoperative ephedrine administration, which may affect the autonomic tone. Consequently, 49 patients were included in the final analysis (β-blocker group, n = 24; non-β-blocker group, n = 25). The enrollment and analysis flows are illustrated in Fig. 2.

The baseline demographic characteristics of the β-blocker and non-β-blocker groups were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). A higher proportion of patients in the β-blocker group had an ASA physical status of ≥ III than those in the non-β-blocker group (66.7% vs. 36.0%, P = 0.032), reflecting a greater burden of cardiovascular comorbidity. Other baseline variables and the distribution of concomitant antihypertensive medications, including calcium channel blockers, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors, and diuretics, were similar for the groups. The detailed drug combinations and subtype distributions are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–3. In the β-blocker group, bisoprolol (50.0%) and nebivolol (29.2%) were the most commonly used agents, whereas carvedilol accounted for 20.8% of patients.

The baseline ANI values were comparable between the two groups (Table 2). Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of time, indicating a consistent decrease in ANI in response to standardized nociceptive stimuli. However, no significant time × group interaction or main effect of group was observed for ΔANI, suggesting that the magnitude of ANI reduction did not differ between the groups.

Repeated-measures ANOVA also showed a significant effect of time on ANI_min, which was the primary outcome (P < 0.001). However, no significant time × group interaction or main effect of group was observed, indicating that temporal patterns of ANI changes were comparable across the groups regardless of β-blocker use (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, ANI consistently decreased in response to standardized nociceptive stimuli during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, with comparable patterns and magnitudes between patients receiving and not receiving preoperative β-blockers. These findings suggest that chronic β-blocker therapy was not associated with substantially altered intraoperative ANI dynamics. The decreases in ANI observed after nociceptive stimulation suggest that autonomic responses to surgical stimuli remained detectable under the propofol–remifentanil anesthetic protocol used in this study. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence that stimulus-related ANI dynamics remained measurable as patient-specific autonomic responses in chronically β-blocked patients, rather than as a direct indicator of the adequacy or inadequacy of antinociceptive management.

The clinical implications of these findings should nonetheless be interpreted with caution. ANI has been proposed as an adjunct for nociception monitoring and opioid titration [10–12], but its overall utility remains controversial, partly because HRV-derived indices may be influenced by factors unrelated to nociceptive stimulation [7, 13]. β-blockers have been considered a potential confounder because of their effects on sympathetic activity and HRV [9]. In this context, our findings suggest that chronic β-blocker therapy may not substantially impair the interpretability of stimulus-related ANI changes under the specific anesthetic conditions used in this study.

The comparable ANI dynamics observed in β-blocked patients are consistent with the physiological basis of this index. ANI is derived from high-frequency HRV and reflects dynamic parasympathetic modulation in response to acute nociceptive stimuli, rather than baseline sympathetic tone [6]. Because β-blockers primarily attenuate sympathetic activity without directly suppressing parasympathetic function [14], it is physiologically plausible that stimulus-related ANI dynamics remained observable despite chronic β-blockade. Perioperative hemodynamic responses, including heart rate and mean arterial pressure, also showed comparable temporal patterns between groups, consistent with the ANI findings and the physiological rationale outlined above. In addition, most patients in the β-blocker group received relatively β1-selective agents, which may have contributed to the stimulus-related ANI dynamics observed in this cohort.

In the present study, the magnitude of ANI reduction varied across the predefined nociceptive events, with relatively greater decreases observed during surgical stimuli such as skin incision compared with tetanic stimulation. This variability suggests that stimulus context influences ANI dynamics, and that within-patient changes may be more informative than comparisons against fixed absolute thresholds [7, 15, 16].

Chronic preoperative β-blocker therapy differs fundamentally from active intraoperative sympatholytic or stress-modulating anesthetic management, and the present findings should not be extrapolated to settings in which β-blockers are titrated intraoperatively or combined with multimodal techniques such as dexmedetomidine or regional anesthesia.

This study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center observational study without randomization; therefore, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be excluded. The higher ASA physical status in the β-blocker group and the exclusion of patients requiring vasoactive agents may limit generalizability, particularly to more hemodynamically unstable patients. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and the sample size calculation was based on a single time-point comparison, whereas the primary analysis evaluated temporal ANI dynamics across multiple nociceptive events. Therefore, the study may have been underpowered to detect subtle differences in ANI responses between groups. Third, detailed data on remifentanil exposure and β-blocker therapy, including exact remifentanil concentrations at each stimulus point, total remifentanil consumption, β-blocker dose, treatment duration, timing of the last dose, and resting heart rate, were not prospectively collected. Finally, ANI was assessed without comparison with other nociception monitoring modalities or postoperative clinical outcomes. Because all patients received remifentanil-based anesthesia, these findings may not be generalizable to opioid-free or enhanced sympatholytic anesthetic strategies. Future studies comparing opioid-based anesthesia with opioid-free or enhanced sympatholytic anesthetic strategies may help clarify whether β-blocker therapy influences ANI dynamics under different antinociceptive conditions.

In conclusion, preoperative β-blocker therapy did not substantially alter stimulus-related ANI dynamics during laparoscopic cholecystectomy under propofol–remifentanil anesthesia. This suggests that ANI may remain interpretable as a dynamic within-patient autonomic response in chronically β-blocked patients.
