Risk of falls, recurrent falls and osteoporotic fractures in SLE patients with comorbid fibromyalgia: a propensity score-matched cohort study using the TriNetX Network
Authors: Wan-Yun Hsu, Li-Yun Tsai, Ching-Yi Lai, Jun-Peng Chen, Chung-Mao Kao, Yen-Ju Chen, I-Chieh Chen, Yi-Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFibromyalgia (FM) frequently co-occurs with SLE, but its independent contribution to musculoskeletal risk is unclear. We aimed to determine whether comorbid FM is independently associated with the risk of falls, recurrent falls, thoracolumbar vertebral fractures and osteoporotic fractures in patients with SLE.MethodsWe conducted a propensity score-matched (PSM) retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network. Adult SLE patients with comorbid FM (SLE+FM) were matched 1:1 to SLE patients without FM (SLE-only) on demographics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters and 19 medications. A 6 month washout period preceded outcome ascertainment to ensure capture of incident events only. Time-to-event analyses over 5 years for risk of falls, recurrent falls, thoracolumbar vertebral fractures and osteoporotic fractures used Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsAfter 1:1 PSM, 23 775 matched pairs were retained with substantially improved covariate balance. At 5 years, SLE+FM patients had significantly elevated risks of incident falls (adjusted HR (aHR) 1.38, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.48), recurrent falls (aHR 1.53, 95% CI 1.32 to 1.76), thoracolumbar vertebral fractures (aHR 1.18, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.37) and osteoporotic fractures (aHR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.27) compared with the SLE-only group. Associations were directionally consistent at 3 years and across all prespecified subgroups. Kaplan-Meier curves showed early and progressive divergence between cohorts from the start of follow-up.ConclusionComorbid FM is significantly and independently associated with increased fall and fracture risk in SLE. These findings support the incorporation of FM evaluation into fall risk assessment, alongside multidisciplinary bone-protective and fall-prevention strategies, in SLE patients with comorbid FM.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Fibromyalgia, Osteoporosis

Introduction

SLE is a prototypic systemic autoimmune disease characterised by dysregulated immune activation, widespread organ inflammation and progressive cumulative damage. With a global prevalence of approximately 20–150 per 100 000 individuals and a pronounced predilection for women of reproductive age, SLE imposes a disproportionate burden of morbidity and premature mortality.1 Among the most clinically consequential complications of SLE is accelerated skeletal fragility: chronic interferon-α-driven inflammation modulates osteoclastogenesis via the RANKL/OPG axis2; active lupus nephritis and sun avoidance impair vitamin D synthesis3 and long-term glucocorticoid therapy suppresses osteoblast differentiation and accelerates bone loss.4 Consequently, SLE patients face substantially elevated risks of osteoporosis and fragility fractures relative to the general population.5

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic, non-inflammatory pain syndrome defined by central sensitisation, widespread musculoskeletal pain, non-restorative sleep, fatigue and cognitive disturbance.6 Its co-occurrence with SLE is well documented, with prevalence estimates of 15%–30%, substantially exceeding that in age-matched and sex-matched general population cohorts.7 8 In the SLE context, FM may amplify perceived disease activity, confound therapeutic decision-making by mimicking inflammatory flares and independently modify health outcomes beyond what is attributable to autoimmune disease activity alone.9

FM introduces several mechanistically distinct fall-risk determinants. At the neurophysiological level, central sensitisation, driven by synaptic long-term potentiation in spinal dorsal horn circuits and dysregulation of descending serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways, extends beyond nociception to impair sensorimotor systems governing balance and locomotion.10 Functional neuroimaging demonstrates aberrant connectivity within the sensorimotor cortex, cerebellum and default mode network in FM patients, resulting in deficits in proprioceptive acuity and the anticipatory postural adjustments essential for dynamic balance control.11 12 FM-associated musculoskeletal hyperalgesia impairs reactive neuromuscular responses, while pervasive fatigue attenuates skeletal muscle recruitment, leading to gait instability that is further compromised in SLE by inflammatory and glucocorticoid-induced myopathy.12 13 FM-targeted pharmacotherapy, including gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, carries sedative, orthostatic and psychomotor-impairing adverse effects that further increase fall propensity.14 Furthermore, pain catastrophising and kinesiophobia promote exercise avoidance, contributing to skeletal fragility through reduced mechanical loading of bone and progressive physical deconditioning.15 Despite the mechanistic plausibility of this relationship, no large-scale, rigorously confound-adjusted epidemiological investigation has quantified the incremental fall and fracture risk specifically attributable to FM comorbidity within an SLE population.

To address this evidence gap, we used the TriNetX US Collaborative Network to conduct a PSM retrospective cohort study. We tested the hypothesis that comorbid FM is independently and significantly associated with the risk of falls, recurrent falls, thoracolumbar vertebral fractures and osteoporotic fractures in patients with SLE, after comprehensive adjustment for demographic, clinical, laboratory and pharmacological confounders.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using de-identified patient-level data from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network (TriNetX, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA), a federated electronic health record (EHR) platform aggregating longitudinal clinical data from geographically and demographically diverse healthcare organisations across the USA.16 TriNetX applies de-identification procedures compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Safe Harbor standard; accordingly, this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (IRB number CE24249A). Individual informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design and use of deidentified data. This study was reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data statement. The overall study design is illustrated in figure 1.

Study design schematic illustrating the overall study framework, including cohort definition, baseline window, 6-month washout period and 5-year follow-up for outcome ascertainment.

Study population

We identified all adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with a documented SLE diagnosis, defined by at least two recorded International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10-Clinical Modification (CM) codes for M32 on separate encounters between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2024; requiring two or more codes on separate encounters.17 As TriNetX harmonises historical records to a common ontology, diagnoses recorded before the October 2015 US transition to ICD-10-CM were captured through ICD-9-CM-to-ICD-10-CM mapping. Fibromyalgia was identified using ICD-10-CM code M79.7, consistent with the expert-consensus code set validated against medical-record text for chronic overlapping pain conditions.18 All patients were required to have a minimum of 12 months of longitudinal EHR data prior to the index date to enable ascertainment of baseline covariates.

Three incident outcomes were ascertained during follow-up using prespecified ICD-10-CM code sets: falls (W00–W19)19; thoracolumbar vertebral fractures (M48.40–M48.58, S22.000–S22.089, S32.000–S32.099) and fractures at osteoporosis-related skeletal sites (M80, S12, S42, S52, S62, S72, S82, S92).20 These code sets are consistent with previously validated claims-based fall-identification and fracture-identification algorithms.19 20 Patients with any of these diagnoses during the 12-month pre-index period were excluded, and only the first qualifying event after the index date was counted, to restrict analyses to incident events. The exposed cohort (SLE+FM) comprised SLE patients who additionally had at least one recorded ICD-10-CM code for fibromyalgia (M79.7) at or prior to the index date (n=24 359), with the index date defined as the date of the first qualifying FM diagnosis code. The unexposed comparator cohort (SLE-only) comprised SLE patients with no recorded FM diagnosis at any time in the database (n=1 61 790), with the index date defined as the date of the first qualifying SLE diagnosis code. In both cohorts, a baseline window of 1 day–1 year prior to the index date was used to ascertain characteristics for PSM, followed by a 6-month washout period after the index date before outcome ascertainment commenced. Patients were then followed for up to 5 years. The complete patient selection process is illustrated in figure 2.

Patient selection flowchart. A total of 321 905 adult SLE patients were identified from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2024. After application of inclusion and exclusion criteria and 1:1 PSM, 23 775 matched pairs were retained for analysis.

Propensity score matching

Propensity scores were estimated via multivariable logistic regression with FM comorbidity as the binary dependent variable. Covariates comprised: age at index date (continuous), sex, race (white, black or African American, Asian, other/unknown), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino; not Hispanic or Latino; unknown), tobacco use disorder, alcohol-related disorders, 10 major comorbidities (hypertensive diseases, hyperlipidaemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, neoplasms, ischaemic heart diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, congestive heart failure, obesity, liver disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), five SLE-relevant laboratory parameters (anti-double-stranded DNA antibody, complement C3, complement C4, estimated glomerular filtration rate and serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D) and 19 medications recorded within the 12-month pre-index look-back period (hydroxychloroquine, prednisone, gabapentin, duloxetine, methotrexate, pregabalin, mycophenolate mofetil, amitriptyline, azathioprine, belimumab, tacrolimus, rituximab, milnacipran, anifrolumab, bisphosphonates, denosumab, teriparatide, calcium supplementation and vitamin D replacement). Anti-osteoporotic agents (bisphosphonates, denosumab, teriparatide, calcium and vitamin D) were included to account for their potential to modify fracture risk. Because these laboratory parameters were frequently incomplete in this real-world EHR dataset, they were not imputed; instead, each was entered into the propensity score model as a categorical variable comprising clinically relevant strata together with a distinct ‘missing’ category (the missing indicator method). One-to-one PSM was implemented using a greedy nearest-neighbour algorithm without replacement, with a calliper width of 0.1 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Adequacy of post-matching covariate balance was quantified by standardised mean differences (SMD), with SMD <0.1 considered indicative of acceptable balance.21 For brevity, table 1 presents the principal matched covariates (all SMD <0.1); the full 19-medication covariate balance for the landmark sensitivity-analysis cohort is provided in online supplemental table S1.

Outcome definitions

Four prespecified primary outcomes were evaluated: (1) incident falls, defined by ICD-10-CM codes W00–W19, which denote unintentional falls; this code range does not encode the precipitating mechanism of the fall; (2) recurrent falls, defined as two or more fall events recorded on distinct encounter dates during follow-up, consistent with the established clinical definition of a recurrent fall (≥two falls within a defined period) and with the threshold at which recurrent-fall risk-reduction interventions are typically initiated; a binary threshold was adopted because, within a claims-based dataset, a single fall may generate multiple encounters, rendering precise enumeration of higher-order fall frequency unreliable; (3) thoracolumbar vertebral fractures, defined by ICD-10-CM codes M48.40–M48.58, S22.000–S22.089 and S32.000–S32.099 and (4) osteoporotic fractures, operationalised as a composite identified using a previously validated claims-based fracture algorithm.20 The composite comprised the four classic major osteoporotic-fracture sites as conventionally defined20—vertebral (M48.4, M48.5; osteoporosis-specific vertebral codes, narrower than the thoracolumbar definition in outcome 3), hip/proximal femur (S72), distal forearm/radius (S52) and proximal humerus (S42)—together with osteoporosis-coded pathological fractures (M80) and additional axial and appendicular sites (S12, S62, S82, S92). Throughout, ‘osteoporotic fracture’ denotes this claims-based composite rather than radiographically adjudicated fragility fractures. Outcomes 3 and 4 partially overlap at the vertebral level and are therefore related rather than statistically independent; they are reported separately by design to distinguish the thoracolumbar vertebral burden from the broader osteoporotic-fracture composite. All outcomes were restricted to incident events occurring after the 6-month washout period and ascertained throughout the 5-year follow-up window. These outcomes were ascertained from recorded ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes and therefore reflect clinically diagnosed and coded events; morphometric vertebral fractures identified incidentally on imaging but not assigned a diagnosis code were not captured. Fracture outcomes at individual anatomical sites (hip, distal radius and proximal humerus) were additionally examined as exploratory secondary outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised as means±SDs for continuous variables and as frequencies with proportions for categorical variables; between-group differences were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests and χ2 tests, as appropriate. Time-to-first-event analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier estimator, with between-group comparisons conducted via two-sided log-rank tests. Follow-up time was calculated from the end of the 6-month washout period to the earliest of the following: (1) first occurrence of the outcome of interest, (2) death or (3) end of the 5-year observation window. Follow-up was bounded by the fixed 5-year administrative window. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate adjusted HRs (aHRs) and 95% CIs, with all PSM covariates included as adjustment variables. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of associations across strata defined by age, sex, race/ethnicity, glucocorticoid use and hydroxychloroquine use. All analyses were executed within the TriNetX analytics platform. A two-sided p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The four primary outcomes were prespecified. Analyses of individual fracture sites and of subgroups were exploratory and hypothesis-generating; no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, and these results should be interpreted with corresponding caution.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Prior to PSM, the SLE+FM cohort comprised 24 359 patients and the SLE-only cohort comprised 1 61 790 patients, with marked between-group differences across all covariate domains (table 1). The mean (±SD) follow-up was 4.1±1.3 years in the SLE+FM cohort and 3.9±1.3 years in the SLE-only cohort, corresponding to approximately 97 500 and 92 700 person-years of observation, respectively. Compared with the SLE-only cohort, SLE+FM patients were more likely to be female, of white race, and of non-Hispanic ethnicity, whereas mean age at index date was comparable between groups. Among comorbidities, SLE+FM patients had higher rates of hypertensive diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity.

Among patients with available laboratory data, recorded testing for complement and autoantibodies was more frequent in the SLE+FM cohort, which also showed a higher proportion of patients with documented vitamin D deficiency prior to matching.

With respect to SLE disease-modifying therapy, the SLE+FM cohort had consistently higher rates of treatment, including hydroxychloroquine, prednisone, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine and belimumab. Substantially higher proportions of SLE+FM patients were also receiving gabapentin, duloxetine and pregabalin, reflecting the pharmacological burden of FM management in this population.

After 1:1 PSM, 23 775 matched pairs were retained (figure 2). Covariate balance was substantially improved, with the majority of covariates achieving SMD<0.1 (table 1). Minor residual imbalances persisted for age and white race, considered unlikely to introduce meaningful residual confounding given the small magnitude of these differences.

Primary outcomes

Over the follow-up period, incident falls, recurrent falls, osteoporotic fractures and thoracolumbar vertebral fractures were all more common in SLE+FM than in SLE-only patients. In Cox proportional hazards regression, comorbid FM was independently associated with significantly elevated risks of incident falls (3-year aHR 1.37, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.48; 5-year aHR 1.38, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.48), recurrent falls (3-year aHR 1.48, 95% CI 1.24 to 1.76; 5-year aHR 1.53, 95% CI 1.32 to 1.76) and osteoporotic fractures (3-year aHR 1.17, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.33; 5-year aHR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.27) at both time points (figure 3). For thoracolumbar vertebral fractures, the association reached statistical significance at 5 years (aHR 1.18, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.37) but not at 3 years (aHR 1.17, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.40). The excess risk was particularly pronounced for recurrent falls, consistent with the compounding neuromotor, pharmacological and musculoskeletal effects of FM comorbidity.

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses at 5 years, stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, glucocorticoid use and hydroxychloroquine use, demonstrated that the increased risks of incident falls and recurrent falls associated with FM comorbidity were broadly consistent in direction across strata (online supplemental figures S1 and S2). Similarly, the elevated risks of osteoporotic fractures and thoracolumbar fractures showed a generally consistent direction of effect across subgroups, although CIs were wider in smaller strata, such as male patients and those aged ≥65 years (online supplemental figures S3 and S4). The broadly consistent associations across subgroups suggest that the adverse musculoskeletal risk conferred by FM comorbidity in SLE is not confined to any particular demographic or treatment subgroup and represents a generalisable phenomenon within this population.

Discussion

In this large, PSM cohort study of 23 775 matched pairs (47 550 patients) with SLE drawn from a real-world US EHR network, we show that comorbid FM is independently and significantly associated with increased risk of falls, recurrent falls, thoracolumbar vertebral fractures and osteoporotic fractures compared with matched SLE patients without FM. The magnitude of risk elevation was greatest for recurrent falls (5-year aHR 1.53), followed by incident falls (aHR 1.38), thoracolumbar vertebral fractures (aHR 1.18) and osteoporotic fractures (aHR 1.14). These associations were directionally consistent at both 3 years and 5 years and across all prespecified subgroup analyses, identifying FM as a clinically important and potentially modifiable musculoskeletal risk marker in SLE. To our knowledge, this represents the largest propensity score-adjusted analysis to quantify the incremental fall and fracture risk specifically attributable to FM comorbidity in an SLE population.

The pathobiological mechanisms underlying these findings are multifactorial. The central sensitisation that defines FM, characterised by amplified nociceptive signalling and maladaptive cortical and subcortical neuroplasticity,10 may extend beyond pain perception to impair the sensorimotor systems governing balance and locomotion. Functional neuroimaging has demonstrated aberrant connectivity between the sensorimotor cortex, cerebellum and default mode network in FM patients, disrupting feedforward motor commands for anticipatory postural stabilisation11; aberrant cerebellar network connectivity impairs the coordination of lower-limb muscle activation during gait perturbations, while somatosensory cortical reorganisation degrades the fidelity of proprioceptive afferent signals,12 jointly increasing postural sway, prolonging reactive stepping latency and reducing the safety margin for fall prevention.15 These neuromotor deficits are compounded by FM-associated pain catastrophising and kinesiophobia, which promote progressive activity avoidance, physical deconditioning and lower-extremity muscle weakness and sarcopenia,15 22 further degrading gait stability, postural control and the capacity to arrest a fall.12 Together, these neuromotor and behavioural pathways constitute a self-reinforcing route from FM to fracture that operates largely independently of bone mineral density, linking impaired postural control and progressive deconditioning to more frequent falls and, in turn, higher fracture risk. Reduced mechanical loading from physical inactivity may compound the skeletal fragility already present in SLE through RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis,2 vitamin D insufficiency3 and glucocorticoid-induced suppression of osteoblast-mediated bone formation.4 Because most low-trauma fractures result directly from a fall rather than from low bone density alone, these fall-promoting pathways provide a coherent, osteoporosis-independent route from FM comorbidity to the excess fracture risk observed. This interpretation is supported by the observed risk gradient, which was greatest for recurrent falls (aHR 1.53) and incident falls (aHR 1.38), outcomes that do not depend on bone density, and more modest for osteoporotic fracture (aHR 1.14), favouring a model in which comorbid FM is associated with higher fracture risk principally through more frequent falls rather than through an FM-specific reduction in bone mineral density (BMD). The predilection for axial (thoracolumbar vertebral) over appendicular fractures is consistent with the compressive loading patterns experienced during fall events in individuals with impaired postural control and with the known susceptibility of trabecular-rich vertebral bone to glucocorticoid-induced osteoblast suppression.23

The pharmacological burden of FM management represents a further clinically actionable dimension. In our pre-matching cohort, SLE+FM patients had substantially higher proportions receiving gabapentin (13.6% vs 4.3%), duloxetine (8.2% vs 1.6%), pregabalin (5.0% vs 1.1%) and amitriptyline (3.9% vs 1.0%), all of which are recognised independent pharmacological fall-risk factors.14 Gabapentinoids exert sedative and ataxic effects through voltage-gated calcium channel blockade24; tricyclic antidepressants impair cognition and promote orthostatic hypotension through anticholinergic mechanisms25; and SNRIs may contribute to fall risk through serotonin-mediated haemodynamic instability.25 Because exposure to these agents was balanced after matching (all SMD<0.1; table 1), the association we observed between FM comorbidity and fracture risk is unlikely to be explained by differential use of these fall-risk-increasing medications.

Our findings extend and contextualise the prior literature on musculoskeletal morbidity in both SLE and FM populations. Prior meta-analytic data have documented fracture rates in SLE cohorts 1.2-fold to 1.9-fold above age-matched and sex-matched general population controls.5 Studies in FM populations have documented impaired functional capacity, reduced muscle strength and an increased propensity for falls.15 22 Crucially, however, the present study uniquely isolates the independent contribution of FM comorbidity within an SLE population, so that the observed aHRs for falls and fractures are additive to the baseline musculoskeletal risk of SLE itself. The propensity score model likewise balanced bone-protective (anti-osteoporotic) agents between cohorts; we note, nonetheless, that these medication records capture prescription or administration rather than adherence or treatment duration, and that the overall prevalence of anti-osteoporotic therapy was low in this relatively young cohort (mean age approximately 49 years), so residual confounding from unmeasured treatment intensity cannot be entirely excluded. To address time-zero misalignment and immortal time bias, we performed a landmark sensitivity analysis indexing both cohorts at SLE diagnosis with comorbid FM as a baseline characteristic (8458 matched pairs; online supplemental figure S5); the association remained robust across all primary outcomes (online supplemental figure S6) and was modestly stronger than in the primary analysis (3-year falls: HR 1.68 vs 1.37). As the primary design required exposed patients to remain event-free until FM diagnosis, it likely biased estimates towards the null, and the concordance between designs indicates that our findings are not an artefact of time-zero misalignment.

Several clinical implications emerge directly from these findings. Because the excess risk we observed was driven predominantly by falls—which precede most low-trauma fractures—fall prevention is of central clinical importance in this group. First, FM is frequently under-recognised in SLE patients, as its symptoms may be misattributed to active lupus disease; fall-risk assessment in SLE patients should therefore include evaluation for FM.6 Second, structured multidimensional fall-risk assessment encompassing proprioceptive function, lower-extremity muscle strength and dynamic balance should be implemented proactively in SLE patients with FM comorbidity. Third, FM pharmacotherapy in patients with SLE should balance therapeutic benefit against iatrogenic fall risk. Fourth, future research should investigate the efficacy of evidence-based non-pharmacological interventions in SLE patients with comorbid FM.26

Limitations

Several limitations merit acknowledgement. As an observational analysis of routinely collected EHR data, this study can establish associations but cannot prove causality, and residual confounding from unmeasured confounders may contribute to the observed associations. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as robust associations warranting prospective confirmation.

Quantitative bone mineral density (eg, DXA T-scores), one of the strongest predictors of osteoporotic fracture, is not reliably recorded as a structured variable in TriNetX and could not be included. While we could not adjust for BMD directly, the propensity score model incorporated several correlates of bone health, including glucocorticoid exposure and use of bone-protective agents (bisphosphonates, denosumab, teriparatide, calcium and vitamin D), which were balanced between cohorts. In addition, the strongest associations in our study were observed for falls and recurrent falls. These outcomes do not depend on bone density, suggesting that the excess fracture risk is driven substantially by elevated fall frequency rather than by reduced BMD alone. Residual confounding from unmeasured BMD therefore cannot be excluded but is unlikely to fully account for the observed associations.

Likewise, several inputs to formal fracture-risk tools such as FRAX were unavailable. Parental hip fracture history was not available in the database; smoking and alcohol use were ascertained only as binary diagnoses (tobacco use and alcohol-related disorders) rather than as quantified exposure intensity. In addition, although prednisone use was balanced between cohorts through propensity score matching, cumulative glucocorticoid dose and duration were not captured.27 Menopausal status and premature ovarian insufficiency were also not ascertained. Frailty, physical activity, disease activity were likewise not available as structured variables. These unmeasured or coarsely measured risk factors may confer residual confounding of the fracture-risk estimates.

Notably, patients with any recorded fracture or fall at the relevant skeletal sites during the 12-month pre-index window were excluded to ensure incident only outcome capture. Therefore, fragility fractures and falls preceding this window could not be ascertained. A history of falls is itself a well-established independent predictor of fracture with risk rising progressively as the number of prior falls increases.28 29 Due to the study design, we were unable to characterise prior fall history, to stratify patients by fall burden or to resolve higher-order fall frequency (eg, three or more falls).

All phenotypes were defined using ICD-10-CM administrative codes rather than direct clinical adjudication. Although these definitions are supported by external validation studies,17–20 the algorithms have not been validated within TriNetX. Therefore, the true positive predictive value and sensitivity of case ascertainment in the present cohort remain unknown. Because administrative coding does not capture injury mechanism, low-trauma fragility fractures cannot be reliably distinguished from high energy events. Falls were identified using ICD-10-CM external-cause-of-injury codes W00–W19, which capture unintentional falls but do not record the precipitating mechanism. Consequently, falls could not be subclassified as accidental, syncope-related, neurological or medication-related, and we were unable to examine whether fracture risk differed across these mechanisms. Because these mechanisms may confer different fracture risks, this lack of granularity represents a limitation. Meanwhile, fracture outcomes were likewise ascertained from diagnosis codes, so asymptomatic morphometric vertebral fractures detected only on imaging would be under captured.

With respect to pharmacological confounding, opioids and other sedative-hypnotics (eg, benzodiazepines), recognised fall-risk-increasing medications, they were not incorporated into the matching model; therefore, residual unmeasured effect could be expected.

Finally, as TriNetX comprises predominantly US healthcare organisations, the generalisability of these findings to other healthcare systems and populations may be limited.

Conclusion

This large-scale, propensity score-matched cohort analysis demonstrates that comorbid fibromyalgia is independently associated with higher risks of falls, recurrent falls, thoracolumbar vertebral fractures and osteoporotic fractures in patients with SLE. Fall risk assessment in SLE patients should incorporate FM evaluation alongside multidisciplinary bone-protective and fall-prevention strategies to reduce fracture-related morbidity in this population.
