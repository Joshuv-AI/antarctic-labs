A novel therapeutic monoclonal antibody targeting PZR demonstrates potent anti-metastatic efficacy in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Jiayu Chen, Jiayi Shao, Ju Huang, Lingxiao Ye, Shuya He, Yaqian Qin, Zhujun Tian, Xiaodong Liu, Licai He, Jiawei Cao, Lan Li, Haihua Gu, Guang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetastasis is the leading cause of mortality in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). PZR, a member of the immunoglobulin super-family, promotes the migration, invasion, and metastasis of cancer cells. Whether targeting PZR can inhibit TNBC dissemination remains unclear. In this study, we developed a novel anti-PZR monoclonal antibody (mAb) and humanized it to explore its impact on TNBC metastasis.MethodsRecombinant PZR-ECD protein was used to generate anti-PZR monoclonal antibodies, whose binding affinity was evaluated by SPR. Their effects on cell migration were assessed via Trans-well assays. Antibody specificity was confirmed by western blotting, Co-IP, and FACS. PZR mediated TNBC cell interactions with the ECM were examined through cell adhesion experiments. The therapeutic efficacy of the PZR antibody was evaluated in a TNBC metastasis mouse model.ResultsOur findings revealed that the mouse anti-PZR mAb (12F6) exhibited high-affinity binding to the recombinant PZR protein. The 12F6 mAb specifically recognized the native PZR protein expressed by TNBC cells and was efficiently internalized by PZR-positive TNBC cells. Furthermore, 12F6 significantly inhibited the migration and metastasis of PZR-positive TNBC cells in both in vitro and in vivo settings. PZR was found to interact with integrin β1, promoting the fibronectin-dependent adhesion and FAK activation of TNBC cells. The humanized 12F6 antibody (Hu12F6) maintained comparable binding affinity to PZR in comparison with the parental 12F6 antibody and effectively inhibited the migration and metastasis of PZR-positive TNBC cells in both in vitro and in vivo experiments.ConclusionsThe results of our study support a model by which PZR interacting with integrin β1 enhances fibronectin-dependent integrin signaling and biological functions. Hu12F6 by blocking the interaction between PZR and integrin β1 may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to treat PZR-positive metastatic cancers including TNBC.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08469-4.Keywords: PZR, Monoclonal antibody, Blockade, Metastasis, TNBC

Background

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide [1]. Among its various subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is particularly aggressive. TNBC is defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 amplification, accounting for approximately 15% of all breast cancer cases [2–4]. Compared to luminal subtypes, both HER2-positive and TNBC tumors exhibit significantly lower survival rates within the first 4–5 years post-diagnosis [5]. TNBC is further distinguished by its high histologic grade, elevated proliferative indices, and early propensity for dissemination. The lungs and liver are the primary sites of distant relapse, which drives its disproportionately high mortality [6].

In recent decades, the development of endocrine and HER2-targeted therapies has significantly improved outcomes for other breast cancer subtypes [7]. Aromatase inhibitors (such as anastrozole and exemestane) and selective estrogen-receptor modulators (such as tamoxifen) form the cornerstone of endocrine therapies for luminal breast cancer [8]. Meanwhile, the combination of trastuzumab or lapatinib with chemotherapy has become the standard of care for metastatic HER2-positive disease, significantly extending median overall survival [9]. In contrast, TNBC still lacks specific molecular targets for treatment, with cytotoxic chemotherapy remaining the primary treatment option that is associated with considerable toxicity, short response durations, and high rates of relapse with distant metastases, making TNBC the subtype with the greatest unmet medical need [10]. Thus, identifying novel oncogenic drivers and developing selective, low-toxicity targeted therapies are urgent priorities.

PZR (Protein zero-related, also known as MPZL1) is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein encoded by MPZL1 located on chromosome 1q24.1–24.2. Its structure includes a signal peptide, an immunoglobulin V-like extracellular domain, a single transmembrane segment, and a cytoplasmic tail containing two immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motifs (ITIMs) [11]. PZR is widely expressed in various human tissues, with particularly high mRNA levels in the placenta, kidney, and pancreas [12]. Two individual PZR SNPs have been linked to schizophrenia [13]. Hypertyrosyl phosphorylation of PZR may mediate the pathophysiological effects of PTPN11 mutations in Noonan syndrome [14] and LEOPARD syndrome [15]. Emerging evidence reveals that PZR acts as a pro-metastatic factor in solid tumors. In hepatocellular carcinoma, the level of PZR expression correlates with advanced TNM stage and portal vein tumor thrombus. PZR via Src promotes cancer cell migration through activation of Src and tyrosyl phosphorylation of cortactin [16]. In ovarian cancer, PZR activates the Src-p130-cortactin axis, accelerating proliferation and invasion [17]. In colorectal cancer, PZR expression increases with pathologic stage and is further up-regulated in liver metastases [18]. These findings highlight the critical role of PZR in promoting metastasis across multiple cancer types.

Metastasis is a complex, multistep process that includes local invasion, intravasation, survival in circulation, extravasation, and eventual colonization, coupled with microenvironmental remodeling at distant organs [19]. Cadherins, integrins, CD44, and immunoglobulin-superfamily receptors mediate the dynamic tumor-cell interactions with the extracellular matrix (ECM), facilitating both the initiation of dissemination and site-specific homing of cancer cells [20, 21]. Integrins consist of non-covalent α and β heterodimers, are the primary sensors of ECM components [22]. Among them, β1-integrin is widely expressed and regulates cell motility, adhesion, proliferation, and survival through FAK, Src, and PI3K/MAPK signaling [23]. However, the context-dependent pleiotropy of integrins has hindered the clinical translation of direct integrin antagonists. Whether PZR via interaction with integrins participates in the multistep process of cancer metastasis remains unclear. Nevertheless, its recurrent pro-metastatic phenotype and accessible cell-surface localization make PZR an attractive target for monoclonal antibody or antibody-drug conjugate therapy in TNBC.

The xenoprotein nature of murine monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) can trigger the production of human anti-mouse antibodies (HAMA), which not only weakens the therapeutic efficacy but also increases the risk of allergic reactions [24]. Additionally, the short half-life of murine mAbs limits their clinical application [24]. To address these issues, humanized antibodies have been developed by grafting the murine complementary-determining regions (CDRs) into human frameworks, reducing the proportion of murine sequences to approximately 3% [25]. This approach maintains the affinity while significantly decreasing immunogenicity [25]. Furthermore, transgenic mouse technology enables the direct production of fully human mAbs with 100% human sequences, achieving simultaneous optimization of structural stability and antigen recognition [24]. Therefore, minimizing the xenogenicity of murine components has become a crucial aspect in the development of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies

In this study, we generated a high-affinity monoclonal antibody targeting the extracellular IgV domain of human PZR and subsequently humanized the antibody. We demonstrated that PZR interacts with β1-integrin and enhances fibronectin-dependent cell adhesion of TNBC cells. The anti-PZR antibody disrupts this interaction, and significantly suppresses metastatic outgrowth in vivo. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for PZR-targeted therapy in aggressive TNBC and highlight the potential of PZR as a therapeutic target for improving outcomes in this challenging subtype.

Methods

Cell culture

Breast cancer cell lines and HEK293T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Maryland, USA), except for the Sum149PT and Sum159PT cell lines, which were obtained from Asterand Bioscience (Detroit, Michigan, USA). All cell lines were subjected to comprehensive authentication, including morphological assessment, post-thaw viability determination, species-specific isoenzyme profiling, karyotyping, and screening for mycoplasma. MDA-MB-468 and Hs578T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a humnidified incubator with 7.5% CO2. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 0.84 µg/mL human recombinant insulin (Cat.I8830, Solarbio, Beijing, China), under the same conditions as above (37 °C, humidified 7.5% CO2). Sum149PT and Sum159PT cells were grown in DMEM/F-12 medium (Cat.C11330500, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 5 µg/mL human recombinant insulin, and 1 µg/mL hydrocortisone (Cat.G8450, Solarbio), at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified environment.

Construction of gene knockout and overexpression cell lines

The production of virus particles was generated as described [26–28]. Briefly, HEK-293T/17 cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 106 cells per 10-cm dish and co-transfected with packaging and transfer plasmids. After incubation for 48 h at 37 °C in 7.5% CO2, the culture medium was collected, cleared of debris by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

For CRISPR-mediated PZR knockout, a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the sequence 5’-CACCGGCGCTGGCTGTGGTCGGTGC-3’ was annealed and ligated into the BsmBI site of the LentiCRISPR v2-GFP-Puro vector. The resultant plasmid was transformed into E. coli Stbl3, and positive clones were validated by Sanger sequencing. Lentiviral supernatant containing the sgRNA construct was used to infect target cells. Infected cells were treated with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Cat. 631,626, TaKaRa, Dalian, China) 72 h post-infection for 7 days. Single-cell cloning was performed by limiting dilution (0.5 cell/well in 96-well plates) and clones were screened for PZR loss via western blot analysis.

For generate PZR-overexpressing cell lines, pBabe-puro-PZR retroviral particles encoding human PZR were produced following the aforementioned protocol. THP-1 cells were seeded at 2 × 105/well in 6-well plates, infected 24 h later with viral particles harboring either empty vector or pBabe-puro-PZR, and selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin 24 h post-infection for 7 days. Polyclonal populations were then expanded into 10-cm culture dishes, and successful PZR overexpression was confirmed by western blot analysis prior to functional assays.

For integrin β1 overexpression, HEK293 cells were transiently transfected with pcDNA3.0-integrin β1 plasmid complexed with polyethyleneimine (PEI) at an optimized ratio. After 16 hours, the transfection medium was aspirated and replaced with 8 mL of fresh complete medium. Cells were cultured for an additional 48 hours, after which cell lysates were harvested and integrin β1 protein expression was assessed by western blotting to evaluate transfection efficiency.

To generate PZR-EC127 mutant-Flag fusion protein-expressing cell lines, the PZR-EC127 cDNA was first subcloned into the pcDNA3.0 eukaryotic expression vector, with a Flag tag fused to its C-terminus, generating the PZR-EC127-WT-Flag plasmid. Site-directed mutagenesis was then employed to introduce single or double amino acid substitutions (e.g., XX→AA), yielding the PZR-EC127-XX/AA-Flag mutant plasmid. These recombinant vectors were transiently transfected into HEK-293T/17 cells via PEI, yielding a cellular model transiently expressing wild-type or mutant PZR-EC127-Flag fusion proteins.

Generation of the mouse anti-PZR monoclonal antibody

To generate anti-PZR monoclonal antibodies, hybridoma technology was employed as previously described [29, 30]. The experimental procedure is outlined as follow: Splenocytes were isolated from mice exhibiting high anti-PZR IgG serum titers and subsequently fused with SP2/0 mouse myeloma cells at an optimized ratio. The resulting hybridomas were initialy cultured in hypoxanthine-aminopterin-thymidine (HAT, Cat.H0262, Sigma) selection medium for,14 days to eliminate unfused parental cells. Viable hybridomas were then progressively expanded in hypoxanthine-thymidine (HT, Cat.H0137, Sigma-Aldrich) medium. PZR-specific IgGs secretion in the culture supernatants was assessed by ELISA to identify and select positive monoclonal hybridoma clones. For ascites production, BALB/c mice were primed via intraperitoneal injection of pristane (Cat.2870, Sigma-Aldrich). Seven days post-priming, mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with 1 × 106 hybridoma cells per mouse. Ascitic fluid was harvested on day 15 post-inoculation and clarified by centrifugation at 2500×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was subsequently purified using Protein-A affinity chromatography (Repligen, Massachusetts, USA) to isolate anti-PZR monoclonal antibodies.

Humanization of the mouse anti-PZR antibody

Human germline frameworks with the highest homology to the parental mouse antibody sequences were selected using the IgBLAST tool (NCBI). The mouse CDR sequences were then grafted onto these human frameworks, and key residues were back-mutated to maintain CDR conformation. The engineered humanized VH and VL sequences were inserted into pcDNA3.4-based vector respectively, co-transfected into CHO cells, and cultured for 6 days before the culture supernatant being collected. The humanized anti-PZR mAb was purified using Protein-A (Repligen, Massachusetts, USA) affinity chromatography and subsequently verified by SDS-PAGE and SPR, as previously described [31, 32].

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

Binding affinities of the mouse anti-PZR monoclonal antibody and the humanized PZR monoclonal antibody (Hu12F6 clone) toward recombinant PZR-ECD-127 protein were determined by SPR analysis. All SPR experiments were conducted on a Biacore X100 instrument (GE Healthcare, Illinois, USA) at 25 °C using CM5 sensor chips (Cytiva, NY, USA). Recombinant PZR-ECD-127 protein was covalently immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip via amine-coupling, achieving a surface density of 150 RU, and antibodies were injected in a concentration series. Data were fitted with a 1:1 binding model using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 for statistical analysis.

Western blot and immunoprecipitation analysis

Cell lysates were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBS-T at RT for 1 h and incubated with PZR Rabbit mAb (1:1000, Cat.9893, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA), Integrin β1 Rabbit mAb (1:1000, Cat.4706, Cell Signaling Technology), or anti-β-actin antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with TBS-T, membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:5000, Cat.711–035-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (1:5000, Cat.715–035-150, Jackson ImmunoResearch) at RT for 1 h and developed using ECL reagent. For immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed in buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 50 mM NaF, 2 mM NaVO3, and protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, Texas, USA). Centrifugation clarified cell lysates were incubated with anti-PZR monoclonal antibody at 4 °C overnight. Protein A magnetic beads (Repligen, Massachusetts, USA) were added to capture the immune complexes, which were subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting with the PZR Rabbit mAb.

Flow cytometry (FACS)

Cancer cells (3 × 105 cell) were blocked with PBS containing 3% BSA, and incubated with anti-PZR monoclonal antibody at 4 °C for 1 h. After three washes with PBS containing 1% BSA, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor-488 conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:300, Cat.A32723, Jackson ImmunoResearch) at 4 °C for 30 min. Samples were acquired on a Flow cytometer (Accuri C6, BD) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.6.2 software. Live cells were gated based on FSC-A/SSC-A to exclude debris and doublets, and the percentage of FITC-positive cells was presented.

Cell migration assay

MDA-MB-231 cells (3 × 104) were resuspended in DMEM containing 1% FBS and preincubated with anti-PZR monoclonal antibody (20 μg/mL) at RT for 20 min. The mixture was transferred into the upper chamber of Trans-well inserts (8-μm pore) (Cat.3422, Corning, NY, USA). The lower chamber was filled with 500 μL DMEM containing 20% FBS as chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with crystal violet for 2 h. Non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Images of six random fields per insert were captured under a 100× bright-field microscope, and counted for quantification.

Cell adhesion assay

Culture plates (96-well) were pre-coated with human fibronectin (Cat.F2006, Sigma) (0.5 μg/cm2) diluted in PBS for 45 min at RT. MDA-MB-231-GFP cells (2 × 104) were pre-incubated with anti-PZR monoclonal antibody (10 μg/mL) for 20 min at RT, then seeded into the coated wells and allowed to adhere for 30 min at 37 °C. After gentle washes with PBS to remove non-adherent cells, the adherent GFP-positive cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell surface staining and internalization assay

Cells were fixed in 4% PFA on ice for 15 min, washed with PBS, and blocked with PBS containing 3% BSA on ice for 30 min. Surface staining was conducted by incubating the cells with mouse anti-PZR mAb (clone 12F6) on ice for 2 h, followed by a 1 h incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Cat.A32723, Jackson ImmunoResearch). Subsequently, the samples were imaged using a laser-scanning confocal microscope. For internalization assays, Dylight-12F6-488 antibody was added to the cells at 37 °C for the specified time intervals. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA at RT for 15 min, and intracellular fluorescence was visualized via confocal microscopy.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Paraffin-embeded tissue sections of human breast cancer (Cat.BRC965) and normal tissues (Cat.MNO381) were purchased from Pantomics Inc (Fairfield, California, USA). The tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene for 45 min and rehydrated with graded ethanol. They underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer using a pressure cocker for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. The sections were incubated with primary anti-PZR antibody (Cat.A13059, Abclonal, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C overnight, followed by treatment with anti-rabbit secondary antibody at RT 30 min. Immunoreactivity was detected using DAB chromogen for 40 s. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min. The slides were mounted with neutral mounting balsam and photographed under a light microscope.

TNBC metastasis model

The construction of the TNBC cell mouse lung metastasis model was performed as previously described [26]. MDA-MB-231-Luciferase cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse) were injected into the tail vein of 5-week-old female NOD/SCID mice (Charles River Laboratories, Beijing, China). 24 h later, antibodies were administered via tail-vein injection at a dose of 10 mg/kg, twice weekly. Metastatic progression was monitored by bioluminescence imaging every seven days using the Lumina Series III IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer, MA, USA). At the end of the study, metastatic foci were evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Five-week-old female NOD/SCID mice (Charles River Laboratories) received a single intravenous dose of Hu12F6 at 200 μg per mouse (~10 mg/kg) via tail vein injection. Blood samples (50–100 μL) were collected from the retro-orbital sinus at designated time points: 6, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 240 and 288 h post-dose (n = 4 mice per time point). Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Serum concentrations of Hu12F6 were determined by sandwich ELISA. Briefly, 96-well immunoplates were coated with goat anti-human IgG Fc-γ fragment-specific antibody (1 μg/well, Cat. 109–005-098, Jackson ImmunoResearch) in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with 3% BSA in PBS for 30 min at RT, serially diluted Hu12F6 standards or serum samples (appropriately diluted to fall within the standard curve) were incubated at RT for 2 h. Plates were washed five times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS-T), followed by addition of HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Cat. 109–035-003, Jackson ImmunoResearch) and incubation at RT for 1 h. After five additional washes, the reaction was developed with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate for 15 min and terminated with 2 N sulfuric acid. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. PK parameters were calculated from the mean serum concentration-time data using non-compartmental analysis (NCA) with Phoenix WinNonlin software (Certara, version 8.1). The following parameters were determined: maximum serum concentration (Cmax), time to reach Cmax (tmax), area under the serum concentration-time curve from time 0 to the last measurable concentration (AUC0–t) and extrapolated to infinity (AUC0–∞), terminal elimination half-life (t1/2), and total body clearance (CL).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences between two groups were evaluated using two-way ANOVA, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All quantitative data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA).

Results

PZR level is significantly upregulated in breast tumor tissues

To investigate the role of PZR in breast cancer, we performed immunohistochemical staining of clinical breast tumor tissues using a commercially available anti-PZR antibody. The results revealed marked overexpression of PZR protein in tumor tissues (Fig. 1A), with a positive rate of 73.4% (Table 1). To further define the expression profile of PZR in normal human tissues, we performed immunohistochemical analysis on a tissue microarray comprising 19 tissue types. The results showed that PZR was highly expressed in the prostate, testis, uterus, liver, and heart, moderately expressed in the stomach, breast, kidney, and pancreas, and weakly expressed or absent in the lung and other tissues (Fig. S1). Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database further showed that breast cancer patients with high PZR mRNA levels had significantly shorter overall survival (p < 0.01, Fig. 1B). Western blot analysis also demonstrated differential PZR protein abundance across different subtypes of breast cancer cell lines. PZR level was high in TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-468 and Sum149PT, HER2+ cell lines HCC1954 and BT474 cells, whereas its level was low in ER+ cell lines MCF-7 and T47D cells (Fig. 1C). PZR protein exhibited heterogeneous molecular weight distribution patterns across various breast cancer cell lines, the majority of which migrated below 70 kDa. Notably, except for BT-474 cells, the observed molecular weights in the remaining cell lines were consistent with previously reported values of approximately 29–68 kDa. Collectively, these findings suggested that PZR may be a therapeutic target for aggressive breast cancer.

Generation of anti-PZR monoclonal antibodies

We planned to generate monoclonal antibodies against the extracellular domain (ECD) of human PZR. The expression vector containing the His-tag human PZR ECD fragment (PZR-ECD-127) was transiently transfected into suspension-adapted CHO cells. Culture supernatant containing the His-PZR-ECD-127 recombinant protein was harvested and purified using the Ni-NTA beads. Coomassie staining showed that the purified protein was > 90% pure (Fig. S2A). Western blotting with anti-His antibody detected a broad band of approximately 15–25 kDa, corresponding to the predicted recombinant human His-PZR-ECD-127 protein (Fig. S2A).

To generate PZR-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), BALB/c mice were immunized three times with the purified recombinant PZR-ECD-127 emulsified in complete/incomplete Freund’s adjuvant. Sera collected by retro-orbital bleeding were analyzed by ELISA, revealing high-titer antibodies against the recombinant PZR antigen from all three immunized mice (Fig. S2B). Subsequently, splenocytes from these mice were fused with the SP2/0 myeloma cell line to create hybridomas that were selected in HAT medium. After subcloning with limiting-dilution, ten stable hybridoma clones secreting anti-PZR mAbs were obtained. Isotype analysis by ELISA identified the antibodies as the IgG1 subclass (data not shown). For antibody production, clones were expanded in mice to generate ascites, from which monoclonal antibodies were purified using protein-A columns. ELISA confirmed that all ten mAbs bound recombinant PZR in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S2C).

To test whether the mAbs recognize endogenous PZR on TNBC cells, flow cytometry was performed on MDA-MB-468 cells. Seven of the ten mAbs (6H12, 4D4, 8D12, 2E12, 5B5, 12F6 and 3A5) stained the cell surface, whereas 22H12, 18A12 and 28A7 gave no signal (Fig. 2A). In immunoprecipitation experiments, Hs578T cell lysates were incubated with each mAb or an isotype control (mouse IgG1). The same seven mAbs also successfully precipitated endogenous PZR, with five (4D4, 8D12, 2E12, 5B5 and 12F6) showing the highest efficiency, whereas 22H12, 18A12, 28A7, precipitated minimal amounts of PZR and the IgG1 control did not precipitate any PZR (Fig. 2B). Collectively, these data demonstrate that seven of the generated anti-PZR mAbs recognize native PZR expressed on the surface and in the cytoplasm of TNBC cells.

All five anti-PZR mAbs specifically stained the surface of these TNBC cell lines, whereas the isotype control gave no signal (Fig. 3A).

Anti-PZR monoclonal antibodies inhibit the migration of TNBC cell lines

Previous studies have implicated PZR in tumor-cell migration and invasion. We next assessed whether these antibodies affect TNBC cell migration. Hs578T and MDA-MB-231 cells were placed in the upper chamber of Trans-well inserts in the presence of 10 µg/mL of each mAb or IgG1 control; 24 h later, migrated cells were stained with crystal violet. Compared with the isotype group, mAbs 5B5, 8D12 and 12F6 significantly reduced the number of migrated cells (Figs. 3B–3E), with 12F6 showing the strongest inhibitory effect. Similar results were obtained in Sum149PT and Sum159PT cell lines (Fig. S3). Surprisingly, under adherent culture conditions, none of the mAbs influenced the growth of these PZR-positive TNBC cell lines (Fig. S4). Collectively, these data demonstrate that 12F6 mAb selectively impairs the migration of PZR-positive TNBC cells without affecting their proliferation.

Specificity and internalization of 12F6 mAb

To verify that 12F6 mAb selectively targets PZR, we performed two validation experiments. First, THP-1 acute myeloid leukemia cells, characterized by low endogenous PZR expression, were transiently transfected to overexpress human PZR (left panel, Fig. 4A). Immunoprecipitation with 12F6 mAb or an isotype control showed that only 12F6 mAb efficiently precipitated PZR from THP-1-PZR lysates, whereas no band was detected in the isotype control lane (right panel, Fig. 4A). Second, we used the CRISPR-Cas9 technology to generate PZR-knockout MDA-MB-231 cells (MDA-MB-231-PZR KO) and compared them with wild-type MDA-MB-231. Again, 12F6 mAb efficiently precipitated PZR from wild-type cells but no detectable PZR from the PZR-KO cells (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these data demonstrate that 12F6 mAb specifically recognizes both exogenous and endogenous PZR expressed by cancer cells.

Next, we investigated whether 12F6 mAb can enter cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Surface staining of intact MDA-MB-231 and Sum149PT cells with 12F6 mAb revealed bright membrane fluorescence, while the isotype control remained negative (Fig. 4C), confirming that the antibody binds native PZR on the plasma membrane. To track antibody fate, cells were incubated at 37 °C with DyLight-488–conjugated 12F6 mAb for various intervals. Confocal microscopy revealed that after 1 h of treatment with the 12F6 mAb, distinct fluorescent signals were observed on the surface of TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231). In contrast, after 3 h of treatment, the fluorescent signals on the cell surface disappeared, while pronounced signals were detected in the cytoplasm. Notably, fluorescence accumulated in a time-dependent manner within both MDA-MB-231 and Sum149PT cells (Fig. 4D). Taken together, these results indicate that TNBC cells exhibit progressively enhanced endocytosis of the 12F6 mAb over time.

12F6 mAb suppresses metastasis in a TNBC xenograft model

To determine whether 12F6 mAb can inhibit the metastasis of PZR-positive TNBC cells in vivo, we established a mouse metastasis model injecting MDA-MB-231 cells expressing luciferase (MDA-MB-231-Luc) into NOD/SCID mice via the tail vein (Fig. 5A). Metastatic progression was monitored using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Three hours post-injection, bioluminescent signals in the lungs were similar across all groups (Fig. 5B), indicating consistent initial tumor cell seeding. One day after cell injection, mice were randomly divided into three groups and treated via tail vein injection with either PBS, isotype control antibody (10 mg/kg), or 12F6 mAb (10 mg/kg). Mice were administered with the antibody twice weekly throughout the experiment. IVIS imaging was performed once a week to assess metastatic burden in the lungs. Over time, bioluminescent signals in the lungs of PBS and isotype control groups gradually increased, indicating progressive metastasis. In contrast, mice treated with 12F6 mAb exhibited a significant reduction in pulmonary bioluminescence compared to the isotype control group (Figs. 5B and C), suggesting that 12F6 mAb effectively suppressed tumor metastasis. Consistently, the lung weight of mice in the 12F6 mAb group was significantly lower than that of the PBS or isotype control groups (Fig. 5D), further supporting its anti-metastatic effect. On day 38, lungs were excised for histological analysis. Results showed that both the number and size of metastatic foci in the lungs of 12F6 mAb-treated mice were markedly reduced compared to those in the PBS or isotype control groups (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these data demonstrated that 12F6 mAb can significantly inhibit the metastasis of PZR-positive TNBC cells in vivo.

Interaction between PZR and integrin β1 promotes fibronectin-dependent cell adhesion and FAK activation of TNBC cells

To begin to understand how 12F6 mAb inhibits the migration and metastasis of TNBC cells, we investigated whether PZR interacts with integrins, cell surface receptors that play critical roles in cell migration and metastasis. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) was performed to assess endogenous binding of PZR to integrin β1 and β4 in MDA-MB-231 cells. A commercial rabbit anti-PZR antibody specifically precipitated integrin β1, whereas no association with integrin β4 was detected (Fig. 6A). To validate direct binding, Flag-PZR and integrin β1 were co-expressed in HEK-293T cells, and immunoprecipitation with Flag-M2 beads confirmed co-precipitation of integrin β1, confirming a direct physical interaction (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence co-localization further revealed pronounced PZR-integrin β1 co-localization on the cell surface (Fig. 6C), substantiating their spatial proximity. Notably, immunoprecipitation with distinct antibodies showed that rabbit anti-PZR antibody pulled down both PZR and integrin β1, whereas the 12F6 mAb only precipitated PZR, suggesting that the12F6 binding site in PZR may involve in interaction with integrin β1 (Fig. 6D).

To investigate whether the PZR epitope recognized by the 12F6 mAb encompasses the integrin β1 binding site, we employed a combined computational prediction and experimental validation strategy. Initially, we utilized the AlphaFold Server 4.0 platform to predict the binding interface between PZR protein and the variable region of the 12F6 mAb, which suggested that 12F6 potentially recognizes residues S86, L97, and N99 of PZR. Based on this prediction, we employed an alanine-scanning mutagenesis strategy to systematically dissect the interaction mode between the 12F6 mAb and the PZR extracellular domain.

Specifically, we constructed a wild-type plasmid encoding Flag-PZR-EC127 (encompassing extracellular residues 36–162), and subsequently introduced single or double amino acid substitutions (e.g., S/A, LGN/AGA, Fig. 6E) to generate a series of Flag-tagged PZR-EC127 mutant expression vectors. Following transient transfection of these vectors into HEK293T cells, immunoprecipitation was performed using mouse IgG1 (as a negative control), anti-Flag antibody, and 12F6 mAb, respectively, with the precipitated products detected via anti-Flag antibody immunoblotting. The results revealed that the IgG1 control group yielded no detectable bands, whereas the anti-Flag antibody efficiently precipitated the fusion proteins in all samples. In contrast, the 12F6 mAb only recognized and precipitated the wild-type PZR-EC127 and the S/A mutant, but failed to immunoprecipitate the LGN/AGA double mutant (Fig. 6F). These findings establish that the LGN motif constitutes the core epitope recognized by the 12F6 antibody.

To precisely delineate the epitope boundaries, we performed a series of double-alanine mutations flanking the LGN motif (including SQ/AA, GQ/AA, VY/AA, YP/AA, PF/AA, and KD/AA) and repeated the immunoprecipitation analyses. These results demonstrated that the 12F6 mAb recognition epitope maps to amino acid residues 95–103 of PZR, with the sequence V95YLGNYPPF103 (Fig. 6G).

Furthermore, to identify the integrin β1 binding site on PZR, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with pcDNA3.0-Flag-PZR full-length wild-type or designated mutants (VY/AA, LGN/AGA, YP/AA, PF/AA) together with integrin β1. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the Flag-tagged fusion proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag antibody, and both the Flag tag and co-precipitated integrin β1 were detected by immunoblotting. The results showed that, compared to wild-type PZR, VY/AA and YP/AA mutations significantly diminished the co-precipitation level of integrin β1, whereas LGN/AGA and PF/AA mutations did not affect their interaction (Fig. 6H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that integrin β1 specifically interacts with the VY and YP sites of PZR protein in a conformation-dependent manner.

Given the pivotal role of integrin β1 in cell adhesion to ECM, we next examined whether 12F6 treatment can block the adherence of TNBC cells to different ECM molecules. MDA-MB-231 cells pre-treated with 12F6 mAb (5 µg/mL) were seeded onto plates pre-coated with various ECM components, including fibronectin (FN), type I collagen, type IV collagen, laminin I, and fibrinogen. 12F6 treatment specifically impaired cell adhesion to FN without affecting cell adherence to other ECM substrates (Fig. 7A). To confirm the critical role of PZR in FN-dependent cell adhesion, we generated PZR-knockout (PZR-KO) MDA-MB-231 cells via CRISPR-Cas9 technology. PZR deficiency markedly attenuated cell adhesion to FN compared to wild-type (WT) cells (Figs. 7B, C). However, 12F6 was unable exert additional inhibitory effect on adhesion to FN in PZR-KO cells (Fig. 7B), establishing that the blocking effect of 12F6 on cell adhesion to FN is strictly PZR-dependent. FN binding to integrin β1 stimulation induces downstream cell signaling including FAK and Src activation. However, 12F6 significantly suppressed FAK phosphorylation during early adhesion (10–30 min) without affecting late-phase FAK activation (60 min) or Src phosphorylation (Fig. 7D), indicating that PZR-integrin β1 interaction predominantly enhances early activation of FAK. Collectively, our findings support a model that PZR interaction with integrin β1 enhances integrin β1 early signaling in TNBC cells, a process specifically disrupted by the 12F6 monoclonal antibody.

The 12F6 mAb cannot recognize mouse PZR protein

To verify its cross-reactivity with the mouse homolog, we constructed plasmids expressing human PZR-EC127-Flag (127 amino acids of the extracellular domain) and mouse PZR-EC127-Flag, and transiently transfected them into HEK-293T cells, respectively. Subsequently, we performed immunoprecipitation on cell lysates from 293T cells over-expressing human or mouse PZR-EC127 proteins using 12F6 antibody. The results showed that the anti-Flag tag antibody could effectively precipitate both human and mouse PZR-EC127 proteins; the 12F6 antibody successfully precipitated the protein from human PZR-EC127-overexpressing cells but failed to precipitate the protein from mouse PZR-EC127-overexpressing cells; the isotype antibody did not precipitate PZR fusion proteins expressed in either cell type (Fig. S5A and B). We have already demonstrated that the epitope recognized by the 12F6 antibody on the target protein PZR is “VYLGNYPPF”(Fig. 6). Based on this finding, we further aligned the amino acid sequences of human and mouse PZR proteins and found that the mouse PZR sequence corresponding to the human PZR epitope “VYLGNYPPF” is “VYIGDYPPF”, with two amino acid differences (L→I at position 3 and N→D at position 5)(Fig. S5C). Therefore, we conclude that the inability of the 12F6 antibody to recognize mouse PZR protein is attributable to these two critical amino acid substitutions in the epitope. In summary, our data demonstrate that the 12F6 antibody does not recognize mouse PZR protein, and this species-specific lack of reactivity arises from key amino acid differences within the antigenic epitope.

Humanization of 12F6 mAb through CDR grafting

To reduce the risk of immune reactions in humans caused by murine antibodies, we performed humanization of the murine PZR antibody 12F6 mAb. First, we extracted total RNA from 12F6 hybridoma cells and synthesized cDNA using reverse transcriptase. We amplified the Fab fragments of the VH and VL using PCR. Subsequently, the amplified Fab fragments were cloned into the pGEM-T Easy vector. After DNA sequencing analysis, we obtained the nucleotide sequences of the VH and VL regions of the 12F6 mAb. Based on the Kabat numbering method, we further analyzed and defined the amino acid sequences of the three CDRs in the VH and the three CDRs in the VL (Fig. S6) of 12F6.

During the humanization process, it is crucial to maintain or essentially maintain the affinity and specificity of the humanized mAb for the target antigen without affecting its drug developability, especially its draggability. The CDRs of a monoclonal antibody are key regions for the interaction between the antibody and the antigen [32]. The classic humanization method is to graft the CDR regions of the murine antibody onto the human antibody framework regions (FRs). We searched the amino acid sequences of the VH (m12F6D9-VH) and VL (m12F6D9-VL) of the 12F6 mAb in the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) open-source database using IgBlast Tool to identify the human heavy chain variable region and light chain variable region with the highest homology to the parent 12F6 mAb, which were IGHV2-70 (vs m12F6 VH, 75.3%) and IGKV1-NL (vs m12F6 VL, 78.5%), respectively. Subsequently, we grafted the CDR regions of the parent 12F6 mAb onto the human antibody framework to obtain the grafted antibody sequences (defined as CDR grafting). By comparing the sequences of the grafted CDR grafting-VH and CDR grafting-VL with the parent VH and VL sequences, we identified all the key residues that differed between the grafted and parent antibody framework sequences. Based on the following principles, we introduced one or more back mutations into the grafted antibody sequence step by step: (1) key amino acids involved in antibody loop interactions; (2) amino acids located at the interface between the light and heavy chains; (3) key amino acids for the formation of the antibody core. After each back mutation, we used the ROSETTA software for modeling and structural analysis. Finally, we selected the humanized candidate molecule Back mutation 1, which was defined as the HuPZR antibody Fab fragment (Fig. S6).

Hu12F6 retains high-affinity and specific binding to PZR

To verify whether the humanized PZR antibody could recognize the native PZR protein expressed by TNBC cells, we first inserted the variable region cDNA sequence of the humanized PZR (HuPZR) antibody into the pcDNA3.4 expression vector, successfully constructing the pcDNA3.4-HuPZR vector that could express recombinant HuPZR monoclonal antibodies. Subsequently, we transiently transfected this expression vector into CHO cells for suspension culture and collected the antibodies from the culture supernatant, which were purified using protein-A agarose beads. Coomassie staining of the purified material revealed discrete heavy- and light-chain bands (Fig. 8A, lane 2) with no additional bands under non-reducing conditions. Analytical size-exclusion chromatography showed a single symmetrical peak, indicating a monodisperse preparation with ≥97% purity (Fig. 8B). SPR measurements yielded an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 1.876 nM for Hu12F6 binding to recombinant human PZR, virtually identical to that of the parental 12F6 mAb (1.986 nM) (Fig. 8C). Flow-cytometric analysis further demonstrated that Hu12F6 specifically recognizes native PZR on the surface of TNBC cells (Fig. 8D). Collectively, these data demonstrate that humanization preserves both the affinity and specificity for PZR. To evaluate the impact of Hu12F6 on TNBC cell migration, we incubated Hu12F6 or an isotype-matched human IgG1 control with three PZR-positive TNBC lines (MDA-MB-231, Sum149PT, and Sum159PT) and performed Trans-well assays. After 24 h, the number of cells that had migrated through the membrane was significantly lower in the Hu12F6 group than in the isotype control (Figs. 9A and B), and the inhibitory potency was comparable to that of the parental murine 12F6 antibody. These results demonstrated that PZR-targeted Hu12F6 effectively suppresses the in vitro migratory capacity of PZR-positive TNBC cells.

Discussion

The development of targeted therapies for TNBC remains a significant challenge due to its aggressive nature and lack of effective molecular targets [33]. In this study, we explored the potential of targeting PZR, a type I trans-membrane glycoprotein that can promote the migration, invasion, and metastasis of various cancer cells, by generating a novel anti-PZR monoclonal antibody 12F6 and its humanized version. Our findings using 12F6 supports a model by which PZR interacting with integrin β1 enhances fibronectin-dependent integrin signaling and promotes metastasis of TNBC.

Initially, we observed markedly elevated PZR expression in breast-cancer patient tissues, identifying the molecule as a promising tumor-associated target. We therefore generated ten anti-PZR monoclonal antibodies by immunizing mice with recombinant PZR-ECD-127 protein and using conventional hybridoma technology. To identify antibodies that specifically recognize native PZR on the surface and in the cytoplasm of TNBC cells, we systematically evaluated these antibodies using flow cytometry and immunoprecipitation. Ultimately, we selected a high-affinity anti-PZR monoclonal antibody, 12F6, which demonstrated significant inhibitory effects on TNBC cell migration, as determined by Trans-well assays. This process not only validated the efficacy of recombinant PZR-ECD-127 protein as an antigen but also laid a solid foundation for subsequent antibody humanization and in vivo experiments.

The specificity and internalization of the therapeutic monoclonal antibody are critical for its therapeutic efficacy [34]. Our results demonstrate that 12F6 mAb specifically recognizes native PZR on the surface of TNBC cells and is efficiently internalized via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Specifically, 12F6 mAb effectively identified PZR in THP-1 cells overexpressing PZR, whereas it failed to recognize PZR in PZR wild-type MDA-MB-231 cells after PZR knockout using CRISPR-Cas9, as confirmed by immunoprecipitation assays. Moreover, 12F6 mAb was internalized in a time-dependent manner by PZR-positive MDA-MB-231 and SUM149PT cells. This internalization process is essential for the antibody to exert its effects, such as disrupting intracellular signaling transduction. Moreover, the high affinity of 12F6 mAb for PZR, with a dissociation constant (KD) of 2.04 nM, ensures robust binding and effective inhibition of PZR-mediated functions. The humanized version of the antibody, Hu12F6, maintained comparable binding affinity and specificity, further validating its potential for clinical application.

The inhibitory effects of 12F6 mAb on PZR-positive TNBC cell migration and metastasis suggest that it may disrupt key steps in the metastatic cascade. Specifically, when 12F6 mAb was administered 24 hours after intravenous injection of MDA-MB-231 cells into mice, it significantly inhibited pulmonary metastasis of TNBC cells. Previous studies have reported that 24 hours after intravenous injection of MDA-MB-231 cells into mice, most cancer cells had extravasated from blood vessels and invaded lung tissue [35], which contains ECM including collagen, fibronectin, and laminin that provides physical support for cells and regulates cell behavior through interactions with cell surface receptors like integrins [36]. In particular, Integrin β1 promotes the adhesion of cancer cells to the ECM via “inside-out” and “outside-in” signaling mechanisms, thereby providing a foundation for cancer cell survival and metastasis [22, 36]. Our study has revealed a direct interaction between PZR and integrin β1, which selectively enhances the fibronectin-dependent adhesion of TNBC cells. Further experiments have shown that stimulation with fibronectin can induce time-dependent activation of FAK, a key effector of integrin signaling, which can be attenuated by 12F6 mAb treatment (Fig. 7D). Therefore, it is possible the interaction between PZR and integrin β1 may enhance the adhesion of TNBC cells to the ECM in the lung after TNBC cells extravasate from blood vessels. 12F6 monoclonal antibody may effectively inhibit the adhesion of TNBC cells to the ECM, reduce the formation of micro-metastases, and ultimately limit the occurrence of distant metastasis. Consistent with our model, a study in ovarian cancer showed that LRRC15 binds to fibronectin, activating the integrin β1 and FAK signaling pathways, which drive metastasis. Targeting LRRC15 with the antibody-drug conjugate ABBV-085 has successfully inhibited metastasis in ovarian cancer models [37].

Both 12F6 and its humanized version Hu12F6 antibodies almost completely inhibited lung metastasis of TNBC cells in mouse models (Figs. 5 and 10). However, in vitro, Hu12F6 inhibited the migration of PZR-positive TNBC cells by only approximately 50% (Fig. 9B). In addition to the blockade of the interaction between PZR and integrin β1 by Hu12F6, other potential mechanisms need to be considered for this discrepancy between the in vivo and in vitro effects. Therapeutic antibodies typically exert anti-tumor effects either by binding to target proteins to block downstream signaling and/or via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). We hypothesize that Hu12F6 may bind to FcγR via its Fc region, thereby recruiting NK cells to mediate ADCC. It should be noted that although NOD/SCID mice are immunodeficient, they retain functional NK cells. This hypothesis is consistent with the mechanism by which therapeutic antibodies such as trastuzumab and rituzimab recruit FcγR-expressing NK cells through their Fc regions to exert ADCC effects [38, 39]. Future studies could utilize NK cells derived from PBMCs to validate the in vitro ADCC cytotoxicity of Hu12F6 antibody against PZR-positive TNBC cells. Meanwhile, constructing an FcγR-binding site mutant of the humanized antibody Fc region and directly assessing the contribution of ADCC to the suppression of TNBC metastasis using an in vivo metastasis model would provide significant research value [40, 41]. The TNBC metastasis model employed in this study was established in NOD/SCID mice. It has been well documented that this mouse strain carries a C5 complement component deficiency [42], which prevents the completion of complement cascade activation in vivo. Therefore, the possibility that Hu12F6 exerts its anti-metastatic effect through complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) can be excluded.

The xenoprotein nature of murine mAbs poses significant challenges for clinical application, including the risk of immunogenicity and short half-life [43]. Humanization of the 12F6 mAb through CDR grafting addressed these issues by reducing the proportion of murine sequences while preserving the antibody’s affinity and specificity. The humanized antibody, Hu12F6, maintained a high binding affinity (KD of 1.876 nM) and effectively inhibited TNBC cell migration and metastasis in both in vitro and in vivo models. This successful humanization highlights the importance of minimizing immunogenicity to enhance the therapeutic potential of monoclonal antibodies.

A critical consideration in the development of therapeutic antibodies is whether antitumor efficacy observed in preclinical models reflects sustained target engagement at pharmacologically relevant exposures, or merely transient receptor occupancy following bolus administration [44, 45]. To address this, we characterized the pharmacokinetic profile of Hu12F6 in NOD/SCID mice and integrated these data with our pulmonary metastasis efficacy findings. Following intravenous administration at 10 mg/kg (~200 μg/mouse), Hu12F6 exhibited a terminal elimination half-life of 89.6 ± 16.6 h (~3.7 days) with low systemic clearance (0.9 ± 0.1 mL/h/kg) and high exposure (AUC0~∞=13,612.7 ± 1,697.3 μg/mL·h). These parameters are similar to those of typical humanized IgG1 antibodies in immunodeficient mouse models [46], where the absence of endogenous human IgG competition and intact FcRn recycling contribute to prolonged circulation [47]. Importantly, the measured half-life directly supports our twice-weekly dosing regimen: based on first-order kinetics, the predicted trough concentration prior to the second dose remains is approximately ~30–40% of Cmax (123.3 ± 32.4 μg/mL). This sustained exposure profile indicates that the observed suppression of pulmonary metastatic outgrowth likely reflects continuous pharmacodynamic activity rather than intermittent target engagement. The translational implications of these findings are noteworthy. Humanized IgG antibodies typically exhibit 20–23 day half-lives in humans due to robust FcRn-mediated recycling [48, 49], substantially longer than the ~3.7 days half-life observed here. Thus, our mouse PK data may actually underestimate the potential for sustained target engagement in clinical settings. Furthermore, the low clearance rate (0.9 mL/h/kg) suggests favorable systemic availability, supporting dose regimens that maintain therapeutic exposure without excessive dosing frequency. These PK parameters, combined with the demonstrated anti-metastatic efficacy, provide a preliminary exposure-response framework for Hu12F6 development.

A recent study successfully generated a PZR-blocking monoclonal antibody (mAb D9a) targeting the extracellular domain of human PZR using phage display technology [50]. The study demonstrated that the D9a monoclonal antibody significantly inhibits cancer stem cell-like properties in vitro, particularly in colorectal cancer cells with high progastrin (PG) expression, and reduces the number of colonospheres formed by these cancer cells. Similarly, D9a targeting PZR attenuates cancer stem cell (CSC)-like properties in vivo and diminishes the tumor-initiating capacity of PG-high colorectal cancer cells in immunodeficient mice. Comparing the antitumor efficacy of our Hu12F6 with that of D9a will be of significant scientific interest in future research. Although PZR protein is widely expressed in various normal tissues (Fig. S1), the potential off-target effects of targeted intervention strategies require careful evaluation. Previous studies have confirmed that Mpzl1 (encoding PZR) knockout mice do not exhibit significant developmental defects or functional impairments under normal physiological conditions [50]. This finding provides preliminary evidence for the safety of PZR inhibitors, suggesting that antibody-based intervention strategies targeting PZR hold considerable potential for clinical translation. In any event, our findings lay the groundwork for exploring PZR as a therapeutic target in TNBC. Clinical trials may be are warranted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of Hu12F6 in TNBC patients, particularly those with high PZR expression.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that targeting PZR with a high-affinity, humanized monoclonal antibody significantly inhibits TNBC metastasis. The findings highlight the therapeutic potential of PZR-targeted antibodies and provide a rationale for further development of this approach in the treatment of aggressive TNBC.
