Predictors of in-hospital mortality among critically ill neonates admitted to a tertiary NICU in Ecuador
Authors: Mariana Noboa-Salgado, Fabricio González-Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal mortality remains a major challenge in low- and middle-income countries, where locally derived evidence from neonatal intensive care units is limited. We aimed to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality among critically ill neonates admitted to a tertiary NICU in Quito, Ecuador, and to evaluate an internally validated mortality prediction model.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of inborn neonates aged ≤ 28 days admitted to the NICU of Isidro Ayora Gynecology and Obstetrics Hospital from January 1 to December 31, 2022. Maternal, perinatal, neonatal, early clinical severity, laboratory, respiratory support, clinical course, and resource utilization variables were extracted from medical records. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Bivariate analyses and multivariable logistic regression were performed. Model performance was assessed using discrimination, calibration, bootstrap internal validation, and decision curve analysis.ResultsAmong 220 critically ill neonates, 87 (39.5%) died. Small-for-gestational-age status was independently associated with mortality after adjustment for birth weight category, gestational age, sex, congenital malformations, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure (adjusted OR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.29–5.14; P=.01). Birth weight < 1400 g was associated with greater clinical severity and resource use but not independently with mortality. Early hemodynamic instability, metabolic acidosis, higher lactate, higher FiO₂, thrombocytopenia, and higher oxygenation index were more frequent among non-survivors. The final model showed good discrimination (apparent AUC, 0.870; optimism-corrected AUC, 0.854) and acceptable calibration. Among survivors, each additional day of invasive mechanical ventilation was associated with longer NICU stay.ConclusionIn this tertiary Ecuadorian NICU cohort, small-for-gestational-age status was independently associated with mortality. These findings support fetal growth assessment, early severity evaluation, and locally validated neonatal risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07156-z.Keywords: Neonatal mortality, Newborn, Neonatal intensive care unit, Small for gestational age, Risk factors, Mechanical ventilation, Cohort study, Development goal 3

Introduction

Neonatal mortality remains a major global public health challenge and accounts for a substantial proportion of deaths in early childhood. In 2022, approximately 2.3 million neonates died worldwide, representing nearly 47% of all deaths among children younger than five years [1]. Despite progress in child survival, neonatal deaths have declined more slowly than deaths occurring after the neonatal period, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [2]. Prematurity remains one of the most important contributors to neonatal mortality worldwide and shows marked variation across regions and health systems [3]. Other leading causes of neonatal death include intrapartum-related complications, infections, and congenital anomalies [4]. The timing and cause structure of neonatal deaths differ between the early and late neonatal periods, which has implications for prevention and clinical care [5].

Small-for-gestational-age status is an important determinant of neonatal survival, especially in low- and middle-income countries where fetal growth restriction and prematurity frequently overlap [6]. The global burden of small-for-gestational-age births remains substantial, with important regional differences in prevalence and associated risk [7]. Fetal growth restriction is associated with impaired respiratory adaptation, metabolic instability, infection risk, and reduced physiological reserve [8]. Maternal and obstetric conditions such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, infection, and premature rupture of membranes contribute to preterm birth and adverse neonatal outcomes [9]. The epidemiology of preterm birth varies across populations, emphasizing the need for context-specific interpretation of neonatal risk factors [10].

Among critically ill neonates, respiratory disease severity is a central determinant of mortality and resource use in neonatal intensive care units. Respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary immaturity, and surfactant deficiency frequently require oxygen therapy, ventilatory support, and specialized neonatal care [11]. Antenatal corticosteroids are an established intervention for reducing respiratory morbidity among neonates at risk of preterm birth [12]. However, extremely preterm, and critically ill neonates remain at high risk of death despite advances in neonatal intensive care practices [13]. Neonatal sepsis is another major contributor to mortality and clinical deterioration among hospitalized neonates requiring intensive monitoring and invasive support [14].

Mortality among neonates admitted to intensive care is influenced not only by individual clinical factors but also by institutional performance, care practices, and available resources [15]. Many neonatal deaths occur during the first days of life, highlighting the importance of early recognition of clinical severity and timely intervention [16]. Prediction models developed in high-income settings may not be directly applicable to middle-income health systems because of differences in case-mix, referral pathways, baseline risk, and access to specialized care [21]. Hospital-based studies from tertiary neonatal intensive care units in low- and middle-income countries can therefore provide clinically useful information for local risk stratification and resource planning [20]. Earlier global analyses also showed that many neonatal deaths in low-resource settings are associated with preventable or treatable conditions, reinforcing the need for locally actionable evidence [23].

Ecuador has demographic and geographic characteristics that support the need for locally derived neonatal evidence. The Ecuadorian population is heterogeneous, including Amerindian Kichwa, Mestizo, and Afro-Ecuadorian groups with documented genetic diversity [17]. Additional genetic studies have confirmed population heterogeneity among Ecuadorian groups, which may be relevant when interpreting growth patterns and disease risk [18]. Quito is located at high altitude, where physiological oxygen saturation values and neonatal screening thresholds may differ from those reported at sea level [19]. These factors may influence neonatal risk profiles, interpretation of clinical variables, and the transferability of evidence generated in other populations.

Data from Ecuadorian tertiary NICU populations remain limited, particularly regarding the combined contribution of fetal growth status, early clinical severity, respiratory support requirements, and prediction model performance. In addition, the potential consequences of fetal growth restriction extend beyond the neonatal period, supporting the clinical importance of early identification of vulnerable neonates [22, 23].

Therefore, this study aimed to identify maternal, perinatal, and early clinical factors associated with in-hospital mortality among critically ill neonates admitted to the NICU of a tertiary referral maternity hospital in Quito, Ecuador. We also aimed to develop and internally validate a mortality prediction model using routinely collected clinical variables and to evaluate its discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility.

Methods

Study design and reporting guidelines

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study using routinely collected clinical data to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality among critically ill neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). The study followed the STROBE guidelines. Although the study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines, a complete patient flow diagram could not be reconstructed because the retrospective anonymized database did not retain complete institutional-level screening logs for all stages of cohort assembly. Available information allowed identification of the final analytical cohort and application of eligibility criteria, but not reliable reconstruction of total deliveries, total neonatal admissions, inborn and outborn admissions, or all exclusions before creation of the analytical dataset.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Isidro Ayora Gynecology and Obstetrics Hospital (HGOIA), Quito, Ecuador. Data were collected from neonates admitted to the NICU between January 1 and December 31, 2022. HGOIA is a public tertiary maternity and neonatal referral hospital that provides care for pregnant women, deliveries, puerperium, and newborns. According to institutional information, the hospital has 63 neonatal beds and 12 NICU posts. The neonatal service includes basic, intermediate, and intensive neonatal care areas. The NICU receives neonates born at HGOIA and referrals from other healthcare facilities, reflecting its role as a tertiary referral center. Neonatal care is provided according to institutional protocols by a multidisciplinary team including neonatologists, pediatric and neonatal trainees, neonatal nurses, respiratory therapy personnel, and other support staff.

Participants and cohort assembly

Eligible participants were inborn neonates aged 28 days or less who were delivered at HGOIA and admitted to the NICU because of severe clinical instability requiring intensive monitoring or advanced life-support interventions. Indications for NICU admission included respiratory failure requiring ventilatory support, hemodynamic instability, suspected or confirmed severe infection, severe metabolic disturbances, complications of extreme prematurity, congenital anomalies requiring intensive monitoring, or other critical neonatal conditions. Neonates of both sexes and all ethnic backgrounds were eligible. Exclusion criteria were age greater than 28 days at admission, admission only to intermediate care, transfer to another hospital during admission, or incomplete medical records for the primary outcome or key study variables. After applying these criteria, 220 neonates were included in the final analytical cohort. Because the study was based on an anonymized analytical database, complete institutional-level screening data were not available for all stages of cohort assembly. Specifically, total deliveries, total neonatal admissions, inborn and outborn NICU admissions, intermediate-care admissions, and exclusions before construction of the final analytical dataset could not be reliably reconstructed. Therefore, a complete STROBE-style patient flow diagram could not be generated without introducing unverifiable assumptions. To maintain reporting accuracy, we explicitly describe the eligibility criteria, exclusion criteria, and final analytical cohort, and we acknowledge the potential for selection bias in the Limitations section.

Outcome

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during the NICU or hospital stay among neonates included in the analytical cohort. Neonates discharged alive were classified as survivors.

Variables and definitions

Variables were grouped according to timing and clinical interpretation to avoid mixing baseline characteristics, early severity markers, and downstream clinical course variables. Baseline maternal and obstetric variables included maternal age, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, premature rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, antenatal corticosteroid exposure, and multiple pregnancy. Baseline perinatal and neonatal variables included gestational age, birth weight, sex, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min, need for advanced resuscitation, congenital anomalies, and small-for-gestational-age status.

Small for gestational age was defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile for gestational age according to the growth reference used in institutional neonatal records and Ministry of Health standards. In routine clinical practice at the study hospital, SGA classification was recorded according to the neonatal growth standards adopted by the Ecuadorian Ministry of Public Health and incorporated into institutional medical records. Because this was a retrospective study based on routinely collected data, reclassification using alternative international growth references, such as Fenton or INTERGROWTH-21st, was not feasible. Population-specific Ecuadorian neonatal growth references and high-altitude-adjusted growth curves were not available for this analysis. Therefore, potential misclassification of fetal growth status cannot be excluded, especially given the high-altitude setting of Quito and the high observed prevalence of SGA in this tertiary NICU cohort.

Early clinical severity variables were defined as physiological, gasometric, hemodynamic, respiratory, and laboratory parameters recorded during NICU admission or within the first 24 h of hospitalization. These included hemodynamic instability, minimum mean arterial pressure, arterial pH, base deficit, serum lactate concentration, maximum fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂), platelet count, oxygenation index, seizures, and early respiratory support requirements. These variables were interpreted as markers of early postnatal illness severity rather than baseline causal predictors. Clinical course variables included complications and interventions occurring after admission and during hospitalization, such as respiratory distress syndrome, neonatal sepsis, septic shock, pulmonary hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, high-frequency ventilation, blood product transfusion, crystalloid bolus administration, and invasive mechanical ventilation. Resource utilization variables were analyzed separately and included duration of invasive mechanical ventilation, NICU length of stay, total hospital length of stay, number of blood product transfusions, and number of crystalloid boluses.

Major neonatal complications were defined using standardized clinical criteria documented in medical records. Respiratory distress syndrome was defined as respiratory failure requiring oxygen therapy or ventilatory support with compatible radiographic findings. Neonatal sepsis was defined as clinical signs of infection with microbiological confirmation by positive blood culture. Septic shock was defined as suspected or confirmed infection associated with hemodynamic instability requiring vasoactive support. Pulmonary hemorrhage was defined as blood in the airway associated with acute respiratory deterioration. Intraventricular hemorrhage was diagnosed by cranial ultrasound according to standard neonatal classification criteria. Congenital anomalies were defined as structural abnormalities diagnosed at birth or during hospitalization.

Respiratory support variables

Invasive mechanical ventilation was defined as ventilatory support delivered through an endotracheal tube using conventional mechanical ventilation. Duration of invasive mechanical ventilation was calculated as the total number of days during which the neonate received endotracheal ventilatory support. High-frequency ventilation was recorded as a separate variable. Non-invasive respiratory support, including continuous positive airway pressure and non-invasive positive pressure ventilation, was used when clinically indicated according to institutional protocols. However, detailed non-invasive ventilation parameters were not consistently available in the retrospective dataset and were therefore not included in the multivariable models. Intubation and invasive ventilation were not based exclusively on Apgar scores. Indications included severe respiratory distress, oxygenation failure despite non-invasive support, recurrent apnea, need for surfactant administration, hemodynamic instability, or need for advanced resuscitation at birth.

Birth-weight stratification

For descriptive and exploratory subgroup analyses, the cohort was stratified according to birth weight using a cutoff of 1400 g. This threshold was selected because it approximated the median birth weight of the cohort and allowed comparison between lower- and higher-birth-weight neonates within this critically ill population. This cutoff was not intended to represent a standard neonatal weight classification. Internationally recognized categories include very low birth weight (VLBW), defined as birth weight < 1500 g, and extremely low birth weight (ELBW), defined as birth weight < 1000 g. Therefore, subgroup analyses based on the 1400 g threshold should be interpreted as cohort-specific and exploratory, rather than directly comparable with studies using standard VLBW or ELBW definitions. No individual matching procedure was used, and group allocation was based only on the predefined exploratory birth-weight cutoff.

Data sources and measurement

Clinical data were obtained from electronic and physical medical records of neonates admitted to the NICU. Laboratory measurements were extracted from hospital laboratory databases. All variables were recorded using standardized measurement units and predefined operational definitions. Data extraction was performed using a structured collection form, and records were reviewed for consistency and completeness before analysis.

Missing data

Data completeness was assessed before statistical analysis. Patients with missing information for the primary outcome or essential eligibility variables were excluded from the analytical cohort. Complete-case analysis was used for multivariable models. Because the anonymized database did not retain complete screening logs or variable-level missingness for all patients assessed before cohort inclusion, the number of patients excluded at each pre-analytical stage could not be reliably reconstructed. This limitation is addressed in the Limitations section. In addition, because the anonymized database did not retain complete pre-analytical screening logs, the number of neonates excluded at each stage before formation of the final analytical cohort could not be reliably reconstructed. This limitation also prevented development of a complete STROBE patient flow diagram.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. Candidate variables were selected based on clinical relevance, temporal availability, bivariate associations, and avoidance of excessive collinearity. The baseline explanatory model included small-for-gestational-age status, birth weight < 1400 g, gestational age, sex, congenital malformations, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure. These variables were selected because they were available at birth or NICU admission and represented clinically relevant potential confounders of the association between small-for-gestational-age status and mortality.

Early clinical severity variables were not included in the baseline explanatory model because they were measured after birth and may lie on the causal pathway between prenatal risk factors and mortality. Instead, they were incorporated into nested predictive models to assess incremental prognostic value. Model A included baseline perinatal variables. Model B added maternal and obstetric variables. Model C incorporated early clinical severity indicators. Model D added laboratory and respiratory variables. Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC). AUCs across nested models were compared using statistical tests for correlated ROC curves. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier score. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations to estimate optimism-corrected model performance. Clinical usefulness was evaluated using decision curve analysis across a range of threshold probabilities. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding neonates with congenital malformations and refitting the baseline multivariable logistic regression model. Model parameters for the sensitivity analysis included adjusted odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and P values. Additional subgroup analyses evaluated selected associations across clinically relevant strata, including birth-weight category, gestational age strata, sex, and fetal growth status.

For survival analysis, time was defined as the number of days from NICU admission to death or discharge. The event of interest was in-hospital mortality, and neonates discharged alive were censored at discharge. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Among survivors, the association between duration of invasive mechanical ventilation and NICU length of stay was evaluated using generalized linear models. Results were expressed as adjusted ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS® statistical software. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study complied with national and international ethical standards for research involving human subjects, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient information obtained from medical records was anonymized before analysis, and personal identifiers were removed to ensure confidentiality. Access to the dataset was restricted to the research team. Because this was a retrospective study using anonymized clinical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Study population

A total of 220 critically ill inborn neonates admitted to the NICU between January 1 and December 31, 2022, were included in the final analysis. In-hospital mortality occurred in 87 neonates (39.5%), while 133 neonates (60.5%) survived to discharge. Baseline and early clinical characteristics according to survival status are presented in Table 1.

Characteristics according to in-hospital mortality

Maternal characteristics were similar between survivors and non-survivors. Maternal age, gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, chorioamnionitis, premature rupture of membranes, antenatal corticosteroid exposure, and multiple pregnancy did not differ significantly between groups. Several perinatal and neonatal characteristics differed by mortality status. Neonates who died had lower gestational age than survivors (31.5 [29.0–35.0] vs. 32.4 [31.0–34.5] weeks; P=.048) and lower birth weight (1,195 [830–2,005] vs. 1,435 [1,145–2,115] g; P=.004). Small-for-gestational-age status was more frequent among non-survivors than survivors (70% vs. 51%; P=.008). Non-survivors also had lower Apgar scores at 1 min (6 [4–7] vs. 7 [6–8]; P< .001) and 5 min (8 [7–9] vs. 8 [8–9]; P=.039), and more frequently required advanced resuscitation at birth (34% vs. 14%; P < .001).

Early clinical severity markers were consistently worse among neonates who died. Hemodynamic instability within the first 24 h was more frequent among non-survivors (34% vs. 7.5%; P<.001). Compared with survivors, non-survivors had lower minimum mean arterial pressure (25 [20–32] vs. 31 [25–36] mm Hg; P<.001), greater base deficit (18 [14–24] vs. 10 [7–14] mmol/L; p<.001), lower arterial pH (7.22 [7.15–7.30] vs. 7.33 [7.27–7.38]; P < .001), higher serum lactate (13.0 [7.9–17.8] vs. 4.5 [3.6–7.5] mmol/L; P < .001), and higher maximum FiO₂ requirements (100 [80–100] vs. 64 [50–80]%; P < .001). Platelet count was lower among non-survivors (87,000 [35,000–144,000]/µL vs. 151,000 [88,000–201,000]/µL; P < .001), and oxygenation index was higher among neonates who died (24 [14–36] vs. 8 [4–15]; P < .001). Hemoglobin, minimum glucose, and seizures did not differ significantly between groups. Clinical course also differed by survival status. Non-survivors required longer invasive mechanical ventilation than survivors (6 [2–12] vs. 2 [0–6] days; P < .001). High-frequency ventilation was more frequent among non-survivors (28% vs. 6%; P < .001), as were pulmonary hemorrhage (16% vs. 3%; P = .002) and septic shock (26% vs. 5%; P < .001). Total hospital length of stay was shorter among non-survivors (13 [4–31] vs. 31 [21–53] days; P < .001), consistent with early in-hospital mortality.

The high frequency of SGA observed in this cohort should be interpreted in the context of the study population. This was not a population-based birth cohort, but a selected group of critically ill neonates admitted to a tertiary referral NICU. Therefore, enrichment for fetal growth restriction, placental dysfunction, prematurity, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and other high-risk obstetric conditions is expected.

Exploratory birth-weight subgroup analysis

For exploratory descriptive analysis, the cohort was stratified using the predefined 1400 g birth-weight cutoff. Baseline characteristics by birth-weight subgroup are shown in Table 2. Neonates with birth weight < 1400 g had lower gestational age than those with birth weight ≥ 1400 g (29.8 ± 2.4 vs. 34.9 ± 2.6 weeks; P < .01) and lower birth weight (1065 ± 218 vs. 2098 ± 436 g; P < .01). Small-for-gestational-age status was more frequent in the < 1400 g group (67.3% vs. 50.0%; P = .01). The < 1400 g group also had higher frequencies of cesarean delivery (64.5% vs. 48.2%; P = .01), premature rupture of membranes (25.7% vs. 10.0%; P < .01), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (41.8% vs. 20.0%; P < .01), multiple pregnancy (25.5% vs. 10.9%; P = .01), and chorioamnionitis (22.7% vs. 10.0%; P = .02). Congenital malformations were more frequent among neonates weighing ≥ 1400 g (38.2% vs. 4.6%; P < .01). Because the 1400 g cutoff was selected for exploratory analysis and does not represent a standard neonatal weight category, these subgroup comparisons should be interpreted descriptively.

Because the 1400 g cutoff was selected as a cohort-specific exploratory threshold and does not correspond to standard VLBW or ELBW categories, these findings should not be interpreted as directly equivalent to analyses based on birth weight < 1500 g or < 1000 g. The purpose of this subgroup analysis was descriptive, focusing on clinical severity and resource utilization within this specific cohort.

Multivariable logistic regression for in-hospital mortality

The baseline multivariable logistic regression model is shown in Table 3; Fig. 1. After adjustment for birth weight category, gestational age, sex, congenital malformations, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure, small-for-gestational-age status remained independently associated with in-hospital mortality. Neonates classified as small for gestational age had more than twice the odds of death compared with neonates who were not small for gestational age (adjusted OR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.29–5.14; P = .01). Birth weight < 1400 g was not independently associated with mortality after adjustment (adjusted OR, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.62–2.23; P = .61). Gestational age, male sex, congenital malformations, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure were also not independently associated with mortality in this baseline explanatory model.

Predictive model performance

The final mortality prediction model showed good discrimination. The apparent AUC was 0.870, and the optimism-corrected AUC after bootstrapping internal validation was 0.854. Calibration was acceptable, with an optimism-corrected calibration slope of 0.96 and calibration intercept of 0.03. The Brier score was 0.143 in the apparent model and 0.152 after optimism correction. The ROC curve for the final model is shown in Fig. 4, and the bootstrap validation results are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis compared in-hospital survival according to exploratory birth-weight subgroup. Time was defined as days from NICU admission to death or discharge, and neonates discharged alive were censored at discharge. Survival trajectories were broadly similar between neonates weighing < 1400 g and ≥ 1400 g, and the log-rank test did not show a statistically significant difference. The Kaplan–Meier curves are shown in Fig. 2.

Composite morbidity and NICU resource utilization

Composite neonatal morbidity by exploratory birth-weight subgroup is shown in Fig. 3. Major morbidities included septic shock, pulmonary hemorrhage, positive blood culture, air leaks, and intraventricular hemorrhage. NICU resource utilization by exploratory birth-weight subgroup is shown in Table 4; Fig. 6. Neonates weighing < 1400 g had longer NICU stays than neonates weighing ≥ 1400 g (21.0 [7.25–38.0] vs. 9.0 [6.0–15.0] days; P < .001). They also required more blood product transfusions (6.0 [3.0–12.75] vs. 2.0 [0.25–5.0] units; P < .001), more crystalloid boluses (2.0 [1.0–3.0] vs. 1.0 [1.0–2.0]; P < .001), and longer invasive mechanical ventilation (12.0 [4.0–23.0] vs. 4.0 [2.0–7.0] days; P < .001). Among survivors, each additional day of invasive mechanical ventilation was associated with a 4% increase in NICU length of stay (adjusted ratio, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.03–1.05; P < .01).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analysis excluding neonates with congenital malformations produced results consistent with the primary model. Small-for-gestational-age status remained independently associated with mortality after exclusion of congenital malformations (adjusted OR, 2.44; 95% CI, 1.18–5.03; P = .02). Birth weight < 1400 g, gestational age, male sex, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure were not independently associated with mortality in this sensitivity analysis. Detailed model parameters are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Additional subgroup analyses evaluated selected associations across birth-weight category, gestational age strata, sex, and fetal growth status. These analyses supported the consistency of the association between fetal growth restriction, early clinical severity, and adverse neonatal outcomes. Discussion

Principal findings

In this retrospective cohort of critically ill inborn neonates admitted to a tertiary NICU in Quito, Ecuador, small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status was independently associated with in-hospital mortality. This association persisted after adjustment for birth weight category, gestational age, sex, congenital malformations, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure. In contrast, birth weight < 1400 g was associated with greater clinical severity and resource use but was not independently associated with mortality after adjustment. Early postnatal severity markers, including hemodynamic instability, metabolic acidosis, high oxygen requirement, elevated oxygenation index, and thrombocytopenia, were more frequent among non-survivors. Among survivors, longer invasive mechanical ventilation was associated with prolonged NICU stay. Together, these findings suggest that impaired fetal growth and early physiological compromise are key markers of mortality risk in this tertiary Ecuadorian NICU. The main contribution of this study is the generation of local evidence from a middle-income country setting where neonatal case-mix, referral pathways, institutional resources, and population characteristics may differ from those reported in high-income countries. Rather than identifying entirely novel risk factors, this study confirms the relevance of known determinants of neonatal mortality within a real-world Ecuadorian tertiary referral NICU and evaluates their combined use in local risk prediction.

Comparison with previous evidence

The association between SGA status and mortality is consistent with international evidence showing that fetal growth restriction increases the risk of neonatal death, particularly when combined with prematurity [6, 7]. Previous studies have shown that SGA neonates may have reduced physiological reserve and higher susceptibility to respiratory failure, metabolic instability, infection, and other complications [8]. In the present cohort, SGA status was more frequent among non-survivors and remained independently associated with mortality after adjustment, suggesting that fetal growth status provided prognostic information beyond birth weight alone. Studies from high-income neonatal networks have emphasized the importance of gestational age, birth weight, illness severity, respiratory support, and unit-level care practices in determining neonatal outcomes [13, 15, 21]. Our findings are consistent with these reports in showing that early clinical instability and respiratory disease severity are strongly associated with mortality and resource utilization. However, the present cohort differs from many high-income settings because it reflects a public tertiary referral hospital in Ecuador, where admissions may include a heterogeneous mixture of preterm infants, growth-restricted neonates, congenital anomalies, severe infection, and neonates referred from high-risk pregnancies. These differences support the need for local validation before applying prediction models developed in other healthcare systems. The high frequency of SGA in this cohort deserves careful interpretation. In a general birth population, SGA is expected to affect approximately 10% of infants when an appropriate growth reference is used. In the present study, the observed proportion was substantially higher, most likely because the cohort was not population-based but restricted to critically ill neonates admitted to a tertiary NICU.

The hospital’s role as a referral center for high-risk pregnancies may have enriched the cohort for placental dysfunction, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, prematurity, and fetal growth restriction. Nevertheless, because population-specific Ecuadorian neonatal growth references and high-altitude-adjusted curves were not available, some misclassification of SGA status cannot be excluded. Therefore, SGA should be interpreted as a clinically useful risk marker in this cohort, but not as definitive evidence of fetal growth restriction in every case. Birth weight, clinical severity, and mortality

The exploratory birth-weight analysis showed that neonates weighing < 1400 g had lower gestational age, greater frequency of SGA status, more maternal and obstetric risk factors, and higher resource utilization than neonates weighing ≥ 1400 g. These findings confirm that the lower-birth-weight subgroup represented a clinically more vulnerable population. However, birth weight category was not independently associated with mortality in the adjusted model. This result suggests that mortality risk was not explained by weight alone. Instead, it likely reflected the combined effect of fetal growth status, gestational age, congenital conditions, and early postnatal illness severity. The 1400 g cutoff should therefore be interpreted only as an exploratory cohort-specific threshold, not as a standard neonatal weight category. This distinction is important because very-low-birthweight or extremely-low-birth-weight classifications used in other studies may not be directly comparable with the present subgroup analysis.

Early clinical severity and respiratory support

Early hemodynamic instability, metabolic acidosis, elevated lactate, high FiO₂ requirement, thrombocytopenia, and elevated oxygenation index were more frequent among non-survivors. These variables are clinically plausible markers of circulatory failure, impaired tissue perfusion, respiratory failure, and systemic illness. Their addition to nested prediction models improved discrimination, indicating that early physiological data added prognostic value beyond baseline maternal and perinatal variables. However, these variables should be interpreted primarily as severity markers rather than baseline causal predictors. Because they were measured after birth and during the early phase of NICU care, they may lie on the causal pathway between prenatal risk factors and death. For this reason, the study separated the baseline explanatory model from predictive models that incorporated early clinical severity, laboratory, and respiratory variables. Respiratory support was also closely related to clinical course and resource use. Neonates who died required longer invasive mechanical ventilation and more frequent high-frequency ventilation, while among survivors, each additional day of invasive mechanical ventilation was associated with longer NICU stay. These findings are consistent with the central role of respiratory disease severity in neonatal intensive care. They also have practical relevance for low- and middle-income settings, where prolonged mechanical ventilation increases demand for trained personnel, equipment, monitoring capacity, blood products, and intensive care beds.

Predictive model performance

The mortality prediction model showed good apparent discrimination and acceptable calibration after bootstrapping internal validation. The optimism-corrected AUC suggested that the model retained stable performance within the available dataset. The progressive increase in AUC across nested models indicated that early clinical severity, laboratory, and respiratory variables improved prediction beyond baseline maternal and perinatal factors. Nevertheless, the model should be considered exploratory and internally validated only. Its performance may differ in other NICUs with different admission criteria, patient characteristics, referral systems, altitude, staffing, and resource availability. External validation in independent Ecuadorian and Latin American neonatal cohorts is required before the model can be recommended for routine clinical use. Future prediction models should also incorporate standardized neonatal severity scores, detailed respiratory parameters, non-invasive ventilation data, surfactant therapy, timing and completeness of antenatal corticosteroids, and dynamic changes during the first days of hospitalization.

Clinical implications

These findings have practical implications for neonatal intensive care in Ecuador and similar middle-income settings. Early recognition of SGA status may help identify neonates at increased mortality risk, particularly when accompanied by hemodynamic instability, metabolic acidosis, high oxygen requirement, thrombocytopenia, or elevated oxygenation index. Because SGA may reflect cumulative prenatal risk, its identification at admission can support closer monitoring and structured risk assessment. The association between invasive mechanical ventilation duration and NICU length of stay also highlights respiratory management as a key determinant of resource utilization. Strategies aimed at optimizing respiratory care, including timely surfactant use, careful oxygen targeting, lung-protective ventilation, and early transition to non-invasive support when clinically feasible, may be important for reducing ventilator exposure and improving bed availability. However, because this study was observational, it cannot determine whether specific interventions would causally reduce mortality or length of stay. Local prediction models based on routinely available variables may support early risk stratification, communication with families, and resource planning. These tools should complement clinical judgment and should not replace individualized decision-making.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it provides local evidence from a public tertiary maternity and neonatal referral hospital in Ecuador, where published NICU outcome data remain limited. Second, the cohort reflects real-world clinical practice and includes maternal, perinatal, neonatal, laboratory, respiratory, clinical course, and resource utilization variables. Third, the analysis distinguished baseline characteristics from early severity markers and downstream clinical course variables, reducing the risk of inappropriate causal interpretation. Fourth, the study used complementary statistical approaches, including multivariable regression, survival analysis, generalized linear modeling, calibration assessment, bootstrap internal validation, decision curve analysis, sensitivity analysis, and subgroup evaluation.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective observational design limits causal inference and depends on the accuracy and completeness of medical records. Second, the study was conducted in a single tertiary referral hospital, which may limit generalizability to other hospitals, lower-complexity neonatal units, or population-based neonatal cohorts. Third, complete institutional-level denominator data were not available for all stages of cohort assembly, including total deliveries, total neonatal admissions, inborn and outborn admissions, intermediate-care admissions, and exclusions before construction of the analytical dataset. Therefore, a complete STROBE-style patient flow diagram could not be reconstructed without unverifiable assumptions. This limits assessment of potential selection bias. Fourth, missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, and detailed variable-level missingness before construction of the anonymized analytical dataset could not be fully reconstructed. Fifth, the birth-weight cutoff of < 1400 g was used only for exploratory stratification because it approximated the cohort median and does not correspond to a standard neonatal weight category. Internationally recognized VLBW and ELBW thresholds are < 1500 g and < 1000 g, respectively; therefore, the present subgroup analysis has limited comparability with studies using those standard categories. Sixth, SGA classification depended on the growth reference used in institutional records and Ministry of Health standards. Because Ecuador-specific neonatal growth references and high-altitude-adjusted growth curves were not available for this retrospective analysis, misclassification of fetal growth status cannot be excluded. This limitation is especially relevant given the high SGA prevalence observed in the cohort and Quito’s high-altitude setting, where birth weight distributions may differ from sea-level populations. Seventh, detailed data on non-invasive respiratory support, surfactant administration, ventilator settings, timing and completeness of antenatal corticosteroid exposure, and standardized neonatal severity scores were not consistently available. These variables may have improved interpretation of respiratory disease severity and prediction model performance. Finally, although the model underwent bootstrap internal validation, external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Future research

Future studies should include multicenter cohorts from different regions and levels of neonatal care in Ecuador and Latin America. Prospective data collection would allow more complete cohort assembly, better characterization of missing data, standardized SGA classification, and more accurate temporal assessment of prenatal risk factors, early clinical severity, interventions, and outcomes. External validation of the mortality prediction model is needed to determine its transportability and clinical usefulness. Future research should also evaluate the role of non-invasive ventilation, surfactant therapy, antenatal corticosteroid timing, infection biomarkers, standardized severity scores, and dynamic risk prediction during the first days of NICU admission.

Conclusion

In this cohort of critically ill inborn neonates admitted to a tertiary NICU in Ecuador, SGA status was independently associated with in-hospital mortality, while birth weight < 1400 g was not independently associated with mortality after adjustment. Early hemodynamic, metabolic, respiratory, and laboratory severity markers were more frequent among non-survivors, and longer invasive mechanical ventilation was associated with prolonged NICU stay among survivors. These findings support the importance of fetal growth assessment, early severity evaluation, and locally validated risk stratification in neonatal intensive care. However, the high prevalence of SGA should be interpreted cautiously because the cohort was restricted to critically ill neonates in a high-altitude tertiary referral setting and because high-altitude-adjusted growth references were not available. Although the prediction model showed promising internal performance, external validation and prospective multicenter studies using standardized growth references and standard birth-weight categories are needed before broader clinical application.
