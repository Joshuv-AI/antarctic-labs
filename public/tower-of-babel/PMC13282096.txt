Nirmatrelvir-Ritonavir–Induced Pancreatitis: A Concern in the COVID Era
Authors: Jake DeBroff, Joseph Abraham, Bo Hyung Yoon, Ian Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe World Health Organization reports that more than 500 drugs can cause drug-induced pancreatitis. Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (Paxlovid; Pfizer, Mission, KS) has been approved to prevent hospitalization and mortality among patients with COVID-19. A 71-year-old man presented with epigastric abdominal pain and weight loss after recently completing a 5-day course of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir. He was found to have acute pancreatitis which resolved with symptomatic treatment. Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir is a commonly used medication for the treatment of COVID-19. Ritonavir has been identified as 1 potential drug, which can cause pancreatitis. Our case report raises the awareness of the rare complication of pancreatitis from nirmatrelvir-ritonavir.

INTRODUCTION

Acute pancreatitis has become one of the leading gastrointestinal causes of hospitalizations in the United States. With more than 100,000 hospital admissions annually, pancreatitis is a major cause of morbidity and healthcare expenditure both in the country and worldwide.1,2 The World Health Organization currently reports more than 500 drugs known to be associated with drug-induced pancreatitis (DIP) which represents up to 5% of cases of acute pancreatitis.3 Since the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic onset in 2019, new antiviral agents have been rapidly developed and approved for widespread utilization. One such antiviral, nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (Paxlovid; Pfizer, Mission, KS), is made up of 2 components: (i) nirmatrelvir and (ii) ritonavir. Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir has been approved for use in preventing hospitalization and mortality among COVID-19 patients.4 We present a rare case of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir induced pancreatitis.

CASE REPORT

A 71-year-old man with a medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, prediabetes, nephrolithiasis, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, nonobstructive coronary artery disease, and a surgical history of an appendectomy presented to the emergency department with 3 days of epigastric abdominal pain and a 5-pound weight loss. The patient tested positive for COVID-19 1 week before, with the only reported symptom being a nonproductive cough. The patient was deemed to be a candidate for nirmatrelvir-ritonavir treatment and received a 5-day course. One day following completion of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, he began having abdominal pain and presented to the emergency department. The patient self-reported a maximum consumption of 6 alcoholic beverages per week and no history of alcohol use disorder. The patient reported taking verapamil, atorvastatin, aspirin, metformin, and a multivitamin regularly. No medications were recently added or required dosage adjustments. Physical examination revealed tenderness to palpation in the right and left upper quadrants of the abdomen. Bloodwork revealed normal liver function, a white blood cell count of 17.8 K/μL, and an elevated lipase of 509 U/L (Table 1). The patient had an elevated glucose level of 383 mg/dL, which was attributed to stress hyperglycemia, as he had been within prediabetic levels per his hemoglobin A1c before and after the event. On repeat bloodwork and without intervention the glucose levels normalized. The triglyceride level was measured to be 163 mg/dL. Further laboratory values are presented in Table 1. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen/pelvis was significant for mild peripancreatic fat stranding, most notably surrounding the distal pancreas body and tail suggesting acute pancreatitis (Figure 1). Owing to the characteristic abdominal pain, elevation in lipase, and CT findings, the patient was diagnosed with acute pancreatitis based on the Revised Atlanta Criteria.5 The patient received intravenous fluids, was able to tolerate food and oral medications, and was discharged to outpatient follow up.

DISCUSSION

During the COVID-19 pandemic, outpatient antiviral medications such as nirmatrelvir-ritonavir were rapidly developed to prevent hospitalizations and reduce mortality.4,6 Ritonavir is classified as a class IV medication for DIP, which is defined as being reported in at least 1 human based on case reports.7 Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir does not have a defined classification. To date, there has been only 1 published case report of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir induced pancreatitis in March of 2023.8 In our case the Naranjo adverse drug reaction probability scale revealed a score of +3, meaning the reaction being caused by nirmatrelvir-ritonavir is categorized as “possible.” However, this must be reconciled with the fact that many aspects of the scale do not apply to nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, which is a fixed regimen rather than a chronically used medication.9

While there is evidence that COVID-19 infection has extrapulmonary effects on the pancreas due to its expression of the angiotensin-converting enzyme-2 receptor on both acinar and islet cells, the time course of medication administration to onset of symptoms represents more than just a correlation in this case.10 A direct link between novel COVID-19 treatments and DIP has not been fully elucidated but some preliminary evidence exists. The broad spectrum antiviral remdesivir, for example, is a COVID-19 treatment which has been observed to cause acute pancreatitis.11 Interferon and lopinavir/ritonavir, initially investigated COVID-19 treatments which were not proven to be efficacious, have also been implicated in DIP.12–14 A South Korean study of over 150,000 patients receiving another antiviral molnupiravir found 0.01% of the cohort to have acute pancreatitis.15

In this case alternative causes of pancreatitis were ruled out. The patient was not taking any new medications nor was he taking any medications which interact with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir. He reported no more than 6 alcoholic drinks per week, did not have transaminase level elevations that would suggest chronic alcohol use, and was found to have no gallstones or dilation of the common bile duct on CT scan. The symptoms began immediately following the course of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, rather than during the mild COVID-19 infectious symptoms which indicates a drug induced pancreatitis rather than viral illness induced.

This case is unique as it highlights a patient possessing important risk factors for drug induced pancreatitis such as old age, multiple chronic medication consumption, and prior bowel inflammation with only slightly elevated triglycerides.12 Studies have proposed a protease inhibitor (PI)-induced hypertriglyceridemia being 1 possible mechanism of acute pancreatitis; however, direct toxic effects may be responsible as well.8,16 Other proposed mechanisms of PI induced damage include delayed idiosyncratic or lymphocyte-T–mediated effects, direct acinar toxicity related to elevated free fatty acids, and mitochondrial dysfunction through reactive oxygen species and increased apoptosis.17–19 In addition, ritonavir is an inhibitor of cytochrome P450 and can cause drug-drug interactions that may lead to pancreatitis.20 In this case, the patient had slightly elevated triglyceride levels, as opposed to the significant elevations seen in previously reported cases, which points to alternative mechanisms as the cause of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir induced pancreatitis.

While not a novel account, this case works to build on 1 prior report of DIP from nirmatrelvir-ritonavir use, as volume of cases plays a crucial factor in the classification of drugs which can cause pancreatitis.17 A single case does not prove causality in DIP, but the timing of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir administration and diagnosis of acute pancreatitis in addition to lack of other causes reveals a significant connection in this case. Case report accumulation contributes to a stronger argument for pharmacovigilance and practitioner awareness when prescribing nirmatrelvir-ritonavir.
