Executive Summary

Soil structure, which can be described as the aggregation and distribution of pore spaces, regulates carbon,
nutrient, and water cycling across the Earth system. Yet the inability to make quantitative, dynamic, in situ
measurements of soil structure and rhizosphere carbon flow has prevented meaningful incorporation of soil
structural processes into Earth System Models (ESMs). The key limitations are: (i) Lack of scale integration
between micron-scale soil structure and ecosystem-scale models, (ii) Poor functional linkage between soil
structural properties and biogeochemical processes, and (iii) Absence of dynamic 3D measurements of
rhizosphere structural changes and carbon transformations.

To address these challenges, we developed a new integrated positron emission tomography (PET) - micro-
computed tomography (CT) imaging platform that enables the first 4D (spatial 3D and time), non-invasive,
quantitative imaging of carbon allocation and rhizosphere structural dynamics in living plants and intact
soils. The system combines:

 • Rhizo-PET (R-PET): a high-resolution positron emission tomography scanner optimized for
 11
 CO₂ tracing in plant roots.
 • a-Se micro-CT: a high-contrast, high-spatial resolution CT system based on amorphous selenium
 (a-Se) direct conversion technology, enabling micron-scale visualization of soil structure and root–
 soil interfaces.

Together, these advances allow us to quantify how carbon exudates move, transform, and stabilize within
the rhizosphere, directly informing missing processes in BER-relevant carbon cycle models.

 A. Project Goal

The project aimed to address three fundamental scientific questions critical for BER’s goal of improving
soil-carbon representation in Earth System Models:

 1. 3D Coordination of Rhizospheric Hotspots: Is there a coordinated spatio-temporal relationship
 between dynamic carbon hotspots and the soil physicochemical matrix?
 2. Root Exudate Partitioning: Are substantial fractions of recently photosynthesized carbon being
 microbially respired in the rhizosphere, but misclassified in current models as root respiration?
 3. Stabilization of Root Exudates: What fraction of root exudates becomes stabilized by mineral
 (especially clay) adsorption?

These questions require non-invasive imaging tools capable of resolving carbon dynamics at millimeter to
sub-millimeter scales, which are capabilities that are unavailable until now. We developed and deployed a
high resolution PET system for dynamic carbon tracking that uses short half-life 11CO₂ to label
photosynthate in real time, captures carbon transport from leaves to roots to soil, and enables quantification
of exudate deposition and microbial use.

We demonstrated the capability of a-Se on small pixel CMOS (8 um) and have initiated a scalable CMOS
design to enable large area (20 cm in length) CMOS-Se hybrid detector. By integrating 3D PET and 3D
CT, the combined PET–CT system provides a four-dimensional view of the rhizosphere structure, carbon
flow, hotspot formation, microbial–plant interaction zones, and exudate diffusion and stabilization. This
approach provides new mechanistic insight previously inaccessible with destructive or low-resolution
methods.

 B. Development of the Rhizo-PET (R-PET) Scanner

We designed and built a high resolution PET system optimized for plant root imaging consisting of:

 • Eight modular detector panels in octagonal geometry
 • 160-mm radial × 48-mm axial field of view, optimized for potted plants
 • H8500 PSPMT + LYSO array (1 mm pitch, 10 mm crystals)
 • 12-bit digitization at 256 MSPS with FPGA-based coincidence logic
 • Energy window: 400–600 keV
 • 0.5 mm³ voxel reconstruction using maximum-likelihood expectation-maximization (MLEM)

The system includes a custom two-chamber shoot root partition that prevents 11CO₂ gas from entering the
imaging volume while allowing controlled labeling of plant leaves. A custom annulus phantom
(normalization phantom) filled with 18F solution provides detector uniformity calibration, ensuring
quantitative imaging essential for BER-relevant carbon flux estimation. For the imaging sample
configuration, plants (Phaseolus vulgaris) were grown in a pot divided into alternating soil quadrants (fine
organic vs. coarse Arkansas soil). A 50 μm mesh barrier confined root growth while preserving water and
nutrient flow. This geometry enabled controlled mapping of carbon exudation into distinct physicochemical
zones.

 C. a-Se Micro-CT Development

We performed a detailed characterization of an amorphous-selenium (a-Se)/CMOS direct-conversion
imaging technology for quantitative soil structure analysis. Its high DQE and minimal lateral charge spread

enables 10-20 µm imaging of soil pore geometry. This CT capability is essential for linking structural soil
features to functional carbon processes, directly addressing a major BER knowledge gap.

D. Experimental Results

High-resolution R-PET images captured root morphology, growth zones, and dynamic arrival of labeled
carbon to the rhizosphere. A representative hotspot appeared 80–90 minutes after introducing 11CO₂,
corresponding to photosynthetic fixation, phloem transport to roots, exudation into surrounding soil, and
early microbial metabolism. These dynamic PET observations demonstrate, for the first time, real-time
visualization of carbon allocation into soil under living conditions.

Using 3D PET images and region of interest (ROI)-based kinetic analysis, we quantified the spatio-temporal
distribution of carbon exudates.The zone around each root segment was divided radially into the following:

 • 0 mm (root surface)
 • 1.4 mm (immediate rhizosphere)
 • 2.8 mm (outer rhizosphere)

This framework enabled extraction of time-activity curves (TACs), time to peak (TTP), and area under
curve (AUC) along the entire length of the root (top, middle, bottom sections). Findings included observing
~50% of carbon arriving at the root surface diffused into the immediate rhizosphere (0–1.4 mm). Only
~10% diffused to the 2.8 mm outer rhizosphere. Diffusion rates and concentrations were heterogeneous
along the root axis, revealing localized zones of microbial activity and root physiology differences.

These findings provide the first empirical, non-destructive confirmation of the classic model of the
rhizosphere: The “immediate rhizosphere” (0–2 mm from the root surface) is the primary zone of
microbial–plant interactions and carbon transformation. This establishes a quantitative, mechanistic
foundation for modeling carbon stabilization, predicting microbial activity patterns, constraining soil
carbon residence times, and building rhizosphere process modules for ESMs.

The a-Se micro-CT system revealed pore size distribution, aggregate geometry, root–soil contact quality,
and micropore networks that regulate carbon diffusion. We will overlay the CT structure with PET hotspot
locations to examine preferential exudate accumulation in zones of higher micro-porosity, structural
bottlenecks influencing diffusion, and textural differences between coarse and fine soil and their impact on
carbon stabilization. This CT-PET co-registration directly advances BER needs to quantify structure–
function relationships in soils.

 D. Conclusion and Major Scientific Contributions to BER Mission

This project delivered the first integrated dynamic imaging platform capable of quantitatively linking soil
structure to carbon flow to microbial activity in the rhizosphere. The Rhizo-PET and a-Se micro-CT
systems reveal previously inaccessible dynamics that are essential for parameterizing rhizosphere carbon
processes in Earth System Models. These findings advance BER’s mission by providing mechanistic data,
new measurement capabilities, and validated conceptual models for soil biogeochemical cycling.

New Measurement Capability: We developed the first 4D PET-CT platform for rhizosphere science,
enabling non-destructive, temporally resolved studies of carbon transport and soil structural control.

Mechanistic Insight: We discovered that (i) exudate diffusion is highly localized, (ii) microbial activity
zones correlate with soil structure, and (iii) soil texture alters carbon stabilization pathways.

Pathway to Improved Earth System Models: The project establishes measurable parameters for rhizosphere
carbon residence times, exudate diffusion coefficients, microbial uptake efficiencies, and structure-
dependent stabilization processes. The data directly supports NSF/DOE efforts to incorporate rhizosphere
processes into next-generation ESMs.
