Urinary exosomal miR-132-3p as a novel non‑invasive biomarker for kidney scarring in pediatric vesicoureteral reflux patients
Authors: Qi Chen, Jiajing Qiu, Liqu Huang, Yunfei Guo, Xun Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the potential of urinary exosomal miRNA as a predictor of kidney scarring in children with vesicoureteral reflux (VUR).MethodsA prospective study was conducted in pediatrics diagnosed with VUR between September 2023 to December 2025. Urinary exosomes were isolated from patients via ultracentrifugation and subsequently characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and Western blot. The expression levels of exosomal miRNAs were profiled using miRNA sequencing and validated by quantitative real-time PCR. Kidney scarring was determined based on dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) scintigraphy, and its association with the identified urinary exosomal miRNAs was investigated.ResultsThis prospective study enrolled 81 patients, comprising 33 males and 48 females, with a median age of 27 months (IQR: 8–82). Urinary exosomal miR-132-3p expression was significantly associated with higher reflux grade, bilateral reflux, and the presence of kidney scarring. Multivariate logistic analysis showed that urinary exosomal miR-132-3p (OR = 4.937, 95% CI: 1.906–12.781, P < 0.001) was a significant predictor of kidney scarring with a high predictive value with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.908 (95% CI: 0.823–0.961, P < 0.001).ConclusionIn this exploratory single-centre cohort, urinary exosomal miR-132-3p serves as a promising non-invasive biomarker for the prediction of kidney scarring in children with VUR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07137-2.Keywords: Urinary exosome, MiR-132-3p, Vesicoureteral reflux, Predictor, Kidney scarring

Introduction

Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), defined as the retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into the upper urinary tract, is one of the most common congenital urological anomalies among the pediatric population, with an estimated incidence of 1–2% [1, 2]. VUR is classified into five grades, and high reflux grade is associated with recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI), which can lead to kidney scarring [3, 4]. In pediatric patients diagnosed with VUR, the development of kidney scarring represents a major clinical concern, given its strong association with long-term complications including hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD) or even progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [5–7]. Consequently, early detection and longitudinal monitoring of kidney scarring are critical for risk stratification and the implementation of timely clinical interventions.

Currently, dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) renal scintigraphy is regarded as the gold standard for detecting kidney scarring [8]. However, the diagnostic accuracy of DMSA scanning is limited in identifying early kidney lesion. Furthermore, the high cost and radiation exposure associated with DMSA scanning further limit its widespread applicability in clinical practice [9]. In light of these challenges, there has been increasing interest in exploring the potential non-invasive biomarkers derived from blood and urine as a promising alternative for detecting kidney scarring in children with VUR.

Non-invasive biomarkers derived from blood and urine, such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), periostin, and inflammatory cytokines, have demonstrated promising diagnostic value in detecting kidney scarring and reducing the reliance on DMSA scanning [10–13]. Exosomes are lipid-bilayer vesicles ranging between 30 and 150 nm, which carry various biomolecules including proteins, lipids and miRNAs to mediate intercellular communication through secretion and endocytosis [14]. Previous studies have reported that exosome-mediated communication is a key mechanism in kidney injury [15–17]. However, the role of urinary exosomes in kidney scarring secondary to VUR remains to be further elucidated.

This study aimed to characterize urinary exosomes in VUR and evaluate its potential as a predictive biomarker for kidney scarring.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective study was conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University from September 2023 to December 2025, with approval from the Institutional Review Board (Approval number: 202301017-1). All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki principles, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians.

Patients and outcomes

The study included pediatric patients who were diagnosed with VUR. Participants were enrolled according to the following inclusion criteria: VUR diagnosis was confirmed by voiding cystourethrography (VCUG). DMSA scanning was conducted in all participants at least six months after the most recent UTI episode to determine kidney scarring and the results were interpreted by two nuclear medicine physicians using a double-blind method. The exclusion criteria were as follows: congenital anomalies (including renal dysplasia, posterior urethral valves, and neurogenic bladder), antibiotics use within 1 month, current UTI, glomerulonephritis, nephrotic syndrome, diabetic nephropathy, history of urological surgery and any other condition that may influence urinary exosome such as autoimmune disorders, malignancies, chronic inflammatory diseases, and CKD.

According to the presence of kidney scarring as confirmed by DMSA scanning, VUR patients were divided into two groups: patients with kidney scarring (Scar+ group), and those without scarring (Scar- group). The primary outcome was to evaluate urinary exosome levels between the two groups. The secondary outcome was to investigate the relationship between urinary exosomal miRNAs and kidney scarring.

Exosomes isolation and identification

Exosomes were harvested from morning urine using differential centrifugation at 4 °C. The protocol began with sequential centrifugation steps at 300 × g for 5 min, 2,000 × g for 20 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min to deplete the samples of cells, debris, and microvesicles. Following filtration of the clarified supernatant through a 0.22 μm membrane, the exosomes were pelleted via ultracentrifugation at 110,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, USA). The morphology of urinary exosomes was examined using transmission electron microscopy (HITACHI H7650 TEM; Tokyo, Japan). The size distribution and particle concentration of purified exosomes were determined using the ZetaView platform (Particle Metrix, Germany). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2023) guidelines from the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles [18].

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and their concentrations were determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Keygen Biotech, Nanjing, China). Western blot analysis was carried out as described in our previous report [19], with densitometric quantification performed using ImageJ software (version 1.51, USA). The primary antibodies used in this experiment are CD9 (Abcam, USA, ab236630), CD63 (Abcam, USA, ab134045) and CD81 (Abcam, USA, ab79559).

miRNA expression profiling

Total RNA was extracted from urinary exosomes with the miRNeasy Micro Kit (217084, QIAGEN, Germany), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. For expression profiling, RNA samples were processed using the miRCURY LNA Array (version 11.0, Exiqon, Denmark); labeling and hybridization were conducted in accordance with Exiqon’s standard procedures. Array scanning was performed on an Axon GenePix 4000B scanner. The resulting data were then analyzed to identify miRNAs differentially expressed between the two groups, defined by thresholds of fold change and statistical significance. Finally, hierarchical clustering analysis was employed to visualize and distinguish the miRNA expression signatures among samples.

For miRNA analysis, reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR were performed using the All-in-One miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit and the All-in-One miRNA qPCR Kit (QP015, GeneCopoeia, USA), respectively. U6 snRNA served as an endogenous control for normalization. The miR-132-3p/U6 ratio was calculated using the 2−ΔCt method, where ΔCt = Ct(miR-132-3p) - Ct(U6). Specific primers for miRNAs and U6 were obtained from GeneCopoeia, and their sequences are as follows: miR-132-3p (5’-CGTAACAGTCCAGCCATG-3’, 3’-TGGTGTCGTGGAGTCG-5’); U6 (5’-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3’, 3’-AACGCTTCAGAATTTGCGT-5’).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), based on the normality of distribution. Group comparisons were conducted using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For comparisons of miRNAs expression across multiple reflux grade groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used due to heterogeneous variances. Dunn’s post‑hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple pairwise comparisons. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to evaluate the predictive value of urinary exosomal miRNAs, with results reported as the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal cutoff value was determined by balancing sensitivity and specificity. ROC curves were generated using MedCalc software (version 20.015) and the 95% confidence interval for the AUC was calculated using the DeLong test. Calibration of the combined model was assessed using the Brier score and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05 indicating good calibration). A calibration plot was generated, and the calibration intercept and slope were calculated. Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated with 1,000 bootstrap resamples. For NRI and IDI calculations, the reference model was the clinical model containing reflux grade and laterality (the two clinical variables that showed significance in univariable analysis). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate net benefit. All statistical tests were performed with Stata (version 15.1), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristic

A total of 81 patients were enrolled in this study. According to the DMSA scanning, 18 patients (22.2%) were divided into Scar+ group and 63 patients (77.8%) were Scar- group (Fig. 1). The median age of the cohort was 27 months (IQR: 8–82), comprising 33 males (40.7%) and 48 females (59.3%). The median weight was 16 kg (IQR: 9.5–26), and bilateral reflux was present in 16 patients (19.8%). Compared to the Scar- group, the Scar+ group exhibited significantly higher reflux grade (P < 0.001) and a greater incidence of bilateral reflux (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to gender, age, weight, and history of pre-UTI. Isolation and characterization of urinary exosomes

Exosomes were isolated from equal volumes of urine obtained from both groups (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that urinary exosomes derived from the Scar- and Scar+ groups exhibited the characteristic cup‑shaped morphology of exosomes (Fig. 2B). The mean particle sizes of exosomes purified from the Scar- and Scar+ groups were 95.8 ± 28.2 nm and 98.3 ± 29.4 nm, respectively (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of exosomal markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 in both groups (Fig. 2D). Notably, the Scar+ group exhibited higher urinary exosome particle concentration compared to the Scar- group (Figure S1).

Expression of miRNAs

To profile miRNA expression, miRNAs derived from urinary exosomes were analyzed using miRNA sequencing. Comparative analysis revealed a distinct expression pattern between the Scar + and Scar- groups, characterized by significant fold changes (Fig. 3A). Hierarchical clustering identified 174 miRNAs with significantly differential expression, consisting of 88 that were upregulated and 86 that were downregulated (Fig. 3B). Functional enrichment analysis via KEGG indicated that the targets of these differentially expressed miRNAs are primarily involved in pathways such as metabolism, cancer, PI3K-Akt signaling, Rap1 signaling, calcium signaling, and actin cytoskeleton regulation (Fig. 3C). The ten most significantly up- and down-regulated exosomal miRNAs were listed in Figure S2. Among the top miRNAs, miR−132−3p was previously reported to promote kidney injury [20–22], and it was selected for further studies.

Predictive value of urinary exosomal miR-132-3p in VUR

According to the reflux grade, the relative urinary exosomal miR-132-3p expression was significantly higher in grade 3 (P = 0.005), grade 4 (P < 0.001) and grade 5 (P < 0.001) compared to grade 1. While no significant difference was found between grade 1 and grade 2 (P > 0.99) (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the relative urinary exosomal miR-132-3p expression was significantly higher in bilateral reflux group compared to unilateral reflux group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). And the relative urinary exosomal miR-132-3p expression was significantly higher in Scar+ group compared to Scar- group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4C).

To determine independent predictors of kidney scarring, a binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. The findings demonstrated that urinary exosomal miR−132−3p (OR = 4.937, 95% CI: 1.906–12.781, P < 0.001) was an independent risk factor. In contrast, other variables, such as reflux grade and laterality, showed no statistically significant association with the development of renal scarring (Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis of kidney scarring

To further evaluate the predictive performance of urinary exosomal miR−132−3p for kidney scarring, ROC curve analysis was conducted, with the corresponding curve presented in Fig. 4D. The results demonstrated that urinary exosomal miR−132−3p possessed substantial predictive accuracy for kidney scarring, yielding an AUC of 0.908 (95% CI: 0.823–0.961, P < 0.001), and the best cutoff value of urinary exosomal miR−132−3p was 2.861, with the sensitivity of 77.78% and the specificity of 92.06%. The calibration curve (Figure S3A) also showed good accordance between the predicted and actual probability of kidney scarring. In the study, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were used to evaluate the potential benefit of urinary exosomal miR−132−3p. Using the clinical model containing reflux grade and laterality as the reference, addition of urinary exosomal miR-132-3p yielded an NRI of 0.42 (P = 0.003) and an IDI of 0.19 (P < 0.001). The DCA curve was shown in Figure S3B. The NRI, IDI, and DCA indicated that adding urinary exosomal miR-132-3p to the clinical model (reflux grade and laterality) improved risk classification and net benefit in average predicted probabilities of kidney scarring.

Discussion

In children with VUR, the development of kidney scarring represents a major clinical concern. As the terminal pathological outcome of acute pyelonephritis progressing to irreversible fibrosis, kidney scarring is histologically characterized by tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis, and peritubular capillary rarefaction [7]. These scars can impede renal growth and lead to impaired renal function, with bilateral and high grade reflux further elevated the risk of kidney fibrosis [23].

Current diagnostic modalities for detecting kidney scarring, particularly DMSA renal scintigraphy, present with several challenges, including high costs, radiation exposure, limited availability in resource-constrained settings, and an inability to detect early fibrotic changes prior to irreversible injury [9]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for reliable and non-invasive biomarkers.

Exosomes, a class of extracellular vesicles released by virtually all cell types, were initially regarded as cellular garbage. However, over the past decades, a growing body of evidence has established their pivotal role in intercellular communication by transferring cell-specific cargo to recipient cells, including proteins, lipids, and miRNAs [15]. Our previous research demonstrated that platelet-derived extracellular vesicles aggravate septic acute kidney injury via delivering ARF6 [19]. Among the various functional cargos, miRNAs, a class of small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression by binding to target mRNAs, have recently attracted great attention [24]. They are frequently enriched in exosomes and, through vesicle-mediated intercellular communication, have emerged as critical regulators of cellular function. Urinary exosomal miR-19b-3p has been identified as a significant correlate of tubulointerstitial inflammation severity in diabetic nephropathy [17]. Similarly, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes have been found to promote recovery from ischemic kidney injury through miR-125b-5p-mediated modulation of the p53 pathway, which suppresses tubular epithelial cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [25]. While the critical involvement of miRNAs in renal and inflammatory conditions is increasingly recognized, the specific role of exosomal miRNAs in VUR-induced kidney scarring remains poorly understood.

Given the established pathophysiology of VUR, the present study was designed to evaluate the potential of urinary exosomes as biomarkers for VUR-associated kidney scarring. We hypothesized that assessing urinary exosomal miRNAs would provide enhanced specificity for detecting kidney pathology, particularly tubular injury and inflammation. In contrast to circulating serum biomarkers, which often reflect systemic physiological states, urinary exosomes originate directly from the kidney, thereby offering a more accurate reflection of kidney function.

In present study, we prospectively enrolled 81 VUR patients and isolated urinary exosomes for research. The results showed that patients with kidney scarring exhibited significantly higher normalized urinary exosome particle concentration compared to those without kidney scarring. To elucidate the role of urinary exosomes in kidney scarring, exosomal miRNA expression were analyzed using miRNA sequencing. According to the expression levels of fold change, 88 miRNAs were significantly upregulated and 86 miRNAs were significantly downregulated in Scar+ group, compared to Scar- group. Further KEGG functional enrichment analysis revealed that the targets of these differentially expressed miRNAs are enriched in pathways of metabolism, cancer, PI3K-Akt signaling, Rap1 signaling, calcium signaling, and actin cytoskeleton regulation. Each of these pathways has well‑documented roles in renal fibrogenesis. Chen et al. [26] reported that PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway regulates pericyte-myofibroblast transition which contributes to renal fibrosis. Liang et al. [27] suggested that PP2Acα promotes macrophage accumulation and activation, hence accelerates tubular cell death and kidney fibrosis through regulating Rap1 activation and TNF-α production. Naillat F et al. [28] proposed that calcium signaling induces partial epithelial-to-mesenchyme transition and renal fibrosis in mouse models. Therefore, the enrichment of these pathways in our results supports the hypothesis that miR-132-3p contributes to VUR‑associated scarring through a multi‑pathway network rather than a single linear mechanism.

In the present study, miR-132-3p was identified as one of the most upregulated miRNAs in urinary exosomes from patients with kidney scarring. Notably, this miRNA has been implicated in the pathophysiology of acute kidney injury, where its expression is significantly altered following ischemia-reperfusion injury and contributes to the promotion of inflammation and apoptosis [22]. Beyond the kidney, miR-132-3p has been shown to drive esophageal squamous cell carcinoma tumorigenesis via suppression of KCNK2 and has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of interstitial lung disease [29]. However, as the present study is a prospective exploratory investigation, future research must include both healthy controls and a control group of children with UTI but without VUR. Thus, miR-132-3p should be regarded as a candidate marker of renal involvementor pathology rather than a VUR‑specific biomarker. Moreover, experiments at the animal and cellular levels are needed to further elucidate the role of miR-132-3p in the development and progression of renal scarring.

This is not the first experiment to explore the non‑invasive biomarker for kidney scarring in pediatric vesicoureteral reflux patients. Farsani RM et al. suggested that urinary periostin is a highly sensitive and specific non-invasive biomarker for detecting kidney scarring in VUR, with the AUC of 0.869 [13]. Nickavar A et al. showed that the levels of urine neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) were significantly higher in patients with VUR and parenchymal function compared to those with normal DMSA scan [10]. In this exploratory single-centre cohort, the results suggest that urinary exosomal miR-132-3p may provide added clinical value beyond readily available clinical variables, although external validation is required. Several observations merit discussion. First, the AUC of urinary exosomal miR-132-3p in our cohort (0.908) is numerically higher than most published estimates for NGAL and periostin, suggesting potentially superior discrimination for established scarring. However, we acknowledge that a head-to-head comparison within the same patient cohort would be required to confirm this advantage. Second, unlike NGAL and periostin, which are predominantly elevated during the acute or subacute phase after UTI, miR-132-3p was measured at a later time point when scarring had already formed. This difference in timing suggests that these biomarkers are not redundant but potentially complementary: NGAL or periostin could be used for early risk stratification, while miR-132-3p might serve as a confirmatory test for the presence of irreversible scarring. Urinary exosomal miRNAs were detected concurrently with DMSA and VCUG, demonstrating their potential as a non-invasive diagnostic adjunct for identifying children with existing renal scarring. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these miRNA signatures can predict the subsequent development of chronic scarring when measured during the acute phase of pyelonephritis, before DMSA changes become apparent.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, we did not collect the number of febrile UTI episodes per patient (only the presence or absence of prior UTI was recorded); thus, we could not adjust for this potentially important confounder. Future studies should include this variable to determine whether miR-132-3p provides incremental predictive value beyond infection frequency. Second, longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to monitor dynamic changes in urinary exosomal miR-132-3p levels and to further elucidate its relationship with the progression of kidney scarring and other VUR-associated complications. Third, the complexity of the current isolation of urinary exosomes and subsequent miRNA quantification is a clear limitation, but ongoing technical advances may improve its clinical practicality. Future studies should perform direct head-to-head comparisons of multiple biomarkers in the same cohort and develop simplified, standardized detection platforms. Lastly, unnecessary DMSA renal scintigraphy may be avoided in cases of mild VUR and negative urinary exosomal miR-132-3p, but prospective validation is needed before clinical implementation.

Conclusion

In this exploratory single-centre cohort, urinary exosomal miR-132-3p is a promising non-invasive biomarker for detecting kidney scarring in pediatric VUR patients and a large-scale, multi-centre prospective diagnostic accuracy study is urgently needed to validate these results.
