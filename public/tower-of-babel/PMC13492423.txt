Sacubitril/Valsartan in Anthracycline-Treated Patients
Authors: Luigi Spadafora, Massimiliano Camilli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical AbstractKey Words: anthracyclines, cardio-oncology, cardiotoxicity, pharmacotherapy, prevention, sacubitril/valsartan

Anthracyclines represent a cornerstone of systemic anticancer therapy1; however, their use is limited by their well-recognized cardiotoxic potential, which may manifest as cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD).1 Contemporary cardio-oncology practice emphasizes the early identification of subclinical myocardial injury, although robust evidence guiding pharmacologic cardioprotection remains limited.1 Sacubitril/valsartan, an angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor, has demonstrated consistent benefits in heart failure populations, but its role in patients exposed to anthracyclines has not been systematically evaluated.

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials testing sacubitril/valsartan in patients receiving anthracycline-based chemotherapy (CRD420261278068). PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to January 2026. Eligible studies enrolled adults exposed to anthracyclines (with or without trastuzumab) and compared sacubitril/valsartan with placebo or standard care for the prevention of CTRCD. Two reviewers independently screened studies.

The primary endpoint was CTRCD according to the 2022 European Society of Cardiology cardio-oncology guidelines.2 Secondary outcomes included changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), global longitudinal strain (GLS), and symptomatic hypotension. Risk ratios and mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were pooled using random-effects models. For continuous outcomes, between-group changes from baseline were preferentially extracted; however, if these were not available, post-treatment differences were calculated using the summary data. Heterogeneity was assessed using Q and I2 statistics. Risk for bias was evaluated with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool.

Our literature search identified 753 records. Sixteen underwent full-text review, with 3 studies included in the final analysis;3, 4, 5 13 were excluded because of nonrandomized design, use of the drug outside the preventive setting, or nonanthracycline regimens. A total of 352 patients were analyzed (mean age 52 ± 9 years, 94% with breast cancer). CTRCD outcomes were heterogeneously defined across studies; thus, no summary statistic was derived. In Hsu et al,3 CTRCD (the primary endpoint) was defined as either a relative ≥15% decline in GLS or a reduction in LVEF ≥10% to a value <50%. No patient in the sacubitril/valsartan group developed CTRCD, whereas 26.3% of patients in the standard care group did; all events were captured exclusively by the GLS criterion, with none meeting the LVEF threshold. In PRADA II (Prevention of Cardiac Dysfunction During Adjuvant Breast Cancer Therapy),4 the primary endpoint was LVEF change by cardiac magnetic resonance; echocardiography-based CTRCD per European Society of Cardiology criteria was a prespecified secondary outcome. Moderate or severe CTRCD was uncommon (2 placebo-treated patients, none with sacubitril/valsartan), whereas mild CTRCD (ie, asymptomatic patients with LVEFs ≥50% and new GLS declines >15% and/or biomarker elevations) occurred in 83% of placebo vs 71% of sacubitril/valsartan patients. In the SARAH (Sacubitril-Valsartan for the Prevention of Anthracycline Cardiotoxicity in Patients With Elevated hs-cTnI Concentrations During Chemotherapy) trial5, deterioration in GLS >15% from baseline occurred in 4 patients (7.1%) with sacubitril/valsartan and in 14 (25.0%) with placebo, whereas LVEF reductions >10% to <53% occurred in 4 patients in both groups.

In the pooled analysis of LVEF change, no significant difference between groups was observed (MD: 0.69; 95% CI: −0.37 to 1.76; P = 0.20; I2 = 0.0%) (Figure 1A). A sensitivity analysis excluding PRADA II, in which LVEF was detected using cardiac magnetic resonance, yielded consistent results (MD: 0.78; 95% CI: −0.64 to 2.20; P = 0.28; I2 = 0.0%). For GLS, sacubitril/valsartan was associated with a favorable profile (MD: 1.08; 95% CI: 0.40-1.76; P = 0.002; I2 = 47.7%) (Figure 1B). Results remained directionally consistent in sensitivity analyses excluding Hsu et al,3 in which change estimates were not reported (MD: 1.23; 95% CI: 0.04-2.42; P = 0.040; I2 = 73.0%). GLS analyses were based on echocardiography across all studies.

Regarding safety, sacubitril/valsartan was associated with a significantly increased risk for symptomatic hypotension (risk ratio: 4.58; 95% CI: 1.55-13.47; P = 0.006; I2 = 0.0%) (Figure 1C).

For the first time, this analysis summarizes randomized evidence on the effect of sacubitril/valsartan on cardiac function in anthracycline-treated patients. Although the impact on CTRCD incidence could not be established, a higher risk for symptomatic hypotension was nonetheless detected.

Although several cardioprotective strategies have shown promise in early-phase studies, these findings have not been consistent in randomized trials. Previous studies have suggested that sacubitril/valsartan may improve cardiac function and functional class in patients with anthracycline-induced cardiac dysfunction;1 however, evidence in primary prevention has been lacking. Although no significant effect on LVEF was observed, there was a modest, favorable benefit on GLS with sacubitril/valsartan. There was also an increased incidence of symptomatic hypotension, which is clinically important, particularly in potentially vulnerable patients with cancer.

Several aspects of our research warrant careful interpretation. First, the limited number of studies and sample size may affect the robustness of findings. Study designs differed, including an open-label trial with unequal randomization and standard care surveillance as the comparator;3 moreover, populations were not fully comparable, with SARAH enrolling a higher risk cohort characterized by elevated troponin levels. In addition, estimates for continuous outcomes should be interpreted with caution because of methodological heterogeneity across studies and differences in how treatment effects were derived. Finally, the predominance of breast cancer limits generalizability.

In conclusion, current evidence is insufficient to determine whether sacubitril/valsartan reduces CTRCD risk because of heterogeneous definitions used in studies, despite signals suggesting attenuation of GLS deterioration. This potential subclinical benefit should be weighed against a higher incidence of symptomatic hypotension.
