Atomically dispersed Ce species adjacent to Ni nanoparticles as hydrogen scavengers for efficient catalytic ammonia decomposition
Authors: Zizhen Xiao, Han Yan, Hao Lei, Jin-Cheng Liu, Haofan Lei, Tao Zhou, Sunpei Hu, Xiaohong Xu, Yongjie Ye, Liangwei Liu, Wenlong Wu, Ming Zuo, Ke Gong, Shui Lin, Xiaobao Li, Hui Zhang, Lili Han, Jie Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCeO2 serves as an essential support or promoter for catalysts in ammonia decomposition, especially those based on Ni, yet its direct contribution to promoting NH3 activation and H2 desorption has rarely been understood. Here, we prepare a Ni-Ce-Al trinary catalyst with high-loading (66.5 wt%) Ni nanoparticles. Connected by the Al2O3 framework, these Ni nanoparticles are surrounded by abundant atomically dispersed Ce species consisting of Ce single atoms and clusters. The activity-stability trade-off induced by intrinsic Ni sintering on the CeO2 support is overcome, achieving a high H2 production rate of 324.0 mmol gcat−1 min−1 at 600 °C. The promoting effect of CeO2 has been elucidated, originating from the alleviation of hydrogen poisoning. Atomically dispersed Ce species with high hydrogen affinity serve as scavengers for adsorbed H atoms (H*). The strong tendency to form Ce3+‒OH groups facilitates the migration of H* from Ni nanoparticles to the oxide, releasing poisoned Ni sites and promoting NH3 activation.Subject terms: Catalyst synthesis, Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

H2 has been regarded as an ideal future energy source due to its high gravimetric energy density and zero carbon emission, yet its economic and safety risks during storage and transportation urge the development of hydrogen carriers1,2. As a non-carbon-containing chemical, ammonia is a promising storage medium for H2 owing to its high energy density (gravimetric energy density of 17.8 wt%). The low transportation cost and stable chemical properties of liquid NH3 are also suitable for distributed H2 production3–5. Unfortunately, the decomposition of ammonia demands higher temperatures (500‒800 °C) to achieve an appropriate hydrogen production rate, raising the need for high-performance catalysts6. Compared to other catalysts using Ru7–11, Mo12–14, Fe15–17, Co18–20, and alloys21–26 as active sites, Ni-based catalysts exhibit significant potential for large-scale applications as they are likely to balance the activity and cost26–32. Therefore, the development of Ni-based catalysts with high activity and stability for ammonia decomposition holds profound scientific significance and potential industrial value.

In recent years, researchers have devoted extensive efforts to improving the activity and stability of Ni-based catalysts. The introduction of CeO2 as a support or promoter has been proven effective in enhancing catalytic performance, attributed to the typical CeO2 role in facilitating electronic donation27,33–37 or increasing metal dispersion38,39. In addition to these effects, hydrogen spillover is widely reported in the CeO2-based catalyst37. The adsorbed H species would migrate from the Ni surface to CeO2, forming hydroxyl groups. However, the direct contribution of CeO2 in the catalytic reaction of ammonia decomposition has rarely been reported and understood. Potential promotion and the coherent mechanism of Ce species in NH3 activation and H2 desorption remain unclear. Besides, the direction modulation of the H* migration on the catalyst surface remains challenging. The establishment of the structure-performance relationship is hindered by the complexity of bulk CeO2, containing both active and inert Ce sites. Therefore, constructing catalysts with uniform active Ce sites is very likely to solve this problem and benefit the activity enhancement of Ni-based catalysts.

Herein, we prepared a Ni-Ce-Al trinary catalyst with high-loading Ni nanoparticles crowded by abundant atomically dispersed Ce species. Ni nanoparticles (66.5 wt%) with an average size of 12.9 nm were separated by an interconnected Al2O3 framework. Ce single atoms and clusters were located on the boundary of Al2O3 and Ni nanoparticles. The construction of the atomically dispersed Ce species broke the trade-off relationship between density and intrinsic activity of Ni sites, which existed when either CeO2 or Al2O3 was used alone as the support. The as-prepared catalyst showed enhanced performance compared to the typical Ni-based catalysts, achieving a H2 production rate of 324.0 mmol gcat−1 min−1 at 600 °C. The catalyst possessed good resistance to sintering, exhibiting stable NH3 conversion around 87% during a 200-h stability test at 600 °C. The kinetic study, combined with temperature-programmed experiments and in situ characterizations, revealed that atomically dispersed Ce species improved activity by alleviating hydrogen poisoning. The Ce sites with high hydrogen affinity served as scavengers for adsorbed H atoms (H*), driving the migration of H* and releasing blocked Ni sites. The formed active Ce3+‒OH groups facilitated the desorption of H*, promoting the recombination and desorption of N*. Consequently, the hydrogen poisoning was mitigated by the atomically dispersed Ce sites, and the catalytic performance was improved. This work offers insight into the promoting effect of CeO2 in ammonia decomposition and paves the way for the rational design of highly efficient catalysts with heterostructures.

Results and discussion

Structural characterization of catalysts

The Ni-Ce-Al ternary catalyst was prepared via a sol-gel method to achieve a uniform element distribution. Pluronic P123, citric acid, and metal nitrate precursors were dissolved in ethanol to form a homogeneous sol. The addition of citric acid facilitated the formation of a homogeneous coordination network and the uniform distribution of metal ions in the solution. After drying the gel and calcination at 400 °C, the freshly prepared sample labeled as Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was obtained, denoting that the molar ratio of Ni, Ce, and Al was 8:1:1. Ni8Ce2Ox and Ni8Al2Ox samples were prepared with the same procedure for comparison. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed that NiO (PDF# 04-016-6318) was the predominant phase in all samples (Supplementary Fig. 1). Unless otherwise stated, all samples were reduced in H2 at 600 °C for 1 h before the catalytic evaluation, as metallic Ni is catalytically active for ammonia decomposition. The Ni loading in reduced samples determined via inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) was 66.5 wt% (Ni8Ce1Al1Ox), 78.2 wt% (Ni8Al2Ox), and 56.5 wt% (Ni8Ce2Ox), respectively (Supplementary Table 1). The specific surface area (SBET) of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Al2Ox, and Ni8Ce2Ox was 50.0, 70.6, and 22.4 m2 g−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2).

The morphology of reduced samples was systematically investigated through multiple electron microscopic techniques. The secondary electron mode of scanning electron microscopy (SE-SEM) images revealed the formation of stacked Ni nanoparticles in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox after reduction (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Three-dimensional electron tomography was employed to visualize the spatial distribution of Ni and Al. As shown in Fig. 1b–d, Ni nanoparticles were partially embedded in the three-dimensional interconnected Al2O3 framework. Cross-section analysis showed the pore structure between Ni nanoparticles and the Al2O3 framework, suggesting accessibility to the gas molecule (Fig. 1e). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping further confirmed that Ni nanoparticles were connected by the Al2O3 framework (Fig. 1f). The average diameter of Ni nanoparticles analyzed from bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images was 12.9 nm (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5). A similar structure was observed in the Ni8Al2Ox sample. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, Ni nanoparticles with an average diameter of 14.4 nm were connected by the Al2O3 matrix. In contrast, Ni nanoparticles with diameters exceeding 20 nm were observed in Ni8Ce2Ox. The average size of Ni nanoparticles increased to 19.0 nm, indicating that high-loading Ni nanoparticles failed to remain stable on CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 7). These comparative observations, together with the SBET results (Supplementary Table 2), demonstrated that the Al2O3 framework efficiently mitigated the sintering of high-loading Ni nanoparticles.

The structure of CeO2 in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was further unveiled. For Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) images showed that Ni nanoparticles were partially embedded within the Al2O3 matrix (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 8). Discernible CeO2 nanoparticles were not observed, indicating the high dispersion of Ce species. To clarify the spatial distribution of Ce, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) spectra were collected at the center (area 2) and boundary (areas 1 and 3) of an individual Ni nanoparticle. As shown in Fig. 1i, boundary areas exhibited pronounced Ce M4,5-edge signals, while Ni L2,3-edge signals were predominant in the center area. AC-HAADF-STEM images at low and high magnifications revealed the form of Ce species in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. As shown in Fig. 1j, k, and Supplementary Figs. 9–11, abundant Ce single atoms were observed at the boundary of Al2O3 and Ni nanoparticles. Besides, clusters exhibiting the lattice fringe featuring CeO2(111) were also observed in some areas (Fig. 1l), suggesting its coexistence with Ce single atoms after reduction. EDS elemental mapping results corroborated these observations, showing Ce signals uniformly distributed around Ni nanoparticles and overlapped with the Al signals (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 12). No discrete CeO2 domain was observed. These microscopic results demonstrated that Ce species in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox were atomically dispersed and crowded around the Ni nanoparticles.

The size and valence state of Ni were comprehensively examined. XRD patterns of reduced catalysts exhibited typical diffraction peaks of face-centered cubic Ni (PDF# 00-001-1258, Fig. 2a). The crystallite size of Ni calculated according to the Scherrer equation is listed in Supplementary Table 3. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited the smallest crystallite size of 5.1 nm compared to those of 11.1 and 17.7 nm for Ni8Al2Ox and Ni8Ce2Ox, respectively. Complementary structural information was obtained through Ni K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis. Ni K-edge EXAFS shown in R space only exhibited Ni–Ni coordination (Fig. 2b). The coordination number obtained from data fitting increased in the following order: Ni8Ce1Al1Ox (7.2), Ni8Al2Ox (8.2), and Ni8Ce2Ox (9.3) (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Figs. 13–15, and Table 4). The fitting results demonstrated that Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited the smallest Ni nanoparticle size, in agreement with the variation trend derived from XRD and TEM data. These results proved that CeO2 failed to stabilize high-loading Ni nanoparticles, leading to the aggregation of Ni nanoparticles. In contrast, the Al2O3 framework mitigated the sintering of nanoparticles, increasing Ni site density. The H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) experiments were conducted to reflect the metal-support interaction (MSI), which is related to the sintering behavior of the metal. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, the NiO sample showed a reduction peak at 337 °C. The Ni8Ce2Ox and Ni8Al2Ox showed single reduction peaks at 396 and 456 °C, respectively. The higher reduction temperature of Ni8Al2Ox indicated that the MSI between Ni and Al2O3 was stronger than that between Ni and CeO2, mitigating the sintering of Ni nanoparticles. The Ni8Ce1Al1Ox showed two reduction peaks at 385 and 480 °C. The double peaks suggested the coexistence of Ni-CeO2 and Ni-Al2O3 interaction. The H2-TPR proved that the MSI in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and Ni8Al2Ox was stronger than Ni8Ce2Ox, consistent with their small Ni nanoparticle sizes and high Ni site density. The valence state of Ni was determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES). Ni 2p XPS results showed a combination of Ni0 and Ni2+ for all three samples (Supplementary Fig. 17). The Ni0 proportion for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Al2Ox, and Ni8Ce2Ox was 33.2%, 61.7%, and 64.5%, respectively. The XANES of Ni K-edge exhibited a near-edge intensity between the Ni foil and NiO reference for three catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 18). As Ni nanoparticles were highly fragile in oxygen, the hybrid Ni valence state was ascribed to partial oxidation upon exposure to air.

The structure and valence state of the Ce species were further unveiled and investigated. The XRD pattern revealed the absence of CeO2 diffraction features in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, in contrast to the distinct CeO2 patterns (PDF# 00-034-0394) observed in Ni8Ce2Ox (Fig. 2a). The Raman spectra of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox showed a broader and less intense F2g and defect peaks of CeO2 compared to those of Ni8Ce2Ox (Fig. 2d). These findings indicated the high dispersion of CeO2 in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox40,41, consistent with TEM results (Fig. 1j, k). The introduction of Al2O3 during sol-gel synthesis provides locales for the dispersion of CeO2. Ce 3d XPS results showed the coexistence of Ce3+ and Ce4+ in reduced catalysts (Fig. 2e). Quantitative analysis revealed a Ce3+ fraction of 53.0% for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, significantly higher than that for Ni8Ce2Ox (15.7%). The Ce L3-edge XANES spectra of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited distinct features. The absorption edge of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox (5724.4 eV) showed a 0.6 eV negative shift relative to bulk CeO2 (5725.0 eV) (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating the reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+42. Previous studies have shown that supported Ce single atoms exhibited a predominant valence state of +332–44. The enhanced reduction degree of Ce in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox corresponded to the formation of atomically dispersed Ce species. The intensity of feature B in Fig. 2 f was size-dependent and inversely correlated with the CeO2 particle size45. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited an intensified feature B compared to bulk CeO2, reinforcing the high dispersion of Ce species. The Ce L3-edge EXAFS analysis of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox showed a shorter Ce‒O coordination at 1.7 Å, compared to 1.9 Å for bulk CeO2 (Fig. 2g). Additionally, the Ce‒O coordination number in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was lower than that of bulk CeO2. EXAFS and the corresponding wavelet transform analysis of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox demonstrated a diminished Ce‒Ce coordination relative to bulk CeO2 (Fig. 2g‒i). Together with the microscopic results, these analyses demonstrated the successful preparation of the Ni8Ce1Al1Ox catalyst with high-loading Ni nanoparticles crowded by atomically dispersed Ce species.

Catalytic performance of catalysts

The catalytic performance of the catalyst with different Ni loadings was first evaluated within the temperature range of 400–600 °C. As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 20, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox with a Ni molar percentage of 80% showed the optimal ammonia decomposition activity. With a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 30 L gcat−1 h−1, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox achieved an NH3 conversion of 11.8% at 400 °C (Fig. 3b). The NH3 conversion reached 77.2% and 96.9% at 500 and 550 °C, respectively. In contrast, Ni8Al2Ox and Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited an NH3 conversion of 64.0 and 79.7% at 550 °C. The temperatures at which NH3 conversion reached 20% (T20) were measured to evaluate the low-temperature activity. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, the T20 of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Ce2Ox, and Ni8Al2Ox was 420, 473, and 466 °C, respectively. The above results demonstrated that atomically dispersed Ce species efficiently promoted ammonia decomposition. The turnover frequency (TOF) was calculated to reveal the intrinsic activity of catalysts. TOF values were derived from the reaction rate at 400 °C with a GHSV of 30 L gcat−1 h−1 to avoid heat and mass transfer. The dispersion of Ni was determined from H2-CO pulse chemisorption results (Supplementary Fig. 22). Calculation details were shown in the supplementary information. As shown in Fig. 3c, Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited a higher TOF value of 1.65 s−1 compared to Ni8Al2Ox (0.53 s−1). The higher TOF value indicated that CeO2 was more effective than Al2O3 in improving the intrinsic activity of Ni sites. This finding revealed that a trade-off between density and intrinsic activity of Ni active sites existed when CeO2 or Al2O3 was employed individually. CeO2 improved the intrinsic activity but failed to stabilize Ni nanoparticles, leading to low Ni site density. Al2O3 is effective in maintaining a low average nanoparticle size and high Ni site density in the catalyst. However, it exhibited a limited promotion in the intrinsic activity for ammonia decomposition. In contrast, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited a high TOF value of 2.73 s−1, revealing the superior promotion effect of atomically dispersed Ce species. The Ni8Ce1Al1Ox catalyst showed both high Ni site density and high intrinsic activity, suggesting that constructing atomically dispersed Ce species on the Al2O3 framework broke the trade-off relationship. Consequently, superior ammonia decomposition activity was achieved over Ni8Ce1Al1Ox.

The difference between Ce single atoms and clusters in promoting NH3 decomposition was then investigated. Since Ni8Ce1Al1Ox contained both types of Ce species, a reference sample containing only Ce single atoms was prepared through high-temperature reduction. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was reduced at 750 °C to facilitate the dispersion of Ce species on the Al2O3 framework46. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23, the average particle size increased from 12.9 to 14.5 nm after reduction. High-magnification AC-HAADF-STEM images revealed that abundant Ce single atoms were crowded around Ni nanoparticles, without observation of CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 24). EDS elemental mapping results proved that the Ce species was atomically dispersed. As shown in Fig. S24c, f, the Ce species was highly dispersed without aggregation. The XRD patterns of reduced samples exhibited a characteristic feature of Ni. Diffraction peaks of CeO2 were not observed, indicating that the Ce species were highly dispersed (Supplementary Fig. 25a). The Ce L3-edge EXAFS analysis of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 750 °C showed a shorter Ce‒O coordination at 1.6 Å, compared to 1.9 Å for bulk CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 25b). The Ce‒O coordination number was also lower than that for bulk CeO2. Additionally, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 750 °C showed a diminished Ce‒Ce coordination compared to bulk CeO2, indicating the absence of discrete CeO2 domains. The above characterization data demonstrated that the Ce species exist fully as isolated single atoms after reduction at 750 °C. The catalytic performance of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 750 °C was then evaluated within the temperature range of 400–600 °C with a high GHSV of 120 L gcat−1 h−1. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26a, the NH3 conversion of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 750 °C was 8.8%, 29.2%, and 64.8% at 450, 500, and 550 °C, respectively. The decrease in NH3 conversion after reduction at 750 °C was ascribed to the increase of Ni nanoparticle size. TOF was then derived to normalize the accessible surface Ni site density. The NH3 consumption rate at 400 °C was applied for the calculation of TOF to avoid diffusion control. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26b, the TOF value of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 750 °C was 4.06 s−1, close to that for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 600 °C (4.33 s−1). The comparable TOF values for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox reduced at different temperatures elucidated that Ce single atoms and Ce clusters exhibited a similar promotion effect for NH3 decomposition.

The influence of the preparation method was then investigated. Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was impregnated on the CeAlOx support prepared by the sol-gel method. After calcination, a reference sample labeled as Ni8Ce1Al1Ox-WI was prepared. As shown in Fig. 3b, despite identical components, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox-WI exhibited a poor activity of 28.4% conversion at 550 °C, significantly lower than Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. The NH3 conversion of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox-WI was only 61.3% even at 600 °C. The TEM images showed the presence of Ni nanoparticles with a diameter exceeding 50 nm after reduction (Supplementary Fig. 27). The crystallite size of Ni calculated from XRD results was 81.8 nm (Supplementary Fig. 28). These observations highlighted the advantage of the sol-gel method in stabilizing high-loading Ni nanoparticles.

The catalyst morphology and Ni particle size vary among different samples, complicating the attribution of the high catalytic activity. To further validate the effect of Ce species structure, a reference sample with the same morphology and matched Ni particle size, but different Ce dispersion to Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, was prepared by a two-step method. First, Ni6Al2Ox was prepared by the same sol-gel method. Ce(NO)3·6H2O was then impregnated on Ni6Al2Ox via rotary evaporation. After calcination at 400 °C and reduction at 600 °C, Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) was obtained. TEM results demonstrated that Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) exhibited a similar morphology, with Ni nanoparticles connected by the Al2O3 framework. The average Ni nanoparticle size of Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) was 12.7 nm (Supplementary Fig. 29), comparable to that of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox (12.9 nm). XRD patterns revealed the existence of CeO2 nanoparticles in Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) (Supplementary Fig. 30). The CeO2 crystallite size derived from the Scherrer equation was 8.0 nm. This observation highlighted the critical role of the sol-gel method in maintaining high dispersion of Ce species. The catalytic performance and TOF value of Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) were evaluated to reveal the effect of Ce species structure. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 31a, despite matching the Ni nanoparticle size and morphology, Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) exhibited inferior activity. The NH3 conversion at 400, 450, 500, and 550 °C was 3.4, 15.2, 45.1, and 81.7%, significantly lower than that for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. The TOF value of Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) was 0.74 s−1 (Supplementary Fig. 31b), while that for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was 2.73 s−1. These results further demonstrated that atomically dispersed Ce species exhibited an enhanced promotional effect compared to bulk CeO2.

Ni-based catalysts are prone to deactivation because of the low Tammann temperature of Ni, particularly under conditions of high temperature and reductive atmosphere. To evaluate the stability of catalysts under harsh conditions, a long-term stability test was carried out at 600 °C with a high GHSV of 240 L gcat−1 h−1. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 32, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox after reduction at 600 °C underwent slow deactivation during the stability test. The deactivation was ascribed to the size increase of the Ni nanoparticle, as the average size increased to 13.3 nm after the stability test (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34). To bypass the early-stage deactivation resulting from particle size increase, the stability of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox pre-reduced at 750 °C was evaluated under the same conditions. As shown in Fig. 3d, e, the initial NH3 conversion dropped to 87.0% due to the increased Ni nanoparticle size. During the stability test, the NH3 conversion decayed by only 0.9%. The average Ni nanoparticle size after the stability test was 14.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 35), confirming the solid stability of the catalyst. For Ni8Al2Ox, the NH3 conversion was maintained at 55% during the test. TEM images revealed that sintering was mitigated, with an average particle size of 14.3 nm after the stability test (Supplementary Fig. 36). In contrast, Ni8Ce2Ox underwent rapid deactivation with activity decline from 78.9 to 58.5% in 40 h. Nanoparticles with diameters larger than 30 nm were observed in the spent catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 37). Moreover, the crystalline size of Ni increased to 21.7 nm (Supplementary Fig. 38). A cycling stability test was performed to evaluate the stability against temperature fluctuation. After nine cycles of time-dependent activity tests with a GHSV of 30 L gcat−1 h−1, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited reproducible activity (Supplementary Fig. 39). These results confirmed that the Al2O3 framework mitigated the sintering of high-loading Ni nanoparticles.

The catalytic performance of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox under harsh conditions was further evaluated. Notably, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox achieved high NH3 conversion and H2 production rates at a GHSV of 360 L gcat−1 h−1 (Fig. 3f). As shown in Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 5, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited a respective H2 production rate of 145.5, 280.0, and 324.0 mmol gcat−1 min−1 at 500, 550, and 600 °C, outperforming the typical Ni-based ammonia decomposition catalysts23,27,29,33,34,36,38,47–53. It should be noted that the high ammonia decomposition activity of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was achieved at a relatively high Ni loading (66.5 wt%). When normalized to per gram of metal, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited a competitive H2 production rate of 447.3 mmol gNi−1 min−1 (Supplementary Fig. 40). These results revealed a trade-off between catalytic activity and Ni utilization efficiency in the current catalyst, especially from an application-oriented perspective. However, given the relatively low cost of Ni, employing a high Ni loading to achieve a higher reaction rate remained a practically viable strategy.

Alleviation of hydrogen poisoning

The catalytic performance evaluation demonstrated that atomically dispersed Ce species exhibited an outstanding promotion effect in ammonia decomposition. To reveal the origin of this promotion effect, the reaction mechanism over different catalysts was systematically studied. The reaction mechanism for ammonia decomposition has been well studied54–56. The process comprises several key steps, beginning with NH3 adsorption, followed by N–H bond cleavage, and ending with the recombination and desorption of N* and H* (Fig. 4a). Kinetic measurements were carried out to examine differences in the reaction mechanism. The apparent activation energy (Ea) was measured from Arrhenius plots. As shown in Fig. 4b, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Al2Ox, and Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited Ea values of 106.3, 104.3, and 101.6 kJ mol−1, respectively. These comparable Ea values suggested that the enhanced catalytic activity did not originate from an accelerated N* recombination step (step 5), which was generally considered as the rate-determining step. For ammonia synthesis and decomposition reactions, strongly adsorbed H atoms (H*) block active sites, hindering the adsorption and activation of NH3. This factor, known as hydrogen poisoning, is responsible for the deactivation of many catalysts8. As shown in Fig. 4c, the H2 reaction order of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Ce2Ox, and Ni8Al2Ox was −0.23, −0.42, and −0.48, respectively. The largest H2 reaction order indicated that the removal of H* from Ni sites was facilitated by Ce single atoms and clusters. Therefore, the alleviation of hydrogen poisoning contributed to the high activity of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. Moreover, Ni8Ce2Ox showed a higher H2 reaction order compared to that for Ni8Al2Ox. This result suggested that CeO2 was more effective in promoting H* desorption than Al2O3, contributing to the higher TOF of Ni8Ce2Ox. It should be noticed that alleviation of hydrogen poisoning did not alter the Ea but significantly increased the TOF value. These findings were also reported in the studies of ammonia synthesis57,58. The actual site density for N* recombination is affected by the total Ni site density (determined from chemisorption) and the extent of hydrogen poisoning. When the desorption of H* species is promoted, more sites are released and available for the N* recombination. In this case, the actual site density for catalysts with mitigated hydrogen poisoning is underestimated by the chemisorption. Consequently, high TOF values are derived.

Temperature-programmed surface reaction (TPSR) experiments were conducted to validate the reaction mechanism. In a gas flow of 10% NH3/Ar, three catalysts showed an identical onset temperature of 300 °C for NH3 consumption and N2 generation (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). H2 desorption was observed at 200 °C, while continuous H2 generation also initiated at 300 °C. The above TPSR results confirmed that the recombination and desorption of N* steps (step 5) were comparable for three catalysts in an NH3 atmosphere, aligned with their similar Ea values. To investigate the N–H bond cleavage steps and H* desorption steps, additional TPSR experiments were performed in 10% NH3 and 5% D2. The purpose of adding D2 was to establish a H2-rich atmosphere for assessing the resistance to hydrogen poisoning of catalysts. The dissociation of NH3 and cracking of the first N–H bond (steps 1 and 2) was reflected by the generation of NH2D species, as NH2* could recombine with D*. The TPSR experiments revealed that the cleavage of the first N–H occurred at around 65 °C for all catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 43a). Similarly, the cracking of the second N–H bond (step 3) was reflected by the generation of NHD2 species, which was similar for the three catalysts (65 °C). These profiles demonstrated that the kinetics of cleavage of the N–H bond were similar over the three samples. The high activity of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox did not stem from the difference in N–H bond cleavage steps.

The recombination of N* (step 5) was the rate-determining step in ammonia decomposition over Ni-based catalysts54–56. The recombination of N* was reflected by the generation of N2. As shown in Fig. 4e, the onset temperatures of N2 were 397, 430, and 453 °C for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Ce2Ox, and Ni8Al2Ox, respectively. The lowest N2 generation temperature of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox under a H2-rich condition indicated that the inhibition effect of H* on the N* recombination and desorption was alleviated. The alleviation of hydrogen poisoning was related to the fast desorption of H*. Consequently, the recombination and desorption of H* (step 6) were further analyzed by the evolution of H2 and HD species. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 43c, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited a H2 and HD onset temperature of 409 °C, lower than that for Ni8Ce2Ox (453 °C) and Ni8Al2Ox (492 °C). The lowest H2/HD generation temperature revealed that the H* desorption step was facilitated in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. These results demonstrated that atomically dispersed Ce species promoted the desorption of H*, facilitating the recombination of N* and contributing to the superior activity. Moreover, Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited a lower H2/HD/N2 generation temperature compared to Ni8Al2Ox. This finding confirmed that bulk CeO2 is more effective in mitigating hydrogen poisoning than Al2O3.

Oriented migration of adsorbed H species

As shown in Fig. 4a, the cleavage of the N–H bond formed adsorbed H species on the Ni surface (Ni–H species). Furthermore, it has been reported that adsorbed H species could migrate from the metal surface to the proximal oxide surface and form hydroxyl groups, known as hydrogen spillover59. To investigate the mechanism of the alleviation of H poisoning by atomically dispersed Ce species, the evolution of these H species on the catalyst surface was investigated.

Ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (AP-XPS) was first employed to identify the types of surface hydroxyl species on the catalyst under the H2-rich condition. The O 1s spectra of the samples were collected at 400, 500, and 600 °C in 0.1 mbar H2. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 44a, Ni8Al2Ox exhibited a peak at 531.1 eV, ascribed to the O species on the Al2O3 surface (Oα)60. Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited two peaks at 530.4 and 529.3 eV, attributed to the surface hydroxyl groups (Oβ) and lattice O (Oγ) on CeO261,62. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox showed different O 1s spectra features in H2, with a wide peak at 530.5 eV. The peak-fitting results revealed the coexistence of O species on the Al2O3 framework (Oα), hydroxyl species on the Ce species (Oβ), and lattice O on CeOx clusters (Oγ). Compared to Ni8Ce2Ox, the surface concentration of hydroxyl groups on Ce species for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox increased. The fraction of Oβ to all the O species for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was 50.8%, while that for Ni8Ce2Ox was 37.8%. This observation suggested that the atomic dispersion of the Ce species improved the hydrogen affinity, facilitating the formation of hydroxyl groups on the Ce species. The Ce 3d spectra of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited a Ce3+ fraction of 86.5 at.% (Supplementary Fig. 44b). In contrast, the fraction of Ce3+ for Ni8Ce2Ox was 50.8 at.%. The Ce 3d and O 1s spectra implied that the hydroxyl groups on the Ni8Ce1Al1Ox surface under H2 conditions were predominantly in the form of Ce3+–OH.

To further validate this assignment, Ce 3d and O 1s results at 500 and 400 °C were analyzed. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 45 and 46, the same variation trend of Ce3+and Oβ across samples and temperatures was observed. As the temperature increased, the fractions of Ce3+ and Oβ both increased (Supplementary Fig. 47). The positive correlation indicated that these species were generated simultaneously, that is, Ce3+–OH formed on the surface of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and Ni8Ce2Ox under a H2-rich condition. Moreover, at 400 °C, the fraction of Ce3+ and Oβ for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox was 66.7 at.% and 39.9 at.%. In contrast, the fraction of Ce3+ and Oβ for Ni8Ce2Ox was 36.6 at.% and 22.6 at.% at 400 °C. These fractions for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox increased to 71.3 at.% and 48.7 at.% at 500 °C, while a Ce3+ fraction of 38.4 at.% and an Oβ fraction of 30.3 at.% was observed for Ni8Ce2Ox. The comparison between Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and Ni8Ce2Ox confirmed that atomically dispersed Ce species possessed a higher hydrogen affinity compared to bulk CeO2. This higher hydrogen affinity could contribute to the migration of H* from Ni nanoparticles to the oxide surface and the subsequent formation of Ce3+−OH groups. In addition, Ni 2p spectra of all three catalysts exhibited a typical feature of Ni0 at 852.2 eV at 400, 500, and 600 °C (Supplementary Fig. 48). This result elucidated that, unlike many other claims, CeO2 exhibited a negligible influence on the electronic structure of Ni.

AP-XPS experiments under dynamic switching between Ar and H2 were subsequently conducted to probe the H* migration. A Ni-free reference sample denoted as CeAlOx was prepared via the sol-gel method. CeAlOx possessed a Ce/(Ce + Al) molar ratio of 50%, which was the same as Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. The detailed preparation process has been supplemented in the experimental sections. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Ce2Ox, and CeAlOx were reduced at 600 °C and then loaded into the chamber. The experiments were conducted at 300 °C. As shown in Fig. 5a, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox showed a Ce3+ fraction of 53.5 at.% in 0.1 mbar Ar, close to the results of ex situ XPS results (53.0 at.%). When switching to 0.1 mbar H2, the fraction of Ce3+ increased to 67.9 at.%. The O 1s exhibited a simultaneous variation, with the fraction of Oβ increased from 28.3 at.% to 55.7 at.% (Fig. 5a), suggesting the formation of Ce3+−OH. In contrast, the fractions of Ce3+ and Oβ of CeAlOx showed insufficient difference when the inlet gas switched from Ar to H2 (Supplementary Fig. 49). The fraction of Ce3+ only increased from 46.4 at.% to 46.6 at.%, with the Oβ concentration increasing from 32.1 at.% to 34.3 at.%. This result indicated that the H2 activation on the bare oxide surface was weak at 300 °C. In addition, the AP-XPS results at 400, 500, and 600 °C revealed that CeAlOx exhibited lower fractions of Ce3+ and Oβ compared to Ni8Ce1Al1Ox under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 50). These observations suggest that the thermal reduction of Ce species is not the primary cause of Ce3+−OH formation. Instead, the generation of Ce3+−OH was associated with H* migration from the Ni surface63–65.

The reversibility of H* migration was then evaluated. After switching back to Ar, the fraction of Ce3+ and Oβ for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox decreased to 59.1 at.% and 33.7 at.%, respectively. These findings suggested that the H* in Ce3+–OH could desorb as H2, which is a typical feature of reversible H migration between metal and oxide66,67. More importantly, the reversible migration provided insight into how H poisoning was alleviated. Atomically dispersed Ce species with high hydrogen affinity could drive the oriented H* migration and desorb H2 via redox cycle between +3 and +4, releasing the blocked Ni sites. The valence state and O specie variation for Ni8Ce2Ox was also evaluated. As shown in Fig. 5b, when switched from Ar to H2, the Ce3+ fraction increased from 20.9 at.% to 30.4 at.%. The fraction of Oβ increased from 22.3 at.% to 30.3 at.% simultaneously, suggesting that H species migrate to the CeO2 surface and form Ce3+–OH. After switching back to Ar, the fraction of Ce3+ and Oβ dropped to 22.5 at.% and 25.6 at.%, respectively. Compared to Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited a lower Ce3+ and Oβ fraction variation in H2 (Fig. 5c), indicating that atomically dispersed Ce species were more effective than bulk CeO2 in capturing H*. The above AP-XPS results provided evidence that the oriented migration of H* from the Ni surface and formation of Ce3+–OH were facilitated by the atomically dispersed Ce species.

The oriented migration of H* was further investigated by in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy in H2 (H2-DRIFTS). After the collection of background spectra in Ar flow at 100 °C, the inlet gas was switched to H2 to monitor the variation of surface species. As shown in Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 51a, a broad band between 2800 and 3700 cm−1 was observed for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, correlated to hydroxyl groups. In addition, a vibration feature around 2100 cm−1 emerged, attributed to the forbidden 2F5/2-to-2F7/2 electronic transition of Ce3+. In contrast, new vibration bands were not observed for CeAlOx (Supplementary Fig. 51b). The simultaneous formation of Ce3+ and hydroxyl groups for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox implied that H* would migrate to the Ce single atoms and clusters, forming Ce3+–OH. This speculation was further confirmed by the comparison to the in situ DRIFTS results of Ni8Ce2Ox and Ni8Al2Ox. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 51c, the hydroxyl band was also observed for Ni8Ce2Ox after switching to H2. The weaker band intensity compared to Ni8Ce1Al1Ox proved the high hydrogen affinity of atomically dispersed Ce species, consistent with the AP-XPS results. For Ni8Al2Ox, variation of hydroxyl groups was not observed (Supplementary Fig. 51d). This difference was ascribed to the slower migration of H species to Al–O sites than to Ce–O sites, providing complementary evidence for the preferential formation of Ce3+–OH on Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. Moreover, the reversibility of migration was evaluated. After adsorption of H2 for 12 min, the gas flow was switched to Ar. As shown in Figs. 5d, e, the intensity of hydroxyl groups decreased by nearly half after 20 min. When the inlet gas was switched back to H2, the hydroxyl groups recovered. This result validated the reversibility of oriented H* migration, which contributed to the alleviation of the H poisoning of Ni sites.

H2-DRIFTS experiments were then conducted at higher temperatures to evaluate the stability of hydroxyl groups. For Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, the intensity of the hydroxyl group decreased when the temperature increased from 100 to 200 °C (Supplementary Fig. 52). At 250 °C, the hydroxyl group was not observed after the introduction of H2. The decrease in band intensity was attributed to the desorption of hydroxyl groups at high temperature. For Ni8Ce2Ox, the hydroxyl band disappeared at 150 °C, proving the lack of Ce3+‒OH on the surface of bulk CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 53). Ni8Al2Ox exhibited a distinct feature in the H2-DRIFTS experiments (Supplementary Fig. 54). Although no band was observed at 100 °C, a hydroxyl band centered at 3275 cm−1 appeared at 150 °C. When the temperature increased to 200 °C, the hydroxyl band disappeared. This result demonstrated that the H* migration on the Al2O3 surface was sluggish and required a higher temperature.

The formation of Ce3+–OH groups was confirmed by the solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). Considering the magnetism of Ni nanoparticles in Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and Ni8Al2Ox, amorphous Al2O3 and Ce-doped Al2O3 (5 wt% Ce dopant, denoted as 5Ce-Al2O3) were prepared to simulate the oxide component for solid-state NMR experiments. Diffraction peaks of CeO2 were not observed in the XRD profile of the reduced 5Ce-Al2O3 (Supplementary Fig. 55). As shown in Supplementary Figs. 56 and 57, reduced 5Ce-Al2O3 possessed abundant Ce single atoms. The 1H NMR spectrum is shown in Supplementary Fig. 58. After H2 reduction at 600 °C for 2 h, 5Ce-Al2O3 exhibited a higher hydroxyl signal than Al2O3. The higher concentration of hydroxyls demonstrated that atomically dispersed Ce species exhibited a higher hydrogen affinity compared to Al2O3 and accelerated the activation of H2. The lower hydrogen affinity of Al2O3 was consistent with the sluggish hydrogen spillover on the Al2O3 surface65. Besides, a distinct signal centered at 1.3 ppm was observed on 5Ce-Al2O3, consistent with the chemical shift of hydroxyl groups on CeO268. This feature provided circumstantial evidence of the formation of Ce3+‒OH on atomically dispersed Ce species.

To further support the interpretation of oriented H* migration, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed. First, the type of surface hydroxyl groups was identified by calculating the energy profile of H migration between Ce–O sites and Al–O sites. As shown in Fig. 5g, a Ce1Al4O12H7 cluster on the Ni(111) surface was employed to model the structure of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, with abundant atomically dispersed Ce species loaded on the Al2O3 framework and crowded around Ni nanoparticles. The energy change for H migration from an Al–O site to a Ce–O site was −0.91 eV, indicating a strong thermodynamic preference for hydrogen to diffuse toward Ce–O sites (Fig. 5f). The energy barrier was 0.73 eV, which was surmountable under the reaction temperature. These results suggested that H preferred to bond with Ce‒O sites rather than Al‒O sites on the oxide surface. The Al2O3 framework plays the dual role of inhibiting Ni nanoparticle sintering and providing anchoring sites for Ce species dispersion. As the Ce‒OH was more favored than Al‒OH, we calculated the energy barrier of H migration from Ni to the oxide surface using a simplified CeO3/Ni(111) model. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 59, the diffusion barrier for H migration on the Ni(111) surface was calculated to be in the range of 0.1–0.2 eV, indicating facile surface diffusion. The migration barriers for H transfer from Ni(111) to CeO3, CeO2(OH), and CeO(OH)2 are 0.76, 0.84, and 0.89 eV, respectively. These barriers are surmountable under the reaction temperature, suggesting that H migration from Ni to Ce-containing species is kinetically feasible. The above DFT calculation results provide additional evidence for the oriented H migration. Importantly, Ce can undergo reversible valence changes between Ce4+ and Ce3+, which enables it to accommodate hydrogen species during the spillover process and thus act as an effective hydrogen scavenger. To evaluate the effect of hydrogen coverage, we further performed machine-learning potential-based molecular dynamics simulations using the MACE framework, which was fine-tuned on the pre-trained mace-mp-0b3 model69. The simulations reveal a clear hydrogen-coverage-dependent spillover behavior. At high hydrogen coverage (5 and 10 H atoms), hydrogen spillover from the Ni surface to adjacent oxygen sites on CeO3 was frequently observed, leading to the formation of Ce–OH species (Supplementary Fig. 60). In contrast, no hydrogen spillover events were detected at low coverage (1 H atom) within the simulation time scale.

The AP-XPS, H2-DRIFTS, solid-state NMR, and DFT calculations together supported the occurrence of oriented H* migration from the Ni surface to Ce single atoms and clusters. Atomically dispersed Ce species exhibited a higher hydrogen affinity than bulk CeO2 and Al2O3. On the surface of CeO2 or Al2O3, only one kind of hydroxyl group was generated after the migration of H*. In contrast, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox possessed a heterogeneous oxide surface. The H* could migrate orientally to the atomically dispersed Ce species with high hydrogen affinity to form abundant Ce3+–OH groups. This reversible migration could offer an additional desorption channel for H* species, contributing to the mitigation of H poisoning.

Promoted desorption of adsorbed H species

As the Ce species could influence the evolution of H* species via forming hydroxyl groups, the kinetics of the H* desorption step were influenced by the reactivity of hydroxyl groups. To reveal the reactivity of different hydroxyl groups, CO temperature-programmed reduction (CO-TPR) experiments were performed. The hydroxyl groups could react with CO, forming H2 and CO270. As shown in Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 61, two groups of peaks were observed in the CO-TPR profiles for three catalysts. The H2 generation peaks below 150 °C without CO consumption were attributed to the desorption of H* on metallic Ni. The H2 desorption peaks above 200 °C, accompanied by CO2 generation, were assigned to the consumption of surface hydroxyls. The hydroxyls on Ni8Ce1Al1Ox reacted with CO at 250 °C, remarkably lower than that for Ni8Al2Ox (333 °C) and Ni8Ce2Ox (325 °C). This observation implied that Ce3+–OH groups on the surface of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox were the most active, which might contribute to the facile H* desorption. To validate this speculation, the desorption behavior of surface H* species was then probed by H2 temperature-programmed desorption (H2-TPD) experiments. As shown in Fig. 6b, broad peaks below 150 °C were observed for all catalysts after H2 treatment at 600 °C, assigned to the desorption of H* from the Ni nanoparticle surface. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and Ni8Al2Ox exhibited distinct high-temperature shoulder peaks at 210 and 260 °C, while Ni8Ce2Ox lacked such features. This desorption behavior matched the quantitative analysis of hydroxyl group variation in the H2-DRIFTS results (Supplementary Fig. 62). Therefore, these shoulder peaks were assigned to the desorption of H2 from the hydroxyls formed during H2 pretreatment. The lower desorption temperature for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox suggested that the Ce3+–OH groups were active and the H* species desorbed easily. Moreover, both H2-TPD and CO-TPR results revealed that Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited a higher hydroxyl capacity, confirming the high hydrogen affinity of atomically dispersed Ce species.

In situ NH3-DRIFTS was employed to monitor the evolution of surface species during ammonia decomposition. The experiments were conducted at 400 °C for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and at 500 °C for Ni8Al2Ox and Ni8Ce2Ox to maintain a similar NH3 conversion. For Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, two vibration bands appeared after introducing NH3 (Fig. 6c). The signal at 3330 cm−1 was assigned to the N‒H stretching vibration of gaseous NH3, while the broad band at 3500 cm−1 was related to the O‒H stretching of surface hydroxyl groups. The appearance of the hydroxyl signals when NH3 was introduced confirmed that the H* species generated by N‒H bond cracking could also orientally migrate to the atomically dispersed Ce species. The reversibility of the migration was examined by cycle measurement. After purging NH3 for 10 min, the feed gas was switched to He. The hydroxyl signals gradually declined (Fig. 6c), indicating that H atoms in surface hydroxyls recombined and desorbed as H2. The second cycle of the experiment exhibited a similar feature, proving the remaining reversibility. This reversible behavior evidenced that the atomically dispersed Ce species accommodated the H* generated by N–H cleavage, providing an additional desorption channel. For Ni8Al2Ox and Ni8Ce2Ox, the hydroxyl signal was not observed even at 500 °C (Supplementary Fig. 63), indicating that the H* migration on these catalysts was sluggish, corroborated by their low hydroxyl capacity (Fig. 6a, b). These observations demonstrated that the active Ce3+–OH groups provided an additional channel for H* desorption, collaborating with the H2-TPD profile.

NH3 temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) experiments were employed to investigate the influence of the hydroxyl reactivity on the NH3 activation and N* desorption. The N2 generation profiles (m/z = 28) were analyzed after H2 or He treatment (Supplementary Figs. 64 and 65). H2 treatment induced the formation of pre-adsorbed Ni‒H and surface hydroxyls. Meanwhile, the H* migration from Ni nanoparticles to oxide was suppressed. He treatment efficiently removed adsorbed H species and initiated the migration of H* (Supplementary Fig. 66). As shown in Fig. 6d, for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, a single N2 generation peak at 550 °C was observed after H2 treatment, which shifted markedly to 428 °C after He treatment. Ni8Al2Ox exhibited a single N2 generation peak at 515 °C following H2 treatment. After the treatment of He, a new low-temperature N2 evolution peak at 422 °C appeared while the original high-temperature peak at 515 °C persisted. In contrast, Ni8Ce2Ox exhibited identical N2 generation profiles after both treatment conditions, featuring a single peak at 466 °C. These N2 generation profiles directly evidenced the essential role of H* migration in facilitating N2 generation. Figure 6e was depicted to illustrate the H* migration and NH3 activation process after different treatment conditions. An inverse configuration using Ni as the substrate to support oxides was adopted for clear presentation. For Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, after H2 treatment, the H* was trapped on Ni nanoparticles, hindering the NH3 activation and N2 generation. After the He treatment, atomically dispersed Ce species served as scavengers for H* and facilitated the migration of H* from Ni nanoparticles to the oxide. Consequently, the Ni sites were released, and the NH3 activation was promoted, consistent with the smallest absolute value of the H2 reaction order for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox (Fig. 4c). For Ni8Al2Ox, the activation of NH3 was also impeded after H2 treatment. However, the sluggish H* migration on Al2O3 limited the release of poisoned Ni sites after He treatment, accounting for the persistence of the N2 generation peak at 515 °C. For Ni8Ce2Ox, the H* migration from Ni nanoparticle to bulk CeO2 was restricted, evidenced by the low hydroxyl capacity (Fig. 6a, b). Consequently, H* was trapped on Ni sites regardless of treatment conditions.

We further analyzed the detailed reaction pathways of NH3 decomposition and the subsequent *N recombination steps. The activation barriers for the N‒H bond cleavage and corresponding structure are shown in Supplementary Figs. 67 and 68. The activation barrier for N‒H bond cleavage was up to 1.39 eV, which could be readily overcome under the reaction conditions, assisted by the stabilization from adjacent Ce(OH)3 species. In contrast, the formation of NHOH and NH2OH species through side reactions between NH or NH2 and surface OH groups is highly endothermic, with energy barriers of 2.52 and 2.03 eV, respectively. In addition, the activation barrier for the N–N recombination step on the Ce(OH)3/Ni(111) surface is 1.27 eV, indicating that this step is also kinetically accessible at the reaction temperature (Supplementary Fig. 69).

Additionally, CO2-TPD experiments were conducted to investigate the surface basicity of catalysts. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 70, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox exhibited three CO2 desorption peaks at 109, 189, and 315 °C. For Ni8Al2Ox, three CO2 desorption peaks at 107, 163, and 238 °C were observed. CO2-TPD profile of Ni8Ce2Ox showed three desorption peaks at 94, 153, and 300 °C. The CO2 chemisorption uptake for Ni8Ce2Ox was obviously lower than that for Ni8Ce1Al1Ox and Ni8Al2Ox. The CO2-TPD results revealed that Ni8Ce1Al1Ox possessed the most abundant strong basic sites among the three samples. It has been demonstrated that strong basic sites facilitated the dissociation of NHx species and desorption of N* species18,33. The high surface basicity of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox promoted the ammonia decomposition, aligned with its excellent catalytic performance.

Recent studies have reported the hydrogen spillover phenomenon during the ammonia decomposition reaction and emphasized the critical role of abundant oxygen vacancies in molecule activation71,72. However, the influence of the formation of hydroxyl groups in the reaction mechanism was rarely reported. Our work further demonstrated that the reactivity of surface hydroxyl groups could be modulated by constructing a heterogeneous oxide surface. Active hydroxyl groups on highly dispersed Ce species were essential for scavenging H* and alleviating hydrogen poisoning.

Ce loading effect and volcano-type relationship

The effect of Ce loading on the catalyst structure and catalytic performance was investigated. A series of samples with a unified Ni loading of 67 wt% and varying Ce loading was prepared via the sol-gel method. These samples were labeled as Ni6.5Al3.5Ox, Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox, Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox, and Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox. The actual element contents were determined by ICP-AES and shown in Supplementary Table 1. The influence of the Ce to Al molar ratio on the morphology of catalysts was first studied. TEM images revealed that Ni6.5Al3.5Ox and Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox exhibited a similar structure to Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, with Ni nanoparticles connected by the Al2O3 framework (Supplementary Figs. 71 and 72). The average Ni nanoparticle size for Ni6.5Al3.5Ox and Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox was 10.3 and 12.1 nm, respectively. For Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox and Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox, the average Ni nanoparticle size increased to 16.1 and 18.7 nm due to the poor stabilization effect of CeO2 (Supplementary Figs. 73 and 74).

The structural evolution of Ce species with varying Ce loading was then revealed. As shown in Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 75, AC-HAADF-STEM images of Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox demonstrated that the Ce species existed as Ce single atoms. Lattice fringes featuring CeO2(111) were not observed in a wide range of images. EDS elemental mapping results showed that the Ce species was uniformly distributed, confirming the existence of Ce single atoms. In contrast, CeO2 nanoparticles with diameters larger than 5 nm were observed for Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 76). For Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox, the Ce species existed as bulk CeO2 nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 73). The structure of the Ce species was further investigated by XRD. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 77, the XRD patterns of all samples exhibited diffraction peaks of Ni. The diffraction peaks of CeO2 were not observed for Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox and Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, but appeared for Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox and Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox. These observations elucidated a clear compositional threshold. When the Ni loading was identical, the structure of the Ce species was influenced by the molar ratio of Ce to Al. The Ce species remained atomically dispersed when the molar ratios of Ce to Al in the samples were ≤1. When the ratio exceeded 1, the Al2O3 framework lacked sufficient sites to stabilize the Ce species, leading to the aggregation.

As the Ce single atoms and clusters exhibited an enhanced promotion effect, the surface concentration of atomically dispersed Ce species was derived from the XPS and EXAFS analysis. XPS was first applied to determine the surface concentration of Ce species semi-quantitatively. Given that Ce species were predominantly located on the Al2O3 framework, the atomic concentration of Ce or Al species was derived from the normalized area of Ce 3d and Al 2p (Supplementary Fig. 78). As shown in Fig. 3c, the atomic concentration of Ce was 0, 33, and 46 at.% for Ni6.5Al3.5Ox, Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox, and Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. These values were close to those derived from ICP-AES results (Supplementary Table 1). In contrast, the atomic concentration of Ce dropped to 44 at.% for Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox, significantly lower than the ICP-AES results. Given that XPS is a surface-sensitive technique, this difference indicated that the Ce species aggregated, leading to a decrease in surface concentration. The fraction of atomically dispersed Ce species to all the Ce species was qualitatively estimated by the Ce L3-edge EXAFS analysis. As shown in Fig. 7d, Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox exhibited a similar spectrum to Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, with a shifted Ce–O coordination at 1.5 Å and diminished Ce–Ce coordination. These features confirmed the atomic dispersion of Ce species. For Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox, two peaks at 1.5 and 1.9 Å were observed in the Ce–O coordination region. This observation suggested the coexistence of atomically dispersed Ce species and CeO2. Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox exhibited the same features as the CeO2 reference. The EXAFS analysis proved that the fraction of atomically dispersed Ce species remained 100% when the Ce/Al molar ratio ≤1, but decreased and dropped to 0% for Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox. Combining the XPS and EXAFS analysis results, a volcano-type relationship of the surface concentration of atomically dispersed Ce species to Ce loading was revealed. The concentration first increased with increasing Ce loading. The highest surface concentration was achieved by Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. Further increase of Ce loading led to the aggregation of Ce species and decreased the concentration of atomically dispersed Ce species.

The catalytic performance of the samples was evaluated with a GHSV of 120 L gcat−1 h−1 due to the outstanding promotion effect of atomically dispersed Ce species. As the Ce/Al molar ratio increased up to 1.0, the activity increased, but thereafter it decreased (Supplementary Fig. 79). TOF was derived to exclude the influence of morphology and Ni site density. As shown in Fig. 7e, a volcano-type relationship between TOF values and Ce loading was observed, with the highest TOF achieved by Ni8Ce1Al1Ox (4.33 s−1). Kinetic measurements were carried out to reveal the origin of the volcano-type curve. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 80a, samples with different Ce loading exhibited comparable apparent activation energy, ranging from 102.3 to 106.3 kJ mol−1. The close Ea value demonstrated that the activity difference did not stem from the N* recombination step. As shown in Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 80b, the H2 reaction order exhibited a volcano-shaped curve against the Ce loading. The H2 reaction order increased from −0.50 for Ni6.5Al3.5Ox to −0.34 for Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox. Ni8Ce1Al1Ox showed the highest H2 reaction order of −0.23. Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox and Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox exhibited intensified H poisoning, with decreased H2 reaction order of −0.31 and −0.45, respectively. The variation of H2 reaction order matched well with the volcano-type curve of TOF values, suggesting that the intrinsic activity of Ni sites was related to the extent of hydrogen poisoning. Samples with alleviated H poisoning possessed higher intrinsic activity.

The similarity in volcano-type relationship between H2 reaction order, TOF values, and surface concentration of atomically dispersed Ce species suggested that the intrinsic activity of Ni sites was determined by the surface concentration of atomically dispersed Ce species. The sample with abundant atomically dispersed Ce species exhibited enhanced resistance to H poisoning, leading to an outstanding intrinsic activity.

In summary, we prepared a Ni-Ce-Al trinary catalyst with atomically dispersed Ce species crowded around Ni nanoparticles and revealed the direct contribution of CeO2 in promoting NH3 activation and H2 desorption. High-loading Ni nanoparticles (66.5 wt%) were connected by the Al2O3 framework, with abundant Ce single atoms and clusters located on the boundary of Al2O3 and Ni nanoparticles. The construction of atomically dispersed Ce species overcame the trade-off between density and intrinsic activity of Ni sites, which existed when either CeO2 or Al2O3 was employed individually. CeO2 was effective in improving the activity of Ni sites, while it failed to stabilize the high-loading Ni nanoparticles. The Al2O3 framework mitigated the sintering of Ni, while it showed minimal effect in improving the intrinsic activity of Ni. The as-prepared catalyst overcame the trade-off, reaching a high H2 production rate of 324.0 mmol gcat−1 min−1 at 600 °C. The catalyst also exhibited outstanding resistance to sintering, achieving stable NH3 conversion for 200 h. Rigorous analysis demonstrated that atomically dispersed Ce species enhanced ammonia decomposition activity by alleviating hydrogen poisoning. Ce single atoms and clusters with high hydrogen affinity served as scavengers for H* during reactions. The H* species migrated orientally to the Ce sites to form active Ce3+‒OH groups, releasing the blocked Ni sites. Consequently, the desorption of H* and the activation of NH3 on poisoning-free Ni sites were promoted. Moreover, we demonstrated that the intrinsic activity of Ni sites in the Ni-Ce-Al trinary catalyst was correlated to the surface concentration of atomically dispersed Ce species. Our work provides insights into the mechanism of the promoting effect of CeO2 in ammonia decomposition, paving the way for the rational design of efficient catalysts with heterostructures.

Methods

Preparation of Ni-Ce-Al catalyst

The Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, aluminum isopropoxide, citric acid, and ethanol used in the catalyst preparation process are from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The Pluronic P123 is from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd.

The Ni-Ce-Al catalysts were prepared using a sol-gel method. For a typical procedure, 1.0 g of Pluronic P123 and 0.525 g of citric acid monohydrate were dissolved in 50 mL of ethanol to form a transparent solution. The target ratios of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, and aluminum isopropoxide were added to the solution. The total amount of metal precursor was 5 mmol. After stirring for 6 h at room temperature, the obtained green homogeneous sol was transferred into an oven and dried at 120 °C overnight to form a gel. Calcination was carried out in the air by slowly increasing the temperature from room temperature to 400 °C at a rate of 1 °C min−1, followed by a dwell of 2 h at 400 °C. The obtained samples were denoted as NiaCebAlcOx, where a, b, and c correspond to the metal molar ratio. The obtained samples were reduced in H2 at 600 °C for 1 h before activity evaluation.

To identify the optimal Ni loading, a series of samples with varied Ni molar ratios was prepared. These samples were denoted as Ni6Ce2Al2Ox, Ni7Ce1.5Al1.5Ox, Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, and Ni9Ce0.5Al0.5Ox. Ni loadings were tuned, but the same Ce/(Ce + Al) molar percentage of 1 was set.

To investigate the influence of Ce loading, samples with different molar percentages of Ce/(Ce + Al) were prepared. Ni loading was set around 67 wt%, close to that of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. These samples were labeled as Ni6.5Al3.5Ox, Ni7.6Ce0.8Al1.6Ox, Ni8.2Ce1.2Al0.6Ox, and Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox. Their Ce/(Ce + Al) molar percentage was 0%, 33%, 67%, and 100%, respectively.

The optimal catalyst was denoted as Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, with a Ni loading of 66.5 wt% and a Ce loading of 19.9 wt% (Supplementary Table 1). Two catalysts, denoted as Ni8Ce2Ox and Ni8Al2Ox, were prepared for comparison. To eliminate the influence of different Ni loading between Ni8Ce1Al1Ox, Ni8Ce2Ox, and Ni8Al2Ox, the activity of Ni6.5Al3.5Ox and Ni8.6Ce1.4Ox was systematically evaluated. They showed unified Ni loadings around 67 wt% (Supplementary Table 1).

Preparation of Ni8Ce1Al1Ox-WI catalyst

Ni8Ce1Al1Ox-WI was prepared using a wet impregnation method. The nomenclature denoted that it had the same element component as Ni8Ce1Al1Ox. CeAlOx support was first prepared using the same sol-gel method mentioned above. After calcination at 400 °C, a yellow powder was obtained. 0.831 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 80 mg of CeAlOx support were dispersed in 35 mL of ethanol. After vigorous stirring for 2 h, ethanol was removed by rotary evaporation. The product was calcinated in the air by slowly increasing the temperature from room temperature to 400 °C at a rate of 1 °C min−1, followed by a dwell of 2 h at 400 °C.

Preparation of Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) catalyst

Ni6Ce2Al2Ox-WI(Ce) was prepared using a wet impregnation method. First, the Ni6Al2Ox sample was prepared using the same sol-gel method mentioned above. After calcination at 400 °C, a black powder was obtained. 0.307 g of Ce(NO3)3·6H2O and 200 mg of Ni6Al2Ox support were dispersed in 50 mL of ethanol. After vigorous stirring for 2 h, ethanol was removed by rotary evaporation. The product was calcinated in the air by slowly increasing the temperature from room temperature to 400 °C at a rate of 1 °C min−1, followed by a dwell of 2 h at 400 °C.

Preparation of Al2O3 and 5Ce-Al2O3

Al2O3 and Ce-doped Al2O3 samples for solid-state NMR spectroscopy were prepared using the same sol-gel method. 1.0 g of Pluronic P123 and 0.420 g of citric acid monohydrate were dissolved in 50 mL of ethanol to form a transparent solution. Target ratio of Al(NO3)3·9H2O and Ce(NO3)3·6H2O were added to the solution. The total amount of metal precursor was 5 mmol. The following steps were the same as the procedure mentioned above. The Ce-doped Al2O3 samples were denoted as yCe-Al2O3, where y was the loading of Ce in wt%.

Catalytic test and kinetics measurement

Ammonia decomposition activity was tested using a fixed-bed reactor. In a typical test procedure, 50 mg of catalyst was mixed with 1.0 g of quartz sand and packed into a quartz tube with an inner diameter of 8 mm. The catalyst bed was supported by the quartz wool plug on both sides. Before the reaction, the catalysts were reduced in a stream of 10% H2/Ar (total flow rate 50 mL min−1) at 600 °C for 1 h. The gas was switched to a pure NH3 stream, and the catalyst bed was adjusted to the target reaction temperature. All the activity tests were carried out in the temperature range of 400–600 °C with a step of 50 °C. The NH3 GHSV varied from 30 to 360 L gcat−1 h−1. At each temperature step, a period of 1 h was allowed for stabilization. The outlet gas was analyzed by an online gas chromatograph (FuLi GC9790Plus) equipped with a TCD detector. The long-term stability test of the catalyst was conducted at 600 °C (GHSV = 240 L gcat−1 h−1) for 200 h. The cycling stability test was examined by continuously performing activity tests 9 times (GHSV = 30 L gcat−1 h−1). After each cycle of activity test, the fixed-bed reactor was cooled to 400 °C naturally, and the next cycle of activity tests was carried out. The apparent activation energy was calculated based on activity with conversion below 15%. The NH3 conversion was adjusted by changing the reaction temperature and catalyst loading. The reaction orders were conducted by varying one gas concentration of H2 or NH3 with Ar as the balance gas and measured at 450 °C. For the kinetic study, the conversion of NH3 was controlled below 15% for each test.
