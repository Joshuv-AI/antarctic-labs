First free microvascular deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap for autologous breast reconstruction in Mali: a case report
Authors: Judy Martin, Dorrit Winterholer, Robin van den Wildenberg, Moussa Baba Daou, Volker Wedler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Access to comprehensive breast cancer care, including reconstructive surgery, remains limited in many low-resource settings, such as Mali. While autologous breast reconstruction using free flaps is well established in high-income countries, its implementation in resource-constrained environments is challenging due to infrastructural and workforce limitations. This case report describes the feasibility of microsurgical breast reconstruction within a collaborative international setting.Presentation of case:A 38-year-old woman with a history of left-sided radical mastectomy for triple-negative breast cancer successfully underwent autologous breast reconstruction using a free DIEP flap. The procedure was performed during a collaborative surgical mission involving Swiss and Malian plastic surgeons. Despite limited preoperative imaging and intraoperative resources, the flap demonstrated reliable perfusion. Postoperative follow-up was conducted locally by a Malian plastic surgeon.Discussion:This case highlights the challenges and suggests the feasibility of microsurgical breast reconstruction in selected patients in a low-resource setting. Key limiting factors include restricted access to oncologic therapies, absence of advanced imaging modalities, limited microsurgical infrastructure, and constrained postoperative monitoring. The collaborative approach, with active local surgical involvement, represents a step toward sustainable capacity building.Conclusion:Microsurgical breast reconstruction using a DIEP flap appears feasible in selected patients in a low-resource setting when embedded within a collaborative framework. Long-term sustainability depends on local training, infrastructure development, and integration into existing healthcare systems.

Introduction

Breast cancer represents a growing public health challenge in Mali, where patients frequently present with advanced-stage disease due to limited access to screening and early diagnostic services. In contrast to high-income countries, where multidisciplinary oncologic treatment is standard, therapeutic options in Mali remain limited by infrastructural and financial constraints, resulting in mastectomy as the most common surgical approach.

Access to breast reconstruction is similarly restricted due to a lack of specialized training, microsurgical infrastructure, and structured breast care programs. At the same time, the relatively young age at diagnosis and high mastectomy rates underline the importance of reconstructive options for quality of life and psychosocial recovery.

This prompted us to bring together all medical professionals caring for breast cancer patients in Bamako, Mali, with the aim of establishing a breast program as the initial step toward a dedicated breast center. The first collaborations with local plastic surgeons, gynecologists, general surgeons, and the Ministry of Health regarding reconstructive options for breast cancer were initiated during two missions between 2022 and 2024. The objective is to optimize breast cancer diagnostics and surgical and conservative tumor therapy and to integrate reconstructive breast surgery. Interplast Switzerland, which has been performing humanitarian missions in Mali since 2019, is committed to improving mastectomy and breast reconstruction techniques through regular missions in cooperation with local colleagues, thereby enhancing outcomes and quality of life and making breast cancer therapy accessible to women from all social backgrounds.

Within this framework, repeated surgical missions aim not only to provide treatment but also to support structured capacity-building through close cooperation with Malian colleagues.

This report presents a case of delayed autologous breast reconstruction using a free deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap, performed within this collaborative framework.

This case report has been presented in line with the SCARE checklist[1].

Case report

During an Interplast mission in Mali in February 2025, a 38-year-old female patient presented for delayed reconstruction of her left breast (Fig. 1). Her primary motivation was the restoration of body image and psychosocial well-being following a mastectomy. Figure 1.Preoperative image of the patient, presenting 4 years after a mastectomy, lymphadenectomy, and radiation therapy of the left breast due to breast carcinoma.

Preoperative image of the patient, presenting 4 years after a mastectomy, lymphadenectomy, and radiation therapy of the left breast due to breast carcinoma.

In 2021, she was diagnosed with triple-negative invasive breast carcinoma (T3N1M0G2), which was initially detected through self-examination. A mastectomy with axillary lymphadenectomy was performed. Systemic chemotherapy was recommended according to international guidelines but was not administered due to limited availability within the local healthcare system and financial constraints. Radiotherapy was provided as a resource-adapted treatment strategy. Hormonal therapy was not indicated.

The patient had a history of caesarean section and an accessory right mammary gland. She was otherwise healthy, with no relevant comorbidities.

Autologous reconstruction using a DIEP flap was selected. Alternative options, such as implant-based reconstruction or pedicled flaps, were considered less favorable.

Preoperative assessment was limited to portable ultrasound (Siemens Acuson P550, VF-16.5 probe) and handheld Doppler (Edan SonoTrax Pro) due to the absence of CT angiography. Two perforators were identified bilaterally.

The procedure was performed jointly by an experienced Swiss microsurgical team, together with a Malian plastic surgeon who was actively involved intraoperatively and is responsible for ongoing postoperative care.

Flap elevation revealed an intramuscular perforator course, making dissection technically demanding. Recipient vessels were identified as an internal mammary perforator. Microsurgical anastomosis was performed under a single-ocular operating microscope. Arterial anastomosis was completed end-to-end using 9-0 Prolene sutures, and venous drainage was established using a 2.5-mm venous coupler. Adequate perfusion was confirmed intraoperatively.

Following inset, zones IV and part of zone III were resected. The flap was rotated 180 degrees to optimize contour (Fig. 2). Additional shaping was performed using a purse-string suture. The donor site was closed as an abdominoplasty. Rectus plication and quilting sutures were performed, and two drains were placed. Figure 2.Intraoperative image after the closure of the DIEP flap.

Intraoperative image after the closure of the DIEP flap.

On the first postoperative day, the patient developed a hematoma underneath the reconstructed breast, necessitating revision with the placement of drains. Thereafter, the postoperative course was uneventful.

The patient was followed locally for 1 week and subsequently managed by the collaborating Malian plastic surgeon (Fig. 3), who ensures ongoing continuity of care. Secondary symmetrization and nipple reconstruction are planned. Figure 3.Image of the patient 4 months postoperatively.

Discussion

Breast cancer remains the most common malignancy among women in Mali and represents a growing public health challenge across sub-Saharan Africa[2]. According to the Bamako cancer registry (2010–2017), breast cancer shows the highest incidence among female malignancies[2]. In 2019, 1545 cases were recorded in Bamako, with 65.8% affecting women, although these figures likely underestimate the true burden due to limited screening and incomplete data collection[3].

Due to the limited access to medical therapy and screening programs, financial barriers, sociocultural beliefs, traditional medicine, fear of surgery, and its stigmatization contribute to locally advanced tumor stages at initial presentation[3]. Systematic analyses report that in Mali, approximately 39.6% of patients present with stage III disease and 25% with stage IV disease[4]. Across sub-Saharan Africa, approximately 65% of women present with advanced-stage breast cancer, and around 20% are metastatic[4]. Hence, 95% of breast cancer patients in Mali underwent mastectomy, primarily due to the advanced tumor stage, tumor biology, and the unavailability of neoadjuvant therapy[4].

Within this context, breast reconstruction is not merely an aesthetic procedure but an integral component of comprehensive breast cancer care, with demonstrated benefits in quality of life, psychosocial recovery, and long-term functional outcomes[5–7]. The relatively young age at diagnosis, with a mean of approximately 45 years in the region[8,9], further underscores the importance of integrating reconstructive options into standard oncologic care pathways.

The DIEP flap, first described by Holmström and later refined by Allen, Treece, and others, is widely regarded as the gold standard for autologous breast reconstruction due to reduced donor-site morbidity and improved long-term outcomes compared to muscle-sacrificing techniques[10–16].

However, the translation of microsurgical breast reconstruction into low-resource settings remains limited. Major barriers include insufficient microsurgical infrastructure, a lack of advanced imaging modalities such as CT angiography or indocyanine green angiography, limited specialized training, and financial constraints restricting access and continuity of care[17–20]. Additional systemic limitations include the inconsistent availability of essential medications such as anticoagulants and antibiotics, as well as geographic barriers requiring long-distance travel to the capital for specialized care.

In the present case, these challenges were further compounded by an unstable electricity supply, limited availability of microsurgical equipment, and restricted postoperative monitoring capacity.

Importantly, this case must be interpreted within the broader context of ongoing efforts to develop a structured breast care program in Bamako. In recent years, multidisciplinary collaborations between local plastic surgeons, gynecologists, general surgeons, and the Ministry of Health have aimed to improve breast cancer diagnostics and surgical and systemic treatment pathways and to progressively integrate reconstructive breast surgery into routine care. The long-term goal of these initiatives is the establishment of dedicated breast centers that allow coordinated, multidisciplinary management and improved accessibility of care.

Within this framework, the present case highlights the importance of structured international–local collaboration. The procedure was performed jointly with a Malian plastic surgeon, who was actively involved intraoperatively and remains responsible for ongoing postoperative care. This continuity represents a key element of sustainability and capacity building rather than an isolated humanitarian intervention.

Training and capacity building remain central components of sustainability[21].

Raising awareness and patient education are equally important. Educational initiatives can help inform patients about available reconstructive options and their potential benefits, while psychological and social support may enable women to make informed decisions regarding their treatment[22].

Finally, while DIEP flap reconstruction offers significant advantages in selected patients, its implementation in low-resource settings must remain individualized and dependent on local expertise, infrastructure, and follow-up capacity. This case, therefore, illustrates both the potential feasibility and the current systemic limitations of microsurgical breast reconstruction in such environments.

Conclusion

Microsurgical breast reconstruction using a DIEP flap appears feasible in selected patients in a low-resource setting when performed within a structured collaborative framework. Sustainable implementation requires ongoing training, infrastructure development, and integration into local healthcare systems.
