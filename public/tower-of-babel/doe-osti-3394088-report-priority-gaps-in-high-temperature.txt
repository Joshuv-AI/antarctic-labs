PRIORITY GAPS IN HIGH TEMPERATURE THERMODYNAMIC DATA - INTERIM

1 INTRODUCTION
This interim progress report (Level 4 Milestone Number M4SF-26LL010203023) summarizes
research conducted at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) within the Argillite Host Rock
Properties & Processes SF-26LL01020302. Our focus is to assess gaps in data availability and
understanding for radionuclide thermodynamics within the context of a “hot repository” concept and
expand SUPCRT-NE database development to address higher temperatures needed for a DPC DGR
disposal concept. The database is intended to inform the argillite GDSA baseline model. The leading
European thermochemical database (Thermochimie) is only applicable to temperatures below 80°C.
Thus, a US effort to integrate and expand upon other international thermodynamics database efforts
is needed, particular if a “hot repository” concept moves forward.

2 APPLICATION OF THERMODYNAMIC MODELS TO THE SHALE GENERIC
 REPOSITORY CONCEPT
In 2025, a draft manuscript describing the approach to thermodynamic database development at
LLNL for the nuclear waste repository reactive transport modeling and performance assessment was
prepared (Wolery et al., 2025). The approach takes advantage of the many well-curated sources of
thermodynamic data available in the literature and provides a workflow to combine these data in a
self-consistent manner. This approach can be used to expedite the development of thermodynamic
databases that capture the wide range of thermal and geochemical conditions that may be present
in the nearfield of a “hot repository” concept under consideration in the US.
As part of a broader FY26 effort to develop a repository concept for shale rock, we developed a
workflow to directly link radionuclide solubility from a thermodynamic database to a Performance
Assessment model. At present, the SUPCRT-NE database and associated codes are not sufficiently
developed to effectively perform this analysis. Instead, we chose to test the application of the
Thermochimie database which has been adopted by UK, French, and Belgian nuclear waste
agencies for geochemical modeling. The Thermochimie database is in active developement and
relies on Nuclear Energy Agency thermodynamic data (e.g. NEA, 2020) as a foundation while
enhancing those data with curated data from the literature.
For a repository concept in shale rock, a model groundwater chemistry must be identified. We used
water chemistries that were developed at Sandia National Laboratories to represent three
mineralogically distinct geologic units that may be present in a shale deposit: chalky marl,
sandstone, and primary shale (Table 1). Importantly, all solubility calculations were performed at
25°C to simplify this initial analysis. In future iterations, we plan to retain this generic rock chemistry
and examine the database limitations for determining solubilities at higher temperatures and
evaluate impacts on predicted solubilities.

To determine solubility limits, we used the water chemistry listed in Table 1 together with the
Thermochimie database (ThermoChimie_v13a O2 format exported to the Geochemist’s WorkBench
software package) and the Geochemist’s WorkBench 2026 software release 19.0.0. Simulated fluid
composition included Na+, Cl-, SO42-, Ca2+, Fe2+, K+, Mg2+ and HCO3-. The HCO3- concentration was
fixed based on equilibrium with calcite. The pH was sampled based on a ±2SD range that was
derived from the original data used to develop Table 1. A lower limit and upper limit solubility was
determined based on sampling the pH range and defining a specific solubility controlling solid phase
(Table 2). Based on the range of solubilities, a Selected Value is identified and reported in Table 2.
For reference, we also list solubility selected values from past estimates as well. Recommended
solubility values here are consistently higher than those of Hansen et al. (2010) except for Am which
has a slightly lower solubility. The thermo.com.V8.R6 database used by Hansen et al. (2010) has not
been updated in many years so we are much more confident in the values from the Thermochimie
database which is based on the latest updates from the NEA TDB effort as well as current literature.
Similarly, one can compare selected values published by NAGRA (Berner, 1995) for reducing
bentonite conditions. In general, values selected here are higher (more conservative) or similar to
those of NAGRA. Again, one should note that thermodynamic databases used to determine
solubilities in 1994 have undergone significant changes and improvements. As a result, values
reported here are more representative of the present-day state of knowledge regarding radionuclide
thermodynamics.
Importantly, the effort to determine solubility limiting phases highlighted a number of limitations that
exist both in the Thermochimie database and our understanding of how to establish solubility limits
in Performance Assessment models. The following are a list of issues that should be considered:
 • The choice of solubility limiting phase is often based on expert judgement rather than
 thermodynamic principles. For example, Pu(+4) solubility may be controlled by a crystalline
 oxide phase or a hydrated amorphous phase. These two phases result in a difference in
 solubility of 6 orders of magnitude. While the expert judgement would generally tend towards
 using the hydrated amorphous phase, this choice also leads to a more conservative estimate
 of solubility. The reality may lie somewhere between the solubility of these two phases.
 • The assumed redox condition in the repository will have a major impact on the solubility
 limiting phase. The results reported in Table 2 are based on the assumption of strongly

 reducing conditions. Thus, Np(+4) is the presumed solubility limiting oxidation state of
 neptunium. The presence of Np(+5) would drastically shift the neptunium solubility to a
 higher value. However, redox will depend on the specific repository conditions and cannot be
 determined a priori.
 • Some radionuclides do not have a clear candidate for determining solubility limits due to
 complex redox chemistry and no consensus regarding likely solubility limiting phases.
 Selenium is a prominent example. Under reducing conditions, Se may be present as Se(+4),
 Se(0), or Se(-2). The most reduced state generally precipitates as a sulfide but likely as a
 coprecipitate with iron sulfides. As the solubility limit of a coprecipitate of this solid phase
 cannot be readily determined, a conservative estimate may be to select a Se(+4) phase.
 However, most Se(+4) phases (e.g. calcium selenite) are very soluble so a simplified model
 may be forced to avoid any solubility limits.
 • Some radionuclides likely form precipitates only via coprecipitation. This would be the case
 of 14C whose solubility is limited by the coprecipitation with calcite. However, precipitation is
 dictated by the concentration of stable HCO3- and a solid solution model is not readily
 implemented as a simple solubility term in models.
 • The solubility of some radionuclides is complicated by a combination of redox chemistry and
 reaction with organic matter. In particular iodine has a high affinity for certain organic
 compounds which form organo-iodine compounds. This process may lead to significant
 retardation but is not attributable to classical sorption or solubility limits.
Finally, it should be noted that solubility is a function of geochemical condition and solution
chemistry in particular. As shown in Table 2, solubility limits vary depending on the solution
chemistry (i.e. Table 1). However, in the case of simple PA models, inclusion of one solubility limiting
value is preferred. To ensure a level of conservatism, the solution condition in the model domain that
lead to the highest solubility limiting concentration are chosen.

3 PLANNED FY27 EFFORTS
Workscope for FY27 is being evaluated based on a shift in R&D program priorities that is now
underway. The US is presently considering multiple waste disposal scenarios and reprocessing
strategies. The direct disposal of dual purpose canisters in deep geologic repositories (DGRs) is
based on a “hot repository” concept which has not been investigated in as much detail as the “cold
repository” concepts moving forward in Europe. The “hot repository” concept requires a more
rigorous assessment of the knowledge gaps regarding the thermodynamic behavior of rocks,
minerals, and waste packages at temperature. LLNL will continue to pursue an effort to determine
data gaps and needs in both thermodynamics and sorption with regards to hot repository conditions,
borehole disposal concepts, and other scenarios (initiated in FY26). In FY27, our primary focus will
be on reducing conservatism in the integration of thermodynamics in PA with an emphasis on risk-
driving and mobile radionuclides (i.e. iodine, selenium, 14C). The ultimate goal will be to determine
defensible and mechanistic thermodynamic models that will allow the PA to take credit for the
retardation of these radionuclides via organo-radionuclide compound formation, coprecipitation, and
redox transformation. We will also continue to collaborate and take advantage of ongoing efforts in
the EURAD-II program that support a reduction in model conservatism. Two activities (THERMOGAP
and RAMPEC) are directly related to the assessment of thermodynamic database and experimental
data limitations with regards to addressing radionuclide reactive transport under “perturbed
conditions”. LLNL and other national labs will continue to engage in several EURAD-II efforts as
Associate Partners.
