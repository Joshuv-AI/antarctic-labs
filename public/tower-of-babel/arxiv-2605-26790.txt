Pretrained Approximators for Low-Thrust Trajectory Cost and Reachability
Zhong Zhang Politecnico di Milano zhong.zhang@polimi.it, Giacomo Acciarini European Space Agency giacomo.acciarini@esa.int, Dario Izzo European Space Agency dario.izzo@esa.int Hexi Baoyin Tsinghua University baoyin@tsinghua.edu.cn Francesco Topputo Politecnico di Milano francesco.topputo@polimi.it

Abstract
Low-thrust trajectory design relies heavily on repeated evaluations of fuel consumption and transfer feasibility, which require expensive optimal control solutions. In this work, we show these quantities can be accurately approximated by machine learning surrogates, enabling fast and scalable evaluation across a wide range of scenarios. By increasing both dataset size and model capacity, we observe that low-thrust trajectory optimization follows a scaling law, with performance improving linearly with the logarithm of training data and network parameters, and no evidence of saturation within the explored regime. Guided by this observation, we construct a large-scale dataset using the proposed homotopy-ray strategy tailored to mission design requirements. A key is the introduction of a self-similar transformation, which allows generalization across semi-major axes, inclinations, and central bodies avoiding retraining. As a result, the same neural approximator can be applied to diverse orbital environments and mission classes. The proposed models accurately predict optimal fuel consumption and minimum transfer time for single- and multi-revolution transfers. Their performance and generalization are demonstrated on a public dataset, a multi-asteroid flyby problem from the Global Trajectory Optimization Competition, and an asteroid rendezvous mission design. The models and datasets are released as open-source to support the space community.

Introduction

Low-thrust electric propulsion has become a key enabling technology for modern
space missions, offering substantially higher specific impulse and mission
flexibility compared to conventional chemical propulsion. Its successful
application in missions such as Deep Space 1
[
47
]
,
Hayabusa
[
35
]
, BepiColombo
[
3
]
,
and Psyche
[
43
]
has demonstrated its potential for ambitious
scientific and exploration objectives. At the same time, low-thrust propulsion
fundamentally changes the nature of trajectory design: optimal transfers are no
longer defined by a small number of impulsive maneuvers, but by continuous
control laws obtained from solving nonlinear optimal control problems
[
66
]
.

While high-fidelity optimal control methods are well established, their
computational cost remains a major bottleneck in practical mission design.
During early-phase analysis and global optimization, mission designers often
need to evaluate millions of candidate transfers under varying boundary
conditions, propulsion parameters, and mission constraints
[
64
]
. Solving a full
low-thrust optimal control problem for each candidate is generally
computationally prohibitive, motivating the widespread use of approximate
methods for estimating key performance indicators such as fuel consumption,
transfer duration, and reachability.
As physicist Lev Landau noted, "the most important part of doing physics is the
knowledge of approximation", a principle that equally applies to this case.

A wide range of approximation techniques have been developed for this purpose.
Analytical and semi-analytical approaches, including Edelbaum-type
approximations
[
8
]
, Lambert-based surrogates
[
31
,
60
,
48
]
,
shape-based methods
[
58
,
61
]
,
and simplified dynamical models
[
19
,
5
,
22
,
51
]
, offer excellent computational
efficiency, but are typically restricted to specific dynamical regimes or
mission geometries. Database-driven approaches can achieve higher accuracy over
narrowly defined problem classes
[
45
,
65
]
,
yet their performance strongly depends on data coverage and often degrades when
applied outside the original training domain.

In recent years, machine-learning-based surrogate models have emerged as a
promising alternative, combining fast evaluation with improved expressive
power. Neural networks, as universal function approximators
[
44
]
,
have been successfully applied to a variety of astrodynamics problems, including orbit uncertainty, periodic
orbit generation in multi-body dynamics, on-board guidance, and autonomous
navigation
[
20
,
59
,
11
,
29
,
28
,
46
,
1
]
.
Existing approaches for low-thrust trajectory optimization can be broadly divided
into two categories: models that predict trajectory-level quantities such as
fuel consumption or minimum transfer time, and models that directly predict
optimal controls or costates. Following the terminology introduced
in
[
29
]
, the former are referred to as

Value Networks
, while the latter are known as
Policy Networks
or
G&CNETs. Value Networks are particularly attractive for preliminary mission
design and global optimization, where fast and repeated evaluation of transfer
costs is required.

Despite encouraging results, current machine learning-based approaches face
several limitations that hinder their broader adoption in mission design.
First, most existing models are trained for narrowly defined mission scenarios,
with fixed propulsion characteristics or limited orbital regimes, requiring
data regeneration and retraining when applied to new problems. Second, the lack of
publicly available datasets and pretrained models makes independent validation
and comparison difficult, reducing confidence in neural surrogate methods among
practitioners.

The objective of this work is to address these limitations by developing a
general-purpose neural approximator for low-thrust trajectory
evaluation that can be applied across a wide range of mission scenarios without
retraining. To this end, we make four main contributions. First, we provide
evidence that low-thrust trajectory approximation exhibits a scaling law:
increasing both dataset size and model capacity leads to systematic performance
improvements without observable saturation within the explored range. Second,
motivated by this observation, we construct a large-scale dataset using a
homotopy-ray data generation strategy specifically designed to match typical
mission design requirements. Third, we introduce a self-similar transformation
of the problem variables that enables a single pretrained model to generalize
across different semi-major axes, inclinations, and central bodies. Finally, we
release both the trained models and the dataset as open-source resources.

Compared with existing reports, the proposed models achieve better accuracy in predicting optimal fuel
consumption and minimum transfer time. Their performance and generalization capabilities are demonstrated on a public
dataset, a multi-asteroid flyby problem from the 4th Global Trajectory
Optimization Competition, and a low-thrust asteroid rendezvous scenario.

Related Work

Neural-network-based methods for low-thrust trajectory design generally address
two complementary problems
[
26
]
. The first concerns the prediction of
trajectory-level performance indicators, such as fuel consumption, transfer
time, or reachability. The second focuses on the direct approximation of optimal
control laws or costates, enabling real-time guidance. Following the terminology
introduced in
[
29
]
, these two classes of
models are referred to as
Value Networks
and
Policy Networks
,
respectively. Value Networks are primarily intended for mission design and
analysis, where fast evaluation of large numbers of candidate transfers is
required, whereas Policy Networks are designed for on-board guidance and
control. This section reviews representative work in both categories, with an
emphasis on Value Networks, which are the focus of the present study.

2.1
Value Network

Value Networks aim to replace computationally intensive low-thrust trajectory
optimization with fast surrogate models that approximate the mapping from
mission parameters to optimal performance metrics. Their main application lies
in preliminary mission design and global optimization, where millions of
trajectory evaluations may be required
[
67
]
.

The early work demonstrating the application of machine learning to approximate fuel-optimal low-thrust transfers was presented by Mereta et al.
[
40
]
. Subsequently, Zhu et al.
[
67
]
and Li et al.
[
37
]
further investigated this direction.
A common formulation involves the use of two separate networks: a classifier to
determine transfer feasibility and a regressor to estimate fuel
consumption
[
67
,
62
]
. Li et
al.
[
37
]
further extended this approach to
impulsive maneuver problems, highlighting the versatility of neural
approximators across propulsion regimes.

For minimum-transfer-time problems, Guo et
al.
[
17
]
addressed the challenge of data sparsity
near the reachability boundary by introducing enhanced sampling strategies and
weighted loss functions, significantly improving prediction accuracy for
asteroid rendezvous missions. Mughal et
al.
[
42
]
similarly employed deep neural networks to predict
electric-propulsion transfer times for LEO-to-GEO missions, achieving rapid and
accurate estimates suitable for preliminary design studies.

Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of Value Networks in capturing nonlinear optimal-control structures that are challenging to model analytically.
The neural representation of optimal guidance problems was formalized by Izzo and Origer
[
28
]
. Subsequently, Acciarini et al.
[
2
]
investigated neural approximators for low-thrust transfer problems and benchmarked their performance against analytical solutions.
Extensions to more complex
dynamical environments, including multi-target solar-sailing
missions
[
53
]
and proximity operations under
perturbations
[
25
]
, further demonstrate the flexibility and broad
applicability of Value Networks in astrodynamics.

Despite these advances, most existing Value Networks are trained for narrowly
defined mission scenarios. As a result, their generalization capabilities
across propulsion parameters, orbital regimes, or central bodies remain
limited, and direct comparisons between different approaches are often
hindered by the lack of publicly available datasets and pretrained models.

2.2
Policy Network

Policy Networks address a complementary problem: the direct approximation of

Subsequent studies investigated the theoretical properties of such systems.

interplanetary transfers, solar sailing, and proximity

6

To improve robustness under model uncertainties and environmental disturbances,

showed that these approaches enhance

unmodeled dynamics. Evans et al.
[
9

investigated the use of
differential algebra to refine neural guidance laws, while Holt et
al.
[
23
]
provided comparative analyses of reinforcement

Recent research has sought to connect neural control with classical stability and safety theory. Approaches based on Lyapunov functions provide formal guarantees of stability and constraint satisfaction
[
23
,
24
,
54

, while the event-transition tensor framework introduced by Izzo et al.

offers a systematic tool for analyzing the behavior of G&CNETs when deployed as controllers for uncertain dynamical systems.

3
Data Generation

Constructing large-scale, mission-oriented low-thrust trajectory datasets
requires both accurate trajectory solutions and efficient coverage of the
engineering-relevant state space. In this work, we adopt an indirect optimal
control method to compute individual trajectories, and combine it with a
continuation strategy, referred to as the
Homotopy Ray Method
, to generate
a diverse set of transfer solutions. The method focuses sampling on regions of
interest, typically transfers with low fuel consumption or minimal transfer
time, which are most relevant for practical mission design and optimization.

Two separate datasets are constructed to train dedicated neural networks for
fuel-optimal and time-optimal problems, respectively. Reachability naturally
arises from the time-optimal formulation: a transfer is unreachable if the
specified transfer time is shorter than the minimum, and reachable only once
this lower bound is exceeded. Trajectories at the boundary of reachability
correspond to the time-optimal solution
[
67
]
. Framing reachability prediction as a time-optimal control problem
thus provides a continuous and physically meaningful measure, offering richer
information than a simple binary classification of reachable versus
unreachable transfers.

The combination of indirect trajectory solutions and the Homotopy Ray
sampling strategy enables the efficient construction of large-scale datasets
that capture both optimal trajectories and the boundary of reachability,
forming the database to be used for the neural surrogates training.

3.1
Indirect Method for Optimal Control

This subsection describes the indirect method used to solve fuel-optimal and
time-optimal low-thrust optimal control problems. In both cases, Pontryagin’s
minimum principle is applied by introducing the costate vector to transform the
original optimal control problem into a two-point boundary value problem
(TPBVP), which is then solved with a shooting method.

In all the problem formulations, we assume that the maximum thrust
T_{\max}
and specific impulse

\displaystyle\dot{m}(t)=-\frac{T_{\max}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}},\quad\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|\leq 1

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(t_{0})=\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\quad m(t_{0})=m_{0},

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(t_{f})=\boldsymbol{x}_{f}.

(5)

For the time-optimal problem, the goal is to minimize the transfer time:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u},t_{f}}\quad

\displaystyle J_{\rm time},

(6)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x,u}),

(7)

\displaystyle\dot{m}(t)=-\frac{T_{\max}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}},\quad\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|\leq 1

3.1.2
Indirect method and TPBVP Formulation

The indirect method involves transforming the original problem into a two-point boundary value problem (TPBVP) using Pontryagin’s minimum principle, and it will be used to solve the two optimal control problems introduced above.

For the fuel-optimal problem, referring to the costate vector

\bm{\lambda}_{x}(t),{\lambda}_{m}(t)
and scalar multiplier
\lambda_{0}
, the
Hamiltonian is constructed as

H_{\rm fuel}=\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\dot{\bm{x}}+\bm{\lambda}_{m}\dot{m}+\lambda_{0}L_{\rm fuel}(\bm{x},\bm{u})=\frac{T_{\max}}{m}\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{M}\,\bm{\alpha}u+\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{D}-\lambda_{m}\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}}u+\lambda_{0}\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\mathrm{sp}}g_{0}}\{u-\varepsilon\ln[u(1-u)]\}.

(25)

The costate dynamics are then given by

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\lambda}}_{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bm{x}}=-\left(\frac{T_{\max}}{m}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{x}}\,\bm{\alpha}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}\,u+\left(\frac{\partial\bm{D}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}\right),

(26)

\displaystyle\dot{{\lambda}}_{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial{m}}=\frac{T_{\max}}{m^{2}}\,\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{M}\,\bm{\alpha}\,u.

where the detailed expressions of partial derivative of
\bm{M}
and
\bm{D}
to
\bm{x}
are reported in the Appendix.

According to Pontryagin’s Minimum Principle,
the Hamiltonian must be minimized with respect to the control, which leads to the
optimal thrust direction and magnitude:

\bm{\alpha}^{*}=-\frac{\bm{M}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}}{\|\bm{M}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}\|},

(27)

u^{*}=\frac{2\epsilon}{\rho+2\epsilon+\sqrt{\rho^{2}+4\epsilon^{2}}},

(28)

where
\rho
is the switching function and expressed by:

\rho=1-\frac{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}\|\bm{M}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}\|}{\lambda_{0}m}-\frac{\lambda_{m}}{\lambda_{0}}.

(29)

For rendezvous problems, the state vector must satisfy the boundary conditions

\bm{x}(t_{0})=\bm{x}_{0},\quad\bm{x}(t_{f})=\bm{x}_{f}.

(30)

Since the terminal mass is free in the problem, the transversality condition is
provided by

\lambda_{m}(t_{f})=0.

(31)

In addition, as suggested in
[
32
]
, the
augmented costate vector

\bm{\lambda}(t)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\lambda}_{x}(t)\\
\lambda_{m}(t)\\
\lambda_{0}\end{bmatrix}

is normalized at the initial time:

\|\bm{\lambda}(t_{0})\|=1.

(32)

Consequently, the fuel-optimal control problem is converted into the following
two-point boundary value problem:

F_{\rm fuel}\left[\bm{\lambda}(t_{0})\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}(t_{f})-\bm{x}_{f}\\
\lambda_{m}(t_{f})\\
\|\bm{\lambda}(t_{0})\|-1\end{bmatrix}=\bm{0}.

(33)

This TPBVP is solved via a shooting method
[
41
]
. Once the optimal initial costate
\bm{\lambda}_{x}(t_{0})
and final time
t_{f}
are determined, the state and costate equations (

) and (
26
) can be integrated to obtain the complete trajectory and corresponding control law. Finally, the remaining mass is computed as

m(t_{f})=m_{0}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}}u(t)\,{\rm d}t.

(34)

For the time optimal problem, only the differences are presented because of
majority similar derivations. The Hamiltonian is defined as

H_{\rm time}=\frac{T_{\max}}{m}\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{M}\,\bm{\alpha}u+\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{D}-\lambda_{m}\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}}u+\lambda_{0}.

(35)

The costate dynamics and the optimal control direction remain the same as in
the fuel-optimal problem, expressed in
Eqs.(
26
-
27
), while the
normalized magnitude of optimal control keeps the constant to 1 in time-optimal

With reference to the transversality conditions provided in

[
36
]
, the optimal final time
t_{f}
is
determined by

H_{\rm time}(t_{f})-\bm{\lambda}_{x}(t_{f})\cdot\dot{\bm{x}}_{f}=H_{\rm time}(t_{f})-\bm{\lambda}_{L}(t_{f})\frac{\sqrt{\mu p_{f}}}{r_{f}^{2}}=0.

(36)

The term

is the natural Keplerian angular rate of the target’s true longitude
L
at the final state.
p_{f}
is the final semilatus rectum, and

, one has
\dot{L}_{f}=h_{f}/r_{f}^{2}

As a result, the time-optimal control problem is converted into the following
two-point boundary value problem:

F_{\rm time}\left[\bm{\lambda}(t_{0});t_{f}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}(t_{f})-\bm{x}_{f}(t_{f})\\
\lambda_{m}(t_{f})=0\\
H_{\rm time}(t_{f})-\bm{\lambda}_{L}(t_{f})\frac{\sqrt{\mu p_{f}}}{r_{f}^{2}}\\
\|\bm{\lambda}(t_{0})\|-1\end{bmatrix}=\bm{0}.

(37)

3.2
Homotopy Ray Method for Large-Scale Data Generation

To tackle the challenge of generating large-scale datasets that can be used to train machine learning surrogates, the Homotopy Ray Method is proposed. The primary goal is to efficiently generate samples tailored that exhibit low fuel consumption or lie close to the reachable boundary.
This focus is essential because optimal solutions of numerical trajectory optimization typically lie in these extreme or boundary regions. If these critical regimes are underrepresented in the training data, a model may achieve low average test error while producing large estimation errors precisely in regions relevant to optimization. Such localized inaccuracies can severely degrade optimization performance, resulting in suboptimal or even infeasible trajectories. It is illustrated through a representative example in Sec.
7.2
.

In this subsection, we first investigate the effects of dataset size and model parameter scale on performance; then, we introduce the data generation method; finally, we discuss the reasons why this method produces a mission-design-oriented data distribution.

3.2.1
Effects of Dataset Size and Model Parameter Scale.

The effect of dataset size and model capacity on approximation accuracy is
examined using fuel-consumption prediction as a representative case. The neural
network inputs, outputs, and training procedure are described in
Sec.
5

An important observation is that the effects of dataset size and model capacity
are largely independent: increasing the size of the training set does not alter
the optimal model scale, and vice versa. For each experimental setting, several
network configurations were evaluated (see Sec.
5
), and the
best-performing hyperparameters were retained. The observed trends are
consistent in form with scaling behaviors reported in other large-scale neural
approximation problems, including large language models
[
34
]
.
We emphasize, however, that the fitted exponents above should be interpreted as
an empirical observation under the specific pipeline used in this work (input
features, normalization, and training protocol described in
Sec.
5
), and whether a similar scaling law holds universally is beyond the scope
of the present study and warrants further investigation.

3.2.2
Large-Scale Data Generation

To further improve approximation accuracy and convergence robustness, the
dataset is expanded to large scale using a structured data generation strategy.
The proposed approach exploits the fact that low-thrust transfers of practical
interest tend to cluster around feasible, near-optimal regions of the design
space. Rather than sampling boundary conditions uniformly, which results in a
high fraction of infeasible or uninformative cases, we generate trajectories by
progressively deforming guaranteed-feasible solutions toward the boundary of
reachability. This strategy, referred to as the
Homotopy Ray Method
, is
illustrated schematically in Fig.
3
.

Figure 3:
Schematic diagram of the homotopy ray method.

The method consists of two stages. First, an initial feasible transfer is
constructed by sampling boundary conditions from a Keplerian orbital
neighborhood. Second, this solution is continuously deformed along a prescribed
direction in boundary-condition space until infeasibility is reached, while
reusing previously computed costates to ensure fast and reliable convergence of
the shooting method.

Initially, random initial guesses are generated through a method termed Keplerian Orbital Neighborhood Sampling. Due to thrust limitations, randomly chosen initial and terminal states are frequently infeasible for low-thrust transfers, leading to significant
computational waste. To avoid this issue, initial boundary conditions are
constructed from Keplerian trajectories, which are trivially feasible in the
absence of thrust. Under the two-body assumption, a Keplerian orbit is randomly
sampled using the orbital elements
(a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,f)
and a transfer
duration
\Delta t
, and converted into the corresponding boundary states

(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
. These
states serve as reliable starting points for subsequent homotopic deformation.

Then, starting from a Keplerian solution, a homotopy ray is defined in the space of
boundary velocities by a random, normalized six-dimensional direction vector

d=[\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{1};\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{2}]
, with

\|\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|^{2}+\|\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|^{2}=1
. A homotopy
parameter
p
controls the deviation from the original Keplerian boundary
conditions, such that

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+p\,\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{2}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{v}_{2}+p\,\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{2}.

Beginning from a small initial value of
p
, the parameter is gradually
increased, generating a sequence of trajectory optimization problems along the
homotopy ray. At each step, the costate obtained from the previous solution is
used as the initial guess for the shooting method, enabling rapid convergence.
The continuation proceeds until the trajectory becomes infeasible or numerical
convergence can no longer be achieved.

Repeating this procedure for multiple Keplerian orbits and homotopy directions
yields a large set of high-quality trajectories that densely populate the
feasible region and its boundary. Algorithm

summarizes the
implementation details of the Homotopy Ray Method.

The physical meaning of infeasibility differs between the two optimal control
formulations considered in this work. For the fuel-optimal problem, the maximum
value of the homotopy parameter corresponds to the boundary of the reachable
set; in this case, the computed time-optimal transfer duration coincides with

\Delta t
. For the time-optimal problem, infeasibility typically arises from
excessive fuel consumption, as the engine operates at maximum thrust throughout
the transfer. This may lead to highly eccentric or even hyperbolic trajectories,
causing numerical difficulties. To avoid such singular cases, an early
termination condition is introduced for the time-optimal formulation.Specifically, the optimization process is terminated when either the initial or final orbit becomes hyperbolic, or when the remaining mass fraction
m_{f}/m_{0}
falls below 0.4.

Finally, the terminal position constraint in the time-optimal problem expressed in Eq. (
24
) is
formulated as an equality constraint rather than being fixed to a specific
point. Within the homotopy ray framework, the initial trajectory must be
feasible; fixing the terminal position can violate this requirement if the
available propellant is exhausted before the prescribed transfer time. The
adopted formulation ensures feasibility of the initial solution and enables
robust homotopic continuation toward the reachability boundary.

3.2.3
Data Distribution Characteristics During Homotopy

Beyond improving computational efficiency, the homotopy ray method reveals
distinct structural characteristics in the resulting data distribution. In
particular, the generated trajectories exhibit an approximately monotonic
relationship between the velocity perturbations
(\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
and the resulting fuel consumption
\Delta v
, as
illustrated in Fig.
4
. Although the figure
corresponds to a specific case, where
\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=0
and only

\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
varies, it captures a representative trend observed
throughout the dataset.

Figure 4:
Graph illustrating the relationship between the fuel consumption and the corresponding variation in departure velocity.

This monotonic behavior arises naturally from the homotopic continuation
process: starting from a feasible Keplerian solution, progressively larger
boundary perturbations push the trajectory toward the reachability boundary,
resulting in steadily increasing control effort. As a consequence, neighboring
samples along a homotopy ray exhibit smooth and ordered variations in fuel
consumption, rather than the highly scattered patterns typical of randomly
generated datasets.

This observation motivates the use of velocity increments

(\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\Delta\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
as network inputs instead of
absolute boundary velocities. These increments better capture the intrinsic
structure of the problem and encode the difficulty of the transfer relative to
a naturally feasible reference. Similar conclusions have been reported
in
[
2
,
37
,
67
]
,
where Lambert-based solutions were employed as input features to improve network
performance. The detailed construction of the input feature set is discussed in
the following section.

An additional advantage of the homotopy ray method is that the resulting dataset naturally focuses on low-fuel-consumption trajectories
and densely samples regions near the reachability boundary, which are of primary
interest in practical mission design. Achieving a comparable distribution
through uniform random sampling would be extremely difficult. The impact of
this property on downstream applications is further demonstrated in
Sec.
7.2
.

4
Data Preparation

To reduce the input dimensionality and improve learning efficiency, first, dimensionality reduction is
introduced in a self-similar space by exploiting the inherent invariance
properties of the low-thrust transfer problem. Next, the influence of different
input configurations on model performance is evaluated. Finally, the complete
input feature sets for the two neural network models, along with intermediate
data processing steps, are summarized.

For both the fuel-optimal and time-optimal formulations
(Eqs. (
15
) and (
20
)), the original input
variables include

\{\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\Delta t,m_{0},T_{\max},I_{\mathrm{sp}},\mu\}.

Cartesian coordinates are used for clarity, although the same analysis applies
when modified equinoctial elements are employed. In total, the problem involves
17 independent input variables.

The gravitational acceleration at sea level
g_{0}
is fixed at
9.80665 m/s
2
. Any minor numerical discrepancies associated with this choice
can be absorbed into the specific impulse
I_{\mathrm{sp}}
, since
g_{0}
and

I_{\mathrm{sp}}
appear only as a product. The objective of this section is to
identify a minimal set of independent variables that preserves the full physical
content of the original problem.

4.1
Dimensionality Reduction in Self-Similar Space

Dimensionality reduction is achieved by exploiting the rotational and
dimensional invariance inherent to low-thrust trajectory optimization. It is
emphasized that these transformations correspond to data preprocessing rather
than data augmentation. Since they preserve the physical equivalence of the
underlying transfer, no information relevant to the optimal solution is lost.

4.1.1
Rotational Invariance

Under a central gravitational field, the equations of motion depend only on the
relative position with respect to the central body and are invariant under
global coordinate rotation. Consequently, if the initial and terminal states

(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
are
simultaneously rotated by the same transformation, the dynamics, constraints,
and optimality conditions remain unchanged.

As a result, the optimal solution, whether minimizing fuel consumption or
transfer time, is invariant under coordinate rotation. This property guarantees
that the physical characteristics of the transfer are independent of the chosen
reference frame and allows the problem to be expressed in a reduced coordinate
system without loss of generality.

As illustrated in Fig.
5
, rotating the boundary
states yields transformed variables

(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}^{\prime})
.
Because the governing equations are rotationally invariant, the resulting
optimal velocity increment satisfies
\Delta v^{\prime}=\Delta v
, with an analogous
result holding for time-optimal transfers.

Figure 5:
The diagram illustrates the rotational invariance.

In this work, the rotational degrees of freedom are eliminated by aligning the
departure position with the
x
-axis and constraining the arrival position to
lie in the
xy
-plane. This transformation removes three redundant degrees of
freedom while preserving the full physical structure of the transfer.

The detailed procedure is summarized in Algorithm
2
. Briefly,
the initial position vector is rotated onto the
x
-axis, followed by a rotation
about the
x
-axis that places the final position in the
xy
-plane. Among the
two admissible orientations, the one yielding a positive projection of the
initial velocity onto the
y
-axis is selected to ensure a unique
representation.

4.1.2
Dimensional Invariance

Dimensional invariance is commonly exploited in low-thrust trajectory
optimization to improve numerical conditioning and to expose fundamental
scaling properties. Typical approaches include adopting canonical units such as
1 AU and 1 year
[
32
]
, or rescaling the
dynamics such that the gravitational parameter becomes unity
[
29
]
.

Figure 6:
The diagram illustrates the dimensional invariance.

Here, a normalization strategy is adopted that is specifically tailored to
neural network input design, as illustrated in Fig.
6
. The
reference length is defined as

L_{0}\triangleq\|\boldsymbol{r}_{0}\|,

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}
denotes the departure position. The corresponding
reference time is

T_{0}\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{L_{0}^{3}}{\mu}},

(40)

which yields
\mu T_{0}^{2}/L_{0}^{3}=1
and normalizes the two-body gravitational
coefficient. The induced velocity and acceleration units are

V_{0}=L_{0}/T_{0}
and
A_{0}=L_{0}/T_{0}^{2}
, respectively.

Starting from the dimensional dynamics:

\dot{\bm{r}}=\bm{v},\qquad\dot{\bm{v}}=-\frac{\mu}{r^{3}}\bm{r}+\frac{T_{\max}}{m}\,u\,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\qquad\dot{m}=-\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\mathrm{sp}}g_{0}}\,u,

(41)

the introduction
of the dimensionless variables:

\tilde{t}=\frac{t}{T_{0}},\qquad\tilde{\bm{r}}=\frac{\bm{r}}{L_{0}},\qquad\tilde{\bm{v}}=\frac{\bm{v}}{V_{0}}=\frac{T_{0}}{L_{0}}\bm{v},\qquad\tilde{m}=\frac{m}{m_{0}},

(42)

leads to the normalized
equations of motion:

\frac{d\tilde{\bm{r}}}{d\tilde{t}}=\tilde{\bm{v}},\qquad\frac{d\tilde{\bm{v}}}{d\tilde{t}}=-\frac{\tilde{\bm{r}}}{\tilde{r}^{3}}+\beta\,u\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{m}},\qquad\frac{d\tilde{m}}{d\tilde{t}}=-\gamma\,u,

(43)

where the dimensionless parameters

\beta
and
\gamma
are defined as:

\beta\triangleq\frac{T_{\max}T_{0}^{2}}{m_{0}L_{0}},\qquad\gamma\triangleq\frac{T_{\max}T_{0}}{I_{\mathrm{sp}}g_{0}m_{0}}.

(44)

This normalization removes explicit dependence on the absolute length scale and
on the gravitational parameter
\mu
. As a result, a family of transfers that
differ only by dimensional scaling is mapped to a single representative case in
the normalized space. The corresponding dimensional solution can be uniquely
recovered via the inverse transformation

t=T_{0}\tilde{t}
,

\boldsymbol{r}=L_{0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}
,

\boldsymbol{v}=V_{0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
, and

m=m_{0}\tilde{m}
.

Moreover, since the trajectory depends only on the ratio
T_{\max}/m_{0}
, the
initial acceleration
a_{s}=T_{\max}/m_{0}
is introduced as a consolidated
propulsion-related input, further reducing dimensionality.

In summary, the original formulation involves 17 independent variables. By
accounting for rotational invariance (three degrees of freedom) and dimensional
invariance (three degrees of freedom), the effective input dimensionality is
reduced to 11 without loss of physical information.

6
Neural Network Training: Multi-Revolution Transfers

Extending the work of Acciarini et al.
[
2
]
, which demonstrated that neural surrogates could be used in transfers with multiple revolutions, we generalize our neural network approximators to handle multi-revolution trajectories. This section describes the methodology for data generation, network training, and performance evaluation in the multi-revolution context. As most aspects are unchanged from the single-revolution case, we focus on the differences and extensions.

6.1
Multi-Revolution Optimal Control Problem

Data generation follows the methodology outlined in Sec.
3
, using the same homotopy continuation procedure. A defining feature of multi-revolution transfers is the existence of multiple local optima due to the additional revolutions, which can make the input-output mapping ambiguous if different branches coexist for the same boundary conditions. In the fuel-optimal setting, Lambert-based features are available (given a prescribed transfer time), which allows the revolution count to be specified a priori. In contrast, in the multi-revolution minimum-time setting the final time is unknown and the revolution count is an outcome of the optimization, so no such prior information can be imposed. As a result, fuel-optimal and time-optimal problems exhibit different characteristics in the multi-revolution setting.

6.1.1
Fuel-Optimal Transfers

For fuel-optimal transfers, the procedure is executed independently for each target number of revolutions, ensuring balanced representation across all cases. Although the existence of multiple distinct solutions even in the single revolution setting remains an open question, substantial numerical evidence suggests that, for a fixed revolution, the fuel optimal solution is typically unique; this assumption is also adopted in prior studies
[
55
,
65
]
. Under this assumption, our homotopy continuation follows a consistent solution branch along each ray and therefore produces single valued training labels. This makes it well defined to train one network for each revolution count. The resulting dataset comprises millions of trajectories spanning zero to three revolutions (roughly 3 million per revolution count), partitioned into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% test samples.

Figure
8
summarizes the statistical distributions of the dataset. The first row shows initial and final orbital elements (semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination), while the second row presents probability density functions for the final-to-initial mass ratio, specific impulse, and time-of-flight. The dataset covers a broad range of mission scenarios: eccentricities from 0 to 1, inclinations from
0^{\circ}\text{ to }180^{\circ}
, semi-major axes spanning below 1 to above 5 AU, specific impulses from 2,000 s to over 5,000 s, and fuel costs ranging from nearly ballistic (
m_{f}/m_{0}\approx 1
) to highly demanding transfers.

Figure 8:
Parameter distributions of the multi-revolution transfer training dataset.

The network inputs remain those identified as optimal in Sec.
4
:
\mathrm{rv_{rotate}}
,
\mathrm{Lambert_{sph}}
, and
t_{\mathrm{Lambert}}
.

6.1.2
Time-Optimal Transfers

In multi-revolution time-optimal problems, the transfer time
t_{f}
is an optimization variable and is therefore unknown a priori. Consequently, the Lambert solver cannot be applied in advance, and the number of revolutions executed during the transfer is not prescribed. This introduces two challenges for large-scale data generation and supervised learning. First, along a continuation path, the shooting method may transition between locally optimal solutions associated with different revolution counts. Second, the mapping from boundary conditions to the time-optimal cost may become effectively multi-valued, as multiple locally optimal transfer times can exist for the same input, introducing label ambiguity and hindering network training.

To address these challenges, we adopt a standard reformulation from multi-revolution minimum-time low-thrust rendezvous problems, in which the point-to-point terminal phase constraint is relaxed into an orbit-to-orbit constraint
[
65
,
18
]
. In this formulation, the terminal orbit elements
(p,f,g,h,k)
are enforced, while the true longitude
L(t_{f})
is left free.

Formally, let
\bm{x}=[p,f,g,h,k,L]^{\mathrm{T}}
denote the modified equinoctial elements (MEE) state and
\bm{\lambda}_{x}=[\lambda_{p},\lambda_{f},\lambda_{g},\lambda_{h},\lambda_{k},\lambda_{L}]^{\mathrm{T}}
the associated costates. For the time-optimal problem with
J{\rm time}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}1,\mathrm{d}t
, the Hamiltonian reads

H_{\rm time}=\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{D}(\bm{x})+\frac{T_{\max}}{m}\bm{M}(\bm{x})\,\bm{\alpha}u\right)+\lambda_{m}\left(-\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}}\,u\right)+\lambda_{0}.

(45)

When a terminal component is free, the corresponding costate satisfies the transversality condition
\lambda_{i}(t_{f})=0

[
36
]
. Therefore, relaxing the terminal true longitude implies

L(t_{f})\ \text{free}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_{L}(t_{f})=0.

(46)

This replaces the point-to-point terminal phase constraint in the shooting equations, yielding an orbit-to-orbit TPBVP that enforces the terminal orbit elements while imposing
\lambda_{L}(t_{f})=0
. The relaxation enlarges the feasible terminal manifold and reduces TPBVP sensitivity to the number of revolutions, mitigating the likelihood of continuation path folds and bifurcations.

Figure 9:
Impact of terminal constraints on homotopy continuation for time-optimal transfers. Left: point-to-point formulation enforcing
L(t_{f})
. Right: orbit-to-orbit formulation enforcing
\lambda_{L}(t_{f})=0
. Signed Jacobian-determinant
\mathrm{sign}(\det\Phi)\log_{10}|\det\Phi|
indicates folds along the continuation path.

Figure
9
illustrates the effect of this relaxation. The only difference between the two continuation runs is the terminal constraint: enforcing
L(t_{f})
(point-to-point) versus
\lambda_{L}(t_{f})=0
(orbit-to-orbit). The point-to-point continuation exhibits multiple zero crossings of
\det\Phi
, indicating local folds, whereas the orbit-to-orbit continuation maintains
\det\Phi
away from zero, demonstrating smoother and more robust convergence.

To further enrich the dataset, multiple terminal longitudes are extracted by truncating a single solved trajectory at intermediate times. Each prefix segment yields a consistent, single-valued time-optimal sample, spanning a wide range of induced terminal
L
values.

Results

This section presents the final performance of the proposed neural network models and evaluates their applicability through three complementary tasks: (i) validation on public datasets, (ii) application to the GTOC4 multi-flyby asteroid trajectory design problem, and (iii) porkchop plot generation for asteroid rendezvous analysis. The goal is to assess the models’ generalization capability, practical usability in mission design, and computational efficiency.

Validation on public datasets tests the models under varying data distributions and verifies the effectiveness of the proposed homotopy ray data generation methodology. The GTOC4 multi-flyby design task provides a benchmark for sequential mission planning, evaluating the models’ accuracy and integration within trajectory optimization frameworks. Finally, the porkchop plot generation task highlights the computational efficiency and usability of the models compared to traditional optimal control methods.

7.4
Mission Analysis

This subsection demonstrates a porkchop-plot workflow for rapid preliminary screening of low-thrust Earth’s asteroid rendezvous missions using the proposed surrogate models. The benchmark scenario considers a CubeSat departing from Earth under a launch-energy constraint and rendezvousing with a near-Earth asteroid. The propulsion is assumed constant at 1 AU, with maximum thrust
T_{\max}=$1.82\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{N}$
and specific impulse
I_{\rm sp}=$3600\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
. The launcher provides a maximum Earth-departure hyperbolic excess speed of
v_{\infty,\max}=$4\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
, with unconstrained departure direction.

The motivation is large-scale target screening. Even after applying maneuverability-based filters, hundreds to thousands of asteroid candidates remain from a population exceeding one million objects. For low-thrust rendezvous, each grid point of a conventional porkchop plot requires solving a numerical optimal-control problem. This becomes computationally prohibitive for dense grids, multiple targets, or iterative preliminary design analyses, particularly when propulsion parameters or mission constraints vary.

A porkchop plot is constructed by sweeping the departure epoch
t_{0}
and time of flight
\Delta t
on a prescribed grid. Since the launch-provided departure velocity is not uniquely defined, the Earth-departure hyperbolic excess velocity
\boldsymbol{v}{\infty}
is treated as a decision variable at each grid point, constrained by
|\boldsymbol{v}\infty|2\leq v{\infty,\max}
. Particle swarm optimization is employed to select
\boldsymbol{v}\infty
that minimizes the surrogate-predicted cost. At each grid point, the
\Delta v
surrogate estimates
\Delta v{\min}(t_{0},\Delta t,\boldsymbol{v}\infty)
, while the
\Delta t
surrogate provides the minimum feasible time
\Delta t{\min}(t_{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{\infty})
to complete the transfer. Feasibility is enforced via a penalty for time-of-flight violations:

\text{Penalty}(\Delta t)=\begin{cases}Q\,(\Delta t-\Delta t_{\min})^{2},&\Delta t<\Delta t_{\min},\\
0,&\Delta t\geq\Delta t_{\min},\end{cases}

(47)

where
Q

Conclusion

This paper has presented a neural network-based framework for rapid and accurate estimation of fuel consumption and transfer feasibility in low-thrust trajectory design. The proposed models advance the performance by enabling broad applicability across a wide variety of mission scenarios without the need for retraining or task-specific dataset generation. This capability stems from two complementary innovations.

At the data level, we observed an approximate scaling law for low-thrust trajectories, which guided the generation of a large-scale dataset encompassing over 100 million trajectory samples. The dataset spans single- and multi-revolution transfers, a wide range of orbital elements, thrust levels, and specific impulses, as well as diverse mission geometries. By employing a homotopy ray-based continuation method for large-scale data generation, the dataset reliably covers low-thrust, low-fuel, and long-duration transfer regimes that are often underrepresented in conventional approaches. This ensures that the trained neural networks generalize well even to previously unseen mission scenarios.

At the astrodynamics level, inherent symmetries in the fuel- and time-optimal low-thrust control problem were exploited to reduce input dimensionality. Rotational invariance, non-dimensionalization, and Lambert solver solutions were incorporated into the input pipeline, providing the neural networks with physically meaningful and compact representations of mission states. For multi-revolution transfers, residual multilayer perceptrons with skip connections were employed to capture the increased nonlinearity and complex optimal control structures associated with longer arcs and multiple revolutions. Systematic hyperparameter optimization demonstrated that architectures with sufficient depth and width, combined with appropriate activation functions (e.g., Softplus), achieve sub-percent-level mean absolute percentage errors across both single- and multi-revolution scenarios.

The accuracy, generalization, and computational efficiency of the proposed models were extensively validated. Benchmarking against an open-source public dataset
[
2
]
showed that the surrogate model maintains high accuracy across the full
\Delta v
range, including low-thrust transfers that are often poorly represented in other datasets. Application to the GTOC4 multi-flyby asteroid mission demonstrated that segment-wise
\Delta v
predictions are highly accurate, enabling near-optimal trajectory assessment and sequential mission planning. Furthermore, porkchop-plot analyses for Earth’s asteroid rendezvous illustrate the ability of the models to quickly identify feasible, low-cost transfer windows across large target populations, achieving substantial reductions in computational time relative to traditional optimal-control solvers.

Overall, the proposed framework constitutes a versatile, high-fidelity surrogate model that bridges the gap between computationally expensive trajectory optimization and rapid mission design exploration. Its general-purpose formulation accommodates a wide range of mission design parameters, and both single- and multi-revolution transfers. The models and datasets have been made publicly available with implementations in C++, Python, and MATLAB.

In summary, the core contribution of this work is a unified modeling and pretraining framework intended to shift low-thrust transfer evaluation from a per-scenario "re-solve and re-train" workflow to a task-reusable pretrained model. The proposed framework reduces the computational and engineering overhead in mission design studies and lowers the barrier imposed by optimal-control expertise. This enables even non-specialists, such as planetary scientists, to perform rapid reachability and cost assessments for next-generation interplanetary and near-Earth mission studies and to iterate mission concepts more efficiently.

Appendix B: Prefix optimality for the minimum-time problem

Proposition 1
.

Consider the minimum-time transfer on
[t_{0},t_{f}]
with free terminal mass,
whose Hamiltonian is
H_{\rm time}
and whose optimal control satisfies

u^{*}(t)\equiv 1
and
\bm{\alpha}^{*}(t)
given by Eq. (
27
).
Let

(\bm{x}^{*}(t),m^{*}(t),\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*}(t),\lambda_{m}^{*}(t),\lambda_{0}^{*})

be an extremal satisfying the PMP conditions for this problem on
[t_{0},t_{f}]
.
Then, for any
\tau\in(t_{0},t_{f})
, the prefix segment

(\bm{x}^{*},m^{*})|_{[t_{0},\tau]}
is also a PMP extremal for the induced
minimum-time problem on
[t_{0},\tau]
with terminal constraints

\bm{x}(\tau)=\bm{x}^{*}(\tau)\quad(\text{hence }L(\tau)=L^{*}(\tau)),

free terminal mass, and free terminal time
\tau
. More precisely, there exist
modified multipliers
\hat{\lambda}_{m}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
such that

(\bm{x}^{*}(t),m^{*}(t),\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*}(t),\hat{\lambda}_{m}(t),\hat{\lambda}_{0}),\qquad t\in[t_{0},\tau],

satisfies the PMP necessary conditions on
[t_{0},\tau]
and generates the same
control
\bm{u}^{*}(t)=\bm{\alpha}^{*}(t)
(with
u^{*}\equiv 1
).

Proof.

We use two structural properties of the time-optimal formulation
[
18
]
.

(i) Decoupling of
(\bm{x},\bm{\lambda}_{x})
from
\lambda_{m}
.

In the time-optimal case,
u^{*}\equiv 1
and the optimal thrust direction is

\bm{\alpha}^{*}(t)=-\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x}^{*}(t))^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*}(t)}{\bigl\|\bm{M}(\bm{x}^{*}(t))^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*}(t)\bigr\|},

which depends only on
\bm{\lambda}_{x}
and not on
\lambda_{m}
. Hence the state
and costate subsystem
(\bm{x},\bm{\lambda}_{x})
evolves independently of

\lambda_{m}
.

(ii) Additive invariance of
\lambda_{m}
.

From Eq. (
26
), the mass costate dynamics can be written as

\dot{\lambda}_{m}=\frac{T_{\max}}{m^{2}}\,\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{M}(\bm{x})\,\bm{\alpha},

whose right-hand side does not explicitly depend on
\lambda_{m}
. Therefore, for
any constant
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
, the shifted function

\hat{\lambda}_{m}(t)\triangleq\lambda_{m}^{*}(t)+\Delta

satisfies the same differential equation on
[t_{0},t_{f}]
.

Fix
\tau\in(t_{0},t_{f})
and choose

\Delta=-\lambda_{m}^{*}(\tau),\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\hat{\lambda}_{m}(\tau)=0.

Thus the free-terminal-mass transversality condition is satisfied at
t=\tau

for the truncated problem. Since
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
depends only on

\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*}
, this shift does not change the control, and therefore does
not change

(\bm{x}^{*}(t),m^{*}(t),\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*}(t)),\qquad t\in[t_{0},\tau].

It remains to enforce the free-final-time condition at
t=\tau
. Because the
mass dynamics under
u^{*}\equiv 1
is constant,

\dot{m}(t)=-\beta,\qquad\beta\triangleq\frac{T_{\max}}{I_{\rm sp}g_{0}},

the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm time}
is affine in
(\lambda_{m},\lambda_{0})
and the
shift
\lambda_{m}\mapsto\lambda_{m}+\Delta
changes the Hamiltonian by a
constant:

\hat{H}_{\rm time}(t)=H_{\rm time}^{*}(t)-\beta\,\Delta,\qquad t\in[t_{0},\tau],

where
H_{\rm time}^{*}
denotes the Hamiltonian evaluated along the original
extremal. Since
\lambda_{0}
is a constant multiplier and does not enter the
state/costate differential equations, we can modify it to restore the terminal
Hamiltonian condition. Define

\hat{\lambda}_{0}\triangleq\lambda_{0}^{*}+\beta\,\Delta.

With this choice, the Hamiltonian evaluated on

(\bm{x}^{*},m^{*},\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*},\hat{\lambda}_{m},\hat{\lambda}_{0})
satisfies

\hat{H}_{\rm time}(t)=H_{\rm time}^{*}(t),\qquad t\in[t_{0},\tau],

and in particular the free-final-time transversality condition at
t=\tau
is
preserved. Because the system is autonomous, the Hamiltonian is constant along
an extremal, hence

\hat{H}_{\rm time}(t)\equiv\hat{H}_{\rm time}(\tau)=0,\qquad t\in[t_{0},\tau].

Finally, the endpoint conditions of the induced problem are satisfied by
construction:

\bm{x}(\tau)=\bm{x}^{*}(\tau),\qquad\hat{\lambda}_{m}(\tau)=0,\qquad\hat{H}_{\rm time}(\tau)=0,

and the control minimizes the Hamiltonian since
\bm{\alpha}^{*}(t)
is unchanged
and remains the minimizer given by Eq. (
27
).
Therefore,
(\bm{x}^{*},m^{*},\bm{\lambda}_{x}^{*},\hat{\lambda}_{m},\hat{\lambda}_{0})

satisfies the PMP necessary conditions on
[t_{0},\tau]
, proving the claim.
