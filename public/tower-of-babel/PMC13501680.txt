Exploring the associations between daily sedentary behavior, physical activity, sleep, and risk of depressive symptoms in Chinese adolescents
Authors: Jingjia He, Din Son Tan, Sizhe Huang, Bin Dong, Xijie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsTo investigate the relationship between depressive symptoms and daily behaviors, including sedentary behavior, walk, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (VMPA) and sleep in Chinese adolescents.Methods11,152 adolescents (51.5% boys; mean age 14.3 ± 0.7 years) were included from the city-wide school physical examination. Depressive symptoms and daily movement data were collected with questionnaires.Results37.9% (32.2% boys and 44.0% girls; P < 0.001) of participants were considered at risk for depressive symptoms. Replacing 30-minute of sedentary behavior with equivalent duration of sleep was associated with a decrease risk of depressive symptoms with ORs of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91, 0.95; P < 0.001) in boys and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88, 0.93; P < 0.001) in girls. In participants who did not meet 1-hour VMPA recommendation, replacing 30-minute of sedentary behavior with VMPA was associated with lower risk of depressive symptoms, with Ors of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.53, 0.83; P < 0.001) in boys and 0.93 (95% CI: 0.76, 1.14; P = 0.475) in girls.ConclusionSignificant associations between depressive symptoms and daily movement were found in Chinese adolescents. Maintaining adequate sleep duration is associated with a lower likelihood of depressive symptoms, while reallocating sedentary time to VMPA represents an option for adolescents, particularly boys, not meeting physical activity guidelines. Future school-based randomized controlled trials and lifestyle-focused mHealth interventions are warranted to evaluate the efficacy of these behavioral modifications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28073-x.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, 24-hour movement guidelines, Isotemporal substitution model, Physical activity, Adolescent health

Background

Depressive disorders accounted for the largest proportion of mental disorder disability-adjusted life years for adolescents aged 15–19 in 2019 both worldwide and in China [1]. Prevalence of depressive symptoms was increasing fast, especially in adolescent population [2], and was estimated to rank as the first cause of burden of disease worldwide by 2030 [3]. According to “Report on the Development of Chinese National Mental Health (2019–2020)” issued by the Institute of Psychology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, the rate of depressive symptoms among the Chinese adolescents was 24.6% in 2020, however, the actual prevalence may be higher [4]. In contrast to the rising disease burden, the shortage of mental health resources, and the numerous challenges associated with mental disorder treatment, primary prevention has emerged as particularly crucial [5].

While certain instances of depression can be attributed to genetic-environmental interactions, a significant portion of depressive symptoms is influenced by modifiable risk factors, which can be addressed and improved [6]. Data from the China Family Panel Studies suggested that increasing the frequency or the duration of vigorous and moderate physical activities (VMPA) can help reduce depressive symptoms in adolescents [7]. The findings were confirmed in several observational studies and meta-analyses worldwide [8–10]. However, most existing studies have focused on clinical populations with specific medical conditions [11]. Additional research is therefore warranted to evaluate the effects of distinct sports types and tailored exercise prescriptions on adolescent psychological distress, which remains essential for developing evidence-based physical activity guidelines to enhance youth mental well-being [12].

Other daily activities, such as walking and sleep, have also been found to be beneficial for improving depressive status [13–15]. Conversely, physical inactivity, particularly sedentary behavior, has been linked to a higher risk of depressive symptoms in both adolescence and adulthood [16, 17]. It, therefore, arises the question of how the allocation of daily activities throughout the day affects the emotional well-being of adolescents and whether there is an optimal recommendation. In recent years, researchers recognized that physical exercise including low-density physical activity and VMPA, sleep and sedentary behavior were co-dependent and should be taken into consideration concurrently [18], and should be evaluated concurrently within a 24-hour movement framework for overall health [19, 20]. While individual behaviors have been extensively studied in relation to depressive symptoms, analyzing them in isolation fails to capture the behavioral reallocations that occur within a finite 24-hour day. The isotemporal substitution model, on the other hand, allows for the evaluation of the theoretical impact of replacing one specific movement behavior with another for an equivalent duration, thereby offering actionable and realistic evidence for public health guidelines [21].

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the associations between the risk of depressive symptoms and daily movement including walk, VMPA, sleep, and sedentary behavior, in Chinese adolescents. Specifically, we assessed the associations between adherence to the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines and depressive symptoms, and utilized the isotemporal substitution model to investigate the potential health implications of theoretically replacing sedentary time with walking, VMPA, or sleep.

Methods

Study design and participants recruitment

The present study used cross-sectional data from the latest physical examinations of secondary school students of the Zhongshan Student Growth Cohort [22]. Between 2021 and 2022, a survey on students’ living habits and psychological health was conducted concurrently with the annual physical examination in all public high schools in Zhongshan. Data on demographic characteristics, physical health, living habits and psychological health status were linked to students’ registration number. The use of de-identified data has been authorized by Zhongshan Health Center for Primary and Secondary Schools. This study has been reviewed and approved by the Institution of Review Board of Peking University (Approval No. IRB B00001052-20011-exempt).

Data collection

Anthropometric measurements, including participants’ weight and height, were conducted in accordance with Chinese National Survey on Students Constitution and Health (CNSSCH) [23]. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 centimeter with a portable stadiometer (model TZG, China). Participants were asked to stand straight barefoot during measurement. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg with a lever-type weight scale (model RGT-140, China). Participants were asked to wear light clothes and be barefoot for measurement. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (in kilogram) divided by the square of height (in meter). Age and sex specific BMI z score was converted on the basis of World Health Organization growth reference for school-aged children and adolescents [24]. Children were categorized as overweight if BMI z score ≥ 1.

Demographic information, including participant’s sex and date of birth, was collected from the students’ registration system. Depressive symptoms were measured using the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children (CES-DC) [25]. CES-DC was a 20-item self-report depressive symptoms with possible scores ranging from 0 to 60. Response options ranged from 0 to 3 for each item (0 = rarely or none of the time, 1 = some or little of the time, 2 = moderately or much of the time, 3 = most or almost all the time). The scoring of positive items (items 4, 8, 12, and 16) was reversed. Scores of 16 and above were indicative of depressive symptoms in children and adolescents [26].

Sedentary behavior, vigorous and moderate physical activity (VMPA), walk habits, and sleep patterns were collected using a self-reported questionnaire. This instrument was originally developed and utilized within a national school-based healthy lifestyles intervention project targeting obesity among Chinese children and adolescents, and has been previously validated in this specific population [27, 28]. Participants were asked to report the number of hours and minutes they typically spent on these activities in a weekday or weekend day. The daily average duration of each activity was then calculated. As all of the participants were high school students with relatively fixed school routines, sedentary behavior was determined as the time spent sitting still during leisure time, excluding class time and sleep. The duration of VMPA was defined as time spent engaging in physical activities that required moderate or intense effort, causing individuals to sweat and feel tired. Walking time was self-reported as a daily average of total walking durations per day. Sleep duration was defined as the duration of night-time sleep, excluding any naps. In addition, the time spent on sedentary behavior, VMPA and sleep was also converted into categorical variables to determine adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines [19, 20]. Specifically, participants who reported a daily sedentary behavior of ≤ 2 h, VMPA of ≥ 1 h, or sleep duration of ≥ 8 h were assigned a score of 1 for the respective variable. Participants who did not meet these criteria were assigned a score of 0.

Unhealthy eating behaviors, such as the weekly frequency of consuming pastry, fried food, fast food and takeaway food, as well as the frequency of eating out, were included in the questionnaire. Participants were asked to report how many times they engaged in these behaviors over the past seven days. The frequency ranged between 0 and 7 for each behavior.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted for each variable, with mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and number and percentage for categorical variables. The weekly frequency of unhealthy eating behaviors were reported as median and interquartile range (P25, P75). Independent associations between depressive symptoms as measured by CES-DC scores and physical activities, were tested using generalized linear models. Log-binomial regression models were used for the binary outcome of depressive symptoms. Two models were further adapted to explore the roles of VMPA, walk, sleep, and sedentary behavior in the relationship between physical activities and risk of depressive symptoms in adolescents [21]. First, partition models were applied to assess if the increases in VMPA, walk or sleep would mitigate the associations between sedentary behavior and risk of depressive symptoms. Next, isotemporal substitution models were applied to estimate the theoretical effects of behavioral reallocations [29]. In these models, the total 24-hour time budget was held constant. This approach simulates a realistic scenario where time spent in one movement behavior (such as sedentary behavior) is replaced by an equivalent duration of another behavior (such as walking, VMPA, or sleep), while all other behaviors in the model remain unchanged. The time reallocation unit was set at 30 min to align with the standard long recess universally mandated for physical activities in Chinese schools. Both the partition model and isotemporal substitution model were built on generalized linear models for CES-DC score and log-binomial regression model for risk of depressive symptoms. According to the Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030), it is recommended that students engage in at least 1 h of VMPA per day. Stratified analysis was conducted in participants who met and not met this recommendation. Confounding factors such as participants’ age and unhealthy eating behaviors, were included in the aforementioned models where applicable.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to recognize the robustness of the primary findings. Firstly, isotemporal substitution model was conducted in overweight and non-overweight children, respectively. Secondly, in isotemporal substitution models, different cutoff of CES-DC score (20) was used for identifying risk of depressive symptoms, which were suggested in previous studies [30, 31]. Thirdly, in isotemporal substitution models, duration of physical activities were measured as per week rather per day.

Stata version 14.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA) was used to perform data cleaning and all analyses. All associations were considered significant when two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the present analysis involved 11,152 participants (51.5% boys) with a mean age of 14.3 (SD: 0.7) years. The median of CES-DC score was 12 (P25, P75: 7, 22) in all participants, and was higher in girls (median: 15; P25, P75: 8, 24) than in boys (median: 12; P25, P75: 6, 20), P < 0.001. The overall prevalence of participants at risk for depression was 37.9%, and was 32.2% and 44.0% in boys and girls respectively (P < 0.001). Boys typically had longer durations of physical activity and shorter sedentary time compared to girls. Furthermore, 76.7% of boys and 68.7% of girls met the standard recommendations of engaging in at least 1 h of VMPA every day.

Independent associations

Lower CES-DC scores and lower risks of depressive symptoms were found in participants who had longer durations of VMPA, adequate sleep, and shorter period of sedentary behavior (Supplementary Table 1). After controlling for potential confounding factors, these associations remained consistent (Supplementary Table 2). Every 30-minute increase in sedentary behavior was associated with an increase in CES-DC score of 0.10 (95% CI: 0.06, 0.14; P < 0.001) in boys and 0.11 (95% CI: 0.07, 0.16; P < 0.001) in girls. In contrast, for participants who did not meet the 1-hour-VMPA standard, every 30-minute increase in VMPA was associated with a decrease in CES-DC score of 2.75 (95% CI: -3.78, -1.73; P < 0.001) in boys and 1.42 (95% CI: -2.51, -0.33; P = 0.011) in girls. Also, every 30-minute increase in sleep was associated with a decrease in CES-DC score of 0.43 (95% CI: -0.52, -0.35; P < 0.001) in boys and 0.69 (95% CI:-0.82, -0.56; P < 0.001) in girls.

Adherence to 24-hour movement guideline

Compared to participants who met all 3 recommendations of the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, those who met less recommendations had higher risks of depressive symptoms, with the P for trend < 0.001 (Table 2). After controlling for potential confounding factors, compared to participants met all three recommendations, CES-DC scores in participants who met only one or non recommendations were 2.82 (95% CI: 1.79, 3.86; P < 0.001) to 3.65 (95% CI: 2.37, 4.93; P < 0.001) higher in boys, and 3.02 (95% CI: 1,77, 4.27; P < 0.001) to 3.80 (95% CI: 2.40, 5.21; P < 0.001) higher in girls. The corresponding odds of at risk for depressive symptoms were 1.90 (95% CI: 1.50, 2.40; P < 0.001) to 2.03 (95% CI: 1.54, 2.68; P < 0.001) in boys and 1.50 (95% CI: 1.19, 1.89; P < 0.001) to 1.70 (95% CI: 1.31, 2.20; P = 0.001) in girls, respectively. CES-DC score and risk of depressive symptoms were relatively high in participants who did not meet the criteria of sleep duration of ≥ 8 h, regardless of other 24-hour movement guideline combinations (Supplementary Table 3).

Isotemporal substitution analysis

As shown in partition models, VMPA, walking and sleep could all offset the association between sedentary behavior and risk of depressive symptoms to some extent (Supplementary Table 4). The results remained robust in the fully adjusted model (Fig. 1). Every 30-minute increase in sedentary behavior was associated with 0.12 (95% CI: 0.08, 0.16; P < 0.001) versus 0.13 (95% CI: 0.08, 0.17; P < 0.001) increase in CES-DC score in boys and girls, and 2.4% (95% CI: 1.01, 1.03; P < 0.001) versus 1.6% (95% CI: 1.01, 1.02; P < 0.001) increase in depressive risk, respectively. Every 30-minute increase in sleep was associated with a decrease in CES-DC score of 0.46 (95% CI: -0.55, -0.38; P < 0.001) in boys and 0.72 (95% CI: -0.85, -0.59; P < 0.001) in girls. The corresponding depressive risk decreased by 5.2% (95% CI: 0.93, 0.96; P < 0.001) in boys and 9.0% (95% CI: 0.90, 0.94; P < 0.001) in girls. By VMPA standard category (< 1 h/day and ≥ 1 h/day), effect of sedentary behavior was largely attenuated by VMPA in participants who had shorter duration of VMPA (< 1 h/day). Every 30-minute increase of VMPA was associated with decreased CES-DC score of 2.46 (95% CI: -3.52, -1.40; P < 0.001) in boys and 1.11 (95% CI: -2.21, -0.02; P = 0.046) in girls. Meanwhile, in participants who met 1-hour VMPA standard, 30-minute increase in walking was associated with a decrease of CES-DC score by 0.27 (95% CI: -0.53, -0.02; P = 0.038) in boys and 0.35 (95% CI: -0.71, -0.003; P = 0.048) in girls.

Replacing sedentary behavior with same amount of time on VMPA, walking and sleep was generally associated with lower CES-DC score and lower risk of depressive symptoms (Supplementary Table 5), and the results remained consistent after adjustment of potential confounders (Fig. 2). Replacing 30-minute of sedentary behavior with equivalent sleep was associated with a decrease in CES-DC score by 0.58 (95% CI: -0.68, -0.48; P < 0.001) in boys and 0.48 (95% CI: -0.82, -0.14; P = 0.005) in girls, with corresponding ORs of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91, 0.95; P < 0.001) and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88, 0.93; P < 0.001). In participants who did not meet 1-hour VMPA standard, replacing 30-minute of sedentary behavior with VMPA was associated with a 2.58 (95% CI: -3.64, -1.51; P < 0.001) and 1.22 (95% CI: -2.32, -0.12; P = 0.030) decrease in CES-DC score in boys and girls, respectively. The corresponding ORs were 0.67 (95% CI: 0.53, 0.83; P < 0.001) and 0.93(95% CI: 0.76, 1.14; P = 0.475). Replacing sedentary behavior with walk was not associated with a decreased risk of depressive symptoms. In participants who already met the VMPA standard, additional duration of VMPA did not relate to a decreased risk of depressive symptoms. However, replacing 30-minute sedentary behavior with walking was related to both lower CES-DC score and risk of depressive symptoms. The difference of CES-DC scores were − 0.43 (95% CI: -0.70, -0.17; P = 0.002) in boys and − 0.54 (95% CI: -0.89, -0.18; P = 0.003) in girls. The corresponding ORs were 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88, 0.98; P = 0.006) in boys and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.84, 0.97; P = 0.003) in girls. Generally, both overweight and non-overweight adolescents had lower risk of depressive symptoms when replacing sedentary behaviors with healthy activities. The effect size, however, was more significant in overweight participants (Supplementary Table 6).

In sensitivity analyses, replacing 30-minute sedentary behavior with equivalent healthy activity every week was also related to lower CES-DC score and risk of depressive symptoms (Supplementary Table 7). The adaptation of different cut-off values for determining at risk for depressive symptoms did not alter the primary findings of this study (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

This study adopted a population-based cross-sectional study design and identified that sedentary behavior was positively associated with risk of depressive symptoms among Chinese adolescence. Conversely, adherence to the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, particularly meeting physical activity and sleep recommendations, was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms in all participants. Replacing sedentary behavior with an equivalent duration of sleep was found to be associated with the lowest depressive risk for all participants. In adolescents who did not meet the recommended 1-hour of VMPA, increasing the time of VMPA or substituting sedentary behavior with VMPA were both associated with a decrease in risk of depressive symptoms. Among those who already met the 1-hour VMPA recommendation, replacing sedentary time with an equivalent duration of walking was also associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the study found that both overweight and non-overweight adolescents equally benefited from these strategies. The proposed strategies were likely to be associated with lower risk of depressive symptoms regardless of weight status, which contrasts with previous findings suggesting an association between excessive body weight and depressive symptoms, particularly among girls [32]. Our primary findings were robust to different operationalizations of depressive risk and measurement timeframes.

Results of this study was similar to what was reported in American adults that isotemporal substitution of television with physical activities could reduce depressive risk [33]. Further, the observed protective effect of sleep on depressive symptoms was as important as that of physical activity, and was independent of unhealthy eating habits or overweight status [34]. Although sleep health is widely recognized as a crucial indicator of overall health and wellbeing [35], its association with depressive symptoms needs to be verified. Recent clinical and meta-analytic evidence suggests that the combination of developmental changes in the bioregulatory systems controlling sleep and wakefulness during adolescence creates unique pathways to depressive symptoms. Therefore, when adolescents experience co-morbid depression and sleep problems, it is recommended to prioritize the sleep interventions as the initial approach [36]. However, sleep deficiency has become a particularly concerning issue in China. The pooled prevalence of sleep problems in school-aged children and adolescents reached 44% [37]. The proportion of adolescents who slept longer than 8 h per night was merely 45.5%. The findings of this study support the notion that maintaining mental health should begin with ensuring an adequate duration of sleep.

Regarding physical activity sub-groups, establishing 24-hour movement behavior targets remains a recognized framework for adolescent health. For physically inactive adolescents, which compromise more than half of the student population [38], achieving the national recommendation of at least 1 h of MVPA per day remains a central objective, aligning with the Healthy China 2030 initiative [39, 40]. Conversely, for adolescents who already meet the MVPA recommendations, our stratified analysis indicates that reallocating sedentary time to lighter physical activities, specifically walking, provides additional mental health benefits. This finding suggests that for already active adolescents, promoting active transport, such as walking to school, may serve as a sustainable and low-barrier strategy to achieve further mental health gains. Incorporating these low-intensity behavioral modifications into daily school routines could complement structured exercise regimens, thereby offering actionable pathways for comprehensive school-based mental health interventions.

The observed associations between 24-hour movement behaviors and depressive risk may be driven by intertwined biological and psychosocial pathways. Biologically, as suggested by the thermoregulatory hypotheses, physical activity serves as an acute heat stimulus that triggers thermoregulatory control to facilitate sleep onset, while also activating energy-conservation pathways that promote deeper sleep architecture in populations with major depressive disorders [41]. Psychosocially, expanding time spent in physical activity or structured walking routines could foster higher self-efficacy and provide increased opportunities for supportive social interactions, both of which serve as protective factors against mental stress [42, 43]. Interventions targeting the 24-hour movement framework should therefore consider these multi-level mechanisms to optimize behavioral design. Furthermore, these pathways may be associated to variations across sexes, as reflected in our sub-group findings. Notably, the 30-minute reallocation to VMPA was associated with a significant lower CES-DC scores among girls, despite the non-significant outcomes for binary depressive symptoms. This consistency indicates that physical activity modification universally lowers the odds of depressive symptom across both sexes, although the magnitude of this effect might be insufficient to shift girls past the screening threshold within a binary framework.

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to address the association between depressive symptoms and daily activities (walk, VMPA, sleep and sedentary behaviors) in a non-clinical adolescent population. The findings of this study highlight the potential importance of promoting healthy full-day activities as a strategy for preventing and managing depressive symptoms in adolescents [44]. Evidence from existing literature has demonstrated the efficacy of physical activity interventions in relieving depressive symptoms in children and adolescents [11, 12, 45]. Beyond single-behavior interventions, a key finding of the present study is that short sleep duration remains consistently associated with an elevated likelihood of depressive symptoms, irrespective of adherence to other 24-hour movement behavior guidelines. This persistent association is further supported by our isotemporal substitution analysis, which demonstrated that reallocating sedentary time to sleep yielded the most pronounced theoretical reduction in depressive risk compared to other behavioral reallocations. Together, these complementary findings highlight the critical role of sleep within the 24-hour movement framework, suggesting that maintaining adequate sleep duration may serve as a foundational priority for adolescent mental health interventions, particularly when cross-behavioral modifications are required. Taking all these factors into account and analyzing their collective impact on depressive symptoms is a novel approach and adds valuable insight into the relationship between daily activities and mental well-being in this population.

There are several limitations of this study. Firstly, data were derived from a cross-sectional city-wide study in Zhongshan, a city located in southeast China. The city represents an economically developed region with a per capita GDP above the national average and a highly urbanized infrastructure, featuring a permanent urban population rate of 87.77% in 2024 compared to the national average of 67.00% [46]. Consequently, adolescents in this area may experience different levels of academic stress, electronic device accessibility, and parental support compared to those in less developed or rural regions of China. These socioeconomic and educational variations may influence the daily distribution of 24-hour movement behaviors and their subsequent associations with depressive risks. Therefore, further research involving broader sample sizes and more diverse geographical locations is warranted to evaluate the generalizability of these findings. Second, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the establishment of temporal sequencing and causal relationships between sleeping, physical activity, and depressive disorders. Accumulating evidence indicates a bidirectional relationship between sleep disturbances and depressive symptoms, supported by demonstrated genetic overlaps and shared neurobiological mechanisms [47, 48]. Similarly, depressive symptoms may lead to reduced physical activity performance due to anhedonia and low energy [49]. Third, despite adjusting for a range of covariates, residual confounding and measurement limitations remain possible. Several determinants of adolescent mental health, including family socioeconomic status, academic stress, and peer relationships, were not adjusted for due to dataset constraints. Furthermore, although sedentary behavior and sleep duration were evaluated, detailed metrics on sleep quality and screen time were unavailable. Screen time may also overlap with general sedentary behavior. Future longitudinal investigations are required to clarify these associations. Fourth, all of the exposure variables were collected from self-reported questionnaires. Although the adolescent participants maintained relatively structured daily routines, the potential for recall bias and residual measurement error cannot be entirely avoided. Meanwhile, depressive symptoms were evaluated using the CES-DC scale, which is a screening tool and not a diagnostic instrument. It provides valuable information about individuals’ depressive symptoms rather than clinical diagnosis of depression. Fifth, it is noteworthy that data collection for this study was conducted between 2021 and 2022, overlapping with the COVID-19 pandemic. This timeframe was characterized by widespread increases in depressive symptoms and decrease in physical activity time [50–52]. Consequently, the observed daily movement behaviors and their associations with depressive symptoms may reflect the unique socio-environmental constraints of the pandemic era. Caution is therefore warranted when extrapolating these associations to the post-pandemic context, and future surveillance data are needed to assess the temporal stability of these behavioral patterns.

Conclusion

The associations between daily activities and risk of depressive symptoms were explored in 11,152 Chinese adolescents. Ensuring adequate sleep duration represents a protective strategy associated with maintained mental health in the general adolescent population. For individuals (especially boys) not meeting the daily recommendation of 1 h of MVPA, substituting sedentary behavior with an equivalent duration of MVPA is also associated with a lower likelihood of depressive symptoms. The findings of this study provide a framework for future sex-specific interventional designs, such as school-based randomized controlled trials testing sleep extension protocols or mobile health (mHealth) platforms prompting real-time behavior substitution.
