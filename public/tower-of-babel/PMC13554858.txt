Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome and Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Alyssa Naimi, Mohamed Ramzi Almajed, Liyan Obeidat, Allison Zimmerman, Raef Fadel, Ileana Lopez-Plaza, Gillian Grafton, Jennifer Cowger, Waleed Al-Darzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac involvement in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) can present as valvular disease, ventricular dysfunction, and thrombosis.Case SummaryA 32-year-old woman with APS and systemic lupus erythematosus presented with cardiogenic shock after a cesarean section delivery for HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets) syndrome. Echocardiogram showed biventricular dysfunction, and coronary angiography was negative. Despite medical management, her condition worsened, and she was placed on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Extensive thrombosis formed at the vascular catheters and ECMO inflow cannula.DiscussionOur patient developed refractory shock with arterial and venous thrombosis concerning for catastrophic APS. She was treated with plasmapheresis, steroids, and anticoagulation with a modified higher intensity protocol. She underwent orthotropic heart transplantation 10 days after hospitalization. Biopsy of the explanted heart revealed myocardium with extensive transmural necrosis and granulation tissue.Take-Home MessagesAPS-associated cardiomyopathy confers a poor prognosis. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach that includes hematologic interventions and advanced therapies with mechanical circulatory support.

History of Presenting Illness

A 32-year-old woman presented with 2 weeks of progressive shortness of breath, orthopnea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. She did not have nasal congestion, sore throat, cough, fever, recent illness, lower extremity edema, chest pain, palpitations, or syncope.Take-Home Messages•APS-associated cardiomyopathy confers a poor prognosis, and catastrophic APS should be considered in patient's presenting with a history of APS and systemic lupus erythematosus.•In addition to anticoagulation, corticosteroids, and plasmapheresis, early mechanical circulatory support evaluation should be considered.•Management requires a multidisciplinary approach that could include hematologic interventions and advanced heart failure therapies.

On physical examination, vital signs were remarkable for tachycardia (heart rate: 118 beats/min). Jugular venous distention was noted. Cardiac auscultation revealed a regular rhythm without murmurs. Lung examination revealed decreased breath sounds bilaterally with basilar crackles. There was no peripheral edema.

One month before this presentation, she had undergone cesarean section delivery at 26 weeks of gestation for HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count) syndrome. Her postpartum course was complicated by cutaneous papules attributed to antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), for which she had received systemic steroids, in addition to portal vein thrombosis, for which she was restarted on systemic anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist. Cardiac work-up at that time with electrocardiography and echocardiography was unremarkable.

The patient had been in her usual state of health before delivery, ambulating independently and performing daily activities without limitation. After delivery, her functional capacity declined gradually over a 2-week period, with increasing dyspnea on exertion and decreased tolerance to activity. At presentation, she was unable to climb a flight of stairs or lie flat because of orthopnea.

Past Medical History

Our patient had a significant medical history of systemic lupus erythematosus and APS.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis in a postpartum patient with shortness of breath is extensive. It includes potential etiologies from various organ systems: hematologic conditions (such as anemia), pulmonary disorders (such as pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pneumothorax, or asthma exacerbation), and cardiac conditions (such as myocarditis, systolic dysfunction, or myocardial infarction). Initial work-up suggested a diagnosis of decompensated heart failure, which narrowed the differential to peripartum cardiomyopathy. Given the patient's medical history, additional considerations included lupus myocarditis, Libman-Sacks endocarditis, and catastrophic APS (CAPS).

Investigations

Electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia and low-voltage QRS; there were no signs of acute ischemia. Compared to prior, low voltage was a new finding. High-sensitivity troponin I was >2,869 ng/L on presentation (reference range: <19 ng/L). B-type natriuretic peptide was 2,457 pg/mL (reference range: <50 pg/mL). Lactic acid was 1.5 mmol/L (reference range: <2.1 mmol/L). Complete blood count, basic metabolic profile, hepatic function test, and coagulation profile were unremarkable. Echocardiogram revealed severe biventricular dysfunction with normal cavity size and wall thickness. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a pulmonary artery pressure of 51/34 mm Hg (mean: 40 mm Hg), a mixed venous oxygen saturation of 63%, and a calculated pulmonary vascular resistance of 248 dyn⋅s⋅cm5. Invasive coronary angiography was negative for coronary artery ischemia or dissection. Our patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit, where she was medically managed for acute decompensated heart failure.

Management

Despite medical management, our patient's clinical status worsened, with hypotension and respiratory distress for which she was intubated, sedated, and maintained on mechanical ventilation. Hemodynamic and laboratory assessment was consistent with cardiogenic shock; right heart catheterization showed a right atrial pressure of 18 mm Hg, wedge pressure of 30 mm Hg, cardiac output of 3.54 L/min, and cardiac index of 1.90 L/min/m2. Her shock state was refractory to inotrope and vasopressor support, and a multidisciplinary heart team recommended escalation to mechanical circulatory support (MCS). MCS was established with placement of peripherally cannulated transcaval left atrial venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

An extensive work-up to evaluate for an etiology of our patient's heart failure was performed. Serum serologies to assess for infectious causes were negative. Toxicology panel was negative. Nutritional and heavy-metal levels were within normal limits. Echocardiogram was not suggestive of an infiltrative process, and no lymphadenopathy was identified on computed tomography. Autoimmune evaluation revealed a significantly elevated β2 glycoprotein IgA and IgM, elevated lupus anticoagulation via positive LA-PTT and positive dRVVT, and elevated cardiolipin IgM and IgG antibodies. An endomyocardial biopsy was bland and unremarkable.

Her course was complicated by the development of extensive thrombosis involving the arterial and venous catheters and ECMO inflow cannula at the inferior vena cava extending into the left atrium despite therapeutic anticoagulation with intravenous heparin.

A multidisciplinary discussion between the cardiology, rheumatology, hematology, and infectious disease teams determined that CAPS with cardiac involvement was the likely etiology of her presentation. Empiric treatment with pulse-dose systemic steroids, plasmapheresis, and intravenous immunoglobulin was performed. In collaboration with transfusion medicine, a protocol was created with modified partial thromboplastic time targets to maintain a higher degree of anticoagulation. Despite the presence of intraluminal thrombus formation ECMO cannulae, they were not exchanged, as the patient remained hemodynamically stable on the circuit and there was no evidence of impaired flow or oxygenator dysfunction.

Despite treatment, her cardiac function and shock state did not improve, and she remained dependent on left atrial venoarterial ECMO. She was approved for orthotropic heart transplantation and listed as United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) Status 1.

Notably, the patient did not receive any blood products during pregnancy or while on ECMO. Panel-reactive antibody testing revealed no evidence of allosensitization before orthotopic heart transplantation. Standard induction immunosuppression was not administered, and no modifications to her immunosuppressive regimen were made based on her underlying APS. Additionally, she did not experience any further thrombotic events after transplantation.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Our patient underwent heart transplantation 10 days after her initial hospitalization. Explanted heart biopsy revealed myocardium with extensive transmural necrosis and granulation tissue. Follow-up after her hospitalization revealed normal graft function, and she had no further cardiac complications. She was maintained on a vitamin K antagonist for anticoagulation without further thrombotic events.

Discussion

Our patient's presentation of refractory cardiogenic shock, in addition to extensive arterial and venous thrombosis, was consistent with catastrophic APS.

APS is a multisystem autoimmune condition. Its pathogenesis involves an interleukin-1 mediated autoimmune inflammation that results in vascular thrombosis and ischemia in multiple organ systems over a short time span.1 Cardiac involvement in CAPS may result in acute myocarditis, as in our case.2,3

The Congress on Antiphospholipid Task Force defines APS through laboratory and clinical criteria. Laboratory criteria require the use of 3 laboratory assays to detect antiphospholipid antibodies: lupus anticoagulation, β2 glycoprotein, and anticardiolipin. Clinical criteria require the presence of at least 1 of the 2 major clinical manifestations: pregnancy and morbidity.4

The diagnosis of CAPS is further classified as “definite” or “probable.” Definite CAPS is defined as meeting the following 4 criteria: evidence of involvement in 3 or more organ systems, development in <1 week, histopathological confirmation on biopsy, and laboratory confirmation of antiphospholipid antibodies. Probable CAPS is defined as meeting all 4 criteria in 2 organ systems, 3 criteria with the absence of laboratory APS, or 3 criteria with the absence of histopathologic diagnosis.5 There are many challenges in the current diagnostic criteria including the possibility of false negatives and positives when testing for antiphospholipid antibodies, the crossover and similarities with other microangiopathies, and the barriers to obtain histopathological samples in the acute setting with thrombocytopenia.5,6 Our patient met criteria for probable CAPS with all 3 antibodies positive and clinically significant arterial and venous thrombosis.

During pregnancy, the diagnosis of CAPS is particularly challenging given the nonspecific symptoms that patients may experience and comorbidities from obstetric conditions. Our patient's case represented a unique challenge given the overlap in clinical manifestations with her recent history of HELLP syndrome; therefore, a high index of suspicion was necessary to establish the diagnosis. Other differentials to consider include disseminated intravascular coagulation, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, and small vessel vasculitis.

Management of CAPS is driven by multidisciplinary care and evolves based on available clinical information. Treatment typically includes a combination of systemic steroids, anticoagulation, and plasma exchange therapy or intravenous immunoglobulin.7 Steroid regimen typically involves a glucocorticoid, such as methylprednisolone; however, no randomized trials exist regarding the agent and dose of glucocorticoids in CAPS. Although plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulin have demonstrated benefit in CAPS, the mechanism behind its efficacy remains unclear.7

The anticoagulant agent of choice is intravenous unfractionated heparin because of the ease of its administration and efficacy in monitoring; alternative agents should be used if heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is suspected. Our institution routinely employs the anti-Xa heparin assay to measure the anticoagulant effect of unfractionated heparin. This chromogenic assay has been shown to have less variability compared to activated partial thromboplastin time, particularly in response to nonanticoagulant variables.8 Notably, antiphospholipid antibodies do not influence anti-Xa assay results, making it a more reliable tool in certain clinical scenarios.8 The ideal target range for factor Xa activity is between 0.4 and 0.6.

Heparin therapy can be initiated with or without an initial bolus, followed by a continuous infusion of 12 units/kg/h (typically equivalent to an initial rate of 700 units/h). Factor Xa activity is assessed 4 hours after initiation of therapy. If the factor Xa level falls below 0.1 or is outside of the target range, a 200 units/h bolus of heparin is administered. Subsequent factor Xa activity measurements are performed 6 hours after dose adjustments and once daily thereafter, unless further interventions are needed.

In the setting of CAPS, our patient had thrombotic events while maintained on the standard MCS anticoagulation protocol. This necessitated the development of a novel protocol tailored for our patient. The anticoagulation protocol aimed to achieve a higher factor Xa target range of 0.6-0.8. Adjustments were made in increments of 200 units/h for each 0.1 deviation from the target range. This individualized approach to anticoagulation may offer improved control over thrombotic events in the context of CAPS, where standard protocols may be insufficient. Because of the higher risk of bleeding incurred by this protocol and the presence of multiple vascular access sites, our patient was assessed at routine intervals for bleeding complications.

Conclusions

Our case underscores the importance of considering CAPS in patients with a history of APS and systemic lupus erythematosus, particularly when they present with cardiogenic shock and thrombotic events. Early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach, including anticoagulation, corticosteroids, plasmapheresis, and advanced heart failure therapies, are essential for optimizing outcomes. Additionally, early consideration for MCS and heart transplantation should be made in patients with refractory heart failure due to APS-related myocarditis and multiorgan involvement.
