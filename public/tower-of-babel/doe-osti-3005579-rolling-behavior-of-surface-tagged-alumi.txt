Summary
Physical tagging of nuclear fuel is potentially one way of tracking throughout the fuel lifecycle.
The process of tagging can take many forms, but one promising method is to embed a small
amount of photoluminescent particles in the surface of a material which are not detectable
under normal illumination but are readily visible under excitation by ultraviolet light. The goal of
this project was to explore the potential to tag simulated nuclear fuel cladding with small ceramic
particles, via laser beam welding or gas tungsten arc welding, and measure the
photoluminescent response. Surface tagging using this method has demonstrated viability and
detectability throughout this project; work in FY24 focused on the survivability of these tags
through subsequent deformation processing similar to what taggants in a fuel manufacturing
environment would be subjected to. This work demonstrated that particles are readily visible in
the as-welded state (i.e., before subsequent rolling treatment to represent mechanical
processing of a fuel material), but visibility is reduced as the deformation is induced. Despite the
reduction in photoluminescence, the taggants are still visible after rolling processes are
performed, making this surface tagging technique a promising candidate for nuclear fuel
cladding tagging. This work demonstrated, though, that targeted optimization work would be
necessary to develop a robust taggant depending on the fidelity and luminescent properties
required.

1.0 Introduction
The ultimate goal of tagging nuclear fuels (or related materials, such as fuel cladding) is to
provide a persistent marker, traceable to the fuel manufacturer, that can be detected after some
length of time, likely after the fuel has been lost or rerouted from the intended path, and
potentially after partial destruction of the fuel. Additional information regarding the broader
intentional forensics project can be found in [1, 2]. Ideally, the tag is complicated enough to
encode data pertaining to the provenance (manufacturer, date or time, constituents, etc.) and
robust enough to survive in-pile exposure and/or further destruction. Additionally, the taggant
should not change the behavior or performance of the fuel in any measurable way.
Photoluminescent tagging was an attractive solution to many of these challenges, as it is
undetectable under normal lighting conditions, visible to the naked eye under specific lighting
conditions, does not require a significant quantity of material to be photoluminescent,
observable with simple commercial detection tools, and is versatile and robust in application.
Previous studies have shown that photoluminescence of many rare earth oxides can survive the
welding process, though typically undergo some degree of change. In many cases, the color
that is emitted after welding changes from the original color [3], which can complicate detection.
For example, the wavelength used for detection may be different after welding, as Al2O3 was
white prior to welding, and red/pink after. This color change was thought to be due to the
complex interactions between the rare earth oxide and the molten pool, possibly doping the rare
earth oxide. Additional information regarding characterization of welded oxides was presented in
the FY23 report [3].
During the fuel manufacturing process, there are several techniques that are used that impart a
certain amount of damage. Although previous work indicates that the taggant can survive the
welding process, there are other processes that impart a different mode of damage, and it is
important to understand if the taggants can also survive these processes as well. An example of
the fuel manufacturing process is shown in Figure 1 [4]. This process, called the picture frame
technique (PFT), can vary depending on the fuel manufacturer or fuel type, but the general
processes are applicable to most fuel types. With respect to the fuel manufacturing cycle, the
most concerning process (from a total damage perspective, as well as from a different mode of
damage) related to taggant damage was the rolling process, shown as step 7 in Figure 1. In this
process, the fuel will be subject to both mechanical deformation and thermal damage. This
challenge can be broken down into two parts:
 1. The taggant adds particulates to the weld that could either strengthen the weld (similar
 to the effect of metal matrix composites) or weaken the weld. To survive the rolling
 process the weld must exhibit some minimum strength and ductility requirements to hold
 the picture frame assembly together.
 2. The rolling process produces complex stresses and strains as well as elevated
 temperatures that could quench the photoluminescence. It is important to know that the
 taggant can maintain its photoluminescence to remain effective as a surface taggant.

cladding were Dy:YAG and Nd:YAG which were of interest to both LANL and ANL.

2.0 Background
Work in FY24 focused on producing PFT samples of aluminum alloy 6061 (Al6061) in order to
simulate the cladding process for nuclear fuels. Al6061 is a typical cladding material for metallic
nuclear fuels, and additional information regarding the selection of this material can be found in
the FY23 report [3]. Multi-layered samples, to mimic the assemblies shown in Figure 1 step 5,
were created to replicate the cladding and frame materials, though no nuclear material was
included in the samples studied in FY24. These samples were welded on the edges, similar to
Figure 1 step 6, and then heated and rolled to investigate the impact of the processing steps.
2.1 Rolling
The key metric for mechanical robustness of the welds through the rolling process is the
absence of any cracks or fissures that may lead to interaction between the outside environment
and the internals of the sample; in actual nuclear fuel, cracks in the Al6061 welds could lead to
oxidation or corrosion concerns for the internal uranium fuel. Specific to this work, it was
important to consider the sample geometry and the rolling process: in a rectangular geometry,
the welds on the leading and trailing edges of the rolling process are subjected to different
stress states than the two lateral edges.
2.2 Potential Strengthening Mechanisms
The effect of the taggant on the weld properties results in one of two scenarios: it will either
strengthen the weld or it will weaken the weld. This effect is not unprecedented; in metal matrix
composites (MMCs), ceramics are added to metals to strengthen the bulk material. On the
macro-level MMCs are relying on the rule of mixtures to improve strength [4]. There are also
micro-level effects with MMCs, such as precipitation strengthening mechanisms and strain
effects that also could help to improve strength. In the case of this work, the taggant has
properties of a ceramic that might improve the weld mechanical properties, thus acting like an
MMC. If, however, the taggant agglomerates or if the particles are too big, the particles or
agglomerates can act as inclusions and a source of stress that can cause premature failure
(and weaken the weld). The work in FY24 does not explicitly quantify the mechanical
performance of the tagged weld, but it does explore the performance during rolling. If there is
consistent success with rolling, it can be hypothesized that the tagged weld has some
strengthening effect or no effect on the weld. If the welds consistently fail during rolling, it is
likely that the taggant has some negative effect on the weld properties.

3.0 Experimental Procedure
The taggant application, welding method, and rolling schedule are summarized in this chapter.
3.1 Surface Taggants
The oxides that were considered in FY24 were Dy:YAG and Nd:YAG. The dopant
concentrations (Dy and Nd) were approximately 5%. The particle sizes were an average of
5 µm. Nd:YAG powders were calcined in air at 1400 °C for 1 hour to remove any residual
organics and to coarsen powder to aid with powder dispersion. AlSi10Mg powders, which were
used as a matrix powder to incorporate the Dy and Nd YAG powders were used as received.
Powders, dispersant (Darvan CN), binder and defoaming agent (PVA and PEG) were mixed in
de-ionized water via rolling on a roller mill with yttria-stabilized zirconia milling media. The
taggant slurry was free of clumps and flowed fluidly without resistance. Taggant slurry was then
applied to prepared Al surfaces dropwise via pipette and dried at 45 °C in a drying oven for 2 to
3 hours.
Though the hole drilling method used in previous years [3] has been proven to work for laser
welding, the necessary depth of the drilled hole needed for gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW) is
harder to achieve. In addition, GTAW exhibits a Lorentz force (electromagnetic force) that can
blow the powder taggants out of the weld. Therefore, other application methods were explored
to ensure the taggant was successfully incorporated into the weld, mainly slurries were explored
and are summarized in Table 1.
The shift from laser welding to GTAW in FY24 was necessary due to several challenges that
were encountered during the year when producing multi-layered rolling samples. Aluminum is
highly reflective of the 1070 nm laser which means that more energy is required to melt the
metal, and aluminum has a relatively higher thermal conductivity compared to other metals,
meaning that heat quickly transfers through the material. These two properties of aluminum
made it difficult to join thicker Al6061 plates together; thus, GTAW was pursued. For rolling
studies, both types of welding processes were investigated. There were two challenges when
moving to GTAW:
 1. GTAW could not achieve the same depth to width ratios that laser welding could and
 there was concern with the GTAW welds not fully consuming the taggant with the hole
 drilling method.
 2. The Lorentz force required a shift in taggant incorporation technique. Since hole drilling
 is impractical for GTAW, the taggant must be painted on in a slurry on the surface prior
 to welding.
Various methods were used to apply the taggant slurry on the surface of the coupons with
limited success. The methods included exploring different surface finishes of the samples in
order to improve the adhesion of the slurry on the surface of Al6061, and adding a groove to the
sample surface to increase the loading of the taggant. This second technique is demonstrated in
Figure 2 on early sample geometries, which were sandwiched together prior to welding. This
configuration was explored to avoid blow out of the taggant since the taggant is contained in the
assembly. The top surface was then welded to simulate the seam weld. Ultimately, the sample
geometry, and groove technique, were not used for rolling studies due to a lack of success in
incorporating the taggant, as well as concerns for the increased effort required in full-scale
manufacturing practices.

3.2 Welding
After exploring the taggant application strategies in Table 1 and Figure 2, a final sample
geometry was chosen. Al6061 was cut into 10.16 x 15.24 x 0.4064 cm (4 x 6 x 0.16 in.) and
heat treated to the O condition (annealed). Three plates were stacked together, as shown in
Figure 3, and the edges (seams) were welded using GTAW. Additional welds were made on the
surface of the cladding parallel and perpendicular to the rolling direction; since these welds do
not require a certain penetration depth (and in fact, limited weld penetration is likely preferred in
order to maintain the Al6061 mechanical properties), a laser marker was used to create the
surface welds. Since taggants were not successfully applied using the GTAW process, the final
strategy that was implemented was to use the GTAW method for structural welding, and then
revert to laser welding for the actual tagging process. Taggant was applied to two rectangular
regions on the surface and sections of the four welded edges by painting a slurry of taggant and
gently drying with a heat gun to remove remaining solvent. These locations were then laser
welded to incorporate the tags. In summary, samples were seam welded using GTAW, followed
by taggant application via slurry on the surface and seam welds shown in Figure 3, then laser
welded to incorporate the tags.

Two targeted sample sets were pursued for exploring the seam welds during rolling studies. The
first was to weld the sample assembly without the taggants, using the legacy laser welding
beam process established in FY23. These samples were produced using a defocused laser
welding process to create the seam welds. The second set of samples were created using the
combined GTAW and laser beam welding process discussed above, with taggants, to explore
the effect of rolling on taggant photoluminescence (PL). For the second study, Dy:YAG was
applied to two of the edges and Nd:YAG was applied to the opposite edges. Surface welding
was performed on the largest face of the cladding assembly. Small grids of various parameters
were used to tag the top surface of the cladding with Dy:YAG and Nd:YAG.

3.3 Rolling Schedule
Two rolling experiments were conducted on the sample sets in Section 3.2 to determine the
manufacturability of the welded and tagged plates. This first sample, produced with laser
welding, did not incorporate any taggant particles and was rolled to give a baseline for the
manufacturability of the cladding, as well as gain operator experience. The second plate was
GTAW welded along the edges, and subsequently tagged using laser welding. All rolling was
performed on a 1-high, Waterbury-Ferrel Mill, with 18 in. diameter rolls. Plates were heated in a
Carbolite Gero 30-3000 °C furnace to 500 °C for rolling. Roll speed was set at 365.76 cm/min
(12 ft/min) for all rolling passes.

3.4 Characterization and Detection Techniques
Various characterization techniques were considered to understand the mechanism for the
change in PL behavior and resulting success of the various processes performed on the Al6061
plates.
3.4.1 Light Optical Microscopy
Light optical microscopy (LOM) was used with and without ultraviolet (UV) excitation to analyze
the welds and photoluminescence of the taggant. The original taggant powders and embedded
welds were illuminated using either an LCNDT PRO-500 Ultra-High intensity UV lamp with a
365 nm excitation or an off-the-shelf UVBeast light with a 365 nm excitation. Images were
collected using a Canon digital single lens reflex EOX.

4.0 Results and Discussion
The effects of the laser welding process on the taggant behavior will be discussed in Section 4.2
and methods used to detect the taggant will be discussed in Section 4.4.
4.1 Taggant Application
The water-based taggant slurry dried and adhered well to the substrate surface, however, a
halo effect of a ring of well-adhered dried slurry surrounding an area of less-adhered slurry
indicates high stresses due to shrinkage of the slurry while drying. The shrinkage stress could
be reduced by further increasing the solids loading of the taggant slurry. Solids loading on the
order of 30-40 vol% should be achievable given more development of the slurry recipe and
would result in a higher taggant density and higher likelihood of being incorporated into the
weld.
Welding of the dried slurries using GTAW resulted in the taggant balling, which would lead to
issues in taggant dispersion and fidelity of the tag. The region outside of the arc would
incorporate into the weld in chunks to create small balls of taggant on the surface of the molten
pool. Figure 4 is a schematic of the challenges associated with using GTAW to incorporate a
painted taggant. The taggant in front of the arc [Figure 4(a)] was already consumed into a balled

 11

taggant. As the arc progressed, another section of taggant balled. This continues until the entire
surface application is consumed. The balled taggants pool towards the center of the weld and
are frozen in place once the molten pool solidifies [Figure 4(b)]. However, taggant was not
incorporated into the weld material, and the larger surface taggant particles did not retain
photoluminescence after welding.

Since the taggant applied to the surface appeared to ball up and chased away by the arc, the
next technique attempted was a shallow groove below the surface (similar to Figure 2) that the
powder taggant can pack into. The thought behind this method was to encapsulate the powder
so that it cannot be chased away by the arc and be introduced directly into the molten pool
instead of being heated by the arc first. This method of using the groove to contain the particles,
however, was unsuccessful because the particles still failed to mix with the weld pool. Rather
than floating to the top of the melt, the clusters of taggant material interrupted the continuity of
the pool, compromising the weld integrity and preventing the two surfaces from joining, as
shown in Figure 5.

Figure 6 shows various Al6061 coupons tagged with the different slurries. The first three
samples, in Figure 6(a-c) demonstrate that the taggant tends to ball and agglomerate towards
the center of the weld pool. This behavior is not advantageous since it concentrates the taggant
to a small area instead of an even distribution throughout the weld. The taggant was also not
well bonded to the weld metal, and much of it either flaked off immediately or over time during
handling. To improve incorporation of the taggant powder with the weld, some particles were
dispersed in a mixture with AlSi10Mg powder. However, when the welding arc was applied, the
powder mixture rapidly oxidized and fractured off the surface. This combined method was not
pursued further.

Using GTAW to incorporate the YAG particles was more difficult than expected. The work in
FY24 clearly demonstrates the benefits of using a laser process due to the speed, lower heat
input (and therefore lower damage to the base material), and it does not exhibit a Lorentz force.
Therefore, a multi-process taggant strategy was explored in FY24: GTAW was performed to
seal the picture frame fit up and a laser was used to add the taggant.

4.2 Light Optical Microscopy with UV Excitation
Prior work has indicated that changes can occur in PL due to the welding process, but that PL is
still detectable after laser welding has occurred [3]; previous work had been limited to studying
this effect and the feasibility/process development associated with laser welding on aluminum
base plates. FY24 work was focused on truly incorporating prior work into a manufacturing
process, resulting in the combined GTAW and laser welding process reported in previous
sections. Due to the changes in taggant application that occurred in FY24, additional laser
welding investigations were necessary to confirm that prior results were still valid. Specifically, a
small-scoping study was needed to demonstrate that the combination of painted taggant (rather
than drilled holes) and lower-power laser parameters would still result in an acceptable tag. The
reduction in laser power, which would greatly reduce damage to the clad surface, was driven by
the change in goal for laser welding in FY24 compared to previous years (taggant incorporation
rather than structural welding).
The greatest PL response from welded taggant was observed after laser beam welding at high
frequency and intermediate power level. Lower laser powers and frequency failed to embed the
particles in the surface sufficiently, while higher powers appeared to oxidize the surface, and no
PL response was observed. 200KHz was the highest frequency offered by the laser welding
system, and it clearly embedded the particles most efficiently, suggesting that even higher
frequencies may be optimal, as shown in Figure 7. The results are encouraging for tagging
purposes, as the painted taggant slurry (Figure 7, left) shows significantly less PL compared to
the welded sample (Figure 7, right).

 and welded taggant on the right, with 365 nm excitation light source.

4.3 Mechanical Deformation
After GTAW to seal the four edges, a stack of three Al6061 plates were laser tagged on all sides
and then rolled following the rolling schedule in Table 3. The surface and all four edges were
tagged with rectangles of PL taggant, similar to the tags shown in Figure 7, and using the laser
welding parameters used in Figure 7 and discussed in Section 4.2. After welding (but prior to
rolling), there was clear PL response from the surface of the plate stack (similar to Figure 7) but
much less on the welded edges, as shown in Figure 8. This is a significant result, as this
indicates that there are limitations for surface tagging within the manufacturing environment.
Surface tagging must be limited to prepped surfaces, or those that have been smoothed to an

 15

appropriate surface roughness and have had surface oxidation cleaned or removed. The GTAW
seam welds were laser welded/tagged in the as-welded state; the resultant surface roughness
can be seen in the bottom image of Figure 8. This surface roughness, coupled with the oxide
layer that readily forms on aluminum, likely reduced the incorporation of taggant and/or made
the detection of incorporated taggant difficult, as the excitation of the taggant (and emitted
response) could be blocked by the surface features.

 Figure 8. Optical images of a Al6061 weld after tagging under UV illumination (top) and
  standard illumination (bottom) showing very limited PL return within the tagged areas (dark
 rectangles in the top image).

After initial rolling steps, the PL signal on the surface was still identifiable, but was completely
unidentified using UV illumination after the full rolling process. Although it is possible that other
characterization tools may be able to detect the taggants after rolling, the field-ready UV light
source was not adequate to detect the taggants. For the full fuel manufacturing process, this
indicates that taggants may need to be incorporated after the rolling steps (Figure 1). If changes
to the rolling schedule are allowable, several studies could be performed to further investigate
when and what process affects the PL the greatest. To determine when the PL effect is no
longer detectable, iterative rolling and PL investigation can be used. Additionally, the effect of
the heating alone (time at temperature needed to reach the rolling temperatures) can be
investigated without the influence of deformation to determine if there is a change in PL
response due to the thermal exposure. Finally, the tags on the seam welds were not detectable
after any rolling.
Apart from PL, rolling of the Al picture frame was largely successful. The laser welded, non-
tagged sample showed small delamination defects, and regions in which air had been trapped
between the plates resulted in blisters and bubbles. The sample that was welded using GTAW
and tagged with laser welding did experience an overall larger reduction, which is due to the
higher rolling temperature as well as the heated rolls in the last two passes. Figure 9 shows the
final, rolled GTAW tagged Al sheet.

  Figure 9. Al sheet that was GTAW welded and tagged using laser welding after rolling. The
 rolling was largely successful, however large defects (A,B) were noted.

The tagged sample displayed some of the same defects that were seen in the laser welded
sample, such as delamination and bubbles due to trapped air. If welding is performed in
vacuum, it is expected that these defects can be mitigated. Additionally, defects such as
heterogeneous deformation Figure 9(a) and cracking in Figure 9(b) were observed. The
heterogenous deformation resulted in buckling the sheet, which manifests as the wavey or
wrinkled features. Ensuring uniform heating across the workpiece and consistent roll
temperatures, as well as higher reductions may mitigate these issues. The cracking shown in
Figure 9(b) appears to be a result of delamination of two of the Al layers. This could be
mitigated by higher reductions. Overall, the welds did not inhibit the rolling process, and were
largely not the sources of defects present.

5.0 Conclusions
In FY24, the surface tagging work was expanded to understand if previous laser welding and PL
investigations could be scaled to manufacturing environments and conditions. Several changes
were necessary to move prior results towards full-scale processes, such as incorporating other
welding processes (such as GTAW and laser welding for taggant incorporation alone),
investigating taggant application strategies, and introducing new deformation processes such as
rolling. Incorporating taggant into Al surfaces by GTAW was found to be ineffective, but laser
beam welding continued to demonstrate success even under new parameters, demonstrating
that increases in PL could be realized through this process. The increases in PL did not survive
the rolling process, and PL was not observed using a UV light source after rolling had been
completed. This indicates that although the taggants demonstrated robustness through the
welding process, additional manufacturing steps can quench the taggants. If
photoluminescent taggants are to be used in nuclear fuel manufacturing, the location
and timing of incorporation is important to maintain the luminescent properties. Finally,
the tagged, welded locations did not demonstrate any deleterious effects on the mechanical
performance of the rolled samples. The results indicate that welded, photoluminescent tags will
require additional work and investigation to retain the taggant properties, but do not create
additional concerns for fuel performance through the manufacturing processes.
