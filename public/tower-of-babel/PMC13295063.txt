Salivary decorin as a potential noninvasive biomarker in chronic kidney disease: a comparative study with serum levels
Authors: Asena Serap Karatutlu, Mehmet Ali Karatutlu, Suleyman Aydin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by progressive renal fibrosis and irreversible loss of kidney function. Decorin, an extracellular matrix proteoglycan with antifibrotic properties, may reflect underlying fibrotic activity and has emerged as a potential biomarker. However, its role in noninvasive biological fluids such as saliva remains unclear. In this cross-sectional study, 88 patients with CKD stages 3–5 and 30 healthy controls were included. Serum and unstimulated saliva samples were collected simultaneously, and decorin levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Group comparisons were performed using non-parametric tests, and correlations were analyzed using Spearman’s method. Serum decorin levels showed an increasing trend with advancing CKD stage; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance and were not correlated with renal function parameters. In contrast, salivary decorin levels were significantly lower in CKD patients compared to controls and demonstrated a numerical decrease across CKD stages. No significant correlation was observed between serum and salivary decorin levels. Salivary decorin may represent a potential noninvasive candidate marker in CKD; however, further large-scale longitudinal studies are required to validate these findings.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive and irreversible condition characterized by gradual loss of renal function, affecting approximately 10–15% of the adult population worldwide and representing a major global health burden [1,2]. The progression of CKD is closely associated with structural and functional alterations in the kidney, ultimately leading to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), which requires renal replacement therapies such as dialysis or transplantation [3].

Regardless of the underlying etiology, tubulointerstitial fibrosis is considered the final common pathway in CKD progression and is strongly associated with renal function decline and poor prognosis [4,5]. This fibrotic process is driven by complex interactions between inflammatory mediators, growth factors, and extracellular matrix (ECM) components. Among these, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) plays a central role by promoting fibroblast activation and excessive ECM accumulation [6].

Decorin is a small leucine-rich proteoglycan that is an essential component of the extracellular matrix and is involved in collagen fibrillogenesis and tissue organization [7]. Importantly, decorin has been shown to exert antifibrotic effects through its ability to bind and neutralize TGF-β, thereby inhibiting downstream signaling pathways involved in fibrosis [8,9]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that decorin can attenuate renal fibrosis and reduce extracellular matrix deposition in various models of kidney injury [10].

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in identifying novel biomarkers that reflect the underlying pathophysiological processes of CKD and allow early detection and monitoring of disease progression. While several biomarkers such as NGAL, cystatin C, and inflammatory markers have been investigated, there is still a need for reliable and noninvasive biomarkers [11,12]. In this context, decorin may represent a promising candidate due to its role in fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Most previous studies have focused on circulating biomarkers; however, noninvasive biological fluids such as saliva have gained attention as potential diagnostic tools. Saliva offers several advantages including easy, noninvasive collection and reduced patient discomfort [13]. Despite these advantages, the role of salivary biomarkers in CKD remains poorly explored, and to the best of our knowledge, data regarding salivary decorin levels in CKD patients are extremely limited.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate serum and salivary decorin levels in patients with stage 3, 4, and 5 CKD and to investigate their potential role as biomarkers of disease progression. Additionally, we aimed to assess the relationship between decorin levels and renal function parameters.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study included patients diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages 3, 4, and 5, who were followed in the nephrology outpatient clinic. A total of 88 patients with CKD and 30 healthy individuals as a control group were enrolled in the study.

CKD staging was performed according to estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) values in accordance with established clinical guidelines. Patients were classified as stage 3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2), stage 4 (15–29 mL/min/1.73 m2), and stage 5 (<15 mL/min/1.73 m2). None of the stage 5 CKD patients were receiving dialysis therapy at the time of sample collection.

Healthy controls were selected from individuals without known renal disease, systemic illness, or acute infection.

Patients who were followed between January 2017 and December 2017 were included in this study.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study.

Sample collection

Venous blood samples were obtained from all participants under standard conditions. Serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at appropriate temperatures until analysis.

Unstimulated saliva samples were collected from all participants under resting conditions. Participants were instructed to avoid eating, drinking, and oral hygiene procedures prior to sample collection. Saliva samples were collected into sterile containers and processed according to standard laboratory procedures.

Biochemical analysis

Serum creatinine levels were measured using standard automated laboratory methods, and eGFR was calculated accordingly.

Serum and salivary decorin levels were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; catalog no: ab54728) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fırat University Faculty of Medicine (approval number: 2017-TBEK 2017/03-01; approval date: 9 February 2017). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Continuous variables were expressed as median (minimum–maximum) due to non-normal distribution. Categorical variables were presented as number and percentage.

Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Pairwise post hoc comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Correlations between variables were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis.

A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 88 patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) (stage 3, n = 30; stage 4, n = 28; stage 5, n = 30) and 30 healthy controls were included in the study.

The median age was significantly higher in CKD patients compared to the control group (p < 0.001). In addition, serum creatinine levels were significantly elevated and eGFR values were significantly lower across advancing CKD stages (p < 0.001 for both), consistent with disease progression (Table 1).

Post hoc analyses demonstrated that salivary decorin levels were significantly lower in stage 5 compared to the control group (p < 0.01). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between intermediate CKD stages.

When all CKD patients were compared with controls, salivary decorin levels were significantly lower in the CKD group (p = 0.00086).

Correlation analyses were performed within the CKD group.

No significant correlation was observed between age and serum decorin (r = −0.056) or salivary decorin levels (r = −0.19).

Similarly, no significant correlation was found between serum decorin levels and renal function parameters, including eGFR and serum creatinine (p > 0.05 for all comparisons), although trends in the expected direction were noted.

In addition, no significant correlation was observed between serum and salivary decorin levels.

Overall, serum decorin levels tended to increase across CKD stages but did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, salivary decorin levels were significantly decreased in CKD patients and showed a numerical decrease with advancing disease stage.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated serum and salivary decorin levels in patients with stage 3, 4, and 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD). The main finding of our study is that salivary decorin levels were significantly lower in CKD patients compared to healthy controls and showed a decreasing trend with advancing disease stage. In contrast, although serum decorin levels demonstrated an increasing tendency with CKD progression, this difference did not reach statistical significance, suggesting limited utility as a discriminatory biomarker.

CKD progression is characterized by progressive renal fibrosis, which represents the final common pathway leading to irreversible kidney damage. Among the molecular mediators involved in fibrosis, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) plays a central role by promoting extracellular matrix accumulation and fibroblast activation. Decorin, a small leucine-rich proteoglycan, exerts antifibrotic effects through its ability to bind and inhibit TGF-β, thereby modulating fibrotic pathways. Experimental studies have demonstrated that decorin can attenuate renal fibrosis and reduce extracellular matrix deposition in various models of kidney injury. Based on these findings, alterations in decorin levels may reflect underlying fibrotic activity in CKD.

In our study, serum decorin levels showed an increasing trend with advancing CKD stage; however, this change did not reach statistical significance. This finding may reflect underlying pathophysiological processes to ongoing fibrotic processes rather than a direct indicator of disease severity. The lack of statistical significance and absence of correlation with renal function parameters suggest that serum decorin alone may not be sufficiently sensitive to discriminate between CKD stages.

One of the most notable findings of our study is the significant decrease in salivary decorin levels in CKD patients. Saliva has emerged as a promising noninvasive diagnostic fluid due to its ease of collection and patient acceptability. The reduction in salivary decorin levels observed in our study may be explained by several mechanisms, including altered salivary gland function, accumulation of uremic toxins, and systemic metabolic disturbances associated with CKD. In addition, differences in protein transport and secretion between systemic circulation and salivary glands may contribute to the discordance observed between serum and salivary decorin levels.

Interestingly, no significant correlation was found between serum and salivary decorin levels, suggesting that salivary decorin does not simply reflect circulating levels but may represent a distinct biological process. Therefore, salivary decorin may reflect biological processes distinct from circulating serum decorin levels.

Furthermore, no significant correlation was observed between serum decorin levels and renal function parameters such as eGFR and serum creatinine. Although trends in the expected direction were noted, these findings indicate that decorin levels may be influenced by multiple factors beyond renal function alone, including extracellular matrix turnover and individual variability.

There was a significant age difference between the control and CKD groups. However, no significant correlation was found between age and either serum or salivary decorin levels. One of the major limitations of this study is the significant age difference between the control and CKD groups. Although no significant correlation was observed between age and decorin levels in our cohort, residual confounding related to age cannot be excluded. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger prospective studies including age-matched control populations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Second, the sample size of certain subgroups, particularly stage 4 CKD patients, was relatively limited. Third, the control and CKD groups were not matched for age and sex, which may have introduced potential confounding effects. Additionally, variability in saliva collection and processing may have influenced the measurements. Several salivary confounding factors, including oral health status, hydration status, salivary flow rate, xerostomia, smoking status, and medication-related oral effects, were not systematically standardized or recorded. In addition, dialysis-related factors may have influenced biomarker measurements in stage 5 CKD patients.

Despite these limitations, our study has important strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few studies evaluating both serum and salivary decorin levels in CKD patients. The inclusion of multiple CKD stages and a control group enabled a comprehensive assessment of disease-related changes. Moreover, the evaluation of salivary decorin provides novel insight into noninvasive biomarker development in CKD.

Therefore, our findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Further large-scale, longitudinal, and mechanistic studies are needed to validate the clinical utility of salivary decorin and to clarify its role in CKD progression.

Conclusion

Salivary decorin levels are significantly decreased in patients with chronic kidney disease and show a numerical decrease across CKD stages. These findings suggest that salivary decorin may have potential as a noninvasive candidate marker in CKD. Because of the cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships and temporal changes in decorin levels during CKD progression cannot be established. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether decorin levels change over time in parallel with disease progression.
