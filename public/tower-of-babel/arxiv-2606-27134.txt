Fast summation on rectangular cuboids with arbitrary periodicity in the DMK framework
David Krantz, Ludvig af Klinteberg, Anna-Karin Tornberg

Abstract
Dual-space multilevel kernel-splitting (DMK) is a fast summation framework that combines ideas from the fast multipole method, Ewald summation, and multilevel summation. Originally formulated for free-space problems, and later extended to fully periodic problems on a cube, it decomposes the kernel interaction into a smooth global contribution and a hierarchy of localized interactions evaluated on an octree. We extend DMK to problems on rectangular cuboids with periodic boundary conditions in one, two, or three coordinate directions. The periodization leverages the fact that interactions on all tree levels below the root are localized, allowing for their evaluation with minimal modification on a cubical tiling of the domain. The remaining smooth root-level far-field contribution is evaluated in Fourier space, with Fourier series in the periodic directions and Fourier integrals in the free directions. For reduced periodicity, truncated kernels are used to regularize singular and near-singular Fourier kernels, yielding rapidly convergent trapezoidal discretizations and a unified treatment of all periodicities. For large-aspect-ratio cuboids, the root-level sum can be accelerated using the fast Fourier transform. We validate the method for the electrostatic potential and Stokeslet, stresslet and rotlet potentials, for all periodicities and a wide range of aspect ratios. Numerical experiments show that the periodization adds only a small overhead to the original free-space DMK algorithm, also for high-aspect-ratio cuboids. The resulting method provides a framework for applying DMK to problems with mixed periodicity on rectangular cuboids, and extends naturally to other non-oscillatory kernels for which a kernel split is available.

Introduction

Large-scale computations of periodic potentials arise frequently in many
areas of science and engineering, including electrostatic potentials
in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and discretized layer
potentials in Stokes simulations of microfluidic flow. We here consider such potentials, written
in the form

u^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{s}}\sum_{\bm{p}\in P_{D\mathcal{P}}}^{*}K({\bm{x}}_{\beta}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}+\bm{p})\rho_{\alpha},\quad\beta=1,\dots,N_{t},

(1)

where the
*
superscript indicates that the term
\bm{p}=0
is ignored
if
{\bm{x}}_{\beta}={\bm{x}}_{\alpha}
.
The kernel
K
maps from scalar-, vector- or tensor-valued source strengths
\rho_{\alpha}
to a scalar- or vector-valued potential
u^{D\mathcal{P}}
, and is typically singular at the origin and decays slowly at infinity. The
N_{s}

are located at points
{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
inside a rectangular cuboid

\Omega=[0,L_{1})\times[0,L_{2})\times[0,L_{3})

cell, and the
N_{t}
targets are in the same cuboid. The sources
and targets commonly coincide, and we will for simplicity of exposition assume

N_{s}=N_{t}=N
.
The set

of periodic images depends on the number of periodic directions
D
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the coordinate axes are relabeled such that the first
D
directions are periodic,

P_{D\mathcal{P}}=\begin{cases}\{(\bar{p}_{1}L_{1},\bar{p}_{2}L_{2},\bar{p}_{3}L_{3})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bar{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if $D=3$ (triply periodic)},\\
\{(\bar{p}_{1}L_{1},\bar{p}_{2}L_{2},0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bar{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if $D=2$ (doubly periodic)},\\
\{(\bar{p}_{1}L_{1},0,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bar{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if $D=1$ (singly periodic)}.\end{cases}

(2)

The free-space case

corresponds to
P_{0\mathcal{P}}=\{\bm{0}\}
.
The computational complexity of evaluating (
1

clearly quadratic,
O(N^{2})

The cases with one or two periodic directions introduce additional
difficulties, since periodic interactions must be combined with

This work is concerned with the efficient evaluation of
(
1

decomposition, or

with a splitting parameter

K(\bm{x})={K_{M}}(\bm{x},\nu)+{K_{R}}(\bm{x},\nu).

The split separates the singular short-range behavior from a smooth long-range part whose Fourier representation decays rapidly enough to be truncated.
Here, the

{K_{M}}(\bm{x},\nu)
is smooth at the origin and
matches the long-range behavior of
K
. Meanwhile, the

at the origin
and then decays rapidly. For the harmonic kernel

\displaystyle H(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi r}=\frac{\operatorname{erf}(r/\nu)}{4\pi r}+\frac{\operatorname{erfc}(r/\nu)}{4\pi r}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H_{M}(r,\nu)+H_{R}(r,\nu),

\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\dif t,\qquad\operatorname{erfc}(x)=1-\operatorname{erf}(x).

(5)

Due to the smoothness of the mollified kernel
H_{M}(|\bm{x}|,\nu)

\widehat{H}_{M}(|\bm{k}|,\nu)
is rapidly decaying,

\widehat{H}_{M}(k,\nu)=\frac{e^{-k^{2}\nu^{2}/4}}{k^{2}},

and can be truncated in Fourier space. The residual kernel

H_{R}(|\bm{x}|,\nu)
acts as a local correction to

; it matches

truncated beyond a cutoff radius. Both decay rates are controlled by

The split (

The above process, known as Ewald summation, remedies the slow

However, for

The classical use of Ewald summation has been in electrostatics (i.e.,

In settings that are not fully periodic, the use of so-called

First introduced for Ewald summation in the free-space

While Ewald methods are highly efficient, their reliance on the FFT,
which operates on a uniform grid, makes the computational work difficult to adapt to clustered point distributions. Tree-based methods, by
contrast, are built on adaptive data structures and can
therefore efficiently handle highly nonuniform point distributions. They can typically achieve
O(N)
complexity, excluding
the tree construction cost, which usually is negligible. Over the
past few decades, a range of such methods has been developed,
including the fast multipole method
(FMM)
[
19
,
20
,
42
,
46
]
,

\mathcal{H}^{2}
-matrix
methods
[
10

18

[
12

13

23

The FMM and
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-matrix methods reduce computational cost by
approximating far-field interactions via hierarchical low-rank
representations of the original kernel. Multilevel summation takes a
different route. Rather than partitioning interactions into near and
far components, it constructs a telescoping expansion in which the
kernel is replaced by a sequence of smooth, increasingly localized
approximations that are efficiently handled at their respective
scales.

Ewald-based methods are especially efficient for triply periodic problems,
while free directions introduce additional costs. Tree-based methods, on
the other hand, are most naturally formulated for free-space problems.
Periodic boundary conditions can be imposed in FMM-based methods by
explicitly summing over a finite set of neighboring image boxes and
representing the remaining images through a far-field
correction. Classical periodic FMMs achieve this by evaluating lattice
sums of multipole expansions, often using Ewald-type
decompositions
[
6
,
47
]
. A second class of
methods constructs the far-field correction using a set of proxy
sources outside the primary cell. In the approach of

[
21
]
, the far field of a periodic point sum is
represented by proxy sources whose coefficients are determined from a
least-squares problem, while
[
9
]
use proxy sources in
an extended 2D boundary-integral formulation to impose periodicity
constraints. In
[
45
]
, the equivalent-source machinery of the
kernel-independent FMM (KIFMM) is used to construct a periodic
multipole-to-local operator from a periodic Green’s function,
evaluated by Ewald summation. Recently,
[
27
]
avoid any
periodization-specific linear solve by introducing a hierarchical
recursion over image boxes using KIFMM equivalent surfaces. In a
distinct approach in 2D,
[
34
]
compute the far-field
correction using plane-wave expansions of the fundamental solutions,
accelerated through the non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT)

[
8
]
. Many of the FMM periodization methods
currently available in 3D are most naturally formulated for cubic or nearly
cubic cells, and may lose efficiency in other cases. For high-aspect-ratio rectangular cells, lattice-sum methods typically require many image boxes or Fourier modes, while proxy- or equivalent-source methods require a larger number of auxiliary points, which increases cost and can affect conditioning.
Thus, while tree methods provide adaptivity, efficient periodization on rectangular cuboids remains nontrivial, especially for large aspect ratios. This motivates adaptive periodic summation methods that can handle such geometries without sacrificing efficiency.

The recently developed dual-space multilevel kernel-splitting (DMK)
framework
[
26
,
3
]
brings together elements
of the methods discussed. It is based on a multilevel
decomposition into regularized kernels, leverages Fourier-space
diagonalization of the interactions at each level, and transfers local expansions through the tree hierarchy.
In reported benchmarks, DMK has been shown to be as fast or faster than state-of-the-art FMM implementations
[
26
,
3
]
.
Like the FMM, DMK is adaptive, but it is more
naturally amenable to periodization. For cubic domains, an extension
of the free-space formulation to full periodicity is straightforward
and computationally efficient, and has already been demonstrated

[
3
]
.

1.1
Contributions and outline

In this paper, we extend the periodic DMK framework from cubic, fully periodic domains to rectangular cuboids with periodicity in one, two, or three directions.
The main observation underlying the extension is that in DMK, kernel interactions are localized at all levels of the tree below the root. Periodicity therefore affects these levels only through periodic wrapping of near-neighbor interactions, while the periodic contribution from the lattice of image cells is isolated in the smooth root-level far-field.

The main components of our contribution are as follows.

•

A cubical tiling of the primary cell into cubes whose side length is the shortest periodic length
L_{\text{min}}
, with one octree associated with each cube. When all periodic side lengths are integer multiples of this length, the tiling is exact. Otherwise, the primary cell is embedded in a bounded extension of width
O(L_{\text{min}})
. The localized part of DMK is evaluated on this tiled domain.

•

A Fourier-space treatment of the smooth root-level far-field contribution for arbitrary periodicity. For reduced periodicity, truncated kernels are used to regularize the singular and near-singular Fourier kernels that arise in the non-periodic directions.

•

An FFT-accelerated evaluation of the root-level far-field contribution for large-aspect-ratio cuboids, reducing the geometry-dependent cost of the far-field contribution.

The resulting method preserves the adaptive structure of DMK, and
the per-source cost stays nearly independent of aspect ratio in experiments at constant point density.
We demonstrate the periodization method for electrostatic potentials and for the Stokeslet, stresslet, and rotlet potentials of Stokes flow, using PSWF-based splits of the harmonic and biharmonic kernels. The periodization strategy itself is not tied to this particular choice of split, and should extend directly to other non-oscillatory kernels for which a suitable kernel split is available.

2
Multilevel splitting and summation in arbitrary periodicity

This section introduces the multilevel kernel splitting used in DMK and formulates the resulting decomposition for singly, doubly, and triply periodic problems. We first describe the telescoping split, then review the PSWF-based kernels used in the numerical examples,
and finally state the compact and far-field contributions that are evaluated by the periodized DMK algorithm.

2.1
Multilevel kernel-splitting

The single-level split introduced in (
3
)
is extended in DMK to the multilevel telescoping expansion

K({\bm{x}})={K_{M}}({\bm{x}},\nu_{0})+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathcal{L}-1}K_{D_{\ell}}({\bm{x}})+{K_{R}}({\bm{x}},\nu_{0}/2^{\mathcal{L}})\qquad(\mathcal{L}\geq 1)

(9)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the number of levels and

K_{D_{\ell}}(\bm{x})={K_{M}}(\bm{x},\nu_{0}/2^{\ell+1})-{K_{M}}(\bm{x},\nu_{0}/2^{\ell}).

(10)

The terms of this expansion are associated with the refinement levels of an octree. If
r_{\ell}=r_{0}/2^{\ell}

denotes the box side length at level
\ell
,
then both
r_{\ell}
and the splitting parameter
\nu_{\ell}=\nu_{0}/2^{\ell}
decrease by a factor two from one level to the next. Thus the terms of the kernel expansion have the same relative scale on every level, see illustration in
fig.

1
.

The difference kernel
K_{D_{\ell}}
will be both rapidly decaying and smooth, and
\nu_{0}
will be chosen depending on an error tolerance such that the contribution from both the difference kernel and the residual kernel can be localized to nearest neighbor boxes.
Both the difference kernel
K_{D_{\ell}}
and the residual kernel
{K_{R}}
are rapidly decaying and either compactly supported (more on this in
section

2.2
) or can be truncated beyond a cutoff radius. We will, with a slight abuse of terminology, refer to the kernels as being compactly supported in both cases.

With the split in (
9
) inserted in (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}u^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta})&=u_{\text{far}}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta},\nu_{0})+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathcal{L}-1}u_{\text{diff},\ell}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta})+u_{\text{local}}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta},\nu_{0}/2^{\mathcal{L}})+u_{\text{self}}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta},\nu_{0})\\
&=u_{\text{far}}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta},\nu_{0})+u_{\text{compact}}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta},\nu_{0},\mathcal{L}),\end{split}

(11)

Here
u_{\text{far}}^{D\mathcal{P}}
is the smooth, root-level contribution which depends on the imposed periodicity, while
u_{\text{self}}^{D\mathcal{P}}
is a pointwise local correction. The compact part

u_{\text{compact}}^{D\mathcal{P}}
consists of the localized terms in the telescoping
split.
For periodization, the key observation is that all far-field
contributions due to periodicity are contained in
u_{\text{far}}^{D\mathcal{P}}
. The compact part requires only nearby periodic images, since all its interactions are restricted to local neighborhoods.
The individual terms in (
11
) are described in
sections

2.3
and
2.5

2.2
Window functions and PSWF-based splits

We now briefly review kernel-splitting and the use of prolate spheroidal wave functions (PSWFs).
Let
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(0)=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\phi}^{(n)}(0)=0\quad\text{for $n$ odd}.

In

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}^{c}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}\psi_{n}^{c}(t)e^{ictx}\dif t,\quad n\geq 0,\>c>0,\>x\in[-1,1].

\displaystyle{\widehat{\psi}_{0}^{c}}(k)=\lambda_{0}^{c}{\psi_{0}^{c}}(k/c),\quad|k|\leq c.

It is well-known that a function cannot be compactly supported in both

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\frac{{\psi_{0}^{c}}(x)}{\lambda_{0}^{c}{\psi_{0}^{c}}(0)}=\frac{{\psi_{0}^{c}}(x)}{\int_{-1}^{1}{\psi_{0}^{c}}(t)\dif t},\quad|x|\leq 1.

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(k)=\frac{{\widehat{\psi}_{0}^{c}}(k)}{{\widehat{\psi}_{0}^{c}}(0)}=\frac{{\psi_{0}^{c}}(k/c)}{{\psi_{0}^{c}}(0)},\quad|k|\leq c.

(18)

The error due to this truncation decreases exponentially with the

(19)

In the numerical experiments,

2.3
Evaluation of contributions from the residual and difference kernels

Consider the four parts in the sum (
11
).
As was stated in
remark

1
, it holds that the scaled residual kernel
{K_{R}}(\bm{x},\nu)=0
, for
|\bm{x}|>\nu
. The residual kernel contains the original singularity at the origin, and with an appropriate scaling, the evaluation of the local contribution

\displaystyle u_{\text{local}}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta},\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{\bm{p}\in P_{D\mathcal{P}}}^{*}{K_{R}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}+\bm{p},\nu)\rho_{\alpha}.

(20)

is done with a direct summation in a local neighborhood of
{\bm{x}}_{\beta}
.

Now, consider the contribution from the difference kernel at level
\ell
,

\displaystyle u_{\text{diff},\ell}^{D\mathcal{P}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{\bm{p}\in P_{D\mathcal{P}}}^{*}K_{D_{\ell}}({\bm{x}}_{\beta}-{\bm{x}}_{\alpha}+\bm{p})\rho_{\alpha}.

(21)

The difference kernel
K_{D_{\ell}}(\bm{x})={K_{M}}(\bm{x},\nu_{0}/2^{\ell+1})-{K_{M}}(\bm{x},\nu_{0}/2^{\ell})
is defined in (
10
). Since
K_{D_{\ell}}
is smooth and rapidly decaying in Fourier space, we evaluate the sum using its Fourier transform. We therefore write

\displaystyle K_{D_{\ell}}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\widehat{K}_{D_{\ell}}({\bm{k}})e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\dif{\bm{k}}.

(22)

This representation is useful because it expresses translations in terms of plane waves, which are used to form the expansion in the DMK algorithm.

Let
K_{\mathrm{max}}
denote the effective Fourier cutoff of the selected kernel split to the prescribed tolerance
\varepsilon
. For a PSWF-based split, this cutoff is the PSWF bandlimit, so that
K_{\mathrm{max}}=c
, with

chosen e.g. according to (
19

Since
{K_{D}}_{\ell}
contains the finer mollified scale
\nu_{\ell+1}
, the level-dependent cutoff is
K_{\ell}=K_{\mathrm{max}}/\nu_{\ell+1}
. Thus the Fourier integral (

[-K_{\ell},K_{\ell}]^{3}
.
We approximate this truncated integral
by the trapezoidal rule. Let
\mathcal{I}_{n_{f}}=\{-n_{f},\ldots,n_{f}\}^{3}
, and
\bar{K}_{F}=2n_{f}+1
denote the total number of Fourier grid points per coordinate direction. With
\bm{k}=h_{\ell}\bm{m}
, we use

\displaystyle K_{D_{\ell}}({\bm{x}})\approx\frac{h_{\ell}^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{I}_{n_{f}}}\widehat{K}_{D_{\ell}}(h_{\ell}\mathbf{m})e^{ih_{\ell}\mathbf{m}\cdot{\bm{x}}}.

The relevant real-space range is determined by the support of
{K_{D}}_{\ell}
. At level
\ell

. Hence the range of
{\bm{x}}
is halved when
\ell
is increased by one. At the same time,
K_{\ell}=K_{\mathrm{max}}/\nu_{\ell+1}=2(K_{\mathrm{max}}/\nu_{\ell})
, so the Fourier interval doubles from one level to the next.
The oscillations in the truncated integral are due to the phase factor
e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
, which retains the same range independent of level
\ell
.
Following
[
26
, Section 3.2]
, we require

to avoid aliasing. We use the limiting value
h_{\ell}=(2\pi)/(3\nu_{\ell})
, and hence
n_{f}=\lceil K_{\ell}/h_{\ell}\rceil=\lceil 3K_{\mathrm{max}}/\pi\rceil
.
With this,
n_{f}
, and therefore
\bar{K}_{F}=2n_{f}+1

\ell

The self-interaction correction

In (

As stated in (

For the electrostatic case, with the mollified kernel

The self-interaction terms for the Stokes kernels can be found in
appendix

The DMK framework

We here briefly describe the original DMK algorithm for free-space
[
26
]
and for a triply periodic cube
[
3
]
. This will be necessary before we can derive its extension to rectangular cuboids and arbitrary periodicities in
sections

4
and
5
, respectively.

3.1
Steps of the algorithm

We summarize the algorithm in five steps.

3.1.1
Octree construction

The sources and targets
are first embedded in a box of equal sides. This is then
subdivided into an octree, where each parent box is recursively
subdivided into 8 children, until each leaf node contains at most
n_{s}

sources. It is then ensured that the tree is
level-restricted
2
2

2

Also called
2:1 balanced
.
; that
is, any two leaf nodes that share a boundary point differ by at most
one refinement level. Each box has a list of neighboring boxes, and on
the triply periodic cube these lists are extended by periodic
wrapping, after which level-restriction is enforced again. Of special
importance is the concept of a
colleague
of a box
\mathcal{B}
,
which is any box at the same refinement level as
\mathcal{B}
that
shares a boundary point with
\mathcal{B}
(this includes
\mathcal{B}
itself).

3.1.2
Upward pass: construction of proxy charges

Every box
on every level is covered by a
p\times p\times p
grid of
first-kind Chebyshev points. Starting at the leaf nodes and going up
the tree,
proxy charges
are formed at the grid nodes in each box through so-called anterpolation, see
[
26
, Section 2.2]
. On level
\ell
of the tree, these proxy charges will by construction reproduce the field of the original sources in the box
to a prescribed precision
\varepsilon
, for the (locally smooth)
difference kernel
K_{D_{\ell}}(\bm{x})
.

3.1.3
Top level: far-field interactions

At the root level,
\ell=0
, we need to compute
u_{\text{far}}^{D\mathcal{P}}
in
(
11
), based on the mollified kernel
{K_{M}}
. In free-space, the mollified kernel is truncated to the domain size and evaluated as a Fourier integral
[
44
]
. In periodicity, the lattice sum over all periodic boxes is evaluated as a Fourier series as in (
25
). The sources are the root-level proxy points, and the potential is evaluated on the Chebyshev grid.

3.1.4
Downward pass: evaluation of difference kernels

Starting at
\ell=0
and going down in the tree, the
p^{3}
proxy points in
each box are used to compute local,
\bar{K}_{F}^{3}=(2n_{f}+1)^{3}
-term Fourier expansions of the
potentials based on the difference kernel
K_{D_{\ell}}
. For each box

\mathcal{B}
, the contributions to
u_{\text{diff},\ell}^{D\mathcal{P}}

are limited to the colleagues of
\mathcal{B}
, see
fig.

1
. The potential is evaluated on the Chebyshev grid
by translating and accumulating the local Fourier expansions. In a
coarse-to-fine sweep, the accumulated potentials are represented as
Chebyshev expansions, which are shifted and rescaled into the
children at level
\ell+1
.

3.1.5
Leaf level: residual interactions

At each leaf node, the accumulated Chebyshev expansion of the smooth
potential is evaluated at each target location. The remaining kernel
interactions governed by
{K_{R}}({\bm{x}},\nu/2^{\mathcal{L}})
are localized
to nearest neighbor boxes and also evaluated directly at each target.

3.2
Computational complexity

In the algorithm, interaction with source and target points only
happens on the leaf level, which is necessary for achieving linear
scaling. Note that
\bar{K}_{F}
and
p
are constant on each level, and hence
the work required per box to evaluate the difference kernel
contribution is independent of the level.
A detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the algorithm can be found in
[
26
, Section 3.5.1]
. In simplified form, it can be written as

\displaystyle C(N,n_{s},p,n_{f})=O\left(Nn_{s}+p^{3}N+p^{4}+p\bar{K}_{F}^{3}\right).

(29)

An important feature contributing to the speed of the algorithm is that both the Fourier grid and the Chebyshev grid have tensor-product structure. This structure can be exploited in all operators acting on and between such grids through
sum factorization
. For example, applying the Kronecker-product operator

A\otimes A\otimes A
, with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, to a tensor
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
has a naive cost of
O(m^{3}n^{3})
, but sum factorization reduces it to

\displaystyle O(mn^{3}+m^{2}n^{2}+m^{3}n).

(30)

4
DMK for cuboids in triple periodicity

In this section, we introduce a DMK algorithm for triply periodic
boundary conditions on a rectangular cuboid. The modifications needed for a problem with reduced
periodicity will be discussed in
section

5
.
Our periodization of DMK relies on the following two key features of
the original algorithm, as formulated in
section

3
:

•

The hierarchical subdivision of the domain into a tree, with proxy points representing the sources at each level.

•

The coupling between the multilevel summation summation and the tree,
such that the difference kernels and the residual kernel are localized
to the nearest neighbor boxes on their levels,

\supp(K_{D_{\ell}})=r_{\ell}
and
\supp({K_{R}}({\bm{x}},\nu_{0}/2^{\mathcal{L}}))=r_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Due to the above features, it is only the mollified kernel
{K_{M}}

that mediates the contribution from distant particles (including
infinity) in a periodic setting.
At all other levels, the only effect
of periodicity is that interactions using difference kernels and the residual kernel should include periodic images of the sources inside the primary box, up to radius
r_{\ell}
.
Our periodization is therefore divided into two distinct parts, one for
compactly supported contribution, and one for the periodic far-field
contribution.

Consider a general rectangular domain (cuboid) with triply periodic

is the

6
Numerical results

We have implemented the DMK algorithm with singly, doubly and triply periodic boundary conditions for the Laplace and Stokes kernels in MATLAB. The code
[
2
]
is intended for experimentation and algorithmic development, and has not been optimized for runtime. All experiments were run on an AMD EPYC 9655P 96-core CPU. Reported timings exclude construction of the adaptive octree(s) and, unless otherwise stated, are measured as the median over 10 repetitions. In our implementation, the subdivision parameter
n_{s}
, defined as the maximum number of points in a leaf box, is set to 250 in the reported runs.
This parameter affects efficiency, but not accuracy.

The numerical experiments are organized as follows.
We first describe the parameter-selection procedure and verify that it delivers the requested accuracy. We then assess the cost of periodization in two steps: first on the unit cube, where periodic DMK is compared with the corresponding free-space DMK evaluation, and then on rectangular cuboids, where we examine how the cost of the periodic part depends on the geometry of the primary cell. Together, these experiments quantify both the overhead introduced by the periodization scheme and its dependence on the cell geometry.
All experiments in this section use the Laplace kernel, except the final one, where we demonstrate the method for a singly periodic Stokeslet problem. Additional results for the Stokes kernels are provided in
appendix

F
.

In the experiments below the primary cell has side lengths
L=[L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}]
. We consider singly periodic problems, with periodicity in the first coordinate direction; doubly periodic problems, with periodicity in the first two coordinate directions; and triply periodic problems, with periodicity in all three coordinate directions. These cases are denoted by
D=1
,
D=2
, and
D=3
, respectively.

6.1
Error control and parameter selection

The parameter choices in DMK are driven by the prescribed tolerance
\varepsilon
. We consider four such parameters: the Chebyshev order
p
, the Fourier cutoff
K_{\mathrm{max}}
associated with the kernel split, the difference-kernel Fourier grid size
\bar{K}_{F}
, and the Fourier discretization sizes used in the far-field evaluation. The order
p
controls the accuracy of the tensor-product Chebyshev proxy representation in the upward and downward passes, while
K_{\mathrm{max}}
determines the Fourier support needed to resolve the mollified and difference kernels. For PSWF-based kernel splits,
K_{\mathrm{max}}=c
, where
c
is the PSWF bandwidth introduced in
section

2.2
. The quantity
\bar{K}_{F}
denotes the number of Fourier grid points per coordinate direction used for the difference-kernel translations, so that the tensor-product grid has
\bar{K}_{F}^{3}
points. The far-field Fourier discretization depends on the periodicity and on the geometry of the primary cell. Periodic directions lead to Fourier series, whereas free directions require quadrature of Fourier integrals; see (
25
)–(
27

\bar{K}^{\mathrm{per}}_{i}
and
\bar{K}^{\mathrm{free}}_{i}
, respectively, and write
\bar{K}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{D\mathcal{P}}
for the total number of Fourier discretization points in the
D
-periodic far-field evaluation. For example, in the doubly periodic case with the first two directions periodic,
\bar{K}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{2\mathcal{P}}=\bar{K}^{\mathrm{per}}_{1}\bar{K}^{\mathrm{per}}_{2}\bar{K}^{\mathrm{free}}_{3}
. This quantity should be distinguished from
\bar{K}_{F}
, which denotes the fixed Fourier grid size used for the difference-kernel translations.

The parameters are selected as follows.
First,
K_{\mathrm{max}}
and
p
is chosen based on
\varepsilon
.
For the PSWF split, this means choosing the bandlimit
c=K_{\mathrm{max}}
large enough that the truncation error of the screening function is below
\varepsilon

Given this kernel split, the order
p
is then chosen so that the level-zero difference kernel

is interpolated to accuracy
\varepsilon

For the Stokes kernels, we use the automatic parameter-selection procedure of
[
3

error of DMK is below
\varepsilon

Once

section

2.3

Finally, the far-field discretization is selected. In the triply periodic case, the retained modes are those in

34

35

section

5.3
.
The zero mode in the periodic variables is always evaluated using the truncated Green’s function. For nonzero periodic modes, we choose between the original and truncated Fourier symbols using the Nyquist criteria and the error estimates in
appendix

E

5.2

7
Conclusions

We have developed an extension of the dual-space multilevel
kernel-splitting (DMK) framework to rectangular cuboids with periodic
boundary conditions in one, two, or three coordinate directions. The
method exploits the multilevel structure of DMK, where a telescoping
kernel split localizes all interactions below the root level, so that
the global contribution of the periodic lattice is confined to the
smooth root-level far field. The localized interactions are evaluated
on a cubical tiling of the primary cell, together with periodic
wrapping of near-neighbor interactions and, when the tiling is not
exact, a bounded domain extension populated by periodic source copies.

Periodicity is imposed on the root-level far field, which is evaluated
in Fourier space. For reduced periodicity, truncated kernels are used
to regularize the singular and near-singular modes that arise in the
non-periodic directions. This allows the Fourier integrals to be
discretized using the uniform trapezoidal rule, yielding a common
structure for all periodicities. For large-aspect-ratio cuboids with
exact tilings, the root-level summation over cubes is accelerated
using FFTs. The corresponding geometry-dependent cost is then
O(M\log M)
, where
M
is the number of cubes tiling the domain.

Numerical experiments for the electrostatic and Stokes kernels show
that the periodization adds only a small cost to the free-space DMK
algorithm. For each periodicity, the total cost on the tested cuboids
remains close to that of the corresponding cubic
reference. Specifically, the cost breakdown shows that the runtime for
periodic problems is dominated by the localized corrections, whose
per-source cost changes little with aspect ratio. The far-field
contribution is more geometry dependent, but its cost remains only a
fraction of the localized part and therefore has little effect on the
total cost. In the large-aspect-ratio tests, FFT acceleration of the
far-field contribution keeps the per-source cost approximately
independent of aspect ratio at constant point density.
The results also confirm linear scaling
with the number of sources and targets, and show that the method
achieves the prescribed precision for the tested tolerances, kernels,
periodicities, and cuboid geometries.

The method provides a unified way of imposing periodic boundary
conditions in DMK, with only minor changes to the multilevel structure
of the original algorithm. We have focused on the harmonic and Stokes
kernels, but the proposed periodic extension is expected to apply
without significant modification to other non-oscillatory kernels for
which a kernel split is available. The implementation used for the
present work is intended for algorithmic development rather than
optimized performance, and incorporating the present extension scheme
into a high-performance DMK code is left for future work. Finally,
although only point-source potentials are considered here, the
periodization strategy should also be applicable to volume-potential
DMK implementations.

Appendix C
Truncated kernels related to the biharmonic equation

Section

5.2
described the truncated kernel modification for the harmonic kernels that that arise in the 1
\mathcal{P}
and 2
\mathcal{P}
Fourier integrals after applying Poisson summation in the periodic directions. We now collect the corresponding formulas for the biharmonic kernel. In the PDE viewpoint of
section

5.1
, these kernels are the Green’s functions of the modified Helmholtz and squared modified Helmholtz problems in (
66
)–(
67
) and (

)–(

appendix

D

We use the notation of

5.2
. Thus

denotes the number of free directions,
\alpha\geq 0
is the magnitude of the discrete periodic wavenumber determined by (
88
), and
\kappa=|{\bm{\kappa}}|
with
{\bm{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each fixed periodic mode, the harmonic problem in the free variables has Fourier symbol
\widehat{H}_{d}^{\alpha}(\kappa)=(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{-1}

\widehat{B}_{d}^{\alpha}(\kappa)=(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{-2}
, associated with the squared modified Helmholtz operator.

For
\alpha=0

\kappa=0
. For small nonzero values of
\alpha

\kappa=0

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=-\Delta_{d}+\alpha^{2}
with
\alpha>0
. Let
H_{d}^{\alpha}
and
B_{d}^{\alpha}
be the Green’s functions associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}
, respectively, so that
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}H_{d}^{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=\delta({\bm{x}})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}B_{d}^{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=\delta({\bm{x}})
,
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function.

Then

B_{d}^{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}H_{d}^{\alpha}({\bm{x}}).

(119)

Consequently,

\widehat{B}_{d}^{\alpha}(\kappa)=-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\widehat{H}_{d}^{\alpha}(\kappa).

(120)

Proof.

Differentiating
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}H_{d}^{\alpha}=\delta
with respect to
\alpha
, and using
\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=2\alpha\mathcal{I}
, gives
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(-\partial_{\alpha}(H_{d}^{\alpha})/(2\alpha))=H_{d}^{\alpha}
. Applying
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
once more gives (
119
). The identity (
120
) follows directly from (
119
) by taking the Fourier transform.

We now list the Fourier transforms of truncated scalar kernels. Let
B_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}
denote the biharmonic kernel, truncated in the free variables to
|{\bm{x}}|\leq\mathcal{R}
, analogously to (
71
). The Fourier transform of
B_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}
for
d=2
and
d=1
is:

\widehat{B}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}_{2}(\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\Big(1-\mathcal{R}J_{0}(\kappa\mathcal{R})\big(\mathcal{R}(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})K_{0}(\alpha\mathcal{R})+2\alpha K_{1}(\alpha\mathcal{R})\big)/2\\
+\kappa\mathcal{R}J_{1}(\kappa\mathcal{R})\big(\mathcal{R}(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})K_{1}(\alpha\mathcal{R})/(2\alpha)+K_{0}(\alpha\mathcal{R})\big)\Big)/(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2},&\alpha\neq 0,\\
\left(1-J_{0}(\kappa\mathcal{R})-\kappa\mathcal{R}J_{1}(\kappa\mathcal{R})/2\right)/\kappa^{4},&\alpha=0,\kappa\neq 0,\\
\mathcal{R}^{4}/64,&\alpha=0,\kappa=0,\end{array}\right.

(121)

\widehat{B}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}_{1}(\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\Big(2\alpha^{3}+\kappa e^{-\alpha\mathcal{R}}\big(\alpha\mathcal{R}\left(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2}\right)+3\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2}\big)\sin(\kappa\mathcal{R})\\
-\alpha^{2}e^{-\alpha\mathcal{R}}\big(\mathcal{R}\left(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2}\right)+2\alpha\big)\cos(\kappa\mathcal{R})\Big)/\big(2\alpha^{3}(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}\big),&\alpha\neq 0,\\
\left(1-\cos(\kappa\mathcal{R})-\kappa\mathcal{R}\sin(\kappa\mathcal{R})/2\right)/\kappa^{4},&\alpha=0,\kappa\neq 0,\\
\mathcal{R}^{4}/24,&\alpha=0,\kappa=0.\end{array}\right.

(122)

Here
J_{m}
denotes the Bessel function of the first kind and
K_{m}
the modified Bessel function of the second kind, both of order
m
. For the zero-mode
\widehat{B}^{0,\mathcal{R}}_{1}
when
\kappa\neq 0
, we have used the non-uniqueness of the one-dimensional biharmonic Green’s function to choose an even kernel that vanishes with
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-regularity at the cut-off.
This improves the decay of the Fourier transform. The limiting values at
\kappa=0
are obtained by taking the limit
\kappa\rightarrow 0
in the corresponding
\kappa\neq 0
formulas.

Remark 10
.

The formulas for
\widehat{B}^{0,\mathcal{R}}_{1}
are included in (
122
) for completeness, since it is not used in practice for the doubly periodic Stokes zero mode. As discussed in
[
7
, Appendix D]
, the biharmonic kernel can be avoided in this case. Instead, the zero-mode Stokes kernels can be written using the one-dimensional harmonic kernel together with the sign-function kernel

Z_{1}(x)=\frac{\operatorname{sgn}(x)}{4}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\widehat{Z}_{1}(\kappa)=-\frac{i}{2\kappa}.

(123)

Truncating
Z_{1}
to
|x|\leq\mathcal{R}
gives

\widehat{Z}^{0,\mathcal{R}}_{1}(\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-i\left(1-\cos(\kappa\mathcal{R})\right)/(2\kappa),&\kappa\neq 0,\\
0,&\kappa=0.\end{array}\right.

(124)

Appendix D
Truncated kernels for Stokes flow in reduced periodicity

The Stokeslet
\bm{\mathsfit{S}}
, stresslet
\bm{\mathsfit{T}}
and rotlet
\bm{\Omega}
were defined in
appendix

B
by applying differential operators to the scalar harmonic kernel
H
or biharmonic kernel
B
. The same construction is used for the truncated kernels, but with
H
and
B
replaced, in the free variables, by their truncated counterpart. Here we collect the Fourier transforms of the truncated Stokes kernels needed for the Fourier integrals in the singly and doubly periodic cases. In the discretized far-field formula (
84
), these kernels replace the generic Fourier kernel
\widehat{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}
. The zero-mode formulas are those derived in
[
7
, Appendix D]
, adapted here to the normalization of
appendix

B
.

We write each kernel in the form

\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}({\bm{r}})=\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}A(r),\qquad A(r)=\begin{cases}B(r),&\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}=\bm{\mathsfit{S}}\ \text{or}\ \bm{\mathsfit{T}},\\
H(r),&\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}=\bm{\Omega},\end{cases}

(125)

where
\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}
is defined by one of the differential operators in (
99
)–(
101
) depending on
\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}
, and with
H
and
B
defined in (
98
).

Let
D\in\{1,2\}
be the number of periodic directions and let
d=3-D
be the number of free directions. Discrete wavenumbers in periodic directions are denoted
k_{i}
, and continuous wavenumbers in free directions are denoted
\kappa_{i}
. For each fixed periodic mode, set
\alpha=|{\bm{k}}|
,
\kappa=|{\bm{\kappa}}|
with
{\bm{k}}=(k_{1},{\bm{\kappa}})=(k_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})
,
{\bm{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
k=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2}}
. The truncated scalar factors are radial in the free Fourier variables, and hence depend on
{\bm{\kappa}}
only through
\kappa
.

Define the scalar truncated kernel symbol by

\widehat{A}_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa)=\begin{cases}\widehat{B}_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa),&\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}=\bm{\mathsfit{S}}\ \text{or}\ \bm{\mathsfit{T}},\\
\widehat{H}_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa),&\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}=\bm{\Omega},\end{cases}

(126)

where
\widehat{H}_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}
and
\widehat{B}_{d}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}
are given in (
72
)–(
73
) and (
121
)–(
122
), respectively.

For the singly periodic case,
D=1
, we write
{\bm{k}}=(k_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})
,
\alpha=|k_{1}|
, and
\kappa=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{3}^{2}}
. The truncated Stokes kernel is defined by

\widehat{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{1\mathcal{P}}(k_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}(k_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})\widehat{A}_{2}^{|k_{1}|,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa),&k_{1}\neq 0,\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}^{H}(\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})\widehat{H}_{2}^{0,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa)+\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}^{B}(\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})\widehat{B}_{2}^{0,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa),&k_{1}=0.\end{cases}

(127)

Here
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}
is the Fourier symbol associated with
\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}
, as defined from (
102
)–(
104
).

For the Stokeslet, the coefficient matrices are

\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}^{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\kappa_{3}^{2}&-\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}\\
0&-\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}&\kappa_{2}^{2}\end{bmatrix},

(128)

For the rotlet,

\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\Omega}}^{H}=-\frac{i}{2}\begin{bmatrix}0&\kappa_{3}&-\kappa_{2}\\
-\kappa_{3}&0&0\\
\kappa_{2}&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\Omega}}^{B}=\bm{\mathsfit{0}}.

(129)

For the stresslet, the coefficients are third-order tensors. We represent them by matrices in the last two indices. That is, for each fixed first index
j
we get
(\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{H})_{lm}
and
(\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{B})_{lm}
, with
\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{H}
and
\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{B}
being symmetric
3\times 3
matrices. Write

\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{H}=i\,\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{H},\qquad\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{B}=i\,\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},j}^{B},

(130)

The harmonic parts are

\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},1}^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\kappa_{2}&\kappa_{3}\\
\kappa_{2}&0&0\\
\kappa_{3}&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},2}^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{2}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},3}^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{3}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(131)

For the biharmonic parts,
\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},1}^{B}=\bm{\mathsfit{0}}
, and

\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},2}^{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\kappa_{2}(3\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{2}^{2})&\kappa_{3}(\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{2}^{2})\\
0&\kappa_{3}(\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{2}^{2})&\kappa_{2}(\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{3}^{2})\end{bmatrix},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\mathsfit{C}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},3}^{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\kappa_{3}(\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{2}^{2})&\kappa_{2}(\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{3}^{2})\\
0&\kappa_{2}(\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{3}^{2})&\kappa_{3}(3\kappa^{2}-2\kappa_{3}^{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(132)

For the doubly periodic case,
D=2
, we write
{\bm{k}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\kappa_{3})
,
\alpha=\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}
,
\kappa=|\kappa_{3}|
, and define

\widehat{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{2\mathcal{P}}(k_{1},k_{2},\kappa_{3})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{D}}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}(k_{1},k_{2},\kappa_{3})\widehat{A}_{1}^{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa),&(k_{1},k_{2})\neq(0,0),\\[5.0pt]
\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}}^{E}(\kappa_{3})\widehat{E}_{1}^{0,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa_{3}),&(k_{1},k_{2})=(0,0).\end{cases}

(133)

Here the scalar zero-mode factor is

\widehat{E}_{1}^{0,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa_{3})=\begin{cases}\widehat{Z}_{1}^{0,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa_{3}),&\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}=\bm{\Omega}\ \text{or}\ \bm{\mathsfit{T}},\\
\widehat{H}_{1}^{0,\mathcal{R}}(\kappa_{3}),&\bm{\mathsfit{\mathcal{G}}}=\bm{\mathsfit{S}}.\end{cases}

(134)

The truncated sign-function kernel
\widehat{Z}_{1}^{0,\mathcal{R}}
is given in (
124
). Since this kernel is odd, it is evaluated at the signed wavenumber
\kappa_{3}
. The harmonic factor
\widehat{H}_{1}^{0,\mathcal{R}}
is even and is therefore written as a function of
\kappa=|\kappa_{3}|
.

The coefficient matrices for the Stokeslet and rotlet are

\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}^{E}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\Omega}}^{E}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(135)

For the stresslet, the coefficient tensor is represented, as in the singly periodic case, by three matrices in the last two indices:

\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},1}^{E}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-2\\
0&0&0\\
-2&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},2}^{E}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-2\\
0&-2&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{\mathsfit{C}}_{\bm{\mathsfit{T}},3}^{E}=\begin{bmatrix}-2&0&0\\
0&-2&0\\
0&0&-2\end{bmatrix}.

(136)

Remark 11
(Constant correction for the singly periodic Stokeslet).

The gauge choices used in the truncated singly periodic zero-mode kernels introduce an additive constant in the Stokeslet far-field contribution. The corresponding correction is zero for the stresslet and rotlet, and no such correction is needed for any of the doubly periodic zero-mode Stokes kernels

. The correction added to the singly periodic far-field term in (

) is

\bm{\mathsfit{u}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{1\mathcal{P},\text{corr}}=-\frac{1}{L_{1}}\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{1+\log\mathcal{R}}{2\pi}&0&0\\[4.30554pt]

(137)

Appendix E
Estimates for Fourier integrals in reduced periodicities

The far-field contribution in reduced periodicity, (
26
) for the
2\mathcal{P}
case and (
27
) for the
1\mathcal{P}
case, contain Fourier integrals over the free directions. Their discretization was described in
section

5.3
and this appendix gives estimates of the errors introduced.

We consider the one-dimensional model integral, representative of (
26
) and (
27
),

I_{m}(\alpha,b)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\widehat{\gamma}\left(\nu\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\right)}{(\alpha^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{m}}e^{i\kappa b}\,\dif\kappa,\qquad\alpha>0,\qquad m=1,2,

(138)

where
b
is the source-to-target separation in the free direction. We write
s=|b|
with
0\leq s\leq L_{j}
, where
L_{j}
is the domain length in the corresponding free direction. The screening function
\widehat{\gamma}
is either the harmonic screening
\widehat{\gamma}^{H}
or the biharmonic screening
\widehat{\gamma}^{B}
, defined in (
18
) and (
105
), respectively, and
\nu>0
is the splitting parameter. The case
m=1
corresponds to the harmonic Green’s function, while
m=2
is associated with the biharmonic Green’s function relevant to kernels of Stokes flow (cf. (
14
) and (
106
)). The infinite trapezoidal rule with spacing
\Delta\kappa
is

Q_{m,\Delta\kappa}(\alpha,b)=\Delta\kappa\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\widehat{\gamma}\left(\nu\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+(n\Delta\kappa)^{2}}\right)}{\left(\alpha^{2}+(n\Delta\kappa)^{2}\right)^{m}}e^{in\Delta\kappa b},

(139)

with the associated quadrature error

\mathcal{E}_{m}(\alpha,b;\Delta\kappa)=\big|I_{m}(\alpha,b)-Q_{m,\Delta\kappa}(\alpha,b)\big|.

(140)

Assuming that
\widehat{\gamma}
is analytic near the origin and normalized by
\widehat{\gamma}(0)=1
, we estimate the error, following the residue calculus in
[
37
, Theorem 2]
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(\alpha,b;\Delta\kappa)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\pi}{\alpha}\frac{\cosh(\alpha s)}{E},

(141)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}(\alpha,b;\Delta\kappa)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\pi}{\alpha^{3}}\bigg|\frac{\cosh(\alpha s)-\alpha(s-\alpha C_{\widehat{\gamma}})\sinh(\alpha s)}{E}+\frac{2\pi\alpha(E+1)\cosh(\alpha s)}{\Delta\kappa E^{2}}\bigg|,

(142)

where

E=e^{2\pi\alpha/\Delta\kappa}-1,\qquad C_{\widehat{\gamma}}=\nu^{2}\widehat{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(0).

(143)

The estimate for
m=1
is the analogue of the estimate in
[
39
, Appendix B]
, here written for a more general screening function. The estimate for
m=2
is obtained by the same residue calculus for the corresponding double-pole case.

Remark 12
.

The estimates are written in terms of
s=|b|
, since the model integral and the symmetric infinite trapezoidal rule are even functions of the separation

b
. For
m=1
, (
141
) is monotone in
s
. For
m=2
, monotonicity is less clear, but numerical maximization over
s\in[0,L_{j}]
indicates that the largest value is attained at the endpoint. Thus, in both cases we use
s=L_{j}
for the corresponding free direction.

Remark 13
.

The constant
C_{\widehat{\gamma}}
depends only on the screening function. For the harmonic and biharmonic screening functions we find
C_{\widehat{\gamma}^{H}}=\nu^{2}\widehat{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(0)
and
C_{\widehat{\gamma}^{B}}=0
, where
\widehat{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(0)
can be evaluated as described in
remark

2
.

In the
2\mathcal{P}
case there is one free Fourier direction. For
(k_{1},k_{2})\neq(0,0)
, we use

\mathcal{E}_{m}^{2\mathcal{P}}(k_{1},k_{2};\Delta\kappa_{3})=\mathcal{E}_{m}\!\left(\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}},L_{3};\Delta\kappa_{3}\right),\qquad m=1,2.

(144)

In the
1\mathcal{P}
case there are two free directions, resulting in a two-dimensional integral in
(\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})
. We estimate this two-dimensional quadrature error by the sum of two one-dimensional estimates, one for each free direction:

\mathcal{E}_{m}^{1\mathcal{P}}(k_{1};\Delta\kappa_{2},\Delta\kappa_{3})=\mathcal{E}_{m}(|k_{1}|,L_{2};\Delta\kappa_{2})+\mathcal{E}_{m}(|k_{1}|,L_{3};\Delta\kappa_{3}),\qquad k_{1}\neq 0.

(145)
