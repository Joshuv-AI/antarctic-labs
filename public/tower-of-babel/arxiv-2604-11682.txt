Semilocalization for inhomogeneous random graphs
Thomas Buc–d’Alché, Antti Knowles

Abstract
We analyse the eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix of a random inhomogeneous graph constructed from a specified degree sequence. We assume that the empirical degree sequence has bounded mean and variance. We show that near the edges of the spectrum, the eigenvectors are semilocalized in the sense that their mass concentrates around a small set of resonant vertices. For the extremal eigenvalues, we establish localization around a single vertex. In order to obtain effective estimates in the presence of highly inhomogeneous degrees, we introduce a new economical pruning procedure that carefully extracts a forest from the original graph, whose adjacency matrix is compared to that of the original graph using a suitably constructed local coupling to random trees with independent edges.



1. Introduction

1.1.  Overview

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph on the vertex set
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
. We are interested in the geometric structure of the eigenvectors of
A
, in particular their
spatial localization
. An
\ell^{2}
-normalized eigenvector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}=(q_{x})_{x\in[N]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
gives rise to a probability measure
x\mapsto q_{x}^{2}
on the set of vertices
[N]
. Informally,
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
is
delocalized
if its mass is approximately uniformly distributed throughout
[N]
, and
localized
if its mass is essentially concentrated on a small number of vertices.

In this paper we study the spatial localization of eigenvectors for a general inhomogeneous random graph model from
Hof 16
;
BJR 07
;
CL 02
, where the typical degree of a vertex is of order one, but these degrees can be of very different sizes. They are constructed from a specified degree sequence, which we assume to have finite first and second empirical moments; see (
1.1
) below. Our result applied when the tail of the empirical degree sequence is no lighter than exponential. In particular, we consider graphs whose empirical degree distribution is exponential or has a power law behaviour (so-called scale-free graphs).

Such inhomogeneous graphs differ substantially from their homogeneous counterpart, the Erdős-Rényi graph. Heuristically, homogeneous random graphs are expected to exhibit random matrix behaviour such as delocalized eigenvectors. For the Erdős-Rényi graph, the delocalized region is well understood
ADK21a
;
ADK 22
;
HKM 19
;
EKYY 13
; it corresponds essentially to the regime where the expected degree is at least of logarithmic size. In contrast, if the graph becomes too inhomogeneous, this random matrix behaviour breaks down and one expects eigenvectors to localize. For the Erdős-Rényi graph, such localized behaviour has been investigated in
ADK21a
;
ADK 24
;
ADK 23
;
HM 25
. In particular, it was shown in
ADK21a
that eigenvectors associated with eigenvalues near the spectral edges are
semilocalized
, which means that their mass is concentrated on a vanishing fraction of the total number of vertices. More precisely, a semilocalized eigenvector has its mass concentrated around a small number of
resonant vertices
, whose local energy is close to the associated eigenvalue, and around which the distribution of mass is radial and exponentially decaying.

An important motivation for our work stems from the localization-delocalization transition for disordered quantum systems, whereby the adjacency matrix
A
is interpreted as the Hamiltonian of a free quantum particle hopping in the random geometry defined by the graph. This transition is an example of an
Anderson transition
, where a disordered quantum system exhibits localized or delocalized states depending on the disorder strength and the location in the spectrum, corresponding to an insulator or conductor, respectively. Originally proposed in the 1950s
And 58
to model conduction in semiconductors with random impurities, this phenomenon is now recognized as a general feature of wave transport in disordered media, and is one of the most influential ideas in modern condensed matter physics
LR 85
;
EM 08
;
LVTW 09
;
Abr 10
. It is expected to occur in great generality whenever linear waves, such as quantum particles, propagate through a disordered medium. One expects localization to occur for strong enough disorder, or inhomogeneity, of the system and for eigenvalues close enough to the spectral edge.

The main result of this paper is a proof of semilocalization for general inhomogeneous random graphs and eigenvectors associated with eigenvalues near the spectral edge. In addition, we prove a stronger localization result around a single vertex for eigenvectors associated with the extremal eigenvalues. This shows that the phenomenon is not tied to the homogeneous, mean-field law of the Erdős-Rényi graph, for which it was established in the works
ADK21a
;
ADK 24
;
ADK 23
;
HM 25
cited above.

A fundamental difference between this paper and the homogeneous result of
ADK21a
on the Erdős-Rényi graph is the source of the inhomogeneity. In
ADK21a
, the inhomogeneity arises from a lack of concentration of the degrees in the regime where the typical degree is of order
\log N
. In contrast, in this paper, the inhomogeneity arises from the inhomogeneity of the degree sequence in the regime where the typical degree is of order 1. Our results are effective as soon as the largest degree is much larger than
\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
, which is the order of the largest degree in the Erdős-Rényi graph at fixed expected degree. In that sense, our results exploiting inhomogeneities of the degree sequence are sharp up to a constant.

We conclude this overview with a brief sketch of the new ideas of our proof. Obtaining sharp control on the optimal scale
\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
in the presence of highly inhomogeneous degrees requires several fundamental changes in the argument. This is also manifested in structure of the semilocalization profile vectors
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\pm}(x)
defined in
Proposition

3.3
below, which are no longer spherical, but incorporate a hierarchical structure among the the neighbours and their neighbours, arising from the presence of vertices whose degrees may of a different order of magnitude. In order to deal with such a large range of vertex degrees, we develop a new pruning procedure of the graph, based on so-called down-up paths (see
Definition

2.3
). This results in a more economical pruning procedure than the ones employed in
ADK21a
;
ADK 24
;
ADK 23
, which would remove too many edges when applied to highly inhomogeneous graphs. This pruning procedure results in a carefully extracted global forest, unlike the locally tree-like pruned graphs from
ADK21a
;
ADK 24
;
ADK 23
. The key tool to analyse the spectrum of this forest is a coupling to a random tree, whose edges, unlike those of the forest obtained from the pruning, are independent, and which contains balls of small enough radius of the original forest. This coupling allows us to obtain sufficiently strong estimates on the operator norm of the error resulting from the pruning. In this latter step, we bypass the arguments of
ADK21a
;
ADK 24
;
ADK 23
relying on spectral bounds on the nonbacktracking matrix and Ihara-Bass-type formulas for norm estimates, which are not effective in the inhomogeneous setting.

.

1.2.  Model and assumptions

Let
G
be a graph on the vertex set
[N]
, whereby we identify
G
with its set of edges. It is characterized by its adjacency matrix
A=(A_{xy})_{x,y\in[N]}
, where
A_{xy}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x,y\}\in G}
. We only consider simple graphs, so that
A_{xx}=0
for all
x\in[N]
. We write
x\sim y
whenever
A_{xy}=1
. We endow
G
with the usual graph distance: the distance between
x
and
y
is the number of edges in the shortest path in the graph joining
x
and
y
. For
x\in[N]
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
B_{r}(x)
the ball of radius
r
around
x
, i.e. the set of vertices at distance at most
r
from
x
, as well as by
S_{r}(x)
the sphere of radius
r
around
x
, i.e. the set of vertices at distance
r
from
x
. We denote by
D_{x}=\sum_{y\in[N]}A_{xy}
the degree of
x
.

In this paper we consider random graphs
G
whose law is given by the Generalized Random Graph (GRG) model (see e.g.
(
Hof 16
, Chapter 6)
): the family
(A_{xy}\vcentcolon x<y)
is independent with

\mathbb{P}(A_{xy}=1)=p_{xy}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\frac{w_{x}w_{y}}{\sum_{z}w_{z}+w_{x}w_{y}}\,,

(1.1)

where
(w_{x})_{x\in[N]}\in(0,\infty)^{N}
is a family of strictly positive weights.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we define the empirical moment

m_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}w_{x}^{k}\,.

(1.2)

We fix two constant exponents
0<\varepsilon<1/2
and
0<\delta<1/3
and make the following two assumptions on the weights
(w_{x})
.

Assumption 1.1
.

For all
x\in[N]
,

w_{x}\leqslant N^{1/2-\varepsilon}\,.

Assumption 1.2
.

The first and second empirical moments satisfy

m_{1}\geqslant N^{-\varepsilon}\,,\qquad\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}=O\Bigl((\log N)^{\delta}\Bigr)\,.

Remark 1.3
.

Another commonly used model of an inhomogeneous random graph is the Chung-Lu model
CL 02
, where, instead of (
1.1
), we set

\mathbb{P}(A_{xy}=1)=\tilde{p}_{xy}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\frac{w_{x}w_{y}}{\sum_{z}w_{z}}\wedge 1\,.

It follows immediately from Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
that
p_{xy}=\tilde{p}_{xy}(1+O(N^{-\varepsilon}))
,
so that, in the regime that we consider, the GRG and Chung-Lu models are asymptotically equivalent. In particular, all of our results easily carry over to the Chung-Lu model.

Remark 1.4
.

A natural way to construct the weights
(w_{x})
is to choose a fixed probability measure
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
and to choose the weights as either (i) independent random variables with law
\mu
or (ii) the
(N+1)
-quantiles of
\mu
. We give two standard examples of
\mu
, to which our main result is applicable.

Example 1.5

(Power law)
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a power law with exponent
\alpha>2
. That is,

\mu([t,\infty))=L(t)\,t^{-\alpha}\,,

(1.3)

where
L
is a slowly varying function, i.e. there exists
u_{0}>0
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{L(tu)}{L(u)}=1\quad\text{for all }u\geqslant u_{0}\,.

It is easy to check that Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
hold (in case (i) of
Remark

1.4
, they hold with high probability). For instance, for
\mu([t,\infty))=(c/t)^{\alpha}\wedge 1
for some
c>0
, the quantile weights from
Remark

1.4
(ii) are given by

w_{x}=c\biggl(\frac{N+1}{x}\biggr)^{1/\alpha}\,.

(1.4)

If the weights
(w_{x})
have a power law distribution with exponent
\alpha
, then with high probability so do the degrees
(D_{x})
of the random graph; see e.g.
(
Hof 16
, Theorem 6.12)
. Hence, the choice (
1.3
) yields random graphs with power-law degree distribution (often also called scale-free graphs).

Example 1.6

(Exponential distribution)
.

Let
\mu
be the exponential distribution with parameter
\alpha>0
. Then Assumptions

1.1
and
1.2
hold (in case (i) of
Remark

1.4
, they hold with high probability). In case (ii) of
Remark

1.4
, the weights are

w_{x}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\frac{N+1}{x}\,,

(1.5)

for
x\in[N]
.

Remark 1.7
.

The condition from
1.1
ensures that every edge in the graph has a probability
o(1)
of being included in the graph. Without it, the graph may exhibit a large subset
S
of vertices, such that
p_{xy}\asymp 1
for all
x,y\in S
. This would lead to the presence of large cliques, to which our analysis does not apply without significant modifications.

We conclude this section with the following quantitative notion of high probability that we use throughout the paper.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
\nu>0
be a constant. An event
E\equiv E_{N}
holds with
\nu
-high probability
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all
N
,

\mathbb{P}(E)\geqslant 1-CN^{-\nu}\,.

1.3.  Semilocalization

Our main result, semilocalization for the eigenvectors of
A
, pertains to vertices whose associated energies
\sqrt{D_{x}}
lie in a interval of width
\eta>0
centered around an energy
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. We define the set of
resonant vertices
by

\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in[N]\vcentcolon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sqrt{D_{x}}-\lambda}}\right|\leqslant\eta}}\right\}\,.

Theorem 1.9

(Semilocalization)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
hold. For any
\nu>0
there exists
C_{\nu}>0
such that the following holds with
\nu
-high probability. For any normalized eigenvector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
of
A
with associated eigenvalue
\lambda
and for any
\eta\leqslant|\lambda|/2
we have

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}\langle\bm{\mathrm{q}}\mspace{2mu},\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sgn(\lambda)}(x)\rangle^{2}\geqslant 1-\frac{C_{\nu}}{\eta^{2}}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\,,

(1.6)

where
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\pm}(x)
is an explicit vector supported in
B_{2}(x)
defined in
Proposition

3.3
below.

The next result,
Proposition

1.10
, shows that in the cases of
interest, the set of resonant vertices is of negligible size compared
to the size of the graph. More precise results leading to
complete localization for the extremal eigenvalues are
discussed in
Section

5
.

Proposition 1.10
.

For any
\nu>0
there exists
C_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high
probability

\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta+1}}}\right]\vee\frac{2\nu\log N}{\log\log N}

whenever

C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}<\eta<\frac{\lambda}{2}.

(1.7)

Moreover, the expectation on the right-hand side can be estimated as follows.

(i)

In general, a second moment method yields

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta+1}}}\right]\leqslant\frac{m_{2}}{(\lambda-\eta)^{4}}N\,.

(ii)

When the weights are taken to be the quantiles of the a power law distribution, as in Example
1.5
, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta+1}}}\right]=O\biggl(\frac{N\eta}{\lambda^{2\alpha+3-\iota}}+(\log N)^{2\delta}\biggr)\,,

for any constant
\iota>0
if
\lim_{t\to\infty}L(t)=\infty
and for
\iota=0
if
L
is bounded.

(iii)

When the weights are taken to be the quantiles of the exponential distribution, as in Example
1.6
, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta+1}}}\right]=O\biggl(\frac{N}{(\alpha+1)^{(\lambda-\eta)^{2}}}+(\log N)^{2\delta}\biggr)\,.

To illustrate
Theorem

1.9
, we apply it to the two examples from
Example

1.5
and
Example

1.6
. For simplicity, we only focus on the largest eigenvalues, although analogous results hold for the smallest eigenvalues.

Example 1.11

(
Example

1.5
continued)
.

Let
w_{x}
be the
x
-th
(N+1)
-quantile of the power law distribution with exponent
\alpha>2
, given in (
1.4
). Abbreviate
t\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=N/x
and suppose that
t\gg(\log N)^{\alpha}
. By (
1.11
) and
Lemma

1.14
below,
D_{x}=c\,t^{1/\alpha}(1+o(1))
with
\nu
-high probability for any fixed
\nu>0
. By
Theorem

5.1
below, we conclude that the
x
-th largest eigenvalue satisfies
\lambda_{x}(A)=\sqrt{c}\,t^{1/2\alpha}(1+o(1))
with
\nu
-high probability. By
Theorem

1.9
and
Proposition

1.10
, we therefore conclude that the eigenvector associated with the
x
-th largest eigenvalue is semilocalized in the sense of (
1.6
), with
\eta=c^{\prime}t^{1/2\alpha}
, around at most

\frac{N}{t^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}+\log N

vertices. In particular, we obtain nontrivial semilocalization for the
O(N(\log N)^{-\alpha})
largest eigenvalues. We note that a slightly more careful analysis for for the largest eigenvalues allows one to upgrade semilocalization to complete localization (i.e. semilocalization around a single vertex), which is not expected to hold in the full semilocalized regime; see
Theorem

5.2
and
Example

5.3
below.

Example 1.12

(
Example

1.6
continued)
.

Let
w_{x}
be the
x
-th
(N+1)
-quantile of the exponential distribution with parameter
\alpha>0
, given in (
1.5
). Let
\nu>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. From (
1.11
),
Lemma

1.14
, and a union bound, we find that with
\nu
-high probability for all
x\leqslant N^{\gamma}
we have

w_{x}(1-c)\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant w_{x}(1+c)

(1.8)

provided that

\frac{4\alpha(\nu+\gamma)}{1-\gamma}<c^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\,.

(1.9)

From

below, we therefore conclude that the

-th largest eigenvalue of

is bounded from below by

b\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\sqrt{\frac{(1-c-o(1))(1-\gamma)}{\alpha}}\sqrt{\log N}\,.

Choosing
\eta=(1-1/\sqrt{2})b
, we therefore conclude from
Theorem

1.9
and
Proposition

1.10
that semilocalization in the sense of (
1.6
) with a right-hand side
1-O_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\frac{1}{\log\log N}\bigr)
holds, around at most
N^{\beta}
vertices for any

\beta>1-\log(1+\alpha)\frac{(1-c)(1-\gamma)}{2\alpha}\,,

under the constraint (

).
While this gives a nontrivial region of semilocalization for any

, the range of
\gamma
and

obtained is far from optimal and far from what

yields for this example. Indeed, to simplify the presentation, we required (

, which imposes the strong constraint (

Without this condition, the strong concentration of the degrees used above breaks down, but a more sparing analysis using the first and second moment method on the counting function of the degrees can still be applied, which yields a far larger region for semilocalization; for brevity, we do not carry this analysis out here. To conclude this example, we emphasize the importance of the optimal factor

Remark 1.13

(Relaxation of the hypothesis
\delta<1/3
.)
.

The condition
\delta<1/3
in
1.2
prevents the application of our results to graphs of high mean degree, such as
\log N
. However, we may relax the assumption that
\delta<1/3
and introduce a parameter

\beta\geqslant 1
with
\delta<\beta/3
. By using the same proof as below, we may
obtain a localization result with the term

\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
replaced by

\frac{(\log N)^{\beta}}{(\log\log N)^{\mu}}
where
\mu=1
if
\beta=1
and
any
\mu\geqslant 1
if
\beta>1
. Such an extension can for instance be used to establish semilocalization for sufficiently inhomogeneous random graphs with polylogarithmic mean degrees.

1.4.  The degrees and the weights

We conclude the introduction with a few basic facts and tools on the relationship between weights and degrees, which we shall use throughout the proofs. In the sequel, we will mainly consider
vertices of high degree. In particular, vertices with weights greater
than
c\log N
, for a fixed
c>0
, are easier to describe.

We denote by

d_{x}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathbb{E}[D_{x}]

the expectation of the degree of
x
. Using

p_{xy}=\frac{w_{x}w_{y}}{m_{1}N+w_{x}w_{y}}\leqslant\frac{w_{x}w_{y}}{m_{1}N}\,.

(1.10)

we find

d_{x}=\sum_{y\in[N]\setminus\{x\}}\frac{w_{x}w_{y}}{\sum_{z}w_{z}+w_{x}w_{y}}\leqslant w_{x}\,.

An asymptotically matching lower bound for
d_{x}
follows from
Remark

1.3
, which yields

d_{x}=\sum_{y\in[N]\setminus\{x\}}\frac{w_{x}w_{y}}{m_{1}N}\Bigl(1+O(N^{-\varepsilon})\Bigr)=w_{x}\biggl(1-\frac{w_{x}}{m_{1}N}\biggr)\Bigl(1+O(N^{-\varepsilon})\Bigr)=w_{x}\Bigl(1+O(N^{-\varepsilon})\Bigr)\,.

(1.11)

As soon as the weight of a vertex
x
is at least of order
\log N
,
the degree
D_{x}
is as well at least of order
\log N
, with
\nu
-high probability.

Lemma 1.14
.

Let
\nu>0
and
x\in[N]
. With
\nu
-high probability,

d_{x}-\sqrt{2\nu d_{x}\log N}\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant d_{x}+2\sqrt{\nu\log N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{x}\vee\frac{4\nu}{9}\log N}}\right)}\,.

Proof.

This is an application of Bennett’s inequality (see

(
BLM 13
, Theorem 2.9)
). We have for
M>0
,

\mathbb{P}(D_{x}\geqslant M+d_{x})=\mathbb{P}(D_{x}-d_{x}\geqslant M)\leqslant\exp\Biggl(-\frac{M^{2}}{2(w_{x}+M/3)}\Biggr)\,.

If we choose
M=2\sqrt{\nu\log N(w_{x}\vee(2/3)\nu\log N)}
, we get
the upper bound.

For the lower bound, we use the slightly sharper bound (see

(
Hof 16
, Theorem 2.21)
),

\mathbb{P}(D_{x}\leqslant d_{x}-\sqrt{2\nu d_{x}\log N})\leqslant\exp\Biggl(-\frac{(\sqrt{2\nu d_{x}\log N})^{2}}{2d_{x}}\Biggr)\leqslant N^{-\nu}\,.\qed

Remark 1.15

(Lower bound for
D_{x}
)
.

Lemma

1.14
implies that as soon as
D_{x}\geqslant 1

and
d_{x}\geqslant 4\nu\log N
, we have that

d_{x}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1}D_{x}\quad\text{ with $\nu$-high probability. }

Otherwise, if
D_{x}\geqslant C\geqslant 1
and
d_{x}<4\nu\log N
, we only have the crude bound

d_{x}\leqslant\frac{4\nu}{C}\log ND_{x}\,.

Hence, we have in any case

d_{x}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1}\vee\frac{4\nu}{C}\log N}}\right)D_{x}\,.

Remark 1.16

(Upper bound for
D_{x}
)
.

Lemma

1.14
implies that if
d_{x}>\frac{4\nu}{9}\log N
, we have

D_{x}\leqslant d_{x}+2\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{x}}\leqslant d_{x}+2\sqrt{\frac{9}{4}d_{x}^{2}}=4d_{x}

with
\nu
-high probability. However, if
d_{x}\leqslant\frac{4\nu}{9}\log N
, we have

D_{x}\leqslant d_{x}+\frac{4\nu}{3}\log N\leqslant\frac{16\nu}{9}\log N

with
\nu
-high probability. Hence, we have in all cases

D_{x}\leqslant 4d_{x}\vee\frac{16\nu}{9}\log N\quad\text{ with $\nu$-high probability.}

In the sequel, it will be convenient to order the vertices in terms of
their degree.

Definition 1.17
.

We define the strict order relation
\prec
on the set of vertices
[N]
as
follows. For any two vertices
x,y\in[N]
,

x\prec y\text{ if and only if }\Bigl((D_{x}<D_{y})\text{ or }(D_{x}=D_{y}\text{ and }x>y)\Bigr)\,.

We define the (random) permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
to be the
unique permutation such that

\pi(N)\prec\pi(N-1)\prec\cdots\prec\pi(2)\prec\pi(1)\,.

Note that in particular
D_{\pi(N)}\leqslant D_{\pi(N-1)}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant D_{\pi(2)}\leqslant D_{\pi(1)}
.
This order allows us to define two notions of neighborhood and degree:

\begin{split}S_{1}^{+}(x)&=\{y\in[N]\vcentcolon x\sim y,x\prec y\}\penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ D_{x}^{+}=\#S_{1}^{+}(x)\,,\\
S_{1}^{-}(x)&=\{y\in[N]\vcentcolon x\sim y,x\succ y\}\penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ D_{x}^{-}=\#S_{1}^{-}(x)\,.\end{split}

(1.12)

Thus,
S_{1}^{+}(x)
and
S_{1}^{-}(x)
partition

with
D_{x}=D_{x}^{+}+D_{x}^{-}
.

2. Pruning the graph

Similarly as in
ADK21b
;
ADK21a
, it is more convenient to work on a
pruned version of the graph. The GRG model is inhomogeneous, compared
to the Erdős-Rényi model: since the laws of the degrees in
the graph are governed by the weights
(w_{x})
, there are greater
differences of degrees in the graph. Because of this greater
heterogeneity, the pruning procedure has to be more subtle. As in the
Erdős-Rényi case, we first prune the graph to remove cycles in
small balls. Then, instead of removing all edges connecting two
vertices of high degree, we remove edges appearing in a very specific
pattern. This procedure is key to simplifying the computations in

Section

3
.

Most of this section is devoted to estimating the error in operator norm we make when working with the adjacency matrix of the prunded graph rather than the adjacency matrix
A
. This is one of the main technical difficulty of our argument.

Throughout the following we fix a constant
r\geqslant 6
. For convenience,
we will sometimes omit it from the notation.

Remark 2.1
.

The pruning of
ADK21a
(besides removing cycles) amounts to
removing all the edges between vertices of high degree. Because of the
inhomogeneity in the GRG case, this would mean cutting a number of
vertices proportional to
w_{x}
around a vertex
x
. This would
prevent us from obtaining good bounds on the error we make when
replacing the adjacency matrix of the original graph by the one of
the pruned graph.

The new pruning presented below is asymmetric, and based on the
order
\prec
introduced in
Definition

1.17
. We orient each edge

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x,y}}\right\}
in
G
from
x
to
y
if
x\prec y
. We
then remove special paths we call
down-up paths
, paths of
length 2 between vertices
x
and
z
, going through a vertex
y
so
that
y\prec x\prec z
.

In the Erdős-Rényi model, one can prune the graph so that
in a ball of small radius around any vertex, the graph is a tree and
contains at most one vertex of high degree. In the GRG model, by
removing down-up paths, we get a pruned graph which is globally a
forest. Furthermore, each connected component is a tree that is
naturally rooted at the vertex of greatest degree in the connected
component.

2.1.  The pruning procedure

We now explain precisely the pruning procedure. It produces a pruned graph
G^{{\rm p}}
. To give the construction, we
introduce notation pertaining to paths.

Definition 2.2
.

A
path

\gamma
in a graph

\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{l})

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma_{i-1},\gamma_{i}}}\right\}\in G

\gamma_{i}\neq\gamma_{j}

The set of paths

, and
its subset of simple paths is denoted by

We mentioned that we will consider a particular set of paths, the
down-up paths, which we define below.

Definition 2.3
.

A

between two distinct vertices

and

is a path

with

Consider a vertex

\begin{split}S_{1}^{\rm cyc}(x)&=\{y\in S_{1}(x)\vcentcolon\exists\gamma\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{xx}(G),l(\gamma)\leqslant 2r+1,\gamma_{1}=y\}\,,\\

(2.1)

i.e.

A down-up path.

To construct the pruned graph

, we proceed in two

around all vertices. Then, we remove the

Definition 2.4

(Pruning procedure)
.

The graph

, and then

for each

.

We denote the graph obtained after the first step by

Note that the definition of

and

We indicate by the superscript

(respectively

(respectively

in

. The event

is denoted by

Proposition 2.5

Let
\nu>0
. We define the threshold

The graph

3.

The graph

The proof of

is delayed to the end of

2.4
, and relies on the next two sections:

explains how many edges are removed when

and

2.4
explains how
many edges are removed when constructing

Remark 2.6
.

Note that we prune the graph around every vertex in the graph, and
not only those of high degree as in

2.4.  Removing the down-up paths

As explained in

, step

•

with

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x,y}}\right\}
from

.

It implies that

D_{x}^{{\rm p}}=D_{x}-\#S_{1}^{\rm cyc}(x)-\#S_{1}^{\rm du}(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{1}^{+}(x)\setminus S_{1}^{\rm cyc}(x)}}\right)-1}}\right)\vee 0\,.

We have upper bounded the second term in

2.7
.

bounds the third term, and

2.18
bounds the fourth one, as

.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\nu>0
,

, and

. With
\nu
-high
probability,

Let
x\in[N]
,

, and

. Introduce

(2.4)

Proof of

.

We start by noticing that if

the

\#S_{1}^{\rm du}(x)\leqslant D_{x}
. We thus assume that

\chi=4\nu\log\log N
and use

1.15
to get

\chi D_{x}\geqslant w_{x}
with
\nu
-high probability. This gives us

\#S_{1}^{\rm du}(x)=\sum_{y\in[N]}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}y\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x,y\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}z,D_{z}\geqslant D_{x}\geqslant D_{y}\}}\leqslant\sum_{y\in[N]}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}y\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x,y\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}z,\chi D_{z}\geqslant w_{x}\}}\,,

with
\nu
-high probability.

We now relate the quantity

to the tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}
. To

2.7
: there exists a constant

\nu
-high probability

Proposition

2.15
implies that

\#S_{1}^{\rm du}(x)\leqslant\sum_{y\in[N]}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}y\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}\bigr\}}\,.

To get the result, we are going to use Bennett’s inequality

(
BLM 13
, Theorem 2.9)
. The Bernoulli random variables

\Bigl(\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}y\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}\bigr\}}\Bigr)_{y\in[N]}

are independent, since by

the edges in

are independent. We compute

v\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\sum_{y\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}y\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}\}}}}\right]=\sum_{y\in[N]}p_{xy}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}}}\right)\,.

The union bound and Markov’s inequality yield

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}}}\right)&\leqslant\sum_{z\neq x}p_{yz}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}-\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z\bigr\}}+1+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}}}\right)\\
&\leqslant\sum_{z\neq x}p_{yz}\chi\frac{d_{z}+1+C_{\nu}}{d_{x}}\,.\end{split}

Using (
1.2
), (
1.10
), and
the crude bound
w_{x}/2\leqslant d_{x}\leqslant w_{x}
, we get

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant d_{x}}}\right)\leqslant\sum_{z}\frac{w_{y}w_{z}^{2}+(1+C_{\nu})w_{y}w_{z}}{m_{1}N}\frac{\chi}{d_{x}}\leqslant 2\chi\frac{w_{y}}{w_{x}}\frac{m_{2}+(C_{\nu}+1)m_{1}}{m_{1}}\,.

Using (
1.2
) and (

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\#S_{1}^{\rm du}&\geqslant\frac{c\nu}{1-2\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\Bigr)\\
&\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\sum_{y\in[N]}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}y\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x,y\overset{\mathcal{T}_{x}}{\sim}z,\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}_{x}}_{z}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\geqslant w_{x}\}}\geqslant\frac{c\nu}{1-2\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\Bigr)+O(N^{-\nu})\\
&\leqslant\exp\Bigl(-(v+\frac{c\nu}{1-2\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N})\ln(1+\frac{c\nu\log N}{v(1-2\delta)\log\log N})(1+o(1))\Bigr)+O(N^{-\nu})\\
&=\exp(-c\nu\log N(1+o(1)))+O(N^{-\nu})=O(N^{-\nu})\,.\qed\end{split}

&\times\mathbbmss{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists z\neq x_{1},x_{2},y_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant\chi D_{z}}}\right\}}\,.\end{split}

The indicator function on the last line can be further simplified

The last inequality has been discussed in

. This implies that with

. We thus have with
\nu
-high probability

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2},x_{3}\in[N]\\
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\text{ distinct }\end{subarray}}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2}\in[N]\\
y_{1}\neq y_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}y_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}x_{2}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}y_{2}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}x_{3}\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{y_{1}}\vee\xi_{\nu}\leqslant\chi D_{x_{2}},d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}\vee d_{x_{2}}\leqslant\chi D_{x_{3}}\bigr\}}\\
&\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}\leqslant 8\nu\log N\bigr\}}+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}>8\nu\log N\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists z\neq x_{1},y_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}}}\right\}}}}\right)\,.\end{split}

Recall that by
Proposition

2.7
, there exists

C_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high probability, we have for all

,

. We thus get

\nu
-high probability

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2},x_{3}\in[N]\\
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\text{ distinct }\end{subarray}}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2}\in[N]\\
y_{1}\neq y_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}y_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}x_{2}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}y_{2}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}x_{3}\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{y_{1}}\vee\xi_{\nu}\leqslant\chi(D_{x_{2}}^{{\rm nc}}+C_{\nu}),d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}\vee d_{x_{2}}\leqslant\chi(D_{x_{3}}^{{\rm nc}}+C_{\nu})\bigr\}}\\
&\times\Bigl(\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}\leqslant 8\nu\log N\bigr\}}+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}>8\nu\log N\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x_{1},y_{1}\overset{{\rm nc}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\Bigr)\,.\end{split}

We can now use the coupling with a rooted tree introduced in

Section

2.3
. To make notation lighter we write
\mathcal{T}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathcal{T}_{x_{1}}
.
Proposition

2.15
implies that

so up to replacing

by

we have with
\nu
-high probability:

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2},x_{3}\in V_{x_{1}}\\
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\text{ distinct }\end{subarray}}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2}\in V_{x_{1}}\\
y_{1}\neq y_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{3}\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{y_{1}}\vee\xi_{\nu}\leqslant\chi(D_{x_{2}}^{\mathcal{T}\uparrow}+C_{\nu}),d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}\vee d_{x_{2}}\leqslant\chi(D_{x_{3}}^{\mathcal{T}\uparrow}+C_{\nu})\bigr\}}\\
&\times\Bigl(\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}\leqslant 8\nu\log N\bigr\}}+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}>8\nu\log N\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x_{1},y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\Bigr)\,.\end{split}

In the sequel, we are going to use the filtration

(\mathcal{F}_{k}(x_{1}))_{k\geqslant 1}
defined in
(
2.2
).

Bounding
\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}
is based on a Chernoff-type
bound. We have for all
\lambda>0
and
k\geqslant 0
:

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\geqslant k}}\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda k}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp(\lambda\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}})}}\right]\,.

We are going to use this bound with the particular choice
\lambda=\frac{\chi^{2}}{\log N}
and
k=2\nu\bigl(\frac{\log N}{\chi}\bigr)^{2}
. Let us bound the Laplace transform in the right-hand term with this choice
\lambda=\frac{\chi^{2}}{\log N}
.
We write

\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}=\sum_{y_{1}\in V_{x}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}}^{(1)}\,,

with

\begin{split}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}&\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{3},d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}\leqslant\chi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D^{\mathcal{T}\uparrow}_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}}}\right)\bigr\}}\\
X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}^{(3)}&\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{2}\bigr\}}\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}\\
X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}^{(2)}&\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{2}\bigr\}}\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}X^{(3)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}\\
X_{x_{1}y_{1}}^{(1)}&\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}\Bigl(\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}\leqslant 8\nu\log N\bigr\}}+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{1}>8\nu\log N\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x_{1},y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\Bigr)\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}\,.\end{split}

We will consider these different random variables starting from

X^{(4)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}
and use the independence
properties of
\mathcal{T}
to estimate the Laplace transform of each of
those. In this computation, the tree structure of
\mathcal{T}
is
critical.

Let us fix
y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},x_{3}
for now, and consider

X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}
. Conditionally to

\mathcal{F}_{4}(x_{1})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{4}(x_{1})\Bigr]=1+\mathbb{P}\Bigl(y_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{3},d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}\leqslant\chi\bigl(D^{\mathcal{T}\uparrow}_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{4}(x_{1})\Bigr)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}-1}}\right).

Markov’s inequality and the fact that
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[D^{\mathcal{T}\uparrow}_{x_{3}}\Bigr]\leqslant\sum_{z}p_{x_{3}z}
give us

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{4}(x_{1})\Bigr]=1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{3}\bigr\}}\chi\frac{d_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}}{d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}-1}}\right).

We choose for
\lambda
the small value
\lambda=\frac{\chi^{2}}{\log N}
. Hence, we
have for
1<c<2
that for
N
big enough:

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}-1\leqslant c\frac{\chi^{2}}{\log N}.

This means that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{4}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{3}\bigr\}}c\frac{\chi^{3}}{\log N}\frac{d_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}}{d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}}.

Still keeping
x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
fixed, we take the product on

x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}
and condition on
\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})
.
The conditional independence property of
\mathcal{T}
gives us

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant\prod_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}\Biggl(1+cp_{y_{2}x_{3}}\frac{\chi^{3}}{\log N}\frac{d_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}}{d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}}\Biggr)\,.

Using (
1.10
), and
\ln(1+u)\leqslant u
, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})\Bigr]&\leqslant\prod_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}\Biggl(1+c\frac{\chi^{3}}{m_{1}N\log N}\frac{w_{y_{2}}w_{x_{3}}\bigl(w_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}\bigr)}{d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}}\Biggr)\\
&\leqslant\exp\Biggl(\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}c\frac{\chi^{3}}{m_{1}N\log N}\frac{w_{y_{2}}w_{x_{3}}\bigl(w_{x_{3}}+C_{\nu}\bigr)}{d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}}\Biggr)\,.\end{split}

We can make the ratio of empirical moments
m_{2}/m_{1}
appear
using (
1.2
) and get with
1.2

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})\Bigr]&\leqslant\exp\Biggl(\frac{c\chi^{3}}{\log N}\frac{m_{2}+C_{\nu}m_{1}}{m_{1}}\frac{w_{y_{2}}}{d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}}\Biggr)\\
&\leqslant\exp\Biggl(\frac{c^{2}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-\delta}}\frac{w_{y_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{y_{2}}}\Biggr)\,.\end{split}

We could replace the average degrees

d_{x_{2}}\vee d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{y_{2}}
by the weights at the cost
of adding a small constant
c
in the product. Finally, we can
linearize the exponential again and get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-\delta}}\frac{w_{y_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{y_{2}}}.

(2.7)

We are going to apply a similar argument to the one we used on

X^{(4)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}
on

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(3)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}}=1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{2}\bigr\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{3}\neq x_{1},x_{2}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}x_{3}}^{(4)}}-1}}\right).

Taking the expectation conditional to
\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})

2.7

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}^{(3)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{3}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{2}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{2}\bigr\}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-\delta}}\frac{w_{y_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{y_{2}}}.

Again, we take the product on
y_{2}\neq y_{1}
and the expectation
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})
. We get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}^{(3)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant\prod_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}\Biggl(1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-\delta}}p_{x_{2}y_{2}}\frac{w_{y_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{y_{2}}}\Biggr)\,.

Using
\log(1+u)\leqslant u
, (
1.10
), and (
1.2

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}^{(3)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]&\leqslant\exp\Bigl(\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-\delta}}p_{x_{2}y_{2}}\frac{w_{y_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{y_{2}}}\Bigr)\\
&\leqslant\exp\Bigl(\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{m_{1}N(\log N)^{1-\delta}}\frac{w_{x_{2}}w_{y_{2}}^{2}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{y_{2}}}\Bigr)\\
&\leqslant\exp\Bigl(\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-2\delta}}\frac{w_{x_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}}\Bigr)\,.\end{split}

By linearizing the exponential, we get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}X_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}^{(3)}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-2\delta}}\frac{w_{x_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}}\,.

Since we have

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}=1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{2}\bigr\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{y_{2}\neq y_{1}}X^{(3)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}}-1}}\right)\,,

We finally get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{2}\bigr\}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-2\delta}}\frac{w_{x_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}}\,.

(2.8)

We may take the product on
x_{2}\neq x_{1}
and the expectation
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})
. We get using the conditional independence of the
\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}x_{2}\bigr\}}
:

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}\lambda X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant\prod_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}\Bigl(1+p_{y_{1}x_{2}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-2\delta}}\frac{w_{x_{2}}}{w_{x_{2}}\vee w_{y_{1}}}\Bigr)\,.

Using again
\log(1+u)\leqslant u
, (
1.10
), and (
1.2
), we get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}\lambda X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant\exp\Bigl(\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}}\Bigr)\,.

Linearizing the exponential, we get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}\lambda X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}}\,.

(2.9)

The computation of the Laplace transform of
X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}

is more involved than the preceding ones. By definition, we have for
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
fixed that if
d_{1}\leqslant 8\nu\log N

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}=1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}-1\Bigr)\,.

In this case, it is straightforward to compute the expectation
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})
using (
2.9
):

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}}\,.

The more involved case is when
d_{x_{1}}>8\nu\log N
. In this case:

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}=1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x_{1},y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}-1\Bigr)\,.

At this point, we notice the union bound

\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{\exists z\neq x_{1},y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\leqslant\sum_{z\neq x_{1}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z,d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\,.

Together with

\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 4\chi d_{z}\bigr\}}\leqslant\frac{4\chi d_{z}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}
,
this allows the bound

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}\leqslant 1+\sum_{z\neq x_{1}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z\bigr\}}\frac{4\chi d_{z}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1}}X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}-1\Bigr)\,.

We first take the expectation conditionally on

\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})
and use (
2.8
)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}&\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\sum_{z\neq x_{1}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z\bigr\}}\frac{4\chi d_{z}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}\Biggl(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}z}+\lambda\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1},z}X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]-1\Biggr)\\
&\leqslant 1+\sum_{z\neq x_{1}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}z\bigr\}}\frac{4\chi d_{z}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}\Biggl(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\sum_{x_{2}\neq x_{1},z}X^{(2)}_{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}}}(1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-2\delta}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{2}(x_{1})\Bigr]-1\Biggr)\,.\end{split}

We can now take the expectation conditionally on

\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})
and use the conditional independence and
(
2.9
) to get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}&\mid\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{2})\Bigr]\\
&\leqslant 1+\sum_{z\neq x_{1}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}p_{y_{1}z}\frac{4\chi d_{z}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}\Bigl((1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}})(1+\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-2\delta}})-1\Bigr)\\
&\leqslant 1+\sum_{z\neq x_{1}}\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}p_{y_{1}z}\frac{d_{z}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}\frac{4c^{4}\chi^{4}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}}\,.\end{split}

We use (
1.10
) and (
1.2
) to get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}\frac{4c^{4}\chi^{4}}{(\log N)^{1-4\delta}}\frac{w_{y_{1}}}{d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}}\,.

Up to adding a factor
c
, we can replace
d_{y_{1}}\vee d_{x_{1}}

by
w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{x_{1}}
. We finally have

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X^{(1)}_{x_{1}y_{1}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})\Bigr]\leqslant 1+\mathbbmss{1}_{\bigl\{x_{1}\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\sim}y_{1}\bigr\}}\frac{4c^{5}\chi^{4}}{(\log N)^{1-4\delta}}\frac{w_{y_{1}}}{w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{x_{1}}}\,.

(2.10)

It remains to take the product on
y_{1}
and the expectation conditional to
\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{1})
. We consider first the case
d_{x_{1}}\leqslant 8\nu\log N
. We have by independence

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}}\Bigr]\leqslant\prod_{y_{1}}\Bigl(1+p_{x_{1}y_{1}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}}\Bigr)\,.

Using as before (
1.10
) and (
1.2
) we get

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}}\Bigr]\leqslant\exp\Bigl(w_{x_{1}}\frac{c^{3}\chi^{3}}{(\log N)^{1-3\delta}}\Bigr)\leqslant\exp\Bigl(c^{4}\nu\chi^{3}(\log N)^{3\delta}\Bigr)\,.

In the case
d_{x_{1}}>8\nu\log N
, we have by independence,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}}\Bigr]\leqslant\prod_{y_{1}}\Bigl(1+p_{x_{1}y_{1}}\frac{4c^{5}\chi^{4}}{(\log N)^{1-4\delta}}\frac{w_{y_{1}}}{w_{y_{1}}\vee w_{x_{1}}}\Bigr)\,.

We have shown in both cases that

This implies

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{x_{1}}\geqslant 2\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)=O(N^{-\nu})\,.\qed

Proof of

3. Construction of approximate eigenvectors

In this section, we construct a family of orthonormal vectors that
will be close to eigenvectors of
A
. The main intuition to show that
the eigenvectors associated to the greatest eigenvalues are
(semi-)localized, is that these eigenvectors are with high probability close to the
vectors

\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{x}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}}\,\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S^{{\rm p}-}_{1}(x)}}}\right)\,,

where
x\in[N]
and
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
. This particular
choice is motivated by the fact that such vectors are eigenvectors of
the adjacency matrix of a star of degree
D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}
, i.e. a tree
with one vertex connected to
D_{x}^{{\rm p}-}
leaves.

Notice that these vectors are normalized, but in the pruned graph

G^{{\rm p}}
, we have (recall
Definition

1.17
)

\langle\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\rho}(x)\mspace{2mu},\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(y)\rangle=\frac{\delta_{xy}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\rho\sigma}}\right)+\frac{\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x\sim y\}}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x\prec y\}}}{\sqrt{D_{x}^{{\rm p}-}}}+\frac{\sigma\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x\succ y\}}}{\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{y}}}}}\right)\,,

that is, we only have
\langle\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{-}(x)\mspace{2mu},\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{+}(x)\rangle=0
for all
x\in[N]
, but
in general
\langle\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\rho}(x)\mspace{2mu},\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(y)\rangle
is not zero for
x\sim y
.

We are going to define a family of pseudo-eigenvectors that are
supported near the vertices in
\mathcal{V}_{\nu}^{(\rm h)}
, with the greatest
degrees in the graph
G
.

Remark 3.1
.

In the pruned graph
G^{{\rm p}}
, for each vertex
x
, there is at most
one vertex in
S_{1}^{{\rm p}+}(x)
. Indeed, if it were not the case, i.e. if there existed two distinct vertices
y,z\in S_{1}^{{\rm p}+}(x)
, then
one of
(y,x,z)
or
(z,x,y)
would be a down-up path. There are
no such paths in the pruned graph.

This remark allows the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
x\in[N]
. The unique element
y\in S_{1}^{{\rm p}+}(x)
, if it
exists, is called the
parent
of
x
, and denoted by
\hat{x}
.
Conversely, the
children
of
x
are the vertices in

S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(x)
. The elements of
S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(\hat{x})
are called
the
siblings
of
x
. The set of siblings of a vertex
x
is denoted
by
\Sib(x)
and is empty if
x
has no parent. The set of smaller
siblings is

\Sib^{-}(x)=\Sib(x)\cap\{y\in[N]\vcentcolon y\prec x\}.

We use the following conventions.

1.

\frac{\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\emptyset}}{0}=0
,

2.

Any term where the symbol
\hat{x}
appears is
0
if the
vertex
x
has no parent.

The orthonormal family we shall use is defined in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3
.

For all
x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
,
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
, define

Z_{x}=2+\frac{2}{\#\Sib^{-}(x)}\mathbbmss{1}_{\{\#\Sib^{-}(x)\neq 0\}}\,,

and

\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z_{x}}}\Biggl(\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{x}+\sigma\frac{\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(x)}}{\sqrt{D_{x}^{{\rm p}-}}}-\frac{\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\Sib^{-}(x)}}{\#\Sib^{-}(x)}\Biggr)\,.

The family
(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu},\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}
is orthonormal.

The family
(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x))
is convenient as it is an orthonormal
family of vectors that are localized around the vertices of

\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
. In the sequel, we will consider the operator

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\,.

(3.1)

In fact,
Proposition

4.1
stated in the sequel implies that this
is a good approximation of
A^{{\rm p}}
restricted to the eigenvectors of
its greatest eigenvalues. The operator (
3.1
) will be further
approximated by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\,,

(3.2)

in
Proposition

3.4
. It is convenient to define
(
3.2
) as it has good geometric properties: it is the
adjacency matrix of the pruned graph restricted to the neighborhood of
the vertices in
\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}
. This geometric insight will be useful in

Section

4.2
. We now show that the matrices (
3.1
) and (
3.2
)
are similar.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\nu>0
. With
\nu
-high probability,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\Bigl(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}-\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\Bigr)}}\right\|\leqslant\sqrt{9\nu\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

We now turn to the proofs. Before showing that the vectors

({\bm{\mathrm{u}}}_{\sigma}(x))
form an orthogonal family, we explain heuristically
how their expression was found. Below, we discuss a Gram-Schmidt
orthonormalization procedure. The vector obtained by this procedure
will provide us with the ansatz that allowed us to define the

(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu},\sigma\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pm 1}}\right\}}
.

Introduce the families of vectors

(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}},(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}
, defined
by

\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x)=\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{x}\,,\quad\text{ and }\quad\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x)=\frac{\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(x)}}{\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}}\,.

In particular, we have
\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)=(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x)+\sigma\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x))/\sqrt{2}
.

The family of vectors we shall consider is the family

(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x),\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}
obtained after applying
the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization procedure on

(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x),\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}
, starting with the vectors
of
(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x))
, and ordering the vectors of
(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x))

decreasingly according to the strict order
\prec
. Working in the pruned
graph makes this procedure simpler.
Proposition

2.5
implies that
the family
(\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x))_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}
is orthonormal. Indeed, if

x\neq y
, then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(x),\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(y)}}\right>
is nonzero if
and only if there is a down-up path between
x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
and
y\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
,
and in the pruned graph there are no such paths. Thus, we only need to consider the vectors
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(x),x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
.

The vectors
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(x),x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
resulting from the
Gram-Schmidt procedure, are defined by

\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}(x)}{\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}(x)\|}\,,\qquad\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x),\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(y)}}\right>\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{1}(y)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
y\succ x\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{0}(x),\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(y)}}\right>\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{0}(y)\,.

We then have

\begin{split}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}(x)&=\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{x}-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{x},\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(y)}}}\right>}{D^{{\rm p}-}_{y}}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(y)}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\

(3.3)

Lemma 3.5
.

For all

\nu>0
. With

We introduce the notation

In this Section, we prove
Theorem

1.9
. To do so, we construct a
block-diagonal approximation
\hat{A}
of
A
, whose eigenvectors
associated with the extremal eigenvalues are
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)
for

\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
and

. A spectral gap property is then proved
for
\hat{A}
, and can be transferred to a spectral gap property for

.

4.1.  The block-diagonal approximation

We introduce some notation. We define the orthogonal
projections

\Pi^{{\rm p}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}=\Id-\Pi^{{\rm p}}\,,

(4.1)

and the block-diagonal approximation of

,

\hat{A}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}+\overline{\Pi}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}\,.

(4.2)

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\nu>0
. There exists
C_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high probability,

\|\hat{A}-A^{{\rm p}}\|\leqslant C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\hat{A}-A\|\leqslant C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

To prove
Proposition

4.1
we use the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Define for all

Proof of

.

.
Thus, there exists a constant
C_{\nu}^{\prime}>0
such that with
\nu
-high
probability,

\|A^{{\rm p}}\Pi^{{\rm p}}\|\leqslant C^{\prime}_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

The result follows from the fact that
\Pi^{{\rm p}}
and

\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}
are orthogonal projections.
∎

4.2.  Bounds on
\overline{\Pi}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}

The last step before proving
Theorem

1.9
is to bound the operator norm
of
\overline{\Pi}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}
. To do so, we use the fact that the
pruned graph is actually a forest.

Let us consider
\overline{\Pi}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}
. By definition,
see (
4.1
), we have

\overline{\Pi}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}=\hat{A}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}=(\hat{A}-A^{{\rm p}})\\
-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\Bigl(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}-\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\Bigr)+\Bigl(A^{{\rm p}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\Bigr)\,.

(4.3)

Proposition

4.1
implies that the error

\|A^{{\rm p}}-\hat{A}\|
is at most of order

\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
, and
Proposition

3.4
that
the second part is at most of order

\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
. We now prove that the third part
is of order
\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

\Biggl\|A^{{\rm p}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\Biggr\|\leqslant 2\sqrt{\xi_{\nu}}\,.

Proof.

The matrix

A^{\prime}=A^{{\rm p}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}\\
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma\sqrt{D^{{\rm p}-}_{x}}\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}

is the adjacency matrix of the graph

\tilde{G}=G^{{\rm p}}|_{(\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu})^{c}}
. The degree of the vertices of
this graph are bounded by
\xi_{\nu}
, and the graph is actually a forest by

Proposition

2.5
.
Thus, a standard estimate – see for instance
(
ADK21a
, Lemma
A.4)
– implies that

\|A^{\prime}\|\leqslant 2\sqrt{\xi_{\nu}}\,.\qed

Together with
Proposition

4.1
,
Proposition

3.4
, and (
4.3
),
Lemma

4.3
yields the following bound on

\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\|
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\nu>0
. There exists a constant
C_{\nu}>0
such that with

\nu
-high probability, we have

\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\|\leqslant C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

4.3.  Semilocalization

We now prove the main result. First, we introduce
some notation.
Let
\eta>0
and
\lambda>0
. We define the sets

\mathcal{W}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}=\Bigl\{x\in[N]\vcentcolon\bigl|\sqrt{D_{x}^{{\rm p}-}}-\lambda\bigr|\leqslant\eta\Bigr\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}=\Bigl\{x\in[N]\vcentcolon\bigl|\sqrt{D_{x}}-\lambda\bigr|\leqslant\eta\Bigr\}\,,

and the orthogonal projections

\Pi_{\lambda,\eta}^{{\rm p}}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{+}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{+}(x)^{*}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\Pi}_{\lambda,\eta}^{{\rm p}}=1-\Pi_{\lambda,\eta}^{{\rm p}}\,.

The proof of our main theorem,
Theorem

1.9
, is from now on very close
to the one of
(
ADK21a
, Theorem 3.4)
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.9
.

As in
(
ADK21a
, Theorem 3.4)
, the core of the proof is the spectral
gap property of the form

\Spec\Bigl(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus[\lambda-\eta,\lambda+\eta]\,.

Consider first the block-diagonal approximation
\hat{A}
. The
orthogonal projections
\Pi^{{\rm p}}
and
\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}
commute and we
have the inclusion property

\Pi^{{\rm p}}\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}=\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\,.

Note that we also have

\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}=\Id-\Pi^{{\rm p}}\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}=\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}+\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Pi^{{\rm p}}\,.

These properties allow us to rewrite
\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\hat{A}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}
as

\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\hat{A}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}=\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Pi^{{\rm p}}\hat{A}\Pi^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}+\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\hat{A}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\,.

(4.4)

The spectral gap property can be shown for

\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\hat{A}\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}
by showing it for each of the
two terms in (
4.4
): they are the two blocks of a block decomposition of
the operator. By definition, we immediately have

\Spec\Bigl(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Pi^{{\rm p}}\hat{A}\Pi^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus[\lambda-\eta,\lambda+\eta]\,.

For the second part,
Proposition

4.4
implies that there exists a
constant
C_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high probability,

\Spec\Bigl(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\hat{A}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\Bigr)=\Spec\Bigl(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}A^{\rm p}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\Bigr)\subset\Biggl[-\frac{C_{\nu}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,,\frac{C_{\nu}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,\Biggr]\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus[\lambda-\eta,\lambda+\eta]\,,

as we chose
\eta
such that
|\lambda\pm\eta|\geqslant|\lambda|/2>C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}/2
.

Since the two summands in (
4.4
) commute, we have proved

\Spec\Bigl(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\hat{A}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus[\lambda-\eta,\lambda+\eta]\,.

Proposition

4.1
allows to upgrade this to a spectral gap property for

\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}
: there exists

c_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high probability

\Spec\Bigl(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\Bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\Biggl[\lambda-\Biggl(\eta-c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,\Biggr),\lambda+\Biggl(\eta-c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,\Biggr)\Biggr]\,.

By convention, if
\eta-c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}>0
, then the interval
is
\emptyset
. Assume that
\eta>c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
, otherwise the
result is vacuous.

We now conclude as follows. Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue associated to
a normalized eigenvector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
, we have
(A-\lambda)\bm{\mathrm{q}}=0
. Multiplying
by
\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}
and introducing

\Id=\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}+\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}
, we get

\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}(A-\lambda)\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}(A-\lambda)\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}=0\,,

which simplifies to

(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}-\lambda)\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}=-\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}\,.

Finally, we have

\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}=-(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}-\lambda)^{-1}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}\,.

The spectral gap property and

Proposition

4.1
imply the bounds

\begin{split}\|(\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}-\lambda)^{-1}\|&\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta-c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}\\
\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}A\Pi^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\|&\leqslant\|\hat{A}-A\|\leqslant c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.\end{split}

They allow us to deduce

\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|\leqslant\frac{c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}{\eta-c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}\wedge 1\leqslant\frac{2c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}{\eta}\,,

(4.5)

which can be restated as

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta}}\langle\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\rangle^{2}\geqslant 1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c_{\nu}}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,}}\right)^{2}\,,

if
\sigma=\sign\lambda
.

To go from a result on the set
\mathcal{W}^{\rm p}_{\lambda,\eta}
to the result on
\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}
, we notice that by
Proposition

2.5
, with
\nu
-high probability,

D_{x}-\xi/2-1\leqslant D_{x}^{{\rm p}-}=D_{x}^{{\rm p}}-1\leqslant D_{x}
. Thus, assuming
\lambda,\eta\geqslant\sqrt{\xi}/2

we get

\mathcal{W}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\eta-\sqrt{\xi/2}}\subset\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\,.

(4.6)

Set
\tilde{\eta}=\eta-\sqrt{\frac{\xi}{2}}
. The inclusion (
4.6
)
implies that for all vectors
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
,

\Biggl\lVert\Biggl(\Id-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\Biggr)\bm{\mathrm{q}}\Biggr\rVert\leqslant\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\tilde{\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|\,.

where
\sigma=\sign\lambda
.

We have by (
4.5
) that with
\nu
-high probability,

\Biggl\lVert\Biggl(\Id-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{\sigma}(x)^{*}\Biggr)\bm{\mathrm{q}}\Biggr\rVert\leqslant\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}_{\lambda,\tilde{\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|\leqslant\frac{c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}{\tilde{\eta}}=\frac{c_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}}{\eta-\sqrt{\xi/2}}\,.

Note that the result is vacuous if
\eta
is not of order
\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
: either
\eta>\sqrt{\xi}
and we have reached the wanted result up to increasing the constant
c_{\nu}
, or
\eta\leqslant\sqrt{\xi}
and we can bound the left-hand term by any constant
c_{\nu}\geqslant 1
.
∎

5. The extremal eigenvalues and the localization phenomenon

We now turn to the extremal eigenvalues. We show that they are close to
the square roots of the degrees of some vertices in
G
. Furthermore,
assuming that the average degrees
d_{x}
are well separated, we
obtain the localization of some eigenvectors around single vertices.

We use the notation
\lambda_{1}(A)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(A)\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{N}(A)
for the ordered eigenvalues of
A
. Moreover, we recall the permutation
\pi
from
Definition

1.17
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high
probability, for all
i\in[N]
, if
\lambda_{i}(A)>C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\lambda_{i}(A)-\sqrt{D_{\pi(i)}}}}\right|\leqslant C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

or if
\lambda_{i}(A)<-C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}
, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\lambda_{i}(A)+\sqrt{D_{\pi(N+1-i)}}}}\right|\leqslant C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

Proof.

This is a consequence of
Proposition

4.1
. There
exists
C^{\prime}_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high probability

|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(\hat{A})|\leqslant\|A-\hat{A}\|\leqslant C^{\prime}_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,,

for all
i\in[N]
.

The
i
-th eigenvalue of
\hat{A}
satisfies

\lambda_{i}(\hat{A})=\pm\sqrt{D_{\pi(i)}^{{\rm p}-}}
if

\lambda_{i}(\hat{A})>\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\|
.
Furthermore,
Proposition

2.5
implies that with
\nu
-high probability, for
all
x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}
,

|D_{x}-D_{x}^{{\rm p}-}|\leqslant\xi/2\,.

Hence, there exists a constant
C_{\nu}
such that with
\nu
-high
probability: for all
i
such that

\lambda_{i}(\hat{A})>\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\|
,

|\lambda_{i}(A)-\sqrt{D_{x}}|\leqslant C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\,.

We have a similar result when

\lambda_{i}(\hat{A})<-\|\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}A^{{\rm p}}\overline{\Pi}^{{\rm p}}\|
.
∎

We now consider the phenomenon of localization around a single vertex,
in general a stronger result than the semilocalization. According to

Theorem

1.9
, it occurs when

\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}=1
for an appropriate pair

(\lambda,\eta)
. We fix
\nu>0
and
\eta>0
.

We introduce the set of isolated vertices:

\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in[N]\vcentcolon\begin{aligned} &\bullet\,\forall y\in[N]\setminus\{x\},|d_{x}-d_{y}|\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{x}}+4\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{y}}}}\right)\vee 16\eta^{2}\\
&\bullet\,d_{x}\geqslant\frac{4\nu}{9}\log N\\
\end{aligned}}}\right\}\,.

(5.1)

Note that while the definition of the set
\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}
is
somewhat involved, it only depends on the sequence of weights. We
shall show that the eigenvectors associated to the vertices in

\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}
are localized with
\nu
-high probability.

Theorem 5.2

(Localization)
.

There exists
C_{\nu}>0
such that with
\nu
-high
probability, for any eigenvalue
\lambda>C_{\nu}\sqrt{\log N}
of
A
, with associated
eigenvector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
, and all
\eta\leqslant\lambda/2
, we have the following property.

If
\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\cap\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}\neq\emptyset
then there exists

x\in\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}
such that

\langle\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{+}(x)\rangle^{2}\geqslant 1-\Biggl(\frac{C_{\nu}}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\Biggr)^{2}\,.

Proof.

We shall show that if

\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\cap\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}\neq\emptyset
,
then
\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}=1
.

Theorem

5.2
is then a consequence of
Theorem

1.9
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\cap\mathcal{V}^{*}_{\nu,\eta}
and

y\in\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}
. We first notice that assuming

\lambda>C_{\nu}\log N
, we get thanks to
Lemma

1.14
that

\frac{C_{\nu}}{2}\log N\ \frac{\lambda}{2}\leqslant D_{y}\leqslant d_{y}+2\sqrt{\nu\log N(d_{y}\vee\frac{4\nu}{9}\log N)}\,.

By choosing
C_{\nu}>0
big enough, we can ensure

d_{y}\geqslant\frac{4\nu}{9}\log N
. A second use of

Lemma

1.14
implies that with
\nu
-high probability,

d_{x}-d_{y}-2\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{x}}-2\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{y}}\leqslant D_{x}-D_{y}\leqslant d_{x}-d_{y}+2\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{x}}+2\sqrt{\nu\log Nd_{y}}\,.

Using (
5.1
), we have

d_{x}-d_{y}-\frac{1}{2}|d_{x}-d_{y}|\leqslant D_{x}-D_{y}\leqslant d_{x}-d_{y}+\frac{1}{2}|d_{x}-d_{y}|\,.

Notice that if
D_{x}>D_{y}
then
d_{x}\geqslant d_{y}
as otherwise we would have

D_{x}-D_{y}\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}|d_{x}-d_{y}|\leqslant 0\,,

a contradiction. We can show similarly that if
D_{x}<D_{y}
then

d_{x}\leqslant d_{y}
. Thus, we have

|D_{x}-D_{y}|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}|d_{x}-d_{y}|\,.

(5.2)

Finally, if
x\neq y
, we have by (
5.2
) and (
5.1
):

\Bigl|\sqrt{D_{x}}-\sqrt{D_{y}}\Bigr|\geqslant\sqrt{\frac{|D_{x}-D_{y}|}{2}}\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{|d_{x}-d_{y}|}}{2}\geqslant 2\eta\,.

Thus, either
x=y
or
y\notin\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}
. Hence,
\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}
is a singleton.
∎

Example 5.3
.

Consider

. The weights are chosen as the

\nu
-high probability

\|B_{3}\|^{2}\leqslant 8\nu\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}\frac{4u_{x}^{2}}{Z_{x}}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}=O\biggl(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\biggr)\,.

Finally, consider the operator

B_{4}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{.}\hbox{.}}}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}}\frac{2}{Z_{x}}\sum_{y,z\in\Sib^{-}(x)}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(y)}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{z}^{*}}{(\#\Sib^{-}(x))^{2}}\,,

and define for
x\in\mathcal{V}^{(\rm h)}_{\nu}
,

\begin{split}\bm{\mathrm{u}}(x)&=\frac{1}{\#\Sib^{-}(x)}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\Sib^{-}(x)}\\
\bm{\mathrm{v}}(x)&=\frac{1}{\#\Sib^{-}(x)}\sum_{y\in\Sib^{-}(x)}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(y)}\,.\end{split}

Notice that

B_{4}\bm{\mathrm{u}}(x)=\frac{2}{Z_{x}(\#\Sib^{-}(x))^{3}}\sum_{y,z\in\Sib^{-}(x)}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(y)}=\frac{2}{Z_{x}(\#\Sib^{-}(x))^{2}}\sum_{y\in\Sib^{-}(x)}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{S_{1}^{{\rm p}-}(y)}=\frac{2}{Z_{x}\#\Sib^{-}(x)}\bm{\mathrm{v}}(x)\,.

Furthermore,

\bm{\mathrm{v}}(x)^{*}B_{4}=\frac{2}{Z_{x}(\#\Sib^{-}(x))^{3}}\sum_{y,z\in\Sib^{-}(x)}D_{y}^{{\rm p}-}\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{z}^{*}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{Z_{x}(\#\Sib^{-}(x))^{2}}\sum_{y\in\Sib^{-}(x)}D_{y}^{{\rm p}-}}}\right)\bm{\mathrm{u}}(x)^{*}\,.

Thus, Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
, and the Schur test
imply that

\|B_{4}\|=O(1)\,.

Appendix B
Estimation of the size of
\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}

Let
\lambda,\eta>0
such that
2\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\leqslant\eta\leqslant\lambda/2
. We
consider the expectation
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]
,
which we rewrite as

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}\leqslant\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)\,.

The first part can be bounded using Bennett’s inequality
(
BLM 13
, Theorem 2.9)
:

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D_{x}\geqslant(\lambda-\eta)^{2}}}\right)\leqslant\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-(\lambda-\eta)^{2}\log(\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}}}\right)}{d_{x}})-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda-\eta}}\right)^{2}+d_{x}}}\right)=O(N^{-2})\,,

so that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)+O(N^{-1})\,.

We start be recalling the following Lemma of approximation of the
degrees by Poisson variables.

Lemma B.1

(Approximation of degrees by a Poisson variable
(
Hof 16
, Theorem 6.7)
)
.

There exists a coupling
(\hat{D}_{x},\hat{P}_{x})
of the degree

D_{x}
of vertex
x
and a Poisson variable
x
with parameter

w_{x}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{D}_{x}\neq\hat{P}_{x}}}\right)\leqslant\frac{w_{x}^{2}}{m_{1}N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+2\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}}}\right)\,.

This Lemma will be key in estimating
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]

for some
\lambda,\eta>0
. Indeed, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)+O(N^{-1})\\
&\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant\hat{P}_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)+\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+2\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}}}\right)+O(N^{-1})\,.\end{split}

By
1.2
, the last term is of order at most
(\log N)^{2/3}
.
The first term can be written in term of incomplete Gamma functions

\Gamma(s,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\,.

Indeed, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant\hat{P}_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma(U_{N},w_{x})}{\Gamma(U_{N})}-\frac{\Gamma(L_{N}-1,w_{x})}{\Gamma(L_{N}-1)}}}\right)\,,

where we set for convenience
L_{N}=\lfloor(\lambda-\eta)^{2}\rfloor
and
U_{N}=\lceil(\lambda+\eta)^{2}\rceil
.

We shall use the two following properties of incomplete Gamma
function.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
s\geqslant 1
and
x>0
. Then,
\Gamma(s)-x^{s-1}\leqslant\Gamma(s,x)\leqslant\Gamma(s)
. Furthermore,

\Gamma(s,x)\sim x^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}
as
x\to\infty
.

Proof.

It is immediate that
\Gamma(s,x)\leqslant\Gamma(s)
. For the other bound, we have

\Gamma(s,x)=\Gamma(s)-\int_{0}^{x}t^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\geqslant\Gamma(s)-x^{s-1}\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\Gamma(s)-x^{s-1}\,.

To prove the asymptotic estimate, we remark that

\frac{\Gamma(s,x)}{x^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}}=\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{x}}}\right)^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-x)}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{x}+1}}\right)^{s-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\,.

The dominated convergence theorem then implies that the limit of the left-hand term is
1
as
x\to\infty
.
∎

We kept in the sum only terms
x
such that
w_{x}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
, hence by
Lemma

B.2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[N]\\
w_{x}>\sqrt{\log N}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}^{-w_{x}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{w_{x}^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}-\frac{w_{x}^{L_{N}-2}}{(L_{N}-2)!}}}\right)(1+o(1))+O((\log N)^{2\delta})\,.

We now consider two cases:

•

The weights
(w_{x})
are the
(N+1)
-quantiles of an
exponential law as in
Example

1.6
.

•

The weights
(w_{x})
are the
(N+1)
-quantiles of a power law as in
Example

1.5
.

In the exponential case, we then have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]\leqslant N\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t-t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}-\frac{t^{L_{N}-2}}{(L_{N}-2)!}}}\right)\mathrm{d}t(1+o(1))+O((\log N)^{2\delta})\,.

Using that the
k
-th moment of an exponential law of parameter

\alpha+1
is
k!/(\alpha+1)^{k}
we get

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]\leqslant N\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)^{L_{N}-2}}-\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)^{U_{N}-1}}}}\right)(1+o(1))+O((\log N)^{2\delta})\,.

That is,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]\leqslant N\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha+1)^{\lfloor(\lambda-\eta)^{2}\rfloor-1}}(1+o(1))+O((\log N)^{2\delta})\,.

(B.1)

In the power law case, we denote the power law measure by
\mu
and recall that

\mu([t,+\infty))=L(t)t^{-\alpha},

where
L
is a positive, slowly varying function. We notice that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}\mu(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}L(t)t^{-\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t^{U_{N}-2}}{(U_{N}-2)!}-\frac{t^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\,.

Then, for all
\iota>0
, there exists
C>0
such that

0\leqslant L(t)\leqslant Ct^{\iota}
. Note that if
L
is bounded we can choose

\iota=0
. We observe that on
(0,\frac{U_{N}-2}{\mathrm{e}})
, the
integrand is positive. Hence, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}L(t)t^{-\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t^{U_{N}-2}}{(U_{N}-2)!}-\frac{t^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t^{U_{N}-\alpha-2+\iota}}{(U_{N}-2)!}-\frac{t^{U_{N}-\alpha-1+\iota}}{(U_{N}-1)!}}}\right)+O\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{U_{N}-2}{2}}\Bigr),

where the error
O(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{U_{N}-2}{2}})
is a bound for
the integral on
(\frac{U_{N}-2}{\mathrm{e}},+\infty)
, with and without

L
. Finally, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}\mu(t)\leqslant C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma(U_{N}-\alpha-1+\iota)}{\Gamma(U_{N}-1)}-\frac{\Gamma(U_{N}-\alpha+\iota)}{\Gamma(U_{N})}}}\right)\,.

Lemma B.3

(Stirling’s approximation)
.

Let
t>0
. We have as
x\to\infty

\frac{\Gamma(x-t)}{\Gamma(x)}=\frac{1}{x^{t}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-t}{2x}+o(1/x)}\,.

Proof.

Stirling’s approximation to the first order,

\log\Gamma(x)=x\log x-x+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{2\pi}{x}+\frac{12}{x}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{x^{2}}\Bigr)\,,

gives

\begin{split}\log\frac{\Gamma(x-t)}{\Gamma(x)}&=-t\log x-(x-t)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{x}+o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x}}}\right)}}\right)+t+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{x}+o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x}}}\right)}}\right)+o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x}}}\right)\\
&=-t\log x+\frac{t}{2x}+o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x}}}\right)\,.\qed\end{split}

Lemma

B.3
immediately gives

\frac{\Gamma(U_{N}-1-\alpha+\iota)}{\Gamma(U_{N}-1)}=\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U_{N}-1}}\right)^{\alpha-\iota}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2U_{N}}+o(1/U_{N})}=\frac{1}{U_{N}^{\alpha-\iota}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\alpha-\iota}{U_{N}}+o(\frac{1}{U_{N}})}}\right)\,.

Similarly, we have

\frac{\Gamma(U_{N}-\alpha+\iota)}{\Gamma(U_{N})}=\frac{1}{U_{N}^{\alpha-\iota}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+o(\frac{1}{U_{N}})}}\right)\,.

This means that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{U_{N}-1}}{(U_{N}-1)!}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}\mu(t)=\frac{\alpha-\iota}{U_{N}^{\alpha-\iota+1}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{U_{N}^{\alpha+1-\iota}}\Bigr)\,.

Similarly,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{L_{N}-2}}{(L_{N}-2)!}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}\mu(t)=\frac{\alpha-\iota}{L_{N}^{\alpha-\iota+1}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{L_{N}^{\alpha+1-\iota}}\Bigr)\,.

Putting everything together, we get that there is a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]\leqslant C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha-\iota}{L_{N}^{\alpha-\iota+1}}-\frac{\alpha-\iota}{U_{N}^{\alpha-\iota+1}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+o(\frac{1}{L_{N}})}}\right)+O\Bigl((\log N)^{2\delta}\Bigr)\,.

This means

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}}}\right]=O\Bigl(\frac{N}{\lambda^{2\alpha+3-2\iota}}\Bigr)+O\Bigl((\log N)^{2\delta}\Bigr)\,.

(B.2)

We now turn to the proof of
Proposition

1.10
. The proof
will use the following variant of
Lemma

2.18
.

Lemma B.4
.

For each vertex
x\in[N]
, define

\hat{D}^{+}_{x}=\#\{y\in S_{1}(x)\vcentcolon w_{y}\geqslant w_{x}\}=\sum_{y\neq x}\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x\sim y,w_{x}\leqslant w_{y}\}}\,.

Let
\nu>0
. With
\nu
-high probability, we have

\hat{D}_{x}^{+}\leqslant\frac{2\nu}{1-\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\,.

Proof.

Let
k\geqslant 1
be an integer. The union bound implies

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{D}^{+}_{x}\geqslant k}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\in[k],x\sim x_{i},w_{x}\leqslant w_{x_{i}}}}\right)\,.

By independence and (
1.10
), we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{D}^{+}_{x}\geqslant k}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{w_{x}w_{x_{i}}}{m_{1}N}\mathbbmss{1}_{\{w_{x}\leqslant w_{x_{i}}\}}}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{w_{x_{i}}^{2}}{m_{1}N}}}\right)\,,

where in the last line, we used that

\mathbbmss{1}_{\{w_{x}\leqslant w_{x_{i}}\}}\leqslant w_{x_{i}}/w_{x}
. By definition
of the second empirical moment, we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{D}^{+}_{x}\geqslant k}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}}}\right)^{k}(1+o(1))\,.

Taking
k=\lfloor\frac{2\nu}{1-\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\rfloor
allows
us to conclude.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

1.10
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
be an integer. We use the union bound to write

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\geqslant k}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\in[k],(\lambda-\eta)^{2}\leqslant D_{x_{i}}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)\,.

Set
\hat{D}^{-}_{x}=D_{x}-\hat{D}_{x}^{+}
for all
x\in[N]
.
By
Lemma

B.4
, we have for all
x\in[N]
that

D_{x}=\hat{D}_{x}^{+}+\hat{D}_{x}^{-}\leqslant\hat{D}_{x}^{-}+\frac{2\nu+2}{1-\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\,,

with
\nu
-high probability. For convenience, write

c_{\nu,N}=\frac{2\nu+2}{1-\delta}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
.

Now, notice that the random variables
\hat{D}_{x}^{-}
are
independent. It implies

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\geqslant k}}\right)&\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}-c_{\nu,N}\leqslant\hat{D}^{-}_{x_{i}}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)+O(N^{-\nu})\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{x}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}-c_{\nu,N}\leqslant\hat{D}^{-}_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}}}\right)}}\right)^{k}+O(N^{-\nu})\,.\end{split}

Notice that we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\geqslant k}}\right)&\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{x}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}-c_{\nu,N}\leqslant D_{x}\leqslant(\lambda+\eta)^{2}+c_{\nu,N}}}\right)}}\right)^{k}+O(N^{-\nu})\\
&\leqslant\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta+c_{\nu,N}/2\lambda}}}\right)^{k}}{k!}+O(N^{-\nu})\,.\end{split}

Markov’s inequality gives the crude bound

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\mathcal{W}_{\lambda,\eta}\geqslant k}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{k!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{x}\frac{w_{x}^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}-c_{\nu,N}}}\right)^{2}}}}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\frac{m_{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\lambda-\eta)^{2}-c_{\nu,N}}}\right)^{2}}}}\right)^{k}\,.

Choosing

k=\lfloor\frac{2m_{2}}{(\lambda-\eta)^{4}}N\vee\frac{2\nu\log N}{\log\log N}\rfloor

gives the result. The result can be improved in our two examples
using expressions (
B.1
) and (
B.2
), derived above.
∎
