Theoretical Constraints on Neutron Star Superfluidity from Her X-1 Precession
Anton Biryukov, Amir Levinson, Pavel Abolmasov

Abstract
Recent IXPE observations of Her X-1 reveal correlations between flux, polarization degree, and polarization angle across its 35-day superorbital cycle. These measurements have been interpreted as strong evidence that the 35-day period is driven by nearly free precession of the neutron star. We show that this interpretation carries far-reaching implications for the dynamics of the crustal superfluid. In particular, maintaining precession over the ∼ 50 \sim 50 -year observational baseline of Her X-1 would require that superfluid vortices remain unpinned for centuries and experience extremely weak mutual friction while traversing the heavy-ion lattice of the inner crust – conditions that challenge conventional wisdom and standard models of glitch dynamics. Under the condition of weak pinning, nearly free precession of the crust may be sustained by a balance between the internal and external torques.

I
Introduction

Neutron stars are born hot but cool rapidly, and a substantial fraction of their interior is expected to transition into a superfluid state within years to decades after formation. In particular, neutrons in the inner crust and outer core are widely believed to form a superfluid capable of sustaining long-lived, weakly dissipative flows on macroscopic scales. In a rotating neutron star, the angular momentum of this superfluid is carried by an array of quantized vortex lines
(
10
)
, whose areal density is directly proportional to the coarse-grained angular velocity of the superfluid
(
26
;
9
)
.

It has long been thought that pulsar glitches – sudden discrete increases in the observed rotation frequency – provide some of the most direct evidence for the presence of a superfluid component in neutron stars
(for reviews, see
22
;
7
, and references therein)
. In the standard picture, vortices in the crustal superfluid are partially pinned to the nuclear lattice, allowing the superfluid to rotate slightly faster than the solid crust as the star spins down under electromagnetic torques. Differential rotation then builds up until a critical threshold is reached, triggering rapid vortex unpinning and outward motion
(
6
;
4
;
3
;
36
)
. The resulting transfer of angular momentum from the superfluid to the crust produces the observed glitch.

Following a glitch, the star typically exhibits a relaxation phase, during which the rotation rate evolves toward a new equilibrium on timescales ranging from days to months. This post-glitch recovery is commonly interpreted as the gradual re-coupling of superfluid components that were only weakly coupled to the crust during the glitch itself. In particular, mutual interactions between vortices and the surrounding charged fluid mediate the exchange of angular momentum between the crust and the superfluid in the core, leading to partial or complete recovery of the pre-glitch spin-down rate. The detailed form of the relaxation thus encodes information about the strength of superfluid coupling and the distribution of superfluid reservoirs within the star.

Vortex pinning can have a profound effect on the free precession of a neutron star by dynamically coupling the superfluid component to the solid crust. In the inner crust, quantized vortices of the neutron superfluid are expected to pin to the nuclear lattice, preventing the superfluid from freely adjusting its angular momentum in response to changes in the crust’s rotation. If vortices remain strongly pinned during precession, the pinned superfluid exerts a dominant gyroscopic restoring force on the crust that controls the crust’s precessional dynamics. As originally shown by
49
(see also
24
;
34
), in case of absolute pinning the precessional angular frequency must be of the order
\omega_{\rm p}\sim(I_{\rm s}/I)\Omega_{s}\gtrsim 10^{-2}\Omega
, where
I_{\rm s}/I
is the fraction of the stellar moment of inertia in the pinned superfluid and
\Omega
is the angular frequency of the star.

The fast precessional periods (a few minutes typically) imposed by absolute pinning are inconsistent with the much longer precessional periods inferred for some neutron stars, most notably Hercules X-1. It has long been suspected that the observed 35-day superorbital period in Hercules X-1 may be due to (nearly free) precession of the neutron star. Recent X-ray polarization measurements with the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE), which can directly probe the spin geometry of the neutron star, have recently been argued to support this interpretation
(
23
;
58
)
.

2
proposed that a steady state may exist in which a pinned superfluid follows the precessional motion of the crust through thermal vortex creep. In this picture, the component of the superfluid–crust lag perpendicular to the vortex lines is of order the precession frequency
\omega_{\rm p}
and therefore much smaller than the critical lag required for unpinning. The relaxation time to this equilibrium depends on the ratio of the pinning energy to the thermal energy and was estimated to be of the order of a few hundred to a few thousand years for Her X–1. However,
2
neglected the non-dissipative torque associated with the component of the mutual-friction force acting in the direction
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\rm n}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\rm s})
, where
\boldsymbol{v}_{\rm n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\rm s}
are the normal-fluid and superfluid velocities, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\omega}}
is the superfluid vorticity. As we shall show, this omission is not justified.

The torque associated with the mutual-friction force was derived rigorously by
48
and applied to the calculation of precessional modes in the case of imperfect pinning. In the strong-drag regime, they found low-frequency, strongly damped free-precession modes and concluded that
their results are qualitatively consistent with
49
, in that no long-lived free precession survives in the strong-drag regime. However, their analysis is perturbative and restricted to oblate deformations, and considers only small (linear) lags about a fixed point in which all angular velocities are equal and aligned with a stable principal axis. It therefore remains unclear how the rotational dynamics behaves in the general case.

In this paper, we present a comprehensive analysis of the rotational evolution, taking into account the internal torque exerted on the normal (rigid) crust by the crustal superfluid and assuming tight coupling between the core and the normal crust. We apply these results to Her X-1 and show that if the 35-d cycle is produced by nearly free precession of the neutron star, then superfluid vortices must remain unpinned for centuries or longer and experience extremely weak mutual friction, with a dimensionless drag coefficient
\lesssim 0.01
, while traversing the heavy-ion lattice of the inner crust. This implies that the existence of long-lived precession is incompatible with the buildup of the large differential rotation required to trigger glitches.

II
Two-component Model

Three main components govern a neutron star’s rotational dynamics: the outer core, the elastic charged lattice in the crust, and the crustal neutron superfluid. In isolated neutron stars, the core temperature is expected to fall below the neutron superfluid transition temperature,
T_{\rm n}\sim 5\times 10^{8}
K, so that neutrons in the core become superfluid. In accreting neutron stars, however, the core temperature may approach
T_{\rm n}
(
16

II.1
Mutual-friction torques in the crust

The coupling between the crustal superfluid and the elastic crust is mediated by interactions between quantized vortices and crustal
nuclei. This interaction is commonly modeled by a phenomenological drag force per unit length of a vortex:
\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{d}}=-\eta(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}})
, where
\eta
is a drag coefficient,
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}
is the vortex velocity and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}\times\boldsymbol{r}
is the velocity of the normal crustal component.
The motion of a vortex is determined by balancing this drag force with the Magnus force arising from the relative motion between the bulk superfluid and the vortex line,

\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{M}}=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\kappa\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}})
, which leads to

\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\kappa\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}})=\eta(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}).

(7)

Here
\kappa=h/2m_{\mathrm{n}}
is the quantum of circulation,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is a unit vector in the direction of local vorticity,

\rho_{s}
is the superfluid mass density, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}}
is its local velocity. The latter equation can be solved to yield

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}&=\frac{{\cal R}}{1+{\cal R}^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{1+{\cal R}^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}})\right),\end{split}

(8)

where
\mathcal{R}=\eta/\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\kappa
is the dimensionless drag coefficient. Substituting
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}
from the last equation into the drag force
\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{d}}

yields the mutual friction force density:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{MF}}&=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\kappa n_{\mathrm{v}}\left[\frac{\mathcal{R}^{2}}{1+\mathcal{R}^{2}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}})\right.\\
&\quad+\left.\frac{\mathcal{R}}{1+\mathcal{R}^{2}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{s}})\right)\right],\end{split}

(9)

where
n_{\mathrm{v}}
is the vortex density.
Assuming that the angular velocity of the normal crust is uniform, the internal torque is given by
(
48
)
:

\boldsymbol{N}_{s}=\int d^{3}r\,(\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{MF}})=\boldsymbol{N}_{s1}+\boldsymbol{N}_{s2},

(10)

where

{\boldsymbol{N}}_{s1}=\frac{\mathcal{R}}{1+\mathcal{R}^{2}}\ I_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\left(2-\dfrac{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{s}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}\right],

(11)

is the
dissipative
torque component, for which
\boldsymbol{N}_{s1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{s}})<0
, and

{\boldsymbol{N}}_{s2}=\frac{\mathcal{R}^{2}}{1+\mathcal{R}^{2}}I_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{s}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}),

(12)

is the non-dissipative torque component. In general, the drag force is nonlinear, specifically, the drag parameter
\mathcal{R}
depends on the relative velocity

\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{n}}

(
17
;
51
, e.g.,)
. However, this nonlinearity should not affect our conclusions. Therefore, to simplify the analysis we adopt
\mathcal{R}=
const.

Euler’s equations

As stated above, the two-component model adopted here treats the core and normal crust as a single deformed rigid body. It is, therefore, convenient to
solve Euler’s equations in the frame rotating with the normal crust. In this frame, the equations read:

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}\times\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{n}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{ext}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{s}},

(13)

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}\times\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{s}}=-\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{s}},

:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\dfrac{n_{\theta}-(\epsilon\sin\theta\cos\theta+n_{\varphi})\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}},

(31)

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\dfrac{\epsilon\sin\theta\cos\theta+n_{\varphi}+n_{\theta}\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}}{\sin\theta(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2})},

(32)

restricted to the range
\mathcal{R}\ll(1+\kappa_{s})/n_{\Omega}
.
As will be clarified below, Eq. (
31
) governs the decay of slow precession modes, owing to the coupling of the crustal
superfluid and the normal crust. The decay of the fast (Shaham) mode occurs during the rapid transient phase, over time

t\lesssim\tau_{\mathrm{d}}
(see Appendix
B
). The spin-down equation for the normal crust (and the coupled core) is obtained, to linear order, by
projecting Eq. (
15
) onto
\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}
:

\begin{split}\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}&=\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{n}}\,n_{\Omega}+\dfrac{2\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}}{1+{\cal R}^{2}}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\delta\Omega_{\mathrm{||eq}}\\
&=\dfrac{\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{n}}n_{\Omega}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{ext}}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}}{I_{\mathrm{0}}+I_{\mathrm{s}}}.\end{split}

(33)

We see that the spin-down rate of the crust coincides with that of a rigid body, as required by angular momentum conservation.
It is instructive to first consider the limit of free precession, as it captures, to a good approximation, the evolution of the system in the general case when
n_{i}\ll\epsilon
, as in the application to Her X-1. In this limit, Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
) reduce to

\dot{\varphi}=\dfrac{\epsilon\Omega_{n}\cos\theta}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}},

(34)

and

\dot{\theta}=-\dfrac{\epsilon\Omega_{n}\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}}\,\cos\theta\sin\theta.

(35)

The solution of the last equation can be written as:

\tan(\theta)=\tan(\theta_{0})\cdot\exp\left(-\dfrac{t}{\tau_{\mathrm{p}}}\right),

(36)

where

\tau_{\mathrm{p}}=\dfrac{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\epsilon\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}}\approx\frac{(1+\kappa^{2}_{s}\mathcal{R}^{2})}{\epsilon\kappa_{s}(1+\mathcal{R}^{2})}\tau_{d}>\frac{\kappa_{s}\tau_{d}}{\epsilon}.

(37)

As seen, for
\epsilon\ll\kappa_{s}
we have
\tau_{p}\gg\tau_{d}
.
Note that the evolution of
\theta
depends on the sign of
\epsilon
(see Fig.
3
). For
\epsilon>0
(an oblate star),
\theta\to 0
as
t\gg\tau_{p}
, and the amplitude of the precession decays. For
\epsilon<0
(a prolate star),
\theta\to\pi/2
as
t\gg\tau_{p}
, while
\dot{\varphi}\to 0
, i.e. the precession period diverges. The evolution of
\theta
reflects the tendency of the system to minimize its rotational energy at fixed angular momentum.
In either case, the slow free–precession mode damps on a timescale
\sim\tau_{p}
. In other words, the slow precession persists for
\gtrsim(\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R})^{-1}
cycles independently on
\epsilon
.

IV
Application to Hercules X-1

The origin of the
\sim 35
-day modulation observed in the X-ray binary Her X-1
(
54
;
21
)
has been debated for several decades. A widely accepted interpretation attributes the cycle to the forced precession of a tilted and warped accretion disk, which periodically obscures the central X-ray source and produces the characteristic sequence of main-on, short-on and off states in the X-ray light curve
(
25
;
41
;
46
;
56
)
. In this picture, tidal torques from the companion star together with radiation-driven warping lead to a precessing disk whose varying orientation modulates the observed flux. An alternative interpretation invokes free precession of the neutron star, motivated in part by the systematic evolution of the X-ray pulse profiles over the 35-day cycle, which may reflect a changing orientation of the emission beam relative to the observer
(
55
;
50
;
44
;
27
)
. Recent X-ray polarimetric observations with the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer have provided new evidence supporting this possibility. Phase-resolved polarization measurements across the superorbital cycle reveal systematic variations in the polarization angle and degree that are consistent with changes in the viewing geometry of the magnetic poles, as expected if the neutron star itself undergoes free precession
(
23
;
58
)
. These observations suggest that the
\sim 35
-day clock may be set by the free precession of the neutron-star crust, implying a small but finite asymmetry in the star’s moment of inertia (of order a few parts per
10^{7}
) required to sustain such motion. Hybrid scenarios have also been proposed in which both the neutron star and the accretion disk precess, forming a system of coupled clocks in which the disk primarily controls the X-ray turn-ons while neutron-star precession modulates the pulse-profile morphology
(
43
;
53
, e.g.,)
. The relative roles of these mechanisms remain an active topic of investigation.

In what follows, we consider the implications of neutron star precession for the superfluid dynamics.
Under the assumption of tight core-crust coupling, the net external torque acts on the star on timescales much longer than the observed 35-d cycle in Her X-1, which in the free-precession interpretation is driven by a stellar deformation with ellipticity
\epsilon\approx 4\times 10^{-7}

(
23
, e.g.,)
. More precisely,

N_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}\ll I_{0}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}|\epsilon|,

(38)

where
N_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}
is the magnitude of the net external torque, which can be decomposed into several components,

{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{acc}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{mag}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{psr}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{warp}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{prec}}.

(39)

From left to right, the terms on the right-hand side represent the accretion spin-up torque, magnetospheric spin-down torque, pulsar losses, and the back-reaction associated with the warping and precession of the accretion disc, respectively. The latter two are due to the interaction of the inclined disc with the NS magnetosphere
(
29
;
30
)
.
The details are provided in Appendix
C
.

It is worth noting that
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{acc}}
,
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{warp}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{prec}}
rotate rapidly about
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}
in the principal frame of the star, as they are linked to the accretion disc. Thus, their contribution effectively averages out over the spin period. The corresponding spin-averaged torque can be written as the sum of two components, parallel and orthogonal to the crust’s spin axis, respectively:

\langle\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{ext,HZ1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{spin}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\parallel}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\perp}.

(40)

The first component is responsible for the neutron star spin-down:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\parallel}&=\Biggl[N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}(1+\sin^{2}\chi)+N_{\mathrm{mag}}\Biggr.\\
&\Biggl.+N_{\mathrm{acc,0}}\cos\alpha\left(1+\dfrac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos^{2}\chi\right)\Biggr]\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega},\end{split}

(41)

while second one is capable of changing the orientation of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}
with respect the principal frame:

\boldsymbol{N}_{\perp}=N_{\chi}\left((\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega})\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}\right)+N_{\mathrm{rad}}(\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}),

(42)

Here
\boldsymbol{m}
is the unit vector aligned with the magnetic axis. It is assumed to be fixed in the principal frame, and misaligned with respect to the deformation axis. The torque

N_{\chi}=\left(N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}+\eta\,A\,N_{\mathrm{acc,0}}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\alpha\right)\cos\chi

(43)

is responsible for the star magnetic obliquity evolution, while

N_{\mathrm{rad}}=\dfrac{N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}}{3}\left(\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{LC}}}{r_{\mathrm{NS}}}\right)\cos\chi

(44)

is the pulsar “anomalous” torque
(
11
, e.g)
.
Here
\chi=\cos^{-1}(\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega})
is the magnetic angle,
\alpha
is the angle between the accretion-disc axis and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}
,
r_{\mathrm{LC}}=c/\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}
is the light-cylinder radius, and
r_{\mathrm{NS}}
is the neutron-star radius. The dimensionless parameters
\eta=0..1
and

A=\left[1-\dfrac{\eta}{2}\left(\sin^{2}\chi\sin^{2}\alpha+2\cos^{2}\chi\cos^{2}\alpha\right)\right]^{-1},

(45)

describe the spin-modulated accretion torque.

The orthogonal component,
\boldsymbol{N}_{\perp}

\dot{\theta}

n_{\theta,\varphi}\equiv\dfrac{N_{\theta,\varphi}}{I_{0}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}=\dfrac{\boldsymbol{N}_{\perp}\,\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta,\varphi}}{I_{0}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}},

(46)

while
\boldsymbol{N}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}\equiv 0

n_{\Omega}\equiv N_{\parallel}/I_{0}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}
.

V
Discussion

The very low effective drag,
\mathcal{R}<10^{-2}
, implied by our analysis of the neutron star free precession in Her X-1, carries important consequences for standard pictures of superfluid dynamics and glitch phenomenology. In conventional glitch models, rapid spin-up events are attributed to the sudden unpinning and redistribution of angular momentum stored in a strongly pinned superfluid component, with subsequent coupling to the crust mediated by finite mutual friction and vortex creep. Unpinning occurs once the lag developed during the spin-down of the crust approaches the critical lag,

\Delta\Omega_{\mathrm{crit}}\simeq\frac{f_{p}}{\rho_{s}\kappa\,r_{NS}}\sim 0.1-1\quad\mbox{rad s}^{-1},

(53)

where
f_{p}
is the pinning force per unit length,
\rho_{s}
the superfluid density, and
\kappa
the quantum of circulation. This is larger by several orders of magnitude than the maximum lag developed during the quasi-steady precession phase (cf. Eq.
27
):

|\mathbf{\Omega}_{s}-\mathbf{\Omega}_{n}|\lesssim\Omega_{n}\epsilon\approx 10^{-6}\quad\text{rad s}^{-1}.

(54)

Consequently, the relative motion between the superfluid and the normal component induced by precession is far too small to trigger vortex unpinning.
In fact, the projected fractional lag obtained from Eq. (
28
) for the net torque inferred in Her X-1,

\frac{|\delta\Omega_{\mathrm{||eq}}|}{\Omega_{n}}\lesssim 10^{-9},

(55)

is several orders of magnitude smaller than the fractional spin jumps observed in glitches in many pulsars. This suggests that the
dynamical state realized during long-lived precession is incompatible with the buildup of the large differential rotation required for
glitch triggering. If the superfluid responsible for storing angular momentum in glitching pulsars behaves similarly in accreting
systems such as Her X-1, the results imply that either the regions involved in glitches are largely decoupled from the precessing
component, or that the effective vortex drag and pinning conditions depend sensitively on the dynamical state of the star.

Alternatively, the 35-day cycle may arise from a different mechanism, and the IXPE observations may admit an alternative interpretation.
A low-mass third body orbiting the system has been proposed to explain the light curve
(
15
;
37
)
. However, this
model predicts eccentricity of a few percents for the main binary orbit, which has been ruled out by accurate pulse-timing observations

(
19
)
. It is also difficult to see how such a model could account for the IXPE data.

57
proposed an alternative scenario in which the periodicities in the light curve of Her X-1 are associated with
oscillatory modes (g-modes) of the companion star, HZ Her. However, this model predicts additional periodicities on a timescale of
\sim 1
day, which are not observed. Moreover, it does not account for the periodic changes in the X-ray pulse profile that track the 35-day
cycle.

The most viable alternative is therefore the precession of a warped, inclined accretion disc, conceivably driven by tidal torques from
the companion star
(
25
;
20
;
42
;
47
;
31
;
32
)
. The modulation of the polarization
signal could then arise from scattering within the accretion-disc corona (
32
, but see
23
) although
whether the IXPE data can be fitted with such a model remains to be demonstrated.

VI
Conclusions

We have examined the implications of long-lived free precession of the neutron star in Her X-1 for the dynamics of the internal
superfluid. Sustaining precession over the
\sim
50 yr observational baseline requires an extremely weak effective coupling between the
superfluid and the normal component, corresponding to a drag parameter
\mathcal{R}\lesssim 10^{-2}
. Even weaker coupling (
\mathcal{R}\lesssim 2\times 10^{-3}
) allows for the scenario where dissipation of the free precession is compensated by external torques.

This result carries important consequences for standard models of pulsar glitches. In particular, the differential rotation that develops during the quasi-steady precession phase is many orders of magnitude smaller than the critical lag expected for vortex unpinning, and is also far below the fractional spin changes observed in glitches. If the superfluid responsible for storing angular momentum in glitching pulsars behaves similarly in accreting neutron stars such as Her X-1, our results suggest that either the region involved in glitches is largely decoupled from the precessing component, or the effective pinning and drag depend sensitively on the dynamical state of the star.

Finally, we note that alternative explanations for the 35-day cycle in Her X-1 remain possible, and further observational and theoretical work will be required to determine whether they can account for the observed polarization properties.

I_{0}=I_{\mathrm{cr}}+I_{\mathrm{co}}=I_{\mathrm{cr}}(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}})

\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}\sim 10^{2}
for neutron stars. However, in reality, the crust-core coupling, even being strong, is not perfectly tight. Following
2
, one may assume that the crust is subject to the torque from the core

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{co}}=I_{\mathrm{co}}\tau^{-1}_{\mathrm{co}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{co}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}),

(A1)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{co}}
is the core angular velocity and
\tau_{\mathrm{co}}
is the coupling time scale.

Consider the effect of imperfect crust–core coupling within a toy rotational model that neglects both the crustal superfluid and any external torques. In particular, we let the crust evolve according to

\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}_{\mathrm{n}}+\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}})+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}\tau^{-1}_{\mathrm{co}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{co}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}),

(A2)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(0,0,\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\theta)^{T}
, with
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}=\Delta I_{\mathrm{cr}}/I_{\mathrm{cr}}
characterizing the crust deformation. Note that
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}
does not represent the asymmetry of the total stellar moment of inertia, unlike the parameter
\epsilon
used in the main body of the paper. As will become clear later,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}
can be significantly larger than
\epsilon
.

The rotational evolution of the core is given by

\boldsymbol{\dot{\Omega}}_{\mathrm{co}}=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{co}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}})-\tau^{-1}_{\mathrm{co}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{co}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}),

(A3)

in the frame pinned to the crust. Let

{\cal C}=\dfrac{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}}{\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{co}}}

(A4)

be the dimensionless coupling parameter. In the limit
{\cal C}\rightarrow\infty
, the crust and core are perfectly coupled and rotate as a single rigid body. Conversely, for
{\cal C}=0
, the core dynamics has no influence on the crust.
Since the second term on the right-hand side of Eqs. (
A2
)–(
A3
) is dissipative, it plays a role analogous to that of the crustal superfluid discussed in the previous sections. Specifically, on a short initial timescale (
\sim\tau_{\mathrm{co}}/\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}
), the crust and core spin axes relax toward a quasi-equilibrium misalignment angle

\gamma_{\mathrm{cr-co,eq}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}\dfrac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+{\cal C}^{2}}}.

(A5)

After this transient stage, both components evolve jointly.

By rewriting Eqs. (
A2
)–(
A3
) and assuming
\gamma_{\mathrm{cr-co}}\ll 1
, one finds that in the equilibrium state the crust precesses about the symmetry axis with angular velocity

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}\dfrac{1+(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}})^{-1}{\cal C}^{2}}{1+{\cal C}^{2}},

(A6)

while the tilt angle decays at the rate

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}\tau^{-1}_{\mathrm{co}}\dfrac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{1+{\cal C}^{2}}.

(A7)

Equivalently, in terms of the number of precession cycles,

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\dot{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}t}=-\dfrac{\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}{\cal C}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}+{\cal C}^{2}}\sin\theta,

(A8)

which is independent of
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}
.

This result implies that in the no-coupling regime (
{\cal C}=0
), the crust precesses at the rate
\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}
without any
damping. This is a trivial result. On the other hand, in the limit of absolute coupling (
{\cal C}\rightarrow\infty
), the precession
becomes significantly slower:

\dot{\varphi}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}},

(A9)

as expected, since in this case the star behaves as a single rigid body with total moment of inertia
I_{\mathrm{cr}}(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}})
. In this limit, the precession is also undamped,
\dot{\theta}=0
.

However, for finite coupling, the precession period exceeds
2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}
, and the spin axis relaxes toward either the
symmetry axis (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}>0
) or the symmetry equator (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}<0
) within a finite number of cycles.
The fastest damping occurs for
{\cal C}=\sqrt{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}}
, corresponding to

\tau_{\mathrm{co}}=\frac{P\sqrt{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}}}{2\pi\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}}\approx\frac{P}{63},

(A10)

for
\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}=10^{2}
. However,
2
estimate
\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{co}}\sim 10^{2}-10^{4}
for realistic neutron stars, which implies
{\cal C}\sim 10^{-2}-1
. For the upper end of this range, the crust–core coupling damps the precession on a timescale of order one cycle. In contrast, if
{\cal C}\sim 10^{-2}
, the precession can survive for hundreds of cycles with period
\approx 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}
.

To illustrate the effect of crust–core coupling, we obtain a full numerical solution of the system (
A2
)–(
A3
) for a biaxial star with the following parameters:
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cr}}=10^{-2}
,
P_{0}=1\,\mathrm{s}
, and
\theta_{0}=60^{\circ}
. The magnetic axis is assumed to be tilted by
45^{\circ}
with respect to the symmetry axis, corresponding to an initial magnetic inclination
\chi_{0}=15^{\circ}
. The ratio of the core to the crust moments of inertia in this toy model is set to
\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}=10
.

In Fig.
7
we show the simulated evolution of the magnetic angle in this model for a range of
{\cal C}
values from
10^{-3}
to
10^{3}
. The results are fully consistent with the theoretical predictions. For very weak coupling (
{\cal C}=10^{-3}
), the system exhibits long-lived precession with period
2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}\approx 200\,\mathrm{s}
. However, a slow decay of the precession amplitude is observed.
For larger values of
{\cal C}
, damping of the precession becomes more significant, reaching its strongest regime near
{\cal C}\approx 3\sim\sqrt{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}}}
. Beyond this regime, strong coupling leads again to long-lived precession, but with a reduced frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{cr}}/(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{co}})
.

Appendix B
Steady-state rotation parameters

The evolution of
\delta\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{s}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}
in the linear approximation, Eq. (
22
), reads:

\dfrac{\rm d{(\delta\boldsymbol{\Omega})}}{\rm dt}=-(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}})-\dfrac{\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{ext}}}{I_{0}}-(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{Sh}}\times\delta\boldsymbol{\Omega})-\dfrac{\delta\boldsymbol{\Omega}+(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}-1)\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{n}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}}.

(B1)

It is convenient to transform to the basis vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi}\pm i\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}
. Then
\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}=\pm i\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}
,
and
\delta\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\delta{\cal W}_{\perp}^{-}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}+\delta{\cal W}_{\perp}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

2\delta{\cal W}_{\perp}^{\pm}=\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}-\varphi)\sin\theta\pm i\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}-\theta)

For the parallel component, Eq. (
B1
) reduces to

\dfrac{\rm d}{\rm dt}(\delta{\Omega}_{\parallel})+2\tau_{\mathrm{d}}^{-1}\delta\Omega_{\parallel}=-\Omega^{2}_{\mathrm{n}}\,n_{\Omega},

(B2)

and for the projection of the orthogonal component on
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mp}
, to

\dfrac{\rm d}{\rm dt}(\delta{\cal W}^{\pm}_{\perp})+{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}\delta{\cal W}^{\pm}_{\perp}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}\dfrac{{\cal N}^{\pm}_{\perp}}{2},

(B3)

where
{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}\equiv\tau_{\mathrm{d}}^{-1}-i\Omega_{\mathrm{Sh}}

{\cal N}^{\pm}_{\perp}\equiv(\epsilon\sin\theta\cos\theta+n_{\varphi})\pm in_{\theta}
are complex representations of the Shaham’s frequency, and the projections of the
torque on
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mp}
, respectively. The dimensionless torque components
n_{\theta}
and

n_{\varphi}
are defined by Eq. (
29
) above. Thus,
\tau_{\mathrm{d}}=(1+{\cal R}^{2})/(\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}{\cal R}(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}))
is simply the decay time of the rapid Shaham’s mode.

The external torque components change on a time-scale given by the minimum of two: the precession damping time
\tau_{\mathrm{p}}
and the
spin-down time. However, both are longer than
\tau_{\mathrm{d}}
provided that
\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}/|\epsilon|\ll 1
. Therefore, the right-
hand sides of Eqs. (
B2
) and (
B3
) can be treated as constants evolving adiabatically. Their solutions are

\delta\Omega_{\parallel}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}n_{\Omega}\tau_{\mathrm{d}}\left(1-e^{-t/2\tau_{\mathrm{d}}}\right)+\delta\Omega_{\parallel,0}\,e^{-t/2\tau_{\mathrm{d}}},

(B4)

and

\delta{\cal W}^{\pm}_{\perp}(t)=-\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}\dfrac{{\cal N}^{\pm}_{\perp}{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}^{*}}{2|{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}|^{2}}\left(1-e^{-{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}t}\right)+\delta{\cal W}^{\pm}_{\perp,0}\,e^{-{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}t}

respectively. These solutions show that if
\tau_{\mathrm{d}}

\delta{\dot{\Omega}}_{\parallel}

\delta\dot{\cal W}^{\pm}_{\perp}

\delta\Omega_{\parallel,\mathrm{eq}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}n_{\Omega}\tau_{\mathrm{d}}/2,

(B6)

and

\gamma^{2}_{\mathrm{eq}}=\delta{\cal W}^{+}_{\perp,\mathrm{eq}}\delta{\cal W}^{-}_{\perp,\mathrm{eq}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{4}\dfrac{{\cal N}^{+}_{\perp}{\cal N}^{-}_{\perp}}{|{\cal W}_{\mathrm{Sh}}|^{2}}.

(B7)

These expressions coincide with those given earlier in Eqs. (
28

27

The components of the latter along the polar and azimuthal directions are

\delta\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}=-\dfrac{1-\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}^{2}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}}\,(n_{\varphi}+\epsilon\sin\theta\cos\theta)-\dfrac{(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}){\cal R}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}}n_{\theta},

(B8)

and

(\delta\varphi\sin\theta)_{\mathrm{eq}}=\dfrac{1-\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}^{2}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}}\,n_{\theta}-\dfrac{(1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}){\cal R}}{1+\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{\cal R}^{2}}\,(n_{\varphi}+\epsilon\sin\theta\cos\theta)

(B9)

respectively.

Following the same approach, the precessional evolution can be treated by introducing the complex quantity
{\cal W}^{+}_{\perp}=\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi\sin\theta+i\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\theta

\dfrac{\rm d}{\rm dt}{\cal W}^{+}_{\perp}=\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}{\cal N}^{+}_{\perp}+\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}\dfrac{\kappa_{\mathrm{s}}{\cal R}}{1+{\cal R}^{2}}(1+i{\cal R})\,\delta{\cal W}^{+}_{\perp}.

(B10)

If in this equation
\delta{\cal W}^{+}_{\perp}=\delta{\cal W}^{+}_{\perp,\mathrm{eq}}

Appendix C
Hercules X-1 external torque

As noted in Sect.
IV
above, the neutron star in the Her X-1 system is subject to the influence of an external torque, which can be considered as a sum of several components:

{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{acc}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{mag}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{psr}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{warp}}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{prec}}.

(C1)

In this Appendix, we provide details of these torques as they have been adopted in the numerical calculations. The first component in the equation above represents the accretion spin-up:

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{acc}}=\dot{M}\,f(\cos\xi)\sqrt{GM_{\mathrm{NS}}r_{\mathrm{m}}}\boldsymbol{d}=N_{\mathrm{acc,0}}\,f(\cos\xi)\boldsymbol{d},

(C2)

where
\dot{M}
is the accretion rate,
M_{\mathrm{NS}}
is the star mass,
\boldsymbol{d}
is the disc angular momentum axis (
|\boldsymbol{d}|=1
),

r_{\mathrm{m}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\mu^{4}}{2GM_{\mathrm{NS}}\dot{M}^{2}}\right)^{1/7}

(C3)

is the inner magnetospheric radius and

f(\cos\xi)=A(\eta,\alpha,\chi)(1-\eta\cos^{2}\xi)

(C4)

is the modulation factor depending on the instant angle between the star’s magnetic
\boldsymbol{m}
and disc axes:
\xi=\cos^{-1}(\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{d})
. The normalizing coefficient
A(\eta,\alpha,\chi)
depend on the magnetic inclination
\chi
, spin-disc inclination

\alpha=\cos^{-1}(\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{d})
and modulation parameter
\eta=0..1

(see
13
, for details)
as

A=\left[1-\dfrac{\eta}{2}\left(\sin^{2}\chi\sin^{2}\alpha+2\cos^{2}\chi\cos^{2}\alpha\right)\right]^{-1}.

(C5)

The second term in Eq. (
C1
) describes the spin-down due to the interaction of the disc by the stellar magnetic field:

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{mag}}=-\dfrac{\mu^{2}}{3r_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}=N_{\mathrm{mag}}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega},

(C6)

where

r_{\mathrm{c}}=\left(\dfrac{GM_{\mathrm{NS}}}{\Omega^{2}}\right)^{1/3}

(C7)

is the corotation radius.

The third term represents pulsar losses:

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{psr}}=N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}(1+\sin^{2}\chi)\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}+N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}\cos\chi((\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega})\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega})+\dfrac{N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}}{3}\left(\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{LC}}}{r_{\mathrm{NS}}}\right)\cos\chi(\boldsymbol{m}\times\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}),

(C8)

and depends on the instant direction of the crust spin axis, magnetic angle
\chi
, and scales as

N_{\mathrm{psr,0}}=-\mu^{2}/r_{\mathrm{LC}}^{3}
, where
r_{\mathrm{LC}}=c/\Omega
is the light cylinder radius
(
1
)
.
At this point, we ignore pulsar losses enhancement due to the extra opening of the magnetic field lines by the disc
(
40
)
,
but take into account the so-called “anomalous” part of the pulsar torque. The latter scales as

(r_{\mathrm{LC}}/r_{\mathrm{NS}})N_{\mathrm{prs,0}}
and hence is much stronger than standard pulsar losses. For this component, we adopted
the equation found by
11
in the assumption that the star’s internal magnetic field is uniform and aligned with the
magnetic axis.

The two final terms account for backreaction from the warping and precession of the tilted disc:

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{warp}}=\dfrac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}N_{\mathrm{acc,0}}\cos^{2}\chi\cos\alpha\,(\boldsymbol{d}\times(\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{d}))

(C9)

and

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{prec}}=\dfrac{32\sqrt{2}}{3\pi}N_{\mathrm{acc,0}}\sin^{2}\chi\cos\alpha\,(\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{d})

(C10)

respectively
(
30
;
29
)
.

Until the disc angular momentum is aligned to the star spin axis (
\alpha=0
), the fundamental equations (
15
) and (
16
) must be supplemented with another one, describing the evolution of
\alpha

I_{0}\Omega\dfrac{\rm d\alpha}{\rm dt}=-{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{l},

(C11)

where
\boldsymbol{l}
is the unit vector that always lies within the
\boldsymbol{e}_{\Omega}-\boldsymbol{d}
plane, orthogonal to the
spin axis, and makes an acute angle with
\boldsymbol{d}
. However,
\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}
includes rapidly oscillating torques
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{acc}}
,
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{warp}}

{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{prec}}

\boldsymbol{\Omega}
with on the spin period time scale, when considered in the principal frame. Thus, following the approach used by
13
, we hereafter average the net torque projections over the spin period to obtain analytic expressions for the components of
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{\mathrm{ext,HX1}}

C11

(C12)
