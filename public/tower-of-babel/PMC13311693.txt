Expanding access to prehospital blood products: a narrative review of clinical evidence, systems barriers, and statewide strategies
Authors: Jamison P Geracci, Spencer M Knierim, Alexander F Bowers, Hannah L Borland, Evan Baines, Cory Lacek, Peter Antevy, Jennifer A Shuford, Kenneth A Scheppke, Traceee Rose, Eric Epley, John B Holcomb, Randall Schaefer, Donald H Jenkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic hemorrhage remains a leading cause of preventable death in the USA, and early administration of blood products improves outcomes in hemorrhagic shock. However, access to prehospital transfusion on ground ambulances remains limited and uneven. This narrative review synthesizes current clinical evidence, implementation barriers, and emerging models for prehospital blood use in the USA, with a focus on the potential role of statewide coordination within trauma systems. A targeted review of peer-reviewed literature, consensus guidelines, and system implementation reports was conducted, examining clinical efficacy, trauma system integration, regulatory frameworks, logistical considerations, reimbursement challenges, and statewide strategies. Approximately 3% of the 11 450 eligible US 9-1-1 ground emergency medical service (EMS) units currently provide prehospital blood transfusion, with access largely determined by local resources rather than patient need. Major barriers include logistical and stewardship challenges, lack of sustainable reimbursement pathways, absence of centralized data infrastructure, and the decentralized nature of the US blood supply. Clinical evidence supports the use of blood products for both traumatic and non-traumatic hemorrhage, demonstrating improvements in early hemodynamics and survival. Emerging efforts, including those led by the Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition and coordinated initiatives in states such as Texas, West Virginia, Florida, Delaware, and Colorado, highlight the feasibility of state-level implementation through shared governance, blood rotation strategies, and public investment. Overall, evidence supports the use of prehospital blood in selected populations, yet access remains limited and uneven. State-directed coordination represents a pragmatic framework to align existing blood centers, hospitals, and EMS agencies, although additional research, data integration, and national policy development are needed to guide broader implementation.

Introduction

Severe hemorrhage is a time-sensitive medical emergency and a leading cause of preventable death worldwide.1 Mortality from hemorrhagic shock is strongly associated with delayed resuscitation, particularly in rural and austere environments where time from injury to first transfusion (often after a long response time and transport to the hospital) exceeds 25 min.2 Over the past decade, mounting evidence has demonstrated that early administration of blood products improves outcomes for patients in hemorrhagic shock.3 4 In 2024, Duchesne et al showed that each minute initial transfusion is delayed results in up to 11% increased mortality per minute.5 Despite this evidence, prehospital blood transfusion on ground ambulances remains the exception rather than the norm in the USA. Of the 11 450 eligible ground emergency medical services (EMS) units nationwide, only 3% are currently equipped to provide blood products in the field.6 Many programs operate at the agency level, relying on individual partnerships between EMS agencies, hospitals, and blood suppliers. Organized statewide prehospital blood programs are largely absent. As a result, access to prehospital blood products is heavily dependent on geography and local initiative rather than clinical need. The disconnect between evidence and practice persists even as national modeling studies estimate that at least 54 000 and potentially as many as 9 00 000 patients annually could benefit from prehospital blood transfusion after injury.6 National-level efforts are progressing, although slowly. However, without statewide coordination, many patients, particularly those in rural or resource-limited areas, remain without access to this intervention.

Despite growing clinical evidence and emerging implementation efforts, access to prehospital blood products remains fragmented and highly dependent on local resources. This review seeks to evaluate the clinical evidence, identify key system-level barriers, and examine whether statewide coordination within trauma systems represents a scalable strategy to expand access to prehospital blood product transfusion.

Methods

This article is a narrative review synthesizing literature on prehospital blood transfusion, resuscitation using whole blood or other blood products, trauma system integration, and system-level implementation barriers.

Relevant articles were identified through targeted searches of PubMed using combinations of keywords including prehospital blood, whole blood, blood products, plasma, low-titer group O whole blood, EMS transfusion, hemorrhagic shock, and trauma systems.

Consensus guidelines and expert recommendations, including those from the Trauma, Hemostasis, and Oxygenation Research–Association for the Advancement of Blood & Biotherapies (AABB) Working Party, the Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition (PHBTC), the Allo Hope Foundation, and the joint position of the American College of Surgeons and National Association of EMS Physicians, were reviewed to contextualize clinical and operational standards.712 Reports describing regional and system-level implementation experiences were included to inform discussion of logistical, regulatory, and financial considerations.101318 State-level and national advocacy materials were also reviewed to inform recommendations.1823 This review was not intended to be systematic or meta-analytic, but rather to summarize current knowledge and identify gaps relevant to statewide implementation. System-level barriers to wider adoption

Logistical and operational challenges

Prehospital blood programs require robust systems for storage, temperature control, transport, and product rotation to minimize wastage. Implementation also requires specialized equipment, including validated cold-chain storage devices, continuous temperature monitoring systems, blood warmers, and transfusion supplies.7 13 These requirements contribute to both startup and ongoing program costs and may represent a barrier for some EMS agencies. These are the same issues that EMS agencies routinely face for medications.

An additional, often under-recognized logistical consideration is the impact of prehospital blood programs on the broader donor supply. Expanding prehospital blood use increases system demand and may require expansion of the donor pool. Without corresponding growth in blood donation, redistribution of existing supply could risk unintended strain on hospital-based transfusion services, particularly in regions already experiencing shortages. As such, successful large-scale implementation of prehospital blood programs must be accompanied by coordinated efforts to sustain and expand the donor base in parallel with distribution infrastructure.

In addition to expanding the donor pool overall, evolving transfusion practices may provide opportunities to increase usable supply without proportionally increasing total donation volume. Expanded utilization of low-titer group O whole blood derived from donors screened for transfusion-related acute lung injury risk, including human leukocyte antigen antibody–negative female donors, represents a potentially underutilized opportunity to increase available blood supply across both hospital and prehospital settings.24 Program-level data from established whole blood initiatives demonstrate robust donor participation while highlighting opportunities to further optimize donor selection and utilization strategies.25 These strategies suggest that addressing supply constraints will require not only increased donation, but also optimization of donor selection and blood product utilization practices.

Although these logistical demands have raised concerns regarding product stewardship, emerging operational data suggest that these challenges can be effectively addressed with coordinated system design.26 In a large urban EMS system, implementation of structured blood rotation and deployment strategies resulted in high utilization with no reported product wastage, demonstrating that careful integration with hospital-based transfusion services can mitigate concerns related to shelf life and inventory management.27 In South Texas specifically, regional partnerships among EMS agencies, trauma centers, and blood suppliers have enabled continuous rotation of whole blood units between ambulances and hospitals, achieving reported wastage rates of less than 1%.28 These findings suggest logistical barriers can be overcome through system-level coordination.

Financial and reimbursement barriers

Early operational analyses of prehospital whole blood programs demonstrate that implementation requires substantial resource utilization, including blood product procurement, logistics, and program infrastructure, highlighting the financial complexity of sustaining these programs.29 30 For many EMS agencies, the costs associated with blood procurement, specialized equipment, training, program administration, and sustainment remain prohibitive under the current reimbursement models where the estimated total cost per patient transfused is $1138 (US Dollars).30 An earlier 2019 analysis of Harris County Emergency Service District 48 Fire Department (HCESD 48; Harris County, Texas, USA) and San Antonio Fire Department (SAFD; San Antonio, Texas, USA) concluded that, for a well-established ground ambulance whole blood transfusion program in its tenth year running, the average comprehensive cost to save a life will be approximately $5136.51.29

Existing literature provides limited granular cost data regarding equipment and supply requirements for prehospital blood programs, but several important financial estimates are available. Analyses of prehospital plasma programs have reported that maintaining thawed plasma capability with a 5-day shelf life for air medical transport may exceed $24 000 per base annually, with some estimates ranging from $24 343 to $30 077 per base.31 Notably, these estimates do not include detailed breakdowns of specific equipment costs. Existing guidelines describe the required infrastructure—including validated cold-chain storage, continuous temperature monitoring systems, blood warmers, and transfusion supplies—but do not provide itemized pricing for these components.7

Cost-effectiveness analyses further suggest that although prehospital blood programs incur upfront and operational expenses, these costs represent only a portion of the total economic impact of hemorrhagic shock care. A secondary analysis of the PAMPer trial found that prehospital plasma transfusion was cost-effective at approximately $50 467 per quality-adjusted life-year, with the majority of incremental costs driven by downstream hospital and long-term care of survivors rather than prehospital program infrastructure.31 Despite this, a major barrier to implementation remains the lack of reimbursement, with a recent nationwide survey demonstrating that only 11% of EMS agencies with active prehospital blood programs receive any form of reimbursement.14 Existing literature identifies cost, equipment, stewardship, and reimbursement as central barriers to adoption.13

The lack of recognized reimbursement pathways for prehospital transfusion shifts the financial burden to local governments and agencies, resulting in fragmented implementation and inequitable access (table 1).

National efforts with Congress and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) to increase reimbursement for EMS have been initiated but will likely take time. State-led funding initiatives, such as those emerging in Texas, Colorado, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, and West Virginia, highlight a faster pathway toward shared public investment and underscore the need to recognize prehospital blood as essential trauma system infrastructure rather than an optional enhancement.1418 19 3234 Using the average of the initial funding proposed by the states of Texas and Florida as a baseline—$10 million and $14.6 million over two fiscal years, respectively—any other state in the USA would have to commit 24 cents per capita annually to sustain a statewide blood program.33 35 Three major limitations to that figure are the assumption that either Texas or Florida committed a sufficient amount, that it does not account for program startup costs, which would likely be closer to 50 cents per capita during the building phase, and the assumption that any other state has equal existing resources.

Data and quality infrastructure gaps

Prehospital and hospital data systems in the USA remain disjointed. The National EMS Information System (NEMSIS) captures EMS care, whereas trauma registries such as the American College of Surgeons’ Trauma Quality Improvement Program (TQIP) track in-hospital outcomes, yet there is no national infrastructure to link these datasets longitudinally.36 The absence of standardized data linkage prevents quantifying the impact of prehospital blood on morbidity, mortality, and resource utilization across the continuum of care. At present, any linkage between EMS events and hospital outcomes is performed locally, on a voluntary and highly variable basis, limiting opportunities for regional, state, and national trauma system performance improvement, research, and reimbursement advocacy. Furthermore, most of these linkages ignore the hospital outcomes of patients with non-trauma transfusions, which account for nearly 50% of transfusions in the rural and suburban settings.13

Absence of statewide blood center funding and coordination in the USA

Blood products are supplied through a decentralized network of independent and regional blood centers, each functioning within distinct governance, contractual, and logistical frameworks. Many countries outside the USA have nationalized blood systems, with all donations, collections, and distribution handled through a centralized system.

Although this decentralized model has historically supported hospital-based transfusion needs, the lack of national or state-level coordination presents significant challenges for scaling prehospital blood access. EMS agencies seeking to implement prehospital transfusion must independently establish agreements with blood suppliers and/or hospitals, often resulting in fragmented coverage, excessive administrative overhead, and inconsistent access across regions within the same state. This fragmentation complicates product rotation, stewardship, data sharing, and cost recovery, limiting the ability to extend access beyond isolated programs.

Importantly, the absence of a state-directed distribution framework does not imply a need for centralized ownership or operation of blood banks. Rather, it highlights an opportunity for blood suppliers to coordinate with hospitals and statewide EMS leadership to develop an implementation plan that does not add unnecessary strain on the donor supply and identifies areas with the greatest need for blood product placement.

Clinical evidence for prehospital blood

Blood products have emerged as the preferred resuscitative product for hemorrhagic shock in both military and civilian settings.26 3740 A prospective, randomized multicenter prehospital study by Sperry et al demonstrated that the administration of thawed plasma was safe and significantly lowered 30-day mortality in patients at risk for hemorrhagic shock.39 Multicenter retrospective studies have consistently demonstrated that prehospital blood administration is associated with improved outcomes, including improved early hemodynamics, reduced use of blood products on hospital arrival, reduced activation of massive transfusion protocols, a reduction of total blood transfused, and significant reductions in mortality among trauma patients.53942

A recent multicenter, randomized controlled trial from the UK (the SWiFT Trial) published in the New England Journal of Medicine found that prehospital transfusion of whole blood was not superior to standard component therapy in reducing death or massive transfusion within 24 hours, with similar outcomes observed across major clinical endpoints.43 These findings suggest that although early administration of blood products remains beneficial, the relative advantage of specific product types in the prehospital setting remains uncertain. This further emphasizes the importance of system-level factors such as timely access, logistics, and integration into trauma systems in optimizing outcomes.

In addition to trauma, transfusing prehospital blood has been associated with improved outcomes for patients with non-traumatic hemorrhage, including gastrointestinal bleeding, obstetric hemorrhage, and other causes of hemorrhagic shock.37 38 44 Observational experience noted by Hashmi et al suggests that as much as half of all prehospital blood is used for non-traumatic hemorrhage, underscoring that the potential impact of statewide programs extends beyond trauma alone.6 Clinician leaders report substantial survival benefits across trauma, medical, and obstetric populations, consistent with emerging civilian literature supporting early balanced resuscitation in hemorrhagic shock.6 38 40

Although low-titer group O whole blood (LTOWB) has emerged as the most transfused product for prehospital resuscitation, it remains unavailable in many regions of the USA. Many civilian hospitals and EMS agencies do not have routine access to LTOWB because their regional blood suppliers do not collect, manufacture, or distribute it at scale. As such, liquid plasma, which has a 26 day shelf life, has emerged as a viable, plentiful, and cheaper alternative with proven efficacy and improved 30-day mortality in patients at risk for hemorrhagic shock.4 39 41

Although prehospital blood products represent a critical advance in early resuscitation, they should be understood as one component of a broader hemorrhage control strategy that includes early bleeding control, tourniquet use, and public health initiatives such as community hemorrhage control training programs. Optimizing outcomes in hemorrhagic shock requires integration of these approaches within coordinated systems of care.

Existing guidance and successful models

Several groups have published detailed recommendations for the development of prehospital blood transfusion programs, emphasizing collaboration among EMS agencies, blood suppliers, and hospital transfusion services.7 9 45 These guidelines provide a practical roadmap for overcoming regulatory, operational, and safety concerns.

Multiple operational models for prehospital blood deployment have been described, and no single approach is universally applicable. In addition to rotation-based systems, alternative models include shared inventory models in which hospitals and EMS agencies jointly manage blood supply, hospital-based programs in which transfusion capability is deployed from centralized trauma centers, and air medical–integrated systems where blood products are primarily carried by aeromedical services.15 Each model reflects adaptation to local regulatory environments, geography, EMS structure, and blood supply logistics.9 15 Successful implementation depends on flexible, context-specific system design rather than a single standardized approach.13

Regional prehospital blood programs are emerging in multiple parts of the USA, illustrating that large-scale coordination is feasible outside of a single state.46 For example, EMS agencies in northern Colorado launched prehospital blood programs in partnership with UCHealth and Thompson Valley EMS, providing blood to patients with severe hemorrhage before hospital arrival across more than 3400 square miles and a service population exceeding 550 000.47 In the first 100 days of related initiatives involving UCHealth and the Colorado Springs Fire Department, these programs report having saved more than 30 lives, demonstrating the impact of coordinated prehospital blood administration even in geographically diverse regions. Other examples of successful ongoing regional efforts include but are not limited to West Virginia, Florida, Delaware, Virginia, Colorado, and Washington, DC.16 18 23 32 33 48 49

To facilitate implementation experience, the Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition maintains an interactive map documenting 9-1-1 ground EMS agencies with active blood transfusion programs, reflecting a rapidly increasing number of agencies incorporating blood products into field care. As of the date of submission of this paper, 350 ground agencies routinely provide prehospital blood products, a number that doubles each year.20 The map in figure 1 illustrates the locations and types of EMS Service Models being used in the USA. National reimbursement efforts and limitations of federal pathways

National reimbursement mechanisms in the USA have difficulty keeping pace with practice innovation. Current federal reimbursement for EMS is largely governed by the Medicare Ambulance Fee Schedule (AFS), which reimburses agencies based on transport level rather than the specific interventions provided during care.14 50 Under this framework, EMS continues to be viewed primarily as a transport service rather than as an integrated component of the acute care continuum, limiting recognition of interventions like blood transfusion.

In a nationwide survey of EMS agencies with active prehospital blood transfusion programs, only 11% reported receiving any form of reimbursement for blood products, despite widespread transfusion of Medicare and Medicaid eligible populations, including geriatric, pediatric, and obstetric patients.14 As such, transfusion costs must be absorbed locally in the absence of consistent reimbursement.

Several structural features of federal reimbursement help explain this lag. First, the AFS does not differentiate between trauma and medical hemorrhage, despite the fact that non-traumatic bleeding accounts for a substantial proportion of prehospital transfusions. Second, reimbursement remains encounter-based rather than system-based, providing no mechanism to support readiness costs such as blood inventory, cold-chain equipment, training, quality assurance, or administrative oversight. Third, national policy changes require prolonged regulatory processes and broad consensus, slowing adoption even when clinical justification is strong.

Potential role of statewide trauma system coordination

In contrast to national pathways, many recent efforts to expand prehospital blood access have occurred through state-level legislative and regulatory processes. Several states, including Texas and Florida, have proposed or implemented dedicated funding initiatives to support prehospital blood programs, reflecting growing recognition of these programs as essential components of trauma system infrastructure. These efforts illustrate how state-level policy mechanisms can be used to align EMS agencies, hospitals, and blood suppliers under shared funding and governance frameworks while federal efforts are advancing in parallel. State-level appropriations processes may also allow for more targeted allocation of resources to EMS systems compared with broader national reimbursement mechanisms, although further evaluation is needed. Although direct comparative evidence between state and national approaches remains limited, these emerging initiatives suggest that state-level coordination may represent an earlier, practical, and scalable pathway for expanding access within existing trauma systems.

Statewide prehospital blood programs enable system-wide standardization, coordination, and sustainability through centralized protocols that harmonize scope of practice, training requirements, quality assurance processes, and data collection across EMS agencies. This coordinated approach helps align blood stewardship models and may facilitate more efficient deployment of public funding. By strengthening collective coordination among EMS agencies, hospitals, and blood suppliers, statewide programs may improve reliable access to blood products, enhance financial sustainability, and have the potential to advance equity in access. As a result, state-directed efforts may represent a more effective near-term strategy for expanding prehospital blood access while national reimbursement frameworks evolve at a slower pace to ensure sustainable, long-term implementation.

Importantly, statewide programs may benefit from more timely funding approvals and allocations through state-level appropriations processes, rather than federal funding. This often means they can be funded sooner. Such programs hold particular promise for reducing long-standing disparities in trauma outcomes by extending early hemorrhage resuscitation to rural and underserved populations.46 These communities experience longer prehospital transport times and disproportionate trauma mortality and remain the least likely to have access to advanced prehospital interventions, training, and financial resources. In this context, statewide coordination represents not only an efficiency strategy but an opportunity to improve nationwide equity in access to healthcare.

Rural EMS agencies face structural barriers to prehospital blood implementation that differ fundamentally from those encountered in urban systems. Although prolonged transport times may be mitigated by the physiologic benefit of early transfusion, rural agencies sometimes operate with lower call volumes, less funding, limited staffing, and minimal administrative infrastructure.

These structural constraints are compounded by transport realities in rural systems. A cross-sectional analysis of the 2012 NEMSIS dataset found that in remote and frontier regions, 98.5% of transports occurred by ground and only 1.5% by air.51 Ground EMS therefore remains the predominant mode of transport in rural environments because of broader availability and lower operational costs. Although utilization of air transport may increase with distance from trauma centers, it continues to represent a small proportion of overall rural EMS transports. Consequently, equipping rural ground EMS units with blood products may represent the most scalable strategy to improve survival from hemorrhagic shock.

These constraints amplify concerns regarding blood product expiration, regulatory compliance, and financial loss. Many smaller agencies lack validated cold-chain storage or transport equipment, continuous temperature monitoring, and established transfusion service partnerships. Together, these factors help explain why prehospital blood access remains geographically uneven despite strong clinical rationale and growing evidence of benefit.

Medical oversight represents an additional, often under-recognized barrier in rural environments. The experience of EMS medical directors in these settings is highly variable, and they may have limited exposure to transfusion medicine, blood stewardship, or the regulatory frameworks governing blood use outside the hospital. This expertise gap fosters understandable risk aversion and slows adoption, particularly when individual agencies are expected to independently develop protocols, quality assurance processes, and blood bank agreements. Importantly, the AABB Standards for Emergency, Prehospital, and Scheduled Out-of-Hospital Transfusions identify the EMS Medical Director as responsible for the oversight and safety of both the patients and the blood products during transfusion operations, underscoring that prehospital transfusion is not merely a procedural intervention but a regulated clinical service.7 8 Implementation guidance consistently emphasizes that successful programs require close integration with transfusion services, standardized protocols, and robust oversight, capabilities that are difficult for isolated agencies or individual medical directors to assemble without external support.7 32

Volunteer-based staffing further shapes implementation considerations in many rural EMS systems. Although many volunteer EMS agencies provide high-quality care and serve as a lifesaving backbone of their communities, they often operate within structural constraints that differ from urban and career-based systems. Limited availability for extensive initial training and ongoing competency maintenance may present challenges for implementing complex interventions such as prehospital transfusion, which require strict protocol adherence and familiarity with infrequently used equipment. Although rural pilot programs demonstrate that these barriers are surmountable, success has depended on coordinated training, shared oversight, and external resources that often extend beyond the capacity of individual agencies.46 The experience in rural, volunteer-based EMS systems serves as an additional reminder that blood products are only a single component of a larger hemorrhage control strategy.

Statewide prehospital blood programs could offer a coherent solution by redistributing these burdens from individual rural agencies to the regional trauma system as a whole. Centralized scope-of-practice authorization, standardized protocols aligned with national best-practice frameworks, and state-supported education can provide medical directors with vetted guidance and expert backing while reducing variability and perceived risk. State-level training infrastructure can support volunteer agencies through shared curricula, regional training hubs, and standardized competency pathways. At the same time, coordinated blood rotation and stewardship models can minimize wastage despite low rural utilization, making participation feasible even for low-volume systems.

In this way, statewide programs do more than expand access to advanced resuscitative therapy. They have the potential to address a fundamental structural inequity in prehospital care by ensuring that rural patients with life-threatening hemorrhage receive timely, evidence-based treatment based on physiological need rather than local administrative capacity, workforce composition, or geographic location. By integrating rural agencies into a coordinated system of care, statewide prehospital blood programs have the potential to meaningfully improve outcomes while strengthening the resilience and equity of trauma systems nationwide. Several states such as Texas, Colorado, Virginia, West Virginia, Florida, Delaware, and Colorado are currently attempting to integrate and organize through existing structures that unify the entire state or organize statewide efforts with unified regional councils. These programs are in various stages of development and have seen varied success. Future research and data from these organizations will likely be useful to gauge cost-effectiveness and guide future implementation in other regions.

Emerging statewide efforts: Texas as a case example

Although still in its early stages of development, Texas is advancing toward a more coordinated, statewide approach to prehospital blood product access, offering an example of how policy, clinical leadership, and operational partnerships can align to address longstanding barriers. As one of the largest and most geographically diverse states in the nation, Texas faces many of the same challenges that have historically limited prehospital blood adoption nationwide, including long transport times, variable EMS capabilities, and fragmented trauma resources, especially in rural and frontier regions.52

In recent years, Texas has seen increasing collaboration among trauma centers, EMS agencies, blood suppliers, and professional organizations to expand prehospital blood availability beyond isolated regional programs. These efforts have focused on standardizing protocols, clarifying scope-of-practice considerations, and developing sustainable logistical and stewardship models that support both urban and rural EMS systems. This work reflects a shift from single agency-level innovation toward system-level coordination, a critical requirement for statewide scalability.

In parallel with these efforts, emerging statewide funding structures have incorporated mechanisms to support the underlying blood supply. For example, in Texas, contractual agreements between the State and Regional Advisory Councils (RACs) receiving funding for prehospital blood programs have incorporated expectations to sponsor additional community blood drives, typically ranging from one to three annually. These efforts are intended to help offset increased system demand and ensure that expansion of prehospital blood access does not compromise existing hospital-based transfusion capacity. This approach illustrates how statewide coordination can align funding, implementation, and donor recruitment within a unified system strategy.

The scale of the potential impact in Texas is substantial. State trauma leaders report that over 13 000 traffic-related deaths occurred in Texas over a recent 3-year period, with approximately one-third of patients initially alive at the scene before succumbing to their injuries.53 This observation highlights a large population of potentially salvageable patients who may benefit from earlier hemorrhage control and resuscitation. Experience from the Southwest Texas Regional Trauma Advocacy (STRAC) program further suggests that prehospital blood administration could reduce trauma mortality by as much as 25% and has been associated with reductions in subsequent in-hospital transfusion requirements, yielding both clinical benefit and potential cost savings.5 28 The STRAC regional program was explicitly designed to eliminate geographic disparities by extending access across urban, rural, and frontier counties through coordinated blood rotation and shared governance structures. Importantly, fewer than 10% of EMS agencies—approximately 60 EMS agencies in 40 of the 254 Texas counties—currently participate in prehospital blood programs, underscoring the difficulty of implementation, the magnitude of unmet need, and the opportunity that statewide expansion seeks to address.28 This pattern reflects a broader national challenge in which access to prehospital blood remains highly dependent on local initiative and resources rather than patient need.

Texas’s evolving approach highlights several lessons relevant to other states considering similar initiatives. First, statewide engagement allows for harmonization of medical oversight and transfusion practices, reducing variability and improving safety. Second, centralized coordination facilitates data collection and quality improvement, enabling continuous evaluation of clinical outcomes, blood utilization, and program performance. Third, emerging and approved state-level funding proposals, like the one approved by the Texas Legislature, illustrate growing recognition that prehospital blood access represents essential trauma system infrastructure, warranting shared public investment rather than reliance on local agency resources alone or waiting on national-level efforts to emerge.54

Although comprehensive outcome data from a fully implemented statewide program are not yet available, Texas’s ongoing efforts demonstrate the feasibility of transitioning from regional pilots to broader system-level implementation.47 Texas EMS leaders, using recent national modeling, predict that 2000 lives could be saved annually in Texas by statewide implementation of a prehospital blood program.55 As these initiatives mature, they will provide critical insights into governance structures, stewardship models, funding strategies, and equity considerations necessary for successful statewide prehospital blood programs. The Texas experience therefore serves as an important transitional model, bridging the gap between localized innovation and true statewide adoption.

Importantly, in December 2025, Florida Governor Ron DeSantis announced his proposed budget for the next fiscal year, which includes a similar commitment of $14.6 million to establish Florida’s own statewide system for prehospital blood products.33 This proposed statewide initiative aligns with the system-level framework discussed in this review. Florida EMS leaders predict that 1000 lives could be saved annually by statewide implementation of a prehospital blood program.6 55

Future directions, research needs, and policy implications

To close the gap between evidence and practice, policy-level interventions are needed. These include explicit inclusion of blood transfusion within EMS scope of practice, development of state and national reimbursement pathways, investment in statewide data infrastructure, and continued research into outcomes and cost-effectiveness.

As trauma systems evolve to address preventable death, prehospital blood products should be viewed not as an optional enhancement but as a core component of hemorrhage control and resuscitation.

The Texas and Florida experience further suggests that modest state investment may catalyze substantial system-level benefit. Legislative proposals have emphasized funding to support blood procurement, equipment, training, and administrative coordination, with the goal of ensuring at least baseline blood access across all counties. Such models offer a rapid blueprint for other states seeking to initiate statewide programs without imposing unsustainable financial burdens on local EMS agencies.

As trauma systems continue to evolve, future iterations of trauma center verification standards should include prehospital blood access as a marker of system readiness for hemorrhage care. Integration of prehospital blood availability into trauma system performance metrics or verification frameworks could further incentivize coordinated implementation, particularly when aligned with regional blood stewardship, EMS medical oversight, and quality improvement processes.

Limitations

This narrative review does not include a formal systematic search strategy, risk-of-bias assessment, or meta-analysis. Findings may therefore reflect limitations of the included literature and unpublished implementation reports. Nonetheless, this approach allows synthesis of emerging clinical evidence and system-level experiences relevant to statewide implementation.

Conclusion

Prehospital blood administration has demonstrated clinical benefit in both traumatic and non-traumatic hemorrhage. Despite this growing evidence, access within the USA remains fragmented, with substantial system-level barriers related to scope of practice, logistics, reimbursement, data integration, and blood supply.

Emerging regional and statewide efforts show that these barriers are not insurmountable. State-directed models that leverage existing trauma system infrastructure can align protocols, blood stewardship, training, quality improvement, and funding in ways that isolated agency-level programs cannot. By treating prehospital blood access as essential emergency care system infrastructure, states have been able to deploy public investment more rapidly and equitably.

Although national reimbursement and policy efforts continue to progress, state-level coordination currently represents a pathway to rapidly expand access. Continued evaluation of statewide implementation models will be critical to informing best practices and guiding policy development as access to prehospital blood continues to expand across the USA.
