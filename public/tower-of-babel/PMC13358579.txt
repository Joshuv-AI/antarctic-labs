GAN-generated target reconstruction CT radiomics for prediction of the novel IASLC grading of pulmonary adenocarcinoma
Authors: Qinling Jiang, Wei Deng, Xin’ang Jiang, Hongbiao Sun, Xiang Wang, Xianpan Pan, Zirui Cao, Gang Xiang, Ya Wen, Qi Chen, Yuxin Cheng, Tianyi Xing, Lei Chen, Yi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe novel International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) pathologic grading system provides a better stratification of prognosis. This study aims to develop a radiomics model based on the generative-adversarial-network (GAN)-generated target reconstruction computed tomography (GTRCT) for the preoperative prediction of IASLC grading and to compare that with the normal-resolution computed tomography (NRCT).MethodsThis retrospective study included patients pathologically diagnosed with invasive pulmonary adenocarcinoma (IPA) from July 2018 to December 2021 from Changzheng Hospital and The Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Southwest Medical University. Among them, 122 patients from Hospital 2 served as a separate external validation set. By extracting image features and using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator to screen the features, radiomics models were constructed to predict the novel IASLC grading of IPA, and the model performance of GTRCT and NRCT was compared. The diagnostic performance of the models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. In addition, calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were applied to validate two different models.ResultsThis retrospective study included 396 patients (mean age: 60.41±10.35 years; male: 184) from the two hospitals. Radiomics models based on GTRCT and NRCT both demonstrated good predictive performance for pathological grading. The GTRCT-based prediction model achieved area under the curve (AUC) of 0.943, 0.942, and 0.844 in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively, compared with 0.92, 0.935 and 0.751 for the NRCT-based model. Calibration and Brier scores confirmed good agreement, the decision curve image showed a positive net benefit for the GTRCT-based model.ConclusionsThe GTRCT-based radiomics model exhibits good accuracy in predicting the novel IASLC grading of IPA and achieves superior performance compared to the NRCT-based model. It thus helps evaluate patient prognosis and offers more comprehensive preoperative guidance for clinical treatment selection.

Introduction

Background

In 2022, there were 2.48 million new cases of lung cancer worldwide, accounting for 12.4% of all newly diagnosed cancers globally (1). According to Cancer Statistics 2024, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with an estimated 611,720 fatalities in the United States (2). Cancer grading has long played an essential role in pathology assessment, providing critical guidance for treatment decisions and patient management across various organ systems (3-5). In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) classified adenocarcinoma into three prognostic types based on the main histopathological type: low grade (lepidic predominant); intermediate grade (acinar or papillary predominant); and high grade (solid or micropapillary predominant). However, this classification overlooked the complex glandular patterns with poor prognosis (6). Additionally, the lack of precise percentage thresholds for high grade led to inconsistencies and low reproducibility in clinical practice (7).

The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) has introduced a novel three-tier grading system for invasive pulmonary adenocarcinoma (IPA) (8), categorizing complex glandular patterns as high-grade due to their poor prognosis. This system has established a practical threshold of ≥20% for classifying high-grade patterns, improving prognostic stratification and facilitating its rapid integration into routine pathology practice. Consequently, the new grading system provides better prognostic stratification compared to its predecessor.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Accurate diagnosis of the pathological grading of IPA relies on histological tumour assessment. However, this approach has inherent limitations, as it cannot guide surgical decision-making preoperatively and is unsuitable for unresectable cases or patients unable to tolerate surgery. In contrast, for the pathological grading of adenocarcinoma, the overall concordance between frozen and permanent sections shows moderate agreement for identifying the predominant pathological pattern (kappa: 0.43–0.58) (9-11). Preoperative small sample biopsies are constrained by both the site and sample size. Among 326 patients, only 211 (66.0%) had a predominant lung adenocarcinoma subtype diagnosed from the biopsy that was consistent with the results of the resection analysis (12). In addition, while percutaneous biopsy can preoperatively detect the pathological subtype of lung adenocarcinoma, it may increase the risk of metastasis (13). Therefore, accurate and non-invasive prediction of pathologic subtypes using preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging is essential for improved clinical treatment.

Numerous studies illustrated that high-resolution CT with a 1,024×1,024 matrix substantially improved image clarity and provided more detailed information compared to conventional CT scanning protocols. This improvement enabled radiologists to detect and visualize lesions (14-16). The targeted CT scan, which focused on specific lesion areas, addressed the limitations of conventional CT in lung cancer screening and improved lesion diagnosis accuracy (17,18). Therefore, in this study, the dual generative-adversarial-network (GAN) cascaded network (DGCN) method was employed to achieve 1,024×1,024 target reconstruction from conventional 512×512 CT images.

Objective

This study aims to develop and validate a radiomics-based prediction model using GAN-generated target reconstruction CT (GTRCT) for the IASLC grading of IPA and to compare its performance with normal-resolution CT (NRCT)-based models, thereby providing a robust tool for preoperative planning and prognosis assessment. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0717/rc).

Methods

Patient

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional review boards of Changzheng Hospital (No. 2024SL005) and The Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Southwest Medical University (No. BY2025078). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

A total of 396 patients with preoperative CT scans were included in this study, with data consecutively collected from Changzheng Hospital and The Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Southwest Medical University between July 2018 and December 2021. Among them, 122 patients from hospital 2 served as a separate external validation set.

The inclusion criteria were (I) patients who underwent CT scans prior to surgery; (II) thin-slice (≤1.0 mm) CT images; (III) pathologically confirmed IPA with comprehensive pathology reports enabling IASLC grading. The exclusion criteria were (I) the lesion appears as a pure ground-glass nodule; (II) insufficient CT quality due to respiratory or motion artifacts; (III) patients who underwent any anti-cancer treatment before surgery. CT acquisition parameters

All patients were scanned in the supine position at the end of inhalation during breath-holding. The CT scanners included SIEMENS iCT 256, Philips CT128, and General Electric 64 in hospital 1, and SIEMENS Domatomemotion16 and DSOMATM Definition Flash in hospital 2. The CT scan parameters in two hospitals were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, automatic mA; rotation time, 0.5 s/rot; matrix size, 512×512; reconstruction algorithm, sharp; slice thickness, 0.5–1 mm; scan range, from lung apex to the costophrenic angle.

Dual GAN cascaded ultra-high-resolution model

Our team has previously completed the construction of a dual GAN cascaded ultra-high-resolution model and achieved good results (PSNR of 52.16, SSIM of 0.9941, FID of 137.713). As shown in Figure 2, here is an overview of the model architecture.

Schematic diagram of two-channel enhanced target reconstruction GAN model. Yellow arrows indicate the location of the lesion. GAN, generative-adversarial-network; LReLU, leaky rectified linear unit; RRDB, residual in residual dense block.

To ensure comparability across imaging data acquired from different devices, all CT images were resampled to a uniform spatial resolution of 0.698×0.698×1 mm3 using bicubic interpolation as an input for the NRCT-based pathology grading prediction model. Additionally, we used the target reconstruction model (19), developed by our group to map the full-scan data into GAN-generated target scan data, as shown in Figure S1. These data were enhanced to GTRCT images with a spatial resolution of 0.1745×0.1745×0.5 mm3. The processed data were used to train the GTRCT-based pathology grading model.

The GTRCT incorporated image data from a total of 411 cases across two centers as the training and validation sets, all of which were completely independent of the cohort in this study. The process of target reconstruction model training involved three main steps. First, lung nodules undergoing pathological examination were centrally cropped, and local CT scan data were extracted with a voxel size of 256×256×64. Second, this localized data was input into a cascade network consisting of two enhanced super-resolution generative adversarial networks (ESRGANs) to generate target reconstruction data with a voxel size of 1,024×1,024×128. Besides, the three-dimensional (3D) CT scan data were sliced along the horizontal plane to obtain 64 two-dimensional (2D) slices with a size of 256×256, which were input into the first ESRGAN to generate 128 slices with a size of 1,024×1,024 and reconstructed by splicing. Last, the data were sliced again along the sagittal plane to obtain 1,024 slices with a size of 1,024×64, which were processed by the second ESRGAN to generate 1,024 slices with a size of 1,024×128. These slices were then spliced and reconstructed to obtain GTRCT data of the lung with a spatial resolution of 0.1745×0.1745×0.5 mm3. For more detailed information about the model, please refer to the previous article (19).

Pathological evaluation

All pathology slides were reviewed using the 2020 IASLC grading criteria. The fundamental histological subtype percentages were recorded in 5% increments and pathologic grades were assigned according to pathologic findings. (I) Grade I (well-differentiated): lepidic-predominant with no or <20% high-grade pattern; (II) Grade II (moderately differentiated): acinar or papillary-predominant no or <20% high-grade pattern; (III) Grade III (poorly differentiated): any tumor with ≥20% high-grade pattern.

Notably, patients with grade III IPA demonstrated significantly worse five-year recurrence-free survival (76.4%) compared to those with grade I and II (96.7% and 85.6%). Besides, grade III patients may benefit from personalized treatments, such as prompt lobectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy (8,20-22). Therefore, early differentiation of grade III from grade I/II may provide additional clinical value. In our study, grades I and II were categorized in the same group. Radiomics model construction

The uAI Research Portal is an artificial intelligence (AI) platform that integrates deep learning and machine learning algorithms. Its intelligent-assisted detection software for lung nodules, centered on this model, has been awarded NMPA Class III certification (23,24). The patient’s lung window image sequences were imported into the uAI research platform in DICOM format, where preprocessing steps such as anonymization and image normalization was performed. The region of interest (ROI) of the lung nodule was then automatically detected and segmented using the platform’s built-in model.

The segmentation results were reviewed and confirmed layer by layer by two radiologists (six years, eight years of experience in diagnosing chest diseases) in the lung window (level, −500 HU; width, 1,500 HU) with necessary modifications. Both radiologists were blinded to the patient’s pathology report, and structures such as blood vessels and bronchial tubes were excluded as much as possible during the outlining process. Any discrepancies between lesion descriptions and measurements on the CT image were resolved by consulting a third senior physician (22 years of experience in the diagnosis of chest diseases).

Manual segmentation is widely accepted as the reference standard. To evaluate its agreement with fully automatic segmentation, we randomly selected 40 patients from the study cohort. For these cases, CT images were transferred to ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0, www.itksnap.org) to perform manual delineation. The spatial overlap between the two segmentation approaches was quantified using the Dice similarity coefficient, an objective metric of agreement. After the consistency assessment, the remaining patients were processed using the automated segmentation pipeline.

The open-source Python package (PyRadiomics, version 3.0.1; https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io) was largely compliant with the image biomarker standardisation initiative (IBSI). The research platform, based on this open-source package, extracted 2,264 radiomics features from the imaged lung nodal lesions. These features are categorized into four groups: first-order, texture, shape, and higher-order statistical features.

To evaluate the robustness of radiomic features, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. Features with a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.90 were considered to have excellent robustness and reproducibility and were retained for subsequent feature selection and model construction.

Before feature selection, radiomics features were normalized using z-score normalization of the main cohort. First, features that significantly differed between the two groups were identified using the Mann-Whitney U test (P<0.05). Second, according to the Max-Relevance and Min-Redundancy (mRMR) index based on a heuristic evaluation criterion, the radiomics features were reduced to ten. Then, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method was selected to identify the most predictive features. To find the optimal adjustment weights in the LASSO logistic regression, we used a minimum standard tenfold cross-validation, where the value of λ produced the smallest binomial bias. Then, multivariate logistic regression with stepwise selection was used to construct the radiomics model. The radiomics features of each patient were calculated as linear combinations of the selected features and weighted by their respective coefficients (Figure 4).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were executed using SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM) and R software (version 4.3.1 https://www.R-project.org). Mean ± standard deviation (SD) and proportions represented continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The normality of quantitative variables was performed by means of the Shapiro-Wilk test. Independent samples t-test was used to assess normally distributed data. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed for the non-normally distributed data. For categorical variables, chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were performed. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The predictive performance of the models was analyzed and evaluated by ROC curves. The DeLong test was applied to determine whether models were statistically significant.

Evaluation of consistency between manual and fully automatic segmentation

To assess segmentation agreement, the Dice similarity coefficient was employed as an objective metric that provides a quantitative measure of spatial overlap across two delineated boundaries. The average Dice value for manual segmentation relative to its automatic counterpart was calculated to be 0.94, with a standard deviation of 0.05.

NRCT-based, GTRCT-based radiomics models predict IASLC grading

A total of 2,264 radiomics features were extracted from CT images. A total of 2,097 radiomic features with Pearson correlation coefficient >0.90 were finally retained, which guaranteed the reliability and repeatability of the subsequent radiomics modeling. Finally, nine features were finally used to construct the NRCT-based radiomics model, while eight features were used for the GTRCT-based radiomics model. The GTRCT-based prediction model achieved accuracies of 0.858, 0.873, and 0.91, respectively, compared with 0.799, 0.836, and 0.779 in the NRCT-based model. As shown in Figure 5, the GTRCT-based prediction model achieved area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.943 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.9090–0.9765], 0.942 (95% CI: 0.8783–1.0000), 0.844 (95% CI: 0.7234–0.9648) in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively, compared with 0.92 (95% CI: 0.8784–0.9626), 0.935 (95% CI: 0.8649–1.0000), and 0.751 (95% CI: 0.6297–0.8730) in the NRCT-based model. NRCT and GTRCT-based radiomics prediction models had good performance, achieving AUC values above 0.75 in all cohorts. Especially in the training set and the internal test set, the ACU values exceeded 0.9. The GTRCT-based prediction model outperformed the NRCT-based prediction model in terms of accuracy and AUC across all cohorts. Model differences and clinical utility of models

DeLong’s test revealed significant differences in the AUC values of the NRCT-based and GTRCT-based prediction models in the training (P=0.04) and external validation sets (P=0.025). The GTRCT-based prediction model outperformed better than the other in distinguishing low differentiation grades of IPA.

Through the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, calibration curves indicated that there was no significant departure between prediction and observation in internal and external validation set (Figure S2). Calibration and Brier scores confirmed good agreement. The decision curve analysis, which balanced the benefits of the model (e.g., accurately identifying cases) against its costs (e.g., misdiagnosis consequences), was used to calculate the net benefit of the model at various cutoff values to evaluate its performance. As shown in Figure 6, both predictive models in this study provided better predictive performance compared to the “no model” approach (i.e., always positive or always negative) across all thresholds. Besides, the GTRCT-based model outperformed the NRCT-based model at most thresholds.

Discussion

Key findings

The new IASLC pathologic grading system for IPA has demonstrated better prognostic accuracy compared to the original guideline. However, there is still a lack of reliable methods for accurately predicting IASLC grading preoperatively. In this study, we used a cascaded GAN network to convert NRCT to GTRCT, and constructed radiomics prediction models for IASLC grading based on NRCT and GTRCT, respectively. The results illustrated that the radiomics model based on preoperative GTRCT was able to predict the pathological grading of IPA, and outperformed the prediction model based on NRCT.

Explanations of findings

The difference in its features may be the direct cause of the difference in efficacy between the two prediction models. The GTRCT-based prediction model extracted eight radiomics features, while the NRCT-based model extracted nine radiomics features. First, shotnoise_gldm_LowGrayLevelEmphasis was common to both prediction models. This metric that may be related to pathological features such as tumor cell density, which helps to understand pathological changes from an imaging perspective. In addition, from the analysis of the overall distribution of features, most of the features in NRCT were focused on describing the distribution of low grey levels of the image. Whereas in GTRCT there were additional features describing the ability to recognise high grey level images and different levels of organisational structure. In summary, the ultra-high resolution model had more dimensions to describe the image although the number of features was reduced, and this might be one of the reasons why the efficacy of the ultra-high resolution model was superior to that of the conventional resolution model.

The practical clinical relevance of the GTRCT model is reflected in several aspects: the model significantly improves sensitivity while maintaining high specificity, thereby helping to avoid missed diagnoses; meanwhile, studies indicate that Grade 3 patients require more aggressive treatment (e.g., lobectomy combined with adjuvant chemotherapy), and the higher AUC and accuracy of the GTRCT model enhance preoperative patient classification, which is of great significance for developing individualized treatment plans; furthermore, the GTRCT model shows a smaller performance decline in the external validation set, demonstrating stronger robustness across different scanners and institutions, making it more suitable for clinical implementation.

Comparison with similar research

Various radiological features have been proposed to help identify different pathological subtypes and classify patients accordingly (25), but the performance of these features in correctly identifying subtypes of lung cancer has not been thoroughly evaluated to reach a consensus. Ding et al. developed a deep learning model to predict the micropapillary structure of lung adenocarcinoma (26). However, only the microscopic pattern was investigated in their study. Dong et al. and Wang et al. demonstrated that CT features such as lobulation and pleural indentation were considered independent predictors of MP/S (27-29). However, a study by Gao et al. (30) found that these imaging features (e.g., lobulation, fine vesicles, pleural indentation, etc.) played a limited role in identifying adenocarcinoma subtypes. This may be due to the fact that many variables affect the interpretation of images, and some of the tumor morphology and margin features appear similar across different lung cancer subtypes (31), resulting in significant inter-observer variability, where different radiologists may interpret the same image in different ways.

The identification of tumor imaging features with the naked eye was subjective and limited by image resolution, while radiomics enabled the rapid extraction of internal quantitative features and their translation into large amounts of analyzable data (32). Previous studies have shown that radiomics models can identify MP/S patterns of IPA (33). Wang et al. (28) and He et al. (34) predicted MP/S patterns by CT radiomics, but their models exhibited only moderate performance, limiting their suitability for clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

Thus, we made the following improvements based on the previous studies. Firstly, we employed a GAN-generated target reconstruction to enhance the resolution and image quality of CT images. Image quality was critical for the accurate diagnosis of pulmonary nodules. There may have been technical limitations in previous examinations, such as the uneven spatial resolution of images and limited voxel data, which may compromise the accuracy of extracted radiomics features (35). HRCT with 1,024×1,024 matrix scanning has been shown to provide better subjective image quality, more precise lesion measurement protocols, and enables radiologists to observe and diagnose lesions comprehensively (16,36). However, most existing studies are based on the subjective evaluation of the image quality of HRCT versus NRCT as an indicator, and there is a lack of comparisons between the two for applications in building prediction models. This study bridges this gap. By applying a dual GAN cascaded network to generate ultra-high-resolution target reconstruction images of 1,024×1,024 matrix, we explore and validate the efficacy of high-resolution CT images in novel IASLC grading prediction of invasive lung adenocarcinoma, which is of practical clinical application. Besides, most existing radiomics studies explored the efficacy of radiomics in predicting the MP/S components of IPA by manually segmenting ROIs. However, manual segmentation is complex and time-consuming (28,37-40), and is inherently prone to inter- and intra-observer variability (41). To minimize these potential biases and enhance segmentation accuracy, we used automatic detection and segmentation of lesions. All segmentation results were reviewed and corrected by two experienced radiologists, ensuring the reliability of results while reducing the manual workload and variability of ROI segmentation. Furthermore, in the study by Park (42), although an automated segmentation method was applied, the imaging data from only one hospital was included. Similarly, most previous prediction studies on high-grade components of IPA were single-center studies that lacked external validation (29,37,43-45), which could lead to potential data selection bias, resulting in limited clinical universality of the findings. In this study, imaging data from two hospitals and several different devices were included, along with an independent validation set comprising 122 cases from Hospital 2. These measures strengthened the robustness and credibility of our findings.

Ground glass nodules (GGNs) were excluded from this study for two main reasons. First, persistent pure ground glass nodules (pGGNs) mostly represent adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) in pathological terms (46,47), both of which are characterized by slow growth (48,49). Some studies indicated that the median growth period for pGGNs was 7.7 years (50), leading to a clinical preference for long-term follow-up rather than surgical resection (51). Second, Lin et al. showed that among 985 cases of adenocarcinoma presenting as GGNs on CT, only 0.8% exhibited micropapillary or solid (MIP/SOL) patterns on pathologic staging (52). This finding aligns with the studies by Katsumata (53) and Chen et al. (54), which reported that ground glass opacity (GGO) lesions are typically low-risk and rarely display a MIP/SOL component. Consequently, IPAs that manifest as pGGN tend to be grade I-II, making pathological grading less challenging compared to partially solid and solid nodules. This study aimed to address the clinical challenges in the pathological grading of adenocarcinoma, and the exclusion of pGGNs aligned with the distribution characteristics of clinical cases and was of more clinical value.

There are several limitations to this study. First, as a retrospective study, selection bias may be inevitable. Moreover, prior researches have shown that MP/S adenocarcinoma are more commonly observed in men and smokers (30,55), suggesting that patient-related clinical information could provide valuable insights. In the future, we plan to conduct further studies incorporating clinical features. In addition, we plan to follow up our patients over time for a more comprehensive analysis of prognosis, which will be of greater clinical significance. Additionally, although we performed image resampling and intensity normalization to reduce technical variability, we did not apply dedicated batch-effect harmonization methods such as ComBat to account for multi-scanner differences across the two hospitals. Given that radiomics features can be influenced by scanner manufacturers and acquisition parameters, the absence of such harmonization may have introduced residual bias and potentially affected the generalizability of our models. Future work will incorporate ComBat or similar approaches to further improve the robustness of GTRCT-based radiomics in multi-center settings. Although SHAP analysis enhanced the interpretability of our radiomics models by revealing the contribution of individual features, it remains primarily descriptive and has not yet been translated into a direct clinical tool. To bridge this gap, future work will focus on developing a simplified SHAP-based scoring system or nomogram that can be easily applied at the bedside. Prospective studies are needed to validate whether such interpretability aids improve clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

The GTRCT-based radiomics models can more accurately predict the new IASLC grading of IPA compared to the radiomics model based on NRCT images, helping choose the appropriate surgical method and develop a personalized diagnosis and treatment plan for the patients.
