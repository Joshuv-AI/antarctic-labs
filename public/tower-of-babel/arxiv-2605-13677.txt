Decoherence of spatial superpositions along stationary worldlines
Clemens Jakubec, Aaron Bartleson, Peter W. Milonni, Kanu Sinha

Abstract
We analyze the decoherence of a particle’s spatial superposition moving along a stationary worldline through the Minkowski vacuum. The particle is modeled via an internal degree of freedom that couples to a scalar field, and an external degree of freedom, i.e., its quantized center-of-mass motion around the stationary worldline. Assuming a separation of time scales between the particle’s internal and external dynamics, we first obtain an effective red-shifted polarizability of the particle, characterizing the trajectory-dependent linear response of the internal oscillator to the field. We then derive a quantum Brownian motion master equation for the particle’s center of mass, under the Born-Markov approximation, which describes its decoherence in the position basis, as well as Hamiltonian modifications corresponding to a dispersive potential. The resulting decoherence has two components: (1) arising from a modified field spectrum observed by the particle; and (2) due to a differential time dilation over the particle’s extended spatial wavefunction. For stationary trajectories, both contributions take an effectively thermal form. We evaluate the decoherence rates for two specific cases, hyperbolic and uniform circular motion.

Introduction

A particle undergoing uniformly accelerated linear motion through the Minkowski vacuum of a quantum field will perceive a thermal field environment. This effective thermality of the environment, also known as the Davies-Unruh effect

In this paper, we analyze the open system dynamics of the quantized center-of-mass motion of a particle moving along a stationary worldline and interacting with a scalar field

The decoherence seen by the particle results both from the modified spectrum of the field seen by the particle (Davies-Unruh decoherence), as well as the differential time dilation over the particle’s wavefunction (time-dilation decoherence).

Open System Dynamics

III.1
Quantum Brownian Motion Master Equation

In order to model the center-of-mass decoherence of our particle, we employ the Quantum Brownian Motion (QBM) master equation, which describes the reduced dynamics of the quantized center-of-mass density matrix
\rho
. We expand the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture (indicated by an explicit time dependence) in small
\hat{X}^{i}

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}(\tau)+\partial_{i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau)\hat{X}^{i}+\mathcal{O}((\hat{X}^{i})^{2})

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}(\tau)+\hat{B}(\tau)\hat{X}^{i}+\mathcal{O}((\hat{X}^{i})^{2}),

(21)

with the bath operator defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{i}(\tau)=\partial_{i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau)

(22)

Following our split of the interaction Hamiltonian into time-dilation and Davies-Unruh terms, we can do the same for the bath operator and define:

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{i}(\tau)=\hat{B}^{\mathrm{DU}}_{i}(\tau)+\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{i}(\tau).

(23)

Here the time-dilation and Davies-Unruh bath operators are defined, respectively, as:

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{\mathrm{DU}}_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau),\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\};

(24)

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{i}}{4c^{2}}\left\{\dot{\hat{d}}_{1,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)-\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau),\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\}.

(25)

The QBM master equation resulting from the above system-bath interaction is thus

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}=

\displaystyle-\Lambda_{ij}\left[\hat{X}^{i},\left[\hat{X}^{j},\hat{\rho}\right]\right]-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\Gamma_{ij}\left[\hat{X}^{i},\left\{\hat{P}^{j},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}C^{(1)}_{i}\left[\hat{X}^{i},\hat{\rho}\right]-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}C^{(2)}_{ij}\left[\hat{X}^{i},\left\{\hat{X}^{j},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right]

(26)

with
i,j
corresponding to the three spatial dimensions in the rest frame of the particle;
repeated indices are summed over.

The diagonal terms

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}=\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\left\{\hat{B}_{i}(\tau),\hat{B}_{j}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\}\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2M\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\left[\hat{B}_{i}(\tau),\hat{B}_{j}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle.

\displaystyle C^{(2)}_{ij}\equiv

III.2
Decoherence

Using the definition of the bath operators from the previous section and the interaction Hamiltonians Eqs. (
20
) and (
19

\displaystyle\Lambda=\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}+\Lambda^{\mathrm{TD}}.

(31)

Here
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}
represents the ‘Davies-Unruh decoherence’, arising from
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\mathrm{DU}}
, i.e., the backaction of the field on the particle, and
\Lambda^{\mathrm{TD}}
corresponds to the ‘time dilation decoherence’ due to
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\mathrm{TD}}
, i.e., the interaction between the field and the system from time dilation. Since the interaction Hamiltonian is quadratic in the field, the decoherence coefficients thus amounts to calculating fourth-order field correlators along the worldline of the particle. We make the following simplifying assumptions: (i) we assume that the field state observed by a particle moving along a stationary worldline is Gaussian, meaning that we can apply Wick’s theorem to write the four-point correlators in terms of two-point correlators along the worldline of the particle. This also implies that the field and its spatial derivative are statistically independent, i.e. that
\langle\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\rangle=0

43
; and, (ii) we assume that different spatial directions of the center-of-mass are uncorrelated. This implies that
\langle\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
, indicating that decoherence along different spatial directions occurs independently of one another. It follows from these assumptions that we can write the four-point correlators in terms of
\langle\hat{\phi}(\tau)\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\rangle
and
\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\rangle
only.

if
i=j
and zero otherwise and we have used the assumptions stated earlier. To proceed we note that the correlator of the spatial derivatives can be turned into the correlator of the time derivative:

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(X)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(X^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{3c^{2}}\left\langle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(X)\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(X^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(33)

Since we are working in the local rest frame of the particle, the coordinate time derivative equals the proper time derivative. Spectrally decomposing the field and evaluating the
\tau
integral, we are left with (see Appendix
C
for details):

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}=\frac{1}{6\pi\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{6}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega).

(34)

Here
D^{+}(\omega)
and
D^{-}(\omega)
are the Fourier transforms of the forward and backward processes respectively, defined as:

\displaystyle D^{+}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{\phi}(\tau+\tau^{\prime})\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\rangle e^{-i\omega\tau^{\prime}},

\displaystyle D^{-}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\rangle e^{-i\omega\tau^{\prime}}.

(35)

They satisfy the relation
D^{+}(-\omega)=D^{-}(\omega)
.

III.2.2
Davies-Unruh Decoherence from Detailed Balance

Remarkably, there is an analogy between the decoherence rate
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}
derived above and the decoherence rate of an inertial detector coupled to an effective thermal bath, which suggests an alternative derivation. We start with the decoherence coefficient of an inertial, polarizable particle interacting with a thermal field
43
;
23
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{1}{3(2\pi)^{3}\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{8}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{8}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left(n(\omega)+1\right)n(\omega).

(36)

Here
n(\omega)=\left[\text{exp}{(\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm{B}}T)}-1\right]^{-1}
is the Bose-Einstein distribution. In
25
it was shown that for any observer moving along a stationary worldline, the field state seen by the observer can be described by a temperature
T
given by the detailed-balance relation:

\displaystyle\frac{D^{-}(\omega)}{D^{+}(\omega)}=e^{\hbar\omega/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)}.

(37)

One can thus derive the Davies-Unruh decoherence rate by starting from the expression for the decoherence in thermal radiation of a polarizable particle at rest in Minkowski spacetime given by Eq. (
36
). Under the assumption that the processes in the environment are in detailed balance [Eq.(
37
)], we can write the the modified spectrum along a stationary worldline in the form of an effective Bose-Einstein distribution with a frequency-dependent temperature as
25
;
19
:

\displaystyle n(\omega)=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_{B}T(\omega))}-1}=\frac{D^{+}(\omega)}{D^{-}(\omega)-D^{+}(\omega)},

(38)

which immediately allows us to see that:

\displaystyle n(\omega)\left(n(\omega)+1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega)}{\left(D^{+}(\omega)-D^{-}(\omega)\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3})^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega),

(39)

In the last step we have used the expression for the density of states in Minkowski spacetime.

Since the Fourier transform of the pullback of the Minkowski spacetime field correlator onto any stationary worldline always satisfies a detailed balance relation, we can associate a temperature with the corresponding field state. For instance, in the case of hyperbolic motion, this temperature would be equivalent to the well known Davies-Unruh temperature. Using Eq. (39) in Eq. (36), we recover Eq.(34). This tells us that the decoherence coefficient of our system can always be expressed in the form of Eq.(36) with an appropriate frequency dependent temperature
T(\omega)
, as long as the processes in the environment are in detailed balance.

III.2.3
Decoherence from time dilation

We similarly derive the contribution to the total decoherence arising from the time-dilation interaction Hamiltonian in Eq. (
20
). As mentioned above, this interaction arises from the differential time-dilation over the extent of the spatial wavefunction of the system. We will thus refer to the associated decoherence effect as time-dilation decoherence,
\Lambda^{\mathrm{TD}}
. To calculate
\Lambda^{\mathrm{TD}}
, we first consider the time-dilation bath operator in Eq. (
25
), wherein both terms depend on the dipole and are thus linear in the polarizability. We can immediately infer the resulting decoherence coefficient
\Lambda_{ij}^{\mathrm{TD}}
, by looking at the similarities between the second and third term in Eq. (
25
) and Eq. (
24
).
In analogy to Eq. (
34
), the decoherence coefficient is thus:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}^{\mathrm{TD}}=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{6\pi\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left(1-2\eta(\omega)\right)^{2}D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega)

(40)

As opposed to the Davies-Unruh decoherence coefficient, the time-dilation decoherence depends on the local acceleration both via the perceived field spectrum of the moving particle
\left(\sim D^{+}\left(\omega\right)D^{-}\left(\omega\right)\right)
, as well as the direct scaling
\sim a^{2}
via the redshift factor.

III.3
Dispersion Potential

The terms
C^{(1)}_{i}
and
C^{(2)}_{ij}
represent derivatives of the dispersion potential arising from the interaction of the particle with the field. Using the split of the bath operator in Eq. (
23
) into a Davies-Unruh and a time-dilation contribution, we see that both
C^{(1)}_{i}
and
C^{(2)}_{ij}
will have contributions from both bath operators. The only nonvanishing contribution to
C_{i}^{(1)}
is due to the bath operator
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{TD}}(\tau)
of the time-dilation Hamiltonian (
20
):

\displaystyle C^{(1),\mathrm{TD}}_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{2\pi c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,S(\omega)\omega^{2}\left(D^{+}(\omega)+D^{-}(\omega)\right),

(41)

where
S(\omega)
is given by:

\displaystyle S(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\left[\alpha_{0}(\omega)\eta(\omega)-\alpha_{0}(\omega)\right].

(42)

C^{(1),\mathrm{TD}}_{i}
can be interpreted as the dispersion force arising from the effective temperature gradient across the extent of the particle’s wavefunction. Similar corrections to the flat-space Casimir force between parallel plates that are linear in the local acceleration have been reported in
9
;
50
;
17
;
30
;
48
;
49
.

The second-order term
C_{i}^{(2)}
receives contributions from both
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{TD}}(\tau)
and the bath operator
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau)
defined by the Davies-Unruh Hamiltonian (
19
). The Davies-Unruh contribution is

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{DU}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{24\pi^{2}\hbar c^{2}}\mathcal{P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\frac{\omega^{4}\omega^{\prime 2}}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\left[D^{+}(\omega)D^{+}(\omega^{\prime})-D^{-}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\right],

(43)

and the time-dilation contribution is

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{TD}}=-\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{32\pi^{2}\hbar c^{4}}\mathcal{P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\frac{\omega^{4}}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\left|\alpha_{0}(\omega)[\eta(\omega)-1]\right|^{2}\left[D^{+}(\omega)D^{+}(\omega^{\prime})-D^{-}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\right].

(44)

While
C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{DU}}
represents an effect analogous to the thermal Lamb shift in atoms arising directly from the interaction of the particle with the effective thermal field,
C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{TD}}
once again arises from the effective thermal gradient across the particles. The seeming contradiction of the appearance of a temperature gradient in an equilibrium force, such as
C_{i}^{(1),\mathrm{DU}}
and
C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{DU}}
, can be related to the Tolman-Ehrenfest effect, which causes the field to equilibrate at a temperature gradient
55
;
54
;
40
.

IV
Example: Hyperbolic motion

IV.1
Decoherence

As a first example of decoherence in non-inertial reference frames, let us consider linear hyperbolic motion through Minkowski space. Without loss of generality we assume that our particle is accelerated along the x-axis with an acceleration
a
. The classical worldline of the particle can then be written as:

\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle=ca^{-1}\text{sinh}\left(a\tau/c\right)

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=c^{2}a^{-1}\text{cosh}\left(a\tau/c\right)

\displaystyle y_{0}

\displaystyle=z_{0}=0

(45)

It was shown in
6
;
26
that the Fourier transforms of the forward and backward Wightman functions
D^{+}(\omega)
and
D^{-}(\omega)
pulled back onto a hyperbolic worldline are given by:

\displaystyle D^{\pm}(\omega)=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left[\Theta(\mp\omega)+n(\omega)\right],

(46)

where
n(\omega)
can be identified as the Bose-Einstein distribution at the Davies-Unruh temperature
T_{\mathrm{DU}}=\hbar a/(2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}c)
, and
\Theta\left(\omega\right)
is the Heaviside function. To find all the decoherence coefficients, we substitute these Wightman functions into Eq. (
34
) and (
40
) to find:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}=\frac{1}{3(2\pi)^{3}\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{8}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{8}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left(n(\omega)+1\right)n(\omega),

(47)

and

Figure 3:
(a) An inertial observer, Alice, in Minkowski vacuum, observes the decoherence of a particle undergoing uniform acceleration, with Bob as a co-moving observer. From Alice’s perspective the particle interacts with the zero-temperature vacuum field, scattering quantum fluctuations and experiencing momentum recoil in the process. This recoil is associated with ‘which-path’ measurement, which leads to decoherence. (b) From Bob’s perspective, the decoherence results from scattering by a thermal field.

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}^{\mathrm{TD}}=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{3(2\pi)^{3}\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{4}}\times

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{6}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left(1-2\eta(\omega)\right)^{2}\left(n(\omega)+1\right)n(\omega)

(48)

The Davies-Unruh coefficient Eq. (
47
) takes on the same form as the decoherence coefficient Eq. (
36
) for a polarizable particle at rest interacting with a thermal field
43
. The numerical prefactor in the Davies-Unruh decoherence rate differs from that in
23
, due to the scalar nature of the field. We further note that the decoherence rate
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}
is related to the momentum diffusion rate of a particle undergoing hyperbolic motion
42
;
43
;
23
:

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\Delta P^{2}\right\rangle}{\Delta t}=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}.

(49)

This can be understood as follows: the rate at which the effective thermal photons scatter off the particle and impart momentum kicks is the same rate at which they gain ‘which-path’ information and decohere the particle’s spatial superposition.
Both the decoherence coefficients
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}
and
\Lambda^{\mathrm{TD}}
contain the factor
n(\omega)\left(n(\omega)+1\right)
, wherein the terms
\sim n(\omega)
and
\sim n^{2}(\omega)
correspond to the particle-like and wave-like contributions of the thermal field, respectively
43
. The appearance of the Bose-Einstein distribution also ensures the convergence of the integral.

Additionally, the decoherence of the particle undergoing hyperbolic motion can be understood from the perspective of an inertial observer in the Minkowski vacuum as follows. Consider Alice to be an inertial observer, while Bob is located in the co-moving frame with the particle (see Fig.
3
). Since the field in Bob’s frame appears thermal, the decoherence in Bob’s frame arises from the scattering of apparent thermal photons by the particle
24
;
41
. In Alice’s frame, as the quantum fluctuations of the vacuum field interact with the accelerating particle and scatter, the particle experiences recoil. This recoil results in both a momentum diffusion, and concomitantly decoherence in the position basis. We note that such an effect is consistent with the fact that Alice does not measure any excitations in the field
39
;
16
.

IV.2
Dispersion Force

Using the same
D^{+}(\omega)
and
D^{-}(\omega)
, we can also calculate the dispersion force experienced by the particle. Substituting the Wightman functions into Eq. (
41
), we get:

\displaystyle C^{(1),\mathrm{TD}}_{i}=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar a_{i}}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c^{5}}\times

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{3}\mathrm{Re}\left[\alpha_{0}(\omega)(\eta(\omega)-1)\right]\left(2n(\omega)+1)\right),

(50)

where
n(\omega)
is once again the Bose-Einstein distribution at the Davies-Unruh temperature. As explained in Section
III.3
, this force is related to the temperature gradient experienced by the particle across its wavefunction.

V
Example: Circular Motion

V.1
Decoherence

The second example we consider is that of relativistic uniform circular motion. Circular motion with relativistic speed is commonly achieved in accelerators and has been studied before in
3
;
4
;
1
. The worldline of a particle undergoing circular motion parameterized by its proper time
\tau
is given by:

\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle=\tau\gamma

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=\rho\mathrm{sin}\left(\xi\tau\gamma\right)

\displaystyle y_{0}

\displaystyle=\rho\mathrm{cos}\left(\xi\tau\gamma\right)

\displaystyle z_{0}

\displaystyle=0

(51)

Here
\rho
is the radius,
\gamma=1/\left(1-v^{2}/c^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
\xi
is the angular frequency and
v=\rho\xi
is the speed of the particle. The local acceleration experience by the particle in its rest frame is:

\displaystyle a=v\xi\gamma.

(52)

To find the decoherence coefficient, we will focus on the ultra-relativistic case
v\approx c
. In this case, the Fourier transform of the Wightman function can be simplified and has been found in
26
to be:

\displaystyle D^{+}(\omega)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\hbar\xi}{8\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}R\gamma}\left[\frac{e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}}{(v/c)\cosh(R)-1}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle D^{-}(\omega)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}+\frac{\hbar\xi}{8\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}R\gamma}\left[\frac{e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}}{(v/c)\cosh(R)-1}\right]

(54)

where
R
is the positive real root of the equation
R=(v/c)\sinh R
. For simplicity we define a function:

\displaystyle C(R)=\frac{\xi}{4\pi\gamma}\frac{1}{2R\left((v/c)\ \mathrm{cosh}R-1\right)}.

(55)

Substituting these expressions into Eqs. (
34
) and (
40
) gives us:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{6}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}C(R)\left[C(R)e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}+\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right]e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma},

(56)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}^{\mathrm{TD}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{6\pi\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left(1-2\eta(\omega)\right)^{2}C(R)\left[C(R)e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}+\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right]e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}.

(57)

The decaying exponential in the integrand once again ensures the convergence of the frequency integral. Compared to hyperbolic motion, the Bose-Einstein distribution is replaced by a distribution determined by the factor
C(R)
.

V.2
Dispersion Force

We again find the dispersion force by substituting the Wightman functions for circular motion into Eq. (
41
), which yields:

\displaystyle C_{i}^{(1),\mathrm{TD}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar a_{i}}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{2}\mathrm{Re}\left[\alpha_{0}(\omega)(1-\eta(\omega))\right]\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\omega}{4\pi}+C(R)e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}\right).

(58)

For the case where the particle moves along a worldline with a constant radius, the local acceleration is the centrifugal force pointing radially outwards. The dispersion force thus becomes:

\displaystyle C_{i}^{(1),\mathrm{TD}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\xi^{2}\gamma^{2}\rho}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{2}\mathrm{Re}\left[\alpha_{0}(\omega)(1-\eta(\omega))\right]\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\omega}{4\pi}+C(R)e^{-2\omega R/\xi\gamma}\right).

(59)

Like the decoherence coefficient, the field spectrum is described by a distribution determined by
C(R)
.

VI
Summary and Outlook

In this paper we analyze the open quantum system dynamics of the center of mass of a point-like particle coupled to a scalar field, and moving along a stationary worldline through 3+1 dimensional Minkowski space. In our model the particle has two distinct degrees of freedom: (1) an internal oscillator that couples to the field, and (2) its quantized center-of-mass motion, which is considered to be spatially delocalized around its worldline. We first derive an effective red-shifted polarizability of the particle undergoing non-inertial motion by solving the coupled dynamics of the internal oscillator and the field (Eq. (
8
)).

We then describe the open system dynamics of the particle’s quantized center-of-mass motion via a QBM master equation, derived under the Born-Markov approximation (Eq. (
III.1
)). The resulting QBM master equation consists of (1) a dispersive contribution corresponding to a center-of-mass Hamiltonian modification (Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
)), akin to Casimir-Polder forces on particles near media
23
; and (2) dissipative terms that include center-of-mass decoherence in the position basis.
For stationary worldlines, we find that the dispersive potential seen by the particle scales linearly with the local acceleration
a_{i}
, resembling the gravitational corrections to Casimir forces found in
9
;
50
;
17
;
30
;
48
;
49
.

We highlight two distinct sources of decoherence arising from two distinct system-bath coupling mechanism:

1.

Davies-Unruh decoherence
: A particle moving along a stationary worldline registers a modified field spectrum, which differs from that of the Minkowski vacuum seen by an inertial observer. When this field interacts with the particle and imparts recoil, which-path information about the particle’s center-of-mass position is acquired, and this manifests itself as decoherence. Such decoherence has a form that is effectively thermal, Eq. (
34
). This is further confirmed by the fact that in the special case of hyperbolic motion, the decoherence rate of a uniformly accelerating particle (Eq. (
47
)) is equivalent to that of a particle at rest immersed in a thermal field at temperature
T_{\mathrm{DU}}=\hbar a/(2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}c)

23
;
43
. Additionally, we find that the same decoherence coefficient can be arrived at by considering the thermal decoherence rate of a polarizable point-particle in flat spacetime (Eq. (
36
)), and imposing the condition that the effective field observed by the particle on a stationary worldline satisfies the detailed balance condition (Eq. (
37
)).

2.

Time-dilation decoherence
: Since the particle contains an internal oscillator with a characteristic frequency, it is sensitive to time-dilation across its delocalized center of mass. A spatial superposition parallel to the acceleration experienced by the particle leads to a differential time dilation across the particle’s wavefunction. This induces an effective coupling between the center of mass and the field, resulting in decoherence. Along a stationary worldline, such time-dilation decoherence (Eq. (
40
)) is also thermal in nature, represented by the appearance of the factor
D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega)
, but with an explicit dependence on the local acceleration as well as on the particle’s red-shifted polarizability (Eq. (
8
)).

We evaluate both the dispersion force and decoherence rates for two specific examples of stationary worldlines: hyperbolic motion (Section
IV
) and circular motion (Section
V
). One of the distinguishing features between these two types of motion is that hyperbolic motion gives rise to a Rindler horizon, whereas circular motion does not.
Considering the recent interest in decoherence near black holes
10
;
11
;
20
;
58
;
31
and the role played by the presence of horizons in engendering it
2
, we remark that in our model we find a finite decoherence rate for both hyperbolic and circular motion. Furthermore, we note that there are striking similarities between the Davies-Unruh decoherence rate of a particle undergoing non-inertial motion (Eq. (
34
)), and the decoherence of a polarizable particle at rest near a medium, immersed in a thermal field
46
;
5
;
23
. This correspondence motivates further investigations into the open system dynamics of test quantum systems undergoing non-inertial motion, or equivalently in a curved spacetime, suggesting a characterization of curved spacetime as an effective reservoir.

Appendix A
System Lagrangian and Hamiltonian

We start with a particle whose center-of-mass moves along a prescribed classical worldline
x_{0}^{\mu}(\tau)
parameterized by the proper time
\tau
. We describe the
spatial
quantized center-of-mass fluctuations of the particle around
x_{0}^{\mu}(\tau)
in the local rest frame of the particle and denote them by
\hat{X}^{i}
. The conjugate momentum in the local rest frame is
\hat{P}_{i}
and we impose the canonical commutation relation
\left[\hat{X}^{i},\hat{P}_{j}\right]=i\hbar\delta^{i}_{j}
.
Following the description in
36
;
59
, we can write the Lagrangian in the local rest frame as:

\displaystyle L=L_{\mathrm{rest}}\left[g_{00}(\hat{X}^{i})-g_{mn}(\hat{X}^{i})\dot{\hat{X}}^{m}\dot{\hat{X}}^{n}/c^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

(60)

where for simplicity we have assumed that
g_{0m}=g_{m0}=0
. The rest Lagrangian
L_{\mathrm{rest}}
is given by:

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{rest}}=-\mathbb{1}\ Mc^{2}+\frac{m\dot{\hat{q}}(\tau)^{2}}{2}-\frac{m\omega_{q}^{2}\hat{q}(\tau)^{2}}{2}.

(61)

Here
M
is the mass of the particle,
\hat{q}(\tau)
is the internal degree of freedom position, which is assumed to be point-like and thus does not transform under general coordinate transformations,
\omega_{q}
is the frequency in the rest frame of the particle, and
m
is the mass associated with the internal oscillator. Using the Lagrangian in the local rest frame, we can calculate the Hamiltonian in the local rest frame by taking the Legendre transform with respect to both the internal and center-of-mass degrees of freedom, which yields:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sqrt{g_{00}(\hat{X}^{i})\left(\hat{E}_{\mathrm{rest}}^{2}+\hat{P}^{i}\hat{P}_{i}c^{2}\right)}

(62)

We note that the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
which appears in the form of the red-shift factor
g_{00}
and in the inner product of
\hat{P}^{i}
as
g_{ij}
is evaluated around the particle worldline
x_{0}^{\mu}(\tau)
and can thus be expanded in the local rest frame in terms of
\hat{X}^{i}
. Finally, we assume that the particle moves non-relativistically in the local rest frame such that the kinetic contribution to total system energy is much smaller than the rest energy contribution. In other words, we assume that there are no special relativistic time-dilation effects in the local rest frame of the particle. We then end up with Eq. (
5
).

Appendix B
Internal oscillator dynamics and polarizability

B.1
Coupled dynamics of the internal oscillator and the field

In order to describe the response of the particle to the external field, we will now derive an effective polarizability of the particle, defined through its linear response to the field.
Using Eq. (
5
), the Heisenberg equations of motion for the internal oscillator are given by:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{q}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\left[\hat{q},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right]=\frac{\sqrt{-g_{00}}}{m}\left(\hat{p}-e\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\right)

(63)

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{p}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\left[\hat{p},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right]=-\sqrt{-g_{00}}\,m\omega_{q}^{2}\hat{q}.

(64)

Combining these two equations leaves us with:

\displaystyle m\ddot{\hat{q}}-g_{00}m\omega_{q}^{2}\hat{q}^{2}=-\sqrt{-g_{00}}{e}\dot{\hat{\phi}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau),

(65)

from which we can see that the time derivative of the field acts as a driving force on the internal oscillator.

Similarly, the Heisenberg equation of motion for the field mode operators are:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{g}}_{\mathbf{k}}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\left[\hat{g}_{\mathbf{k}},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{F}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\right]=-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{k}\hat{g}_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{\mathrm{i}e\sqrt{-g_{00}}}{\hbar m}\left(\hat{p}-e\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\right)\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau),

(66)

which we solve to get:

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\tau\right)=\hat{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\left(0\right)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}\tau}+\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}\left(\tau^{\prime}\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau^{\prime})e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}.

(67)

Therefore the field is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}\left(\tau^{\prime}\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau^{\prime})e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}-\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\phi_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau^{\prime})e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\right]

(68)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\underbrace{+\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}\left(\tau^{\prime}\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\left[\left\langle\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle-\left\langle\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau^{\prime})\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)\right\rangle\right]}_{\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{rr}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)}

(69)

where
\hat{\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)
is the free-evolving field (i.e., the part evolved by
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{F}}
). The radiation-reaction part
\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{rr}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)
can be simplified by taking the Fourier transform with respect to
\tau^{\prime}
, using the definitions of
D^{\pm}\left(\Omega\right)
in Eq. (
35
) and neglecting
\mathcal{O}((\hat{X}^{i})^{2})
terms:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{rr}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{2\pi\hbar}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Omega\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}(\tau^{\prime})}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\left[D^{+}(\Omega)-D^{-}(\Omega)\right]e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{2\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Omega\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}(\tau^{\prime})}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\Omega\sin(\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime})),

(71)

The time derivative of the radiation-reaction term is therefore:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\phi}}_{\mathrm{rr}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{2\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Omega\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}\left(\tau^{\prime}\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\Omega^{2}\cos(\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))

(72)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{2\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Omega\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}(\tau^{\prime})}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\partial_{\tau}^{2}\cos(\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))

(73)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\hat{q}(\tau^{\prime})}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}}\partial_{\tau}^{2}\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})

(74)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{3}\hat{q}\left(\tau\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{3}}-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}\hat{q}\left(\tau\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{2}}\delta(0)\right).

(75)

Then from Eqs. (
65
), (
B.1
), and (
72
) we get:

\displaystyle(m+\delta m)\ddot{\hat{q}}-g_{00}m\omega_{q}^{2}\hat{q}-\sqrt{-g_{00}}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}\dddot{\hat{q}}=-\sqrt{-g_{00}}e\dot{\hat{\phi}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau),.

(76)

\delta m
is a contribution to the particle mass resulting from the coupling of the particle to its own radiation reaction field, and is given by the divergent expression
(e^{2}/4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3})\delta(0)
in our calculation assuming a point-like particle. To be consistent with our nonrelativistic approximation for the internal dynamics of the particle we can replace
\delta(0)
by
\Omega/\pi
, where
\Omega
is a high-frequency cutoff; this results in the replacement of
\delta m/m
by

\displaystyle\frac{\delta m}{m}=\frac{e^{2}\Omega}{4\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}mc^{3}}=\frac{r_{0}}{\pi c}\Omega,

(77)

where
r_{0}=e^{2}/(4\pi\epsilon_{0}mc^{2})\cong 2.8\times 10^{-15}m
is the classical electron radius. Thus
\delta m/m
may be assumed to be negligible for any cutoff frequency
\Omega
much smaller than
10^{23}
Hz, and then we can ignore the mass renormalization in Eq. (
76
).

B.2
Effective polarizability

Taking the Fourier transform of Eq. (
76
) allows us to write the steady state induced dipole
\hat{d}_{\mathrm{st}}
as:

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{\mathrm{st}}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)=e\hat{q}(\hat{X}^{i},\tau)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\sqrt{-g_{00}}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega\alpha(\omega)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\hat{X}^{i},\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau},

(78)

where
\alpha(\omega)
is the polarizability of the particle, which describes its response to an external field:

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)\coloneqq\frac{e^{2}/m}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{-g_{00}}\beta\omega^{3}-\omega^{2}-g_{00}\omega_{q}^{2}};\qquad\beta\coloneqq\frac{e^{2}/m}{8\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}}.

(79)

From this expression we can see that the characteristic frequencies that determine the response of the particle are red-shifted across the spread of the wavefunction, which leads to a coupling between the the quantized center of mass and the field. This can be seen by expanding the polarizability to first order in
\hat{X}^{i}
using Eq. (
13
) to get:

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega)\approx\alpha_{0}(\omega)\left[1-\eta(\omega)a_{i}\hat{X}^{i}/c^{2}\right].

(80)

Here
\alpha_{0}(\omega)
is the bare polarizability in the rest frame of the particle and
\eta(\omega)
is a redshift-induced correction (see Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) respectively). Combining Eq. (
4
), Eq. (
63
) and Eq. (
8
) we can rewrite Eq. (
5
) in the Schrödinger picture as given by Eq. (
II
).

Appendix C
Decoherence rate

C.1
Davies-Unruh Decoherence from First Principles

The derivation of
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}_{ij}
, with
i=j
corresponding to the Davies-Unruh decoherence coefficient, goes as follows. Using the bath operator
B_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau)
in the interaction picture (Eq. (
24
)):

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\left\{\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau),\hat{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\}\right\rangle

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}\left(\tau\right)\hat{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau)\right\rangle.

(82)

Substituting the expressions for the Davies-Unruh bath operator gives:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\rangle,

(83)

where we use the shorthand notation
\hat{\phi}(\tau)\equiv\hat{\phi}(x_{0}^{\mu}(\tau))
. In the next step we apply Wick’s theorem to break up the 4-point correlators into products of 2-point correlators. Additionally, we make use of our assumption that the field and its first derivative are statistically independent. Together these assumptions yield:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\rangle.

(84)

Now we use the assumption that different spatial directions of the center-of-mass are uncorrelated. This implies that
\langle\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\rangle=0
and leaves us with:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}_{ii}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\rangle.

(85)

Since
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
is a function of
\sqrt{a_{i}a^{i}}
and thus isotropic, we assume that
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}_{ij}
is independent of which axis the superposition is prepared in. In combination with
\langle\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\rangle=0
this allows us to simplify the correlator of the spatial derivatives:

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(X)\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(X^{\prime})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{3c^{2}}\left\langle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(X)\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(X^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(86)

After this,
\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}
is:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\partial_{\tau}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\right\rangle.

(87)

We now write the field and induced polarization in the interaction picture in terms of their spectral decomposition:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau),

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega\alpha_{0}(\omega)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau),

(88)

which yields:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}6\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\,\omega_{1}^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega_{1})|^{2}\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{1},\tau)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{1},\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{2}\,\omega_{2}^{2}\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{2},\tau)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{2},\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}6\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\,\omega_{1}^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega_{1})|^{2}\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{1},\tau-\tau^{\prime})\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{1},\tau)\right\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{2}\,\omega_{2}^{2}\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{ \hat\phi}(\omega_{2},\tau-\tau^{\prime})\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{2},\tau)\right\rangle.

(89)

In the second term we now make the transformation
\tau^{\prime}\rightarrow-\tau^{\prime}
, which will cause the correlators to change:

\displaystyle\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau-\tau^{\prime})\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau)\right\rangle\rightarrow\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau+\tau^{\prime})\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau)\right\rangle=\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega,\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle,

(90)

where in the last equality we have used the fact that the pullback of the correlator onto the worldline of the particle only depends on
\tau^{\prime}
; this follows from the stationarity of the particle’s worldline. Thus the correlation function is invariant under proper time translations along the worldline. With this we can simplify the decoherence expression to:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}6\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\,\omega_{1}^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega_{1})|^{2}\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{1},\tau)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{1},\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{2}\,\omega_{2}^{2}\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{2},\tau)\underaccent{\bar}{\hat\phi}(\omega_{2},\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(91)

Remembering that the field operators are in the interaction picture, we can write:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}6\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega_{2}\,\omega_{1}^{4}\omega_{2}^{2}|\alpha_{0}(\omega_{1})|^{2}D^{+}(\omega_{1})D^{+}(\omega_{2})e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})\tau^{\prime}}.

(92)

Simplifying further:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{DU}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12\pi\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{6}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega)

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6\pi\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{6}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega),

(94)

which is our final form of the Davies-Unruh decoherence coefficient. Here
D^{+}(\omega)
and
D^{-}(\omega)
are the Fourier transforms of the pullback of the forward and backward processes respectively.

C.2
Time Dilation Decoherence

As before, we start by writing the time-dilation decoherence coefficient as:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathrm{TD}}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\left\{\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{i}(\tau),\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{j}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{i}(\tau)\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{j}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{j}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{B}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{j}(\tau)\right\rangle

(95)

Comparing the bath operators
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau)
and
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{TD}}(\tau)
(Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
)), we can obtain immediately the time-dilation decoherence coefficient by realizing that it is of the same structure as Eq. (
C.1
), except with red-shifted polarizabilities:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}^{\mathrm{TD}}=\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{6\pi\hbar^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left(1-2\eta(\omega)\right)^{2}D^{+}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega).

(96)

Here we compute the second-order contributions to the dispersion potential by substituting the bath operators

and
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{TD}}(\tau)
(Eqs. (

) and (

D.1
Davies-Unruh Dispersion Potential

The Davies-Unruh term of the dispersion potential is

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{DU}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\left[\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau),\hat{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle.

(97)

As done in the decoherence calculations, we substitute
\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{DU}}(\tau)
and split the 4-point correlators into 2-point correlators,

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{DU}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau)\dot{\hat{d}}_{0,\mathrm{st}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}(\tau)\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle-(\tau\leftrightarrow\tau-\tau^{\prime})

(98)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}\delta_{ij}}{(2\pi)^{2}6\hbar c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\,\omega^{4}\omega^{\prime 2}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}e^{\mathrm{i}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\tau^{\prime}}\left[D^{+}(\omega)D^{+}(\omega^{\prime})-D^{-}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\right]

(99)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{(2\pi)^{2}6\hbar c^{2}}\mathcal{P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\frac{\omega^{4}\omega^{\prime 2}}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left[D^{+}(\omega)D^{+}(\omega^{\prime})-D^{-}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\right],

(100)

in the second line using Eq. (
86
) to simplify the correlator of spatial derivatives. Here
\mathcal{P}
denotes the Cauchy principal value.

D.2
Time-Dilation Dispersion Potential

The time-dilation term of the dispersion potential is computed in a similar manner:

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{(2),\mathrm{TD}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\left\langle\hat{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{TD}}(\tau),\hat{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{TD}}(0)\right\rangle\right]

(101)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}a_{i}a_{j}}{8\hbar c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\left\langle\left(\dot{\hat{d}}_{\mathrm{st},1}(\tau)-\dot{\hat{d}}_{\mathrm{st},0}(\tau)\right)\left(\dot{\hat{d}}_{\mathrm{st},1}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})-\dot{\hat{d}}_{\mathrm{st},0}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\hat{\phi}(\tau)\hat{\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right\rangle-(\tau\leftrightarrow\tau-\tau^{\prime})

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}a_{i}a_{j}}{(2\pi)^{2}8\hbar c^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\,\omega^{4}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)\eta(\omega)-\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}e^{\mathrm{i}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\tau^{\prime}}\left[D^{+}(\omega)D^{+}(\omega^{\prime})-D^{-}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\right]

(103)

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{(2\pi)^{2}8\hbar c^{4}}\mathcal{P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\frac{\omega^{4}}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}|\alpha_{0}(\omega)\eta(\omega)-\alpha_{0}(\omega)|^{2}\left[D^{+}(\omega)D^{+}(\omega^{\prime})-D^{-}(\omega)D^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\right].

(104)
