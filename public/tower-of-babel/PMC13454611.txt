Multiple human transgenes prolong survival of triple-carbohydrate knockout porcine kidney xenografts in nonhuman primates
Authors: Ahmad Karadagi, Takayuki Hirose, Grace Lassiter, Ivy Rosales, David Ma, Toshihide Tomosugi, Ryo Otsuka, Ranjith P Anand, Jacob V Layer, Jia-Yun Chen, Sandro Santagata, Michael Curtis, Susan Low, Wenning Qin, Robert B Colvin, Tatsuo Kawai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetically modified pigs are being developed to address the critical shortage of human organs for transplantation. Although porcine xenografts lacking the three major carbohydrate xenoantigens (3KO) have been considered ideal for human transplantation, the optimal combination of human transgenes (HTGs) to mitigate protein incompatibility remains undefined. In the current study, we evaluate immune responses and transplant outcomes of 3KO kidney xenografts with four different combinations of HTGs in a nonhuman primate model. Here, we show that the addition of HTGs significantly reduces transcripts associated with early immune activation, resulting in markedly prolonged survival of 3KO xenografts. Notably, the inclusion of the anti-inflammatory genes TNFAIP3 and HMOX1 is associated with improved graft survival, significantly reduced T-cell and CD11b⁺ myeloid cell infiltration, and lower expression of rejection-related gene sets in protocol xenograft biopsies.Subject terms: Preclinical research, Renal replacement therapy

Introduction

A critical shortage of transplantable human organs has emerged as kidney transplantation has become the standard of care for end-stage kidney disease. Of the approximately 145,000 patients on the U.S. kidney waitlist, only 19.4% received a kidney transplant in 20231. One promising approach to address this critical healthcare challenge is xenotransplantation, using porcine organs for human transplantation.

The primary barrier to successful pig-to-human xenotransplantation is the typically high level of preformed “natural” anti-pig antibodies in human blood. Most of these antibodies bind to three glycan antigens expressed on porcine cells, αGal (galactose-α−1,3-galactose), Neu5GC (N-glycolylneuraminic acid), and SD(a) (Sia-α2.3-[GalNAc-β1.4]Gal-β1.4-GlcNAc)2. Knock-out of the pig enzyme encoding genes significantly reduces human natural antibody binding to porcine cells, making pig organs devoid of these three carbohydrate antigens (3KO) ideal for xenotransplantation3,4. Therefore, 3KO kidneys with various combinations of human transgenes (HTGs) have been used in recent clinical kidney xenotransplant cases. These HTGs were incorporated to mitigate complement dysregulation, coagulation imbalances, and inflammatory responses arising from molecular incompatibility between pigs and primates. Although preclinical studies using nonhuman primates5–7 and decedents8,9 have shown favorable immune and physiological responses in these gene edits, the necessity of HTGs, particularly in 3KO pigs, remains controversial, and no studies have specifically assessed the usefulness of incorporating HTGs in the context of 3KO pigs.

For our clinical kidney xenotransplantation10, we used pig kidneys, designated EGEN-2784, which is 3KO incorporated with porcine endogenous retrovirus (PERV) inactivation and seven HTGs. Our decision to include these gene edits for human use was made based on preclinical studies comparing various combinations of HTGs in 3KO pig xenografts. In our preclinical studies, we first evaluated the addition of representative immune modulatory (CD47 and HLA-E) and complement regulatory HTGs (CD46, CD55, and CD59) to 3KO pigs and compared transplant outcomes with those of 3KO alone. We then assessed whether adding coagulation-regulatory HTGs to this genetic background further improve the transplant outcomes. Finally, two inflammation-regulatory HTGs (TNFAIP3 and HMOX1) were incorporated alongside immune, complement, and coagulation regulatory HTGs (EGEN-2784), and transplant outcomes and immune responses were evaluated. Here, we show that incorporating multiple HTGs, particularly those with anti-inflammatory properties, appears to be essential for prolonging 3KO kidney xenograft survival with favorable cellular and transcriptomic responses.

Results

By adding two HTGs involved in the self-recognition pathway and three HTGs involved in the complement regulatory pathway to 3KO in HTG-A, the HR was reduced to 0.18 (CI: 0.05-0.71, p = 0.014) compared to No-HTG, with maximal graft survival of 316 days. Addition of two or even three coagulation pathway regulatory molecules in HTG-B and C, respectively, resulted in similar HR to HTG-A and failed to further extend graft survival (Fig. 2a–c). Interestingly, progressive thrombocytopenia was observed in all HTG-B recipients (Fig. 2d) despite the addition of THBD and TFPI transgenes. This progressive thrombocytopenia was not observed in Group C, where PROCR was added. Importantly, in association with adding the two anti-inflammatory HTGs, TNFAIP3 and HMOX1 in HTG-D, xenograft survival with excellent kidney function beyond 1 year was achieved, with the longest survival exceeding 2 years despite omitting HLA-E and TFPI transgenes from the donor pigs (Fig. 2a–c). There was no clear correlation between pre-transplant anti-porcine IgG and IgM levels and the graft survival among HTG-A to D (Supplementary Fig. 2). Marked elevation of anti-porcine IgG and IgM titers was associated with some recipients who lost the xenograft due to antibody mediated rejection (AMR). In contrast, anti-porcine antibody titers generally remained unchanged in recipients who lost their xenografts due to thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) alone (Supplementary Fig. 3).

TMA was the primary cause of kidney xenograft loss in 3KO with multiple HTGs

The primary cause of graft loss in No-HTG recipients was severe acute tubular injury (ATI) present in three NHPs (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Table 1). One recipient with no-HTG survived until day 50 but lost graft function due to ATI with T cell mediated rejection (TCMR). Among the 31 recipients in Group HTG A–D, one lost the graft due to thrombosis on day 20, and three recipients were euthanized because of weight loss or infection without evidence of xenograft dysfunction (Supplementary Table 1). Of the remaining 27 recipients, TMA (Fig. 3d–f) contributed to all graft losses occurring within the first 100 days (10 TMA alone and 2 TMA with conventional rejection, such as AMR or TCMR). In contrast, graft loss after day 100 showed a more heterogeneous pattern, including TMA alone (4), TMA with conventional rejection (6), and conventional rejection alone (5). Accordingly, the distribution of causes of graft loss differed significantly between the early and late post-transplant periods (Fig. 4, p = 0.01, Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test).

Significantly higher transcriptomic signatures associated with immune activation without HTGs in 1-h biopsies

To investigate possible differences in recipient immune responses with or without HTGs, we assessed bulk mRNA expression in kidney biopsies. Since three of four recipients in the No-HTG group lost their kidney transplant function within one week, we evaluated 1-h biopsies to define possible differences in immune responses between No-HTG and HTG-D, where sufficient number of biopsies were available for statistical analysis. For this transcriptomic analysis, we used NanoString nCounter platform using 554 probes listed in Supplementary Table 2. The principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that No-HTG and HTG-D recipients were two distinct populations in terms of transcriptomic responses (Fig. 5a). In the HTG-D group, volcano plots showed markedly high expression of three HTGs, CD46, CD47 and THBD (Fig. 5b). It also revealed significantly high mRNA expression of genes related to immune activation, including HLA-DRA (human leukocyte antigen, class II, DR alpha), IGHG3 (immunoglobulin heavy constant gamma 3) and KDR (kinase insert domain receptor) in the No-HTG group compared to HTG-D. Conversely, KIT and BMP4 (Bone Morphogenetic Protein 4) were expressed at higher levels in HTG-D biopsies (Fig. 5c).

Kidney xenografts with additional TNFAIP3 and HMOX1 in HTG-D showed significantly reduced transcriptomic signatures associated with rejection

Kidney xenograft survival beyond one year was achieved by adding TNFAIP3 and HMOX1 in HTG-D. This was achieved in the absence of HLA-E/β2microglobulin(β2M) and TFPI transgenes. To elucidate the mechanisms of this survival difference, we compared bulk mRNA expression in the HTG-B and D protocol biopsies taken between 3-6 months after kidney xenotransplantation, when the transplant kidney function was stable. The biopsy analyses, using a modified Banff Human Organ Transplant (B-HOT) panel (Supplementary Table 3) with the NanoString nCounter platform, revealed upregulation of five distinct pathways in the HTG-B group, including kidney injury (AKI), AMR, chronic AMT (CAMR), donor specific antibody transcripts (DSAST), and endothelial-associated transcripts (ENDAT) signal. In contrast, the HTG-D group showed high levels of IL-7 signaling and quantitative cytotoxic T lymphocytes-associated transcripts (QCAT), along with promotion of T cell checkpoint signaling and Th2 differentiation pathways (Fig. 6).

To further define cellular immune differences between HTG-B and HTG-D xenografts, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence profiling on matched protocol biopsies obtained 3–6 months post-transplantation (n = 3 per group) (Supplementary Table 4). Across anatomical compartments, HTG-D xenografts showed a consistently reduced inflammatory immune composition, characterized by decreased leukocyte-normalized T-cell (CD3ε+) and myeloid (CD11b+) populations. In contrast, HTG-B xenografts showed prominent immune infiltration across glomerular, vascular, tubular and interstitial regions, consistent with a more pro-inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 7a–c and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5, 6).

Compartment-resolved analysis further revealed attenuation of immune activation within glomeruli in HTG-D xenografts, including reduced proliferative (Ki67⁺) T cell and myeloid subsets (Fig. 7c). In parallel, β-catenin—a key component of adherens junctions—was increased in HTG-D xenografts (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 4), consistent with improved preservation of epithelial architecture. Notably, NK cell–associated features were enriched in HTG-D xenografts, particularly within peritubular capillaries (Fig. 7d), potentially reflecting the absence of hHLA-E/β2M in this construct.

Transcriptomic responses in the HTG-D group

Although duration of overall graft survival of the HTG-D group xenograft was the longest, six out of 16 recipients in this group lost their xenograft early, within 25 days. To identify any contributing factors causing early xenograft loss, we evaluated pre-transplant anti-porcine donor specific antibody (DSA) titers among the HTG-D group recipients. There was no significant difference in pre-transplant DSA titers between short (< 25 days) and long-term(> 100 days) survivors (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We next compared the transcriptomic responses in 1-h biopsies between short vs. long-term survivors. Although HLA-DRA and SLPI Sa, both of which may indicate immune/inflammation activation, were high in short-term vs. long-term survivors, there were no significant differences in overall transcripts between short- vs. long-term survivors (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

In the current study, expressions of three HTGs (CD46, CD47, and THBD) were evaluable using specific probes available on the NanoString platform. As previously reported using scRNAseq4, we confirmed that pre-transplant levels of these three HTGs were maintained without significant changes until study termination (Fig. 8a).

To evaluate long-term transcriptomic responses, bulk mRNA analysis of sequential biopsies of NHPs from the long-term (> 1 year) survivors in HTG-D recipients were analyzed using the B-HOT panel (Supplementary Table 3). At 1 h, upregulation of innate and adaptive immune pathways—including innate-IS, adaptive-IS, ABMR, TCMR-RATS, and NK gene sets—was already observed. However, expression of these pathways decreased significantly by 3 months and was further reduced by 1 year, suggesting that innate and adaptive immune responses toward xenografts were effectively suppressed by ongoing immunosuppressive therapy and HTGs. In contrast, terminal necropsy samples showed significantly higher innate and adaptive immune responses compared with those observed at 3 months or 1 year (Fig. 8b).

Discussion

The specific HTGs required to prolong xenograft survival remain controversial. Since the 1990s, extensive studies in NHPs have investigated the role of complement-regulatory HTGs—such as hCD55 (decay-accelerating factor, DAF), hCD46 (membrane cofactor protein, MCP), and hCD59 (membrane inhibitor of reactive lysis, MIRL)—in prolonging xenograft survival. Early studies have demonstrated that expression of the human CD55 transgene could prolong heterotopic heart xenograft survival in NHPs13,14. Loveland et al. demonstrated that insertion of the human CD46 transgene alone could improve kidney xenograft survival, even in the absence of immunosuppression15. Notably, these studies were conducted without deletion of carbohydrate xenoantigens, yet still clearly demonstrated the beneficial effects of complement-regulatory HTGs in prolonging xenograft survival. In contrast, although Singh et al. demonstrated improved α1,3-galactotosyltransferase knockout (GTKO) xenograft survival by the addition of hTHBD in a heart xenograft model, studies evaluating the contribution of other HTGs—including those involved in coagulation (e.g., hTHBD, hPROCR, hTFPI), immune regulation (e.g., hCD47 and HLA-E/β2M), and inflammation (e.g., hHMOX1, hTNFAIP3)—remain limited in GTKO pigs, and no studies have specifically evaluated the added benefit of incorporating HTGs into 3KO pigs.

The present study was conducted in NHPs to evaluate the role of HTGs in the context of 3KO kidneys. We assessed graft survival and immune responses using 3KO kidneys expressing different combinations of HTGs, all transplanted under an identical immunosuppressive protocol. An anti-CD154 monoclonal antibody-based regimen was selected, based on our previous preliminary findings3 and other studies that have demonstrated long-term kidney xenograft survival in GTKO porcine xenograft recipients16–18. In our kidney xenotransplant studies using genetically engineered porcine donors, human CD55, CD46, and CD59 were initially added to 3 KO pigs in HTG-A to reduce activation of the complement cascade. Human CD47 was also included to suppress macrophage-mediated autologous phagocytosis through the ligation to signal regulatory protein-alpha (SIRPα) as well as hHLA-E and β2M to prevent activation of NK cells19,20. In association with these HTGs, graft survival was significantly prolonged (No-HTG vs. HTG-A), but survival beyond 6 months was unusual. Addition of human coagulation-related genes, hTHBD21,22 and hTFPI23, or hPROCR failed to further extend xenograft survival compared to HTG-A.

Notably, kidney xenograft survival exceeding one year was achieved only in association with the HTG-D gene construct, which included the anti-inflammatory genes TNFAIP3, encoding the protein A20, and HMOX1, encoding the enzyme heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) along with complement and coagulation pathway regulatory gene cassettes. To more precisely evaluate possible differences in immune responses associated with adding the HTG-D gene cassette, we first compared transcriptomic responses in 1-h xenograft biopsies taken from No-HTG vs. HTG-D recipients, in which sufficient number of biopsies were available for statistical analysis. No-HTG kidneys exhibited significantly higher expression of HLA-DR and IGHG3, which may reflect enhanced recipient immune cell activation immediately after transplantation. In addition, the higher KDR pathway transcripts indicate vascular endothelial growth factor-induced endothelial activation. Conversely, KIT and BMP4 (bone morphogenetic protein 4) were relatively elevated in HTG-D xenografts. This may suggest that BMP4, part of the TGFβ superfamily, may be associated in HTG-D recipients with promoting anti-inflammatory regulatory function24, though exact interpretation of high KIT remains to be defined.

We further evaluated the effect of adding anti-inflammatory genes by comparing transcriptomic responses in group HTG-B and D in protocol biopsies taken at 3-6 months from recipients with clinically healthy xenografts that were histologically normal. Despite stable renal function in both groups, the transcriptomic analyses revealed significantly higher expression of five distinct pathways for rejection in the HTG-B group, including AKI, AMR, CAMR, DSAST, and ENDAT. These results suggest that, despite absence of histologic evidence of rejection, when compared to HTG-D grafts, HTG-B grafts at 3-6 months already had relative enrichment of pathways associated with rejection, inflammation, and tissue damage. In contrast, HTG-D showed higher levels of IL-7 signaling and Th2 differentiation, which are associated with regulatory immune responses in several experimental transplant models25,26. Importantly, multiplex immunofluorescence provided spatial and cellular resolution which complements and extends the transcriptomic analyses. Across multiple renal compartments, including glomeruli, tubules, peritubular capillaries, and interstitium, HTG-D xenografts exhibited reduced leukocyte-normalized myeloid and T-cell composition and diminished immune proliferation. This indicates that the anti-inflammatory HTG-D cassette reshapes the immune microenvironment toward a less activated state. The concordance between reduced rejection-associated transcriptomic pathways and compartment-resolved attenuation of CD11b⁺ and CD3ε⁺ immune populations supports a model in which TNFAIP3 (A20) – which inhibits TNF-induced apoptosis27,28—and HMOX-1(HO-1)—a cytoprotective enzyme29—act to dampen both innate and adaptive immune activation within the xenograft. Interestingly, multiplex analyses revealed increased NK cell infiltration—particularly within the peritubular capillaries (PTCs)—in the HTG-D kidneys, in which HLA-E/β2M was omitted, compared with the HTG-B kidney, where HLA-E/β2M was expressed. This may also explain the elevated QCAT score in the HTG-D kidneys. QCAT score includes transcripts of cytotoxic effector molecules such as Granzyme A and B, perforin, IFN-γ, which are not specific to cytotoxic T cells and may also reflect activation of NK cells. Enhanced NK cell infiltration may contribute to endothelial injury either directly or through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity30, potentially promoting the development of TMA31. These findings suggest that reintroduction of HLA-E/β2M may be beneficial to further improve xenograft survival.

Although Group HTG-D recipients demonstrated the most encouraging long-term kidney xenograft survival, approximately 38% experienced graft loss due to TMA within 25 days. Neither pre-transplant recipient serum DSA levels nor bulk RNA expression in one-hour post-transplant biopsies correlated with or predicted graft fate. It is possible that the human proteins encoded by the transgenes designed for clinical use do not interact physiologically with cynomolgus monkey complement, coagulation, and self-recognition analogues32, in which case these candidate mechanisms driving TMA may be more prominent in the monkey model that they will prove to be clinically.

Our current and previous4 studies have shown that the expression of HTGs remained stable at the mRNA and protein level even in the terminal necropsy samples, suggesting that xenograft injury is not primarily caused by down-modulation of ‘protective’ human transgenes. Our longitudinal observations of transcriptomic responses in the long-term HTG-D survivors revealed that both innate and adaptive immune responses were effectively suppressed for up to one year after transplantation. However, most HTG-D recipients eventually rejected their xenografts, sometimes as late as 758 days post-transplant, in associated with elevated innate and adaptive transcriptomic responses. We conclude that maintenance immunosuppression with anti-CD154 and MMF as dosed in these studies is not sufficient to suppress rejection long-term in NHP and addition of treatments against innate immunity, such as complement inhibitors, may be necessary to further improve the results.

Although the high level of genetic homology to humans coupled with their outbred nature has made NHPs invaluable preclinical models33,34, there are several limitations in using NHPs to study xenotransplantation. First, the current studies are limited by the inability to isolate and evaluate the individual contributions of each HTG at both clinical and mechanistic levels. However, systematically assessing the numerous possible combinations of HTGs incorporated into the pig constructs used for life-supporting kidney xenografts would be both cost-prohibitive and logistically impractical. Second, higher anti-pig antibody responses to 3KO pig cells in old-world monkeys35 may make the incidence of AMR in 3KO xenografts higher in NHPs. In contrast, because NHPs are not typically exposed to dietary pork, their anti-pig memory T cell responses may be weaker than those of humans who regularly consume pork36. Indeed, despite the use of a similar immunosuppressive regimen, our recent human xenotransplant recipients developed early and robust T cell–mediated rejection10, which has never been observed within 100 days post-transplant in NHPs. The third limitation of the NHP model is the reduced cross-reactivity of certain clinical reagents (e.g., ATG), which may limit the efficacy of these agents in preclinical testing. Finally, the use of predominantly male cynomolgus monkeys (32 vs. 3) reflects practical considerations, as male animals are more readily available in research colonies, where females are preferentially retained for breeding to maintain colony sustainability. While we acknowledge this imbalance as a limitation, this approach is consistent with common practice in the field. Future studies with more balanced sex representation will be important to formally assess potential sex-specific effects. Despite these limitations, any novel gene edits or new medications that have not yet been used in humans must be thoroughly evaluated in preclinical studies using NHPs before their application in humans.

In conclusion, the current studies demonstrated that multiple combinations of HTGs, particularly those with anti-inflammatory properties, significantly extended the survival of 3KO kidney xenografts, accompanied by more favorable transcriptomic responses. While the optimal HTG combinations and immunosuppressive protocols remain to be determined, the currently available pig constructs are promising and now justify initial application of this approach in human patients with end-stage renal disease.

Methods

Animals

Thirty five cynomolgus monkeys (purchased from Charles River Primates, Wilmington, MA), 32 males and 3 females, age 3–10 years old, weighing 6–11 kg were used. Gene edited porcine donors were provided by eGenesis (Cambridge, MA). Yucatan miniature pigs weighing 10–25 kg were used as the kidney donor. All surgeries and postoperative care of animals were performed in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC) certified facility, per National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for the care and use of nonhuman primates. These studies were approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Husbandry, veterinary supervision, and daily care of animals were provided by Massachusetts General Hospital, Center for Comparative Medicine (CCM).

Animal husbandry

Swine were socially housed in individual pens on tenderfoot flooring in spaces with at least one and up to seven additional pigs. Swine were surveilled for pathogens of potential concern to NHP recipients. Non-human primates (NHPs) were housed in modulated stainless steel cages. Cages were equipped with stainless steel perches or resting surfaces, with additional structural complexity provided by activity modules. Temperature was maintained between 24 and 29 °C, humidity maintained between 30-70%, and lighting was provided on a light/dark cycle for approximately 12 h each day. Animals were fed Purina Lab Diet Monkey Diet 5038 and 5045 High Protein, supplemented daily with fresh produce. Additional nutritional enrichment was provided routinely or as needed. Tap water, filtered to 5 microns, was available ad libitum via an automatic watering system. Animals were socially housed in compatible pairs or groups when possible, based on structured compatibility assessments and in accordance with space guidelines. Cage space supported normal activity, with additional exercise provided through expanded housing, activity modules, or compatible group sessions as appropriate. The major surgical procedure is performed under general anesthesia with isoflurane following induction with propofol or ketamine. Prior to the procedure, animals are also treated with sustained-release buprenorphine (0.2 mg/kg) and meloxicam. Following the procedure, all post-surgical animals are monitored at a minimum of twice daily for the first 72 h, and then daily for the following 2–4 days. Then animals are monitored 1-3x/week; animals closer to the pre-operative/pre-procedural period or with any clinical instabilities are usually monitored with more frequency than those further out from a procedure or who are chronically stable. As post-procedure analgesics, meloxicam 0.1 mg/kg SC once a day for 4 days and buprenorphine 0.01–0.03 mg/kg every 8–12 h for 72 h, or Fentanyl patch 0.1–0.2 mg/kg is used.

Pentobarbital euthanasia solution (Euthanol) 100 mg/kg IV is used for euthanasia at end of the study.

Kidney transplant procedure

The kidney xenograft was transplanted intraperitoneally, anastomosing renal vein to recipient vena cava and renal artery to recipient abdominal aorta. Ureterovesical anastomosis was performed according to the Lich-Gregoir technique. Bilateral nephrectomy was performed at the time of transplantation if urine output from the transplanted kidney was observed during the procedure. In cases delayed graft function was suspected, only a unilateral nephrectomy was performed initially, with removal of the remaining native kidney within two weeks post-transplantation. Protocol biopsies were obtained at 1 h, and at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 18 months following transplantation. For-cause biopsies were performed whenever serum creatinine increased by more than 50% from baseline levels.

Immunosuppressive regimen (Fig. 1)

The rationale for adding CD40/CD154 blockade was based on reported nonhuman primate studies, which consistently demonstrated superior xenograft survival compared with conventional immunosuppression alone11. Long-term kidney xenograft survival exceeding one year has only been achieved with aCD154-based regimens to date4,16,17,37. In addition, anti-CD154 monoclonal antibody can also block CD11b12, a key mediator of innate immune activation that plays a critical role in xenotransplantation4,16,17,37. NHP recipients received induction therapy with 20 mg/kg of anti-CD20 mAb on day 0 (Anti-CD20 [2B8R1F8]-Afucosylated, NIH Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource, Worcester, MA) and 5 mg/kg of anti-rhesus thymocyte [rhATG] (NIH Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource, Worcester, MA) on days −1 and 0. Immunosuppression was maintained with daily 200 mg mycophenolate mofetil (CellCept, Genentech, San Francisco, CA) given orally and anti-CD154 mAb (Anti-CD154 [5C8H1] antibody, Cat#PR-1547, RRID:AB_2716324, NIH Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource Worcester, MA) on days 0, 2, 5, 7, and weekly. Tacrolimus (Prograf, Astellas, Northbrook, IL) was administered as intramuscular injection with trough levels of 10-15 ng/ml dictating the doses for the initial period and was discontinued by post operative day 60. Additionally, tapered methylprednisone (Solu-Medrol, Pfizer, New york, NY) was administered as intramuscular injection over the first 60 days. The recipients were followed by serial blood tests (CBC and chemistries) and ultrasound of the kidney xenograft weekly.

Donor specific nonhuman primate anti-porcine endothelial cell IgM and IgG analysis

NHP serum IgG and IgM binding to donor porcine endothelial cells were measured by flow cytometry38. Briefly, the porcine aortic endothelial cell preparation (1 × 105 cells per test) was incubated with 50 µl of diluted serum 1:64 at 4 °C for 45 min. Cells were then washed and incubated with diluted 1:100 anti-human IgG and anti-human IgM secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Inc.). Porcine endothelial cells were stained using and anti-rat CD31 (TLD-3A12) (BD Bioscience) diluted 1:50 at 4 °C for 30 min. Staining buffer containing 7-AAD (BD Bioscience) was used to exclude non-viable cells. Fluorescence was acquired on the FACSVerse (BD Bioscience), and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Histological analyses

Protocol renal biopsies were obtained every 2–4 months in recipients with stable function as well as “for cause” biopsy whenever a rise in serum creatinine occurred. Tissue was processed for light microscopy and a portion frozen for immunofluorescence staining. Other organs obtained surgically (lymph nodes, native kidney, and spleen) were similarly processed. Following euthanasia of a xenograft recipient, a complete necropsy was performed for histopathologic examination of the renal xenograft, lymph nodes, heart, lung, liver, pancreas, thymus, and skin. Xenograft H&E and PAS-stained samples were scored by current Banff criteria39 including C4d deposition by immunohistochemistry40.

Multiplex Orion imaging

Six FFPE slides from NHP pig-kidney xenograft biopsies (HTG-B: XT418 d100, XT280 d166, XT417A d90 necropsy; HTG-D: XT328 d193, XT344 d167, XT375 d156) were processed for multiplex imaging on the Orion platform (RareCyte), following Lin et al.41. Two cycles of an Orion multiplex antibody panel were used to profile immune cell subsets (see Supplementary Table 4).

In brief, the slides were prepared by removing the wax and performing antigen retrieval through heating at 95 °C and then 105 °C for 5 min each with EZ-AR 2 Elegance buffer. To reduce autofluorescence, the slides were treated with a hydrogen peroxide solution, washed with a surfactant solution, and subjected to an enhancer treatment. The slides were then incubated at room temperature for 2 h with an unconjugated primary antibody against CD31, followed by a 30 min incubation with AlexaFluor-750 Goat anti-Rabbit secondary antibody. Subsequently, the remaining ArgoFluor-conjugated antibodies and Hoechst 33342 were added to the slides for overnight incubation at 4 °C. The next day, slides were washed extensively in PBS, mounted in ArgoFluor mounting medium (RareCyte 42-1214-000), and imaged on an Orion microscope. Fluorophores were then quenched with hydrogen peroxide solution, and a second round of overnight 4 °C staining was performed with a different set of ArgoFluor-conjugated antibodies (see Supplementary Table 4) plus Hoechst 33342, followed by mounting and imaging.

Image stitching, segmentation, and single-cell quantification were conducted using the MCMICRO pipeline (https://github.com/labsyspharm/mcmicro)42. Cells exhibiting excessive autofluorescence were excluded via a Gaussian mixture model implemented in MATLAB (MathWorks)43. Marker positivity was defined at the single-cell level as binary expression, with cells classified as marker-positive or marker-negative based on empirically determined thresholds used in the multiplex analysis pipeline. All features were quantified as the percentage of marker-positive cells within each anatomical compartment unless otherwise specified.

Marker gating strategy

Immune populations and composite features were defined using direct marker or marker co-expression. No additional hierarchical or exclusion gating beyond these plotted definitions was applied. CD45 was used to define leukocyte-restricted features and for normalization. Examples of plotted gating definitions include single-marker features (e.g., CD11b⁺, CD4⁺, CD31⁺), co-expression features (e.g., CD8α⁺Ki67⁺, CD4⁺HLA-DR⁻, HLA-DR⁺CD31⁺), and lineage-restricted immune features (e.g., CD45⁺CD3ε⁺, CD45⁺CD56⁺). NK cells were defined as CD45⁺CD3ε⁻NKG2A⁺. All reported features correspond directly to those shown in the supplementary forest plots and slide-level dot plots.

Features prefixed with norm mean_* represent leukocyte-normalized compositions. For each slide and compartment, these were calculated as the percentage of CD45⁺ cells that were marker-positive, defined as 100 × (N_{CD45⁺marker⁺} / N_{CD45⁺}), to account for differences in overall leukocyte infiltration across samples.

Bulk transcriptomic analysis

mRNA isolated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded samples was analyzed using the nCounter MAX System (Bruker Spatial Biology). The B-HOT gene panel44 was screened for homology with pig and NHP transcripts by Erin Piazza (Bruker Spatial Biology). Probes that had >85% homology with pig sequences for parenchymal cells or endothelium and probes that had >85% homology for NHP leukocytes45 were supplemented with 35 pig-specific probes. The final panel, termed “B-HOTX,” had 554 probes, and 251 were omitted (Supplementary Table 2). Mean homology with NHP was 97.3%.

All samples passed quality control assessment for mRNA content, imaging, binding density, and positive and negative control44. Raw counts were normalized with probes for 7 housekeeping genes (NRDE2, POLR2A, ABCF1, OAZI, PPIA, G6PD, TBP) that had >90% homology with both pig and NHP genes. Gene set pathways were chosen from previous human allograft studies44, edited to exclude nonhomologous probes and probes for human transgenes and include pig specific probes (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3)46. Pathways were manually curated from KEGG, Gene Ontology, and Reactome databases (genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html, geneontology.org, and reactome.org), as well as from prior publications44,46. Data were normalized using seven housekeeping probes (NRDE2, POLR2A, ABCF1, OAZ1, PPIA, G6PD, and TBP), each demonstrating >90% homology to the corresponding porcine transcripts. Pathway analysis was performed using nSolver software (Bruker Spatial Biology, Seattle, WA). Probes used in the pathway analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and plots were performed and created using R programming version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Values of P < 0.05 were considered to be significant.

Graft survival

Post-transplant survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared with log-rank tests. Associations between genetic edits and graft survival were assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression (coxph function, survival package in R), with 95% confidence intervals were estimated, and statistical significance was assessed using two-sided tests.

Causes of graft loss

The proportion of graft losses attributed to TMA before and after day 100 was compared using Fisher’s exact test (GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0)

mRNA expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the NanoString nCounter Advanced Analysis module. Normalized counts were modeled with a generalized linear model, and statistical significance was assessed using Wald tests. Volcano plots display log₂ fold change versus −log₁₀(p value). For complementary analysis, raw count data were filtered to exclude genes with counts <50 and analyzed in R using the DESeq2 package (version 1.44.0). Differential gene expressions in 1-h biopsies are presented using principal component analysis (PCA) and volcano plots.

Pathway scores, summarizing normalized gene expression within each pathway, were generated using NanoString nSolver software with the Advanced Analysis module (version 2.0.134). Comparisons between HTG-B and HTG-D groups were performed using Welch’s t test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Significant pathways are presented as violin plots, reporting both nominal p values and false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted values.

Longitudinal changes in rejection-related pathways were analyzed using a two-way mixed-effects ANOVA.

Multiplex Orion imaging

Six pig kidney xenograft slides from non-human primate recipients were analyzed. Group differences between HTG-D (n = 3) and HTG-B (n = 3) were assessed using slide-level bar and dot plots for selected features, and forest plots to summarize between-group differences across features and compartments. Bar and dot plots display slide-level values with group means and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, whereas forest plots report differences in mean marker abundance as Δ mean (HTG-D − HTG-B) in percentage points. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were estimated using 10,000 resamples. Only features for which the confidence interval for Δ mean excluded zero are shown, with negative values indicating lower abundance and positive values indicating higher abundance in HTG-D. An asterisk denotes features with confidence intervals excluding zero.
