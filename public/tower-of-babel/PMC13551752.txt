Unilateral phaeochromocytoma in a dog with pre-existing, long-standing primary hypoadrenocorticism: a case report
Authors: Sabrina KX Tan, Giselle L Hosgood, Megan Curnow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis is a rare occurrence of a phaeochromocytoma in a dog with pre-existing, long-standing primary hypoadrenocorticism that underwent successful surgical intervention with a favourable outcome. The objective of this case report is to describe the diagnosis, management and outcome for unilateral phaeochromocytoma in a dog with pre-existing chronic adrenal insufficiency and atrophy.Case presentationA 14-year-old neutered female Jack Russell Terrier had a 1-week history of hyporexia, lethargy and progressive weight loss of 1.4 kg in 5 months. Background

Primary hypoadrenocorticism, caused by inadequate production of both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids, is commonly immune-mediated, with lymphocytic infiltration destroying the hormone-secreting adrenocortical layers [1]. Clinical signs develop when at least 85% to 90% of the adrenocortical tissue is destroyed, and eventually, there will be a complete loss of adrenocortical function [2]. Phaeochromocytoma originates from chromaffin cells in the medulla and produces catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine) [3]. Excessive catecholamine production can lead to the activation of α- and β-receptors, causing episodic or sustained hypertension, as well as signs of sympathetic overactivity [3]. These neoplasms can be benign or malignant; this determination is often based on the presence or absence of lymphovascular or capsular invasion in histological sections. Malignant forms are frequently locally invasive, infiltrating the phrenicoabdominal vein, caudal vena cava, and surrounding tissues. There have been reports of phaeochromocytomas or other adrenal neoplasms that have destroyed the adrenal cortex, leading to hypoadrenocorticism in dogs [4–6]. In people, phaeochromocytoma has been described in an atrophied adrenal gland in a person with pre-existing hypoadrenocorticism [7]. A similar disease chronology was described by Soler Arias et al. (2017) based on post-mortem findings, as the dog had been humanely euthanised without undergoing surgical intervention [8] .

This case report presents the unique findings of phaeochromocytoma in a dog with long standing primary hypoadrenocorticism and adrenal atrophy. The objective of this report is to describe the clinical, histologic and immunohistochemical findings, management, and outcome for unilateral phaeochromocytoma in a dog with pre-existing, chronic adrenal gland insufficiency and atrophy.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old female neutered Jack Russell Terrier presented with a 1-week history of hyporexia and lethargy, as well as progressive weight loss of 1.4 kg over 5 months. The dog had been diagnosed with primary hypoadrenocorticism 6 years previous (8 years of age); results of an ACTH stimulation test were consistent with hypoadrenocorticism with baseline and post-ACTH serum cortisol concentrations of < 5.5nmol/L. The sodium/potassium ratio at time of diagnosis was 17 and abdominal ultrasonographic findings identified bilaterally small adrenal glands measuring 1.5 mm thick for the left and 2.2 mm for the right, both with normal shape and echogenicity. The dog was managed with cortisone acetate (1 mg/kg, PO, q 24 h) and desoxycorticosterone pivalate (DOCP; 1.2 mg/kg, SC, q 28 d). On the current presentation, a large, firm, cranial abdominal mass was palpable. The dog was normotensive with a systolic blood pressure of 125 mmHg.

Haematology, serum biochemistry and urinalysis revealed mild leukocytosis characterised by neutrophilia and thrombocytopenia; mild azotemia, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP); and inadequately concentrated urine, respectively (Table 1). The results were indicative of mild systemic inflammation, mineralocorticoid deficiency and possible renal insufficiency. Rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) identified mild hypercoagulability in intrinsic rotational thromboelastometry (INTEM) assay. DOCP (1.2 mg/kg, SC) was administered to address the mineralcorticoid deficiency, despite the previous dose having been administered twenty days earlier.

A computed tomography (CT) study (Aquilion Lightning 160; Canon Medical Systems ANZ Pty Ltd) of the thorax and abdomen captured pre- and post-contrast images using soft tissue, bone and lung algorithms. There were several 1–2 mm, hyperattenuating nodules at the periphery of the right cranial lung lobe, and similar but non-mineral attenuating foci in the left caudal lobe (Fig. 1). There was a large, smoothly marginated, heterogeneously contrast-enhancing mass (53 mm long, 61 mm wide, 65 mm high) that distorted the normal architecture of the cranial pole of the right kidney; there was mild mineralisation within the lateral aspect of the mass (Fig. 2). The left adrenal gland was small with a cranial pole width of 2.4 mm, and a 5.3 mm wide, round, soft tissue attenuating nodule in its caudal pole (Fig. 3). The phrenicoabdominal vein was of normal and comparable size to the right without filling defects to suggest tumour invasion or thrombi. There was no mass effect or compression to the adjacent renal vein and caudal vena cava. The right adrenal gland could not be definitively identified, as it appeared severely atrophied. The CT images suggest the presence of a primary renal carcinoma, to be confirmed by further investigations, bilateral small adrenal glands consistent with a history of chronic hypoadrenocorticism, left adrenal nodule, and small pulmonary nodules and foci. A contralateral adrenal tumour could be reasonably excluded given the absence of a soft tissue attenutating nodule at the level of the expected right adrenal gland.

Right nephroureterectomy and left adrenalectomy were performed. The dog was premedicated with methadone (0.1 mg/kg, IV) and dexmedetomidine (1 µg/kg, IV), followed by induction of anaesthesia with propofol (3 mg/kg, IV). The dog was intubated and maintained under general anaesthesia with sevoflurane in oxygen. An ultrasound-guided 3-point transabdominal plane peripheral nerve block was administered with ropivacaine (2 mg/kg). Intraoperative medication administered included cefazolin (22 mg/kg, q 90 min), glycopyrrolate (5 µg/kg, IV, pro re nata [PRN]) for hypotensive episodes with bradycardia, methadone (0.1 mg/kg, IV, PRN) and lidocaine (1 mg/kg, IV, PRN) for intraoperative nociception. During surgery, the dog received lactated Ringer’s solution (5 mL/kg/h, IV, CRI).

With the dog in dorsal recumbency, a ventral midline celiotomy exposed the large right renal mass. The kidney was dissected and mobilised from its retroperitoneal attachments with a combination of electrodissection and a vessel-sealing device. The renal vasculatures were ligated. The ureter was isolated to the level of the urinary bladder, ligated and transected at the ureterovesicular junction. The left adrenal gland was identified with a nodule on the caudal pole; there was no gross evidence of tumour invasion or thrombi into the renal vein, caudal vena cava and phrenicoabdominal vein. The adrenal gland was removed in total using a combination of blunt dissection, electrodissection and a vessel-sealing device. A hypertensive episode of 164 mmHg mean arterial pressure was associated with the handling of the left adrenal gland, which resolved with the removal of the left adrenal gland. No intra-abdominal haemorrhage was observed. The celiotomy incision was closed in 3 layers. Transsection of the right kidney postoperatively showed a well-demarcated, firm, white cortical mass that was grossly consistent with a neoplasm (Fig. 4). The left adrenal gland was transected sagittally postoperatively, revealing a distinct fleshy, dark purple nodule (Fig. 5).

The dog recovered from anaesthesia. Methadone (0.2 mg/kg, IV, q 4 h), paracetamol (10 mg/kg, IV, q 8 h), cortisone acetate (1 mg/kg, PO, q 24 h) and lactated Ringer’s solution (3 ml/kg/hr, IV, CRI) were continued for 24 h postoperatively. Venous blood gas was repeated 24 and 48 h postoperatively, which identified normonatremia at 144 mmol/L (RI, 140 to 150 mmol/L) and 145 mmol/L, respectively, and mild hypokalaemia at 3.8 mmol/L (RI, 3.9 to 4.0 mmol/L) and 3.5 mmol/L, respectively, consistent with over supplementation of mineralocorticoid. The dog was discharged 2 days postoperatively with paracetamol (10 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h), cortisone acetate (1 mg/kg, PO, q 24 h) and trazodone (5 mg/kg, PO, q 8 h PRN). Paracetamol was administered at a therapeutic dose for a limited period of time to provide post-operative analgesia, since the use of non-steroid anti-inflammatories was contraindicated in dogs with renal disease and co-administration with cortisone acetate.

Microscopic examination of the left adrenal gland showed a densely cellular and well-demarcated neoplasm occupying 80% of the adrenal gland. The neoplasm was composed of polygonal cells arranged into nests and packets supported by a fine fibrovascular stroma (Fig. 6). These cells had round nuclei with stippled chromatin and abundant clear to finely granular amphophilic cytoplasm. There was mild pleomorphism with occasional karyomegaly, and mitoses were up to 6 in a single high-power field (0.237 mm2). The neoplasm was surrounded by a thin fibrous capsule in some areas and a rim of compressed tissue ranging from 0.3 to 1 mm thick (Fig. 7). The compressed outer rim of tissue did not resemble adrenal cortex; there was no layering, and the zona glomerulosa, the zona fasciculata or the zona reticularis could not be identified. This outer rim of tissue was composed of polygonal cells with moderate to abundant cytoplasm, which was either finely granular or contained feathery, wispy cytoplasmic vacuolation. Some clusters of smaller cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm were also observed; these cells were immunonegative for lymphocyte markers CD3 and CD20 and were assumed to be haematopoietic precursor cells. The observed morphology supported a diagnosis of adrenocortical atrophy, consistent with the history of long-standing primary hypoadrenocorticism. The cells in the compressed outer rim of tissue and the neoplastic cells were diffusely, strongly immunopositive for Chromogranin A (Fig. 8) and immunonegative for Melan A; most consistent with medullary tissue [9]. The histomorphology and immunohistochemical staining pattern were supportive of a diagnosis of phaeochromocytoma. There was no evidence of capsular or vascular invasion, but increased mitotic activity and pleomorphism raised concerns for malignant transformation.

Microscopic examination of the right renal neoplasm was well-demarcated, non-encapsulated and composed of polygonal to cuboidal cell aggregates. The neoplastic cells exhibited moderate pleomorphism, and mitoses were 3 in 2.37mm [2]. There was extensive necrosis, but no evidence of lymphovascular invasion and excision appeared complete. The findings were most consistent with a renal cell carcinoma with a mixed histologic subtype. Immunohistochemistry was performed to ensure this was a discrete neoplasm not related to the adrenal neoplasm, which revealed immunonegative for Melan A and Chromogranin A.

At 3 weeks post-operative, the owner reported the dog was clinically well with no lethargy or hyporexia. Venous blood gas identified normonatremia (147 mmol/L [RI, 140 to 150 mmol/L]) and normokalemia (3.9 mmol/L [RI, 3.9 to 4.0 mmol/L]). Haematology and serum biochemistry identified mild azotemia (urea 17.5 mmol/L [RI, 3.2 to 9.3 mmol/L], creatinine 125 umol/L [RI, 53 to 124 umol/L]) and mildly elevated CRP (25 mg/L [RI, 0 to 10 mg/L]). At 7 weeks postoperative, the dog had regained the previous weight loss, from 4.1 kg to 5.5 kg. Bicavitary CT at 11 weeks postoperatively showed multiple, new, ill-defined perivascular areas of focal ground-glass attenuation throughout the lungs and static pulmonary nodules. Absence of the right kidney and left adrenal gland was noted, with a small degree of left kidney enlargement from previous imaging. There were no clinical signs associated with the pulmonary changes, and the hypoadrenocorticism was well controlled.

Discussion and conclusions

This case report describes a unique clinical scenario of a dog with pre-existing primary hypoadrenocorticism and adrenal gland atrophy that subsequently developed a phaeochromocytoma. To the author’s knowledge, majority of published veterinary cases described adrenal neoplasms as the underlying cause of adrenal insufficiency, and a single case of a subsequent development was reported by Soler Arias et al. (2017). Therefore, the temporal sequence where adrenocortical insufficiency was diagnosed well before the development of adrenal neoplasm is unique based on the available veterinary literature. Since primary hypoadrenocorticism affects the adrenal cortex, the later development of a medullary neoplasm (phaeochromocytoma) is biologically plausible and consistent with this case.

In contrast to the present case, Kook et al. (2010) reported a case of hypoadrenocorticism due to bilateral adrenal malignancy, highlighting the potential for adrenal cortical neoplasms to cause hypoadrenocorticism due to destruction of the adrenal cortex [4]. Similarly, invasive medullary or metastatic neoplasia can lead to clinical signs of adrenal insufficiency when they infiltrate a significant portion of the adrenal gland [5, 6] Collectively, these reports support a causal relationship in which destruction or neoplastic invasion of the adrenal cortex resulted in adrenal insufficiency. Soler Arias et al. (2017) described a dog with bilateral phaeochromocytoma and a parathyroid adenoma in the setting of long-standing primary adrenal insufficiency, paralleling aspects of the present case [8]. However, surgical intervention was not pursued in that case due to extensive tumour invasion of the abdominal phrenic vein and caudal vena cava, which led to the decision for euthanasia. Notably, the concurrent occurrence of renal cell carcinoma and phaeochromocytoma in dogs has also not been previously published. In people, renal neoplasia is considered a risk factor for the development of additional neoplasia, due to shared underlying gene mutations such as UROC28, which has been identified in cancers of different organs [10]. Therefore, an underlying shared pathophysiologic mechanism, potentially involving hormonal influences or genetic predispositions, warrants further investigation.

Although a medullary neoplasm (phaeochromocytoma) was the primary differential diagnosis for the adrenal mass in this dog, the history of hypoadrenocorticism complicated the clinical picture. Primary adrenal insufficiency typically leads to hypotension, which may have overshadowed hypertensive episodes commonly seen in functional phaeochromocytoma. Nonetheless, the intraoperative hypertensive response to adrenal manipulation does support the diagnosis of a functional phaeochromocytoma, which released catecholamines upon surgical manipulation [11]. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong immunopositivity for Chromogranin A and immunonegativity for Melan A within the adrenal mass, confirming the diagnosis of a phaeochromocytoma. Determining the functional status of an adrenal tumour prior to surgery can be challenging, particularly in cases of phaeochromocytoma or tumours that secrete sex hormones and mineralocorticoids [12-13].Pre-surgical biochemical characterisation of adrenal tumours is not routinely performed at this institution. Nevertheless, urine normetanephrine: creatinine ratio and plasma normetanephrine, where available, should be considered as part of the diagnostic workup for suspected phaeochromocytoma [12, 14]. This is of particular importance given that general anaesthesia and adrenalectomy in dogs with phaeochromocytoma have been associated with significant complications, including blood pressure variability and tachyarrhythmias [15]. Identifying a functional phaeochromocytoma allows perioperative risk stratification and ensures that appropriate management is implemented across the pre-, peri-, and postoperative periods.

Regarding preoperative medical management, phenoxybenzamine, an alpha-adrenergic receptor antagonist, was previously shown in one study to significantly reduce perioperative mortality from 48% to 13% [15]. Phenoxybenzamine administration perioperative was recommended days to weeks before adrenalectomy to block the alpha-adrenergic response to circulating catecholamines and minimise deleterious effects of excessive catecholamine secretion that occur during the perioperative period [15]. However, more recent studies have not demonstrated a correlation between phenoxybenzamine administration and mortality, nor a significant effect on perioperative cardiovascular stability, specifically with regard to perioperative hypertensive episodes and tachyarrhythmias [16–17]. Additionally, one study reported that dogs managed without preoperative treatment can achieve favourable short-term outcomes, with low complication and mortality rates following elective adrenalectomy [18] Phenoxybenzamine is also associated with potential adverse effects, including hypotension, vomiting, and tachycardia [18]. As the dog in this case report was normotensive at initial presentation and throughout the preoperative period, preoperative therapy aimed at mitigating the effects of a functional adrenal tumour was not administered. Instead, our institutional protocol emphasises careful intraoperative monitoring, including continuous blood pressure measurement via arterial catheter and electrocardiography, with effective communication between the surgeon and anaesthetist to anticipate and rapidly address catecholamine surges caused by tumour manipulation.

Retrospective studies indicated that phaeochromocytoma is more frequently associated with vascular invasion and intravascular thrombi than adrenocortical carcinomas. Reported rates of local vascular invasion range from 11% to 21.5% for adrenocortical carcinomas and from 15% to 55% for phaeochromocytoma [19–20]. Caval thrombi occur in 11% to 16% of dogs with adrenocortical tumours and in 35% to 55% of dogs with phaeochromocytoma [21–22]. In the present case, CT revealed no evidence of vascular invasion and intravascular thrombi within the caudal vena cava, phrenicoabdominal vein and renal vein. Accordingly, adrenalectomy was advocated to obtain a histological diagnosis and mitigate the future risk of vascular invasion or thrombus formation. The low perioperative complication rate for adrenalectomy in dogs (5.7%) for elective procedures, along with the small tumour size and absence of vascular invasion, further supported surgical intervention [19]. Although long-term outcome could not be assessed in this case, a previous study supports excellent survival following adrenalectomy, with more than 60% of dogs with phaeochromocytoma and over 90% of dogs with adrenocortical carcinoma surviving beyond three years post-operatively [20].

Lastly, although the pulmonary lesions could represent primary pulmonary tumours or pulmonary metastases, histopathological confirmation was not achieved in this case. Pulmonary metastasis of renal cell carcinoma was considered the most likely diagnosis, given the well-documented propensity of renal carcinoma to metastasise to the lungs [23]. Renal cell carcinoma is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, and presumed pulmonary metastases have been identified on thoracic radiographs in 11% to 48% of dogs at initial presentation [23–24]. Dogs with metastatic disease at presentation have shorter survival times compared to those without evidence of metastasis, that may have extended survival of up to four years following nephrectomy [23]. However, dogs with a mitotic index of fewer than 10 have been shown to have a significantly increased median survival time of 1,184 days following nephrectomy [24], which further supported surgical intervention in this case.

This case is particularly significant because it provides the first documented evidence of a phaeochromocytoma arising in a dog with established primary hypoadrenocorticism that has undergone surgical intervention. This chronology challenges the conventional understanding of adrenal disease progression and demonstrates that medullary neoplasia can occur independently after the onset of cortical insufficiency that only affects the adrenal cortex. This rare combination of primary hypoadrenocorticism with a later-developing phaeochromocytoma and renal cell carcinoma may have contributed to the adrenal neoplasm being initially overlooked, since the occurrence of a tumour in an atrophied adrenal gland is unexpected. Therefore, it highlights CT as a valuable diagnostic modality in identifying adrenal changes in an atrophied adrenal gland. The adrenal gland consists of two morphologic and physiologic endocrine glands, the cortex and medulla, and it is imperative to consider that a neoplasm of each gland can develop independently [25]. This case emphasised the importance of ongoing surveillance for other adrenal pathologies in dogs with known hypoadrenocorticism, particularly when clinical signs evolve. Managing concurrent renal and adrenal neoplasms in dogs is not well-documented, and this case contributes insights into the pre-operative, surgical and postoperative considerations. Right nephroureterectomy and left adrenalectomy allowed excision of both affected organs and the opportunity for histologic clarification of a primary or metastatic adrenal neoplasm. The dog’s recovery was uneventful, with normalisation of electrolyte concentrations and no immediate postoperative complications. Repeat CT performed at 11 weeks post-operatively also showed no changes in the local region of excision or changes in the pulmonary nodules.

This report contributes novel insights to the development of adrenal disease in dogs and underscores the need to consider phaeochromocytoma as a differential diagnosis even in dogs with known cortical insufficiency and adrenal atrophy. The successful surgical management and uncomplicated recovery further demonstrate that adrenalectomy is a viable treatment option in such complex endocrine cases.
