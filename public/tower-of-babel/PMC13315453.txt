Analysis on the formation mechanism of porous microstructure on aluminum grooves by ultrasonic cavitation treatment
Authors: Guisheng Zhong, Yang Qiu, Jiahao Xia, Zhenxing Yu, Qingxian Liu, Changqing Gao, Jiajun Wu, Longshi Rao, Yong Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the ultrasonic cavitation treatment, the morphology of groove surface can be changed obviously due to the cavitation impact. This study intends to investigate the formation mechanism of porous microstructure on the groove surface. Experiments were conducted to explore the effects of ultrasonic cavitation duration and clearance on the formation of microstructure on two types of aluminum grooves. Meanwhile, an acoustic-fluid coupling simulation is executed to analyze the acoustic pressure distribution and flow velocity characteristics under different ultrasonic treatment clearances. The results show that the porous microstructure appears preferentially at the bottom of the groove, and gradually extends to the peak as the treatment duration increases. The bottom corner of the groove is more susceptible to cavitation impact due to its smaller radius of curvature and higher flow velocity. The ultrasonic clearance mainly affects the distribution and size of the porous microstructure at the peak of the groove due to the variation of flow velocity. As the clearance decreases, the high-pressure region tends to concentrate and expand into the grooves, and the flow velocity also increases obviously. The higher flow velocity may accelerate the movement of bubbles to the grooves, thereby increasing the likelihood of cavitation impacts on the groove surfaces. And too smaller clearance also results in the smaller size of micro-hollows at the groove peak due to the relatively weak cavitation impact form the restricted cavitation bubbles. These results will contribute to the parameter optimization of the treatment process.Keywords: Ultrasonic cavitation, Porous microstructure, Numerical simulation, Aluminum groove

Introduction

Ultrasonic vibration is utilized in a wide variety of industrial applications, such as ultrasonic burnishing [1], ultrasonic machining [2], sonochemistry [3] and so on. The above applications take advantage of ultrasonic cavitation induced by ultrasonic vibration, as it can lead to localized high temperatures and pressures during the collapse of cavitation bubbles. Ultrasonic cavitation refers to the phenomenon of microbubble growth and collapse in a liquid within the ultrasonic field [4]. As ultrasonic waves propagate in the liquid, they cause the molecules in the medium to vibrate, thereby changing the average distance between molecules. When the distance surpasses a critical threshold, the structural integrity of the liquid is destroyed, leading to the formation of cavities [5]. Under continuous ultrasonic irradiation, these cavities evolve into cavitation bubbles and undergo a series of dynamic behaviors such as growth and collapse. In the final stage of bubble collapse, cavitation shock waves and microjets are also generated, ultimately resulting in the cavitation effect [6].

Grooved wick is one of the most popular wick structures in heat pipes due to its advantages of low cost and easy fabrication. However, a smooth grooved wick possesses low capillary pressure properties, which may limit the thermal performance of heat pipes. With the intensive studies on porous microstructure, it was found that the groove with porous microstructure can yield more excellent properties than the smooth one, including high heat transfer coefficient, excellent capillary performance [7], and high nucleation site density [8]. So far several technologies have been developed for manufacturing the grooves with porous microstructure on different material surfaces, such as powder sintering [9], laser etching [10], [11], additive manufacturing [12], and electrochemical deposition [13], etc. These methods are limited in applicable materials, and the bonding strength between the porous microstructure and the base surface is not strong enough to meet the requirements of some vibration conditions, such as in spacecrafts and satellites. In the aerospace industry, aluminum heat pipes with grooved wick play a vital role in the thermal management of the electronic system [14]. Owing to the hindrance effect of the dense oxide film on aluminum surface, it is challenging to fabricate porous structure with excellent capillary performance on the aluminum groove surfaces. Therefore, developing a simple and effective method to realize the fabrication of porous structure on the aluminum grooved wick has recently attracted intensive interests.

Ultrasonic cavitation has great potential to create porous structure on metal surfaces. Cavitation erosion is one of the main phenomena during ultrasonic cavitation, which can produce microcavities and microcracks on the material surface. Recently, ultrasonic cavitation is extensively developed to produce porous micro/nanostructures on the flat surface of metal and glass substrates [15], [16], [17]. Zhukova et al. [16] studied the effects of ultrasound intensity and duration of treatment on the properties and morphology of titania nanofoam on the titanium surface. Virot et al. [18] studied physical and chemical effects resulting from ultrasound-induced erosion on glass surfaces. In our previous work [19], the ultrasonic cavitation method was proposed to fabricate porous microstructure on the surface of grooved aluminum wicks, and the effect of ultrasonic modification time on their wettability and capillary performance was studied. Moreover, the aluminum plate modified with ultrasonic cavitation porous microstructure was proved to enhancing the boiling heat transfer performance [20].

Although the ultrasonic cavitation is validated as an effective method for fabricating porous microstructure on aluminum grooves, the formation mechanism of porous microstructure on the groove surface under ultrasonic cavitation remains unclear. Recent advances in numerical and experimental investigations have substantially improved our understanding of cavitation dynamics and cavitation-induced erosion in engineering components [21], [22], [23]. Only a few studies revealed the mechanism of cavitating flow-induced microplastic deformation on solid surfaces. Qin et al. [24] studied the bubble oscillation behaviors interacting with bulk graphite under the multiscale interactions of liquid, bubbles and solid phases in ultrasonic fields. Wang et al. [25] simulated and validated the effect of ultrasonic cavitation micro-jet impact on surface morphology of 6061 aluminum alloy in micromachining. Dabir-Moghaddam et al. [26] developed a model to study the cavitating flow close to a solid surface with a micro hole. Ye et al. [27] established a three-dimensional fluid–solid coupling model to study the effects of near-wall acoustic bubble collapse micro-jet on the near-wall surface of Al 1060 plate. However, the existing research did not conduct modeling works to explore the effect of cavitating flow on the ultrasonic cavitation impact near the groove surface.

To explore the formation mechanism of porous microstructure on aluminum groove surface under ultrasonic cavitation, experiments are conducted on two types of 1060 aluminum groove plates to investigate the effects of ultrasonic cavitation duration and clearance. The formation mechanism of porous microstructure on the groove surface caused by ultrasonic cavitation is further discussed by establishing a 3D acoustic-fluid coupling model. The distributions of acoustic pressure and flow velocity are analyzed to reveal the effects of ultrasonic treatment clearance on the plastic deformation of groove surface. The preferential cavitation impact occurs at the bottom corner of the groove is also verified. These research results will contribute to the parameter optimization and application of the treatment process.

Ultrasonic cavitation experimental process

Fig. 1 shows the ultrasonic treatment experimental apparatus for the generation of ultrasonic cavitation processes. The apparatus mainly consists of an ultrasonic generator, an ultrasonic transducer, a horn, a liquid container, and a three-axis displacement controller. The ultrasonic transducer was driven by the ultrasonic generator at a fixed frequency of 20 kHz, which was mounted at a computer-controlled X-Z-direction holder. The ultrasonic vibration was created by the transducer in the vertical direction, and the horn was used to amplify the ultrasonic amplitude to realize a powerful ultrasonic oscillation at the tip. The diameter of the horn tip was 8 mm and the ultrasonic amplitude on the tip measured in air was about 16 μm. The workpiece was mounted at the bottom of the container which was fixed at a Y-direction working stage. Distilled water was chosen as the liquid medium which contains little impurities and is commonly used in ultrasonic cavitation experiments for the generation of cavitation bubbles. During the ultrasonic treatment process, the horn tip should be submerged into distilled water completely for the continuous cavitation process. Hence, the distance between the horn tip and the water surface was maintained in 10 mm for experimental consistency.

Ultrasonic treatment experimental apparatus.

The groove plates used in this study were fabricated by aluminum extrusion and the samples were cut into the dimension of 10 mm × 10 mm by wire cutting. Two types of grooves were employed in the test, trapezoidal grooves and rectangular grooves. The dimension of the grooves is shown in Fig. 2. Their average radius of curvature at the bottom corner are measured to be 0.22 mm and 0.14 mm, respectively. Before each ultrasonic cavitation treatment, the position of the sample relative to the horn was measured and recorded on the computer. The treatment process was achieved with a computer program that moved the operating horn over the workpiece to a constant clearance, then keeping ultrasonically modifying each sample for a desired duration and finally returning to the origin. Moreover, in order to suppress the rise in liquid temperature caused by cavitation, a constant temperature water bath system (Scientz DC-1006) was utilized to circulate distilled water in the container. The liquid temperature around the horn tip is maintained at 25 ± 1℃ by monitoring with a K-type thermocouple near the horn tip. More details on the parameters of the ultrasonic treatment process are shown in Table 1. After ultrasonic cavitation treatment, the morphologies of the modified groove samples were characterized by the Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (Merlin & LEO@, 1530VP, Germany). For better observation of the microstructure evolution, the cross sections of the modified groove samples were also examined using an optical microscopy as auxiliary observations.

To clarify the intrinsic relationship between the formation of microstructures and the distribution of the acoustic field and flow field on the groove surface, COMSOL Multiphysics was used to couple the acoustic module with the CFD module for calculation.

Physics field governing equations

The liquid medium used in the ultrasonic cavitation experiment is water, the bulk modulus of which is about 2.1GPa. In a wide range of temperature and pressure (298–343 K, up to 100 MPa), the isothermal compressibility of water is very small and it is generally regarded as an incompressible fluid [28]. For acoustic field, it can assume that the liquid medium is incompressible. When the liquid pressure is decreased beyond a critical tension, the liquid will rupture and form cavitation bubbles. Cavitation bubbles can scatter sound waves thereby attenuating their propagation [29]. Geometrical model, boundary conditions and mesh

Taking advantage of the symmetry, it is only necessary to model a quarter of the ultrasonic cavitation computational domain for reducing the subsequent meshing and accelerating computation. Fig. 3 shows the computational model and its mesh division. The blue region represents the aluminum rectangular groove, and the horn is positioned vertically above the peak of the groove. The basic parameters used in the simulation are shown in Table 2. For the mesh division, the fluid dynamics mesh is selected and at least five mesh elements are contained within one wavelength. The free tetrahedral mesh is used for grid segmentation. The fluid dynamics finer mesh is applied for the groove structure at the bottom of the model and the horn tip (maximum size 0.861 mm, minimum size 0.163 mm, curvature factor 0.5, growth rate 1.13), with configuring a three-layer boundary layer mesh. While the remaining areas of the model is meshed using the fluid dynamics normal mesh setting (maximum size 1.09 mm, minimum size 0.325 mm, curvature factor 0.6, growth rate 1.15).

The ultrasonic cavitation computation domain is first solved by using Pressure Acoustics, Frequency Domain interface of the Acoustics Module. A quadratic Lagrange discretization is applied to provide a more detailed approximation of the solution, and the attenuation of acoustic pressure induced by the intrinsic properties of the liquid medium is considered in the process.

To mimic the wave propagation in open domain, the impedance boundary conditions are imposed on the outer boundary of the computational domain, as shown in Fig. 4. The cylindrical side of the horn in contact with the working medium is set to the acoustic impedance of titanium alloy (Z = 4470 kg/m3 × 4987 m/s), the liquid–air boundary is set to the acoustic impedance of air (Z = 1.2 kg/m3 × 343 m/s), and the aluminum groove plate boundary is set to the impedance of aluminum (Z = 2375 kg/m3 × 4600 m/s). The liquid–container boundary is modeled as an absorbing layer with the acoustic impedance of water (Z = 997.05 kg/m3 × 1496.7 m/s). The ultrasonic wave is introduced into the liquid medium through the horn, so the horn tip is set as the pressure boundary with a pressure amplitude (PA) [38]. The remaining surfaces of the model are set as the symmetric boundary.

Results and discussion

Experimental results and discussion

Effect of treatment duration

During the ultrasonic treatment, numerous cavitation bubbles form in the clearance between the horn tip and the sample surface. As cavitation bubbles expand to a critical size and approach the sample surface, they will collapse asymmetrically and yield a few micro-jets and shock waves, leading to intense cavitation impacts on the sample surface. The SEM images of the sample surfaces under different ultrasonic treatment durations are given in Fig. 7. The findings indicate a notable impact of ultrasonic cavitation on the morphology of the aluminum groove surface. After the ultrasonic cavitation treatment, the originally smooth surface became uneven with the appearance of microstructures. Under 100 × magnification, it can be found that the bottom of the grooves was modified with microstructures gradually after the beginning of the ultrasonic treatment, which occurs at the bottom corner of the grooves firstly. As the ultrasonic treatment proceeded (more than 7.2 mins), microstructural development spread to the peak and sidewall of the grooves. This phenomenon is attributable to the fact that in the early stage of ultrasonic treatment, the density of cavitation bubbles collapsed near the bottom corner of the grooves is higher than in any other region on the groove surface. Under 400 × magnification, the bottom of the groove was relatively smooth, with only a few slight extrusion traces. Under the action of ultrasonic cavitation, it transformed into a rough, rock-like form, characterized by numerous micro-hollows and ridges. Simultaneously, with the extension of the treatment duration, the ridges deteriorated into rougher, smaller rock-like aggregates, leading to a rise in micro-hollows and the creation of numerous complex internal microchannels within these micro-hollows. These micro-hollows and microchannels construct to the porous microstructure on the aluminum groove surface. Namely, although the effect of ultrasonic cavitation impact on groove surface is heterogeneous in the early stage of ultrasonic treatment, the entire groove surface can be modified with homogeneous porous microstructure by appropriately prolonging the ultrasonic treatment duration.

SEM images of the trapezoidal aluminum grooves with the treatment clearance of 0.5 mm and different treatment time: (a) T1, (b) T2, (c) T3, (d) T4, (e) T5 and (f) T6.

For better analyzing the effect of ultrasonic cavitation on the groove surface, the optical microscopies of the cross section of grooves under different treatment durations are shown in Fig. 8. According to the evolution of the groove profile in Fig. 8a-b, it is obvious that the first modified region is the bottom corner of the grooves (marked by white dotted outlines) where the significant plastic deformation occurs in 2 mins due to the cavitation impact. Subsequently, the plastic deformation region extends towards the bottom center and the peak of the grooves. This evolution process is consistent with the above analysis of microstructure formation, indicating that most of the cavitation bubbles may grow and collapse at the bottom corners of the grooves at the beginning of ultrasonic treatment, producing strong cavitation impacts at the bottom corners, causing plastic deformation and forming porous microstructure. Numerous studies conclude that the microscopic hollows and cracks on the solid surface can trap air and serve as nucleation sites for cavitation bubbles upon immersion in water [29], [41]. As shown in Fig. 7a, the bottom of the initial groove is smoother than its sidewall and peak which have several notable extrusion traces. Measured by the laser confocal microscopy, the roughness Ra of the original groove bottom, corner, sidewall and peak are about 1.84 μm, 1.64 μm, 2.3 μm and 2.41 μm, respectively. So, cavitation bubbles are supposed to generate and collapse on the sidewall or peak of groove firstly, but this is contrary to the experimental result. Thus, there must be another reason for promoting the generation and collapse of cavitation bubbles near the bottom corner of the groove. In terms of the radius of curvature, the bottom corner has the minimum value on the entire groove surface. It can be assumed that the region with smaller radius of curvature have a positive effect on the generation of cavitation bubbles, thereby enhancing the cavitation impact intensity at the bottom conner of the groove.

Optical micrographs of the cross-section of the modified trapezoidal grooves: (a) T1, (b) T2, (c) T3, (d) T4, (e) T5, and (f) T6.

In addition, as shown in Fig. 8f, the peak of the groove suffered high-intensity cavitation damage and became very fragile with the increase of ultrasonic treatment duration (up to 10 min). The depth of the groove tended to decrease, while the width became larger. It is indicated that excessive ultrasonic cavitation treatment not only produces a more homogeneous distribution of porous microstructure on the entire groove surface, but also causes some damage to the inherent structure of the groove. Consequently, the optimal treatment duration for the trapezoidal grooves may be 7.2 min, which can achieve the optimal balance between achieving homogeneous porous microstructure and avoiding structural damage.

Effect of clearance

In order to verify the assumption that the region with smaller radius of curvature is conducive to the generation of ultrasonic cavitation bubbles, a series of ultrasonic treatment experiments were carried out on the rectangular groove plates which has a smaller radius of curvature at the bottom corner compared to the trapezoidal one. Meanwhile, the effect of clearance between the horn tip and the peak of the groove on the formation of porous microstructure was further investigated. Fig. 9 shows the SEM images of the rectangular grooves treated with ultrasonic cavitation at different clearances. First, the obvious plastic deformation with the appearance of porous microstructure is observed at the bottom corner of the rectangular grooves at all different clearances. It is obvious that the bottom corners of the rectangular grooves are subjected to cavitation impacts in all cases of different clearances. As increasing the ultrasonic treatment clearance, the ultrasonic cavitation intensity at the bottom corner is degraded, resulting in low efficiency in the formation of porous microstructure. Second, when the clearance is less than 0.5 mm (R1 and R2), the peak of all grooves is modified with porous microstructure, whereas no significant change occurs in these regions when the clearance exceeds 0.7 mm (R3 and R4). It is indicated that the influence of the cavitation impact on the peak of the grooves is very slight when the clearance is large enough. In addition, the size of micro-hollows at the peak of grooves in R1 is smaller compared to R2, indicating that the excessively narrow space restricts the growth of cavitation bubbles and weakens the cavitation impact from the bubble collapse. Hence, with a large clearance, porous microstructure can only be modified at the bottom corner of the grooves, and the modification effect gradually weakens as the clearance increases. With a small clearance, both the peak and bottom corner of the grooves can be modified with porous microstructure. As the clearance further decreases, the size of microstructures at the peak of the grooves also become smaller, while those at the bottom of the grooves change negligibly. Besides, combined with the appearance of micro-hollows at the groove bottom under a large clearance, it also indirectly confirms that the bottom corner of the groove with smaller radius of curvature is favorable for the generation of cavitation bubbles, and thus preferentially suffers from cavitation impact. Under the same ultrasonic treatment parameters (0.5 mm, 7.2 min), the size of micro-hollows on the rectangular groove is larger than that on the trapezoidal groove, while the microstructure distribution on the trapezoidal groove is denser and more uniform than that on the rectangular groove. This may be because the peak width of the rectangular groove is greater than that of the trapezoidal groove, making it more susceptible to direct cavitation impact. Simultaneously, the depth of the rectangular groove is greater than that of the trapezoidal groove, resulting in the weaker cavitation impact on its bottom surface.

SEM images of the rectangular aluminum grooves with a treatment duration of 7.2 mins: (a) R1, (b) R2, (c) R3, and (d) R4.

Besides, the mass loss results in Table 3 indicate that minimal material removal occurred during ultrasonic cavitation treatment. For the trapezoidal groove, a clearance of 0.5 mm and a treatment duration of 7.2 min result in a mass loss of just 0.9 mg. By comparison, the rectangular grooves experienced a slightly larger mass loss with a maximum loss of 1.3 mg, which is still very limited. It is further proved that the formation of porous microstructures is mainly due to microplastic deformation of the groove surface under cavitation impact.

Simulation results and discussion

Effect of clearance on the acoustic field

Fig. 10 illustrates the acoustic pressure distribution characteristics near the rectangular groove surface under different ultrasonic treatment clearances. It can be observed that the acoustic pressure distributions under different clearances are similar, exhibiting a fan-like formation with radial attenuation. The high-pressure region is predominantly situated beneath the horn tip. When the clearance exceeds 0.5 mm, the maximum acoustic pressure occurs near the horn tip and is about 1.89 × 106 Pa. This could be linked to the pressure amplitude of the horn tip. Given the constancy of ultrasonic power and its direct correlation with pressure amplitude, the maximum acoustic pressure values are consistent when the clearance ranges from 0.5 mm and 1 mm. At the treatment clearance of 0.3 mm, the high-pressure region can nearly cover the entire groove beneath the horn tip, with the maximum acoustic pressure marginally rising to approximately 1.92 × 106 Pa. This can be attributed to the enhanced reflection of sound waves by the groove walls at smaller clearances, increasing the acoustic pressure. Hence, the high-pressure region expands and extends further towards the bottom of the groove as the clearance decreases.

The predicted active cavitation region under different ultrasonic treatment clearances is shown in Fig. 11. Regardless of the clearance, the active cavitation region is confined within the clearance zone. As the clearance decreases, the active cavitation region expands radially. When the clearance is less than 0.5 mm, the active cavitation region almost completely covers the entire groove surface below the horn tip. This indicates that the groove surface below the horn tip satisfy the cavitation conditions within the tested clearance range.

Predicted active cavitation region of micro-groove models under different ultrasonic treatment clearances: (a) 0.3 mm, (b) 0.5 mm, (c) 0.7 mm, (d) 1 mm.

Fig. 12 depicts the acoustic pressure distribution in the z direction along lines a and b. Typically, there's an increase in acoustic pressure along the Z-axis's positive axis, and the acoustic pressure at the bottom of the grooves also increases as the clearance decreases. The acoustic pressure at the bottom of the groove in the center area below the horn tip is higher than that in the edge area, but the difference also decreases as the ultrasonic clearance becomes narrow. When the clearance decreases to 0.3 mm, the entire area from the horn tip to the bottom of the groove is completely transformed into a uniform high-pressure region. The acoustic pressure near the center of horn tip is only slightly greater than that at the bottom of the groove. Namely, with reduced clearance, the high-pressure region tends to concentrate and expand into the grooves, leading to a more uniform distribution of acoustic pressure close to the groove surface. Since acoustic pressure is one of the key factors in the generation of bubble cavitation, uniform high acoustic pressure distribution means that the probability of cavitation bubble occurring at any point in the region is similar [33]. Hence, the smaller clearance may lead to more intense and even cavitation effect on the groove surface.

Effect of clearance on the flow field

Fig. 13 illustrates the distribution of the flow velocity under different ultrasonic treatment clearances. For the clearances of 1.0 mm, 0.7 mm, 0.5 mm and 0.3 mm, the maximum flow velocities within the active cavitation region are 9.21 m/s, 10.7 m/s, 12.0 m/s and 13.1 m/s, respectively. The maximum flow velocity value increases gradually at a similar rate as the clearance decreases. Meanwhile, the velocity distributions at the center section in the cavitation region show that there is a significant velocity difference in the area below the horn, and the higher flow velocity is located near the edge of the horn parallel to the groove direction. For the clearances of 1.0 mm, 0.7 mm, 0.5 mm and 0.3 mm, the maximum velocity differences around the edge of the horn are approximately 3.75 m/s, 5.43 m/s, 6.53 m/s and 7.02 m/s, respectively. It is clear that, as the clearance decreases, the flow velocity perpendicular to the groove direction gradually decreases, while the high flow velocity region parallel to the groove expands towards the bottom of the groove. It is evident that the high flow velocity region forms a ring-like shape near the horn tip with higher flow velocities concentrated near the outer diameter of the horn, which is similar to the findings of Bai et al. [42] and Dular et al. [43]. Additionally, a reduction in clearance leads to a corresponding decrease in the size of the ring-like region.

Predicted flowing velocity distribution of micro-groove models under different ultrasonic treatment clearances: (a) 0.3 mm, (b) 0.5 mm, (c) 0.7 mm, (d) 1 mm. (á-d́) correspond to the velocity distribution of the center section in the cavitation region.

Fig. 14 further shows the flow velocity distribution in the planes vertical to Y-axis at the peak and bottom of the groove, as well as the flow velocity profile in the plane vertical to X-axis at the midpoint of the horn radius. As the clearance decreases, the high flow velocity region at the peak of the grooves is gradually compressed, moving away from the horn tip and extending towards the center of the groove bottom. There is an obvious expansion in the area of the high flow velocity region at the bottom of the grooves. This indicates that the decreasing clearance extends the high-pressure region towards the bottom of the groove, thereby enhancing the flow in this region. The high acoustic pressure can facilitate the formation of cavitation bubbles, and the high flow velocity can accelerate the movement of bubbles to the wall, thereby heightening the likelihood of cavitation impact on the groove surface. In addition, the flow velocity distribution in the section perpendicular to the groove direction shows that the near-wall velocity at the peak is lower than that at the bottom. The region near the edge of the horn inside the groove exhibits a significantly higher flow velocity compared to other regions. For the clearance of 0.3 mm, the highest flow velocity region is completely located inside the groove, resulting in the relatively weak cavitation impact at the groove peak. This also corresponds to the reduction in the size of the micro-hollows at the groove peak in R1. Hence, the reduction in clearance can increase the flow velocity at the bottom of the groove, which may help to enhance the ultrasonic cavitation effect.

The velocity distribution of at the bottom and the peak of groove

Fig. 16 shows the near-wall flow velocity variation curves of the peak and bottom corner of the groove at a distance of 2 mm from the center section of the horn in the y-axis direction. The x-coordinate represents the distance from the horn center section in the direction parallel to the groove. Given that the liquid velocity on the groove wall is almost zero, the flow velocity at a distance of 0.01 mm from the wall serves as a basis for comparison. Symbols P and B represent the peak and the bottom corner of the groove, respectively. The x1 indicates the length of the region where the flow velocity at the bottom corner is greater than that at the peak, and the x2 denotes the reverse. Regardless of the treatment clearance, x1 is consistently larger than x2, indicating that the high flow velocity region at the bottom is generally larger than that at the peak and the corresponding flow velocity is also higher. The high-velocity fluid has the ability to entrain bubbles, potentially leading to an increased number of cavitation bubbles moving into the bottom of the groove along the streamline simultaneously. When the cavitation bubble approaches the groove surface, the asymmetric force causes the bubble to deform and collapse towards the surface. Therefore, the likelihood of asymmetric collapse of cavitation bubbles is higher at the bottom of the groove. Additionally, since the bottom corner of the groove with a small radius is more conducive to the formation of cavitation bubbles, the density of the cavitation bubble collapsed at the bottom of the groove is higher. Namely, under the same conditions, the cavitation impact at the bottom is stronger than that at the peak of the groove, making the bottom more susceptible to plastic deformation. This also helps explain why the micro-hollows will appear at the bottom of the groove as the ultrasonic treatment proceeds.

Near-wall velocity curves at the bottom and peak of micro-groove models under different ultrasonic treatment clearances: (a) 0.3 mm, (b) 0.5 mm, (c) 0.7 mm, (d)1mm. x1: Length of the interval where the velocity at the bottom is greater than the peak of the groove; x2: Length of the interval where the velocity at the peak is greater than the bottom of the groove.

Combined with the experimental results, Fig. 16 further reveals the substantial effect of the flow velocity and clearance on the formation of porous microstructure at the groove peak. At the clearances of 0.7 mm and 1 mm, x2 is much less than x1, and the flow velocity is relatively small, indicating the efficiency of cavitation bubbles reaching the groove surface is lower. Meanwhile, there are no significant morphological changes observed at the peak of the groove. This can be attributed to the fact that, when the ultrasonic treatment clearance is too large and the flow velocity is small, most cavitation bubbles may collapse before reaching the groove surface due to the high-frequency rarefaction-compression cycle of the acoustic pressure. As a result, the cavitation impact dissipates rapidly during propagation, failing to cause notable plastic deformation at the peak of the groove. However, when the clearance is decreased to less than 0.5 mm, x2 increases slightly and the flow velocity also increase obviously. Both the smaller clearance and the higher flow velocity of fluid is beneficial to improve the efficiency of cavitation bubbles reaching the groove surface, enhancing the effective cavitation impact on the groove peak. Hence, the high flow velocity is a key factor to realize the formation of porous microstructure at the groove peak.

On the other hand, the small clearance may inhibit the growth of cavitation bubbles and intensify their collision. Some cavitation bubbles collapse at the groove peak before reaching the critical radius, leading to relatively weak cavitation impact, which significantly influences the morphology of the groove peak. The narrower the clearance, the weaker the cavitation impact, resulting in the smaller size of micro-hollows at the peak of the R1.

Conclusions

In this paper, the formation mechanism of porous microstructure on aluminum groove surface under ultrasonic cavitation are explored by combining experiments and simulation analysis. The main focus of experiments is on the effects of ultrasonic treatment clearance and duration on the evolution and distribution of microstructure on the groove surface. A 3D model was established to perform the coupled simulation of the acoustic field and flow field. The effects of ultrasonic treatment clearance on the acoustic field and flow field distribution were analyzed to explain the formation process of microstructure. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1)As the ultrasonic treatment duration increases, the microstructure evolves from preferential appearance at the bottom corner of grooves to a more uniform porous texture across the entire groove surface. This is caused by serious plastic deformation due to the strong cavitation impacts, particularly at the bottom corner which is conductive to the generation of cavitation bubbles due to its smaller radius of curvature. But excessive ultrasonic cavitation treatment may cause some damage to the inherent structure of the groove. The optimal treatment duration may be 7.2 min, considering the optimal balance between achieving homogeneous porous microstructure and avoiding structural damage.

(2)The clearance between the ultrasonic horn and the aluminum groove surface significantly influences the formation of the porous microstructure. Smaller clearances (less than 0.5 mm) can realize a more pronounced microstructure formation at both the peak and bottom of the grooves. Conversely, larger clearances (exceed 0.5 mm) reduce the efficiency of cavitation impact, resulting in a less uniform microstructure, primarily concentrated at the bottom of the groove.

In summary, this study offers fundamental insights into the formation mechanism of porous microstructure on the groove surface during ultrasonic cavitation treatment. Building upon these findings, future work will focus on establishing a theoretical model to simulate the dynamic impact of cavitation bubble on the groove surface in the clearance under the simulated acoustic pressure and flow field conditions, and exploring the effect of curvature radius on porous microstructure.
