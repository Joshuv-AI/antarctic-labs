Baseline vitamin D levels and progression-free survival in patients with HR+/HER2−metastatic breast cancer treated with ribociclib: a retrospective single-center analysis
Authors: Selahattin Çelik, Bengü Dursun, Serkan Gülcü, Dılşa Mızrak Kaya, Ömür Berna Öksüzoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin D has been implicated in cancer biology and immunity, yet its prognostic relevance in patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors remains unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 89 patients with HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer (MBC) treated with ribociclib plus endocrine therapy between January 2018 and December 2023 at a single institution. Baseline serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 [25(OH)D3] levels obtained prior to treatment initiation were categorized as deficient (≤ 20 ng/mL) or sufficient (> 20 ng/mL). The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier estimates, log-rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsThe median baseline vitamin D level was 19 ng/mL. The overall median PFS was 36 months. Patients with sufficient vitamin D had not reached median PFS, whereas those with deficiency had a median PFS of 14 months (95% confidence interval (CI), 12.6–15.3; p < 0.001). At 24 months, PFS was 100% in the sufficient group versus 23.1% in the deficient group. In multivariate Cox analysis, baseline vitamin D deficiency remained associated with inferior PFS (HR 32.33, 95% CI 6.90–151.5; p < 0.001), although the wide confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of progression events in the vitamin D–sufficient subgroup. Other clinicopathological variables did not show statistically significant associations with PFS.ConclusionBaseline vitamin D status was associated with progression-free survival outcomes in patients with HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer receiving ribociclib-based therapy. Patients with sufficient vitamin D levels demonstrated longer progression-free survival compared with those with vitamin D deficiency. However, given the retrospective design, limited sample size, residual confounding, and substantial censoring, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Larger prospective studies are required to validate these observations and to clarify the potential clinical relevance of baseline vitamin D status in patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors.Keywords: Breast neoplasms, Vitamin D, CDK4/6 inhibitors, Prognosis, Ribociclib

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 670,000 deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. Among molecular subtypes, hormone receptor (HR)-positive, HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−) breast cancer accounts for approximately 70% of cases and is primarily managed with endocrine therapy [2]. Nonetheless, many patients with advanced disease eventually develop resistance to endocrine therapy, underscoring the urgent need for more effective treatment options.

To address this clinical challenge, cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors have transformed the treatment landscape of HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer (MBC). Among these, ribociclib has shown consistent and robust improvements in overall survival (OS) across premenopausal and postmenopausal populations, as demonstrated in the MONALEESA-2, -3, and -7 trials [3–5]. Beyond their anti-proliferative activity, CDK4/6 inhibitors also exert immunomodulatory effects, including enhanced CD8 + T-cell infiltration and reduced regulatory T-cell function, thereby strengthening antitumor immunity [6].

Vitamin D, traditionally recognized for its role in calcium-phosphorus homeostasis, has emerged as a regulator of cancer biology through effects on cell proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis and immune modulation [7, 8]. Low serum vitamin D levels have been associated with aggressive tumor features, increased risk of metastasis, and poorer survival in breast cancer [9]. Moreover, preclinical studies suggest that vitamin D, through activation of the vitamin D receptor (VDR), shapes the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment, potentially modifying the immune effects induced by CDK4/6 inhibitors [10, 11]. Preclinical and translational data indicate that vitamin D–VDR signaling regulates cell-cycle progression through modulation of cyclin-dependent kinase pathways, particularly via downregulation of cyclin D1, suppression of tumor cell proliferation, and enhancement of antitumor immune responses [7, 10, 12]. These biological interactions suggest that vitamin D status may influence the tumor and immune context in patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors.

Although several clinical studies have evaluated the prognostic value of vitamin D in early-stage breast cancer, its relevance in the context of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy remains largely unexplored. We hypothesize that vitamin D deficiency may be associated with inferior outcomes in patients with HR+/HER2 − MBC receiving ribociclib. The primary objective of this study was to assess the association between baseline vitamin D levels and PFS, while the secondary objective was to determine whether vitamin D status could serve as an accessible biomarker to guide personalized treatment.

Method

This retrospective study was conducted at Ankara Etlik City Training and Research Hospital and included patients with HR+/HER2- MBC who received ribociclib plus endocrine therapy (ET) between January 2018 and December 2023. Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years old, had histologically confirmed HR+/HER2– disease, and available baseline serum vitamin D data. Patients with prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure in the adjuvant setting, incomplete records, or concomitant malignancies were excluded. A total of 158 patients were screened for eligibility, of whom 89 had available baseline vitamin D levels and adequate follow-up for analysis. The remaining 69 patients were excluded due to missing baseline vitamin D data or insufficient clinical documentation; therefore, the selection process is described narratively rather than by a flow diagram.

Clinical and pathological variables collected included age, menopausal status, ECOG performance status (PS), disease presentation (de novo vs. recurrent), metastatic sites, treatment line, concomitant ET, ER/PR/HER2 status, tumor grade and Ki-67 index. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 [25(OH)D3] levels obtained within four weeks prior to ribociclib initiation were recorded. Patients were classified as vitamin D deficient (≤ 20 ng/mL) or sufficient (> 20 ng/mL). This threshold was based on widely accepted clinical criteria and used in previous oncologic studies. This cut-off facilitates comparability with prior clinical and oncologic cohorts and reflects commonly used definitions of vitamin D deficiency in routine practice. The primary endpoint was PFS, defined as the time from initiation of ribociclib-based therapy to radiological disease progression or death from any cause. Patients without progression or death were censored at the date of last follow-up. Clinical data were retrieved from electronic hospital records and oncology follow-up files and were cross-checked independently by two investigators.

Group comparisons were performed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Survival was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Prognostic factors were explored using Cox proportional hazards regression, with variables showing p < 0.10 in univariate analysis entered into multivariate models. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number AEŞH-BADEK2-2025-225) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study included 89 patients with HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors. The cohort’s median age was 52 years (range, 25–81), and nearly all patients were female (98.9%). The majority of patients had a postmenopausal status at the initiation of therapy (62.9%). Approximately 70% of the patients presented with metastatic disease at diagnosis, and 71.9% received a CDK4/6 inhibitor as first-line treatment. Bone-only disease was observed in 65.2% of patients, whereas the remainder had visceral involvement. None of the patients presented with a visceral crisis at the initiation of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy, and all received ribociclib. Most of the patients received an aromatase inhibitor (AI) as concomitant ET (84.3%). The baseline clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1.

Survival analyses

The cohort had a median follow-up of 20 months (range, 2–86), with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 36 months. When stratified by vitamin D status, the median PFS was not reached in patients with serum 25(OH)D3 levels > 20 ng/mL, whereas it was 14 months (95% confidence interval (CI), 12.6–15.3) in those with levels ≤ 20 ng/mL (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). At landmark analyses, the estimated 12- and 24-month PFS rates were 100% (95% CI, not estimable) in the > 20 ng/mL group, compared with 75.9% (95% CI, 64–88) and 23.1% (95% CI, 9–37) in the ≤ 20 ng/mL group, respectively. These landmark estimates and Kaplan–Meier survival curves should be interpreted cautiously, as the vitamin D–sufficient subgroup had a limited number of progression events and substantial censoring due to ongoing progression-free follow-up. Importantly, censoring predominantly reflected ongoing progression-free follow-up at the time of data cutoff rather than undocumented loss to follow-up.

In univariate Cox regression analyses, poorer ECOG performance status was significantly associated with reduced PFS (HR 3.34, 95% CI 1.46–7.64; p = 0.004). Other clinical and pathological parameters—including age, menopausal status, disease presentation (de novo vs. recurrent metastatic), metastatic sites, treatment line, concomitant endocrine therapy type, ER/PR/HER2 status, and Ki-67 index—did not demonstrate a significant association with PFS. In the multivariate Cox regression model, ECOG performance status showed a borderline association with PFS (HR 2.24, 95% CI 0.90–5.56; p = 0.083). Serum vitamin D deficiency (≤ 20 ng/mL) remained independently associated with inferior PFS in both univariate (HR 30.94, 95% CI 7.10–134.4; p < 0.001) and multivariate analyses (HR 32.33, 95% CI 6.90–151.5; p < 0.001). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 89 patients with HR+/HER2 − MBC receiving ribociclib-based therapy, we demonstrated that lower baseline serum vitamin D levels were significantly associated with shorter PFS. Patients with serum levels ≤ 20 ng/mL experienced a median PFS of only 14 months, whereas the median PFS was not reached in those with higher vitamin D levels, indicating a notable difference in PFS. Early separation of Kaplan–Meier curves was evident, with 12-month PFS rates of 100% versus 75.9%, and 24-month rates of 100% versus 23.1% in the sufficient and deficient groups, respectively.

Several prior observational studies and meta-analyses have reported that vitamin D deficiency correlates with increased breast cancer incidence, more advanced stage at diagnosis, higher risk of metastasis, and non-responsiveness or progression following neoadjuvant therapy [9, 13]. However, these data largely derive from early-stage disease and are seldom stratified by molecular subtype. To our knowledge, this appears to be among the first studies to evaluate baseline vitamin D in the setting of CDK4/6 inhibition for metastatic luminal-type breast cancer. Given the broad clinical use of CDK4/6 inhibitors in both endocrine-sensitive and resistant disease, the identification of accessible biomarkers remains an unmet need—suggesting that baseline vitamin D status may represent a clinically relevant candidate. However, the available clinical evidence remains heterogeneous, and not all studies have demonstrated a consistent association between serum vitamin D levels and breast cancer prognosis. For example, Hatse et al. reported variable prognostic associations according to menopausal status, highlighting the complexity of the relationship between vitamin D biology and breast cancer outcomes [14].

Vitamin D has been shown to exert antitumor effects through diverse mechanisms, including inhibition of proliferation, induction of apoptosis, suppression of angiogenesis, and immune modulation [6, 15, 16]. These effects are mediated via the nuclear vitamin D receptor (VDR), expressed in malignant breast epithelial cells as well as immune populations within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Experimental evidence suggests that vitamin D shapes a more tolerogenic TME by modulating T-cell responses and impairing the function of key suppressor subsets such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [10, 17]. These immunologic effects provide a mechanistic rationale for exploring vitamin D as a biologically relevant clinical factor.

Notably, CDK4/6 inhibitors exert immunomodulatory effects beyond cell-cycle arrest, including enhancement of antigen presentation, increased CD8 + T-cell infiltration, and reduction of Treg-mediated suppression [18, 19]. The overlap between these pathways provides a biologically plausible framework in which sufficient vitamin D status could potentiate antitumor immunity and be associated with treatment outcomes in patients receiving ribociclib. Adequate vitamin D enhances cytotoxic T-cell activity while limiting suppressor cell function, complementing the immune-activating profile of CDK4/6 blockade. In this context, vitamin D status may reflect the host and tumor immune context. Furthermore, polymorphisms in genes regulating vitamin D metabolism and signaling (VDR, CYP27B1, CYP24A1) may influence treatment sensitivity and represent a relevant target for future translational research.

Interestingly, classical prognostic factors—including metastatic distribution, menopausal status, disease presentation, and treatment line—did not demonstrate statistical significance, likely reflecting the relatively homogeneous clinical characteristics of the cohort and the low number of progression events in vitamin D–sufficient patients, which may have limited the power to detect more subtle prognostic effects.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design may have introduced selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Serum vitamin D levels were assessed only at baseline, without longitudinal monitoring during treatment. Serial measurements during follow-up or at the time of disease progression were not consistently available in the retrospective records. Therefore, it remains unclear whether patients maintained persistent vitamin D deficiency throughout treatment, and temporal changes in vitamin D status may have influenced survival outcomes. In addition, many patients with bone metastases may have received bone-modifying agents and supportive vitamin D supplementation during follow-up according to routine clinical practice. However, detailed longitudinal data regarding vitamin D supplementation, treatment adherence, dose adjustments, and serial vitamin D measurements were not consistently available in the retrospective records. Therefore, baseline serum vitamin D levels should be interpreted primarily as a pretreatment biomarker rather than a stable longitudinal exposure variable. Consequently, the association between baseline vitamin D levels and long-term progression-free survival should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of a direct causal relationship.

Potential confounding factors—including body mass index, dietary habits, seasonal timing of blood sampling, seasonal variation in sunlight exposure, and sun exposure—were not systematically evaluated. In addition, patients with sufficient vitamin D levels had a more favorable ECOG performance status distribution at baseline. Although ECOG PS was included in multivariate analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded given the relatively small sample size and limited number of progression events. Therefore, vitamin D deficiency may partly reflect poorer overall health status or reduced functional reserve rather than representing a purely independent biological prognostic factor.

Although the sample size was sufficient for an exploratory analysis, it remained relatively modest, and the predefined cutoff value of 20 ng/mL for vitamin D deficiency requires validation in larger and more heterogeneous populations. Furthermore, the markedly elevated hazard ratio and the wide confidence intervals observed in multivariate analyses likely reflect substantial model instability related to the limited number of progression events in the vitamin D–sufficient subgroup, which may have amplified and inflated the effect estimate. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously until externally validated. In addition, the relatively short median follow-up duration compared with the estimated median progression-free survival, together with the low number of progression events in the vitamin D–sufficient group, may have contributed to substantial censoring and reduced the robustness of the Kaplan–Meier survival estimates. Importantly, censoring predominantly reflected ongoing progression-free follow-up at the time of data cutoff rather than undocumented loss to follow-up.

In summary, to our knowledge, this study is among the first to evaluate the association between baseline serum vitamin D status and progression-free survival in patients with HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer receiving ribociclib-based therapy. The observed association between sufficient vitamin D levels and longer PFS, together with biologically plausible mechanisms, supports further investigation of baseline vitamin D status as a potential biomarker in prospective studies. Larger, adequately powered prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the potential clinical relevance of baseline vitamin D status in patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors.

Conclusion

This retrospective study suggests that baseline serum vitamin D status may be associated with progression-free survival outcomes in patients with HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer receiving ribociclib-based therapy. Patients with sufficient vitamin D levels demonstrated longer progression-free survival compared with those with vitamin D deficiency. The biological interplay between vitamin D signaling and the immunomodulatory and cell-cycle regulatory effects of CDK4/6 inhibition further supports the plausibility of this association.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The retrospective single-center design, relatively limited sample size, substantial censoring, residual confounding, and wide confidence intervals—largely related to the low number of progression events in the vitamin D–sufficient subgroup—limit the robustness of the survival estimates and preclude causal inference. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Larger multicenter prospective studies with longer follow-up are required to validate these findings and to determine whether baseline vitamin D status may have potential clinical relevance in patients with HR+/HER2 − metastatic breast cancer treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors.
