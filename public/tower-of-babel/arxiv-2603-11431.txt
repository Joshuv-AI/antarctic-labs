A Generalized Theory of Load Distribution in Redundantly-actuated Robotic Systems
Joshua Flight, Clément Gosselin

Abstract
This paper presents a generalized theory which describes how applied loads are distributed within rigid bodies handled by redundantly-actuated robotic systems composed of multiple independent closed-loop kinematic chains. The theory fully characterizes the feasible set of manipulating wrench distributions for a given resultant wrench applied to the rigid body and has important implications for the force-control of multifingered grippers, legged robots, cooperating robots, and other overconstrained mechanisms. We also derive explicit solutions to the wrench synthesis and wrench analysis problems. These solutions are computationally efficient and scale linearly with the number of applied wrenches, requiring neither numerical methods nor the inversion of large matrices. Finally, we identify significant shortcomings in current state-of-the-art approaches and propose corrections. These are supported by illustrative examples that demonstrate the advantages of the improved methods.

In these systems, the wrenches applied to the object are typically redundant and thus infinitely many applied wrench combinations can produce the same net wrench on the object. On the one hand, this redundancy may be leveraged to improve grip tightness, distribute applied loads more evenly among the actuators, or to allow singular configurations to be avoided. On the other hand, the applied wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{i}
may have antagonistic components which produce internal loads (i.e., combinations of applied wrenches that cancel and thus are invisible to the net wrench but produce nonzero stresses within the object). Internal loads do not affect the acceleration of the object, yet they have important consequences for the performance of the system. They can:

•

induce undesired contact pressures that can damage a grasped object;

•

create friction and wear that reduce the efficiency and lifespan of equipment;

•

bias force/torque sensors leading to issues in compensation and control;

•

hinder feasible force/torque capabilities due to actuator limits.

Because internal loads are intrinsic to these hyperstatic structures, and given their near-ubiquity in robotic applications, it is of great importance to be able to characterize them and, depending on the application, limit or exploit them.

II
Related Work

II-A

Wrench Synthesis

The principles of rigid body dynamics have been understood for centuries, and they are a fundamental tool for the analysis of robotic systems. They are of particular importance for the study of load distribution in objects handled by redundantly-actuated robotic systems. Despite having been studied for decades, no consensus has emerged from the robotics literature concerning the characterization and determination of internal loads generated within the object by the wrenches applied by the kinematic chains.

Kumar and Waldron’s study of pure forces applied to rigid body objects in
[
14

\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{G}^{T}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{T})^{-1}

II-B

Wrench Analysis

Wrench analysis is a less developed area of the literature. Although some of the wrench synthesis techniques described above have been adapted for use in the decomposition of applied wrenches into sets of manipulating wrenches and constraint wrenches
[
1
,
9
]
, the latest contributions to this line of research have focused on establishing physical plausibility criteria for the decomposition
[
16
,
17
]
.

Schmidts et al. were the first to propose these criteria in
[
16
]
. They arrived at this conclusion by first stating (without much justification) that each applied force can be written as

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{m,i}+\mathbf{f}_{c,i}

(8)

where
\mathbf{f}_{i}
is the
i
-th force applied to the object by a robotic manipulator, and
\mathbf{f}_{m,i}
and
\mathbf{f}_{c,i}
are the manipulating and constraint force components of
\mathbf{f}_{i}
. They then state that any component of an applied force cannot be greater than the applied force itself and derive physical plausibility inequalities based on this assumption. They incorporate these criteria into a constrained optimization problem which they use to perform the decomposition.

Later, Donner et al.
[
17
]
expanded the work done in
[
16
]
to include torques and used the same physical plausibility criteria, but showed graphically that the results returned by the method proposed in
[
16
]
were erroneous. They therefore proposed a corrected optimization problem that returns results that are consistent with their assumptions about manipulating and constraint wrenches.

We have found that the decomposition (
8
) used in
[
16
,
17
]
inaccurately describes the nature of load distribution in objects handled by redundant robotic systems, as it violates some of the fundamental principles of rigid body motion. In this paper, we propose a corrected decomposition algorithm which corrects these oversights, thus making it the first explicit wrench decomposition algorithm that guarantees both physically plausibility and consistency with rigid body motion.

II-C

Contributions

While the work of Erhart and Hirche
[
11
]
and Donner et al.
[
17
]
represents the current state of the art in wrench synthesis and decomposition, this paper makes a significant advancement by establishing a unified framework specifically adapted to the real-time force-control of redundantly-actuated robotic systems. The specific contributions of this work are the following:

1.

A Unifying Theory of Load Distribution:
We identify and correct critical inconsistencies in the assumptions and theorems that have guided wrench synthesis and analysis research; some of which have stood uncontested for decades. We propose a rigorous theoretical framework that unifies these two fundamental problems in robot force-control as a final solution to this long-standing problem.

2.

General, Closed-Form Solutions:
The parametrized Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse
\mathbf{G}^{+}_{M}
is obtained directly in closed-form, eliminating the need to invert large matrices. This is a significant advancement over modern wrench decomposition algorithms that rely on iterative optimization routines and allows for the precise and stable determination of manipulating and constraint wrench components without the computational overhead of optimization.

3.

A Complete Mathematical Characterization of the Solution:
We propose the first complete characterization of the uniqueness of the manipulating wrench distribution and describe the physical significance of wrench components in the null-space of the grasp matrix by dividing it into two novel sub-spaces.

III
Problem Definition

III-A

Problem Statement

The generalized theory presented in this work aims to solve two interconnected problems that are fundamental to the force control of redundantly-actuated robotic systems:

1.

Wrench synthesis
: Given a desired resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
to be applied to the object by the kinematic chains, compute a distribution of wrenches to generate it while eliminating or prescribing internal loads.

2.

Wrench analysis (decomposition)
: Given an arbitrary set of
n
wrenches applied to the object, decompose this set into a set of manipulating wrenches and a set of constraint wrenches

III-B

Assumptions

In this work, we assume:

1.

that the object handled by the kinematic chains is rigid, and;

2.

that the vectors
\mathbf{r}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}&y_{i}&z_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
that define the application points of the wrenches applied to the object with respect to its center of mass are known.

III-C

Applicability

The resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
to be applied to the object by the kinematic chains to complete a given dynamic trajectory is given by

\mathbf{h}_{o}=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}+\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{o})\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{h}_{ext}

(9)

where
\mathbf{h}_{o}
is the resultant wrench applied to the object,
\mathbf{x}_{o}
,
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}
, and
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}
are the position, velocity, and acceleration of the rigid body at the instant, respectively,
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}_{o},\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{o})
are the inertia and centripetal matrices,
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
is the gravitational force, and
\mathbf{h}_{ext}
is the wrench applied to the rigid body by its environment.

The method for wrench synthesis that we propose is an
instantaneous wrench distribution solver
. It is independent from the individual components that make up
\mathbf{h}_{o}
and simply serves to calculate the required set of wrenches to be applied by the kinematic chains to generate
\mathbf{h}_{o}
while either eliminating or prescribing internal loads at a given instant. It can be used as a solver in broader control algorithms used in modern robotics applications that involve dynamic trajectories and changing contact topology, since even in these applications it is assumed that the wrench application point vectors
\mathbf{r}_{i}
are known at each time step.

In most modern robotic systems, contact wrenches are often solved for implicitly using whole-body or model-predictive controllers that rely on iterative optimization routines. We do not intend for our proposed results to replace them, but rather to complement them by offering the following advantages:

1.

Computational efficiency:
Modern controllers rely on iterative optimization routines which can be computationally expensive and suffer from convergence issues, especially in high-frequency control loops. Our proposed methods are explicit, closed-form solutions with predictable computational times. This could prove essential for synthesizing wrench distributions in high-frequency control loops, or, if optimization-based solvers are necessary, providing the solver with a physically informed initial guess (“warm start”) to accelerate convergence.

2.

Analytical decomposition and metrics:
Modern controllers return a wrench vector that satisfies the constraints (friction cones, torque limits, etc.), but rarely distinguish between the wrench components that cause motion (the manipulating wrenches) and those that squeeze the object (the constraint wrenches). The method that we propose for wrench decomposition is mathematically rigorous and also explicit. This is useful for quantifying the squeezing on delicate objects or analyzing solutions returned by black-box controllers by calculating performance metrics with our physically informed analysis methods.

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x},t)=\text{diag}(m_{1}\mathbf{I}_{3},\mathbf{J}_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\mathbf{I}_{3},\mathbf{J}_{n})
).
\hat{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x},\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{d},t)
is the known stacked vector of wrenches acting on the LMIEs that includes the applied wrenches
\mathbf{h}
and any other centripetal forces that may be present.
\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{d},\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
are the stacked vectors of unconstrained velocities and accelerations of the LMIEs, respectively where superscript
d
stands for
desired
. Because the LMIEs are considered unconstrained,
\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{d}
can take any arbitrary value, and
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{d}
is the acceleration the LMIEs
would have had
, had they not been fixed to the rigid body in motion
[
20
]
.

In the presence of kinematic constraints (
18
), the actual
constrained
accelerations of the LMIEs will differ from the free unconstrained accelerations they would have had. This incompatibility between the desired motion and constrained motion causes additional forces of constraint to be applied to the LMIEs

19

. The dynamic equation that describes the constrained motion of the LMIEs now takes the form

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x},t)\ddot{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x},\dot{\mathbf{x}},t)+\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\dot{\mathbf{x}},t)

(25)

where
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\dot{\mathbf{x}},t)=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c,1}^{T}&\ldots&\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c,n}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
is the generalized stacked vector of constraint wrenches acting on the LMIEs. It is these constraint wrenches which attempt to deform the rigid body and cause the generation of internal loads. They are added to the applied wrenches such that the resulting motion of each LMIE is consistent with the motion of the rigid body induced by the resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
. This is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

(a)

(b)

The term on the left-hand side of (

\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{m,1}^{T}&\ldots&\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{m,n}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. It is the set of wrenches required to impart the proper constrained accelerations on the LMIEs. We may therefore rewrite (

\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{m}=\hat{\mathbf{h}}+\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c}

The generalized constraint wrenches can be obtained explicitly with

\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c}=\mathbf{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{B}^{+}(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{d})

and
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{d}
is the kinematic error (i.e., the extent to which the unconstrained accelerations
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{d}_{i}

V
Implications of the Udwadia-Kalaba Equation

V-A

Internal Loads Generated by Centripetal Forces

Some researchers have made the assumption that constraint wrenches are components of the applied wrenches
[
16
,
17
]
(see Eq. (
8
)). However, the Udwadia-Kalaba equation states that constraint wrenches are applied to an LMIE by the rigid body so as to make its constrained motion consistent with the kinematic constraints. In fact, constraint wrenches can be present when no wrenches are applied at all.

Imagine a planar, triangular rigid body spinning with a constant angular velocity
\omega_{o}
about an axis that passes through its CoM and is normal to the plane. Each vertex of the triangle is at a distance
r
from the CoM. A dynamically equivalent system of this rigid body can be constructed with an LMIE of mass
m_{i}
located at each vertex.

The velocity vectors
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
of the vertices are perpendicular to the axis of rotation. Were they not rigidly attached to the rigid body (i.e., unconstrained), they would travel in a straight line in that direction. However, the normal acceleration
a_{n}=r\omega_{o}^{2}
keep them spinning about the axis with the same angular velocity as every other point on the planar body such that the constraints imposed by rigid body motion are satisfied. This normal acceleration
a_{n,i}
is therefore the required constrained acceleration of the
i
-th vertex, and if no external forces are applied, a constraint force is necessary to induce this constrained acceleration on the LMIE. This is illustrated in Fig.
5
.

Fig. 5
:
When a body is in motion, constraint wrenches are present even in the absence of applied wrenches.

In this work, we are primarily concerned with the internal loads generated by the interplay between the applied wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{i}
, not those due to the centripetal forces which can be present even when no external wrenches are applied. For this reason, we adopt Erhart and Hirche’s virtual rigid body model where they substitute the actual rigid body of mass
m_{o}
, inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}_{o}
and instantaneous velocity
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}
with a virtual rigid body of mass
m^{*}_{o}
and inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}
which we assume to be
at rest
. Note that the inertial parameters
(m^{*}_{o},\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})
of the virtual rigid body cannot be chosen arbitrarily, as we will show in the next sub-sections. In what follows, any variable identified with an asterisk
(^{*})
corresponds to a dynamic quantity of the virtual rigid body or of the LMIEs in its dynamically equivalent system.

The Udwadia-Kalaba equation in its general form discussed in Section
IV-E
describes the constrained motion of the LMIEs using generalized wrench vectors
\hat{\mathbf{h}}
,
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{c}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{m}
which also include centripetal forces. Since these are now zero in the rigid body at rest, we rewrite this equation as

\mathbf{h}_{m}=\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{h}_{c}

(28)

where
\mathbf{h}
contains the wrenches applied to the LMIEs by the kinematic chains,
\mathbf{h}_{c}
contains the constraint wrenches applied to the LMIEs by the rigid body to ensure the compatibility of their motion with the kinematic constraints, and
\mathbf{h}_{m}
contains the manipulating wrenches required to induce the proper constrained accelerations on the LMIEs. None of them include additional contributions from inertial forces such as the
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{o}\times(\mathbf{J}_{o}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{o})
term in (
11
).

An interesting consequence of considering the virtual rigid body to be at rest is that the constrained accelerations of the LMIEs in its dynamically equivalent system are now given by

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{i}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{o}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}\times\mathbf{r}_{i}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}\end{bmatrix},

(29)

since
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}_{o}=\mathbf{0}
. This is analogous to the twist vector of a rigid body in motion which defines the helicoidal field of velocities
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
of the points on the rigid body. As a result, the vector field of constrained accelerations
\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
of the LMIEs on the virtual rigid body also forms a helicoidal vector field.

V-B

Scale Invariance of the Dynamically Equivalent System

The virtual rigid body and its dynamically equivalent system are simply convenient mathematical tools used to distribute the resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
among the applied wrenches
\mathbf{h}
while satisfying certain constraints to obtain the manipulating wrench distribution. They serve to discretize the handled object with a continuous mass distribution into a set of discrete elements (i.e., the LMIEs) and to ensure that we are only considering the internal loads generated by the applied wrenches. The instantaneous acceleration of the virtual rigid body
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}
induced by
\mathbf{h}_{o}
may differ from the instantaneous acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}
of the actual object if
\mathbf{h}_{o}
were applied to it. This does not negate the validity of our proposed framework, since calculating the virtual instantaneous accelerations induced by the wrenches is only an intermediary step which serves to translate the resultant wrench to the wrenches required to generate it at the wrench application points. In fact, the inertial properties of the virtual rigid body
(m^{*}_{o},\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})
need not match those of the handled object
(m_{o},\mathbf{J}_{o})
exactly, they need only be
proportional
.

Suppose a wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
is applied to the CoM a rigid body of mass
m_{o}
and inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}_{o}
. The resulting acceleration is given by

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}=\mathbf{M}_{o}^{-1}\mathbf{h}_{o}

(30)

where the centripetal terms have been omitted so as not to consider the internal loads generated by centripetal forces as described in the previous sub-section.

If we construct a dynamically equivalent system of
n
LMIEs for this rigid body, the constrained acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
of each LMIE can be calculated with the kinematic constraint equations (
16
) and (
17
). The manipulating wrenches needed to induce these constrained accelerations can in turn be found with

\mathbf{h}_{m,i}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}\mathbf{I}_{3}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{J}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

Now, let us do the same using a dynamically equivalent system of the virtual rigid body with mass
m^{*}_{o}=km_{o}
and inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}=k\mathbf{J}_{o}
where
k
is a proportionality constant. We can therefore write

\mathbf{M}^{*}_{o}=k\mathbf{M}_{o}.

(32)

In order for constraints (
21
)-(
23
) to still be satisfied, we must also scale the inertial properties of the LMIEs by the same amount:

\mathbf{M}^{*}_{i}=k\mathbf{M}_{i}\quad\forall i.

(33)

The acceleration induced on the dynamically equivalent system is then

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}=(\mathbf{M}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\mathbf{h}_{o}=\frac{1}{k}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}

(34)

and, consequently, the constrained acceleration of every LMIE in this case is

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}=\frac{1}{k}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

(35)

Finally, if we denote by
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{m,i}
the manipulating wrench needed to induce the scaled constrained acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}
on the
i
-th LMIE of mass
m^{*}_{i}
and inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}
, we can obtain it with

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{m,i}=\mathbf{M}^{*}_{i}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}.

(36)

Substituting (
33
) and (
35
) into (
36
), we see that

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{m,i}=k\mathbf{M}_{i}\left(\frac{1}{k}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right)=\mathbf{h}_{m,i},

(37)

which is the same solution.

In other words, if the virtual rigid body has, for example, double the inertia of the physical object, its instantaneous acceleration will be half that of the physical object and so will the instantaneous accelerations of the LMIEs. However, the inertia of the LMIEs will be double those of the physical object, and the wrenches required to induce half the acceleration on a rigid body with double the inertia will be the same since the effects of the scaling cancel.

V-C

The Need for an Additional Constraint

In their original theorem (Theorem
1
) Erhart and Hirche use the same constraints as those required for constructing a dynamically equivalent system (see Eqs. (
21
)-(
23
)) to limit the feasible solutions for the virtual inertia parameters
(m^{*}_{i},\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})
. However, we have found that these are not sufficient for calculating valid manipulating wrench distributions and that an additional constraint is required.

The additional constraint pertains to the alignment of the manipulating torques. The resultant torque
\mathbf{t}_{o}
is obtained with

\mathbf{t}_{o}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{t}_{m,i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{r}_{i}\times\mathbf{f}_{m,i}),

(38)

where the second term on the right-hand side is the manipulating force-induced torque
\mathbf{t}_{fm}
. The manipulating torques
\mathbf{t}_{m,i}
and the manipulating force-induced torque
\mathbf{t}_{fm}
of an admissible manipulating wrench distribution will sum to
\mathbf{t}_{o}
yet not have any antagonistic components that, when summed, cancel and have no effect on the net wrench applied to the object. This is illustrated in Fig.
6
where a pure torque
\mathbf{t}
and a force-induced torque
\mathbf{t}_{f}
sum to a resultant torque
\mathbf{t}_{o}
. In Fig.
6a
,
\mathbf{t}_{f}
has a component that is orthogonal to
\mathbf{t}_{o}
. This
x
-component must be compensated by
\mathbf{t}
in order for their resultant to equal
\mathbf{t}_{o}
. This compensation induces constraint wrenches. Conversely, in Fig.
6b
, both
\mathbf{t}_{f}
and
\mathbf{t}
are parallel and sum to
\mathbf{t}_{o}
without need for compensation and therefore do not generate constraint wrenches.

(a)

\mathbf{t}_{f}
and
\mathbf{t}
have components perpendicular to
\mathbf{t}_{o}
that cancel and cause internal loads.

(b)

\mathbf{t}_{f}
and
\mathbf{t}
have no antagonistic components and sum to
\mathbf{t}_{o}
without generating internal loads.

Fig. 6
:
Example of orthogonal torque component cancellation in non-parallel torques causing internal loads.

With this realization, we may rewrite (
38
) as

||\mathbf{t}_{o}||\mathbf{u}_{o}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}||\mathbf{t}_{m,i}||\mathbf{u}_{o}+||\mathbf{t}_{fm}||\mathbf{u}_{o}

(39)

where
\mathbf{u}_{o}
is a unit vector in the direction of
\mathbf{t}_{o}
.

The angular acceleration of the virtual rigid body is found with

\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}=(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\mathbf{t}_{o}.

(40)

To satisfy constraint (
17
), we may consider a set of
n+1
sub-elements (
n
sub-elements with inertia
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}
and one sub-element with inertia
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}
). The angular acceleration of the
n
elements with moment of inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}
is given by

\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{i}=(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{t}_{m,i},

(41)

and the angular acceleration of the system of
n
point masses is given by

\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{f}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{t}_{fm}.

(42)

Substituting (
40
) and (
41
) into (
17
) yields

\displaystyle(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\mathbf{t}_{o}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{t}_{m,i}

(43)

\displaystyle||\mathbf{t}_{o}||(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{o}

\displaystyle=||\mathbf{t}_{m,i}||(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{o}\quad\forall i,

since every
\mathbf{t}_{m,i}
must be parallel to
\mathbf{t}_{o}
. For (
43
) to hold for all vectors
\mathbf{u}_{o}
, we must have

||\mathbf{t}_{o}||(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})^{-1}=||\mathbf{t}_{m,i}||(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})^{-1}.

(44)

Solving for
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}
, we obtain

\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}=\frac{||\mathbf{t}_{m,i}||}{||\mathbf{t}_{o}||}\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o},

(45)

where we see that
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}
and
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}
must be proportional.

Repeating this process for the manipulating force-induced torque
\mathbf{t}_{fm}
yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}=\frac{||\mathbf{t}_{fm}||}{||\mathbf{t}_{o}||}\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}.

(46)

We therefore state that the inertia parameters
(m^{*}_{i},\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})
chosen for the LMIEs must also satisfy

\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}\ \propto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}\propto\ \mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}\ \forall i

(47)

in order for
\mathbf{G}^{+}_{M}
to return valid manipulating wrench distributions. Note that the symbol
\propto
means “proportional to”.

The reason for this oversight is likely that Erhart and Hirche only provide simplified examples in one and two dimensions. In these cases, there are either none or only one axis of rotation (no rotations are permitted in 1 dimension and only rotation about an axis normal to the plane is permitted in 2 dimensions). Hence, the proportionality of the rotational inertia was guaranteed because it was defined by a scalar value. In 3 dimensions, rotational inertia is defined by a
3\times 3
inertia tensor, and proportionality is an important consideration that cannot be omitted when choosing the parameters
(m^{*}_{i},\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i})
as we have shown here.

VI
Generalized Theory of Load Distribution in Robotic Systems

VI-A

Overview

Our theorem describes the conditions under which no internal loads are generated in a rigid body subjected to multiple wrenches applied by independent kinematic chains. Given the resultant wrench and wrench application points, it fully characterizes the feasible set of
n
manipulating wrenches. It does so by applying the Udwadia-Kalaba equation to a dynamically equivalent system of the virtual rigid body consisting of
n
LMIEs located at the wrench application points.

The theorem works by considering the instantaneous acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}
that would be induced on the virtual rigid body at rest by the resultant wrench obtained from (
9
) at a given time step in a dynamic trajectory. This instantaneous acceleration is distinct from the actual instantaneous acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{o}
of the physical object. For example, in a static application where
\mathbf{h}_{o}=\mathbf{h}_{ext}
, the physical object is held still by wrenches applied to it by its environment. Nonetheless, we calculate the instantaneous acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}
that would be induced on the virtual rigid body by
\mathbf{h}_{o}
were it not held still, and use this to calculate the wrenches required to generate
\mathbf{h}_{o}
while not generating internal loads.

The procedure can be explained in three steps. First, the instantaneous acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}
of the virtual rigid body induced by the resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
is calculated. Second, the resulting constrained acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}
of each LMIE is determined using the kinematic constraints. Third, the manipulating wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{m,i}
required to induce these constrained accelerations are obtained from the dynamics equations of the LMIEs.

VI-B

Fundamental Theorem of Load Distribution in Robotic Systems

We now present our main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let a virtual rigid body of mass
m^{*}_{o}
and inertia tensor
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}
at rest be subjected to a resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
with respect to its CoM obtained from (
9
). Denote by

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{o}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}\end{bmatrix}=(\mathbf{M}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\mathbf{h}_{o}

(48)

the instantaneous acceleration of the virtual rigid body induced by
\mathbf{h}_{o}
. Let the instantaneous acceleration at a location
\mathbf{r}_{i}
with respect to the CoM of the virtual rigid body be obtained with the kinematic constraints such that

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{i}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{o}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}\times\mathbf{r}_{i}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}\end{bmatrix}.

(49)

For each stacked wrench vector
\mathbf{h}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{1}^{T}&\ldots&\mathbf{h}_{n}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
that solves (

(50)

onto the LMIE at
\mathbf{r}_{i}

with

\displaystyle m^{*}_{o}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{*}_{i},

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{J}_{i}^{*}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T},

(52)

VI-C

The Helicoidal Virtual Acceleration Field as the Sum of Two Fields

In our characterization of manipulating wrenches, we are primarily concerned with the instantaneous accelerations
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i}
of the LMIEs that form the dynamically equivalent system of the virtual rigid body. Additionally, a manipulating force
\mathbf{f}_{m,i}
can induce linear and angular acceleration in the rigid body, since it also applies a torque
\mathbf{r}_{i}\times\mathbf{f}_{m,i}
, whereas a manipulating torque
\mathbf{t}_{m,i}
can only induce angular acceleration. Therefore, we deduce that all of the linear acceleration of the rigid body must be produced by the manipulating forces, while the angular acceleration can be produced by a combination of the manipulating forces and the manipulating torques.

The portion of the angular acceleration generated by either the manipulating forces or a given manipulating torque depends on its respective contribution to the overall inertia of the virtual rigid body. If all the angular acceleration is to be generated by the manipulating forces, then the inertia contribution of the masses of the LMIEs
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}
must be equal to the total inertia we assign to the virtual rigid body
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}
and we set
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}=\mathbf{0},\ \forall i
. Conversely, if we wish for the manipulating torques to produce some of the angular acceleration, we must increase their contributions to the overall inertia of the system. For example, if the manipulating torques are to generate half of the angular acceleration while the manipulating forces generate the other half, we need to design our dynamically equivalent system such that the system of LMIE masses and the sum of the LMIE inertia tensors contribute equally to the overall inertia of the system. In this case, we set
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}
and
\sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}
.

We can therefore write the instantaneous acceleration of the object as

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}=\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{f}+\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{t}

(56)

where
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{f}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*T}_{o}&\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*T}_{f}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}&\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*T}_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
are the components of acceleration of the virtual rigid body generated by the manipulating forces and manipulating torques, respectively. Note that vectors
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{f}
and
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{t}
are different from vectors
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{f}
and
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{i}
discussed in section
V-C
. On the one hand,
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{f}
and
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}
denote the angular acceleration of sub-elements in the equimomental system of the virtual rigid body.
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{f}
is the angular acceleration of the sub-system of point masses
m^{*}_{i}
with inertia
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})m^{*}_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{i})^{T}
and
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{i}
is the angular acceleration of the
i
-th LMIE with moment of inertia
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}
. On the other hand,
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{f}
and
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{t}
are the individual contributions of the individual wrench components on the virtual rigid body’s acceleration considering its
total
inertia. Therefore, we have
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{i}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{f}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}
and
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{*}_{o}=\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{f}+\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{t}
.

\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{f}
and
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{t}
both define helicoidal vector fields and have parallel directions
(\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{f}\parallel\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}^{*}_{t})
. They are obtained with

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{f}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{m^{*}_{o}}\mathbf{I}_{3}&\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}&(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{o}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}_{i}\times\mathbf{f}_{m,i}\end{bmatrix},

(57)

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}&\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}&(\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{t}_{m,i}\end{bmatrix}.

(58)

VI-D

Uniqueness of the Manipulating Wrench Distribution

The uniqueness of the solution is discussed briefly in
[
11
]
. Here, we aim to correct and complete their description.

The uniqueness of the manipulating wrench distribution is governed by the number of solutions for the LMIE inertia parameters

VI-E

Unique Representation of the Internal Loading State of the Rigid Body

The fact that an infinite number of manipulating wrench distributions can exist may seem counterintuitive, especially in the context of wrench analysis. If we have a given arbitrary applied wrench distribution
\mathbf{h}
and wish to find all feasible decompositions into the manipulating wrench components
\mathbf{h}_{m}
, which induce motion, and the constraint wrench components
\mathbf{h}_{c}
, which contribute to the “squeezing” of the object, the choice of solution for
\mathbf{h}_{m}
will affect the corresponding constraint wrench vector
\mathbf{h}_{c}
(see Eq. (
28
)). However, for a given wrench distribution applied to the real object, we do not expect the squeezing on the object to depend on our choice of
\mathbf{h}_{m}
. There should therefore exist a unique way of describing this internal loading state, despite there being infinitely many solutions for
\mathbf{h}_{m}
.

Assume there are an infinite number of possible manipulating wrench distributions for a given resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{c}
be the infinite sets of feasible solutions for the manipulating and constraint wrench distributions, respectively. Every vector in set
\mathcal{H}_{m}
can be obtained by applying Theorem
2
. The mapping between
\mathcal{H}_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{c}
is one-to-one, and
\mathcal{H}_{c}
can be described as

\mathcal{H}_{c}=\{\mathbf{h}_{cj}\in\mathbb{R}^{6n}\mid\mathbf{h}_{cj}=\mathbf{h}_{mj}-\mathbf{h},\ \mathbf{h}_{mj}\in\mathcal{H}_{m}\}

(67)

where
\mathbf{h}_{mj}
and
\mathbf{h}_{cj}
are the
j
-th elements of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{c}
, respectively, and
\mathbf{h}
is the unique vector containing the applied wrenches.
\mathcal{H}_{c}
is therefore simply a translation of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.

In
[
14
]
, interaction forces are defined as the components of the applied forces that lie in the null space of the grasp matrix,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
. However, we have found that this reasoning cannot be applied to the manipulating wrench distribution, since it may have a null-space component yet still not generate internal loads.

Let
\mathbf{h}_{m1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{m2}
be two manipulating wrench distributions in the feasible set
\mathcal{H}_{m}
for a resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathbf{h}_{m1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}_{o},

(68)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathbf{h}_{m2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}_{o}.

(69)

If we subtract these two equations, we obtain

\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{h}_{m2}-\mathbf{h}_{m1})=\mathbf{0}_{6\times 1}.

(70)

Hence, the vector
\Delta\mathbf{h}_{m}=\mathbf{h}_{m2}-\mathbf{h}_{m1}
also lies in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
, yet adding
\Delta\mathbf{h}_{m}
to a manipulating wrench distribution does not generate internal loads since the resulting vector is also a manipulating wrench distribution.

Note that constraint wrenches do still lie in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
since they must satisfy the condition

\mathbf{G}\mathbf{h}_{c}=\mathbf{0}_{6\times 1},

(71)

i.e., they are invisible to the net wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
.

With this observation, we are able to find a unique representation of the set of constraint wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{c}
in a feasible set
\mathcal{H}_{c}
by defining two sub-spaces within
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
.

We assume that the grasp matrix
\mathbf{G}
is full rank. The null space of
\mathbf{G}
is therefore
(6n-6)
-dimensional. Let
\mathcal{N}_{m}
be the
d
-dimensional subspace within
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
that corresponds to the vectors
\Delta\mathbf{h}_{m}
which may be added to a particular solution for the manipulating wrench distribution, denoted
\mathbf{h}_{m,p}
, to span the entire set of feasible solutions for
\mathbf{h}_{m}
. Any vector in the feasible set
\mathcal{H}_{m}
can then be written as

\mathbf{h}_{m}=\mathbf{h}_{m,p}+\Delta\mathbf{h}_{m},\quad\Delta\mathbf{h}_{m}\in\mathcal{N}_{m}.

(72)

Alternatively, this can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{m}=\mathbf{h}_{m,p}+\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}

(73)

where
\mathbf{K}
is a
(6n\times d)
matrix whose columns are a set of basis vectors that span
\mathcal{N}_{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}
is a
d
-dimensional vector which uniquely determines the location of a given vector
\Delta\mathbf{h}_{m}
within this space.

The choice of
\mathbf{h}_{m,p}
will affect
\mathcal{N}_{m}
. For consistency, we propose the convention of using the unique forces-only solution
(\mathbf{t}_{m,i}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1},\ \forall i)
for
\mathbf{h}_{m}
as
\mathbf{h}_{m,p}
when
\mathbf{R}
is square, or the unique forces-only solution for
\mathbf{h}_{m}
that corresponds to the minimum-norm solution for
\mathbf{m}^{*}
as
\mathbf{h}_{m,p}
when
\mathbf{R}
is a wide matrix.

The second
(6n-6-d)
-dimensional subspace within
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
, denoted
\mathcal{N}_{c}
, may be used to generate or eliminate constraint wrenches. We know that a vector
\mathbf{h}_{c}
within this space lies in the null space of
\mathbf{G}
and is orthogonal to subspace
\mathcal{N}_{m}
(i.e. orthogonal to the column space of
\mathbf{K}
). These conditions may be written as

\mathbf{G}\mathbf{h}_{c}=\mathbf{0}_{6\times 1},\ \text{and}\ \mathbf{K}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{c}=\mathbf{0}_{d\times 1}.

(74)

Collecting these linear equalities, we obtain

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{G}\\
\mathbf{K}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{c}=\mathbf{0}_{(6+d)\times 1}

(75)

and thus,

\mathcal{N}_{c}=\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{G}\\
\mathbf{K}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(76)

where
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
denotes the null-space of the argument. Finally, we may define
6n-6-d
linearly independent basis vectors that span
\mathcal{N}_{c}
such that any vector
\mathbf{h}_{c}
can be expressed as

\mathbf{h}_{c}=\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}

(77)

where
\mathbf{Z}
is a matrix of dimension
6n\times(6n-6-d)
whose columns correspond to linearly independent basis vectors that span
\mathcal{N}_{c}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}
is a
(6n-6-d)
-dimensional vector. An interesting consequence of defining
\mathbf{h}_{c}
in this manner is that, for a wrench distribution which admits infinitely many solutions, we may write

\mathbf{Z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{h}_{ck}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}\ \forall k,\ \text{with}\ \mathbf{h}_{ck}\in\mathcal{N}_{c},

(78)

where
\mathbf{Z}^{\dagger}=(\mathbf{Z}^{T}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{T}
is the left Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{h}_{ck}
is the
k
-th vector in the infinite set
\mathcal{N}_{c}
. The physical significance of this result is that every constraint wrench vector
\mathbf{h}_{cj}
in a given feasible set
\mathcal{H}_{c}
has the same corresponding vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}
which uniquely defines the location in the constraint wrench space
\mathcal{N}_{c}
of the set of applied wrenches
\mathbf{h}
and thus uniquely defines the internal loading state of the virtual rigid body. In other words, for a given set of applied wrenches,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}
is independent of the choice of solution for
\mathbf{h}_{m}
.

VII
Implementation

VII-A

Theorem
2
for Wrench Synthesis

The problem of wrench synthesis consists in prescribing the wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{i}
to be applied by the kinematic chains in the context of robot force-control to generate an output wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
while either eliminating internal loads or generating a prescribed internal loading state on the object. When doing so with our proposed framework, we must first decide how closely we wish the inertial properties of the virtual body to match the inertial properties of the real body. This will determine how many wrenches are required to eliminate internal loads (see Section
VI-D
). Then, with the results presented in Section
VI-E
, we are able to write the applied wrenches as

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{h}_{m,p}+\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}-\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}.

(79)

For a given
\mathbf{h}_{o}
,
\mathbf{h}_{m,p}
uniquely describes the set of manipulating wrench distributions. The departure from this unique solution within
\mathcal{N}_{m}
is governed by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}
and dictates how the load is balanced between the manipulating wrench components. Finally,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}
uniquely defines the internal loading state induced by the remaining components in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})
. When
\mathbf{h}_{o}
is given, we may obtain
\mathbf{h}_{m,p}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}
from Theorem
2
. If we wish to apply resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
without generating internal loads, we set
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}=\mathbf{0}
. Alternatively, a designer may prescribe specific internal loading states at each time
t
for a given task with vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{c}
.

VII-B

Theorem
2
for Wrench Analysis

It is trivial to apply Theorem
2
to wrench analysis where the aim is to decompose an arbitrary set of
n
wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{i}
applied at locations
\mathbf{r}_{i}
for
i=1,...,n
into a set of
n
manipulating wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{m,i}
and
n
constraint wrenches
\mathbf{h}_{c,i}
. Since every
\mathbf{h}_{i}
is known, we may calculate
\mathbf{h}_{o}
from (
1
). Then, a valid manipulating wrench distribution
\mathbf{h}_{m}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{h}_{o}
by applying Theorem
2
. The corresponding constraint wrench distribution
\mathbf{h}_{c}
may be easily obtained from the Udwadia-Kalaba equation (
28
) as

\mathbf{h}_{c}=\mathbf{h}_{m}-\mathbf{h}.

(80)

This is a very simple and non-iterative method for performing wrench decomposition. The fact that it is non-iterative also makes it much more suitable for real-time force-control compared to the optimization-based approaches proposed in
[
16
,
17
]
.

VIII-A

Implications for the Parametrized Moore-Penrose Pseudo-inverse of the Grasp Matrix

The authors of

propose the parametrized Moore-Penrose inverse of the grasp matrix shown in (

, we have found this form of
\mathbf{G}^{+}_{M}
to be incorrect, and we have already justified the need for an additional constraint in Section

Theorem 1

(Corrected)
.

The load distribution given by

\mathbf{G}^{+}_{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{m^{*}_{1}}{m^{*}_{o}}\mathbf{I}_{3}&m^{*}_{1}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}_{1})^{T}[\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}]^{-1}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3}&\mathbf{J}^{*}_{1}[\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}]^{-1}\\

\mathbf{0}_{3}&\mathbf{J}^{*}_{n}[\mathbf{J}^{*}_{o}]^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

(81)

for some positive-definite weighting coefficients
m^{*}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
subject to constraints (
51
)-(

VIII-B

Implications for the Equilibrating Wrench Distribution

In
[
14
]
, the authors state that the equilibrating force distribution returned by
\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}
is free of interaction forces and that the forces in this distribution lie on a helicoidal vector field. Although the former statement is always true, the latter only holds if constraints (
51
)-(
54
) can be satisfied by
m^{*}_{i}=1
and
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}=\mathbf{I}_{3}\ \forall i
.

It was shown in Section
V-A
that the instantaneous acceleration of the virtual rigid body
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{o}
defines a helicoidal vector field since we assume it to be at rest. It was also shown in Section
VIII-A
that
\mathbf{G}^{+}_{M}=\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}
when
m^{*}_{i}=1
and
\mathbf{J}^{*}_{i}=\mathbf{I}_{3}\ \forall i
solves (
51
)-(
54
). As the authors of
[
14
]
consider only pure forces, we note that the forces-only solution can be found by omitting the blocks
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{3}&\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
in (
83
).

With this realization, we can see that it is only by coincidence that the equilibrating force field forms a helicoidal field in some cases. Under the circumstances described above where
m^{*}_{i}=1\ \forall i
, it is trivial to see that
\mathbf{f}_{m,i}=\ddot{\mathbf{p}}^{*}_{i}\ \forall i
. Thus, since the virtual instantaneous acceleration field is known to be helicoidal, the vector field of manipulating forces will be too. In addition, since
\mathbf{G}^{+}_{M}=\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}
when
m^{*}_{i}=1\ \forall i
, the manipulating force distribution and the equilibrating force distribution are identical in this case and the latter is therefore a helicoidal field as well. The result is that all three vector fields are identical and helicoidal when
m^{*}_{i}=1\ \forall i
solves (
51
)-(
54
). Otherwise, neither the equilibrating force distribution nor the manipulating force distribution form helicoidal fields.

VIII-C

Implications for Physically Feasible Wrench Decomposition Algorithms

Current state-of-the-art algorithms for wrench decomposition naively assume that manipulating wrenches and constraint wrenches are both components of the applied wrenches and perform the decomposition using an equation similar to the one shown in (
8
). This decision is not properly justified in
[
16
]
nor in
[
17
]
.

As we have shown, the constraint wrench components are not generated
by
the applied wrenches. Rather, they are
added
to the applied wrenches in order for the resultant (the manipulating wrench) to generate the proper constrained acceleration of its corresponding point on the rigid body.

This is not a matter of opinion. It is a direct consequence of the principles of rigid body mechanics. The work of Udwadia and Kalaba in
[
19
]
provides a clear, physically intuitive interpretation of constrained motion that clearly highlights the roles of the manipulating and constraint wrenches and makes this distinction clear.

As a result, we argue that there are two major issues with the method for wrench decomposition proposed in
[
17
]
. First, any constraint wrenches found with (
8
) will be of the wrong sign, since they are on the wrong side of the wrench equilibrium equation which should have the form shown in (
28
). Second, the feasible region for the manipulating and constraint wrench components will be needlessly limited by physical plausibility conditions derived from an erroneous assumption. The Udwadia-Kalaba equation, on the other hand, guarantees physical plausibility, having been derived from fundamental laws of rigid body mechanics.

IX-A

Planar Point Mass

This example would seldom be seen in real robotics applications, but enables us to demonstrate how our proposed framework corrects misconceptions in the literature related to wrench decomposition. To this end, we retake the example from
[
17
]
where three planar forces are applied to a point mass (see Fig.
7
).

Fig. 7
:
Three forces applied to a point mass.

The three forces
\mathbf{f}_{i}
sum to
\mathbf{f}_{o}
. The physical feasibility criteria derived in
[
17
]
constrain both the manipulating and constraint components to lie within the circles shown in Fig.
7
. The authors also state that the manipulating force component
\mathbf{f}_{m,i}
of an applied force
\mathbf{f}_{i}
cannot be larger than its projection on
\mathbf{f}_{o}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{f}_{m,i}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{m,i}\leq\mathbf{f}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{m,i}

\mathbf{f}_{o}

X
Conclusion

The aim of this work is to propose a generalized theory of load distribution in rigid bodies subjected to wrenches applied by multiple closed-loop kinematic chains. We first examine the physical significance of well established principles of rigid body motion described by the Udwadia-Kalaba equation
[
18
]
to better highlight the roles of constraint and manipulating wrenches. Then, we present our theorem which unifies many existing theories and methods related to wrench synthesis and analysis in the context of redundantly-actuated robot force control.

Our theorem fully characterizes the feasible set of manipulating wrench distributions
\mathbf{h}_{m}
for a given resultant wrench
\mathbf{h}_{o}
. We examine in detail the uniqueness of the feasible manipulating wrench distribution set, show how the concept may be extended to wrench synthesis and wrench analysis, and determine a unique metric to describe the internal loading state of a rigid body for a given set of applied wrenches. We also provide a detailed review of the implications of our proposed theorem for other approaches in the literature. Finally, case studies are presented to illustrate the advantages of our proposed methods.

This general theory and the methods derived from it are intended to supplement modern robotics controllers used in real-time force control applications. Our results are proposed in the hopes that we may consider the problem of load distributions in rigid bodies handled by redundantly-actuated closed kinematic chains—either the determination of load distributions with prescribed internal loads or the decomposition of known distributions into manipulating and constraint components—to be a solved one. Such a complete description of the nature of internal loads combined with the methods that we propose for wrench synthesis and analysis may prove to be a useful tool for the real-time force control of redundantly-actuated robotic systems since examples of multiple wrenches applied to a single object are seen in many areas of robotics.
