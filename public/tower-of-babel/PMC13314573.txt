Unrecognized second-trimester cesarean scar ectopic pregnancy in a patient presenting for surgical abortion: A case report
Authors: Emma Schindler, Ester Goldfeld, Allegra Deucher, Danielle Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCesarean scar ectopic pregnancy (CSEP) is a rare but increasingly recognized complication of prior cesarean delivery that carries significant risk of maternal morbidity. This report concerns the case of a 31-year-old patient who presented at 14 weeks of gestation for surgical abortion and was found intraoperatively to have a CSEP. Despite successful evacuation, she developed severe hemorrhage requiring uterine artery embolization and blood product transfusion. Medical records from the patient's previous encounter at another facility indicated that the CSEP had been interpreted there as an intrauterine pregnancy, leading her to travel out of state for legal abortion care. This case underscores the importance of maintaining suspicion for CSEP in patients with prior cesarean delivery and highlights the role of timely recognition, multidisciplinary management, and the complex impact of restricted abortion access on patient safety.Keywords: Abortion, Cesarean scar ectopic, Ectopic pregnancy, Hemorrhage, Uterine artery embolization

Introduction

Cesarean scar ectopic pregnancy (CSEP) occurs when an embryo implants in a hysterotomy scar and affects about 1 in 2000 pregnancies. [1], [2], [3] Affected individuals may be asymptomatic or have abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding. [1], [2] CSEP is diagnosed via transvaginal ultrasound, but can easily be mistaken for a low-lying intrauterine or cervical pregnancy. [1], [4] There is no consensus regarding optimal treatment, but expectant management is not recommended due to the significant associated morbidity and mortality, especially when identified at later gestational ages. [1], [5], [6], [7], [8] This report presents the case of a second-trimester CSEP diagnosed intraoperatively during a surgical abortion, and subsequent hemorrhage requiring uterine artery embolization (UAE). This case highlights diagnostic challenges as well as the increased morbidity when CSEP is diagnosed after the first trimester.

Case Presentation

A 31-year-old woman (G3P2002) at 14 weeks of gestation presented to an outpatient abortion clinic for dilation and curettage (D&C). She had one prior vaginal delivery and one Cesarean. Because she had traveled from a state with restrictive abortion legislation, the care team did not have access to previous medical records or imaging. A brief ultrasound confirmed the gestational age (Fig. 1) and she received buccal misoprostol for cervical preparation. Procedural sedation was induced and the cervix was serially dilated under ultrasound guidance. After the first pass with the suction cannula, she started to bleed heavily. Ultrasound assessment at that time demonstrated a collapsed endometrium above an anterior low-lying gestational sac, findings consistent with CSEP.

Transabdominal ultrasound performed pre-procedure demonstrates a singleton fetus with biometry consistent with established dates and anterior low-lying placenta. There was not concern for Cesarean scar ectopic pregnancy on review of these images though image quality may have limited interpretation.

The procedure was completed, but she continued to bleed despite bimanual massage and uterotonics. A Foley catheter was inflated within the niche to tamponade bleeding. Due to an estimated one-liter blood loss and evidence of hemodynamic stability, she was transferred to the nearest tertiary care center where she proceeded to the operating room for ultrasound-guided suction D&C under general anesthesia. In the operating room, she continued to bleed after multiple passes with the suction cannula. Digital exam confirmed that the uterus was intact within the niche. Given ongoing bleeding, interventional radiology was consulted for UAE. A Cook catheter was placed before transfer to tamponade bleeding. Pelvic angiography demonstrated bleeding from the right uterine artery (Fig. 2) and a bilateral UAE with gelfoam was performed.

She was transfused intra- and postoperatively and briefly recovered in the surgical intensive care unit. Her postoperative course was otherwise uncomplicated. The Cook catheter was removed on postoperative day 1, with no increase in bleeding. She received an etonogestrel implant (Nexplanon) for contraception and was discharged on postoperative day 3. Two weeks later, her bleeding remained light and she had returned to normal activities.

After these events, additional history was obtained from the patient. Three days before presenting to the abortion clinic, she went to an emergency room in her home state with vaginal bleeding and abdominal pain. There, a pelvic ultrasound was interpreted as an ongoing intrauterine pregnancy despite an attempted medication abortion one month prior. She subsequently traveled out of state to seek legal abortion care. With permission, her imaging and clinical documentation from that encounter was accessed. Evaluation of the ultrasound images was highly concerning for an unrecognized CSEP. Findings included a gestational sac ballooning from the lower uterine segment beneath a collapsed endometrium (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Cesarean scar ectopic pregnancy (CSEP) is usually diagnosed in the first trimester; second-trimester diagnosis less common, more challenging, and associated with higher morbidity. [8], [9], [10], [11] Multiple sonographic criteria have been proposed, including: 1) empty uterus, 2) empty cervical canal, 3) gestational sac in the anterior lower uterine segment, and 4) thin or absent myometrium between the gestational sac and bladder. [4], [6], [12] There are multiple surgical approaches for CSEP, including ultrasound-guided suction D&C, hysteroscopy, or laparoscopy. [1], [2], [5], [6], [13] UAE is a minimally invasive technique that can be used alone or as an adjunct to prevent or treat associated hemorrhage, as CSEP is highly vascular due to recruitment of blood vessels from the uterine arteries. [1], [5], [6], [14], [15] Early engagement of interventional radiology colleagues for UAE in the setting of obstetric or gynecologic hemorrhage can help reduce morbidity and cumulative blood loss. Recurrence rates of 5–40% underscore the importance of contraceptive counseling after treatment. [3], [16], [17] Finally, individuals with a prior CSEP warrant early obstetric evaluation and transvaginal ultrasound in subsequent pregnancies. [17] Some literature even supports early sonographic screening in all pregnant women with a prior cesarean for CSEP. [8]

CSEP is a critical diagnosis due to the risk of severe morbidity, especially when diagnosed at later gestational ages. One systematic review and meta-analysis of 724 pregnancies found that diagnosis after 9 weeks was associated with increased composite morbidity, including hemorrhage, need for blood transfusion, uterine rupture, and need for hysterectomy. [8] Clinicians must maintain a broad differential in pregnant patients with prior Cesarean delivery presenting with abdominal pain and bleeding. [1], [2], [4] Failed medication abortion in patients with a prior Cesarean should always raise concern for CSEP, as these pregnancies do not respond to mifepristone and misoprostol due to lack of functional decidua within the Cesarean scar niche. [5], [6], [18]

Rupture of this patient's pregnancy could have led to hemorrhage or death. She required urgent obstetric intervention when she first presented to the emergency room, but because the CSEP was interpreted as an intrauterine pregnancy, she had to travel out of state to seek legal abortion care. [4], [7], [11] The resulting fragmented care she received across multiple states and healthcare systems likely contributed to the diagnostic challenges that led to her having a more morbid outpatient procedure requiring transfer to a tertiary care center, additional intervention, and an intensive care unit stay. The complex and multidimensional impact of restricted reproductive healthcare on patients needs continued consideration.
