Skin Cancer Awareness and Sun Safety Practices Among California Surfers
Authors: Nadine Ben Romdhane, David A. Csuka, Rachel K. Greene, Ella A. Csuka, Christopher Leone, Sean Condon, Natasha A. Mesinkovska
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Skin cancer is the most common malignancy in the United States, with an estimated 6.1 million adults treated annually with annual treatment costs approaching $8.9 billion.1,2 Surfing, a popular aquatic activity in California, involves prolonged UV radiation exposure, a major risk factor for skin cancer. Prior studies have primarily focused on Australian cohorts, reporting a 13.6% lifetime prevalence of skin cancer among surfers.3

We conducted a voluntary, anonymous, cross-sectional survey of recreational and competitive surfers across California (October 2020-March 2021). Participants ≥18 years who surfed regularly were recruited via surf clubs, competitions, and social media. Of 527 responses, 500 complete surveys were analyzed. The survey assessed demographics, surfing habits, sun exposure, sunscreen use, protective behaviors, Fitzpatrick skin type (FST), and self-reported biopsy-confirmed skin cancer history.

Among 500 participants (mean age 29.9 years; 340 males and 160 females), the average number of surf sessions per month was 8.53, with 2.22 hours per session. Ninety-five participants (19.0%) reported a history of biopsy-confirmed skin cancer, including basal cell carcinoma (57.5%), squamous cell carcinoma (22.0%), and melanoma (19.7%). The proportion reporting skin cancer increased with age, reaching 75.8% among surfers ≥50 years (Fig 1). Compared with FST III and IV, types I to II were associated with higher odds of skin cancer (OR 2.62), whereas no cases were reported among FST V-VI (P = .030, Fisher’s exact [probability] test). Prior work in 1348 Australian recreational and competitive surfers found a much lower overall lifetime skin cancer prevalence and did not quantify sun protection behaviors, underscoring the novelty of our findings.3 The elevated prevalence in older surfers likely reflects cumulative UV radiation exposure over decades, consistent with patterns observed in outdoor athletes, including swimmers, runners, and cyclists.4,5 The concentration of lesions on the head and neck aligns with exposure patterns where wetsuits and surf apparel provide limited coverage. Behavioral findings suggest that sun protection practices among California surfers remain inadequate relative to recommended standards.

Limitations include convenience sampling, potential selection and recall bias, reliance on self-reported diagnoses without histological validation, and the cross-sectional design. The high proportion of reported skin cancer among older participants (75.8%) should be interpreted cautiously, as it may overestimate true population prevalence. Lack of age at diagnosis and duration of surfing prior to cancer onset limits assessment of temporal relationships.

This study highlights a substantial burden of self-reported skin cancer and suboptimal sun protection behaviors among California surfers, underscoring a critical gap in targeted prevention strategies for high UV-exposed populations.
