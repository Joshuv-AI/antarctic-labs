YIELD: A Large-Scale Dataset and Evaluation Framework for Information Elicitation Agents
Victor De Lima, Grace Hui Yang

Abstract
Most conversational agents (CAs) are designed to satisfy user needs through user-driven interactions. However, many real-world settings, such as academic interviewing, judicial proceedings, and journalistic investigations, involve broader institutional decision-making processes and require agents that can elicit information from users. In this paper, we introduce Information Elicitation Agents (IEAs) in which the agent’s goal is to elicit information from users to support the agent’s institutional or task-oriented objectives. To enable systematic research on this setting, we present YIELD , a 26M-token dataset of 2,281 ethically sourced, human-to-human dialogues. Moreover, we formalize information elicitation as a finite-horizon POMDP and propose novel metrics tailored to IEAs. Pilot experiments on multiple foundation LLMs show that training on YIELD improves their alignment with real elicitation behavior and findings are corroborated by human evaluation. We release YIELD under CC BY 4.0. The dataset, project code, evaluation tools, and fine-tuned model adapters are available at: https://github.com/infosenselab/yield .

IEAs differ fundamentally from other conversational agents in both whose goals they serve and how success is defined. Conversational agents are generally designed to help users accomplish user-initiated tasks, with the user controlling the agenda and direction of the interaction. IEAs, by contrast, act on behalf of an external institutional objective and use conversation as a means to extract information from the respondent. Crucially, at any point in the dialogue there is not a single optimal question or expected answer; instead, there are many possible directions, each potentially yielding valuable information. The agent’s task is therefore to choose questions that are likely to surface informative and relevant facts, maximizing the cumulative value of what is uncovered rather than converging on a single correct outcome.

The task of creating IEAs is challenging for several reasons. First, the agent must act in an extremely high-dimensional action space, where every utterance can shift the trajectory of the dialogue. Second, defining what makes information “informative” is inherently nuanced and context-dependent, bringing subjectivity into both training and evaluation. These difficulties are further amplified in long dialogues, where agents must maintain topic coherence over many turns while responding adaptively to unpredictable or even adversarial user replies. Additionally, the amount of publicly available data for training IEAs is limited. To the best of our knowledge, no existing dataset is available to support the development of IEAs. Related Work

Dialogue datasets are instrumental in advancing research in conversational agents. Annotated corpora were central to the development of CAs. Early work like the ATIS corpus used labeled intents to drive system research
6
, while more recent ones, such as the Dialog State Tracking Challenge (DSTC) series
7
;
46
, shifted attention toward dialogue state tracking. This emphasis on interaction laid the groundwork for exploring multi-turn information-seeking, where systems make progress through user feedback rather than executing static plans
31
. More recent datasets, such as Schema-Guided Dialogue (SGD)
24
and MultiWOZ
1
, expanded in size and domain coverage, with 16,142 and 8,438 dialogues, respectively. Nonetheless, these existing datasets typically have short dialogues. For instance, DSTC2, SGD, and MultiWOZ averaged 14.49, 20.44, and 13.46 turns per dialogue, respectively. In contrast, the proposed YIELD dataset offers substantially longer dialogues, averaging 171 turns per dialogue. Table
2
provides a comparison among these popular dialogue datasets and YIELD.

There is growing interest in role-aware CAs, which adopt defined personas and pursue structured goals. These agents have gained popularity in a range of applications, from mental health support
4
to legal assistance
39
. Common development approaches use foundation LLMs fine-tuned with domain-specific datasets, often combined with reinforcement learning and human feedback to maintain role consistency and behavioral alignment
11
. In parallel, research on proactive CAs explores how conversational systems can drive interactions forward, such as initiating questions, shifting topics, and helping users make decisions
3
;
13
. These systems have been used in exploratory search and evaluative tasks such as LLM-as-an-Interviewer
10
and LLM-as-a-Judge
47
. Both role-aware and proactive agents benefit from curated dialogue datasets tailored to their specific domains and objectives.

The Information Elicitation Agents (IEAs) presented in this paper represent a natural extension of these trends. IEAs obtain information from human respondents to support the agents’ information needs, such as information needs to serve for institutional goals. Yet, despite increasing interest in these capabilities, no existing dataset has been designed to support IEAs. To our knowledge, YIELD is the first large-scale, ethically sourced benchmark created to enable the training and evaluation of IEAs across multiple domains.

3
Dataset Construction

Our dataset construction process consists of three stages: data acquisition, annotation, and dataset standardization.

3.1
Data Acquisition

We chose the data sources with the following aims: (1) to maximize coverage of elicitation settings and (2) to ensure legal usability through public domain or Creative Commons licensing. Many of the files, particularly PDF and DOCX transcripts, to the best of our knowledge, had not previously been processed into any dialogue dataset. We categorized the identified sources into four domains of elicitation dialogues. For a full description of the data sources, see Appendix
A.1
.

3.2
Annotation

The collected transcripts appeared in various formats, including HTML, TXT, PDF, and DOCX. Unifying these different formats and heterogeneous materials required extensive pre-processing. Automated text extractors regularly introduced errors during conversion and sources contained individual issues such as speaker names that appeared without distinguishing markers and often varied in form within the same document. These and other issues made automatic parsing and tagging unreliable. Therefore, we manually corrected and annotated the transcripts over several weeks, removing extraneous text, standardizing speaker references, and marking turns and speaker roles. Further details on the annotation and preprocessing pipeline are provided in the dataset documentation.

3.3
Dataset Standardization

We then passed the manually tagged files through standardization scripts to enforce a uniform schema (Table
3
). The resulting dataset includes 148 dialogues from the Academic Interviews domain, 129 Journalistic Investigations, 621 Judicial Dialogue, and 1,383 Oral History; with a total of 2,281 dialogues, 390,205 turns, and 26,249,014 tokens. Table
4
shows a breakdown of YIELD’s dataset statistics by domain and source. We split the dialogues into training, development, and test sets using stratified sampling for each domain. The development and test sets each contain 10% of the data (228 and 229 dialogues, respectively), while the training set contains the remaining 80% (1,824). We grouped the dialogue files into sets of 128 dialogues each. The last file in a set may contain fewer than 128 dialogues. The unzipped size of YIELD is 201.06 MB. All dialogues are organized around two roles (elicitor and respondent), but may include multiple speakers within a role (e.g., group interviews). In this work, all implementations are done at the role level, while the released dataset preserves speaker identities to enable future multi-speaker modeling.

4.1
States, Actions, and Observations

We model the process of elicitation as a sequence of interactions between an
elicitor
(interviewer) and a
respondent
(interviewee), where the elicitor is the agent and the respondent is part of the environment. The respondent possesses information that is unobservable by the elicitor, such as knowledge, opinions, or preferences, that the elicitor aims to infer from conversational interaction. This setup aligns naturally with the framework of a POMDP.

At each turn, the environment has a latent state
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
that represents the respondent’s hidden information. The elicitor takes an action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
in the form of a natural language utterance, after which the environment responds with a respondent utterance (observation)
O_{t+1}\in\mathcal{O}
that depends on both the latent state
X_{t}
and the elicitor’s action
A_{t}
, leading to the next elicitor utterance
A_{t+1}
. This dynamic continues until the conversation is over. The
conversation history
at time
t
is a sequence of elicitor and respondent utterances

H_{t}=(A_{t-w},O_{t-w+1},\ldots,A_{t-1},O_{t}),

(1)

where
w
is the window size in turns that is available to the agent at time
t
when producing its next utterance. When the conversation history includes the elicitor’s latest utterance
A_{t}
, we denote it
H^{s}_{t}
.

In the traditional POMDP formulation, the agent maintains a belief state
S_{t}
, which is a probability distribution over possible hidden environment states
X_{t}
. This distribution encodes the agent’s available information about the hidden variables governing the environment at time
t
and serves as the agent’s basis for decision making. In the dialogue setting, however, explicitly maintaining such a distribution is not feasible as
X_{t}
represents unbounded and continuously changing information (everything the respondent might know, think, or believe). To make the problem tractable, we represent the information available to the elicitor at time
t
with token-level hidden representations
S_{t}=f_{\theta}(H^{s}_{t})
produced by a causal language model, where
f_{\theta}(H^{s}_{t})
denotes the model’s forward pass over the full segment
H^{s}_{t}
parameterized by
\theta
. Because the model is autoregressive, each token’s representation depends only on the tokens preceding it, meaning that the hidden states corresponding to the context
H_{t}
encode everything the model has “seen” in the current turn’s context.
S_{t}
provides the sufficient context from which the likelihood of
A_{t}
can be evaluated, functionally mirroring the role of the belief state in the traditional POMDP formulation.

4.2
Reward Signals

Each elicitor action
A_{t}
is associated with a scalar reward
R_{t+1}=r(H_{t},A_{t},O_{t+1})
reflecting the informational value of the respondent’s subsequent response
O_{t+1}
. We convert the reward signals into return-to-go format, in which
R_{t+1}
at each elicitor turn is computed in reverse temporal order as the discounted sum of future rewards,
R^{\text{tg}}_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{T-t}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}
, where
\gamma
is a discount factor. Unlike supervised fine-tuning, incorporating returns-to-go allows the learning process to take into account the effects of the agent’s utterance on the entire remaining dialogue, even if the agent only sees a dialogue window at each time step.

4.3
Value Function and Advantage Estimation

We follow Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR)
(
20
)
to train the elicitor policy in an offline setting to bias learning toward elicitor utterances that yield higher long-term informational return. AWR estimates a state-value function
v_{\psi}(S_{t})
and computes advantages as the difference between empirical returns-to-go and the value prediction. In our implementation, the value function is realized as a linear value head on top of the LLM’s hidden representations. Because dialogue windows are padded to a fixed length, we use the hidden state of the end-of-sequence token following the elicitor’s utterance
A_{t}
as input to the value head instead of the final hidden state.

4.4
Policy and Policy Optimization

The elicitor’s policy
\pi_{\theta}
is implemented by the same causal language model that produces the state representations
S_{t}=f_{\theta}(H^{s}_{t})
. The policy defines an autoregressive distribution over token sequences representing the elicitor’s next utterance
A_{t}
, conditioned on the preceding dialogue context:
\pi_{\theta}(A_{t}\mid H_{t})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\pi_{\theta}(y_{\ell}\mid H_{t},y_{<\ell})
, where
A_{t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})
denotes the sequence of output tokens for the elicitor’s turn.

During policy optimization, we aim to maximize the likelihood the model assigns to the offline dataset’s utterances
A_{t}
. We employ parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters
9
to enable efficient learning, in which the base causal language model remains frozen, and trainable low-rank matrices are inserted into its attention projection layers. During AWR optimization, only these adapter parameters and the value head receive gradient updates. The model processes the full segment
H^{s}_{t}=(H_{t},A_{t})
, predicting token probabilities autoregressively. Then, we mask and remove context and padding tokens so that gradients consider only the
A_{t}
. The weighted policy loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\bar{w}_{i}\log\pi_{\theta}(A_{i}\mid S_{i}),

(2)

where each
\bar{w}_{i}
scales the contribution of sample
i
within the mini-batch
\mathcal{B}
according to its estimated advantage, departing from standard supervised fine-tuning where all examples contribute equally regardless of their quality.

The critic (value head) is trained jointly with the policy network to minimize the mean-squared error between predicted and empirical returns:

\mathcal{L}_{v}(\psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\big(v_{\psi}(S_{i})-R^{\text{tg}}_{i}\big)^{2}.

(3)

The combined optimization objective is:

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\psi)=\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\theta)+\mathcal{L}_{v}(\psi),

(4)

which jointly updates the actor (policy) and critic (value head).

5
Evaluation for Information Elicitation Agents

In this section, we propose a set of key dimensions for evaluating IEAs. These dimensions go beyond conventional dialogue evaluation approaches, such as BLEU
19
, task success
43
, or response appropriateness
26
, by focusing on the goals and dynamics of information elicitation. We release an open-source Python package for computing all proposed evaluation metrics (included in the project repository).

5.1
Conformity

Conformity
measures whether an IEA agent could generate dialogue output that follows the distributional patterns typical of real human elicitation dialogues. We identify two dimensions that are particularly informative as to whether the agent acquired the data’s characteristics: (1)
perplexity
computed by evaluating real human utterances from the testing set under the trained IEA model and (2)
Response length
, measured as a simple token count. Perplexity reflects how well the model captures the linguistic content of human dialogue, while utterance length captures a critical stylistic property that varies significantly across large language models and strongly influences conversational behavior.

For perplexity, we condition the models on the dialogue history and score the
real
human elicitor utterance for each block in the test set. We restrict the loss calculation to elicitor tokens so that context tokens do not affect the score and compute perplexity in the standard manner as the exponential of the token-level cross-entropy:
\text{PPL}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)\right)
, where
x
denotes the dialogue context and
y_{t}
denotes the
t
-th token of the elicitor’s reference utterance. We report micro perplexity, which consists of pooling across all target tokens before exponentiation. Response length is measured as the number of tokens produced by the model’s tokenizer.

5.2
Progression

Progression
(P) measures the extent to which a conversation moves forward rather than stagnating on the same topic. For IEAs, this is a crucial behavioral indicator that an effective agent should guide the dialogue toward new, relevant information, rather than looping over already-covered ground. Traditional metrics often focus on utterance coherence or intent-matching, but these do not account for
forward momentum
in the interaction. We quantify Progression as the average (decayed) cosine distance between each utterance embedding and a window of preceding utterance embeddings:

P(U)=\frac{1}{L-k}\sum_{t=k+1}^{L}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma^{j}\left(1-\frac{u_{t-j}\cdot u_{t}}{\|u_{t-j}\|\|u_{t}\|}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma^{j}},

(5)

where
U=(u_{1},...,u_{L})
is the sequence of embeddings of the dialogue’s utterances,
u_{t}
is the embedding of the
t
-th utterance, and
L
is the total number of utterances.
k
is the context window size and
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a decay factor that reduces the importance of older utterances. Parameters
k>1
or
\gamma<1
give a direction to the metric by introducing asymmetry. Setting
k=1
(the conversation becomes a chain of pairs) and
\gamma=1
(no decay) can make the formula symmetric:
P(U)=\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\left(1-\frac{u_{t}\cdot u_{t+1}}{\|u_{t}\|\,\|u_{t+1}\|}\right)
. Since cosine distance lies in
[0,2]
, so does
P
.

5.3
Turn-Length Ratio

Turn-Length Ratio
(TLR) compares the average verbosity of the respondent against that of the elicitor. In effective elicitation settings, the agent should speak concisely and strategically to draw out longer, content-rich responses from the user. This asymmetry suggests a successful elicitation dynamic. The ratio is defined as:

TLR(T_{r},T_{e})=\frac{\tfrac{1}{n_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{r}}T_{r,i}}{\tfrac{1}{n_{e}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}T_{e,i}},

(6)

where
T_{r}=(T_{r,1},...,T_{r,n_{r}})
is the sequence of token counts for respondent turns,
T_{e}=(T_{e,1},...,T_{e,n_{e}})
is the sequence of token counts for elicitor turns, and
n_{r}
,
n_{e}
are their respective turn counts. A ratio
TLR>1
indicates that respondents are producing more verbose turns than elicitors.

6.2
Reward Signal

As a first-step reward formulation to validate the proposed elicitation framework, we operationalize rewards
r
using a factual novelty signal as proxy for informational gain, measuring how much previously unmentioned content an elicitor action surfaces. We apply a spaCy-based NER model
8
to each utterance and maintain a cumulative set of previously mentioned entities, events, or facts. After the elicitor’s turn, the respondent’s reply is scanned for entities not yet observed; the elicitor is rewarded in proportion to each newly introduced entity. Formally, the reward is
R_{t+1}=\big|\mathcal{E}_{t+1}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\leq t}\big|
, where
\mathcal{E}_{t+1}
denotes the set of entities extracted from the respondent’s response,
\mathcal{E}_{\leq t}
the accumulated set from all prior turns, and
\setminus
denotes the set difference operator. To mitigate potential reward-gaming behaviors, we compute factual novelty under several constraints. Entities produced by the elicitor are also added to the cumulative entity set, but they do not contribute reward, so that entities subsequently repeated by the respondent are not misattributed. Repeated entities yield no additional credit, therefore, agents that allow conversations to linger on the same content accrue fewer reward. While entity count alone could be gamed in unconstrained settings (e.g., a model providing a list of entities as utterance), this risk is mitigated here because rewards are computed on curated, real human–human dialogues.

6.3
Experimental Dataset

We adapted YIELD to fit the experimental setup by segmenting the dialogues into fixed length
blocks
using a sliding window of six turns. Each block ends with an elicitor utterance, with the preceding five turns serving as conversational context. We added a base system instruction to each block (“
Act as an information elicitation agent for {domain}.
”). We removed blocks exceeding 512 tokens to maintain computational feasibility and consistent utterance length. We removed segments ending with an elicitor utterance of two words or fewer as we found these generally impacted training negatively by oversampling
backchannel
statements (e.g., “Okay”, “Hmm”, “Got it”). Then, we computed rewards and returns-to-go with a
\gamma=0.9
discount factor on the full dialogues and annotated them. There are a total of 103,454 segments with 83,181 on the training set and 9,988 and 10,285 on the development and testing sets, respectively. Descriptive statistics are available in Table
5
, while a sample line is available in Appendix
A.4
.

6.5.1
Conformity

Table
6
shows a clear gap between prompt-only models and those adapted to information elicitation via SFT or ORL (e.g., the prompted Llama-3.1-8B averaged a micro perplexity of 46.9 in Academic Interviews, while the ORL model averaged 12.5). This effect is especially strong for DeepSeek-R1, since its reasoning baseline prefaces responses with meta-level deliberation. Fine-tuning suppresses this pattern and brings its outputs in line with human data. Among adapted models, SFT achieves the lowest perplexity, while ORL is slightly higher, consistent with advantage-weighted training prioritizing longer-horizon behavior over token-level likelihood.

In terms of response length, prompted models consistently produce elicitor turns far longer than those observed in real dialogues (e.g., the prompted Llama-3.1-8B averaged 39.5 tokens in Academic Interviews, while the ORL model averaged 11.6 tokens). Beyond misalignment, prompting is also expensive, as it relies on substantially longer prompts (system instructions and dialogue context) and higher token usage (e.g., the prompted models averaged 540.4 tokens in Academic Interviews, while SFT and ORL models averaged 180.4 tokens). Although answer length could be constrained through even longer prompting, doing so is inherently unreliable because it requires the model to deviate from its default behavior. In contrast, SFT and ORL produce elicitor turns much closer to real elicitation behavior with far shorter prompts.

6.5.2
Progression

Given that there are 6 turns, we compute Progression with
k=5
, and since only the last utterance is generated we set
\gamma=0.5
to more heavily decrease the importance of non-generated utterances. We include as a comparison baseline a version of the test set where turns within a dialogue have been randomly shuffled. Progression embeddings are obtained using the all-MiniLM-L12-v2 model from Sentence Transformers
27
. The real and randomly shuffled references in Table
7
show that the Progression metric captures the intended semantic drift within the dialogues. Real human elicitation dialogues consistently fall within a lower range (0.713–0.755), while randomly shuffled dialogues yield much higher values (0.800–0.848). Within this reference frame, both SFT and ORL models closely track real dialogues across domains, whereas prompt-only models are uniformly lower. This pattern indicates that prompted models tend to linger on previously covered topics.

6.5.3
Turn-Length Ratio

Table
8
presents turn-length ratio results, with the shuffled dataset also included. In the shuffled baseline, elicitor and respondent turns are randomly reassigned, causing answers to appear as questions and vice versa. This role mixing neutralizes the systematic asymmetry present in real dialogues, leading to ratios close to 1. Meanwhile, real dialogues show ratios well above 1 across domains, reflecting the elicitation dynamic in which elicitor turns prompt longer respondent utterances. Both SFT and ORL closely reproduce this asymmetry, learning to limit elicitor verbosity in proportions similar to human interviewers, a behavior that prompted models largely fail to exhibit. As with response length, this gap cannot be reliably closed through additional prompting, since doing so does not make models internalize elicitor behavior.

We collected human ratings of elicitor question quality using Amazon Mechanical Turk. For each HIT, workers reviewed the five-turn dialogue context followed by corresponding real, prompted, SFT, and ORL responses. We randomized the order of the response sources across HITs to prevent order effects. Workers scored questions on five-point scales representing different aspects of elicitation. . In total, 300 dialogue evaluations were collected (100 dialogue blocks sampled with stratified sampling, rated by three workers). Workers were required to have more than 500 prior HITs and approval rate above 90%. Workers were compensated at a rate consistent with U.S. minimum wage, based on estimated task completion time.

The results in Table

show that fine-tuned models more closely approximate the distribution of real utterances than prompted models, supporting automatic evaluation findings. Fine-tuned model scores track the real responses more consistently, even though their absolute ratings are lower. This outcome is particularly interesting, as it suggests that crowd workers rewarded fuller elicitor utterances, which prompted models reliably generate. However, this behavior departs from real dialogue as prompted responses tend to be more verbose, leading, and polished as shown in Section
6.5
. By contrast, fine-tuned models, while receiving lower absolute scores, overwhelmingly align more closely with the distribution of real utterances.

6.6.1
Potential Misuse and Mitigations

Information elicitation agents raise ethical concerns due to their goal of guiding conversations toward information that serves external objectives, which can introduce risks of biased or leading questioning, power imbalances, and undue influence, particularly in domains such as journalism, legal proceedings, or public opinion polling where neutrality is critical. These risks are compounded in settings involving sensitive personal information (e.g., healthcare or counseling), where issues of data privacy, storage, and potential misuse arise. Mitigations include enforcing constraints on questioning strategies (e.g., prompt-level guidelines or post-generation filtering) and extending evaluation protocols to detect and penalize biased behavior. Systems should also implement data minimization, secure storage, and clear user consent and transparency practices. In high-stakes or sensitive contexts, IEAs should only be deployed under appropriate institutional and regulatory oversight (e.g., IRB or clinical governance), with human monitoring and auditing to ensure compliance with ethical and legal standards, rather than relying solely on model-level safeguards.

7
Conclusion

Our evaluation demonstrates YIELD effectively supports the development of models for the elicitation setting and our finite-horizon POMDP formulation with offline reinforcement learning shows that dialogue-level optimization is competitive with SFT even when trained with a minimal reward signal. This work opens several directions for future research including extended reward formulations that capture domain-specific notions of informational value, interactive evaluation with live respondents, and additional evaluation dimensions that capture a broader range of elicitation characteristics.

8
Limitations

The reward formulation used in offline reinforcement learning is intentionally minimal and may not adequately capture informational value across elicitation contexts. Additionally, evaluation is limited to offline, next-turn generation on fixed contexts rather than interactive settings. Moreover, some proposed metrics are sensitive to design choices (e.g., Progression depends on the choice of embedding model and hyper-parameters) and may not capture all aspects relevant to effective elicitation behavior. Finally, this work focuses on modeling the base elicitor agent and does not address conversation management. While a fine-tuned LLM can serve as the core of an elicitation system, it is likely insufficient on its own for sustaining structured and effective long-horizon interactions.

Ethical Considerations

We have taken all reasonable steps to ensure that the content in this dataset either resides in the public domain or adheres to applicable licenses, such as Creative Commons. We encourage users to notify us of any content that may be subject to restrictions not accounted for so that we can address the matter appropriately and update the dataset as necessary.

A.5
Baseline’s Prompt

The following is the prompt used on the baseline models. The prompt was not tailored to any specific model and was generated using an LLM to avoid researcher bias.

You are participating in an oral history interview
as the "elicitor" (interviewer). Your role is to
elicit personal stories, reflections, and details
from the "respondent" (interviewee) through natural,
conversational questions.

Follow these principles:

•

Be friendly, engaged, and curious.

•

Ask clear, open-ended questions.

•

Let the conversation flow naturally build on what the provider just said.

•

Use informal, real spoken language (e.g., “So tell me…” or “What was that like?”).

•

Occasionally use acknowledgments or restatements like “OK” or “Wow” to maintain flow.

•

Focus on memories, motivations, feelings, and personal experiences.

Here’s an example start:

[conversation]

elicitor: OK, Larry, why don’t you tell us a little
bit about what you’re doing now.

respondent: I’m retired and I do a lot of scroll
signs, which keeps me busy in the cold months of
the winter and the hot months of the summer.

elicitor: Where are you living now?

respondent: In Green Bay, Wisconsin. We farmed for
30 years and then I retired and we bought a farm
house and remodeled that…

elicitor: Too much work. OK.

respondent: So that’s brought me up to today.

elicitor: Well, then the next thing that I want to
do is have you think back to the year that you
joined the Peace Corps. What were you doing before?
How did you hear about it? What made you decide to
join?

respondent: [responds…]

elicitor: [Your turn — ask the next natural question
based on what they just said.]
