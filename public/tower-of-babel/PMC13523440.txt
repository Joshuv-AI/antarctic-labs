Repair of Access-Related Femoral Artery Pseudoaneurysms Using Percutaneous Suture-Mediated Closure With Ultrasound-Only Guidance
Authors: Dominic N Facciponte, Ravi S Sandhu, Jenaya L Goldwag, Mark A Eid, Brianna M Krafcik, Bjoern D Suckow, Thomas W Cheng, Kirthi S Bellamkonda, Matthew J Alef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemoral artery pseudoaneurysms (PSAs) are a complication of endovascular access procedures that typically require repair. Patients with contraindications or failed attempts at traditional repair methods or those who are unfit for open surgery may benefit from an alternative, minimally invasive and durable approach. We describe our procedural technique and experience using the Abbott Perclose ProStyle percutaneous suture-mediated closure repair (PSMCR) device with ultrasound-only guidance to treat noninfectious postprocedural femoral PSAs (n = 5) with appropriately sized arteriotomy diameters. Our technique uses longitudinal-axis ultrasound imaging to guide real-time access through the PSA neck for deployment of the PSMCR device. PSMCR was successful in 3 of 5 patients; 2 required alternative repairs. Failures resulted from inability to access the PSA neck or ineffective suture closure. In select patients, ultrasound-guided PSMCR is a radiation-free alternative that should be considered in the management algorithm for common femoral artery PSAs, particularly in patients considered too high risk for open repair.Key words: access-site complication, closure device ultrasound, pseudoaneurysm, transfemoral access

Femoral artery pseudoaneurysms (PSAs) are a recognized complication of percutaneous transfemoral access, with increased risk in older patients, those receiving anticoagulation, and following large-bore access.1,2 Traditional management options include ultrasound (US)–guided compression, percutaneous thrombin injection, covered stent grafting, or open vascular reconstruction.1, 2, 3 Although effective, these approaches may be limited by unfavorable anatomy, overlying skin changes, thrombin allergy, and increased morbidity in medically complex patients requiring open repair.1,3,4 Consequently, there is a need for a durable, minimally invasive alternative for high-risk patients with contraindications to standard approaches.

Percutaneous suture-mediated closure repair (PSMCR) devices have emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for select patients with femoral artery PSAs.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Prior reports demonstrate feasibility, but most techniques rely on fluoroscopy, contrast, or secondary arterial access, which increases cost, radiation exposure, and interventional hazard, particularly in high-risk patients. We describe our procedural approach and experience of performing US-only guided PSMCR in a case series of 5 highly comorbid patients who developed postendovascular access femoral artery PSAs.

Methods

We reviewed the cases of patients who underwent PSCMR at a single institution from March 1, 2025 to March 31, 2026. During this period, any patient with a postprocedural femoral PSA with an arteriotomy diameter of ≤8.8 mm was considered eligible for this approach. This size was selected based on the Prolene suture injection length for the Perclose ProStyle (Abbott Cardiovascular) device. Cases in which there was evidence of infection necessitating operative debridement or active hemorrhage were not eligible for PSMCR. Manual chart review was performed for 5 identified cases. Data regarding patient demographics, clinical presentation, preprocedural imaging, PSA size, clinical course, procedural interventions, and discharge status were collected. This case series was formatted according to the case reporting (CARE) guidelines.10

PSMCR procedural technique and postprocedural imaging

PSMCR was performed using the Perclose ProStyle device (Figure 1). Under longitudinal-axis view US guidance, percutaneous access was obtained directly through the PSA, with needle advancement toward the PSA neck. A Cope mandril wire (Cook), was maneuvered through the PSA neck to obtain retrograde femoral artery access (Figure 2). The working platform was upsized to an 0.035-inch wire, and sheath exchange was performed. Definitive closure was performed with 2 Perclose ProStyle devices (Video 1). Intraoperative duplex US confirmed PSA thrombosis. Patients remained flat for 4 hours after the procedure and were maintained on bedrest overnight. Postprocedural day 1 duplex ultrasound was used to confirm successful repair (Figure 3). Procedures were performed in the operating room with monitored anesthesia care.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 46-year-old man with alcoholic cirrhosis presented with sepsis secondary to spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and an incidentally discovered right femoral PSA (Figures 4A and 4B) from a recent admission during which he underwent embolization of a left profunda femoris branch for a traumatic thigh hematoma, which was complicated by a repeat bleed necessitating reintervention via right femoral access (6-F sheath) with a collagen-plug-based arterial closure device (Table 2). Intervention was deferred until stabilization and clearance of blood cultures. An attempted US-guided thrombin injection was aborted because of acute anaphylaxis, which resolved with supportive measures. Follow-up duplex demonstrated PSA enlargement to 2.0 × 2.5 × 3.5 cm with partial thrombosis (Figures 4C and 4D). Given prohibitive surgical risk, percutaneous PSMCR was pursued. In this case, thrombus in the PSA prevented easy retrograde wire passage into the external iliac artery (EIA), resulting in antegrade wire advancement into the superficial femoral artery (SFA). A microcatheter was advanced into the SFA, and an 0.018-inch wire was manipulated with serial catheter retraction to “flip” the catheter and achieve retrograde access into the common femoral artery (CFA) and EIA. The patient recovered uneventfully.

Case 2

A 66-year-old man with cirrhosis secondary to hepatitis C presented with acute spinal cord compression due to a T6 epidural mass from newly diagnosed metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma (Table 1). Neurointerventional radiology performed emergent embolization of the right segmental T6 artery which supplied the tumor via right 6-F CFA access that was closed with a collagen-based arterial closure device. This was then followed by emergent T6 decompression/corpectomy and T4-T8 fusion. Given a persistent poor motor examination, mean arterial pressure augmentation was continued postoperatively for approximately 72 hours along with a systemic steroid taper. Roughly 4 weeks later, a computed tomography scan was notable for a large right CFA PSA. Given the high likelihood of morbidity or mortality of an open vascular repair for this patient, we proceeded with PSMCR. The procedure was performed without issue using 2 Perclose ProStyle devices. Thereafter, the patient had a prolonged hospital course for initiation of treatment for metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma as well as rehabilitation placement.

Case 3

A 73-year-old man presented with a 7-cm thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm with impending rupture as well as an occluded 5.3-cm right common iliac artery aneurysm; he underwent repair with a thoracic endograft and a physician-modified endograft which was performed via bilateral CFA access (20-F right, 7-F left) (Table 1). Given the extent of aortic coverage during the index case, mean arterial augmentation was performed for ∼48 hours postprocedurally for spinal cord protection. He noted tenderness in the right groin postprocedurally and was identified to have a right CFA PSA. Two attempts at compression were performed with incomplete thrombosis and interval growth. Given his recent symptomatic aneurysm, open surgical repair was deemed high risk, and at this point we elected to proceed with PSMCR. The procedure was performed as previously described, and the patient recovered uneventfully.

Case 4

An 86-year-old woman with severe aortic stenosis underwent elective transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) via bilateral CFA access (6-F left, 14-F right) and was discharged home on aspirin and Eliquis (apixaban) (Table 1). She returned to the cardiology clinic and was noted to have a pulsatile left groin mass which was confirmed on duplex to be a large CFA PSA. Given her recent TAVR and frailty, we elected to proceed with PSMCR. Per operator preference, in this case axial-view US was used, which potentially limited needle visualization as it traversed the PSA toward the neck. Ultimately a wire could not be established in the native CFA access despite numerous attempts, and an open femoral cutdown with primary repair was performed.

Case 5

A 74-year-old woman with an abdominal aortic aneurysm and severe epilepsy underwent elective endovascular aortic aneurysm repair (EVAR) and renal artery stenting via bilateral CFA access (10-F right, 16-F left) and was discharged home on Plavix (clopidogrel) and coumadin (Table 1). She returned with a pulsatile right groin pass and was noted to have a large right CFA. Given her recent EVAR and epilepsy, major open reconstruction was deemed high risk, and PSMCR was attempted. During the procedure, the Prolene sutures would not engage the arterial wall despite a measured preoperative atriotomy of <8.8 mm and numerous attempts with good visualization and footplate engagement. It was discovered that during the index EVAR, the footplate of the closure device became stuck, which likely damaged the adjacent segments of the femoral artery wall during closure. It is hypothesized that the “effective” arteriotomy size was likely too large for PSMCR, which may explain this failure (Figure 5). Given intraprocedural bleeding during the attempted repair, SFA cutdown and distal EIA/proximal CFA stent grafting was performed.

Case series takeaways

Table 1 summarizes the 5 patients at our center who had attempted PSMCR, including the 2 unsuccessful cases. These patients represent a high-risk cohort with substantial comorbidities and femoral PSAs that developed after endovascular access for major vascular or structural heart procedures, or in the context of postprocedural anticoagulation or blood pressure augmentation. Three PSAs were successfully treated using PSMCR, all of which used 2 Perclose ProStyle devices and longitudinal US visualization. As previously described, 2 cases failed because of inability to obtain stable access through the PSA neck or the Prolene suture pulling through a friable arterial wall, necessitating open repair or stent graft bailout. Given the early experience with this technique, it is unclear what specific clinical and anatomic factors are most suitable for PSMCR. However, from our experience and known risks associated with other techniques, we believe PSMCR can be applied to femoral PSAs with an anterior arteriotomy and a diameter of ≤8.8 mm, without a broad-based neck.

Discussion

Experience with PSMCR for femoral artery PSAs is limited, however our series demonstrates that this technique is feasible in carefully selected, high-risk patients. We noted 3 key takeaways from our experience that can guide future applications of this approach.

First, in our practice, this strategy was only used in patients with substantial operative risk after failed, or contraindicated, conservative therapies. There is no consensus guideline for femoral PSA management, resulting in wide practice variability. Further, anatomic constraints, thrombin intolerance (albeit rare), anatomically short or wide PSA necks, and surgical morbidity create a treatment gap for high-risk patients.1,4 Although ultrasound-guided thrombin injection is the most common approach, it is limited in instances of lesions with short, broad-based necks given the risk of distal thrombin embolization and acute limb ischemia.1 In these situations, PSMCR may be a suitable alternative. Additionally, 2 patients had advanced cirrhosis, one of whom also had metastatic disease and spinal cord compression, while an additional patient had a recent repair of a symptomatic thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm with impending rupture (Table 1). In all cases, standard therapies such as compression or thrombin injection were considered or attempted but were contraindicated or unsuccessful, and open surgical repair was discussed with careful consideration of individualized risk. PSMCR accordingly provides an alternative approach in the traditional treatment algorithm.

Second, meticulous US visualization is essential for successful PSMCR. Longitudinal-axis imaging allows clear delineation of femoral bifurcation anatomy and real-time assessment of arteriotomy closure. In our series, successful access through the PSA neck was achieved in all 4 cases using longitudinal US views, whereas the only case that failed to achieve wire access through the PSA neck relied on transverse US imaging. Published experiences include similar small case series and single-case reports, largely using fluoroscopy with ipsilateral or contralateral femoral access,5, 6, 7, 8 and, in one instance, a collagen-plug-based closure device rather than suture-mediated repair.9 In contrast, our approach can eliminate the need for radiation, contrast, or additional access sites.

Finally, given the early stage of adoption and the potential need for immediate surgical conversion, we perform these procedures in the hybrid operating room to ensure rapid access to open repair if required. This was necessary in 2 cases, given either limited visualization of the PSA neck with transverse ultrasound imaging or failure of the PSMCR sutures to close the arteriotomy despite appropriate footplate engagement. Femoral artery cutdown with direct suture repair was performed in one case, with bailout stent grafting in the other. For patient safety, we anticipate continuing to perform this procedure in the hybrid operating room rather than in an interventional suite or at the bedside.

Our experience highlights some limitations. Percutaneous access to the PSA neck can be challenging, particularly in the setting of partial thrombosis, where the needle may deflect off thrombus or the wire may coil within the sac despite longitudinal-axis US guidance. In addition, procedures were performed in the operating room with a standard vascular setup, potentially limiting cost savings to the health care system. Finally, given the small sample size and lack of comparative data, outcomes cannot be directly compared with established treatments, and results should be interpreted cautiously. Additional experience and future studies examining this approach in comparison to standard treatment modalities are warranted.

Conclusions

Longitudinal-axis, US-guided PSMCR of femoral artery PSAs is expedient, eliminates radiation and contrast exposure, and may obviate the risks associated with open vascular reconstruction in select patients. In the management algorithm for femoral PSAs, this novel technique should be considered as a durable repair option for anatomically appropriate, high risk-profile patients.
