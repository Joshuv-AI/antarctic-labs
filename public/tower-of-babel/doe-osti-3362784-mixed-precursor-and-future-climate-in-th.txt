1.0 Summary
 The generation of atmospheric aerosols is a fundamental process in aerosol-climate interactions, where
 particles act as nuclei for cloud formation and ultimately influence Earth’s radiative balance.
 Understanding aerosol precursors, chemical compositions, and formation mechanisms, particularly for
 secondary organic aerosols (SOAs), is essential for assessing their overall impact on climate.
 Aerosol-climate interactions are especially important in the Southeastern United States (SE-US), where
 the atmosphere is strongly influenced by both biogenic and anthropogenic emissions, as well as intense
 land-atmosphere convective coupling. Current mechanistic representations of SOA formation under
 ambient conditions are largely extrapolated from laboratory-scale oxidation experiments involving
 individual volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which are often assumed to behave independently when
 present in mixtures. However, recent empirical evidence demonstrates that coexisting VOC precursors
 can undergo previously unexplored molecular-scale interactions that significantly alter SOA
 physicochemical properties, including mass loading, yield, and water uptake.1 For example, the
 coexistence of isoprene and monoterpenes has been shown to reduce hydroxyl radical availability, thereby
 limiting oxidation processes². Similarly, interactions among anthropogenic VOCs (AVOCs), such as
 toluene, xylene, and trimethylbenzene, can enhance SOA production, whereas the addition of biogenic
 VOCs (BVOCs) may suppress SOA yields through cross-reactions among reactive intermediates.³
 Furthermore, mixtures of biogenic and anthropogenic VOCs can produce chemically complex SOA with
 distinct hygroscopic properties that influence the variability of cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) activity.⁴
 These findings highlight the need for mechanistic studies of SOA formation under more
 atmospherically realistic conditions that explicitly account for interactions between AVOCs and
 BVOCs. Incorporating these interactions is critical for accurately simulating SOA formation and
 its subsequent effects on cloud formation and regional climate.

 There is a critical need to quantify the impacts of multiple coexisting precursors on aerosol formation
 beyond the constraints of laboratory-scale measurements. Aerosol processes, particularly in the SE-US,
 represent a priority research area for the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric System Research
 (ASR) program, which seeks to improve understanding of aerosol radiative effects and their influence on
 climate. This research aims to advance the mechanistic understanding of SOA formation in the SE-US by
 investigating interactions among coexisting VOC precursors observed at the third ARM Mobile Facility
 (AMF3) located in the Bankhead National Forest (BNF). Specifically, this work addresses critical
 knowledge gaps associated with previously unresolved molecular-scale interactions within VOC mixtures
 that govern SOA formation and evolution under atmospherically relevant conditions.

 Anthropogenic and biogenic organic compounds with a wide range of volatilities in both gas and particle
 phases was studied using Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s proton transfer reaction time-of-flight mass
 Spectrometer (PTR-ToF-MS). PTR-ToF-MS is one of the requested instruments for the analysis of VOCs
 in AMF3-BNF’s science plan, particularly during intensive operational periods.5 In this work, the
 PTR-ToF-MS was deployed at the BNF Main (BNF M1) Instrument Field (34.342217, −87.338297) from
 August 19 to September 16, 2026. Hydronium ions were used to charge the VOC through a
 non-dissociative proton transfer in the reaction chamber of the instrument. PTR-ToF-MS can identify a
 wide range of compounds (e.g., carboxylic acids, carbonyls, and aromatic hydrocarbons) as long as the
 target compound has a proton affinity higher than that of water (691 kJ/mol)1 . The protonation occurs as
 follows:

The PTR-ToF-MS was deployed within the Aerosol Observing System (AOS) cabin adjacent to the ARM
instrumentation facility, where complementary gas-phase and aerosol measurements were simultaneously
conducted. The PTR-ToF-MS inlet was connected to the cabin flow line by replacing the existing Tygon
tubing with 1/2-inch-outer-diameter perfluoroalkoxy (PFA) tubing to minimize sampling artifacts and
compound losses. The instrument operated on an hourly measurement cycle consisting of 8 minutes of
zero-air measurements followed by 52 minutes of ambient air sampling. A camera system was integrated
into the setup to continuously monitor the zero-air cylinder level and ensure uninterrupted instrument
operation.

 Post-processing and peak detection analyses using IDA identified 1,676 ions and assigned 454 molecular
 formulas with mass accuracies below 30 ppm throughout the field campaign. Figure 1 presents the mean
 concentrations of representative AVOCs and BVOCs. Among these compounds, isoprene (C₅H₈) is
 particularly important because it is the dominant BVOC globally, accounting for nearly 50% of total
 global BVOC emissions.² Variability in isoprene concentrations strongly influences surface ozone
 production and aerosol growth through reactions with atmospheric oxidants, including hydroxyl radicals
 (OH), ozone (O₃), and nitrate radicals (NO₃).The average isoprene concentration measured at the BNF
 site was 0.20 ± 0.16 ppb, with daytime and nighttime averages of 0.24 ± 0.17 ppb and 0.14 ± 0.01 ppb,
 respectively. Maximum and minimum hourly concentrations reached 0.92 ppb and 0.02 ppb. The diurnal
 profile shown in Figure 1 reveals a pronounced isoprene peak near 18:00 and reduced nighttime
 concentrations, consistent with enhanced biogenic emissions driven by sunlight and temperature. The
 observed isoprene concentrations at BNF were substantially lower than those reported during previous
 intensive measurements conducted in a temperate forest in Missouri, where average concentrations

reported was 10.32 ppb.³ Figure 1 also includes the temporal profile of methyl vinyl ketone and
methacrolein (MVK + MACr), which exhibited an average concentration of 0.25 ± 0.14 ppb and a diurnal
pattern similar to that of isoprene. MVK and MACr are major daytime oxidation products of isoprene.
Their maximum and minimum concentrations were 0.78 ppb and 0.03 ppb, respectively. The observed
isoprene-to-(MVK + MACr) ratio of approximately 1.24 suggests a strong local contribution of isoprene
to the abundance of these oxidation products within the forest environment, although alternative sources
such as biomass burning and gasoline vehicle emissions may also contribute. Furthermore, ratios
exceeding 1.0 indicate that isoprene dispersion times are shorter than its atmospheric lifetime at BNF,
implying that local chemical reactivity exerts a stronger influence than atmospheric transport on observed
isoprene concentrations.⁴

Another important biogenic BVOC monitored at BNF was monoterpene (C₁₀H₁₆), a major precursor for
SOA formation in forested environments and an important contributor to tropospheric ozone
production.²⁻⁴ Monoterpenes represent a class of compounds that includes α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene,
δ-carene, ocimene, and sabinene, although these individual species cannot be differentiated using
PTR-ToF-MS measurements. The average monoterpene concentration measured at BNF was
0.53 ± 0.32 ppb, with maximum and minimum mixing ratios of 2.0 ppb and 0.11 ppb, respectively. The
diurnal profile exhibited clear daytime minima and elevated evening concentrations, with peak values
occurring near 18:00. Notably, isoprene and monoterpene concentrations at BNF were of comparable
magnitude, a condition that can substantially influence atmospheric oxidation pathways and aerosol
formation processes.⁵ The median isoprene-to-monoterpene ratio at BNF was approximately 0.17,
substantially lower than values previously reported in Missouri, where ratios reached 42.⁶ Ratios
exceeding 20 have been associated with suppression of new particle formation events, as observed in the
Missouri study. In contrast, the relatively low ratio observed at BNF suggests a chemical environment
where monoterpene-driven oxidation pathways may play a stronger role in aerosol production. Mixtures
of BVOCs from diverse biogenic sources can also generate chemically complex aerosols with distinct
hygroscopic and water uptake properties, which ultimately influence the variability and effectiveness of
CCN activity.⁷

In addition to biogenic VOCs, several gases associated with anthropogenic activities were also detected at
BNF. Figure 1 presents the concentrations and diurnal profiles of benzene and toluene, which exhibited
average concentrations of 0.14 ± 0.04 ppb and 0.15 ± 0.07 ppb, respectively. Compared with urban
environments, the BNF region experiences limited transportation and industrial activities, resulting in
relatively low concentrations of these AVOCs. Despite their low abundance, both benzene and toluene
exhibited enhanced nighttime concentrations. This behavior is likely associated with the reduction of the
nocturnal boundary layer, which limits atmospheric mixing and promotes the accumulation of long-lived
AVOCs near the surface. Figure 1 also illustrates the temporal variability of catechol, a known tracer for
biomass burning emissions. Catechol exhibited an average concentration of 0.11 ± 0.02 ppb, with no
significant concentration spikes observed throughout the field campaign. The absence of episodic
enhancements suggests that the BNF site was minimally influenced by biomass burning during the
measurement period. Overall, the observed VOC composition indicates that atmospheric chemistry at
BNF was predominantly governed by biogenic emissions, with comparatively minor contributions from
anthropogenic sources. The mixed distribution of BVOCs at BNF, where no single compound
overwhelmingly dominates the chemical environment, has important implications for the
physicochemical evolution of atmospheric aerosols. Interactions among coexisting BVOCs can alter
aerosol composition, oxidation pathways, hygroscopicity, and phase behavior, ultimately influencing the

 4

 efficiency of aerosols as CCN. These processes can directly affect cloud microphysical properties,
 including cloud formation, lifetime, and precipitation efficiency, with broader implications for the
 intensity, duration, and spatial variability of precipitation across the SE-US.
