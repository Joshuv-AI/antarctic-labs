One shot, two kills: an apical thrombus causing concomitant cerebrovascular stroke and MINOCA
Authors: Maria Milo, Marco Rella, Alessandra de Castro, Eluisa Muscogiuri, Edin Begic, Zorica Miladenovic, Elvis J Amao-Ruiz
Published: 2026

Summary

A newly formed apical thrombus in a patient with prior anterior myocardial infarction resulted in simultaneous cerebral embolism and myocardial injury. Multimodality imaging was essential in revealing the embolic mechanism and establishing a diagnosis of cardioembolic MINOCA.

Case Description

A 41-year-old man with a history of prior anterior myocardial infarction (MI), previously treated with primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and staged PCI of the left circumflex (LCx) and right coronary artery (RCA), was admitted for sudden left-sided paraesthesia.

Blood tests revealed marked myocardial injury (peak high-sensitivity troponin 21 931 ng/mL), and electrocardiogram showed ischaemic changes without chest pain (Figure A). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated chronic apical akinesia with a newly detected, round, homogeneous, sessile hyperechoic apical thrombus measuring 10 mm (Figure B).

(A) Electrocardiogram showing ischaemic ST-T changes at presentation. (B) Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrating a newly detected round, homogeneous, sessile apical thrombus (arrow). (C) Brain magnetic resonance imaging showing a large right occipito-mesial ischaemic stroke with ipsilateral thalamic involvement (arrow). (D–E) Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirming a residual left ventricular apical thrombus (arrows) adjacent to the area of chronic apical infarction. (F–G) Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating an acute basal posterior myocardial infarction with microvascular obstruction (arrows). (H) Coronary angiography showing chronic occlusion of the left anterior descending artery stent (arrow). (I) Coronary angiography demonstrating patency of the previously implanted left circumflex artery stent (arrow). (L) Coronary angiography demonstrating patency of the previously implanted right coronary artery stent (arrow).

Given the risk of haemorrhagic transformation while on triple antithrombotic therapy1—and in the absence of clinical instability—coronary angiography was deferred.

Initial head CT was unremarkable; however, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed the following day revealed a large right occipito-mesial infarction with ipsilateral thalamic involvement (Figure C). Anticoagulation with adjusted-dose low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated in agreement with neurology. Follow-up computed tomography (CT) at day 5 excluded haemorrhagic conversion.

Cardiac MRI (CMR) at day 4 confirmed a small residual apical thrombus (5 mm; Figure D–E). The reduction in thrombus size from 10 mm on echocardiography to 5 mm on CMR 4 days later is consistent with recent thrombus formation and early response to anticoagulation therapy. Nevertheless, inherent differences in spatial resolution and tissue characterization between the two modalities should be considered when interpreting this apparent change. CMR also demonstrated an acute basal posterior MI with microvascular obstruction (Figure F–G). Coronary angiography revealed chronic LAD stent occlusion (Figure H), with patency of the previously implanted stents in the hypoplastic LCx (Figure I) and in the posterolateral branch of the RCA (Figure L).

The overall findings were consistent with MINOCA, most likely of cardioembolic origin.2 Alternative mechanisms—including coronary spasm, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, and plaque disruption—were considered less likely given the microvascular obstruction on CMR, the absence of angiographic dissection, and the myocardial injury distribution remote from the occluded LAD. The patient was discharged with neurological improvement on aspirin and therapeutic LMWH, with transition to oral anticoagulation planned after 1-month CT reassessment.

This case emphasizes the importance of systematic embolic source evaluation in MINOCA, particularly when neurological and myocardial ischaemia coexist. The simultaneous multifocal presentation, thrombus evolution under anticoagulation, and multimodality imaging findings collectively support a cardioembolic aetiology, with direct implications for antithrombotic management. Management was further complicated by the competing risks of recurrent embolism and haemorrhagic transformation, highlighting the need for individualized, imaging-guided anticoagulation strategies.
