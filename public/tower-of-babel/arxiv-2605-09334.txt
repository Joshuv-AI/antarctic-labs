The Non-Symmetric Mahler Conjecture in Dimension Three
Shibing Chen, Yuanyuan Li, Dongmeng Xi, Zhe-Feng Xu

Abstract
. We prove the non-symmetric Mahler conjecture in dimension three. More precisely, we prove the sharp lower bound 𝒫 ⁡ ( K ) ≥ 64 9 \mathcal{P}(K)\geq\frac{64}{9} for every convex body K ⊂ ℝ 3 K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3} , where 𝒫 \mathcal{P} denotes the non-symmetric volume product with respect to the Santaló point.



1.
Introduction

The volume product is one of the basic affine invariants of a convex body. If

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex body and
z\in\operatorname{int}K
, we write

K^{z}
for the polar body taken with center
z
; see Section
2
for the precise definition. The Santaló point
s(K)
is
characterized by minimizing
z\mapsto|K^{z}|
; see
[
21
]
, and the non-symmetric volume
product is

\mathcal{P}(K)=|K|\,|K^{s(K)}|.

The Blaschke–Santaló inequality gives the sharp upper bound for this
quantity, with ellipsoids as the extremal bodies. The reverse problem is
Mahler’s lower-bound problem. In its non-symmetric form it predicts

\mathcal{P}(K)\geq\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{n})=\frac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{(n!)^{2}},

with simplices as the expected minimizers. Thus the conjectured value in
dimension three is

\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{3})=\frac{64}{9}.

The purpose of this paper is to prove this three-dimensional non-symmetric
case.

The volume product has long been studied in affine geometry. Besides the
classical Blaschke–Santaló inequality, related polar-volume inequalities of
Lutwak–Zhang and Lutwak–Yang–Zhang provide part of the modern background for
Mahler-type problems
[
11
,
12
]
. These works illustrate the
same general theme: sharp affine inequalities for polar bodies, with extremal
bodies depending on the symmetry and on the direction of the inequality.

Let us recall some of the known progress on the centrally symmetric Mahler conjecture.
For an origin-symmetric body
K
, the conjecture reads

\mathcal{P}(K)=|K|\,|K^{\circ}|\geq\frac{4^{n}}{n!},

with equality expected for Hanner polytopes, including the cube and the cross-polytope. Mahler
[
13
]
proved the planar case with the case of equality proved by Reisner
[
18
]
. Important special classes
in higher dimensions were settled earlier: Reisner proved the conjecture for
zonoids
[
18
]
, Gordon–Meyer–Reisner gave a shorter proof for
that class
[
7
]
, and Saint-Raymond proved the unconditional case

[
20
]
(see
[
15
]
for a
short proof). The Bourgain–Milman reverse Santaló inequality
showed that the symmetric conjecture is true up to an absolute exponential
factor
[
2
]
; Kuperberg later improved the constant by a
different method involving Gauss linking integrals
[
10
]
. In
dimension three the full centrally symmetric conjecture, including the equality
case, was proved by Iriyeh–Shibata
[
8
]
. A streamlined proof, based on the Iriyeh–Shibata approach and emphasizing
the equipartitions, was subsequently given by
Fradelizi–Hubard–Meyer–Roldán-Pensado–Zvavitch
[
4
]
.

The non-symmetric problem has a somewhat different character from the
centrally symmetric one. The planar non-symmetric inequality goes back to Mahler, with the equality
case due to Meyer
[
16
]
; see the survey
[
6
]
for references and further history.

The shadow-system method goes back to Rogers and Shephard

[
19
]
. For the volume product, Campi and
Gronchi
[
3
]
established a shadow-system theorem in
the centrally symmetric setting. Meyer and Reisner
[
17
]
established a non-symmetric shadow-system theorem that is very useful in the present paper: for a shadow system
K_{t}
, the reciprocal of
the volume of the Santaló polar is convex in
t
, and the equality case is
rigid under appropriate hypotheses. Their method proves the sharp
inequality for polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with at most
n+3
vertices. In
dimension three, Fradelizi–Meyer–Zvavitch
[
5
]
used this shadow-system approach to
prove Mahler’s bound for bodies that are convex hulls of two planar convex
bodies. The simplex is also known to be a strict local minimizer
in the Banach–Mazur space, by the theorem of Kim–Reisner
[
9
]
.

In the non-symmetric case, shadow systems have usually been used in a very
local way. The standard argument is to move only one object at a time: one
vertex, one generating point, or one slice. One then tries to show that, under
this motion, the volume changes affinely while the volume of the polar changes
convexly. This is a useful method, but it has a serious limitation. For a
general three-dimensional polytope, moving a single vertex may destroy existing
facets, create new ones, or change the
combinatorial structure of the polytope. Once this happens, the usual affine
formula for the volume is no longer available. This is the main difficulty in
adapting the existing local shadow-system arguments to a global proof.

In the present paper, we verify the three-dimensional non-symmetric Mahler conjecture. Our main result reads as follows.

Main Result.

Every convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfies

\mathcal{P}(K)\geq\frac{64}{9}.

If
K
is a polytope and attains the minimum, then
K
must be a tetrahedron (three dimensional simplex).

The first new point of the present paper is the introduction of
“admissible speeds” and “admissible shadow systems”, which allow us to
pass from one-point motions to coordinated shadow movements of vertices that
preserve the face lattice, and hence to treat general convex polytopes. Fixing a direction
\theta
,
we assign a speed vector to all the vertices. The speeds are not arbitrary: on each facet
whose plane is not parallel to
\theta
, the speed vector is required to be
the restriction of an affine function on that facet plane; on a facet parallel
to
\theta
no condition is needed. This condition is exactly what is required
to keep every old facet planar during the deformation. After a short-time
stability argument in Section
3
, the old facets remain supporting, no new facets occur, and
the face correspondence is unchanged. Consequently, the volume of the moving polytope is affine along the so-called admissible shadow system.

Once such deformations (shadow systems) are constructed so that the face
lattice is preserved, the proof has two main parts.
First, the equality case in the Meyer–Reisner shadow-system theorem rules out any non-trivial
admissible shadow system at an interior local minimizer: if the volume
product is locally minimized along such a deformation, then the vertex speeds must
be globally affine. Second, a purely three-dimensional counting
argument shows that a non-tetrahedral polytope cannot avoid these deformations on both sides of polarity. More precisely, either the polytope itself admits a
non-trivial admissible movement, or its Santaló polar does. The
counting argument uses Euler’s formula, the maximum number of vertices of a facet, and
the maximum degree of a vertex. This is where the simultaneous nature of the new deformations is essential:
by moving many vertices at once, the dimension of the space of admissible
speeds becomes large enough to force a non-trivial admissible speed, namely one
which is not given by a single affine function, unless the polytope is already
a tetrahedron.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows.
Section
2
sets up the notations and recalls the basic polarity, compactness, and shadow-system tools. Section
3

defines admissible speeds and proves the local stability and volume-affineness
of the associated multi-vertex shadow systems. Section
4

combines these deformations with the Meyer–Reisner theorem and with Santaló
polarity to show that a minimizer in a bounded vertex class cannot admit a
non-trivial admissible shadow system, nor can its Santaló polar. Section
5

proves a three-dimensional Euler-type inequality used in the counting argument. Finally, Section
6
completes the proof of the
three-dimensional Mahler conjecture.

2.
Basic notations and tools

Throughout,
B^{n}_{2}
denotes the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By a polytope, we always mean a convex polytope. By
\aff A
,
\lin A
,
\conv A
, we mean the affine hull, linear span, and convex hull of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The dimension of a set is defined as the dimension of its affine hull. We shall also use the standard notions of vertices (or extreme points),
faces, and facets of a convex set; see Schneider
[
22
]
for a
general reference.

For a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a point
z\in\operatorname{int}K
, define the
polar body
with center
z
by

K^{z}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(y-z)\cdot(x-z)\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in K\}.

Equivalently,
K^{z}=(K-z)^{\circ}+z
, where
(\cdot)^{\circ}
is the usual polar with respect to the origin. The bipolar identity gives

(K^{z})^{z}=K.

Let
s(K)
be the Santaló point of
K
, namely the unique point of
\operatorname{int}K
minimizing
z\mapsto|K^{z}|
; see
[
21
]
.
The volume product
\mathcal{P}
is invariant under affine isomorphisms; see, for example,
[
5
, Section 1]
. The simplex value in dimension three is

\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{3})=\frac{(3+1)^{3+1}}{(3!)^{2}}=\frac{64}{9}.

We record some basic properties of volume product and polar bodies.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathcal{P}(K^{s(K)})\leq\mathcal{P}(K).

Indeed, if we denote
K^{*}=K^{s(K)}
, then

\mathcal{P}(K^{s(K)})=|K^{*}||(K^{*})^{s(K^{*})}|\leq|K^{*}||(K^{s(K)})^{s(K)}|=|K||K^{*}|=\mathcal{P}(K).

Lemma 2.2
.

[
1
, Theorem 1.3]

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a full-dimensional polytope and let
z\in\operatorname{int}P
. Then
P^{z}
is a full-dimensional polytope and polarity with center
z,
and

V(P^{z})=F(P),\qquad F(P^{z})=V(P).

The following lemma follows from the affine invariance property of volume product, and the basic fact that the
number of vertices of the limit polytope is no more than the limsup of the number of vertices of the convergent sequence of polytopes.

Lemma 2.3

(Compactness in a bounded vertex class)
.

Fix
N\geq 4
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{N}
be the class of all full-dimensional convex polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with at most
N
vertices. Then
\mathcal{P}
attains its infimum on
\mathcal{C}_{N}
.

Proof.

Let
(P_{m})\subset\mathcal{C}_{N}
be a minimizing sequence. By affine invariance of
\mathcal{P}
, we may assume
each
P_{m}
is in John position after performing an affine transformation. Thus,
by John’s lemma we have

B^{n}_{2}\subset P_{m}\subset 3B^{n}_{2}.

Write

P_{m}=\conv\{x_{m,1},\ldots,x_{m,N}\},

allowing repetitions when
P_{m}
has fewer than
N
vertices. Passing to a subsequence we may assume
x_{m,i}\to x_{i}
for every
i
. Set

P=\conv\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}.

The inclusions pass to the Hausdorff limit, so
B^{n}_{2}\subset P
and
P
is full-dimensional. Also
P\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Hausdorff-continuity of the volume product
[
5
, Lemma 3]
then gives

\mathcal{P}(P)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(P_{m}),

so
P
is a minimizer.
∎

A
shadow system
along a unit direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, as introduced by Rogers and Shephard
[
19
]
, is a family

L_{t}=\conv\{x+t\alpha(x)\theta:x\in B\},

where the
base set

B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded, the function
\alpha:B\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded, and
t
ranges over an interval. It is non-degenerate if every
L_{t}
has non-empty interior. The following results are very important to our proof, which is the combination of Theorem 1 together with
Proposition 7 and their consequences in
[
17
]
. One can also refer to the complete statement in
[
5
, Proposition 1]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
17
]

Let
(L_{t})_{t\in[-a,a]}
be a non-degenerate shadow system in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
along a direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Then

t\longmapsto|L_{t}^{s(L_{t})}|^{-1}

is convex on
[-a,a]
. If, in addition,
t\mapsto|L_{t}|
is affine and
t\mapsto\mathcal{P}(L_{t})
is constant on
[-a,a]
, then there exist
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
t\in[-a,a]
, one has
L_{t}=A_{t}(L_{0})
, where
A_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the affine map defined by

A_{t}(x)=x+t(w\cdot x+\beta)\theta.

The following corollary is a variation of the above Theorem that we shall need, which is stated and proved in
[
5
, Corollary 2]
.

Corollary 2.5
.

[
5
]

Let
(L_{t})_{t\in[-a,a]}
be a non-degenerate shadow system in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
along a direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and suppose that
t\mapsto|L_{t}|
is affine. If

\mathcal{P}(L_{0})=\min_{t\in[-a,a]}\mathcal{P}(L_{t}),

then
\mathcal{P}(L_{t})
is constant on
[0,a]
or constant on
[-a,0]
.

For completeness, we include a short verification of Corollary
2.5

from Theorem
2.4
. In fact, the same argument gives the
following slightly stronger conclusion: under the assumptions of
Corollary
2.5
, the volume product is constant throughout the
whole interval
[-a,a]
. We record this as the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

Under the same assumptions as in Corollary
2.5
, the quantity

\mathcal{P}(L_{t})
is constant on
[-a,a]
.

A verification of Corollary
2.5
via Theorem
2.4
.

Denote
f(t)=|L_{t}|
,
g(t)=|L_{t}^{s(L_{t})}|
, and
h(t)=1/g(t)
, for
t\in[-a,a]
.
By Theorem
2.4
,
h
is convex. By the assumption,
f,g,h>0
, the function
f
is affine, and

\frac{f(t)}{h(t)}\geq\frac{f(0)}{h(0)},\qquad\forall t\in[-a,a].

Then,

D(t)=h(0)f(t)-f(0)h(t)\geq 0,\qquad\forall t\in[-a,a].

Note that
D(t)
is concave, since it is the difference between an affine function and a convex function. A nonnegative concave function on
[-a,a]
vanishes at
t=0
if and only if it vanishes identically. Therefore
D\equiv 0
, which implies that
\mathcal{P}(L_{t})
is a constant on
[-a,a]
.
∎

3.
Shadow systems with admissible speeds

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a fixed three-dimensional polytope with
V

distinct vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{V}
.
We first introduce our key notion, that of an
admissible speed. An

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{V}

will be called a
speed vector
. Given a direction

\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and a speed vector

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, define a shadow system

x_{i}(t)=x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta,\qquad P_{t}=\conv\{x_{i}(t):1\leq i\leq V\},\quad t\in[-c,c],

where
c>0
.
In the following, for a facet
F
of
P
,
\lin(F-F)
denotes the two-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
parallel to
F.

(We may use the convention
\conv(\varnothing)=\varnothing
for completeness.)

Definition 3.1

(Admissible speed)
.

A speed vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is called
\theta
-admissible
if the following condition holds for every facet
F
of
P
.

(1)

If
\theta\notin\lin(F-F)
, then there exists an affine function
\ell_{F}:\aff(F)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\ell_{F}(x_{i})=\alpha_{i}
for every vertex
x_{i}
of
F
.

(2)

If
\theta\in\lin(F-F)
, no constraint is imposed on the values
\alpha_{i}
on the vertices of
F
.

In the parallel case, each moved vertex of
F
stays in the original facet plane because all displacements are in the two-dimensional vector space
\lin(F-F)
; this is why no planarity constraint is needed for such a facet.
When the direction
\theta
is fixed or clear from the context, we simply say that
\alpha
is
admissible
.

It is simple to see that the set of all
\theta
-admissible speeds forms a linear space, denoted by
A_{\theta}(P)
,
and contains the following globally affine speeds as a subspace.

Definition 3.2

(Trivial speeds, or globally affine speeds)
.

A speed vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is called
trivial
, or
globally affine
, if there exist
w\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\alpha_{i}=w\cdot x_{i}+\beta\qquad(1\leq i\leq V).

Such speeds are exactly the infinitesimal speeds on the vertex set produced by affine shears

A_{t}(x)=x+t(w\cdot x+\beta)\theta.

Every globally affine speed is
\theta
-admissible for every direction
\theta
: on a non-parallel facet one restricts the affine function
x\mapsto w\cdot x+\beta
to the facet plane, while on a parallel facet there is no condition. The space of globally affine speeds has dimension exactly
4
. Indeed, the space of affine functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has dimension
4
, and evaluation on the vertex set is injective because a full-dimensional polytope has vertices that affinely span
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Remark 3.3

(Non-trivial speeds)
.

A
non-trivial
admissible speed means that it is not globally affine.
The Lemma
4.1
shows that, at an interior minimum of
\mathcal{P}
along a volume-affine shadow system, admissibility forces the speed to be globally affine. Therefore, any admissible speed lying outside the four-dimensional globally affine subspace gives a contradiction to local minimality.

The main goal of this section is to establish Lemmas

3.7
,
3.8
and Proposition
3.9
, for shadow systems
with admissible speeds. Since the first lemma is rather intuitive, we
introduce the following definitions to make the argument clear.
Throughout, we denote the vertices, edges, and facets of the three-dimensional polytope
P
by

\mathcal{V}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{V}\},\qquad\mathcal{E}=\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{n_{1}}\},\qquad\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{n_{2}}\}.

Let

\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{1,\ldots,V\},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{1,\ldots,n_{1}\},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{1,\ldots,n_{2}\}

be the corresponding sets of labels.

Definition 3.4

(Face lattice)
.

The
face lattice
(or labeled face lattice) of
P
,
denoted by
\mathcal{S}(P)
, is encoded by the index sets

\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}

together with the set-valued incidence maps

\mathcal{I}_{0}\ \xrightarrow{\ \Phi_{1}\ }\ 2^{\mathcal{I}_{1}},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{1}\ \xrightarrow{\ \Phi_{2}\ }\ 2^{\mathcal{I}_{2}},

where

\Phi_{1}(i)=\{j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}:x_{i}\in E_{j}\},\qquad\Phi_{2}(j)=\{k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}:E_{j}\subset F_{k}\}.

The above notations
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
induce the following set-valued incidence map

\Phi_{0}(k)=\left\{i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}:\text{ there exists }j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\text{ such that }j\in\Phi_{1}(i)\text{ and }k\in\Phi_{2}(j)\right\}.

Then, the vertex-facet incidence reads

x_{i}\in F_{k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\in\Phi_{0}(k).

Definition 3.5

(Persistence of the face lattice)
.

We say that a family
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in[-c,c]}
of polytopes with
P_{0}=P

preserves the face lattice
if

\mathcal{S}(P_{t})=\mathcal{S}(P)\qquad{\rm for~all~}t.

More precisely,
\mathcal{S}(P_{t})=\mathcal{S}(P)
means that the
vertices, edges, and facets of
P_{t}
can be labeled as

\{x_{i}(t):i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}\},\qquad\{E_{j}(t):j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\},\qquad\{F_{k}(t):k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\},

where
x_{i}(0)=x_{i}
,
E_{j}(0)=E_{j}
, and
F_{k}(0)=F_{k}
, such that

x_{i}(t)\in E_{j}(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{i}\in E_{j}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad j\in\Phi_{1}(i),

and

E_{j}(t)\subset F_{k}(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_{j}\subset F_{k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k\in\Phi_{2}(j),

for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
,
j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
, and

k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
.

Remark 3.6
.

Our labeled face lattice is just the usual face lattice, i.e. the
inclusion poset of all faces, with fixed labels attached to the faces.
Usually, saying that two polytopes have the same face lattice means that
there is an inclusion-preserving isomorphism between their face lattices.
Here the labels play the role of this isomorphism: faces with the same
label are identified, and the incidence relations among the labeled faces are required to be the same.

Next, we introduce a lemma showing the shadow system
P_{t}
with an admissible speed preserves the face lattice.

Lemma 3.7

(Persistence of the face lattice)
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a convex polytope with vertices

x_{1},\ldots,x_{V}
. Put

x_{i}(t)=x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta,\qquad P_{t}=\operatorname{conv}\{x_{i}(t):1\leq i\leq V\}.

Assume that the speed vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is
\theta
-admissible. Then, there exists
c>0
such that

\{P_{t}\}_{t\in[-c,c]}
is a continuous shadow system that preserves the face lattice.

Proof.

Let
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
be any facet of
P
,
k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
. We shall make use of the notations
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
in the definition of face lattice, and the induced vertex-facet incidence map
\Phi_{0}
.
Recall that
i\in\Phi_{0}(k)
means that
x_{i}
is a vertex of
F_{k}
.

Since
\alpha
is
\theta
-admissible, the moved points

x_{i}(t),\qquad i\in\Phi_{0}(k),

remain contained in a single plane, which is denoted by
\Pi_{F_{k}}(t)
.
In fact, if Case (1) in Definition
3.1
holds, after extending
\ell_{F_{k}}
to an affine function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, there are
w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

x_{i}(t)=x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta=x_{i}+t(w_{k}\cdot x_{i}+\beta_{k})\theta,\qquad i\in\Phi_{0}(k);

if Case (2) in Definition
3.1
holds, then
\Pi_{F_{k}}(t)=\aff F_{k}
for all
t
, and
x_{i}(t)=x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta

keeps in the same plane
\aff F_{k}
.

Let
v_{k}
be the unit outer normal vector of
P
corresponding to the
facet
F_{k}
. In Case (1), set

v_{k}(t)=(1+t\,\theta\cdot w_{k})v_{k}-t(\theta\cdot v_{k})w_{k};

in Case (2), set

v_{k}(t)=v_{k}.

Then a direct computation gives

(1)

x_{i}(t)\cdot v_{k}(t)=h_{P}(v_{k})+t((\theta\cdot w_{k})h_{P}(v_{k})+(\theta\cdot v_{k})\beta_{k}),\qquad\forall i\in\Phi_{0}(k),

which is independent of
i
.

First, for sufficiently small
|t|
,

x_{i}(t)
still cannot be expressed as a convex combination of the remaining points. Hence, the points
x_{i}(t)
remain extreme points (vertices) of
P_{t}
. Second, since

x_{l}\cdot v_{k}<h_{P}(v_{k})
for all
l\notin\Phi_{0}(k),

by the continuity of
x_{i}(t)
,
x_{l}(t)
and
v_{k}(t)
, and by
(
1
), we have, for sufficiently small
|t|
,

x_{l}(t)\cdot v_{k}(t)<x_{i}(t)\cdot v_{k}(t),\qquad i\in\Phi_{0}(k),\quad l\notin\Phi_{0}(k).

Hence, for every pair
(i,k)
with
i\in\Phi_{0}(k)
, we have

(2)

x_{i}(t)\cdot v_{k}(t)=h_{k}(t),

where
h_{k}(t)=h_{P_{t}}(v_{k}(t))
equals the right-hand side of (
1
).
Thus

F_{k}(t)=\conv\{x_{i}(t):i\in\Phi_{0}(k)\}

is still a facet of
P_{t}
.
Third, we consider the one-dimensional faces (edges) of
P_{t}
. Suppose
E_{j}=F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{2}}=\conv\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}}\}
with
\{k_{1},k_{2}\}=\Phi_{2}(j)
and
j\in\Phi_{1}(i_{1})\cap\Phi_{1}(i_{2}).
Then, by the conclusions above,

x_{i_{1}}(t)\cdot v_{k_{s}}(t)=x_{i_{2}}(t)\cdot v_{k_{s}}(t)=h_{P_{t}}(v_{k_{s}}(t)),\qquad s=1,2,

which means that
E_{j}(t)=F_{k_{1}}(t)\cap F_{k_{2}}(t)=\conv\{x_{i_{1}}(t),x_{i_{2}}(t)\}
is an edge of
P_{t}
.

It remains to show that no additional faces occur.
Define

Q_{t}=\bigcap_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x\cdot v_{k}(t)\leq h_{k}(t)\}.

By (
2
) and the inequality in the preceding line,

P_{t}\subset Q_{t},

and

x_{i}(t)\cdot v_{k}(t)=h_{k}(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\in\Phi_{0}(k).

We claim that
Q_{t}=P_{t}
for all sufficiently small
|t|
. Since
Q_{0}=P
is bounded,
the outer facet normals of
P
positively span
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. By continuity, the same is
true for the vectors
v_{k}(t)
for small
|t|
. Since the numbers
h_{k}(t)
are also
uniformly bounded, the polytopes
Q_{t}
are uniformly bounded.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
Q_{t}\neq P_{t}
for arbitrarily small
t
. Then there are

t_{n}\to 0
and a vertex
y_{n}
of
Q_{t_{n}}
such that

y_{n}\notin P_{t_{n}}.

By uniform boundedness, after passing to a subsequence,

y_{n}\to y\in Q_{0}=P.

At the vertex
y_{n}
of
Q_{t_{n}}
, choose a triple

k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
such that

v_{k_{1}}(t_{n}),\ v_{k_{2}}(t_{n}),\ v_{k_{3}}(t_{n})

are the normals of facets containing
y_{n}
, and are linearly independent. Since there are only finitely many triples, we may pass to a
subsequence and assume that the same triple
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
works for every
n
. Then

y_{n}\cdot v_{k_{r}}(t_{n})=h_{k_{r}}(t_{n}),\qquad r=1,2,3.

Letting
n\to\infty
, we get

y\in F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{2}}\cap F_{k_{3}}.

This intersection is a non-empty face of
P
. Since
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
are distinct and every
edge of a three-dimensional polytope is incident to exactly two facets, this face must be a vertex, say

F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{2}}\cap F_{k_{3}}=\{x_{i}\}.

Thus
i\in\Phi_{0}(k_{r})
for
r=1,2,3
, and therefore

x_{i}(t_{n})\cdot v_{k_{r}}(t_{n})=h_{k_{r}}(t_{n}),\qquad r=1,2,3.

Both
y_{n}
and
x_{i}(t_{n})
lie in the three affine planes indexed by

k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
. Since their normals are linearly independent, these three planes have a
unique intersection point. Hence

y_{n}=x_{i}(t_{n})\in P_{t_{n}},

contradicting the choice of
y_{n}
.

Therefore
Q_{t}=P_{t}
for all sufficiently small
|t|
. Thus, every facet of

P_{t}
is one of the facets
F_{k}(t)
. Since every zero- or one-dimensional face of
P_{t}
is a face of some facet, no new faces occur. By the above discussion, the labeled face lattice is preserved.
∎

A byproduct of the above lemma is that the faces

E_{j}(t)=\conv\{x_{i}(t):j\in\Phi_{1}(i)\},\quad F_{k}(t)=\conv\{x_{i}(t):i\in\Phi_{0}(k)\},\qquad j\in\mathcal{I}_{1},\quad k\in\mathcal{I}_{2},

depend continuously on
t
.

The following lemma shows that the volume of
P_{t}
is affine in
t
once the face lattice is preserved.

Lemma 3.8
.

If there exists
c>0
such that the family of shadow system

P_{t}=\conv\{x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta:1\leq i\leq V\}

preserves the face lattice, for all
t\in[-c,c]
, then

t\longmapsto|P_{t}|

is affine on
[-c,c]
.

Proof.

For each facet
F
of
P
, choose a triangulation using only vertices of
P
. Let
\mathcal{T}
denote
the collection of all vertex-index triples

(i,j,k)

which occur as triangles in these triangulations of the facets of
P
.
Since the labeled face lattice is preserved, the same collection

\mathcal{T}
gives triangulations of the corresponding facets of

P_{t}
.

Denote

p(t)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{i=1}^{V}x_{i}(t).

This point belongs to
\operatorname{int}P_{t}
. Indeed, every facet of
P_{t}
misses at least one vertex,
and
p(t)
is a convex combination of all vertices with positive coefficients. Hence

p(t)\cdot v_{l}(t)<h_{P_{t}}(v_{l}(t)),

for every outer normal
v_{l}(t)
of
P_{t}
.

Consider the tetrahedra obtained by joining
p(t)
to the triangles in
the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
:

\conv\,\{p(t),x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t),x_{k}(t)\},\qquad(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{T}.

These tetrahedra
cover
P_{t}
and have disjoint interiors. Therefore

|P_{t}|=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{T}}\left|\det\bigl(x_{i}(t)-p(t),x_{j}(t)-p(t),x_{k}(t)-p(t)\bigr)\right|.

Put

\bar{\alpha}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{r=1}^{V}\alpha_{r}.

Then

p(t)=p(0)+t\bar{\alpha}\theta,

and hence

x_{i}(t)-p(t)=x_{i}-p(0)+t(\alpha_{i}-\bar{\alpha})\theta.

Thus each column in the determinant has a constant part plus a multiple of the fixed
direction
\theta
.

For each index triple
(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{T}
, define

D_{ijk}(t)=\det\bigl(x_{i}(t)-p(t),x_{j}(t)-p(t),x_{k}(t)-p(t)\bigr).

By multilinearity of the determinant,
D_{ijk}(t)
is a polynomial in
t
. Every term
of degree at least two contains at least two columns parallel to
\theta
, and therefore
vanishes. Hence
D_{ijk}(t)
is affine in
t
.

Moreover,
D_{ijk}(t)\neq 0
for every
t\in I:=[-c,c]
, because
p(t)
is an interior point
of
P_{t}
and the triangle
\conv\{x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t),x_{k}(t)\}
lies in a boundary facet.
Since
I
is connected, the sign of
D_{ijk}(t)
is constant on
I
. Therefore

|D_{ijk}(t)|

is also affine in
t
. Summing over the fixed finite set
\mathcal{T}
, we conclude
that
t\mapsto|P_{t}|
is affine on
I
.
∎

The following lemma shows that the shadow systems with admissible speeds are in fact volume-affine.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a three-dimensional convex polytope. If
\alpha
is
\theta
-admissible, then there is
c>0
such that
(P_{t})_{t\in[-c,c]}
is a non-degenerate shadow system, has the same face lattice as
P
, and satisfies that
t\mapsto|P_{t}|
is affine on
[-c,c]
.

Proof.

Note that

P_{t}=\conv\{x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta:1\leq i\leq V\}

is a shadow system with finite basis
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{V}\}
. Lemma
3.7
gives preservation of the face lattice after shrinking the interval; in particular the bodies remain full-dimensional, so the shadow system is non-degenerate. Lemma
3.8
gives the affine dependence of the volume.
∎

4.
Minimizers admit only trivial admissible shadow systems

For convenience, we call
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in[-c,c]}
an

admissible shadow system
along the direction

\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
if the speed vector

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is admissible. An admissible shadow system, with its underlying direction
\theta

understood, is called non-trivial or trivial, if its speed vector is non-trivial or trivial, respectively.

The main purpose of this section is to establish
Lemma
4.2
;
for this, we first need the following local version.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

P_{t}=\conv\{x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta:1\leq i\leq V\},\qquad t\in[-c,c],

be an admissible shadow system, where
c>0
is sufficiently small so that Proposition
3.9
applies. If

\mathcal{P}(P_{0})=\min_{t\in[-c,c]}\mathcal{P}(P_{t}),

then
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is globally affine.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.9
, the family

\{P_{t}\}_{t\in[-c,c]}
is a non-degenerate volume-affine shaf jdow system, and

P_{t}
preserves the face lattice for all
t\in[-c,c]
. Since

\mathcal{P}(P_{0})=\min_{t\in[-c,c]}\mathcal{P}(P_{t}),

Lemma
2.6
implies that
\mathcal{P}(P_{t})
is constant on
[-c,c]
.
Then, by Theorem
2.4
, there exists an affine map

A_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the form

A_{t}(x)=x+t(w\cdot x+\beta)\theta

such that

P_{t}=A_{t}(P_{0}).

By the continuity in
t
of both
A_{t}
and the vertices
x_{i}(t)=x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta
, we must have

A_{t}(x_{i})=x_{i}(t),

when
|t|>0
is sufficiently small.
Hence the speed vector
(w\cdot x_{i}+\beta)_{i\in V}
is globally affine.

∎

By Lemma
2.3
,
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
attains its infimum in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
.
The following lemma shows that
a minimizer of
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{C}_{N}
can admit only trivial admissible shadow system.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
N\geq 4
, and let
Q\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
minimizes
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. Then neither
Q
nor its Santaló polar
Q^{s(Q)}
admits a non-trivial admissible shadow system along any direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof.

First suppose that
Q
admits such a shadow system
Q_{t}
along some direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. By Proposition
3.9
, after shrinking the interval the face lattice is preserved. Hence
Q_{t}
has the same number of vertices as
Q
, and
Q_{t}\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
for small
|t|
. By minimality,

\mathcal{P}(Q_{t})\geq\mathcal{P}(Q)=\mathcal{P}(Q_{0}).

Thus
Q_{0}
is an interior minimum along the shadow system. Lemma
4.1
forces the speed to be globally affine, contradicting non-triviality.

Now put
L=Q^{s(Q)}
, and suppose that
L
admits a non-trivial admissible shadow system
L_{t}
. Again shrink the interval so that Proposition
3.9
applies. We claim that
L_{0}
is an interior minimum along the admissible shadow system
L_{t}.

By Lemma
2.2
, polarity with respect to an interior point, in particular with respect to the Santaló point, reverses the face lattice.
Since the face lattice of
L_{t}
is preserved for sufficiently small
t
, we have

V(L_{t}^{s(L_{t})})=F(L_{t})=F(L_{0})=V(Q)\leq N,

and hence

(3)

L_{t}^{s(L_{t})}\in\mathcal{C}_{N}\qquad\text{for all small }t.

Since
Q\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
minimizes
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{C}_{N}
,

(4)

\mathcal{P}(Q)\leq\mathcal{P}(L_{t}^{s(L_{t})}).

By Lemma
2.1
, applied to
L_{t}
, we have

(5)

\mathcal{P}(L_{t}^{s(L_{t})})\leq\mathcal{P}(L_{t}).

Recalling that
L_{0}=Q^{s(Q)}
, by (
4
) and (
5
), and Lemma
2.1
applied to
Q
, we obtain

(6)

\mathcal{P}(L_{0})\leq\mathcal{P}(Q)\leq\mathcal{P}(L_{t}).

Thus
L_{0}
is an interior minimum along the admissible shadow system
L_{t}
. Lemma
4.1
forces the speed of
L_{t}
to be globally affine, again contradicting non-triviality.
∎

5.
An Euler-type inequality for three-dimensional polytopes

Let
P
be a three-dimensional polytope. In this section, let

V=V(P),\qquad E=E(P),\qquad F=F(P)

denote the numbers of vertices, edges, and facets.
Let

\Delta(P)=\max\{\#\Phi_{0}(k):\,k\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\},

denote the maximum number of vertices of a facet of
P.

Let

d(P)=\max\{\#\Phi_{1}(i):\,i\in\mathcal{I}_{0}\}.

In dimension three,
\#\Phi_{1}(i)
is also the number of facets incident to
x_{i}
, because the vertex figure at
x_{i}
is a polygon whose edges correspond to incident facets.

The well-known Euler’s formula reads

V+F-E=2.

We shall derive the following Euler-type inequality, which relates the
dimension of the linear space of admissible speeds to the quantities

V
,
F
, and
\Delta(P)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
P
be a three-dimensional convex polytope. Choose a facet
G_{0}

with
\Delta(P)
vertices, and choose a non-zero direction

\theta\in\lin(G_{0}-G_{0}).

Let
A_{\theta}(P)
be the vector space of
\theta
-admissible speeds. Then

(7)

\dim A_{\theta}(P)\geq F(P)-V(P)+\Delta(P)+1.

Moreover, if

(8)

\Delta(P)>V(P)-F(P)+3,

then
P
admits a non-trivial admissible shadow system.

Proof.

Write

V=V(P),\qquad E=E(P),\qquad F=F(P).

Let the vertices of
P
be
x_{1},\ldots,x_{V}
. For a facet
G
, let

I(G)=\{i:x_{i}\in G\}=\mathcal{V}\cap G,\qquad m(G)=\#I(G).

Thus
m(G)
is the number of vertices of
G
.

We first describe explicitly the linear conditions imposed by one facet. Let

\mathcal{A}(\aff G)

denote the vector space of real-valued affine functions on the affine plane

\aff G
. Since
\aff G
is two-dimensional,

\dim\mathcal{A}(\aff G)=3.

Consider the evaluation map

\operatorname{ev}_{G}:\mathcal{A}(\aff G)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m(G)},\qquad\ell\longmapsto\bigl(\ell(x_{i})\bigr)_{i\in I(G)}.

This map is injective. Indeed, if an affine function on
\aff G
vanishes at
all vertices of
G
, then in particular it vanishes at three non-collinear
vertices of
G
. An affine function on a plane which vanishes at three
non-collinear points is identically zero. Therefore

\dim\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_{G})=3.

Hence the subspace

\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_{G})\subset\mathbb{R}^{m(G)}

has codimension

m(G)-3.

Now let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
be an admissible
speed vector. Its restriction to the vertices of
G
is

\alpha|_{G}=(\alpha_{i})_{i\in I(G)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m(G)}.

If

\theta\notin\lin(G-G),

then the definition of
\theta
-admissibility says exactly that there exists
an affine function
\ell_{G}\in\mathcal{A}(\aff G)
such that

\ell_{G}(x_{i})=\alpha_{i},\qquad i\in I(G).

Equivalently,

\alpha|_{G}\in\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_{G}).

If instead

\theta\in\lin(G-G),

then
G
imposes no constraint by the definition of admissibility.

Let

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\{G\in\mathcal{F}:\theta\notin\lin(G-G)\}

be the set of facets not parallel to
\theta
. Define the linear map

T:\mathbb{R}^{V}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}}\mathbb{R}^{m(G)}/\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_{G})

by

T(\alpha)=\bigl(\alpha|_{G}+\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_{G})\bigr)_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}}.

By construction,

A_{\theta}(P)=\ker T.

Therefore, by rank-nullity,

\dim A_{\theta}(P)=V-\operatorname{rank}T.

Since the rank of
T
is at most the dimension of its target space,

\operatorname{rank}T\leq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}}\bigl(m(G)-3\bigr).

Consequently,

(9)

\dim A_{\theta}(P)\geq V-\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}}\bigl(m(G)-3\bigr).

It remains to estimate the sum in (
9
). Since

\theta\in\lin(G_{0}-G_{0}),

the chosen facet
G_{0}
is parallel to
\theta
, and hence
G_{0}\notin\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
. Since
m(G_{0})=\Delta(P)
, we get

(10)

\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}}\bigl(m(G)-3\bigr)\leq\sum_{G\text{ facet of }P}\bigl(m(G)-3\bigr)-\bigl(\Delta(P)-3\bigr).

Now compute the full facet sum. Each facet
G
is a polygon with
m(G)

vertices and
m(G)
edges. Therefore

\sum_{G\text{ facet of }P}m(G)=2E,

because every edge of a three-dimensional polytope belongs to exactly two
facets. Hence

(11)

\sum_{G\text{ facet of }P}\bigl(m(G)-3\bigr)=2E-3F.

Substituting the Euler’s formula into (
11
) gives

(12)

2E-3F=2(V+F-2)-3F=2V-F-4.

Combining (
10
) and (
12
), we obtain

(13)

\sum_{G\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}}\bigl(m(G)-3\bigr)\leq(2V-F-4)-(\Delta(P)-3)=2V-F-\Delta(P)-1.

Putting (
13
) into (
9
), we get

\dim A_{\theta}(P)\geq V-\bigl(2V-F-\Delta(P)-1\bigr)=F-V+\Delta(P)+1.

This proves (
7
).

It remains to prove the final assertion. Let

\mathcal{T}(P)=\left\{\bigl(w\cdot x_{i}+\beta\bigr)_{i=1}^{V}:w\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ \beta\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

be the space of globally affine speeds on the vertices of
P
. Every element
of
\mathcal{T}(P)
is
\theta
-admissible. Indeed, on every non-parallel
facet
G
, the required affine function is just the restriction of

x\longmapsto w\cdot x+\beta

to
\aff G
, while parallel facets impose no condition.

The space
\mathcal{T}(P)
has dimension
4
. To see this, suppose

w\cdot x_{i}+\beta=0\qquad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,V.

Since
P
is full-dimensional, its vertices affinely span
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Hence the affine function
x\mapsto w\cdot x+\beta
vanishes on all of

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so
w=0
and
\beta=0
. Thus the evaluation map from the
four-dimensional space of affine functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to

\mathbb{R}^{V}
is injective, and therefore

\dim\mathcal{T}(P)=4.

If

\Delta(P)>V(P)-F(P)+3,

then

F(P)-V(P)+\Delta(P)+1>4.

By (
7
),

\dim A_{\theta}(P)>4.

Since
\mathcal{T}(P)\subset A_{\theta}(P)
and
\dim\mathcal{T}(P)=4
, there
exists

\alpha\in A_{\theta}(P)\setminus\mathcal{T}(P).

This speed vector is
\theta
-admissible but not globally affine. Therefore it
defines a non-trivial admissible shadow system

P_{t}=\conv\{x_{i}+t\alpha_{i}\theta:1\leq i\leq V\}

for sufficiently small
|t|
.
∎

Remark 5.2

(Triangular and non-triangular facets)
.

A triangular non-parallel facet contributes no restriction beyond the three-dimensional affine data on its plane, because
m(G)-3=0
. A quadrilateral facet contributes at most one independent linear condition, a pentagonal facet at most two, and so on. Lemma
5.1
is therefore a global rank estimate for these facet-wise homogeneous linear constraints, with one largest parallel facet omitted entirely.

The following lemma implies that a three-dimensional convex polytope with only trivial admissible shadow systems must be a tetrahedron.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
P
is a three-dimensional convex polytope which is not a tetrahedron, then either
P
or every polar realization
P^{z}
,
z\in\operatorname{int}P
, admits a non-trivial admissible shadow system. In particular, either
P
or its Santaló polar
P^{s(P)}
admits a non-trivial admissible shadow system.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
,
P
has a non-trivial admissible shadow system whenever

(14)

\Delta(P)>V(P)-F(P)+3.

Now let
z\in\operatorname{int}P
. By Lemma
2.2
, the face lattice of
P^{z}
is dual to that of
P
, so

V(P^{z})=F(P),\qquad F(P^{z})=V(P).

A facet of
P^{z}
corresponding to a vertex
v
of
P
has as many vertices as the number of facets of
P
incident to
v
. Therefore

(15)

\Delta(P^{z})=d(P).

Applying Lemma
5.1
to the polytope
P^{z}
, we get a non-trivial admissible shadow system of
P^{z}
whenever

(16)

d(P)>F(P)-V(P)+3.

This criterion depends only on the face lattice of
P
, not on the interior point
z
.

Assume that neither (
14
) nor (
16
) holds. Then

(17)

\Delta(P)\leq V-F+3,\qquad d(P)\leq F-V+3.

Every facet has at least three vertices and every vertex has degree at least three. Combining these lower bounds with (
17
) gives
V\geq F
and
F\geq V
, hence
V=F
. Substituting this into (
17
) gives
\Delta(P)\leq 3
and
d(P)\leq 3
. Thus every facet is triangular and every vertex has degree three. Consequently

2E=3F,\qquad 2E=3V.

Since
V=F
, Euler’s formula yields

2=V-E+F=2V-\frac{3}{2}V=\frac{1}{2}V,

so
V=4
. A full-dimensional three-dimensional polytope with four vertices is a tetrahedron, contradicting the assumption. Therefore at least one of (
14
) and (
16
) holds, giving the desired alternative.
∎

6.
The three-dimensional non-symmetric inequality

Theorem 6.1

(Three-dimensional non-symmetric Mahler inequality)
.

Every convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfies

\mathcal{P}(K)\geq\frac{64}{9}.

Proof.

We first prove the assertion for polytopes. Let
N\geq 4
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{N}
be the class of three-dimensional convex polytopes with at most
N
vertices. By Lemma
2.3
, choose a minimizer
Q\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
of
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{C}_{N}
.

Suppose that
Q
is not a tetrahedron. By Lemma
5.3
, either
Q
has a non-trivial admissible shadow system, or the Santaló polar
Q^{s(Q)}
has one. In either case, it contradicts Lemma
4.2
. Hence every minimizer in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
must be a tetrahedron.

Every tetrahedron belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{N}
and is an affine image of a simplex. Since
\mathcal{P}
is affine invariant and the simplex value is
64/9
, the minimum of
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is exactly
64/9
. Thus every polytope with at most
N
vertices satisfies
\mathcal{P}(P)\geq 64/9
. Since
N
was arbitrary, the lower bound holds for every three-dimensional convex polytope.

Now let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an arbitrary convex body. Choose a sequence
(P_{m})_{m\geq 1}

of three-dimensional polytopes such that
P_{m}\to K
in the Hausdorff metric as
m\to\infty
. By the polytope case,

\mathcal{P}(P_{m})\geq\frac{64}{9}

for every
m
. Hausdorff-continuity of the volume product, including the continuity of the Santaló point and of the corresponding polar volume, gives

\mathcal{P}(K)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(P_{m})\geq\frac{64}{9}.\qed

Corollary 6.2

(Equality for polytopes)
.

If a three-dimensional convex polytope
P
satisfies
\mathcal{P}(P)=64/9
, then
P
is a tetrahedron. Thus equality is attained by tetrahedra among polytopes. The equality question for arbitrary convex bodies is not addressed here.

Proof.

If
P
has
N
vertices and
\mathcal{P}(P)=64/9
, then
P
is a minimizer in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
. The proof of Theorem
6.1
showed that every minimizer in
\mathcal{C}_{N}
is a tetrahedron.
∎
