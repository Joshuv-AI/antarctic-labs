Somatic BRCA alterations in breast cancer are associated with distinct biological and clinical patterns according to germline BRCA status
Authors: M. Oshi, K. Kawashima, M. Sugimori, A. Yamada, Z. Shah, K. Onel, I. Endo, K. Takabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComprehensive genomic profiling increasingly identifies BRCA alterations in breast cancer (BC). While germline BRCA mutations are well established as biologically and clinically relevant, the significance of somatic BRCA alterations remains unclear. We hypothesized that the implications of BRCA alterations may differ according to germline BRCA alterations.Patients and methodsAmong 5411 BC cases registered in the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics database, we analyzed 2978 BC cases with available germline (g) and somatic (s) BRCA status. Patients were categorized into four groups (g+/s+, g+/s−, g−/s+, and g−/s−). Mutational landscapes, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)-related alterations, specimen origin, and time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) were evaluated, focusing on estrogen receptor (ER)-positive/HER2-negative and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).Resultsg−/s+ tumors were more prevalent than g+/s+ tumors, and concordance between germline and somatic BRCA status was limited. Somatic BRCA alterations were more frequently detected in metastatic lesions and were more frequently observed in metastatic specimens. Genomically, g−/s+ tumors were enriched for oncogenic signaling mutations, including PIK3CA and TP53, whereas g+/s+ tumors showed HRD-related features dominated by BRCA alterations. Non-BRCA HRD-related alterations were most frequent in g−/s+ tumors (52%; P = 0.004). In ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, germline BRCA positivity was associated with shorter cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitor TTD, and g+/s+ status remained independently associated with shorter TTD (hazard ratio 1.3, P < 0.001), whereas g−/s+ tumors were comparable with g−/s− tumors. In contrast, g−/s+ tumors showed TTD comparable with g−/s− tumors. In TNBC, immune checkpoint inhibitor outcomes were not associated with BRCA status.ConclusionsBRCA alteration groups defined by combined germline and somatic status showed distinct genomic and clinical patterns in BC. Somatic BRCA alterations should not be interpreted in isolation and require integration with germline status and broader genomic context.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy among women worldwide,1 and defects in homologous recombination repair (HRR) represent one of the most clinically actionable genomic vulnerabilities. Germline pathogenic variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 are well-established drivers of homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) and confer marked sensitivity to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors through synthetic lethality.2,3 Pivotal clinical trials, including OlympiAD4 and EMBRACA,5 demonstrated significant clinical benefit of PARP inhibition in germline BRCA-mutant BCs, establishing BRCA status as a central biomarker in precision oncology.

Beyond PARP inhibitors, accumulating evidence suggests that BRCA gene alterations may influence response to other systemic therapies. Several studies suggest that patients with germline BRCA mutations may derive less benefit from endocrine therapy plus cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors than noncarriers.6,7 In addition, BRCA deficiency has been associated with altered tumor immune microenvironments and increased genomic instability, which may influence sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade.8 These findings suggest that BRCA alterations may influence therapeutic response beyond PARP inhibition, serving as broader determinants of treatment sensitivity and resistance.

Somatic BRCA alterations are increasingly identified through comprehensive genomic profiling of tumors in routine clinical practice. While germline BRCA pathogenic variants reflect inherited susceptibility and early HRD, somatic BRCA alterations arise within an established tumor genome during tumor evolution and may be heterogeneous or subclonal events.9,10 These differences suggest that the clinical significance of BRCA alterations may depend on their origin (germline versus somatic) and allelic status, which is often not captured by tumor-only sequencing. Notably, the TBCRC048 trial demonstrated PARP inhibitor activity in BCs with somatic BRCA mutations,11 and ongoing phase II trials continue to evaluate a molecularly defined subgroup.12 Nevertheless, clinical trials evaluating BRCA-directed therapies have largely focused on germline carriers, and systematic comparisons between germline and somatic BRCA alterations within the same clinical framework are lacking.

Addressing this question has been challenging because paired germline and somatic BRCA data linked to longitudinal outcomes are limited. Consequently, it remains unclear whether somatic BRCA alterations confer the same HRD seen in germline carriers or reflect distinct tumor evolutionary processes with different therapeutic implications. To address this unmet need, we leveraged a large national real-world genomic database integrating germline testing, comprehensive tumor genomic profiling, clinical subtype annotation, and treatment history. By jointly evaluating germline and somatic BRCA status, we classified BCs into four genomic-context categories and examined their mutational landscapes, HRD-related genomic features, specimen origin, and treatment outcomes across major systemic therapies. Through this approach, we sought to determine whether the significance of BRCA alterations differs according to germline and somatic status, while recognizing that allelic context may also contribute.

Patients and methods

Data source and study population

BC cases registered in the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C-CAT), a nationwide Japanese real-world genomic database, were analyzed, as we previously reported.13 Cases were based on tissue comprehensive genomic profiling carried out using FoundationOne CDx (Foundation Medicine, Boston, MA). For each registered patient, one profiled tumor specimen was included in the present analysis, classified according to specimen origin as either primary tumor or metastatic lesion. Among 5411 registered cases from 2019 to 2024, patients with available somatic BRCA1/2 results from comprehensive genomic profiling, germline BRCA1/2 testing by BRACAnalysis CDx (Myriad Genetics, Salt Lake City, UT), and BC subtype information as estrogen receptor (ER)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative, HER-positive, or triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) were identified.

Definition of BRCA status

Germline BRCA1/2 alterations (gBRCA) were defined as clinically classified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants identified by BRACAnalysis CDx® (Myriad Genetics). Somatic BRCA1/2 alterations were defined as tumor comprehensive genomic profiling-reported BRCA1/2 alterations identified in cases registered in the C-CAT database. Somatic BRCA positivity (s+) was defined as the presence of at least one BRCA1 or BRCA2 alteration detected in the tumor. Because somatic BRCA status was based on tumor-only sequencing, detected alterations may include variants of germline origin not identified by prior germline testing. Patients were classified into four groups according to their combined germline and somatic BRCA status: g+/s+, g+/s−, g−/s+, and g−/s−.

Genomic analyses

Somatic mutation data were obtained from comprehensive genomic profiling. Differential mutation frequencies between BRCA status groups were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Analysis of non-BRCA genomic features excludes BRCA1/2. Non-BRCA HRD-related alterations were defined as pathogenic variants in established homologous recombination pathway genes (ATM, ATR, BARD1, BRIP1, CHEK1, CHEK2, FANCA, FANCD2, FNACL, PALB2, RAD51C, and RAD51D). Variant allele frequency (VAF) was extracted from the reported allele frequency fields for BRCA1 and BRCA2 alterations. When multiple BRCA alterations were present in a tumor, the maximum VAF across BRCA1 and BRCA2 was used as a representative value. To ensure consistency, nonnumeric annotations were removed, and values were analyzed as continuous variables between 0 and 1. To approximate allelic complexity, a multihit status was defined based on the number of BRCA1/2 alterations reported per tumor, with tumors classified as single-hit tumors (one alteration) or multihit tumors (two or more alterations). This definition does not distinguish cis versus trans events and was used as a pragmatic surrogate for allelic burden. Global genomic alteration burden was assessed using the following metrics provided by the database: the number of DNA somatic substitutions/insertions/deletions [single-nucleotide variant (SNV)-like], the number of DNA somatic copy number alterations (CAN)-like, and tumor mutational burden (TMB). These variables were analyzed as continuous measures. For comparisons between g+/s+ and g−/s+ tumors, continuous variables (VAF, SNV-like, CAN-like, and TMB) were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and categorical variables (multihit versus single-hit) were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Multiple testing correction was carried out using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method. VAF was defined as the maximum reported allele frequency among BRCA1/2 alterations.

Treatment outcome analyses

Because standardized radiographic progression data were not available across the cohort, we used time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) as a pragmatic real-world endpoint. TTD was operationally defined as the interval from initiation of a given therapy to the recorded start date of the subsequent treatment line. Patients without subsequent recorded treatment were censored at the last available follow-up. As treatment changes or discontinuation in routine practice may occur for multiple reasons, including disease progression, treatment-related toxicity, physician decision, or patient preference, TTD reflects real-world treatment duration but does not directly measure treatment efficacy or resistance. Analyses focused on CDK4/6 inhibitors (abemaciclib and palbociclib) in ER-positive/HER2-negative BC and immune checkpoint inhibitors in TNBC. Because ribociclib has not been approved for BC in Japan, no real-world treatment data were available for inclusion.

Statistical analysis

TTD was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed, adjusting for treatment line and specimen origin, with selected models including TP53 and PIK3CA status. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All analyses were carried out using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. FDR indicates FDR-adjusted P values calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics of the study population stratified by combined germline and somatic BRCA status are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Similar distributions were observed across major BC subtypes, with no statistically significant difference in BRCA status distribution (Figure 1E) (P = 0.370). Notably, g−/s+ tumors were consistently more frequent than g+/s+ tumors across subtypes.

Somatic BRCA alterations were modestly enriched in metastatic specimens

To explore whether somatic BRCA detection differed according to specimen origin, we compared primary tumor and metastatic lesion specimens recorded in the C-CAT database. The distribution of specimen sources differed significantly across combined germline/somatic BRCA groups (Figure 2A). Primary tumors (n = 1602) accounted for 57% of g+/s+ cases, whereas metastatic lesions (n = 1367) were more common in g−/s+ and g−/s− groups (Figure 2A) (52.6% and 55.8%, respectively; P < 0.001). Overall, somatic BRCA alterations were more frequently detected in metastatic than in primary specimens (Figure 2B) (29.1% versus 23.8%). Similar trends were observed across major BC subtypes. In subtype-stratified genome-wide analyses, ESR1 was the only significantly differentially expressed gene after FDR correction between metastatic and primary samples in ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, whereas no significant differences were observed in TNBC (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108311). Notably, BRCA1/2 were not among the most strongly differential genes in these exploratory comparisons. Asymmetric concordance and distinct genomic patterns between germline and somatic BRCA groups

We next examined the relationship between germline and somatic BRCA status. Among germline BRCA-positive cases, the corresponding BRCA alteration was detectable in tumor samples in >85% of cases across subtypes (Figure 3A). In contrast, only a minority of tumors with somatic BRCA alterations had an underlying germline variant (37.4% for BRCA1 and 35.3% for BRCA2 overall), with subtype-specific variation (Figure 3B). We then compared genomic profiles between g+/s+ and g−/s+ tumors. In ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, g−/s+ tumors showed higher mutation frequencies in several oncogenic pathway genes, including PIK3CA and TP53, whereas BRCA2 alterations were more frequent in the g+/s+ tumors (Figure 3C). In TNBC, BRCA1 alterations were more frequent in g+/s+ tumors, whereas BRCA2 alterations were more common in g−/s+ tumors (Figure 3D). To further characterize the allelic features of BRCA alterations, VAF and the number of BRCA alterations per tumor were examined. Among somatic BRCA-positive tumors, BRCA VAF was significantly higher in g+/s+ than in g−/s+ tumors in the overall cohort [median 0.74 (interquartile range 0.62-0.83) versus 0.49 (0.33-0.59), P < 0.001; n = 152 versus 340], and this pattern was directionally consistent across subtypes (Figure 3E). Tumors harboring multiple BRCA alterations (multihit) were also more frequent in the g+/s+ than in g−/s+ tumors (Figure 3F) (36.4% versus 10.2%, P < 0.001; n = 302 versus 479), with similar trends observed across subtypes. Differential distribution of non-BRCA HRD-related gene alterations according to combined germline and somatic BRCA status

Given the marked differences in mutational landscapes between g+/s+ and g−/s+ tumors, we next examined whether these distinct genomic contexts were also reflected in the distribution of HRD-related gene alterations beyond BRCA. In the overall cohort, the prevalence of non-BRCA HRD-related gene alterations differed modestly but significantly among groups, occurring in 42.7% of g+/s+ tumors, 47% of g−/s+ tumors, and 40.2% of g−/s− tumors (Figure 4A) (P = 0.023). Subtype-specific analyses revealed heterogeneity. In ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, these alterations were more frequent in the g−/s+ group (51.6%) than in g+/s+ (42.9%) and g−/s− tumors (40.9%) (Figure 4B, left) (P = 0.004). Germline, but not somatic-only, BRCA status was associated with shorter CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment duration

To determine whether the biological differences associated with germline-somatic BRCA status translated into differences in treatment duration, we next evaluated TTD across major systemic therapies. In TNBC, pembrolizumab TTD did not differ by germline BRCA status (Figure 5A, left) (log-rank P = 0.818) or across the three BRCA groups (Figure 5A, right) (P = 0.902). In contrast, in ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, CDK4/6 inhibitor TTD differed by BRCA status. In the combined CDK4/6 inhibitor cohort, germline BRCA-positive patients had shorter TTD than germline BRCA-negative patients (Figure 5B, left) (P < 0.001), and a significant difference was also observed across the three BRCA groups (Figure 5B, right) (P < 0.001). For abemaciclib, TTD differed by germline BRCA status (Figure 5C, left) (P = 0.026), whereas the three-group comparison was not statistically significant (Figure 5C, right) (P = 0.075). For palbociclib, significant differences were observed in both the germline-based comparison (Figure 5D, left) (P = 0.011) and the three-group comparison (Figure 5D) (P = 0.003). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, as expected, later treatment line was independently associated with shorter CDK4/6 inhibitor TTD (Figure 5E) (HR 1.03 per additional line, 95% CI 1.02-1.05, P < 0.001). After adjustment for treatment line, specimen origin, TP53, and PIK3CA status, g+/s+ status remained independently associated with shorter TTD compared with g−/s− tumors (Figure 5E) (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.21-1.74, P < 0.001), whereas g−/s+ status was not significantly different from g−/s− status (Figure 5E) (HR 0.88, P = 0.075). TP53 mutation was also independently associated with shorter TTD, whereas no independent association was observed for PIK3CA mutation. Discussion

Previous studies have established subtype-specific differences in germline BRCA1/2 prevalence,15,16 forming the basis of current genetic testing strategies in BC. However, these findings largely derive from hereditary cancer or clinically enriched cohorts. Using a large real-world genomic database, we confirmed germline BRCA frequencies across BC subtypes consistent with prior reports, supporting the validity of our cohort. Importantly, we extend existing knowledge by demonstrating that somatic BRCA alterations account for a substantial proportion of BRCA-altered tumors across all subtypes, with limited concordance between germline and somatic status. This finding highlights that BRCA alterations detected by comprehensive genomic profiling cannot be interpreted in isolation. Rather, both somatic and germline BRCA alterations must be contextualized with respect to the presence or absence of a germline pathogenic variant, allelic status, and their functional consequences, as approximated by available HRD-related genomic surrogates. Somatic and germline BRCA alterations are not uniform biological entities, and their clinical significance depends on genomic context and the extent of functional inactivation. These findings highlight the limitations of mutation-centered or germline-centric classification frameworks and support an integrated approach incorporating germline status, zygosity, and functional readouts. Defining this landscape is essential for interpreting differences in tumor biology and treatment response.

The increasing use of comprehensive genomic profiling has led to the frequent identification of somatic BRCA alterations in BC; however, whether these alterations represent biological equivalents of germline BRCA deficiency has remained unclear. Prior studies have reported limited concordance between germline and tumor-only BRCA alterations, particularly in advanced disease settings, raising concerns regarding interpretation of somatic BRCA findings in clinical practice.17,18 In the present real-world cohort, we observed a pronounced asymmetry between germline and somatic BRCA alterations. While germline BRCA-positive tumors almost uniformly harbored corresponding somatic BRCA events, the majority of somatic BRCA-positive tumors lacked germline alterations. This directional discordance is consistent with the previous pan-cancer and BC-specific analyses based on tumor-only sequencing18,19 and underscores that somatic BRCA positivity does not necessarily indicate inherited HRD. Notably, the rarity of the g+/s− group may be biologically informative and is consistent with the established two-hit model of BRCA-associated tumorigenesis. However, technical factors such as tumor purity, assay sensitivity, copy number resolution, and specimen quality may also influence the detection of secondary somatic events, and therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, somatic BRCA alterations were more frequently detected in metastatic lesions than in primary tumors. Similar enrichment of BRCA and other DNA repair-related alterations in metastatic samples has been reported in large-scale sequencing efforts.20,21 Because our analysis used cross-sectional, nonpaired samples, we could not determine whether somatic BRCA alterations were acquired during tumor evolution or reflected treatment-related selection or sampling bias. Genome-wide comparisons between primary and metastatic tumors identified only limited mutation differences confined to ER-positive/HER2-negative disease and absent in TNBC, suggesting that the enrichment of somatic BRCA alterations in metastatic lesions is unlikely to be driven solely by generalized genomic instability. Beyond detection patterns, our data demonstrate that the genomic patterns associated with somatic BRCA alterations differ according to germline BRCA classification. Despite sharing somatic BRCA positivity, g+/s+ and g−/s+ tumors exhibited clearly distinct mutational landscapes. In ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, g−/s+ tumors were enriched for oncogenic signaling-related alterations such as PIK3CA and TP53, whereas g+/s+ tumors showed preferential accumulation of BRCA1/2-centered alterations, consistent with a BRCA-centered genomic pattern potentially related to HRD. Similar germline-dependent divergence was observed in TNBC, where BRCA1/2 alterations dominated the discriminating features between the two groups. These findings extend prior observations that BRCA-altered tumors are genomically heterogeneous22,23 by demonstrating that such heterogeneity is not random but, instead, associated with germline classification, although unresolved allelic status may contribute. In this framework, somatic BRCA alterations in g−/s+ tumors appear to arise within a genomic context enriched for oncogenic signaling-related alterations, whereas in g+/s+ tumors, they represent part of a genomic context for HRD-related alterations.

In ER-positive/HER2-negative BC, the present study revealed a marked difference in CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment duration according to the germline-somatic mutation status of BRCA alterations. While germline BRCA-positive tumors, particularly those with concurrent somatic BRCA alterations (g+/s+), showed significantly shorter treatment duration, tumors with somatic BRCA alterations alone (g−/s+) exhibited treatment outcomes comparable with BRCA-negative tumors. This finding appears paradoxical, as g−/s+ tumors were enriched for oncogenic signaling-related mutations, including TP53 and PIK3CA, which have been associated with aggressive tumor biology and reduced endocrine sensitivity. However, prior studies have suggested that such oncogenic alterations modulate, but do not abolish, dependence on the RB-Cyclin D-CDK4/6 axis.24, 25, 26 Consistent with this concept, the presence of these mutations alone did not translate into shorter CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment duration in the absence of germline BRCA deficiency. In contrast, germline BRCA alterations represent an early and systemic defect in HRR, shaping tumor evolution from its earliest stages. Prior studies have suggested that chronic genomic instability associated with germline BRCA deficiency may influence the cell-cycle regulatory pathway and CDK4/6 dependence.27,28 In our cohort, multivariable analyses showed that g+/s+ status remained independently associated with treatment line and specimen origin, together with TP53 mutation. These findings suggest that the clinical associations of BRCA alterations may differ according to combined germline and somatic status. Collectively, the results support biological heterogeneity among somatic BRCA-positive tumors, although the mechanisms underlying these differences remain uncertain. This mutation status-dependent framework may offer one possible interpretation for the preserved CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment duration observed in g−/s+ tumors despite frequent oncogenic signaling alterations.

TTD is a composite real-world endpoint reflecting treatment duration, tolerability, and clinical decision making. Therefore, the shorter TTD observed in germline BRCA-positive tumors should be interpreted as an association in this dataset rather than direct evidence of reduced treatment efficacy, biological resistance, or any specific underlying mechanism. TTD differences may reflect multiple measured and unmeasured factors and cannot be attributed to any single cause.29, 30, 31 These findings require validation using datasets with direct endpoints such as progression-free survival or overall survival.

A key question raised by our results is whether the differences between g+/s+ and g−/s+ tumors reflect the germline background itself or unresolved differences in allelic status, such as biallelic versus monoallelic BRCA inactivation. We acknowledge that allelic status cannot be comprehensively determined in the current dataset and that g+/s+ tumors may be enriched for biallelic events, whereas g−/s+ tumors likely represent a heterogeneous mixture of allelic states. Nevertheless, we observed distinct genomic patterns between these groups. For example, in ER-positive/HER2-negative disease, g−/s+ tumors were enriched for oncogenic signaling-related alterations, whereas g+/s+ tumors showed a more BRCA-centered genomic profile. In addition, VAF and multihit analyses suggest differences in the allelic architecture of BRCA alterations between groups. However, the relative contribution of germline status versus functional BRCA inactivation cannot be definitively separated in the current dataset. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as group-level differences associated with the combined germline/somatic classification used in this study, rather than direct evidence of germline-specific biological effects. Future studies incorporating paired tumor-normal sequencing, copy number resolution, and allele-specific analyses will be required to disentangle these effects.

The clinical use of PARP inhibitors in BC has recently expanded beyond germline BRCA alterations through MyChoice-guided olaparib selection. However, real-world evidence for PARP inhibitor efficacy in tumors with somatic BRCA alterations remains limited. Our findings show that germline and somatic BRCA alterations arise in distinct genomic contexts, indicating that BRCA alterations cannot be interpreted uniformly across biological settings. These findings raise a critical unanswered question: whether somatic BRCA alterations, particularly those arising in a genomic background not clearly enriched for HRD-related features, are associated with sensitivity to PARP inhibition? Given the biological heterogeneity observed among somatic BRCA-positive tumors, future studies will be needed to evaluate PARP inhibitor outcomes according to combined germline and somatic BRCA status rather than BRCA status alone. Integration of HRD assessment with germline status may help refine patient stratification in future studies. Longitudinal real-world datasets and molecularly stratified clinical studies will be required to clarify whether somatic BRCA alterations represent clinically meaningful therapeutic vulnerabilities or broader genomic markers of tumor evolution.

This study has several limitations. Although based on a large real-world genomic database, its retrospective design precludes causal inference. Most analyses were cross-sectional and lacked a detailed treatment history, limiting assessment of temporal acquisition and treatment-driven selection. Somatic BRCA alterations were identified from single timepoint tumor samples, preventing direct evaluation of genomic evolution; thus, their enrichment in metastatic lesions is compatible with but does not prove acquisition during tumor progression. Moreover, specimen origin was closely linked to disease stage and prior treatment exposure and could not be fully disentangled. Functional data were also limited, including information on biallelic inactivation, loss of heterozygosity, and HRD status. Because the study largely relied on tumor-only sequencing, allelic status could not be determined, and somatic BRCA-positive tumors likely represent a heterogeneous mixture, precluding assumptions of biological equivalence with germline BRCA alterations. In addition, HRD was inferred using mutation-based proxies rather than validated genomic scar metrics, mutational signatures, or functional assays, and CNAs and transcriptomic consequences such as aberrant splicing or nonsense-mediated decay could not be systematically assessed. Treatment outcomes were evaluated using TTD, a pragmatic real-world endpoint that may be influenced by toxicity, patient preference, and treatment sequencing. Finally, although the cohort reflects nationwide practice in Japan, ethnic and health care system differences may limit generalizability. Accordingly, these findings should be considered hypothesis generating and warrant prospective validation with paired tumor-normal sequencing, longitudinal sampling, and functional HRD assessment.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that BRCA alteration groups defined by combined germline and somatic status show distinct genomic and clinical patterns in BC. These findings highlight an important limitation of tumor-only, mutation-centered interpretation, particularly in the absence of information on allelic status and functional inactivation. Because the current dataset cannot fully disentangle these factors, future integrative genomic approaches incorporating germline status, allele-specific analyses, and functional readouts to more accurately define clinically meaningful BRCA deficiency.
