ABSTRACT
This project evaluated the pulse shaping capabilities of next-generation pulsed power (NGPP)
architectures. NGPP architectures share several common attributes including multiple independent
pulse-generation lines, a radial water-insulated impedance transformer, and a central vacuum-
insulated load region. A multi-module circuit model was developed, incorporating independent
pulse-generation lines and a 2-D transmission line mesh of the radial impedance transformer to
assess the effects of azimuthal asymmetry in pulse-shaped experiments. Circuit model simulations
demonstrated that NGPP architectures are able to produce the the desired current pulse shapes for
exemplar NGPP experiments.
Additionally, the project explored automated methods for experiment design, including derivative-
free optimization and machine learning. Pulse-shaped experiments require designers to determine
machine parameters that reliably produce the desired current pulse at the load, a process that
typically relies on expert knowledge and iterative adjustments using the Z circuit model. Given
the increased complexity of NGPP systems, this manual approach may be impractical. While the
evaluated methods do not eliminate the need for manual iteration, they can reduce the time required
for experiment design. Derivative-free optimization automates much of the trial-and-error process,
providing a close starting point for manual adjustments or making small modifications to near-final
designs. Meanwhile, deep neural network methods can generate a good qualitative match to the
desired current pulse in under one second without requiring circuit model simulations.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Project Motivation

The Z pulsed power accelerator is used for three general classes of experiments: radiation ef-
fects (RES), inertial confinement fusion (ICF), and dynamic materials properties (DMP). A next-
generation pulsed power (NGPP) accelerator will need to continue conducting experiments for
all three of these missions as part of the National Nuclear Security Administration’s Stockpile
Stewardship Program.
There are several driver technology options for NGPP, including conventional Marx generators,
fast Marx generators, linear transformer drivers, and impedance matched Marx generators [1, 2, 3].
While the energy storage and pulse generation sections of these accelerators are different, these
technologies share common features; multiple energy storage and pulse forming modules, a large
radial impedance transformer, and a central vacuum-insulated load region as shown in Figure 1-1.
Initial assessments of NGPP have primarily focused on synchronous operation, where the energy
storage and pulse forming sections produce current pulses on the order of 100 ns, relevant for
RES and ICF experiments. Simulations for synchronous experiments require relatively simple
circuit models. The multiple energy storage and pulse forming lines can be represented as a single
equivalent model. The radial impedance transformers can be modeled in one dimension due to the
symmetrical drive from the pulse forming lines. Short-pulse, synchronous, experiment design can
mostly be done simply using an open-circuit output voltage from the driver coupled to the load
through a Thevenin equivalent circuit [4].
In contrast, DMP experiments require asynchronous operation of the pulse forming section to
precisely shape the current pulse over a duration of up to ∼1.5 µs. Simulations of asynchronous

operation require more extensive circuit models [5], including independent representations of
energy storage and pulse-forming lines to enable control over output timing and pulse shape of each
line. Additionally, a two-dimensional representation of the radial impedance transformers is needed
to capture the effects of azimuthally asymmetric drive from the pulse-forming lines. Experiment
design requires the full circuit model to determine the load current produced by specific machine
configurations.

1.2. Project Approach

To demonstrate that a NGPP facility can meet future DMP mission requirements, we developed
circuit models to evaluate the pulse-shaping capabilities of conceptual NGPP architectures and
created tools to automate experiment design. The circuit model was derived from an existing
design created for synchronous, short-pulse operation. We extended the single-line equivalent
model to an independent line model for asynchronous operation and created two-dimensional
transmission line mesh models of the impedance transformer. This new model was used to assess
the capabilities of next-generation architectures to produce the desired pulse shapes for future DMP
experiments.
To speed up the experiment design process and reduce the time required from experiment design-
ers, we developed and tested automated optimization and machine learning techniques for DMP
experiment design on both Z and NGPP. Two methods were evaluated. The first method uses a
mesh adaptive search to automate circuit model runs, varying machine parameters until the desired
current pulse shape was achieved. The second method uses machine learning techniques to develop
and train a deep neural network to act as a proxy for the circuit model, eliminating the need for
iterative circuit model runs.

1.3. Project Components

This project comprises four main efforts, which will be detailed in the following chapters. Chapter 2
discusses the selection of a representative NGPP architecture and the development and validation
of the circuit model for asynchronous pulse generation. Chapter 3 explores the use of the circuit
model to develop machine configurations necessary for DMP experiments. Chapter 4 details the
application of ASTRID, an automated design tool developed for Z DMP experiment design, to
NGPP. Chapter 5 discusses the application of deep neural network models to DMP experiment
design on Z. Chapter 6 provides a summary and conclusions from the project.
The appendices provide supplemental information. Appendix A provides additional details on the
experiments developed in Chapter 3. Appendices B and C include additional details on the forward
and reverse neural network models extracted from the end of project reports from Western Michigan
University.

2. NGPP CIRCUIT MODEL

2.1. NGPP design description

For this study we evaluated pulse shaping using a point design based on a conventional Marx
based system with two stages of pulse compression. Figure 1-1 is representative of the chosen
design. Designs based on conventional Marx generators have some advantages for pulse-shaped
experiments because they use several stages of pulse compression to reduce the initial ∼1.5 µs
output of the Marx generator to ∼300 ns at the PFL and finally to ∼100 ns at the pulse forming line
output. The switch components between stages can be modified to alter the pulse compression and
tailor the output pulse length of each individual line. Pulse-shaped experiments can take advantage
of independent control of both the timing and output pulse length of the individual pulse-forming
lines.

2.2. Circuit description

The point design uses 75 Marx generators. Each Marx generator outputs into three parallel pulse-
forming lines arranged in three vertical levels. Each pulse-forming line consists of an intermediate
store capacitor, laser triggered gas switch, pulse forming line (PFL) capacitor, and a self-break gas
switch. Each level of pulse-forming lines output into a tri-plate radial impedance transformer. The
three levels of impedance transformers then output into six levels of a vacuum-insulator stack /
MITL system in the central load region.

Bertha [6], a transmission line circuit code, was used for the circuit simulations. The single line
equivalent model from the point design was expanded to include independent models of the pulse-
forming lines. The impedance transformer could be modeled as either a 1-D or transmission line or
a 2-D transmission line mesh to capture azimuthal asymmetries from the individual pulse-forming
lines. Details on each region of the machine are found in the following subsections and explain
how parameters could be varied in the circuit model to enable pulse shaping of the load current.

2.2.1. Marx Generator

The system has 75 bipolar charged Marx generators, each with 45 stages. For pulse-shaped
experiment design each Marx is assumed to be able to be charged independently from ±50 kV to
±95 kV per capacitor and able to be triggered independently at a specified time from 0 ns to 1000 ns.
Details of the Marx are shown in Table 2-1.

2.2.2. Intermediate Storage Capacitor

The intermediate storage capacitor (ISC) is a coaxial, water-insulated transmission line. Details

pulse shaping it is assumed that the LTGS switching time can vary from −1000 ns to +200 ns from
the nominal LTGS time. We assume that the three parallel ISCs connected to a single Marx are
triggered with the same time advance. This prevents level-to-level asymmetries in the impedance
transformers and vacuum region.

2.2.3. PFL capacitor

The pulse-forming line capacitor (PFL) is a coaxial, water-insulated transmission line. Details of
the PFL are shown in Table 2-3. Each PFL is charged by an ISC through the LTGS. Each PFL is

that the SBGS switching time can be varied to produce a short, medium, or long pulse mode from
each pulse-forming line. In short pulse mode the SBGS closes 210 ns after the LTGS trigger time

closes 110 ns after the LTGS trigger time and produces an output pulse with a ∼115 ns 10–90
risetime. In long pulse mode the SBGS is shorted (closed at t=0) and produces an output pulse
with a ∼150 ns 10–90 risetime. The three output pulses are shown in Figure 2-1.

2.2.4. Impedance Transformers

Water-insulated radial impedance transformers connect the pulse-forming lines at a radius of ∼32 m
to the central vacuum chamber at a radius of ∼3 m. The impedance transformers have a transit
time of approximately 850 ns. In the point design, the impedance varies linearly along the length,
constant d𝑍/d𝑙, of the transformer from input to output as shown in Table 2-4. An exponential
impedance variation, constant d𝑍/d𝑟, may be a better choice to reduce the maximum gap spacing
between electrodes. A comparison of the electrode gap profiles for a linear and exponential profile
is shown in Figure 2-2.

A Matlab script was created to aid in generating the 2-D transmission line mesh for the impedance
transformer. The Matlab script uses input parameters of the input/output impedance, input/output
radius, impedance profile, number of external connections, and desired mesh resolution. The
Matlab script then outputs a list of transmission line element impedances and locations, shunt
resistors, and external connections in TLCODE format [7, 8] to be compatible with Bertha’s 2-D
mesh capabilities. A sample building block of the 2-D mesh is shown in Figure 2-3 and consists

2.2.5.     Stack / MITLs

A six level stack and MITL system delivers energy to the load. The insulator stack rings are
approximately 3 m in diameter. Each level contains from 8 to 10 insulator rings. The outer MITLs
are 3 Ω constant impedance outside a radius of 20 cm. From a radius of 20 cm to 15 cm the outer
MITLs have a constant gap of 1 cm. The inductance values assumed for the water flare, insulator
stack, and MITLs are shown in Table 2-5. The six outer MITL levels are combined at a radius of
15 cm with triple-post-hole convolute. The convolute extends from a radius of 15 cm to 9 cm. The
convolute has an inductance of approximately 2 nH.

2.2.6. Inner MITL and Load

The inner MITL is a simple radial feed from a radius of 9 cm to 2 cm with a 5 mm A-K gap. The
inner MITL has an inductance of 1.7 nH. The load is modeled as either a fixed or a variable
inductance with a user-supplied waveform.

2.3. Circuit model validation

Several validation tests were done when developing the circuit model to make sure that the DMP
circuit model and 2-D transmission line mesh did not introduce any errors.

2.3.1. 1-D single line equivalent vs 2-D TL mesh

The original 1-D, single line equivalent model was compared to the independent line model when
driven synchronously and a 2-D transmission line mesh resolution scan was done to determine the
necessary mesh size to balance accuracy to the 1-D model and simulation run time. Figure 2-5
shows the load current produced by the 1-D single line equivalent model compared to several
versions of the independent line model with different impedance transformer mesh sizes. The 4 ns
mesh resolution was chosen to balance agreement with the 1D model with simulation run time.
The impact of mesh resolution on the circuit size and simulation run time is shown in Table 2-6.

The simulation runtime of 16 minutes with the 4 ns mesh resolution is adequate for a single
simulation, but would be prohibitive for the iterative simulations needed in DMP experiment
design. To reduce the runtime, an update was made to Bertha enabling each TL mesh to run in a
separate process using MPI. The parallel version of the 4 ns model ran in 2–3 minutes.

2.3.2. 2-D TL mesh circuit model vs 3-D COMSOL

COMSOL Multiphysics [9] software was used to validate the 2-D transmission line mesh circuit

COMSOL simulation verified that the capacitive coupling is negligible at large radius. This made
a circuit model of the slotted transformer straightforward to develop, modeling the slotted region
as independent, parallel 1-D transmission lines connected to a smaller radius 2-D transmission line
mesh. A modified circuit model was created to model an equivalent design with slotted impedance
transformers to a radius of 15 m, providing approximately 1 µs of transit time isolation between
pulse-forming lines.

pressure to match the voltage at the time of triggering, increasing the risk of a gas switch pre-fire.

forward voltage at t=1880 ns.

3. DMP EXEMPLAR EXPERIMENTS

3.1. Targeted pulse shapes

Magnetically driven dynamic compression experiments on Z use a wide range of pulse shapes
to generate flyer-plate acceleration for shock experiments, ramp waves for quasi-isentropic com-
pression, and double-ramp shapes for shock-ramp compression. Since the desired (targeted) load
current pulse shape for a given experiment depends only on physics of the load itself, as predicted
by 1-D and 2-D magneto-hydrodynamic (MHD) simulations, the machine configuration to obtain
a given pulse shape can be determined using a circuit model of the machine in a manner decoupled
from the load physics. The load inductance history used with the circuit model is determined by
the 2-D MHD load simulation, with self-consistency achieved when the circuit model run with this
inductance waveform reproduces the targeted load current waveform that drove the MHD simula-
tion. In many cases, a successful pulse shape need only match the front side of the target pulse, up
to some current level below peak.
Examples of relevant target DMP pulse shapes were developed as part of previous work attempting
to define requirements for various proposed next-generation machines. No consideration was given
to how a machine would generate shaped pulses; rather, each pulse shape was based on extending
present experiment designs to reach significantly higher pressures of interest using the available
energy of a particular proposed machine. Over time, six such pulse shapes were defined, three for
simple ramp compression and three for shock-ramp compression, with one of each type designed
specifically for experiments on special nuclear materials (SNM). Here, we consider only two of
these, one for shock-ramp compression of copper (Cu) and the other for pure ramp compression of
aluminum (Al), i.e., the two electrode materials typically used in DMP experiments. Both pulse
shapes, shown in Figure 3-1 along with their associated load inductance waveforms from 2-D MHD,
were developed to be consistent with the stored energy of the Z800 machine design [3]. While the
ramp and shock-ramp pulse shapes for SNM may have higher programmatic relevance, their only
salient difference from the exemplar pulse shapes selected for this work is longer rise time, which
impacts only the issue of back-pulses discussed in Section 2.4 and below in Section 3.3.

75 Marx charge voltages, 225 LTGS time advances, and 225 SBGS modes) to be practical for
manual development of machine configurations to match desired load current waveforms as is done

3.2.1. Making manual iteration tractable

Determining a machine configuration by manual iteration means repeatedly making (often small)
changes to model parameters, typically only one or a few such parameters at a time, while executing
the circuit model simulation and inspecting changes in the resulting load current between each
change of parameters. This process becomes a reasonable task when the simulation step takes less

 34

than about 10 seconds. By creating a computationally parallel version of the 1-D circuit model, with
five groups of 15 pulse-line triplets and the three ITX levels each executing in separate processes
from the rest of the model, and also running without the MITL loss subroutines, we were able to
get simulation run time down to a few seconds for nearly 3.3 µs of model time. Of course, the load
current waveform computed this way is not the same as a more realistic waveform computed using
the full 2-D circuit model with losses.
To determine a configuration that matches the 2-D model’s output to a targeted pulse shape, we
added an outer loop to manual iteration for adjusting the target load current shape to be matched by
the no-loss 1-D model. Each outer-loop iteration consists of running the 2-D model once (using the
configuration just found by the current manual iteration of the no-loss 1-D model), generating a new
time-varying adjustment scale factor as the ratio of no-loss 1-D to full 2-D predicted load currents,
and applying this scale factor to the original target waveform to obtain a new adjusted target pulse
shape. The smaller the difference between no-loss 1-D and full 2-D load current outputs, the
fewer outer iterations are needed to reach convergence. As shown in Section 2.3.3, arranging pulse
line assignments to reduce the magnitude of azimuthal non-uniformity in current delivered to the
ITX input reduces the difference between 1-D and 2-D load current predictions. This approach of
distributing the earliest-triggered pulse lines among 𝑁 equidistant points around the circumference
is often referred to in pulse-shaping terminology as assigning pulse lines for "𝑁-point symmetry,"
even though current is in general not distributed symmetrically. For the present targeted pulse
shapes, we found setting 𝑁 = 8 to work well for the present 75-module NGPP point design .

3.2.2. Making manual control tractable

Reducing the number of adjustable parameters one must think about during each manual adjustment
of parameter values (i.e., to control the pulse shape) helps immensely in speeding up the manual
iteration procedure. Thus, in addition to considering each pulse line triplet as a single pair of time
and mode settings, we began each pulse-shape design by setting all 75 Marx modules to a single
timing 𝑡MARX and a single charge voltage 𝑉MARX , only making some modules different if doing so
improved the match to the targeted load current. Manual control was further eased by the following
three techniques:

 1. define LTGS times in order of decreasing time advance 𝑡adv at which energy arrives to the
 ITX input, and assign these to modules at different azimuths in a fixed, predetermined order;
 2. define SBGS modes by subranges of modules in the above timing order (instead of considering
 75 independent modes); and
 3. define individual-module LTGS times only for the first 10–20 modules in the timing order,
 with the remaining modules specified as a smaller number of subsets each containing multiple
 modules and defined by up to 5 parameters (excluding 𝑉MARX and SBGS mode).

As a first step, we shift the reference time, with respect to which time advances are specified, to a
time Δ𝑡 later than the nominal LTGS time (but within the suggested maximum delay of 200 ns);
this makes it easier to match a target pulse within the suggested maximum advance of 1000 ns from
nominal while avoiding confusion over negative time advances during manual iteration. Similarly

 35

to pulse shaping on Z, we set time advances always in multiples of 5-ns steps, commensurate with
the typical magnitude of switch timing jitter. Five parameters (excluding possibly different per-set
values of 𝑡MARX and 𝑉MARX ) describe each subset of modules at time advances smaller than those
of the individually-specified modules or previous larger-advance set:

 1. the largest time advance of the subset (as a delay Δ𝑡set ≥ 0 ns from the next largest advance),
 2. the number 𝑛adv ≥ 1 of unique time advances assigned to the subset,
 3. the constant delay Δ𝑡 adv ≥ 0 ns between unique time advances,
 4. the number of modules 𝑛mod ≥ 1 assigned to each unique time advance, and
 5. the SBGS mode of all modules in the subset.

For example, a subset of four time advances 15 ns apart and each assigned to three modules
(𝑛adv = 4, 𝑛mod = 3, and Δ𝑡adv = 15 ns) defines timings for 12 modules across a 45-ns range
of time advance, starting at an advance Δ𝑡set smaller than the smallest advance of the previous
(larger-advance) set or module. The final subset used to round out 75 module definitions typically
sets a single time advance and mode for all remaining unassigned modules. Once all 75 time
advances are specified, they are converted to absolute LTGS times for use in the circuit model by
first subtracting the advances from the reference LTGS time, then subtracting an additional 45 ns
from medium-pulse modules and 120 ns from short-pulse modules to set LTGS times that result in
the desired arrival time at the load of current contributed by modules in those open-SBGS modes.
These mode-dependent time shifts were determined by comparing the time at which load current
reaches 1 MA in synchronous, single-mode 95-kV simulations using the full 2-D model for each
of the long, medium, and short modes.
The algorithm to define a predetermined module order for 𝑁-point symmetry places the largest
time advance at module 1, determines module numbers for the other 𝑁 − 1 "symmetry" points, and
orders those modules so that each one is the furthest (of the points not yet assigned) in azimuth from
the previous one; this pattern is akin to that used when tightening lug nuts on an automotive wheel.
When 𝑁 is not a factor of 75, the symmetry-point modules will not all lie exactly equidistant from
each other, in which case we distribute evenly around the machine those azimuthal arcs containing
an extra module. The algorithm next repeats the symmetry-point order to assign the next available
module clockwise from each point, then repeats the same order but assigning the next available
module counter-clockwise from each point, and so on. In this way, diminishing advance times are
assigned to modules in order of increasing azimuthal distance from the ordered symmetry points.
The final, smaller group of the latest times (consisting of 3 modules when 𝑁 = 8) gets assigned
to the remaining modules in the middle of the longer adjacent-point arcs. This order is depicted
graphically for 𝑁 = 8 in Figure 3-2.

3.3. Discussion

The issue of back-pulse effects on LTGS and SBGS, introduced in Section 2.4, was not considered
during the above exercise of finding an acceptable machine configuration by manual iteration of the
circuit models. Back pulses on later-triggered pulse-line triplets tend to be more significant with
double-ramp pulse shapes for shock-ramp experiments, because they use more modules triggered
earlier during the pulse. The Cu Shock-Ramp configuration exhibited significant back pulse that
looked particularly bad on LTGS voltage waveforms from the nine long-pulse modules having time
advances smaller than 500 ns; predictable closure times for these LTGS would likely be impossible.
Fortunately, a relatively minor change to the ITX geometry (see Section 2.4) to greatly increase
isolation (transit) time between pulse-line triplets should eliminate this concern. For the Al 10-
Mbar Ramp machine configuration, developed with a circuit model of the original azimuthally
continuous ITX geometry, we deliberately assigned long-pulse mode only to modules triggered
early in the pulse, thinking that, based on experience shaping pulses for Z, back pulse may pose
a larger problem for LTGS than for SBGS. The final Al 10-Mbar Ramp configuration exhibited
unacceptable LTGS back-pulse effects on only four of the 10 long-pulse modules, specifically those
with time advance smaller than 500 ns.
Another issue faced in pulse-shape design that was not considered here is that of power-flow
losses due to anode-ion plasma generated in the convolute and inner MITL. While the MITL-loss
subroutine in Bertha includes a model for this type of loss [5], its use for shaped pulses is limited
even at the electrical current levels of Z, let alone under extrapolation to NGPP current levels.
The results presented here for manual pulse-shape design lead to the same conclusions discussed
more thoroughly in Section 4.4 for automated pulse-shape design by computational optimization,
primarily that obtaining the desired exemplar pulse shapes requires use of multiple pulse-shape

 40

modes, but does not need either individual control of each module’s Marx charge voltage, nor
individual control of the LTGS and SBGS in each level of a pulse-line triplet. The somewhat
simplified method of setting a reduced number of parameters used above for manual control and
iteration was implemented in a Jupyter notebook using Python with the Stella Python interface
package available with Bertha. Whether using this simplification method or some other improved
one, a graphical user interface to the NGPP circuit model as described in Section 4.4.4 would
help.

4. COMPUTER OPTIMIZATION

4.1. The ASTRID Toolset

The Automated Shot Trial Iterative Design (ASTRID) toolset allows for the automated optimization
of experiment designs for the Z Machine. ASTRID has been used in the development of over a
dozen successful DMP experiments. Traditionally, shot designers must manually adjust Z Machine
pulse forming line LTGS timings and pulse shape type, as well as global Marx charge. All told,
there are over 80 parameters in a single DMP pulse shape design, each of which has discrete, but
sometimes wide ranging, acceptable values, and most DMP pulse shapes are bespoke based on the
experiment objectives. Figure 4-1 presents a conceptual layout of how the ASTRID toolset works
in the most generic sense. Since we already possess target load current pulse shapes, we will use
ASTRID to minimize the RMS difference between the target load current and that produced by the
circuit model values that ASTRID has selected using the Dakota Mesh Adaptive Search (MAD)
algorithm.

4.2. The Challenge of NGPP

As mentioned in Section 2.2, the NGPP point design adopted for this work is an architecture similar
to the Z Machine but with far more pulse forming lines and per-Marx charge voltage capability.
There are 75 Marx banks, each bank feeding three pulse forming lines. Each pulse forming line
has an acceptable LTGS timing from −1000 ns to +200 ns. That means that before including pulse
shape type for each of the pulse forming lines we already have 300 parameters to optimize (75
Marx charge voltages + 225 LTGS times), up from approximately 80 on the Z Machine. We found
that ASTRID was indeed able to optimize this large parameter set, however such design freedom
was not necessary to match exemplar designs.
It was discovered that the parameter space could be substantially reduced after an initial exploration
allowing for optimization of all 225 LTGS times. We found that setting only one of the LTGS times
for each Marx triplet provided sufficient range of accessible pulse shapes. Additionally, setting only
75 LTGS times and allowing for the native offset of the lower two LTGS times (see Section 2.2.2)
allowed for the easy incorporation of pulse shape modes, which was not implemented in the initial
model in a convenient way. Thus in addition to the 75 LTGS times we also have 75 pulse shape
type parameters (termed MODES in the NGPP model), each with three possible pulse shape modes
(Short, Medium, and Long) representing different current rise times.
Optimization of the exemplar problems required the ability to modify 1-75 Marx charges – ev-
erything from a single global Marx charge to each Marx having its own independent charge, with
everything in-between – as well as the pulse shape type. Neither the Marx charge optimization nor

4.3. Reproducing the NGPP Exemplars

Two exemplar problems discussed in Chapter 3 were selected for trial optimization. Keep in mind,

design is capable of producing the exemplar problems, which were developed prior to the selection
of any specific engineering architecture. The problems selected were a 10 MBar aluminum ramp
and a copper shock-ramp (Section 3.1). These were selected because a high pressure ramp is the

flexibility/versatility needed in the NGPP design, and for general debugging and speed testing of

prior to, those in Chapter 3. They also rely exclusively on the serial 1-D circuit model, which, until

4.3.2. Copper Shock-Ramp

For a DMP shot designer, the most difficult target current to pulse shape is the shock-ramp. ASTRID
development targeted shock-ramp optimization from a very early stage, as it is not only the most
difficult to fit but is also of increasing interest to the DMP community. Any future NGPP design
will have to accomplish shock-ramp style pulses if it is to be relevant to DMP mission objectives
and related Stockpile Stewardship goals.
As stated earlier, all tests started with the same initial pulse shape, shown in orange in Figure 4-3,
with the target objective shown in red. A large number of trials were attempted to fit this target pulse,
each time changing the parameter space, increasing, decreasing, or reallocating free parameters
between different circuit model components. All in, approximately two dozen ASTRID trials were
conducted for this exemplar, all with the same starting current. It was quickly found that it was not
possible to converge to even a qualitative match without variable pulse shape modes. Even when
modeling 75 individual Marx charges and 225 LTGS times, an approximate fit to the target could
not be achieved. This is the strongest evidence obtained that NGPP must offer variable pulse shape
modes, at least on each Marx line.
Three pulse shape modes, conceptually equivalent to the Short, Medium, and Long modes on the
Z Machine, were coded into a newer version of the NGPP circuit model (see Section 2.2.3). A
simplification was implemented such that the model took a pulse shape type and LTGS timing
for each Marx line, eliminating the need to individually set LTGS times for every triplet of pulse
forming lines per Marx bank. This had an additional benefit as the range of timing differences
between the three LTGSs in a triplet is smaller than the allowable range of LTGS times due to
reflected pulses and coupling to the same Marx transmission line. Eliminating the ability to set
LTGS times for all 225 lines eliminates this concern. This does mean that each Marx line triplet

4.4. Lessons Learned Concerning Required NGPP Capabilities

Several observations concerning the required NGPP capability to match these above two exemplar
problems have previously been discussed. We will summarize them here and include further
discussion based on ASTRID trial observations.

4.4.1. Fully individualized Marx bank charges are unnecessary.

It has long been felt that the ability to set some subset of Marx banks to different total stored energy
levels would radically benefit pulse shaping for DMP. This has been demonstrated computationally
in the Z Machine circuit model, hence the reason that fully individualized Marx bank charges
were added to the NGPP model. The authors believed that this level of customization would not be
necessary, but we did not have intuitive understanding of what capability would be needed. Through
various trials it appears that DMP pulses would likely be accommodated with no compromise if
NGPP had the ability to adjust the Marx charge across a set of 2–4 groups. Meaning instead of a
global Marx charge for all 75 banks, perhaps there will be two or four Marx charges, each for a
subset of Marx banks. There is benefit to not have these banks evenly split. For comparison, on
the Z Machine a dual Marx firing time trigger is used to extend the temporal duration of realizable
pulse shapes, with adjacent sets of four Marx tied to the same trigger time. This A/B Marx timing
sometimes causes problems when pulse shaping because a designer might find that they need two
of four Marx banks to trigger very early, but those remaining two banks may have to be unused
(bussed out) to accommodate the target current pulse. This type of problem would be exacerbated
if we were changing not the firing time, but the total stored energy in those lines. One suggestion
for Marx charge variability would be to perhaps have three equal size groups with a fourth that is
smaller, something along the line of 23/23/23/6 banks. This would allow for the smaller (6 Marx)
bank to have a much lower charge to accommodate the very early rise time (when low Marx charge
is most often needed) and the remainder of the pulse shape could be at uniform or slightly different
Marx charge.

4.4.2. Pulse shape modes are necessary

As discussed earlier, it was not immediately clear if multiple pulse shape modes would be necessary
on an NGPP machine. This is because of the possible other configuration options available to
designers. It was quickly found, however, that multiple pulse shape modes (initially tested as a
Short, Medium, and Long) are necessary to match any of the DMP exemplar problems discussed in
Chapter 3. Following Z Machine pulse shape modes as a standard, it would likely be beneficial to
include a bussed out mode such that energy from that line does not reach the load region. Doing this
for individual pulse line levels may be complicated by each line’s connection to the two other lines
in the same Marx bank triplet, however if a bussed out configuration, like the Short/Medium/Long
configuration tested here, is set for all lines connected to a single Marx, it may be possible to simply
disconnect that Marx as is presently done on Z when a pulse forming line is damaged.

 50

4.4.3. Fully customizable LTGS timing for each Marx line triplet is likely
 unnecessary

This conclusion is dependent on several of the other factors identified. For instance, the ability to
customize the timing of all 225 pulse forming lines would be necessary if, for example, multiple
pulse shape modes were not implemented. When freely varying all three lines in each Marx triplet,
it was found that while this greatly expanded the parameter space to solve, it did not substantially
improve the customizability of the target current. Much bigger – and easier to comprehend – lever
arms were found in the Marx charge and subsequent addition of pulse shape modes.

4.4.4. A simplified circuit model graphical interface (GUI) is likely necessary

Although we have demonstrated that ASTRID can solve relevant pulse shapes from a default
synchronous starting point, the total computational time suffers. This can, and should, be fixed
by implementing parallelization capability, but in our experience, there will always be a desire to
manually pulse shape certain edge case configurations. A one to one implementation of the ZRBI
GUI presently used on the Z Machine is not tractable for a parameter space of 200+ dimensions.
Greatly simplifying the parameter space (perhaps linking four adjacent Marx lines into a single
configuration, or the specification of subsets as in Section 3.2.2) would reduce the parameter space
to something of the scale that shot designers presently use. It would be straightforward to pair this
simplified GUI to the existing NGPP circuit model, but there may be substantial computational
gains if a matching simplified model were developed. This simplified model could also be used to
accelerate the convergence of ASTRID at the start of a trial, adjusting fewer parameters with large
allowable range in order to get qualitative agreement with a target current pulse. ASTRID could
then switch to the standard, or full, NGPP circuit model to refine, now with more optimization
parameters but with a smaller range of allowable values centered around the convergence of the
simplified model.

4.5. Future Work

Looking forward, many of the capabilities implemented in the NGPP fork of the ASTRID toolset can
benefit work on the Z Machine. This project greatly expanded the number and type of optimization
parameters that can directly aid Z DMP shot design in the near term. Should development work
on ASTRID continue, the first priority should be to implement many of the changes and code
development philosophy tested on the NGPP design into the main Z Machine ASTRID code.
Substantial computation gains can be found by repairing the NGPP parallel circuit model imple-
mentation to cooperate with simultaneous circuit model runs executed by ASTRID. This fix may
be straightforward and can give theoretical gains as discussed earlier in this chapter.

5. NEURAL NETWORK MODELING OF Z

The Z-Machine at Sandia National Laboratories is an electromagnetic pulse generator. The ma-
chine’s behavior is governed by the precise configuration of its 36 independent lines. Predicting
the output current pulse resulting from a specific configuration is computationally intensive using
traditional physics-based simulations, such as the established Bertha circuit model.

This project seeks to develop and validate deep neural network (DNN) models as high-fidelity,
computationally efficient surrogates for the Z-Machine’s operational dynamics that can perform
these predictions orders of magnitude faster. The project was divided into two main principal
thrusts: the development of a Forward Model capable of predicting the output current profile
from a given set of machine input parameters, and a Reverse Model designed to solve the inverse
problem, determining the requisite machine parameters to generate a desired target current profile.
A successful Forward Model enables rapid exploration of the parameter space. A Reverse Model
represents a transformative capability, allowing researchers to design experiments by specifying
a desired outcome and having the model provide the necessary machine settings. This inverse
problem is substantially more difficult due to the non-uniqueness of the solution space, where
multiple input configurations can yield nearly identical output profiles.

5.1. Model Input and Output Representation

The models operate on a standardized representation of the Z-Machine’s configuration and output.

 • Input Parameters: The configuration of the 36 lines is defined by 72 parameters (two per
 line) plus a global Marx voltage and timing group offset.

 1. Pulse Shape: A categorical parameter representing one of a discrete set of allowable
 pulse shapes. This is converted to a one-hot encoded vector for model ingestion.

 2. Timing: A continuous value representing the firing time offset for each line relative to
 a global clock. These values are normalized to a [0, 1] range for stable training.

 • Output Profile: The output current waveform is represented as a time-series vector. To focus
 the model on shape and relative timing, these profiles are also normalized, as to first order
 they all have the same general shape.

 53

5.2. Forward Models

Several types of model architectures were explored for the forward model. All of the models
utilized long short term memory (LSTM), a type of recurrent neural network (RNN), which has
demonstrated an improved ability to handle long time series. The breakthrough came with the
implementation of attentional networks, which demonstrated superior performance by effectively
learning the complex interplay between the 36 input lines. Consequently, this architecture was
selected for all subsequent forward model development.

5.2.1. Monolithic LSTM

The monolithic model was the first attempted because of it’s structural simplicity. It is referred to as
"monolithic" because there is only one sequence of layers in the architecture: all input parameters
are given at the start of the network, and the model produces a current profile. Various numbers of
intermediate layers were tried, as well as passing input parameters to subsequent layers, in addition
to trying residual network techniques. None of the monolithic layers were found to perform well.

5.2.2. Parallel LSTM

The parallel LSTM models are named as such because the first section of the model is a set of
parallel networks, each corresponding to a different line in the Z-machine. As such, this is a
physically informed architecture. The line models share weights because the lines in the Z-machine
are identical, and this also reduces the number of weights in the model.
After the parallel models, there is a monolithic section which aggregates the output of the line models
into a single current profile. Various types of parallel models were tried with different settings
for the intermediate layers, different numbers of intermediate layers, passing input parameters to
intermediate layers, as well as passing the output of intermediate layers to all of the other line
models (so that the architecture could learn how to model the interference between lines). These
architectures were found to produce some of the best results. A block diagram of the parallel LSTM
architecture is shown in Figure 5-2.

This end-to-end approach forces the Reverse Model to learn to produce parameter sets that are valid
and effective, as judged by the Forward Model’s understanding of the system’s physics.

5.3.2. Addressing Parameter Range Constraints

A key issue observed early was that the reverse model would often generate timing parameters out-
side the valid [0, 1] normalized range. We investigated two techniques to enforce this constraint.
 • Technique 1 - Wrap-Around: This method applied a modulus operator to the output timings.
 If a value exceeded 1.0, it would be wrapped back to the start of the range (e.g., 1.05 becomes
 0.05). This technique proved unsuccessful. We hypothesize that the sharp discontinuity in the
 function’s derivative at the wrap-around boundary created an unstable gradient, preventing
 the model from converging effectively.
 • Technique 2 - CLAMP: This method, named after the ‘torch.clamp‘ operation, simply clips
 the output values to the desired range. Any value below 0.0 is set to 0.0, and any value
 above 1.0 is set to 1.0. While there was a concern that parameters might "stick" at the
 boundaries, this was not observed in practice. The CLAMP method successfully constrained
 the parameters to their valid range with no degradation in model performance. It has been
 adopted as the standard practice in our main codebase.

5.3.3. Mitigating Overfitting with Cross-Validation

A significant concern was that the reverse model might overfit to the specific imperfections and
biases of the single forward model instance it was trained against. If this were the case, the
parameters it generates might be effective for that specific forward model but not for the true
physical system or other forward model instances. To diagnose this, we developed a cross-validation
technique for the testing phase. During training, the reverse model uses a single, consistent
forward model to calculate loss. However, at the end of each epoch, during the validation step, a
randomly selected forward model from a pool of independently trained forward models is used for
evaluation.

 59

The results were highly encouraging. The validation loss exhibited smooth and consistent con-
vergence, with a trajectory indistinguishable from tests run without this cross-validation. This
result strongly suggests that the reverse model is learning a generalized mapping that is not over-
specialized to any single forward model. It is effectively generating robust parameter sets that are
valid across an ensemble of forward models, giving us confidence that they are more likely to be
physically meaningful.
An alternative technique—randomizing the forward model during the training loop itself—was
considered but deemed unnecessary at this stage, as our validation tests showed no evidence of
overfitting. This remains a potential strategy for future work if overfitting emerges with larger
datasets or significantly longer training cycles.

5.4. Conclusions and Future Work

This portion of the project advanced the maturity and credibility of neural network surrogate models
for the Z-Machine.
 1. Forward Model Soundness: Continued success with the attentional network architecture
 confirms it is a robust and effective choice for modeling the forward dynamics of the Z-
 Machine.
 2. Reverse Model Viability: We have demonstrated that the autoencoder-style training paradigm
 is a sound and effective method for solving the ill-posed inverse problem.
 3. Technical Challenges Solved: The development of the CLAMP technique provides a simple
 and effective solution for enforcing physical constraints on model outputs. Furthermore, our
 cross-validation results provide strong evidence that the reverse model is not overfitting and
 is learning a generalizable solution to the inverse problem.
The ultimate validation of the reverse model is to test its generated parameters on the authoritative
Bertha circuit model or on the Z-Machine itself. We recommend that our partners at Sandia
National Laboratories use the line parameters generated by our final reverse model as inputs for
the Bertha code. This would provide an objective, quantitative measure of the model’s real-world
accuracy. While the Bertha model cannot be used directly within our training loop (as we cannot
backpropagate gradients through it), this offline validation would be an invaluable final assessment
of the project’s success and a critical step toward operational deployment.

6. CONCLUSIONS

This project evaluated the pulse shaping capabilities of next-generation pulsed power architectures
necessary for dynamic materials properties experiments and looked at automated techniques to
assist in experiment design.
A circuit model of a proposed NGPP point design was developed. The circuit model included inde-
pendent representations of the pulse forming lines to allow for independent timing and pulse modes
to be generated by each line. It also included a 2-D transmission line mesh of the radial impedance
transformer to model effects of azimuthal non-uniformity within the impedance transformer.
The circuit model was used to demonstrate the applicability of NGPP designs to the DMP mission.
A manual design process iteratively ran the circuit model to determine machine configurations that
produce the necessary load currents for next-generation DMP experiments. It was identified that
the design approach had to initially use a simplified 1-D model, before confirming the designs
with the 2-D model, in order to achieve practical simulation run times. This approach highlighted
the importance of arranging the pulse-forming lines to enhance azimuthal uniformity within the
impedance transformer, minimizing discrepancies between the 1-D and 2-D models. Additionally,
features such as multiple Marx charge voltages and varying pulse lengths from the pulse forming
lines were found to be crucial for achieving the desired load current pulse shapes.
Two computer automated methods were explored to assist in DMP experiment design. The first
method extended a Z experiment design tool, ASTRID, to the NGPP circuit model, utilizing
derivative-free optimization to automate running the Bertha circuit model to converge on the
desired load current. The second method developed deep neural network surrogate models of
Z, eliminating the need to run the Bertha circuit model altogether. Bertha simulation data from
Z was used to train a forward model (machine parameters to load current) and a reverse model
(load current to machine parameters). These neural network models were used in combination
and shown to generate a machine configuration from a desired load current and verify it in under
one second. While these methods would likely require more development to be used without any
manual interaction, they are both promising to be used in conjunction with manual design to reduce
the amount of manual iteration necessary in experiment design.

C.5.4.1. Morlet Wavelet Generation

def _create_wavelet_kernels(self) -> torch.Tensor:
 """Create Morlet wavelet kernels for different scales"""
 kernels = []

    for scale in self.scales: # [8, 16, 32, 64, 128]
 kernel_size = min(scale * 8, 200) # Adaptive kernel size
 t = torch.linspace(-3, 3, kernel_size)

 # Morlet wavelet: exp(-t²/2) * cos(5t)
 gaussian = torch.exp(-t**2 / 2)
 oscillation = torch.cos(5 * t)
 wavelet = gaussian * oscillation

 # Normalize for energy conservation
 wavelet = wavelet / torch.norm(wavelet)
 kernels.append(wavelet)

 return torch.stack(kernels) # [num_scales, kernel_size]

class MultiScaleAttention(nn.Module):
  """Attention mechanism that focuses on relevant frequency scales"""

  def __init__(self, d_model, num_scales):
    super().__init__()
 self.scale_weights = nn.Linear(d_model, num_scales)
 self.feature_transform = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model)

 # Enhanced Morlet wavelet with configurable central frequency
 t = torch.linspace(-4, 4, scale * 6)

        # Morlet: exp(-t²/2) * exp(iw_0t)

C.7.6.1. Natural Gradient Balance

def compute_coupled_loss(self, predictions, targets):
 """Coupled loss ensures balanced learning between timing and shape"""
 timing_pred, shape_pred = predictions
 timing_target, shape_target = targets

 # Compute losses for each pulse individually
 pulse_losses = []
 for i in range(self.num_pulses):
 timing_loss = F.mse_loss(timing_pred[:, i], timing_target[:, i])
 shape_loss = F.cross_entropy(shape_pred[:, i], shape_target[:, i])

 # Balanced combination for each pulse
 pulse_loss = timing_loss + shape_loss * self.timing_scale_factor
 pulse_losses.append(pulse_loss)

 return torch.stack(pulse_losses).mean()

C.8. Training Metrics and Monitoring

C.8.1. MSE Ratio Metric

This metric is essentially the ratio between the MSE from the current predicted by the reverse
model and the MSE predicted by the forward model, both with respect to the original current. This
metric is sensitive to the forward model performance, purely for user monitoring.

MSE_ratio = MSE_reverse_model / MSE_forward_model

 • MSE_ratio = 1.0: Perfect reconstruction - reverse model performs as well as forward model
 • MSE_ratio > 1.0: Reverse model is worse than forward model baseline
 • MSE_ratio < 1.0: Reverse model reconstruction is better than baseline
