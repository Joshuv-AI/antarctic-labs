Reply to: Reassessing the evidence linking clinical leadership to AI deployment outcomes
Authors: Qilu Li, Haijing Hao, Rema Padman, J Travis Gossey, Jiang Bian, Yiye Zhang
Published: 2026

We thank Dr Bair for the interest in our article. Dr Bair raises three primary concerns: (1) the definition of “impact” in a field prone to publication bias, (2) the stability of our statistical modeling given the small number of non-significant results, and (3) the validity of using last authorship as a proxy for leadership. In this response to Dr Bair, we clarify the organizational science perspective underpinning our analysis of leadership in healthcare AI deployment. At the same time, we wish to reaffirm our view that the original findings represent a meaningful first step toward characterizing the role of team leadership in AI deployment in healthcare. In what follows, we (1) acknowledge explicitly that the causal language used in parts of our original paper should be interpreted with caution and clarify how we interpret the observed association; (2) engage with the mediation problem that Dr. Bair identified in relation to trial design and geography; and (3) describe the expanded contributor-level data collection currently underway in our group and outline what we believe this research needs next.

The definition of ‘impact’ and publication bias

Dr Bair observes that 88% of the included studies reported positive findings, and rightfully argues that our outcome measure may therefore proxy the probability of publication rather than intrinsic clinical efficacy. We agree and recognize this constraint in our original manuscript, in Discussion (page 5), noting that our analysis is “limited by the publication bias… potentially restricting underlying patterns.” We further acknowledge that utilizing a binary classification based on statistical significance may overlook the granular heterogeneity of trial endpoints. We would like to clarify that we interpret these findings as indicators of organizational output, viewed through our theoretical framework, the Upper Echelons Theory. In the complex landscape of AI deployment, the capacity of a team lead by IT and/or clinical leaders to design, execute, and successfully disseminate a study with a favorable conclusion represents a measurable organizational output signal. Furthermore, even within a literature skewed toward positive reporting, the significant divergence in success rates between clinician-led and technologist-led teams remains a salient phenomenon that warrants empirical investigation. Accordingly, we interpret the observed association as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of underlying clinical efficacy or causal leadership effects, and as motivation for future work using richer outcome definitions in the team science setting. We do recognize that the depth of concern extends well beyond the existence of publication bias. The outcome can be approximating the probability that a study is publishable rather than the probability that an AI intervention produced meaningful clinical benefit. This conflation places a constraint on the causal claims that the data can support, and we accordingly agree that statements in the original paper that may be interpreted causally should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Statistical robustness of results

Regarding statistical instability due to sparse non-events, Dr Bair notes that logistic regression can yield unstable estimates when the outcome is rare. We recognize this statistical hazard. As we reported, when we restricted our analysis to the subset of 96 randomized clinical trials (RCTs), the association between clinical leadership and impact strengthened with a very wide range of confidence interval, and this can be due to the sparsity of data (OR 19.90, P = 0.047). We do agree with Dr Bair’s characterization of our work as “hypothesis-generating”; accordingly, these estimates should be interpreted as suggestive rather than definitive in the presence of rare non-events. As we concluded in our discussion, our goal was to “add empirical evidence… thereby quantifying the relationship between team leadership structure and real-world impact”, serving as a prompt for future, more granular analyses. As Dr Bair said, the concern is structural rather than statistical: an issue is the position of these variables within the underlying causal graph, not whether we included them as covariates. As we initially stated in the Discussion, we intend to incorporate mediation analyses into our follow-up work, decomposing observed leadership-outcome association into direct and indirect (design-mediated and region-mediated) effects, and we commit to reporting these results transparently, including analyses that fail to replicate the original associations.

Authorship as a proxy

Regarding authorship as a proxy for leadership, Dr Bair highlights that last authorship is a noisy signal that may mask hybrid expertise or vary by discipline, including cross-trained physician–informaticians who may not be well captured by clinician/technologist labels. We agree and devoted significant space in our original limitations section to this exact issue. We also acknowledge that we could not fully discern the “hybrid” training of leaders, such as clinicians with dual technical degrees. To mitigate this, we attempted to validate leadership through a secondary variable, authors’ “Organizational Leadership” role (e.g., Directors, Chiefs), though we find this data is often inconsistent or outdated in certain public profiles. We agree that future work should move beyond bibliometric proxies.

We agree with the comments made by Dr Bair that a binary clinician/technologist label applied only to first and last authors is only an imperfect proxy for the leadership and expertise structures it is intended to capture, particularly in multidisciplinary AI work, where hybrid clinician–informaticians and substantive middle-author contributions are common. To address this limitation, our team has undertaken an expanded data-collection effort. Rather than relying on first- and last-author labels alone, we are coding contributor-level data for every author in every included study. Specifically, we are collecting: educational background, including all degrees and postgraduate training, with explicit identification of dual MD-PhD holders for every author; professional roles and titles at or near the time of the study, where retrievable from contemporaneous sources, in addition to current titles; team-level composition variables, including the proportion of clinicians, technologists, and hybrid clinician-informaticians across the full author list, allowing the unit of analysis to shift from a single-author proxy to a team-structure measure. These data will support several analyses that the original paper could not, including whether team composition, rather than last-author label drives the observed association; whether hybrid expertise, when separated out, accounts for some part of the apparent clinician advantage; and whether middle-author composition, which often reflecting the contributions of implementers, statisticians, and clinical-workflow leads carries independent signal. We anticipate that these analyses will refine and clarify parts of our initial associations, and they will, in turn, motivate the prospective implementation-science evaluations of team and organizational determinants of AI deployment that we believe the field needs next.

Confounders

We agree that the distinction between “AI-assisted” (augmentation) and “AI versus routine care” (substitution) is fundamental and might further shape the results. Our study also tried to control for these factors. In our analysis, we control for “Comparison Type” (Assisted vs. Routine) and “Region” (North America, Asia, Others) in our logistic regression. However, these covariates were not statistically significant in our multivariable model, and the association between leadership background and the outcome persisted after adjustment. This suggests that the “leadership effect” we observed is not solely explained by these measured design and geography variables, while we acknowledge that residual confounding and mediation remain plausible given the observational nature of the dataset. Furthermore, from the perspective of Upper Echelons Theory, we argue that clinician leaders’ disproportionate preference for “augmentation” designs (75% vs. 35% for technologists) may represent one plausible pathway linking leadership background to observed outcomes. If clinician leaders are more likely to select trial designs that are feasible, workflow-compatible, and likely to succeed (augmentation), this is consistent with our premise that domain expertise may shape strategic design choices in AI deployment studies.

In summary, we view our findings neither as a dismissal of technical leadership nor a simple and direct causal claim about clinicians’ superiority, but rather as an initial quantitative step toward understanding how team composition in team science influences the conduct and reporting of AI deployment and implementation in healthcare. As health systems continue to scale AI-enabled care, a deeper, prospective evaluation of team dynamics is essential. We welcome this commentary as a constructive dialogue that moves the field toward more rigorous standards for studying contributor roles (e.g., who led workflow design, implementation, and evaluation) and organizational determinants (e.g., resources, readiness, governance) in AI deployment.
