A novel method to quantify viable Enterococcus faecium during feed manufacturing
Authors: Ying Zhang, Songjun Jiao, Xuetao Liu, Cong Ren, Hu Zhang, Yuming Wang, Feng Zhao, Jingjing Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium) is vulnerable to feed manufacturing, and viability loss in pellet feed has led to compromised probiotic efficacy. This study aimed to develop a propidium monoazide (PMA)-qPCR method to quantify viable E. faecium in feed and to investigate the protective effect of different coating technologies during feed manufacturing. First, High amplication efficiency (E = 96.2%, R2 = 0.997) and no cross-reactivity with 7 feed-related bacteria was validated by EF2 primers and no cross-reactivity with 7 feed-relevant bacterial species. An optimal concentration of PMA at 40 μM was confirmed to bind to over 99% of deal cells by flow cytometry. Artificial intestinal fluid with trysin could release 94.2% cells from coated E. faecium, which was superior than physiological saline, sodium citrate, artificial intestinal fluid (P < 0.05). With the addition of hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB), the DNA extraction efficiency for coating E. faecium was increased by 54.3%. Collectively, this established PMA-qPCR method had high sensitivity (E = 101.1%; R2 = 0.991) and could detect live and dead cells with high accuracy (observed vs. set, R2 = 0.998). Next, using this method, the survival of different coated E. faecium during feed manufacturing was investigated through a split-split-plot design. As the conditioning temperature rose from 65 °C to 85 °C, overall survival decreased from 72.4% to 12.2% (P < 0.05), in which uncoated and post-encapsulated E. faecium significantly dropped from 85.7% to 8.1% and 13.8% to 3.2% from conditioning to pelleting at 65 °C (P < 0.05), with no further change after cooling. In contrast, pre-encapsulated E. faecium exhibited significantly higher survival than both uncoated and post-encapsulated products at all processing steps and temperatures (P < 0.05). In conclusion, the PMA-qPCR method is valuable to quantify viable E. faecium in feed. The pre‑encapsulated E. faecium tested in this study could improve resistance to heat and mechanical force compared to uncoated and post-encapsulated formulations.

Introduction

Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium) is a Gram-positive bacterium that has been recognized as a safe feeding probiotic for over forty years. According to our prior study, it was revealed that the total amount of E. faecium in the ileum was positively correlated with the average daily gain of broiler chickens (Zhang et al., 2024). Although E. faecium can survive in a wide range of temperatures and pH, feed processing poses significant challenges to its viability (Franz et al., 2011; Min et al., 2020). Heating E. faecium in a water bath at 75, 85, and 95 °C for 180, 120, and 70 s could reduce 3, 6 and > 6.5 log10CFU/g of viable cells (Coe et al., 2022). Recently, a pilot feed mill test also reported that pelleting mash broiler feed at 70, 75, 80, and 85 °C decreased E. faecium cell counts by 0.81, 1.18, 1.69, and 1.94 log10CFU/g after 115 s, respectively (Coe et al., 2025). These results clearly showed that E. faecium could be inactivated during feed processing, which compromises its viability and colonization in the gut.

Coating techniques are widely used to enhance heat tolerance for different probiotics (Abbaszadeh et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015; Yao et al., 2020; Kwon et al., 2025). There are two major ways to apply coating techniques, either pre-encapsulating probiotics inside coating matrices to grow or encapsulating probiotics after harvesting (Yao et al., 2020). Several studies have confirmed that these two coating technologies did not provide equal protection for microorganisms against heat exposure (Abbaszadeh et al., 2014; Kwon et al., 2025). The viability of E. faecium in the pellet feed is a good indicator of the efficacy of different coating technologies during the feed process. However, with the plate count method, coating structures restrain E. faecium from forming clones on the plate, leading to inaccurate enumeration (Champagne et al., 2011; Pereira et al., 2023).

Therefore, this study aimed to develop a new method to enumerate viable E. faecium cells in feed. qPCR has been used for bacterial population quantification, which has several advantages over the plate count method (Lima et al., 2023). qPCR has high specificity. In contrast, the culture method using the selective medium for E. faecium can also grow Enterococcus faecalis and Lactococcus lactis, which are commonly used as feed additives (Maheux et al., 2011; Davis et al., 2022). Compared to the culture method, qPCR does not require growing bacterial cells and is very time-efficient. However, the application of qPCR cannot quantify viable bacterial cells, because DNA can be extracted from both dead and live cells. In recent studies, propidium monoazide (PMA), a dye that labels the DNA of dead cells with membrane damage (Nocker et al., 2006; Chiao et al., 2014; Scariot et al., 2018), has been used to develop a PCR-based method to quantify viable E. faecium cells. This method was optimized to quantify viable E. faecium regardless of coating treatment and further used to compare the effects of different coating technologies on the survival of E. faecium during feed processing. These findings will benefit the rational application of E. faecium additives in poultry production.

Materials and methods

The culture method to enumerate viable Enterococcus faecium

Enterococcus faecium (CICC 20430) purchased from the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC, Beijing, China) was used as the reference strain to develop the PMA-qPCR method. It was first grown in the de Man-Rogosa-Sharpe (MRS) broth (Cat. # 02-293, Beijing Aoboxing Biology Technology CO., Ltd., Beijing, China) at 37 °C overnight. Then, 1% (v/v) liquid bacterial culture was inoculated into fresh MRS broth with shaking at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 24 h to activate and later maintained as glycerol stocks (50% v/v, Cat. # G8190, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at −80 °C. To enumerate E. faecium cells, 3% (v/v) glycerol stock was reinoculated into MRS broth and shaken at 37 °C, 200 rpm until the culture reached the logarithmic growth phase and then diluted to approximately 2 log10CFU/mL cells with physiological saline (Cat. # IN9004, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and 1 mL of the dilution was transferred into a sterile Petri dish in duplicate. Subsequently, 20 mL of MRS agar at 50 °C was poured into each dish, swirled gently to mix with the bacteria, and then allowed to solidify at room temperature. All plates were invertedly incubated at 37 °C for 48 h and colonies were counted to quantify viable E. faecium cells.

Specificity of qPCR for E. faecium amplification

Genomic DNA of E. faecium cells was extracted using the TIANamp bacterial DNA kit (Cat. # DP302-02, Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The protocol was modified as follows: the lysozyme treatment was extended from 30 min to 80 min at 37 °C, and the proteinase K digestion condition was changed from 70 °C for 10 min to 65 °C for 30 min. To quantify E. faecium via qPCR, primer sets were designed targeted at the pbp5, ddl, csp, dbp, tnase and recG genes of E. faecium (Table 1), respectively, and synthesized by Liuhe BGI Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). QPCR was performed in a QuantStudio 7 Flex Fast Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, USA). Reactions were performed in a 20 μL system containing 10 μL of 2 × TB Green Premix Ex Taq Ⅱ (Cat. # RR802A, Takara Biotechnology, Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), 0.4 μL of ROX Reference Dye Ⅱ (50 ×), 0.8 μL (10 μM) of each primer, 2 μL of DNA templates, and 6 μL of nuclease-free water. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 34 s. Primers for testing.

The qPCR primers for E. faecium were also used to test cross-reactivity with several other feed-relevant probiotics or pathogens, including Bacillus subtilis (CICC 10090), Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (CICC 23089), Bacillus licheniformis (CICC 10108), Escherichia coli (CICC 10413), Lactobacillus reuteri (CICC 6119), Lactobacillus reuteri (CICC 6123), and Lactobacillus johnsonii (CICC 10862). They were all purchased from CICC. The qPCR products were then also subjected to 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized with a UV transilluminator.

Finally, the amplification efficiency (E) of the specific primer for E. faecium was determined by performing qPCR on serially diluted E. faecium cells ranging from 8 to 1 log10CFU and calculated using the formula: E = (10slope/−1 − 1) × 100%. The predicted equation for total E. faecium cell count was obtained by the regression of Ct values against the cell counts quantified by the culture method.

Optimized concentration of PMA for labeling inactivated E. faecium

A suspension of viable E. faecium at a final concentration of 8.48 log10CFU/mL was prepared. To obtain inactivated cells, the viable suspension was heated in a 100 °C water bath for 5 min. Complete inactivation was confirmed by the absence of colony growth on the MRS agar plates after incubation at 37 °C for 72 h. Then, a 2 mM working solution of PMA (Cat. # 40013, Biotium, Inc., Hayward, CA) was prepared by dissolving 1 mg PMA in 980 μL of sterile enzyme-free water and stored at −20 °C away from light. The viable and inactivated E. faecium cells (500 μL each) were resuspended in 0.85% sterile saline to prepare bacterial suspensions. Then, the PMA working solution was added to 500 μL of these bacterial suspensions to achieve final concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 μM, respectively. The mixtures were incubated on a shaker at 180 rpm in the dark for 15 min at room temperature to allow the penetration of PMA into the inactivated cells. Then the mixtures were laid on the top of ice and exposed to a 460 ∼ 470 nm LED light (model 2835DB, Xuzhou Aijia Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) at a distance of 20 cm for 15 min in a dark box. Each tube was shaken every 5 min to ensure complete exposure to the light. Afterwards, bacterial pellets were obtained by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 5 min and washed once with sterile enzyme-free water to remove residual PMA. The efficacy of PMA binding to the DNA of inactivated cells and the potential toxicity to viable E. faecium cells were confirmed using flow cytometry (FCM) analyses.

For the FCM analysis, bacterial pellets were resuspended in 500 μL of 0.85% sterile saline and then analyzed on a FACSVerse flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ). A 488 nm laser was used, and each sample was run at a medium flow rate, ensuring no less than 10000 events were acquired. The fluorescence from PMA staining was detected at a wavelength of 585 nm with a bandpass of 42 nm. The intensity of PMA of 100% inactivated cells and the Forward Scatter Area (FSC-A) was used to gate the live/dead cells. Viable E. faecium treated without PMA was used as a negative control to set the gating fluorescence threshold. The FCM data were visualized using the 2D plots. The ratios of live and dead cells out of the total cells were analyzed. The results of FCM were also validated by the qPCR to compare the Ct values of E. faecium cells when subjected to different concentrations of PMA.

The release solution and DNA extraction for coated E. faecium

Because coating techniques commonly applied to improve the resistance of E. faecium products could interfere with cell release and DNA extraction, proper releasing solution and DNA extraction procedure were investigated to ensure the efficacy of genomic DNA extraction for coated E. faecium. The coated E. faecium product with a cell count of 11 log10CFU/g was a courtesy of Dr. Weiwei Wang from the Academy of National Food and Strategic Reserves Administration (Beijing, China), and the identity of E. faecium was confirmed by DNA sequencing. Five releasing solutions, including 0.85% physiological saline, 67 mM sodium citrate solution (pH 8.5), 67 mM sodium citrate solution (pH 9.6), artificial intestinal fluid (20 mM KCl, 95 mM NaCl, 33 mM Na2HPO4, 145 mM NaH2PO4, pH = 6.8) and artificial intestinal fluid with 48 U/mL trypsin (Cat. # A600626, Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used to release E. faecium from the microcapsules. Fifty milligrams of coated E. faecium were added with 50 mL of one of the releasing solutions, respectively, and shaken at 220 rpm for 30 min at 37 °C with 6.8 g of 4 mm diameter glass beads. The number of E. faecium cells was enumerated using the plate method as abovementioned and quantified by the PMA-qPCR as well. For total E. faecium cells, the release efficiency of each releasing solution was expressed as the ratio of enumerated cell counts by the culture method to their total cell counts. For viable E. faecium cells, the release efficiency was expressed as the ratio of the PMA-qPCR-detected cell counts to cell counts enumerated by the culture method.

To mitigate the interference from coating materials during DNA extraction, an additional treatment with hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium Bromide (CTAB) was involved in genomic DNA purification. After incubating E. faecium cells with lysosome and proteinase K, 280 μL of CTAB solution (2% w/v CTAB in 100 mM pH = 8.0 Tris-HCl, 20 mM EDTA, 1.4 M NaCl) was added, and incubation was continued at 65 °C for 15 min. Then, 500 μL of phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) was added, mixed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with 360 μL of 75% ethanol, and DNA was finally purified using the spin column provided by TIANamp bacterial DNA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total DNA extraction efficiency was estimated as the ratio of the qPCR-detected cell counts to the total cell counts.

Validation of the PMA-qPCR method in quantifying viable E. faecium in feed

To test whether the feed interferes with the PMA-qPCR, E. faecium was inoculated into the feed extract and then proceeded with the PMA-qPCR to quantify the viable cells. A corn-soybean basal diet was sterilized by 30 kGy irradiation for 17 h to eliminate viable bacteria avoid false-positive results. The sterile feed extract (10 g) was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube and suspended in 39 mL of artificial intestinal fluid with trypsin. One milliliter of serial dilutions of E. faecium was inoculated to the feed extract to reach final concentrations from 1 to 8 log10CFU/mL viable cells. The optimized PMA-qPCR for E. faecium was conducted with 500 μL of the bacterial inoculum and the predicted equation for E. faecium cell count was obtained by the best fit of the Ct values of PMA-qPCR against the cell counts.

To further validate the accuracy of the PMA-qPCR method in quantifying viable E. faecium in feed, 8.63 log10CFU/mL live and inactivated cells were mixed in various portions to prepare bacterial suspensions containing 0, 10, 30, 50, 70, 90, and 100% live cells and inoculated to the sterilized feed extract. The optimized PMA-qPCR procedure was followed for viable E. faecium quantification. The observed proportion of live cells was calculated by dividing the viable cell counts quantified by PMA-qPCR to the total cell counts quantified by the culture method. A linear regression analysis was conducted between the setting and the observed proportion of live cells in the feed extract. A regression line that did not significantly deviate from the line y = x indicated high accuracy.

Survival of E. faecium during steam conditioning-pelleting feed process

Three types of E. faecium products, uncoated, pre-encapsulated and post-encapsulated E. faecium, were used to investigate the survival of E. faecium during the feed process using a pilot feed mill. Of them, the uncoated and pre-encapsulated products were provided by the Challenge Group Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) with a cell count of 11 log10CFU/g. The post-encapsulated E. faecium was provided by the Academy of National Food and Strategic Reserves Administration (Beijing, China) with a cell count of 11 log10CFU/g. The identity of E. faecium in all products was confirmed with DNA sequencing.

A split-split-plot design was implemented to determine the survival rate of three types of E. faecium after conditioning, pelleting and cooling when conditioned at the temperatures of 65, 75, and 85 °C, respectively, with temperature as the main plot and feed processing step, bacterial formulation as subplots. A corn-soybean meal basal diet was formulated to meet or exceed the nutritional requirements of 0 ∼ 21 d Arbor Acres (AA) broilers (Table 2). A masterbatch of mash feed was first mixed and allocated to 18 experimental batches. For each experimental batch, one of the E. faecium products was added to the basal diet at a concentration of 6 log10CFU/g and mixed thoroughly before feed manufacture and subjected to steam conditioning at 65, 75, and 85 °C sequentially. The pilot feed mill (Jiangsu Muyang Group Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) was equipped with three temperature sensors and steam valves at the beginning, middle and end of the conditioners, respectively. The temperature for conditioning was automatically adjusted by turning on/off the steam valves and precisely maintained at the set temperature throughout the conditioner. Before each batch of feed manufacture, the feed mill was cleaned with 10 ∼ 15 kg of corn. A total of 120 kg of the premixed experimental feed was loaded into the storage bin. The experimental feed was conveyed to the conditioner and first conditioned at 65 °C for 30 s. Conditioned feed was then extruded through a 4 mm die at the ring-die compression ratio of 6:1. Once the conditioning temperature reached 65 °C and the pelleting mill operated smoothly for 15 min, feed samples were simultaneously taken from four outlets every minute for 10 min. The four outlets were located at the bottom of the storage bin (pre-conditioning), at the end of the conditioner (conditioning), the pelletor (pelleting) and the cooler (cooling), respectively. Each sample was stored in a sterile sampling bag at 4 °C. After completing the sampling, conditioning temperature was elevated to 75 °C, and then feed samples were collected once the temperature stabilized at 75 °C. The same procedure was followed to collect feed samples conditioned at 85 °C.

To enumerate viable E. faecium cells, the 10 feed samples collected from each processing step were first pooled and homogenized. Then, 10 g subsamples was suspended in 40 mL of artificial intestinal fluid with trypsin within a 50 mL sterile centrifuge tube and incubated in a shaker at 37 °C, 220 rpm for 30 min to release E. faecium cells. Then, 500 μL of feed suspension was sampled and treated with PMA solution. Genomic DNA was then extracted using the CTAB-assisted DNA extraction method and amplified by qPCR as described above. For each feed sample, the PMA-PCR was repeated twice. The number of viable E. faecium (log10CFU/g) was calculated by the prediction equation. The survival rates of E. faecium after conditioning, pelleting and cooling were calculated by divided by the total viable count in the feed sample collected from the storage bin, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The experiments for optimizing PMA, the release solution, and DNA extraction adopted a completely randomized design and were performed in triplicate. The experimental results were subjected to one-way ANOVA using the REML model of JMP 16.0 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC). A linear model was used to generate the predicted equation from the Ct values of the PMA-qPCR and viable cell counts using the REG model of SAS 9.4 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC). A TEST statement in the REG model of SAS 9.4 was performed to determine whether the intercept and slope of the regression between the observed and set viable E. faecium ratio differ from 0 to 1, respectively, reflecting the deviation of the regression line from the line y = x. The mixed model was used to test the fixed effects of E. faecium formulation, conditioning temperature, feed processing step and their interactions on the survival rates of E. faecium and the randomized effects of block, the two-way interaction of block and conditioning temperature, and the three-way interaction of block, conditioning temperature and feed processing step. All data were expressed as mean ± SEM and statistical significance was only considered when P < 0.05.

Results

Specificity of qPCR for E. faecium amplification

The qPCR results revealed that five out of the seven primer sets, including TraP, DUF4231, FAEFOR, Ef2, and Ef1, successfully amplified E. faecium (Fig. 1A). Among these, only the Ef2 primer set did not exhibit cross-reactivity with the other 7 feed-relevant bacterial species (Fig. 1B). The Ef2 primer set had an amplification efficiency of 96.2% (Fig. 1C). According to the serial dilution tests, a good linear correlation between Ct values and E. faecium counts (Log10CFU) was obtained from qPCR standard curve (y = −0.2928x + 11.772, whereas x as Ct value of qPCR, y as cell count, R2 = 0.997). The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) of the qPCR method were 2 log10CFU and 2.38 log10CFU per reaction, respectively. Optimized concentration of PMA for labeling inactivated E. faecium

To selectively quantify viable E. faecium cells via PMA-qPCR by removing genomic DNA from dead cells, the optimal PMA concentration was studied. The FCM analysis showed that 10 μM of PMA was sufficient to label inactivated E. faecium cells, as over 99% of cells were PMA-positive (Table 3). The proportion of PMA-positive stained cells peaked at 99.63% with 40 μM PMA and decreased at higher concentrations (Table 3, Fig. 2B). In contrast, of live E. faecium cells, the proportion of PMA-positive cells increased as the concentration of PMA increased from 0 to 60 μM (Table 3, Fig. 2C-D). When using the PMA-qPCR method, the Ct values of inactivated E. faecium increased as the PMA concentration rose, peaking at 50 μM (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E). Conversely, the Ct values of viable E. faecium remained stable when cells were treated with 0 ∼ 40 μM PMA but increased significantly when PMA exceeded 50 μM (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E). Optimization of releasing solution and DNA extraction for coated E. faecium

Using the culture-based method to enumerate E. faecium cells, it was found that only 34.0% of cells were released when coated E. faecium cells were suspended in 0.85% physiological saline (Fig. 3A). The cell counts of coated E. faecium determined using the culture method were significantly increased by 59.6%, 55.2%, 55.8%, and 41.5% when suspended in the sodium citrate solution (pH 8.5), sodium citrate solution (pH 9.6), artificial intestinal fluid, or artificial intestinal fluid with trypsin, respectively (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A). Sensitivity and accuracy of PMA-qPCR method in quantifying viable E. faecium in feed

A strong linear correlation was observed between the Ct values of PMA-qPCR and the viable E. faecium counts from the three independent experiments (R2 = 0.991, P < 0.05, Fig. 4A), which generated the best fit of the prediction equation: Log10CFU = −0.3034 × Ct + 11.966. The LOD and LOQ of the PMA-qPCR method were 2 log10CFU and 2.67 log10CFU per reaction, respectively. When adjusted with the inclusion level, the LOD and LOQ in feed were 3 log10CFU/g and 3.97 log10CFU/g, respectively. With the mock mixture of viable and inactivated cells, the observed proportion of viable cells quantified by PMA-qPCR was found to be highly correlated with the set proportion (R2 = 0.998, P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). Survival of viable E. faecium during different feed processing steps

A significant two-way interaction was observed between processing step and E. faecium formulation on the survival rates of both uncoated and post-encapsulated E. faecium (F4,90 = 3.02, P = 0.022). Therefore, two statistical models were used to investigate the main effects of temperature, formulation and feed processing. In model 1 (Table 4), which analyzed the fixed effects of temperature, processing step and their interaction for each E. faecium formulation, the survival rates of uncoated E. faecium showed a significant drop from conditioning to pelleting at 65 °C, decreasing from 85.7% to 8.1% (P < 0.05), and at 75 °C, decreasing from 51.7% to 7.0% (P < 0.05), with no further significant change after cooling (P > 0.05). Post-encapsulated E. faecium followed a similar trend at 65 °C, falling from 13.8% to 3.2% (P < 0.05). At higher temperatures, survival rates remained low across all processing steps and did not differ significantly for uncoated E. faecium conditioned under 85 °C and post-encapsulated E. faecium conditioned under 75 °C and 85 °C (P > 0.05). In contrast, the survival rate of pre-encapsulated E. faecium did not differ significantly across either conditioning temperatures or processing steps (P > 0.05). In model 2 (Table 5), which focused on conditioning temperature effects across formulations, increasing conditioning temperature from 65 to 85 °C led to a significant reduction in survival during the conditioning step from 72.4% to 12.2% (P < 0.05), an effect driven primarily by the uncoated E. faecium. No significant temperature-dependent decline was observed during pelleting and cooling steps. Notably, pre-encapsulated E. faecium exhibited persistently higher survival than uncoated and post-encapsulated E. faecium across all processing stages (P < 0.05). Discussion

Given that the quantity of viable E. faecium in feed was critical for its function, a novel and simple PMA-qPCR method was developed in the current study for quantifying viable E. faecium in feed. The PMA-qPCR method had greater advantages over the culture method to quantify E. faecium in several aspects. First of all, the PMA-qPCR method could handle more samples in a batch than the culture method. It took about 8 h to get the quantification results, which was much faster than the culture method. Secondly, it was demonstrated by the qPCR results that the primer set Ef2 used in the PMA-qPCR for E. faecium had good specificity for E. faecium without amplifying 7 feed-relevant probiotics or pathogens commonly present in feed. The high specificity of the qPCR-based method could effectively avoid contamination from other bacteria in feed; however, the culture method was hard to distinguish E. faecium from other species of Enterococcus. Thirdly, the interference from the coating technology of E. faecium was fully addressed in the PMA-qPCR by pretreating with trypsin-containing artificial intestinal fluid to release cells and adding CTAB to eliminate the adverse effect of coating materials on DNA extraction. E. faecium was commonly encapsulated to enhance the vitality of probiotics during feed processing and gastrointestinal digestion (Seyedehhamideh Razavi, 2021; Xie et al., 2023). However, it was noted that encapsulated E. faecium couldn’t form colonies well on the agar plate, which significantly lowered the viable cell counts when enumerating cells using the culture method.

The viability of E. faecium when it reaches the intestine can be affected by many aspects, from feed processing to stomach digestion (Strasser et al., 2009; Azarkhavarani et al., 2019; Coe et al., 2025). To achieve probiotic effects in a metabolically active state through all different conditions, the most cost-effective way is to encapsulate probiotics with different coating materials, such as alginate, starch, chitosan, milk proteins and so on (Misra et al., 2022). The ideal coating technology for probiotics is resistant to harsh conditions but can readily release the bacterium in the intestine to grow in the target place. Some studies reported that the probiotics inside microcapsules were released at the neutral pH in the intestine while maintaining high cell viability (Cook et al., 2012; Kvakova et al., 2021). But the physiological saline could only release about 30% of the coated E. faecium used in the current study (Fig. 3A). One of the characteristic features of commonly used coating materials is pH-sensitivity, which enables the bacteria to survive in low-pH environments in the stomach and release in high-pH environments in the intestine. Therefore, the sodium citrate of pH 8.5 was reported to release alginate-coated E. faecium (Smidsrød and Skjåk-Braek, 1990). Here, approximately 90% of E. faecium was noted to be released in the sodium citrate solutions with pH 8.5 and 9.6 when the culture method was used to quantify viable cells, but only about 60% of free E. faecium was noted when the PMA-qPCR method was used to quantify viable cells (Fig. 3A). The difference in the release efficiency between the two methods indicated the possible damage caused by alkaline pH to E. faecium. The results showed that 94.2% of live cells were set free in the artificial intestinal fluid, with trypsin used to release the coated E. faecium regardless of the viable cell quantitation method. In this situation, the pH, ionic strength and hydrolytic effects of digestive enzymes worked together to decompose the coating material complex without damaging E. faecium cells. Therefore, it was more reliable to predict the release pattern of coated E. faecium inside the intestine in the trypsin-containing artificial intestinal fluid.

In addition to the release of free cells, the polymers for encapsulation significantly lowered the efficiency of extracting genomic DNA from encapsulated E. faecium using the commercial kit because only 14.2% of DNA was quantified (Fig. 3B). To improve DNA extraction efficiency, the CTAB was used to overcome the negative impacts. The CTAB is commonly used in plant genomic DNA extraction to remove polysaccharide polymers before DNA extraction and precipitation (Ali et al., 2019). It was demonstrated that DNA extraction efficiency was improved by over 50% with CTAB addition (Fig. 3B). Over 90% of viable bacterial DNA could be quantified when the PMA-qPCR method was modified with trypsin-containing artificial intestinal fluid and CTAB pretreatments (Fig. 3A). Therefore, this optimized PMA-qPCR method was suitable for quantification of viable E. faecium whether coated or not. Using the mock E. faecium mixtures with different ratios of viable and dead cells, the optimized PMA-qPCR method was demonstrated to accurately quantify the proportion of viable E. faecium because the observed data were not only highly correlated with but also statistically indistinguishable from the set data (Fig. 4B).

Since the optimized PMA-qPCR method was validated for accuracy and sensitivity in viable E. faecium quantification, it was then employed to investigate the loss of viable cells throughout the practical feed manufacturing and evaluate the effectiveness of different coating techniques. Our results revealed that there were interactive effects of temperature and processing step on the viability of E. faecium during the whole feed processing procedure. Conditioning with steam at 70 ∼ 80 °C was a critical process to gelatinize starch in feed ingredients to increase feed digestibility and sterilize feed for hygiene purposes (Jones, 2011; M.R. Abdollahi, 2013; Teixeira Netto et al., 2019). However, the survival rate of E. faecium significantly decreased from 72.4% to 12.2% when the conditioning temperature increased from 65 °C to 75 °C and then 85 °C (Table 5). The temperature-dependent loss of viable E. faecium was consistent with previous studies (Coe et al., 2025). Heat stress not only directly inactivated E. faecium but also caused cell membrane damage. In the study of Stummer et al. (2012), the CFU of E. faecium was only slightly reduced when the drying temperature was increased from 37 to 57 °C, but the propidium iodide intensity was increased by 6-fold in cells dried at 57 °C compared to those dried at 37 °C, indicating significantly increased cell membrane permeability. Therefore, the culture method might overestimate the quantity of healthy and functional E. faecium because it could not detect cell membrane damage. According to the principles of the PMA-qPCR, it is more sensitive to quantify viable and healthy cells than the culture method. The increased cell membrane permeability by heat during conditioning made the E. faecium cells more vulnerable to the extrusion and friction during pelleting (Abe, 2015). Evans et al. (2020) reported temperature of pellet feed reached 65 ∼ 85 °C with cold pelleting compression, suggesting that pelleting compression can negatively affect the bacteria cells by the mechanical force and friction heat. In our study, the overall survival rate of E. faecium in pelleted feed dropped to a comparably low level regardless of conditioning temperatures (Table 4). Liu et al. (2012) also found that pelleting decreased the survival rate of coated E. faecium to 67.2%, whereas conditioning at 65 °C and 85 °C did not significantly inactivate E. faecium. These findings demonstrated that there is a substantial reduction in viable E. faecium with pelleting compression, which should not be overlooked.

Regarding different coating techniques, it was found that the pre-encapsulated E. faecium had a greater survival rate at all three steps of feed manufacturing. This pre-encapsulation offered protection not only against heat during conditioning but also against mechanical force during pelleting. Several factors may explain the huge variations in the survival rate for pre-encapsulated E. faecium. First of all, the conditioning temperature was the primary factor, reducing the survival rate of E. faecium from 117.8% to 24% as the temperature increased. Secondly, mixing homogeneity during feed production also contributed to the variation, as the survival rate was calculated by comparing viable cell count in conditioned or pelleted feed to that in mash feed. In practice, 90% mixing homogeneity is qualified for feed production. Thirdly, while our release method achieved 94.2% recovery of coated E. faecium, the unreleased fraction was likely disrupted during pelleting compression, which may account for the slight increase in viable cell counts observed in some samples. The post-coating E. faecium used in the current study did not benefit the survival of E. faecium through the feed manufacturing processes. The poor performance is largely attributed to the post-encapsulation technology. Coating materials, such as starch, maltodextrin and skimmed milk powder are often used for post-encapsulation by spray drying and fluidized bed. These matrices did not offer a solid shell against shear force. During these processes, bacterial cells are exposed to elevated temperatures (65 ∼ 90 °C), which may compromise the viability of E. faecium cells before pelleting even begins (Jayaprakash et al., 2023). In contrast, pre‑encapsulated cells grew inside the protective matrix and retained their integrity, allowing them to withstand processing stresses far better. Uncoated cells, while lacking any physical protection, at least entered the processing process undamaged and therefore outperformed post‑encapsulated cells.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the optimized PMA-qPCR method developed in this study was sensitive and could accurately quantify viable E. faecium regardless of coating treatments. Taken advantage of the PMA-qPCR, both conditioning and pelleting processes during feed manufacture contributed substantially to overall inactivation of E. faecium. And the pre-encapsulated product tested demonstrated superior survival rate over the uncoated and post-encapsulated counterparts. An effective coating technology should be resistant to heat as well as mechanical force; therefore, it should be carefully evaluated through pilot feed manufacturing rather than simplified laboratory tests, such as a water bath.
