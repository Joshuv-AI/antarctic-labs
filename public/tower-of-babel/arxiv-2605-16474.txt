LERA: LLM-Enhanced RAG for Ad Auction in Generative Chatbots
Haoran Sun, Xinrui Song, Xinyu Zhang, Zhaohua Chen, Xu Chu, Zhilin Zhang, Chuan Yu, Jian Xu, Bo Zheng, Xiaotie Deng

Abstract
The integration of advertising auction mechanisms into large language model (LLM)-based chatbots presents a significant opportunity for commercialization, yet poses unique challenges in balancing relevance, efficiency, and user experience. Recently, Feizi et al. ( 7 ) and Hajiaghayi et al. ( 9 ) outlined a retrieve-then-generate paradigm that decouples retrieval and generation, offering lightweight ad insertion and payment determination. However, current retrieval relies solely on text embedding similarity, which may lead to commercial misinterpretation and issues such as repetitive insertions. In this paper, we propose LERA, a two-stage retrieve-then-generate auction framework tailored for LLM chatbots. In the first stage, embedding-based coarse filtering pre-selects a small set of candidate advertisers. In the second stage, the LLM itself is queried with a carefully designed prompt to produce logits over candidates, which serve as refined organic relevance scores. These scores are combined with bids, and a critical-value payment rule accounts for both the coarse-filtering and fine-ranking thresholds, ensuring truthfulness for utility-maximizing advertisers. The framework naturally extends to multiple ad insertions within dynamic dialogue flows and long responses. Experiments on a synthetic advertiser-query benchmark show that LERA substantially improves ad selection accuracy and insertion diversity while incurring only controllable latency overhead.



1
Introduction

With the rapid progress of foundation models, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly serving as conversational gateways through which users access information, products, and services
(
27
;
28
;
3
)
.
This shift creates new opportunities for monetizing generative systems beyond subscription and token-based pricing.
A central challenge is how to integrate advertising into LLM-based chatbots in a way that is contextually appropriate for users, commercially meaningful for advertisers, and compatible with auction-based pricing
(
7
;
26
)
.
Unlike sponsored search or display advertising, where ads are placed in predefined slots, chatbot advertising must decide whether and how an advertisement should be woven into a generated response.
This makes the auction problem inseparable from language understanding and generation.

A recent line of work studies advertising auctions for generative AI systems, which can be broadly categorized into two design paradigms:
retrieve-then-generate
and
generate-then-retrieve
.
Retrieve-then-generate methods, such as the RAG-based auction of Hajiaghayi et al.
(
9
)
, perform lightweight retrieval and insert ads before generation, offering high efficiency but relying on embedding similarity that may not fully capture commercial intent or avoid repetitive insertions.
Generate-then-retrieve approaches leverage the LLM’s contextual understanding to produce response-level signals or advertiser-specific distributions
(
7
;
19
;
25
;
5
)
, but they often require multiple LLM calls per query or advertiser-specific scoring, incurring significant latency and inference cost.
Placeholder-based methods instead generate responses with designated ad slots and fill them afterward
(
1
;
30
)
, potentially compromising fluency because off-the-shelf LLMs are not generally trained to produce natural responses around explicit placeholders.

At the same time, directly using LLMs as ad selectors is not by itself a complete solution.
Although LLMs have strong contextual understanding and have been used for ranking and recommendation
(
31
;
13
;
22
)
, incorporating LLM judgments into an auction requires a payment rule that remains compatible with strategic bidding.
Moreover, scoring all advertisers with LLM logits can be prohibitively expensive under practical high-throughput inference engines.

These trade-offs motivate
LERA
, a two-stage retrieve-then-generate auction framework for ad insertion in LLM-based chatbots.
The central idea is to preserve the efficiency of retrieval-based allocation while using the LLM’s contextual judgment only where it is most valuable: on a small candidate set.
As is shown in Figure
1
, in the first stage,
LERA
applies embedding-based coarse filtering to preselect candidate advertisers from a large pool.
In the second stage, it presents these candidates, together with a no-insertion option, to the LLM and extracts logits over candidate labels as refined organic relevance scores. These scores are combined with bids to determine the winner, and a critical-value payment rule accounts for both the coarse-filtering and fine-ranking thresholds.
Thus, although using LLM logits for ranking is common in broader retrieval and recommendation settings,
LERA
makes such scoring practical for real-time chatbot ad auctions by embedding it inside a throughput-aware two-stage mechanism.
Crucially, in high-throughput inference engines like SGLang, logit extraction relies on parallel pre-filling across all candidates. By restricting this computation to only the small set of first-stage winners,
LERA
significantly improves throughput while preserving accuracy.
Experiments on single-ad and multi-ad insertion tasks show that
LERA
improves ad selection quality and dialogue-level satisfaction with only controllable latency overhead.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
LERA
, a context-aware ad auction framework for LLM chatbots that combines embedding-based coarse filtering with LLM-logit-based fine ranking.

•

We design a critical-value payment rule tailored to the two-stage allocation process, accounting for both candidate-set entry and final-stage competition.

•

Qualitative and quantitative experiments show that
LERA
better captures nuanced commercial intent, improves insertion diversity, and achieves a favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off compared with embedding-only and LLM-only alternatives.

2
Related Work

Our framework lies at the intersection of LLM-mediated advertising, retrieval-augmented generation, LLM-based recommendation, and externality-aware auction design. We briefly review these areas and highlight our differences; an extended discussion is provided in Appendix
B
.

Ad Auctions in LLMs.

Existing frameworks can generally be categorized into generate-then-retrieve and retrieve-then-generate paradigms. Generate-then-retrieve methods auction the right to speak or insert ads based on generated probabilities or responses
(
5
;
19
;
25
)
, often requiring multiple inference passes. Retrieve-then-generate methods, such as Hajiaghayi et al.
9
, treat advertisers as retrievable documents and are more efficient, but they rely on embedding similarity that may miss nuanced commercial intent. Training-based approaches
(
33
)
embed bid monotonicity directly into the LLM, but are costly to retrain. Our work stays within the efficient retrieve-then-generate paradigm and introduces a modular two-stage scoring that uses LLM judgments only on a shortlist, balancing accuracy and throughput.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

Standard RAG pipelines maximize semantic fidelity to a query
(
11
;
8
)
and have been adapted for recommendation
(
24
)
. Ad insertion, however, demands more than topical similarity: it must respect commercial intent, avoid repetitive insertions across a dialogue, and preserve the coherence of the final response. Our two-stage process adapts RAG to this competitive and revenue-driven setting by augmenting dense retrieval with an LLM-based fine-ranking stage.

LLM-based Recommendation.

LLMs have shown strong context understanding for zero-shot ranking and recommendation
(
34
;
14
)
, often outperforming embedding-based methods in capturing nuanced user intent
(
17
;
2
)
. However, these works focus solely on relevance or user satisfaction, without an economic layer. We integrate the LLM’s relevance assessment as an organic compatibility score into a truthful auction, accommodating strategic bidding and incentive-compatible pricing.

Externality in Ad Auctions.

From classic position auctions
(
6
;
29
)
to deep learning-based mechanisms
(
32
;
18
)
and whole-slate optimization
(
15
;
36
)
, auction design has progressively addressed allocative externalities among ads. LLM-generated responses introduce a distinct generative externality: an inserted ad can alter the flow, tone, specificity, and length of the entire narrative. Our payment rule explicitly accounts for the dynamic organic probability of an ad within a generated response, extending externality-aware design to generative AI.

3
Preliminary

We consider a practical scenario for single ad insertion. Given a user query
x
, there exists a candidate set of
N
advertisers bidding for the opportunity to display their product within the response.
Each advertiser
i
has a
public
description
d_{i}
for its product and estimates a prior value-per-click
v_{i}
based on the query, representing the expected value of a click should their ad be inserted.
This value
v_{i}
constitutes
private
information; the platform observes only the reported bid
b_{i}
and the public
d_{i}
from each advertiser.

Formally, the input to the ad insertion mechanism comprises the query
x
, the set of ad descriptions
\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and the reported bids
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. The mechanism outputs a distribution over the allocation space (the response
y
) and a payment vector (the cost per click for each advertiser).

Let
\mathcal{Y}
denote the space of possible responses. The mechanism is formally defined by a pair of functions
(g,p)
:

g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y}),\quad p:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
g
is the allocation rule and
p
is the payment rule. The final response is sampled from the distribution
g(x,\bm{d},\bm{b})
. Upon the generation of response
y\sim g(x,\bm{d},\bm{b})
, each advertiser forms a posterior value-per-click
\hat{v}_{i}(y)
, which may differ from the prior estimate
v_{i}
derived solely from the query. The advertiser’s utility
u_{i}
is realized upon a user’s click outcome
o_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, such that
u_{i}=(\hat{v}_{i}(y)-p_{i})\cdot o_{i}
.

The allocation function
g
inevitably relies on a foundation model for text generation. Although LLMs exhibit remarkable instruction-following capabilities
20
, expecting an off-the-shelf model to strictly adhere to scalar bids during allocation proves challenging, as LLMs often lack numerical sensitivity
12
;
21
. While existing decoupled approaches compute feature scores using simple embedding similarity to guide the allocation
9
, such coarse features often fail to maximize user satisfaction and platform revenue. Therefore, we propose a two-stage score calculation method that leverages the LLM’s own capabilities to better handle nuanced user intent, which we detail in the next section.

3.1
Retrieval-Generation Decoupling

A representative approach, proposed by Hajiaghayi et al.
9
, decomposes the allocation rule as
g=g_{\text{retrieval}}\odot g_{\text{generate}}
. For a single ad insertion, a winning advertiser
i^{*}
is first determined by

i^{*}=\arg\max_{i}\big(s(x,d_{i})\cdot b_{i}\big),

where the score
s(x,d_{i})
is computed via embedding similarity with optional noise:

s(x,d_{i})=\text{Norm}(\text{Similarity}(\text{Emb}(x),\text{Emb}(d_{i})))\cdot\text{Noise}_{i},

and
\text{Norm}(\cdot)
maps the raw similarity (e.g., cosine similarity in
[-1,1]
) to a strictly positive value, e.g., via min-max scaling to
(0,1]
, to avoid non
-
positive bid multipliers.
A predefined prompt template then incorporates the winning ad to generate the final response

y\sim\text{GenAI}\big(\text{Prompt}(x,d_{i^{*}})\big).

The baseline second-price payment rule is then

p_{i}=\max_{j\neq i^{*}}\frac{s(x,d_{j})\cdot b_{j}}{s(x,d_{i})}.

Dubey et al.
4
explore a related idea where the retrieval stage predicts a prominence vector over ads, but this requires the LLM to be sensitive to such a vector, which is difficult in practice.

This decoupled design requires only one expensive LLM call (the final generation), keeping the system lightweight. However, it places high demands on the accuracy of
s
: a poor score can make ad insertion counterproductive. To address this, we propose a two-stage scoring method that leverages the LLM’s own judgment for more nuanced intent handling and diversity, while retaining the single-call full-text generation property. The next section details our mechanism with refined scoring and a critical-value payment rule.

4
Two-Stage Scoring for Ad Auction

We first present the proposed mechanism for single ad insertion.
The key idea is to retain the efficiency of retrieve-then-generate allocation while improving the relevance score used in the auction.
To this end, the mechanism uses a two-stage ranking process, as is illudstrated in Figure
1
.
The first stage applies inexpensive embedding-based filtering to reduce the advertiser pool from
N
to a small candidate set of size
K
, where
K\ll N
.
The second stage uses the LLM’s contextual judgment to refine the relevance scores only within this candidate set.
The refined scores are then combined with advertisers’ bids to determine the final allocation.
We further design a critical-value payment rule for this two-stage selection process and discuss its extension to multiple ad insertions.

4.1
Two-Stage Allocation for Single Ad Insertion

Given a user query
x
, advertiser descriptions
\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and bids
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the first stage aims to efficiently identify a small set of plausible advertisers.
Rather than directly embedding the raw query, we first prompt an LLM to generate a concise set of intent keywords, denoted by
\mathbf{w}\sim\text{GenAI}(\text{Prompt}_{\text{keywords}}(x))
.
These keywords summarize the user’s underlying intent and provide a cleaner signal for embedding-based retrieval.

For each advertiser
i
, we compute a Stage 1 relevance score using the normalized cosine similarity between the keyword embedding and the advertiser description embedding:

s_{i}^{(1)}=\text{Norm}\big(\text{Similarity}(\text{Emb}(\mathbf{w}),\text{Emb}(d_{i}))\big).

The relevance score is then weighted by the advertiser’s bid as
\text{Score}_{i}^{(1)}=s_{i}^{(1)}\cdot b_{i}
.
The top-
K
advertisers according to
\text{Score}_{i}^{(1)}
form the candidate set:

\mathcal{S}=\arg\underset{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}{\text{Top-}K}\;\text{Score}_{i}^{(1)}.

The second stage refines the relevance assessment within
\mathcal{S}
using the LLM itself.
This stage is motivated by the fact that ad insertion often requires understanding implicit constraints, negative preferences, and intent shifts that may not be captured by embedding similarity alone.
We present the original query
x
and the candidate descriptions
\{d_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{S}}
to the LLM and ask it to identify the most appropriate ad for insertion.
To avoid forcing an irrelevant advertisement into the response, we include a no-insertion option
\phi
and define the extended candidate set as
\hat{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}\cup\{\phi\}
.

Instead of relying on generated textual output, we extract the model’s logits for the candidate labels.
Specifically, the LLM produces a
(K+1)
-dimensional logit vector
\mathbf{z}
from
\text{Prompt}_{\text{ranking}}(x,\{d_{j}\}_{j\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}})
.
We normalize these logits with a softmax function to obtain the Stage 2 relevance score:

s_{j}^{(2)}=\frac{\exp(z_{j})}{\sum_{k\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}}\exp(z_{k})}.

This score can be interpreted as the LLM’s refined estimate of organic relevance within the candidate set.
The final auction score is
\text{Score}_{j}^{(2)}=s_{j}^{(2)}\cdot b_{j}
, where we set
b_{\phi}=1
for the no-insertion option.
The winner is then selected as

i^{*}=\arg\max_{j\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}}\;\text{Score}_{j}^{(2)}.

If
i^{*}=\phi
, no advertisement is inserted.
Otherwise, the winning advertiser’s description is incorporated into the final generation prompt:

y\sim\begin{cases}\text{GenAI}\big(\text{Prompt}(x,d_{i^{*}})\big),&\text{if }i^{*}\neq\phi,\\
\text{GenAI}\big(\text{Prompt}(x)\big),&\text{if }i^{*}=\phi.\end{cases}

4.2
Critical-Value Payment and Incentive Compatibility

We next define the payment rule for the two-stage allocation process.
Following critical-value pricing
23
;
10
, the winning advertiser should pay the minimum bid required to remain the winner.

If no advertiser is selected, i.e.,
i^{*}=\phi
, no payment is charged.
Otherwise, let
i^{*}
be the winning advertiser.
Let
\text{Score}^{(1)}_{(K+1)}
denote the
(K+1)
-th highest Stage 1 score, which is the threshold for entering the candidate set.
Let
\text{Score}^{(2)}_{(2)}
denote the second-highest Stage 2 score within
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
, which is the threshold for winning the final ranking.
The payment of advertiser
i^{*}
is defined as

p_{i^{*}}=\max\Bigg\{\underbrace{\frac{\text{Score}_{(K+1)}^{(1)}}{s^{(1)}_{i^{*}}}}_{\text{critical value for Stage 1}},\underbrace{\frac{\text{Score}_{(2)}^{(2)}}{s^{(2)}_{i^{*}}}}_{\text{critical value for Stage 2}}\Bigg\}.

Intuitively, the winner must pay enough to both enter the Stage 1 top-
K
set and defeat all competitors in Stage 2.
Taking the maximum of the two thresholds ensures that both requirements are satisfied.

We now state the incentive-compatibility property of the mechanism.
As in Section
3
, advertiser
i
has a prior value-per-click estimate
v_{i}
, while the realized value
\hat{v}_{i}(y)
depends on the generated response.
Let
o_{i}(y)
denote the click outcome or click probability associated with response
y
.
The expected utility of the winning advertiser is

u_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\text{GenAI}(\text{Prompt}(x,d_{i}))}\big[(\hat{v}_{i}(y)-p_{i})\cdot o_{i}(y)\big].

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that the relevance scores used by the allocation rule are independent of advertiser
i
’s reported bid except through the bid-weighted ranking rule.
Further assume that
o_{i}(y)
and
\hat{v}_{i}(y)
are independent conditioned on the ad being displayed, and that the prior estimate is unbiased, i.e.,
v_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{y}[\hat{v}_{i}(y)]
.
Then the two-stage auction mechanism with the payment rule above is incentive compatible for a utility-maximizing advertiser.

Proof sketch.

Under the independence and unbiasedness assumptions, advertiser
i
’s expected utility can be written as

u_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{y}[(\hat{v}_{i}(y)-p_{i})\cdot o_{i}(y)]=(v_{i}-p_{i})\cdot\bar{o}_{i},

where
\bar{o}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{y}[o_{i}(y)]
is the expected click probability.
Since
\bar{o}_{i}
is nonnegative, the advertiser’s bidding decision reduces to a standard critical-value comparison between value and payment.
By construction,
p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}
is the minimum bid required to pass both the Stage 1 entry threshold and the Stage 2 winning threshold.
Bidding above this value yields the same allocation and payment, while bidding below it loses the allocation.
Therefore, truthful bidding weakly dominates both underbidding and overbidding.
The full proof is deferred to Appendix
G
.
∎

This payment rule differs from a classic single-stage second-price auction because the final-stage competitors are themselves selected by the first-stage auction.
An advertiser must therefore satisfy two constraints: being included in the candidate set and winning the LLM-based ranking.
The proposed payment captures both constraints through the maximum of the two critical values.

4.3
Extension to Multiple Ad Insertions

The same two-stage mechanism can be extended to multiple ad insertions, either across turns in a dialogue or within a long-form response.
Following the retrieve-then-generate workflow, we divide the response into
T
segments.
For segment
t
, the auction is conditioned on the original query
x
and the response prefix
y_{<t}
.
The mechanism selects either an advertiser
i_{t}^{*}
or the no-insertion option, after which the LLM generates the next segment from
\text{Prompt}(x,y_{<t},d_{i_{t}^{*}})
, with
d_{i_{t}^{*}}
omitted when
i_{t}^{*}=\phi
.
The final response is the concatenation
y=y_{1}\oplus y_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{T}
.

The main additional cost comes from three components: reprocessing the prefix for each segment, keyword generation for Stage 1, and logit computation for Stage 2.
In practice,
T
is small because each segment typically contains several sentences.
Prefix reprocessing is comparable to a small number of additional decoding steps, while Stage 2 requires only a single forward pass over a small candidate set.
Keyword generation is the most visible extra cost, but it produces only a short sequence and can be traded off against retrieval quality.
Thus, the overall latency overhead remains controllable, as empirically verified in Section
5.3
.

5
Experimental Results

In this section, we evaluate
LERA
against an Embedding-only baseline and several ablation variants. Across different model scales, we show that
LERA
improves single ad selection accuracy. In more realistic long-form responses with multiple ad insertions,
LERA
further achieves a favorable balance between user satisfaction and inference efficiency.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets.

All experiments are conducted on a synthetic advertiser dataset consisting of 100 advertisers from 8 product categories. The advertisers are curated by prompting an LLM. Each advertiser is associated with a brand description of approximately 20 words, which serves as the reference text for both embedding-based retrieval and LLM-based scoring. Representative examples are provided in Appendix
C
.

Methods.

We compare methods under the retrieve-then-generate paradigm. The retrieval and ranking strategies are as follows.
Embedding-only

(
9
)
ranks advertisers solely by the embedding similarity between the user query and advertiser descriptions.
LERA
is our proposed two-stage method, which first performs coarse filtering with embedding similarity and then applies LLM logits for fine-grained ranking.
LERA
(LLM-only)
removes the coarse filtering stage and directly applies LLM scoring to all
N
advertisers.
LERA
(Keyword-Match)
uses LLM-generated keywords for embedding-based retrieval but omits the fine-grained LLM-logit scoring stage.

Implementation.

All experiments use uniform bids (
b_{i}=1.0
) to isolate the allocation and scoring mechanisms from auction dynamics under heterogeneous bidding.
We distinguish between two types of models used in our system: the
Embed Model
, which produces embeddings for retrieval, and the
Gen Model
, which is used for keyword generation, logit-based scoring, and final response generation. For the Gen Model, we evaluate three model scales: 4B, 30B, and 235B parameters. All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A800 GPUs. Since our experiments only require LLM inference, we deploy all LLM inference services with SGLang
(
35
)
, which better reflects the behavior of modern high-throughput inference engines.

5.2
Selection Accuracy in Single Ad Insertion

We first evaluate single ad selection accuracy, which serves as the elementary allocation task in LLM-based chatbot advertising. Given a user query, each strategy is required to select the advertiser that is most suitable for insertion into the generated response.

To comprehensively evaluate different strategies, we construct three types of test queries that reflect different user intent scenarios:

1.

Complex Intent Analysis
(80 queries): queries containing nuanced or implicit constraints that require multifaceted reasoning.

2.

Natural Intent Shift
(80 queries): queries that initially mention one product or category but naturally shift toward a different target need.

3.

Negative Product Refinement
(80 queries): queries that explicitly reject an initially mentioned product before stating the actual need.

Although ad insertion can be subjective in general, each test query in our benchmark is constructed to have a unique ground-truth advertiser in the advertiser pool. This allows us to report selection accuracy as a direct metric. Representative examples are provided in Appendix
D
.

Table
1
reports the selection accuracy across retrieval strategies and model scales. The Embedding-only baseline achieves relatively low matching accuracy, even when using an 8B embedding model. This is expected: embedding models are primarily trained to capture semantic proximity, whereas ad selection often requires reasoning over intent shifts, explicit exclusions, and implicit constraints. In contrast,
LERA
consistently improves selection accuracy across settings.

The advantage of
LERA
is particularly pronounced for smaller generative models, such as the 4B model. In this regime, the two-stage design provides stronger guidance than either pure embedding retrieval or keyword-only retrieval. Moreover, for the 4B and 30B models, directly ranking all 100 candidate advertisers with LLM logits, i.e.,
LERA
(LLM-only), performs worse than our two-stage method. This suggests that the coarse filtering stage does not merely improve efficiency; it also reduces the difficulty of the fine-grained ranking problem by presenting the LLM with a smaller and more relevant candidate set.

On the other hand,
LERA
(LLM-only) benefits most clearly from increased model capability. With the 235B model, this variant achieves perfect accuracy across all test categories. However, it is poorly suited to practical high-throughput inference engines such as SGLang and vLLM. These systems do not directly expose full logits over large candidate sets in the most efficient generation path, and computing logits over all advertisers requires parallel prefilling across the candidate set. This creates a substantial throughput bottleneck in real-time applications. We provide a detailed discussion in Appendix
C.2
, and the latency results in the next subsection further demonstrate the overhead of
LERA
(LLM-only).

For
LERA
, we observe that the 235B model is slightly worse than the 30B model in some settings. In our experiments, this is mainly because the larger model sometimes adopts a more conservative behavior and chooses not to insert an advertisement when the candidates appear marginally relevant. Under our accuracy metric, such outcomes are counted as incorrect, which explains why the 235B model can underperform the 30B model in these cases.

5.3
Multi Ad Insertion in Dynamic Chatting Flow

We next evaluate multiple ad insertion, a more realistic setting in which ad allocation must be performed dynamically throughout a generated response. In real chatbot interactions, ad selection should evolve with the generated context. An ideal strategy should decide not only which ad to insert, but also whether an additional ad is still needed after previous insertions. It should also avoid repeatedly selecting advertisers from the same category when the user requests recommendations for multiple distinct needs.

To evaluate this setting, we construct 50 queries in which users explicitly request recommendations from three distinct ad categories. Following the multi-ad insertion workflow of Hajiaghayi et al.
9
, we split response generation into multiple segments. Before each segment, an auction is conducted to decide whether to insert an ad and which advertiser to select. The model then continues the response for two or three sentences using a prompt conditioned on the auction outcome. This process is repeated until the full response is generated. For a fair comparison, we limit the maximum number of inserted ads to three and set the number of first-stage winners in
LERA
to
K=8
for the multi-ad dynamic setting (we use
K=5
for the single-ad setting reported in Table
1
).

6
Conclusion

In this work, we presented a novel two-stage auction mechanism for advertisement insertion in LLM-based chatbots that effectively balances semantic relevance with commercial objectives. Our approach combines the efficiency of embedding-based coarse filtering with the semantic sophistication of LLM-based fine-grained ranking, addressing key limitations of existing methods. The proposed framework demonstrates superior performance in handling complex user intents, maintaining ad diversity, and preserving reasonable computational overhead. Future work could explore adaptive mechanisms for dynamically adjusting the balance between semantic relevance and commercial value based on contextual factors.

Limitations

This paper studies auction design within the retrieve-then-generate paradigm. Due to the lack of an off-the-shelf open-source dataset, our experiments rely on a synthetic dataset constructed by an LLM. The theoretical results are based on an independence assumption, which we discuss further in Appendix
G
. Despite these limitations, we believe the proposed framework represents a step toward the practical deployment of auction-based ad insertion in LLM-powered chatbots.

Broader Impacts

As
LERA
has strong practical significance, we believe it can be used to enhance the commercial effectiveness of LLM-based chatbots. However, we also acknowledge that the proposed framework may have potential negative societal impacts. For example, it could be used to insert advertisements into user responses, which might be perceived as intrusive or annoying. Therefore, careful design of the auction mechanism is necessary to mitigate such negative societal impacts when the method is deployed in real-world applications.

Appendix B
Additional Related Work

This section provides a more detailed discussion of the literature related to our work. Our framework lies at the intersection of LLM-mediated advertising, retrieval-augmented generation, LLM-based recommendation, and externality-aware auction design. We therefore organize the discussion around four themes: ad auctions in LLM interfaces, retrieval-augmented generation, ad recommendation with LLMs, and externalities in auction mechanisms.

B.1
Ad Auctions in LLMs

Research on integrating advertising auctions into LLM-based chatbots is a rapidly emerging area. Existing frameworks can generally be categorized into
generate-then-retrieve
and
retrieve-then-generate
paradigms, depending on whether the auction is applied after a candidate response is generated or before the final response is produced.

In the
generate-then-retrieve
, or post-generation, approach, the model first generates candidate responses or probability distributions, which are then used as the basis for auction decisions. Duetting et al.
[
5
]
pioneered a mechanism in which advertisers bid on token probabilities, aggregating preferences from multiple LLMs to produce a joint output. Similarly, Mordo et al.
[
19
]
and Soumalias et al.
[
25
]
explore auctioning the “right to speak” or inserting ads into generated placeholders. While these methods are theoretically grounded, they often require multiple inference passes or complex output aggregation, leading to high latency and inference costs
[
26
]
.

In contrast, the
retrieve-then-generate
paradigm, recently formalized by Hajiaghayi et al.
[
9
]
, treats advertisers as retrievable documents within a RAG framework. An auction determines which ads are retrieved and inserted into the prompt context before generation begins. This approach is modular and computationally efficient because it decouples the auction stage from the expensive generation process. However, existing implementations often rely heavily on standard retrieval scores, which may not fully align with commercial objectives. This limitation motivates our framework, which introduces a selection mechanism that explicitly combines coarse retrieval with LLM-based ad relevance estimation.

More recently, Zhao et al.
[
33
]
proposed a training method that enables an LLM to generate responses satisfying bid monotonicity. Although this approach combines retrieval and generation, it relies on costly model training and may be less flexible when the ad inventory changes frequently. Our work instead focuses on a modular inference-time mechanism that can be applied without retraining the underlying LLM.

B.2
Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-augmented generation was introduced to ground LLM responses in external and up-to-date knowledge
[
11
]
. A standard RAG pipeline retrieves documents based on vector similarity, often using dense embeddings, and then feeds the retrieved documents into the generator
[
8
]
. This paradigm has been widely adapted for downstream tasks, including recommendation systems where items are retrieved as contextual evidence
[
24
]
.

As discussed in the main paper, RAG offers a natural abstraction for ad insertion: advertisements can be viewed as external knowledge chunks that may be inserted into the LLM context. However, a fundamental difference remains between ordinary RAG and sponsored generation. Standard RAG primarily aims to maximize semantic fidelity to the user query, whereas ad insertion must also account for revenue, advertiser value, and the likelihood that an ad can be integrated without degrading user experience. In this sense, the relevant retrieval signal is not merely semantic similarity, but a broader notion of commercial compatibility. Our two-stage retrieval and scoring process adapts standard RAG principles to this competitive and revenue-driven setting.

B.3
Ad Recommendation with LLMs

Beyond auction design, a growing body of work studies LLMs as ranking and recommendation engines. Recent surveys suggest that LLMs possess strong semantic understanding that can be leveraged for zero-shot item recommendation and ranking
[
34
,
14
]
. Liu et al.
[
17
]
and Dai et al.
[
2
]
show that LLMs can evaluate the relevance of candidate items to a user’s query and history, often outperforming traditional embedding-based methods in capturing nuanced user intent.

The key distinction between this literature and our work is the economic layer. LLM-based recommendation studies typically focus on maximizing relevance, utility, or user satisfaction, while abstracting away from strategic advertiser behavior and incentive-compatible pricing. In contrast, our framework uses the LLM’s relevance assessment as an organic compatibility score within a truthful auction mechanism. This allows us to preserve the semantic strengths of LLM-based recommendation while incorporating advertiser bids and payment rules.

B.4
Externality in Ad Auction Design

Early work on sponsored search auctions, such as the Generalized Second Price auction, primarily modeled externalities as position effects, assuming that an ad’s click probability depends on its slot and inherent quality
[
6
,
29
]
. As advertising shifted from search pages to recommendation feeds and mixed-content environments, later work began to study richer
allocative externalities
, where an ad’s value and user impact depend on neighboring content.

To address such effects, researchers have proposed deep learning-based mechanisms.
Deep GSP

[
32
]
and
Neural Auctions

[
18
]
use deep networks to learn non-linear ranking scores that capture inter-item dependencies while maintaining incentive compatibility through monotone allocation rules. More recent mechanisms, including WCVCG
[
15
]
,
MIAA

[
16
]
, and
Contextual Generative Auctions

[
36
]
, explicitly model whole-slate effects by jointly optimizing the arrangement of ads and organic items to balance platform revenue with user experience.

While these works successfully handle externalities in static lists or feeds, LLM responses introduce a different type of
generative externality
. Unlike a fixed feed, an LLM response is a cohesive narrative in which an ad insertion can alter the text’s flow, tone, specificity, and length
[
1
]
. Our work extends externality-aware design to this generative setting by proposing a payment rule that accounts for the dynamic organic probability of an ad within a generated response.

Overall, existing work has studied auction design for LLM-generated content, LLM-based recommendation, and externalities in traditional ad allocation settings. However, these lines of work have not fully addressed the setting where advertisements are selected before or during LLM generation and where the final response itself determines the organic compatibility of an ad. Our work complements this literature by introducing a retrieval-based auction framework that combines efficient candidate selection, LLM-based relevance estimation, and an externality-aware payment rule tailored to generative responses.

C.2
Logit Computation with SGLang

As introduced in Section
5.1
, we utilize SGLang
[
35
]
for our LLM inference to reflect the performance characteristics of modern, high-throughput deployment environments. However, extracting log probabilities (log probs) for the second-stage scoring requires specific engineering considerations due to the design of such inference engines.

In the second stage, the mechanism evaluates
K+1
candidate choices to compute refined relevance scores. Standard inference libraries, such as Hugging Face
transformers
, allow direct access to the model’s raw computational results, enabling the extraction of full vocabulary logits in a single forward pass. In contrast, highly optimized serving frameworks like SGLang prioritize overall throughput and memory management, and therefore do not natively expose intermediate logits for arbitrary token choices during a standard generation call.

To obtain the required log probabilities, SGLang evaluates multiple choices through parallel prefilling. Under this mechanism,
K+1
distinct prompt variations are constructed—each explicitly appending one of the candidate selections. The inference engine processes these prompts concurrently and returns the sequence-level log probabilities, which are then used directly to derive the final Stage 2 auction scores.

Although the Hugging Face engine could theoretically achieve this logit extraction with fewer forward passes, its inference efficiency is significantly lower. Because most practical, large-scale LLM services today are deployed on high-throughput engines like SGLang or vLLM, choosing SGLang for our experiments ensures that the measured efficiency metrics, latency tradeoffs, and observed throughput bottlenecks accurately reflect real-world production constraints.

F.2
Multi-Ad Queries

Multi-ad queries evaluate whether a method can cover multiple distinct user needs within a single long-form response. We report category coverage, defined as the fraction of requested product categories represented by the selected advertisements. This metric captures whether the mechanism diversifies ad selection across the user’s stated needs, rather than repeatedly selecting ads from a single dominant category. It should be interpreted as a category-level metric rather than an exact product-level satisfaction metric.

Under the independence assumption and unbiased prior estimates, the expected utility of advertiser

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[(\hat{v}_{i}(y)-p_{i})\cdot o_{i}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=(v_{i}-p_{i})\cdot\bar{o}_{i},

Because the allocation rule is monotone in advertiser

p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}
such that advertiser

b_{i}\geq p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}

Case 1:
v_{i}\geq p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}

If advertiser
i

b_{i}=v_{i}
, then the advertiser wins because
b_{i}\geq p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}

(v_{i}-p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}})\bar{o}_{i}\geq 0.

Any deviation
b_{i}^{\prime}\geq p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}
leads to the same allocation and the same payment, and therefore yields the same utility. Any deviation
b_{i}^{\prime}<p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}
causes the advertiser to lose, resulting in utility
0

v_{i}<p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}

If advertiser
i

b_{i}=v_{i}<p_{i}^{\mathrm{crit}}

Remark G.1

(On the independence assumption)
.

The independence assumption in Proposition
4.1
is introduced as a simplifying condition for the theoretical analysis rather than as a literal claim about all real-world ad markets. In our setting, the randomness arises from the LLM generation process: even for a fixed query and a fixed winning advertiser, the final response
y
may vary because of stochastic decoding. Both the click-through outcome
o_{i}(y)
and the ex-post advertiser value
\hat{v}_{i}(y)
are therefore functions of the realized response.

When the product price is fixed,
\hat{v}_{i}(y)
can be interpreted as post-click conversion value, so the assumption amounts to separating CTR and post-click value conditional on display. We view this as a reasonable first-order approximation because the relationship between these two quantities can be highly context-dependent: some responses may increase clicks but reduce downstream conversion, for example by using overly aggressive or misleading phrasing, whereas others may increase both click propensity and conversion intent by providing clear and well-matched recommendations. This relationship can further depend on product characteristics and user context.

Hence, rather than assuming a universal positive correlation, we adopt independence as a tractable benchmark under which the critical-value argument is transparent. Relaxing this assumption and analyzing richer dependence structures between click probability and advertiser value is an important direction for future work.
