Independent domination polynomial of comaximal graphs of commutative rings
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
The comaximal graph Γ ⁡ ( R ) \displaystyle\Gamma(R) of a commutative ring R \displaystyle R is a simple graph with vertex set R \displaystyle R and two distinct vertices a \displaystyle a and b \displaystyle b of Γ ⁡ ( R ) \displaystyle\Gamma(R) are adjacent if and only if a ​ R + b ​ R = R \displaystyle aR+bR=R , where a ​ R \displaystyle aR is the ideal generated by a \displaystyle a in R \displaystyle R . In this article, the independent domination polynomial D i ​ ( Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) , x ) \displaystyle D_{i}(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x) of Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) is discussed, along with its unimodal and log-concave properties for certain values of n \displaystyle n . Some auxiliary results related to D i ​ ( Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) , x ) \displaystyle D_{i}(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x) are presented in terms of their zeros. In addition, we determine the independence polynomial I ⁡ ( Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) , x ) \displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x) of Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) for special values of n \displaystyle n and provide a general result associated with it. The bounds for the zero of the polynomial I ⁡ ( Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) , x ) \displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x) are established, and their log-concave and unimodal properties are examined.



Keywords: Independence polynomial; independent domination polynomial; comaximal graphs; unimodal; log-concave; zeros

A finite sequence of real numbers
\displaystyle S=\{s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
is said to be
unimodal
if there exists a positive integer
\displaystyle t~(0\leq t\leq n),
called the
mode
of
\displaystyle S
such that
\displaystyle s_{i}
increases up to
\displaystyle i=t
and decreases from then on, that is,

s_{0}\leq s_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{t}\geq s_{t+1}\geq\dots\geq s_{n}.

A polynomial
\displaystyle p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}
is said to be unimodal, if its sequence of coefficients
\displaystyle\{a_{i}\}
is unimodal. Equivalently,
\displaystyle p(x)
is unimodal if its coefficient sequence has a single peak, meaning that it rises to a certain point before falling.
We note that the cases
\displaystyle t=0
and
\displaystyle t=n
allows the sequence to be decreasing and increasing, respectively. So, in such cases
\displaystyle p(x)
is unimodal. A polynomial
\displaystyle p(x)
is symmetric if
\displaystyle a_{i}=a_{n-i},
for
\displaystyle 0\leq i\leq n
and
log-concave
if

a_{i}^{2}\geq a_{i-1}a_{i+1},~\text{for all}~1\leq i\leq n-1.

(2.2)

Suppose that
\displaystyle a_{i}
’s are non negative numbers and
\displaystyle p(x)
has only real zeros. Then the basic approach for unimodality and log-concave
[
15
]
is the Newton’s inequalities:

a_{i}^{2}\geq a_{i-1}a_{i+1}\left(1+\frac{1}{i}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{n-i}\right),~i=1,2,\dots,n-1.

(2.3)

Clearly,
\displaystyle 3+8x+9x^{2}+50x^{3}+7x^{4}+3x^{5}
is unimodal while
\displaystyle 14+7x+9x^{2}+7x^{3}+3x^{4}
is not unimodal.

The number of oscillations
\displaystyle\eta(p(x))
in the sequence of coefficients
\displaystyle a_{i}
’s of
\displaystyle p(x)
is defined as the number of changes of directions (increasing vs decreasing) in the sequence. If
\displaystyle p(x)=171+151x+180x^{2}+13x^{3}+5x^{4}+2x^{5}
, then
\displaystyle\eta(p(x))=2,
as change of decreasing directions are two. Thus, it immediately follows that a polynomial
\displaystyle p(x)
is unimodal if its oscillation is at most one, that is,
\displaystyle\eta(p(x))\leq 1.

.

(ii) For
\displaystyle n=p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}},
with primes
\displaystyle p<q
and
\displaystyle n_{i}\geq 1
are positive integer, the independent domination polynomial

\displaystyle D_{i}(G,x)=\phi(n)x+x^{p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}}+x^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}}}
is unimodal if and only if difference between the exponents of
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle x^{p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}}
is one and that of
\displaystyle x^{p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}}
and
\displaystyle x^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}}}
is one, otherwise there exists zero terms between them and oscillations are more than one, thereby unimodal property fails. That is, same as saying
\displaystyle p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}=2
and
\displaystyle p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}\big(q-p\big)=1,
which after simplification gives
\displaystyle n_{1}=n_{2}=1,p=2
and
\displaystyle q
is arbitrary, the second condition
\displaystyle p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-p)=1
implies that
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle q=3
. Together it follows that
\displaystyle p=2,q=3
and
\displaystyle n_{1}=n_{2}=1
. and here the unimodal property is valid. Conversely, for
\displaystyle p=2,q=3
and
\displaystyle n_{1}=n_{2}=1
, the independent domination polynomial is
\displaystyle 2x+x^{2}+x^{3}
, which is clearly unimodal. The polynomial
\displaystyle D_{i}(G,x)
is not log-concave if and only if there is exactly one term missing between the first two non-zero terms or between the last two non zero terms, that is,
\displaystyle p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}=3
or
\displaystyle p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-p)=2
. After simplifying first case, we obtain
\displaystyle n_{1}=n_{2}=1,p=3
, and there is a zero term between
\displaystyle 1
and
\displaystyle p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}
such that log-concavity ceases. Also, from the second condition, we get
\displaystyle n_{1}=n_{2}=1,p-q=2
or
\displaystyle n_{1}=2,n_{2}=1,p=2
and
\displaystyle q=3.
For this condition there is a term such that
\displaystyle a_{p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}+1}=0\ngeq a_{p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}-1}}a_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}}}
, so log-concavity fails. Conversely, with these conditions, it is easy to verify that log-concavity is not true.
∎

3
Independence polynomial of comaximal graphs of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}

In this section, we discuss the independence polynomial of comaximal graphs of commutative ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
for various values of
\displaystyle n.
We recall some basic facts about the polynomial
\displaystyle I(G,x).

It is well known that the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle K_{n},n\geq 2
is
\displaystyle 1+nx,
the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle K_{1}
is
\displaystyle 1+x
and hence that of
\displaystyle nK_{1}
is
\displaystyle(1+x)^{n}.

We recall that the independence polynomials of graph operations
\displaystyle G_{1}\cup G_{2}
and
\displaystyle G_{1}\vee G_{2}
(see,
[
11
]
) satisfy the following

I(G_{1}\cup G_{2},x)=I(G_{1},x)I(G_{2},x),

(3.4)

I(G_{1}\vee G_{2},x)=I(G_{1},x)+I(G_{2},x)-1.

(3.5)

The following result gives the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n},n\geq 2
for some values of
\displaystyle n.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
be the comaximal graph of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n},n\geq 2
. Then the following hold.

(i)

The independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
for
\displaystyle n=p
is
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)=1+px,
where
\displaystyle p
is prime.

(ii)

For
\displaystyle n=pq,
where
\displaystyle(p<q)
are primes, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is

I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)=\big(\phi(n)-1\big)x+(1+x)^{p}+(1+x)^{q}-1.

(iii)

For
\displaystyle n=p^{m}
where
\displaystyle p
is prime and
\displaystyle m
is positive integer, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is

I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)=\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p^{m-1}}.

(iv)

For
\displaystyle n=p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}},
where
\displaystyle(p<q)
are primes and
\displaystyle n_{1},n_{2}
are positive integers, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is

I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)=\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p^{2(n_{1}-1)}q^{2(n_{2}-1)}(q-1)}+(1+x)^{p^{2(n_{1}-1)}q^{2(n_{2}-1)}(p-1)}-(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}}.

Proof.

(i) For
\displaystyle n=p,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is isomorphic to
\displaystyle K_{p}
and the result follows.

(ii) For
\displaystyle n=pq
,
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})=K_{\phi(n)}\vee(K_{1}\cup K_{p-1,q-1})
and
\displaystyle K_{p-1,q-1}=\overline{K}_{p-1}\vee\overline{K}_{q-1}.
By (
3.5
) and (
3.4
), the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\overline{K}_{p-1}\vee\overline{K}_{q-1}
is
\displaystyle I(\overline{K}_{p-1},x)+I(\overline{K}_{q-1},x)=(1+x)^{p-1}+(1+x)^{q-1}-1
and that of
\displaystyle K_{1}\cup\Big(\overline{K}_{p-1}\vee\overline{K}_{q-1}\Big)
is
\displaystyle(1+x)^{p}+(1+x)^{q}-(1+x).
Again using (
3.5
), the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p}+(1+x)^{q}-(1+x)-1

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\big(\phi(n)-1\big)x+(1+x)^{p}+(1+x)^{q}-1.

(iii) For
\displaystyle n=p^{m},m\geq 2,
the comaximal graph is
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})\cong K_{\phi(n)}\vee\overline{K}_{p^{m-1}}
and by (
3.5
) of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=I(K_{\phi(n)},x)+I(\overline{K}_{p^{m-1}},x)-1=1+\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p^{m-1}}-1

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p^{m-1}}.

(iv) For
\displaystyle n=p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}},

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})\cong K_{\phi(n)}\vee\Big(\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}}\cup\big(\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-1)}\vee\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(p-1)}\big)\Big)
and by repeated applications of (
3.5
) and
3.4
, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-1)}\vee\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(p-1)}
is

(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-1)}+(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(p-1)}-1,

and that of
\displaystyle\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}}\cup\big(\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-1)}\vee\overline{K}_{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(p-1)}\big)
is

(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}}\Big((1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-1)}+(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(p-1)}-1\Big).

Finally, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})\,x)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}}\Big((1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(q-1)}+(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}(p-1)}-1\Big)-1

\displaystyle\displaystyle+1+\phi(n)x

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p^{2(n_{1}-1)}q^{2(n_{2}-1)}(q-1)}+(1+x)^{p^{2(n_{1}-1)}q^{2(n_{2}-1)}(p-1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-(1+x)^{p^{n_{1}-1}q^{n_{2}-1}}.

∎

With similar idea, Theorem
3.1
can be generalized for
\displaystyle n=p^{n_{1}}p^{n_{2}}r^{n_{3}},
where
\displaystyle n_{i}
’s are non negative integers and
\displaystyle p,q,r
are primes. It is interesting to investigate the zeros of the independence polynomials of comaximal graphs. The umimodality and log-concavity of
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(R),x)
remains an open topic for comaximal graphs of arbitrary rings.

Similar to Theorem
2.2
, we have the following result for the independence polynomial for the comaximal graph of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G\cong\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
be the comaximal graph of order
\displaystyle n.
Then the following hold.

(i)

If
\displaystyle n
is product of distinct primes, then the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle G
is

I(G,x)=\phi(n)x+(1+x)I(G_{2},x),

where
\displaystyle I(G_{2},x)
is the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle G_{2}
.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{n_{i}},
then the independent domination polynomial of
\displaystyle G
is

I(G,x)=\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p_{1}^{n_{1}-1}p_{2}^{n_{2}-1}\dots p_{k}^{n_{k}-1}}I(G_{2},x),

where
\displaystyle I(G_{2},x)
is the independence polynomial of the
\displaystyle G_{2}.

From, the above results of
\displaystyle I(G,x)
for
\displaystyle G\cong\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, it follows that the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
can be completely determined if we are able to find
\displaystyle I(G_{2},x)
for the graph
\displaystyle G_{2}
. The structure of
\displaystyle G_{n}
becomes very complex as the value of
\displaystyle k
increases in
\displaystyle n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{n_{i}}.
So, it becomes quit technical and seems hard to find its explicit expression as we increase value of
\displaystyle k
beyond
\displaystyle 3.

Now, for the zeros of the independence polynomial. We first consider
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}),x)=1+px,
which is clearly log-concave and unimodal. Also, its zeros are real. Now, for
\displaystyle n=pq
, the independence polynomial is

I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)=\big(\phi(n)-1\big)x+(1+x)^{p}+(1+x)^{q}-1.

Expending the above equation, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+pqx+\Bigg(\binom{p}{2}+\binom{q}{2}\Bigg)x^{2}+\Bigg(\binom{p}{3}+\binom{q}{3}\Bigg)x^{3}+\sum_{i=4}^{p}\binom{p}{i}+\sum_{i=4}^{q}\binom{q}{i}.

(3.6)

We note that
\displaystyle 2pq\geq p(p-1)+q(q-1)
is not true in general. However, for
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle q=3
, it is true and for
\displaystyle q\geq 3
, it is false. So,
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2\cdot 3}),x)
is unimodal and log-concave. For
\displaystyle q\geq 3
, it is not unimodal. Also, it is not necessarily log-concave for
\displaystyle q\geq 3
(can be seen for
\displaystyle q=7
). Thus, in general
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
is neither unimodal nor log-concave. It is interesting to characterize the values of
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle q
such that
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
is neither unimodal nor log-concave.

We recall the famous Eneström-Kakeya theorem (see,
[
7
]
) for finding the region in plane for the zero of a polynomial. For a polynomial
\displaystyle p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
with positive coefficients, its zeros lie in the annulus
\displaystyle a\leq|Z|\leq b,

where

a=\min\left\{\left|\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i+1}}\right|:0\leq i\leq n-1\right\}~\text{and}~b=\max\left\{\left|\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i+1}}\right|:0\leq i\leq n-1\right\}.

From Equation
3.6
, it is clear that
\displaystyle 0<a<1
and
\displaystyle b=\frac{q(q-1)}{2q}=\frac{q-1}{2}.
Therefore, the non-zero zeros of
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
given in (
3.6
) lies in the region
\displaystyle 0<|Z|<\frac{q-1}{2}.
We illustrate it with the following example.

For
\displaystyle p=7
and
\displaystyle q=11
, the independence polynomial is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{7\cdot 11}),x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+77x+76x^{2}+200x^{3}+365x^{4}+483x^{5}+469x^{6}+331x^{7}+165x^{8}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+55x^{9}+11x^{10}+x^{11}.

It is obvious that above polynomial is neither log-concave nor unimodal. The approximated zeros of the above polynomial are

\displaystyle\displaystyle-0.013152,

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2.49122\pm 0.488584i,-1.98695\pm 1.24354i,-1.09244\pm 1.50704i,

\displaystyle\displaystyle-0.18907\pm 1.25975i,0.266252\pm 0.55771i.

The above zeros lie in
\displaystyle 0<|Z|<5
. Pictorially, these zero are represented by blue dots in Figure
4
.

Representation of zeros of
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{7\cdot 11}),x)
and
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{5}}),x)
on plane.

From Figure
4
, the zeros of
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{7\cdot 11}),x)
form an interesting pattern and lie on boundary of some disc centred on negative x-axis. By computations, we observed this patter for large values of
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle q.
So, it will of interest to find the curve of the limit of zeros.

Next, for
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{m}}),x)=\phi(n)x+(1+x)^{p^{m-1}},
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{m}}),x)=1+p^{m}x+\binom{p^{m-1}}{2}x^{2}+\binom{p^{m-1}}{3}x^{2}+\dots+x^{p^{m-1}}.

It easily follows from the pattern of the binomial coefficients that the oscillation is exactly one and the polynomial is unimodal. However, it is not log-concave as

\binom{p^{m-1}}{2}^{2}\ngeq p^{m}\binom{p^{m-1}}{3},

as can be verified for
\displaystyle p=2
. Also, by Eneström-Kakeya theorem, its zeros lie in
\displaystyle 0<|Z|<\frac{p^{m-1}-1}{2}.
For
\displaystyle 2^{5}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{5}}),x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+32x+120x^{2}+560x^{3}+1820x^{4}+4368x^{5}+8008x^{6}+11440x^{7}+12870x^{8}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+11440x^{9}+8008x^{10}+4368x^{11}+1820x^{12}+560x^{13}+120x^{14}+16x^{15}+x^{16},

and its zeros are

\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{5}}),x)

4
Conclusion

The current articles present the results on the independence polynomial and the independent domination polynomial for comaximal graphs of the commutative ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}.

The independent domination polynomials
\displaystyle D_{i}(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
for
\displaystyle n\in\{p^{n_{1}},p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}},p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}}r^{n_{3}}\}
were presented explicitly. The polynomial
\displaystyle D_{i}(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
is unimodal and log-concave with
\displaystyle n\in\{p^{n_{1}},p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}}\}
. Similarly, the independence polynomial
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
were found for
\displaystyle n\in\{p^{n_{1}},p^{n_{1}}q^{n_{2}}\}.

The problem of determining the independent domination polynomial/independence polynomial, as well as properties such as unimodality (log-concave), remains open for general values of
\displaystyle n.
The most intriguing aspect is discovering relationships between these polynomials and their corresponding rings. The location of zeros (or their bounds) in such polynomials for general
\displaystyle n
is a non-trivial remaining part.
