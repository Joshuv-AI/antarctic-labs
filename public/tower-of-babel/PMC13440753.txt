INTRODUCTION

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) constitute a group of malignant neoplasms with neuroectodermal origin. MPNSTs are rare, with an expected incidence of 0.1/100,000 per year.[22] They constitute only 5% to 10% of all soft-tissue sarcomas.[23-25] A variety of terminologies, including neurofibrosarcoma, neurogenic sarcoma, malignant schwannoma, and malignant neurilemmoma, have been used before to describe the tumor.[28] Approximately 25–50% of observed MPNSTs occur in patients with NF-1, 10% are linked to previous radiotherapy treatments, and the remaining cases arise sporadically.[1,15,23] Like other soft-tissue sarcomas, MPNSTs tend to recur locally (28–57%) and spread hematogenously (distant metastasis 28–34%).[2]

Theoretically, MPNSTs can develop in any peripheral nerve, often occurring in the trunk and proximal parts of limbs such as the buttocks, thighs, brachial plexus, sciatic nerve, and paraspinal region.[9,28] Occurrences of MPNSTs on the scalp are exceptionally rare, with only 26 cases involving the scalp reported in the published literature.[30] We wish to report a rare case of giant scalp MPNST and discuss the management options of this rare entity, along with the literature review pertaining to the same.

CASE REPORT

A 50-year-old male presented with a firm to hard swelling on the top of his head for the past 8 months. The swelling was initially the size of a pea, but it gradually increased to the size of a tennis ball. There was no history of rupture or discharge from it. There was no history of headache, seizure, fever, vomiting, blurring of vision, or trauma. He had no comorbidities. On examination, there was a midline swelling in the frontoparietal region, measuring about 10 cm × 9 cm × 7 cm. Swelling was firm with intermittent areas of hard consistency with well-defined margins and a smooth surface. There were no ulcers, discharging sinuses, or signs of inflammation. The cough impulse was negative. There were dilated vessels visible over the skin [Figure 1]. There were no neurocutaneous markers. The rest of the neurological examination was unremarkable. Computer tomography [Figure 2] and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [Figure 3] showed an extra-axial, transcalvarial heterogeneously enhancing lesion of size 10 cm × 9 cm × 7 cm in midline in the frontoparietal convexity. Few calcific foci were seen [Figure 2]. Multiple flow voids are seen within the lesion on T2-weighted MRI sequences. There was significant destruction of the overlying bones. The anterior and middle parts of the superior sagittal sinus were not visualized.

The patient was planned for a bi-frontoparietal craniectomy, excision of the tumor, and mesh cranioplasty. A bi-coronal skin incision was made. Subperiosteal dissection was done, and the skin flap was elevated after careful dissection from the lesion, as it was lying beneath the periosteal layer in the central part.

Intraoperatively, the tumor was seen to be overlying the superior sagittal sinus and extending bilaterally in the frontoparietal regions. It was adherent to the superior sagittal sinus in places. It was infiltrating the overlying frontal and parietal bone through which it was coming out and lying just below the peri-cranium. A portion of the involved bone with clear margins was resected [Figure 4]. The underlying dura was coagulated. There was no intradural extension. The calvarial defect was closed using two titanium mesh (Johnson and Johnson, India), size 4” × 4” [Figure 5]. His postoperative period was uneventful, and he was discharged on the 7th postoperative day. Histopathology revealed MPNSTs. Immunohistochemistry was positive for S100, and Ki 67 was more than 20% [Figure 6a-f].

The patient was advised radiotherapy, but did not comply because of financial constraints.

He is doing well at 30 months of follow-up without any recurrence of symptoms [Figure 7].

DISCUSSION

MPNSTs are uncommon malignant tumors that originate from peripheral nerve fibroblasts or Schwann cells.[4,12] According to the recent World Health Organization classification of 2021, MPNST is further divided into two categories: epithelioid MPNST and MPNST with perineurial differentiation.[21] Histopathologically, MPNSTs are characterized by high cellularity and a pattern of elongated spindle-shaped cells arranged in bundles.[9,13,23,28,32] The invasion of surrounding tissues, variation in cell nuclei, presence of necrosis, and high rate of cell division indicate their malignant nature.[9] Immunostaining for the S-100 indicates their origin from Schwann cells, although it is not entirely specific.[13,14,23,26,32]

Patients usually present with a slowly growing, round swelling on the scalp, which can be either on the skin’s surface or beneath it.[4,9,12,28] Initially, these masses tend to be firm, round, painless, nonpulsatile, and noncompressible.[28] If left untreated, these tumors can grow rapidly. Rapid growth can lead to invasion of the calvaria and protective layers around the brain (dura mater).[28] A lesion >10 cm in size is considered a giant MPNST.[3] Some of the giant tumors can erode the overlying skin.[20] If it invades the brain parenchyma, it can cause symptoms such as headaches, vomiting, seizures, and focal neurological deficits.[9,28]

Diagnostic imaging plays a crucial role in determining tumor resectability, assessing surgical risks, and devising reconstruction strategies. Computed tomography scan illustrates the degree of bone involvement in the form of invasion or destruction. MRI is the preferred investigative method due to its ability to delineate the anatomical connections between the lesion and nearby soft tissues, encompassing muscles, blood vessels, and nerves.[9,29]

Of the 26 reported cases of scalp MPNSTs in the literature [Table 1], 16 cases were giant. Ours is also a case of giant MPNSTs with dimensions of 10 × 8 × 6 cm. On average, these large tumors grew for about 77 months before treatment; interestingly, it took only 8 months in our case to reach such a gigantic size. It is more common in males, with an average age of 47 years, as in our case.[21]

Of the previously reported cases, the occipital region was involved in 53% of cases, followed by parietal, frontal, and temporal regions. Our case involved the bi-frontoparietal region over the superior sagittal sinus, making its resection challenging. In the current scenario, surgery takes precedence in managing MPNS.[5] Complete excision is challenging, and local recurrence is unavoidable due to the proximity of such giant-sized tumors to tiny deposits and surrounding essential neurovascular systems.[16]

The surgical objective is to completely remove the tumor with negative margins of at least 2 cm.[9,28] Following resection, these cases often require reconstruction of the calvarial using an implant.

Certain authors have proposed the complete removal of the tumor with 2-cm clear margins, followed by radiation therapy, as a customary approach.[17] Despite the absence of dedicated literature supporting a definitive standard treatment for MPNSTs, these authors suggest the use of supplementary radiotherapy to enhance local tumor control. However, the potential benefits lack clear substantiation based on available evidence.[6,27,28,31] Chemotherapy was initiated for patients with NF1 and large lesions, but metastases and recurrence could not be shortened, casting doubt on the use of chemotherapy in such lesions after surgery.[16] Chemotherapy is reserved for high-grade lesions with distant metastasis. In the reported literature of 26 cases, 38% underwent excision only, while 38% underwent excision followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, 20% underwent excision followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and 4% patient underwent excision followed by chemotherapy. These patients had a follow-up period ranging from 4 months to 7 years, during which 3 patients died, 2 patients had recurrence, and 5 were lost to follow-up. We managed the case with complete surgical excision with wide margins. Our patient was advised radiotherapy, but the patient refused it because of financial constraints.

Despite apparent successful complete surgical removals, local recurrence at the previously operated site remains a common issue, necessitating subsequent interventions such as radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy.

Local recurrence of the tumor has been documented in 52–88.9% cases of MPNSTs originating in various locations.[28] The overall survival rate for these highly aggressive tumors is frequently restricted, with a reported figure of approximately 30% over 5 years.[5,17,19,28]

CONCLUSION

The cases of MPNST should be treated by resection of the affected bone and cranioplasty followed by radiation therapy. Chemotherapy is reserved for high grade lesions with distant metastasis. The overall survival rate for these highly aggressive tumors is frequently restricted, with a reported figure of approximately 30% over a 5-year period.
