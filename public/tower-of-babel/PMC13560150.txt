Can curriculum-integrated health education improve postgraduate student well-Being? One-group pretest–posttest design
Authors: Erika da Silva Maciel, André Pontes-Silva, Fernando Rodrigues Peixoto Quaresma, Ladislau Ribeiro do Nascimento
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether a curriculum-integrated educational intervention focused on psychosocial well-being is associated with changes in perceived stress and quality of life among postgraduate students.MethodsThis pre-experimental study (one-group pretest–posttest design without a control group) was conducted over one academic semester. A total of 31 postgraduate students were enrolled. The intervention consisted of a curriculum-integrated course addressing mental health, self-care, and well-being strategies through structured weekly sessions. Outcomes included perceived stress (PSS-10), quality of life (WHOQOL-bref), self-compassion, and life satisfaction. Pre–post comparisons were performed using paired-sample t-tests, with 95% confidence intervals and effect sizes (Cohen’s d).ResultsA total of 31 postgraduate students were enrolled and completed both baseline and post-intervention assessments, with no loss to follow-up. A statistically significant reduction in perceived stress was observed (mean difference: −3.63; 95% CI: −6.08 to − 1.18; p = 0.005; d = 0.59). Overall quality of life showed a statistically significant increase (mean difference: 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.27; p = 0.010; d = 0.30). No statistically significant changes were observed for self-compassion, life satisfaction, or health satisfaction (p > 0.05). Domain-specific analyses of quality of life showed no significant changes in the environmental domain (p = 0.325), while other domains presented identical mean values at both time points, precluding inferential analysis.ConclusionThe curriculum-integrated educational intervention was associated with reduced perceived stress and improved overall quality of life among postgraduate students. Given the pre-experimental design and absence of a control group, causal inferences cannot be established. These findings suggest that curriculum-based strategies may represent a feasible approach to promote psychosocial well-being in postgraduate education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28256-6.

Introduction

Studies conducted across different countries and institutional contexts indicate a growing increase in levels of stress, anxiety, and depression among university students, particularly those enrolled in postgraduate programs. Evidence suggests that the prevalence of mental disorders, as well as associated symptoms such as panic attacks, sleep disturbances, and suicide risk, is consistently higher among postgraduate—and especially graduate—students compared with the general population [1–4].

Robust data underscore the magnitude of this issue. An international study involving 2,279 master’s and doctoral students from 26 countries and 234 institutions found that 39% presented moderate to severe depressive symptoms and 41% reported anxiety symptoms. These students were approximately six times more likely to experience such disorders compared with the general population. These findings demonstrate that psychological distress in the context of postgraduate education constitutes a global and structural phenomenon [5].

Impairment of mental health in this population extends beyond individual suffering, directly affecting academic performance, program retention, and the overall quality of training, thereby generating cascading effects on scientific output, innovation, and the personal lives of postgraduate students. Students experiencing high levels of stress tend to engage less in research activities, face a greater risk of attrition, and report poorer perceptions of their academic experience [6].

In the Brazilian context, there has been substantial growth in scientific production, associated with the consolidation of a comprehensive national postgraduate education system. However, researchers’ health and well-being have emerged as indispensable factors for the sustainability and advancement of this system, requiring specific attention from higher education and research institutions. Despite the strategic relevance of postgraduate education for the country’s scientific development, postgraduate students’ mental health continues to receive limited attention through systematic institutional policies [7].

Among the primary factors associated with psychological distress and impaired well-being are emotional exhaustion, recurrent negative affect, dissatisfaction with the program, uncertainty regarding future career prospects, productivity pressure, abrupt routine changes, and difficulties adapting to the academic environment, particularly in highly competitive settings [2, 3, 6]. These factors were significantly exacerbated during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, a period marked by increased social isolation, emotional overload, heightened suicide risk, and reduced access to mental health services [8].

Although the determinants of psychological distress in postgraduate education are widely recognized, a substantial gap persists between accumulated knowledge and the implementation of effective coping strategies, particularly those integrated into the academic structure. Factors such as productivity pressure, scholarship instability, professional uncertainty, and excessive workload continue to contribute significantly to this scenario, with direct repercussions for scientific and institutional development.

In this regard, institutional strategies aimed at promoting well-being—including psychological support programs, academic mentoring, health education initiatives, and policies fostering work–life balance—have been identified as essential to mitigate these effects. Evidence indicates that universities that systematically invest in the mental health of their postgraduate students achieve relevant institutional benefits, such as increased scientific productivity, lower attrition rates, and higher student satisfaction [6].

Despite the growing body of evidence on mental health challenges in postgraduate education, there remains a critical gap between problem recognition and the implementation of scalable, systematically evaluated interventions within academic programs. Most existing strategies are extracurricular, voluntary, or focused on individual-level support, which limits their reach, sustainability, and institutional impact [9]. Furthermore, there is a notable lack of rigorously designed studies evaluating interventions embedded directly within the formal curriculum, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where structural and institutional constraints may further limit access to mental health resources [10].

Emerging evidence suggests that structured educational interventions targeting mental health literacy and self-regulation may contribute to improvements in psychological well-being among university students, although findings remain heterogeneous and context-dependent [9].

Curriculum-integrated interventions represent a promising approach because they are embedded within students’ academic routines, ensuring higher participation rates, reducing self-selection bias, and promoting institutional responsibility for student well-being. Unlike optional or extracurricular programs, curriculum-integrated strategies may facilitate sustained exposure to well-being–oriented content and foster behavioral change through continuous engagement and peer interaction [11]. Additionally, such approaches align with contemporary perspectives that emphasize structural and educational determinants of mental health rather than solely individual responsibility [12].

However, empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of curriculum-integrated interventions in improving psychosocial outcomes among postgraduate students remains limited. In particular, there is a lack of studies simultaneously examining perceived stress and quality of life within a single structured academic intervention, highlighting the need for integrative and context-sensitive approaches [13].

In light of this context and the identified research gap, the present study aimed to evaluate whether a curriculum-integrated educational component structured around psychosocial well-being and quality of life strategies can reduce stress-related indicators and improve aspects of well-being among postgraduate students.

We hypothesized that participation in this curriculum-integrated intervention would be associated with reduced perceived stress and improved overall quality of life.

Methods

Study design

This was a one-group pretest–posttest design without a control group, conducted to evaluate outcomes before and after an educational intervention aimed at promoting academic well-being among postgraduate students. The study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND statement) [14]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Tocantins (protocol no. 6657900).

Due to its pre-experimental nature, this design was considered appropriate for preliminary evaluation of intervention feasibility and potential effectiveness in a real-world educational context, particularly when randomization and control groups are not feasible.

Study setting and context

The study was carried out within the elective course Happiness and Academic Well-Being, offered by the Postgraduate Program in Science and Health Education (PPGECS) at the Federal University of Tocantins (UFT). The course is open to students regularly enrolled in stricto sensu postgraduate programs at UFT, regardless of academic background. Data collection took place over one academic semester, according to the institutional calendar, from March to July 2024.

Participants

All students enrolled in the course during the study period were invited to participate on a voluntary basis. The inclusion criteria were regular enrollment in the course and provision of written informed consent. Since all enrolled participants completed assessments at both time points (baseline and post-intervention), no exclusions were necessary.

A convenience sampling strategy was used, including all students enrolled in the course during the study period. Although this approach may limit external validity, it is commonly adopted in educational intervention studies conducted within formal academic settings.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1), based on the observed effect size for the primary outcome (perceived stress), with α = 0.05. The achieved statistical power was 0.82 (1 − β), indicating adequate statistical power to detect the observed effect size. Participant flow was straightforward, as all enrolled students completed both baseline and post-intervention assessments, with no loss to follow-up.

A total of 31 students were enrolled in the course during the study period, and all participants completed both baseline and post-intervention assessments, resulting in no loss to follow-up. Therefore, the final analytical sample consisted of all enrolled participants (n = 31).

Intervention

The intervention consisted of pedagogical activities developed throughout the Happiness and Academic Well-Being course, totaling approximately 60 contact hours distributed across weekly sessions. The course was delivered by faculty members from different academic fields, as well as invited external contributors, adopting an interdisciplinary approach [15].

Course content addressed factors associated with illness in the academic context and educational strategies aimed at comprehensive health promotion. Topics included nutrition in the university setting, physical activity, time management, mental health, stress, anxiety, depression, sleep quality, and social relationships. Pedagogical strategies comprised lectures, reflective activities, development and sharing of educational materials, and collaborative group dynamics among participants [16, 17].

The intervention followed a structured curriculum delivered over 16 weekly sessions (approximately 3.5–4 h per session). The program was conducted once per week over a period of approximately 16 weeks, corresponding to a full academic semester. This scheduling ensured regular exposure to the intervention while maintaining feasibility within the academic routine.

Each session included three components: (1) theoretical exposition (40–60 min), (2) guided reflective or experiential activity (60–90 min), and (3) group discussion and feedback (60–90 min). The content was organized into thematic modules: (a) mental health literacy (stress, anxiety, depression), (b) lifestyle behaviors (physical activity, nutrition, sleep), (c) self-regulation and time management, and (d) social relationships and academic engagement.

The experiential component included structured activities such as guided reflection exercises, group discussions on personal stressors, goal-setting tasks related to health behaviors (e.g., sleep hygiene and physical activity planning), and brief mindfulness and breathing exercises aimed at stress regulation. Participants were also encouraged to engage in self-monitoring practices between sessions, such as tracking daily routines and reflecting on behavioral changes.

The intervention did not include supervised physical exercise sessions (e.g., aerobic or resistance training). Instead, physical activity was addressed from an educational and behavioral perspective, focusing on awareness, planning, and encouragement of autonomous engagement in regular activity. Active learning strategies were employed, including problem-based discussions, peer interaction, and self-monitoring tasks.

Sessions were delivered by faculty members with expertise in health sciences and education, supported by invited professionals (e.g., psychologists, physiotherapists, and nutritionists). This multidisciplinary approach aimed to address both behavioral and psychosocial dimensions of health promotion. To ensure consistency across sessions, a predefined teaching plan was followed. Session content and structure were standardized across instructors to ensure consistency in delivery.

Self-administered instruments were applied through an electronic survey platform. Anthropometric and clinical assessments were conducted in person by a previously trained research team, following standardized protocols.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed according to the specific scoring recommendations of each instrument. Descriptive analyses were initially performed. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed variables, pre- and post-intervention comparisons were conducted using paired-sample t-tests, with results reported as t-values, degrees of freedom (df), and p-values. For non-normally distributed variables, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied, with results reported as z-values and p-values.

Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for parametric analyses and r for nonparametric analyses, with interpretation based on established thresholds. Given the small sample size, categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test when appropriate, instead of chi-square tests. For parametric analyses, mean differences (Δ) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27, with a significance level set at 5% (p < 0.05).

A significant reduction in perceived stress was observed following the intervention (t = 3.044, df = 26, p = 0.005), with a mean decrease of 3.63 points (95% CI: −6.08 to − 1.18), corresponding to a moderate effect size (d = 0.59). Similarly, overall quality of life showed a statistically significant increase (t = − 2.74, df = 30, p = 0.010), with a mean increase of 0.15 points (95% CI: 0.03 to 0.27), representing a small-to-moderate effect size (d = 0.30).

No statistically significant differences were observed for self-compassion (t = 0.296, df = 25, p = 0.770; d = 0.06), life satisfaction (t = − 0.178, df = 21, p = 0.861; d = 0.04), or health satisfaction (t = 1.000, df = 30, p = 0.325; d = 0.18).

Domain-specific analyses of the WHOQOL-bref indicated no statistically significant changes in the environmental domain (t = − 1.000, df = 30, p = 0.325). For the psychological, physical, and social relationships domains, identical mean values at both time points resulted in zero variance in difference scores, precluding inferential statistical analysis. These findings suggest that the intervention was associated with improvements in global quality of life, whereas domain-specific components remained stable over the study period.

Discussion

In the present study, a curriculum-integrated educational intervention was associated with a reduction in perceived stress and an improvement in overall quality of life among postgraduate students over one academic semester. The magnitude of these findings should be considered when interpreting their relevance: the reduction in perceived stress showed a moderate effect size (d = 0.59), whereas the improvement in overall quality of life was small-to-moderate (d = 0.30). These results suggest that the intervention may have had a more pronounced impact on outcomes that are sensitive to short-term contextual changes, such as perceived stress, while broader constructs may be less responsive over a single semester.

Mental health challenges among postgraduate students are well documented and have been consistently associated with impaired academic performance, reduced productivity, and increased risk of attrition [2, 5]. In this context, curriculum-integrated approaches may represent a relevant institutional strategy, as they are embedded within the academic structure and may increase exposure to well-being–oriented content while reducing barriers to participation. Rather than targeting only individual-level factors, such approaches may contribute to modifying aspects of the educational environment that are associated with psychological distress [4, 5, 24, 25].

It is well established that mental health problems negatively affect academic productivity, performance, and contribute to attrition in postgraduate programs [2]. This phenomenon raises concerns not only regarding individual and collective well-being but also concerning financial and developmental repercussions for higher education and research institutions. The urgency of addressing this issue was already evident prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2018, Evans et al. [5] reported that postgraduate students were six times more likely to develop mental health problems compared with the general population.

Importantly, no statistically significant changes were observed in self-compassion, life satisfaction, or health satisfaction. The very small effect sizes observed for these outcomes (d ≤ 0.18) suggest that the intervention had limited impact on more stable or trait-like psychological constructs. These findings are consistent with the notion that deeper psychological dimensions often require more targeted, intensive, or longer-term interventions to achieve measurable change. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle effects.

Similarly, domain-specific measures of quality of life remained unchanged. In some cases, identical mean values at both time points resulted in zero variance, precluding inferential analysis. This pattern reinforces the interpretation that global perceptions of quality of life may be more sensitive to short-term interventions, whereas domain-specific components may require sustained or more specific strategies to demonstrate meaningful change.

The findings should be interpreted with caution given the pre-experimental design without a control group, which precludes causal inference. The observed changes cannot be attributed exclusively to the intervention, as alternative explanations—such as regression to the mean, temporal effects, or external influences—cannot be ruled out. Therefore, the results should be understood as associative and exploratory. Additionally, several outcomes were assessed using self-reported instruments, including perceived stress (PSS-10), self-compassion (Self-Compassion Scale), life satisfaction (Satisfaction With Life Scale), and quality of life (WHOQOL-bref), which may introduce response bias.

In addition, selection bias may have influenced the findings, as participation was based on voluntary enrollment in an elective course. This may have attracted students with a pre-existing interest in well-being or health-related topics. However, no loss to follow-up was observed, as all participants completed both assessments, which strengthens the internal consistency of the findings.

Despite these limitations, the findings have practical implications. The intervention was implemented within an existing academic structure, requiring relatively low cost and no major institutional restructuring. The observed moderate reduction in perceived stress suggests that curriculum-integrated strategies may represent a feasible approach to support student well-being in postgraduate education. However, the modest magnitude of improvement in quality of life and the absence of changes in other psychosocial outcomes indicate that such strategies should likely be complemented by more targeted or sustained interventions.

A notable strength of the study is the complete follow-up of participants, with no missing outcome data at the individual level, which reduces the risk of attrition bias and enhances the internal validity of the pre–post comparisons. This study should be interpreted as an exploratory evaluation of a curriculum-based approach, providing preliminary evidence to inform future research. Studies with controlled designs, larger samples, and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these findings and to better understand the mechanisms underlying changes in psychosocial outcomes.

Conclusion

The curriculum-integrated educational intervention was associated with a significant reduction in perceived stress and a modest improvement in overall quality of life among postgraduate students over one academic semester. These findings suggest that embedding health promotion and well-being strategies within the academic curriculum may represent a feasible and low-cost approach to support student well-being in higher education settings.

No significant changes were observed in self-compassion, life satisfaction, or domain-specific quality of life measures, indicating that broader or more stable psychosocial constructs may require longer-term or more targeted interventions to achieve measurable effects.

Given the pre-experimental design and absence of a control group, causal inferences cannot be established. Nevertheless, the observed improvements in key outcomes highlight the potential relevance of curriculum-based strategies as part of institutional efforts to promote mental health and quality of life in postgraduate education. Future studies using controlled designs and larger samples are warranted to confirm these findings and further explore their long-term impact.
