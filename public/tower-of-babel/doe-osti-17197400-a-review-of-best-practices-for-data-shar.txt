 ABSTRACT

The Department of Energy’s (DOE) rich portfolio of research data assets reflects a variety of different
domains and their respective standards, purposes, file types, and storage practices. Such multidisciplinary
data creates challenges for federated discovery platforms attempting to reconcile the different use cases,
schemas, designated communities, and licensing requirements along with needed controls and risk
mitigation techniques. Implementation of data management best practices such as a governance
framework, metadata standards and risk mitigation strategies across disciplines ensures not only
standardization of data and its accessibility but also enables long-range use and repurposing for future
user communities. In this document, we discuss possible applications of best practices for multi-
stakeholder data sharing to meet the challenge of uniting teams around common purposes and uses,
alongside proactive risk management and human oversight, to facilitate accessible use of connected tools,
data, and other scientific research assets.

 1. INTRODUCTION

In the past 10+ years, the Open Science movement has promoted the concept of data sharing to ensure
better access and usability of research data assets and foster better collaboration between organizations
and across disciplines. Data Management Plans (DMPs) are developed by research teams to indicate how
data will be collected, managed, used, stored, and shared. Included information is often related to the
validation and quality of the data, accessibility, storage choice and size, management of data and its
oversight, security controls, and details on licensing and regulatory requirements and policies (DCN n.d.).

Using standards modeled by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the Data
Curation Network (DCN), Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility (OLCF), and other leading
organizations in the fields of energy research, high-performance computing, and national and homeland
security, we provide a federated approach to research data discovery, use, and interoperability. To
facilitate seamless data sharing that promotes usability across researchers and stakeholders, while
ensuring that relevant data is discoverable by intended user communities, we highlight best practices
using a multi-tiered approach that combines governance, data management, curation and security. This
ensures that we meet the challenges of varied data across entities, core capabilities, tools, and the
available data that is created or used to support the agency’s mission and vision. For projects related to
improving, maintaining, and protecting critical infrastructure, these processes assist in the uniting of
shared data around a particular function.

 2. CURRENT PRACTICES FOR DATA SHARING RESEARCH DATA ASSETS

Scientific data strategies have stressed the importance of data management to promote FAIR (findable,
accessible, interoperable, and reproducible) principles (Wilkinson 2016) to ensure that data remains a
first-class citizen of scholarship. Currently, Data Curators are advancing beyond FAIR for findability and
discovery – and emerging methods focus largely on protocol documentation, systematic application of
policies through shared definitions and vocabularies, and automation for classification, flagging, and
tagging. For specific purposes and use cases, standardized metadata are classified and tagged during
curation processes and machine learning is employed to enforce regulations, policies, procedures, and
common definitions – preemptively flagging datasets for potential risk and aiding in the development of
role-based access controls (RBACs). Intellectual property rights, application of legal requirements, and

preferences of data owners can be applied during auto-tagging. Comprehensive documentation outlined in
governance and management schema ensure defensibility for audits, sponsor reviews, and crosswalks.

To maintain and secure critical infrastructure data throughout its entire lifecycle, it is recommended to
employ proactive and integrated data governance, management, and risk mitigation that aligns with
federal regulations and standards. This anticipatory curation enables improved long-term usability,
adaptability to evolving needs, while safeguarding the integrity and utility of research data assets.
Leveraging established capabilities and subject matter expertise in data curation, governance,
management, national security, and risk assessment and mitigation are supported by adoption and
adaptation of the Data Curation Network’s (DCN) strategies combined with the National Institute of
Standards and Technology’s Research Data Management Framework (NIST RDaF). These a phased
approach to decision-making, including:

 • Performance of SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats) analyses on existing
 individual and aggregated datasets and tools;

 • Engaging in asset inventories to understand current compliance with federal and state regulations,
 alongside DOE and laboratory policies;

 • Upholding federal standards for safeguard and security while increasing findability, accessibility,
 interoperability, and repeatability (FAIR) for intended user communities and in anticipation of
 data democratization (Hanisch 2024; Wilkinson 2016);

 • Ecosystem design and development prioritizing data quality and completeness, to enable partners
 to provide feedback and add datasets and through iterative augmentation capabilities.

Following this pilots can then be tested, adapted, and unified under this holistic approach to data
governance, curation, and management. Ultimately this enables accelerated scientific discovery while
guarding against unwanted access or misaligned utilization of data.

 3. DATA SHARING BEST PRACTICES FOR SCIENTIFIC ACCELERATION

The 2023 DOE Public Access Plan required research assets and outputs to be made available to the public
upon project completion. For stakeholder partners and research organizations, ensuring that the processes
for data sharing, including agreements and short-to-long-range planning and strategies adhere to policies
of both their organization and the overall agency itself, is challenged by fragmentation across norms,
practices, and storage, among others (Fishler & May 2026; Sprague et al. 2017). Across entities, there are
variations among the range in capabilities and tools, single organizational goals and objectives, and those
of individual contributors. Additionally, data sharing can often be subject to intentional non-
transparencies for several reasons – whether for protection of critical infrastructure assets, proprietary
information, or even due to a data owner’s views on intellectual property (Catalano et al. 2025).

Data sharing across jurisdictions is further hampered by the ad-hoc and disparate nature of previous
informal standards and common practices for data collection and storage, both within organizations and
among even those with like-missions and visions. To date, there is no one true policy or standard for data
sharing across laboratories or agencies, especially not at scale. Challenges emerge specifically where
regulatory practices and applications of controls and standards are subject to human interpretation.
Without standards for sharing, there are existing barriers to using and accessing research data assets.

The implementation of automated workflows for cross-sector curation and management for sensitive and
non-classified, controlled data – in alignment with federal policies and agency standards, can be informed
by practices led by consortiums like World Data Systems, NIST, DCN, and DOE-CESER, among others.
Establishment of rigorous and unified standards for how the data and information will be managed
throughout its lifecycle, according to its purpose, and which personnel are responsible, accountable,
consulted, and informed.

3.1 Building Trust through Validated Data, Engagement, and Strategic Management

The National Academies Institute of Medicine addresses sharing of the most sensitive clinical trial data
for the advancement of public health, acknowledging that trust-building from all members of the “data
community” is of great importance in promoting confidence in the medical research community, study
institution, and data utilized by research partners and stakeholders. Guiding principles for data sharing
focus on respect for trial participants, data owners, and promotion of protocols and protections for data
through its entire lifecycle. Prioritization of stakeholder and data owner engagement is focused on
development of trust in both the data and the process of sharing the data. Additionally, cost-benefit
analyses of harms of data sharing versus the benefits drives the needed protections and guardrails that
mitigate risk (NASEM 2014).

In 2023, the National Science and Technology Council (NSTC) highlighted data repositories and catalogs
as the best method for sharing scientific research across federally funded projects, and their
recommendations aligned with conventions in information sciences, archival management, and curatorial
spaces. Promotion of user guides and policies, long-term strategies and planning, and documentation and
tracking of provenance, reuse, and policies and procedures were aimed at boosting the quality and validity
of research data assets (NSTC 2023). The Data Curation Network further explores how trust is built
between researchers through provenance and reputation of individual contributors, their scientific outputs,
and institutional hosts of catalogs and repositories (DCN n.d.)

Recognizing that data catalogs are only as effective as their ability to convey trust to the end-user, best
practices across disciplines, agencies, and institutions reinforce the importance of data governance,
management, and standards and policies that guide high-quality curatorial practices and long-range
management. This not only ensures trust for an end-user and promotes the reputation of the repository
institution as trustworthy but can also ensures that the data identified and linked can be reused, and if
enabled, can provide ground-truth for AI systems (DCN n.d.). Even so, in practice, it is the responsibility
of those who govern, manage, and curate the final repository to ensure that there is enough information
and enough risk management tactics to ensure that the data is usable, but also protected from sharing with
unwanted actors or organizations (NIST RDaF).

3.2 Democratization to Overcome Fragmentation

To better ensure transparency, and that the data catalog is a trusted asset, it is recommended that existing
data catalog formats and solutions provide foundations for implementation of data sharing. This also
assists in informing the mitigation of risk of critical energy infrastructure data exposure beyond
designated or intended user communities. To do this, it is important to understand what types of data
members of a collaborative data sharing network have, and what data is lacking. This “data asset
inventory informs the coalition about the volume and size of data, its original purpose, formats, and
intended users. Additionally, establishing a mission and vision for how the data will be used – beyond use
cases, will inform how sharing will improve upon existing system – what the needs of the user base will

be, how the data is currently being used (or not used), workflows that will enable the highest quality, most
valuable data, and finally, how this data should be shared and used to better inform science and decision-
making capabilities across designated communities (Digital Preservation Coalition n.d.) Following this,
practices related to the development of a data sharing platform can take place. These include, but are not
limited to:

 • Establish a data governance plan and its overarching policies.
 • Establish roles, responsibilities, and processes from the formation of a governance committee to
 the roles and responsibilities of the data stewards.
 • Develop a data catalog based on needs, use cases, and user community needs
 • Complete a data management checklist when gathering datasets, answering questions about their
 provenance, storage, collection, and purpose.
 • Follow established curation standards, processes, and naming conventions for data and metadata.
 • Draft portal management plan and develop training & requirements concurrently with other tasks.
 • Begin linking datasets and perform risk assessments to inform role-based access controls and
 mitigation strategies.

3.3 Application of Data Sharing Policies and Procedures

When sharing data across organizations, either through repository infrastructure or other platforms, best
practices promote the standardization of documentation. Following DCN’s steps for curation, repository
or catalog staff may collect information about data in multiple stages to best track, manage, and display
for intended user communities. Curators and data stewards will encourage points of contact and/or
principal investigators to describe their data as completely as possible, and avoid using acronyms, jargon,
and/or abbreviations. If information is incomplete, missing, or confusing, curatorial professionals will
often engage data owners to better inform provenance, controls, and intended uses. A sample of this
information below provides an overview of needed information from PIs and data owners to expedite
curation processes and improve data use. Data management plans may include topics such as:

 • Methods of documentation, recording decision-making, pivots, and use of systems to complete
 and store information related to the project, including versioning;
 • Governance, responsibilities, accountabilities, and oversight;
 • Validation, ensuring adherence to standards and compliance with policies;
 • Maintenance and upkeep of data, storage, and reviews;
 • Audit defensibility, reasoning behind decision-making, and process implementation;
 • Risk assessment and management activities, particularly for datasets with unknown provenance
 or validity;
 • Risk to real-world systems and challenges, including challenges induced by policy changes;
 • Varied policy and procedures across organizations; and
 • Scalable requirements for designated communities, stakeholders, and personnel.

 4. CONCLUSION

This overview of best practices in data sharing among institutions and organizations across sectors is a
first step in the design, refinement, and implementation of data governance, management, and curation –
which are critical in operationalizing data sharing across jurisdictions. While subject matter experts have
previously discussed implementation of best practices for improving the findability, accessibility,
interoperability, and reproducibility of multidisciplinary research data assets – particularly across energy
and national security spaces – the application of these best practices in multi-laboratory spaces hinges
largely on the willingness of each stakeholder to align the mission, vision, and purpose behind the data
sharing activity itself. Alignment on collaborative processes, data governance and management, alongside
long-range strategic planning are the first steps to ensuring that open science and the subsequent call for
data are best utilized and maintained in short, mid, and long-term scenarios.
