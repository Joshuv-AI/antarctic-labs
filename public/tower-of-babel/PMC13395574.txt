Long‐Term Outcomes of Surgical Management for Submandibular Sialolithiasis: Gland Resection Versus Preservation
Authors: Claire Orth, Edward Tannenbaum, Evgeniya Molotkova, Piper Vander Waal, Kailey Henkle, Henry Hoffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDescribe long‐term outcomes of submandibular sialolithiasis treatment, comparing gland‐preserving procedures to gland resection.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study with prospective patient‐reported outcome assessment study included all patients who underwent initial surgical management of submandibular sialolithiasis by a single surgeon from 2013 to 2018. Patients were contacted in 2024 and surveyed regarding treatment response. The primary outcome was xerostomia at long‐term follow‐up, measured using the Xerostomia Questionnaire (XQ). Secondary outcomes included stone recurrence, postoperative complications, and “ultimate surgical success,” defined as resolution of salivary symptoms without additional interventions during the 5‐to‐10‐year interval following the initial series of procedures. Eighty‐two patients were identified; 36 (43.9%) completed follow‐up.ResultsOf 36 respondents (median follow‐up 97.4 months), 11 (30.6%) underwent initial gland resection and 25 (69.4%) underwent initial gland‐preserving, sialendoscopy‐assisted procedures. Among the preservation group, 1 patient required a second gland‐preserving procedure, and 3 ultimately underwent gland removal due to persistent symptoms. Resolution of symptoms after the first procedure occurred in all resection patients (100%) and 18 of 25 (72%) in the preservation group. Among those with preserved glands at follow‐up (n = 22), 6 (27.3%) reported xerostomia (mean XQ 8.0) compared to 4 of 14 (28.6%) in the resection group (mean XQ 3.4). Ultimate surgical success was achieved in 90.9% of the preservation group and 100% of the resection group.ConclusionsBoth surgical approaches offer durable outcomes, though gland preservation may carry a higher risk of persistent symptoms and potential need for additional intervention. Introduction

Approximately 80%–85% of sialoliths occur in the submandibular gland [1]. Contributing anatomical factors include the ascending course of Wharton's duct, which impairs salivary flow, and the gland's secretion of more viscous and alkaline saliva that increases the risk of mineral precipitation [1, 2]. Additional etiologies include obstructive processes such as ductal strictures or tumors and retrograde infection [3]. Clinically, sialolithiasis can cause significant symptom burden, with patients frequently reporting postprandial gland swelling and pain that can disrupt daily functioning and quality of life. Diagnosis is typically based on history and physical examination, supported by imaging modalities such as ultrasound, MRI, and CT [4, 5].

Surgical management of submandibular sialolithiasis has evolved over time. Historically, sialoadenectomy (gland removal) has been the primary treatment [6]. The advent of sialendoscopy has enabled minimally invasive, gland‐preserving techniques to enable transoral stone removal with gland preservation [6, 7]. Transoral approaches through open ductoplasty are also employed for larger stones [6, 7, 8]. These interventions have gained wide acceptance due to their assumed favorable postoperative profiles [9, 10].

The shift toward gland‐preserving techniques has generated controversy regarding the role of submandibular gland resection, which some surgeons continue to favor in cases where stone burden or ductal anatomy limits endoscopic access. A frequently cited concern following gland removal is exacerbation of xerostomia due to reduced salivary flow [11]. Additional reported complications include increased risk of infection, taste disturbance, altered sensation around the surgical scar, lingual nerve injury, and injury to the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve [11]. In contrast, gland‐preserving approaches are generally considered to carry a lower risk of postoperative complications [9, 10].

Although xerostomia following submandibular gland resection has been described in the context of head and neck malignancy, there is limited literature evaluating xerostomia outcomes after submandibular gland resection performed for sialolithiasis, particularly in studies directly comparing gland‐resecting and gland‐preserving approaches [12, 13, 14]. Given that xerostomia can significantly impair quality of life—affecting taste, speech, mastication, swallowing, and increasing dental caries risk—further investigation is warranted [11, 12].

This study aims to compare long‐term outcomes of patients who underwent submandibular gland‐preserving procedures versus those who received gland resection for the treatment of sialolithiasis. The primary outcome is the rate of patient‐reported xerostomia. Secondary outcomes include ultimate surgical success—defined as resolution of salivary symptoms without additional interventions during the 5‐to‐10‐year interval following the initial series of procedures—as well as stone recurrence and procedure‐related complications, including facial or lingual nerve changes and bothersome scarring. This study seeks to provide meaningful data to guide preoperative counseling as well as surgical decision‐making for patients with submandibular sialolithiasis.

Methods

Study Approval and Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB ID: 202403166). All patients provided written informed consent. All study procedures were conducted according to institutional, national, and international research standards, including the Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. This study used a retrospective cohort design with prospective patient‐reported outcome assessment: clinical and procedural data were collected through retrospective chart review, while xerostomia and long‐term symptom data were obtained prospectively via structured patient contact in 2024.

Patient Identification and Eligibility

Eligible research subjects were identified through review of a single surgeon's operative log from 2013 to 2018 at a single academic tertiary care center. Subjects were eligible for inclusion if they underwent surgical management of submandibular sialolithiasis via either a gland‐preserving or gland‐resection approach. The study was designed to include only patients with a minimum potential follow‐up of 5 years based on the date of surgery.

A total of 82 patients met inclusion criteria. The research team contacted all 82 patients via phone, mail, or e‐mail for enrollment in the study. Patients who could not be reached after 3 attempts were excluded from the cohort. Thirty‐six patients consented to participate in the study. Long‐term follow‐up in this cohort ranged from approximately 5–10 years after the initial procedure.

Clinical and Imaging Data Collection

Additional clinical data were collected through chart review and included demographics, current age, age at procedure, sex, history of autoimmune disease, prior radiotherapy or radioactive iodine therapy to the head and neck, alcohol use disorder, and HIV infection.

All patients had pre‐surgical evaluations that included either computed tomography (CT) imaging or ultrasound. These studies were re‐reviewed to characterize stone burden, including stone number, size, and location, in addition to underlying ductal anatomy. Sialography was performed in select cases and was limited to those whose CT imaging was indeterminate for sialolith or if there was high suspicion for underlying ductal anomaly.

Stone dimensions were assessed using CT imaging. The greatest vertical and horizontal lengths in the axial plane were recorded as stone length and width, and the greatest vertical length in the coronal plane was recorded as stone height. All measurements were reported in millimeters.

Patients who underwent staged procedures were categorized by their final surgical intervention to ensure clarity in outcome analysis. Outcome Measures

Long‐term outcomes were assessed with particular focus on xerostomia, which was measured with the Xerostomia Questionnaire (XQ) which was created and validated at the University of Michigan [15]. This questionnaire is publicly accessible and permissible for use with proper citation. Subjects rated each item on the questionnaire on a scale from 0 to 10; 0 being none and 10 being severe, for a maximum score of 80 (Figure 1). Additionally, operative reports and follow‐up documentation were reviewed for evidence of persistent or recurrent symptoms. Patient‐reported outcomes were collected via the survey in Figure 2. “Ultimate surgical success” was defined as resolution of salivary symptoms without additional interventions during the 5‐to‐10‐year interval following the initial series of procedures.

Statistical Analysis

Symptom resolution, presence of xerostomia, and complication rates were analyzed as dependent categorical variables in relation to procedure type (gland preservation vs. gland resection) using Chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests. Continuous outcomes, including XQ scores and follow‐up duration, were compared between procedure groups using Wilcoxon rank‐sum or Wilcoxon exact tests. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort Selection and Follow‐Up

A total of 82 patients met initial inclusion criteria, representing 89 procedures directed at the management of sialolithiasis. Of these, 36 patients (response rate = 43.9%) responded to follow‐up contact efforts and were included in the analysis. The median follow‐up duration was 97.4 months (range: 68.6–136.4 months). The mean age at follow‐up was 56.4 years, and the mean age at the time of the procedure was 48.2 years. The cohort consisted of 20 females (56%) and 16 males (44%). Four patients had a documented history of autoimmune disease. One patient with a diagnosis of multiple sclerosis underwent gland resection. The remaining 3 patients, each with rheumatoid arthritis, Graves' disease, and ulcerative colitis, respectively had a gland‐preserving procedure. Notably, none of these autoimmune conditions (rheumatoid arthritis, Graves' disease, ulcerative colitis, and multiple sclerosis) are classically associated with primary salivary gland dysfunction or xerostomia in the manner of Sjögren's syndrome, though secondary sicca symptoms may occur with some conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis). These patients were retained in the primary analysis; a sensitivity analysis excluding all patients with autoimmune conditions is reported below. Three patients had a documented history of any radiation therapy, none of which had external beam radiation to the neck or radioactive iodine treatment. One patient in the gland resection group had localized radiation therapy for treatment of prostate cancer. Two patients in the gland preservation group had previously undergone radiation therapy, 1 for prostate cancer and 1 for treatment of breast cancer and lymphoma.

Surgical Interventions

Gland resection was performed initially in 11 patients (30.6%) after an attempted endoscopic stone removal during the same anesthetic was unsuccessful. A gland‐preserving procedure was initially performed in 25 patients (69.4%). Among these, 1 patient (4%) required an additional gland‐preserving procedure 42 days later due to persistent pain and swelling, and 1 other (4%) underwent a repeat gland‐preserving procedure 450 days later, followed by eventual gland resection 13 days afterward due to inability to reach a deeply seated stone using sialendoscopy alone. Two additional patients (8%) required gland resection after their initial gland‐preserving procedure due to continued pain and swelling (57 and 118 days later). Overall, 3 of 25 patients (12%) who initially underwent gland‐preserving procedures ultimately required gland removal, while 22 of 25 patients (88%) retained their glands following sialendoscopy‐assisted transoral procedures. Symptom Resolution

Resolution of pain and swelling following the first surgical intervention was achieved in all 11 patients (100%) who underwent initial gland resection and in 18 of 25 patients (72%) who underwent primary sialendoscopy‐assisted gland‐preserving procedures (p > 0.9). Two patients in the endoscopic cohort continued to report postprandial submandibular pain, with one of these also experiencing gland swelling. Follow‐up durations were 122.2 months for the patient with pain only and 103.8 months for the patient with both pain and swelling. Overall, ultimate surgical success was achieved in 20 of 22 patients (90.9%) with gland preservation and in all 14 patients (100%) who underwent gland resection (p = 0.5).

Xerostomia Outcomes

Overall, patient‐reported xerostomia rates were similar between the gland preservation (27.3%) and gland resection (28.6%) groups (p > 0.9) (Figure 4).

Patient‐reported Xerostomia Questionnaire (XQ) scores 5–10 years after submandibular sialolithiasis procedure(s).

Among the 14 patients who underwent gland resection, 4 (28.6%) reported xerostomia, with a mean XQ score of 3.4. Of these 4 patients, 1 attributed their symptoms to a medication rather than the procedure itself (XQ score: 4). One patient in this group had autoimmune conditions (Hashimoto's thyroiditis and multiple sclerosis) but reported no xerostomia. Another patient had undergone radiation therapy for prostate cancer and also reported no symptoms of dry mouth.

In comparison, among the 22 patients who retained their glands, 6 (27.3%) reported xerostomia at long‐term follow‐up, with a mean XQ score of 8.0. The single patient who required a second gland‐preserving procedure reported xerostomia with an XQ score of 10.5. One patient had a history of chronic xerostomia prior to surgery, which worsened postoperatively, yielding an XQ score of 52. Three patients in the gland preservation group had autoimmune conditions—rheumatoid arthritis, Graves' disease, and ulcerative colitis—and all reported xerostomia at follow‐up, with XQ scores of 42, 19, and 32, respectively. Two patients had a history of radiation therapy (for Hodgkin lymphoma/breast cancer and prostate cancer); neither reported xerostomia at follow‐up. Two additional patients responded “no” to having dry mouth on the survey yet recorded XQ scores of 2 and 14; these were not included in the xerostomia group due to their self‐reported absence of symptoms.

In a sensitivity analysis excluding all four patients with autoimmune conditions (3 in the gland preservation group and 1 in the gland resection group), xerostomia rates remained statistically comparable between groups, consistent with the primary analysis: 3 of 19 patients (15.8%) in the preservation group versus 4 of 13 patients (30.8%) in the resection group (p = 0.40). Mean XQ scores were similarly close (4.4 vs. 3.7, p = 0.88). The preservation group showed a numerically lower xerostomia rate after exclusion of autoimmune patients, further supporting the conclusion that the surgical approach itself does not significantly affect long‐term xerostomia risk, and suggesting that autoimmune conditions—particularly those associated with secondary sicca symptoms—contributed to the higher mean XQ scores observed in the preservation group in the primary analysis.

Complications

No patients in either the gland resection or gland‐preservation groups reported changes in taste, tongue numbness, or tongue weakness. Facial numbness localized to the incision site was reported by two patients, both in the gland resection group, and 1 of these patients described the scar as bothersome. Among patients who underwent gland‐preserving procedures with no external incisions, one reported a noticeable change to their smile, possibly related to traction on the lower lip during the procedure. No patients in the gland resection group reported changes to their smile, and facial numbness in this group was limited to the incision site, with no findings suggestive of marginal mandibular nerve injury.

Discussion

This study provides long‐term outcome data comparing gland‐preserving and gland‐resecting surgical approaches for submandibular sialolithiasis, with a primary focus on xerostomia and secondarily that of salivary symptom resolution. Sialendoscopy‐assisted transoral procedures successfully allowed 61.1% of patients to retain their submandibular gland. The ultimate surgical success rate—defined as resolution of salivary symptoms without additional interventions during the 5‐to‐10‐year interval following the initial series of procedures—was slightly lower among the gland preservation group (90.9%) compared to gland resection (100%). Notably, the finding that 3 patients (12%) in the gland preservation group ultimately required gland excision underscores the value in preoperative counseling regarding the potential for incomplete resolution and ultimate gland removal.

Rates of xerostomia at long‐term follow‐up were similar between groups (27.3% in the gland preservation group vs. 28.6% in the gland resection group). However, symptom severity appeared greater in the gland preservation cohort, as reflected by higher mean XQ scores (8.0 vs. 3.4). This is unexpected, as the submandibular glands contribute approximately 60%–70% of unstimulated salivary flow, and removal therefore would be expected to cause more pronounced xerostomia following removal [16]. It is possible that preserved glands in the setting of chronic sialolithiasis may be functionally impaired due to longstanding inflammation, ductal obstruction, and acinar atrophy. Chronic sialadenitis has been shown to reduce salivary output even when gland architecture is maintained. In contrast, patients undergoing gland resection may have had non‐functioning glands at baseline and thus experienced minimal change in salivary function postoperatively. These findings suggest that anatomical preservation does not necessarily correspond to functional preservation.

The value in use of a gland‐preserving technique to diminish the potential for facial numbness and scarring should be balanced with the higher likelihood of requiring additional procedures when compared to gland resection. Capaccio et al. [17] reported that recurrent or persistent obstructive symptoms occur in up to 10%–15% of patients following sialendoscopy, often due to residual stones, ductal stenosis, or irreversible parenchymal damage. Similarly, Melo et al. [18] found that while most patients experienced improved quality of life, about one‐third continued to have salivary‐related symptoms, indicating incomplete functional recovery in a subset. It is important to note that both studies examined sialendoscopy for salivary gland disease more broadly, not exclusively submandibular sialolithiasis as in the present study. These findings demonstrate that anatomical preservation does not necessarily guarantee durable symptom resolution or gland function, and the need for repeat interventions can impose additional burden.

These results question the presumed superiority of gland‐preserving techniques. While long‐term outcomes such as xerostomia were comparable—and symptom resolution was more reliably achieved with resection—it may be reasonable to consider gland removal as a primary strategy for select patients, depending on individual clinical factors such as gland function or stone characteristics. These findings suggest that gland‐preserving approaches remain valuable, but they should not be assumed superior in all cases. Selection of the most effective intervention is informed by careful preoperative evaluation, including assessment of gland function, stone burden, and ductal anatomy. Incorporating patient priorities and quality‐of‐life considerations into surgical planning may further optimize outcomes and reduce the risk of repeat procedures.

Limitations of this study include its small sample size, incomplete long‐term follow‐up, and single‐surgeon setting, which may limit generalizability. Patient‐reported outcomes are subject to recall bias, and heterogeneity within gland‐preserving techniques may have influenced results. The study was also underpowered to assess the impact of comorbid conditions on xerostomia. Additionally, the absence of preoperative xerostomia assessments limits our ability to determine the degree to which postoperative dry mouth represents a change from baseline. Future prospective studies should incorporate validated baseline xerostomia measurements prior to surgical intervention to allow for more rigorous before‐and‐after comparisons. Several patients in the gland‐preservation group with autoimmune conditions reported xerostomia, suggesting that underlying disease and associated medication use may have contributed to symptoms independent of the surgical approach. A few patients had prior localized radiation therapy, but none received radiation to the head or neck, limiting its relevance as a potential confounder. Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable insight into long‐term functional and symptomatic outcomes following submandibular sialolithiasis treatment and may help guide shared decision‐making in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In this study, long‐term xerostomia outcomes were comparable between gland‐preserving and gland‐resecting techniques, and symptom resolution was more consistently achieved with gland removal. These findings indicate that anatomical preservation does not always translate into functional benefit and that gland resection may be an appropriate initial strategy in select patients. Individualized decision‐making that incorporates clinical factors, gland function, and patient preferences may help guide selection of the most appropriate intervention. Future research should prospectively evaluate functional outcomes and symptom resolution following gland‐preserving versus gland‐resecting procedures, incorporating both objective salivary flow measurements and validated patient‐reported outcomes in order to better identify which patients are most likely to benefit from conservative approaches.
