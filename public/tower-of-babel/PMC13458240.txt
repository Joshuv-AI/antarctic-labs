A prospective study of 12‐month survival of patients with cirrhosis admitted to intensive care
Authors: Minnie Au, Xian J Mah, Sern W Yeoh, Anh D Pham, Ashley Bloom, Nina Parthasarathy, Stephen D Bloom, Rohit Sawhney, Peter Oziemski, Amanda J Nicoll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe assessment on whether to escalate care in patients with cirrhosis to the intensive care unit (ICU) is complex, with little data on long‐term outcomes. The primary aim of this study was to prospectively examine the ability of liver‐specific and ICU prognostic scores to predict 12‐month survival among patients with cirrhosis in one non‐transplant health service. The secondary aim was to assess the prognostic scores in predicting shorter‐term survival and the correlation between other demographic and biochemical factors.MethodsAll patients with cirrhosis who had an unplanned admission to the ICU were included in the study prospectively. Patient demographics and biochemical markers were collected, liver‐specific and ICU prognostic scores, as well as the Chronic Liver Failure Consortium acute liver failure and acute decompensation scores were calculated. The pre‐acute deterioration Child Turcotte Pugh, Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) and MELD‐Na scores from the 1–6 months prior to the admission were also obtained.ResultsOne hundred patients were included over 33 months, with a median follow‐up of 25 months. Sixty percent of patients survived longer than 12 months. Sepsis was the most common cause of cacute on chronic liver failure; (ACLF), which was associated with a lower 30‐day survival. Patients with non‐variceal gastrointestinal bleeding were more likely to survive 12 months. In patients with ACLF (n = 57), the day 1 MELD score was the best at predicting long‐term survival, whereas in patients without ACLF, none of the scores performed particularly well, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve below 0.6.ConclusionPatients with cirrhosis may have excellent long‐term outcomes following ICU admissions. Distinguishing whether a patient is in ACLF or non‐ACLF may aid with determining the appropriate prognostic score to apply to aid decision making on limitations of patient care.

Introduction

The prevalence of liver cirrhosis is increasing worldwide, now representing the fifth leading cause of mortality in the United States and the eighth leading cause of medical socioeconomic costs. 1 Although the prognosis of patients with cirrhosis requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission was previously regarded as poor, more recent studies have shown better long‐term survival. 2 Previously reported 30‐day mortality rates were as high as 89% in patients with cirrhosis. However, in the past decade, the annual mortality rate for patients with cirrhosis admitted to ICUs in Australia and New Zealand is similar to that of patients without cirrhosis, at 29%. 2 This progress is likely due to improvements in patient management in ICU with severe sepsis, as well as advancements in the management of bleeding and complications of cirrhosis. 3 , 4 ICU resources are finite, and healthcare staff are faced with a challenging situation when assessing significantly comorbid patients, such as those with cirrhosis, for admission. 5 The decision should prioritise preserving resources for patients with a higher chance of long‐term survival.

Multiple prognostic scoring systems have been derived to aid the prediction of mortality for patients with cirrhosis. Liver‐specific scores focus on indicators of hepatic function and objective measures of acute decompensation, such as the Child Turcotte Pugh (CTP) score, 6 , 7 Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) 8 and MELD‐Na 9 scores. More generalised ICU scores include features of systemic organ dysfunction and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE), 10 Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) 11 and Simplified Acute Physiology II (SAPS II) scores. 12 The CLIF‐C‐ACLF score system developed by the CANONIC consortium combines liver‐specific measurements with indicators of organ failure. 13 The scoring system determines whether a patient has acute decompensation versus acute on chronic liver failure (ACLF) and then derives a prognostic CLIF‐AD (AD) or CLIF‐C‐ACLF (ACLF) score respectively. It was developed to identify patients with cirrhosis who would most benefit from aggressive ICU management and liver transplantation. 14 However, the current prognostic scores have only been validated to assess short‐term mortality, and no Australian data have been validated. To date, only one study in the United Kingdom has looked at long‐term prognosis. 15

At the time of acute deterioration, it is likely that the parameters used in the prognostic scoring systems are more deranged compared to baseline. If a decision on whether to escalate to intensive care is based on those scores, there is a risk that some patients may be denied the opportunity to receive potentially life‐saving treatment. Studies validating the scores are also mostly retrospective in nature, which comes with inherent biases. To our knowledge, there are no prospectively performed studies in Australia to date analysing the predictive ability of the pre‐acute deterioration score on the survival of patients with cirrhosis in the ICU. We hypothesised that hepatologists may be able to identify the best ICU candidates by looking at their pre‐admission baseline function during the 6 months prior to their presentation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the utility of patients’ pre‐acute deterioration (PAD) score versus admission‐based prognostic scores, to determine the prediction of 12‐month survival in patients with decompensated cirrhosis admitted to the ICU. In addition to this, the study assessed the ability of the prognostic scores to predict 1‐ and 3‐month survival. We also aimed to identify the reasons for cirrhotic decompensation and admission to ICU and to identify any predictive clinical prognostic markers.

Methods

This project was approved through the local Human Research Ethics Committee (Eastern Health, Monash University) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (QA 18‐2016). All patients with an underlying history of cirrhosis and unplanned admission to ICU were included in the study prospectively, on admission to ICU, at one tertiary health service in Melbourne, Australia from April 2016 to December 2019 (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram showing patient recruitment and survival.

Inclusion criteria included patients above 18 years of age with liver cirrhosis requiring ICU care. Cirrhosis of any aetiology was identified by radiological, transient elastography, histological or clinical findings consistent with the diagnosis of cirrhosis. 16 Baseline data regarding the patients’ demographics, aetiology of liver disease and precipitating factors for deterioration were recorded from the clinical file (Cerner PowerChart® and Epic® Systems Corporation) and patient medical records (CPF® InfoMedix Pty Ltd., Melbourne, Australia 2019).

The primary outcome measured was the predictive ability of prognostic scores to predict survival at 12 months after ICU admission. Secondary outcomes were survival rates at 1 and 3 months, as well as the correlations with demographic and biochemical markers. PAD prognostic scores, including CTP, MELD and MELD‐Na, were collected within the 6‐month period prior to admission. Patient data during the admission were collected prospectively. Prognostic scores for day 1 (D1) of the admission were calculated prospectively and derived from parameters taken from the time of arrival to the emergency department or time of requesting the ICU admission to the first 24 h of admission. The prognostic scores calculated include the CTP, MELD, MELD‐Na, CLIF‐C‐ACLF, AD, SOFA, APACHE and SAPS II scores. Patients were separated into ACLF and non‐ACLF groups based on the European Foundation for the Study of Chronic Liver Failure score calculators. 17 , 18 The CLIF‐organ failure (OF) score was used to diagnose ACLF, then CLIF‐C‐ACLF and AD scores respectively to determine the prognosis of patients with ACLF and acute decompensation.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 27.0.1 (IBM Corp). Comparisons between means of groups were made using multivariate analysis. For categorical data, the chi‐squared test was used, except where outcomes had low frequencies, where the Fisher's exact test was used. For continuous data, the t‐test and ANOVA were used. A P‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The ability of the scores to predict survival was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve analysis, and cumulative mortality rates were compared using Kaplan–Meier survival curve.

Results

A total of 100 patients were prospectively recruited over a 33‐month study period with a median follow‐up of 25 months (IQR 2–43 months). The demographics and clinical characteristics of the cohort on admission to ICU are shown in Table 1. Seventy per cent of patients were male, and the median age was 58 years (IQR 51–63 years). The most common aetiology of liver disease was alcohol‐related liver disease (77%) followed by cirrhosis secondary to metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (8.8%); three had known hepatocellular carcinoma. Most patients admitted to ICU were CTP A or B, prior to acute deterioration, with only nine patients who were CTP C. Non‐variceal gastrointestinal bleeding and severe sepsis were the most common reasons to require ICU admission.

ACLF

Fifty‐three patients fulfilled the definition of ACLF (Table S1). Patients with ACLF were less likely to survive during the inpatient admission than those with acute decompensation (non‐ACLF), with a survival rate of 68% compared to 89%, P = 0.01. Of the patients with ACLF, 25% (n = 13) were grade 1, 47% (n = 24) were grade 2, and 30% (n = 16) were grade 3; the 12‐month survival rates were 46%, 42% and 25% respectively. Patients with ACLF were more likely to have sepsis (OR 4.77, 95% CI 1.27–17.96).

Predictors of 12‐month survival

Survival at 12 months was seen in patients who were younger (mean age 55 years vs 60 years, P < 0.0001), had less jaundice (mean bilirubin 67 umol/L vs 150 umol/L, P = 0.0001), less coagulopathy (mean INR 1.7 vs 2.1, p < 0.0001) and with preserved renal function (mean creatinine 92 umol/L vs 146 umol/L, P = 0.006) at index presentation (Table 2).

Scoring systems

The prognostic scores were examined to look at the best predictor of 1‐, 3‐ and 12‐month survival. Eighty‐four patients had sufficient data available to calculate the PAD‐CTP scores. The PAD‐MELD‐NA and PAD‐MELD scores were able to be calculated for 80 patients. Sufficient data were available to calculate the D1 CTP score for 99 patients, the D1 MELD score for 97 patients, the DD1 MELD‐Na score for 97 patients, the day 1 CLIF‐C‐ACLF/AD score for 100 patients, the D1 SOFA score for 88 patients, the D1 SAPS II score for 84 patients and the D1 APACHE score for 89 patients.

Inpatient survival

Twenty‐two patients died as hospital inpatients, of which 20 (91%) were CTP C on D1. Inpatient survival was better predicted by most of D1 CTP scores, rather than the PAD scores (Figs. 2 and 3).

12‐month survival

The overall survival of patients with cirrhosis was 60% 12 months after an admission to ICU. The survival of patients with D1 CTP A was 100% at 12 months, 80% for patients with D1 CTP B and 42% for patients with D1 CTP C. D1 MELD (AUROC 0.872) and pre‐acute deterioration MELD‐Na (AUROC 0.8) are good predictors of survival for patients with ACLF. None of the scores strongly predict survival in patients with non‐ACLF with AUROC below 0.6 (Table 3).

Factors associated with 12‐month survival

Patients who were admitted to ICU with a non‐variceal upper GI bleed were more likely to survive 12 months compared to variceal bleeders (n = 21 vs 7, P = 0.05) (Table 2). Long‐term survivors had a higher average BMI of 35 kg/m2 compared to an average BMI of 25 kg/m2 in non‐survivors; however, this was not statistically significant. There was no difference in lactate levels between survivors and non‐survivors.

3‐month survival

The D1 MELD score was the best predictor of survival with an AUROC of 0.894 in patients with ACLF (Table S5). No score performed well as a predictor of survival in non‐ACLF patients (Table S5).

1‐month survival

The D1 MELD score Iine patients with ACLF was the best predictor of survival, with an AUROC of 0.875 (Table S4). In non‐ACLF patients, none of the scores performed particularly well, with the PAD‐CTP score having the highest AUROC of only 0.470 (Table S4).

Day 3 scores

Sufficient data were available to calculate the CLIF‐C‐ACLF, AD, CTP, MELD and MELD‐NA scores for 87 patients (Table S6). For the SOFA, APACHE and SAPS II scores, there were only sufficient data to calculate scores for 31, 35 and 34 patients respectively. This is due to the lack of data on urine output and pH as most patients had their care de‐escalated by day 3. Due to incomplete data for the ICU scores, they have not been included in this analysis. Based on these limited data, the CLIF‐C score and AD score had the highest AUROC for patients with ACLF (AUROC 0.75) and the non‐ACLF (AUROC 0.573) respectively.

Discussion

This is the first study, to our knowledge, to collect data prospectively on patients with cirrhosis admitted to an ICU in Australia that assesses long‐term survival. It shows that most patients (60%) with cirrhosis who require ICU support manage to survive for a further 12 months. Also, it is the first study to assess the validity of the pre‐deterioration status of patients against other ICU and liver disease prognostic scores. It shows that the D1 MELD score is the best predictor of long‐term survival for patients with ACLF after discharge from ICU. These results concur with a study by Cholangitas et al. 19 which showed that the MELD score had a good predictive ability with an AUROC of 0.81. Although it does not incorporate any substitute measure of portal hypertension, which has been shown by other studies to improve its discriminatory power, 18 , 19 this study suggests that it performs better than the MELD‐Na score. Admission to ICU for upper gastrointestinal (non‐variceal) bleeding has a good prognosis, in agreement with other internationally conducted studies. 20

The PAD scores did not perform as we hypothesised; the PAD CTP scores were inferior at predicting short‐term mortality compared to the D1 CTP scores. This may be due to having a lower number of patients with data available to calculate the PAD scores, which reduces the power of the study to detect a statistical difference in the performance of these scores. The PAD scores have merit as they provide an assessment of baseline liver reserve compared to scores at the time of ICU admission, which are usually based on acutely worsened parameters that are potentially reversible.

The CTP score has limitations as a marker of prognosis as it does not include any severity of other organ dysfunction. 20 , 21 Furthermore, it includes clinician subjective measures such as the presence and degree of abdominal ascites and hepatic encephalopathy, which are difficult to assess in patients who are ventilated. A limitation of this study is the low number of patients with CTP C cirrhosis included in the study. The CTP‐C patients who were not admitted to ICU are a heterogenous group, with some patients having advanced care directives from previous encounters based on their clinical prognosis and/or history of recurrent hospital admissions. Hence, patients with severe liver disease (CTP C) were already accepted to be poor ICU candidates based on previous literature and were not included in this study. This study focused predominantly on CTP A and CTP B patients, with the knowledge that this may limit the generalisability of these results; however, our findings are reflective of current clinical scenarios.

A notable finding in this study was that there was no significant difference in the 12‐month survival in patients with ACLF vs without ACLF (P = 0.299), although they had a significantly lower inpatient survival rate. This implies that the patients with ACLF who survive the patient admission may go on to have a relatively good long‐term survival. Thus, identifying patients who have ACLF allows clinicians to distinguish those who are most likely to deteriorate in the short term and should be treated aggressively. The non‐significance in 12‐month survival in the ACLF vs non‐ACLF groups may also be due to the small number of patients affecting the power of the study to detect a difference. Sepsis causing ACLF is a common indication for ICU admission, and rapid identification and aggressive therapy are valuable. 13 Infection in patients with cirrhosis is associated with a high risk of progressing to hepatic decompensation and septic shock leading to rapid deterioration. 22 , 23 This study showed that serum lactate was not a good discriminator at predicting survival at 12 months. This is in contrast to previous studies showing lactate is a good indicator of prognosis as it is a marker of hypoperfusion and cellular dysfunction in sepsis. 24 This may reflect that only a quarter of our cohort had sepsis as a cause of acute deterioration.

Age was a predictor of long‐term survival in this study (P < 0.0001). Younger patients who are in multiorgan failure tend to be less comorbid and have a higher cardiorespiratory reserve. Clinicians are therefore more likely to support ICU admission in these patients. This could also suggest that there are other factors involved in long‐term survival after discharge from ICU that were not studied, such as relapse of disease, development of malignancy, cardiovascular disease and complications in the older MASLD cirrhotic patients. Additionally, BMI demonstrated a trend towards improved survival, which might also indicate that sarcopenia is negatively correlated to survival. While not directly measured in this study, prior literature would support this notion. 25 Thus, there may be other patient factors that are predictors of long‐term survival that could be studied in the future, such as age and significant comorbidities. 26 Incorporating the Charlson Comorbidity Index 27 and markers of sarcopenia into current prognostic scores may be more relevant given the ageing population globally. This is supported by our data showing that patients with MASLD cirrhosis were less likely to survive at 12 months after ICU admission.

Prognostic scoring systems have limitations; however, the results of this study may be of use to intensivists and hepatologists weighing up the futility of ICU admission in borderline cases. This study was prospectively performed, reducing the bias that is present in retrospectively performed studies.

Conclusion

Selecting patients with cirrhosis who are most likely to have a long‐term benefit from ICU therapy remains difficult but does benefit from the assessment of liver function, the patient's comorbidities and age in addition to ICU‐specific measures. Determining whether patients have ACLF and the indication for ICU admission is useful in establishing their prognosis and guiding the prognostic scoring system to use; however, in patients without ACLF, none of the scoring systems were particularly helpful. Improvements in ICU support for patients with cirrhosis have translated into very good long‐term outcomes in patients with reasonable baseline liver function, and patients should not be denied intensive care on the basis of a cirrhosis diagnosis.
