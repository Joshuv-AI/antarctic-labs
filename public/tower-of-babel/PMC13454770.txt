Four Key Challenges After a Decade of Consecutive Paradigm Shifts in Wild-Type Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Toru Kubota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wild-type transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy has undergone profound transformation over the past decade, evolving from a rarely recognized condition to a disease that should not be missed. This shift has been driven by the use of bone-avid tracer scintigraphy enabling non-biopsy diagnosis, as well as the advent and expansion of disease-modifying therapies. As a result, the number of diagnosed and treated patients has increased substantially. However, important gaps remain in clinical practice, particularly in detection, diagnostic accuracy, and therapeutic decision-making. This review highlights 4 key challenges that will shape the next decade. First, marked geographic disparities in detection suggest underdiagnosis and inequitable access to care. Second, variability in scintigraphic interpretation, particularly due to reliance on planar imaging and modified grading systems, may compromise diagnostic accuracy. Third, the expanding range of therapeutic options, including stabilizers and gene silencers, has increased the complexity of treatment selection and underscores the need for biologically guided strategies. Fourth, patient selection at the extremes of disease severity raises unresolved clinical, ethical, and economic questions regarding when to initiate or withhold therapy. Looking ahead, continued therapeutic innovation and a deeper understanding of disease pathogenesis will be essential. Importantly, characterization of the underlying disease in each patient remains central to advancing clinical care and therapeutic development. If these challenges are addressed, the decade ahead will be even more fruitful.

The Decade That Redefined a Rare Disease

In 2015, wild-type transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR) in Japan was estimated to affect approximately 320 individuals, whereas variant ATTR was estimated at approximately 840 cases.1 Accordingly, wild-type ATTR was widely regarded as an exceptionally rare disorder at that time. However, the clinical landscape changed dramatically over the subsequent decade,2 driven by the introduction of bone-avid tracer scintigraphy enabling noninvasive diagnosis of ATTR cardiomyopathy,3–6 and the expansion of tafamidis indications to this condition in 2019, irrespective of transthyretin mutation status.7,8

Nationwide registry data demonstrate a 3.5-fold increase in registered systemic amyloidosis cases, including wild-type and variant ATTR, as well as immunoglobulin light-chain amyloidosis (AL), from 2,304 in 2015 to 8,168 in 2024, with a particularly steep rise after 2019, coinciding with the approval of tafamidis for ATTR cardiomyopathy.9 By 2025, more than 4,000 patients in Japan are estimated to be receiving tafamidis, and epidemiological analyses suggest that the number of individuals with ATTR cardiomyopathy may exceed 40,000. Thus, within a single decade, the number of treated patients has increased more than 10-fold, whereas the estimated disease burden has expanded by nearly 2 orders of magnitude.

Furthermore, 2025 represents a pivotal turning point in the treatment of ATTR cardiomyopathy, marked by the approval of acoramidis, another transthyretin stabilizer,10 the extension of vutrisiran from variant ATTR polyneuropathy to ATTR cardiomyopathy irrespective of transthyretin mutation status,11 and broader reimbursement for disease-modifying therapies based on scintigraphy-defined probable diagnosis alone.12 After a decade of consecutive paradigm shifts, the focus has moved from whether wild-type ATTR is present to how it can be systematically identified, reflecting a transition from rarely found to never missed. In this context, this review highlights the key challenges currently faced that will shape the decade ahead.

Key Challenges

Challenge 1: Geographical Disparities in Detection of ATTR Cardiomyopathy

One of the most striking observations is the marked regional heterogeneity in the detection and certification of systemic amyloidosis.9 Nationwide registry data show that the population-adjusted rate of systemic amyloidosis increased from 0.18 in 2015 to 0.65 per 10,000 population in 2024. There are marked geographic disparities, with a pronounced west–east gradient and consistently higher rates in western Japan. In 2024, Oita Prefecture had the highest rate (2.2 per 10,000 population), followed by Kumamoto, Kochi, Nagano, Ehime, and Fukuoka. Although the registry does not distinguish amyloidosis subtypes, the sharp increase observed after 2019 most plausibly reflects enhanced detection of wild-type ATTR cardiomyopathy.

Because wild-type ATTR is not associated with inherited pathogenic mutations, regional genetic clustering is unlikely to explain this gradient. In light of the nationwide increase observed after 2019, albeit to varying degrees across regions, the gradient likely reflects differences in disease awareness, diagnostic practices, and access to specialized care rather than true regional differences in disease prevalence.13,14 Although detection has increased nationwide, comparison with high-detection regions suggests that case identification may still be substantially incomplete in many areas.

Extrapolation from high-detection prefectures provides a conceptual estimate of potential under-recognition. If nationwide diagnostic practice approximated that observed in Oita, the prefecture with the highest population-adjusted rate in 2024, the number of identified cases could be approximately 3-fold higher than currently registered. Even alignment with Fukuoka would be expected to roughly double current numbers. The continued upward trajectory in Oita, without clear evidence of a plateau, further suggests that case identification remains incomplete even in high-detection regions.

Reducing these disparities requires a shift from wild-type ATTR being rarely recognized to it being systematically not missed. This shift redefines the role of bone scintigraphy from a noninvasive alternative to endomyocardial biopsy to a screening tool incorporated into routine heart failure (HF) care.15,16 Identifying wild-type ATTR within the population of those with HF with preserved ejection fraction in routine clinical practice may therefore be more effective than reserving scintigraphy for diagnostic confirmation in selected patients.

Challenge 2: Diagnostic Accuracy of Bone Scintigraphy Beyond Planar Imaging

Bone-avid tracer scintigraphy has revolutionized the diagnosis of ATTR cardiomyopathy by enabling reliable non-biopsy identification.5 Real-world experience has demonstrated that 18% of HF patients undergoing scintigraphy are positive, underscoring the substantial hidden prevalence of ATTR cardiomyopathy.6

Strategies to refine patient selection for scintigraphy, such as the Kumamoto criteria, improve pretest probability;17 however, even low-score patients may test positive. Because low-score patients greatly outnumber high-score patients, the absolute number of positive cases may be substantial even in this group, indicating that strict clinical preselection risks underdiagnosis.18 Red flag-based approaches have been advocated,19 but in practice scintigraphy is often deferred until multiple red flags are present, potentially delaying diagnosis. Because early ATTR cardiomyopathy is difficult to identify based on clinical features alone,6 a lower threshold for imaging is warranted. Bone scintigraphy should be considered in patients aged >60 years with unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy. Importantly, red flags should be regarded not as tools to identify ATTR cardiomyopathy, but as safeguards to avoid missing it.

With disease-modifying therapy increasingly initiated based on non-biopsy diagnosis, the diagnostic accuracy of scintigraphy becomes critical, as both sensitivity and specificity directly influence clinical decision-making. In contrast, the definition adopted in the Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) guideline relies solely on comparison with rib uptake and does not require the reduction in bone uptake that is central to the original Perugini definition of Grade 3.21 Although this simplification may have been introduced by experts familiar with the original concept, it can be misinterpreted by clinicians less familiar with the Perugini classification, leading to overestimation of the grade.

For example, cardiac uptake that exceeds rib uptake but remains lower than uptake in the sternum or spine may be classified as Grade 3 in the JCS grading system, although it corresponds to Grade 2 under the original Perugini classification. This discrepancy arises because replacing bone with rib as the reference simplifies the assessment to a relative comparison, thereby obscuring the concept of bone signal suppression. Using bone as the reference requires cardiac uptake to exceed uptake in the ribs, sternum, and spine, resulting in mild or absent bone uptake. Similarly, mild cardiac uptake corresponding to Grade 1 in the original Perugini classification may be classified as Grade 2 in the JCS grading system when it is equal to rib uptake but remains lower than uptake in the sternum or spine.

Therefore, when initiating disease-modifying therapy, strict adherence to the original Perugini concept is essential to avoid overdiagnosis. However, this does not imply that planar imaging should remain the basis for interpretation, as blood pool activity and overlapping structures often limit reliable identification of myocardial tracer uptake.22 In contemporary practice, assessment by SPECT is generally preferred to confirm true myocardial tracer localization, and several statements recommend SPECT-based evaluation rather than planar-only grading.23–25 Accordingly, the conceptual framework of the original Perugini classification should be preserved, whereas diagnostic interpretation should be based on SPECT in the modern era.

Khor et al.23 advocate a 6-step approach: (1) perform quality control; (2) confirm the presence of rib uptake as a check for appropriate scan timing; (3) distinguish myocardial 99mTc-PYP uptake from blood pool activity; (4) interpret myocardial uptake through visual assessment and grading; (5) exclude a monoclonal process using serum and urine immunofixation electrophoresis and a serum free light-chain assay in all patients with suspected cardiac amyloidosis; and (6) review physiologic tracer distribution and extracardiac tracer activity. They omit the planar H/CL ratio and rely solely on visual assessment based on SPECT/CT images acquired 2–3 h after tracer injection.

When bone scintigraphy is appropriately performed and interpreted, endomyocardial biopsy is generally unnecessary.5 However, even under such conditions, it remains necessary in some clinical situations. A review by Gonzalez-Lopez et al.26 proposed 4 such scenarios: (1) suspected AL amyloidosis, (2) Grade 1 uptake, (3) Grade 2 or 3 uptake with monoclonal or free light-chain abnormalities, and (4) suspected infrequent types of amyloidosis. It should be noted that certain variants of ATTR, such as Val30Met with type B fibrils, may yield false-negative scintigraphy and require biopsy for definitive diagnosis.23,27

Challenge 3: Transthyretin-Guided Therapy Amid Expanding Treatment Options

The approval of tafamidis in 2019 marked the first disease-modifying breakthrough for wild-type ATTR.8 In the author’s decade-long single-center experience, tafamidis was associated with improved survival, particularly in patients who were younger, had less advanced NYHA functional class, and lower levels of NT-proB-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and troponin T. In contrast, the survival benefit appeared limited in older patients and in those with more advanced disease and higher biomarker levels.2

In 2025, the introduction of acoramidis, another transthyretin stabilizer,10 and vutrisiran, a transthyretin gene silencer,11 for ATTR cardiomyopathy further expanded the treatment landscape. As a result, clinicians now face increasing complexity in treatment decision-making. However, there is currently no clear evidence demonstrating the superiority of 1 stabilizer over another or over gene silencers.25,28,29

In variant ATTR polyneuropathy, gene silencers are often preferred over stabilizers.30 One reason is that the approved dose of tafamidis for polyneuropathy (20 mg) is exactly one-quarter of that used for cardiomyopathy (80 mg). In addition, from a mechanistic perspective, suppressing the production of unstable mutant transthyretin may be more rational than attempting to stabilize an inherently unstable protein. In contrast, in wild-type ATTR, transthyretin is structurally normal. Although reducing the circulating transthyretin levels has been shown to be effective even in this setting,11 it remains unclear whether removal of normal transthyretin confers greater benefit than its stabilization.

Emerging evidence suggests that baseline transthyretin levels and the response to disease-modifying therapy may carry important prognostic information.31–34 Patients with lower baseline transthyretin levels appear to have worse outcomes, whereas those with a greater increase after treatment initiation tend to have better outcomes. These observations raise the possibility that circulating transthyretin may serve not only as a pharmacodynamic marker but also as a guide for therapeutic decision-making.35 When selecting between stabilizers, choosing the agent that achieves a greater increase in transthyretin levels may therefore represent a rational approach to individualized treatment and maximized therapeutic benefit.

In contrast, gene silencers reduce circulating transthyretin levels, typically by 80–90%.11 However, the magnitude of reduction varies substantially among individuals, and in certain patients, levels may decrease by only about 50–70%. Although greater suppression is generally assumed to confer stronger inhibition of amyloid deposition, the degree of suppression required for meaningful clinical benefit remains unclear. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether absolute levels or the relative degree of reduction is more clinically relevant. If suppression of transthyretin is insufficient with a gene silencer, switching to a stabilizer that achieves a marked increase in circulating transthyretin may represent a reasonable therapeutic strategy.

These observations suggest that interindividual variability in pharmacodynamic response is a common feature across both therapeutic classes, and that the optimal treatment strategy may differ among patients. Circulating transthyretin levels may therefore serve as a practical biomarker to monitor response and guide therapy. The next paradigm shift in ATTR cardiomyopathy management may lie not in the introduction of new agents, but in the development of a strategy that integrates transthyretin response into clinical decision-making, analogous to targeting blood pressure or low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol in hypertension and hypercholesterolemia, making a shift from a “fire-and-forget” approach to transthyretin-guided therapy.

Challenge 4: The Ethical Frontier of Treating the Very Old and the Very Mild

The final and perhaps most difficult challenge concerns patient selection at the extremes of the disease spectrum. In daily practice, clinicians are increasingly faced with 2 contrasting groups: very elderly patients with advanced frailty and those with very early or even asymptomatic disease identified through screening.

Although early detection of ATTR cardiomyopathy is undoubtedly important, it raises a fundamental question as to whether treatment should be initiated before the onset of HF. ATTR-related manifestations such as carpal tunnel syndrome often precede overt cardiomyopathy by 5–10 years, creating a prolonged preclinical phase.36–38 During longitudinal follow-up, some patients with ATTR-positive carpal tunnel syndrome are found to have positive bone scintigraphy despite the absence of left ventricular hypertrophy. In patients with normal NT-proBNP and troponin T levels, deferral of disease-modifying therapy may be reasonable. However, the optimal management of patients with elevated cardiac biomarkers in the absence of overt HF or left ventricular hypertrophy remains uncertain.

In current clinical practice, treatment is largely determined by reimbursement criteria, which require both medical history of hospitalization for HF or a HF condition requiring treatment with a diuretic for improvement, and evidence of cardiac involvement on echocardiography with an end-diastolic interventricular septal wall thickness >12 mm.8 This relatively stringent criterion may be supported by observations that patients with National Amyloidosis Centre Stage 1a ATTR cardiomyopathy, defined by a furosemide-equivalent diuretic requirement of <0.75 mg/kg and an NT-proBNP ≤500 ng/L or ≤1,000 ng/L in the presence of atrial fibrillation, have an expected survival comparable to that of the matched general population in the UK.39 Therefore, the benefit of therapeutic intervention at this early stage remains to be determined. Given the high cost of disease-modifying therapies, deferring treatment until symptom progression or the development of significant hypertrophy may be economically justifiable, even if not clinically optimal.

Wild-type ATTR predominantly affects older individuals, and patients are typically diagnosed at an advanced age. In my group’s cohort, the mean age at diagnosis was 80 years.2 Nearly 7 years have passed since the first patient was treated with tafamidis, and several patients receiving disease-modifying therapy have now reached their 90s. Although tafamidis has been shown to be effective even in octogenarians,40 its effectiveness in nonagenarians remains uncertain.

As with treatment initiation, the question of when to discontinue therapy is equally challenging. The optimal timing and criteria for treatment discontinuation remain unclear, not only from a clinical perspective but also from ethical and societal standpoints. In our practice, preserved functional status, such as the ability to attend outpatient visits independently, is used as a pragmatic indicator; however, the validity of this approach remains uncertain. Ultimately, decisions regarding treatment continuation or discontinuation cannot be determined by clinical criteria alone and must be addressed within a broader societal framework.

Thus, both ends of the disease spectrum present distinct but equally challenging dilemmas: potential overtreatment in the very mild and limited efficacy in the very advanced. The key issue is no longer whether ATTR cardiomyopathy can be treated, but rather when treatment should be initiated and when it should be withheld to achieve clinically meaningful benefit. Defining this threshold is critical, yet current evidence remains insufficient to guide such decisions. This challenge extends beyond clinical cardiology into health economics and medical ethics, where considerations of cost-effectiveness, resource allocation, and patient-centered outcomes must be integrated into therapeutic decision-making.

Conclusion: More to Come in the Next Decade

Further therapeutic advances are anticipated, including additional gene silencers,41 ongoing development of antibody-based therapies targeting amyloid deposition,42,43 and the potential emergence of gene-editing approaches.44,45 Beyond therapeutic innovation, fundamental questions remain regarding disease pathogenesis, including why wild-type ATTR develops in only a subset of older individuals, suggesting potential roles of environmental and lifestyle factors, as well as genetic modifiers that may influence amyloid production or clearance.

Over the past decade, it has become clear that HF in older adults should not be attributed to aging alone. Even in the absence of established therapies, precise characterization of the underlying disease and identification of the cause of HF in each patient are essential, as they ultimately lead to the development of new treatments. The role of physicians is not merely to follow guidelines, but to recognize and address the questions and challenges that arise in daily clinical practice. The past decade has demonstrated the value of this collective effort. Building on this foundation, we believe that the next decade will be even more fruitful.
