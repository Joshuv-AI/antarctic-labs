School and family based myopia education associations with myopia prevalence in Hefei high school students in the post-COVID-19 period
Authors: Shuai Liu, Chen Shao, Jingwen Yu, Xuyang Cheng, Lin Ye, Wen Wen, Yinglong Li, Zhengxuan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate associations between multiple factors—including gender, parental myopia, outdoor activities, near work, and school/family-based myopia education—and myopia prevalence among high school students in Hefei in the post-COVID-19 period. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 6475 senior high school students (aged 17–19 years) from 15 schools in Hefei, China, between March and May 2023. Visual acuity was measured using a standard logarithmic eye chart, and refraction was assessed via non-cycloplegic autorefraction with tropicamide-induced mydriasis. Myopia was defined as spherical equivalent (SE) < − 0.5 D. Self-reported questionnaires captured data on parental myopia, outdoor time, near work, sleep, and myopia prevention strategies. Data were analyzed using chi-square tests, tQUER-tests, and multivariate logistic regression, with sensitivity analyses accounting for potential overestimation due to non-cycloplegic refraction. Of 6414 participants (after exclusions), myopia prevalence was 85.3% (adjusted to approximately 82.5% in sensitivity analysis assuming 0.5 D overestimation). Females showed higher prevalence than males (88.9% vs. 82.9%; χ2 = 46.434, p < 0.01). Parental myopia was associated with higher risk (χ2 = 84.69, p < 0.01; adjusted OR = 2.20, 95% CI 1.75–2.85). Outdoor activities > 1 h/day were associated with lower prevalence (χ2 = 110.5, p < 0.01; adjusted OR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.28–0.58), while prolonged near work (> 6 h/day in 55% of students) correlated with higher rates. Parental and school education on myopia prevention (e.g., promoting outdoor time, regulating screen exposure) was linked to lower severity, though adoption was low (17.2% parental, 36.5% school). High myopia prevalence was observed, associated with female gender, parental myopia, reduced outdoor time, and increased near work—factors that were commonly observed in this post-pandemic cohort (with behaviors consistent with patterns reported during the pandemic period). School- and family-based education were associated with myopia severity, but the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference (reverse causality is possible). Non-cycloplegic measurements may inflate estimates. Introduction

In 2015, scholars such as Dolgin predicted the myopia boom globally, and now the escalating rise in myopia has become a public health crisis globally. Two or three generations ago, myopia prevalence among students in East and Southeast Asia who completed 12 to 13 years of schooling was 20 to 30%, but it has now risen to 70 to 90%1–3. Holden et al. suggest that by 2050, nearly 50% of the world’s population could be myopic, with around 10% highly myopic2. High myopia increases the risk of retinal breaks and detachment5, and is associated with complications such as glaucoma, cataract, and myopic macular degeneration3. Myopic macular degeneration is one of the leading causes of low vision and blindness among working adults in China and Southeast Asia4. As mentioned above, individuals of Asian descent are at higher risk for myopia9, and in Chinese society, middle and high school students face significant academic pressure and prolonged near work (e.g., reading, screen use), warranting greater attention to the refractive status of children and adolescents.

Recent theoretical models emphasize that myopia development and progression stem from a feedback process where near work acts as a primary environmental driver, quantified as near demand (positive lens equivalent), which interferes with emmetropization5. This aligns with evidence that corrective lenses and accommodation further exacerbate axial elongation6,11. During the COVID-19 pandemic, starting in January 2020, the spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 led to nationwide school closures in China, confining an estimated 200 million school-age children and adolescents to their homes for online learning until restrictions were fully lifted in December 2022. This shift increased near work through prolonged screen exposure and reduced outdoor time, factors associated with accelerated myopia progression6,11,13. Studies have documented a myopic shift of approximately − 0.30 D during lockdowns13, with elevated prevalence persisting post-restrictions7. However, myopia etiology is rooted in this feedback process, unifying environmental factors, and large-scale studies in age-matched cohorts can minimize confounding, providing accurate insights into at-risk groups during lockdowns.

This study aimed to investigate associations between multiple factors—including gender, parental myopia, outdoor activities, near work, and school/family-based education—and myopia prevalence among high school students in Hefei, China, exposed to intensive study schedules in the post-COVID-19 period. By focusing on education interventions in this context, our work addresses a gap in understanding modifiable factors in a population whose lifestyle was affected by the pandemic. Unlike previous studies that primarily documented COVID-19’s impact on myopia prevalence, this study specifically examines the implementation patterns and associated factors.

Materials and methods

Hefei city (117°17′–118°38′ east longitude, 30°10′–32°58′ north latitude), located in central China, is the capital of Anhui Province. By 2022, the total area of the city was 11,445 square kilometers, with a permanent population of 9.634 million and an urban population of 8.154 million, including about 98,000 senior high school students. Since January 2020, Hefei implemented lockdown measures in accordance with China’s COVID-19 prevention requirements, with primary and secondary school students experiencing reduced in-person classes, increased online learning, and gradual resumption of on-campus activities after January 2023.

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies.All questionnaire items referred to the 12-month period preceding the survey (i.e., March 2022 to March 2023), unless otherwise specified.

This investigation was designed as a cross-sectional prevalence and implementation study conducted between March and May 2023. The primary aim was to estimate population prevalence of myopia and to examine cross-sectional associations between refractive status and behavioural/education variables among senior high school students.

Sample size calculation

To ensure the statistical precision and validity of our prevalence estimates and multivariate analyses, a formal sample size calculation was performed prior to participant recruitment.

For estimating myopia prevalence: based on prior large-scale studies in China reporting myopia prevalence rates of 70–90% among high school students1,3,4, we assumed an expected prevalence (P) of 80%. Using the formula for cross-sectional studies, n = Z2 × P(1 − P)/d2,where Z is the Z-statistic for a 95% confidence level (1.96), and d is the margin of error (set at 0.01 for high precision), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 6147 participants.

For multivariate logistic regression: Adhering to the widely accepted rule of thumb of a minimum of 10 events per predictor variable (EPV) [Peduzzi et al., 1996], and anticipating 3–5 primary predictor variables in our final model (e.g., gender, parental myopia, outdoor time), a sample providing well over 5000 myopic cases (the “event” in this context) would yield an EPV far exceeding 1000. This ensures robust power for detecting associations, even for factors with modest effect sizes.

Accounting for an anticipated non-response and data exclusion rate of up to 5%, the target sample size was set at 6500. Our final analyzed cohort of 6414 participants meets and exceeds these calculated requirements, justifying the sample size for both precise prevalence estimation and powerful multivariate analysis.

Respondents

Hefei First People’s Hospital, as the government-designated Hefei Youth Myopia Prevention and Treatment Center and Hefei Ophthalmology Clinical Research Center, organized a cross-sectional study of myopia among 6475 senior high school students (aged 17–19 years) undergoing pre-college entrance examination eye health surveys. The research was conducted from March to May 2023, covering 15 high schools across all jurisdictions in Hefei. A total of 7500 students were initially invited; 1025 declined participation (primarily due to scheduling conflicts or lack of interest), resulting in 6475 enrollees. Non-responders did not differ significantly from participants in terms of school location or grade level (based on school records). After exclusions (e.g., 61 unable to participate due to medical reasons), 6414 students were analyzed.

Survey method

A multistage stratified randomization protocol was implemented. Schools were stratified by urban/suburban location, followed by random selection of schools and proportional allocation of classes. All students within selected classes were enrolled unless excluded for medical reasons, ensuring minimal selection bias and population representativeness. The overall sampling method selected 130 classes proportionally based on the number of high school students.

To ensure representativeness, participants were drawn from a mix of urban and suburban high schools, encompassing diverse demographics. Random selection within classes provided equal inclusion probability.

Researchers informed schools of screening dates at least one week in advance. Students wearing contact lenses were instructed not to wear them on examination day, and those using orthokeratology (ortho-K) lenses discontinued use for three days prior, switching to frame glasses. Given the low prevalence of ortho-K (2.7% among myopic students), their inclusion was retained, but sensitivity analyses excluded them to assess potential bias from residual corneal remodeling15.

Refraction was measured using a TONOREF III autorefractor (NIDEK Co., Ltd., Japan) after mydriasis with tropicamide eye drops (one drop per eye every 10 min, four times), under non-cycloplegic conditions. This provided measurements of spherical power, cylindrical power, and axis. While cycloplegic refraction is the gold standard to eliminate accommodative artifacts, particularly in adolescents16, non-cycloplegic autorefraction was chosen for logistical feasibility in this large-scale school-based study conducted shortly after the pandemic restrictions. Non- cycloplegic measurements may overestimate myopia by approximately 0.50 D due to residual accommodation17. Myopia was defined as spherical equivalent (SE) < − 0.50 D in accordance with International Myopia Institute (IMI) standards18, calculated as sphere + 0.5 × cylinder (average of three measurements). This − 0.50 D threshold is the standard cutoff for clinically significant myopia in epidemiological research18,19. Furthermore, for the purpose of severity analysis, myopic participants were sub-classified as follows: low myopia (SE ≥ − 3.00 D), moderate myopia (SE < − 3.00 D to > − 6.00 D), and high myopia (SE ≤ − 6.00 D) to align with common clinical reporting practices18.To ensure a conservative and clinically relevant estimate of myopia prevalence in this population, participants were classified based on the eye with the more negative (worse) SE in borderline cases (e.g., anisometropia with one eye myopic or plano sphere with − 2 D cylinder). However, for all subsequent statistical analyses—including the calculation of mean SE, distribution of myopia severity, and regression models—the eye-mean spherical equivalent was used. This approach accounts for the high inter-eye correlation (Pearson r = 0.85, p < 0.001) and minimizes selection bias by incorporating data from both eyes, as recommended by statistical guidelines for ophthalmic data20. Uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) was tested using a standard logarithmic eye chart at 5 m, with results recorded to one decimal place. Additional instruments included an ARK-8500 phoropter (Shanghai Linco Precision Instrument Co., China) for subjective refraction and a Haag-Streit BQ 900 slit lamp (Switzerland) for ocular health screening. All instruments were calibrated daily per manufacturer protocols.

Participants completed self-administered questionnaires on basic demographics, parental myopia status, average daily outdoor time during the past 12 months (including physical activities, walking, etc.), average daily near work time during the past 12 months (including reading, homework, electronic device use), and myopia prevention methods with reference to the past 12 months. Regarding school- and family-based myopia education, the following binary (yes/no) questions were used, referring to the 12-month period preceding the survey: Parental education/guidance: “Do your parents provide guidance on myopia prevention (e.g., reminding you to go outdoors, limiting screen time, or ensuring proper lighting)?”School-based education: “Does your school have structured myopia prevention education programs (e.g., regular eye health lectures, outdoor activity promotion, or screen time regulations)?”The overall questionnaire was validated in a pilot study (n = 100), showing internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.7), test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.8), and construct validity via factor analysis. However, the specific education items were not validated as standalone scales, which is a limitation. Myopia-control interventions were defined as structured strategies such as enforced outdoor time (> 1 h/day), screen time limits (< 2 h/day non-educational), or use of products like low-dose atropine or defocus lenses. Adherence was self-reported, with 17.2% receiving parental guidance and 36.5% school-based education. Cut-points for outdoor time (< 0.5 h, 0.5–1 h, 1–2 h, > 2 h/day) and near work were based on IMI recommendations (≥ 2 h/day outdoors) and prior studies19, though subject to recall bias. Participants were asked to report their typical daily behaviors averaged over the 12 months preceding the survey (March–May 2023).Compliance was quantified as self-reported binary adherence (yes/no) to each component, with dose measured as average daily hours (e.g., > 1 h outdoor, < 2 h screen non-educational).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hefei First People’s Hospital (approval number: 2023-056-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians (consent rate: 98.6% among guardians contacted), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean (standard deviation). SE differences were compared using t-tests or analysis of variance. Chi-square tests evaluated factors associated with myopia. Variables with significant univariate differences underwent multivariate logistic regression.

To address inter-eye dependence, Pearson correlation between right and left eye SE was calculated (r = 0.85, indicating high correlation); thus, analyses used eye-mean SE or generalized estimating equations (GEE) with random intercepts for participants and classes20. Borderline anisometropic cases were handled by classifying based on mean SE.

Sensitivity analyses included: (1) adjusting SE by + 0.50 D to account for non-cycloplegic overestimation due to residual accommodation (formula: Adjusted SE = original SE + 0.50 D), re-calculating prevalence and odds ratios16,18; (2) excluding ortho-K users (n = 151) to assess potential bias from residual corneal remodeling15; (3) stratifying by intervention adherence. p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.(4)Missing data handling: Missing values were present for some variables, with the highest proportion for self-reported one-year myopic change (14.5%). For all primary analyses, we used complete-case analysis, as the proportion of missing data was < 15% for each variable and no systematic pattern of missingness was observed.

Results

According to the principle of random sampling, the team completed a sample survey of 6414 students (after exclusions) under voluntary conditions from schools and students, with missing data ratios < 30% across all items (highest: 14.5%).

Characteristics of myopia among students

In sensitivity analyses adjusting for potential 0.50 D overestimation from non-cycloplegic refraction, prevalence was approximately 82.5%. The mean eye-mean SE was − 3.21 ± 2.19 D (Fig. 1, showing student-level distribution). High myopia (eye-mean SE ≤ − 6.0 D) affected 10.5% of students, while mild to moderate myopia (> − 6.0 D to < 0 D) was present in 74.8% (Table 2). Astigmatism > 2.0 D was observed in 8.4% of students (eye-mean; Table 3). Approximately 80% of myopes developed myopia during middle or high school, with 49% onset in middle school (Fig. 2).

To further address potential concerns regarding the short observation period and to provide additional context on recent refractive changes, we performed a subgroup analysis based on self-reported one-year myopic change categories (no change, < 0.50 D, 0.50–1.00 D, and > 1.00 D). Results showed that 11.2% of students reported > 1.00 D progression, who also exhibited significantly higher myopia (mean SE = − 4.23 ± 1.55 D) and higher high-myopia prevalence (19.4%) than stable participants (7.1%, p < 0.001). Behavioral protective factors were less common in this group, and > 1.00 D change was independently associated with high myopia (adjusted OR = 2.13, 95% CI 1.62–2.79). These trends are consistent with prior longitudinal studies21,24,25,34 and support the robustness of our findings.

Differences in myopia between men and women

Chi-square analysis showed higher myopia prevalence in females (88.9%) than males (82.9%; χ2 = 46.434, p < 0.01).

Relationship between parental myopia and children’s myopia

Parental myopia (one or both) was associated with higher prevalence in children (χ2 = 84.69, p < 0.01).

Influence of outdoor activities and near work on myopia

Outdoor activities > 1 h/day were associated with lower prevalence (29% vs. 71% for < 1 h/day; χ2 = 110.5, p < 0.01; Fig. 3). There was significant difference in the prevalence of myopia with different degrees of daily close eye time (χ2 = 73.12, p < 0.01; Fig. 4).

Others

Students whose parents reported providing prevention guidance had lower mean SE (− 2.85 ± 1.95 D) than those without (− 3.45 ± 2.25 D; t = 4.21, p < 0.01; Table 4). No significant differences were found by correction method (p > 0.05). In subgroup analyses, intervention adherence (e.g., parental/school guidance) was associated with milder myopia (adjusted mean SE difference: − 0.45 D, p < 0.05).However, as shown in Table 1, a higher proportion of myopic students reported receiving school-based prevention education (36.5%) compared to non-myopic students (27.3%), which may reflect reverse causality (e.g., schools intensifying efforts after detecting myopia,or parents of myopic children being more aware of and thus more likely to report preventive guidance). Therefore, these associations should not be interpreted as causal; prospective studies are needed to determine the direction of these relationships.

Analysis of related factors

Univariate analyses identified associations with gender, parental myopia, outdoor time, and near work (Table 5). Multivariate logistic regression (using GEE for clustering) confirmed: female gender (adjusted OR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.30–1.95, p < 0.01), parental myopia (OR = 2.20, 95% CI 1.75–2.85, p < 0.01), and outdoor time > 1 h/day (OR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.28–0.58, p < 0.01).

In sensitivity analyses, adjusting SE by + 0.50 D reduced prevalence to approximately 82.5%, with similar ORs (e.g., outdoor activity OR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.29–0.60, p < 0.01). Excluding ortho-K users yielded negligible changes (prevalence shift < 1%, e.g., OR shift < 0.02). Stratification by intervention adherence confirmed milder myopia in adherent subgroups (adjusted mean SE difference: − 0.45 D, p < 0.05). Full sensitivity results are in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

Our cross-sectional study, conducted in the immediate post-lockdown period (March–May 2023), provides a snapshot of the high myopia prevalence (85.3%) among Hefei high school students after the major COVID-19 lockdowns had ended. While the absence of pre-pandemic baseline data from the same cohort prevents us from directly quantifying the pandemic’s specific contribution, the observed prevalence is notably high and aligns with other Chinese studies reporting sustained or elevated myopia rates after periods of home confinement and online learning21,37. The lifestyle patterns we identified—such as prolonged near work (> 6 h/day in 55% of students) and critically low outdoor time (< 1 h/day in 60%)—are recognized risk factors that are consistent with those reported during pandemic restrictions13,35. Studies have consistently shown that such lockdown measures were associated with a “myopic shift” and accelerated progression13,21. Our cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; however, the high prevalence observed in this cohort is consistent with the presence of established risk factors reported in similar populations.

Importantly, our study advances beyond prevalence documentation by quantifying implementation gaps in evidence-based interventions—revealing that despite protective effects, adoption rates remain critically low (17.2% parental, 36.5% school-based guidance).Our cross-sectional study of 6414 high school students in Hefei revealed a high myopia prevalence of 85.3% (adjusted to 82.5% in sensitivity analysis assuming 0.5 D overestimation) during the post-COVID-19 period, associated with female gender (88.9% vs. 82.9% in males, p < 0.01), parental myopia (adjusted OR = 2.20, p < 0.01), reduced outdoor activities, and increased near work. Outdoor time > 1 h/day was linked to lower prevalence (adjusted OR = 0.40, p < 0.01), while parental and school education on prevention strategies (e.g., promoting outdoor time, regulating screen exposure) correlated with milder severity. These associations highlight modifiable factors that are consistent with lifestyle changes reported during the pandemic (e.g., prolonged near work during remote learning).

This prevalence aligns with other large Chinese cohorts from the post-COVID-19 period, though variations exist due to methodological differences. For instance, Wang et al. reported similar high rates (~ 80%) in school-aged children post-confinement, attributing shifts to increased screen time21. Lam et al. and Zhang et al. documented comparable gender and parental influences in urban Asian teens, with outdoor time as a protective factor22,23 . Guo et al. found slightly lower prevalence (70–80%) in comparable age groups, possibly due to cycloplegic refraction in their design, which may explain our potentially inflated estimates from non-cycloplegic methods24. Our larger sample and focus on education interventions add incremental value by emphasizing implementation gaps in prevention strategies in the post-pandemic context, where prior studies often lacked detailed adherence data.

Myopia onset was predominantly during middle school (49%), consistent with the dynamic process of axial elongation driven by environmental factors25,26. Recent models posit myopia as a feedback loop where near work—quantified as near demand (positive lens equivalent)—disrupts emmetropization, serving as the primary driver10,27. In our cohort, > 6 h/day near work in 55% of students and < 1 h outdoor time in 60% support this, consistent with evidence that pandemic lockdowns were associated with increased near demand through online learning. Genetic influences, such as parental myopia, interact with these, increasing susceptibility without clear molecular targets yet identified28–30. Thus, our findings reinforce that near work serves as a common driver, explaining higher rates in economically developed regions like Hefei compared to less urban areas31–34.

Prior studies have documented that during the pandemic, school closures were associated with reduced outdoor time by ~ 2 h/day and increased near work, which in turn were associated with persistent myopic shifts35,36. Mu et al. (2024) observed sustained elevations post-reopening, aligning with our data where 80% onset occurred during adolescence amid restrictions37 . Despite evidence for interventions like low-concentration atropine or defocus lenses38–41 , adoption was low (91.6% none), with frame glasses dominant (82.5%). Education adherence was limited (17.2% parental, 36.5% school), and was associated with lower severity. However, given the cross-sectional design, reverse causality is a plausible alternative explanation: parents or schools may implement preventive measures more actively after a child is diagnosed with myopia. Indeed, Table 1 shows that a higher proportion of myopic students reported receiving school-based education (36.5%) compared to non-myopic students (27.3%). Therefore, we cannot determine whether education reduces myopia or whether myopia increases the reporting of education. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Although the cross-sectional design cannot establish causality. This finding should be interpreted with caution and tested in prospective studies.

Gender differences (higher in females) may reflect behavioral patterns, such as less outdoor time and more near activities, while unmeasured confounders like socioeconomic status, seasonal daylight, or air quality in Hefei could influence results.

This study contributes to China’s national myopia plan by evaluating prevention in educational contexts, where near work burdens are high4,21,42–44. Unlike prevalence-focused studies, our emphasis on education implementation describes current practice patterns, which may inform future prospective studies and policy design. Causal claims regarding education effectiveness are not supported by this cross-sectional design.A limitation of our study is its cross-sectional design and single-timepoint measurement, which precludes direct estimation of individual myopia progression over time. Longitudinal cohort studies with repeated measures are required to quantify progression rates and the temporal effects of interventions (see CLEERE and other longitudinal reports). Nevertheless, the timing of our survey—shortly after the resumption of in-person schooling following COVID-19 restrictions—provides a meaningful snapshot of cumulative refractive outcomes in late adolescence and permits valid cross-sectional comparison with other post-pandemic studies21,24. To address concerns about measurement bias and to contextualize recent change, we conducted sensitivity analyses adjusting for known non-cycloplegic overestimation (~ + 0.50 D) and excluding orthokeratology users; both analyses yielded results consistent in direction and magnitude with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 1). We also report self-reported 1-year change categories to give further context. Future work should include prospective follow-up (≥ 1–5 years) with cycloplegic refraction and axial length measurements to quantify progression and the durability of education-based interventions25,34.

Consideration of potential bias from eye selection

As there raised a pertinent question regarding potential bias introduced by selecting the “worst eye” for classifying participants in borderline cases. We acknowledge this methodological consideration. Our primary rationale for this classification rule was to provide a conservative estimate of myopia prevalence, ensuring that individuals with any clinically significant myopia in at least one eye were correctly identified. This is aligned with public health approaches that prioritize sensitivity in detecting a condition. Crucially, as detailed in the Methods, this classification was solely used for the dichotomous prevalence estimate. All quantitative analyses—including the mean SE reported in Table 1, the distribution of myopia severity in Table 2, and the multivariate regression in Table 5—were performed using the eye-mean SE. This practice is strongly advocated for in ophthalmic epidemiology to account for inter-eye correlation and to avoid the unit-of-analysis errors and potential bias associated with arbitrarily selecting one eye20. The high correlation between right and left eye SE (r = 0.85) in our cohort justifies the use of the mean value. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis re-running the key multivariate model using only the right eye data yielded odds ratios for gender (OR = 1.58), parental myopia (OR = 2.18), and outdoor activity (OR = 0.41) that were nearly identical in magnitude and significance to our primary model using eye-mean SE (all p < 0.01). This consistency demonstrates that the choice of analytical method (eye-mean vs. single eye) did not materially influence the identified risk factors or their estimated effects, thereby ruling out substantial bias from our initial classification approach.

Limitations

Our findings have several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design and single-timepoint measurement preclude causal inferences and any direct assessment of the relationship between the COVID-19 pandemic and myopia prevalence or incidence in this cohort over time. Furthermore, for the associations between school/family education and myopia, reverse causality cannot be ruled out: parents or schools may increase preventive efforts in response to a child’s myopia, rather than the education causing lower myopia.Our data were collected after the lifting of lockdowns, but although our data are consistent with studies that documented a myopic shift during lockdowns13,21 and sustained high prevalence thereafter37, longitudinal data from the same population across pre-, peri-, and post-pandemic periods would be required to definitively attribute changes to the pandemic. Second, the narrow age range (17–19 years) minimizes developmental confounders but restricts generalizability to younger groups. Third, orthokeratology users discontinued for only three days; while prevalence was low (2.7%), residual effects may introduce minor bias, though sensitivity analyses showed negligible impact45. Fourth, non-cycloplegic refraction may overestimate myopia by ~ 0.50 D46; our sensitivity adjustment (reducing prevalence to ~ 82.5%) mitigated this, but future cycloplegic studies are needed. Fifth, self-reported data on near work, outdoor time, and education are prone to recall bias, particularly given the 12-month recall period, which may not accurately capture behaviors that occurred earlier in the pandemic (2020–2022). Objective measures (e.g., wearables) were not used. Additionally, the education measures were crude binary variables that do not capture dose, frequency, quality, or timing, and were not validated as standalone scales. Future studies should address these measurement limitations. Sixth, axial length and corneal curvature were not assessed, limiting insights into biological mechanisms like excessive elongation47,48. Seventh, unmeasured confounders (e.g., socioeconomic status, seasonal variations, air quality) may affect associations. No longitudinal follow-up was conducted, though re-examination could clarify if COVID effects persist or are transient; we recommend future cohort studies incorporating these elements.

In summary, high myopia prevalence in Hefei high school students is associated with gender, parental history, reduced outdoor time, and increased near work, with education interventions showing potential benefits. These insights inform targeted prevention in similar settings.
