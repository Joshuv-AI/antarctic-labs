Stein’s method and the modular behavior of Eulerian numbers
Jason Fulman, Adrian Röllin

Abstract
. The Eulerian number A ⁡ ( n , k ) A(n,k) counts permutations of { 1 , … , n } \{1,\dots,n\} with exactly k k descents. Motivated by problems in cryptography, several authors have studied the sum 1 n ! ​ ∑ k ≡ j ⁡ ( mod ​ b ) A ⁡ ( n , k ) \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k\equiv j\,(\mathrm{mod}\,b)}A(n,k) and its convergence to 1 / b 1/b . We give an explicit error bound for this convergence using Stein’s method for translated Poisson approximation.



1.
Introduction

We say that a permutation
\pi
on
n
symbols has a
descent
at position
i
, where
1\leq i\leq n-1
, if
\pi(i)>\pi(i+1)
. For example, the permutation
\underline{4}1\underline{5}23
has descents at positions
1
and
3
, giving a total of
2
descents. The
Eulerian number

A(n,k)
is the number of permutations on
n
symbols with exactly
k
descents; for instance,
A(3,0)=1
,
A(3,1)=4
,
A(3,2)=1
. We refer to
Petersen 2015
for a comprehensive treatment of Eulerian numbers, and to
Bayer and Diaconis 1992
,
Diaconis and Fulman 2023
and
Holte 1997
for their connections to card shuffling and carries in addition.

For
b\geq 2
and
0\leq j\leq b-1
, consider the quantity

(1.1)

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k\equiv j\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)}A(n,k),

which is the probability that the number of descents of a uniform random permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
is congruent to
j
modulo
b
. This quantity arises naturally in the cryptography literature on carries in addition; see
Staffelbach and Meier 1991
,
Sarkar 2009
and
Alquié 2010
. For
b=2
, the well known relation between Eulerian numbers and the Bernoulli numbers
B_{n}
gives the exact formula

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\text{$k$ even}}A(n,k)=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(1+\frac{2^{n+1}(2^{n+1}-1)B_{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\biggr),

from which it follows that the convergence to
1/2
is at rate
\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\bigl((2/\pi)^{n}\bigr)
; see
Alquié 2010
. For general fixed
b
,
Tanny 1973
proved that (
1.1
) converges to
1/b
as
n\to\infty
. Tanny’s argument uses the classical fact that
(1/n!)A(n,k)
equals the probability that the sum of
n
independent
\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]
random variables lies in
[k,k+1)
; the convergence then follows by wrapping this sum around a circle of circumference
b
and applying the central limit theorem.

The goal of this note is to provide an explicit error bound for this convergence. Our main result (Theorem
2.1
) gives a bound that, unlike Tanny’s asymptotic statement, allows
b
to grow with
n
. The proof combines two ingredients: a Fourier-analytic bound on the modular behavior of the Poisson distribution (Theorem
2.2
), and a translated Poisson approximation for the number of descents via Stein’s method of exchangeable pairs (Theorem
2.5
). The number of descents is well known to be asymptotically normal, and a proof via Stein’s method was given by
Fulman 2004
. However, since the number of descents is integer valued and we are interested in its residue modulo
b
, normal approximation is not directly useful; instead, we approximate by the translated Poisson distribution, which for large variances serves as a discrete analogue of the normal distribution. We apply
(
Röllin 2007
, Theorem 3.1)
, which provides a framework for translated Poisson approximation via exchangeable pairs, building on the exchangeable pair construction of
Fulman 2004
. We refer to
Ross 2011
for an introduction into Stein’s method.

We conclude by noting that the cryptography papers cited above are also interested in the behavior of the
i
-th carry for fixed
i
. The discussion above concerns the
i\to\infty
limit, in which Eulerian numbers arise. Theorem 3.4 of
Diaconis and Fulman 2009
shows that if one works base
b
, then slightly more than
\frac{1}{2}\log_{b}(n)
steps suffice for the total variation convergence of Holte’s carries Markov chain when
n
is large. For
i
this large, the probability that the
i
-th carry is congruent to
j
modulo
b
is therefore close to (
1.1
).

2.
Main results

Since we are working with permutations from the uniform distribution, it is equivalent to work with the
random variable
W(\pi)
which is the number of descents of
\pi^{-1}
. This is equal to the number of
i
(
1\leq i\leq n-1)

such that
i
and
i+1
are out of order in
\pi
.

The following theorem is our main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
n\geq 6
.
Let
b\geq 2
and
k\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}
. Then

\biggl\lvert\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{r\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)}A(n,r)-\frac{1}{b}\biggr\rvert\leq\sqrt{\frac{23}{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}+\frac{24}{n+1}+\frac{b-1}{b}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n+1}{12}\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi/b)\bigr)\Bigr).

The proof of the above result relies on two ingredients. The first is a bound on the modular behavior of the Poisson distribution, which is of independent interest. The second is an approximation of
W
by a translated Poisson distribution, which we obtain by applying Theorem 3.1 of
Röllin 2007
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X\sim\mathop{\mathrm{Po}}\mathopen{}(\lambda)
and let
b\geq 2
. Then for any

k\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}
,

\biggl\lvert\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\bigl[X\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\bigr]-\frac{1}{b}\biggr\rvert\leq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=1}^{b-1}\exp\bigl(-\lambda(1-\cos(2\pi j/b))\bigr)\leq\frac{b-1}{b}\exp\bigl(-\lambda(1-\cos(2\pi/b))\bigr).

Proof.

We use Fourier analysis on the finite abelian group
\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
.
For any integer-valued random variable
X
and any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
the indicator of the congruence class
\{X\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\}
admits
the Fourier expansion

\mathop{{}\mathrm{I}}\mathopen{}\bigl[X\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\bigr]=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\exp\biggl(\frac{2\pi ij(X-k)}{b}\biggr).

Indeed, for a fixed integer
x
,

\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\exp\biggl(\frac{2\pi ij(x-k)}{b}\biggr)=\begin{cases}1,&x\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b),\\
0,&x\not\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b),\end{cases}

by orthogonality of characters of
\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
.
Taking expectations yields

\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\bigl[X\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\bigr]=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\bigl\{e^{{2\pi ij(X-k)}/{b}}\bigr\}.

The term
j=0
equals
1/b
. For
j\neq 0
, using the characteristic
function of the Poisson distribution,

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\bigl\{e^{2\pi ijX/b}\bigr\}=\exp\bigl(\lambda(e^{2\pi ij/b}-1)\bigr).

Taking absolute values gives

\bigl\lvert\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\bigl\{e^{2\pi ij(X-k)/b}\bigr\}\bigr\rvert=\Bigl\lvert\exp\bigl(\lambda(e^{2\pi ij/b}-1)\bigr)\Bigr\rvert\,\bigl\lvert e^{2\pi ijk/b}\bigr\rvert=e^{-\lambda(1-\cos(2\pi j/b))}.

Summing over
j=1,\dots,b-1
yields the first inequality. The second inequality follows by bounding the sum by
b-1
times its largest term.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

For
b=2
, the above proof gives the exact formula

\biggl\lvert\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[X\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)]-\frac{1}{2}\biggr\rvert=\frac{e^{-2\lambda}}{2},\qquad k\in\{0,1\}.

We proceed as in
Röllin 2007
and introduce the following notion. An integer-valued random variable
Y
is said to have a
translated Poisson distribution
with parameters

\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
, written

\mathop{{}\mathcal{L}}(Y)=\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\mu,\sigma^{2})

if

\mathop{{}\mathcal{L}}(Y-\mu+\sigma^{2}+\gamma)=\mathop{\mathrm{Po}}\mathopen{}(\sigma^{2}+\gamma),

where
\gamma=\langle\mu-\sigma^{2}\rangle
denotes the fractional part of

\mu-\sigma^{2}
, with
\langle x\rangle=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
.
In particular,
\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\sigma^{2},\sigma^{2})=\mathop{\mathrm{Po}}\mathopen{}(\sigma^{2})
. Approximating a random variable
W
by
\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
allows the mean to be matched exactly. The variance, however, can only be matched up to an additive error of at most
1
, where

\sigma^{2}\leq\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}Y=\sigma^{2}+\gamma\leq\sigma^{2}+1.

This discrepancy causes no difficulty, since for the total variation distance, the resulting error is of order
\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}(\sigma^{-2})
.

The next goal is to approximate
W(\pi)
, the number of descents of the inverse of
\pi
, by a translated Poisson distribution. We apply the following result, which is a special case of Theorem 3.1 of
Röllin 2007
. Recall that
(W,W^{\prime})
is called an exchangeable pair if
(W,W^{\prime})
has the same distribution as
(W^{\prime},W)
. We also let

\mathop{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lvert P(x)-Q(x)\rvert=\max_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\lvert P(A)-Q(A)\rvert

denote the total variation distance between probability distributions
P
and
Q
on a finite set
\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem 2.4

(See
(
Röllin 2007
, Theorem 3.1)
)
.

Assume that
(W,W^{\prime})
is an exchangeable pair with values in the integers, such that

•

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}W=\mu
and
\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}W=\sigma^{2}<\infty
,

•

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}-\mu\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15muW]=(1-\lambda)(W-\mu)
for some
0<\lambda<1
,

•

W^{\prime}-W\in\{-1,0,+1\}
.

Then, with
S=S(W)=\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15muW]
,

\mathop{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}\bigl(\mathop{{}\mathcal{L}}(W),\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\bigr)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}S}}{\lambda\sigma^{2}}+\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}.

As a consequence of Theorem
2.4
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
W(\pi)
be the number of descents of
\pi^{-1}
where
\pi
is chosen uniformly at random from the set of
permutations of
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. Then for
n\geq 6
,
\mu=\frac{n-1}{2}
and
\sigma^{2}=\frac{n+1}{12}
,

\mathop{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}\bigl(\mathop{{}\mathcal{L}}(W),\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\bigr)\leq\sqrt{\frac{23}{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}+\frac{24}{n+1}.

Proof.

We use an exchangeable pair
(W,W^{\prime})
constructed in
Fulman 2004
. Letting
W(\pi)
be the number of descents of
\pi^{-1}
, we define
W^{\prime}=W^{\prime}(\pi)
as follows. Pick an integer
I
uniformly at random between
1
and
n
and move the number in position
I
in the second row of
\pi
to the end of this second row. This results in a new permutation
\pi^{\prime}
, and we define
W^{\prime}(\pi)=W(\pi^{\prime})
. For example, suppose that
n=7
and
I=3
. Then the permutation
\pi
in two-line form is

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=\ 1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7

\displaystyle\pi(i)

\displaystyle=\ 6\ 4\ 1\ 5\ 3\ 2\ 7

is transformed to

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=\ 1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(i)

\displaystyle=\ 6\ 4\ 5\ 3\ 2\ 7\ 1.

Fulman 2004
proved that despite the non-reversibility of the “move random to end” Markov chain, the pair
(W,W^{\prime})
is exchangeable. The exchangeability also follows from Lemma 1.1 of
Rinott and Rotar 1997
and the fact that
W^{\prime}-W\in\{-1,0,+1\}
.

It is well known that
\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}W=(n-1)/2
and
\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}W=(n+1)/12
. Clearly
W^{\prime}-W\in\{-1,0,+1\}
. Moreover, Lemma 1 of
Fulman 2004

yields

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}-\mu\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15muW]=(1-\lambda)(W-\mu)

for
\lambda=2/n
. Thus the hypotheses of Theorem
2.4
are satisfied.

To apply Theorem
2.4
with
\lambda=2/n
and
\sigma^{2}=(n+1)/12
, it remains only to bound
\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}S
. Since
W
is a function of
\pi
, it follows from the standard conditional variance decomposition of
\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15mu\pi]
and the projection property that

\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}S\leq\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\bigl(\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15mu\pi]\bigr).

Clearly,

\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15mu\pi]=\frac{1}{n}\bigl(X_{1}(\pi)+X_{2}(\pi)+\cdots+X_{n-1}(\pi)\bigr),

where

X_{1}(\pi)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $1$ and $2$ are in order in $\pi$,}\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}

and for
2\leq i\leq n-1
,

X_{i}(\pi)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i-1,i,i+1$ are in order in $\pi$,}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Clearly

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\bigl\{\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15mu\pi]\bigr\}=\frac{1}{n}(\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}X_{1}+\cdots+\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}X_{n-1})=\frac{1}{n}\biggl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{n-2}{6}\biggr)=\frac{n+1}{6n}.

Now for
n\geq 6
, one has that

\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\bigl\{\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15mu\pi]^{2}\bigr\}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}(T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}+T_{5}),

where

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{1}^{2}\}+\cdots+\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{n-1}^{2}\}=\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}X_{1}+\cdots+\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}X_{n-1}=\frac{n+1}{6},

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{2}&=2\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{1}X_{2}\}+2\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{1}X_{3}\}+2\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{1}X_{4}\}+\cdots+2\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{1}X_{n-1}\}\\
&=\frac{2}{6}+\frac{2}{24}+\frac{2(n-4)}{12},\end{split}

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{i}X_{i+1}\}=\frac{2(n-3)}{24},\qquad T_{4}=2\sum_{i=2}^{n-3}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{i}X_{i+2}\}=\frac{2(n-4)}{120},

\displaystyle T_{5}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=2}^{n-4}\sum_{j=i+3}^{n-1}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\{X_{i}X_{j}\}=\frac{2}{36}\sum_{i=2}^{n-4}\sum_{j=i+3}^{n-1}1=\frac{2}{36}\frac{(n-4)(n-5)}{2}.

Thus, straightforward algebraic manipulation yields

\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\bigl(\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}[W^{\prime}=W+1\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.25mu|\mskip 0.5mu plus 0.15mu\pi]\bigr)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}(T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}+T_{5})-\biggl(\frac{n+1}{6n}\biggr)^{2}=\frac{23(n+1)}{180n^{2}}.

Plugging into Theorem
2.4
completes the proof.
∎

We can now give the proof of our main result.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Since
W
is the number of descents of the inverse of a uniform random permutation,
\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\bigl[W=r\bigr]=A(n,r)/n!
for each
r
, and hence
\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\bigl[W\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\bigr]=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{r\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)}A(n,r)
.
Let
Y\sim\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, where
\mu=(n-1)/2
and
\sigma^{2}=(n+1)/12
. By the triangle inequality,

\biggl\lvert\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\bigl[W\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\bigr]-\frac{1}{b}\biggr\rvert\leq\mathop{d_{\mathrm{TV}}}\bigl(\mathop{{}\mathcal{L}}(W),\mathop{\mathrm{TP}}\mathopen{}(\mu,\sigma^{2})\bigr)+\biggl\lvert\mathop{{}\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\bigl[Y\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\bigr]-\frac{1}{b}\biggr\rvert.

The first term is bounded by
\sqrt{23/5}\cdot(n+1)^{-1/2}+24/(n+1)
by Theorem
2.5
. For the second term, note that
Y
shifted by
\mu-\sigma^{2}-\gamma
has distribution
\mathop{\mathrm{Po}}\mathopen{}(\sigma^{2}+\gamma)
, where
\gamma=\langle\mu-\sigma^{2}\rangle\in[0,1)
and
\sigma^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}+\gamma<\sigma^{2}+1
, so applying Theorem
2.2
with
\lambda=\sigma^{2}+\gamma\geq(n+1)/12
yields a bound of

\frac{b-1}{b}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n+1}{12}\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi/b)\bigr)\Bigr).

Combining the two bounds completes the proof.
∎
