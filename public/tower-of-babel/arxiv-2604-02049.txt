A variationally consistent beam-to-beam point coupling formulation for geometrically exact beam theories
Ivo Steinbrecher, Nora Hagmeyer, Christoph Meier, Alexander Popp

Abstract
Slender beam-like structures frequently occur in engineering applications and often interact at discrete locations through joints or connectors. Accurate modeling of such interactions is particularly challenging when different numerical formulations are involved in terms of underlying beam theory, interpolation schemes, and rotation parametrization. In this work, a versatile formulation-independent beam-to-beam point coupling approach is proposed within the framework of the geometrically exact beam theory discretized by the finite element method. The coupling constraints are expressed solely in terms of cross-section kinematics, namely centroid positions and orientations. Suitable generalized deformation measures for positional and rotational coupling are introduced, allowing for general coupling configurations, including relative rotations and non-coincident cross-section centroids in the reference configuration. The contribution of the coupling conditions to the weak form of the balance equations is derived in a variationally consistent manner and can be incorporated directly into the weak form of existing beam finite element models. Constraint enforcement is formulated using a Lagrange multiplier method and a penalty regularization. The proposed approach satisfies key properties such as objectivity, symmetry, and consistency with a stress-free reference configuration. Numerical examples demonstrate the robustness and flexibility of the method for coupling beams with different formulations and discretizations, even when the interaction points are located at arbitrary positions within beam elements.



keywords
geometrically exact beam theory, beam-to-beam coupling, positional constraints, rotational constraints

Introduction

Slender fiber- or rod-like components are key elements in many engineering applications and scientific fields, including fiber-reinforced structures, lattice and truss systems, flexible mechanisms, and biological filaments.
Such slender structures can be efficiently modeled using one-dimensional Cosserat continua, based on structural beam theories.
In many practical applications, these components interact with each other at discrete locations, forming joints or connectors that play an essential role in the overall mechanical behavior of the system.
Accurate and robust modeling of such couplings is therefore essential, particularly when interacting beams are described by different beam formulations or discretization schemes.

In this work, we consider beam models based on the geometrically exact beam theory.
The theoretical groundwork for the geometrically exact Simo–Reissner beam theory was laid in
23
;
22
, the first finite element implementation was presented in
26
and has subsequently been further developed and improved.
In
14
;
9
, objective strain measures and corresponding objective finite element discretizations were introduced for geometrically exact beam formulations.
A director based rotational interpolation scheme for geometrically exact beams was presented in
4
.
An efficient
SE(3)
interpolation for a uniform treatment of beam centerline and orientation fields was proposed in
27
.
All of these works consider shear-deformable beam formulations.
A shear-rigid (Kirchhoff–Love) geometrically exact beam formulation was developed in
18
;
19
.
More recently, a mixed geometrically exact beam formulation was proposed in
12
, which allows for a unified formulation that can model shear-deformable and shear-rigid beams as well as extensible and inextensible beams.

Despite their differences, these beam formulations share a common kinematic description: each beam cross-section is uniquely defined by its centroid position
\underline{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and its orientation
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}\in SO(3)
.
Here,
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}=[\underline{\bm{g}}_{1},\underline{\bm{g}}_{2},\underline{\bm{g}}_{3}]
denotes an orthonormal triad formed by the cross-section base vectors
\underline{\bm{g}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
While the underlying kinematic variables are identical, their parametrization and finite element interpolation differ between beam formulations.

Rigid joints between beams have been considered in several of the previously mentioned works by directly coupling the nodal degrees of freedom of the interacting beams
26
;
14
;
27
.
However, this approach is limited to coupling beams at finite element nodes.
Furthermore, when more complex parametrizations of the orientation field are used, as in Kirchhoff–Love beam formulations, such direct coupling becomes less straightforward.
An alternative approach is to formulate explicit coupling constraints in terms of the cross-section kinematics.
Typically, three positional constraint equations enforce the coincidence of the centroid positions, while three rotational constraint equations enforce the relative orientation of the cross-section frames.
For the latter, a commonly used approach is to enforce the orthogonality of selected pairs of base vectors of the two cross-sections.
Although this approach is valid, it may introduce numerical difficulties.
For instance, if the orientational constraints are enforced using a penalty method, the resulting system matrix may lose positive definiteness, which can lead to severe convergence issues in nonlinear solution schemes
(
31
, Section 3.7.2)
.

In this work, we follow a different strategy to formulate the coupling constraints, which is inspired by the framework proposed in
17
.
In that work, general interaction potentials between beams were introduced within the geometrically exact beam theory.
This framework has already been successfully applied to model potential-based beam interactions
11
;
10
and the coupling of beams with three-dimensional continua
29
;
30
;
28
.
In
17
, the authors also outlined how two beam cross-sections can be coupled to each other.
In the present work, we build upon this approach and modify the coupling constraints to ensure symmetry with respect to the interacting cross-sections.
The main idea is to introduce suitable
generalized deformation measures
for positional and rotational coupling that describe the relative deformation between interacting beam cross-sections.
The formulation is designed to handle general coupling configurations in which the cross-sections may exhibit relative rotations in the reference configuration and their centroid positions do not necessarily coincide.
Also, the coupling point is not restricted to be located at a finite element node, but can be located at an arbitrary position along the beam elements.
We show that the proposed coupling formulation satisfies fundamental properties such as objectivity, symmetry, and consistency with a stress-free reference configuration.
Furthermore, we present the discrete coupling equations in a general form such that they can be applied to arbitrary geometrically exact beam formulations, independent of the choice of primary variables, rotational parametrizations, or interpolation schemes.
As a result, the proposed formulation provides a robust and formulation-independent framework for coupling geometrically exact beam models in complex beam assemblies.

2
Large rotations

This section briefly summarizes the notation and fundamental relations for finite rotations required in this work.
For a more comprehensive treatment, the reader is referred to the literature, e.g.,
7
;
13
;
24
;
8
;
20
;
3
.

A rotation can be described by an orthonormal triad

\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{g}}_{1},\underline{\bm{g}}_{2},\underline{\bm{g}}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\in SO(3),

(1)

where
SO(3)
denotes the special orthogonal group, i.e., the triad satisfies
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}=\underline{\bm{I}}
and
\det\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}\right)=1
.
The columns
\underline{\bm{g}}_{i}
represent the basis vectors of the rotated configuration.

For parametrization, a rotation vector
\underline{\bm{\psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is introduced such that
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}(\underline{\bm{\psi}})
.
The vector
\underline{\bm{\psi}}
defines a rotation with magnitude
\psi=\left\|\underline{\bm{\psi}}\right\|
about the axis
\underline{\bm{e}}_{\psi}=\underline{\bm{\psi}}/\psi
.
The corresponding rotation tensor is obtained via the exponential map

\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}(\underline{\bm{\psi}})=\exp\!\big(\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}\right)}\big),

(2)

which can be expressed using Rodrigues’ formula as

\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}(\underline{\bm{\psi}})=\underline{\bm{I}}+\frac{\sin\psi}{\psi}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}\right)}+\frac{1-\cos\psi}{\psi^{2}}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}\right)}^{2}.

(3)

Here,
\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\cdot\right)}
denotes the skew-symmetric operator associated with the cross product, i.e.,
\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{a}}\right)}\underline{\bm{b}}=\underline{\bm{a}}\times\underline{\bm{b}}\,\forall\,\underline{\bm{a}},\underline{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The inverse mapping is denoted by

\underline{\bm{\psi}}=\rv(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}),

(4)

which extracts the rotation vector from a given rotation tensor.

Given two rotations
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}
and
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}
, the relative rotation between them is defined by

\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{21}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}},

(5)

with the corresponding rotation vector
\underline{\bm{\psi}}_{21}=\rv(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{21})
.
Due to the nonlinear structure of the rotation group, this relative rotation vector does not satisfy
\underline{\bm{\psi}}_{21}=\underline{\bm{\psi}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{\psi}}_{1}
.

Variations of the rotation tensor can be expressed using a multiplicative increment in terms of the spin vector
\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\delta\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}=\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}\right)}\,\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}.

(6)

The variation of the rotation tensor can also be expressed in terms of additive variations of the rotation vector
\delta\underline{\bm{\psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\delta\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}=\frac{\partial\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\partial\underline{\bm{\psi}}}\delta\underline{\bm{\psi}}.

(7)

Here, the transformation matrix
\underline{\bm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
maps the multiplicative variations to the additive variations, i.e.,

\delta\underline{\bm{\psi}}=\underline{\bm{T}}(\underline{\bm{\psi}})\,\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}.

(8)

A closed-form expression reads

\underline{\bm{T}}(\underline{\bm{\psi}})=\frac{1}{\psi^{2}}\underline{\bm{\psi}}\underline{\bm{\psi}}^{\mathrm{T}}-\frac{1}{2}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}\right)}+\frac{\psi}{2\tan(\psi/2)}\left(\underline{\bm{I}}-\frac{1}{\psi^{2}}\underline{\bm{\psi}}\underline{\bm{\psi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\right).

(9)

3
Coupling equations

In this section, we present the governing equations of the proposed beam-to-beam point coupling formulation.
We consider two cross-sections
1
and
2
that are coupled to each other.
It is sufficient to consider a single cross-section pair, since the formulation generalizes straightforwardly to an arbitrary number of coupling pairs.
The kinematics of both cross-sections are fully described by their centroid positions
\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and orientations
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\in SO(3)
in the current configuration.
The corresponding quantities in the reference configuration are denoted by
\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{2}\in SO(3)
.
In this work, a superscript
0
denotes quantities in the reference configuration.
In the following, we derive the variational formulation of the coupling constraints in terms of these kinematic quantities and their variations.
The resulting virtual work contributions can be added directly to the weak formulation of the underlying beam model.
Consequently, the presented coupling formulation is independent of the specific beam theory employed and can be combined with any geometrically exact beam formulation, such as the Simo–Reissner or Kirchhoff–Love beam theory.

3.1
Enforcement of coupling constraints

Before specifying the explicit forms of the positional and rotational constraints, we briefly introduce the enforcement of coupling constraints employed in this work in a generic manner.
This applies to both positional and rotational types and will therefore not be repeated in the subsequent subsections.
We state the constraint equations in the following general form:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}_{2},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\right)=\underline{\bm{0}},

(10)

where
(\cdot)
is a placeholder for either the positional or rotational coupling constraint, which will be denoted by
r
and
\theta
, respectively, in the following sections, cf.
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
.
Each constraint is vector-valued with
\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Consequently, a total of six scalar constraint equations restrict the six relative degrees of freedom between the two cross-sections.

3.1.1
Lagrange multiplier method

In this subsection, we describe the enforcement of the coupling constraints using the Lagrange multiplier method, which provides a consistent variational framework for deriving the generalized coupling equations.
For each vector-valued constraint, a Lagrange multiplier vector
\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is introduced.
The associated Lagrange multiplier potential is defined as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}=\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}},

(11)

where the explicit dependence of the coupling constraints on the kinematic variables is omitted for readability.
Taking the variation of the Lagrange multiplier potential yields the virtual work contribution of the coupling constraints,

\displaystyle\delta W^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}=\delta\Pi^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}=\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}+\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\mathrm{T}}\delta_{o}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}.

(12)

Here, the variation of the coupling constraints is expressed in terms of objective variations, which is required to ensure the objectivity of the formulation, see condition (
iii
) below.
The virtual work contribution (
12
) can be written in matrix-vector form as

\displaystyle\delta W^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}=\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}+\delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}}_{\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\lambda}_{1,(\cdot)}}+\delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}}_{\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\lambda}_{2,(\cdot)}}.

(13)

Here,
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{i}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 3}
are coupling matrices that map the Lagrange multiplier vector to the corresponding generalized force and moment contributions
\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\lambda}_{i,(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
acting on the cross-sections, i.e.,
\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\lambda}_{i,(\cdot)}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{i}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}
.
Moreover, the vector
\delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
collects the variations of all kinematic variables of a single cross-section, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}&\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(14)

Finally, we provide the linearization of the generalized forces, which is required for the consistent incorporation of the coupling constraints into tangent-based nonlinear solution schemes.
The linearization reads

\displaystyle\Delta\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\lambda}_{1,(\cdot)}\\
\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\lambda}_{2,(\cdot)}\\
\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{1}\\
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{2}\\
\Delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

Here,
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{i}q_{j}}_{(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}
denote the linearization of the generalized forces with respect to the kinematic variables, while
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{i}}_{(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 6}
represents the linearization of the coupling constraints with respect to the kinematic variables.
Furthermore,
\Delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the increment of the Lagrange multipliers and
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
collects the increments of the kinematic variables, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}&\Delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(16)

3.1.2
Penalty regularization

The linear system resulting from the Lagrange multiplier formulation (
15
) contains the Lagrange multipliers as additional unknowns and exhibits saddle-point structure.
To avoid these additional complexities, a penalty regularization can be employed to eliminate the Lagrange multipliers from the system.
We employ a simple penalty regularization of the form

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}\approx\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}_{2},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\right),

(17)

where
\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes a scalar penalty parameter.
For
\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\rightarrow\infty
, the penalty regularization (
17
) becomes equivalent to the original constraint equations (
10
).
With the penalty regularization, the Lagrange multipliers are no longer explicit unknowns and the variational form (
13
) can be written as

\displaystyle\delta W^{\epsilon}_{(\cdot)}=\delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\underbrace{\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}}_{\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\epsilon}_{1,(\cdot)}}+\delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\underbrace{\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}}_{\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\epsilon}_{2,(\cdot)}}.

(18)

Here,
\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\epsilon}_{i,(\cdot)}
denote the generalized penalty forces and moments resulting from the coupling constraints.
The linearization of the generalized penalty contributions reads

\displaystyle\Delta\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\epsilon}_{1,(\cdot)}\\
\bm{\mathsf{f}}^{\epsilon}_{2,(\cdot)}\end{bmatrix}=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{(\cdot)}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{(\cdot)}\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{1}\\
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{q}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(19)

The penalty parameters
\epsilon_{(\cdot)}
control the enforcement of the constraints when a penalty regularization is employed.
In practice, they should be chosen sufficiently large to enforce the constraints accurately, while avoiding ill-conditioning of the resulting system of equations.
Suitable values for the penalty parameters can be estimated based on the stiffness and geometry of the beams.
As a rule of thumb, the positional penalty parameter may be chosen as
\epsilon_{r}\approx\bar{E}\bar{R}
,
where
\bar{E}=\tfrac{1}{2}(E_{1}+E_{2})
and
\bar{R}=\tfrac{1}{2}(R_{1}+R_{2})
denote the average Young’s modulus and cross-section size of the two beams, respectively.
For the rotational penalty parameter, a suitable choice is
\epsilon_{\theta}\approx\bar{E}\bar{R}^{3}
.
These estimates depend on the specific problem and may require some tuning, but they provide a good starting point for the selection of the penalty parameters.

Remark 1
.

The presented penalty regularization can alternatively be derived from a quadratic penalty potential of the form
\Pi^{\epsilon}_{(\cdot)}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{(\cdot)}(\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}
.

Remark 2
.

The presented derivation of the penalty regularized system of equations can also be interpreted in an algebraic way, cf.
32
, by adding the term
-\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\mathrm{T}}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{(\cdot)}}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}
to the variational formulation (
12
), which vanishes for
\epsilon_{(\cdot)}\rightarrow\infty
.
By adding this term, the Lagrange multipliers can be condensed from the last row of (
13
), yielding (
18
).

Remark 3
.

In this work, a simple penalty regularization with a scalar penalty parameter is considered.
The general framework for employing a symmetric material penalty tensor has been outlined in
17
.
Incorporating such a tensor into the present formulation would allow the modeling of complex geometric and material behavior of the connection between the two cross-sections.
However, this extension is beyond the scope of the present work.

3.2
Requirements for coupling constraints

In order to ensure mechanical consistency and frame-independence of the formulation, the coupling constraints are required to satisfy the following fundamental properties:

(i)

Stress-free reference configuration:

The coupling constraints must vanish in the reference configuration, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{g}}^{0}_{(\cdot)}=\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{2},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{2}\right)=\underline{\bm{0}}.

(20)

(ii)

Symmetry:

A permutation of the cross-section indices
1
and
2
shall only lead to a sign change of the constraint equations,

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}_{2},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\right)=-\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{2},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2},\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}\right).

(21)

(iii)

Objectivity:

The variational formulation must be invariant under superimposed rigid body motions.
For an arbitrary rigid body motion defined by a constant rotation
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\in SO(3)
and translation
\underline{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the kinematic variables, Lagrange multipliers, and variations transform as

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}^{\ast}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}+\underline{\bm{a}},\quad\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{\ast}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i},\quad\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\ast}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)},\quad\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}^{\ast}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{i},\quad\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{\ast}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\ast}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\ast}\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}.

(22)

To guarantee objectivity, the virtual work contribution of the Lagrange multiplier potential (
12
) must satisfy

\displaystyle\delta W^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}

\displaystyle=\delta W^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\bigl(\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\underline{\bm{r}}_{2},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2},\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{1},\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{1},\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{2},\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{2},\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}\bigr)

(23)

\displaystyle=\delta W^{\lambda}_{(\cdot)}\bigl(\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}^{\ast},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}^{\ast},\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}^{\ast},\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}^{\ast},\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}^{\ast},\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{1}^{\ast},\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}^{\ast},\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{2}^{\ast},\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{(\cdot)}^{\ast}\bigr)=\delta W^{\lambda\ast}_{(\cdot)}.

The fulfillment of these requirements will be demonstrated for the positional and rotational constraints in the subsequent sections.

Remark 4
.

The symmetry requirement (
ii
) stated above is only relevant for the penalty regularization.
In the Lagrange multiplier case, the constraint equations are satisfied exactly, i.e.,
\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}=\underline{\bm{0}}
, such that a permutation of the cross-section indices yields the same result.
In contrast, when the constraint equations are enforced with a penalty regularization, a small deviation from the solution with exact constraint enforcement will occur, i.e.,
\mathchoice{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}{}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}{\underline{\bm{g}}_{(\cdot)}}\neq\underline{\bm{0}}
.
In this case, the symmetry requirement becomes essential to ensure that the formulation does not depend on the ordering of the two cross-sections.

3.3
Positional coupling

In this section, we present the positional coupling between the two cross-sections
1
and
2
.
The positional constraint enforces the coincidence of selected material points of the two cross-sections.
Since these material points are not necessarily located at the centroids of the cross-sections, the positional constraint generally introduces coupling moments.
As a first step, we define the relative distance vector
\underline{\bm{r}}_{21}
between the centroids of the two cross-sections:

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{r}}_{21}=\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}.

(24)

The relative distance vector
\underline{\bm{r}}_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
constitutes a
generalized spatial deformation measure

17
.
By introducing suitable push-forward and pull-back operators, a material representation of
\underline{\bm{r}}_{21}
can be obtained.
The definition of these operators corresponds to the choice of a reference frame in which the relative distance vector is expressed.
At this stage, the appropriate reference frame for the material representation is not yet specified.
Therefore, we formulate the push-forward and pull-back operations with respect to both cross-section frames and denote them by
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}
and
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}
, respectively, with
i=1,2
.
The specific choice will become apparent once the explicit form of the positional deformation measures is introduced.
The material relative distance vector can be stated as

\displaystyle{}^{i}\underline{\bm{R}}_{21}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{r}}_{21}.

(25)

In this work, a leading superscript is employed to identify the reference frame in which a given quantity is expressed.
The reference material distance vector is defined accordingly as
{}^{i}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}=(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{21}
.
The material deformation measure associated with the positional coupling constraint is defined as the difference between the current and reference material distance vectors,

\displaystyle{}^{i}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}={}^{i}\underline{\bm{R}}_{21}-{}^{i}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}.

(26)

To obtain a spatial deformation measure that is symmetric (
ii
) with respect to the ordering of the two cross-sections, we take the push-forward of the two material deformation measures
{}^{1}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}
and
{}^{2}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}
and introduce their arithmetic mean.
The resulting spatial deformation measure can thus be stated as

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\bm{r}}}_{21}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}{}^{1}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}+\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}{}^{2}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}\right)=\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}{}^{1}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}+\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}{}^{2}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}\right).

(27)

The verification that the deformation measure leads to a coupling formulation fulfilling the fundamental properties (
i
)–(
iii
) stated in
Section

3.2
is provided in Appendix
8
.
Since these requirements are satisfied, the deformation measure can be directly employed as the positional coupling constraint, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{g}}_{r}=\hat{\underline{\bm{r}}}_{21}.

(28)

For the variational form arising from the Lagrange multiplier method, cf. (
12
), we need to define the objective variation of the spatial deformation measure.
The objective variation is given by the push-forward of the respective material variations
17
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta_{o}\hat{\underline{\bm{r}}}_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}\delta{}^{1}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}+\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\delta{}^{2}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}\right).

(29)

Here,
\delta{}^{1}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}
and
\delta{}^{2}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}
are the variations of the material deformation measures, which are obtained as

\displaystyle\delta{}^{i}\hat{\underline{\bm{R}}}_{21}=\delta\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}\right)-\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{1}\right)\right)=-\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\right)}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}\right)+\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}\right).

(30)

Inserting (
29
) and (
30
) into (
12
) yields the variational form of the positional coupling constraint:

\displaystyle\delta W^{\lambda}_{r}=\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}{}^{1}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}+\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}{}^{2}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}\right)\right)+\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\right)}+\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\right)}\bigr)\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}\right)\right).

(31)

It is well-known from geometrically exact beam theory that the variation of the cross-section position is work-conjugate to the cross-section force resultants.
Thus, the positional Lagrange multipliers can be interpreted as coupling forces.
Furthermore, the coupling forces induce a moment contribution that is work-conjugate to the cross-section spin vectors.
This moment arises when the cross-section centroids do not coincide in the reference configuration, i.e.,
\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{1}\neq\underline{\bm{r}}^{0}_{2}
.

In a final step, we can express the variational form (
31
) in matrix-vector form (
13
) with

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{r}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{I}}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{r}}_{2}-\underline{\bm{r}}_{1}\right)}\end{bmatrix}.

(32)

The linearizations read

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{1}}_{r}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{1}}_{r}&=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&\underline{\bm{0}}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}\right)}&\underline{\bm{0}}\end{bmatrix},&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{r}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{2}}_{r}&=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&\underline{\bm{0}}\\
\frac{1}{2}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}\right)}&\underline{\bm{0}}\end{bmatrix},\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{r}&=\begin{bmatrix}-\underline{\bm{I}}&\frac{1}{2}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}{}^{1}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}\right)}\end{bmatrix},&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{r}&=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{I}}&\frac{1}{2}\underline{\bm{S}}{\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}{}^{2}\underline{\bm{R}}^{0}_{21}\right)}\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}

(33)

3.4
Rotational coupling

In this section, we derive the rotational coupling between the two cross-sections.
The rotational coupling enforces a prescribed relative orientation between the cross-section triads.
The formulation of the rotational coupling constraint is based on relative rotations between the two cross-sections, which is in close analogy to the generalized cross-section interaction laws proposed in
17
.
We first define the effective rotation that maps the reference cross-section triad to the current configuration,

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{i}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}.

(34)

The relative rotation between the effective rotations of both cross-sections is then given by

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{21}=\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{2}\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{2}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{1}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}.

(35)

In
17
, it has been shown that the relative rotation (pseudo-)vector associated with
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{21}=\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{2}(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1})^{\mathrm{T}}
constitutes a suitable
generalized spatial deformation measure
for rotational coupling.
In the present work, we employ a slightly modified definition of the relative rotation vector, which is based on the effective rotations
\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{i}
and therefore directly accounts for the stress-free reference configuration.
The rotation vector can be extracted from
\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{21}
with relation (
4
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}=\rv(\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{21}).

(36)

The verification that the relative rotation vector leads to a coupling formulation fulfilling the fundamental properties (
i
)–(
iii
) stated in
Section

3.2
is provided in Appendix
9
.
The relative rotation vector
\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}
can therefore be used directly as the rotational coupling constraint, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{g}}_{\theta}=\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}.

(37)

The objective variation of the relative rotation vector
\delta_{o}\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}
is derived in
17
and reads

\displaystyle\delta_{o}\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}=\underline{\bm{T}}(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21})\bigl(\hat{\underline{\bm{\theta}}}_{2}-\hat{\underline{\bm{\theta}}}_{1}\bigr).

(38)

It remains to relate the spin vectors
\delta\hat{\underline{\bm{\theta}}}_{i}
to the cross-section spin vectors
\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
.
Since the reference triads
\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{i}
are constant, variation of (
34
) yields

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{S}}(\delta\hat{\underline{\bm{\theta}}}_{i})\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{i}=\underline{\bm{S}}\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\right)\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}\left(\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}^{0}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}=\underline{\bm{S}}\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\right)\hat{\underline{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{i},

(39)

which directly implies

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\underline{\bm{\theta}}}_{i}=\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}.

(40)

With these relations, the variational form of the rotational coupling constraint becomes

\displaystyle\delta W^{\lambda}_{\theta}=\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}+\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{T}}(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21})\left(\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{2}-\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\right).

(41)

As multiplicative rotation variations
\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
are work-conjugate to the cross-section moment stress resultants, the associated term
\pm\underline{\bm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21})\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}
represents a discrete coupling moment acting between the two beam cross-sections.

In a final step, we can express the variational form (
41
) in matrix-vector form (
13
) with

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}\\
-\underline{\bm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}\right)\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}\\
-\underline{\bm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(42)

The consistent linearization follows as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{1}}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&\underline{\bm{0}}\\
\underline{\bm{0}}&-\frac{\partial\left(\underline{\bm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}\right)\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}\right)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{1}}\underline{\bm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{1}\right)\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{2}}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&\underline{\bm{0}}\\
\underline{\bm{0}}&\frac{\partial\left(\underline{\bm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}\right)\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}\right)}{\partial\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{2}}\underline{\bm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{2}\right)\end{bmatrix},\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{\theta}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{1}}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&\underline{\bm{0}}\\
\underline{\bm{0}}&\underline{\bm{0}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&\frac{\partial\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}}{\partial\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{1}}\underline{\bm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{1}\right)\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\bm{0}}&&\frac{\partial\hat{\underline{\bm{\psi}}}_{21}}{\partial\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{2}}\underline{\bm{T}}\left(\underline{\bm{\psi}}{}_{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}

(43)

In practice, all derivatives explicitly stated in (
43
) are evaluated using forward automatic differentiation (FAD), cf.
15
, using the Sacado software package
25
, which is part of the Trilinos project
16
.

4
Coupling equations in a discretized setting

In this section, we illustrate how the coupling equations presented in
Section

3
can be incorporated into a discretized setting, i.e., how the kinematic variables and generalized forces are mapped to the discrete degrees of freedom of the underlying beam formulation.
The derivation is demonstrated for the Lagrange multiplier formulation, but can be stated for the penalty regularization in an analogous manner.
We do not assume a specific beam formulation, but present the equations in a general form such that they can be applied to any geometrically exact beam formulation, independent of the choice of primary variables and interpolation schemes.
For clarity, we restrict the following derivation to the interaction of two beam elements only.
The extension to multiple interactions and the assembly into the global system follow standard finite element procedures.
Each beam element is assumed to possess
n_{i}
discrete degrees of freedom.
The mapping between the discrete variations of the beam element degrees of freedom and the generalized variations of the cross-section kinematics is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}\\
\delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{i}(\xi_{i},\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i})\delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i}.

(44)

Here,
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the cross-section parameter coordinate along the beam axis,
\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
collects the discrete degrees of freedom of a single beam element,
\delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
represents their variations and
\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n_{i}}
is the shape function matrix for the variations (which in general depends non-linearly on the current deformation state).
It is emphasized that the two beam elements can be of different beam formulation type and also different discretization scheme, i.e.,
n_{A}\neq n_{B}
.
Using (
44
), the variational form (
13
) of the coupling constraints can be expressed in terms of the discrete degrees of freedom as

\displaystyle\delta W^{\lambda}_{h}=\delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\underbrace{\left(\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{r}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{\theta}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}\right)}_{\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{1}}+\delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\underbrace{\left(\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{r}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{\theta}\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}\right)}_{\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{2}}+\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{g}}_{r}+\delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}\underline{\bm{g}}_{\theta}.

(45)

Here, the subscript
h
indicates that the variational form is evaluated in a discretized setting and
\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
denotes the nodal forces and moments corresponding to the coupling forces and moments acting on the cross-sections.
Moreover, the explicit dependence of the shape function matrices on the parameter coordinate and the discrete degrees of freedom has been omitted for brevity.
The mapping of the discrete increments to the increments of the cross-section kinematics is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\underline{\bm{r}}_{i}\\
\Delta\underline{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{\mathsf{N}}_{i}(\xi_{i},\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i})\Delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i},

(46)

where
\bm{\mathsf{N}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times n_{i}}
denotes the shape function matrix for the increments.
For the consistent linearization of (
45
), we need to account for the linearization of the shape functions as well as the linearization of the coupling forces, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i}\right)=\underbrace{\Delta\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i}}_{\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\Delta}_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i})\Delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i}}+\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i}.

(47)

Usually, the linearization of the shape functions
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}
is not evaluated explicitly and applied to
\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i}
.
Instead, the expression
\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\Delta}_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i}}
is computed, which maps the discrete increments
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{i}
to the stiffness contributions resulting from the linearization of the shape functions.
With this, we can state the linearization of (
45
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{1}\\
\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{2}\\
\underline{\bm{g}}_{r}\\
\underline{\bm{g}}_{\theta}\end{bmatrix}=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{H}}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{1}}_{r}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{1}}_{\theta}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{r}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}q_{2}}_{\theta}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{r}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{1}\lambda}_{\theta}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{1}}_{r}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{1}}_{\theta}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{2}}_{r}+\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}q_{2}}_{\theta}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{r}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{q_{2}\lambda}_{\theta}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{r}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{r}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{1}}_{\theta}&\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\lambda q_{2}}_{\theta}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{N}}_{1}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{N}}_{2}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{I}}\end{bmatrix}\right.\\
\left.+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\Delta}_{1}(\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{1})&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\Delta}_{2}(\bm{\mathsf{f}}_{2})&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}&\bm{\mathsf{0}}\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{1}\\
\Delta\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{2}\\
\Delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{r}\\
\Delta\underline{\bm{\lambda}}_{\theta}\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}

(48)

Remark 5
.

The parameter coordinates
\xi_{i}
defining the interacting cross-sections are obtained by a closest-point projection (CPP) between the two beam centerlines in the reference configuration.
This procedure is well-defined as long as the interaction angle between the beam centerlines is sufficiently large.
For the configurations considered in the present work, this condition is satisfied.
For very small interaction angles, a line-to-line coupling formulation would be required, which is beyond the scope of the present work.
For more details on the CPP in the context of point-to-point interactions, the reader is referred to
21
.

5
Examples

In this section, we present several examples to demonstrate the applicability of the proposed coupling formulation.
In
Section

5.1
, we investigate an L-shaped beam structure and show that the presented coupling formulation is able to exactly model the connection between the two beams.
In
Section

5.2
, we investigate the spatial convergence behavior of a coupled beam system using the proposed coupling approach.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that both the Lagrange multiplier and the penalty regularization formulations are objective.
In
Section

5.3
, we present a dynamic simulation of a double-helix structure, demonstrating that the proposed coupling formulation can be used in combination with Simo–Reissner and Kirchhoff–Love beams and with various beam interpolations and rotational parameterizations.
Finally, in
Section

5.4
, we investigate the buckling behavior of a wire-wound cylinder.

All numerical examples are set up using the open-source beam finite element pre-processor BeamMe
2
and are simulated with the open-source multi-physics research code 4C
1
.
The resulting nonlinear systems of equations are solved using a Newton–Raphson scheme.

The resulting convergence plot for the present coupling formulation with Lagrange multipliers is shown in

Simulations are performed for meshes with

The reference solution is obtained with a fine discretization of

Two-noded Simo–Reissner beam elements are employed for spatial discretization, cf.

For a pure beam problem, the expected convergence order for the beam positions is
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, with
h=2L/n_{e}
being the beam finite element length.
One can see that the convergence behavior differs for even and odd number of elements.
For meshes with an even number of elements, the obtained convergence order is the expected optimal order of
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
.
However, for meshes with an odd number of elements, the convergence order is reduced to
\mathcal{O}(h)
.
This behavior can be attributed to the fact that for meshes with an even number of elements, the coupling points are located at discrete nodes of the finite element mesh, while for meshes with an odd number of elements, the coupling points are located exactly in the middle of the central finite element of each beam.
In the derivation of the governing weak form of the employed Simo–Reissner beam formulation, discrete forces and moments only appear at inter-element boundaries
14
;
20
.
When the coupling point coincides with a node (even meshes), the discrete constraint forces and moments directly act at element boundaries (i.e., nodes), preserving the optimal convergence order.
When it lies inside an element (odd meshes), the discrete constraint forces and moments act in the middle of an element, thus degrading the convergence order.
It is important to note that this effect is not caused by the presented coupling formulation itself, but is an inherent property of geometrically exact beam formulations.
We can conclude that coupling points should, if possible, be chosen to coincide with finite element nodes, but even if this is not the case, the formulation still provides a converging solution.

6
Conclusion

In this work, a beam-to-beam point coupling formulation has been presented that enables the modeling of general rigid connections between beam cross-sections.
The proposed approach expresses the coupling constraints solely in terms of the cross-section kinematics of the geometrically exact beam formulation, i.e., centroid positions and orientations, which makes the formulation independent of the specific beam theory and discretization scheme.
As a consequence, beam elements with different interpolation schemes, primary variables, and rotational parameterizations can be coupled in a unified manner.
Constraint enforcement was formulated using the Lagrange multiplier method, resulting in a saddle-point system.
Additionally, a penalty regularization was presented that allows for the condensation of the Lagrange multipliers.
The formulation was analyzed with respect to fundamental properties such as symmetry, objectivity, and consistency with a stress-free reference configuration.
Furthermore, the discrete coupling terms were described in a formulation-independent manner, allowing straightforward integration into existing beam finite element frameworks.
Several numerical examples were presented to demonstrate the accuracy and robustness of the proposed coupling formulation, as well as its applicability to challenging and practically relevant interaction scenarios.

Future work may extend the formulation towards more general interaction scenarios, such as line-to-line beam coupling.
