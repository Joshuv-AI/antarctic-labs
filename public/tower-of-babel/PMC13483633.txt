Introduction

Most cases of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) occur when a thrombus forms secondarily on the basis of the rupture or erosion of a coronary atherosclerotic plaque, leading to the acute and persistent occlusion of a coronary artery and ultimately causing ischemic injury and necrosis of the myocardium. Revascularization is the core treatment method [1]. However, on the basis of conventional dual antiplatelet therapy, there are few case reports of more than 3 in-stent total occlusion (ISIO). The possible mechanism of repeated in-stent occlusion is a question worthy of consideration. The selection of treatment strategies for recurrent in-stent occlusion is more challenging than that for the initial percutaneous coronary intervention. We recorded the coronary images and medications of a patient with repeated ISIO in right coronary artery (RCA), and analyzed the possible causes and mechanisms to improve people’s understanding of this type of patient.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old female presented with paroxysmal chest tightness for 4 days, which worsened over 19 h, leading to hospitalization. Prior to admission, the patient had experienced persistent chest pain for 1 h over the preceding 19 h. She had a long history of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and had been on long-term analgesic therapy, including recent use of diclofenac sodium. At admission, her blood pressure was 160/70 mmHg, heart rate was 78 beats per minute, and scant moist rales were heard at the lung bases, with no cardiac murmurs detected. Deformities were noted in both hands. The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed STII, III, AVF elevation of 0.1–0.2 mV, with QIII, AVF (Fig. 1A). Serum troponin I rose to 2.40 ng/ml (range: 0–0.023 ng/ml), serum N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide increased to 1880 pg/ml (range: 0–125 pg/ml), C-reactive protein reached 12.98 mg/L (range: 0–6 mg/L), white blood cell count was 2.32 × 109/L (range: 3.5–9.5 × 109/L), and hemoglobin was 99 g/L (range: 115–150 g/L). Transthoracic echo revealed weakened inferior wall motion, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 49%. The preliminary diagnosis was acute STEMI with cardiac function Killip class II. The patient received a loading dose of aspirin and clopidogrel, followed by long-term oral dual antiplatelet therapy and statins. During hospitalization, the ECG showed dynamic changes (Fig. 1A-B).

Coronary angiography (CAG) performed 6 days later revealed: diffuse lesions in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) with 60% stenosis in the proximal segment (Fig. 2A), multiple plaques in the left circumflex artery (LCX) with 90% stenosis in the midsegment (Fig. 2B), and midsegment occlusion in the right coronary artery (RCA) (Fig. 2C). The patient refused to undergo coronary artery bypass grafting. Considering the RCA as the culprit vessel, three stents (Firebird2 2.75 × 33 mm, Firebird2 3.0 × 33 mm, and RDES II 3.5 × 36 mm drug-eluting rapamycin stents) were placed in series in the middle and distal segments (Fig. 2D), and elective intervention for the LCX at a later date was decided. After discharge, the patient was prescribed oral indobufen, clopidogrel bisulfate, statins, etc. In addition, during hospitalization, the maximum platelet aggregation rate of the patient was 29.4% (AA) and 32.8% (ADP) respectively.

The patient had no obvious discomfort after discharge. Two months later, the patient was scheduled for left circumflex artery (LCX) interventional therapy. CAG showed in-stent occlusion in the mid - right coronary artery (RCA) (Fig. 2E). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) examination revealed thrombus and suspected stent beam fracture at the proximal in-stent occlusion site (first turn of the RCA) (Supplementary Material 1). A Promus PREMIER 3.5 × 12 mm stent was implanted in the stent of the mid - RCA (Fig. 2F). IVUS indicated good stent apposition, without dissection or hematoma. Twenty - four hours after PCI, the patient received intravenous infusion of platelet glycoprotein Ⅱb/Ⅲa receptor antagonist (tirofiban) for enhanced anti - platelet therapy. During hospitalization, platelet function tests (arachidonic acid [AA], adenosine diphosphate [ADP]) showed maximum aggregation rate (MAR) of 29.4% and 32.8%, respectively (MAR < 35% indicates significant pharmacological effect). After discharge, the patient continued the aforementioned dual anti - platelet therapy. Additionally, all antibody tests for anti-streptolysin O, rheumatoid factor, anti-cyclopeptidin antibody, and antinuclear antibody spectrum were negative or within normal levels. Genetic testing for statins revealed a *1b/*1b genotype for SLCO1B1 and an E3/E3 genotype for ApoE, suggesting a high tolerated dose of statins and normal lipid-lowering efficacy.

Nine months later, the patient was admitted to another hospital for emergency CAG + RCA stent drug balloon dilation (the stent occlusion was located at the first turn of the RCA) (Fig. 2G-H) due to ‘chest tightness with syncope’.The patient was also treated with twice-daily 0.1 g indobufen and twice-daily 90 mg ticagrelor.

Nine and a half months later, the patient was scheduled for interventional therapy of LCX. Angiography showed recurrent stent-in-stent occlusion in the middle segment of the RCA (the initial site of occlusion remained at the first turn) (Fig. 2I), with the middle segment of the LCX still showing 90% stenosis (Fig. 2K). After recanalization of the RCA occluded segment, optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed extensive thrombus within the stent without evidence of stent trabecular fracture (Supplementary Material 2), with thrombus shadows visible at the second turn (Fig. 2J). A 3.0 × 23 mm Firebird2 stent was deployed to address the LCX stenosis (Fig. 2L). The patient was prescribed 100 mg aspirin enteric-coated tablets daily, 90 mg ticagrelor twice daily, and statins, along with 0.5 mg colchicine tablets daily. During the subsequent six-month follow-up, the patient experienced no significant symptoms or adverse cardiovascular events.

Discussion

The clinical manifestations of repeated ISIO are diverse, primarily including angina pectoris, acute heart failure, or arrhythmias. Some patients may be asymptomatic (especially when collateral circulation is well established). ECG may reveal pathological Q waves and/or ST-T changes. CAG can demonstrate the location, length, and collateral circulation status of coronary artery occlusion, as well as assess the condition of previously implanted stents (e.g., rupture, severe restenosis, etc.), serving as the ‘gold standard’ for diagnosing intrastent restenosis [1]. IVUS or OCT are crucial for evaluating recurrent occlusion, as they can precisely determine stent expansion and adhesion, detect the presence of severe calcified nodules or intrastent restenosis, assess thrombotic burden, and guide new interventional strategies [2–4]. In this case, CAG confirmed intrastent occlusion three times within 9.5 months post-PCI, with the shortest interval being merely two weeks. Both IVUS and OCT identified thrombi at the proximal origin of the intrastent occlusion. We hypothesized that the patient experienced thrombotic occlusion within the stent. Additionally, IVUS revealed plaque formation and stenosis at the distal end of the stent.

Potential influencing factors of repeated ISIO

Coronary artery disease itself

When the lesion is long and diffuse, the implanted stent is longer, and the stress change in the vascular wall is complex, requiring a prolonged time for endothelial healing [5]. In cases of severe calcification, inadequate balloon dilation and insufficient expansion of the implanted stent are prone to occur, leading to poor stent apposition, which is a core cause of late stent thrombosis and restenosis [6, 7]. In coronary artery openings or bifurcations, ‘incomplete stent coverage’ or ‘plaque displacement’ are more likely to occur [8–10].

Interventional procedures and technical factors

Inadequate stent expansion and poor apposition [7]; the stent fails to fully cover the proximal or distal margins of the lesion, resulting in a ‘marginal effect’ that leads to rapid progression in the uncovered lesion; or the presence of slow flow or no reflow during the procedure [11].

Insufficient antithrombotic therapy

Poor medication compliance in patients, or poor response to or resistance to antiplatelet therapy; additionally, patients with hypercoagulable states such as myelodysplastic syndrome with trisomy 8 are prone to thrombosis [12].

Other relevant factors

Poor management of risk factors such as smoking and dyslipidemia [13, 14]; impaired vascular endothelial repair and severe inflammatory responses in diabetic patients, which are independent risk factors for restenosis and reocclusion [15]; severe renal insufficiency, which affects contrast agent excretion and exacerbates vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [16]; additionally, allergic or hypersensitivity reactions are also risk factors [17, 18].

In this case, the coronary artery stenosis was extensive, involving the middle and distal segments of the RCA. During interventional therapy, three stents were serially placed. The highly mobile RCA subjected the serially placed long stent to significant mechanical stress, particularly at the initial segment of the serial stents, which coincided with the turning point of the RCA. This area was subjected to the highest stress, leading to persistent stimulation and damage to the vascular endothelium. Additionally, due to the patient’s long - standing rheumatoid arthritis, coronary artery endothelial damage might have already occurred. Consequently, the inflammatory injury to the coronary endothelium at the first turning point of the RCA stent was more severe, increasing the risk of thrombus formation. Furthermore, due to insufficient experience in IVUS diagnosis, we misdiagnosed stent fracture at the first turning point of the RCA (Supplementary Materials 1 and 2) and placed a second stent. This not only failed to prevent subsequent restenosis at this site but also further increased the risk of thrombus formation.

Treatment and prevention

Studies indicate that intensive antiplatelet therapy may be considered for asymptomatic patients with good collateral circulation, no viable myocardial supply, or extremely high interventional risks [1, 8]. When attempting revascularization, IVUS/OCT-guided precision interventional therapy is recommended [4, 19]. For severe calcification, plaque modification techniques such as rotational atherectomy, shockwave balloon, or laser ablation may be required to ensure adequate stent expansion and complete coverage of the lesion, with drug-eluting stents being the standard choice [1, 6, 8]. Coronary artery bypass grafting is an important option for extremely complex lesions (e.g., long diffuse restenosis, multi-vessel disease), failed PCI, or high-risk patients. Strengthening risk factor control, ensuring medication compliance, and maintaining aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitor use are essential. High-risk ischemic patients require strict clinical monitoring and regular follow-ups [20]. In this case, multiple stent placements were performed to treat the long and diffuse RCA occlusion, which led to thrombotic occlusion at the first turn of the RCA. The specific mechanisms have been analyzed previously. Therefore, for PCI of long or multiple RCA lesions, if plaque size and distribution are clearly visualized via IVUS to guide the avoidance of multiple stent placements, the risk of intrastent thrombosis can be reduced. Placing another stent within the stent failed to prevent in-stent thrombosis in this case. Therefore, minimizing stent placement at the turn of the RCA, particularly avoiding overlapping stents or secondary stent implantation, remains a critical challenge and a key consideration in clinical practice.

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the initiation, progression, and rupture of atherosclerotic plaques. Inflammatory cells and cytokines drive plaque formation, destabilization, and ultimately rupture and thrombosis, leading to myocardial infarction [21–23]. Colchicine, a classical anti-inflammatory agent, interferes with intracellular microtubule function by inhibiting microtubule polymerization. It blocks the maturation and release of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1β by effectively suppressing the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NACHT, leucine-rich repeat and pyrin domain protein 3), thereby mitigating vascular wall inflammation at its source [24, 25]. Additionally, colchicine inhibits smooth muscle cell proliferation, stabilizing plaques [26]. Furthermore, colchicine significantly increases anti-thrombotic secretory factors [27], exerting antithrombotic effects. Clinical trials have demonstrated that low-dose colchicine reduces the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with recent myocardial infarction [28], lowering the risk of major composite endpoints such as cardiovascular death, spontaneous myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization by 31% [24]. In this case, the patient received low-dose colchicine therapy following three instances of in-stent thrombotic occlusion. During a 6-month follow-up, the patient exhibited no significant symptoms or adverse cardiovascular events. Therefore, adding low-dose colchicine to dual antiplatelet therapy and statins may more effectively prevent intrastent thrombotic events.

Limitations in diagnosis, treatment and management

In the initial interventional treatment, if intravascular imaging examination could be applied and then drug-coated balloon treatment could be determined, it might be the optimal choice and might avoid recurrence of coronary artery occlusion. In addition, the exact adjunctive benefit after adding colchicine still needs long-term observation and clarification.

Conclusions

Repeated ISIO of the RCA is a complex clinical problem. Long stents with tandem configurations are more prone to thrombus formation at coronary turning point, and re-stenting at this site fails to prevent recurrent stent thromboembolism, which might be related to the pockets under expanded and mal apposed stent segments. For the patients with autoimmune diseases, it remains to be further clarified whether combining low-dose colchicine with dual antiplatelet therapy and statins may offer adjunctive benefit. Guided by intracavitary imaging examinations, interventional treatment for diffuse lesions of the RCA lesions should prioritize avoiding tandem stacking of stents and minimizing stent placement at the RCA turning points. This approach represents a therapeutic strategy worth considering.
