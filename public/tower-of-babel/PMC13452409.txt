Insights into the potential metabolic basis of flavor formation during fermentation of black truffle (Tuber indicum) extract: combining sensory evaluation with metabolomics
Authors: Yiye Luo, Lin Yuan, Ruohan Qi, Pingzhang Yao, Junzhang Wu, Shuo Zhang, Rongfei Ye, Guangxiang Jin, Lusi Ouyang, Zhiyong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve flavor profile of lower-grade Chinese black truffle, a method combining water extraction and fermentation was developed. Sensory evaluation, volatile and non-volatile metabolomics were employed to trace the dynamic changes of aroma, VOCs, and non-volatile metabolites during fermentation. The fermentation process shifted the sensory profile from earthy/leather-like characteristics toward more pleasant buttery, fruity, and fermentation-related notes. Day 6 represented the most fermentation stage observed under the experimental conditions, where the content of VOCs also reached the peak. Odor activity value (OAV) analysis identified 24 key aroma compounds, predominantly alcohols and esters, including phenethyl alcohol. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that flavor formation was primarily driven by alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, and the TCA cycle. These findings elucidate the potential metabolic basis underlying aroma development in fermented truffle products, providing a theoretical basis for producing high-value seasonings.

Introduction

Black truffles (Tuber indicum), a worldwide cuisine, is famous for its wild, complex, elegant flavor and its nutritional value which have been reported to contain a large amount of fat, pectin, food energy, total phenolic and flavonoids, showing significant in anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anti-tumor (Feng et al., 2019; Lee, Nam, Zahra, & Farooqi, 2020; Wu, Meenu, & Xu, 2021). However, lower-grade Chinese black truffles have been found to have a less complexity of aroma and a lower market value (Luo et al., 2025).

It is a common practice in the food industry to process low-grade raw materials into high-value-added products by adopting appropriate techniques. Nowadays, food processing methods have been greatly investigated. Taking edible fungi as an example, drying, freezing, salting, canning, and fermentation were seen as effective treatments to selectively preserve flavor and nutrients (Cao, Wu, Xia, Yi, & Li, 2024; Xue, Hao, Yu, & Kou, 2017). Among them, fermentation was often mentioned because of its stable quality, high efficiency, and considerable productivity in nutritional and dietary value, which contribute to the healing and health-promoting properties (Navarro-Simarro, Gómez-Gómez, Ahrazem, & Rubio-Moraga, 2024). Sivamaruthi et al. reviewed the nutritional profiles, microbial dynamics, and health impacts of fermented mushrooms, and the results indicated that fermentation induces several biochemical changes in edible fungi, influencing their nutrients, flavor, and texture (Sivamaruthi, Sisubalan, Kesika, Sureka, & Chaiyasut, 2024). To date, studies on fermentation process of truffle have been widely implemented. Cheng et al. investigated the effects of media and processes on the aromas of white truffle solid-state fermented products, and the result showed that four weeks of solid-state fermentation can better preserve the aroma (Cheng & Chen, 2024). Vahdatzadeh et al. explored whether strain selection could improve truffle mycelium flavor. The result indicated that aroma variability among strains is predominantly linked to amino acid catabolism through the Ehrlich pathway, and flavors derived from relavant metabolites have a strong influence on aroma characteristics confirmed by sensory analysis (Vahdatzadeh & Splivallo, 2018). Based on literature research, we found that fermentation methods utilized in processing truffles mainly include solid-phase and submerged fermentation, focusing on the antioxidant, biosafety, flavor, and pharmaceutical use of truffle fermented products, and some metabolic pathways during fermentation were considered important for the formation of characteristic aroma of truffle (Chih-Yuan & Su-Der, 2024; Tang, Liu, & Li, 2015; Tang, Zhu, Li, Mi, & Li, 2008). However, little study reported regards the usage of exogenous microorganisms as fermenting media to process truffles water extract, which have been widely applied in dairy, wine, fruit, and other food and beverage sphere, and the results revealed that this method is applicable to the production of high-value-added products with good sensory quality and rich nutrition, such as flavored beverages, heath foods, and premium seasonings (De Guidi, Legras, Galeote, & Sicard, 2023; Nemo & Bacha, 2021; Siddiqui et al., 2023). Among various aroma-enhancing oriented yeasts, non-saccharomyces yeast species have attracted increasing attention due to their ability to release aroma precursor substances through extracellular enzymes and generate esters, higher alcohols, and other volatile compounds, thereby enhancing the organoleptic quality (Chen, Li, Chen, & Li, 2023; Pires, Teixeira, Brányik, & Vicente, 2014). Pichia pastoris, a mature industrial yeast that possesses vigorous amino acid metabolic capacity, ester biosynthesis ability and extracellular enzyme activity, which can efficiently convert complex substrates into aroma-active metabolites. (Zhuang et al., 2015). These characteristics make it a promising candidate for the fermentation of black truffle water extract, as the extract is rich in amino acids, carbohydrates, and potential aroma precursor substances. (Chauhan, Vijay, Pandey, & Semwal, 2021). Nevertheless, the application of it in the fermentation of black truffle water extract and the related metabolic basis of aroma evolution have largely remained unexplored. Hence, this research plans to utilize a commonly used exogenous microbial strain-Pichia pastoris to develop a new liquid fermentation method for black truffle (Tuber indicum) water extract.

Microbial fermentation is a form of biological activity, and its rate, quality and safety are influenced by many factors, such as the speed of the shaker, the fermentation temperature, the inoculation amount of the strain, and the fermentation time, etc. (Lee et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2022). Among these factors, fermentation time have been recognized as a pivotal factor affecting the degree of fermentation, which ultimately determines the quality and performance of the fermented products (Chai, Teo, Tan, & Tham, 2022). Wang et al. investigated the effect of fermentation time on quality of Yunnan congou black tea, revealing that an intermediate time (3 h) was an optimal fermentation time (Wang et al., 2022). Accordingly, the present study aimed to compare aroma evolution at different fermentation stages.

The sensory quality is one of the most interesting and important factors for foods, which is responsible for the food characteristics, emotion, and the appetite, eventually affecting the consumer acceptance (Yin, Hewson, Linforth, Taylor, & Fisk, 2017). Behind the sensory quality, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were recognized as the main contributors (Spence, 2021). Nowadays, the characterization methods applied in VOCs detection have gained much progress, for example, headspace-solid phase microextraction-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) was deemed as the “gold standard” for VOCs characterization due to its time-saving, labor-saving, and highly efficient characters (Menis-Henrique, 2020).

Along with the increasing interest in sensory quality and VOCs behind the flavor, scientists and engineers in food fermentation also focused on the non-volatile metabolites, especially those key differential non-volatile metabolites that can reflect the potential formation route of the VOCs (Zhang et al., 2023). Normally, there is a recognized principle: microbial activities cause non-volatile substances to transform into small-molecule volatile components through glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, amino acid metabolism, and their side reactions, thereby generating various aroma components (Zhang et al., 2023). Thus, adopting an appropriate approach to track the non-volatile metabolites variation during fermentation is a key to uncover the metabolic basis underlying flavor formation of black truffle fermented products (TFPs). Untargeted non-volatile metabolomics, based on Ultra high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS), have been reported excelled in achieving comprehensive, unbiased profiling of diverse metabolites with high sensitivity, resolution, and throughput, enabling biomarker discovery and metabolic pathway analysis in biomedical, food science, and fermentation research (Mialon, Roig, Capodanno, & Cadiere, 2023). Tan et al. studied the dynamic changes in the non-volatile chemical constituents of black tea during fermentation processing by an untargeted metabolomics approach, and the results indicated that non-volatile metabolites varied significantly during fermentation (Tan et al., 2016). Herein, UHPLC-MS-based untargeted metabolomics was applied in this study to characterize the non-volatile metabolite variations to provide insights into the potential metabolic basis of flavor formation during fermentation.

Briefly, a Pichia pastoris-assisted fermentation method for black truffle water extract was studied for the first time. Sensory evaluation, HS-SPME-GC–MS and UHPLC-MS based metabolomics were implemented to uncover the dynamic changes of VOCs and non-volatile metabolites. Multivariate statistical analysis, such as principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted to perform dimensionality reduction. Key differential metabolites and the metabolic pathways were identified, offering a theoretical basis for future industrial process optimization.

Materials and methods

Materials and equipment

Materials

Fresh Chinese black truffles (Tuber indicum) of grade 3 were obtained from a local edible fungi market in Yonghe county, Yunnan province, China. Glucose and VB6 of analytical grade were purchased from Macklin Reagents Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). n-Alkanes (C5–C25) and 2-methyl-3-heptanone of chromatographically pure were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China).

Instruments and equipment

500 mL conical flask, headspace vial, beaker and other glassware (Sichuan Shuniu glassware Co., Ltd.); Headspace autosampler (GERSTEL GmbH &Co. KG, Germany) for HS-SPME were loaded in a GC–MS consisting of a 7890 A GC system and a 5977B MS (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA) with HP-5MS served as capillary column. Liquid-phase chromatography was carried out with an ACQUITY UHPLC HSS T3 (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA) and mass spectrometric detection of metabolites was performed on Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Methods

Pichia pastoris culture

Pichia pastoris was purchased from China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC). Malt extract agar (MEA) medium, consisting of peptone (4.0 g/L), yeast extract powder (3.0 g/L), malt extract (10 g/L), glucose (10 g/L), agar (10 g/L) and 1000 mL of distilled water with a final pH of 5.6 ± 0.2, was used as solid culture medium to conduct the subculture every month to ensure the viability of Pichia pastoris. In order to activate the P. pastoris prior to fermentation, the yeast was transferred from solid medium to liquid MEA medium (without agar), and cultured in a shaking incubator (200 rpm, 30 °C) until the culture reached an OD600 of approximately 1.0, which last approximately 2 days.

Black truffle water extract preparation

Fresh Chinese black truffle was chopped into particles (sized approximately 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3) and then was heated in boiling water bath mixed with ultrapure water (100 g/L) for 2 h, cooled to room temperature, filtered, and set aside.

Fermentation methods

Glucose (10 g/L) was added into the black truffle water extract as an external carbon source to provide an initial carbon and energy source, which was expected to further stimulate the synthesis of flavor compounds by Pichia pastoris. According to previous literature, vitamin B6 (5 mg/L) was added to act as a metabolic cofactor to support yeast physiological activity during fermentation (Rosenberg, Ischebeck, & Commichau, 2017). 200 mL of the above-mentioned mixture was transferred into a 500 mL conical flask, and the pH was adjusted to 4.5 ± 0.1 using citric acid. After being sterilized at 115 °C for 30 min, 2.0% (v/v) yeast inoculum (OD600 = 1.0) was inoculated into the flask. The fermentation conditions of black truffle water extract were set at 200 rpm and 30 °C in a temperature-controlled shaking incubator, and the fermentation was carried out for 8 days. The dynamic changes of volatile and non-volatile metabolites in supernatant were analyzed at different fermentation times (0d, 2d, 4d, 6d and 8d), and were named T-0, T-2, T-4, T-6 and T-8, respectively. The controlled samples (non-inoculated groups) were also set in same way to provide a reference, and the samples obtained at different fermentation stages (0d, 2d, 4d, 6d and 8d) were named as K-0, K-2, K-4, K-6 and K-8, respectively. Except for the inoculation of P. pastoris, all pretreatment procedures, including glucose addition, vitamin B6 supplementation, thermal sterilization, and incubation conditions, were in line with treatment groups. Both groups comprised of three independent fermentation flasks (biological replicates), which were inoculated, fermented, sampled, extracted, and analyzed independently.

Monitoring of the fermentation process

The pH, OD600 and residual glucose content were monitored to characterize the fermentation process in terms of the metabolic environment, cell growth and substrate utilization, respectively. All the samples to be tested were taken out from the −80 °C freezer, slowly thawed in a 4 °C refrigerator, and then vortexed before undergoing the following tests immediately. The pH was determined using a digital pH meter (PH50–4, Lichen scientific Co., Ltd., China) at room temperature. Before testing, the pH meter was calibrated with standard buffer solutions in pH of 4.00 and 7.00, respectively. Cell growth was monitored by determining the OD600, using a UV–visible spectrometer (LC-UV1800, Lichen scientific Co., Ltd., China) The residual glucose concentration was tested using the GOD-POD glucose kit (BC2500-50 T/48S, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the protocol. All analyses were performed using three independent biological replicates, and the results were obtained after conducting a one-way ANOVA, followed by a Duncan multiple comparison test (p < 0.05).

HS–SPME was adopted to extract the VOCs from black truffle fermentation samples. Briefly, 2.0 g of each fermentation sample (0d, 2d, 4d, 6d and 8d) was accurately weighed and transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial, and 10 μL of 2-methyl-3-heptanone (100 ppm, dissolved in methanol) was injected into each vial as an internal standard. GC–MS condition:

The GC–MS analysis of VOCs was performed using an Agilent GC–MS system consisting of a 7890 A GC system and a 5977B mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA). A HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm, 0.25 μm, HP Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) was applied to separate the volatiles. The oven temperature program was as follows: Initial temperature 45 °C (hold 2 min), ramp at 4 °C/min to 200 °C, then at 10 °C/min to 225 °C (hold 5 min); Injector temperature: 225 °C; The carrier gas: High-purity helium (99.999%) at 1 mL/min in splitless mode. Mass spectrometric conditions: Ionization: Electron impact (EI) at 70 eV; MS quadrupole temperature: 150 °C; Ion source temperature: 200 °C; Scan range: m/z 35–250. The retention indices (RI) of all detected VOCs were calculated by the linear interpolation calculations of retention times (RT) of n-alkanes (C5–C25). The NIST 2020 library was adopted to compare and identify the compounds detected by GC–MS. Finally, the concentrations of volatile compounds were quantified by the ratio of the peak area of the compound to the peak area of 2-methyl-3-heptanone. The analysis of the experimental samples was conducted triplicate.

Raw data were transformed into mzXML format using MSConvert implemented in ProteoWizard (v3.0.8789). The XCMS package (v3.12.0) was adopted to perform peak detection, retention time correction, and peak alignment with the centWave algorithm (parameters: ppm = 15, peakwidth = 5–30 s, bw = 2, mzwid = 0.015, and mzdiff = 0.01), for later analyses. Quality control (QC) samples were obtained by pooling equal aliquots from all samples and were analyzed throughout the analytical process to monitor the stability of instrument. Signal drift was corrected using a QC-based signal correction strategy. To ensure the reproducibility of the analysis, metabolites with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% in the quality control samples were excluded. Missing values were interpolated by taking half of the smallest positive value detected for each metabolite. The processed data were then subjected to statistical analysis. The metabolites were annotated hypothetically by comparing their accurate mass, adduct information, and MS/MS fragment spectra with the HMDB, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, KEGG databases, and the internal spectral library of Panomix, and matching was performed with a mass tolerance of 15 ppm.

Descriptive sensory evaluation

Descriptive sensory evaluation was conducted to investigate the aroma profile changes of the fermented truffle water extracts during different fermentation stages (K0–K8, T0–T8) (Lawless & Heymann, 2010; Loucks, Eggett, Dunn, Steele, & Jefferies, 2017). A total of 16 untrained panelists (aged 22–50 years, 8 males and 8 females) were recruited from the laboratory and surrounding campus. Prior to evaluation, the panelists were briefly familiarized with the sensory attributes and evaluation procedure.

Based on the initial aroma screening and the characteristic descriptors previously reported for black truffles and fermented products, five aroma characteristics were selected: buttery flavor, nutty flavor, fermented flavor, fruity flavor and leather flavor (Mustafa et al., 2020a; Tejedor-Calvo, García-Barreda, Sánchez, Sanz, & Marco, 2024). The samples were assigned with three-digit random codes and presented in a random order. The intensity of the aroma was rated on a nine-point scale (1 = undetectable, 9 = extremely strong). The average score was obtained after conducting a one-way ANOVA, followed by a Duncan multiple comparison test (p < 0.05).

Data analysis

Dunken-test and one-way ANOVA were employed using SPSS.27 (IBM, NY, USA). All multivariate statistical analyses were implemented using SIMCA 14.1 software (Sartorius, Co., Ltd., Gottingen). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Metware Cloud, an online platform for data analysis (https://cloud.metware.cn). All Figures presenting in the article were created using Origin 2022 (OriginLab corporation, Northampton, Massachusetts).

Results and discussion

Monitoring of the fermentation process

To characterize the fermentation process, cell growth, residual glucose concentration, and pH were monitored throughout fermentation (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Changes in cell growth (a), the pH (b), and residual glucose concentration (c) in different fermentation stages of treatment (T0–T8) and control group (uninoculated, K0–K8).Fig. 1

Changes in cell growth (a), the pH (b), and residual glucose concentration (c) in different fermentation stages of treatment (T0–T8) and control group (uninoculated, K0–K8).

As shown in Fig. 1a, the OD600 values of the treatment group (T0–T8) rose rapidly from day 0 and slightly decreased after day 6. In contrast, the control group (uninoculated, K0–K8) showed a stable change. This indicates active proliferation of P. pastoris. Meanwhile, during the fermentation process, the residual glucose concentration in the treatment group decreased steadily from 10.5 ± 0.28 g/L to 0.5 ± 0.06 g/L, while the control group remained almost unchanged (Fig. 1c). This suggests that the yeast has high efficiency in utilizing carbon sources. Additionally, the pH of the inoculated samples gradually decreased from 4.51 ± 0.03 to 4.28 ± 0.03, while no significant change was observed in the control group (Fig. 1b). The slight decrease in pH suggests that organic acid accumulation remained limited throughout the fermentation.

Overall, these results indicate that the physiological changes around the day 6 occur simultaneously with the depletion of the residual glucose, and provide a physiological context for the subsequent analyses of volatile compounds and metabolite profiles.

Sensory evaluation

In terms of aroma, descriptive sensory evaluation, which can provide detailed descriptions of flavor characteristics and intensity, was performed to characterize the aroma attributes of uninoculated samples (Fig. 2a), and inoculated samples (Fig. 2b) throughout fermentation.Fig. 2Radar plots of descriptive sensory evaluation showing the aroma attributes of the uninoculated samples (a), and inoculated samples (b).Fig. 2

Radar plots of descriptive sensory evaluation showing the aroma attributes of the uninoculated samples (a), and inoculated samples (b).

The results of this sensory evaluation showed that the uninoculated samples did not change greatly in five flavor notes, except for the leather flavor. Presumably, the uninoculated treatment and sterilization provided an environment where K0–K8 samples keep relatively stationary metabolic activity. The sensory attributes of the truffle aqueous extract underwent pronounced changes during fermentation (T0–T8), indicating a dynamic aroma evolution driven by yeast metabolism. Specifically, the buttery note showed a gradual increase from T0 to T8, with a sharp rise observed after T4, suggesting the accumulation of buttery-related compounds in the late fermentation stage. The nutty attribute exhibited a relatively high initial intensity, reached its maximum at T2, and then gradually declined at T8. This pattern implies that nutty-related compounds were rapidly produced during the early stage but were subsequently metabolized or masked by other dominant aroma compounds. In contrast, the fermentation note increased dramatically from T0 to a peak at T6, followed by a notable decrease at T8. This suggests that typical fermentation-derived volatiles (e.g., alcohols and acids) accumulated during the mid-stage and may have been further metabolized or volatilized at prolonged fermentation times. Similarly, the fruity attribute increased sharply from T0 to T4 and reached a maximum at T6, before slightly decreasing at T8. This trend is consistent with the biosynthesis of ester compounds, which are commonly enhanced during yeast-mediated fermentation and contribute significantly to desirable flavor characteristics. Notably, the leather note, a double-edged sword in edible fungi, shows a pleasant aroma at an appropriate content, while it transforms to pungent odor at high abundance (Strojnik, Grebenc, & Ogrinc, 2020). Conversely, similar to uninoculated samples (K0–K8), the leather note showed a continuous decline from T0 to T8, suggesting that undesirable or earthy-related compounds inherent to the raw material were gradually reduced or bio-transformed during fermentation.

Overall, the fermentation process transformed the sensory characteristics from the earthy/leather-like qualities to more pleasant flavors such as creaminess, fruity notes, and fermentation-related aromas. Especially on the 6d, the aroma characteristics were the most ideal under our experimental condition. The sensory score obtained on the 6d was the highest, which is in line with the physiological changes that occurred during the fermentation process, where the residual glucose was almost depleted and microbial growth was approaching the stationary phase. And the lower organoleptic quality of prolonged fermented product (T8) might also result from this change.

VOCs variation during inoculated fermentation

To visualize the trends of VOCs variation during fermentation, a chord diagram (Fig. 3(b)) and bar graph (Fig. 4(b)) were constructed. As shown in both figures, the types and content of volatiles significantly altered. Consistent with sensory study, the total content of VOCs rapidly increased, and reached the peak at 6d (T6), while a reduction was observed at 8d (T8). The accumulation of volatile compounds mainly occurred between the 4 and 6 days, which is concurrent with the transition of cell growth from exponential phase to stationary phase and the significant reduction of residual glucose. Alcohols were the dominant type of VOCs during fermentation, and esters also played an important role, which is consistent with the characteristic metabolic behavior Pichia pastoris. These two main volatile substances also reached peak concentrations on the 6d. This phenomenon may indicate that the 6d is the most favorable stage for fermentation under the test condition, as during this time, the Pichia pastoris can efficiently utilize the substrate to produce volatile components. These observations suggest that during the carbon metabolism reorganization process in the later stage of fermentation, the generation of aroma becomes more active. Screening of differential volatile metabolites

Based on OPLS-DA results, a total of 68 differential volatile metabolites (VIP ≥ 1) throughout the fermentation (T0–T8) were screened, and the detailed information was provided in Table S2. Unlike the overall detected VOCs, the total content of differential volatile metabolites reached the peak at 8d (Fig. 4(b)).

Furthermore, OAV analysis was conducted to measure the contribution of each volatile to the overall flavor of truffle extract fermentation products. VOCs with VIP ≥ 1.0 and OAV ≥ 1.0 were considered as key flavor compounds. The OAV analysis suggested that 24 key flavor compounds were identified, and the detailed information can be gathered in Table S3. Notably, these 24 key flavor compounds were grouped into 5 clusters according to their alteration trends using k-means clustering analysis (Fig. 4(c and d)).

Volatiles grouped into cluster 1 and 3 showed an overall decrease in content with fermentation time extended. These 9 VOCs included 1-octen-3-ol, phenylacetaldehyde, 5-nonanone, isoamyl valerianate, linalool, isovaleraldehyde, (+)-limonene, 3-methylanisole, and acetone. Among them, 1-octen-3-ol was deemed as characteristic compound of truffle and other edible fungi, with mushroom and earthy flavor (Luo et al., 2025). However, its content generally decreased due to degradation or volatilization, which also exhibit in this research.

Compared with cluster 1 and 3, in contrast, volatiles grouped into cluster 2, 4, and 5 generally increased in content, including isoamyl acetate, ethyl propionate, phenethyl alcohol, γ-dodecalactone, γ-decalactone, isoamyl propionate, (Z)-4-decenoic acid methyl ester, 3-methyl-1-butanol, naphthalene, 3-methyl-1-pentanol, 2-dodecanone, 2-nonanone, methylvalerate, 2-methylbutyl propionate, and p-cresol, in total of 15 key flavor compounds. Among those compounds, phenethyl alcohol was reported characteristic marker during fermentation via Pichia pastoris (Yuan et al., 2023), with identical rose, woody, and citrus aroma, increasing from 56.76 μg/ L in 0d to peak of 6222.59 μg/ L in 6d, suggesting active metabolic transformation by Pichia pastoris during fermentation. Moreover, 6 out of 15 key flavor compounds were esters, with flavor of green apple, banana and tropical fruit, representing another typical aroma profile generated by yeast fermentation (Garg et al., 2018).

In this section, 68 volatile differential metabolites were screened out. The variation condition of these compounds was also discussed and 24 key flavor compounds was believed to play an important role in truffle extract fermentation products. However, it should be noted that the OAV values are calculated based on a semi-quantitative concentration estimation using a single internal standard, rather than using a compound-specific calibration curve. Future studies that employ real standards for absolute quantification will help to further improve the accuracy of the OAV estimation.

Correlation analysis between sensory profile and flavor compounds

To elucidate the relationship between key flavor compounds (VIP ≥ 1 and OAV ≥ 1) and sensory attributes, a Pearson's correlation was performed. The results were visualized using a Pearson's correlation heatmap to uncover the associations between key aroma compounds and sensory descriptors (Fig. 4(e)). The correlation analysis indicated clear associations between sensory attributes and key volatile compounds, suggesting the evolution of aroma during fermentation was associated with specific groups of odor-active compounds.

As shown in Fig. 4(e), five flavor attributes including buttery, nutty, fermentation, fruity, and leather notes, showed significant correlations with several flavor compounds. For example, fermentation and fruity notes, showed positive correlations with isoamyl acetate and phenethyl alcohol. Isoamyl acetate, with tropical fruit aroma, was a typical esterification reaction product during fermentation (Garg et al., 2018), while the rose and litchi flavor compound, phenethyl alcohol have been reported generated through the Ehrlich pathway via Pichia pastoris-mediated fermentation (Wang et al., 2023). Isoamyl acetate and isovaleraldehyde were positively correlated with nutty note, and the latter has been reported to exhibit peach, cherry and almond aroma referred to literature reported (Garg et al., 2018). The leather note showed significant correlations with 1-octen-3-ol, phenylacetaldehyde, 5-nonanone, and isoamyl valerianate. Among them, 1-octen-3-ol and 5-nonanone were reported as characteristic flavor compounds, with mushroom, earthy, oakmoss, and leather aroma (Mustafa et al., 2020b). Last but not least, buttery flavor, was considered to be associated with γ-dodecalactone, γ-decalactone, (Z)-4-decenoic acid methyl ester, 3-methyl-1-butanol, naphthalene, 3-methyl-1-pentanol, 2-dodecanone, isoamyl propionate, p-cresol, methylvalerate, 2-methylbutyl propionate, and 2-nonanone.

Overall, the correlation analysis revealed significant associations between sensory attributes and key odor-active compounds, suggesting that the aroma evolution during fermentation was closely associated with specific odor-active compounds, and the coordinated metabolic pathways. The enhancement of fruity and fermentation notes was mainly associated with esters and alcohols, while buttery and leather attributes were linked to lactones and truffle-characteristic volatiles.

These findings clarify the chemical basis underlying sensory changes. However, the potential metabolic basis of volatile flavor components formation requires further experimental validation. Therefore, further investigation of non-volatile metabolites and pathway enrichment is essential to comprehensively elucidate the metabolic basis governing aroma formation.

In the yeast-mediated fermentation process, sugars serve as the primary carbon and energy source for driving yeast growth, metabolism and generating aroma precursor substances (Wang et al., 2023). Sugars can also be directly converted into key aroma compounds such as esters, alcohols, and acids through a series of biochemical reactions, for example, the Ehrlich pathway (Wang et al., 2023). Notably, α-d-glucose, a primary carbon source, decreased dramatically from T0 to T2, indicating rapid consumption by P. pastoris for energy production and biomass growth. This decline persisted through T4 and T6, suggesting sustained glucose utilization via glycolysis. Similarly, d-ribose levels dropped, likely due to its involvement in nucleotide synthesis and the pentose phosphate pathway (Mitsuishi, Motohashi, & Yamamoto, 2012), which provided precursors for aromatic compounds. In contrast, erythritol and arabitol showed initial increases. Erythritol rose from T0 to T2, possibly as a stress response or compatible solute accumulation under fermentation conditions. Arabitol peaked at T2 before declining, indicating potential production from glucose reduction via aldose reductases, common in yeasts under oxidative stress. Alcohols, amines, and terpenes

Many alcohols, amines, and terpenoids function either as aroma compounds or as precursors for volatile flavor formation. These compounds can be converted into volatile aroma compounds through yeast metabolic activities (glycosidic bond hydrolysis, amino acid conversion, etc.) (Kumar, Venkatraman, & Bruheim, 2021), thereby indirectly shaping the aroma profile of fermented products. In this study, 4 compounds significantly increased in concentration, while 8 compounds decreased. Tryptophol concentration rose rapidly, indicating active yeast metabolism producing this aromatic alcohol. Conversely, several nitrogen-containing compounds decreased substantially, for example, indoline, tryptamine, and indoleacrylate. These changes suggest P. pastoris actively consumed these amines as nitrogen sources for protein synthesis or other cellular processes.(3)Amino, acids, and derivatives

(6)Organic acids

Organic acids

Organic acids in yeast-mediated fermentation are not only key intermediate products of yeast metabolism (such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle), providing energy and precursor substances for cell growth, but also their own metabolic transformation directly regulates the acidity and buffering capacity of the fermentation system, and ultimately shapes the flavor profile of the product by participating in or influencing the synthesis of flavor substances such as esters (Chidi, Bauer, & Rossouw, 2018; Mendes Ferreira & Mendes-Faia, 2020). Among them, citric acid, L-malic acid, and succinic acid were believed to play critical roles in tricarboxylic acid cycle (Li et al., 2025). In this study, these three compounds varied significantly, characterized by the content first increased and then decreased, indicating metabolic transformation during fermentation. These changes are consistent with the metabolic transition observed at T6 in the OPLS-DA analysis. Besides, compounds like 2-Isopropylmalic acid and 2-hydroxyisovaleric acid were considered correlated with the leucine and valine metabolism, which can further generate 3-methylbutanol (with chocolate flavor) through the Ehrlich pathway (Hazelwood, Daran, Van Maris, Pronk, & Dickinson, 2008).

Overall, dynamic changes in differential non-volatile metabolites reflected substantial metabolic reprogramming throughout fermentation. Specifically, 18 compounds (α-d-glucose, d-ribose, erythritol, arabitol, D-sorbitol, tryptophol, indoline, tryptamine, indoleacrylate, L-phenylalanine, L-tyrosine, L-asparagine, uridine-5′-diphosphate, citric acid, L-malic acid, succinic acid, 2-isopropylmalic acid, and 2-hydroxyisovaleric acid) reported correlated with flavor formation during fermentation were also discussed. Nevertheless, metabolic pathways correlated with the non-volatile differential metabolites and the relationship of these 18 key metabolites with volatile key flavor compounds needs to be further investigated.

Potential fermentation association analysis

KEGG pathway analysis

Fermentation is a complex process involving metabolic transformation and flavor compounds generation. However, the potential metabolic association during fermentation cannot be suggested only by comparative analysis of volatile compounds and non-volatile metabolites. Hence, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed based on differential non-volatile metabolites.

Table S5 summarized pathway enrichment result, and a total of 53 metabolic pathways were identified during fermentation. Among the top 20 enriched pathways, six were significantly enriched (p-value ≤0.05) Fig. 7(a), that is, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, β-alanine metabolism, and citrate cycle (TCA cycle). These pathways have previously been reported to contribute to flavor formation during yeast fermentation (Wei et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). Furthermore, KEGG topology analysis was also implemented to screen for key metabolic pathways (Fig. 7(a)), and the results were in line with enrichment analysis, except that the TCA cycle was not retained. The enhancement of glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis, and the TCA cycle indicates that extensive metabolic reprogramming occurred during the fermentation process. These pathways collectively connect the central carbon metabolism with amino acid metabolism, possibly providing a metabolic basis for the accumulation of key aromatic active compounds.

In the enriched metabolic pathways, the metabolism of phenylalanine and the biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids occur simultaneously with the accumulation of phenylethanol, phenylacetaldehyde and phenylpropionate, suggesting that these processes may have a potential role in enhancing floral fragrance and honey-like aroma. The metabolism of pyruvate is related to the changes of lactic acid, ethanol and acetic acid, reflecting the activity of carbon metabolism during fermentation. While the enhancement of the biosynthesis of valine, leucine and isoleucine indicates an increase in the metabolic activity of branched-chain amino acids, which is often associated with the formation of aroma precursor substances in yeast fermentation. Concurrently, the significant changes in the intermediate products of the TCA cycle, as well as the enhancement of the metabolism of alanine, aspartate and glutamate, the biosynthesis of arginine, and the metabolism of β-alanine, indicate a coordinated metabolic reprogramming, which may promote the synthesis and accumulation of flavor-related metabolites. Combining fermentation kinetics and comprehensive metabolomics analysis, it was indicated that P. pastoris underwent a physiological transformation around the 6th day, characterized by reduced cell proliferation, almost depleted residual glucose, and significant metabolic reconfiguration. This physiological state coincided with the period when the aroma quality improvement was the most significant under the current experimental conditions.

It is important to note that these metabolic pathways were inferred through non-targeted metabolomics and KEGG enrichment analysis. Therefore, they only represent potential metabolic associations rather than direct precursor-product relationships or metabolic fluxes. To validate the proposed metabolic pathways, future studies that combine isotope tracing, enzyme activity detection, transcriptomics or proteomics are necessary. Nevertheless, the current results provide evidence based on metabolomics, indicating that in the P. pastoris fermentation process, the aroma evolution of the Chinese black truffle extract is closely related to the coordinated regulation of central carbon metabolism and amino acid metabolism.

Conclusion

In this study, a Chinese black truffle extract fermentation process was addressed for the first time, with the exogenous yeast Pichia pastoris significantly enhanced its aroma profile. The sensory evaluation indicates that under the current experimental conditions, the six-day fermented samples exhibited the most abundant and complex aroma characteristics during the studied fermentation stage, featuring a more pleasant buttery flavor, fruity aroma, and fermentation-related flavors. 24 key aroma compounds were considered important during fermentation, primarily alcohols and esters such as phenethyl alcohol and isoamyl acetate. KEGG metabolic pathway enrichment analysis further elucidated alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, and other 7 metabolic pathways as the main metabolic pathways affecting flavor compounds generation during fermentation.
