Unbounded Communication Power of a Qubit
Souradeep Sasmal, Som Kanjilal, Debarshi Das

Abstract
Quantum mechanics enables information-processing advantages even at the level of a single qubit. A paradigmatic example is the 2 → \to 1 random access code (RAC), where a qubit outperforms a classical bit in retrieving encoded information. In the standard form, however, this quantum advantage is restricted to a single receiver, since decoding measurements inevitably destroy the encoded information. Contrary to this, we address how long the information encoded in a single qubit remains accessible even after multiple decoding, each with a quantum advantage. Introducing preparation distinguishability as an operational resource associated with the sender, we show that its interplay with measurement incompatibility on the receiver’s side can mitigate measurement-induced disturbance, thereby enabling an arbitrarily long sequence of receivers to each retain a quantum advantage. Our results show that, even under repeated measurements, the information encoded in a qubit need not be entirely exhausted, revealing a stronger communication feature than previously recognised.

We address this question by relating the standard 2

Building on this framework, we analyse different decoding strategies. When both measurements are unsharp with the same unsharpness parameter, as considered in

4

In contrast, when one measurement is projective and the other unsharp, a reduced measurement incompatibility suffices to retain quantum advantage, even when the preparation distinguishabilities approach the classical bound. This leads to a situation in which such decoding measurements preserve the preparation distinguishabilities maximally. This observation underpins our main result: despite the unavoidable disturbance induced by each measurement, an arbitrarily long sequence of independent receivers can, in principle, extract information from a single qubit, each with a quantum advantage in the sequential RAC scenario. II.1
Preparation Distinguishability

In any operational theory, a
preparation

P\in\mathscr{P}
specifies a procedure for generating physical systems according to some ensemble description, while a
measurement

M\in\mathscr{M}
is a test applied to those systems, producing outcomes
m\in\mathcal{O}_{m}
with probability
p(m|P,M)
i.e.
p(m|P,M)\geq 0
for all
m
and
\sum_{m}p(m|P,M)=1

30
.

Definition 1
.

The preparation distinguishability between the two preparations
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
is the maximum total variation distance between their outcome distributions, optimised over all possible measurements

\Delta(P_{0},P_{1})=\max_{M}\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\absolutevalue{p(m|P_0,M)-p(m|P_1,M)}.

(1)

By definition,
0\leq\Delta(P_{0},P_{1})\leq 1
. If
\Delta(P_{0},P_{1})=0
, the preparations are operationally equivalent, since no measurement can distinguish between them
27
. If
\Delta(P_{0},P_{1})=1
, their outcome distributions have disjoint support, i.e. there exists a measurement that perfectly distinguishes them.

To understand its operational significance, consider a binary discrimination task in which one of
P_{0}
or
P_{1}
is chosen uniformly and given to Bob, who performs a measurement
M
with outcomes
m\in\quantity{0,1}
. Bob guesses
P_{0}
upon observing
m=0
, and
P_{1}
otherwise. His average success probability, optimised over all measurements, is

p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\max_{M}\sum_{m\in\{0,1\}}p(m|P_{m},M).

(2)

For any pair of preparations
P_{0},P_{1}
(see Appendix
A
),

p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\Delta(P_0,P_1)].

(3)

This operational equivalence between
p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})
and
\Delta(P_{0},P_{1})
will play a central role in what follows.

II.2
2
\to
1 RAC task as preparation discrimination tasks

After receiving the encoded preparation
P_{x}\in\{P_{x_{1}x_{2}}\}
from Alice via one classical-/quantum-bit communication, Bob performs a dichotomic measurement
M_{y}
, depending on his input
y\in\{1,2\}
, yielding an outcome
b\in\quantity{0,1}
. The task is successful if Bob correctly outputs the
y^{th}
-bit of Alice’s input, i.e. when
b=x_{y}
. The average success probability is
31

\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{y\in\{1,2\},x\in\{0,1\}^{2}}p(b=x_{y}|P_{x},M_{y}).

(4)

The key observation is that this RAC task can be decomposed into two distinct, although not independent, binary discrimination tasks. To make this precise, consider the first bit
x_{1}
. Define the marginal ensembles

P_{0}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity{P_{00},P_{01}};\ \ P_{1}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity{P_{10},P_{11}}.

(5)

Operationally,
P_{x_{1}}^{(1)}
corresponds to preparing either
P_{x_{1}0}
or
P_{x_{1}1}
with equal probability. Thus, when Bob receives the input
y=1
, his task is to infer whether the communicated preparation originated from the ensemble
P_{0}^{(1)}
or
P_{1}^{(1)}
. An analogous construction applies to the second bit
x_{2}
.

Proposition 1
.

In a 2
\to
1 RAC, the average success probability can be expressed in terms of the distinguishabilities of the marginal ensembles as

\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}{4},

(6)

where
\Delta_{y}:=\Delta(P_{0}^{(y)},P_{1}^{(y)})
. The bound is saturated when Bob performs the optimal measurements for the corresponding discrimination tasks.

Proof.

For each input
y\in\quantity{1,2}
, Bob’s task reduces to discriminating operationally between two marginal ensembles
P_{0}^{(y)}
and
P_{1}^{(y)}
. The optimal success probability for this binary discrimination problem is

p_{g}^{\rm opt}\quantity(P_0^{(y)},P_1^{(y)})=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(1+\Delta_y).

(7)

Averaging over the uniformly distributed inputs
y
yields the stated bound. Thus, the RAC task reduces to maximising
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}
, subject to the constraints imposed by the underlying physical theory. Further details are provided in Appendix
B
.
∎

Since both marginal ensembles originate from the same underlying set of preparations,
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
are not independent, but are jointly constrained by the underlying physical theory. In particular, within the preparation-noncontextual ontological models, the RAC success probability is bounded by
3/4

31
, which also coincides with the optimal average success probability when communication between Alice and Bob is restricted to a single classical bit
1
. Substituting this into Eq. (
6
) yields the corresponding constraint:
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\underset{\rm PNC/1cbit}{\leq}1
.
By contrast, we show that quantum theory allows larger jointly attainable values of
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
for suitably incompatible measurements. This leads to a violation of the above bound and, hence, to a quantum advantage in the RAC.

II.3
Quantum bound on preparation distinguishabilities

Here, Alice’s preparations are represented by density operators
\rho_{x}\in\mathscr{L}\quantity(\mathcal{H}_2)
, and Bob’s measurements are dichotomic qubit measurements
\quantity{E_{b|y}}
. The average success probability is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}^{(Q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\sum_{y\in\{1,2\}}\Tr[\rho_{x}E_{b=x_{y}|y}].

(8)

The marginal ensembles are related to
\rho_{x_{1}x_{2}}
as follows

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\rho_{00}+\rho_{01});\ \ \rho_{1}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\rho_{10}+\rho_{11});

(9)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\rho_{00}+\rho_{10});\ \ \rho_{1}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\rho_{01}+\rho_{11}).

The distinguishability between
\rho_{0}^{(y)}
and
\rho_{1}^{(y)}
is

\Delta_{y}:=\Delta(P_{0}^{(y)},P_{1}^{(y)})=\max_{\quantity{E_{b|y}}}\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}\absolutevalue{\Tr[E_{b|y}\qty(\rho_0^{(y)}-\rho_1^{(y)})]}.

(10)

For any pair of quantum states, the optimal measurement is the Helstrom measurement
33
. Thus, the preparation distinguishability becomes
33

\Delta_{y}=\Delta(P_{0}^{(y)},P_{1}^{(y)})=\frac{1}{2}\norm{\rho_0^{(y)}-\rho_1^{(y)}}_{1},

(11)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{1}=\Tr[\absolutevalue{\cdot}]=\Tr[\sqrt{(\cdot)^{\dagger}(\cdot)}]
denotes the trace norm.

Theorem 1
.

For any set of qubit preparations
\quantity{\rho_{x_1x_2}}
, the associated distinguishabilities satisfy

\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

(12)

Proof.

Let
S=\rho_{00}-\rho_{11}
and
T=\rho_{01}-\rho_{10}
. Then

\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{16}\!\left(\|S{+}T\|_{1}^{2}+\|S{-}T\|_{1}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{R}{16}\!\left(\|S{+}T\|_{2}^{2}+\|S{-}T\|_{2}^{2}\right),

where we used
\|X\|_{1}^{2}\leq\mathrm{rank}(X)\|X\|_{2}^{2}

33
, with
\norm{X}_{2}
being the Schatten-2 norm,
R=\max\{\mathrm{rank}(S\pm T)\}\leq 2
for qubit preparations. Applying the parallelogram identity for the Schatten-2 norm leads to (
12
). Further details are provided in Appendix
C
.

∎

Corollary 1
.

For any set of qubit preparations
\quantity{\rho_{x_1x_2}}
,

\max_{\rho_{x_{1}x_{2}}}\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}=\sqrt{2},\ \ \text{subject to }\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

(13)

Consequently, the optimal quantum success probability is

\quantity(\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}^{(Q)})_{\rm opt}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}).

(14)

Proof.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to
\quantity(\Delta_1,\Delta_2)
, and
(1,1)
gives
\quantity(\Delta_1+\Delta_2 )^{2}\leq 2\quantity(\Delta_1^2+\Delta_2^2 )
,
which implies Eq. (
13
). Eq. (

) is achieved by substituting (
13
) into

\quantity(\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}^{(Q)})_{\rm opt}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\max_{\rho_{x_{1}x_{2}}}\quantity(\Delta_1+\Delta_2),\ \text{subject to }\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

∎

Remark 1
.

The set of quantum-achievable distinguishability pairs satisfying
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
forms a quarter unit disc, while classical/preparation-noncontextual models are restricted to the simplex
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\leq 1
. The bound
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}

satisfying

is quantum realisable. On the other hand, for each value of
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\in[0,\sqrt{2}]
, there exists at least one pair of
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
that is quantum realisable (see Appendix
C

III
Trade-off between preparation distinguishability, measurement incompatibility, and success probability

Consider the case, where Bob performs dichotomic unbiased qubit POVMs (unsharp measurements) of the form
8

\mathcal{B}_{y}\equiv\quantity{ E_{b|y} = \frac{\openone+ (-1)^b \lambda_y B_y}{2} };\ \ \lambda_{y}\in[0,1],

(15)

where
B_{y}
is a Hermitian operator with eigenvalues
\pm 1
.

Theorem 2
.

For the 2
\to
1 RAC with preparations
\quantity{\rho_{x_1x_2}}
and dichotomic unbiased POVMs of the form in Eq. (
15
), the average success probability satisfies

\mathcal{P}^{(Q)}_{\rm avg}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\quantity(\lambda_1 \Delta_1+\lambda_2 \Delta_2),

(16)

where
\Delta_{y}
denotes the preparation distinguishability of the marginal ensembles defined in Eq. (
11
). Equality will be achieved when
B_{y}
is the Helstrom measurement corresponding to
\Delta_{y}
. Consequently, a quantum advantage is obtained whenever

\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\lambda_{2}\Delta_{2}>1,\ \ \text{subject to }\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

(17)

Proof.

It follows from (
8
) that

\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}^{(Q)}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{16}\Big\{\lambda_{1}\Tr[\quantity(S+T)B_{1}]+\lambda_{2}\Tr[\quantity(S-T)B_{2}]\Big\}.

(18)

Let
X_{y}:=(S-(-1)^{y}T)
. Maximising
\Tr[X_{y}B_{y}]
over observables
-\openone\leq B\leq\openone
yields
\max\Tr[X_{y}B_{y}]=\norm{X_y}_{1}=4\Delta_{y}
. Thus we obtain Eq. (
16
), and hence, the condition for quantum advantage is given by Eq. (
17
).
∎

Lemma 1
.

A quantum advantage
\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\lambda_{2}\Delta_{2}>1
is achievable if and only if Bob’s measurement are incompatible, i.e.
\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}>1
, provided
\quantity{B_1,B_2}=0
.

Proof.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\lambda_{2}\Delta_{2}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}}.

(19)

Since
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
, it follows that
\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\lambda_{2}\Delta_{2}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}
. Therefore, achieving a quantum advantage requires
\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}>1
. For
\quantity{B_1,B_2}=0
, this condition is equivalent to measurement incompatibility
11
;
18
, thereby establishing necessity.

To prove sufficiency, consider preparations satisfying
\Delta_{y}=\lambda_{y}/\sqrt{\lambda^{2}_{1}+\lambda^{2}_{2}}
. Then
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}=1
, and
\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\lambda_{2}\Delta_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}
. Hence, whenever
\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}>1
, a quantum advantage follows immediately.
∎

We now consider the symmetric scenario in which
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda^{(s)}
. The threshold value of the unsharpness parameter for quantum advantage is

\lambda^{(s)}>\frac{1}{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}\geq\lambda_{c}^{(s)}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{1}+\sqrt{1-\Delta_{1}^{2}}}.

(20)

The minimal threshold corresponds to
\max(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2})=\sqrt{2}
, subject to
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
, yielding
\min\lambda_{c}^{(s)}=1/\sqrt{2}
.

We next consider the asymmetric scenario,
\lambda_{1}=1
and
\lambda_{2}=\lambda^{(\rm as)}
. The condition for quantum advantage becomes

\lambda^{(\rm as)}>\frac{1-\Delta_{1}}{\Delta_{2}}\geq\lambda^{(\rm as)}_{c}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\Delta_{1}}{1+\Delta_{1}}}.

(21)

Here,
\lambda^{(\rm as)}_{c}
is a monotonically decreasing function of
\Delta_{1}
. Unlike the symmetric case, the threshold
\lambda^{(\rm as)}_{c}
is not minimised at
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{2}=1/\sqrt{2}
. Instead, in the limit
\Delta_{1}\to 1
(and hence
\Delta_{2}\to 0
), the threshold approaches zero,
\lambda^{(\rm as)}_{c}\to 0
. This shows that even when the preparation distinguishabilities approach the classical boundary,
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\to 1
, nearly compatible measurements can still yield a quantum advantage, thereby opening a new possibility for sequential RACs. The details are presented in Fig.
2
and Appendix
D
.

V
Outlook

We have shown that a single qubit can exhibit quantum advantage in a sequential RAC scenario comprising a single sender and an arbitrarily long sequence of independent receivers. This reveals a previously unexplored operational feature of a qubit: its ability to retain encoded information even after repetitive decoding attempts with quantum advantages, despite the inherently invasive nature of the decoding measurements. Our results, therefore, bring out a non-trivial information-disturbance trade-off in sequential measurement scenarios.

To establish this result, we have identified preparation distinguishability as one of the operational resources governing RAC performance. The notion introduced here is closely related to the concept of
p
-distinguishability introduced in the framework of bounded ontological distinctness
13
. Unlike that framework, however, our approach provides an operational characterisation within a communication task. This perspective also suggests a possible route towards a more complete resource-theoretic understanding of RACs, incorporating not only measurement incompatibility
24
;
25
;
12
, but also preparation distinguishability.

Recent studies have explored the recycling of spatial quantum correlations among sequential observers
10
. By contrast, the present work establishes unbounded sequential sharing in a fundamentally different setting, viz. a qubit-based prepare-measure communication task. In particular, our result goes beyond the phenomenon of unbounded sequential sharing of nonlocal quantum correlations

;
26
to a communication framework that is operationally distinct and relies on different underlying resources. More broadly, it opens up the possibility of one-to-many communication protocols not only across space, but also across time.

Several directions for future investigation naturally emerge from our results. In particular, it would be worthwhile to explore the role of preparation distinguishability in more general prepare-measure-based communication scenarios

;
15
;
20
;
28
;
6

k

(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=(\cos\omega,r\sin\omega)

\omega
and

are independent of
k

\omega\to 0^{+}

(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=(r^{\prime}(k)\cos\theta(k),r^{\prime}(k)\sin\theta(k))

Appendix A
Relating Guessing Probability and Preparation Distinguishability

Consider a binary discrimination task between the two preparations
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
chosen equal prior probabilities. Suppose, under a fixed measurement
M
with outcomes
m\in\quantity{0,1}
,
P_{0}
is guessed upon observing
m=0
, and
P_{1}
is guessed otherwise. Hence, with this fixed measurement, the average success probability is

p_{\rm g}(P_{0},P_{1}|M)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[p(0|P_0,M)+p(1|P_1,M)].

(31)

Optimising over all measurements, we get

\displaystyle p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\max_{M}\sum_{m\in\{0,1\}}p(m|P_{m},M)

(32)

Lemma 3
.

For any pair of preparations
P_{0},P_{1}
,

p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\Delta(P_0,P_1)].

(33)

Proof.

Rewriting Eq.(
32
), it follows that

\displaystyle p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\max_{M}\quantity{p(0|P_0,M)-p(0|P_1,M)}.

(34)

The term in brackets quantifies how strongly outcome ‘
0
’ of the optimal measurement favours the preparation
P_{0}
over
P_{1}
. The optimisation over all measurement strategies ensures that, for the optimal measurement
M*
, this bias is always non-negative., i.e.
p(0|P_{0},M*)>p(0|P_{1},M*)
. Consequently, Bob’s optimal strategy is to guess
P_{0}
whenever he observes outcome ‘
0
’, and to guess
P_{1}
otherwise. This choice guarantees that the difference term in Eq. (
34
) contributes positively to the success probability, so that the maximisation can be written as

p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\max_{M}\absolutevalue{p(0|P_0,M)-p(0|P_1,M)}.

(35)

However, this expression implicitly assumes that outcome ‘
0
’ is the one that favours
P_{0}
. In general, the outcome that provides the higher bias could be either ‘
0
’ or ‘
1
’, depending on the measurement and the preparation. For dichotomic measurements, the outcome probabilities are related by
p(1|P,M)=1-p(0|P,M)
. Therefore, the absolute difference for one outcome equals that for the other, and we can express it symmetrically as

\absolutevalue{p(0|P_0,M)-p(0|P_1,M)}=\absolutevalue{p(1|P_0,M)-p(1|P_1,M)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\absolutevalue{p(m|P_0,M)-p(m|P_1,M)}.

(36)

Substituting this into the previous expression gives

p_{\rm g}^{\rm opt}(P_{0},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\max_{M}\sum_{m}\absolutevalue{p(m|P_0,M)-p(m|P_1,M)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\Delta(P_0,P_1)],

(37)

which follows from the definition of
\Delta(P_{0},P_{1})
.
∎

Appendix B
Expressing the Average Success Probability of
2\to 1
RAC in terms of Preparation Distinguishabilities

In the
2\to 1
RAC, the marginal ensembles associated with the first bit
x_{1}
received by Alice is defined as

P_{0}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity{P_{00},P_{01}};\ \ P_{1}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity{P_{10},P_{11}}.

(38)

Operationally,
P_{x_{1}}^{(1)}
corresponds to preparing
P_{x_{1}0}
or
P_{x_{1}1}
with equal probability. Consequently, for any measurement
M_{1}
,

p\quantity(m|P_{x_1}^{(1)},M_1)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[p(m|P_{x_1 0},M_1)+p(m|P_{x_1 1},M_1)].

(39)

An analogous construction applies for the second bit
x_{2}
, for which we define

P_{0}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity{P_{00},P_{10}};\ \ P_{1}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity{P_{01}, P_{11}};

(40)

with the corresponding operational relation for any measurement
M_{2}
:

p\quantity(m|P_{x_2}^{(2)},M_2)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[p(m|P_{0x_2},M_2)+p(m|P_{1 x_2},M_2)].

(41)

For each input
y
received by Bob, Bob’s task of recovering
x_{y}
is operationally equivalent to a binary discrimination problem between the two ensembles
P_{0}^{(y)}
or
P_{1}^{(y)}
. His optimal success probability for this task is, therefore, given by the optimal guessing probability

p^{\rm opt}_{g}\quantity(P_0^{(y)},P_1^{(y)})=\frac{1+\Delta_{y}}{2};\ \ \Delta_{y}:=\Delta\quantity(P_0^{(y)},P_1^{(y)}),

(42)

where
\Delta_{y}
quantifies the preparation distinguishability of the marginal ensembles associated with the bit
x_{y}
.

In the RAC task, for a fixed value of
y
, Bob aims to correctly guess the bit
x_{y}
encoded in Alice’s preparation, by performing the measurement
M_{y}
with outcomes being denoted by
b
. Since Alice’s input
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
is uniformly distributed, each preparation
P_{x}
occurs with probability
\frac{1}{4}
. The corresponding success probability is therefore

p_{\rm succ}(x_{y})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x}p(b=x_{y}|P_{x},M_{y}).

(43)

We now show that this quantity coincides with the success probability of discriminating between the ensembles
P_{0}^{(y)}
and
P_{1}^{(y)}
using the measurement
M_{y}
. At first, consider the case
y=1
. From Eq. (
43
), we obtain

\displaystyle p_{\rm succ}(x_{y=1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}p\quantity(b=x_1 \mid P_{x_1x_2},M_1)

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x_{1}}\quantity[ p\qty(b=x_1 \mid P_{x_10},M_1) +p\qty(b=x_1 \mid P_{x_1 1},M_1)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\quantity[p\qty(b=0 \mid P_{00},M_1)+p\qty(b=0 \mid P_{01},M_1)+p\qty(b=1 \mid P_{10},M_1)+p\qty(b=1 \mid P_{11},M_1)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\frac{1}{2}\qty{p\qty(b=0 \mid P_{00},M_1)+p\qty(b=0 \mid P_{01},M_1)}+\frac{1}{2}\qty{p\qty(b=1 \mid P_{10},M_1)+p\qty(b=1 \mid P_{11},M_1)}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[p\qty(0 \mid P_{0}^{(1)},M_1)+p\qty(1 \mid P_{1}^{(1)},M_1)]\quad\text{[From Eq.~(\ref{ensdesopx10})]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}p\quantity(m \mid P_m^{(1)},M_1)

\displaystyle=p_{\rm g}\quantity(P_0^{(1)},P_1^{(1)}|M_1).\quad\text{[From Eq.~(\ref{fixmgdistas})]}

Thus, it follows from Eqs. (
42
) and (
44
) that

p_{\rm succ}(x_{y=1})=p_{\rm g}\quantity(P_0^{(1)},P_1^{(1)}|M_1)\leq\frac{1}{2}\quantity(1+\Delta_1).

(45)

with equality when
M_{1}=M_{1}^{*}
is chosen for optimally discriminating between
P_{0}^{(1)}
and
P_{1}^{(1)}
. An analogous argument applies for
y=2
, yielding

p_{\rm succ}(x_{y=2})=p_{\rm g}\quantity(P^{(2)}_{0},P^{(2)}_1|M_2)\leq\frac{1}{2}\quantity(1+\Delta_2).

(46)

Averaging over both inputs
y\in\quantity{1,2}
, from Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
), the
average
RAC success probability becomes

\mathcal{P}_{\rm avg}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[p_{\rm succ}(x_{y=1})+p_{\rm succ}(x_{y=2})]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}{4},

(47)

with the bound being tight whenever Bob implements the optimal measurement
(M_{y}^{*})
for each marginal ensemble description.

Appendix C
Derivation of
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
for Qubit Preparations.

We now derive an quantum upper bound on the quantities
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
. Define the Hermitian operators

S:=\rho_{00}-\rho_{11};\quad T:=\rho_{01}-\rho_{10}.

(48)

From the definition of the marginal ensembles, the preparation distinguishabilities are given by

\begin{aligned} \Delta_{1}&:=\frac{1}{2}\norm{\rho_{0}^{(1)}-\rho_{1}^{(1)}}_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\norm{S+T}_{1},\\
\Delta_{2}&:=\frac{1}{2}\norm{\rho_{0}^{(2)}-\rho_{1}^{(2)}}_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\norm{S-T}_{1}.\end{aligned}\implies\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{16}\quantity(\norm{S+T}^2_1+\norm{S-T}^2_1).

(49)

The trace norm appearing above generally requires diagonalising the operator
\rho_{0}^{(y)}-\rho_{1}^{(y)}
, which may become computationally demanding. It is, therefore, useful to relate it to the Schatten-2 norm
\norm{\cdot}_{2}
, which is also related to the Hilbert-Schmid (HS) distance
D_{\rm HS}(\cdot)
as follows

\norm{\rho_0-\rho_1}^{2}_{2}=D_{\rm HS}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1}):=\Tr[(\rho_{0}-\rho_{1})^{2}].

(50)

Although the HS distance does not possess the same direct operational interpretation as the trace distance
23
, it admits a simpler algebraic form. The Schatten norm inequalities imply
33

\norm{X}_{2}\leq\norm{X}_{1}\leq\sqrt{\rank(X)}\norm{X}_{2},

(51)

where the first inequality follows from the monotonicity of the Schatten norms and the second one follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality
14
. From Eqs.(
49
) and (
51
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{16}(R_{+}\norm{S+T}^{2}_{2}+R_{-}\norm{S-T}^{2}_{2})\leq\frac{R}{16}(\norm{S+T}^{2}_{2}+\norm{S-T}^{2}_{2}),

(52)

where
R_{\pm}=\rank(S \pm T)
and
R:=\max(R_{+},R_{-})
. Since
S\pm T
are operators on two-dimensional Hilbert spaces,
R\leq 2
. Using the parallelogram identity,

\norm{S+T}^{2}_{2}+\norm{S-T}^{2}_{2}=2\quantity(\norm{S}^2_2+\norm{T}^2_2)\implies\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{R}{8}\quantity(\norm{S}^2_2+\norm{T}^2_2).

(53)

Figure 3
:
Comparison of constraints on preparation distinguishabilities
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})

\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}\leq 1
, whereas the set of quantum-achievable distinguishability pairs forms a quarter unit disc
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
, with the optimal quantum point at
\quantity(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
.

Since
S
and
T
are Hermitian and are differences of density operators, we have

\displaystyle\norm{S}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr[(\rho_{00}-\rho_{11})^{2}]=\Tr[\rho_{00}^{2}]+\Tr[\rho_{11}^{2}]-2\Tr[\rho_{00}\rho_{11}],

(54)

\displaystyle\norm{T}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr[(\rho_{01}-\rho_{10})^{2}]=\Tr[\rho_{01}^{2}]+\Tr[\rho_{10}^{2}]-2\Tr[\rho_{01}\rho_{10}].

For any density operator
\rho
,
\Tr[\rho^{2}]\leq 1
, with equality only for pure states. Also, for any two density operators
\rho,\sigma
, the term
\Tr[\rho\sigma]\geq 0
. Therefore,

\norm{S}^{2}_{2}\leq\Tr[\rho_{00}^{2}]+\Tr[\rho_{11}^{2}]\leq 2,\ \ \norm{T}^{2}_{2}\leq\Tr[\rho_{01}^{2}]+\Tr[\rho_{10}^{2}]\leq 2.

(55)

Hence,

\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{R}{2}\leq 1.

(56)

In the above, for the equality to be attained,
\rho_{00}
and
\rho_{11}
must be mutually orthogonal pure states, and
\rho_{01}
and
\rho_{10}
must likewise be mutually orthogonal pure states.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it can be shown that for any set of qubit preparations
\quantity{\rho_{x_1x_2}}
,

\max_{\rho_{x_{1}x_{2}}}\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}=\sqrt{2},\ \ \text{subject to }\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

(57)

The constraint
\Delta_{1}^{2}+\Delta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
admits a simple geometric interpretation in the
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
plane as shown in Fig.
3
. The quantum maximum of
\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}
is attained for
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{2}=1/\sqrt{2}
. Note that
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{2}=1/\sqrt{2}
implies that Alice’s four pure qubit states lie on the vertices of a square in the equatorial plane of the Bloch sphere.

Evolution of Preparation Distinguishabilities under Sequential Measurements

We analyse how preparation distinguishabilities evolve under the measurement channel applied sequentially by multiple receivers. Let us denote by
\Phi^{(k)}
the non-selective measurement channel corresponding to the
k
-th receiver, Bob
(k)

(k-1)

and

(k)

(k)

At first, we present the formal proof of the Theorem 3 mentioned in the main paper.

Proof.

We begin by recalling the evolution of the preparation distinguishabilities. Iterating the recursion relations given in Eq. (

\Delta_{1}^{(k)}=\frac{M_{k}}{2^{k-1}}\Delta_{1}^{(1)},\quad\Delta_{2}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\Delta_{2}^{(1)},\quad\quad M_{k\geq 2}:=\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\quantity(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda_{l}^{2}}),\ M_{1}:=1.

Without loss of generality, we parametrise the initial distinguishabilities as

\forall r_{1},r_{2}\in[0,1],\omega\in\quantity(0, \frac{\pi}{2}),\ \ \Delta^{(1)}_{1}:=r_{1}\cos\omega,\quad\Delta^{(1)}_{2}:=r_{2}\sin\omega,\ \ \text{with }\quantity(\Delta^{(1)}_1)^{2}+\quantity(\Delta^{(1)}_2)^{2}\leq\max(r_{1}^{2},r_{2}^{2})\leq 1.

(78)

Using the condition for quantum advantage,
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{\rm avg}>\frac{3}{4}
, we now establish the existence of a sequence
\quantity{\lambda_k \in(0,1)}

r_{1}=1

(79)

Introducing a parameter
\epsilon>0

\forall r\in(0,1],\ \ \lambda_{k}(\omega):=\begin{cases}(1+\epsilon)\frac{1}{r}\tan\frac{\omega}{2}&\text{for }k=1\\
(1+\epsilon)\frac{\quantity(2^{k-1} -\cos\omega M_k)}{r\sin\omega}&\text{for }k\geq 2,\end{cases},

\quantity{\lambda_k}_{k=1}^{N}\subset(0,1)

80

The feasibility of the sequence
\quantity{\lambda_k}
is established as follows. First, we show that whenever the sequence satisfies
\lambda_{k}<1\ \forall k

\omega

\omega

\lambda_{k}

Using Eq. (
80

In the following we show that there exists a sub-interval of assumed
