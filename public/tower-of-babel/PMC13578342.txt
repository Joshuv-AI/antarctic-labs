Plasma-based flow control for performance enhancement of vertical-axis wind turbines
Authors: Mohammad Javad Zarei, Somayeh Davoodabadi Farahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A two-dimensional numerical investigation was conducted to evaluate the effects of plasma actuators (PLA) on the aerodynamic performance of a Darrieus vertical-axis wind turbine (VAWT). In the present study, PLAs mounted on turbine blades are employed to control flow separation. First, the flow field around the turbine and the induced field produced by the PLA are numerically simulated and validated. The influences of applied voltage, electrode length, and position on the power curve and flow features are explored. The performance of the PLA under optimal conditions is then evaluated for rotors with two to five blades, and the corresponding power curves are obtained. The results indicate that for a three-bladed rotor, the optimal use of the PLA increases the average power coefficient from 0.241 to 0.447. Moreover, due to the significant effectiveness of the PLA at low tip-speed ratios, the operational stability of the turbine is improved, and the difference between the maximum and minimum power coefficients of the three-bladed rotor over the tip-speed ratio (TSR) range of 1.64 to 3.3 is reduced from 0.24 to 0.09.

Introduction

As a means of generating electricity based on wind, wind turbines play a vital role in reducing dependence on fossil fuels and climate change1–4. The International Renewable Energy Agency has announced that wind energy accounts for 25% of the total global renewable energy capacity in 20245. Wind turbines are mainly developed in two configurations: horizontal-axis (HAWTs) and VAWTs, each with specific advantages and applications6.

There are several approaches to preventing flow separation on wind turbine blades, each with advantages and disadvantages. The known flow control methods, such as vortex generators and mechanical flaps, have inherent disadvantages such as structural complexity, increased rotor weight, reduced mechanical durability, and increased acoustic noise levels7–9, which highlights the importance of research into new methods. At the low‑to‑moderate chord Reynolds numbers experienced by the present VAWT (35 000–221 000), surface roughness is known to significantly influence boundary‑layer transition and stall10; however, in contrast to permanent passive roughness devices, PLAs provide an on‑demand, reversible flow control capability without altering the blade geometry.

One of the new flow control methods is to use PLAs on wind turbine blades. A PLA consists of two metallic electrodes separated by a dielectric layer. When an AC voltage is applied to the exposed electrode, the surrounding gas is ionized, resulting in controlled flow within the fluid boundary layer. This mechanism can be used to control flow separation and improve flow stability11. Although plasma-based flow control is newer compared to other methods, its advantages—such as low cost, high efficiency, low power consumption, and the lack of required blade geometry modifications—have sparked significant research interest recently12.

Recent studies have shown that PLAs are an effective and innovative technology for optimizing HAWT performance. Cuny et al.13 conducted experiments that challenged conventional approaches to active stall control and demonstrated the flexibility and effectiveness of PLAs for active flow control. Matsuda et al.14 demonstrated that, under constant free-stream velocity, the installation of PLAs on a MW-class HAWT could increase the turbine’s rotational speed and increase the average power output by up to 4.9%. Furthermore, studies show that PLAs can increase turbine torque and power output15; however, the physical and geometric parameters of these devices have a significant impact on the magnitude of power enhancement, making operating condition optimization critical16–18.

VAWTs have advantages such as omnidirectional operation and lower noise levels; however, due to dynamic stall and torque ripple, they are generally less efficient than HAWTs, emphasizing the importance of effective flow control strategies.

Over the last decade, PLAs have emerged as a promising active flow control technology for improving the aerodynamic performance of VAWTs. Greenblatt and colleagues conducted preliminary experimental studies on the use of PLAs in VAWTs between 2012 and 201519–21. These studies used wind tunnel testing and particle image velocimetry (PIV) to assess the effect of active flow separation control on turbine performance.

Initial research19,21 examined the feasibility of using PLAs to control dynamic stall and assess their impact on the power output of VAWTs, confirming plasma actuation’s positive effects on turbine performance. Ben-Harav and Greenblatt22 expanded on these findings by conducting parametric studies to determine the optimal azimuthal angles for plasma excitation initiation and termination. The results showed that activating the PLA at appropriate azimuth angles significantly increased output power while decreasing PLA power consumption.

Greenblatt et al.23 presented an innovative PLA configuration with actuators installed on both the inner and outer surfaces of the blades. They developed a more precise method for applying excitation pulses by conducting experimental studies using open-loop, feedforward, and combined feedforward-feedback excitation control strategies, resulting in a 7% increase in net power. Although Greenblatt et al. explored the influence of activation azimuth angles in several experimental studies, the significance of this parameter has prompted numerous numerical investigations24,25, which have identified the optimal activation positions for the rotors under consideration.

Ben‐Harav and Greenblatt26 investigated the placement of PLA electrodes in three different configurations: inboard, outboard, and double-sided. Two-dimensional simulations revealed that the inboard and double-sided arrangements were the optimal. Experiments also show that the inboard configuration consistently produces better results than the outboard configuration.

Previous research has investigated parameters such as activation azimuth angles, electrode placement (inboard vs. outboard), and actuator control mechanisms with the goal of increasing the power coefficient. The current study takes a more comprehensive approach, examining broader categories of parameters that influence overall system performance. Consequently, the effects of electrode length, installation position on the blade, and applied voltage are investigated for a three-bladed H-type Darrieus rotor. After analyzing the effect of each variable on the power coefficient, the optimal values are determined. Following that, simulations for rotors with two to five blades are run using the optimized parameters, and the effect of PLA deployment in each configuration is evaluated. Finally, this study provides a thorough understanding of how PLAs influence the power output of VAWTs under various conditions.

Numerical method

In the present study, the effect of employing PLAs on the power output of an H-type Darrieus VAWT is investigated. Since the phenomenon under consideration involves two distinct physical processes (namely, the aerodynamic flow around the blades and the plasma field along with the induced flow generated by it), the numerical solution procedure is divided into two separate parts. Each part is simulated independently and validated based on experimental data. Subsequently, the two physical domains are coupled, and the performance of the rotor equipped with PLAs is numerically analyzed. Accordingly, this section presents the governing equations and physical models associated with three primary stages: (1) aerodynamic simulation of the turbine, (2) simulation of the plasma field and induced flow, and (3) the coupled solution for the turbine equipped with PLAs.

Aerodynamic simulation

In this section, a VAWT of the Darrieus type is simulated using ANSYS Fluent. For the aerodynamic simulation of this problem, continuity, momentum, and turbulence equations are used. The numerical simulation employs the unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (URANS) methodology27–30. In contrast to steady RANS, URANS retains the temporal derivatives in the governing equations, which is essential for capturing the periodic unsteadiness arising from blade rotation, dynamic stall, and the cyclic variation of the angle of attack in a VAWT. The RANS framework (with time‑averaged closure) reduces computational time compared to scale‑resolving simulations, while the unsteady formulation provides adequate accuracy for the dependable estimation of the power coefficient of VAWTs across a range of TSRs18.

Within this approach, the instantaneous variables in the Navier–Stokes equations31 are decomposed into mean and fluctuating components so that the effects of turbulence can be incorporated statistically into the governing equations. Choosing a suitable turbulence model within the RANS framework is a critical challenge in simulating turbulent flows and can profoundly affect the reliability of numerical outcomes. Due to the significance of this issue, various studies have investigated and contrasted the accuracy of different turbulence models in the numerical analysis of Darrieus wind turbines32,33.

Lanzafame et al.32 compared the accuracy of the SST k-ω and Transition SST turbulence models, concluding that the Transition SST model, when combined with local correlation variables, provides better simulation accuracy. Rezaeiha et al.33 conducted two-dimensional simulations to compare the accuracy of several turbulence models, which include Spalart–Allmaras, realizable k-ε, RNG k-ε, SST k-ω, and Transition SST. The study found that SST-based models have the best agreement with experimental data, and the k-ω SST model is the most accurate one for wind turbine flow simulation. This study employs the SST k-ω turbulence model to simulate the turbulent flow around the wind turbine34–36. The model’s governing equations and theoretical foundations are derived from Menter’s research37,38.

After defining the governing equations, selecting an appropriate discretization method plays a decisive role in the accuracy of numerical results. The SIMPLE algorithm was used to couple the pressure and velocity fields in the RANS equations. Gradients were computed using the Green–Gauss cell-based method. A second-order upwind scheme was applied to the convection terms and turbulence equations, while the pressure term was discretized using a second-order scheme, and transient formulation was discretized using a second-order implicit method.

The appropriate time step for the two-dimensional numerical solution of a Darrieus VAWT was determined based on the findings reported by Ebrahimi et al.15. A review of previous studies indicates that three-dimensional simulations, including the work of Ghasemian et al.46, in which the Large Eddy Simulation (LES) approach was employed, exhibit an error of approximately 12%. This level of discrepancy is comparable to the results obtained in the present study as well as those reported by Mohamed et al.45 across many regions of the computational domain. These findings suggest that, in Darrieus VAWTs, the influence of three-dimensional effects—such as blade tip vortex shedding, spanwise flow transport, and three-dimensional blade–blade interactions—along the third dimension is relatively negligible. Consequently, in the majority of previous investigations whose results are presented in validation plots, simulations of this class of turbines have been predominantly conducted using two-dimensional models.

Plasma modeling

PLA models fall into two categories: simplified (phenomenological) models and physics-based models, each with its advantages and limitations. Simplified models reduce complexity through phenomenological approximations. These models consist of electrostatic models featuring Gaussian charge distributions47, equivalent circuit models48, linearized body-force models49, and potential-flow models50. Owing to their high computational efficiency and ease of implementation, these approaches are particularly suitable for engineering applications that require rapid simulations.

Physics-based models, such as kinetic approaches and full solutions to the Boltzmann equation, provide high theoretical accuracy but are inefficient for conventional CFD simulation. This inefficiency predominantly arises from their multidimensional and time-dependent characteristics, coupled with the extremely short time scales related to electron release and particle collisions. Therefore, despite their powerful physical foundation, the excessive computational cost makes their application in CFD simulations impossible.

In the present work, the model proposed by Suzen et al.47 is employed to simulate the plasma field. This model assumes a quasi-steady system with sufficient time for charge redistribution; therefore, the magnetic field and the time derivatives of both magnetic and electric induction are considered negligible. Given that charge rearrangement in the plasma field occurs on the nanosecond scale—approximately ten million times faster than the fluid’s response to the PLA—this assumption is regarded as valid for modeling the plasma-induced flow51. Design of experiments

Since the primary objective of this research is the geometric and parametric optimization of PLAs in H-type Darrieus turbines, conducting a systematic set of simulations is essential. The geometric parameters selected for analysis include the electrode length and installation position on the blade.

In the first stage, to enable a comprehensive comparison among different configurations, both the electrode lengths and installation coordinates were non-dimensionalized with respect to the airfoil chord. To assess the impact of electrode length on power output, three nondimensional values—0.1, 0.2, and 0.3—were considered. In all cases, the electrodes had identical lengths and were arranged continuously along the blade surface adjacent to one another. Moreover, according to the present design, electrodes were installed on both the upper and lower surfaces of the blade.

To analyze the effect of electrode placement on improving turbine aerodynamic performance, the electrode positions were shifted along the blade surface. The nondimensional coordinates of the positive electrode were defined at 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 relative to the leading edge. Because electrode length was also treated as a variable, it was not possible to examine all positions for larger electrodes. Accordingly, electrodes with a nondimensional length of 0.1 were evaluated at all coordinates; a length of 0.2 was assessed at positions 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5; and a length of 0.3 was examined only at coordinates 0.1 and 0.3. A graphic of the PLA electrode arrangement is shown in Fig. 11.Fig. 11Dimensional and coordinates schematic of the PLA electrodes in the optimization case.

Dimensional and coordinates schematic of the PLA electrodes in the optimization case.

Another important parameter evaluated in the simulations is the voltage applied to the PLAs. In this study, peak-to-peak square-wave voltages of 8, 10, 12, and 14 kV were applied. This voltage range was selected both because induced flow becomes ineffective at low voltages12 and to avoid issues such as nitrogen oxide production53, physical erosion of the electrodes and dielectric material54, and increased power consumption at higher voltages.

Given the variations in the external electrode voltage, it is necessary to evaluate the maximum charge density on the internal electrode. Analysis by Bushnell55 and experimental observations by Kotsonis et al.56 indicate that the maximum charge density varies linearly with the applied voltage. Accordingly, the maximum charge density reported in the Suzen et al. model47—0.0008 C/m3 at 5 kV—was used as the reference value, and the maximum charge density was assumed to vary linearly with the applied voltage with a zero intercept. The charge density distribution over the internal electrode surface was also defined according to the standards presented by Suzen et al.47, with the ratio of the Gaussian parameter to electrode length set to 0.3 and the Debye length fixed at 1 mm. Furthermore, due to the presence of body forces and plasma actuation effects in the vicinity of the plasma region, a refined mesh was employed near the actuator, with a local cell size of 0.25 mm.

The parameters related to electrode length and position, together with the applied voltage variations, were implemented in the wind turbine simulation model. Since the aerodynamic conditions around the blades vary with different TSRs, the effectiveness of the PLAs is likewise dependent on these changes. Therefore, all defined geometric and electrical configurations were simulated at four different TSRs: low (1.68), medium (2.33), optimal operating ratio (2.64), and high (3.3). The turbine power coefficient was extracted for each condition.

Using simulations of the specified parameter set, the optimization process for the geometric and electrical parameters of the PLAs was performed. Subsequently, to achieve a more detailed understanding of turbine aerodynamic behavior, the optimized parameters were employed to simulate rotors with different configurations ranging from two to five blades, both in the presence and absence of PLAs. In this stage, the influence of PLAs on rotor aerodynamic performance under various operating conditions is systematically examined and analyzed.

Effect of electrode length

Table 2 shows that electrode length has a significant impact on turbine performance. Under constant electrode placement and applied voltage, increasing electrode length results in significant improvements in both average and maximum power coefficients. Specifically, at a voltage of 14 kV and an electrode near the leading edge, the longest electrode (0.3) produced the highest average power coefficient, which is approximately 0.447. In contrast, shorter electrodes (0.2 and 0.1 in length) resulted in progressively smaller increases in the average power coefficient.

Longer electrodes generate a broader spatial extent of momentum injection, which explains this behavior. In VAWTs, the blades’ angle of attack varies continuously throughout the rotational cycle, resulting in dynamic stall and extensive flow separation. Longer electrodes interact with a larger portion of the blade surface, allowing for more efficient energy transfer to the boundary layer across a wider chordwise region. As a result, flow separation is delayed or weakened across a broader range of azimuthal positions, increasing the useful force acting on the blade and improving aerodynamic torque production.

Furthermore, increasing electrode length improves the minimum power coefficient, indicating better operational stability. This finding suggests that plasma excitation with a wider spatial coverage reduces severe torque drops caused by dynamic stall, lowering unsteady loads and contributing to improved structural durability.

As the results show, decreasing rotor speed (and thus increasing angle of attack) intensifies flow separation and creates less favorable aerodynamic conditions for torque generation. However, plasma excitation causes localized flow over the blade surface, effectively delaying flow separation10,57 and resulting in more uniform streamline patterns around the blades. Furthermore, vortices formed near the blades are significantly reduced or eliminated, indicating improved flow-blade interaction and aerodynamic performance over a wide range of TSRs.

The effect of electrode placement along the blade circumference

Electrode placement along the blade circumference is recognized as an important factor influencing plasma actuation efficiency. The results show that electrodes near the leading edge (starting at coordinates 0.1 and 0.3) consistently outperform those installed further downstream along the chord. For all electrode lengths and voltages tested, leading-edge configurations produce higher average and maximum power coefficients than mid-chord or trailing locations.

Shifting the electrode starting point to the mid- or aft-chord regions (0.5 and 0.7) causes a noticeable decrease in turbine performance. These conditions significantly reduce the average power coefficient increase and decrease maximum and minimum values. This behavior can be attributed to the nature of flow separation on VAWT blades. At high angles of attack, separation usually occurs near the leading edge. As a result, plasma activation applied upstream of the separation point can energize the boundary layer, delaying or suppressing separation, whereas activation downstream—where the flow is already separated and highly unstable—has limited effectiveness.

Effect of applied voltage

The applied voltage has a significant impact on turbine performance, with higher voltages consistently improving aerodynamic characteristics. The results show that increasing the voltage from 8 to 14 kV causes a steady rise in the average power coefficient across all electrode lengths and placements. At the highest voltage tested (14 kV), the average power coefficient reaches its maximum recorded value of approximately 0.447, while at 8 kV it remains limited to approximately 0.27–0.29.

The maximum power coefficient follows a similar trend, increasing from around 0.36 at 8 kV to nearly 0.50 at 14 kV. The stronger electric field intensity at higher voltages causes a greater induced body force and more efficient momentum transfer from the plasma to the near-wall flow, as evidenced by this trend. At lower voltages, the PLA’s forces are insufficient to overcome the adverse pressure gradient during dynamic stall, reducing flow-control effectiveness.

All simulations conducted in this study employed continuous plasma actuation. In practical applications, however, pulsed or azimuth‑gated actuation—such as limiting activation to azimuthal locations near stall onset—may reduce power consumption by avoiding unnecessary forcing in regions where the flow remains attached. Previous experimental investigations22–25 have shown that such control strategies can enhance the net power gain. Accordingly, future work will focus on the implementation and assessment of cycle‑resolved actuation schemes.

The findings indicate that the number of blades plays a decisive role in the effect of plasma actuation on the Cp, and the effectiveness of plasma is not uniform across different turbine configurations. The highest average power coefficient (average Cp = 0.447) is achieved by the three-bladed rotor, while any decrease or increase in the quantity of blades results in reduced overall performance.

In the two-bladed rotor, although plasma application significantly increases the power coefficient, the average value (0.389) remains lower than the three-bladed rotor. This behavior can be attributed to the more unsteady flow condition in rotors with fewer blades. The two-bladed rotor experiences stronger angle-of-attack fluctuations, and although the PLA partially controls flow separation, the relatively long interval between blade passages through critical regions increases torque oscillations and reduces operational stability.

When the number of blades is augmented to four, the average power coefficient falls to 0.382, despite the initial expectation of a more uniform flow field. This decrease in performance is due to increased aerodynamic interference between blades. Wake interactions intensify, and the incoming flow to each blade is influenced by upstream blades prior to arrival. Although the PLA has a positive effect on boundary-layer conditions, some of the potential power is lost due to blade wake interactions.

This trend becomes more pronounced in the five-bladed rotor, which exhibits the lowest average power coefficient (0.313) as well as the smallest maximum Cp (0.379) among all plasma-equipped configurations. Here, the increased blade numbers exacerbate flow blockage and wake interactions, severely limiting the ability to improve the flow field—even when plasma actuation is performed on each blade. This finding suggests that PLA efficiency depends not only on local blade characteristics but also on the overall rotor structure.

From the perspective of turbine operating points, the presence of plasma causes the TSR corresponding to maximum Cp to decrease. This phenomenon suggests that plasma actuation allows for higher power output at lower angular velocities, which improves startup capability and performance in low-wind conditions—a particularly useful feature for urban applications and regions with limited wind potential.

In summary, the three-bladed rotor achieves the best conditions for power continuity, aerodynamic blade interactions, and PLA effectiveness. The high average and minimum Cp of the three-bladed turbine demonstrate how plasma increases peak power output while effectively reducing aerodynamic fluctuations. Notably, at low TSRs the plasma‑equipped Darrieus rotor achieves power coefficients (e.g., Cp ≈ 0.405 at λ = 1.68) that surpass the peak Cp values of typical drag‑based Savonius turbines, which rarely exceed 0.2557. The PLAs suppress the dynamic‑stall vortex on the suction side during the high‑angle‑of‑attack portion of the cycle, enabling the blade to sustain lift instead of succumbing to massive separation and thereby extending efficient lift‑based operation into regimes normally dominated by drag‑based machines.

The results demonstrate that as the number of blades increases, the effectiveness of plasma actuation diminishes considerably. Furthermore, an increase in TSR leads to a reduction in PLA efficiency. To elucidate this behavior, two primary mechanisms that limit turbine power output—namely, flow separation and inter-blade flow interference—must be examined more rigorously.

The effectiveness of the PLA is highly dependent on both the rotor configuration and the operating TSR. For the 3-blade rotor at the low TSR of 1.68, the reduction in vorticity intensity is substantial, which directly correlates with the reported 85.5% increase in average power coefficient. In contrast, at the high TSR of 3.3 (where baseline separation is naturally weaker), the PLA’s influence on vorticity is less pronounced, though still beneficial. Furthermore, comparing the 3-blade and 5-blade rotors at TSR = 1.68 in Fig. 18 reveals that while the PLA reduces separation on the 5-blade rotor, the vorticity field remains more complex and intense than that of the 3-blade rotor. This observation supports the paper’s conclusion that increased blade numbers exacerbate wake interactions, which the PLA cannot fully mitigate, making the 3-blade configuration the most responsive to plasma-based flow control.

Despite the significant aerodynamic benefits demonstrated in the present study, transitioning PLA technology to full‑scale outdoor wind turbines presents considerable practical implementation challenges that currently limit its Technology Readiness Level. A primary obstacle is the high‑voltage demand required for operation, which traditionally necessitates large, heavy amplifiers; the added mass and complexity of integrating such equipment onto rotating blades can easily offset the aerodynamic gains12. Furthermore, maintaining the long‑term durability of the actuators is a critical concern. Continuous operation leads to physical wear and tear of the dielectric layers54, a degradation process that is severely accelerated in outdoor environments by UV exposure and rain erosion, ultimately necessitating rigorous maintenance schedules.

Moreover, there are notable environmental and safety hazards associated with industrial‑scale plasma actuation, primarily the continuous generation of substantial amounts of ozone and nitrous oxides, which pose significant respiratory hazards53,58. To overcome these limitations and enable practical commercial deployment on wind turbines, several critical issues must be addressed in future work: (1) development of lightweight, compact high‑voltage power supplies with power‑to‑weight ratios suitable for blade integration; (2) design of weather‑resistant dielectric materials and electrode configurations capable of withstanding prolonged UV exposure, moisture ingress, and erosion; (3) implementation of real‑time health monitoring systems to predict actuator degradation and optimize maintenance intervals; (4) mitigation strategies for ozone and NOₓ emissions; and (5) techno‑economic analysis to quantify the trade‑offs between energy gains from flow control and the parasitic power consumption, maintenance costs, and system complexity introduced by plasma actuation systems. Addressing these challenges will be essential for raising the technology readiness level of plasma‑based flow control and realizing its practical benefits in wind energy applications.

Conclusion

In this study, the effect of PLA implementation on improving aerodynamic performance and increasing power output of a VAWT is numerically examined. After validating the turbine power coefficient and the velocity field generated by the PLA, parametric simulations are run to optimize PLA electrode placement, length, and applied voltage. The effect of these parameters on the power coefficient and flow structure are then extracted. Furthermore, the effect of rotor blade number on plasma actuation efficiency is investigated by applying the optimal PLA configuration to rotors with two to five blades.

The results show that increasing electrode length, which involves momentum injection over a larger region of the blade surface, improves both the average and maximum power coefficients while reducing torque drops at low TSR. Electrode placement studies revealed that placing electrodes near the leading edge is more effective than placing them near the trailing edge or mid-chord regions. In these upstream locations, plasma actuation energizes the boundary layer before separation, improving flow control and increasing the power coefficient over a wide range of operating TSRs. Conversely, in downstream regions where separated flow has already developed, actuator effectiveness decreases.

Analysis of the applied voltage revealed that higher voltage increases the induced body force and allows for more effective momentum transfer to the near-wall flow. These phenomena prevent the occurrence of flow separation while increasing the average turbine power coefficient. Investigations also revealed that implementing PLAs shifts the optimal TSR toward lower values, and consequently increases aerodynamic torque at lower rotational speeds and reduces its fluctuations.

Finally, an evaluation of blade numbers reveals that the three-bladed rotor has the highest average power coefficient with and without plasma actuation. This configuration strikes an effective balance between flow-field continuity, aerodynamic blade interaction, and boundary-layer control via the PLA. Rotors with fewer or more blades, on the other hand, perform worse due to flow instability and increased wake interactions. These findings indicate that PLA efficiency is determined not only by local blade characteristics, but also by the overall rotor flow-field structure.
