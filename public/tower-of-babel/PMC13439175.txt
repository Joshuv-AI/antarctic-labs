Single-molecule imaging reveals RNA polymerase II dynamics and TAF1-dependent promoter-proximal pause release
Authors: Nayem Haque, Ronald Cutler, Simone Sidoli, Robert A Coleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly transcriptional regulation is a major kinetic bottleneck in gene expression, yet the dynamics of RNA polymerase II in living cells remain poorly understood. Here, we use single-molecule imaging to quantify RNA polymerase II chromatin engagement in live human cells. We find that RNA polymerase II infrequently enters a long-lived binding state associated with productive elongation, with ~94% of chromatin-associated events dissociating within tens of seconds. Single-cell analysis reveals substantial heterogeneity in the fraction of RNA polymerase II reaching productive elongation. Spatial heatmap analysis identifies RNA polymerase II clusters enriched for repeated long-lived binding events whose abundance is selectively perturbed by transcriptional inhibitors. Acute degradation of the TFIID subunit TAF1 reduces RNA polymerase II clustering and increases the global pausing index, supporting a model in which TAF1 promotes pause release and entry into productive elongation. Together, these findings provide a single-molecule framework for quantifying transcription kinetics in individual living cells.Subject terms: Transcription, Super-resolution microscopy, Single-molecule biophysics

Introduction

Transcription initiation is a highly regulated and dynamic process that governs gene expression outcomes across all living cells1,2. It begins with the binding of TFIID to the core promoter. TFIID is a large multi-subunit protein complex composed of TBP and TBP-associated factors (TAFs), including TAF1. TFIID serves as a scaffold to facilitate recruitment of RNA polymerase II (Pol II) and assembly of the pre-initiation complex (PIC)3–5. After initiating nascent RNA synthesis, Pol II undergoes promoter-proximal pausing approximately 20–60 base pairs downstream of the transcription start site (TSS) at many metazoan genes6,7. The transition of paused Pol II to productive elongation represents a major regulatory checkpoint and rate-limiting step in transcriptional activation, influencing cell identity, developmental programs, and responses to environmental stimuli8.

The fate of paused Pol II is dependent on the recruitment and activity of CDK9, the kinase module of the positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb). CDK9 phosphorylates multiple components of the paused complex, including SPT5. SPT5 phosphorylation is a key step associated with pause release and productive elongation9,10. An alternative fate of promoter-proximally paused Pol II is premature termination, which is driven by the Integrator complex11. Two modules of the Integrator, particularly relevant to regulating paused Pol II, are PP2A and INTS11. PP2A dephosphorylates SPT5, thereby preventing pause release, whereas INTS11 is an RNA endonuclease, allowing it to cleave the nascent RNA and destabilize the paused complex, leading to its dissociation12. Together, CDK9 and Integrator play opposing roles in regulating promoter-proximal pausing.

Additional factors can regulate promoter-proximal pausing. It has previously been reported that TFIID enforces promoter-proximal pausing based on an in vitro transcription assay and PRO-seq through a mechanism that remains unclear13. In contrast, in vitro kinase assays also show that purified recombinant TAF7, which interacts with both TAF1 and P-TEFb, enhances P-TEFb-mediated phosphorylation of SPT5, thereby facilitating pause release14. Thus, the precise role of the TFIID complex and its components in regulating promoter-proximal pausing remains unclear.

Previous fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)-based imaging studies have suggested that transcription is highly inefficient, with most Pol II molecules dissociating during the early stages of chromatin engagement15,16. These live-cell imaging studies have suggested that Pol II pause release is infrequent and occurs on the timescale of ~40–50 s after Pol II engages chromatin15,16. However, because these approaches rely on engineered reporters, population-averaged measurements, and mathematical modeling, they may not fully capture global features of endogenous transcription or cell-to-cell variability. Moreover, the extent to which TAF1 and promoter-proximal pausing regulate transcription in vivo remains poorly defined. Addressing these questions requires tools that can resolve individual Pol II binding events in living cells while preserving the endogenous regulatory architecture. Single-molecule imaging enables tracking of individual Pol II molecules during chromatin engagement, providing a powerful framework to dissect early transcription kinetics17.

Here, we develop a live-cell single-molecule imaging approach that combines endogenously tagged Pol II and TAF1 to dissect the kinetics of transcription and promoter-proximal pausing at single-cell resolution. We show that Pol II entry into productive elongation is globally inefficient and heterogeneous between cells, independent of cell-cycle state, and that ‘Pol II clusters’ mark sites enriched for repeated long-lived Pol II binding events. Finally, we rapidly depleted TAF1 and found that loss of TAF1 reduces Pol II cluster abundance and impairs pause release. Together, this study provides a powerful framework for quantifying transcription efficiency in vivo and supports a model in which TAF1 promotes promoter-proximal pause release.

Results

Generating an endogenously tagged human Pol II and TAF1 cell line

To visualize the dynamics of transcription initiation in live cells, we generated a CRISPR knock-in U2OS cell line expressing SNAP-tagged Pol II and Halo-tagged TAF1. The SNAP-tag was inserted at the N-terminus of POLR2A, which encodes the large subunit of Pol II. A HaloTag was inserted at the C-terminus of TAF1 via homology-directed repair (Fig. 1a, b; Supplementary Table 1, 2). Dual knock-in lines were created by sequential editing, beginning with SNAP-Pol II (Supplementary Fig. 1a), followed by a second round of sorting to enrich for homozygous integrations (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The TAF1-Halo construct was then knocked into the SNAP-Pol II background (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

Immunoblotting confirmed equivalent expression of both tagged proteins with migration shifts relative to wild-type cells (Fig. 1c). Single and dual knock-in lines exhibited normal proliferation rates (Fig. 1d) and nuclear localization of the tagged proteins by live-cell imaging (Fig. 1e). Together, this system enables quantitative, endogenous analysis of Pol II chromatin binding dynamics in the context of TAF1.

Single-molecule imaging reveals multiple Pol II dissociation states

During transcription initiation and early elongation, Pol II dissociates at multiple regulatory checkpoints before encountering the +1 nucleosome18–20 (Fig. 2a). Only a small fraction of Pol II molecules are expected to transition into productive elongation by releasing from the proximal pause and entering the gene body. Indeed, previous work using FRAP at a single transgenic locus revealed that only ~1% of alpha amanitin-resistant Pol II molecules that bind the promoter ultimately engage in productive elongation into the gene body, with the vast majority of Pol II dissociating shortly after binding chromatin16. These results were further confirmed via global FRAP using endogenously tagged Pol II15. Together, these studies suggested that transcription is highly inefficient. However, the conclusions drawn from these studies were based on a highly engineered locus-specific reporter system, ensemble measurements, and mathematical modeling.

To confirm if this inefficiency is a global feature of transcription, we performed live-cell single-molecule imaging in U2OS cells expressing endogenous SNAP-tagged Pol II and Halo-tagged TAF1. We imaged cells under sparse-labeling conditions using a 500 millisecond exposure time to resolve individual chromatin-bound Pol II binding events21 (Fig. 2b). Fluorescent spots appeared and disappeared over time within the nucleus, reflecting dynamic Pol II engagement and dissociation (or photobleaching) on chromatin (Supplementary Movie 1 – 3). We extracted dwell times for ~340,000 individual Pol II binding events across 52 nuclei and applied GRID (Genuine Rate Identification) analysis22 to identify discrete kinetic populations corresponding to Pol II dissociating during different phases of transcription (Fig. 2a). GRID provided a superior fit to the dwell time distribution compared to conventional two or three-exponential fit models (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

We first analyzed the data with GRID using an ‘event spectrum’ analysis. The event spectrum reports the frequency with which a dissociation event of a certain rate occurs during our imaging period ( ~ 17 minutes). Because the event spectrum accumulates dissociation events throughout the imaging window, progressively fewer events persist long enough to contribute to the longer-lived fractions. In DMSO-treated cells, GRID resolved four distinct Pol II binding populations with mean residence times of ~1.3, 11, 43, and 122 s (Fig. 2c). The shortest-lived population ( ~ 1.3 s) accounted for ~72% of binding events, while the most stable ( ~ 122 s) represented ~1.7% of total events. Because the shortest-lived fraction has a residence time of 1.3 s (<1 frame), we classify this population as ‘Transient binding’, which likely reflects Pol II probing chromatin for its genomic targets.

To characterize additional populations, we treated cells with transcription inhibitors. We first treated cells with Triptolide, an XPB-helicase inhibitor that blocks promoter opening and promoter escape23,24. At high doses and long exposure times, Triptolide can also induce proteasome-mediated degradation of Pol II’s largest subunit25,26. After 30 min of Triptolide treatment, we did not observe any effect on Pol II levels or the number of detected Pol II binding events (Fig. 2d, e). Using GRID analysis, we found that Triptolide produced similar kinetics as the control (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These results suggest that under non-degrading Triptolide conditions, the GRID event spectrum is unable to resolve changes in Pol II dwell-time populations relative to control.

A snapshot readout of dissociation rates can be obtained by performing a “state spectrum” analysis22. A state spectrum weighs the frequency of dissociation rates by the residence times so that long-lived binding events, which exist in multiple frames, are weighted much heavier than short-lived events. Fundamentally, a state spectrum provides information on what dissociation pathways are present in an instantaneous snapshot in time and is thus more analogous to a genomics-based readout.

Using a state spectrum analysis revealed that Triptolide led to a clear redistribution toward the transient-binding and ~11 s fractions, with a corresponding decrease in the long-lived population (Supplementary Fig. 2b). We interpret these changes as reflecting increased occupancy of the two early populations and reduced occupancy of the long-lived fraction at steady state. Therefore, the ~11 s fraction likely represents Pol II in an “Unstable early engagement” state. This fraction may include unstable early Pol II initiation/early-elongation complexes, consistent with prior work showing that complexes with very short nascent RNAs are especially labile, whereas complexes with nascent RNAs of >10 nucleotides are much more stable27,28.

We next analyzed how Pol II dynamics changed after treatment with NVP-2, a highly potent and selective CDK9 inhibitor that blocks promoter-proximal pause release29. Acute disruption of CDK9 activity led to rapid dephosphorylation of SPT5, confirming potent kinase inhibition (Fig. 2d). An event spectrum GRID analysis revealed that NVP-2 selectively targets the most stable, long-lived fraction (Supplementary Fig. 2c), indicating the ~43 s population failed to transition into the ~122 s fraction. Thus, the ~43 s population includes Pol II that dissociates from “Pause instability”, which may be due to active termination by the Integrator complex. The residence time of this population is in excellent agreement with the promoter-proximal paused fraction in a previous study that modeled live cell Pol II kinetics using FRAP15. The most stable long-lived fraction has a residence time that is near our photobleaching limit of ~130 s, indicating that the loss of fluorescence signal could be due to photobleaching rather than true dissociation (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Because Pol II fails to transition to this population with NVP-2 treatment, we conclude that this fraction contains Polymerases that enter the “Productive elongation” state. Notably, we use the term productive elongation to include Pol II that has progressed beyond the pause site at both protein-coding genes and enhancers.

To capture the effects of NVP-2 using a “snapshot” measurement, we performed a state spectrum analysis, which revealed a clear redistribution of the productive elongation state toward the shorter-lived fractions (Fig. 2f, g). Most notably, the Pause-instability fraction increased following NVP-2 treatment compared to DMSO, which is consistent with enhanced Integrator-mediated termination of paused Pol II. The effects of transcriptional perturbation were independent of nuclear area (Supplementary Fig. 2e). We further validated these results by extending NVP-2 inhibition to 2 hours, similar to treatment durations previously used to demonstrate impaired pause release. Accordingly, we observed a decrease in phospho-SPT5 levels and further redistribution of Pol II dissociation states toward the pause instability fraction (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, these results suggest that only a small fraction of chromatin-associated Pol II can enter productive elongation.

Single-cell analysis shows varied entry into productive elongation

Transcription efficiency can be measured by the percentage of Pol II that reaches the most stable kinetic population, which contains long-lived Pol II that have entered productive elongation. To address whether this efficiency varies between individual cells, we performed single-cell GRID analysis. In DMSO treated cells, we observed substantial heterogeneity in the percentage of Pol II reaching the productive elongation state (Fig. 3a). For example, one cell exhibited ~2.8% of binding events in the productive elongation fraction, with a residence time of ~98 s (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 4a), while another showed only ~0.4% at ~152 s (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 4b). These differences suggest that some cells are markedly better than others at driving Pol II into productive elongation.

Although GRID analysis can be performed at the single-cell level, per-cell fits become unreliable because each cell contributes relatively few long-lived events. To quantify productive transcription in a manner that is robust at single-cell resolution, we used the dwell-time boundaries identified by GRID to define simple threshold-based metrics that can be applied directly to raw tracks. Based on prior FRAP studies of endogenously tagged Pol II, promoter-proximal pausing is estimated to occur ~40 s after Pol II engages the promoter15. From our GRID analysis, we observed a distinct population of Pol II with residence times in this range on two separate imaging sessions, which failed to transition into the productive elongation state following CDK9 inhibition with NVP-2. This supports a model that, on average, global Pol II reaches the pause site ~40 s after binding to chromatin. Previous studies have reported a pause duration on the order of several minutes, with some estimates well below 2.5 min30–33. Based on these reports, we conservatively defined the percentage of binding events with dwell times exceeding 90 s as ‘% Pol II entering productive elongation’ (Supplementary Fig. 4c). As expected, Pol II binding events exceeding this threshold were sensitive to transcriptional perturbations following 30 min (Figs. 3d) and 2 h of NVP-2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4d).

Using the above classification, we defined a global cellular pausing index as the ratio of the number of Pol II binding events with dwell times between 40 and 90 s (pause window) to those exceeding 90 s (productive elongation) (Supplementary Fig. 4c). CDK9 inhibition is expected to increase the pausing index by impairing pause release34,35. Consistent with this, NVP-2 treatment increased the global pausing index relative to control (30 min, Fig. 3e; 2 h, Supplementary Fig. 4e). NVP-2 also increased the pausing index over DMSO across a wide range of pause entry and pause duration threshold pairs (Supplementary Fig. 4f), demonstrating the robustness of this metric. Effect size varied from 20 to 27%, with a 40 s pause entry and 50 s pause duration pair (40/90) = 22% (Supplementary Fig. 4g). In contrast, Triptolide did not change the pausing index (Fig. 3e). Instead, Triptolide reduced both paused and productive elongation fractions proportionally, resulting in little net change in the pausing index.

From the single-cell data, we were able to resolve clear differences both across and within drug conditions. Most notably, the percentage of Pol II that can enter productive elongation was highly heterogeneous across individual DMSO-treated cells, ranging from ~0.7 to ~1.9%, representing an almost threefold difference (Fig. 3d). In other words, in some cells, only ~1 in 143 chromatin-bound Pol II enters productive elongation, whereas in others, ~1 in 53 does so. This heterogeneity persists across all drug conditions, indicating that it is a robust feature of single-cell Pol II dynamics rather than a condition-specific effect. These results suggest that entry into productive elongation is not uniform across individual cells, and that this heterogeneity may instead reflect intrinsic cell-state differences.

Productive elongation variation persists across cell-cycle states

Given the substantial cell-to-cell heterogeneity in the percentage of Pol II that reaches productive elongation, we asked whether this variability could be due to differences in cell-cycle state. To address this, we generated a dual knock-in U2OS cell line where an N-terminal HaloTag was inserted at the endogenous PCNA locus in the SNAP-Pol II background (Supplementary Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table 2, 3). Immunoblotting confirmed expression of Halo-PCNA at the expected molecular weight alongside SNAP-Pol II (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In live cells, Halo-PCNA displayed the characteristic nuclear patterns previously used to assign cell-cycle phases36. Diffuse nuclear PCNA-fluorescence marked cells in G1 and G2, numerous heterogeneous puncta in early S, many large peripheral foci in mid S, and a few large foci in late S (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Dual-color imaging verified that SNAP-Pol II and Halo-PCNA signals could be detected simultaneously in the same nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 5d).

We then classified individual nuclei into G (G1/G2), early S, mid S, or late S based on their nuclear Halo-PCNA signal and measured Pol II binding kinetics within each class. Cells were selected based upon their nuclear PCNA pattern ( ~ 72% S-phase, ~28% G-phase, Supplementary Fig. 5e). As expected, the total number of PCNA binding events per nucleus varied between cell-cycle states, with higher event counts during S-phase (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Interestingly, the percentage of Pol II reaching productive elongation and the pausing index showed no significant differences across cell-cycle states (Supplementary Fig. 5g, h). These results suggest that the variability in productive elongation entry may not primarily be driven by cell-cycle state, but instead likely reflects other sources of cell-state heterogeneity.

Variation in productive elongation lacks a protein or genetic basis

We next asked whether variations in Pol II or TAF1 levels might contribute to the heterogeneity of Pol II reaching productive elongation by using the total number of nuclear binding events as an imaging-derived proxy for protein abundance. To do this, we plotted the % Pol II reaching productive elongation as a function of the total number of Pol II/TAF1 binding events detected during imaging. We did not observe a linear correlation in either case (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), suggesting that variations in Pol II or TAF1 levels are not the primary driver of the observed heterogeneity.

Finally, we tested whether the use of a polyclonal pool of CRISPR-edited cells may contribute to this heterogeneity. To do this, we performed a limiting dilution assay and seeded ~0.3 cells/well directly into a 96-well imaging plate. After ~2 weeks, we identified a single colony growing in one of the wells (Supplementary Fig. 6c). We imaged the cells within this colony and observed a similar spread in the percentage of Pol II entering the productive elongation state as the polyclonal line (Supplementary Fig. 6d). We also measured this parameter in cells expressing exogenous α-amanitin-resistant Pol II, a CRISPR-independent system, and again observed a similar spread (Supplementary Fig. 6d). These results suggest that the heterogeneity in the percent of Pol II entering productive elongation across individual cells is not primarily driven by protein levels or CRISPR-editing.

Pol II clusters are sites of enhanced entry into productive elongation

Previous live-cell imaging studies identified Pol II clusters in the nucleus as transient and dynamic assemblies37. Subsequent work showed that at the β-actin gene locus, long-lived Pol II clusters were positively correlated with increased nascent transcriptional output38. More recently, Pol II clusters were found to transiently form around promoters of protein-coding genes during transcriptional bursts, and promoter-associated Pol II clustering was linked to high transcriptional efficiency39,40. Together, these studies suggest that regions of frequent Pol II binding may mark sites of active transcription at protein-coding genes.

To map nuclear regions of Pol II clustering and measure the kinetics of transcription within those sites, we developed a pipeline called Spatial Heatmap Analysis of Residence-time Probability (SHARP) to compute a two-dimensional kernel density estimate (KDE) of all Pol II binding events for each nucleus (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 7a, b, see Methods). We first examined how the number of Pol II clusters changed after 30 min of transcription inhibition. Triptolide caused a significant decrease in the number of Pol II clusters, whereas NVP-2 showed a non-significant trend toward fewer clusters and greater variability in the number of detected Pol II clusters (Fig. 4b). We repeated these measurements using 2-hour of NVP-2 treatment and observed a much stronger effect with significantly fewer Pol II clusters and less cell-to-cell variability (Supplementary Fig. 7c). We interpret the difference between the 30 min and 2-hour NVP-2 treatment as reflecting a more uniform steady state response at the longer timepoint.

We hypothesized that Pol II clusters may represent sites of enhanced transcription into the productive elongation state. To test this, we compared the percentage of Pol II entering the productive elongation state and the pausing index inside versus outside clusters. Under control conditions, we found that Pol II inside clusters had a significantly higher percentage of Pol II entering into the productive elongation state and a lower pausing index compared to Pol II outside clusters (Fig. 4c, d). Based on these results and prior studies, it is likely that clusters are enriched for Pol II binding events occurring on active mRNA promoters39,40. The percentage of Pol II entering productive elongation ranged from 0.7% to 4.1% inside clusters, indicating greater heterogeneity than observed in global Pol II (0.6%–1.8%). This broad range suggests that characteristics of Pol II clusters may drive high transcriptional efficiency. To investigate this, we compared cluster density, mean cluster size, and the average number of Pol II binding events within clusters against the percentage of Pol II entering productive elongation in clusters (Fig. 4e–g). Among these metrics, only the average number of Pol II events within clusters showed a modest linear relationship, suggesting that cluster occupancy may be an important determinant driving Pol II into productive elongation. Together, these results suggest that Pol II clusters are sites associated with high transcriptional efficiency.

Loss of TAF1 impairs Pol II entry into productive elongation

To understand how TAF1 regulates Pol II chromatin-binding dynamics in vivo, we acutely degraded TAF1 using HaloPROTAC3. Immunoblotting revealed robust loss of TAF1 after 2 h of treatment with 300 nM HaloPROTAC3 and only partial loss at 1 µM (Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with a hook effect at the higher dose41. A time-course of HaloPROTAC3 treatment showed maximal TAF1 depletion after 2 h (Fig. 5a). Importantly, we did not observe a reduction in TBP or TAF7, two components of the TFIID complex that are in close spatial proximity to TAF1 in TFIID’s unbound (TBP and TAF7) and DNA-bound (TAF7) states. We further assessed HaloPROTAC3 specificity by performing quantitative proteomics of dual knock-in cells treated with DMSO or 300 nM HaloPROTAC3 for 2 h, which detected multiple TFIID subunits42 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). At this time point, no significant changes were detected in the abundance of several TFIID subunits and other basal transcription factors (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Fig. 9b–g). Finally, live-cell imaging of labeled TAF1-Halo confirmed loss of nuclear TAF1 signal (Fig. 5c). To assess the global role of TAF1 in Pol II transcription kinetics, we analyzed per-cell Pol II dwell-time distributions after transient TAF1 degradation. We observed a decrease in the percentage of Pol II entering productive elongation (Fig. 5d), which could reflect impaired pause release or a reduced rate of initiation upon TAF1 loss. Importantly, because TAF7 abundance was not reduced under these conditions, the decrease in productive elongation is unlikely to result from off-target TAF7 degradation and instead supports a model in which acute TAF1 loss may specifically impair pause release.

TAF1 enables phosphorylation of SPT5 and pause release of Pol II

To address whether loss of TAF1 disrupts pausing, we analyzed the pausing index across DMSO and HaloPROTAC3-treated cells. Strikingly, TAF1 degradation caused a significant increase in the pausing index (Fig. 6a), indicating that TAF1 may be involved in releasing paused Pol II. We further corroborated these findings by performing a state spectrum GRID analysis and observed a significant shift from the productive elongation fraction to the pause instability fraction (Fig. 6b), suggesting increased turnover of paused Pol II at steady state. To test whether TAF1 acts through the CDK9–SPT5 axis, we performed a Western blot for phospho-SPT5 following acute TAF1 depletion. Partial loss of TAF1 led to a strong reduction in phospho-SPT5 (Fig. 6c), indicating that CDK9-mediated SPT5 phosphorylation is compromised when TAF1 is rapidly depleted. Together, these results suggest that TAF1 is critical for maintaining both SPT5 phosphorylation and the release of promoter-proximally paused Pol II.

To determine whether this phenotype is reversible, we performed an endogenous rescue experiment by washing out HaloPROTAC3 after 2 hours of treatment. We examined 4-hour, 10-hour, and 24-hour washout timepoints. Western blotting showed partial recovery of TAF1 at 10 hours and near-complete recovery at 24 h (Fig. 6d). The 10-hour time point therefore represents partial TAF1 restoration, whereas the 24-hour washout restores TAF1 levels, potentially due to cell division. Phospho-SPT5 levels also progressively recovered after HaloPROTAC3 washout (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). We then imaged SNAP-Pol II TAF1-Halo cells treated with DMSO, NVP-2, HaloPROTAC3, and after 10 hours or 24 hours HaloPROTAC3 washout and measured the pausing index. Remarkably, after 10 h of washout, when TAF1 levels are only partially restored (Supplementary Fig. 10c), the pausing defect was rescued (Fig. 6e). Acute TAF1 depletion caused a significant but reversible decrease in the number of Pol II clusters. In addition, the average number of Pol II binding events within Pol II clusters decreased following TAF1 depletion, consistent with reduced local Pol II engagement at these sites (Fig. 6g). Importantly, rapid TAF1 depletion phenocopied the increase in pausing index, decrease in Pol II cluster number, and reduction in the average number of Pol II binding events within clusters observed after NVP-2 treatment. Together, these results support a model in which TAF1 promotes promoter-proximal pause release, at least in part through a pathway linked to CDK9 activity and SPT5 phosphorylation.

Discussion

By combining endogenous tagging of Pol II and TAF1 with live-cell single-molecule imaging, we directly quantified the kinetics of early Pol II transcriptional engagement in human cells. We observed that transcription initiation is globally inefficient, with only ~1.2% of chromatin-associated Pol II reaching a long-lived state consistent with entry into productive elongation, in agreement with earlier FRAP-based estimates. Unlike FRAP, which requires fitting ensemble recovery curves with multi-parameter kinetic models, the GRID-based analysis used in this study decomposes the dwell-time distributions of individual Pol II binding events directly. GRID resolved four dissociation states of Pol II, which we interpret as transient binding, unstable early engagement, promoter-proximal pause instability, and entry into productive elongation.

Importantly, the populations detected in this study include Pol II interactions occurring at multiple classes of genomic initiation sites, including protein-coding promoters as well as noncoding and regulatory loci. Although mRNA promoters and enhancers have functionally distinct roles, they share notable similarities in core promoter sequence elements, nucleosome positioning, and divergent transcription behavior, suggesting that transcription initiation is organized in a broadly similar manner at promoters and enhancers43. Enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) are often short-lived and unstable relative to mRNAs and are therefore better captured by nascent-RNA approaches, such as GRO-cap. However, Pol II pause duration at enhancers is thought to be ~2 min, suggesting the unstable binding of Pol II ( ~ 10–40 s) is unlikely to represent productive transcription through to proper termination of relatively short full-length eRNAs30. Nevertheless, NVP-2 treatment produced a small but reproducible shift in these populations, particularly in the longer-lived states, supporting the interpretation that these states capture global changes in promoter-proximal pausing and productive elongation at both eRNA and mRNA encoding regions.

The dwell times of the paused and productive elongation Pol II populations align well with genomic measurements of nascent transcription. Our kinetic partitioning of Pol II recapitulates these timescales and provides a simple way to define a per-cell pausing index from dwell time data. As expected for CDK9-dependent pause release, pharmacological inhibition with NVP-2 selectively decreases the percentage of Pol II reaching productive elongation and elevates the pausing index. Together, these results support a model in which Pol II typically dwells for tens of seconds in promoter-proximal states before either dissociating or transitioning into the long-lived state associated with productive elongation. Thus, these dwell time-based thresholds provide a useful live-cell proxy for measuring a global pausing index and percentage of Pol II entering productive elongation at single-cell resolution.

A key advantage of the single-molecule approach is that it reveals substantial cell-to-cell variability that is not apparent in ensemble assays. Even under control conditions, more than a threefold range in the percentage of Pol II reaching productive elongation is observed globally, and a nearly sixfold range was observed for Pol II within clusters. We initially considered whether cell cycle state may underlie this heterogeneity, given that basal transcription is generally thought to be low in the S and G2 phase44. However, when we directly classified cells by their PCNA patterns, the percentage of Pol II reaching productive elongation was remarkably similar across G1/G2 and S-phase. These findings suggest that the dominant source of variability is more likely to be other aspects of cell state, such as chromatin environment or metabolite availability, rather than cell cycle state alone.

Our SHARP analysis identifies discrete Pol II clusters, defined as nuclear subregions containing a high density of Pol II binding events. Functionally, these regions are associated with elevated levels of Pol II reaching productive elongation and may support transcriptional bursting. This interpretation is consistent with previous live-cell imaging studies showing that clustering of Pol II and its cofactors can locally enhance transcription by increasing factor concentration and stabilizing productive transcriptional assemblies37,38,45–48. Consistent with this idea, Pol II clusters were particularly sensitive to perturbations that disrupt promoter function. We also observed a modest positive linear correlation between the average number of Pol II binding events within clusters and the percentage of Pol II entering productive elongation, suggesting that variations in Pol II binding frequency within clusters may contribute to heterogeneity in transcriptional output. Frequent Pol II binding may help maintain TSS accessibility by limiting nucleosome encroachment, thereby promoting transcription49. Together, these findings suggest that Pol II cluster abundance may serve as a robust readout of a cell’s capacity to sustain productive transcriptional engagement.

Rapid and highly specific degradation of TAF1 provides mechanistic insight into how a core component of TFIID can regulate transcription. A prior biochemical and genomics-based paper reported that TFIID enforces promoter-proximal pausing13. However, metagene profiles from their PRO-seq experiments following TAF1/2 depletion showed increased Pol II density at the promoter-proximal pause site and reduced density in the gene body. Increased Pol II occupancy at the proximal pause site is typically associated with defects in pause release. To reconcile these effects with their in vitro result (which showed that TFIID establishes a pause), the authors proposed that loss of TAF1/2 leads to enhanced reinitiation of Pol II at promoters and increased promoter-proximal pause release of Pol II into the gene body.

Importantly, a metagene analysis alone cannot measure changes in reinitiation of Pol II, since it relies on a snapshot of ensemble Pol II engagements across multiple cells. This makes it difficult to distinguish Pol II that is cycling more frequently at the promoter (due to increased reinitiation) from Pol II that is stalled at a downstream barrier (due to enhanced pausing). Our live-cell measurements indicate that partial depletion of TAF1 in human cells decreases the ability of Pol II to reach the productive elongation state and significantly increases the pausing index. This interpretation is further supported by our immunoblots, which show decreased SPT5 phosphorylation following TAF1 loss. Phospho-SPT5 plays a critical role in facilitating promoter-proximal pause release, as it mechanistically triggers dissociation of the negative transcription elongation factor (NELF) from the paused complex and transition into productive elongation7,9,50. In addition, both NVP-2 treatment and acute TAF1 depletion reduced the average number of Pol II binding events within Pol II clusters. In the case of NVP-2, this is consistent with the previously described pause-initiation limit, in which prolonged promoter-proximal pausing reduces productive initiation30. Similarly, reduced Pol II binding within clusters after TAF1 loss argues against a model in which the increased promoter-proximal signal primarily reflects enhanced reinitiation. Notably, TAF1 loss did not eliminate Pol II binding within clusters. Thus, although both our model and the model proposed by Fant et al. are consistent with their PRO-seq metagene profiles, they differ in how those profiles are interpreted. Our single-molecule imaging results support impaired pause release as a major consequence of TAF1 loss.

The mechanism by which TAF1 facilitates SPT5 phosphorylation and Pol II pause release remains unclear. However, prior studies suggest that TAF1 may support BRD4 and P-TEFb function during promoter-proximal pause release. TAF1 has been reported to physically interact with BRD4, a bromodomain-containing protein that forms a complex with P-TEFb. BRD4 has long been implicated in the release of promoter-proximally paused Pol II via its interaction with P-TEFb51. This connection is further strengthened by a study showing that inhibition of TAF1 bromodomain 2 phenocopies the effects of BRD4 inhibition, suggesting that TAF1 and BRD4 function in the same pathway52. These observations are consistent with a model in which TAF1 promotes efficient BRD4 and P-TEFb activity at paused Pol II, thereby supporting SPT5 phosphorylation and pause release.

There are several important limitations associated with our single-particle tracking approach. Our imaging assay measures global chromatin binding rather than gene-specific engagements. We infer pause instability and productive elongation states from dwell times and drug sensitivity rather than from direct locus-specific measurements using conventional ‘snapshot’ genomic assays that average over hundreds of thousands of cells. We emphasize that these global chromatin binding events occur on both protein-coding genes and regions that produce noncoding RNA, such as enhancers. We also define clusters strictly by Pol II binding frequency, and although their drug sensitivity argues that they are promoter-rich foci, we do not yet know their precise genomic composition. A limitation of our proteomics dataset is that several proteins were not detected, including TAF1 and TAF7. To partially address this, we complemented the proteomic analysis with immunoblotting for TAF1 and TAF7, for which suitable antibodies were available. Finally, the precise mechanism by which TAF1 affects SPT5 phosphorylation remains unknown. Specifically, it is unknown whether loss of TAF1 perturbs recruitment of CDK9 or affects activation of the kinase. Addressing these limitations will require extensive genomic and kinase assays, which are beyond the scope of this study. Future work addressing these limitations will be needed to elucidate the precise mechanism of TAF1-mediated pause release and to directly position TAF1 within the CDK9-SPT5 axis.

Our findings support a model in which the presence of TAF1 is required to facilitate phosphorylation of SPT5 and Pol II pause release (Fig. 7). In addition, this study establishes a framework to measure early transcription kinetics in single live cells at single-molecule resolution. Our strategy for rapid endogenous tagging of transcription factors, along with automated tools for processing and analyzing single-molecule imaging data, will facilitate mechanistic studies of transcriptional regulation and provide an orthogonal approach to complement genomic and biochemical measurements.

Model of promoter-proximal pausing in the presence and absence of TAF1.

When TAF1 is present, Pol II forms clusters and SPT5 (PDB: 6GML) is phosphorylated, which triggers the release of promoter-proximally paused Pol II into productive elongation. When TAF1 is acutely depleted, fewer Pol II clusters are formed, and SPT5 does not get phosphorylated, thereby stabilizing the paused-Pol II complex and resulting in fewer Pol II moving into productive elongation. The precise mechanism by which TAF1 enables SPT5 phosphorylation is unknown. Methods

Cell line generation using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing

Human osteosarcoma (U-2 OS) cells (ATCC HTB-96) were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 10569044) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, vol/vol) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (500 U/mL). Cells were passaged at ~70% confluency in 10 cm tissue culture dishes.

Guide RNAs (gRNAs) were prepared by mixing 3.6 μL of 200 μM tracrRNA (IDT, 1072532) with 3.6 μL of 200 μM gene-specific crRNA (Supplementary Table 3) in nuclease-free duplex buffer (IDT, 11010301). The mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 5 minutes and allowed to cool to room temperature for 20 min to facilitate crRNA-tracrRNA duplex formation. Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes were formed by combining 7.2 μL of gRNA (720 pmol), 4 μL of HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3 ( ~ 250 pmol, IDT, 1081061), and 0.8 μL of PBS at room temperature for 20 min. Homology-directed repair (HDR) templates were generated from PCR-amplified double-stranded gBlock HiFi gene fragments (IDT, for SNAP-POLR2A) or clonal genes (Twist Bioscience, for TAF1-Halo and Halo-PCNA) containing either the SNAP or HaloTag flanked by sequence-specific homology arms (Supplementary Tables 1, 3, 4). The amplified double-stranded DNA sequences were converted to single-stranded DNA using the Guide-it long ssDNA kit (Takara, 632666).

Approximately 200,000 cells were resuspended in 100 μL of nucleofection solution containing 20 μL of ssDNA ( ~ 5 μg), 10 μL of the RNP mix, and 70 μL of Nucleofector solution V + Supplement I (mixed at a 4.5:1 ratio; Lonza, VCA-1003). The resuspended cells were transferred to an aluminum cuvette (Lonza) and electroporated using the Amaxa Nucleofector II with the X-001 program. Immediately after electroporation, 300 μL of pre-warmed culture media was added to the cuvette and gently mixed before plating in a 6-well culture plate containing 1.6 mL of pre-warmed culture media (DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin-streptomycin) supplemented with 3 μL of 690 μM Alt-R HDR Enhancer V2 ( ~ 1 μM final; IDT, 10007910). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h before being washed with PBS and adding fresh culture media.

Once cells reached ~70–80% confluency, they were expanded into progressively larger vessels and sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) based on SNAP-Cell 647-SiR dye (NEB, S9102S) or HaloTag JF635 (Janelia Research Center, gift from Luke Lavis) fluorescence.

Generating dual SNAP-POLR2A and Halo-tag knock-in lines

To generate the dual knock-in cell lines, validated SNAP-POLR2A cells were re-targeted using a gRNA specific to the human C-terminus of TAF1 or the N-terminus of PCNA. RNP complexes were formed as described above, using a gene-specific crRNA and a HaloTag repair template obtained as a clonal gene fragment (Twist Bioscience) with homology arms flanking the start/stop codon. Single-stranded DNA was generated as described in the previous section.

Approximately 200,000 SNAP-POLR2A cells were electroporated with the RNP complex and ssDNA repair template using the same nucleofection conditions. Dual-labeled cells were identified using HaloTag JF635 and sorted via FACS. A second round of FACS was performed to enrich for a stable, high-penetrance polyclonal population. These dual knock-in strains were expanded and used for all subsequent experiments.

Cell line validation assays

For western blotting, whole-cell extracts were harvested from ~70% confluent 15 cm tissue culture dishes using a cold cell scraper on ice and lysed in RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 140 mM NaCl, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, filter sterile) supplemented with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo, 78442). Lysates were sonicated using a Diagenode Bioruptor (30 seconds on/off, high power, 5 min) and clarified by high-speed centrifugation at 18,000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a detergent-compatible Bradford assay (Thermo, 23246). For western blots using nuclear extracts, cells were harvested using the Nuclei Isolation Kit: Nuclei EZ Prep (Sigma, NUC101-1KT) and processed following the manufacturers procedure for adherent cells. The final nuclei pellet was resuspended in RIPA buffer supplemented with 20 mM β-Glycerophosphate, 20 mM NaF, and 1x Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitors.

Equal amounts of total protein (30 μg) were resolved on 6% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to Amersham Protran 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Cytvia, 10600003) using a wet transfer (Constant current at 400 mA for 2 h at 4 °C). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad, 1706404) in 1x TBS for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Pol II (A-10) mouse monoclonal (Santa Cruz, sc-17798, 1:500 dilution), anti-TAF1 (D6J8B) rabbit monoclonal (Cell Signaling Technologies, 12781, 1:1000 dilution), anti-TBP rabbit polyclonal (Cell Signaling Technologies, 8515S, 1:1000 dilution), anti-PCNA (D3H8P) rabbit monoclonal (Cell Signaling Technologies, 13110, 1:1000 dilution), anti-SUPT5H polyclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher, A300-868A, 1:1000 dilution), anti-Phospho-SPT5 (Thr794 in mouse, Thr799 in human) polyclonal (FabGennix, PSPT5-140AP, 1:500 dilution), anti-Lamin A/C rabbit polyclonal (Cell Signaling Technologies, 2032 T, 1:1000 dilution), anti-TAF7 mouse monoclonal (Invitrogen, 19TA-2C7, 1:0000 dilution), or anti-Vinculin (E1E9V) rabbit monoclonal (Cell Signaling Technologies, 13901, 1:1000 dilution). Blots were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A32735, 1:20,000) or IRDye-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (LI-COR, 926-32210, 1:20,000) and imaged using an Odyssey CLx imaging system.

To assess whether the SNAP- or Halo-tagged cell lines maintained normal growth rates, 10,000 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. Cell growth was quantified every ~24 h for 5 days using manual counting with a grid reticle. Each cell line was quantified in triplicate, and data were plotted as mean ± SEM.

For validating cellular localization, 7×105 cells were seeded on 35 mm imaging dishes (MATTEK, P35G-1.5-14-C) 24 hours before imaging. Cells were labeled with SPY505-DNA (Cytoskeleton, CY-SC101, 1:1000 in DMSO, 1 h), SNAP-Cell 647-SiR (100 nM, 30 minutes), and HaloTag JFX549 (250 pM, 30 min) at 37 °C. After labeling, cells were washed with pre-warmed PBS before adding imaging media, which consists of Leibovitz’s L-15 (Fisher, 21083027) + 10% FBS. A single, 1 second exposure image was captured on a customized inverted Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope equipped with a 100x oil-immersion objective (NA 1.49, Nikon). Fluorophores were excited using 488 nm, 561 nm, and 639 nm lasers, and images were captured using a Prime 95B sCMOS camera.

Single-molecule imaging

Live-cell single-molecule imaging was performed on dual knock-in U2OS cells expressing SNAP-tagged Pol II and Halo-tagged TAF1 or Halo-tagged PCNA. Cells were labeled with 6 nM SNAP-Cell TMR-Star (NEB, S9105S) and 5 nM HaloTag JF646 ligand (Promega, HT1060) for 20 min at 37 °C in pre-warmed DMEM. After labeling, cells were washed with complete culture media and pre-warmed PBS. Cells were then incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in 2 mL of Leibovitz’s L-15 medium (Thermo Fisher, 21083027) supplemented with 10% FBS prior to imaging. During this incubation, the SNAP-Pol II TAF1-Halo cells were treated with 2 μL of DMSO, 2 μL of 500 μM Triptolide, 2 μL of 50 μM or 500 μM NVP-2, or 2 uL of 300 μM HaloPROTAC3 (Promega). Triptolide, NVP-2, and HaloPROTAC3 were all resuspended in DMSO. Cells treated with HaloPROTAC3 were not labeled with HaloTag JF646 ligand. For the washout experiments, cells were treated with 300 nM HaloPROTAC3 for 2 h and immediately washed several times with culture media to significantly dilute out and remove HaloPROTAC3. Cells were then placed in an incubator for the indicated washout time before being prepared for imaging.

Single-molecule imaging was performed using motion-blur highly inclined and laminated optical sheet (HILO) microscopy on a customized Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope equipped with a 100× oil-immersion objective (NA 1.49, Nikon), two independent Prime 95B sCMOS cameras (Teledyne Photometrics), and 100 mW 561 nm and 640 nm lasers for excitation. Images were acquired with a 500 ms exposure every 2 seconds for a total of 500 frames ( ~ 17 min with 1.5-second dark time) under sparse labeling conditions to ensure single-molecule resolution.

Image organization and pre-processing

Single-molecule TIFF files were organized and pre-processed using custom Python scripts, ImageJ macros, and MATLAB code53. First, the Python script Generate_folders.py was used to create a standardized directory structure for each experiment (PyCharm Community Edition 2024.1). Raw TIFF stacks for each cell were then processed with the ImageJ macro ImageJ_processing.ijm (Fiji/ImageJ), which generated “photobleaching” and “background-subtracted” TIFF files. Background subtraction was performed using a rolling-ball algorithm with a radius of 50 pixels, and the resulting files were saved into their respective directories.

Single-molecule tracking and trajectory analysis

Single-molecule trajectories were extracted with the MATLAB script STRAP.m (MATLAB R2024b)53. For each cell, the script first identified the corresponding photobleaching movie and fit the global decay of Pol II and TAF1 fluorescence to an exponential function to estimate bleaching kinetics. Background-subtracted movies were then subjected to multi-target tracking using an automated, deconstructed implementation of SLIMfast and evalSPT54,55. The following SLIMfast parameters were used: pixel size = 110 nm, numerical aperture = 1.49, exposure time = 500 ms, maximum off-frames = 2, and maximum diffusion coefficient = 0.05 µm²/s. Individual binding events were localized by fitting the point-spread function of each spot with a 2D Gaussian, and localizations were linked frame-by-frame to generate trajectories. For each cell, we computed a 2D projection map of Pol II and TAF1 by averaging the x, y positions of all binding events across the entire acquisition. Nuclear Pol II/TAF1 events were defined using nuclear boundaries derived from these projection maps, and events outside the nucleus were excluded from analysis.

Detailed instructions for running the Python scripts, ImageJ macro, and MATLAB code, including required toolboxes and dependencies, are provided in the GitHub repository listed in the Code Availability section.

GRID analysis

Dwell-time distributions were analyzed using GRID (Genuine Rate Identification) as described previously22. For each dataset, dwell times of chromatin-bound Pol II were compiled and corrected for photobleaching by supplying the experimentally measured bleaching rate as an additional decay component in the GRID fit. For single-cell analyses, the bleaching rate constant was determined separately for each cell; for aggregated analyses, the mean bleaching rate across all cells in a condition was used.

GRID decomposes the dwell-time survival function into a spectrum of exponential rate constants (“event spectrum”), from which we calculated the fractional contribution of each kinetic population to the total number of binding events during the imaging window. Events with fitted lifetimes shorter than the imaging frame interval ( < 1 s) were excluded from biological interpretation. For single-cell GRID analysis, we only included cells whose fitted model accounted for >94% of events. To assess the stability of the solutions, aggregated GRID data were bootstrap-resampled 100 times using 80% of events per iteration, and single-cell GRID data were resampled 100 times using 90% of each cell’s binding events.

Spatial heatmap analysis of residence-time probability (SHARP)

Spatial heatmap of Pol II binding was determined using a custom MATLAB pipeline (SHARP)56. For each nucleus, Pol II localizations were represented by their mean XY position and restricted to the nuclear envelope. We generated a per-nucleus 2D kernel density estimate (KDE) of event counts by placing each Pol II binding event within its average XY position in the nucleus and smoothing this with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM ≈ 3 pixels), yielding a “visit-frequency” heatmap in µm units.

In the DMSO dataset, Pol II clusters were defined as contiguous regions where the KDE exceeded the 97.5th percentile of values within that nucleus. Clusters with area <0.05 µm² and those with fewer than three binding events were excluded. The total number of Pol II clusters per cell was normalized to the total number of Pol II binding events. The normalized number of Pol II clusters was obtained by dividing the number of detected Pol II clusters by the total number of Pol II binding events for each cell. This value was then multiplied by 10,000 to obtain the total number of Pol II clusters formed per 10,000 Pol II binding events, as previously described21. For comparisons of cluster number across drug conditions, we applied the same clustering procedure but used a fixed global KDE threshold derived from the DMSO control (defined as the mean of the per-nucleus 97.5th-percentile KDE values). This allowed clusters in all conditions to be compared relative to a common density threshold. For the analysis in Fig. 6g, however, we used condition-specific KDE thresholds since the use of a fixed DMSO-derived threshold would artificially yield similar Pol II cycling events within clusters. We quantified the total number of clusters and mean cluster area across all conditions. Using the cluster masks, we also computed the percent Pol II entering productive elongation and the pausing index for events inside and outside clusters.

To limit the impact of extreme outlier clusters, we performed a robust z-score-based quality control on cluster area and event count across the dataset; nuclei containing clusters with very large robust |z| scores were flagged and excluded from analysis. These cells often contain very few ( < 5), large, contiguous clusters which ‘hug’ the outer nuclear envelope, likely representing extranuclear proteins (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

Proteomics analysis

Cell pellets were lysed and digested using the S-Trap micro kit (Protifi) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following digestion, peptides were desalted using a 96-well plate filter (Orochem) packed with 1 mg Oasis HLB C-18 resin (Waters). Samples were resuspended in 100 µL 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and loaded onto the equilibrated resin in the same buffer. The resin was washed with 100 µL 0.1% TFA, and peptides were eluted with 70 µL of 60% acetonitrile containing 0.1% TFA. Eluates were dried using a vacuum centrifuge. Peptide concentrations were measured using the Pierce quantitative colorimetric peptide assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 200 ng peptide was injected per LC-MS run.

Liquid Chromatography was performed on an UltiMate 3000 nanoLC system (Thermo Scientific) using a PepSep® Classic C18 column (25 cm × 75 µm, 1.5 µm particle size; Bruker), held at 50 °C. A 30-min linear gradient was applied at 200 nL/min, starting at 1% solvent B and increasing to 50% B between 5.1 and 35 min, then rising to 99% B at 35.1 min (held until 40 min), followed by re-equilibration to 1% B until 45 min. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile.

Mass spectrometry acquisition was performed on a timsTOF HT (Bruker Daltonics) using dia-PASEF mode. The instrument was set to scan 100-1700 m/z with an ion mobility (1/K0) range of 0.60–1.60 Vs/cm². Ion accumulation time was 100 ms across 26 Da isolation windows, yielding a duty cycle of approximately 1.8 ms per window. The CaptiveSpray source was operated at 1600 V with a dry gas flow of 3.0 L/min and a dry temperature of 180 °C.

Quantitative protein intensities (MaxLFQ) were obtained using the FragPipe DIA_SpecLib_Quant_diaPASEF workflow in conjunction with DIA-NN (v2.1) using default parameters and the UP000005640 (human) sequence database57,58. Proteins and peptides were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate (q ≤ 0.01). Missing values were imputed using missForest59 and differential protein abundance was assessed using limma60. KEGG pathway visualization was performed with pathview61.

Data Availability Statement

Raw imaging files have been deposited to BioImage Archive under the accession number S-BIAD3606. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository under the dataset identifier PXD072625. Source data are provided with this paper.

The STRAP (Single-molecule TRacking And Processing) and SHARP (Spatial Heatmap Analysis of Residence-time Probability) pipelines used in this study are available at https://github.com/codedbynayem. The software used for the proteomics dataset is available at https://github.com/cutleraging/Haque-2025. Pipelines and software are available under the GNU General Public License v3.0.
