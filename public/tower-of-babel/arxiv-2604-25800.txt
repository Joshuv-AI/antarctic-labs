Barriers to Universal Reasoning With Transformers (And How to Overcome Them)
Oliver Kraus, Yash Sarrof, Yuekun Yao, Alexander Koller, Michael Hahn, Saarland University

Abstract
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) has been shown to empirically improve Transformers’ performance, and theoretically increase their expressivity to Turing completeness. However, whether Transformers can learn to generalize to CoT traces longer than those seen during training is understudied. We use recent theoretical frameworks for Transformer length generalization and find that – under standard positional encodings and a finite alphabet – Transformers with CoT cannot solve problems beyond TC 0 \textbf{TC}^{0} , i.e. the expressivity benefits do not hold under the stricter requirement of length-generalizable learnability. However, if we allow the vocabulary to grow with problem size, we attain a length-generalizable simulation of Turing machines where the CoT trace length is linear in the simulated runtime up to a constant. Our construction overcomes two core obstacles to reliable length generalization: repeated copying and last-occurrence retrieval. We assign each tape position a unique signpost token, and log only value changes to enable recovery of the current tape symbol through counts circumventing both barriers. Further, we empirically show that the use of such signpost tokens and value change encodings provide actionable guidance to improve length generalization on hard problems.

3.1
Formalizing Length-Generalizable Learning of CoT

Formalization of CoT generation.

In
C-RASP
, one evaluates the program at the final input token to check if the input string is accepted. However, for CoT, beyond acceptance of a string, we also need a way to generate intermediate tokens autoregressively with
C-RASP
. We therefore introduce a corresponding autoregressive formalization of CoT applying both to Transformers and to CoT
C-RASP
programs, closely aligning with
21
.

Let
\Sigma
be the input alphabet, and let
\Xi\supseteq\Sigma
be a finite alphabet of symbols that may appear during chain-of-thought generation. We assume that
\Xi
contains two distinguished symbols

\langle SEP\rangle,\ \langle EOS\rangle\in\Xi\setminus\Sigma

used to mark the final answer and the end of generation, respectively. At the level relevant here, both transformers and CoT C-RASP programs induce a deterministic
next-token map

M:\Xi^{*}\to\Xi

which specifies, for each current prefix, which token is generated next. Informally, we start with input prefix
w
, and the model emits a token at a time, conditioning each prediction on the input and the trace generated so far.

Definition 3.1

(Autoregressive CoT generation)
.

Let
M:\Xi^{*}\to\Xi
be a next-token map, and let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.
We say that
M

generates
a string

U=U_{1}\cdots U_{m}\in\Xi^{*}

on input
w
if for each
k=1,\dots,m

M(wU_{1}\cdots U_{k-1})=U_{k}

Transformers with CoT.

We model a decoder-only transformer as a length-preserving map
T:\Xi^{*}\to\Xi^{*}

where, for any string

C-RASP with CoT.

A

Definition 3.2

(Computation of a partial function by CoT)

Let

Let

We use a function

When

Signposts:
Since we do not control the tokenizer or embedding space of these models, introducing entirely new signpost symbols is not straightforward. Instead, we use natural numbers as signpost-like markers: in our tasks these numbers do not otherwise appear in the input vocabulary, so they provide a simple way to introduce structured intermediate markers while remaining largely disjoint from the task tokens.
We evaluate Boolean Evaluation and S5 Permutation on 3 open-source LLMs spanning different model families and scales:

Prompts with signposts do not face such an issue, providing further evidence of the utility of how keeping in mind the learnability of the underlying CoT can result in better CoT formats and in turn better performance, even without any task-specific finetuning.

Value change:
To further show the universality of the value change idea, we consider the task of evaluating the value of a variable after various steps of code using LLMs (as depicted in Figure

Our results suggest that these ideas of signposts and value change can have practical implications for LLMs. We think that practitioners could consider training LLMs with some CoT traces that take these theoretically principled suggestions into account to achieve better performance.

5
Discussion

Comparison to Prior Constructions for Learnability of CoT.

Our work most closely relates to
21
, who obtained the first length-generalizable Turing completeness construction for Transformers, by extending C-RASP with
custom relative positional encodings
. We complement those results in two regards: (i) we target simulation of TMs and not Counter Machines as done in
21
. This allows us to make statements more closely linked to most prior constructions, and especially to establish a direct link between TM time complexity and CoT length. In contrast,
21
’s construction scales with the time complexity of Counter Machines, which may be higher because it relies on unary encoding as an intermediate step; (ii) we use APE and NoPE whereas
21
uses custom PEs that have not yet been used to train language models.

15
propose
Turing Programs
, a CoT strategy supporting length generalization.
Importantly,
15
disallow
n
-gram repetition in both input and CoT. While this mitigates the difficulty of copying, it also restricts the generality of their strategy, and appears incompatible with full Turing completeness.
Finally,
18
prove length generalization with gradient descent for CoT on permutation tracking. Crucially, they also require the presence of a growing infinite alphabet with identifiers for individual steps, making their work complementary to ours.

Relevance for Practical CoT Reasoning.
We now discuss the practical takeaways of our theoretical results.
The negative result (Theorem
3.4
) states that
only problems in
\textbf{TC}^{0}
can be solved by C-RASP CoT programs. Under the caveats discussed at the end of Section
3.2
, this explains why it is difficult to get transformers to length-generalize over nontrivial CoTs in practice. Our positive result is harder to map to practical situations: Theorem
3.8
assumes an infinite signpost alphabet, and Corollary
3.9
is about a potentially infinite sequence of transformers that are “trained” in a very particular way. While the empirical results of Section
4.1
illustrate that length generalization can be boosted with ordinary gradient descent (GD) learning, it remains an essential question for future research how GD can achieve this reliably. Furthermore, real transformers do not have infinite token alphabets. However, every real transformer has a fixed context window and can therefore only ingest instances up to a fixed length
N
. A signpost alphabet of size
N/2
is sufficient to index these positions, which makes length-generalizing training of real transformers on finite data theoretically feasible. This resonates with earlier empirical findings that larger token vocabularies can improve performance
(
51
;
50
)
. Finally, Theorem
3.8
relies on a specific CoT scheme tracking value changes of tape cells. It is conceivable that earlier empirical work would have found better length generalization with a value-change CoT scheme. However, actually defining value-change CoT for real-world problems, for which it is not obvious what a Turing machine would look like, can be tricky. We still hope that our results will facilitate the design of effective CoT schemes in the future.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we revisit the role of CoT in Transformer reasoning. If we consider length-generalizable learnability (unlike expressivity) – we show that the learnable regime remains bounded by
\textbf{TC}^{0}
, under standard PEs and a finite alphabet. In contrast, with a growing alphabet, Transformers can simulate TMs. Our construction uses signpost tokens to overcome the problem of repeated copying and value-change encodings to avoid non-unique retrievals. These ideas also provide practical principles for designing CoTs, improving performance on known ‘hard’ problems, suggesting that taking CoT learnability into account during formatting can improve performance at large.

Limitations of C-RASP CoTs

It was shown in
21
(their Proposition 4.1) that any function with super-polynomial NFA complexity has no C-RASP CoT; the same applies to
\textsc{C-RASP}[\text{Pos}]
.
This implies that certain tasks, such as copying a long string with repetitions, are out of reach for C-RASP even when CoT is allowed.
However, the result doesn’t rule out C-RASP CoTs for regular languages.
Here, we show a result that even has implications for regular languages:

Theorem A.1

(Restated from Theorem
3.4
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\Sigma^{*}

be its indicator function (i.e.,

has a CoT in
\textsc{C-RASP}[\text{Pos}]
, then
\mathcal{L}
is definable in
\textbf{TC}^{0}
.

Remarkably, this holds irrespective of the length of the CoT.

Proof.

The proof idea is an adaptation of Theorem 12 in
17
. Previously,
21
had used that theorem to show that C-RASP CoTs are not Turing-complete. Here, we strengthen this to showing membership in
\textbf{TC}^{0}
.

We give the input to Alice, and have Bob compute the CoT.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of all count terms in the C-RASP program; each of the form
C(i):=\#[j\leq i,\phi(j,i)]\psi(j)\wedge P(j)
where
\phi
is local or
\top
, and
\psi
is periodic or
\top
.
Let
L
be the length of the input.
In order to allow Bob to compute the CoT, it is sufficient for Alice to communicate
C(L-l),\dots,C(L)
for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, where

is a bound determined by the set of local functions included.

We now build a
\textbf{TC}^{0}
circuit deciding
\mathcal{L}
.
We compute the binary representation of each

for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
Each of these has
O(\log N)
bits, when
N
is the input length.
We thus obtain a string of length

By assumption, we can determine Bob’s output (and thus membership in
\mathcal{L}
) from this string.
As the set of such strings has cardinality polynomial in
N

\textbf{TC}^{0}
.

Alice can communicate
O(\log N)
bits that are sufficient for Bob to compute the CoT.
As these are counts definable in C-RASP, these bits are computable in
\textbf{TC}^{0}
.
Now, a
\textbf{TC}^{0}
circuit can hard-code the mapping from these

Hence, these labels are computable by
\textbf{TC}^{0}
circuits.
∎

Theorem A.3
.

(

Proof.

We explain the proof in the special case of a single-tape Turing machine to avoid notational clutter.
For the more general case of a multi-tape Turing machine, it suffices to add identifiers for the (finitely many) tapes to

We assume the convention that the output of the TM is

Let

\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Gamma,\sqcup,\delta,q_{0},H)

computing

is a finite set of states;

2.

\Gamma
is a finite tape alphabet with
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma

3.

\sqcup\in\Gamma\setminus\Sigma

4.

is the initial state;

5.

is the set of halting states;

6.

\delta:(Q\setminus H)\times\Gamma\to Q\times\Gamma\times\{L,R,S\}

B.1
Task Descriptions

This section describes the evaluated algorithmic tasks on a high level. For each task we give a high-level example. For the respective trained CoT constructions see section

Parity

In the Parity task, the model receives a string consisting of 0s and 1s and must evaluate whether the total number of 1s in the sequence is even or odd. After processing the full sequence, the model outputs either ”E” or ”O”, representing an even or odd final result, respectively. For both the training and evaluation datasets, the individual bits within the sequences are randomly sampled.

Task Example: Parity

Problem:
Evaluate whether the number of 1s in a sequence of binary digits is even or odd.

Input:

01101100

Expected Output:

Even

Boolean Evaluation

In the Boolean Evaluation task, the model is tasked with evaluating the truth value of a boolean formula. The input consists of a nested sequence of logical operations (i.e., AND, OR, NOT) applied to the primitives ”True” and ”False”. The model must parse the hierarchical structure of the expression and compute the intermediate truth values to arrive at the correct final state. After evaluating the full formula, the model outputs either ”True” or ”False”. For the training and evaluation sets, the Boolean formulas and their corresponding primitives are generated randomly to ensure diverse logical depths and sequence lengths.

Task Example: Boolean Evaluation

Problem:
Evaluate the final truth value of a nested logical expression.

Input Formula (AST Size = 4):

( NOT true ) AND false

Conceptual Resolution:

1.

Evaluate inner clause:
NOT true

\rightarrow

False

2.

Evaluate root operator:
False AND false

\rightarrow

False

Expected Output:

False

S5 Permutation

In this task, the model receives a natural language description of an initial state, which assigns five distinct objects to five variables (e.g., A = Apple, B = Banana). This initialization is followed by a sequence of state-changing operations consisting of pairwise ”swap” instructions that exchange the contents of two variables. The model must continuously track the shifting positions of all five objects throughout the sequence and output their final arrangement. Task length is defined as the number of pairwise swap operations. Both the initial object assignments and the sequence of swaps are randomly sampled for both the training and out-of-domain evaluation sets.

Task Example: S5 Permutation

Problem:
Track the locations of 5 distinct items across a series of swap operations.

1. Initialization:

A = Apple, B = Banana, C = Cat, D = Dog, E = Hat

2. Operations (Sequence Length = 2):

Step 1: Swap A and E

Step 2: Swap B and C

Expected Output (Final State):

A = Hat, B = Cat, C = Banana, D = Dog, E = Apple

Binary Permutation

This is the same task as S5 Permutation, but we restrict the object pool to only two objects (Cat, Dog) that are assigned to the five variables. We ensure to generate no problem instance where all variables are initialized to the same object, so the task does not become trivial.

Task Example: Binary Permutation

Problem:
Track the locations of 2 distinct items across a series of swap operations.

1. Initialization:

A = Cat, B = Dog, C = Cat, D = Dog, E = Dog

2. Operations (Sequence Length = 2):

Step 2: Swap B and C

Expected Output (Final State):

A = Dog, B = Cat, C = Dog, D = Dog, E = Cat

B.2.1
Parity

Data Generation:
For both training and evaluation, binary sequences of lengths bounded by the respective experimental setups are randomly sampled.

CoT Formats:
We compare a standard dense trace against our proposed value-change trace. The
Naive CoT
outputs the running parity state (’E’ or ’O’) after processing every single bit in the input sequence. B.2.2
Boolean Evaluation

Data Generation:
For both training and evaluation, we generate random Boolean formulas represented as Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) containing the literals
true
and
false
, and the operators
NOT
,
AND
, and
OR
. Sequence length is defined by the total number of nodes in the AST. To construct the dataset, we first sample a target sequence length uniformly at random from the length bounds specified by the given experiment. To guarantee that our dataset is structurally unbiased, we uniformly sample from the space of all valid Boolean formulas of that exact size. We achieve this by precomputing the exact combinatorial counts of valid formulas for each size under our grammar, and then recursively sampling operations weighted by these exact counts. This ensures a uniform distribution over all possible AST structures, avoiding the depth and branching biases inherent to naive top-down generation.

CoT Formats:
The Naive CoT requires the model to output the truth value (’T’ or ’F’) of each subformula in standard post-order without any explicit structural pointers. The signpost CoT annotates the input formula with unique signpost tokens for every operation and literal. Each literal is preceded by an index hint.
In addition, an operator is preceded by square brackets which point to the child operand it depends on
(e.g.,
[<0>, <1>] <2> AND

\rightarrow
evaluate the truth values associated with signpost tokens 0 and 1 with the operator AND). The formula can thus be solved by following the signpost tokens in their natural order.
In the trace, the model must prefix every evaluated truth value with its corresponding index, providing explicit retrieval targets for deeply nested clauses. The signpost tokens (numbers in angle brackets) are implemented as atomic tokens in the tokenizer.

B.2.3
S5 Permutation

Data Generation:
For both training and evaluation, an initial state is created by uniformly sampling five distinct items from a predefined object pool and assigning them to variables A through E. To prevent the model from relying on precise token distances to locate the next operation, the object pool is constructed with items of varying token lengths (four objects of length 1, four of length 2, and four of length 3). We find that this improves OOD generalization performance, as the model can not always rely on relative token distances to find the next operation. The sequence length is defined by the total number of subsequent pairwise swap operations. Swap operations are randomly sampled for each problem instance. As an additional difficulty, we introduce a ”repetitive” variant of the task. In this version, there is a 90% probability that a step in the prompt simply repeats the same swap operation from the previous step. This creates extended sequences of identical operations followed by abrupt changes to new swaps.

CoT Formats:
In the naive CoT, operations are provided in strictly chronological order, and the trace simply logs the updated state after each step. For the signpost CoT, we introduce two variants.
Version A prepends a unique signpost token to every swap operation. The operations themselves remain in chronological order, but we assign the signpost IDs randomly rather than sequentially. We find that forcing the model to retrieve these arbitrary IDs (rather than allowing it to rely on a predictable numerical counter) improves OOD performance.
Version B introduces a ”tape” block before the operations block. The tape lists the correct execution sequence of signpost tokens, but the actual operations block is randomly shuffled. Consequently, the task instance can only be solved by looking at the next signpost token in the tape and then retrieving the corresponding signpost token from the operations block. At test time, the operations are presented in the correct chronological order in the operations block to ensure comparability with the naive CoT format.
We find that this enforces the utilization of signpost tokens, boosting OOD performance on longer sequences.

Version B exhibits superior performance on longer training lengths. Therefore, we use version B for evaluations at training length 50, and version A for evaluations at training length 30. Finally, we note that the signpost tokens (numbers in angle brackets) are implemented as atomic tokens in the whitespace tokenizer.

B.2.4
Binary Permutation

Data Generation:

Data is generated the same way as for S5 Permutation. As described before, we restrict the object set to two objects. We ensure to generate problem instances where each object occurs at least once, to avoid trivial instances.

CoT Formats:

The naive and signpost CoT are the same as for S5 Permutation. The value-change CoT format introduces write (W_) and keep markers (K_) to denote whether the state for a given variable was changed or not. The final state is then computed in a resolution block by comparing the total number of incoming transitions to a state (e.g., Dog_Cat representing a transition from Dog to Cat) against the outgoing transitions from that state (e.g., Cat_Dog). We implement the object transitions as atomic tokens, as we find that this helps the model to learn this counting logic.

Training Setup:

As the Binary Permutation task includes some reasoning tokens that disproportionally contribute to task success compared to simple marker tokens (e.g. ’swap’, or ’.’), standard SGD can struggle to reliably isolate and learn these sparse, critical operations without falling into local minima. Specifically, we applied a 7x multiplier to the cross-entropy loss for comparison tokens (e.g., ==,
<
,
>
) to enforce the underlying counting logic, a 7x weight to variable identifiers (A through E), and a 2x weight to the signpost tokens.

B.3
Discussion on S5

Empirically, we found
S5 Permutation
to be harder to train than
Parity
and
Boolean Evaluation
: it was more seed-sensitive, and required more optimization effort, and as Figure
4
and Figure
6
show, they exhibit weaker length generalization than others. A plausible explanation is that
S5
admits more
shortcuts
, i.e., shallow heuristics that fit the finite training distribution without implementing the intended algorithm in a length-generalizable way. We think this might be true, as each reasoning step of S5 uses many more tokens per reasoning step compared to the other tasks. For permutation tasks, perhaps the presence of local patterns and heuristics can yield good performance, without having to solve the entire task. However, the harder cases would not be solved by such methods. To bring out the difference more starkly, our reported accuracy numbers in Figure
4
and Figure
6
are on harder datasets for S5, involving many repetitions of the same operation. However, in Figure
7
we compare the performance of S5 on a dataset involving uniform sampling of operations (standard) vs. on a dataset with many more repeated operations. We see that although non-signpost runs are worse than the ones with signposts, they do exhibit higher performance than in the repeated case. This indicates that in a normal uniform sampling of the task, the underlying answer might have many shortcuts. Additionally, as each reasoning step takes more tokens, the CoT traces for S5 are overall much longer than for
Parity
and
Boolean
. This leads to extensive training times, and we think our models might have been undertrained. Sustained training and better hyperparameter optimization should help in achieving even higher generalization.

Figure 6:
Evaluation curves for length 30 for all our tasks. We observe similar trends as seen in Figure
4
, where versions with signpost tokens generalize better than ones without.

Figure 7:
S5 becomes harder when the data contains more repetitive operations. We compare the standard S5 dataset (left) with a harder variant containing more repetitive operations (right), for training lengths 30 and 50. Although signpost-augmented CoTs improve generalization in both cases, performance degrades earlier on the harder variant. We hypothesize that this is because on the easier case, since there are not enough repetitions, there might local patterns that the models could exploit to help solve the S5 task without actually solving it fully. Availability of such shortcut strategies makes optimization toward the intended algorithm more difficult.

B.4
Binary Permutation Experiments

To further highlight the potential of learning value-change formats, we evaluate
Binary Permutation
as an ablation of the
S5 Permutation
task. This task restricts the object set to just two objects which can be assigned to more than one variable (Task description in Appendix
B.1
). As a consequence, this task produces more repetitions of objects in the state updates, as well as swap operations that do not alter the state (i.e. when the incoming and outgoing object is the same). Next to the CoT formats we evaluated for
S5 Permutation
, we also introduce a value-change CoT scheme (see Appendix
B.2.4
). In Figure
8
we report the results for training length 30 on this task. The results confirm that combining signpost tokens with value-change encoding yields the strongest length generalization, outperforming both the naive CoT and signpost-only formats, across both the standard and repetitive variant of the task.

Notably, the signpost-only format performs worse on this task than on the
S5 Permutation
task. We quantitatively confirm that this degradation is driven by highly repetitive state updates through an error analysis (see Figure
9
). We check the best performing seed for each CoT scheme for the first occurring error in the trace across all test lengths. We find that the majority of errors are caused by incorrect copy operations (i.e. copying the wrong signpost token or the wrong variables in a swap operation) and incorrect state updates (i.e. the model outputs the correct swap operation, but fails to correctly update the state from the previous state). For the value-change CoT format, an incorrect state update is registered when the model outputs a keep-token instead of a write-token (and vice-versa). Generally, we observe that signpost tokens substantially reduce wrong copy errors relative to the naive CoT, consistent with their theoretical role in addressing the copying barrier. For longer test lengths, incorrect state updates become the most dominant error case for both the signpost and naive CoT format. In contrast, the number of such errors remain low for the value-change format, providing further evidence that such an encoding successfully helps to overcome the non-unique retrieval barrier. However, as test lengths increase further, we still observe a gradual rise in ’wrong copy’ errors for the value-change format. This suggests that while our formatting neutralizes the non-unique retrieval bottleneck, the model still struggles to use signposts to reliably disambiguate the swap operations as test lengths approach 2x training length.

Figure 8:
Evaluation curves for training length 30 for the Binary Permutation task. Even with signpost CoT, the task is still harder than the S5 Permutation task, as the model struggles to correctly update the state after swap operations, because increased repetition of objects in the trace makes the difficulty of non-unique retrieval. The Value-Change CoT successfully mitigates this non-unique retrieval problem. For analysis of the error cases, see Figure
9
.

Figure 9:
Frequency of first-occurring errors across test lengths for our three evaluated CoT formats on Binary Permutation. Wrong copy operations (incorrect signpost retrieval or variable pairs) and incorrect state updates are the most prevalent error types. At longer test lengths, incorrect state updates become the dominant failure mode for naive and signpost CoTs - consistent with the non-unique retrieval barrier - while the value-change format successfully mitigates this.

Signpost Experiment:
We slightly adapt the task formats for our algorithmic tasks to make them more natural looking. While a more minimalist task format is advantageous for the from-scratch experiments (as it reduces the total number of processed tokens), pretrained LLMs benefit from formats that align more closely with the natural language distribution of their pretraining data.
For instance, we remove unnecessary whitespaces (which we needed for the whitespace tokenizer in the from-scratch experiments), replace the signpost tokens from the from-scratch experiments with natural numbers, and add ”=” signs for the S5 Permutation task, to make models better understand the assignment of objects to variables.

Consistent with our from-scratch evaluations, all models are evaluated using greedy decoding to ensure reproducible results. For both the baseline and signpost CoT formats, we evaluate the models in a 5-shot setting, providing five in-context examples that mirror the respective CoT traces used in our from-scratch training setups.
