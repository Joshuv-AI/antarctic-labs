Metabolomics reveals phospholipid metabolism drives appressorium turgor in Colletotrichum gloeosporioides
Authors: Manyang Zhang, Fuhan Li, Fanli Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColletotrichum gloeosporioides infects the plant host via an appressorium, which generates turgor pressure by metabolizing certain internal compounds. The mechanical force created by the turgor pressure is required for the production of infection pegs that penetrate host epidermal cells. Thus, appressorium turgor pressure must be sufficient for C. gloeosporioides infection. The regulated metabolism of intracellular metabolites or compounds plays a key role in the process underlying increases in turgor pressure.ResultsIn this study, we analyzed appressorial metabolites at different developmental stages using a non-targeted metabolomics approach. We identified 39 differentially accumulated metabolites associated with turgor pressure and classified them into six major groups: amino acids, fatty acids, phospholipids, glycerolipids, carbohydrates, and organic acids. Four notable metabolic pathways related to turgor pressure were identified: degradation of carbohydrates, degradation of lipids, amino acid (arginine) synthesis, and phospholipid metabolism. Moreover, the phospholipid metabolism may be important for the development of appressoria and the required increase in turgor pressure. Three representative inhibitors of phospholipid metabolism (Neomycin, Doxorubicin, and alexidine dihydrochloride) were selected to study the relationship of phospholipid metabolism on the development of appressorium and changes in turgor pressure in C. gloeosporioides. Neomycin was shown to be a potent inhibitor of C. gloeosporioides and could effectively control poplar anthracnose.ConclusionsIt was found that phospholipid metabolism is associated with the required increase in appressorium turgor. Our findings provide new insights into the mechanism underlying appressorium turgor pressure formation as well as potential targets for improving the control of C. gloeosporioides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13014-0.

Background

Poplar anthracnose is mainly caused by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides [1], which is a fungus that can infect a wide range of economic and timber tree species, such as walnut, mango, and Camellia oleifera, with the associated damage to leaves, branches, or fruits resulting in substantial decreases in agroforestry production [2]. Plants infected with C. gloeosporioides develop brown circular leaf spots that eventually form concentric rings [3].

The pathogenicity of plant pathogenic fungi depends on their ability to infect and then grow in susceptible host plants. When plant pathogenic fungi invade the host, they usually produce specialized infestation structures (e.g., appressorium, infection cushion, and adhesion structure) to facilitate the infection and establish a parasitic relationship with the host, which is a result of the long-term co-evolution of pathogenic fungi and host plants [4]. Many plant pathogenic fungi produce an appressorium, including the pathogens responsible for anthracnose, rice blast, and rust [5, 6]. Earlier research indicated the C. gloeosporioides appressorium germinates penetration pegs that can break through the leaf cuticle [7]. When conidia attach to the leaf surface, their apical part begins to sprout and form a germ tube, after which the apical portion of the germ tube expands to form an appressorium that accumulates relatively large amounts of various metabolites. Simultaneously, a melanin layer forms on the inner side of the appressorial wall, thereby decreasing cell porosity and preventing the outflow of glycogen, glycerol produced by lipid degradation, and other macromolecular substances. Through osmosis, water is drawn into the appressorium, which subsequently generates turgor pressure that is converted to mechanical force, leading to the production of slender penetration pegs at the base of the appressorium for the subsequent penetration of host cells [8]. Thus, appressorium formation and development are critical for the pathogenicity of C. gloeosporioides.

A previous study revealed that the appressorium turgor pressure of wild-type C. gloeosporioides increases over time (9.60 ± 1.04 MPa at 5 h, 11.38 ± 0.65 MPa at 9 h, and 13.02 ± 1.54 MPa at 12 h) [9]. Both C. gloeosporioides and Magnaporthe oryzae have similar infection processes involving the production of germ tubes and appressoria after conidia germinate. However, because of differences in physiological structures and the growth environment at the leaf surface of woody and herbaceous plants, M. oryzae requires 24 h to produce mature appressoria (turgor pressure of approximately 8 Mpa), whereas C. gloeosporioides requires only 12 h. Appressorium development depends on compounds transferred from the conidium rather than exogenous nutrients. The most abundant solutes in appressoria are sugars, glycerol derived from lipid degradation, and other alcohols; the germinating conidium uses glycogen and lipid as sources of energy and material necessary for appressorium development in different plant and animal pathogenic fungi [10–12]. Turgor pressure in appressoria is mainly generated by various metabolic substances that accumulate to considerable levels. Hence, appressorium turgor pressure changes are regulated by the metabolism of certain compounds within the appressorium. Earlier research showed that glycogen and lipid droplets are the main storage reserves in conidia. Lipid catabolism produces glycerol, which is the main source of turgor pressure, while glycogen metabolism provides the energy required for infections [13, 14]. However, relatively little is known about other metabolic pathways associated with appressorium development. More specifically, the metabolites contributing to the C. gloeosporioides appressorium turgor pressure remain uncharacterized.

As a prevalent metabolic pathway in eukaryotes, phospholipid metabolism is closely related to a series of processes such as conidia development, appressorium formation, changes in turgor pressure, and infestation capacity during the early infestation stage of plant pathogenic fungi. In M. oryzae, MoPsd2 encoded the phosphatidylserine decarboxylase gene, resulting in elevated phosphatidylserine levels and reduced phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) levels, which suggested that MoPsd2 was involved in PE biosynthesis and contributes to M. oryzae pathogen development and plant invasion. Doxorubicin, a well-recognised PSD inhibitor, induces lipid peroxidation and inhibits PSD activity [15]. In M. oryzae, the cardiolipin biosynthesis-related gene MoGep4 regulated its mycelial growth, conidiation, cardiolipin biosynthesis and virulence. Chemical alexidine dihydrochloride (AXD) inhibited the enzyme activity of MoGep4, cardiolipin biosynthesis and mitophagy [16]. Phospholipase C (PLC)-mediated Ca2+ signal responds to the pathogenicity of fungi [17, 18], and the PLC-specific inhibitor Neomycin can interfere with intracellular Ca2+ release by inhibiting PLC activity and suppressing PIP2 degradation, thereby attenuating pathogenicity [19].

In this study, a non-targeted metabolomics analysis [20] of different appressorium developmental stages revealed differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) associated with appressorium turgor pressure as well as significant changes in four key metabolic pathways. Moreover, we have identified a fungicide candidate, Neomycin, which may inhibit C. gloeosporioides appressorium formation, turgor pressure reduction, and plant infestation. The results indicated that Neomycin may be effective in the control of poplar anthracnose. The findings of this study may be useful for further elucidating the metabolic regulation related to turgor pressure changes in developing C. gloeosporioides appressoria, while also providing the theoretical basis for developing new methods for preventing and managing anthracnose in forest trees.

Methods

Fungal strain and culture conditions

The fungal strain used in this study was C. gloeosporioides CFCC80308 [21], which was isolated from a lesion on a leaf of an infected Populus × beijingensis tree growing in Beijing, China. Axenic cultures were deposited in the China Forestry Culture Collection Center (CFCC). The strain was cultured on potato dextrose agar medium (Oxoid, UK) in plates at 25 °C with 70% relative humidity (RH).

Selection and collection of fungal materials

Appressoria were induced on a hydrophobic surface of the GelBond® Film Agarose gel support medium (53734, Lonza, America), with samples collected at the following five time points: 0 h (conidium), 3 h (germ tube), 5 h (early appressorium), 9 h (gradually maturing appressorium), and 12 h (mature appressorium). These time points were selected according to a previous study on C. gloeosporioides appressorium turgor pressure formation [9]. The group of 0 h treatments was used as a control. The conidia of the C. gloeosporioides CFCC80308 strain was harvested from 5-day-old cultures growing on PDA medium in plates at 25 °C with 70% RH. Conidial suspensions (30 µL, 2 × 104 conidia/mL ) were prepared using sterile distilled water and then carefully added to the Gelbond membrane. To induce appressorium formation, the Gelbond membrane was incubated at 25 °C with 70% RH in darkness. The samples collected at the five time points were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen prior to storage at − 80 °C prior to lyophilization.

Metabolome assay sample preparation

The freeze-dried samples were ground to a uniform powder and storage at − 80 °C [22]. For each sample collection time point, metabolites were extracted from 8 mg powder samples for a metabolome assay (Each group consisted of six biological replicates). Briefly, each sample was soaked in an extraction solvent containing isopropanol/acetonitrile/water (3:3:2, v: v:v) with 1 mL internal standard (umbelliferone, 10 µg/mL) to extract and vortexed thoroughly before metabolites were extracted via sonication using circulating water for 60 min at room temperature. The solution was vortexed for 30 s and then centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected for an analysis of the metabolites using a liquid chromatography (LC)-mass spectrometry (MS) system [23]. Alternatively, the supernatant was retained for a metabolite derivatization step prior to the gas chromatography (GC)-MS analysis. Specifically, 800 µL supernatant was added to a pointed-bottom injection vial and dried using nitrogen gas. The dried metabolite sample was added to 50 µL pyridine methoxylate, after which the solution was vortexed and mixed (180 rpm) for 90 min at 37 °C. Next, 50 µL 20 mg/mL silylation reagent (i.e., MSTFA) was added and the solution was vortexed and then mixed (180 rpm) for 60 min at 60 °C. To complete the metabolite derivatization step, the solution was cooled to room temperature before being analyzed using a GC-MS system.

GC-MS and LC-MS analyses

Extracted metabolites were quantified and characterized on the basis of GC-MS and LC-MS analyses, which identified the differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs) during C. gloeosporioides appressorium formation. The GC-MS analysis was conducted using an Agilent DB-5MS column (30 × 0.25 × 0.25 mm), an Agilent 7890 A series GC system and a 5975 C mass selective detector with an Agilent G4513A injector (Agilent, USA). The test conditions were as follows: maintain at 70 °C for 4 min, then increase to 310 °C at 15 °C /min, and hold for 15 min, with helium as the carrier gas. The flow rate was 1.2 mL/min, with an injection temperature of 300 °C and a sample size of 1 µL with a 10:1 split ratio. The LC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 LC system coupled with an Agilent 6540 ultra-high definition quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source.

Nutritional growth test

To investigate the effects of Neomycin, Doxorubicin, and AXD on the nutrient growth of C. gloeosporioides, agar plugs (5 mm in diameter) were taken from the edge of WT colonies using a hole punch. These plugs were then inoculated onto PDA plates supplemented with 0.1 mM Neomycin, 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, or 0.1 mM alexidine dihydrochloride (AXD), respectively. The plates were incubated in the dark at 25 °C in a constant temperature incubator for 4 days. The nutrient growth of each strain was observed and photographed for analysis. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Conidial germination, appressorium development, formation rate and blackening rate tests

WT strains cultured for 5 days on PDA solid medium supplemented with 0.1 mM Neomycin, 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, or 0.1 mM AXD, respectively. Medium was rinsed with sterile water, without inhibitors added, to obtain conidial suspensions. The concentration of the conidial suspension was adjusted to 2 × 10⁴ conidia/mL using a hemocytometer. An equal volume (30 µL) of conidial suspension from each strain was dropwise added to the hydrophobic surface of a GelBond membrane and incubated in the dark at 25 °C in a constant temperature incubator. The development of appressoria in each strain was observed and counted at different time points (0 h, 3 h, 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h) using a light microscope, and images were captured. After 12 h of culture, the appressoria of each strain were selected to determine their formation rate and blackening rate. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Turgor pressure determination

In this study, PEG 2000 solutions with concentrations of 0.4 g/mL, 0.3 g/mL, and 0.2 g/mL were used as hyperosmotic agents to determine the turgor pressure inside appressoria. The inhibitor was added to PDA plates, medium was rinsed with sterile water, without inhibitors added, to obtain conidial suspensions Conidial suspensions (30 µL, 2 × 10⁴ conidia/mL) of WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin, WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, and WT + 0.1 mM AXD were inoculated onto the hydrophobic surface of GelBond membranes, and appressorium formation was induced in the dark at 25 °C. At 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h, the GelBond membranes containing conidia were transferred to slides, and the spore solution was replaced with different concentrations of PEG 2000. The samples were then incubated for 10 min in a dark environment. Subsequently, the collapse of appressoria at different time points was observed and counted using a light microscope, and the collapse rate of appressoria was calculated. The osmotic pressure of the solution that caused 50% of the appressoria to collapse was recorded as an estimate of the appressorium turgor pressure. A minimum of 200 appressoria were counted for each strain, and all experiments were performed in triplicate.

Pathogenicity test

Poplar branches were hydroponically cultivated for 6–8 weeks, and new leaves were collected. The leaves were surface-sterilized by treatment with 75% alcohol once (Surface sterilization time: approximately 10 s), followed by three rinses with sterile water. The inhibitor was added to PDA plates, then washed with sterile water to obtain a conidial suspension. The left side of each leaf was inoculated with a conidial suspension of the WT strain, while the right side was inoculated with a conidial suspension of the WT strain supplemented with 0.1 mM Neomycin, 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, or 0.1 mM AXD (30 µL, 2 × 10⁴ conidia/mL). The base of each petiole was wrapped with moistened absorbent cotton, and sterile water was added daily to maintain moisture. The leaves were incubated at 25 °C for one week, during which photographs were taken to document disease progression. The size of the lesions was measured on the sixth day. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed according to the orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) method, which allowed us to filter out the orthogonal variables, which are not correlated with categorical variables, and analyze the non-orthogonal and orthogonal variables separately, thereby obtaining reliable information regarding the degree of the correlation between the DAMs and experimental groups. The ropls package in R (3.3.2) was used for the OPLS-DA model calculation. To analyze biological replicates, a method combining the multiplicity of differences, the P value of the t-test, and the variable importance in projection (VIP) value of the OPLS-DA model was adopted to screen for DAMs. The following criteria were applied to screen for DAMs: fold-change > 1.50, P < 0.05, and VIP > 1. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.kegg.jp/) was used to identify enriched pathways among the DAMs. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. When the ANOVA indicated a statistically significant overall effect (p < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using LSD and Duncan’s multiple range test to identify specific group differences.

Results

Appressorial metabolome profile

Conidial suspensions (2 × 104 conidia/mL) were added to the surface of a GelBond hydrophobic membrane, which was subsequently incubated for 0, 3, 5, 9, and 12 h. Images of the conidia (0 h), germ tubes (3 h), early appressoria (5 h), gradually maturing appressoria (9 h), and mature appressoria (12 h) were captured (Fig. 1a). A total of 214 metabolites were identified by GC-MS (Table S1). Additionally, 133, 163, 120, and 104 DAMs were revealed by the 0 h vs. 3 h, 0 h vs. 5 h, 0 h vs. 9 h, and 0 h vs. 12 h comparisons, respectively (Table S2). The identified metabolites included amino acids, organic acids, sugars, polyols, and nitrogenous compounds. Using the negative ion mode of the LC-MS system, we identified 112 metabolites, including 66, 67, 67, and 71 of the DAMs revealed by the 0 h vs. 3 h, 0 h vs. 5 h, 0 h vs. 9 h, and 0 h vs. 12 h comparisons, respectively (Table S3). The identified metabolites included phosphocholines, fatty acids, organic acids, sugars, and nitrogenous compounds. In contrast, using the positive ion mode of the LC-MS system, we identified 666 metabolites, including 271, 276, 275, and 357 of the DAMs revealed by the 0 h vs. 3 h, 0 h vs. 5 h, 0 h vs. 9 h, and 0 h vs. 12 h comparisons, respectively (Table S4). The identified metabolites included fatty acids, organic acids, phosphocholines, polyols, sugars and nitrogenous compounds. The metabolomics data were included in a principal component analysis, which divided 15 samples (three replicates at five time points) into four groups (Fig. S2a–c), reflecting the significant changes in the metabolite profiles of developing appressoria.

An OPLS-DA was conducted using the GC-MS and LC-MS data for the 0 h and 12 h samples. The OPLS-DA model clearly distinguished between the 0 h and 12 h samples, indicative of significant differences between the two time points (Fig. S1a–c). By combining the t-test and the VIP value analysis, we identified 39 metabolites that accumulated differentially between the conidium stage (0 h) and the mature appressorium stage (12 h) (Fig. 1b). The lysine, threonine, glutamate, and arginine contents (amino acids) increased significantly after appressoria formed. Similarly, the linolenic acid, stearic acid, and palmitoleic acid contents (fatty acid compounds) increased significantly after appressoria formed. Increases and decreases in phospholipid and organic acid contents were detected; the compounds whose content increased significantly after appressoria formed included phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphoethanolamine (PE), phosphoglycolic acid, and citric acid. The polyol content also increased significantly.

To explore the changes in metabolite contents during appressorium formation, we completed a heat map analysis using their relative contents at all time points (Fig. 1c). We also examined the changes in glucose-1-phosphate and glucose contents. These carbohydrate analogs initially accumulated, reaching peak levels at 5 h, after which their contents decreased at 9 and 12 h (Fig. 1c). These findings suggest that carbohydrates may be degraded as the turgor pressure increases.

Significant changes in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism during C. gloeosporioides appressorium development and turgor pressure formation

We next assigned DAMs to biosynthesis pathways on the basis of the KEGG database. Moreover, we conducted a literature search to determine whether any of the DAMs are reportedly involved in C. gloeosporioides metabolic processes. The KEGG analysis of the 0 h and 12 h samples revealed the following four significantly enriched pathways: degradation of lipids, degradation of carbohydrates, amino acid (arginine) synthesis, and phospholipid metabolism (Fig. 2a).

Carbohydrate degradation provides the energy required for turgor pressure formation. The metabolism of glucose and its derivatives involves a complex NADP(H)-dependent genetic switch controlled primarily by trehalose-6-phosphate synthase 1 (TPS1), which activates glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) in the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, resulting in the production of NADPH from glucose-6-phosphate (G6P). NADPH can bind to TPS1 to maintain a balance between G6P consumption and NADPH production. Subsequently, energy in the form of NADPH and ATP is generated via glycolysis (Fig. 2b).

Our LC-MS analysis in the positive ion mode detected changes in galactosylglycerol accumulation (Fig. 2c). During glycerolipid metabolism, galactosylglycerol is the final metabolite. As appressoria developed and the turgor pressure increased (i.e., 0–12 h), the amount of galactosylglycerol gradually increased, indicative of an active lipolytic process. In addition, β-oxidation produces acetyl CoA, accompanied by NADH and FADH2 molecules, which, in the mitochondria, can be oxidised by oxidative phosphorylation of the TCA cycle, thus generating ATP, which provides energy to the appressorium development. But these reducing equivalents in the peroxisome must be dependent on other forms of reoxidation (Fig. 2b). According to our GC-MS metabolomics analysis, lactic acid accumulated in C. gloeosporioides appressoria (Fig. 2d). The production of lactic acid, coupled with the generation of NAD+, serves to resolve the issue of insufficient oxidative capacity, providing energy for subsequent appressorium development and turgor pressure formation.

Amino acid (arginine) synthesis

Our results indicated that arginine biosynthesis was an enriched pathway among the DAMs (Fig. 3) at 5 h and the subsequent time points, which corresponded to the period in which appressoria were developing and turgor pressure was being generated. From 5 to 12 h, the glutamine content decreased, which contrasted to the accumulation of glutamate and N-acetylglutamate. The precursor metabolite glutamine was continuously degraded for the synthesis of other metabolites as appressoria developed and the turgor pressure increased from 5 h onwards. The ornithine and arginine content were maintained at a high level throughout the analyzed time period, suggestive of the importance of the arginine biosynthetic pathway for appressorium development and turgor pressure formation in C. gloeosporioides.

Phospholipid metabolism

Among the identified DAMs, the main glycerophospholipids were phosphatidylcholine (PC), 1-acyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (LysoPC), PE, sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (GPE), and sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (GPC). We analyzed the contents of specific phospholipids rather than the total phospholipid content. The PC and PE contents had an overall increasing trend from 0 to 12 h. In contrast, LysoPC accumulated gradually from 5 to 12 h, whereas the GPC and GPE contents had an overall decreasing trend (Fig. 4b). In this study, PS was detected, but LysoPE was not. The catabolism of PS results in the production of large amounts of PC and PE as well as the generation of LysoPC and LysoPE, which are then converted to GPC and GPE, respectively, in reactions mediated by phospholipase A1 (PLA1). Both compounds are catabolized by glycerophosphoryldiester phosphodiesterase (GDE) to sn-glycerol-3-phosphate (Fig. 4a), which may be converted to glycerol by glycerol-1-phosphatase (GPP) (Fig. S3), ultimately resulting in an increase in turgor pressure.

Effects of Neomycin, Doxorubicin, and AXD on mycelial growth, appressorium development, turgor pressure changes, and pathogenicity of C. gloeosporioides

Due to the possible correlation between phospholipid metabolism and the formation of an appressorium, we carried out experiments to investigate effects of Neomycin, Doxorubicin, and AXD on mycelial growth, appressorium development, turgor pressure changes, and pathogenicity of C. gloeosporioides. The results showed that the colony morphology and diameter of WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin, WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, and WT were essentially the same, while the colony diameter of WT + 0.1 mM AXD was significantly reduced (Fig. 5a). In this study, conidial suspensions of WT, WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin, WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, and WT + 0.1 mM AXD were inoculated onto GelBond membranes for hydrophobic culture, and their developmental status was observed and photographed at 0 h, 3 h, 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h. It was found that the developmental process of appressoria in the presence of Neomycin, Doxorubicin, and AXD showed no significant abnormalities compared to that of WT (Fig. 5b). This indicates that none of the three phospholipid metabolism inhibitors affected conidial germination, germ tube development, or appressorium formation in C. gloeosporioides (Fig. 5b).

Next, the formation rate and blackening rate of appressoria incubated for 12 h were determined. The appressorium formation rate of WT was 80.05%, and the blackening rate was 85.35%. In contrast, the appressorium formation and blackening rates of WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin, WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, and WT + 0.1 mM AXD were significantly decreased, with appressorium formation rates of 30.51%, 45.14%, and 35.46%, and blackening rates of 37.26%, 64.93%, and 45.43%, respectively. The most significant decrease in both appressorium formation and blackening rates was observed for WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin (Fig. 5c). These results indicate that the addition of these three phospholipid metabolism inhibitors suppressed both the appressorium formation rate and the blackening rate, although C. gloeosporioides was still able to generate appressoria.

To investigate whether these three phospholipid metabolism inhibitors affect turgor pressure changes in C. gloeosporioides, we replaced the spore solution with PEG 2000 at concentrations of 0.4 g/mL, 0.3 g/mL, and 0.2 g/mL at 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h, followed by dark treatment for 10 min. Subsequently, the collapse rate of appressoria for each strain was counted, and the turgor pressure was calculated. The turgor pressure of WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin was found to be 4.66 ± 1.14 MPa, 8.28 ± 1.87 MPa, and 10.82 ± 1.33 MPa at 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h, respectively. The turgor pressure of WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin was 3.04 ± 0.97 MPa, 8.26 ± 0.32 MPa, and 9.8 ± 0.74 MPa at 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h, respectively. The turgor pressure of WT + 0.1 mM AXD was 5.94 ± 1.13 MPa, 9.3 ± 1.36 MPa, and 11.42 ± 1.86 MPa at 5 h, 9 h, and 12 h, respectively. Compared with the turgor pressure of WT, all turgor pressures decreased after the addition of the inhibitors, with the turgor pressures of WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin and WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin showing more significant reductions (Fig. 5d–e).

The appressorium of C. gloeosporioides is closely related to pathogenicity. In this study, phospholipid metabolism inhibitors exerted inhibitory effects on the appressorium formation rate, blackening rate, and turgor pressure of C. gloeosporioides. To further investigate the potential effects of phospholipid metabolism on pathogenicity, conidial suspensions (30 µL, 2 × 10⁴/mL) of WT, WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin, WT + 0.4 mM Doxorubicin, and WT + 0.1 mM AXD were inoculated onto intact isolated poplar leaves, and lesion formation was recorded. As shown in the Fig. 5f-i, only WT + 0.1 mM Neomycin exhibited reduced lesion size, indicating that Neomycin alone could inhibit the pathogenicity of C. gloeosporioides.

Discussion

After C. gloeosporioides conidia attach to the surface of poplar leaves, they germinate and form a germ tube. The bud tube tip expands to form an early attachment cell, in which the metabolism of specific substances generates turgor pressure. The turgor pressure provides the mechanical force for the infection peg formed at the base of the appressorium, thereby enabling penetration of the poplar leaf epidermis and successful invasion of the underlying cells. Therefore, the regulated metabolism of attachment cell components is critical for C. gloeosporioides appressorial development, including the generation of turgor pressure, and the completion of the infection process [9].

In the present study, after analyzing the main DAMs during appressorium development, we determined the identification of 214 metabolites by GC-MS, 112 metabolites by LC-MS (negative ion mode), and 666 metabolites by LC-MS (positive ion mode). The identified metabolites included amino acids, fatty acids, organic acids, phospholipids, carbohydrates and glycerolipids, and these metabolites are enriched in lipid degradation, carbohydrate degradation, amino acid (arginine) synthesis, and phospholipid metabolic pathways. In the study, the amino acids, fatty acids, polyols, PC, PE, phosphoglycolic acid, and citric acid increased significantly after appressoria formed. On the other hand, glucose-1-phosphate and glucose contents analog initially accumulated, reaching peak levels at 5 h, after which their contents decreased at 9 and 12 h (Fig. 1c), which is suggesting that the changes in metabolites may contribute to the accumulation of turgor pressure in appressoria of C. gloeosporioides.

Meanwhile, we also identified that the greatest differences between the samples collected at 0 h and those collected at 12 h are likely related to physiological activities that are required to generate turgor pressure for the subsequent penetration of the host surface. The M. oryzae appressorium can penetrate the host in approximately 24 h, suggesting that the internal components of the appressorium undergo substantial changes in the first 24 h to increase the turgor pressure. After a successful colonization, the nutrients within appressoria are basically depleted, necessitating the absorption of plant cellular nutrients to satisfy physiological needs [24]. Similarly, in our collected samples, there were major changes in metabolite contents between 0 h (conidium) and 12 h (mature appressorium). Glycerol derived from lipolysis and triacylglycerol are the main sources of turgor pressure [24]. The glycogen and lipid droplets are the main storage reserves in conidia. A previous study showed that glycogen metabolism is involved in supplying the energy required for an infection [10]. In M. oryzae, the metabolism of glucose and its derivatives involves a complex NADP(H)-dependent genetic switch regulated by TPS1, which activates G6PDH in the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, leading to the production of NADPH from G6P. The interaction between NADPH and TPS1 helps to maintain an appropriate balance between G6P consumption and NADPH production [25]. Related studies indicated that glucose is first degraded by the pentose phosphate pathway and then by the glycolytic pathway to generate energy (NADPH and ATP) [26]. Considered together, these findings imply carbohydrates are rapidly degraded via the pentose phosphate and glycolytic pathways during appressorium development. Moreover, the deactivation of the NADP(H)-dependent genetic switch is conducive to the colonization of the host by the pathogen (Fig. 2b) [24]. Besides, it was reported that the ΔMaNCP1 strain of Metarhizium acridum on the locust hind wings was significantly decreased and lipid droplet degradation in appressoria via the autophagy pathway, which enables rapid turgor generation and penetration of insect hosts [27]. In summary, metabolite contents change dramatically at different appressorium developmental stages of different fungi, while turgor pressure is generated in the appressorium because of the regulated metabolism of substances. Turgor pressure increases as the appressorium matures in C. gloeosporioides, ultimately reaching a level that enables the final functional appressorium to successfully invade the plant host.

During its formation and development, the appressorium of C. gloeosporioides undergoes a series of processes involving the transfer and degradation of storage compounds within conidia to generate energy. In conidia, carbon is primarily stored as glycogen and lipid droplets, whereas nitrogen is mainly stored as amino acids [24]. Arginine, which is a semi-essential amino acid, is important for protein synthesis, cell growth, hormone secretion, immune function, and tissue integrity in mammals [28, 29]. It is also a precursor of NO, which regulates conidial germination and appressorium formation in the phytopathogenic fungi responsible for anthracnose and powdery mildew [30, 31]. In M. oryzae, three genes related to the de novo synthesis of arginine, MoARG1 (MGG-15868), MoARG5/6 (MGG-02690), and MoARG7 (MGG-04210), have been knocked out and functionally characterized. Mutations to these three genes lead to significant decreases in sporulation and pathogenicity, but the addition of exogenous arginine can restore both sporulation and pathogenicity. Notably, all three genes are expressed at significantly higher levels in developing appressoria than in mycelia [32]. Additionally, a cytomics analysis showed that arginine depletion adversely affects the ability of appressoria to penetrate the host [33]. Our experimental results indicated arginine biosynthesis was an enriched metabolic pathway (Fig. 3). From 5 to 12 h, the precursor metabolite glutamine was continuously degraded for the synthesis of other metabolites (N-acetylglutamate and arginine) as appressoria developed and the turgor pressure increased from 5 h onwards. The ornithine and arginine content were maintained at a high level throughout the analyzed time period, indicating that arginine biosynthetic pathway may be required for the development of appressoria and increases in turgor pressure in C. gloeosporioides.

Focused on the phospholipid metabolism, it is reported that both PE in the phospholipid metabolic pathway and galactosylglycerol tended to accumulate in developing appressoria, reaching peak levels at 12 h in the current study. Therefore, we speculate that similar to glycerol, PE may also be involved in generating turgor pressure. There are four main pathways for PE synthesis: acylation of LysoPE, CDP-acetamide pathway, phosphatidylserine (PS) headgroup exchange reaction, and decarboxylation of PS in the mitochondrion [34]. Although LysoPE was not detected in this study, we detected PS. The catabolism of PS produces large amounts of PC and PE. PC and PE contents increased from 0 to 12 h at different appressorium developmental stages of C. gloeosporioides. In M. oryzae, the levels of PC, LysoPC, GPC, and LysoPE decrease during appressoria formation (from 0 to 48 h) [24], which suggested that phospholipid metabolism may play an important role in the appressorium turgor generation of C. gloeosporioides. This is followed by the generation of LysoPC and LysoPE, which are then transformed into GPC and GPE, respectively, by PLA1. Finally, both compounds are converted by GDE to sn-glycerol-3-phosphate (Fig. 4a), which is used to generate glycerol in a reaction catalyzed by GPP, ultimately leading to increases in turgor pressure. Meanwhile, the deletion of autophagy-related genes (e.g., ATG1, ATG4, ATG8, and ATG9) result in decreases in appressorium turgor pressure, reflecting the importance of autophagy for turgor pressure formation. During autophagy, the expansion of autophagic vesicles mainly depends on the binding of Atg8 and PE [24, 35–37]. Hence, we speculate that the accumulation of PE within the C. gloeosporioides appressorium may be associated with the autophagic process and affects turgor pressure formation. There has been no research on the association between the C. gloeosporioides PC degradation pathway and appressorium turgor pressure. The degradation of PC, LysoPC, and GPC is regulated by various esterases that may be functionally redundant. The relationship between the degradation of PC-like metabolites (PC, LysoPC, and GPC) and appressorium turgor pressure formation may be assessed by conducting gene knockdown experiments. In addition, this degradation may be blocked using specific inhibitors of these esterases to further characterize the functions of PC-like metabolites influencing the growth and development of appressoria.

Typically, phospholipids play diverse and critical roles in cellular functions, and the associated phospholipid biosynthetic enzymes are potential targets for fungicides. For example, the widely used fungicide isoprothiolane inhibits methylation in phosphatidylcholine (PC) biosynthesis [38]. Doxorubicin, which targets phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) biosynthesis, has been shown to inhibit M. oryzae infection. Doxorubicin significantly suppresses conidial germination, mycelial growth, and plant infection by M. oryzae through targeting PSD2. Additionally, Doxorubicin inhibits the enzymatic activity of MoPsd2, leading to increased phosphatidylserine (PS) levels and altered expression of genes involved in development, lipid biosynthesis, and pathogenesis [15]. In M. oryzae, the effect of Doxorubicin on turgor pressure has not been experimentally investigated. However, in the present study, we used Doxorubicin-treated C. gloeosporioides to measure turgor pressure and found that it was reduced to 9.8 ± 0.74 MPa at 12 h. Doxorubicin also inhibited appressorium formation and blackening but did not affect mycelial growth or conidial germination in C. gloeosporioides. Therefore, we speculate that this may be due to the low concentration of Doxorubicin used in this experiment, or C. gloeosporioides may have compensated for pathogenicity through other mechanisms.

Similar to Doxorubicin, AXD also did not affect pathogenicity in this study, although it inhibited mycelial growth, appressorium formation, and blackening, and reduced turgor pressure in C. gloeosporioides. In M. oryzae, most of the phenotypes induced by AXD were similar to those of the MoGep4 mutant, including inhibition of mycelial growth, conidial and appressorium development, reduced virulence, and altered phospholipid levels. However, it is important to note that AXD treatment produced a broader range of phenotypic effects in rice compared to the MoGep4 mutant. For example, AXD-treated M. oryzae exhibited reduced mycelial pigmentation compared to the MoGep4 mutant, suggesting that AXD may have multiple targets [16].

In the present study, it was experimentally demonstrated that Neomycin effectively reduced turgor pressure and attenuated pathogenicity in C. gloeosporioides. In phytopathogenic fungi, Neomycin has been used as a phospholipase C (PLC)-specific inhibitor to study the role of PLC activity in the formation of fungal infection structures. Treatment of Alternaria alternata with Neomycin significantly inhibited wax- and hydrophobicity-mediated conidial germination and appressorium formation on pear fruit epidermis. Exogenous calcium treatment partially restored the impaired conidial germination induced by Neomycin but had no effect on the inhibition of appressorium formation. Neomycin also did not significantly inhibit the expansion of lesions caused by A. alternata [19]. However, in this study, Neomycin significantly inhibited appressorium formation and blackening, reduced turgor pressure, and attenuated pathogenicity, although it did not affect mycelial growth or conidial germination. Among the three phospholipid metabolism inhibitors tested, while Doxorubicin and AXD both reduced turgor pressure and appressorium formation rates, only Neomycin attenuated pathogenicity (Fig. 5f). This suggests that Neomycin is a potent inhibitor of C. gloeosporioides. In subsequent experiments, we will establish different concentration gradients and combine molecular techniques such as gene knockdown to further investigate the mechanisms of Neomycin. Additionally, we will carry out some phospholipid signalling studies to identify the signalling pathways in the future.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified a variety of metabolites that may affect the turgor pressure of C. gloeosporioides appressoria. We also revealed four significant metabolic pathways (carbohydrate, lipid, arginine, and phospholipid metabolism) that regulate changes in the appressorium turgor pressure. We have investigated the function and mechanism of phospholipid biosynthesis in C. gloeosporioides. It was identified that Neomycin was an effective fungicide targeting C. gloeosporioides. Additionally, other enzymes related to phospholipid biosynthesis could also be potential targets for fungicide development and warrant further exploration. This metabolic information may be relevant to future functional analyses of genes associated with appressorium turgor pressure as well as comprehensive analyses of C. gloeosporioides pathogenesis, with potential implications for the development of novel chemical treatments for controlling C. gloeosporioides.
