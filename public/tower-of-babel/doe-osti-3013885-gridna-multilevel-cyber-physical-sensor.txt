ABSTRACT
For safeguarding power grids and other critical infrastructure, the griDNA technology is an AI
sentry that can see through virtual walls. Unlike current “siloed” monitoring solutions that provide
narrow views of individual systems, griDNA can simultaneously see and process disparate data from
operational sensors and control networks across multiple connected systems. This broadly
distributed vision also adds depth, merging layers of whole-system visibility with local details to find
early warning signs of potential cascading failures. The tool’s cutting-edge AI algorithms
continuously fuse and analyze the many data streams, looking for deviations from the expected
interplay of network activity and physical operations. The result is holistic, cyber-physical situational
awareness - delivered in real time - that rapidly alerts system operators and defenders to the
stealthiest cyber intrusions, as well as potential device failures and other problems. Further, the
technology uses inexpensive single-board computers or flexible software containers on existing
devices for implementation that is easily tailored and scaled to the requirements of individual
systems.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Current critical infrastructure monitoring sensors operate in siloes – either purely focused on the
cyber system (e.g., network traffic, host data) or the physical system (e.g., physics-based
measurements). No critical infrastructure system sensor exists that fuses together the cyber-physical
data for a united view of the system to determine when a cyber-, physical-, or cyber-physical event is
occurring and how it may be propagating. griDNA fills this gap and can augment existing
monitoring devices for a comprehensive CPSA data ecosystem that provides observability at various
levels and interconnections of the system. The griDNA AI/machine learning (AI/ML) methods
provide a unique approach to classifying real-time cyber-physical data jointly and adapting to new
system conditions as they arise. Additionally, this combined cyber-physical correlation analysis helps
automatically connect physical system operators and cyber defenders for faster and more effective
coordination for responding to disturbances/attacks. Lastly, griDNA not only enables improved
cybersecurity but also more efficient planning and operational processes. For resource-constrained
system owners without monitoring capabilities, griDNA can provide low-cost and advanced cyber-
physical monitoring and AI/ML analytics to help identify system bottlenecks, inefficiencies, and
critical functions. Figure 1 shows an overview of griDNA function within a multi-level grid system.

1. INTRODUCTION
Cyber-Physical Situational Awareness (CPSA) is critical for understanding how different grid
systems and components interact to improve planning, integrated operation, and adaptive response.
Achieving CPSA is a challenging goal due to the multilevel and decentralized nature of the grid as
well as the lack of synchronized cyber-physical sensors and fusion techniques to gather concurrent
data and perform analysis for holistic situational awareness (SA). However, as more distributed
energy resources (DERs) and edge systems are connected, it’s of paramount importance that grid
operators have CPSA into the entire system – including connected critical infrastructure. This
involves both local and global CPSA to understand how system changes and/or disturbances
propagate through the interconnected system [1].
The 2021 Texas winter storm demonstrated the critical need for SA across utility systems;
furthermore, as cyber-attacks increase in frequency, SA cannot be limited to physical system
dynamics [2]. Multi-hazard events, for which severe, unpredictable weather stemming from climate
change could combine with cyber-attacks and/or electromagnetic pulses, require cyber-physical
sensing and analysis capabilities.
To address this CPSA gap, we propose to develop griDNA, multilevel CPSA sensors and their
implementation architecture, that collects cyber-physical data at varying, decentralized grid levels and
applies advanced sensor data-fusion techniques, both locally and globally, to understand the cyber-
physical system state, characterize interdependent systems, and inform comprehensive planning,
operation, and mitigation decisions.
Project objectives:
 1. Sensors that collect multilevel cyber-physical data (both hardware and virtual prototypes).
 2. Implementation architecture of sensors at each level that defines CPSA-based placement and
 secure data exchange needs.
 3. Novel sensor data-fusion and classification methodology enables local and global CPSA
 insights.
 4. Cyber-physical emulation and field testing using Emulytics™ tools and PNM facilities.
 5. Intuitive, CPSA-focused visualization and alerting
These project objectives are summarized in Figure 2. Additionally, the project milestones that were
completed within the griDNA project are shown in Table 1. The remainder of this report will detail
the research and development completed for each of the tasks and how each milestone was
achieved. The next section will delve into relevant background for griDNA.

2. BACKGROUND
For the development of griDNA, it is important to understand how monitoring and detection is
traditionally implemented separately in the physical system and cyber system. A brief overview is
provided next to motivate the need to break down those separate siloes and unite the cyber-physical
system for comprehensive situational awareness.

2.1. Sensing in the Electric Grid
To ensure safe and reliable operation of the electric grid, monitoring and state estimation is crucial
for assessing power system health and stability. For many industrial control systems, supervisory
control and data acquisition (SCADA) networks are leveraged to collect measurements across the
physical process system. For power systems, this can include measurements of real and reactive
power, voltage, frequency, current, etc. Using these measurements to estimate the system states
enables operators to make decisions to deploy control to adjust generation/load, mitigate faults or
disturbances, etc. [3].
For fast-sampled, GPS-synchronized power system data, phasor measurement units (PMUs) have
been utilized in the recent years. PMUs have enabled access to fast and distributed power system
data that can be used for a variety of applications, including more accurate fault detection and
adaptive control schemes. From a cybersecurity perspective, PMU GPS-spoofing and bad data
injection attacks are of concern [4,5]. Additionally, these measurements can be leveraged for physics-
informed AI methodologies to detect anomalous behavior by leveraging state estimation principles
for greater context and accuracy. In griDNA, this PMU data is used to provide the physics context
to the collected communication traffic. Many other sensors and measurement approaches exist in
the grid domain, but for the griDNA development, we will focus on PMUs [6].

2.2. Cybersecurity Monitoring
Network monitoring is critical for the security of information technology (IT) and operational
technology (OT) systems. Various commercial and government cyber monitoring tools are available
[7,8] that specialize in collecting, parsing, and analyzing different communication protocols, deep
packet inspection, and pattern analysis (using established rules/thresholds or AI/ML for adaptive
detection). These include intrusion detection systems (IDSs) that are crucial for detecting anomalous
cyber activity and, at times, the type and location of attack. Systems that can deploy varying levels of
mitigation for the detected compromise(s) are known as intrusion prevention systems (IPSs). These
monitoring and detection capabilities are longstanding functions of IT security ecosystems and are
often part of security, orchestration, automation, and response (SOAR) processes. In recent years,
the need for IDS and SOAR capabilities customized to OT system characteristics and processes has
been highlighted. Several OT-specific capabilities have been or are being developed to account for
OT communication protocols such as Modbus and DNP3 as well as field device configuration and
function. Nonetheless, a gap remains in accounting for the physics of the system that the OT
network is connected to.

2.3. Cyber-Physical Situational Awareness
For joint understanding and insight into cyber-physical system operation, cyber-physical situational
awareness (CPSA) is needed [9]. This involves analyzing the physics data concurrently with the cyber
data (IT or OT) to provide context and understanding if a cyber compromise is causing physical
system consequence or vice versa. To achieve this, time-synced cyber-physical data and distributed

 14

correlation analysis methods are needed to pinpoint when combined cyber-physical events occur,
identify the location of those events, and determine if the event is propagating or not. griDNA seeks
to provide this capability with distributed cyber-physical analysis using AI/ML to classify events as
cyber, physical, or combined cyber-physical. The next few sections provide details on the griDNA
design, cyber-physical feature analysis, AI/ML approach, and both emulation and field-testing
results.

3. GRIDNA DESIGN AND FORM FACTOR
The griDNA technology was prototyped on various form factors, including single-board computers
and software containers. For the single-board computers, Raspberry Pi, NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano,
and SNC Binary Armor devices were used. In this section, the griDNA implementation on each is
described and overall recommendations for selecting a specific form factor for different systems.
Figure 3 shows an example architecture of distributed griDNA sensors, including local, enclave, and
global versions.
The local griDNA models are distributed across a single-owner system and processes raw data and
provides localized classification results of detected cyber, physical, or combined cyber-physical
events. The enclave griDNA sensor is within a single-owner system and provides aggregate analysis
using all raw data streams as well as localized griDNA classification results to provide a system level
view of CPSA and provide early indicators of propagating disturbances. Lastly, the global griDNA
sensor is used across multi-owner systems (e.g., utility-owned transmission and privately-owned
DER) to share alerts about propagating disturbances and/or growing compromise but also
obfuscate any sensitive data such as system topology and does not share raw data.

3.1. Software Containers
Software containers are lightweight processing environments that utilize the host's kernel while
maintaining isolation from the rest of the system. To create a software container, users must specify
all the libraries necessary for deploying a particular application. Additionally, configuration files are
incorporated into the container to ensure that the application is deployed according to the user's
specifications. Software containers offer remarkable portability, enabling applications to run
consistently across various computing environments. By encapsulating an application along with its
dependencies, libraries, and configuration files, containers ensure that the software behaves the same
way regardless of where it is deployed whether it is on a developer's local machine, a testing
environment, or a production server. This eliminates the common problem of troubleshooting when
running software on separate systems, as the containerized application carries its environment with
it.
Furthermore, containers can be easily shared and distributed through container registries, allowing
teams to collaborate seamlessly and deploy applications across different platforms, including cloud
services and on-premises infrastructure. This level of portability not only streamlines development
and deployment processes but also enhances scalability and flexibility, making it easier for
organizations to adapt to changing technological landscapes.
A suite of software packages was containerized as part of this effort so that our griDNA solution is
portable and compatible with several commercial products already fielded in utility environments.

4. GRIDNA DATA FUSION AND CLASSIFICATION FRAMEWORK
To achieve CPSA, concurrent cyber-physical analysis is needed across distributed griDNA
implementations. This includes correlation analysis to understand when cyber, physical, or combined
cyber-physical events occur. To enable this, griDNA employs combined cyber-physical dataset
feature analysis and extraction that is then analyzed and correlated using AI/ML, Figure 8. This
section provides details on the overall griDNA data fusion and classification framework.

 Figure 8: griDNA cyber-physical AI/ML approach.

4.1. Cyber-Physical Data Feature Extraction
The data fusion task is focused on the combining of cyber data streams (packet captures) with
physical measured data (PMU timeseries data in this case). The primary challenge in this area is
combining the disparate time domains of the cyber and physical data. The cyber data is
asynchronous packet captures while the physical data is a well-formed timeseries. The cyber packet
rates can vary dramatically through time, even within the same system during normal operations
depending on system activity. Other changing factors include the set of IPs communicating and the
type of packet present in the system. The physical data measured by the PMUs is always sampled at
a consistent interval from the same set of PMUs with the same set of measured fields. Integrating
these two characteristics is the core challenge of the data fusion task. Within griDNA, the general
approach has been to coerce the cyber data into timeseries form by windowing.
Any packets within a given window (say 1 second) are considered together and features are extracted
from that set of packets. This provides a consistent way to interact with both the cyber and physical
data through a timeseries lens. Considering those windows, features can be extracted from the cyber
and physical data and used as input to the machine learning algorithms.

 22

4.1.1. Cyber Feature Extraction
For the cyber data, there is not a clear path towards using the ‘raw’ cyber data due to the fact that
the data is a set of packets (probably of multiple packet types). Thus, for the cyber data, features
must be extracted to make the cyber data useable for ML applications.
Three types of cyber features were created to be extract information from the cyber packet captures.
These are single window features, rolling window features, and paired IP features. Figure 9 shows

Entropy-Based Distance Metrics
The second stage of the analysis introduced entropy-based distance metrics to quantify deviations in
network traffic behavior by comparing the statistical distributions of categorical features between
test traffic and a known baseline. To achieve this, Shannon entropy is employed as a measure of
uncertainty or unpredictability within a categorical distribution. In the context of network traffic,
entropy captures the degree of diversity in categorical features, such as how many different IP
addresses or ports are observed and how evenly their occurrences are distributed.
 1. Shannon Entropy

 of information or uncertainty associated with a random variable. For a discrete random
 variable 𝑋 with possible outcomes 𝑥 and corresponding probabilities { }, the 1 , 𝑥 2 , 𝑥 3 ,...,

 reaches its maximum when all outcomes are equally likely, indicating the highest level of
 unpredictability. In contrast, lower entropy values indicate a more deterministic distribution,
 where certain outcomes are more dominant.
 2. Entropy Distance Computation
 To identify potential anomalies, entropy values computed from the test traffic are compared
 against those of a baseline (normal) traffic distribution. The comparison process involves
 three key steps: 1. Baseline Entropy Calculation: Entropy is computed over the entire
 baseline dataset without temporal aggregation. This provides a stable reference representing

 25

 expected, normal behavior for each feature. 2. Test Entropy Calculation: For the traffic
 under test, entropy is calculated within 1-second time windows. This windowed approach
 enables the detection of short-lived anomalies that may occur within specific time frames. 3.
 Distance Metric Computation: The difference between the baseline entropy and the entropy
 of the test traffic is calculated for each categorical feature. These differences serve as
 entropy-based distance metrics, with larger values indicating greater deviation from normal
 behavior, potentially signaling abnormal or malicious activity

4.2. LSTM AE-driven Cyber-Physical Threat Detection
The griDNA framework leverages Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)-based Autoencoders (AEs) for
cyber-physical data fusion and anomaly detection in the electric grid [15]. Threat detection in this
context involves identifying abnormal patterns in multimodal input data, where the fusion of cyber-
physical features enhances predictive performance and situational awareness. LSTM-based AEs are
particularly suited for processing time-series data due to their ability to capture temporal dynamics and
learn non-linear patterns [16]. These models consist of an encoder-decoder architecture, where the
encoder compresses high-dimensional input data into a latent space, and the decoder reconstructs the
data from this representation. This reconstruction error (RE), defined as the Mean Squared Error
(MSE) between the original and reconstructed data, serves as the primary metric for anomaly
detection.
The framework was trained using a 3-hour baseline dataset consisting of normal cyber-physical data
(normal operations), augmented with entropy-based features to improve model performance.
Hyperparameter optimization was conducted via grid search to refine the LSTM AE architecture, in
terms of layers, optimizer, weight decay, learning rate, batch size, dropout rate and compression rate.
Offline training and validation were performed exclusively on normal data to enable the model to
learn inherent temporal patterns and minimize reconstruction error. Subsequently, the anomaly
detection threshold was determined through offline analysis, using the 99th percentile of the RE
distribution as the threshold value. New unseen data points exceeding this threshold are classified as
abnormal.
The evaluation of the enhanced griDNA framework highlights its improved performance in anomaly
detection. During training and validation, the loss curves converged to the same minimized value, as
illustrated in Figure 17, demonstrating the stability of the training process. Offline analysis of the RE
distribution revealed a threshold of 0.3, corresponding to the 99th percentile, which was used to classify
data points as normal or abnormal (see Figure 18).
Further insights into the model’s performance are provided in Figure 19, which illustrates the
reconstruction results for unseen data points. The top row depicts two normal cyber-physical data
points (representing normal operating conditions), while the bottom row shows two abnormal data
points (representing abnormal operating conditions), while the bottom row shows two abnormal data
points (representing abnormal operating conditions). In each panel, the blue line represents the
original normalized feature values provided to the trained model, and the orange line represents the
reconstructed values. The model demonstrates near-perfect reconstruction for normal data points,
with reconstruction errors of approximately 0.02. In contrast, abnormal data points exhibit
significantly higher reconstruction errors, ranging from 35 to 640 – two to three orders of magnitude
greater than those of normal data points. This stark difference in RE enables the LSTM-based AE
model to accurately classify data points as normal or abnormal.
Additionally, the RE metrics provide valuable insights into the classification of anomalies as cyber,
physical, or cyber-physical attacks. Using the established threshold of 0.3 for anomaly detection, the
average RE values for normal and abnormal data across cyber, physical, and cyber-physical features
were analyzed, as summarized in Table 3. Specifically:

The aforementioned results demonstrate the model’s ability to distinguish between cyber, physical,
and cyber-physical anomalies based on RE values. Cyber anomalies primarily affect cyber features,
with limited propagation into the fused feature space, while physical anomalies are localized to physical
features but strongly influence the fused feature space. Cyber-physical anomalies, however, impact all
feature categories, resulting in elevated RE values across the board. By comparing average RE values
against the threshold, the griDNA framework provides a reliable mechanism for identifying the type
of anomaly, enhancing situational awareness and enabling targeted mitigation strategies.
To enhance the reliability of the griDNA framework, we implemented a mechanism to quantify the
confidence levels of the LSTM AE’s predictions, providing uncertainty quantification for anomaly
detection. This approach involves passing the RE through a sigmoid function, which compares the
RE value to the established threshold. The sigmoid output reflects the confidence level of the model’s
prediction: the greater the difference between the RE and the threshold, the higher the confidence
that the data point is abnormal. This method not only improves interpretability but also ensures that
predictions are accompanied by a measure of certainty, enabling operators to prioritize responses to
high-confidence anomalies.
Overall, the framework achieved 97% accuracy in predicting normal data points and 91% accuracy in
predicting abnormal data points, compared to the original 93% and 85% with older ML models,
respectively. These results underscore the effectiveness of the new baseline dataset, entropy-based
features, and optimized training process in enhancing the model’s predictive performance and
reliability.

Cyber-Physical Threat Detection as a Service
To operationalize the griDNA framework for real-time cyber-physical threat detection, we developed
a lightweight Flask-based API designed to host the ML model and facilitate seamless integration into
the griDNA ecosystem. This implementation elevates griDNA to high-TRL product, making it easily
deployable and maintainable on single-board computers (SBCs) such as the Raspberry Pi and NVIDIA
Jetson Orin Nano.

The Flask API was designed to meet two critical requirements: lightweight operation to ensure
compatibility with SBCs and high-speed performance to enable the ML pipeline (data cleaning, feature
extraction, and classification) to process each data point in under 1 second. Flask [17], a minimalist
web framework, was chosen for its simplicity and efficiency in building lightweight web applications.

Operators interact with the API via POST requests, providing:
 • Data Points: The cyber-physical data under examination, which could be provided in a window
 format from an Elasticsearch database
 • Model: The name of the ML model to be used for data fusion and classification
 • Threshold: Applicable for LSTM AEs, this value is used to compare the reconstruction error
 of a data point and determine its abnormality.

To ensure robust performance monitoring during inference, the API includes a Python-based module
that captures system metrics from the SBC in real-time: a) CPU temperature, b) CPU Utilization
Percentage, c) Average CPU Core Utilization in 1-minute window, d) RAM Utilization Percentage,
and e) RAM Utilization in GB. These metrics are collected asynchronously using threaded
programming, where a dedicated thread captures and updates the metrics every 5 seconds. The
collected data is sent to an Elasticsearch (ES) database via the ES python library, enabling further
exploration through Kibana dashboards. Additionally, the metrics are visualized in real-time on
Grafana dashboards, providing operators with actionable insights into system performance.

The Flask API also stores key outputs from the ML model in ES for further analysis and visualization.
These outputs include:
 • Predictions: Whether a data point is classified as normal or abnormal
 • Confidence levels: Quantified using the sigmoid-based uncertainty mechanism, which reflects
 the model’s confidence in its predictions
 • Inference Times: The time in seconds taken by the ML pipeline to process each data point
These stored outputs are displayed in Kibana dashboards, allowing operators to monitor the model’s
predictions, performance and confidence levels in real-time. This integration provides a
comprehensive view of both the anomaly detection process and the underlying system metrics,
enhancing situational awareness and enabling informed decision-making.

The Flask API enables real-time cyber-physical threat detection as a service by hosting the ML model
and processing incoming data efficiently. Its lightweight design ensures compatibility with resource-
constrained SPCs, while its modular architecture facilitates easy deployment and maintenance. By
integrating system monitoring, prediction storage, and visualization tools, the API provides a holistic
solution for anomaly detection and operational oversight, making griDNA a high-TRL product
suitable for practical deployment in grid security applications.

5. SECURE IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGIES
To enable confidentiality, integrity, and availability between distributed griDNA instances, secure
implementation strategies are needed. Designing secure data exchange across interconnected systems
of systems (SoS) can be challenging without considering unique characteristics of the underlying
cyber and physical processes. For example, in the electric power grid, it is important to consider the
types of communication protocols, frequency of communications, and types of control schemes
alongside the physics-based processes of power flow and types of measurements such as voltage,
frequency, and current. In [18], these important considerations were explored for heterogenous
sensors, networks, and data types and several secure data exchange approaches were presented.
These secure data exchange approaches included:
 • Multichain [19]
 o Enables the operation of multiple independent chains within a single network,
 allowing each chain to be tailored to specific use cases and requirements.
 • Synchronic Web [20]
 o A distributed network for securing data provenance by enabling diverse clients to
 freely commit digital information into a single shared view of history, it provides a
 foundational basis of truth on which to build decentralized and scalable trust across
 the Internet
 • InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) [21]
 o A peer-to-peer (P2P) fully distributed file system run by multiple nodes, that enables
 resilient data storage and retrieval.
 • Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) [22]
 o MQTT is a standard messaging protocol for the Internet of Things (IoT) and
 designed as an extremely lightweight publish/subscribe messaging transport
Further information on these approaches as well as additional techniques and cybersecurity design
paradigms are found in [18]. Initial experimental results with the griDNA are also provided for
Synchronic Web, IPFS, and Multichain. In the next subsection, griDNA and MQTT experimental
results are presented.

5.1. griDNA Secure Data Exchange Testing with MQTT
To test secure data exchange between distributed griDNA sensors, an experiment with MQTT was
designed where secure communication between two different griDNA virtual clients using a
Mosquitto Broker within gridFACE emulation environment was tested. Specifically, the team tested
the exchange of data between griDNA VMs and verified traffic was indeed encrypted. Additionally,
the MQTT publish/subscribe framework was tested with griDNA prototypes (on SNC Binary
Armor, SEL-3355’s with Blue Frame container technology, and rPis) at PNM Prosperity Site and
verified secure communications. The gridFACE emulation environment experimental setup is
shown in Figure 23.
Secure communications were established using X.509 certificates, facilitated by a self-signed
certificate authority (CA). The self-signed CA was utilized for demonstration purposes; however, in
a production environment, a CA signed by a trusted authority would be preferred.

 37

It was essential to include the IP address of the Mosquitto broker and any gateway systems
forwarding requests to the broker within the CA. Failing to do so would result in certificate
validation errors in the OpenSSL software, preventing a secure connection to the broker.

In the Binary Armor system, the IP address of the Docker gateway must be included in the CA
certificate under the extended SubjectAltName field to ensure proper validation when connecting to
the Mosquitto broker. In this scenario, multiple IP addresses were specified in the SubjectAltName,
including those of both the Mosquitto broker and the Docker gateway. This configuration is
necessary when the self-signed CA needs to be validated across a different subnet from the
Mosquitto broker.

A potential challenge arises when multiple IP addresses are required in the CA certificate, especially
if several certificates are already deployed and new devices on a different subnet are introduced. In
such cases, the new gateway systems that have been added may need to be incorporated into the CA
certificate, necessitating the generation and distribution of a new CA certificate, along with the
creation of new certificates for all end devices signed by the previous CA certificate.

Alternatively, using a CA that is not self-signed or introducing a delegated CA could mitigate this
issue, although these options were not tested.

6.2.1. Cyber-Physical Kill Chain Attack
Work has also been done to begin the implementation of our first Cyber-Physical attack. This is a
full “kill-chain” (the process by which an attacker exploits a system from beginning to end) that
includes compromise of IT systems, a pivot to OT devices, and eventually disruption of the physical
process simulation. In this case, we have decided to model our attack after the real-world
CRASHOVERRIDE [25]. This will give us a realistic attack scenario in which we can test our
griDNA toolset.
A preliminary cyber-physical attack was created to help understand how the power simulation and
the emulated control system respond when compromised by a malicious actor. For this, we created a
more complex cyber kill-chain modelled after behavior seen in CRASHOVERRIDE, which we
named GRIDOVERRIDE. The intent of this attack is to compromise the SCADA control system
and send malicious commands to turn off and on breakers in the power system, resulting in
fluctuating power and ultimately a loss of generation and distribution. This kill-chain encompasses
an attacker, from outside the Control Center’s network, gains access to this network and
compromises a Windows IT system. From there, the attacker pivots to SCADA systems, with the
initial target being the HMI. Once the HMI in compromised, it can then send malicious breaker
On/Off commands to the OPC system. Once the OPC system starts receiving malicious commands
(which it considers legitimate), it will send the commands to a field device, and from there these
commands are propagated to the actual breaker in the power simulation.
For our initial work in implementing this cyber kill-chain, we focused on the compromise of the
HMI, and subsequent malicious commands to the OPC which are propagated to a breaker that
connects the power generation system to the power distribution system, with the end result of
disconnecting the two. Figure 29 below illustrates the 6 main steps of this attack.
 1. In step 1, we use a component of SCEPTRE called Scorch (Scenario Orchestration tool)
 which injects a Python file containing malicious code onto the HMI.
 2. In step 2, this code generates the breaker Open/Close values.
 3. In step 3, these Open/Close values are sent via “write” commands to the OPC.
 4. In step 4, these commands are seen by the OPC, the breaker value is updated, and a
 command is sent from the OPC to the SCEPTRE field device to update the breaker value in
 the power simulation.
 5. In step 5, the command is sent over Modbus/TCP to write the new value to the breaker in
 the power simulation.
 6. Finally in step 6, the breaker in the power simulation reads this new value, and either closes
 (for an “Close” command, or opens (for an “Open” command). When the breaker closes, it
 stops the current from flowing through, disrupting power to the distribution system.

6.3.2. Field Test Scenarios
The main test scenarios are described next. The cyber attacks are launched by the adversary through
the ethernet switch shown in Figure 32. The griDNA installed as a BITW device in front of the PV
meter and its dashboard visualization can be accessed by a connected machine. To avoid
interruption to the PV site operation, a victim machine is setup for the Denial-of-Service attack to
avoid interruption to the PV meter.

6.3.3. griDNA Integration and Data Sparsity Considerations
Field testing the griDNA algorithms at the PNM site required one adjustment in the data collection
and processing phase. Processing raw packet captures from the field is more difficult than in the
emulation environment, thus for the field test we used Zeek logs instead of the raw packets. Zeek is
a tool which analyzes and aggregates packets into logs depending on the configuration the user
specifies. Those logs were then stored in Elasticsearch. Custom pre-processing functions were used
to ingest the log files and create a set of derived cyber features that were as similar as possible to the
features described in Section 4.1.1. There were some variations due to the log files not containing all
of the same information that was present in the packet captures from the emulation environment. In
future work the Zeek configuration can be fine-tuned to provide more comprehensive data for the
ML features, and a direct comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of using raw packet
captures versus Zeek logs will be performed. For the physical features, the raw measured values
from the PV inverter were used, similar to how the raw measured values from the PMU sensors
were used in the emulation. One difference is that in the emulated environment there are more
physical features than cyber features, while at the PNM site the reverse was true. There were more
cyber features than physical features.
Another note from the field testing is that the cyber data is fairly sparse, and highly variable at the
PNM site. Whereas in the emulated environment, using a window size of 1 sec resulted in very few
windows without any packet traffic, a 1 second window size for the PNM data resulted in a large
number of windows without any activity. It may be necessary for future work to move towards
dynamically sized windows, or specifying the window size based on the normal traffic patterns for a
particular site. This will also result in a requirement for a longer training dataset due to the sparsity
of the cyber data.

6.3.4. Field Test Results
The griDNA system was successfully integrated and tested at the PNM Prosperity PV Site, where it
effectively collected, processed, and classified data from the live system of data by leveraging a
custom API. Multiple griDNA sensors were deployed, enabling secure communication among them
through MQTT pub/sub messaging to share health status information. A Kibana dashboard was
developed to visualize the data and provide alert information, enhancing situational awareness for
operators.

 48

During normal operation, the griDNA AE accurately classified data points as normal. In the case of
an attack scenario involving a False Data Injection Attack, the griDNA AE successfully flagged the
abnormal data points. Similarly, during a Denial of Service (DoS) attack scenario, the AE also
identified the abnormal data points. However, in the ARP Spoof attack scenario, while the griDNA
ARP alert tool detected the issue, the AE was not configured to monitor ARP traffic, which is a
feature that can be added in the future.
Additionally, the system underwent successful testing of Binary Armor and SEL-3355 with MQTT
peer-to-peer communication. As mentioned in previous sections, prior cyber-physical SCEPTRE™
emulation testing evaluated the system against generator and line outages, load shedding commands
via DoS, and other DoS scenarios. Throughout these tests, the griDNA system accurately classified
normal operations, cyber-only events, physical-only events, and combined cyber-physical events.
During our field testing (Figures 33-35), we observed no dropped packets when integrating our
griDNA sensors as bump-in-the-wire systems. The PV site operated seamlessly, with no noticeable
delays in communication. Furthermore, our prototype systems effectively handled the traffic volume
while simultaneously hosting a suite of software designed to detect and respond to threats in real-
time. The demonstration was successful, confirming that our prototype is suitable for field
deployments in operational technology (OT) environments. Additionally, our technology can be
applied to other settings where multiple data streams can be analyzed for inconsistencies or
abnormal behavior.

7. GRIDNA ALERTING AND CP-ISOC VISUALIZATION
A next-generation grid cyber-physical integrated security operations center (CP-ISOC) offers critical
visibility into the interconnected states IT, OT, and power systems, supporting more informed
operational and response decisions. As the electric grid becomes increasingly complex and
decentralized, especially with the expansion of distributed, grid-edge assets, achieving CPSA across
both cyber and physical domains is essential for maintaining system reliability and resilience. The
CP-ISOC achieves CPSA by integrating the capabilities of a traditional security operations center
(SOC), which detects physical system changes and security threats, with those of a network
operations center (NOC), which monitors and maintains CPS infrastructure and networks.
The CP-ISOC addresses the challenge of informing and providing quick-turn analysis for both OT
defenders and power system operators of cyber-physical event correlations through unified, readable
visualizations that integrate relevant data across IT, OT, and power domains. It enables correlation
analysis between detected cyber and physical events by leveraging real-time data collection and
detection analysis of cyber and physical data. The aggregation and prioritization of cyber-physical
alerts allows cohesive visualization of integrated data streams within the CP-ISOC. Table 4 informs
CP-ISOC design needs by detailing data, analysis, alert, response, and visualization types across
cyber, physical, and cyber-physical systems, highlighting that cyber-IT, cyber-OT, physical, and
cyber-physical components share similar functions and vulnerabilities. Please see [27] for more
background and design considerations for CP-ISOC implementation.

 Table 4: CP-ISOC design and input structure [27].

A smart grid case study on visualization methods for cyber-physical security systems defines a
workflow of desired features and characteristics for developing CPS visualization techniques such as
those considered for a CP-ISOC. The workflow outlines the progression from acquiring
unstructured cyber data (e.g., SCADA, firewalls, networks) and physical data (e.g., PMUs, RTUs,
smart meters) through data processing to visualization development. It also considers the role of
feature-rich end-user interactions, which enhance the usability and interpretability of visual outputs.
The case study emphasizes differentiating visualization methods by data trends, correlations, and
distributions using network, hierarchical, and iconic structures to convey varying levels of actionable
insight. This includes distinguishing between visualizations designed for real-time monitoring and
those intended for planning and discovery, reflecting the ability to address various operational needs.
The CP-ISOC seeks to leverage this diversity and the fusion of visualization techniques to deliver a
more comprehensive view of security within the smart grid.

8. GRIDNA CONCLUSIONS AND NEXT STEPS

In this project, a successful prototype of the griDNA technology was developed that can process
and learn diverse cyber-physical data and provide distributed anomaly detection and CPSA using
AI/ML. The griDNA was tested both in real-time emulation and live utility systems, validating this
initial design and implementation. Both hardware (on SBCs) and virtual (software container)
prototypes were developed to ease integration into a variety of systems and devices. Additionally,
secure data exchange between distributed griDNA prototypes was tested using a variety of
blockchain-based methods. Lastly, alerting and visualization of griDNA anomalies and other
findings was explored using CP-ISOC design principles.
Overall, with the phased testing approach of griDNA from the emulation to the field, the next steps
include further field testing with a variety of system types, configurations, and data integrity. Thus,
longer duration field testing for training data is crucial to explore studying sparsity, robustness, and
availability issues. Advancements can be made to the griDNA (and its API) to handle these
variations by implementing multiple LSTM AE models to account for different data characteristics.
Meta-learning and causal AI could also be integrated to increase flexibility and understanding of
what model should be used with what data. Therefore, further real-world testing and scalability
advancements can greatly improve griDNA’ s technical readiness level (TRL). Lastly, different
hardware options can be explored to increase griDNA resiliency and performance, including SSD
hardware-attached-on-top features, trusted platform modules, and confidential computing.
As griDNA is a CPSA tool that provides detection capability, it can also be integrated within
security, orchestration, automation, and response (SOAR) pipelines to provide monitoring and
detection to inform mitigations. Within this scope, we can explore expanding the use of hardware
prototypes such as SNC BA for which griDNA prototype testing was performed and leverage the
BA’s operational mode capabilities. BA Operational Modes could be set to dynamically change the
cybersecurity posture of the system and determine what commands are allowed for a given system
state. As an example, a default protected Operational Mode could be established, with the ability to
go into a less secure mode for maintenance activities (and allow access to a configuration-related
GUI) or even a read-only mode in the event of an attack in progress. Thus, increasing the resilience
of the system to disturbances after griDNA detection of anomalies. Integration of griDNA with
other mitigation tools for a cyber-physical SOAR pipeline would increase its impact and useability.
These next steps will be explored in future projects iterating on the griDNA technology and expand
its useability, flexibility, and trustworthiness across critical infrastructure domains.
