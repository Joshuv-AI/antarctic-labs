1.
Introduction

The theory of Hardy space traces its origin to the early twentieth century, around 1910, arising naturally in the study of Fourier series and complex analysis in one variable. From that period onward, Hardy spaces have become a fundamental tool in modern harmonic analysis, providing deep insights into core topics such as maximal functions, singular integrals, and the structure of
L^{p}
spaces
[
37
,
39
]
. Hardy spaces can also be seen as the natural substitute for
L^{p}
spaces when
0<p<1
. In this range, many fundamental operators in harmonic analysis cannot be handled effectively using the standard
L^{p}
norm. This difficulty arises because functions in
L^{p}
for
0<p<1
do not necessarily define distributions, and the space lacks a nontrivial dual. In this setting, Hardy spaces
H^{p}
provide a more suitable framework for analysis. For instance, certain Sobolev-type embedding results that fail in the
L^{p}
setting for
0<p<1
can be recovered by replacing
L^{p}
with
H^{p}

[
38
]
.

Functions in Hardy spaces are characterized by an atomic decomposition, where the atoms satisfy the delicate balance between integrability and cancellation properties, making them particularly well-suited for studying the boundedness of singular integral operators. Moreover, the atomic decomposition paves the way for the formation of the dual space. The celebrated result of Fefferman and Stein
[
20
]
identifies
BMO
as the dual of
H^{1}
. The space
BMO
is the collection of functions of bounded mean oscillation, introduced by John and Nirenberg
[
29
]
, which has come to play a central role in harmonic analysis and partial differential equations. Apart from their favorable properties, Hardy spaces exhibit certain drawbacks. In particular,
H^{p}
does not contain the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it is not well defined on manifolds, and does not admit bounded pseudo-differential operators. These difficulties are closely related to the strong cancellation property of functions in
H^{p}
, for instance, the fact that
\int f=0
for
f\in H^{p}
. As a consequence, multiplication by smooth functions, which may be regarded as pseudo-differential operators, does not behave well on
H^{p}
. These limitations create technical difficulties in various analytical settings. To address these issues, Goldberg introduced local versions of Hardy and
BMO
spaces, denoted by
h^{p}
and
bmo
, respectively
[
21
]
. These spaces retain the essential features of their global counterparts while incorporating localization, thereby allowing smoother function classes such as
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be included. Goldberg also established the duality between
h^{1}
and
bmo
, thereby extending the classical Fefferman-Stein theory to a local framework. Gradually, the Hardy space theory has been generalized far beyond the Euclidean setting. The classical Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has been replaced by more general operators, and Euclidean spaces have been extended to manifolds and, more broadly, to metric measure spaces equipped with a doubling measure
[
6
,
31
,
9
]
.

In recent decades, increasing attention has been devoted to Hardy spaces associated with differential operators, particularly the Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{L}=-\Delta+V,
where
V\geq 0
and
V\not\equiv 0

[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. The introduction of the potential term significantly alters the analytical behavior of the operator, requiring refined tools. This framework has been further generalized to families of Laplace-type operators, see for example
[
30
]
. One notable example in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the Dunkl Laplacian
\Delta_{k}
, introduced by Dunkl, which arises from a system of differential-difference operators associated with a finite reflection group
[
12
]
.

Dunkl operators have attracted considerable interest due to their rich algebraic structure and wide range of applications in mathematics and physics. Dunkl theory can be viewed as a far-reaching generalization of classical Fourier analysis. It includes special functions associated with root systems, spherical functions on Riemannian symmetric spaces
[
24
]
. For further developments, we refer the reader to the works on rational Dunkl theory
[
13
,
35
]
, trigonometric Dunkl theory
[
33
]
, affine Hecke algebras and
q
-Dunkl theory
[
8
,
32
]
, probabilistic aspects of Dunkl theory
[
22
]
, and integrable systems related to Dunkl operators
[
19
]
.

The study of Schrödinger operator associated with the Dunkl Laplacian

\mathcal{L}_{k}=-\Delta_{k}+V,\quad\text{for $V\geq 0$ and $V\neq 0$}

was initiated by Amri and Hammi in 2018, where they developed the foundational theory
[
3
]
and later investigated the corresponding Riesz transforms and the boundedness of the Riesz transforms
[
4
]
. Subsequently, Hejna established an atomic decomposition for the Hardy space associated with the Dunkl-Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}
with the reverse Hölder class
RH_{k}^{q}
potential function
V

[
26
]
.

In this article, we focus on identifying the dual space of the Hardy space associated with
\mathcal{L}_{k}
. In the classical setting, Dziubański
et al.
established that the dual of the Hardy space associated with the Schrödinger differential operator is
BMO(\mathcal{L})
, which is a subspace of
BMO

[
15
]
. Motivated by this result, we introduce a new function space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
, which serves as a natural analogue of the classical
BMO(\mathcal{L})
space in the Dunkl-Schrödinger framework. We provide a characterization of
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
and show, in particular, that the local
bmo
space can be realized as a special case of
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
.

In
[
15
]
, the duality result is based on the atomic decomposition of the Hardy space, where the cancellation property of the atoms depends on the critical radius function. In contrast, the currently available decomposition of the Hardy space associated with
\mathcal{L}_{k}
is formulated in terms of atoms whose cancellation condition depends on the size of the cubes
[
26
]
. To establish the desired duality in our setting, we impose suitable assumptions on the potential function to obtain the corresponding atomic decomposition. Furthermore, we study the boundedness of the uncentered maximal function associated with the Dunkl weight function on the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. In the Euclidean setting, the boundedness of the maximal operator is typically established using the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition
[
7
]
. Although Calderón-Zygmund decompositions are available in spaces of homogeneous type and are useful in various contexts, the structural differences between the Euclidean framework and the present setting make their direct application less convenient. Therefore, we adapt the proof technique developed in the context of the Heisenberg group
[
31
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the necessary preliminaries from Dunkl theory. Section
3
is devoted to the definition and characterization of the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. In Section
4
, we establish the duality between the Hardy space associated with the Dunkl-Schrödinger operator and
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. We study the behavior of the maximal function over the
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
space in Section
5
.

Preliminaries

The Dunkl theory started with the seminal work of Dunkl and developed extensively afterward (see references
[
12
,
35
]
). In this section, we present some preliminary materials on the Dunkl analysis and the Dunkl-Schrödinger operator.

2.1.
Dunkl theory

We consider the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
associated with the inner product
\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}y_{j}
and the norm
|x|^{2}=\langle x,x\rangle
, where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
. For a nonzero vector
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the reflection map
\sigma_{\zeta}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\zeta}(x)=x-2\frac{\langle x,\zeta\rangle}{|\zeta|^{2}}\zeta.

A finite set of non-zero vectors
\mathcal{R}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the root system, if for any
\zeta\in\mathcal{R}
,
\sigma_{\zeta}(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{R}
, and
\mathcal{R}\cap\mathbb{R}\zeta=\{\pm\zeta\}
.
\mathcal{R}
can be represented as a disjoint union of sets
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{+}\cup\mathcal{R}_{-}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{+}=\{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}:\langle\zeta,b\rangle>0\}
for a fixed
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}}\{\zeta\}^{\perp}
. This decomposition is not unique; it may vary depending on the vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We normalize the root system by
|\zeta|^{2}=2
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{R}
. The associated group generated by the reflections
\{\sigma_{\zeta};\zeta\in\mathcal{R}\}
is called the reflection group
G
. The group
G
induces a distance function, known as the orbital distance, defined by
d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)=\min\limits_{\sigma_{\zeta}\in G}\|x-\sigma_{\zeta}(y)\|
, where
\sigma_{\zeta}
are reflections of
G
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{R}
. The complex valued function
k
on
\mathcal{R}
is called the multiplicity function if
k(\zeta)=k(g(\zeta))
, for all
g\in G
. Throughout the paper, we consider
k\geq 0
. The weight function is given by
w_{k}(x)=\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{+}}|\langle\zeta,x\rangle|^{2k(\zeta)}
, which is group invariant and homogeneous of degree
2\gamma
, where
\gamma=\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{+}}k(\zeta)
. The weight function induces a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\omega_{k}(A)=\int_{A}w_{k}(x)dx
, for any Borel measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is given in
[
5
]
that there exists positive constants
c
and
C
such that

\displaystyle cr^{n}\prod\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{+}}\left(|\langle x,\zeta\rangle|+r\right)^{2k(\zeta)}\leq\omega_{k}(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{n}\prod\limits_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{+}}\left(|\langle x,\zeta\rangle|+r\right)^{2k(\zeta)},

where
B(x,r)
is the Euclidean ball with center
x
and radius
r
.
This implies that measure
\omega_{k}
satisfies the doubling condition; that is, there exists a constant
C_{\omega_{k}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\omega_{k}(B(x,2r))\leq C_{\omega_{k}}\omega_{k}(B(x,r))\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }r>0.

(2.1)

In addition, there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle C^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{n+2\gamma}\leq\frac{\omega_{k}(B(x,r))}{\omega_{k}(B(x,R))}\leq C\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{n+2\gamma},

(2.2)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0<r\leq R
.

The associated weighted Lebesgue space
L_{k}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for
1\leq p<\infty
, is defined by

L_{k}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\ \text{measurable}:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)|^{p}w_{k}(x)\,dx<\infty\right\},

where
dx
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
p=\infty
, we define

L_{k}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\ \text{measurable}:\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)|<\infty\right\}.

Moreover,
L^{1}_{k,\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the locally integrable function over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
associated with the weight
w_{k}
.

For a fixed root system
\mathcal{R}
and multiplicity function
k
, the Dunkl operator (or differential-difference operator)
[
12
]
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\xi}f(x)=\partial_{\xi}f(x)+\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{+}}k(\zeta)\langle\zeta,\xi\rangle\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{\zeta}(x))}{\langle\zeta,x\rangle},\text{ for }f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Here,
\partial_{\xi}
is the directional derivative in the direction of
\xi
; correspondingly,
\mathcal{T}_{\xi}
can be viewed as the perturbation of the directional derivative in the Euclidean framework. Let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n}\}
denote the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For notational convenience, we write

\mathcal{T}_{j}=\mathcal{T}_{e_{j}};\,j=1,2,\dots,n.
The Dunkl Laplacian
[
12
]
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}_{j}^{2}.

The operator
-\Delta_{k}
is densely defined, positive, and symmetric over
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and has a unique positive extension
[
3
]
. In addition,
-\Delta_{k}
generates a semigroup
H_{t}
of linear self-adjoint contractions on
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and it admits the following representation

\displaystyle H_{t}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{t}(x,y)f(y)w_{k}(y)dy,

where

\displaystyle h_{t}(x,y)=\tau_{x}\left(c_{k}^{-1}(2t)^{-(n+2\gamma)/2}e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/4t}\right)(-y),

The operator
\tau_{x}
denotes the generalized translation operator; for further details, readers are referred to
[
34
]
. The heat kernel
h_{t}(x,y)
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function on the variables
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
. For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, it satisfies the following conditions
[
5
]
:

(i)

Symmetric property:
h_{t}(x,y)=h_{t}(y,x)
.

(ii)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{t}(x,y)w_{k}(y)dy=1

(iii)

There exists constants
C,c>0
such that

\displaystyle h_{t}(x,y)\leq C\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{-2}\mathcal{G}_{t/c}(x,y),

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y)=\left(\max\{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t})),\omega_{k}(B(y,\sqrt{t}))\}\right)^{-1}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)^{2}}{t}\right).

Dunkl-Schrödinger operator

Given a non-negative function
V\in L_{k,\text{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathcal{L}_{k}=-\Delta_{k}+V
. The operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}
is densely defined on the Hilbert space
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with domain

\operatorname{Dom}(\mathcal{L}_{k})=\left\{f\in L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\Delta_{k}f\ \text{and}\ M_{V}f\in L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right\},

where
M_{V}f(x)=V(x)f(x)
isfor the multiplication operator
[
3
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(f,g)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle\mathcal{T}_{j}f,g\rangle+\langle Vf,g\rangle

with the domain

\text{Dom}(\mathcal{Q})=\Bigg\{f\in L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\mathcal{T}_{j}f|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\text{and }M_{\sqrt{V}}f\in L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\Bigg\}.

Since the space
\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is invariant under Dunkl operators
[

]
, we have

\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\mathcal{Q})
. Hence, the quadratic form
\mathcal{Q}
is densely defined in
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
. Also note that
\mathcal{Q}
is a closed quadratic form. Consequently, there exists a unique positive self-adjoint operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}f,f\rangle=\mathcal{Q}(f,f),\quad f\in\text{Dom}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}).

In addition,
\text{Dom}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k})=\text{Dom}(\mathcal{Q})\text{ and }\mathcal{Q}(f,f)=\|\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{1/2}f\|^{2}_{L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Here
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{1/2}
is the unique positive self-adjoint operator on
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{1/2})^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
and we have the followings
[
3
]
:

(i)

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
is the closure of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}
.

(ii)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup of self-adjoint contractions

\{e^{-t\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}\}_{t>0}
on
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Moreover, for each
t>0
, the semigroup admits the integral representation

\displaystyle e^{-t\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}f(x)=\mathbf{K}_{t}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{t}(x,y)\,f(y)\,w_{k}(y)dy,

(2.4)

where the kernel
k_{t}(x,y)
is nonnegative and satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle 0\leq k_{t}(x,y)\leq h_{t}(x,y),\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;t>0.

2.3.
Critical radius function

We assume that
q>\max\left\{1,\frac{n+2\gamma}{2}\right\}
and the potential function
V\geq 0
belongs to the reverse Hölder class
RH_{k}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
26
]
, that is, there exists a constant
C_{RH}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}V(x)^{q}\,w_{k}(x)\,dx\right)^{1/q}\leq C_{RH}\,\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}V(x)\,w_{k}(x)\,dx,

(2.6)

for every ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The potential
V
induces a function
\rho_{k}
, called the critical radius function, on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

\rho_{k}(x)=\sup\left\{r>0:\frac{r^{2}}{\omega_{k}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}V(\xi)\,w_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi\leq 1\right\}.

The function

was introduced in
[

]

[

]
and Dziubański
[

The fundamental properties of

For each

\displaystyle{\digamma}_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:2^{j/2}\leq m_{k}(x,V)<2^{(j+1)/2}\}.

Using

. The following result gives the finite overlapping property of the collection

and is analogous to

.

Let

, for

. Then there exists a constant

such that for every

Proof.

\displaystyle m_{k}(y,V)\leq C_{0}m_{k}(x,V)\left(1+m_{k}(x,V)|x-y|\right)^{\kappa_{0}}

3.
The space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})

The space
BMO
can be viewed as a natural replacement of
L^{\infty}
in harmonic analysis, particularly in endpoint estimates, since it strictly contains
L^{\infty}
and enjoys better stability properties under singular integral operators. For a detailed discussion, we refer the reader to
[
37
]
. The theory of
BMO
has been extended in various settings, for example, see
[
10
,
31
]
. In the Dunkl framework, the corresponding
BMO
space is defined by

\displaystyle BMO_{k}=\left\{f\in L_{k,\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\sup_{B}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}\lvert f(x)-f_{B}\rvert\,w_{k}(x)\,dx<\infty\right\},

where the supremum is taken over all balls
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

f_{B}=\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}f(x)\,w_{k}(x)\,dx.

The associated seminorm is given by

\|f\|_{BMO_{k}}=\sup_{B}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}\lvert f(x)-f_{B}\rvert\,w_{k}(x)\,dx.

Since constant functions have zero seminorm, the quotient space of
BMO_{k}
modulo constants is a Banach space. Further details on
BMO_{k}
spaces can be found in
[
17
]
. Several related function spaces of
BMO
type, such as
CMO
,
VMO
,
BLO
etc., have been studied in the literature
[
2
,
17
,
23
,
28
]
. In this section, we introduce the space
BMO(\mathcal{L})
in the Dunkl setting and present its characterizations together with some fundamental properties.

Definition 3.1
.

A function
f\in L_{k,\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is said to
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
function, if there exists a constant
C_{f}\geq 0
such that

\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(x,s))}\int_{B(x,s)}\left|f(y)-f_{B(x,s)}\right|w_{k}(y)\,dy\leq C_{f},

and

\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(y)|\,w_{k}(y)\,dy\leq C_{f},

for every ball
B(x,s)
and
B(x,r)
satisfying

s<\rho_{k}(x)\leq r,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The smallest
C_{f}
satisfying the above conditions we denote as
\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}
. Let
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. Then the following inequality holds:

\|f\|_{BMO_{k}}\leq 2\,\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

(3.1)

As an immediate consequence, we obtain the continuous embedding
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})\hookrightarrow BMO_{k},

that is,
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})\subset BMO_{k}
. The inclusion is strict. For
k=0
, we have the classical example
f(x)=\log^{+}(|x|)\in BMO\setminus BMO(\mathcal{L})

[
7
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
is a Banach space endowed with the norm

\|\cdot\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}
.

Proof.

The proof is standard and straightforward.
∎

In the following, we discuss the properties of
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
functions. To this end, we require the John–Nirenberg inequality for
BMO_{k}
functions. We note that the Dunkl theory induces a metric measure space whose associated measure
\omega_{k}
satisfies the doubling condition (
2.1
). Consequently, many classical results from the theory of
BMO
spaces on spaces of homogeneous type remain valid in this setting.

Theorem 3.3
.

(John-Nirenberg inequality for
BMO_{k}
) Let
f\in BMO_{k}
and
\lambda>0
. Then there exist positive constants
c
and
C
such that

\displaystyle\omega_{k}\left(\{x\in B:|f(x)-f_{B}|>\lambda\}\right)\leq C\omega_{k}(B)e^{-\frac{c\lambda}{\|f\|_{BMO_{k}}}},

(3.2)

for all balls
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

The result is a particular case of
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
.
∎

The following are the consequences of John-Nirenberg type inequality.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. Then there exist positive constants
C
and
C^{*}
such that, for every
p\in[1,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)-f_{B}|^{p}w_{k}(x)\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})},\quad\text{for all balls }B,

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)|^{p}w_{k}(x)\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{*}\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})},\quad\text{for all balls }B=B(x_{0},r),

(3.4)

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\geq\rho_{k}(x_{0})
. Moreover, the constant
C
depends only on the doubling constant
C_{w_{k}}
,
p
, and
\rho_{k}
.

Proof.

From the layer cake representation of a non-negative measurable function, along with the John-Nirenberg type inequality (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{B}|f(x)-f_{B}|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}w_{k}\left(\{x\in B:|f(x)-f_{B}|>t\}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq C\omega_{k}(B)p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}e^{-\frac{ct}{\|f\|_{BMO_{k}}}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{C\|f\|_{BMO_{k}}^{p}\omega_{k}(B)\Gamma(p+1)}{c^{p}},

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the gamma function. This establishes (
3.3
) by invoking (
3.1
)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)-f_{B}|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{BMO_{k}},\,\text{ for all balls }B.

The inequality (
3.4
) can be derived from (
3.3
) by applying the Minkowski’s inequality

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)-f_{B}|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+|f_{B}|.

Note that
|f_{B}|\leq|f|_{B}
and
|f|_{B}\leq\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}
for all
B=B(x_{0},r)
with
r\geq{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})
.
∎

We establish a characterization of the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
that avoids distinguishing between different types of balls. The proof is obtained by adapting the corresponding characterization of the
bmo
space on metric measure spaces with doubling measures
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\in L_{k,\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
if and only if for every ball
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists a constant
\mathfrak{C}_{B}
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

\displaystyle C_{p}=\sup\limits_{B}\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)-\mathfrak{C}_{B}|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty,

\text{ where }1\leq p<\infty.

(ii)

\displaystyle C=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B=B(x,r)\\
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;r>0\end{subarray}}\,\frac{|\mathfrak{C}_{B}|}{\log_{2}\!\left(1+\frac{{\rho_{k}}(x)}{r}\right)}<\infty.

Moreover, the
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
is related with the constants
C_{p}
and
C
by following relation

\displaystyle\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}\approx\inf\limits_{\mathfrak{C}_{B}}\{\max\{C_{p},C\}\},

where the infimum is evaluated over all
\mathfrak{C}_{B}
satisfying the above two conditions.

Proof.

We begin the proof by assuming that
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. It is standard to define the constant
\mathfrak{C}_{B}
, associated with a ball
B=B(x,r)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by

\mathfrak{C}_{B}=\begin{cases}f_{B},&r<\rho_{k}(x),\\
0,&r\geq\rho_{k}(x).\end{cases}

Then Corollary
3.4
and the fact that
f
is a
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
function imply condition
(i)
for each
p\in[1,\infty)
. Also, the choice of
\mathfrak{C}_{B}
for larger balls reduces the proof of the condition
(ii)
. It is enough to show that

\displaystyle\frac{|f_{B}|}{\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{{\rho_{k}}(x)}{r}\right)}\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}\,\text{ for }r\leq{\rho_{k}}(x),

where the constant
C
is independent of
r
.
We choose
m
as the smallest positive integer such that,
2^{m}r\geq{\rho_{k}}(x)
and consider the collection of balls
B_{j}=B(x,2^{j}r)
for
j=0,1,\cdots,m
. Using Corollary
3.4
and (
2.1
), we deduce that

\displaystyle|f_{B}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}|f_{B_{j}}-f_{B_{j+1}}|+|f_{B_{m}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{j})}\int_{B_{j}}|f(y)-f_{B_{j+1}}|w_{k}(y)dy+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{m})}\int_{B_{m}}|f(y)|w_{k}(y)dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{C_{w_{k}}}{\omega_{k}(B_{j+1})}\int_{B_{j+1}}|f(y)-f_{B_{j+1}}|w_{k}(y)dy+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{m})}\int_{B_{m}}|f(y)|w_{k}(y)dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(mC_{w_{k}}+1\right)\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

Since
\frac{\rho_{k}(x)}{r}\geq 1
, we can find a constant
C>0
, independent of the radius, such that

m\leq\log_{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{k}(x)}{r}\right)+1\leq C\log_{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{k}(x)}{r}+1\right).

Thus, we obtain the desired inequality.

Now we consider the converse part, assuming that
f\in L_{k,\text{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and satisfying conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
. Then by condition
(i)
with
p=1
, there exists a constant
\mathfrak{C}_{B_{0}}
for the ball
B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|f(x)-f_{B_{0}}|w_{k}(x)dx\leq\frac{2}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|f(x)-\mathfrak{C}_{B_{0}}|w_{k}(x)dx\leq 2C_{1}.

(3.5)

In particular, for
r\geq{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|f(x)|w_{k}(x)dx\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|f(x)-\mathfrak{C}_{B_{0}}|w_{k}(x)dx\,+\,|\mathfrak{C}_{B_{0}}|\leq C_{1}+C.

(3.6)

The estimates (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) show that the
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
norm of
f
is the
\max\{2C_{1},C_{1}+C\}
, which is nothing but the constant multiple of
\max\{C_{1},C\}
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The local
bmo
space is first defined in the Euclidean setting
[
21
]
and later extended to more general settings, such as metric measure spaces with a doubling measure
[
10
]
. Similarly, we define the
bmo
space in the Dunkl setting using the doubling measure
\omega_{k}
. Fix a radius
R>0
. The space
bmo
is the collection of all
f\in L_{k,\text{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\|f\|_{bmo}=\sup_{B}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)-\mathfrak{C}_{B}|\,w_{k}(x)\,dx<\infty.

where the supremum is taken over all balls
B=B(x,r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

\mathfrak{C}_{B}=\begin{cases}f_{B},&\text{if }r<R,\\
0,&\text{if }r\geq R.\end{cases}

Here
r
is the radius of the ball. The space
bmo
can be viewed as a particular case of the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})

corresponding to a constant potential function
V
. Indeed, let
V=C

for some constant
C>0
. Then the critical radius associated with
V
is
given by

\rho_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}},\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Now, if
V\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\rho_{k}(x)=\infty
for all

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this case, the localization disappears and

BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
coincides with
BMO_{k}
.

4.
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
as the dual space of Hardy space

In this section, we identify the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
as the dual of the Hardy space associated with the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}_{k}}
, whose potential function satisfies the reverse Hölder inequality. In
[
26
]
, the Hardy space
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
is defined by

\displaystyle H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}=\left\{f\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):f^{*}(x)=\sup_{t>0}|\mathbf{K}_{t}f(x)|\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right\},

with norm
\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}}=\|f^{*}\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Here
\mathbf{K}_{t}
is the integral transform associated with the Dunkl-Schrödinger operator (
2.4
). An atomic characterization of
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
was also obtained in terms of atoms satisfying appropriate size and cancellation conditions, analogous to those in the classical Hardy space theory associated with Schrödinger operators
[
16
]
. Moreover, the cancellation conditions imposed on the atoms are determined by a suitable collection of cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Further results related to the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
can be found in
[
18
,
26
,
27
]
.

The available atomic decomposition for
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
is formulated in terms of cubes. To establish the duality, we require a characterization of
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
in terms of atoms whose cancellation depends on the critical radius (cf.
[
15
]
). An analogous characterization is available for the Hardy space associated with the Dunkl harmonic oscillator
[
25
]
. In this section, we also derive an atomic decomposition for
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
under the following assumption on the potential function
V
:

(i)

For all
N>0
, there exist positive constants
c
and
C
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0

\displaystyle k_{t}(x,y)\leq C{\left(1+\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)\mathcal{}\right)^{-N}}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)^{2}}{ct}\right)\left(\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))\right)^{-1},

(4.1)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}(x,y)=\frac{t}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\int_{B(x,\sqrt{t})}V(\xi)w_{k}(\xi)d\xi+\frac{t}{\omega_{k}(B(y,\sqrt{t}))}\int_{B(y,\sqrt{t})}V(\xi)w_{k}(\xi)d\xi.

The proof techniques for the atomic decomposition are inspired by
[
14
]
and
[
16
]
. In
[
14
]
, the arguments rely on the fundamental solutions of Schrödinger operators. However, such fundamental solutions are not available for the Dunkl-Schrödinger operators. To overcome this difficulty, we incorporate suitable modifications of the methods developed in
[
16
]
.

4.1.
Atomic decomposition:

We recall the definition of atoms associated with the balls and the atomic Hardy space in the following definition.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
a
be a measurable function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
a
is called an
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
-atom associated with the ball
B(x_{0},r_{0})
if

(i)

supp(
a)\subset B(x_{0},r_{0})
,

(ii)

\|a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(x_{0},r_{0}))},

(iii)

If
x_{0}\in\digamma_{j}
and

0<r_{0}<2^{-1-\frac{j}{2}}
, then
a
satisfies the cancellation condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx=0
.

H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}
is the space of functions
f\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which admits a representation of the form

\displaystyle f=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{m}a_{m},

where
c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
and
a_{m}
are atoms associated with
H^{1}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}
such that
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|c_{m}|<\infty
. The space
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}
is a Banach space with the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}}=\inf\left\{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}|c_{j}|:f=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{j}a_{j}\text{ where, }c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\text{ and }a_{j}\text{ are }H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}\text{-atoms}\right\}.

The following theorem explains the atomic decomposition of
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
functions.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
V
satisfies (
4.1
). Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every
f\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\displaystyle C^{-1}\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}}\leq\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}}\leq C\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}}.

We prove the theorem by showing the containment
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}\subset H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
and
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}\subset H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}
. The proof is based on the local Hardy space
[
14
,
16
]
. For more details on the local Hardy space, readers can refer to
[
21
]
for the Euclidean setting and
[
25
]
for the Dunkl setting.
In order to prove the results, we need some lemmas, which are stated below.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
N>0
, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that

\displaystyle k_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)^{2}}{ct}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(x)}+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(y)}\right)^{-N}

(4.2)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
.

Proof.

Invoking the symmetry of the kernel
k_{t}
, (
2.5
) and, (
2.3
), it is enough to prove

\displaystyle k_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)^{2}}{ct}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(x)}\right)^{-N},

for
\sqrt{t}\leq\rho_{k}(x)
. Using
[
26
, Lemma 7]
, we have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{k}(x)^{2}}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\int_{B(x,\rho_{k}(x))}V(\xi)w_{k}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\rho_{k}(x)}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{2-\frac{n+2\gamma}{q}}\frac{t}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\int_{B(x,\sqrt{t})}V(\xi)w_{k}(\xi)d\xi.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\frac{t}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\int_{B(x,\sqrt{t})}V(\xi)w_{k}(\xi)d\xi\geq\frac{1}{C}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(x)}\right)^{2-\frac{n+2\gamma}{q}}.

(4.3)

Applying (
4.3
) in (
4.1
), we deduce that

\displaystyle k_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{\omega_{k}(B(x,\sqrt{t}))}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)^{2}}{ct}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(x)}\right)^{-\left({2-\frac{n+2\gamma}{q}}\right)m},

true for all
m>0
. This concludes the desired inequality.
∎

Before stating the next result, we recall the measure
\mu_{V}
associated with the potential function
V
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
introduced in
[
26
]
. For every measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mu_{V}(A)=\int_{A}V(x)\,w_{k}(x)\,dx.

It was shown in
[
26
]
that there exists a constant
C_{\mu_{V}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{V}(B(x,2r))\leq C_{\mu_{V}}\mu_{V}(B(x,r)),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;r>0.

(4.4)

Further properties of the measure
\mu_{V}
can also be found in
[
26
]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
x\in B_{(j,d)}
and

\sqrt{t}\leq c_{0}\rho_{k}(x)
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y)V(y)w_{k}(y)dy\leq Ct^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(x)}\right)^{2-\frac{n+2\gamma}{q}}.

(4.5)

Proof.

Using the fact that
V\in RH_{k}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and Hölders inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y)\,V(y)\,w_{k}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}\left(B_{(j,d)}^{**}\right)}\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}V(y)^{q}w_{k}(y)dy\right)^{1/q}\omega_{k}\left(B_{(j,d)}^{**}\right)^{1/q}\left(\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y)^{q^{\prime}}w_{k}(y)dy\right)^{1/q^{\prime}},

where
q^{\prime}
is the conjugate exponent of
q
. Observe that for each
x\in B_{(j,d)}
, we have
\omega_{k}(B(x,\rho_{k}(x)))\sim\omega_{k}\left(B_{(j,d)}^{**}\right)
. Then

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\omega_{k}\left(B_{(j,d)}^{**}\right)^{1/q}\left(\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y)^{q^{\prime}}w_{k}(y)dy\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}\frac{\omega_{k}\left(B(x,\rho_{k}(x))\right)^{q^{\prime}/q}}{\omega_{k}\left(B(x,\sqrt{t}))\right)^{q^{\prime}-1}}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{-q^{\prime}d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,y)^{2}}{t}\right)}{\omega_{k}\left(B(y,\sqrt{t}))\right)}w_{k}(y)dy\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}.

Since
\sqrt{t}\leq c_{0}\rho_{k}(x)
, it follows from (
2.2
) that
I_{1}\leq C\left(\frac{\rho_{k}(x)}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{(n+2\gamma)/q}
. Using (
2.6
) and (
4.4
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}\left(B_{(j,d)}^{**}\right)}\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}V(y)^{q}w_{k}(y)dy\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{RH}}{\omega_{k}\left(B_{(j,d)}^{**}\right)}\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}V(y)w_{k}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\rho_{k}(x)^{2}}.

Thus, we have the desired inequality

\displaystyle\int_{B_{(j,d)}^{**}}\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y)V(y)w_{k}(y)dy\leq Ct^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\rho_{k}(x)}\right)^{2-\frac{n+2\gamma}{q}}.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
f\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for each
\Psi_{(j,d)}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{(j,d)^{*}}}\,\sup\limits_{0<t\leq 2^{-j}}H_{t}(|\Psi_{(j,d)}f|)(x)w_{k}(x)dx\leq C\|\Psi_{(j,d)}f\|_{L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(4.6)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{(j,d)^{*}}}\,\sup\limits_{0<t\leq 2^{-j}}\mathbf{K}_{t}(|\Psi_{(j,d)}f|)(x)w_{k}(x)dx\leq C\|\Psi_{(j,d)}f\|_{L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(4.7)

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{(j,d)}^{*}}\,\sup\limits_{0<t\leq 2^{-j}}H_{t}(|\Psi_{(j,d)}f|)(x)w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{(j,d)}^{*}}\,\sup\limits_{2^{-(j+m+1)}<t\leq 2^{-(j+m)}}\int\limits_{B(x_{(j,d)},2^{1-j/2})}h_{t}(x,y)\Psi_{(j,d)}(y)|f(y)|w_{k}(y)dyw_{k}(x)dx.

Note that
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{(j,d)}^{*}
and
y\in B(x_{(j,d)},2^{1-j/2})
, then we have
|x-y|\geq 2^{-j/2}
. Invoking (
2.3
), we get

\displaystyle I

Lemma 4.6
.

There exists a constant

Thus, we get

\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}}.

On the other hand, it is enough to show the existence of a constant
C>0
, so that

\displaystyle\|a\|_{H^{1}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}}\leq C\quad\text{ for all $H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\text{atm}}$-atoms.}

Let
a
be an

H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}\text{-atoms}
associated with the ball
B(x_{0},r_{0})
. Then by Definition
4.1
and
[
26
, Definition 5]
, we obtain

\Bigg\|\sup\limits_{0<t\leq 2^{-j}}\big|H_{t}\left(a\right)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C.

Using Lemma
4.6
, we deduce that

\Bigg\|\sup\limits_{0<t\leq 2^{-j}}\big|\mathbf{K}_{t}\left(a\right)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C.

Now it remains to prove that

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sup_{t>2^{-j}}\big|\mathbf{K}_{t}\left(a\right)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C.

We write

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sup_{t>2^{-j}}\big|\mathbf{K}_{t}\left(a\right)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigg\|\sup_{2^{-j+m}<t\leq 2^{-j+m+1}}\big|\mathbf{K}_{t}\left(a\right)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Employing the semigroup property, along with (
2.3
), and (
2.5
), for
2^{-j+m}<t\leq 2^{-j+m+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle k_{t}(x,y)|a(y)|w_{k}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{t-2^{-j+m+1}}(x,z)k_{2^{-j+m+1}}(z,y)\,w_{k}(z)dz\,|a(y)|w_{k}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{exp\left(-\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(x,z)^{2}}{2^{-j+m+1}}\right)}{\omega_{k}(B(z,2^{-j+m}))}k_{2^{-j+m+1}}(z,y)\,w_{k}(z)dz\,|a(y)|w_{k}(y)dy.

Taking integration on both sides with respect to
w_{k}(x)\,dx
, and applying (
4.2
) together with Tonelli’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\Bigg\|

\displaystyle\sup_{2^{-j+m}<t\leq 2^{-j+m+1}}\big|\mathbf{K}_{t}\left(a\right)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|a(y)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{2^{-j+m+1}}(z,y)\,w_{k}(z)dz\,w_{k}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|a(y)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{exp\left(\frac{d_{\mathcal{O}}(z,y)}{c2^{-j+m+1}}\right)}{\omega_{k}\left(B(z,2^{(-j+m+1)/2})\right)}\left(1+\frac{2^{(-j+m+1)/2}}{\rho_{k}(y)}\right)^{-N}w_{k}(z)dz\,w_{k}(y)dy.

Since
\operatorname{supp}(a)\subset B(x_{0},r_{0})
, we have
\rho_{k}(y)\sim\rho_{k}(x_{0})=2^{-j/2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigg\|\sup_{2^{-j+m}<t\leq 2^{-j+m+1}}\big|\mathbf{K}_{t}(a)\big|\Bigg\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-mN/2}\|a\|_{L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C.

Hence,

\|f\|_{H^{1}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}}=\Bigg\|\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}c_{l}a_{l}\Bigg\|_{H^{1}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}}\leq\sum_{l}|c_{l}|\,\|a_{l}\|_{H^{1}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}}\leq C\,\|f\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1,\mathrm{atm}}}.

∎

4.2.
Characterization of dual space

The following results concern the duality between
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
and
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
.
Our approach follows Stein’s method, which is based on the atomic decomposition of Hardy spaces
[
37
]
.
We recall below the definition of a
(1,q)_{\rho_{k}}
-atom.

Definition 4.8

(cf.
[
37
]
)
.

Let
1<q\leq\infty
and let
a
be a measurable function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We say that
a
is a
(1,q)_{\rho_{k}}
-atom if the following conditions hold:

(i)

\operatorname{supp}(a)\subset B(x_{0},r_{0})
;

(ii)

\|a\|_{L_{k}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\omega_{k}(B(x_{0},r_{0}))^{-1/p}
,
where
p
is the conjugate exponent of
q
;

(iii)

if
r_{0}<\rho_{k}(x_{0})
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\,w_{k}(x)\,dx=0.

Theorem 4.9
.

There exists a linear isomorphism between the Banach spaces
\left(H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}\right)^{*}
and
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
, we define
\Phi_{f}
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{f}(h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)h(x)w_{k}(x)dx\quad\text{for }h\in H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}.

By the atomic decomposition of
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
, it suffices to check the boundedness of
\Phi_{f}
on
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}\text{-atoms}
. Let
a
be an atom with
\operatorname{supp}(a)\subseteq B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
.

If
r_{0}<\rho_{k}(x_{0})
, then using the cancellation property of
a
we obtain

\displaystyle|\Phi_{f}(a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)a(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx\right|=\left|\int_{B_{0}}\big(f(x)-f_{B_{0}}\big)a(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|f(x)-f_{B_{0}}|\,w_{k}(x)\,dx\leq\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

On the other hand, if
r_{0}\geq\rho_{k}(x_{0})
, we use the size condition of
a
and obtain

\displaystyle|\Phi_{f}(a)|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)a(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx\right|\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|f(x)|\,w_{k}(x)\,dx\leq\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

Since finite linear combinations of atoms are dense in
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
, it follows that
\Phi_{f}
extends to a continuous linear functional on
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
, with

\|\Phi_{f}\|\leq\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

We now consider
\Phi\in\left(H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}\right)^{*}
. Let
B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
with
r_{0}\geq\rho_{k}(x_{0})
, and let
g\in L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})
be nonzero. Define

a=\frac{g}{\|g\|_{L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})}\,\omega_{k}(B_{0})^{1/2}}.

Then
a
is a
(1,2)
-atom. Since
\Phi
is continuous, we have

|\Phi(a)|\leq\|a\|_{H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}}\,\|\Phi\|.

Hence, for every
g\in L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})
,

|\Phi(g)|\leq C\|\Phi\|\|g\|_{L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})}\omega_{k}(B_{0})^{1/2},

where
C
is the constant appearing in Theorem
4.2
. It follows that
\Phi
defines a bounded linear functional on
L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})
with operator norm bounded by
C\|\Phi\|\omega_{k}(B_{0})^{1/2}
. By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists
f_{\Phi}^{B_{0}}\in L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})
such that

\Phi(g)=\int_{B_{0}}g(x)f_{\Phi}^{B_{0}}(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx,

and

\|f_{\Phi}^{B_{0}}\|_{L_{k}^{2}(B_{0})}\leq C\|\Phi\|\omega_{k}(B_{0})^{1/2}.

If
B(y,r_{1})\subset B(y,r_{2})
with
r_{1}\geq\rho_{k}(y)
, then for every
g\in L_{k}^{2}(B(y,r_{1}))
,

\Phi(g)=\int_{B(y,r_{1})}g(x)f_{\Phi}^{B(y,r_{1})}(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx=\int_{B(y,r_{1})}g(x)f_{\Phi}^{B(y,r_{2})}(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx.

Hence
f_{\Phi}^{B(y,r_{1})}=f_{\Phi}^{B(y,r_{2})}
a.e. on
B(y,r_{1})
. Consequently, there exists a unique function
f\in L_{k,\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\Phi(g)=\int_{B}g(x)f(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx

for every
g\in L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\operatorname{supp}(g)\subset B=B(y,r)
and
r\geq\rho_{k}(y)
. In particular, this holds for all
(1,\infty)
-atoms and their finite linear combinations. Hence,
\Phi
admits an integral representation on a dense subset and extends to the whole space
H_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{1}
. It remains to show that
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. Let
B=B(y,r)
with
r\geq\rho_{k}(y)
. Then

\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)|w_{k}(x)\,dx\leq\frac{\|f\|_{L_{k}^{2}(B)}}{\omega_{k}(B)^{1/2}}\leq C\|\Phi\|.

For balls
B=B(y,s)
with
s<\rho_{k}(y)
, take a nonzero
g\in L_{k}^{2}(B)
satisfying

\int_{B}g(x)w_{k}(x)\,dx=0.

In this case, the representative
f
is determined by taking the modulo with constants, that is, as an element of
L_{k}^{2}(B)/\mathcal{C}
. Moreover,

\inf_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\|f-c\|_{L_{k}^{2}(B)}\leq C\|\Phi\|\omega_{k}(B)^{1/2}.

By Corollary
3.4
,

\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|w_{k}(x)\,dx\leq\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|^{2}w_{k}(x)\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leq C\|\Phi\|,

for every ball whose radius is smaller than the critical radius. Therefore
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
.
∎

5.
Maximal operators on
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
space

In this section, we study the behavior of maximal operators on the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. In the classical setting, the maximal operator on
BMO
is either identically infinite or satisfies

\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{BMO}\leq C\|f\|_{BMO}.

Bennett
et al.
proved the boundedness of the maximal function restricted to a cube
Q
on the space
BMO(Q)

[
7
]
. Later, Dziubański
et al.
established the boundedness of maximal operators on the space
BMO(\mathcal{L})
. Similar results also hold in the setting of the Heisenberg group
[
31
]
and metric measure spaces
[
10
]
.
In our analysis, we use the weighted uncentered Hardy-Littlewood maximal function, which we denote as
\mathcal{M}_{k}
and defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)=\sup\limits_{x\in B}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(y)|w_{k}(y)dy\quad\text{ for }f\in L_{k,\text{loc}}^{1}.

In the Dunkl setting, there are many versions of the maximal operators. For more details, readers can refer to
[
2
]
.
We decompose the family of all balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into two disjoint subcollections according to their radii. Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{B=B(x,r):r\leq{\rho_{k}}(x)\text{ where }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{B=B(x,s):s>{\rho_{k}}(x)\text{ where }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

Associated with the subcollection
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, we define the localized maximal operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}f(x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\\
x\in B\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(y)|\,w_{k}(y)dy,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}f(x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}_{2}\\
x\in B\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(y)|\,w_{k}(y)dy,

respectively. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)=\max\{\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}f(x),\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}f(x)\}.

(5.1)

We begin by defining the maximal function over the
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
space and further discuss the boundedness property. Before proving the boundedness of the weighted maximal function over the
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
space, we observe some properties of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
function over
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
C_{0}\geq 1
, and
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. Then
\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)
is finite almost everywhere on
B(x_{0},C_{0}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0}))
.

Proof.

Decompose
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
, where
f_{1}=f\chi_{B(x_{0},C_{0}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0}))^{*}}
and
f_{2}=f\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(x_{0},C_{0}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0}))^{*}}
. Since
f_{1}\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the weighted maximal operator
\mathcal{M}_{k}f_{1}
is finite almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Now we consider
\mathcal{M}_{k}f_{2}
. Let
x\in B(x_{0},C_{0}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0}))
. For any ball
B
containing
x
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f_{2}(\xi)|\,w_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi\neq 0\quad\text{only if}\quad B\cap B(x_{0},C_{0}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0}))^{*}\neq\emptyset.

Writing
B=B(y,r_{y})
, this implies
2r_{y}>C_{0}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})
, and hence
B\subset B(x_{0},4r_{y})
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(y,r_{y}))}\int_{B(y,r_{y})}|f_{2}(\xi)|w_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(y,r_{y}))}\int_{B(x_{0},4r_{y})}|f(\xi)|w_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Since
f_{1}\in L_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows from the weak-type
(1,1)
boundedness of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
that
\mathcal{M}_{k}f_{1}(x)
is finite almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(y,r_{y}))}

\displaystyle\int_{B(y,r_{y})}|f_{2}(\xi)|w_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq C_{w_{k}}\frac{\omega_{k}(B(x_{0},4r_{y}))}{\omega_{k}(B(y,r_{y}))}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B(x_{0},4r_{y}))}\int_{B(x_{0},4r_{y})}|f(\xi)|w_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq C_{w_{k}}^{2}4^{n+2\gamma}\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}<\infty.

(5.2)

∎

In the following proposition, we establish the local integrability of the function
\mathcal{M}_{k}f
, whenever
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. Then
\mathcal{M}_{k}f\in L^{1}_{k,\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

To prove
\mathcal{M}_{k}f\in L^{1}_{k,\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows from (
5.1
) that it is enough to show that

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}f,\,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}f\in L_{k}^{1}(B_{0}),

where
B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
with
r_{0}<\infty
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}f
, it follows directly from the fact that
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
that

\int_{B_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}f(x)\,w_{k}(x)\,dx\leq\omega_{k}(B_{0})\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}<\infty.

On the other hand, for every
x\in B_{0}
,
[
26
, Lemma 8 ]
gives the existence of
c,\kappa_{0}>0
so that

\displaystyle c^{-1}{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})\left(1+\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{-\kappa_{0}}\leq{\rho_{k}}(x)\leq c{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})\left(1+\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}}.

(5.3)

The inequality (
5.3
) rewritten as

\displaystyle c^{-1}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{-\kappa_{0}}\leq\frac{{\rho_{k}}(x)}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\leq c\left(1+\frac{r_{0}}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}}.

(5.4)

Moreover, if
x\in B_{0}\cap B
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, where
B=B(y,r)
, then

\displaystyle c^{-1}{\rho_{k}}(y)2^{-\kappa_{0}}\leq{\rho_{k}}(x)\leq c{\rho_{k}}(y)2^{\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}}.

(5.5)

Combining (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), we have

\displaystyle(c^{-1})^{2}2^{-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{-\kappa_{0}}\leq\frac{{\rho_{k}}(y)}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\leq c^{2}2^{\left(\kappa_{0}+\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}\right)}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}}.

Let
R_{0}=r_{0}+(1+2C){\rho_{k}}(x_{0})
, where
C=c^{2}2^{\left(\kappa_{0}+\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}\right)}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}}{{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})}\right)^{\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}+1}}
. Then
B\subset\widetilde{B_{0}}=B(x_{0},R_{0})
for all balls
B
such that
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
B\cap B_{0}\neq\emptyset
.
Therefore, for
x\in B_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}f(x)\leq\mathcal{M}_{k}(f\chi_{\widetilde{B_{0}}})(x).

At this point, Corollary
3.4
implies that
f\chi_{\widetilde{B_{0}}}\in L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Invoking the
L^{2}
boundedness of the weighted maximal function
[
1
]
and Hölder’s inequality reduces that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{k}(f\chi_{\widetilde{B_{0}}})(x)w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{k}(B_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{M}_{k}(f\chi_{\widetilde{B_{0}}})\|_{L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\omega_{k}(B_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{k}^{2}({\widetilde{B_{0}}})}

\displaystyle\leq Cw_{k}(\widetilde{B_{0}})\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}<\infty.

Thus, we have the integrability.
∎

Note 5.3
.

Observe that we have shown the local integrability of the function
\mathcal{M}_{k}f
whenever
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. In the same way one can prove the local
p
-integrability of
\mathcal{M}_{k}f
, where
p\in[1,\infty)
. Using the fact
f\in BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
, we have

\int_{B_{0}}|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}f(x)|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx\leq\omega_{k}(B_{0})\|f\|^{p}_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

Together with the
L^{p}
boundedness of the weighted maximal function
[
1
]
and Corollary
3.4
, it is straightforward that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{0}}|\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}f(x)|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{0}}|\mathcal{M}_{k}(f\chi_{\widetilde{B_{0}}})(x)|^{p}w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{M}_{k}(f\chi_{\widetilde{B_{0}}})\|^{p}_{L_{k}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|^{p}_{L_{k}^{p}({\widetilde{B_{0}}})}

\displaystyle\leq Cw_{k}(\widetilde{B_{0}})\|f\|^{p}_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}<\infty.

In the following theorem, we prove the boundedness of the maximal function on the space
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
. Our approach is inspired by the method developed in
[
7
]
and
[
31
]
.

Theorem 5.4
.

The maximal operator
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is a bounded operator on
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
space.

Proof.

We split the proof into two cases based on the two cases in the definition of
BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})
.

i)

Case-1:
B=B(x_{0},r_{0})
for
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r_{0}\geq{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})
. Here we have to show

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f(y)|w_{k}(y)dy\,\leq\,C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

(5.6)

To this end, we decompose the function
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
, where
f_{1}=f\chi_{B^{*}}
and
f_{2}=f\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B^{*}}
. In light of (
5.2
), we observe that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f_{2}(y)|w_{k}(y)dy\,\leq\,C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

(5.7)

For the function
f_{1}
, we use the boundedness of weighted maximal function
\mathcal{M}_{k}
on
L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
1
]
and Corollary
3.4
yields that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f_{1}(x)|w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\left(\int_{B}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f_{1}(x)|^{2}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\omega_{k}(B))^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f_{1}(x)|^{2}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}|f(x)|^{2}w_{k}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

(5.8)

ii)

Case-2:
B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
for
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r_{0}<{\rho_{k}}(x_{0})
. The desired estimate we have to prove is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f(y)-(\mathcal{M}_{k}f)_{B_{0}}\big|w_{k}(y)dy\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

(5.9)

We may assume that
f
is nonnegative function.
We decompose the family of all balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into two disjoint subcollections as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{1}

\displaystyle=\{B:B=B(y_{0},r)\subset B_{0}^{*},\text{ where $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $r>0$}\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\{B:B=B(y_{0},r):B\not\subset B_{0}^{*},\text{ where $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $r>0$}\}.

Associated with the subcollection
\mathcal{B}^{1}
and
\mathcal{B}^{2}
, we define the localized maximal operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}f(x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}^{1}\\
x\in B\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}f(y)\,w_{k}(y)dy,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}f(x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}^{2}\\
x\in B\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B)}\int_{B}f(y)\,w_{k}(y)dy,

respectively. Clearly
\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)=\max\{\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}f(x),\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}f(x)\}
, and if
\Omega_{0}=\{x\in B_{0}:\,\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)\geq(\mathcal{M}_{k}f)_{B_{0}}\},\,\Omega_{1}=\{x\in\Omega_{0}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}f(x)\geq\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}f(x)\},\text{ and }\Omega_{2}=\Omega_{0}\setminus\Omega_{1}.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}

\displaystyle\int_{B_{0}}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}|w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\right)w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{j}}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\right)w_{k}(x)dx.

We deduce (
5.9
) by establishing that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{j}}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\right)w_{k}(x)dx\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}\quad\text{ for }j=1,2.

Consider the function
\tilde{f_{1}}=\left(f-f_{B_{0}^{*}}\right)\chi_{B_{0}^{*}}
. Since
f_{B_{0}^{*}}\leq\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}
, we have

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}f(x)\leq\mathcal{M}_{k}(\tilde{f_{1}})(x)+\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}.

Then Corollary
3.4
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\right)w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{\Omega_{1}}{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\tilde{f_{1}}(x)w_{k}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{\Omega_{1}}{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\tilde{f_{1}}(x)^{2}w_{k}(x)dx\ \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{B_{0}}\tilde{f_{1}}(x)^{2}w_{k}(x)dx\ \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{*}\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

On the other hand, let
x\in\Omega_{2}
and
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{2}
with
B^{\prime}\cap(B_{0}^{*})^{c}\neq\emptyset
. Consider
B^{\prime\prime}=B^{\prime***}
. Then
B_{0}\subset B^{\prime\prime}
, we have
f_{B^{\prime\prime}}\leq\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}
. Thus we obtained

\displaystyle f_{B^{\prime}}-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\leq f_{B^{\prime}}-f_{B^{\prime\prime}}\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

Taking supremum over
\mathcal{B}^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{k}(B_{0})}\int_{\Omega_{2}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}f(x)-\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}f\right)_{B_{0}}\right)w_{k}(x)dx\leq C\|f\|_{BMO(\mathcal{L}_{k})}.

This completes the proof.
