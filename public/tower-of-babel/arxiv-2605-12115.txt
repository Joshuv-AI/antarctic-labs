Thermal and spatial confinement effects in Podolsky electrodynamics
L. H. A. R. Ferreira, A. F. Santos

Abstract
In this work, Podolsky theory, a second-order, Lorentz- and gauge-invariant extension of classical electrodynamics, is considered. The effects of Podolsky’s modification on fundamental phenomena such as the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Casimir effect for the electromagnetic field are investigated. The Thermo Field Dynamics (TFD) formalism is employed to describe quantum fields at finite temperature and under spatial confinement through its topological structure.



I
Introduction

The classical theory of electromagnetism is fundamentally described by Maxwell electrodynamics, from which a vast range of scientific and technological advancements has emerged. This theory is grounded in Maxwell’s four equations, which inherently ensure charge conservation through the continuity equation and reflect the underlying symmetries of the electromagnetic field. However, despite its robustness, Maxwell’s theory exhibits certain limitations, particularly in the presence of divergences at small scales. In response to these challenges, an extension known as Podolsky electrodynamics was proposed by B. Podolsky in 1942
p1
;
p2
. This generalized framework introduces a second-order derivative term into the Lagrangian, while preserving both Lorentz and gauge invariance. One of the key features of this modification is the emergence of a massive sector for the photon, achieved without violating gauge symmetry. The additional higher-derivative term serves to regularize the theory at high frequencies, effectively acting as a natural cutoff. A notable example of this regularizing effect appears in the electrostatic potential: whereas Maxwell’s theory predicts a divergence as
\displaystyle r\to 0
, Podolsky electrodynamics yields a finite, non-zero value at the origin
p3
. Podolsky electrodynamics has been investigated in a variety of physical contexts, particularly in the formulation of plasma models based on generalized electrodynamics with higher-order derivatives. The presence of an additional massive mode, alongside the usual massless photon, modifies both the propagation properties of the gauge field and the effective interactions it mediates across different energy scales. In the finite-temperature regime, such modifications directly affect the collective properties of the plasma. In
ref1
, it is shown that although the general structure of electrostatic screening is preserved, the Debye mass becomes dependent not only on temperature, as in conventional QED, but also on the parameter associated with the Podolsky term. Within the Matsubara formalism, an explicit one-loop calculation of the polarization tensor reveals that the static limit of the longitudinal sector incorporates contributions from the additional massive mode, leading to quantitative corrections to the exponential suppression of the electrostatic potential.

At the level of effective descriptions, these modifications are reflected in the formulation of Abelian plasma theories coupled to quantized electromagnetic fields with Podolsky corrections
ref2
. The resulting Hamiltonian structure exhibits modified photon dispersion relations and new propagation modes. Upon integrating out the photonic degrees of freedom, an effective plasma Hamiltonian emerges in which particle-mediated mode–mode couplings arise, along with collective effects such as mode mixing, photon condensation, and non-Markovian behavior in finite systems. The interplay between thermal screening and ultraviolet regularization leads to more stable intermediate-range interactions at high energies, suggesting potential relevance for confined plasmas, solid-state systems, and laser–plasma interactions. Furthermore, the incorporation of gauge-preserving mass-generation mechanisms further extends the theoretical scope of the model. In
ref3
, a formulation combining Podolsky electrodynamics with a Stueckelberg-type mechanism is developed, introducing a compensating scalar field that preserves the
\displaystyle U(1)
gauge symmetry while dynamically linking the infrared regime, associated with a Proca-type mass, to the ultraviolet regime controlled by the higher-derivative scale. Finally, a consistent microscopic foundation for relativistic kinetic plasma theory is provided in
ref4
, where it is argued that the Vlasov–Maxwell equations should be derived from the regularized Bopp–Landé–Thomas–Podolsky electrodynamics. In the continuum limit, this approach leads to a Vlasov–Maxwell–BLTP-type system including
\displaystyle 1/N
corrections and radiation-reaction effects, while recovering the standard Vlasov–Maxwell system in the singular limit
\displaystyle\kappa\to\infty
. Taken together, these results indicate that higher–derivative gauge theories not only provide improved ultraviolet behavior and better mathematical control, but also lead to potentially measurable physical consequences in the collective properties and effective dynamics of interacting systems across multiple energy scales.

II
Thermo Field Dynamics formalism

The Thermo Field Dynamics (TFD) formalism provides a powerful framework for describing quantum systems at finite temperature. It is formulated as a real-time thermal quantum field theory, based on the equivalence between the statistical average of an operator and its vacuum expectation value. This approach allows one to retain the full temporal structure of the system. Within this formulation, the thermal Hilbert space emerges as the tensor product of the original Hilbert space and an auxiliary, or tilde, Hilbert space:
\displaystyle{\cal H}_{T}={\cal H}\otimes\tilde{\cal H}
. This construction leads to a doubling of the degrees of freedom
thermofield
.

Another essential element of the TFD formalism is the Bogoliubov transformation. This transformation performs a rotation between the tilde and non-tilde variables, introducing a dependence on a parameter
\displaystyle\alpha=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{D})
, known as the compactification parameter. The term “compactification” highlights a topological feature of the TFD approach: the ability to wrap spacetime dimensions into a toroidal topology. This structure enables the simultaneous description of both thermal (temperature-related) and spatial (finite-size) effects.

\xi\tilde{\cal O}^{\dagger}(k)\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle{\cal U}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal U}(\alpha)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u(\alpha)&-v(\alpha)\\

\displaystyle u^{2}(\alpha)+\xi w^{2}(\alpha)=1

\displaystyle\xi=-1(+1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-I^{\nu\alpha,\hskip 8.19447pt\beta\mu}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\alpha\beta}(x,x^{\prime})+I_{\tau\hskip 8.19447pt\gamma,}^{\;\;\tau,\mu\;\;\gamma\nu}(x,x^{\prime}){\bigg)}{\Bigg\}},

where have been defined

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho,\alpha}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\hskip 8.19447pt\;\;\alpha}(x;x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\lambda\rho}\partial^{\mu}-g^{\mu\lambda}\partial^{\alpha})(g^{\rho}_{\;\;\alpha}\partial^{\prime\nu}-g^{\nu\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}),

(38)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha\beta,}^{\hskip 8.19447pt\lambda\rho,\alpha\beta}(x;x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\lambda}_{\;\;\beta}\partial_{\alpha}-g^{\lambda}_{\;\;\alpha}\partial_{\beta})(g^{\beta\rho}\partial^{\prime\alpha}-g^{\alpha\rho}\partial^{\prime\beta}),

(39)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi^{\alpha\gamma,\hskip 8.19447pt\;\beta\hskip 8.19447pt\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\alpha\gamma,\hskip 8.19447pt\beta,}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\beta\lambda}\Box-g^{\alpha\lambda}\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{\beta})(g^{\rho}_{\;\;\beta}\Box^{\prime}-g^{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\gamma}\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}),

(40)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Theta^{\mu\gamma,\;\;\;\nu,\alpha\;\;\;\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\;\gamma\hskip 8.19447pt\;\alpha,}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\alpha\lambda}\partial^{\mu}-g^{\mu\lambda}\partial^{\alpha})(g_{\alpha}^{\;\;\rho}\Box^{\prime}\partial^{\prime\nu}-g^{\nu\rho}\Box^{\prime}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}),

(41)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Theta^{\nu\gamma,\;\;\;\mu,\alpha\;\;\;\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\;\gamma\hskip 8.19447pt\;\alpha,}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\alpha\lambda}\partial^{\nu}-g^{\nu\lambda}\partial^{\alpha})(g_{\alpha}^{\;\;\rho}\Box^{\prime}\partial^{\prime\mu}-g^{\mu\rho}\Box^{\prime}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}),

(42)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Sigma^{\mu\hskip 8.19447pt\beta,\alpha\nu,\lambda\rho}_{\;\;\alpha,\beta}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\alpha\lambda}\partial^{\mu}-g^{\mu\lambda}\partial^{\alpha})(g^{\nu\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}\Box^{\prime}-g^{\beta\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}\partial^{\prime\nu}),

(43)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Sigma^{\nu\hskip 8.19447pt\beta,\alpha\mu,\lambda\rho}_{\;\;\alpha,\beta}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\alpha\lambda}\partial^{\nu}-g^{\nu\lambda}\partial^{\alpha})(g^{\mu\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}\Box^{\prime}-g^{\beta\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}\partial^{\prime}_{\beta}\partial^{\prime\mu}),

(44)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Pi^{\tau\gamma,\hskip 8.19447pt\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\tau\gamma,}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(g^{\mu\lambda}\Box-g^{\tau\lambda}\partial_{\tau}\partial^{\mu})(g^{\nu\rho}\Box^{\prime}-g^{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\gamma}\partial^{\prime\nu}),

(45)

and
\displaystyle I^{\mu\nu,\rho\lambda,\sigma\zeta}(x,x^{\prime})
represents the full set of commutation relations between the field components and their derivatives.

By taking the vacuum expectation value of the energy-momentum tensor and applying the elements of the TFD formalism, the topological effects – encoded through the Bogoliubov transformation and the compactification parameter – can be introduced. As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\expectationvalue{T^{\mu\nu}(x;\alpha)}=\expectationvalue{T^{\mu\nu}(x)}{0(\alpha)}.

(46)

As a consequence, the photon propagator naturally arises and is defined through the thermal vacuum expectation value as
\displaystyle\braket{0(\alpha)|\tau[A^{\mu}(x)A^{\nu}(x^{\prime})]|0(\alpha)}=iD^{\mu\nu}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)
.

To proceed, a renormalization procedure must be carried out. In the Casimir and thermal contexts, the vacuum expectation value of the energy–momentum tensor is obtained from derivatives of the two-point function in the coincident limit
\displaystyle x^{\prime}\to x
. The divergences arising in this limit are related to the local short-distance structure of the Green function and are independent of the presence of boundaries or temperature. A renormalized quantity is thus defined by subtracting the corresponding Minkowski vacuum contribution,

\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{\text{ren}}=\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{\text{BC/T}}-\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{0}.

This subtraction removes the state-independent local singular structure of the Green function, which includes contributions associated with timelike and lightlike propagation. As a result, the finite physical contribution is determined by the modification of the equal-time spatial correlations of the quantum field induced by the boundary conditions or temperature.

In this context, we adopt the Casimir prescription, where the finite energy-momentum tensor is obtained by taking the difference

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)=\braket{T^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)}-\braket{T^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x)}.

(47)

As a result of this renormalization procedure, the finite energy-momentum tensor is obtained as

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-i\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}{\Bigg\{}\Gamma^{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho,\alpha}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\hskip 8.19447pt\;\;\alpha}(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{4}g^{\mu\nu}\Gamma_{\alpha\beta,}^{\hskip 8.19447pt\lambda\rho,\alpha\beta}(x,x^{\prime})-a^{2}{\bigg(}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\Pi^{\alpha\gamma,\hskip 8.19447pt\;\beta\hskip 8.19447pt\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\alpha\gamma,\hskip 8.19447pt\beta,}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Theta^{\mu\gamma,\;\;\;\nu,\alpha\;\;\;\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\;\gamma\hskip 8.19447pt\;\alpha,}(x,x^{\prime})-\Theta^{\nu\gamma,\;\;\;\mu,\alpha\;\;\;\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\;\gamma\hskip 8.19447pt\;\alpha,}(x,x^{\prime})-\Sigma^{\mu\hskip 8.19447pt\beta,\alpha\nu,\lambda\rho}_{\;\;\alpha,\beta}(x,x^{\prime})-\Sigma^{\nu\hskip 8.19447pt\beta,\alpha\mu,\lambda\rho}_{\;\;\alpha,\beta}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\Pi^{\tau\gamma,\hskip 8.19447pt\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\tau\gamma,}(x,x^{\prime}){\bigg)}{\Bigg\}}\left(g^{\lambda\rho}+a^{2}\partial^{\lambda}\partial^{\rho}\right)\overline{G}_{0}^{(ab)P}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha),

(48)

where the subtracted propagator

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{G}_{0}^{(ab)P}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)=G^{(ab)P}_{0}(x-x;\alpha)-G^{(ab)P}_{0}(x-x^{\prime}),

(49)

is defined in terms of the Podolsky propagator in position space as

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime})=\frac{i}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}+\frac{im}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{K_{1}\left(m\sqrt{-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}\right)}{\sqrt{-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}}.

(50)

In Eq. (
48
), the term
\displaystyle\left(g^{\lambda\rho}+a^{2}\partial^{\lambda}\partial^{\rho}\right)
multiplies the derivative operators. By carrying out this operation explicitly, the energy-momentum tensor takes the final form

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-i\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}{\Bigg\{}2\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\prime\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\beta\alpha}\partial^{\beta}\partial^{\prime\alpha}-\frac{1}{m^{2}}{\bigg(}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\gamma\rho}g_{\alpha\beta}\partial^{\gamma}\partial^{\beta}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\alpha}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-2g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\gamma}\partial^{\prime\nu}-2g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\nu}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\gamma}\partial^{\mu}-2g_{\alpha\beta}\partial^{\alpha}\partial^{\prime\beta}\partial^{\prime\mu}\partial^{\prime\nu}-g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\rho}\partial^{\gamma}\partial^{\prime\mu}\partial^{\prime\nu}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\gamma}+g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\gamma}\partial^{\prime\rho}\partial^{\prime\nu}{\bigg)}{\Bigg\}}\overline{G}_{0}^{(ab)P}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha).

(51)

With the full expression of the energy-momentum tensor now obtained as a function of the topological parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
, we are in a position to explore its applications. In the next section, specific components of the tensor will be computed to investigate the thermal and spatial compactification effects within the Podolsky theory. These analyses will provide insights into how topology and finite-temperature environments modify the behavior of the field.

Applications

To analyze the thermal and spatial confinement effects in Podolsky’s electrodynamics, we now turn to the topological framework provided by the TFD formalism. In this context, three distinct topologies will be examined: (i)
\displaystyle\Gamma^{1}_{4}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\alpha=(\beta,0,0,0)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{1}_{4}
but with
\displaystyle\alpha=(0,0,0,i2d)
, representing spatial compactification along the
\displaystyle z
-axis; (iii)
\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}_{4}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where both the time and
\displaystyle z
axes are compactified, incorporating simultaneous thermal and spatial effects

V.1
Thermal effects

To account for thermal effects, the compactification parameter is chosen as
\displaystyle\alpha=(\beta,0,0,0)
. This choice allows for the calculation of the Stefan-Boltzmann law within the framework of Podolsky’s electrodynamics. In this thermal scenario, the corresponding Bogoliubov transformation is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle v^{2}(\beta)=\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{\infty}e^{-\beta k^{0}l_{0}}

(52)

and the Green function is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{G}_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime};\beta)=2\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{\infty}G_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime}-i\beta l_{0}n_{0}).

(53)

To derive the Stefan-Boltzmann law within the context of Podolsky’s electrodynamics, we consider the component
\displaystyle\mu=\nu=0
of Eq. (
51
), leading to

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{00(11)}(\beta)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{6}{\beta^{4}l_{0}^{4}}+\frac{9m^{2}}{2\beta^{2}l_{0}^{2}}K_{2}(m\beta l_{0})+\frac{3m^{3}}{2\beta l_{0}}K_{1}(m\beta l_{0})\right\}.

(54)

This expression represents the generalized Stefan-Boltzmann law for the electromagnetic field, now modified by the presence of the Podolsky mass parameter
\displaystyle m
.

In the limit where the Podolsky mass becomes very large,
\displaystyle m\gg 1
, corresponding to
\displaystyle a\to 0
, Eq. (
54
) reduces to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho(T)=\frac{\pi^{2}T^{4}}{15}+\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{\infty}\left[9\left(\frac{m^{3}T^{5}}{8\pi^{3}l_{0}^{5}}\right)^{1/2}+3\left(\frac{m^{5}T^{3}}{8\pi^{3}l_{0}^{3}}\right)^{1/2}\right]e^{\frac{-ml_{0}}{T}},

(55)

where
\displaystyle{\cal T}^{00(11)}=\rho
. In this limit, an approximate expression for the Stefan-Boltzmann law is obtained, now including a correction arising from Podolsky’s theory. Although the additional terms in brackets decay rapidly due to the exponential suppression, they still contribute non-negligibly to the total energy density. A comparison with the result presented in Ref.
b2
shows that our expression reproduces the same leading term, but includes an additional contribution proportional to
\displaystyle m^{5/2}
. Figure

illustrates the influence of the Podolski parameter on the Stefan-Boltzmann law where all physical constants have been restored to provide a more accurate description.

Starting from Eq. (
54
), we examine the high-temperature limit, i.e.,
\displaystyle\beta\to 0
. In this asymptotic regime, the Stefan-Boltzmann law takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho(T)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{6}{\beta^{4}l_{0}^{4}}+\frac{9}{\beta^{4}l_{0}^{4}}+\frac{3m^{2}}{2\beta^{2}l_{0}^{2}}\right\}=\frac{\pi^{2}T^{4}}{15}+\left[\frac{\pi^{2}T^{4}}{10}+\frac{m^{2}T^{2}}{4}\right],

(56)

where the terms in brackets represent the corrections introduced by Podolsky’s theory.

V.2
Spatial confinement effects

In this section, the Casimir effect is investigated by compactifying one of the spatial dimensions–specifically, the
\displaystyle z
-axis. This is implemented through the choice of the compactification parameter
\displaystyle\alpha=(0,0,0,i2d)
. Under this condition, the Bogoliubov transformation takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle v^{2}(d)=\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}e^{-i2dk^{3}l_{3}},

(57)

and the Green function becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{G}_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime};d)=2\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}G_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime}-2dl_{3}n_{3}).

(58)

By selecting the component
\displaystyle\mu=\nu=0
, which correspond to the Casimir energy, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{00(11)}(d)=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{8d^{4}l_{3}^{4}}+\frac{3m^{2}}{8d^{2}l_{3}^{2}}K_{2}(2mdl_{3})\right\}.

(59)

In the limit
\displaystyle m>>1
, the expression reduces to

\displaystyle\displaystyle E(d)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{720d^{4}}-\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}3\left(\frac{m^{3}}{256\pi^{3}d^{5}l_{3}^{5}}\right)^{1/2}e^{-2mdl_{3}},

(60)

where
\displaystyle{\cal T}^{00(11)}(d)=E(d)
. It is worth noting that the Casimir energy receives a small correction due to Podolsky’s theory. Figure
2
illustrates the behavior of the energy as a function of the separation distance, highlighting how the presence of the Podolsky parameter modifies the standard result.

To obtain the Casimir pressure, the component with
\displaystyle\mu=\nu=3
is considered, and as a result, the following expression is obtained

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{33(11)}(d)=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{3}{8d^{4}l_{3}^{4}}+\frac{9m^{2}}{8d^{2}l_{3}^{2}}K_{2}(2mdl_{3})+\frac{3m^{3}}{4dl_{3}}K_{1}(2mdl_{3})\right\}.

This represents the general expression for the Casimir pressure in terms of Podolsky’s parameter. In the same asymptotic limit,
\displaystyle m>>1

\displaystyle\displaystyle P(d)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{240d^{4}}-\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}\left[9\left(\frac{m^{3}\pi}{256d^{5}l_{3}^{5}}\right)^{1/2}+3\left(\frac{m^{5}\pi}{64d^{3}l_{3}^{3}}\right)^{1/2}\right]e^{-2mdl_{3}}.

(62)

Similar to the behavior observed for the Casimir energy, the pressure also acquires a correction due to Podolsky’s electrodynamics, resulting in an overall increase in its magnitude.

V.3
Thermal and spatial confinement effects

The main objective here is to describe the Casimir effect at finite temperature by compactifying both the time and one spatial dimension, such that
\displaystyle\alpha=(\beta,0,0,i2d)
. Accordingly, the Bogoliubov transformation is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle v^{2}(\beta,d)=\sum_{l_{0}=1}^{\infty}e^{-\beta k^{0}l_{0}}+\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}e^{-i2dk^{3}l_{3}}+\sum_{l_{0},l_{3}=1}^{\infty}e^{-\beta k^{0}l_{0}-i2dk^{3}l_{3}}.

(63)

The first two terms describe the thermal and size effects separately, as demonstrated in the previous subsections, while the third term accounts for the combined influence of thermal and size effects. The Green function associated with this third term is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{G}_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime};\beta,d)=4\sum_{l_{0},l_{3}=1}^{\infty}G_{0}^{P}(x-x^{\prime}-i\beta l_{0}n_{0}-2dl_{3}n_{3}).

(64)

\displaystyle\mu=\nu=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{00(11)}(\beta,d)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\sum_{l_{3}=1}^{\infty}3\left(\frac{m^{3}}{256\pi^{3}d^{5}l_{3}^{5}}\right)^{1/2}e^{-2mdl_{3}}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{l_{0},l_{3}=1}^{\infty}{\bigg\{}\frac{3(2\beta l_{0})^{2}-(4dl_{3})^{2}}{[(\beta l_{0})^{2}+(2dl_{3})^{2}]^{3}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{[2(3\beta l_{0})^{2}-6(2dl_{3})^{2}]}{[(\beta l_{0})^{2}+(2dl_{3})^{2}]^{5/2}}mK_{1}(m\alpha)+\frac{[3(\beta l_{0})^{2}+3(2dl_{3})^{2}]}{[(\beta l_{0})^{2}+(2dl_{3})^{2}]^{5/2}}m^{3}\beta^{2}l_{0}^{2}K_{1}(m\alpha){\bigg\}},

VI
Conclusions

In this work, a generalized form of electrodynamics proposed by B. Podolsky was investigated. This theory introduces a second-order derivative term in the electromagnetic field tensor along with a parameter
\displaystyle a
, which can be interpreted as generating a massive sector for the photon. Additionally, the Podolsky modification effectively acts as a high-frequency cutoff, addressing the divergences that arise in standard Maxwell electrodynamics
p1
;
p2
;
p3
. As expected, the presence of this term leads to corrections in physical quantities such as the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Casimir effect for the electromagnetic field.

The TFD formalism, a quantum field theory at finite temperature, was employed to describe thermal and spatial confinement effects through the analysis of different spacetime topologies. This approach is made possible by compactifying spacetime dimensions into a circular topology, effectively introducing thermal or boundary conditions
tqft
;
ume
. In this work, three distinct topologies were considered: the compactification of the time axis, enabling the derivation of the Stefan-Boltzmann law; the compactification of the
\displaystyle z
-axis, associated with spatial confinement and the Casimir effect; and the simultaneous compactification of both axes, allowing for the analysis of the Casimir effect at finite temperature.

Therefore, it has been shown that the considered physical quantities are modified by the generalized electrodynamics. The Stefan-Boltzmann law is enhanced by the presence of the Podolsky parameter in both limits analyzed. Similarly, the Casimir energy and pressure at zero temperature are increased, leading to a stronger attractive force between the plates. At finite temperature, the behavior of the Casimir energy becomes more intricate due to the presence of multiple terms involving Bessel functions. However, under the condition
\displaystyle m\gg 1
, two regimes were explored. At low temperatures, a slight increase in energy was observed, with the correction depending solely on
\displaystyle\beta
. At high temperatures, the energy exhibits an exponential decay with temperature, driven by the influence of the Podolsky parameter.
