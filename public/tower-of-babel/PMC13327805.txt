Boron neutron capture therapy for recurrent pediatric sialoblastoma: the first clinical experience and dosimetric considerations for skeletal safety with 4-year follow-up
Authors: Hung-Ruei Liao, Yi-Yen Lee, Chih-Ying Lee, Syuan-Ting Chang, Ko-Han Lin, Fong-In Chou, Jinn-Jer Peir, Hong-Ming Liu, Yi-Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSialoblastoma is a rare, aggressive pediatric salivary gland tumor often refractory to conventional therapies, which carry significant risks of growth impairment in children. We report the 52-month follow-up of a 6-year-old girl with recurrent sialoblastoma treated with two sessions of boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT). She achieved a 23-month complete response after the first session and a sustained partial response following the second. Dosimetric analysis using a conservative α/β ratio of 2.0 Gy for late-responding tissues showed that the equivalent dose in 2-Gy fractions to the mandible area was approximately 4.3 Gy-Eq. This is substantially below the 15 Gy threshold recognized for skeletal impairment. Our findings suggest that BNCT is a highly selective salvage option that effectively controls refractory pediatric tumors while preserving craniofacial development.Keywords: Recurrent, Sialoblastoma, Boron neutron capture therapy, Pediatrics, Bone development

Introduction

Sialoblastoma is a rare salivary gland tumor originating from the primitive duct epithelium, with an annual incidence of approximately 0.8 cases per million among individuals aged 0 to 19 years [1,2]. Sialoblastoma is characterized by a high recurrence rate, necessitating aggressive multimodal management. However, both chemotherapy and conventional radiotherapy (RT) pose severe long-term risks to young children, including reduced bone density and permanent impairment of skeletal growth [3,4]. For patients who have already exhausted conventional RT limits, selecting a salvage modality with high therapeutic selectivity is paramount. Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) offers a unique biological advantage by delivering localized high–linear energy transfer radiation via the 10B(n,α)7Li reaction [5]. This allows for the delivery of curative doses to tumor cells while effectively sparing adjacent healthy skeletal structures and growth centers.

BNCT, an emerging treatment modality, has shown remarkable efficacy in head and neck cancers, and its clinical implementation is increasingly recognized in the East Asian oncology community [6,7]. Notably, BNCT has also exhibited outstanding results in the treatment of salivary gland tumors, with the added benefit of extremely low side effects [8]. The underlying mechanism of BNCT enables highly localized radiation delivery, effectively confining damage to tumor cells and significantly minimizing harm to surrounding normal tissues.

This case involves a 6-year-old patient diagnosed with sialoblastoma who underwent multiple surgeries, two chemotherapy courses, and low-dose conventional photon RT. Despite these interventions, the tumor recurred, leading to the selection of BNCT to minimize long-term negative effects on growth and development.

Case Report

A 6-year-old girl presented with a recurrent right facial mass in July 2020. She had been diagnosed with sialoblastoma of the right parotid gland shortly after birth and underwent three surgical resections between 2015 and 2018. Despite postoperative RT (4,500 cGy in 25 fractions) and multiple rounds of chemotherapy (including cisplatin, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide), the tumor recurred locally by July 2020. Given the exhaustion of conventional treatment options and the high risk of further radiation-induced skeletal damage, BNCT was proposed as a salvage treatment.

1. Treatment planning and delivery

In this study, patients were administered boronophenylalanine (BPA) as the boron compound via intravenous injection. BPA was prepared by dissolving 6.25 mg of the compound in 250 mL of fructose solution. The administered dose was determined based on the patient’s body weight, with a total dosage of 0.5 g/kg.

The boron concentration in the tumor and normal tissues was estimated based on individualized 18F-fluoro-boronophenylalanine positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography imaging performed prior to treatment. The tumor-to-normal (T/N) ratio derived from the standardized uptake value (SUV) mean was 3.76 for the first BNCT session and 2.50 for the second session. These ratios were used to calculate the biologically equivalent dose (Gy-Eq) for both the target volume and critical organs at risk.

The neutron source used was from the reactor, which provides a high-quality epithermal neutron beam. The beam hole was tailored to the tumor location on the right cheek (Fig. 1A, 1B). Irradiation time was determined by the treatment planning system to ensure the prescribed dose was delivered while respecting normal tissue constraints, specifically the skin and oral mucosa. The dose prescription principles at our institution integrate the biological selectivity of BNCT with strict anatomical constraints similar to those in conventional RT. The primary objective is to deliver a curative dose to boron-containing tumor cells while minimizing the thermal neutron exposure to critical structures such as the brain and carotid arteries. For pediatric patients, our institutional protocol specifically prioritizes the preservation of growth and development. In this case, the treatment plan was optimized to ensure that the biologically equivalent dose to the skeletal growth centers and the central nervous system remained well below the thresholds for late toxicity, thereby safeguarding the patient’s craniofacial symmetry and long-term quality of life.

On the day of irradiation, the blood boron concentration was monitored via inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy to adjust the real-time dosimetry.

The biological equivalent dose was calculated by applying tissue-specific factors derived from established models: a compound biological effectiveness (CBE) of 3.8 for the tumor [5], 4.9 for the oral mucosa [9], 2.27 for normal bone [10], and 1.35 for spinal cord [11].

According to the SIOPE Radiotherapy Working Group consensus, the threshold for clinically observable growth impairment in the pediatric skeleton begins at an EQD2 of 15–18 Gy [14], with severe developmental risks occurring at doses exceeding 30 Gy. Given that pediatric growth plates and mandibular bone are late-responding tissues, an α/β ratio of 2.0 was considered for the skeletal safety analysis [15]. By maintaining the mean dose to the mandible significantly below the thresholds established by these models, the treatment plan prioritized the prevention of permanent growth arrest and midfacial asymmetry.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) follow-up showed a gradual reduction in tumor size, eventually leading to complete disappearance and classification as a complete response (Fig. 2B–2D). She remained progression-free for 23 months. During this period, no additional chemotherapy or surgical interventions were administered. Regarding side effects, the patient experienced only Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 2 mucositis [16], which resolved within a few weeks.

3. 2nd BNCT and clinical response

During FDG PET imaging on August 17, 2022 (Fig. 2E), the previously identified right facial nodule had disappeared. However, increased enhancement was observed in the right buccal region (SUV max 3.17). Additionally, an MRI report from August 25, 2022, indicated increased enhancement and thickening in the right buccal region, retromolar area, and masticator space. A biopsy conducted in October 2022 confirmed recurrent sialoblastoma, and BNCT was administered on November 11, 2022.

During this BNCT session, the tumor received a maximum radiation dose of 27.16 Gy-Eq, with a Dave of 26.39 Gy-Eq. The mucosa received an average dose of 5.85 Gy-Eq, and the right submandibular gland received 3.26 Gy-Eq (Table 1). Subsequent follow-up confirmed a partial response in tumor control (Fig. 2F–2H). The patient experienced only mild mucositis, which resolved spontaneously within a few weeks.

At the latest follow-up (52 months after the first BNCT), the patient was surviving with persistent disease, maintaining a stable partial response. The patient's long-term development was assessed using structured clinical parameters. Growth and physical development were monitored using standardized pediatric growth charts; both her height and weight remained consistently within the 50th–75th percentile, indicating no systemic endocrine or growth impairment. Regarding late toxicity, a mild, asymptomatic fibrosis was noted in the right buccal mucosa, corresponding to a CTCAE v5.0 Grade 1 oral soft tissue fibrosis [16], with no associated pain or restriction in jaw movement. Regarding facial development, clinical symmetry was evaluated by comparing bilateral anatomical landmarks (tragus to commissure of the lips and mandibular angle depth). No significant mandibular hypoplasia or facial asymmetry was observed, despite the cumulative radiation dose to the right cheek. Furthermore, while formal Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory scores were not collected at baseline, retrospective assessment of her school progress reports demonstrated age-appropriate neurocognitive functioning and social integration, with no learning disabilities identified during her primary school years.

Discussion

The management of pediatric head and neck oncology requires a precise balance between local tumor control and the preservation of long-term developmental integrity. In the present case, the patient had already received 45 Gy of prior photon therapy, reaching the biological threshold for skeletal tolerance. The subsequent successful application of BNCT demonstrates the modality’s capacity for safe re-irradiation in sensitive pediatric regions.

To rigorously evaluate skeletal safety, we converted the BNCT doses to an EQD2. We categorized the mandible and its associated growth centers as late-responding tissues with a conservative α/β ratio of 2.0 Gy [15]. Our analysis showed that the average dose to the mandible was 2.35 Gy-Eq; to ensure a worst-case safety estimate, we also evaluated the high-dose submandibular region (Dave, 3.26 Gy-Eq), resulting in an EQD2 of approximately 4.3 Gy-Eq. This value remains far below the 15–18 Gy threshold recommended by the SIOPE Radiotherapy Working Group (2019) for preventing clinically significant growth retardation [14].

It is important to distinguish between the curative dose delivered to the tumor and the dose received by adjacent normal tissues. In the first BNCT session, the average tumor dose (Dave) reached 21.66 Gy-Eq, while the normal oral mucosa was limited to 7.39 Gy-Eq to prevent severe radiation-induced sequelae. Notably, the subsequent recurrence did not occur on the mucosal surface, but was located in the deeper right buccal region, retromolar area, and masticator space. This suggests that the treatment failure was likely a result of the aggressive biological behavior of the recurrent sialoblastoma and the conservative margins required in a re-irradiation setting, rather than under-dosage to the mucosa.

Clinical evidence at the 52-month follow-up corroborated these dosimetric findings. The patient maintained facial symmetry and physical growth within the 50th–75th percentiles. Regarding late toxicity, a mild, asymptomatic fibrosis was noted in the right buccal mucosa (CTCAE v5.0 Grade 1) [16], with no associated pain or mandibular hypoplasia. While no clinical evidence of peripheral neurotoxicity was identified at the 52-month follow-up, the potential for delayed neurological sequelae remains a critical consideration for pediatric survivors. Late radiation-induced peripheral nerve injury often results from a combination of direct axonal damage and progressive perineural fibrosis, which may manifest years after the completion of therapy [17,18]. Given that this patient presented with mild, asymptomatic grade 1 oral soft tissue fibrosis, continued long-term monitoring is essential, as progressive fibrotic changes can eventually lead to nerve entrapment or ischemic injury to the peripheral nerves in the masticator space [19]. The high therapeutic selectivity of BNCT may theoretically reduce the risk of such sequelae by sparing the surrounding stromal architecture [11]; however, further studies with extended longitudinal follow-up are required to fully establish the long-term neurotoxicity profile of BNCT in the pediatric population. This case provides robust evidence that BNCT’s high T/N tissue ratio enables effective salvage treatment while minimizing the long-term developmental morbidity often associated with conventional photon therapy.

Our 52-month follow-up demonstrates that BNCT is a viable salvage treatment for recurrent pediatric sialoblastoma. By maintaining the biological EQD2 to the mandible significantly below the SIOPE-recommended 15 Gy threshold—even when utilizing a conservative α/β ratio of 2.0 Gy for growth centers—we achieved robust local control without compromising craniofacial development. Our findings confirm that BNCT’s high therapeutic selectivity may enable safe re-irradiation in the pediatric head and neck region, offering a promising alternative to conventional RT for preserving skeletal integrity and quality of life in young survivors.
