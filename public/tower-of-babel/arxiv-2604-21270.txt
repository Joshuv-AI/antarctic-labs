CLT-Optimal Parameter Error Bounds for Linear System Identification
Yichen Zhou, Stephen Tu

Abstract
. There has been remarkable progress over the past decade in establishing finite-sample, non-asymptotic bounds on recovering unknown system parameters from observed system behavior. Surprisingly, however, we show that the current state-of-the-art bounds do not accurately capture the statistical complexity of system identification, even in the most fundamental setting of estimating a discrete-time linear dynamical system (LDS) via ordinary least-squares regression (OLS). Specifically, we utilize asymptotic normality to identify classes of problem instances for which current bounds overstate the squared parameter error, in both spectral and Frobenius norm, by a factor of the state-dimension of the system. Informed by this discrepancy, we then sharpen the OLS parameter error bounds via a novel second-order decomposition of the parameter error, where crucially the lower-order term is a matrix-valued martingale that we show correctly captures the CLT scaling. From our analysis we obtain finite-sample bounds for both (i) stable systems and (ii) the many-trajectories setting that match the instance-specific optimal rates up to constant factors in Frobenius norm, and polylogarithmic state-dimension factors in spectral norm.



1.
Introduction

System identification—the task of recovering unknown parameters of a dynamical system from observed system behavior—is a central problem in control. While system identification has a long and rich history in the control theory literature, the rise of machine learning approaches to control has led to a recent surge of results, particularly in establishing non-asymptotic, finite-sample guarantees for parameter recovery
[
28
,
26
,
27
,
34
]
. Such bounds are valuable both for characterizing the statistical limits of system identification, and for providing principled guidance on the data requirements needed to learn accurate models in practice.

Despite this remarkable progress, we argue that existing state-of-the-art bounds still fail to fully capture the statistical complexity of system identification—surprisingly even in the most basic setting of estimating the dynamics matrix of a discrete-time linear dynamical system (LDS) given one or many trajectories of state observations using ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression, a model known as the
\mathrm{VAR}(1)
(vector auto-regressive) model which we focus on in this work. At first glance, this statement seems to contradict existing claims of minimax-optimality of OLS for this setting
[
28
,
14
,
15
]
. However, a more careful introspection reveals that minimax-optimal rates are only sharp on a carefully selected set of problem instances; they do
not
guarantee sharp rates on every problem instance.

Concretely, we consider two setups where asymptotic normality can be used to compute the exact instance-specific problem scaling: (i) strictly stable transition dynamics, and (ii) the many trajectories
[
30
]
setting, where the number of independent trajectories outnumber the dimension.
We remark that (i) and (ii) are not mutually exclusive.
Our analysis reveals many problem instances where the sharpest known OLS bounds
overestimate
the squared parameter estimation error, in either Frobenius or operator norm, by a factor of the state-dimension. These instances are characterized by a sufficiently fast decay of the eigenvalues of either the noise covariance matrix or the inverse state covariance matrix, so that trace
\Tr(M)
of said matrices is significantly smaller than
d\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
, where
d
is the state-dimension and
\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
is the operator norm. A practical setting where this naturally arises is when disturbances enter the system in a non-uniform way, such as through an approximately low-rank subspace, or due to different physical scaling across state variables.

We then turn to the problem of sharpening the non-asymptotic OLS bounds to match the instance-specific optimal rates from asymptotic normality. We establish sufficient conditions on the trajectory length in the case of stable transition dynamics, and on the number of trajectories in the many trajectories setting, for the squared parameter error of the OLS estimator to match the instance-specific optimal rate up to a constant factor in Frobenius norm, and up to
\mathrm{polylog}(d)
factors in operator norm. At a high level, the deficiency of existing approaches is rooted in the standard decomposition of the OLS error introduced by
[
28
]
, which splits the OLS error into two terms that are analyzed separately: (i) a self-normalized martingale term
[
1
]
, and (ii) the minimum eigenvalue of the empirical state covariance matrix. We propose a novel second-order decomposition where the lowest order term becomes a simpler matrix-valued martingale that captures the CLT scaling. In Frobenius norm analyzing said martingale is immediate thanks to the inner-product structure, but for the operator norm extracting the CLT scaling is non-trivial and relies on the machinery of non-commutative Burkholder inequalities
[
24
,
16
]
. Returning back to our second-order decomposition, the new higher-order term places a stronger requirement on the empirical state covariance—namely a
p
-th moment approximate isometry condition—for which we establish by taking inspiration from the Hanson-Wright approach described in
[
15
]
.

Our paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we formalize the problem setting, discuss in detail what target rates we should expect from asymptotic normality,
and illustrate examples where existing bounds are loose. In
Section

3
, we state our main non-asymptotic results
for both Frobenius and operator norms.

Section

4
provides a detailed proof outline,
and
Section

5
concludes with future directions. All proof details are deferred to
Appendices

A
to
C
.

Related Work

We focus our literature discussion on
both asymptotic and finite-sample analysis of
parameter estimation in discrete-time linear dynamical systems with full state observation;
see
[
34
,
12
]
for a broader overview of recent

[

The starting point for non-asymptotic error analysis of the OLS estimator

[

improves the required sample complexity
for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC,
although as

We will show that, in the case where the noise matrix

The work
[

controls the excess risk (squared parameter error in a weighted Frobenius norm by the process covariance) of OLS

However as we will also discuss, this conversion
introduces un-necessary conservatism in the bound.
Most related to this paper is the work
[
30
]
,
which initiates a study of multi-trajectory estimation for dependent
linear regression (in contrast, all aforementioned papers consider
the single trajectory setting). Similar to
[
35
]
, the presented
bounds are also for excess risk, and suffer from the same conversion issue.
One additional notable digression in
[
30
]
compared to the aforementioned papers is in controlling the
expected value
of excess risk, instead of with high probability; this requires several key technical tools, which we build off of in this work.

On the asymptotic side, there is a well-established literature
on the strong consistency and asymptotic normality of single trajectory OLS
for both autoregressive
[
18
,
17
]
and vector autoregressive
[
4
]

models.
In
Section

2.1
we will discuss these results, specifically
[
4
]
, in more detail.
However, we limit our discussions to the setting when
the transition matrix
is strictly stable, as
single trajectory asymptotic distributions are significantly more
involved in the marginal and explosive cases
[

,

]
.
On the other hand, for the multi-trajectory setting, when the number of

-estimation theory
[
31
]

to study the asymptotics regardless of the stability
of the dynamics matrix; we also discuss this in detail in
Section

2.1
.

2.
Background and Problem Statement

Consider the linear dynamical system evolving in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+w_{t},\quad x_{0}=0,

where
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a noise process
satisfying
w_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{W}
.
We assume
\mathcal{W}
is zero-mean and has a finite positive definite covariance, which we denote
\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}:=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[ww^{\mathsf{T}}\right].

We observe
m
i.i.d. trajectories from (
2.1
),
in the form of
\mathcal{D}_{m,T}:=\{\{x_{t}^{(i)}\}_{t=1}^{T+1}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.
The OLS estimator for
A
is:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{m,T}:=Y_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T}(X_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T})^{-1},

where
X_{m,T}\in\mathbb{R}^{mT\times d}
has rows
\{\{x_{t}^{(i)}\}_{t=1}^{T}\}_{i=1}^{m}

and
Y_{m,T}\in\mathbb{R}^{mT\times d}
has rows
\{\{x_{t}^{(i)}\}_{t=2}^{T+1}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.

Our goal is to study the squared parameter error
under both the Frobenius and operator (spectral) norm. Specifically, denoting a problem instance by a tuple
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
, we seek optimal
instance-specific
upper-bounds
\gamma_{F}
and
\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}
:

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\|\hat{A}_{m,T}-A\big\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\gamma_{F}\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right),

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\|\hat{A}_{m,T}-A\big\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right).

where the expectation is taken w.r.t. the training data
\mathcal{D}_{m,T}
.

In this paper, we consider two primary problem regimes.
The first is the
strictly stable
regime,
where the dynamics matrix
A
satisifes
\rho(A)<1
, with
\rho(\cdot)
denoting
the spectral radius. The second is the
many trajectories
regime
[
30
]
,
where no assumptions are made on
A
, but instead we assume that
m\geqslant Cd
,
where
C
is a universal constant
independent
of
A
.
Note that these two regimes are
not
mutually exclusive;
for problem instances that fall into both regimes, both sets of
corresponding results apply.

2.1.
Asymptotic Normality of OLS

We first discuss the asymptotic normality of the
OLS estimator, which is the key tool for us in
establishing the correct forms of
the bounds
\gamma_{F}
and
\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}
in
(
2.3
).

In our formulation, there are two variables,
m
and
T
, that
can either be separately or jointly sent to infinity, depending on the problem
instance.
We first consider holding
T
fixed and taking
m
to infinity, which
does not require any assumptions on
A
.
Indeed, by classical asymptotic normality of
M
-estimation (see e.g.
[
31
]
),
as
m\to\infty
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sqrt{m}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A}_{m,T}-A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}\left(0,\left(\Gamma_{T}^{-1}/T\right)\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right),

where
\Gamma_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Sigma_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}:=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}A^{k}\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}(A^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

On the flip side, in order to send
T
to infinity (either while holding
m
fixed or
jointly tending to infinity as well), we need to impose some assumptions on
A
.
In general, the OLS estimator is not consistent when
A
is irregular, i.e., the geometric multiplicity of any
unstable eigenvalues of
A
exceeds one (see e.g.,
[
23
,
27
,
26
]
).
Furthermore, when
A
is regular but not strictly stable, the
limiting error distributions are not Gaussian
[
33
,
9
]
and require functional CLT arguments to study.
Hence, for sending
T
to infinity, we will focus only on
strictly stable dynamics.
Specifically, when
\rho(A)<1
, extending classical results such as
[
4
, Theorem 1]
, we have as
T\to\infty
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A}_{m,T}-A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}\left(0,\left(\Sigma^{-1}_{\infty}/m\right)\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right),

where
\Sigma_{\infty}:=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}A^{t}\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}(A^{t})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

We note that the right-hand-side of both (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) can be further unified via a joint limit on
(m,T)
.
Let
\phi:\mathbb{N}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{N}_{+}
be any function such that
\phi(m)\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
.
Then for the joint limit
(m,T_{m})
with
T_{m}:=\phi(m)
, as
m\to\infty
,
one can show using the Lindeberg-Feller CLT
[
31
, Proposition 2.27]
,

(2.6)

\sqrt{mT_{m}}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A}_{m,T_{m}}-A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}\left(0,\Sigma^{-1}_{\infty}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right).

With the asymptotic limits
(
2.4
),
(
2.5
), and
(
2.6
) in place,
we now discuss how these limit distributions imply
the correct form of both
\gamma_{F}
and
\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}
.
The key idea is the following fact.
Suppose
\{\Delta_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is a sequence of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfying

\sqrt{n}\Delta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}(0,V)

and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is an arbitrary norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
If the sequence
\{n\lVert\Delta_{n}\rVert^{2}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is uniformly integrable, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}n\cdot\mathbb{E}\lVert\Delta_{n}\rVert^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I_{k})}\lVert V^{1/2}g\rVert^{2}
.
Hence we will utilize the expression
\mathbb{E}_{g}\lVert V^{1/2}g\rVert^{2}
to compute
what the optimal form of both

\gamma_{F}
(with
\lVert\cdot\rVert
the
\ell_{2}
-norm)
and
\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}
(with
\lVert\cdot\rVert=\lVert\mathrm{mat}(\cdot)\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
) should be.

2.3.
Asymptotic Analysis in Operator (Spectral) Norm

Computing the correct form of
\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}

for the operator norm is more involved,
as the
limiting asymptotic risk involves computing the largest singular value
of a Gaussian random matrix, which does not typically admit a closed form expression.
Concretely, for e.g., (

), we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}m\cdot\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{A}_{m,T}-A\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I_{d^{2}})}\lVert\mathrm{mat}((\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}/\sqrt{T}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}^{1/2})g)\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}
.
The RHS expression is equal to
T^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{G}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}^{1/2}G\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}
, where
G
is a
d\times d
matrix with i.i.d.
\mathsf{N}(0,1)
entries.
Fortunately, the correct order of this expression is
available via the Gaussian Chevet inequality
[
32
, Section 8.6]
, as summarized below.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
have i.i.d.
\mathsf{N}(0,1)
entries, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
be fixed matrices.
We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert AGB\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\asymp\lVert A\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\lVert B\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert A\rVert_{F}^{2}\lVert B\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}.

By
2.1
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{G}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}^{1/2}G\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\asymp\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
.
Similar then to the Frobenius norm case, this yields the following target
goal for the quantity
\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}=\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
in
(
2.3b
).

Goal 2:
Show under certain requirements on
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathsf{op}}\lesssim\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{mT}+\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})}{mT\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{T})}.

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{A}_{1,T}-A\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{(\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\vee 1)\nu_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}\cdot\tilde{O}(\Psi(A))}{T\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\infty})},

.
We again see the two sources of gaps as
we did in the Frobenius norm case;
both in the gap between
K_{\mathrm{vec}}
and one,
and due to non-isotropic state covariance matrices.

2.4.
Assumptions for Non-Asymptotic Analysis

Towards setting up and presenting our main non-asymtotic results,
we first collect and discuss the various assumptions
on the noise process
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
that we utilize in our analysis.
Throughout the article, we utilize the following notation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(w_{0:t-1}),\quad\mathbb{P}_{t}(\cdot):=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}),\quad\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}],

with the understanding that
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is the trivial
\sigma
-algebra.
It is clear that
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-adapted and
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted.
We begin by formalizing our aforementioned problem setting with an additional sub-Gaussianity condition.

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfies:

(i)

w_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{W}
,
\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{W}}[w]=0
,
\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{W}}[ww^{\mathsf{T}}]\succ 0
.

(ii)

For
w\sim\mathcal{W}
,
\bar{w}:=\Sigma^{-1/2}_{\mathcal{W}}w
is directionally
\nu
-sub-Gaussian: for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\left\langle v,\bar{w}\right\rangle\right)\right]\leqslant\exp\left(\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}/2\right).

2.2
is fairly standard in the non-asymptotic LDS identification literature
[
28
,
26
,
30
]
, as it provides access to sub-Gaussian self-normalized martingale tail bounds
[
1
]
.
This assumption alone, however, is insufficient for our
purposes, as we also need to control the behaviour of the empirical covariance
\Sigma_{m,T}:=(mT)^{-1}X_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T}
as part of the OLS error.
We now introduce two additional assumptions. The first provides, in the two regimes we consider, high probability control on empirical covariance error
\lVert\Sigma_{m,T}-\Gamma_{T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
.
To state the assumption, we denote the entropy functional of a nonnegative integrable function
f
with respect to a probability distribution
\mu
as:

\mathsf{Ent}_{\mu}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[f(x)\log f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[f(x)]\log\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[f(x)].

We also denote the whitened noise process
\{\bar{w}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}:=\left\{\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}^{-1/2}w_{t}\right\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, and the whitened distribution of
\mathcal{W}
as
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
, such that
\bar{w}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\bar{\mathcal{W}}
.

Assumption 2.3
.

We assume that
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
satisfies
either
of the following:

(i)

\bar{\mathcal{W}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{d}\bar{\mathcal{W}}_{i}
for one-dimensional distributions
\bar{\mathcal{W}}_{i}
, i.e.,
\bar{w}_{t}
has independent coordinates.

(ii)

\bar{\mathcal{W}}
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality
\mathrm{LS}(\nu^{2})
: for any continuously differentiable
f\in L^{2}(\bar{\mathcal{W}})
such that
\nabla f\in L^{2}(\bar{\mathcal{W}})
,

\mathsf{Ent}_{\bar{\mathcal{W}}}(f^{2})\leqslant 2\nu^{2}\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\bar{\mathcal{W}}}\left[\lVert\nabla f(w)\rVert^{2}\right].

2.3

(i)
is fairly standard, having appeared in several prior works on LTI identification
[
26
,
15
]
.
On the other hand,
2.3

(ii)
is non-standard, and we will discuss verifying it in a moment.
The role of
2.3
is to allow us to obtain concentration of
\lVert\Sigma_{m,T}-\Gamma_{T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
via different Hanson-Wright inequalities
[
25
,
3
]
.
Specifically,
2.3

(i)
gives access to the classical Hanson-Wright inequality
[
25
]
requiring independence, here across time
t
and across the coordinates of
\bar{w}_{t}
.
On the other hand,
2.3

(ii)

enables the use of a Hanson-Wright inequality under the convex concentration property
[
3
]
,
where the whitened noise distribution
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
is allowed to have dependencies between coordinates, but must instead satisfy a geometric restriction. We note that for
2.3

(ii)
, requiring the constant for log-Sobolev inequality to coincide with the sub-Gaussian constant in
2.2

(ii)
is without loss of generality, as
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
satisfying
\mathrm{LS}(C)
implies
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
is directionally
\sqrt{C}
-sub-Gaussian (cf.
D.4
).

Now off the typical events where we have concentration of
\lVert\Sigma_{m,T}-\Gamma_{T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
, we still require control on how degenerate
\Sigma_{m,T}
can become.
Hence, our next assumption,
from
[
30
, Definition 4.1]
,
yields control on the
lower tail
of
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{m,T})
, and
generalizes small-ball conditions in the i.i.d. setting
[
21
]
.

Assumption 2.4

(Trajectory small-ball (Traj-SB))
.

Assume the state trajectory
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
from (
2.1
) defined by
(A,\mathcal{W})

satisfies the trajectory small-ball condition: for any trajectory length
T
, there exists constants
c\geqslant 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that for any excitation window size
k\in[T]
,
window index
j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor T/k\rfloor\}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(j-1)k}\left\{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=(j-1)k+1}^{jk}\langle v,x_{t}\rangle^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot v^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{k}v\right\}\leqslant(c\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

We now discuss verifying
2.2
to
2.4
.
It turns out that a very broad family of distributions
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
can be readily shown to simultaneously satisfy these assumptions, namely the class of log-concave distributions.

\bar{\mathcal{W}}
is said to be log-concave if it is (a) absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(denoted
\lambda_{d}
),
(b) supported on a convex set
S
, and
(c) the negative log density
\psi_{\bar{\mathcal{W}}}:=-\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathcal{W}}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{d}}
is convex on
S
.
By Carbery and Wright’s classical work on
anti-concentration for polynomials of log-concave distributions (see
[
8
, Theorem 8]
and
[
30
, Example 4.6]
),
a log-concave
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
implies
2.4
.
Next, it turns out that
there are two cases where
additionally we have that
2.3

(ii)
holds (which then implies
2.2
).
The first case (
strongly log-concave
, or SLC for short), is when
\psi_{\bar{\mathcal{W}}}
is twice-differentiable and
\nu^{-2}
-strongly-convex (i.e.,
\nabla^{2}\psi_{\bar{\mathcal{W}}}\succcurlyeq\nu^{-2}I_{d}
everywhere on
S
);
this follows from the classic Bakry-Émery criteria
[
5
]
.
The second case (
bounded log-concave
, or BLC for short), is when the support
S
has diameter bounded as
O(\nu^{2})
, which is the result of recent
work on stochastic localization
[
19
]
.
This discussion is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Given a problem instance
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
, suppose that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
2.2

(i)
, and
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
satisfies
either
of the following (let
S
denote the support of
\bar{\mathcal{W}}
,
which we assume is convex):

\mathrm{(SLC)}

\psi_{\bar{\mathcal{W}}}
is
twice-differentiable and
\nu^{-2}
-strongly-convex on
S
.

\mathrm{(BLC)}

\psi_{\bar{\mathcal{W}}}
is
convex and
S
has a bounded diameter
O(\nu^{2})
.

Then
2.2
to
2.4
are satisfied.

We conclude our discussion on the noise assumptions by noting that although the natural intersection of
2.2
to
2.4
are log-concave distributions (as presented in
2.5
), we present
2.3

(i)
as there readily exist product measures of one-dimensional sub-Gaussian distributions that violate the log-Sobolev inequality; see
[
2
, Section 2]
.
However, we leave a general distributional characterization that implies

2.2
,

2.3

(i)
and
2.4
as an open question.

Beside conditions on noise process, another important concept for the analysis of LDS identification is stability of
A
.
Here we utilize a quantified definition of stability equivalent to the typical spectral radius definition.

Definition 2.6

(Strict stability)
.

A
is
(M,\rho)
-
strictly-stable
if
there exists
M>0
and
\rho<1
such that
\lVert A^{k}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\leqslant M\rho^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
A
that is
(M,\rho)
-strictly-stable, we
define the following quantity:

(2.13)

\displaystyle\kappa(A):=2\left\lceil\frac{1}{\log(1/\rho)}\log\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{2\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}M}{\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\infty})}}\right)\right\rceil.

\kappa(A)
is intuitively a “burn-in” time for stable systems, in a sense that for any
T\geqslant\kappa(A)
,
\Gamma_{T}
and
\Sigma_{\infty}
are sufficiently approximately isometric for analysis purposes. Such a “burn-in” property plays an important role in obtaining sharp results for stable systems. We will revisit in detail the above discussions in
Section

4.3
.

Main Results

We now state our main results in light of
Goal 1
and
Goal 2
. For what follows, we denote
the parameter error
\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}:=\hat{A}_{m,T}-A
.
We start with the Frobenius norm case.

Theorem 3.1

(Frobenius norm case)
.

Suppose the problem instance
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)

satisfies
2.2
to
2.4
.
Then we have for any
q>0
,

(i)

Many trajectories:
If

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leqslant(1+q)\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{mT}+(1+q^{-1})\frac{c_{1}\nu^{6}d^{3}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{T})m^{2}T}.

(ii)

Stable dynamics:
If
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly-stable (cf.
2.6
),

T\geqslant\kappa(A)
,
and
mT\gtrsim\max\left\{\kappa(A),\frac{M}{1-\rho}\sqrt{\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{\infty})}}\right\}d

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leqslant(1+q)\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{mT}+(1+q^{-1})\frac{c_{2}\nu^{6}d^{3}M\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{3/2}}{(1-\rho)\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\infty})^{3/2}(mT)^{2}}.

Recall that
\kappa(A)
is defined (
2.13
).

Here,

are universal constants.

With the tuning parameter
q
,
3.1
allows us to match
Goal 1
arbitrarily closely.
Fix a
q\in(0,1)
, and in addition to the relevant
hypothesis in
3.1

(i)

Many trajectories:
suppose further that

(3.1)

\displaystyle m\gtrsim\frac{\nu^{6}d^{3}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{q^{2}\Tr\left(\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right)\lambda_{\min}\left(\Gamma_{T}\right)\Tr\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right)},

(ii)

Stable dynamics:
suppose further that

(3.2)

\displaystyle mT\gtrsim\frac{q^{-2}(1-\rho)^{-1}\cdot\nu^{6}d^{3}M\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{3/2}}{\Tr\left(\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right)\lambda_{\min}\left(\Sigma_{\infty}\right)^{3/2}\Tr\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right)}.

Then we have,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+2q)\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{mT}.

Therefore, by letting

, we can drive the bound (

) arbitrarily close to

.
We can also derive sufficient conditions for

Specifically, (

)

and also (

.
We leave the necessity of the conditions (

) and (

We now turn to the operator norm case. For simplicity of notations, let us define
for a PSD matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\bar{\Tr}(M):=\max\{\Tr(M),\log(d)\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\}
.
We also define the shorthand

.

Theorem 3.2

(Operator norm case)
.

Suppose the problem instance
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)

2.2
to

,
and that

. Then:

(i)

Many trajectories:
If

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{2}(d)\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{mT}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left[\log^{2}(d)\frac{\nu^{4}\bar{\Tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\bar{\Tr}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{m^{2(1-1/\log{d})}T^{2(1/2-1/\log{d})}}+\frac{\nu^{6}d^{2}\varphi_{T}}{m^{2}T}\right].

(ii)

Stable dynamics:
If
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly-stable (cf.

),

, and
mT\gtrsim\max\left\{\kappa(A),\frac{\max\{1,\nu^{4}\}M}{1-\rho}\sqrt{\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{\infty})}}\right\}d

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{2}(d)\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{mT}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left[\log^{2}(d)\frac{\nu^{4}\bar{\Tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\bar{\Tr}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{(mT)^{2(1-1/\log{d})}}+\frac{\nu^{6}d^{2}M\varphi_{\infty}^{3/2}}{(1-\rho)(mT)^{2}}\right].

Recall that
\kappa(A)
is defined (
2.13
).

Unlike

,

Many trajectories:

\displaystyle m^{1-\frac{2}{\log d}}\gtrsim\left[\frac{\nu^{4}\bar{\Tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\bar{\Tr}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\nu^{6}d^{2}\varphi_{T}/\log^{2}(d)}{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}\right]T^{\frac{2}{\log d}},

)

,

4.
Proof Ideas: Key Decompositions and Technical Tools

We first derive a new second-order error decomposition that
is shared between the paths to both

3.1

and
3.2
.
We start with the standard decomposition of the OLS error:

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}=W_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T}(X_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T})^{-1}=\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{mT}W_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T}\right)}_{:=\hat{Q}_{m,T}}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{mT}X_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T}\right)^{-1}}_{:=\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}}.

Note that under
2.4
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}
is invertible a.s. by
[
30
, Corollary B.14]

given our requirements on
m
(resp.
mT
)
in the many trajectories setting (resp. stable dynamics setting),
and therefore the above decomposition is well defined. The typical route for analyzing the OLS error proceeds by first decomposing the product above as:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}=\hat{Q}_{m,T}\hat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\cdot\hat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T},

where the first term is analyzed using
concentration inequalities for self-normalized martingales,
and the second term is controlled using small-ball techniques
to lower bound the minimum eigenvalue.
We take a departure from this route and instead write:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}=\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}+\hat{Q}_{m,T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1}).

By triangle inequality and the AM-GM inequality, we have for any
q>0
the following
basic error inequality
:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{\Delta}_{m,T}\rVert^{2}_{S_{p}}\leqslant(1+q)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert^{2}_{S_{p}}}_{:=T_{1}}+(1+q^{-1})\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1})\rVert^{2}_{S_{p}}}_{:=T_{2}},

where for a matrix
M
,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{S_{p}}
denotes
the Schatten-
p
norm

\lVert M\rVert_{S_{p}}:=\left(\Tr((M^{\mathsf{T}}M)^{p/2})\right)^{1/p}
;
note that
p=2
yields the Frobenius norm,
and
p=\infty
yields the operator norm.

Outline:

We will show that
T_{1}
matches the corresponding rates in
Goal 1
or
Goal 2
, whereas
T_{2}
is of higher order.
The analysis of
T_{1}
differs depending on the norm:
it is simple to analyze under Frobenius norm due to inner product structure (cf.
Section

4.1
),
but requires a noncommutative Burkholder inequality
[
24
]
for the operator norm (cf.
Section

4.2
).
On the other hand, the analysis of term
T_{2}

is independent of the norm,
and builds on the Hanson-Wright inequality
[
32
,
3
]
in addition to existing OLS analysis under the trajectory small-ball condition
[
30
]
(cf.
Section

4.3
).

4.1.
Analysis of Term
T_{1}
for Frobenius Norm

For the Frobenius norm, we have:

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\Tr\left(\Gamma_{T}^{-2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{Q}_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{Q}_{m,T}\right]\right).

Furthermore,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{Q}_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{Q}_{m,T}]
admits a simple closed form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{Q}_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{Q}_{m,T}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}T^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{(i,t),(j,s)=1}^{m,T}w^{(i)\mathsf{T}}_{t}w^{(j)}_{s}x_{t}^{(i)}x_{s}^{(j)\mathsf{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}T^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[w^{(i)\mathsf{T}}_{t}w^{(i)}_{s}x_{t}^{(i)}x_{s}^{(i)\mathsf{T}}\right]+\frac{1}{m^{2}T^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{m}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{t}^{(i)\mathsf{T}}x_{t}^{(i)}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[w_{s}^{(j)\mathsf{T}}x_{s}^{(j)}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{mT^{2}}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[w^{(1)\mathsf{T}}_{t}w^{(1)}_{s}x_{t}^{(1)}x_{s}^{(1)\mathsf{T}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{mT^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[w^{(1)\mathsf{T}}_{t}w^{(1)}_{t}x_{t}^{(1)}x_{t}^{(1)\mathsf{T}}\right]

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right)}{mT^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Sigma_{t}=\frac{\Tr\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right)\Gamma_{T}}{mT},

where
(a)
follows from independence across trajectories, and
(b)
follows from applying iterated expection with respect to smaller time indices.
Hence,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{mT}.

4.2.
Analysis of Term
T_{1}
for Operator Norm

Unlike the Frobenius norm analysis in
Section

4.1
,
the operator norm analysis for
T_{1}
is more challenging, as there is no inherent inner-product structure.
Instead, we will exploit that
T_{1}
is the second moment of the Schatten-
\infty
norm of a matrix-valued martingale
\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}
.
Such types of moment bounds in their full generality are known as noncommutative Burkholder inequalities
[
24
,
16
]
. The following lemma is a direct implication of
[
24
, Theorem 4.1]
tailored to bounding
T_{1}
under the operator norm.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix a filtration
\{\mathcal{G}_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 0}
. Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 1}
be a matrix-valued martingale difference sequence where
D_{i}
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and is
\mathcal{G}_{i}
-measurable,
and let
M_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}D_{i}
. Then if
d\geqslant 8
, for any

t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
r\geqslant\log d
,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim r\left[\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{t}}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\left[D_{i}D_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{r/2}\right)^{1/r}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad+\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{t}}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\left[D_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}D_{i}\right]\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{r/2}\right)^{1/r}+\left({\sum_{i=1}^{t}}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert D_{i}\right\rVert_{S_{r}}^{r}\right)^{1/r}\right].

Some remarks are in order. First, the requirement that
d\geqslant 8
and
r\geqslant\log d
results from converting the Haagerup
L^{r}
-norm, under which the original result is stated, to operator norm. Eliminating such a requirement is of future interest.
Second, aside from the Burkholder inequality, one can also use the matrix Freedman inequality
[
29
]
to establish control on
\mathbb{E}\lVert M_{t}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}
.
However, due to the boundedness assumption in
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
, this approach will introduce a
\log(mT)
factor in the final bound due to a truncation argument.

To apply
4.1

for analyzing
T_{1}
, we denote
M_{mT}:=\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{mT}D_{j}
,
where

(4.6)

D_{j}:=(mT)^{-1}w_{t_{j}}^{(i_{j})}(x_{t_{j}}^{(i_{j})})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{T}^{-1},\quad j\in[mT],

and where the ordering is
i_{j}:=\lfloor(j-1)/T\rfloor+1
and
t_{j}:=((j-1)\mod T)+1
;
this corresponds to flattening the time index
t
first.
Focusing on the first two terms
of the bound in
4.1
(the main terms that generalize quadratic variation),
for any
r\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{mT}}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\left[D_{i}D_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{r}}{(mT)^{r}}\cdot\mathbb{E}|\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-2}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T})|^{r},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{mT}}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\left[D_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}D_{i}\right]\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})^{r}}{(mT)^{r}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{r}.

A key tool for bounding
these two terms is an approximate isometry condition on the empirical covariance

\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}
. This approximate isometry also plays a key role in the analysis of
term
T_{2}
, which we will turn to shortly in
Section

4.3
.
Bounding the last term in
4.1

involves controlling moments of the form
\mathbb{E}[\lVert w_{t}\rVert^{r}\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}x_{t}\rVert^{r}]
,
which we handle using standard
concentration inequalities for sub-Gaussian random vectors.
We remark that it is precisely
this third term that gives rise
to the
T^{2/\log{d}}
dependence in (
3.4
)
in the context of
3.2
. We conclude this section with the result obtained from the above analysis.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given a problem instance
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
, suppose that
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfies
2.2
to
2.4
, and furthermore
d\geqslant 8
. We have the following:

(i)

If
m\gtrsim\max\{\nu^{2},\nu^{4}\}d
, then

\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\lesssim\log^{2}(d)\left(\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{mT}+\frac{\nu^{4}\bar{\Tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\bar{\Tr}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{m^{2(1-1/\log{d})}T^{2(1/2-1/\log{d})}}\right).

(ii)

If
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly stable,
mT\gtrsim\frac{\max\{\nu^{2},\nu^{4}\}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}^{1/2}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}Md}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{T}^{1/2})(1-\rho)}
and
T\geqslant\kappa(A)
, then

\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\lesssim\log^{2}(d)\left(\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\Tr(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})+\Tr(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\lVert\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}{mT}+\frac{\nu^{4}\bar{\Tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}})\bar{\Tr}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1})}{(mT)^{2(1-1/\log{d})}}\right).

4.3.
Analysis of Term
T_{2}

We now turn to our analysis of term
T_{2}
.
We denote the whitened version of
\hat{Q}_{m,T}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}
as follows:

\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}:=\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2},\quad\bar{Q}_{m,T}:=\hat{Q}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}.

Via elementary operations,
we write
\hat{Q}_{m,T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1})
as:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{m,T}\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\right)=\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\left(I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right)\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}.

We now introduce an arbitrary positive definite normalization matrix
N\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
whose purpose will be explained soon in
Section

4.3.2
.
We can upper bound
\left\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1})\right\rVert_{S_{p}}^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1})\right\rVert_{S_{p}}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\right\rVert_{S_{p}}^{2}\left\|(I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T})\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\right\rVert_{S_{p}}^{2}\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\left\|\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\right\rVert_{S_{p}}^{2}\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\right\rVert_{S_{p}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(N)\lambda_{\min}\left(N^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}N^{-1/2}\right)}\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2},

where
(a)
applies the
inequality
\lVert MN\rVert_{S_{p}}\leqslant\lVert M\rVert_{S_{p}}\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
.
Now using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
and the inequality
\lVert M\rVert_{S_{p}}\leqslant\min\{d_{1},d_{2}\}^{1/p}\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
for

M\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
, we have:

(4.7)

\displaystyle T_{2}=\mathbb{E}\lVert\hat{Q}_{m,T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}-\Gamma_{T}^{-1})\rVert^{2}_{S_{p}}\leqslant\frac{d^{2/p}}{\lambda_{\min}(N)}T_{2}^{\text{AIP}}\cdot T_{2}^{\text{OLS}},

where

\displaystyle T_{2}^{\text{AIP}}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{4}},\quad T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(N^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}N^{-1/2})}\right)^{2}}.

As suggested by the notation,
T_{2}^{\text{AIP}}
measures the approximate isometry property (AIP) of the whitened empirical covariance
\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}
,
whereas
T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}
measures the typical bound on OLS error (cf.
Equation

4.1
),
specifically the norm of a self-normalized martingale
divided by the minimum eigenvalue of an empirical covariance matrix.

4.3.1.
Upper bounding
T_{2}^{\textnormal{AIP}}

Taking inspiration from the work of
[
15
]
, we utilize the
Hanson-Wright inequality
[
25
,
3
]

to control the
r
-th moments of the approximate isometry error.
For what follows, we let
\phi(\tau):=\max\{\tau,\tau^{2}\}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
denote a problem instance satisfying
2.2
and
2.3
.
For any
r\geqslant 1
,

(4.8)

\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{r}\right)^{1/r}\lesssim\nu^{2}\phi\left(\sqrt{\frac{d+r}{m}}\right).

If in addition
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly-stable, then for any
r\geqslant 1
,

(4.9)

\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{r}\right)^{1/r}\lesssim\nu^{2}\phi\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}^{1/2}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}M\left(d+r\right)}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{T}^{1/2})(1-\rho)mT}}\right).

Remark 4.4
.

(
4.8
) cannot generally be improved to
o_{T}(1)
whenever
\rho(A)\geqslant 1
. This can be seen from considering a random walk with a scaled identity noise covariance, i.e.,
A=I_{d}
and
\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}=\sigma^{2}I_{d}
. Therefore,
\Sigma_{t}=t\sigma^{2}I_{d}
, and
\Gamma_{T}=\frac{T+1}{2}\sigma^{2}I_{d}
. We can further compute

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{T}{T+1}\left(\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}T^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{(i)}(x_{t}^{(i)})^{\mathsf{T}}\right).

Therefore, fixing
m=1
and taking
T\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\left\|I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{1,T}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\geqslant\left|1-\frac{T}{T+1}\left(\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}T^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(x_{t}^{(i)}\right)_{1}^{2}\right)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}2\left|\frac{1}{2}-\int_{0}^{1}W(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right|,

where
\left(x_{t}^{(i)}\right)_{1}
denotes the first coordinate of
x_{t}^{(i)}
, and
W(\cdot)
denotes the standard Wiener process.
The second step holds by that
\left(x_{t}^{(i)}\right)_{1}
is a 1D random walk, and therefore
one can apply the 1D Donsker’s theorem
[
6
, Theorem 8.2]
combined with the continuous mapping theorem
[
31
, Theorem 2.3]
.
Now, by the portmanteau lemma
[
31
, Lemma 2.2]
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\lVert I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{1,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\geqslant 2\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1}{2}-\int_{0}^{1}W(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right|>0.

Hence, this shows that
(\mathbb{E}\lVert I_{d}-\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{r})^{1/r}
cannot be
o_{T}(1)
in general
when
\rho(A)\geqslant 1
.

4.3.2.
Upper bounding
T_{2}^{\textnormal{OLS}}

Our analysis of
T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}
builds on the arguments given in

[
30
, Lemma 5.1]
.
In order to utilize this result, we need to choose the correct normalization factor
N

depending on if we are in the (i) many trajectories setting or (ii) the strictly stable setting.
We remark it is the analysis of
T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}
which crucially relies on
the trajectory small-ball assumption (
2.4
).

We first start with the many trajectories (
m\gtrsim d
) setting.
Here, we pick
N=\Gamma_{T}
, in which case
T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}
reduces to

T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}=T^{\text{OLS}}_{\text{many}}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T})}\right)^{2}}.

This is precisely the quantity studied in
[
30
, Lemma 5.1]
.

Next, we turn to the strictly stable (
\rho(A)<1
) setting. Here,
after the burn-in time
\kappa=\kappa(A)
,
where
\kappa(A)
is defined in (
2.13
),
both

\Gamma_{\kappa}
and
\Gamma_{T}
are approximately isometric with respect to each other, independent of the relative scale between
\kappa
and
T
.
This approximate isometry is formalized by the following
result.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose that
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly-stable,
and that
T\geqslant\kappa(A)
. Then,

\Gamma_{T}\succcurlyeq\frac{1}{4}\Sigma_{\infty},\quad\frac{1}{4}\leqslant\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\Gamma_{\kappa(A)}),\quad\lambda_{\max}(\Gamma_{T}^{-1}\Gamma_{\kappa(A)})\leqslant 4.

With
4.5
in place,
we use the normalizer
N=\Gamma_{\kappa(A)}
,
for which
T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}
reduces to

T_{2}^{\text{OLS}}=T^{\text{OLS}}_{\text{stable}}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{m,T,\kappa})}\right)^{2}},\quad\tilde{\Sigma}_{m,T,\kappa}:=\Gamma_{\kappa}^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}\Gamma_{\kappa}^{-1/2},\quad\kappa:=\kappa(A).

Similar to
T^{\text{OLS}}_{\text{many}}
, the quantity

T^{\text{OLS}}_{\text{stable}}
can also be controlled via
similar arguments as in
[
30
, Lemma 5.1]
.
The following result collects the resulting bounds
for both cases.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\left(A,\mathcal{W}\right)
denote a problem instance with
\mathcal{W}
satisfying

2.2
and

2.4
. Let
r\geqslant 1
be arbitrary. We have:

(i)

If
m\gtrsim rd
, then

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T})}\right)^{r}\right)^{1/r}\lesssim\frac{r\nu^{2}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}d}{mT}.

(ii)

If
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly stable, and we further assume that both

T\gtrsim\kappa
and
mT\gtrsim r\kappa d
, then

\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\lVert\bar{Q}_{m,T}\bar{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{m,T}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{m,T,\kappa})}\right)^{r}\right)^{1/r}\lesssim\frac{r\nu^{2}\lVert\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}d}{mT}.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

We derived non-asymptotic parameter error bounds for
linear system identification with general non-isotropic sub-Gaussian noise that
nearly matches the rate predicted by asymptotic normality,
for error measured in both the Frobenius norm and operator norm.

These results open up several further research directions.
One immediate direction is in studying to what extent that the
“burn-in” requirements prescribed by (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) (resp. (
3.4
) and (
3.5
)) to get within a
(1+\varepsilon)
-factor (resp.
O(1)
) of the
asymptotic error for Frobenius norm (resp. for operator norm) are sharp.
Another technical extension would be eliminating the extra
\log^{2}(d)
in the operator norm bound, so that it matches the CLT-predicted rate exactly. This would require a sharper analysis of the operator norm of a matrix-valued martingale.
More broadly, our results remain valid when the error metric is the general Schatten-
p
norm. However, the central limit behaviour of OLS error under general Schatten-
p
norms is unexplored, and it is of interest to establish both asymptotic and non-asymptotic bounds
in this setting.
Finally, deriving optimal non-asymptotic results in the
m=o(d)
setting when

\rho(A)\geqslant 1
and
A
is regular (so that OLS is consistent) remains a challenging
open question.

Appendix A
Supporting Proofs for Asymptotic Analysis

In this section we supply proofs to our claims in
Section

2.1
. Some proofs will crucially rely on
4.3
, whose proof we further delay to
Appendix

B
. Before we start, we remind ourselves of two important quantities:

\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}=\frac{1}{mT}X_{m,T}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{m,T},\quad\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}=\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}.

We further introduce the corresponding quantities within the
i
-th trajectory:

(A.1)

\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}:=\frac{1}{T}X_{T}^{(i)\mathsf{T}}X_{T}^{(i)},\quad\bar{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}:=\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}\Gamma_{T}^{-1/2}.

This allows us to write
\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}
and
\bar{\Sigma}_{m,T}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}
.

A.1.
Asymptotic normality of
\hat{A}_{m,T}-A

In the following, we supply the proofs for the asymptotic normality of
\hat{A}_{m,T}-A
under two cases:
(i)
holding
m
fixed while sending
T\to\infty
, i.e. (
2.5
), and
(ii)
joint limit when
(m,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
along certain paths, i.e. (
2.6
). For both cases, we will assume that
A
is
(M,\rho)
-strictly-stable.
We note that the case where
T
is fixed and
m\to\infty
follows immediately from classical
M
-estimation.

(i).
Fixed
m
,
T\to\infty
:

We first rewrite the OLS error as a weighted average of OLS error along each trajectory:

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{m,T}-A

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}W^{(i)\mathsf{T}}_{T}X^{(i)}_{T}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X^{(i)\mathsf{T}}_{T}X^{(i)}_{T}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{A}_{T}^{(i)}-A\right)\left(X^{(i)\mathsf{T}}_{T}X^{(i)}_{T}\right)\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X^{(i)\mathsf{T}}_{T}X^{(i)}_{T}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{A}_{T}^{(i)}-A\right)\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1},

where
W^{(i)}_{T}
and
\hat{A}^{(i)}_{T}
denote the
i
-th row’s noise process and the OLS estimator obtained from using only the
i
-th trajectory, respectively. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathrm{vec}\left(\hat{A}_{m,T}-A\right)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}\right)\otimes I_{d}\right)\left(\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathrm{vec}\left(\hat{A}_{T}^{(i)}-A\right)\right).

The result
[
4
, Theorem 1]
yields the following asymptotic distribution for each
\hat{A}_{T}^{(i)}-A
error term:

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A}_{T}^{(i)}-A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Sigma^{-1}_{\infty}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right),

Under stability of
A
, as
T\to\infty
, for each
i
we have both
\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}\to\Sigma_{\infty}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}\to\Sigma_{\infty}
a.s., and therefore
\hat{\Sigma}_{T}^{(i)}\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}\to I_{d}
a.s.. Using Slutsky’s theorem
[
31
, Theorem 2.8]
, we have as
T\to\infty

the result (
2.5
):

\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A}_{m,T}-A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}\left(0,\left(\Sigma^{-1}_{\infty}/m\right)\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}\right).

(ii).
Jointly sending
(m,T)\to(\infty,\infty)
:

Suppose we have a function
k:\mathbb{N}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{N}_{+}
which maps the number of trajectories
m

to the length
T
of each trajectory. We also suppose that
\phi(m)\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
. We start by writing:

\displaystyle\sqrt{m\cdot\phi(m)}\mathrm{vec}\left(\hat{A}_{m,\phi(m)}-A\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,\phi(m)}^{-1}\otimes I_{d}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi(m)}}\sum_{t=1}^{\phi(m)}\mathrm{vec}\left(w_{t}^{(i)}(x_{t}^{(i)})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right).

We denote
X_{m,i}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi(m)}}\sum_{t=1}^{\phi(m)}\mathrm{vec}\left(w_{t}^{(i)}(x_{t}^{(i)})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)
. Similar to the previous case, we proceed in two steps. First, by viewing
\{X_{m,i}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}_{+},\,\,i\in[m]\}
as a triangular array, we show via the Lindeberg-Feller CLT
[
31
, Proposition 2.27]
that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{m,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}(0,\Sigma_{\infty}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}).

Second, we show under stability, as
m\to\infty
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{m,T}^{-1}\to\Sigma_{\infty}^{-1}
a.s. Then again by using Slutsky’s theorem, we can conclude the result (
2.6
):

\displaystyle\sqrt{m\cdot\phi(m)}\mathrm{vec}\left(\hat{A}_{m,\phi(m)}-A\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{m,\phi(m)}^{-1}\otimes I_{d}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{m,i}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathsf{N}(0,\Sigma_{\infty}^{-1}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}).

We now carry out the details for the first step. We know for a fixed
m
,
X_{m,i}
are i.i.d. across
i
, and for each
i
,

\displaystyle V_{m}:=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m,i}X_{m,i}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\phi(m)}\sum_{t=1}^{\phi(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t}^{(i)}(x_{t}^{(i)})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes w_{t}^{(i)}(w_{t}^{(i)})^{\mathsf{T}}\right]=\Gamma_{\phi(m)}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}.

We also note that
(i)

\mathbb{E}[X_{m,i}]=0
, and
(ii)
since
\phi(m)\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
, we have that under stability of
A
,
V_{m}\to\Sigma_{\infty}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{W}}
.
Since
X_{m,i}
is i.i.d. across
i
, to be able to apply the Lindeberg-Feller CLT, it remains to verify the Lyapunov condition: there exists
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert X_{m,1}\right\rVert^{2+\delta}\right]<\infty.

We will verify this for
\delta=2
. Simplifying the notation by writing
w_{t}:=w_{t}^{(1)}
and similarly
x_{t}:=x_{t}^{(1)}
, we start by writing the norm of
X_{m,1}
as the Frobenius norm of a matrix-valued martingale:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert X_{m,1}\right\rVert^{4}\right]=\frac{1}{\phi(m)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\sum_{t=1}^{\phi(m)}w_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{F}^{4}.

Recall that under the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\Tr(A^{\mathsf{T}}B)
, the space of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
matrices form a Hilbert space with the norm being Frobenius norm. Therefore here, we can apply a Hilbert space martingale Burkholder-Rosenthal inequality
[
22
, Theorem 1.1]
to obtain for any
T\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{F}^{4}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert w_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{2}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert w_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{F}^{4}\right],

where we recall
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(w_{0:t-1})

Fact D.1
.

Proposition D.7

(Moments of sub-Gaussian vector norms)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a zero-mean random vector with finite positive definite covariance
Q:=\mathbb{E}[xx^{\mathsf{T}}]
.
Suppose that
z:=Q^{-1/2}x
is directionally
R
-sub-Gaussian.
Then, we have for any fixed
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
r\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}\lVert Mx\rVert^{r})^{1/r}\lesssim R\left(\sqrt{\Tr(MQM^{\mathsf{T}})}+\sqrt{r}\left\|Q^{1/2}M^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\right).

Proof.

This result follows as a consequence of
generic chaining moment inequalities for sub-Gaussian processes
[
10
, Theorem 3.2]
.
Here we provide a more direct proof.

Consider the random variable
\lVert MQ^{1/2}z\rVert^{2}
. From
[
13
, Thoerem 1]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\lVert MQ^{1/2}z\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant R^{2}\left(\Tr(\Sigma)+2\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma^{2})\log(1/\delta)}+2\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\log(1/\delta)\right),\quad\Sigma:=Q^{1/2}M^{\mathsf{T}}MQ^{1/2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}R^{2}\left(\Tr(\Sigma)+2\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\log(1/\delta)}+2\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\log(1/\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}R^{2}\left(2\Tr(\Sigma)+3\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\log(1/\delta)\right),

where (a) applies Hölder’s inequality
and (b) applies Young’s inequality.
Hence, defining the random variable

Y:=\lVert MQ^{1/2}z\rVert^{2}-2R^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)
,
we can write for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\max\{Y,0\}>t)=\mathbb{P}(Y>t)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{t}{3R^{2}\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}\right).

From
D.9
, for
p\geqslant 1
, we have

(\mathbb{E}\max\{Y,0\}^{p})^{1/p}\lesssim pR^{2}\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
.

On the other hand, we have surely that:

\displaystyle\lVert MQ^{1/2}z\rVert^{2}=Y+2R^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)\leqslant\max\{Y,0\}+2R^{2}\Tr(\Sigma).

Therefore:

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}\lVert MQ^{1/2}z\rVert^{r})^{1/r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}(\mathbb{E}\lVert MQ^{1/2}z\rVert^{2r})^{1/(2r)}\leqslant(\mathbb{E}[(\max\{Y,0\}+2R^{2}\Tr(\Sigma))^{r}])^{1/(2r)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}((\mathbb{E}\max\{Y,0\}^{r})^{1/r}+2R^{2}\Tr(\Sigma))^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(rR^{2}\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}+R^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)\right)^{1/2}\leqslant R\left(\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}+\sqrt{r}\sqrt{\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}}\right),

where
(a)
follows from Jensen’s inequality and
(b)
follows from Minkowski inequality.
∎

Proposition D.8
.

Let
A
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
-valued symmetric random matrix. Suppose there exists a family of functions
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{v}:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\;|\;v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\}
and a non-negative universal constant
b
such that for every
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|v^{\mathsf{T}}Av|\leqslant g_{v}(\log(1/\delta)+b),

then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\lVert A\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\leqslant 2\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g_{v}\left(\log(1/\delta)+b+d\log 9\right),

Proof.

We proceed with a standard union bound argument.
Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and let
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}
denote a minimal
\varepsilon
-net on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. By
[
32
, Lemma 5.2]
we have
\left|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leqslant\left(1+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}
, and by
[
32
, Lemma 4.4.2]
:

\lVert A\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\max_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}}\left|v^{\mathsf{T}}Av\right|.

We know for every
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}
, with probability at least
\delta/\left|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}\right|
,

v^{\mathsf{T}}Av\leqslant g_{v}\left(\log\left(\left|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}\right|/\delta\right)+b\right).

Therefore by union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\lVert A\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}}\left|v^{\mathsf{T}}Av\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}}g_{v}\left(\log\left(\left|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\varepsilon}\right|/\delta\right)+b\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g_{v}\left(\log(1/\delta)+b+d\log\left(1+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

Now taking
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4}
finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition D.9
.

Let
Y
be a real-valued random variable. Suppose there exists constants
b\geqslant 0
, and a continuous
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
that satisfies (i)
f(0)=0
and (ii)
\forall\;t>0,\;f(t)/t>0
and is nondecreasing, such that for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Y|>t\right)\leqslant\exp(-f(t)+b).

Then we have for
r\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|Y|^{r}\right]\leqslant 2\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}.

Proof.

This result is standard when
f
is instantiated to be quadratic (i.e., a sub-Gaussian tail)
[
32
, Proposition 2.6.1]
or linear (i.e., a sub-exponential tail)
[
32
, Proposition 2.8.1]
. Here we prove a more generic version with the same proof technique.

We first note that by the assumption that
f(t)/t
is nondecreasing, for any
c\geqslant 1
and
t>0
,

(D.1)

f(ct)/ct\geqslant f(t)/t\implies f(ct)\geqslant cf(t).

Now by the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|Y|^{r}\right]

\displaystyle=r\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(|Y|>t\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant r\int_{0}^{f^{-1}(r+b)}t^{r-1}\mathrm{d}t+r\int_{f^{-1}(r+b)}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\exp(-f(t)+b)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant r\int_{0}^{f^{-1}(r+b)}t^{r-1}\mathrm{d}t+r\int_{f^{-1}(r+b)}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\exp\left(-f\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\left(t/f^{-1}(r+b)\right)\right)+b\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}+r\int_{f^{-1}(r+b)}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\exp\left(-\left(t(r+b)/f^{-1}(r+b)\right)+b\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}+r\int_{f^{-1}(r+b)}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\exp\left(-tr/f^{-1}(r+b)\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}+r\int_{r}^{\infty}\left(f^{-1}(r+b)r^{-1}s\right)^{r-1}\exp\left(-s\right)f^{-1}(r+b)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}+\frac{1}{r^{r-1}}\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{r-1}\exp\left(-s\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}+\frac{\Gamma(r)}{r^{r-1}}\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}2\left(f^{-1}(r+b)\right)^{r},

where at
(a)
we invoke (
D.1
) with
c=t/f^{-1}(r+b)\geqslant 1
,
(b)
follows from standard moment computation for exponential distribution, and
(c)
follows from when
r\geqslant 1
,
\Gamma(r)\leqslant r^{r-1}
.
∎

Proposition D.10
.

Let
Y
be a real-valued nonnegative random variable. Suppose for some
\delta_{0}\leqslant 1
there exists positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
:

Y\leqslant C_{1}\delta^{-C_{2}}.

Then for any
r>0
satisfying
rC_{2}<1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{r}\right]\leqslant\frac{\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r}}{1-rC_{2}}.

Proof.

By the tail assumption, we have for any
t\geqslant C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(Y>t\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{C_{1}}{t}\right)^{1/C_{2}}.

By the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, when
r-1/C_{2}<0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{r}\right]

\displaystyle=r\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Y>t\right)\mathrm{d}t=r\int_{0}^{C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}}t^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Y>t\right)\mathrm{d}t+r\int_{C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Y>t\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant r\int_{0}^{C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}}t^{r-1}\mathrm{d}t+r\int_{C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}}^{\infty}t^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Y>t\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r}+rC_{1}^{1/C_{2}}\int_{C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}}^{\infty}t^{r-1-1/C_{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r}+rC_{1}^{1/C_{2}}\frac{-\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r-1/C_{2}}}{r-1/C_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{r\delta_{0}}{r-1/C_{2}}\right)\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r}=\frac{1-rC_{2}+rC_{2}\delta_{0}}{1-rC_{2}}\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r}\leqslant\frac{\left(C_{1}\delta_{0}^{-C_{2}}\right)^{r}}{1-rC_{2}},

which finishes the proof.
∎
