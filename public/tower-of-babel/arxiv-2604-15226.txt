Nonlinear Schrödinger equations with spatial white noise potential on full space for d ≤ 3 d\leq 3
Antoine MOUZARD, Immanuel ZACHHUBER

Abstract
In this paper, we prove existence and uniqueness of energy solutions for nonlinear Schrödinger equations with a multiplicative white noise on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with d ≤ 3 d\leq 3 . We rely on an exponential transform and conserved quantities for existence of energy solutions. Using paracontrolled calculus, we prove Strichartz inequalities which encode the dispersive properties of the solutions. This allows to obtain local well-posedness for low regularity solutions and uniqueness of energy solutions for various equations. In particular, our results are the first results of propagation without loss of both regularity and localization for such equations in full space as well as the first results on ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} for such singular dispersive SPDEs. We are also obtain local well-posedness in two dimensions for deterministic initial data.



1 –  Introduction

We study the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with multiplicative white noise

i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta u+u\xi+|u|^{m-1}u

(NLS)

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\leq 3
and
m\geq 1
and the Hartree equation

i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta u+u\xi+u\cdot V_{\beta}*|u|^{2}

(Hartree)

on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with nonnegative symmetric
V_{\beta}\in W^{\beta,1}
for
\beta>0
. The noise
\xi
is a spatial Gaussian white noise with covariance
\mathbb{E}[\xi(x)\xi(y)]=\delta_{0}(x-y)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The difficulty lies in the irregularity of the noise as well as its behavior at infinity since
\xi\in C_{-\delta}^{-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}
for any
\delta,\kappa>0
, that is growing at infinity slower than any polynomial
\langle x\rangle^{\delta}
with negative Hölder regularity. In dimension
d\geq 2
, the noise is too irregular for the equation to make sense by itself and its resolution involves a probabilistic renormalization procedure. The resolution of such singular dispersive SPDEs have been recently achieved following the progess on their parabolic counterparts with regularity structures
[
17
]
and paracontrolled calculus
[
13
]
. For dispersive PDEs, the lack of strong regularization of the Schrödinger group in comparison to the heat semigroup makes this study very different as regularity is key for singular SPDEs.

The first resolution of dispersive singular SPDEs was done by by Debussche and Weber
[
8
]
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for the cubic (
NLS
) equation based on an exponential transform with the new unknown

u=e^{X}v

(1.1)

with suitable random field
X
following Hairer and Labbé
[
18
]
on the parabolic Anderson model on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. To solve the equation, one considers a regularization
(\xi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
of the potential and relies on uniform bounds on the mass and energy after a renormalization procedure to construct solutions in the limit. This gives existence of solutions while one proves uniqueness with strong enough a priori bounds on the solution, again obtained via bounds on the regularized solutions uniform in
\varepsilon>0
. This was improved in various way on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
by Tzvetkov and Visciglia
[
30
,
31
]
and adapted to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in different context, see
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. The main drawback of this approach based on an exponential transform is the loss of regularity of the solution with respect to the initial data, as well as loss of spatial localization on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the compact case, an approach based on paracontrolled calculus can also be used with the construction of the Anderson Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}=-\Delta+\xi

(1.2)

as first done by Gubinelli, Ugurcan and Zachhuber
[
16
]
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
. This operator can be constructed with a random domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, it has a compact resolvent hence a basis of eigenfunctions with bounded from below eigenvalues. This allows to define the Schrödinger group
e^{it\mathcal{H}}
and one can solve the cubic (
NLS
) equation with the associated mild formulation. This gives global well-posedness for initial data
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
as well as the existence of energy solution for initial data in the form domain
\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{\mathcal{H}})
. Uniqueness for such equations in the case without noise requires Strichartz inequalities which encodes the dispersive regularization properties of the Schrödinger flow. For the Anderson Hamiltonian, this was obtained by the authors in the previous work
[
27
]
on any compact surfaces, using the construction by the first author in
[
25
]
based on second order paracontrolled calculus. For example, the operator satisfies the Strichartz inequalities

\|e^{it\mathcal{H}}u_{0}\|_{L^{p}([0,T],L^{q}(M))}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\frac{1}{p}+\kappa}}

(1.3)

for
(p,q)
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{2}
and any
\kappa>0
on a compact surfaces without boundary. This allows to obtain low regularity solutions, that is local well-posedess for the cubic (
NLS
) equation for deterministic initial data
u_{0}\in H^{\sigma}
with
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<1
from which one recovers uniqueness of energy solutions. In the deterministic case setting
\xi
to zero, this result was obtained by Burq, Gérard and Tzvetkov
[
3
]
without the
\kappa
loss of derivatives which is optimal for general compact surfaces. The approach based on the Anderson Hamiltonian can also be considered via the exponential transform on compact spaces since it gives the form domain of the operator, see for example
[
24
,
26
]
, however paracontrolled calculus seems necessary to obtain Strichartz inequalities. One of the main differences also lies in the well-posedness result obtained, exponential transform induces a loss of regularity at positive time as one can see in
[
8
]
while the use of the Anderson Hamiltonian gives a random space that is propagated by the dynamic. In full space, the study of this random operator and its spectral properties are too involved for the moment to help the study of the associated equation, for example its spectrum is
\mathbb{R}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\leq 3
, see
[
19
,
32
]
and references therein.

In this work, we prove global well-posedness of energy solutions without loss of regularity or localization on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\leq 3
. In two dimensions, we consider (
NLS
) for general
m\geq 2
. In three dimensions, we are restricted to the (
Hartree
) equation for
\beta>\frac{1}{2}
. We obtain existence for more general parameters such as (
NLS
) for
1\leq m<6
or (
Hartree
) for
\beta>0
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the restriction is needed to prove uniqueness of solutions. Our approach being based on an exponential transform, we rely on uniform bounds with respect to
\varepsilon>0
of a suitable renormalized equation and compactness to obtain existence of solutions in the limit with
X
a well-chosen random field.

For
\mu\in(0,1]

for (

) and
[
33

using paracontrolled calculus. We follow the perturbative argument of our previous work
[
27

using paracontrolled calculus with a modified operator
\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}=\Gamma^{-1}\mathcal{H}\Gamma
which is a better behaved perturbation of the Laplacian in comparison to
\mathcal{H}
. The map
\Gamma

C
and the only ingredient needed from paracontrolled calculus, see
[
7

For any

, (

L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for

As mentionned, the only results in three dimensions were obtained in
[
16
,
33
]
on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Local well-posdness for (

[

, see Sections

with respect to

e^{it\Delta}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
than on

. While this is natural to expect, this is the first result of this type for such singular dispersive PDEs. Indeed, singular stochastic PDEs are harder to solve on full space due to the invariance by translation of the noise. In order to prove the previous result, we follow a classical fixed point argument which requires to work with a space stable by the flow. This is only possible because we are able to solve the equation without loss of regularization and localization and relies crucially on our spatial localization argument. One of the main interest of the previous result is that we are able to consider deterministic initial data for (
NLS
) on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. On compact surfaces, this result is only known for low regularity initial data with our previous work

, we prove here that this is also possible on

Since the work by Debussche and Weber

on

for strong solutions with loss of regularity and localisation. This was generalised to any

in

with additional arguments from
[

using modified energy methods. Remark that they prove global well-posedness in the sense of existence and uniquenss of a global solution, without propagation of a space of initial data for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In a different direction, Chauleur and Mouzard
[
4
]
considered a logarithmic nonlinearity which behave nicely with the exponential transform despite not being Lipschitz. More recently, we mention the recent work
[
21
]
where the convergence of solutions on large torus to the solutions on full space is studied. In a different direction, Mackowiak considered (
NLS
) with an additional confining harmonic potential on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

. To the best of our knowledge, this covers all results on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
while no results were known on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We now describe the main arguments of the present work. In this context of singular dispersive SPDEs one needs to define carefully a notion of energy solutions, we consider (
NLS
) equation in two dimensions in this introduction for the sake of clarity. For the usual equation without noise, energy solutions are defined for
u_{0}\in H^{1}
satisfying the equation in the dual space
(H^{1})^{*}=H^{-1}
. In our case,
u_{0}\in e^{X}(H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2})
gives a solution in the dual space of
e^{X}(H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2})
which does not contain smooth functions. The new variable
u=e^{X}v
gives the formal equation

ie^{X}\partial_{t}v=-e^{X}\Delta v-2e^{X}\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+e^{X}(\xi-\Delta X-|\nabla X|^{2})v+e^{(m-1)X}|v|^{m-1}v

(1.4)

which usually motivates the choice
\Delta X=\xi
to cancel the roughest part of the equation. This is the path followed by Debussche and Weber
[
8
]
, they considered this new equation on
v
with
\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:}
interpreted as a Wick product since
\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{-1-\kappa}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
hence
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, this is the probabilistic renormalization of the singular SPDE. For the product term
\nabla X\cdot\nabla v
to be well-defined, one needs regularity higher than one thus motivating them to consider strong solution with better regularity, that is
v_{0}\in e^{X}H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. To get a notion of energy solutions, the equation should be satisfied in the dual space of
e^{X}H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
hence tested against
e^{X}\varphi
for
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to get

\big\langle i\partial_{t}ve^{X},e^{X}\varphi\big\rangle=\big\langle-e^{X}\Delta v-e^{X}2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v-e^{X}\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:}v+e^{(m-1)X}|v|^{m-2}v,e^{X}\varphi\big\rangle

(1.5)

as weak formulation of the equation. This amounts to a multiplication of the equation by
e^{X}
which gives the equation

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=-\big(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla\big)v-e^{2X}\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:}v+e^{mX}|v|^{m-2}v

(1.6)

in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
where
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is enough for all the terms to make sense. Since
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, multiplying the equation by
e^{-2X}
is a singular product which corresponds to the singular product
\nabla X\cdot\nabla v
for
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
as in
[
8
]
. Remark that this computation is similar to the construction of the Anderson Hamiltonian on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
for
d\in\{2,3\}
via its quadratic form from
[
26
]
. In the compact case of the torus, the energy of the equation is formally

\langle\mathcal{H}u,u\rangle=\langle\mathcal{H}e^{X}v,e^{X}v\rangle=\langle e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v,v\rangle

(1.7)

for any
v\in H^{1}
where
\mathcal{H}=-\Delta+\xi
corresponds to the Anderson Hamiltonian. Using the notation
Tv=e^{X}v
, the equation on
v
is

i\partial_{t}v=T^{-1}\mathcal{H}Tv+T^{-1}(|Tv|^{m-2}Tv)

(1.8)

where
T^{-1}u=e^{-X}u
which involves the singular gradient product for energy solution while the equation

iT^{*}T\partial_{t}v=T^{*}\mathcal{H}Tv+T^{*}(|Tv|^{m-2}Tv)

(1.9)

with
T^{*}=T
is the equation to consider for energy solution. Since
T
is not a unitary transform, this yields two different equations, one of our contributions is to identify the second one as a natural notion of energy solutions. For example, this allows to get existence of energy solutions using a compactness argument relying only on uniform bounds for the energy with the exponential transform without loss of derivatives in the case of the two dimensional torus with
u_{0}\in e^{X}H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. For uniqueness, Strichartz inequalities are needed which a priori requires paracontrolled calculus. This is the strategy we follow to get global well-posedness for (
NLS
) and (
Hartree
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
without any loss of regularity. While we do not rely on the spectral properties of the Anderson operator in full space, we use the notation

\mathcal{H}u=-\Delta u+u\xi

(1.10)

as the computation are similar to the ones on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

On full space, there is an additional problem of growth at infinity and previous work considered localised initial data. In addition to the loss of regularity, the use of the exponential transform induces a loss of localization at positive time due to the growth at infinity of
e^{X}
and
e^{-X}
. To get around this problem, we consider a new random field
X
such that
e^{X},e^{-X}\in L^{\infty}
while still cancelling the roughest part of the equation. Following a decomposition introduced by Gubinelli and Hofmanovà in
[
12
]
, one can split the white noise
\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in two terms as

\xi=\xi^{\leq}+\xi^{>}

(1.11)

with
\xi^{\leq}\in L_{-\delta}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\xi^{>}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
\delta,\kappa>0
using Lemma
B.2
. The first term encodes the growth at infinity while being more regular and the second term has the same local regularity with decay at infinity. Considering for example
\Delta X=\xi^{>}
gives the equation

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=-\big(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla\big)v+e^{2X}(\xi^{\leq}-\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:})v+e^{mX}|v|^{m-2}v

(1.12)

where the product with the growing smooth term
\xi^{\leq}
is not singular. The important property is that the norms of the spaces
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},e^{qX}\mathrm{d}x)
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathrm{d}x)
are equivalent for any
q\in\mathbb{R}
thus it avoids loss of localization between
u
and
v
. Given this new localized rough field, we adapt the argument from Debussche and Weber to the full space without any loss which give the existence of energy solutions. Note that in three dimensions, the renormalization procedure is more involved, as done on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
in
[
16
]
. To prove uniqueness, we obtain Strichartz inequalities for the linear equation

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v

(1.13)

with
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{1}
. We follow a perturbative argument using the result for the linear flow
e^{it\Delta}
, the idea being to consider a mild formulation of the linear equation as

v_{t}=e^{it\Delta}v_{0}-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}(e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}+\Delta)v_{s}\mathrm{d}s

(1.14)

with the use of spectral projections both for the solution and its initial condition. However
e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}+\Delta
is not well-behaved enough for this proof to work and we need refined tools, as it was already the case on compact surfaces, see
[
27
]
. This argument also appears in
[
5
]
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
where the authors obtained Strichartz inequalities for the regularized equation that blow up logarithmically as
\varepsilon>0
goes to
0
which still alllows them to obtain uniqueness. This does not yield Strichartz inequalities for the solution in the limit and does not apply to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the blow up is polynomial. In this work, we use paracontrolled calculus to prove Strichartz inequalities for the limiting equation in two and three dimensions. The main tool is a random transformation
\Gamma
such that

\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}=(\Gamma e^{X})^{-1}\mathcal{H}(\Gamma e^{X})

(1.15)

is a better behaved perturbation of the Laplacian which was first used in
[
16
]
, see also
[
10
,
22
,
25
,
33
]
and references therein. The mild formulation

v_{t}^{\sharp}=e^{it\Delta}v_{0}^{\sharp}-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}(\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}+\Delta)v_{s}^{\sharp}\mathrm{d}s

(1.16)

with
v=\Gamma v^{\sharp}
allows to obtain Strichartz inequalities, see Theorem
4.6
for a precise statement. This allows to obtain low regularity solutions using a fixed point formulation as well as uniqueness of energy solutions. While this was done on compact spaces in
[
27
]
, there is the additionnal difficulty of growth at infinity of the potential. Indeed, the previous mild formulation can not be used a priori since the right hand side induces a arbitrary small loss of localization thus preventing the closure of the fixed point. While a mild formulation with respect to the Laplacian is crucial to obtain Strichartz inequalities for the linear equation, we consider the mild formulation associated to
\mathcal{H}
to solve the associated nonlinear equations. In particular, the linear equation

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v

(1.17)

defined a linear flow
S_{t}
a priori defined on
L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with our notion of energy solutions. Since we do not have loss of localization in our construction using energy, this allows to avoid the previous loss of localization in the mild formulation with the local well-posedness result mentioned above.

) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for

2 –  Energy solutions and propagation of regularity in 1D

In one dimension, the equation is not singular and one can get global energy solutions with uniform energy bounds for the solution with regularized noise and pass to the limit. The initial data must have polynomial decay for the energy to be bounded due to the growth of the noise, we are able to propagate
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
0<\mu\leq 1
. However one can not propagate strong solutions in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
because of the local irregularity of the noise. We prove that a space of well-prepared data can be propagated using the exponential transform, this was done for example in section
5
of
[
4
]
for the stochastic logarithmic NLS equation. In particular, we already improve this result using the spatial localization defined in Appendix
B
from
[
12
]
. We prove the propagation of the random space

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):=e^{X}\big(H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\big)

(2.1)

for
0<\mu\leq 1
with no loss of localization or regularity where
X
is a suitable Gaussian field depending on
\xi
. The main argument is to consider
u=e^{X}v
which satisfies the equation

i\partial_{t}v=-\Delta v-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+(\xi-\Delta X-|\nabla X|^{2})v+e^{-(m-1)X}|v|^{m-1}v

(2.2)

with
v_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, the solution belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{3}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for any
\kappa>0
using the regularity of
X
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
0<\mu\leq 1
, there exists a unique solution
u\in\mathcal{C}\big(\mathbb{R},H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\big)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Given two solutions
u,u^{\prime}
with respective initial data
u_{0},u_{0}^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
T>0
, we have

\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\|u(t)-u^{\prime}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C_{T,u_{0},u_{0}^{\prime}}\|u_{0}-u_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

(2.3)

with
C_{T,u_{0},u_{0}^{\prime}}>0
. If moreover
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{2}
, then
u\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{2})
.

Proof :

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to the regularized equation

i\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+u_{\varepsilon}\xi_{\varepsilon}+|u_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}u_{\varepsilon}

(2.4)

with initial data
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=u_{0}
. It has conserved mass

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

(2.5)

and energy

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{m+1}\mathrm{d}x

(2.6)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Since the equation is defocusing, this gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(2.7)

hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{2\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+\kappa,1}}\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\|_{C_{-2\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+2\kappa}}^{2}\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\|_{C_{-2\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\frac{\delta}{\frac{1}{2}-2\kappa}}^{2}}^{1-4\kappa}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{1+4\kappa}\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\|_{C_{-2\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}}

(2.10)

where
\mathcal{W}_{2\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+\kappa,1}=\mathcal{B}_{1,1,2\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+\kappa}
for
\delta,\kappa>0
arbitrary small using Lemmas
A.3
,
A.4
and
A.5
. For the weighted norm, we use the equation to get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\text{Im}\Big(2\eta\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{x}{\langle x\rangle^{2\eta-2}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\nabla\overline{u}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(2.11)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{2\eta-1}^{2}}

(2.12)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(2.13)

for
2\eta-1\leq 0
using the conservation of mass. We get

\sup_{t\in I}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}+|I|\cdot\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\sup_{t\in I}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(2.14)

for any time interval
I
containing
0
with
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2}\wedge\mu
. Together with the previous energy bound, this yields

\sup_{t\in I}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+|I|\sup_{t\in I}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{3}{2}+2\kappa}

(2.15)

using that
\xi_{\varepsilon}
in uniformly bounded in
C_{-2\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
. Since
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the energy of the initial data
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)
is uniformly bounded and

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\ \sup_{t\in I}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}<\infty

(2.16)

for any finite interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. Up to extraction using compactness, this yields the existence of weak solution
u\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},H^{1}\cap L_{\nu}^{2})
satisfying the equation in
H^{-1}\cap L_{-\mu}^{2}
, see for example Ginibre and Velo
[
11
]
or Proposition
4.1
in
[
4
]
. To propagate localization up to
\mu=1
, we write

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\text{Im}\Big(2\eta\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{x}{\langle x\rangle^{2\eta-2}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\nabla\overline{u}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(2.17)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}}\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{2\eta-\frac{3}{2}}^{2}}

(2.18)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L_{\frac{1}{2}}^{2}}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(2.19)

for
\eta\leq\frac{3}{4}
using that one has already propage
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, one can go up to
\eta=1
with iteration of this argument. Uniqueness follows directly using
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, for
u
and
u^{\prime}
two solutions with respective initial data
u_{0}
and
u_{0}^{\prime}
, we have

i\partial_{t}w=-\Delta w+w\xi+|u|^{m-1}u-|u^{\prime}|^{m-1}u^{\prime}

(2.20)

where
w=u-u^{\prime}
. We get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\text{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(|u(t,x)|^{m-1}u(t,x)-|u^{\prime}(t,x)|^{m-1}u^{\prime}(t,x)\big)w(t,x)\mathrm{d}x

(2.21)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|u(t,x)|^{m-1}+|u^{\prime}(t,x)|^{m-1})|w(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

(2.22)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{m-1}+\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{m-1}\big)\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(2.23)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1+\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{m-1}+\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{m-1}\big)\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(2.24)

which yields

\|u(t)-u^{\prime}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim e^{\int_{0}^{t}\big(1+\|u(s)\|_{H^{1}}^{m-1}+\|u^{\prime}(s)\|_{H^{1}}^{m-1}\big)\mathrm{d}s}\|u_{0}-u_{0}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(2.25)

with Gronwall’s lemma hence uniqueness and dependance on the initial data. Finally, we prove the propagation of the random space

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{2}=e^{X}(L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{2})

(2.26)

where
X\in C_{\delta}^{\frac{3}{2}-\kappa}
such that

Y=\xi-\Delta X-|\nabla X|^{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}

(2.27)

for any
\kappa,\delta>0
and
e^{qX}\in L^{\infty}
for any
q\in\mathbb{R}
, see Appendix
B
for details on localization. Since
u(t)\in L_{\mu}^{2}
and
e^{-X}\in L^{\infty}
, we have

v(t)=e^{-X}u(t)\in L_{\mu}^{2}

(2.28)

and it only remains to prove that
v(t)=e^{-X}u(t)\in H^{2}
. It satisfies the transformed equation

i\partial_{t}v=-\Delta v-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+Yv+e^{(m-1)X}|v|^{m-1}v

(2.29)

with initial data
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{2}
. The time derivative
w=\partial_{t}v
satisfies the equation

i\partial_{t}w=-\Delta w-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla w+Yw+e^{(m-1)X}\big(\frac{m-1}{2}+1\big)|v|^{m-1}w+e^{(m-1)X}\frac{m-1}{2}|v|^{m-3}v^{2}\overline{w}

(2.30)

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w(t,x)|^{2}e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{m-1}{2}\textrm{Im}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v(t,x)|^{m-3}v(t,x)^{2}\overline{w}(t,x)e^{(m-1)X(x)}\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(2.31)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v^{m-1}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|w(t)e^{X}\|_{L^{2}}

(2.32)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{m-1}\|w(t)e^{X}\|_{L^{2}}

(2.33)

using that
e^{(m-2)X}\in L^{\infty}
. Gronwall’s lemma gives

\|w(t)e^{X}\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{ct}\|w(0)e^{X}\|_{L^{2}}

(2.34)

for a positive constant
c>0
depending on the initial data. Using the equation on
v
at time
t=0
, we have

iw(0)=-\Delta v_{0}-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v_{0}+Yv_{0}+e^{(m-1)X}|v_{0}|^{m-1}v_{0}

(2.35)

which belongs to
L^{2}
for
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{2}
with
\mu>0
since
X\in C_{\delta}^{\frac{3}{2}-\kappa}
and
Y\in C_{-\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
. This implies that
w(t)e^{X}\in L^{2}
for any
t>0
thus
w(t)\in L^{2}
since
e^{-X}\in L^{\infty}
. Again, the equation on
v
gives

\Delta v=-i\partial_{t}v-2\nabla X\nabla v+Yv+e^{(m-1)X}|v|^{m-1}v

(2.36)

hence
\Delta v(t)\in L^{2}
which completes the proof of propagation of regularity for
v
.

\square

or
m=6
with small initial data using the same interpolation argument for the
L^{p}

3 –  Existence of energy solutions in higher dimensions

We define a notion of energy solution for the equation

i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta u+u\xi+f(u)

(3.1)

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
and
f(u)
the nonlinearity. The initial data
u_{0}
is taken in the random space

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=e^{X}\big(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)

(3.2)

for
0<\mu\leq 1
and a suitable random field
X
depending on
\xi
and the dimension. As for
d=1
, one can consider
u=e^{X}v
and multiply the equation by
e^{X}
to formally get

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=-(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla)v+e^{2X}(\xi-\Delta X-|\nabla X|^{2})v-e^{X}f(e^{X}v)

(3.3)

with initial data
v_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
d\in\{2,3\}
, this falls in the range of singular dispersive SPDEs and requires a probabilistic renormalization procedure. Indeed,
\nabla X
is a distribution of Hölder regularity
\gamma<1-\frac{d}{2}
thus its square
|\nabla X|^{2}
is ill-defined. The equation makes sense for
\xi_{\varepsilon}
a smooth regularization of the noise and the renormalization procedure amounts to the construction

Y=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\xi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta X_{\varepsilon}-|\nabla X_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-c_{\varepsilon})=:\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}Y_{\varepsilon}

(3.4)

for a suitable random field
Y
and
c_{\varepsilon}
a diverging constant. This idea was used on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
by Debussche and Weber
[
8
]
with
\Delta X=\xi
up to the mode zero, see also
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
30
,
31
]
for similar constructions on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. On full space, this choice induces a growth at infinity of the random field
Y
which causes a loss in localization at positive time with respect to the initial data. We consider a different random field
X
using a spatial localization, see Appendix
B
where we prove the convergence of
Y_{\varepsilon}
to a limit
Y
in
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{\frac{2-d}{2}-\kappa}
for any
\delta,\kappa>0
. The main idea is that one can cancel the local irregularity of the rough distributions without including the growth at infinity in the field
X
. The equation

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=-(\nabla e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla)v_{\varepsilon}+e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}(\xi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta X_{\varepsilon}-|\nabla X_{\varepsilon}|^{2})v_{\varepsilon}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}f(e^{X_{\varepsilon}}v)

(3.5)

then converges as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
to

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=-(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla)v+e^{2X}Yv-e^{X}f(e^{X}v)

(3.6)

where
e^{2X}Yv
makes sense for
v\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. If one multiply the initial equation by
e^{-X}
instead of
e^{X}
, this gives the formal equation

i\partial_{t}v=-\Delta v-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+Yv-e^{-X}f(e^{X}v)

(3.7)

where the term
\nabla X\cdot\nabla v
is ill-defined for
v\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is the main reason for which all previous work considered strong solutions with a loss of regularization, see for example the remark following Corollary
3.2
in
[
8
]
even on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. With our formulation, one can then consider weak solutions with

\big\langle ie^{2X}v_{t}-ie^{2X}v_{0},\varphi\big\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle-(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla)v_{s}+e^{2X}Yv_{s}+e^{X}f(e^{X}v_{s}),\varphi\big\rangle\mathrm{d}s

(3.8)

for any test function
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For the equation on a compact domain such as
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, this is natural as one considers initial data in the form domain of the random Anderson operator which gives solutions of the equation in its dual, the equation is tested against functions themselves in the form domain. This is not the case on full space due to the random growth of the potential at infinity thus our notation of energy solutions depends on the choice of
X
and the initial growth
0<\mu\leq 1
.

Definition 3.1
.

A function
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T],\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1})
is called an energy solution to (
3.1
) if

\big\langle ie^{2X}v(t)-ie^{2X}v_{0},\varphi\big\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle-(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla)v(s)+e^{2X}Yv(s)+e^{X}f(e^{X}v(s)),\varphi\big\rangle\mathrm{d}s

(3.9)

for any test function
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and almost all
t\in[0,T]
where
v(t)=e^{-X}u(t)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, one can consider the renormalized equation

i\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+u_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{\varepsilon}-c_{\varepsilon})+f(u_{\varepsilon})

(3.10)

for which existence of global energy solutions follows from classical theory, for example with a spatial trunction and compactness argument. In this section, we prove existence of energy solutions using uniform bounds on
v_{\varepsilon}(t)=e^{-X_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}(t)
and a compactness argument. We consider the (
NLS
) equation in two and three dimensions as well as the (
Hartree
) equation in three dimensions.

3.1 –  Existence in two dimensions

We consider the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to

i\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+u_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{\varepsilon}-c_{\varepsilon})+|u_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}u_{\varepsilon}

(3.11)

with initial data
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=e^{X_{\varepsilon}-X}u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
where
c_{\varepsilon}
is a logarithmic diverging constant defined in Appendix
B
. This choice of initial data is to have regular initial condition that converges to
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}
, this is often refered to as well-prepared initial data in this context. We consider
u_{\varepsilon}(t)=e^{X_{\varepsilon}}v_{\varepsilon}(t)
to get

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=-(\nabla e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla)v_{\varepsilon}+e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}+e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}

(3.12)

with initial data
v_{\varepsilon}(0)=e^{-X}u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
and

Y_{\varepsilon}=\xi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta X_{\varepsilon}-|\nabla X_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-c_{\varepsilon}

(3.13)

which converges to
Y
in
C_{-\delta}^{-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any
\kappa,\delta>0
. Using uniform bounds on
Y_{\varepsilon}
and conservation of energy, we prove the following result with
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}=e^{X}\big(H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}\big)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
0<\mu\leq 1
. For any initial data
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}
, there exists energy solutions in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1})
to (
NLS
) on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for
m\geq 1
. For any time interval
I
containing
0
, we have the bound

\sup_{t\in I}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}\lesssim(1+|I|)\|u_{0}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}

(3.14)

with a constant that depends only on the noise.

Proof :

The equation

i\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+(\xi_{\varepsilon}-c_{\varepsilon})u_{\varepsilon}+|u_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}u_{\varepsilon}

(3.15)

with
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=e^{X_{\varepsilon}-X}u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
has conserved mass
\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}
and energy

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}(\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)-c_{\varepsilon})\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{m+1}\mathrm{d}x

(3.16)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. For
v_{\varepsilon}(t)=e^{X_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}(t)
with
v_{0}=e^{-X}u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
, we get conserved modified mass

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v_{0}|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.17)

which is uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon>0
as
e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}
converges to
e^{-2X}
in
L^{\infty}
. For the energy, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}

\displaystyle|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}Y_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.18)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{m+1}e^{-(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.19)

which is constant in time. For
v_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
with
\mu>0
, the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)
is also uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon>0
since
Y_{\varepsilon}
converges to
Y
in
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
\delta,\kappa>0
. The equation being defocusing, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}Y_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.20)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{2\delta}^{\kappa,1}}\|Y_{\varepsilon}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}\|_{\mathcal{C}_{-2\delta}^{-\kappa}}

(3.21)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H_{2\delta}^{2\kappa}}^{2}

(3.22)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\frac{2\delta}{1-2\kappa}}^{2}}^{2-4\kappa}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{4\kappa}

(3.23)

using that
Y_{\varepsilon}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{C}_{-2\delta}^{-\kappa}
for any
\delta,\kappa>0
with using duality, product and interpolation of Besov spaces with Lemmas
A.3
,
A.4
and
A.5
. Since
e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon>0
in
L^{\infty}
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+z\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\frac{2\delta}{1-2\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}+z^{-\frac{1-2\kappa}{2\kappa}}c_{2\kappa}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

(3.24)

for any
z>0
using the bound
x^{1-\theta}y^{\theta}\leq zx+c_{\theta}z^{-\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}y
for
x,y>0
and
c_{\theta}=(1-\theta)^{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}\theta
with
\theta\in(0,1)
which is a scaled version of Lemma I.3.1 from
[
34
]
. It remains to bound the weighted norm. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)e^{-X_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle x\rangle^{2\eta}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\overline{v_{\varepsilon}}(t,x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle x\rangle^{2\eta}\big(\Delta v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-2\nabla X_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\big)\overline{v_{\varepsilon}}(t,x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\Big)\mathrm{d}x

(3.26)

\displaystyle=-\text{Im}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\eta x}{\langle x\rangle^{2\eta-2}}v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\nabla\overline{v}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(3.27)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{2\eta-1}^{2}}

(3.28)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v_{0}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(3.29)

for
2\eta-1\leq 0
using the conservation of modified mass. We get

\sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}+|I|\cdot\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(3.30)

for any finite interval
I
containing
0
with
\eta\leq\mu\wedge\frac{1}{2}
thus the energy bound with
z>0
small enough yields

\sup_{t\in I}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}^{2}+|I|\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}.

(3.31)

Indeed, one can put some weight on the gradient to get up to
0<\mu\leq 1
using iterated Gronwall’s lemma as in one dimensions. We get

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\ \sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim(1+|I|)\|v_{0}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}

(3.32)

for any finite interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
which is uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon>0
. Using classical compactness argument as explained in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we get that there exists
v\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1})
such that
v_{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
at almost all time
t\in\mathbb{R}
and to
v
in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},H_{\delta}^{\sigma})
for any
\delta<\mu
and
\sigma<1
up to extraction. Using this convergence in the equation

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=-\nabla e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}+e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}+e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}

(3.33)

gives that
v
sastifies

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=-\nabla e^{2X}\nabla v+e^{2X}Yv+e^{(m+1)X}|v|^{m-1}v

(3.34)

using also that
X_{\varepsilon}
converges to
X
and
Y_{\varepsilon}
to
Y
respectively in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{1-\kappa}
and
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\kappa}
hence
v
is an energy solution in the sense of Definition
3.1
. For example, the nonlinear term is controlled in
H_{\delta}^{-1}
using the embeddings

H_{\delta}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hookrightarrow L_{\delta}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hookrightarrow H_{\delta}^{-1}

(3.35)

for
\sigma\geq 1-\frac{2}{m}
and
e^{(m+1)X}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

\square

Remark 3.3
.

In the previous proof, we consider the regularized equation

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=-\nabla e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}+e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}+e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}

(3.36)

where both sides converges in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. One can also consider the equation

i\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=-e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}+Y_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}+e^{(m-1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}

(3.37)

however this does not converges a priori since
e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}
converges only in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In two dimensions, the sum of the regularity is
-2\kappa<0
and one can consider this equation as soon as one propagate any regularity better than
H_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. This is the main reason that motivated Debussche and Weber to consider strong solutions in
[
8
]
and all previous works.

3.2 –  Existence in three dimensions

With our notation, the computations are similar in three dimensions with different regularity exponent. The difficulty here lies in the construction of
X
and
Y
such as

Y=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\xi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta X_{\varepsilon}-|\nabla X_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-c_{\varepsilon})

(3.38)

in
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
while
X_{\varepsilon}
converges to
X
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
for any
\delta,\kappa>0
, this is done in Appendix
B
. We also consider the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to

i\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+u_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{\varepsilon}-c_{\varepsilon})+|u_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}u_{\varepsilon}

(3.39)

with well-prepared initial data
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=e^{X_{\varepsilon}-X}u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
. For
u_{\varepsilon}=e^{X_{\varepsilon}}v_{\varepsilon}
, we get

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=-\nabla e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}+e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}+e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}

(3.40)

with initial data
v_{\varepsilon}(0)=v_{0}=e^{-X}u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
. Using uniform bounds on
Y_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and conservation of energy, we prove the following result with
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}=e^{X}\big(H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}\big)
with a condition on
m\geq 1
due to the regularity of
Y
and the Besov embedding in three dimensions.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
0<\mu\leq 1
. For any initial data
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}
, there exists energy solutions in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1})
to (
NLS
) on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
1\leq m<6
. For any time interval
I
containing
0
, we have the bound

\sup_{t\in I}\|u(t)\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}\lesssim(1+|I|)\|u_{0}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}

(3.41)

with a constant that depends only on the noise.

Proof :

We follow the proof for the same result on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and emphasize the difference in the regularity exponents. We have the same convervation of modified mass

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{0}|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.42)

and modified energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}Y_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.43)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{m+1}e^{-(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.44)

which are finite at
t=0
since
v_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}
with
\mu>0
. In particular,
Y_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
hence
v\in H^{1}
is enough for all the term to be well-defined. We get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}Y_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.45)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{2\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+\kappa,1}}\|e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathcal{C}_{-2\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}}

(3.46)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H_{2\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+2\kappa}}^{2}

(3.47)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\frac{4\delta}{1-4\kappa}}^{2}}^{1-4\kappa}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{1+4\kappa}

(3.48)

using that
e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{C}_{-2\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
for any
\delta,\kappa>0
with Lemmas
A.3
,
A.4
and
A.5
. Since
e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}
is uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon>0
in
L^{\infty}
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(0)+z\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\frac{4\delta}{1-4\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}+z^{-\frac{1-4\kappa}{1+4\kappa}}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

(3.49)

for any
z>0
as in two dimensions and it remains to bound the weighted norm. We have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)e^{-X_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|v_{0}e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\|_{L^{2}}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(3.50)

for
2\eta-1\leq 0
using the conservation of modified mass hence

\sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}+|I|\sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}

(3.51)

for any finite interval
I
containing
0
with
\eta\leq\mu\wedge\frac{1}{2}
. Again, the energy bound then yields

\sup_{t\in I}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}+|I|\sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{\frac{3}{2}+2\kappa}

(3.52)

which gives the bound

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\ \sup_{t\in I}\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim(1+|I|)\|v_{0}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}}

(3.53)

for any finite interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
uniform with respect to
\varepsilon>0
. The compactness argument gives a solution
v\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1})
such that
v_{\varepsilon}
converges to
v
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},H_{\delta}^{\sigma})
for any
\delta<\mu
and
\sigma<1
up to extraction. It is a solution to

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=-\nabla e^{2X}\nabla v+e^{2X}Yv+e^{(m+1)X}|v|^{m-1}v

(3.54)

using also that
X_{\varepsilon}
converges to
X
and
Y_{\varepsilon}
to
Y
respectively in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
and
\mathcal{C}_{-\frac{1}{2}-\delta}^{-\kappa}
hence
v
is an energy solution in the sense of Definition
3.1
. The main difference here is the control of the nonlinear term which imposes a restriction on
m\geq 1
. In three dimension, we have the embeddings

H_{\delta}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow L_{\delta}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(3.55)

for
\sigma\geq\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{p}
, that is
p\leq\frac{6}{3-2\sigma}
. Since
\sigma<1
, we can control
L_{\delta}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with
p<6
hence the restriction.

\square

We now prove a similar theorem for (
Hartree
) equation.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
0<\mu\leq 1
. For any initial data
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1}
, there exists energy solutions in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{1})
to (
Hartree
) on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
\beta>0
.

Proof :

The difference with the previous proof is the control of the nonlinearity

u_{\varepsilon}\cdot V_{\beta}*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}

(3.56)

for
\varepsilon>0
. The energy is

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}(\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)-c_{\varepsilon})\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}(V_{\beta}*|u_{\varepsilon}(t)|^{2})(x)\mathrm{d}x

(3.57)

where
V
is symmetric thus the only difference in the modified energy is the term

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}(V_{\beta}*|e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}v_{\varepsilon}(t)|^{2})(x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(3.58)

which is nonnegative since
V\geq 0
. Following the previous proof, we only need to bound this at
t=0
using
v_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Using
e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}\in L^{\infty}
is uniformly bounded, we have

\displaystyle\|V_{\beta}*|e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}|^{2}\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|V_{\beta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}|^{2}\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

(3.59)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|V_{\beta}\|_{W^{1,\beta}}\|v_{0}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(3.60)

using Young inequality for convolution and
W^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for any
\beta>0
. We get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{0}|^{2}(V_{\beta}*|e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}|^{2})(x)e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}}^{2}\|V_{\beta}*|e^{-2X_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}|^{2}\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

(3.61)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{3}\|V_{\beta}\|_{W^{1,\beta}}

(3.62)

which completes the proof.

\square

4 –  Strong solutions and Strichartz inequalities

This section deals with the linear equation, propagation of regularity for strong solutions is obtained which is then used in our proof of Strichartz inequalities.

4.1 –  Propagation of regularity

The previous construction also gives existence of solutions to the linear equation

i\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{H}u

(4.1)

interpreted as

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v

(4.2)

with the new variable
u=e^{X}v
for
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
0<\mu\leq 1
. For the linear equation, one can directly obtain uniqueness which gives a construction of the associated linear flow.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
0<\mu\leq 1
. There exists a unique solution in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
to the linear equation (
4.2
) for
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Denoting as
S_{t}
the associated flow, it satisfies

\|S_{t}v_{0}\|_{L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(4.3)

for any
\sigma\in[0,1]
.

Proof :

The existence follows from the same bounds as for the nonlinear equation for
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For uniqueness, it is enough to prove that the solution is
0
for
v_{0}=0
by linearity. It follows from the computation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,x)|^{2}e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}v(t,x)\overline{v(t,x)}e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(4.4)

\displaystyle=-\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ie^{X}(\mathcal{H}e^{X}v)(t,x)\overline{v(t,x)}\mathrm{d}x

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(i\nabla e^{2X}\nabla v)(t,x)+Y(x)v(t,x)\big)\overline{v(t,x)}\mathrm{d}x

(4.6)

\displaystyle=0

(4.7)

which is nothing more that the conservation of the modified mass. Since there exists a unique solution for any
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any horizon time
T>0
, we denote as
S_{t}
the associated flow. The conservation of modified mass implies that the flow satisfies

\|S_{t}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x)}=\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x)}

(4.8)

for any
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which is a dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since the usual norm on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is equivalent to the modified mass,
S_{t}
extends uniquely as a continuous operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Considering
\mu=1
, we also proved the bound

\|S_{t}v_{0}\|_{L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(4.9)

hence interpolation, for example see
[
2
]
Lemma 3, gives

\|S_{t}v_{0}\|_{L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\sigma}^{2}\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(4.10)

for any
\sigma\in[0,1]
.

\square

Remark 4.2
.

(i)
We have a bound where the exponent weight has to be precisely the regularity exponent, this comes from the interpolation between
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which is the energy space of the harmonic oscillator. We did not find a precise interpolation between
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
L_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for general
\mu\in(0,1)
while we believe that it follows from the same line of reasoning. It would give the more general bound

\|S_{t}v_{0}\|_{L_{\sigma\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\sigma\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(4.11)

for
0<\mu\leq\sigma\leq 1
hence remove the condition
\mu\geq\gamma
in Theorem
4.6
.

(ii)
The flow is also continuous from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself by extension of a continuous linear operator. This naturally gives a definition of the Anderson Hamiltonian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\leq 3
as the generator of the strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group
(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
. Hsu and Labbé
[
19
]
recently used the heat semigroup to construct this operator. In their case, the domain of the semigroup depends on
t>0
while this is not the case for the Schrödinger group defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. See also
[
32
]
for a direct construction of the operator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
using paracontrolled calculus.

We now prove propagation of regularity beyond energy solution for the linear equation in our weighted framework with the space

L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\Gamma H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(4.12)

for any
0<\mu\leq 1
where
\Gamma
is defined using paracontrolled calculus in Appendix
C
. It is implicitly defined by the paracontrolled expansion

\Gamma u^{\sharp}=\mathsf{P}_{\nabla\Gamma u^{\sharp}}\nabla Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{\Gamma u^{\sharp}}Z_{2}+u^{\sharp}

(4.13)

for well chosen random field
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
for
u^{\sharp}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the construction being harder in three dimensions. The map
\Gamma
can be extended to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and we have the following Proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
\mu\in(0,1]
, the flow
S_{t}
is continuous from
L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\Gamma H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself. In particular, we have

\|S_{t}v_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\Gamma H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\Gamma H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(4.14)

for any
\sigma\in[1,2]
.

Proof :

For
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}\cap\Gamma H^{2}\subset L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{1}
, there exists a unique solution
v
to the linear equation

ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v=e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v

(4.15)

with
v(0)=v_{0}
. Then
w=\partial_{t}v
satisfies the same linear equation with

w(0)=e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v_{0}\in L_{\mu^{\prime}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(4.16)

for any
\mu^{\prime}<\mu
using that
v_{0}\in L_{\mu}^{2}\cap\Gamma H^{2}
. Since
w_{t}=S_{t}w(0)
, we get

\|w_{t}\|_{L_{\mu^{\prime}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}\cap\Gamma H^{2}}

(4.17)

hence

\|e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v_{t}\|_{L_{\mu^{\prime}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}\cap\Gamma H^{2}}

(4.18)

using the equation on
v
. This gives

\|v_{t}\|_{L_{\mu^{\prime}}^{2}\cap\Gamma H^{2}}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}\cap\Gamma H^{2}}

(4.19)

using Corollary
C.4
thus the flow

S_{t}^{\sharp}:=\Gamma^{-1}S_{t}\Gamma

(4.20)

is continuous from
L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself for
\sigma\in\{1,2\}
and interpolation completes the proof.

\square

Remark 4.4
.

One can prove that
\Gamma H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
\sigma<2
in two dimensions and
\sigma<\frac{3}{2}
in three dimensions hence propagation of regularity beyond energy solutions for the equation on
v
. For
d=2
, we get
v_{t}\in H^{1+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
v_{0}\in H^{1+\gamma}
and any
\gamma\in(0,1)
. We get that
e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v\in H^{-1+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
v\in H^{1+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
hence its products with
e^{-2X}\in\mathcal{C}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is well-defined. This gives the equation

i\partial_{t}v=e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v

(4.21)

which involves the gradient term
\nabla X\cdot\nabla v
since
\gamma>0
. For the linear equation, this recovers the notion of solution of Debussche and Weber
[
8
]
on the torus for example. In three dimensions, we have propagation of
H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
\sigma<\frac{3}{2}
while
e^{-2X}\in\mathcal{C}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}
hence this does not work. Using the paracontrolled calculus, one can consider the conjugated flow

S_{t}^{\sharp}=\Gamma^{-1}S_{t}\Gamma

(4.22)

which is then continuous from
L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself for any
\sigma\in[1,2]
and
0<\mu\leq 1
and we get the equation

i\partial_{t}v^{\sharp}=\Gamma^{-1}e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}\Gamma v^{\sharp}

(4.23)

with
v=\Gamma v^{\sharp}
for
\sigma\in(\frac{3}{2},2)
. In particular,
S_{t}^{\sharp}
is the flow associated to
\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}=(\Gamma e^{X})^{-1}\mathcal{H}(e^{X}\Gamma)
.

4.2 –  Strichartz inequalities

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
and
u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
be two solutions to (
3.1
) with a regularized renormalized noise and consider
w_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
. We get

i\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}+f(u_{\varepsilon})-f(u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})

(4.24)

with
f
the polynomial or Hartree nonlinearity. Multiply the equation by
\overline{w_{\varepsilon}}
, integrate in time and take the imaginary part gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\text{Im}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(u_{\varepsilon})-f(u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})\big)(t,x)\overline{w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)}\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(4.25)

where one has to specify the nonlinearity at this point. For example in the cubic case, we have

f(u_{\varepsilon})-f(u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})=|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}u_{\varepsilon}-|u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|^{2}u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}

(4.26)

which gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}+|u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t,x)|^{2})|w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(4.27)

hence with Grönwall’s lemma, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w_{\varepsilon}(T,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim e^{c\int_{0}^{T}(\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2})\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{0}(x)-u_{0}^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

(4.28)

for any
T>0
. From this estimate, uniqueness follows from a bound on
u
and
u^{\prime}
in
L^{2}([0,T],L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for the cubic nonlinearity. While conservation of mass yields solutions in
L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, the goal is to trade time integrability for spatial integrability. In particular, the initial data does not belong to
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for energy solutions with
d\in\{2,3\}
and this requires dispersive properties of the solution. This is exactly encoded in Strichartz inequalities with the following result from Keel and Tao
[
20
]
in the deterministic case.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
T>0
and
\mu\in(0,1)
. Let
(p_{1},q_{2})
and
(p_{2},q_{2})
two Strichartz pairs, that is

\frac{2}{p_{i}}+\frac{d}{q_{i}}=\frac{d}{2}

(4.29)

for
i\in\{1,2\}
with
(p_{i},q_{i})\neq(2,\infty)
in two dimensions. Let
w
be a solution to

i\partial_{t}w=-\Delta w+F

(4.30)

with initial data
w_{0}
. Then the solution satisfies the bound

\|w\|_{L^{p}([0,T],L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\lesssim\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|F\|_{L^{p_{2}^{\prime}}([0,T],L^{q_{2}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}

(4.31)

where
p_{2}^{\prime}
and
q_{2}^{\prime}
denotes the conjugated exponent of
p_{2}
and
q_{2}
.

In this section, we obtain Strichartz inequalities for (
3.1
) in the linear case
f=0
using this result for the deterministic equation. The proof relies on the transformed operator

\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}=(e^{X}\Gamma)^{-1}\mathcal{H}(e^{X}\Gamma)

(4.32)

introduced in Appendix
C
with Proposition
C.3
which gives

\|(\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}+\Delta)v^{\sharp}\|_{H^{\gamma-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v^{\sharp}\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(4.33)

5 –  Local well-posedness for low regularity initial data

In this section, the nonlinear equation

i\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{H}u+f(u)

. For

and initial data

\displaystyle ie^{2X}\partial_{t}v_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}S_{t}v_{0}+e^{X}f(e^{X}v_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}S_{t-s}e^{-X}f(e^{X}v_{s})\mathrm{d}s

hence it is an energy solutions for

(5.7)

\|\Phi(v)\|_{L^{2}([0,T],W^{\gamma,6})}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{\frac{3}{4}+\gamma+\kappa}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}\cdot V_{\beta}*|e^{X}v_{s}|^{2}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}\cap H^{\frac{3}{4}+\gamma+\kappa}}\mathrm{d}t

(5.39)

for
\mu=\frac{3}{4}+\gamma+\kappa<1
which is bounded as before for
\sigma=\frac{3}{4}+\gamma+\kappa
. Following the previous proof, one can find a contraction using these bounds to obtain the following Theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\sigma=\frac{3}{4}+\gamma
with
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
. For any
v_{0}\in L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

\beta>1-2\gamma,

(5.40)

there exists a time
T>0
until which there exists a unique solution

v\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap L^{2}([0,T],W^{\gamma,6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

(5.41)

to the mild formulation

v_{t}=S_{t}v_{0}-i\int_{0}^{t}S_{t-s}v_{s}\cdot V_{\beta}*|e^{X}v_{s}|^{2}\mathrm{d}s

(5.42)

where
V_{\beta}\in W^{\beta,1}
. Moreover, the solution depends continuously on the initial data
v_{0}\in L_{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In particular, there exists a unique mild solution for
\sigma_{\beta}<\sigma<1
with

\sigma_{\beta}=\frac{5}{4}-\frac{\beta}{2}

(5.43)

for any
\beta>\frac{1}{2}
.

5.3 –  Uniqueness of energy solutions

Our definition of energy solutions is a priori different than mild solutions, one has that a mild solution in the energy space is indeed an energy solution however the opposite is not immediate. Uniqueness of energy solutions direclty follows from Strichartz inequalities with Gronwall’s lemma. In two dimensions, we consider any polynomial nonlinearity
f(u)=|u|^{m-1}u
with
m\geq 1
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
0<\mu\leq 1
and
m\geq 1
. For
u_{0}\in e^{X}(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, consider the unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to the regularized equation associated to (
NLS
). Then there exists a unique function
u\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},e^{X}(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})))
such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ \sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\|e^{-X_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}(t)-e^{-X}u(t)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=0

(5.44)

for any
T>0
.

Proof :

Consider the unique solution to the regularized equation

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}=e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}}v_{\varepsilon}+e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}

(5.45)

with
v_{\varepsilon}(0)=v_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. To complete the proof, we need to show that
v_{\varepsilon}
has a unique limit as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
. Let
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
and consider
w=u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
. We have

ie^{2X_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}w=e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}}w+(e^{X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}})v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}-e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}

(5.46)

which we multply by
\overline{w}(t,x)
and integrate in space. We get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|w(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}}v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})(t,x)\overline{w}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x

(5.47)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon}-e^{(m+1)X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}|^{m-1}v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})(t,x)\overline{w}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(5.48)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}})v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\|w\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

(5.49)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(1+\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{m-1}+\|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{m-1}\Big)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|w(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

(5.50)

hence Gronwall’s lemma

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|w(T,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\big(M_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|w(0,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\big)e^{c\int_{0}^{T}(\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{m-1}+\|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{m-1})\mathrm{d}t}

(5.51)

for any
T>0
and

M_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\|(e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}})v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}(\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})})

(5.52)

since
\|w\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Since

e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}}=-\nabla(e^{2X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}-e^{2X_{\varepsilon}})\nabla+e^{2X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}Y_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-e^{2X_{\varepsilon}}Y_{\varepsilon}

(5.53)

and
v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
being uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we get that
(e^{X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}e^{X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{\varepsilon}})v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
converges to
0
in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
goes to
0
thus
M_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}
goes to
0
. With our Strichartz inequalities,
v_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{p}([0,T],L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
thus and we have

w(0)=(e^{X_{\varepsilon}}-e^{X_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}})v_{0}

(5.54)

which converges to
0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
goes to
0
. This proves that
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and complete the proof.

\square

Hartree

Appendix A
Function spaces

Since the law of the white noise is invariant by translation, it does not decay at infinity. To deal with this, we work in weighted Lebesgue and Besov spaces. For any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we consider

\|u\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x\rangle^{\mu}|f(x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}

with
\langle x\rangle=\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}
. An important tool is the Paley-Littlewood decomposition

u=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Delta_{n}u

where

\big(\Delta_{n}u\big)(x):=2^{d(n-1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi\big(2^{n-1}(x-y)\big)u(y)\mathrm{d}y

with
\chi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathrm{supp}\ \widehat{\chi}\subset\{\frac{1}{2}\leq|z|\leq 2\}
for
n\geq 1
and

\big(\Delta_{0}u\big)(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi_{0}(x-y)u(y)\mathrm{d}y

with
\chi_{0}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathrm{supp}\ \widehat{\chi}_{0}\subset\{|z|\leq 1\}
. Most of the following definitions and properties can be found in
[
9
, Section 4]
. See also
[
8
]
and references therein, in particular the book
[
1
]
.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\mu,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty)
. The weighted Besov space
\mathcal{B}_{p,q,\mu}^{\alpha}
is the set of distribution
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,q,\mu}^{\alpha}}:=\Big(\sum_{n\geq 0}2^{\alpha nq}\|\Delta_{n}u\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty.

For
p=q=2
, one recovers the usual weighted Sobolev spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{B}_{2,2,\mu}^{\alpha}
with

\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|(\mathscr{F}^{-1}\langle\cdot\rangle^{\alpha}\mathscr{F})u\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

We also denote the case
p=q=\infty
as
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{B}_{\infty,\infty,\mu}^{\alpha}
which corresponds to the usual weighted Hölder spaces for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending on the spaces parameters such that

C_{1}\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{\mu}u\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,q,0}^{\alpha}}\leq\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,q,\mu}^{\alpha}}\leq C_{2}\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{\mu}u\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,q,0}^{\alpha}},

and
\mathcal{B}_{p,q,0}^{\alpha}
corresponds to the usual Besov spaces hence weighted Besov spaces satisfy the following embeddings.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\in[1,\infty]
and
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{1}\leq p_{2},q_{1}\leq q_{2}
and
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}
. For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, one has the Besov embeddings

\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p_{1},q_{1},\mu_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{B}^{\alpha-d\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right)}_{p_{2},q_{2},\mu_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

as well as the Sobolev embeddings

\forall\alpha\geq\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{p},\quad\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

for
p\in[2,\infty]
. Finally, the embedding

\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is compact for
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}
and
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}
.

The following lemma is a useful interpolation estimate that we shall use in the following, and which can be found in
[
28
, Theorem 3.8]
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
p_{0},p_{1},q_{0},q_{1}\in[1,\infty]
and
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
p,q,\alpha,\mu
such that

\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1-\theta}{p_{0}}+\frac{\theta}{p_{1}},\quad\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1-\theta}{q_{0}}+\frac{\theta}{q_{1}},

and

\alpha=(1-\theta)\alpha_{0}+\theta\alpha_{1},\quad\mu=(1-\theta)\mu_{0}+\theta\mu_{1}

for
\theta\in[0,1]
. Then there exists
C>0
such that

\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q,\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha_{0}}_{p_{0},q_{0},\mu_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{1-\theta}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha_{1}}_{p_{1},q_{1},\mu_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{\theta}.

In particular, we have

\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\leq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{0}}^{\alpha_{0}}}^{1-\theta}\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}}^{\theta}.

In general, one can only multiply a distribution by a test function. The following lemma gives a product rule in weighted Besov space, as a generalisation of Young condition, see
[
29
, Section 2.8.2]
.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}>0
. Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\mu=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
and
p_{1},p_{2}\in[1,\infty]
with
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}
. Then for any
\kappa>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|uv\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,p,\mu}^{\alpha-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p_{1},p_{1},\mu_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p_{2},p_{2},\mu_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

where
\alpha=\min(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
. In the case where
p=p_{1}
, one can take
\kappa=0
. We also have

\|uv\|_{\mathcal{W}^{\alpha,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{W}^{\alpha,p_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{L^{p_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+C\|u\|_{L^{p_{1}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{\mathcal{W}^{\alpha,p_{2}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

for
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}=\frac{1}{p_{1}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}^{\prime}}
.

As we will control energy associated to dispersive PDEs, the following duality result from in weighted Besov spaces from
[
29
, Theorem 2.11.2]
will be useful.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\alpha,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)v(x)\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,q,\mu}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},-\mu}^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
and
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1
for any
u,v\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Appendix B
Stochastic bounds and renormalization

The Gaussian spatial white noise
\xi
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}
for any
\kappa,\delta>0
, that is spatial growth at infinity of order
\langle x\rangle^{\delta}
. We consider

X_{1}:=(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{2-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}

(B.1)

such that
\nabla X_{1}
is a distribution for
d\in\{2,3\}
. Following usual renormalization, its Wick square can be defined as follows given
X_{1,\varepsilon}=(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi_{\varepsilon}
where
(\xi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is a regularization of the noise, see for example
[
18
]
.

Proposition B.1
.

There exists a distribution
\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{2-d-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\big(|\nabla X_{1,\varepsilon}|^{2}-c_{\varepsilon}^{1}\big)

(B.2)

in probability in the space
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{2-d-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
\kappa,\delta>0
with
c_{\varepsilon}^{1}=\mathbb{E}\big[|\nabla X_{1,\varepsilon}|^{2}\big]
a diverging constant.

As introduced by Gubinelli and Hofmanovà
[
12
]
, one can consider the decomposition

f=\mathscr{U}_{\leq}f+\mathscr{U}_{>}f

(B.3)

for any distribution
f
where
\mathscr{U}_{\leq}f
and
\mathscr{U}_{>}f
are projected respectively on low or high frequency according to an inhomogeneous spatial decomposition. Precisely, we have

\mathscr{U}_{\leq}f=\sum_{k\geq 0}w_{k}\Delta_{\leq L_{k}}f\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{U}_{>}f=\sum_{k\geq 0}w_{k}\Delta_{>L_{k}}f

(B.4)

given
(w_{k})_{k\geq 0}
a smooth partition of the unity,
(L_{k})_{k\geq 0}\subset[-1,+\infty)
a suitable sequence with
\Delta_{\leq L}
and
\Delta_{>L}
the sum of the Paley-Littlewood projectors
\Delta_{j}
respectively for
j\leq L
and
j>L
. The main properties for the projection used here are recalled in the following result, that is Lemma
2.4
from
[
12
]
. To obtain the following lemma, consider with their notation

\alpha=-\alpha,\quad\gamma=\delta_{1},\quad\delta=\delta_{2},\quad a=\frac{\mu}{m}-\delta,\quad b=-\frac{\mu}{m}-\gamma

(B.5)

with
\rho(x)=\langle x\rangle^{\mu}
.

Lemma B.2
.

For any
\alpha,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and

\|\mathscr{U}_{\leq}f\|_{\mathcal{C}_{-\mu-m\delta_{1}}^{\alpha+\delta_{1}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}_{-\mu}^{\alpha}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathscr{U}_{>}f\|_{\mathcal{C}_{-\mu+m\delta_{2}}^{\alpha-\delta_{2}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}_{-\mu}^{\alpha}}

(B.6)

where the constant depends on the parameters

.

where all the spatial weight are abitrary small for
\mu>0
small and fixed

\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}=|\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}|^{2}+2\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{1}^{>}+\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}

for

There exists distributions

|\nabla(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})|^{2}=|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}+2\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla(X_{2}+X_{3})+|\nabla X_{2}|^{2}+2\nabla X_{2}\cdot\nabla X_{3}+|\nabla X_{3}|^{2}

X_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. With the renormalization procedure, we have the Wick product

\mathbf{:}|\nabla(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})|^{2}\mathbf{:}=\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}+2\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla X_{2}\mathbf{:}+2\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla X_{3}+\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{2}|^{2}\mathbf{:}+2\nabla X_{2}\cdot\nabla X_{3}+|\nabla X_{3}|^{2}

(B.21)

where we also need to add a localization operator. For the first term, we already have

\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}=|\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}|^{2}+2\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{1}^{>}+\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}

(B.22)

where the first two terms are well-defined since

X_{1}^{\leq}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\mu(1+\frac{1+4\kappa}{\kappa})}^{\frac{3}{2}+3\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\quad\text{and}\quad X_{1}^{>}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{\frac{1}{2}-2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(B.23)

hence this gives a definition of
\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}
without additional renormalization procedure. For the second term we have

\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla X_{2}\mathbf{:}=\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}+\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}+\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}+\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}\mathbf{:}

(B.24)

where again only the last term is singular. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{-\frac{1}{2}-2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(B.25)

\displaystyle\nabla X_{1}^{>}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{C}_{-\mu(1+2\frac{1+4\kappa}{\kappa})}^{\frac{1}{2}+3\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(B.26)

\displaystyle\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{-2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(B.27)

\displaystyle\nabla X_{2}^{>}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{C}_{-\mu(1+2\frac{\frac{1}{2}+4\kappa}{\kappa})}^{\frac{1}{2}+3\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(B.28)

for a suitable choice of parameters with Lemma
B.2
. Finally, we have

\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{2}|^{2}\mathbf{:}=|\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}|^{2}+2\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}+\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{2}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}

(B.29)

which is also well-defined.

Appendix C
Construction and transformation of
\mathcal{H}

The Anderson Hamiltonian is the operator

\mathcal{H}=-\Delta+\xi

(C.1)

where the white noise
\xi
belongs
\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
\kappa,\delta>0
. We do not describe the spectral properties of this random operator, see
[
19
,
32
]
and references therein as well as Remark
4.2
. This operator is a singular stochastic operator for
d\in\{2,3\}
, we use an exponential transform to study the associated equation. Given
u=e^{X}v
, we formally have

e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v=-(\nabla e^{2X}\nabla)v+e^{2X}(\xi-\Delta X-|\nabla X|^{2})v

(C.2)

which should be better behaved than
\mathcal{H}
given a random field
X
such that
\xi-\Delta X
is smoother than
\xi
. This requires
X
to be locally of Hölder regularity
2-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa
hence the square
|\nabla X|^{2}
is ill-defined for
d\geq 2
, this falls in the range of singular dispersive SPDEs and requires a probabilistic renormalization procedure. The operator makes sense for
\xi_{\varepsilon}
a smooth regularization of the noise and we want to construct
X_{\varepsilon}
such that

Y_{\varepsilon}:=\xi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta X_{\varepsilon}-|\nabla X_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-c_{\varepsilon}

(C.3)

converges to a limit
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{1-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}
with
c_{\varepsilon}
a diverging quantity, this is the renormalization procedure explained above. We first describe the exponential transform in two and three dimensions and then introduce a paracontrolled expansion to obtain a finer description. In both cases, we have

e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v=-\nabla(e^{2X}\nabla v)+e^{2X}Yv

(C.4)

for any
v\in H_{\delta}^{\frac{d}{2}-1+2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
where the definition of
X
and
Y
is more involved in three dimensions.

Construction in 2D.
In two dimensions, we consider

X_{1}=-(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(C.5)

and

X:=\mathscr{U}_{>}X_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{1-2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(C.6)

for any
\delta,\kappa>0
. The choice
X_{1}
is natural to ensure that
\Delta X_{1}-\xi
is smooth while the projection
\mathscr{U}_{>}
is used to localize in space the transformation. The term
|\nabla X|^{2}
is defined as a Wick product and we have

\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:}=\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}-2\nabla\mathscr{U}_{\leq}X_{1}\cdot\nabla X-|\mathscr{U}_{\leq}X_{1}|^{2}

(C.7)

as explained above. We also have

\displaystyle\xi-\Delta X

\displaystyle=\xi+\Delta\mathscr{U}_{>}(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi

(C.8)

\displaystyle=\xi+\Delta(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi-\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi

(C.9)

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi-\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}(1-\Delta)^{-1}\xi

(C.10)

\displaystyle=-X_{1}-\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}X_{1}

(C.11)

using
\mathscr{U}_{>}=\text{Id}-\mathscr{U}_{\leq}
thus we get

Y=-X_{1}-\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}X_{1}-\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}+2\nabla\mathscr{U}_{\leq}X_{1}\cdot\nabla X+|\mathscr{U}_{\leq}X_{1}|^{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(C.12)

In the end, we have

e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v=-\nabla(e^{2X}\nabla v)+e^{2X}Yv

(C.13)

thus the associated quadratic form is

\langle\mathcal{H}u,u\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v(x)|^{2}e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v(x)|^{2}Y(x)e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(C.14)

for any
u=e^{X}v
with
v\in H_{\delta}^{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the second term being interpreted as a duality bracket as
Y
can not be evaluated pointwise. Since
X\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{1-2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

c_{1}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|w(x)|^{2}e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x\leq c_{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}

(C.15)

for
c_{1},c_{2}>0
random, this is the main motivation for the localization operator
\mathscr{U}_{>}
.

Construction in 3D.
In three dimensions, we have
X_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}
is still defined as a Wick product however additionnals terms are required for the renormalization, see for example
[
16
,
33
]
and references therein. We defined above

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta)^{-1}(\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:})\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{1-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(C.16)

\displaystyle X_{3}

\displaystyle=2(1-\Delta)^{-1}(\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla X_{2}\mathbf{:})\in\mathcal{C}_{-\delta}^{\frac{3}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(C.17)

and we consider

X=\mathscr{U}_{>}(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(C.18)

for any
\delta,\kappa>0
. Using similar computations as for
d=2
for localization operators, we have

\displaystyle-\Delta X

\displaystyle=-(1-\Delta)X+X+\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})

(C.19)

\displaystyle=-\xi-\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{:}-2\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla X_{2}\mathbf{:}+X+\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})

(C.20)

where
X+\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})
is a nicely behaved remainder. We have the Wick product

\displaystyle\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:}=\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{1}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}+2\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}\mathbf{:}+2\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{3}^{>}+\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{2}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}+2\nabla X_{2}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{3}^{>}+|\nabla X_{3}^{>}|^{2}

(C.21)

where

\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{i}^{>}|^{2}\mathbf{:}=\mathbf{:}|\nabla X_{i}|^{2}\mathbf{:}-|\nabla X_{i}^{\leq}|^{2}-2\nabla X_{i}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{i}^{>}

(C.22)

for
i\in\{1,2\}
and

\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}\mathbf{:}=\mathbf{:}\nabla X_{1}\cdot\nabla X_{2}\mathbf{:}-\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}-\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}-\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}

(C.23)

using the computations of the previous Appendix. Thus we get

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\xi-\Delta X-\mathbf{:}|\nabla X|^{2}\mathbf{:}

(C.24)

\displaystyle=X+\Delta\mathscr{U}_{\leq}(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})+2\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{3}^{>}+2\nabla X_{2}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{3}^{>}+|\nabla X_{3}^{>}|^{2}

(C.25)

\displaystyle\quad-|\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}|^{2}-2\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{1}^{>}-|\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}|^{2}-2\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}

(C.26)

\displaystyle\quad-\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}-\nabla X_{1}^{\leq}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{>}-\nabla X_{1}^{>}\cdot\nabla X_{2}^{\leq}

(C.27)

and a careful track of each terms give
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{-\mu}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. As in two dimensions, we have

e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v=-\nabla(e^{2X}\nabla v)+e^{2X}Yv

(C.28)

with

\langle\mathcal{H}u,u\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla v(x)|^{2}e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|v(x)|^{2}Y(x)e^{2X(x)}\mathrm{d}x

(C.29)

where the second integral is interpreted as a duality bracket since
Y
is not a function, well-defined for
v\in H_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}+2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Paracontrolled calculus.
We do not give here a general introduction on paracontrolled calculus, see for example the seminal work
[
13
]
or the lecture notes
[
15
]
as well as references therein. In this work, we only need the definition of the paraproduct and resonant terms with their continuity results that we recall here. Given Paley-Littlewood projectors defined before, one can consider formally the product as

\displaystyle u\cdot v

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m\geq 0}\Delta_{n}u\cdot\Delta_{m}v

(C.30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n<m-1}\Delta_{n}u\cdot\Delta_{m}v+\sum_{|n-m|\leq 1}\Delta_{n}u\cdot\Delta_{m}v+\sum_{n>m+1}\Delta_{n}u\cdot\Delta_{m}v

(C.31)

\displaystyle=:\mathsf{P}_{u}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(u,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}u

(C.32)

with
\mathsf{P}_{u}v
the paraproduct of
v
by
u
and
\mathsf{\Pi}(u,v)
the resonant term. The following continuity result can be proved, this can be proved for example with a proof in the same lines as Lemma
2.1
in
[
13
]
.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

\|\mathsf{P}_{u}v\|_{H_{\mu+\nu}^{\alpha\wedge 0+\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|u\|_{H_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{\mathcal{C}_{\nu}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(C.33)

and

\|\mathsf{P}_{u}v\|_{H_{\mu+\nu}^{\alpha\wedge 0+\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{H_{\nu}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(C.34)

If moreover
\alpha+\beta>0
, we get

\|\mathsf{\Pi}(u,v)\|_{H_{\mu+\nu}^{\alpha+\beta}}\lesssim\|u\|_{H_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{\mathcal{C}_{\nu}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(C.35)

In addition, one also needs to bound the corrector

\mathsf{C}(u,v,w)=\mathsf{\Pi}(\mathsf{P}_{u}v,w)-u\mathsf{\Pi}(v,w)

(C.36)

introduced initially in
[
13
]
. We have the following continuity result which is proved in a similar proof.

Proposition C.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\beta+\gamma<0<\alpha+\beta+\gamma
and
\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0
. The trilinear operator
\mathsf{C}
extends uniquely as a continuous map from
H_{\mu_{0}}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{C}_{\mu_{1}}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{C}_{\mu_{2}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
H_{\mu_{0}+\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

To obtain Strichartz inequalities for the linear equation, our proof follows a perturbative argument with respect to the Laplacian. Given the previous construction with (
C.4
) which makes sense for any
v\in H_{\delta}^{\frac{d}{2}-1+2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v=-\Delta v-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+Yv

(C.37)

if
v
is smooth enough for the cross term to be well-defined, that is the stronger condition
v\in H_{\delta}^{\frac{d}{2}+2\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This gives the bound

\|(e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}+\Delta)v\|_{H^{1-\frac{d}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(C.38)

for any
\gamma>0
. To obtain Strichartz inequalities, we use paracontrolled calculus to improve this bound to gain regularity using
\gamma
with a conjugated operator
\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}
, as done in our previous work
[
27
]
on compact surfaces, see also
[
7
,
33
]
. We introduce the operator

\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}:=(e^{X}\Gamma)^{-1}\mathcal{H}(e^{X}\Gamma)

(C.39)

for a suitable random map
\Gamma
in order to improve the previous comparison. This corresponds to a paracontrolled ansatz based on (
C.37
), again more involved in three dimensions. For
d=2
, we consider

\Phi(v):=v-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{1}-\mathsf{P}_{v}Z_{2}

(C.40)

with

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle=2(1-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla(X-X_{N})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{2-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(C.41)

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta)^{-1}(Y^{>}-Y_{N}^{>})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{2-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(C.42)

with
X_{N}
and
Y_{N}^{>}
smooth approximations respectively of
X
and
Y^{>}:=\mathscr{U}_{>}Y
. In three dimensions, the higher order paracontrolled expansion

\Phi(v):=v-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(Z_{1}+Z_{3})-\mathsf{P}_{v}(Z_{2}+Z_{4})

(C.43)

is needed with

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle=2(1-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla(X-X_{N})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{\frac{3}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(C.44)

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta)^{-1}(Y^{>}-Y_{N}^{>})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{\frac{3}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(C.45)

\displaystyle Z_{3}

\displaystyle=2(1-\Delta)^{-1}\big(\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,Z_{1})+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}Z_{1}\big)\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{2-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(C.46)

\displaystyle Z_{4}

\displaystyle=2(1-\Delta)^{-1}\big(\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,Z_{2})+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}Z_{2}\big)\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{2-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

(C.47)

The map
\Phi
is a compact perturbation of the identity on
H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
\sigma<2
in both cases. While it is not a priori invertible, one can truncate the stochastic terms in order to have a small pertubation of the identity without impacting the regularity of the objects involved. We do not keep the dependance with respect to
N
in the notation to simplify the computations however the map
\Phi=\Phi_{N}
converges to the identity as
N
goes to infinity thus this map is invertible for
N=N(X,Y_{>})
large enough. For such parameter fixed high enough, we denote as
\Gamma:H^{\sigma}\to H^{\sigma}
its inverse as done in
[
16
]
, see also
[
25
]
for a different choice of truncation. Since
X_{N}
and
Y_{>,N}
are smooth functions, this truncation has no impact on the regularity study needed for this work and the map
\Gamma
is defined by an implicit relation, for example

\Gamma v^{\sharp}=\mathsf{P}_{\nabla\Gamma v^{\sharp}}2\nabla(1-\Delta)^{-1}(X-X_{N})+\mathsf{P}_{\Gamma v^{\sharp}}(1-\Delta)^{-1}(-Y^{>}+Y_{N}^{>})+v^{\sharp}

(C.48)

with
v^{\sharp}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in two dimensions. It allows to obtain the following proposition.

Proposition C.3
.

Let
\mu>0
and
\gamma\in(0,2-\frac{d}{2})
. We have

\|(\mathcal{H}^{\sharp}+\Delta)v^{\sharp}\|_{H^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v^{\sharp}\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+\gamma+\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(C.49)

for any
\kappa>0
.

Proof :

The operator is given by

e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v=-\Delta v-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+Yv

(C.50)

and we now assume that
v=\Gamma v^{\sharp}
with
v^{\sharp}\in H^{\frac{d}{2}+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\gamma>0
. We first consider
d=2
, the computations are heavier but similar in three dimensions. We have

v=\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{v}Z_{2}+v^{\sharp}

(C.51)

with
(1-\Delta)Z_{1}=\nabla(X-X_{N})
and
(1-\Delta)Z_{2}=-Y^{>}+Y_{N}^{>}
thus

\displaystyle e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}\Gamma v^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=-\Delta v^{\sharp}-\Delta\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{1}-\Delta\mathsf{P}_{v}Z_{2}-2\nabla X\cdot\nabla v+Yv

(C.52)

\displaystyle=-\Delta v^{\sharp}-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}\Delta Z_{1}-\mathsf{P}_{v}\Delta Z_{2}-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}]Z_{1}-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{v}]Z_{2}

(C.53)

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v-2\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v)-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}2\nabla X+\mathsf{P}_{Y}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(Y,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}Y

(C.54)

\displaystyle=-\Delta v^{\sharp}-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{1}-\mathsf{P}_{v}Z_{2}-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}]Z_{1}-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{v}]Z_{2}

(C.55)

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v-2\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v)-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}2\nabla X_{N}+\mathsf{P}_{Y}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(Y,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}(Y_{N}^{>}+Y^{\leq})

(C.56)

where the choice of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
cancels the roughest term with
Y^{\leq}:=\mathscr{U}_{\leq}Y
. We get

\displaystyle(e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}\Gamma+\Delta)v^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{1}-\mathsf{P}_{v}Z_{2}-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}]Z_{1}-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{v}]Z_{2}-2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v-2\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v)

(C.57)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}2\nabla X_{N}+\mathsf{P}_{Y}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(Y,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}(Y_{N}^{>}+Y^{\leq})

(C.58)

where the roughest term are
\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v)
and
\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v
. Since
v\in H_{\delta}^{1+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\gamma>0
, these terms belong to
H^{\gamma-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
which is the claimed result up to applying
\Gamma^{-1}
which is the identity up to a regularizing perturbation. In three dimensions, we have

v=\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(Z_{1}+Z_{3})+\mathsf{P}_{v}(Z_{2}+Z_{4})+v^{\sharp}

(C.59)

with
(1-\Delta)Z_{3}=2\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,Z_{1})+2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}Z_{1}
and
(1-\Delta)Z_{4}=2\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,Z_{2})+2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}Z_{2}
. Using the previous computation, we get

\displaystyle e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}\Gamma v^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=-\Delta v^{\sharp}-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(Z_{1}+Z_{3})-\mathsf{P}_{v}(Z_{2}+Z_{4})-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}](Z_{1}+Z_{3})-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{v}](Z_{2}+Z_{4})

(C.60)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(1-\Delta)Z_{3}+\mathsf{P}_{v}(1-\Delta)Z_{4}-2\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v)

(C.61)

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}2\nabla X_{N}+\mathsf{P}_{Y}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(Y,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}(Y_{N}^{>}+Y^{\leq})

(C.62)

where the terms of the last line are well-controlled. Using the paracontrolled expansion for
v
, we get

\nabla v=\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}\nabla Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{\Delta v}Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{v}\nabla Z_{2}+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{2}+\nabla v^{\sharp}

(C.63)

hence

\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v)=\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}\nabla Z_{1})+\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\mathsf{P}_{v}\nabla Z_{2})+\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\mathsf{P}_{\Delta v}Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{2}+\nabla v^{\sharp})

(C.64)

where the last term is well-defined for
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
since
\nabla X\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. For the first two terms, we use the corrector from paracontrolled calculus to get

\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}\nabla Z_{i})=\nabla v\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{i})+\mathsf{C}(\nabla v,\nabla X,\nabla Z_{i})

(C.65)

for
i\in\{1,2\}
. The corrector is well-defined since
\nabla v\in H^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\nabla X\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\nabla Z_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
thus the sum of the exponents is positive for
\gamma>2\kappa
. The product
\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
are defined as the limit of the regularization without renormalization content, as done in the previous section. We get

\displaystyle e^{-X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}\Gamma v^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=-\Delta v^{\sharp}-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(Z_{1}+Z_{3})-\mathsf{P}_{v}(Z_{2}+Z_{4})-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}](Z_{1}+Z_{3})-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{v}](Z_{2}+Z_{4})

(C.66)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(1-\Delta)Z_{3}+\nabla v\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{1})+\mathsf{C}(\nabla v,\nabla X,\nabla Z_{1})

(C.67)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(1-\Delta)Z_{4}+\nabla v\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{2})+\mathsf{C}(\nabla v,\nabla X,\nabla Z_{2})

(C.68)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\mathsf{P}_{\Delta v}Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{2}+\nabla v^{\sharp})

(C.69)

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}2\nabla X_{N}+\mathsf{P}_{Y}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(Y,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}(Y_{N}^{>}+Y^{\leq})

(C.70)

\displaystyle=-\Delta v^{\sharp}-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}(Z_{1}+Z_{3})-\mathsf{P}_{v}(Z_{2}+Z_{4})-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}](Z_{1}+Z_{3})-[\Delta,\mathsf{P}_{v}](Z_{2}+Z_{4})

(C.71)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{1})}\nabla v+\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla v,\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{1}))+\mathsf{C}(\nabla v,\nabla X,\nabla Z_{1})

(C.72)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{2})}\nabla v+\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla v,\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{2}))+\mathsf{C}(\nabla v,\nabla X,\nabla Z_{2})

(C.73)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\mathsf{P}_{\Delta v}Z_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}Z_{2}+\nabla v^{\sharp})

(C.74)

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathsf{P}_{\nabla X}\nabla v-\mathsf{P}_{\nabla v}2\nabla X_{N}+\mathsf{P}_{Y}v+\mathsf{\Pi}(Y,v)+\mathsf{P}_{v}(Y_{N}^{>}+Y^{\leq})

(C.75)

using again the paraproduct decomposition for products with
\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla Z_{i})
and the cancellation with
Z_{3}
and
Z_{4}
. Tracking the precise regularity of each terms yield the result up to applying again
\Gamma^{-1}
. For example, the roughest term is

\|\mathsf{\Pi}(\nabla X,\nabla v^{\sharp})\|_{H^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|\nabla X\|_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|v^{\sharp}\|_{H^{1+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

(C.76)

where
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
which is a loss of
\frac{3}{2}
derivatives and completes the proof.

\square

While
\Gamma e^{X}H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not the domain of the Anderson Hamiltonian in full space due to the growth condition at infinity, one can show that this is a core however we do not pursue any spectral theory in this work. The following Corollary states that given
u\in L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it is equivalent to bound
v\in\Gamma H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
or
e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
v=\Gamma v^{\sharp}
.

Corollary C.4
.

For any
\mu,\kappa>0
, one has

\|e^{X}\mathcal{H}e^{X}v+\Delta\Gamma^{-1}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|v\|_{\Gamma H^{\frac{d}{2}+\kappa}\cap L_{\mu}^{2}}.

(C.77)
