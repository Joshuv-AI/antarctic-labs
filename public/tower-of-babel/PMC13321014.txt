Protocol for genomic mapping of chromatin targets using high-throughput CUT&RUN
Authors: Viswakalyan Kotapalli, Polina Iakova, Yue Lu, Abhinav K Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe genomic distribution of proteins that define chromatin state, including transcription factors, chromatin modifiers, and histone modifications, is critical for understanding gene regulation, cellular identity, and disease development. Here, we present a high-throughput cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN) protocol performed in 96-well plate format. We describe steps for targeted DNA enrichment, high-quality library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis. This protocol enables high-resolution genomic mapping of proteins of interest and factors that define the epigenome.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Skene and Henikoff1 and Meers et al.2Subject areas: ChIPseq, Antibody, Chromatin immunoprecipitation ChIP

Before you begin

Genomic distribution of proteins that define the chromatin state (histone post-translational modifications), DNA binding factors (transcription factors) and proteins that regulate chromatin structure (chromatin modifiers) is critical to understand precise regulation of gene expression, cellular identity, and development of diseases. Many existing mapping assays to determine distribution of these factors across the genome are successful, however they are cumbersome and time consuming. Cleavage Under Targets and Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) assay is a powerful tool for high-resolution genomic mapping of factors that define the epigenome.1,2,3 CUT&RUN assay require much less material compared to traditional chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays and have been used widely to map the epigenomic elements in various tissues, model systems and rare cell types.4,5,6 In comparison to ChIP, it is easier to concurrently profile many targets in multiple samples using CUT&RUN, however handling of multiple tubes is both time and resource intensive.

Here, we provide a detailed step-by-step protocol for performing high-throughput CUT&RUN assays in a 96-well plate format, covering: 1) sample preparation, 2) enrichment of protein-bound target DNA, and 3) high-quality library preparation to ensure efficient and robust genomic mapping of proteins of interest and epigenomic profiling. This protocol has been tested with MCF7 cell lines but is applicable to a wide range of cell types and can be used with lower starting cell numbers (∼10,000–250,000). It has resulted in successful generation of CUT&RUN libraries for abundant targets such as histone marks and DNA binding transcription factors using substantially fewer cells (e.g., 10K for histone marks and 100K for CTCF). The recommended starting point is 500,000 healthy cells (or nuclei) with normal morphology per CUT&RUN reaction; however, the optimal starting cell number should be determined based on the abundance of the target proteins.

Innovation

Our high-throughput workflow represents an adaptation of the conventional CUT&RUN protocol, traditionally performed in single or 8-strip tubes, for implementation in a 96-well plate format. This efficient and robust workflow offers several advantages over the conventional tube-based CUT&RUN assay: (1) a streamLined, highly efficient, and scalable approach for performing CUT&RUN in a high-throughput format; (2) execution in a 96-well plate format, ensuring high reproducibility across reactions; and (3) minimal to no transfer of enriched DNA prior to the generation of high-quality libraries. Direct comparative analysis of genomic mapping using histone marks and CTCF antibodies demonstrates that our modified high-throughput CUT&RUN (HT-CUT&RUN) assay performs comparably to the conventional tube-based assay.

Preparation of cells (or nuclei)

Timing: 1.5 h

Successful CUT&RUN assays depend on high-quality input material, specifically fresh, morphologically normal cells or nuclei with minimal contaminating debris. Culture MCF7 cells (or any user preferred cell line) in incubators under humidified conditions at 37°C, 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin and streptomycin antibiotics. Harvest, count and collect the cells (or nuclei) as follows.

Optimize digitonin concentration

Timing: 1 h

Digitonin, a nonionic detergent, is used in CUT&RUN assays to gently permeabilize cellular and nuclear membranes, enabling the antibody and pAG–MNase enzyme to enter the nucleus and mediate antibody-targeted chromatin cleavage without the need for cell lysis. Efficient cell permeabilization with digitonin is critical for the success of the CUT&RUN assay. It is recommended to optimize the digitonin concentration when working with a new sample type and to use this optimized concentration in assay buffers. The minimal concentration of digitonin required to achieve >90%–95% cell permeabilization is considered optimal. A standard concentration of digitonin (0.01%) used in the Cell Permeabilization and Antibody Buffers is generally suitable for common cell lines such as MCF7.

Expected outcomes

We used our HT-CUT&RUN assay to profile H3K4me3, H3K27ac and H3K4me1 in MCF7 cells and observed robust occupancy signals for each histone mark (Figures 2A–2C). We viewed the peak enrichment profiles across the genome using IGV browser and observed high signal intensity of CUT&RUN peaks with minimal to no noise to demonstrate expected occupancy of histone marks in MCF7 cells (Figure 2A). In addition, we observed expected genomic distribution of H3K4me3, H3K27ac and H3K4me1 peaks in MCF7 cells, H3K4me3 primarily enriched at gene promoters, whereas both H3K4me1 and H3K27ac also enriched at distal and intergenic regions (Figure 2B). To validate the specificity and robustness of the assay we classified HT-CUT&RUN- enriched genomic elements as active promoter (H3K4me3High and H3K27acHigh), inactive promoter (absence of H3K4me3, H3K4me1 and H3K27ac), active enhancer (H3K4me1High, H3K27acHigh and absence of H3K4me3), and inactive enhancer (H3K4me1High, H3K27acLow and absence of H3K4me3) (Figure 2C). We further used this assay to profile CTCF and observed strong and specific enrichment (Figure 2D) with a high signal to noise ratio of enrichment (Figure 2A). To validate the specificity of CTCF enrichment, we performed motif analysis under CTCF peaks. Motif enrichment analysis using HOMER identified the de novo CTCF motif that matched to canonical CTCF motif (MA0139.1) as the top enriched binding element (Figure 2E). Furthermore, FIMO analysis (p value 1e−4) revealed that more than 80% of CTCF CUT&RUN peaks contained a CTCF motif within 500bp of the peak summit (Figure 2F), suggesting high specificity of CTCF occupancy detected by HT-CUT&RUN. Taken together, these results demonstrate that HT-CUT&RUN efficiently profiles the genomic localization of both histone and non-histone proteins.

To further validate the performance of our HT-CUT&RUN assay, we compared the enrichment profiles of H3K4me3, H3K27ac and H3K4me1 generated using our plate-based CUT&RUN workflow with those obtained from the conventional tube-based CUT&RUN assay performed in parallel (Figure 3). We observed comparable FRiP (Fraction of Reads in Peaks) scores, within the expected range (>0.2), for H3K4me3, H3K27ac and H3K4me1 peaks generated by CUT&RUN performed in plates and tubes (Figure 3A). Analysis of genomic distribution of histone marks profiled by CUT&RUN performed in plates and tubes revealed the expected patterns, with H3K4me3 primarily enriched at gene promoters, while H3K4me1 and H3K27ac were enriched at distal and intergenic regions (Figure 3B). Furthermore, comparison of enrichment profiles revealed a significant overlap in both the genomic distribution, the number of H3K4me3, H3K4me1 and H3K27ac peaks and signal intensity between the two approaches (Figures 3C–3E). Taken together, these results indicate that plate-CUT&RUN assay produces enrichment profiles comparable to those obtained using conventional tube-based assay. Thus, HT-CUT&RUN workflow enables reproducible, efficient, and robust genomic mapping of protein of interests and epigenomic profiling.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For these analyses, CUT&RUN libraries and their corresponding IgG controls were sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to obtain 5–10 million pairs of reads per sample, with each read pair corresponding to a single DNA fragment. The in-house bioinformatics workflows13,14 used to analyze and compare datasets generated by high-throughput and conventional CUT&RUN are summarized below. The same analysis workflows were applied consistently across all datasets, thereby supporting an unbiased comparison and demonstrating the feasibility of the proposed protocol. More detailed documentation, including step-by-step commands, is available in two GitHub repositories. The preprocessing workflow, including read mapping and signal track generation, is available https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20347208 (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20347208). The peak-calling workflow, including peak calling and gene-based peak annotation, is available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20347212 (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20347212).

Mapping: Adapter sequences were removed from the 3′ ends of reads using Trim Galore! (version 0.6.7)15 and cutadapt (version 4.1).16 Reads were then mapped to the human genome (hg38) using Bowtie (version 1.1.2)17 with the following parameters: --allow-contain --maxins 2000 -v 2 -m 1 --best --strata. Approximately 88%–96% of fragments were mapped to the human genome, with 78%–90% being uniquely mapped. To avoid PCR bias, only one copy of multiple fragments mapped to the same genomic position was retained for further analysis. Fragments longer than 1000 bp (less than 1% of the total) were excluded from subsequent analysis. For fair comparisons between plate-based and conventional tube-based CUT&RUN assays in Figure 3, fragment counts were downsampled to the same depth for each histone mark across the two assays for all analyses shown in this figure.

Signal track: each fragment was resized to a length of 151 bp, spanning from −75 bp to +75 bp around its midpoint. The count of fragments covering each genomic position was normalized to 10M total number of fragments. These values were then averaged over a 10 bp resolution. The resulting averaged values were displayed using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).

Peak Calling: Peaks were identified using MACS (version 1.4.2)10 by comparing against the corresponding IgG sample. The middle point of each fragment was considered as the position of the fragment. A window size of 500 bp (for histone marks) or 300 bp (for CTCF) was used, and a p-value cutoff of 1e−5 was applied. The peaks that overlapped with ENCODE blacklisted regions18 and were removed. The peaks can also be compared to CUT&RUN suspect list of problematic regions and removed.19

Genomic distribution of peaks: Each peak was assigned to the gene (from GENCODE Release 45 basic gene annotation) with the closest transcription start site (TSS). Peaks were then classified according to their location relative to the gene: upstream (−50 kb to −5 kb from TSS), promoter (−5 kb to +1 kb from TSS), exon, intron, TES (−1 kb to +5 kb from transcription end site, TES), downstream (+5 kb to +50 kb from TES), or intergenic.

Venn diagram of peaks: For each histone mark, peaks from the two assays were merged, and the numbers of merged peaks shared between or unique to each assay were visualized using a Venn diagram.

Heatmaps and metaplots: regions spanning 3 kb upstream to 3 kb downstream of each anchor point were divided into 20 bp bins. For each CUT&RUN sample, all fragments were resized to 151 bp, spanning from −75 bp to +75 bp relative to the fragment midpoint. Mean fragment coverage per 10 million total fragments was calculated for each bin and then background-corrected by subtracting the corresponding IgG signal. The resulting value matrices were visualized as heatmaps using the R/Bioconductor ComplexHeatmap package (version 2.18.0),20 with the color scale saturated at the 95th percentile of the matrix values. For metaplots, values were averaged within each sample group and plotted accordingly. For Figure 2C, the promoter anchor point was defined as the transcription start site (TSS) of the longest transcript for each protein-coding gene. TSSs overlapping H3K4me3 peaks were classified as active promoters, and the remaining TSSs were classified as inactive promoters. Enhancer anchor points were defined as H3K4me1 peak summits, with those overlapping H3K27ac peaks classified as active enhancers and the remainder classified as inactive enhancers. For Figure 2D, anchor points were defined as CTCF peak summits. For Figure 3C, anchor points were defined as peak summits of the respective histone marks.

Fraction of Reads in Peaks (FRiP): The midpoint of each fragment was used to represent its genomic position, and FRiP was calculated as the fraction of fragments in peaks. Motif analysis for CTCF: Motif analysis was performed using MEME-ChIP from the MEME Suite (version 5.5.5).21 Sequences spanning −300 bp to +300 bp around CTCF peak summits were used as input. De novo motif discovery was conducted using the STREME component of MEME-ChIP. Identified motifs were aligned to the canonical CTCF motif (MA0139.1 from JASPAR 202222) using the Tomtom component. The probability distribution of the canonical motif relative to peak summits were visualized using the CentriMo component. The presence of the canonical CTCF motif around CTCF peak summits was assessed using FIMO from the MEME Suite with default parameters.

Limitations

Our data show that the HT-CUT&RUN assay is robust, cost-effective and performs comparably to the conventional tube-based assay in tubes. Although the assay performed well for histone marks and DNA-binding proteins in adherent cell lines, small-scale preliminary optimization should be prioritized for fragile cells, primary tissues, low-abundance transcription factors, and fixed samples. While pipetting cross multiple columns with a multi-channel pipette may expedite the number of reactions/assay, it may not be practical or suitable for all users. The hands-on bench time required for HT-CUT&RUN is comparable to that of conventional CUT&RUN and does not reduce manual processing time relative to protocols that are fully automated using liquid handling systems. Nevertheless, HT-CUT&RUN is likely to be particularly advantageous for researchers seeking to process large numbers of samples in parallel but who lack the resources or infrastructure necessary to implement automated liquid handling systems.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Concanavalin A (ConA) beads sedimentation on ice, during the experiment (step 5–7).

Potential solution

ConA beads begin to sediment if stored on ice for more than 5 min. To avoid this, do not prepare the beads in advance or store them on ice for extended periods. Once the beads are activated, store them at 4°C and gently resuspend them using a wide-bore pipette tip just before use.

Problem 2

Concanavalin A (ConA) beads become stuck to the wall, while rotating on the Nutator overnight (step 27).

Potential solution

Some beads will get stuck to the wall and it is normal. However, too much clumping of the beads can occur if more than 500,00 cells are used or the sample quality was not optimal and will affect CUT&RUN efficiency. To ensure this does not happen, use the exact cell count per reaction and avoid harsh handling. Keep the plates top right on the nutator.

Problem 3

Very low or undetectable DNA yields after CUT&RUN (step 72).

Potential solution

CUT&RUN using an antibody targeting a low abundant protein or a co-factor that does not directly bind DNA can result in undetectable or very low final DNA yields due to limited target material. Although low DNA yields do not necessarily indicate assay failure, in such cases, adjusting the starting cell number, using a different antibody, and applying variations of the STOP buffer, such as those used in CUT&RUN LoV-U (low volume and urea),23 may improve efficiency.

Problem 4

Low concentration of final libraries (step 83).

Potential solution

Libraries prepared from low CUT&RUN DNA can sometimes result in very low final library concentrations (<1 nM). In such cases, repeat the library preparation using a higher amount of CUT&RUN DNA. If this is not feasible, either increase the number of PCR cycles during library preparation or include as much of the low-concentration library as possible to the final sequencing pool.

Problem 5

Adapter dimers present in the CUT&RUN library (step 84).

Potential solution

Preparing libraries from low CUT&RUN DNA yields can lead to higher levels of adapter dimers in the final library. Adapter dimers (visible peak between 100–175 bp) can sometimes carry over into the final library and affect sequencing results; we recommend removing them if they exceed 5% of the total DNA. These adapters can be removed from individual libraries or pooled libraries by an additional cleanup using 1× SPRI beads prior to sequencing.

Problem 6

Absence of signal (or higher background) after CUT&RUN.

Potential solution

High-quality CUT&RUN output depends on the specificity and efficiency of the antibodies used. In some cases, antibodies that perform well in ChIP-seq may not perform effectively in CUT&RUN, resulting in lower signal and higher background. Thorough antibody validation is critical for achieving a high signal-to-noise ratio in CUT&RUN experiments. Typically, antibodies validated for immunofluorescence and FACS are good candidates when selecting antibodies for CUT&RUN. In the absence of an antibody validated for CUT&RUN for a specific target, multiple antibodies against the same target may need to be tested. Data robustness can be ensured by confirming that two or more antibodies produce a high signal-to-noise ratio, similar genomic enrichment patterns, and peak distributions consistent with previously reported functions, if known (e.g., ChIP-seq data).

Problem 7

Low FRiP scores observed in the analyzed CUT&RUN data.

Potential solution

FRiP (Fraction of Reads in Peaks) is a widely used parameter for evaluating CUT&RUN data quality representing the proportion of sequencing reads that map to called peaks. Higher FRiP scores generally reflect high signal-to-noise ratios and efficient enrichment of antibody-targeted DNA. Low FRiP scores may indicate suboptimal assay performance and could be due to technical factors such as antibody efficiency, sample quality or the appropriateness of peak caller used during bioinformatic analyses. It is important to examine the peak landscape and evaluate alternative peak-calling approaches before making a final assessment of assay performance.
