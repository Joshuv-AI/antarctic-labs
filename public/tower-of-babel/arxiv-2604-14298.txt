Precision Limits of Multiparameter Markovian-Noise Metrology
Anthony J. Brady, Yu-Xin Wang (王语馨), Luis Pedro García-Pintos, Alexey V. Gorshkov

Abstract
Measuring stochastic signals (“noise metrology”) constitutes a central task in quantum sensing and the characterization of open quantum systems. Here we establish ultimate precision bounds for multiparameter estimation of stochastic signals encoded through Markovian Lindblad dynamics, allowing for arbitrary quantum control and noiseless ancillae. Although Markovianity enforces standard-quantum-limit scaling with sensing time T T , our bounds reveal Heisenberg-type scaling in the number of dissipative channels, R R : when the stochastic signal exhibits high-rank correlations across the R R channels and the probe is entangled, the average variance (per parameter) scales no better than Ω ⁡ ( 1 / ( T ​ R 2 ) ) \Omega(1/(TR^{2})) . For collective k k -body dissipation, R = Θ ⁡ ( N k ) R=\Theta(N^{k}) , signifying super-Heisenberg scaling with the system size N N . We further show that, when the unknown parameters enter through the dissipative eigenrates, a Rapid Prepare-and-Measure (RPM) protocol that tracks many distinct quantum jumps in parallel attains these limits. In this regime, the estimation problem reduces to a multi-Poisson counting model, providing a conceptually clean route to optimal quantum noise metrology. We illustrate the breadth of the framework with applications to networked noise metrology, collective many-body dissipation, learning Pauli noise, and subdiffraction quantum imaging.

II
Setup

We study estimation of parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{\alpha})_{\alpha=1}^{d}
encoded in a quantum probe through a dissipative, memoryless (Markovian) process. The probe is prepared in an initial state
\psi\triangleq\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
which may include ancillae, evolves under Lindbladian evolution
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\theta}}(\psi)
with parametric dissipator
\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}
and arbitrary control, and is then measured to produce estimators
\check{\bm{\theta}}
. To analyze estimation precision, we use local (asymptotic) quantum-estimation theory based on Fisher information (see Refs.
86
;
110
;
71
;
77
;
89
for background). We also introduce a purification of
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\theta}}(\psi)
that allows us to compute upper bounds on the precision for arbitrary Markovian dissipative encodings.

II.1
Parameter Estimation

Estimation precision.

Let
\check{\bm{\theta}}
denote the unbiased estimators constructed from measurement outcomes, with uncertainty characterized by the covariance matrix

\bm{\Sigma}_{\alpha\beta}\ \triangleq\ \mathbb{E}\left[(\check{\theta}_{\alpha}-\theta_{\alpha})(\check{\theta}_{\beta}-\theta_{\beta})\right].

(1)

Throughout, we also use the (diagonal) absolute precision,

\tau_{\alpha}\triangleq\frac{1}{\bm{\Sigma}_{\alpha\alpha}},

(2)

as a simple but informative performance metric.

Classical and quantum Fisher information.

Given a POVM
\{\Pi_{j}\}
, outcome probabilities derive from the Born rule
p_{j}(\bm{\theta})=\Tr(\Pi_j\rho(\bm{\theta}))
with
\sum_{j}\Pi_{j}=I
, and the classical Fisher information matrix reads

(F_{C})_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{j}\frac{(\partial_{\alpha}p_{j})(\partial_{\beta}p_{j})}{p_{j}}=4\sum_{j}(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{p_{j}})(\partial_{\beta}\sqrt{p_{j}}).

(3)

For
\nu
i.i.d. repetitions and unbiased estimators, the classical Cramér-Rao bound establishes a (protocol-dependent) precision limit,

\bm{\Sigma}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\nu}F_{C}^{-1}.

(4)

For any POVM, the classical Fisher information matrix
F_{C}
is bounded from above by the (protocol-independent) QFI matrix
F_{Q}
, leading to the celebrated quantum Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB)
56
;
57
;
10
;
38

\bm{\Sigma}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\nu}F_{C}^{-1}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\nu}F_{Q}^{-1}.

(5)

A useful inequality is
(F_{Q}^{-1})_{\alpha\alpha}\geq 1/(F_{Q})_{\alpha\alpha}
; hence, correlations in
F_{Q}
typically degrade marginal precisions relative to what the diagonal entries alone suggest. (In general, the multiparameter QCRB involving
F_{Q}
may not always be achievable due to measurement incompatibility
97
;
87
;
71
;
2
;
89
but tight, closely related bounds can be formulated, e.g., for the case with two parameters
111
.)

Purification viewpoint.

For a parametric quantum channel
\rho(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\theta}}(\psi)
, any Stinespring dilation
112
produces a purification

\Psi(\bm{\theta})=U(\bm{\theta})(\psi\otimes\varphi_{\rm env})U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})
such that

\rho(\bm{\theta})=\Tr_{\rm env}\Psi(\bm{\theta})
. Since partial trace is parameter-independent,

F_{Q}[\Psi(\bm{\theta})]\ \geq\ F_{Q}[\rho(\bm{\theta})]

(6)

via quantum data processing
105
. The choice of dilation is not unique; in fact, minimizing
F_{Q}[\Psi]
over environment unitaries recovers
F_{Q}[\rho]
(per Uhlmann’s theorem
119
). In this work we focus on a specific purification—the
canonical collisional purification
below—whose QFI is computable and yields an upper bound which is often tight; we discuss tightness more generally in Appendix
C
.

II.2
Dissipative Dynamics and Collisional Purification

Markovian evolution.

We consider a quantum system evolving under a Lindbladian generator with parametrized dissipator
70
;
48
;
12
;
3
,

\partial_{t}\rho(\bm{\theta};t)=\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\rho),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\theta},t}(\rho)\triangleq-i\commutator{H_c(t)}{\rho}+\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}(\rho),

(7)

where
H_{c}(t)
is a parameter-independent control Hamiltonian and
\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}
is the parametric dissipator. The formal solution of the Lindblad equation is

\rho(\bm{\theta};t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\,\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\theta},s}\Big)(\psi),

(8)

where
\psi=\rho(0)
. We assume an initial pure state throughout,
\psi\triangleq\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, which may include ancillary degrees of freedom that evolve trivially. By convexity of the QFI, sensors prepared in a mixed initial state satisfy the same upper bounds.

Let
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{R}
denote jump operators in a local basis (independent of
\bm{\theta}
). Parameter dependence enters through the Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix
\Gamma(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times R}
,

\Gamma(\bm{\theta})=V(\bm{\theta})\Lambda(\bm{\theta})V^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}),\quad\Lambda={\rm diag}(\gamma_{k}),\quad\gamma_{k}\geq 0,

(9)

where
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{R\times R}
and
V\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times R}
and
k=1,\dots,R
.
From which we define the canonical jump operators

J_{k}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{R}V_{ik}(\bm{\theta})L_{i}.

(10)

Thus the eigenrates
\gamma_{k}
and the eigenframe
V
both depond on
\bm{\theta}
. The dissipator may be written in the physical frame and in the canonical frame, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{R}\Gamma_{ij}(\bm{\theta})\left(L_{i}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{L_j^\dagger L_i}{\rho}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})\left(J_{k}(\bm{\theta})\rho J_{k}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{J_k^\dagger J_k(\bm{\theta})}{\rho}\right).

(12)

Collisional purification.

We realize the Markovian dissipator by coupling the probe to a memoryless bosonic bath through a purification of the Lindbladian evolution; see Appendix
A
for further details. Let
U(\bm{\theta};t)=\mathcal{T}{\rm exp}(-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}H_{\rm tot}(\bm{\theta};s))
act on the joint system-environment Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{S}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\rm env}
, with

H_{\rm tot}(\bm{\theta};t)=H_{c}(t)+H(\bm{\theta};t),

(13)

and interaction Hamiltonian

H(\bm{\theta};t)=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})}\,J_{k}(\bm{\theta})\otimes e_{k}^{\dagger}(t)+{\rm h.c.}

(14)

We take the bath operators
\{e_{k}(t)\}
as bosonic annihilation operators satisfying

\commutator*{e_k(t)}{e_{k'}^\dagger(t')}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and assume the bath state
\varphi_{\rm env}
is the (memoryless, Gaussian) vacuum state, such that

\Tr(\varphi_{\rm env}e_k(t)e_{k'}^\dagger(t'))=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(15)

with all other first and second moments vanishing. Defining the global state

\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)=U(\bm{\theta};t)\,(\psi\otimes\varphi_{\rm env})\,U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta};t),

(16)

we have
\rho(\bm{\theta};t)=\Tr_{\rm env}\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)
, and this dilation reproduces the Lindblad evolution in Eq. (
8
).

Operationally, the purification corresponds to a collisional model in which the system couples at each instant to a fresh bath element prepared in the vacuum, thereby enforcing Markovianity. This dilation is not unique: Taking
U(\bm{\theta};t)\mapsto(I\otimes U_{E}(\bm{\theta};t))\,U(\bm{\theta};t)
leaves the reduced evolution unchanged.

In what follows, we employ the canonical (
U_{E}=I
) collisional purification to derive precision bounds and infer protocols. The resulting bounds need not be universally tight, precisely because different choices
U_{E}
correspond to different purifications of the same reduced dynamics; we do, however, identify regimes in which the bounds are tight. Appendix
C
further discusses the general issue of tightness from the perspective of Uhlmann’s theorem.

III.1
Collisional QFI and Generalized Heisenberg Limit

Temporal structure and flow.

For parametric memoryless processes, the achievable precision is bounded by a linear scaling in time. This behavior is expected when estimating dissipative processes
37
;
34
;
30
;
65
;
17
;
52
;
67
and is well known in single-parameter Markovian settings
25
;
106
;
125
;
26
;
42
.
Though not our main result, we derive this scaling using collisional purification techniques. For clarity, we consider single-parameter estimation and a single jump operator; the same time-scaling extends to multiple parameters and multiple jump operators by similar reasoning.

Lemma 1

(QFI Flow)
.

Consider a parametric dissipator

\mathscr{D}_{\theta}(\rho)=\gamma_{\theta}\!\left(L\rho L^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{L^\dagger L}{\rho}\right),

(17)

where the rate
\gamma_{\theta}
encodes the parameter
\theta
.
Then the QFI of the collisional purification
F_{Q}(t)
satisfies

F_{Q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\,\dot{F}_{Q}(s)\ \leq\ 4t(\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{\gamma_{\theta}})^{2}\,\norm{L}^{2},

(18)

where
\dot{F}_{Q}\triangleq\partial_{t}F_{Q}(t)
is the QFI flow and
\norm{\cdot}
denotes the spectral norm.

Proof sketch.

The linear time scaling follows directly from the memoryless assumption of the bath (viz.,
\Tr(\varphi_{\rm env}e(s)e^\dagger(s'))=\delta(s-s^{\prime})
) and can be recovered within the collisional purification; we provide a full proof in Appendix
B.1
by modeling the environment as a product of independent bath elements in the discrete-time approximation.
∎

In words, estimation precision for parametric Markovian dynamics obeys the SQL in time
t
. This is consistent with temporal locality of Markovian evolution, for which coherent buildup across disjoint time intervals is absent; the QFI flow thus represents the natural object to analyze in the Markovian setting
72
. This perspective is conceptually related to Fisher-information-based speed limits for stochastic quantum dynamics
91
;
61
;
40
, though the QFI flow plays a metrological (rather than dynamical) role in our work.

Collisional QFI matrix.

We now turn to the general multiparameter problem with multiple (potentially many-body) jump operators. We derive the QFI matrix flow for the collisional purification, obtaining a general expression that upper bounds the metrological performance of arbitrary parametric dissipative processes. For related information-geometric treatments of dissipative processes, see Refs.
17
;
52
.

Define the (disconnected) correlator

\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t)\ \triangleq\ \Tr\!\big(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{j}\,\rho(t)\big),

(19)

with
\rho(t)
the time-evolved state of Eq. (
8
), which may include (noiseless) ancillary degrees of freedom, and the dissipative amplitude

\zeta(\bm{\theta})\ \triangleq\ V\Lambda^{1/2},\qquad\Gamma=\zeta\zeta^{\dagger},

(20)

where
\Gamma\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times R}
is the Hermitian dissipation matrix in the local jump basis
\{L_{a}\}
[Eq. (
9
)].

Theorem 1

(Collisional QFI Matrix)
.

Consider the collisional purification (Sec.
II.2
) of the dissipative process
\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}
[Eqs. (
11
)–(
12
)]. Given the correlators
\mathcal{C}(t)
in Eq. (
19
), the collisional QFI matrix flow obeys

(\dot{F}_{Q})_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\real\,\Tr\left(\mathcal{K}_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathcal{C}(t)\right),

(21)

where the trace runs over the
R
-dimensional jump-index space and

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\triangleq 4\big(\partial_{\beta}\zeta\big)\big(\partial_{\alpha}\zeta\big)^{\dagger}

(22)

\displaystyle=\big(\partial_{\beta}\Gamma+\anticommutator{K_\beta}{\Gamma}\big)\,\Gamma^{-1}\big(\partial_{\alpha}\Gamma+\anticommutator{K_\alpha}{\Gamma}\big)^{\dagger},

(23)

with eigenframe connection
K_{\alpha}\triangleq(\partial_{\alpha}V)V^{\dagger}
in the local basis determined by
\{L_{a}\}
.

Proof sketch.

The result follows from the collisional purification by expressing the QFI flow as a covariance of the joint system-bath generator associated with the collisional interaction Hamiltonian [Eq. (
14
)]. The linear-in-time structure again originates from the memoryless assumption, in direct analogy with Lemma
1
. See Appendix
B.2
for the full proof.
∎

For Hermitian operators,
L_{i}^{\dagger}=L_{i}
, we can tighten Theorem
1
by replacing the disconnected correlator
\mathcal{C}
with its connected version, obtained by taking
{L_{i}\to L_{i}-\Tr(L_i\rho(t))}
. We state this precisely in Proposition
B.1
of Appendix
B.2
;
see also Ref.
126
.

The dissipation kernel
\mathcal{K}
captures metrological sensitivity arising from correlations in the parametric dissipator
\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}
, while the correlator
\mathcal{C}
captures sensitivity arising from quantum correlations of the probe state. The separation of terms appearing in the kernel follows from the fundamental rate equation

\partial_{\alpha}\Gamma=V(\partial_{\alpha}\Lambda)V^{\dagger}+\commutator{K_\alpha}{\Gamma},

(24)

which decomposes parameter dependence into eigenrate variations
\partial_{\alpha}\Lambda
and eigenframe rotations
\commutator{K_\alpha}{\Gamma}
; see Appendix
B.2
for details.

Finally, observe that Hamiltonian control
H_{c}(t)
appears only through the state
\rho(t)
per Eq. (
8
). Thus control can modify the constants in the bound by shaping
\rho(t)
, but it does not change the linear time scaling. The scaling with the jump-index dimension
R
is controlled by the joint correlation structure entering both
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{C}
, which we quantify below through a generalized Heisenberg limit (Theorem
2
).

Generalized Heisenberg limit.

We establish an upper bound on the average QFI (per parameter) in terms of the number of local jump operators
R
and the connectivity of both the dissipation kernel
\mathcal{K}
and the quantum correlator matrix
\mathcal{C}
. We first introduce notation that we use in the statement of our result (Theorem
2
).

Definition 1

(Parameter Averaging)
.

Given a collection of matrices
\{M_{\alpha\beta}\}
with
M_{\alpha\beta}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and parameter indices
\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,d
, we define the parameter-averaged matrix

\bar{M}\ \triangleq\ \frac{1}{d}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}M_{\alpha\alpha}\ \in\ \mathbb{C}^{m\times m}.

(25)

From which we define the average QFI
\bar{F}_{Q}(t)
(
m=1
) and the average dissipation kernel
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
(
m=R
).

Definition 2

(Max Norms)
.

For any matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times R}
, define the max-entry norm

\norm{M}_{\max}\triangleq\max_{a,b\in[R]}\absolutevalue{M_{ab}}.

(26)

Similarly, for a collection of operators
\{L_{a}\}_{a=1}^{R}
on the probe Hilbert space, define

\norm{L}_{\max}\ \triangleq\ \max_{a\in[R]}\norm{L_a},

(27)

where
\norm{\cdot}
denotes the spectral norm.

Definition 3

(Support and Row Connectivity)
.

For any matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times R}
define its support

{\rm supp}(M)\ \triangleq\ \{(a,b)\in[R]^{2}:\ M_{ab}\neq 0\},

(28)

and its row connectivity

r_{M}\ \triangleq\ \max_{a\in[R]}\absolutevalue{\{b\in[R]:(a,b)\in{\rm supp}(M)\}}.

(29)

Intuitively,
{\rm supp}(M)
records which jump-index pairs
(a,b)
contribute to, e.g., contractions such as
\Tr(\mathcal{K} \mathcal{C})
, while
r_{M}
measures the maximal number of nonzero entries in any row (e.g.,
r_{\mathcal{C}}
quantifies the connectivity of probe correlations in jump-index space).

Theorem 2

(Generalized Heisenberg Limit)
.

Consider a parametric dissipative encoding
\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}
with
R
distinct dissipation channels, and fix a local jump-operator basis
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{R}
. For a sensing protocol consisting of
\nu
i.i.d. repetitions of a single-shot experiment of duration
t
and total sensing time
T=\nu t
, the average QFI (per parameter) satisfies

\bar{F}_{Q}(T)\leq T\,\norm{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}_{\max}\,\norm{L}_{\max}^{2}\,R\,\min(r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}},\,r_{\mathcal{C}}),

(30)

where
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the row connectivity of
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
and
r_{\mathcal{C}}\ \triangleq\ \sup_{s\in[0,t]}r_{\mathcal{C}(s)}
denotes any uniform upper bound on the row connectivity of
\mathcal{C}(s)
over a single shot. Ultimately,
\bar{F}_{Q}(T)\leq\order{TR^2}
since
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}},r_{\mathcal{C}}\leq R
.

Proof sketch.

The result derives from Theorem
1
and the requirement that
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{C}
must have overlapping support in the jump-index space (of linear dimension
R
) via
\Tr\,(\mathcal{K}_{\alpha\beta}^{\top}\mathcal{C}(t))
. See Appendix
B.3
for the full proof.
∎

Discussion.

We distinguish scaling regimes by the connectivity pair
(r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}},r_{\mathcal{C}})
, which provides necessary structural conditions to attain
\order{TR^2}
scaling:

(i)

Sparse-sparse.
If
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}=\order{1}
and
r_{\mathcal{C}}=\order{1}
, then

\bar{F}_{Q}(T)\leq\order{TR},

(31)

corresponding to uncorrelated dissipation probed by uncorrelated, independent sensors.

(ii)

Sparse-dense.
If
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}=\order{1}
but
r_{\mathcal{C}}=\order{R}
, then still

\bar{F}_{Q}(T)\leq\order{TR}.

(32)

That is, strong probe correlations cannot overcome an effectively uncorrelated stochastic signal (no-go entanglement advantage).

(iii)

Dense-sparse.
If
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}=\order{R}
but
r_{\mathcal{C}}=\order{1}
, then again

\bar{F}_{Q}(T)\leq\order{TR}.

(33)

That is, strong correlations in the stochastic signal do not yield Heisenberg scaling without correspondingly strong probe correlations.

(iv)

Dense-dense.
If
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}=\order{R}
and
r_{\mathcal{C}}=\order{R}
, then the bound is compatible with

\bar{F}_{Q}(T)\leq\order{TR^2}.

(34)

Conversely, joint high-connectivity is necessary to even allow quadratic scaling in
R
; see Sections
IV.1
and
IV.2
for concrete examples.

The SQL regimes in (i)-(iii) are consistent with scenarios where one estimates many independent rates in parallel, or where classical correlations in the stochastic signal can be leveraged through multiplexed readout. The Heisenberg-in-
R
regime of (iv) is unique to noise metrology because enhancement requires high connectivity in both the quantum probes as well as the stochastic signal, as noted in Ref.
9
for a network of quantum sensors.

As of now, the jump-index dimension
R
is treated abstractly. Physically,
R
denotes the number of independent dissipation channels—equivalently, the number of linearly independent jump operators [Eqs. (
11
)–(
12
)]. Relating
R
to the system size
N
requires additional locality assumptions on the canonical jump operators in
\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}
, which we defer to Sec.
IV.2
.

Eigenrate QFI.

In much of this work, we consider the case where the parameters enter solely through the eigenrates
\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})
of the dissipation matrix
\Gamma
, i.e., the eigenframe is parameter-independent,
\partial_{\alpha}V=0
, so that
K_{\alpha}=(\partial_{\alpha}V)V^{\dagger}=0
in Theorem

. This is a physically important setting, e.g., learning error rates, thermometry, decay/dephasing estimation etc., and it yields an explicit form of the QFI matrix flow.

Proposition 1

(Eigenrate QCRB)
.

Suppose the parameters
\bm{\theta}
are encoded solely in the eigenrates
\gamma_{k}
, so that
\partial_{\alpha}V=0

(and hence
K_{\alpha}=0
in Theorem
1
). Then

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha\beta}=(\partial_{\beta}\Gamma)\,\Gamma^{-1}\,(\partial_{\alpha}\Gamma).

(35)

Let
\Gamma=V\Lambda V^{\dagger}
with
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{R})
and let
J_{k}
denote the canonical
jump operators [Eq. (
10
)]. In this eigenbasis, the QFI matrix flow takes the explicit form

(\dot{F}_{Q})_{\alpha\beta}(t)=4\sum_{k=1}^{R}\big(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\big)\big(\partial_{\beta}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\big)\,\Tr\!\big(J_{k}^{\dagger}J_{k}\,\rho(t)\big).

(36)

For a protocol of
\nu
i.i.d. repetitions, each of duration
t
(total time
T=\nu t
), the multiparameter QCRB implies the following precision bound on the diagonal precision
\tau_{\alpha}
defined in Eq. (
2
) (cf.
106
),

\tau_{\alpha}\leq T\sum_{k=1}^{R}\frac{(\partial_{\alpha}\gamma_{k})^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\norm{J_k}^{2}.

(37)

Proof.

The explicit form (
36
) follows from Theorem
1
, while the precision bound (
37
) follows from the facts
(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})^{2}=(\partial_{\alpha}\gamma_{k})^{2}/\gamma_{k}
and
\Tr\,(J_{k}^{\dagger}J_{k}\rho(s))\leq\norm{J_k}^{2}
.
∎

Proposition
1
extends the single-parameter eigenrate bounds of Refs.
106
;
42
to the multiparameter setting and applies broadly to finite- and infinite-dimensional systems (spins and bosons).
1

1

Though for bosons, care must be taken with the operator-norm bound.
Moreover, for Hermitian jump operators,
J_{k}^{\dagger}=J_{k}

As of now, the physical setting and the canonical jump operators

III.2
Operational Scenarios

To apply the collisional QCRB inferred from the QFI-matrix relations, we specify the operating scenarios relevant to experiments conducted over a long total sensing time
T=\nu t
, comprised of
\nu
i.i.d. repetitions of a single-shot protocol of duration
t
. The scenarios below correspond to different experimental constraints and the resulting optimization.

Scenario 1: Unlimited shots.

Here the total sensing time
T
is fixed and the number of repetitions
\nu=T/t
can be taken large
by choosing small
t
(subject to experimental overheads discussed below). The relevant figure of merit is the (average) QFI flow,
F_{Q}(t)/t
, so the time-optimal performance obeys

F_{Q}^{\rm opt}(T)=T\,\max_{t}\frac{F_{Q}(t)}{t}.

(38)

Scenario 2: Shot limited.

If the number of repetitions
\nu
is constrained, e.g., by slow state preparation or long measurement overhead, one instead optimizes the per-shot sensitivity,

F_{Q}^{\rm opt}(\nu)=\nu\,\max_{t}F_{Q}(t).

(39)

A subtlety is that the optimizing time
t
can depend on the unknown parameters themselves, e.g., the rates
\gamma_{k}
, so this scenario is naturally addressed by adaptive protocols.

Scenario 3: Short time / weak signal.

In many practical settings, the single-shot duration is constrained to be small,
t=\delta t
, so that only the initial-time behavior is accessible; mathematically,
\delta t\ll 1/\big(\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\norm{J_k}^{2}\big)
. In this regime,

F_{Q}(T)\approx T\,\dot{F}_{Q}(0^{+}),\quad\dot{F}_{Q}(0^{+})\triangleq\lim_{\delta t\to 0^{+}}\frac{F_{Q}(\delta t)}{\delta t},

(40)

for
\nu
repetitions (
T=\nu\delta t
). Thus, the initial QFI growth rate controls the attainable precision.

In this work, we focus on Scenarios 1 and 3, for which the central objective is to bound and attain a large Fisher-information rate,
F_{Q}(t)/t
. In the Markovian setting, the optimal operating point in both scenarios proves to be the continuous short-time limit,
\delta t\to 0^{+}
. An RPM strategy realizes this by rapidly repeating short-time experiments and tracking many quantum jumps in parallel, thereby attaining the eigenrate QCRB of Proposition
1
.

We note that Scenario 2 can exhibit qualitatively different behavior, e.g., exponential entanglement advantage
127
;
92
, which we do not pursue here.

III.3
Quickly Tracking Quantum Jumps

In the eigenrate-estimation setting (viz., Proposition
1
), the parameter dependence enters solely through the canonical rates
\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})
, so that the canonical jump operators
J_{k}
are known and only the jump statistics depend on
\bm{\theta}
. The estimation problem in this regime is “quasi-classical”
76
: if we can resolve which jump channel occurred, then the measurement record becomes a multi-Poisson model whose Fisher information matches the collisional eigenrate QFI of Proposition
1
[Eq. (
36
)].

In this Section, we prove that an RPM protocol, which rapidly tracks many quantum jumps in parallel, reduces the problem to a multi-Poisson counting model in the sense above and saturates the parameter-averaged QCRB (Theorem
3
). This extends single-parameter, fast-reset strategies (cf.
25
;
106
;
42
;
126
) to the multiparameter setting and highlights the need to resolve
each
jump channel, rather than a single or aggregate jump record which often suffices for single-parameter estimation.

Distinguishable jump basis.

Fix a pure probe state
\ket{\psi}
and define the normalized jump vectors

\ket{\mathfrak{j}_k}\ \triangleq\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{k}}}J_{k}\ket{\psi},\quad\mu_{k}\ \triangleq\ \expectationvalue{J_k^\dagger J_k}{\psi}.

(41)

We say that
\ket{\psi}
admits a
distinguishable jump basis
if the vectors

\{\ket{\mathfrak{j}_k}\}_{k=1}^{R}
are mutually orthonormal and orthogonal to
\ket{\psi}
, i.e.

\innerproduct{\mathfrak{j}_k}{\mathfrak{j}_\ell}=\delta_{k\ell},\qquad\innerproduct{\psi}{\mathfrak{j}_k}=0\ \ \forall\ k.

(42)

(Note that
\innerproduct{\psi}{\mathfrak{j}_k}=\expectationvalue{J_k}{\psi}=0
.) In this case, the projective POVM

\bm{\mathcal{J}}\ \triangleq\ \Big\{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\ \outerproduct{\mathfrak{j}_1}{\mathfrak{j}_1},\ \dots,\ \outerproduct{\mathfrak{j}_R}{\mathfrak{j}_R},\ \Pi_{\perp}\Big\},

(43)

where
\Pi_{\perp}\triangleq I-\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\sum_{k=1}^{R}\outerproduct{\mathfrak{j}_k}{\mathfrak{j}_k}
represents projection outside the jump space, resolves whether a jump occurred (to leading order
\delta t
) and in which channel
k
.

We emphasize that if the conditions in (
42
) do not hold on the system alone, they can often be enforced by enlarging the Hilbert space via noiseless ancillae.

Rapid prepare-and-measure.

The RPM protocol prepares
\psi
, evolves for a short time
\delta t
, measures using the POVM (
43
), and repeats for
\nu
i.i.d. shots (total time
T=\nu\delta t
). Here we show that, for fixed
T
and in the limit
\delta t\to 0^{+}
, the resulting RPM Fisher information matches the collisional eigenrate QFI [Eq. (
36
) of Proposition
1
].

Assume eigenrate-only encoding, so that
J_{k}
are parameter-independent and all parameter dependence enters through
\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})
. Within a single time-bin
\delta t
, the Lindblad evolution (without control,
H_{c}=0
) gives

\rho(\bm{\theta};\delta t)=\psi+\delta t\sum_{k=1}^{R}\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})\left(J_{k}\psi J_{k}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{J_k^\dagger J_k}{\psi}\right)\\
+\order{\delta t^2},

(44)

where
\psi\triangleq\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
. Using (
43
) and (
42
), the single-bin detection probabilities are thus

\displaystyle p_{k}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)

\displaystyle=\delta t\,\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})\,\mu_{k}+\order{\delta t^2},

(45)

\displaystyle p_{0}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k}p_{k}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)+\order{\delta t^2},

(46)

where
p_{k}
is the
k
th jump probability and
p_{0}
collects the no-jump outcomes, including
\ket{\psi}
and
\Pi_{\perp}
(cf.
96
).

Lemma 2

(RPM Fisher Matrix)
.

Assume eigenrate-only encoding and suppose
\ket{\psi}
admits a distinguishable jump basis (
42
).
Consider the RPM protocol with bin size
\delta t
and
\nu=T/\delta t
i.i.d. shots (total time
T
). Then, in the limit

\delta t\to 0^{+}
with
T
fixed,

(F_{\rm rpm})_{\alpha\beta}(T)=4T\,\sum_{k=1}^{R}(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})(\partial_{\beta}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})\,\expectationvalue{J_k^\dagger J_k}{\psi}.

(47)

Equivalently,

(F_{\rm rpm})_{\alpha\beta}(T)=T\,(\dot{F}_{Q})_{\alpha\beta}(0^{+}),

(48)

where
(\dot{F}_{Q})_{\alpha\beta}(0^{+})
is the eigenrate QFI flow of Proposition
1
[Eq. (
36
)]
evaluated at
\rho(0)=\psi
.

We provide a short intuitive proof below, based on multi-Poisson counting statistics of quantum jumps.

Proof.

Use the single-bin jump outcomes from Eqs. (
45
)–(
46
). Over
\nu=T/\delta t
i.i.d. bins, the jump counts
N_{k}
converge jointly (as
\delta t\to 0^{+}
with
T
fixed) to
independent Poisson random variables with means

\lambda_{k}(\bm{\theta})=T\,\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})\,\mu_{k}.

(49)

For independent Poisson counts, the classical Fisher information is additive over
k
and equals

\displaystyle(F_{\rm rpm})_{\alpha\beta}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\frac{(\partial_{\alpha}\lambda_{k})(\partial_{\beta}\lambda_{k})}{\lambda_{k}}

(50)

\displaystyle=T\sum_{k=1}^{R}\frac{(\partial_{\alpha}\gamma_{k})(\partial_{\beta}\gamma_{k})}{\gamma_{k}}\,\mu_{k}

(51)

\displaystyle=4T\sum_{k=1}^{R}(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})(\partial_{\beta}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})\,\mu_{k},

(52)

which proves Eq. (
47
). Finally, Proposition
1
implies

(\dot{F}_{Q})_{\alpha\beta}(0^{+})=4\sum_{k}(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})(\partial_{\beta}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})\,\mu_{k}
,
which proves Eq. (
48
).
∎

Lemma
2
demonstrates that, as
\delta t\to 0^{+}
, the RPM protocol reduces eigenrate estimation to a multi-Poisson counting model if
\ket{\psi}
admits a distinguishable jump basis. Hence standard efficient estimators, such as the MLE, attain the classical Cramér-Rao bound asymptotically as
T\to\infty

63
;
56
.

We now connect RPM to the physical setting involving a large sensing time
T
and unlimited shots (
Scenario 1
; see Eq. (
38
)). As we show below, the optimal average (per parameter) QFI rate,
\max_{t}\sup_{\psi}\bar{F}_{Q}(t;\psi)/t
, coincides with the corresponding optimal RPM rate, and is attained by a RPM protocol in the limit
\delta t\to 0^{+}
. We point to Appendix
B.4
for a formal proof.

Theorem 3

(RPM Achieves the Eigenrate QCRB)
.

Assume eigenrate-only encoding (Proposition
1
) and define the optimal average (per parameter) QFI rate

\mathcal{R}^{\star}\triangleq\max_{t}\ \sup_{\psi}\ \frac{\bar{F}_{Q}(t;\psi)}{t},

(53)

where
\bar{F}_{Q}(t)\triangleq\Tr F_{Q}(t)/d
and
F_{Q}
is the collisional eigenrate QFI matrix (
36
).
Then
\mathcal{R}^{\star}=\lambda_{\max}(A)
, with positive operator
A=\sum_{k=1}^{R}c_{k}J_{k}^{\dagger}J_{k}
and
c_{k}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}4(\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}})^{2}/d
. If a maximizing state
\ket{\psi^\star}
admits a distinguishable jump POVM (
43
), then RPM with
\psi^{\star}
achieves
\mathcal{R}^{\star}
as
\delta t\to 0^{+}
:

\lim_{\delta t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\bar{F}_{\rm rpm}(\delta t;\psi^{\star})}{\delta t}=\mathcal{R}^{\star}.

(54)

Consequently, in the unlimited-shots scenario (
38
), RPM saturates the optimal eigenrate QCRB per parameter.

A notable feature of the RPM protocol is that optimality does not require coherent control during sensing. Rather, control enters only through our ability to prepare an optimal probe state, measure in the jump basis, and rapidly repeat the protocol.

Finally, we note that Theorem
3
straightforwardly extends to any quadratic form
F_{Q}[u]=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}(F_{Q})_{\alpha\beta}
, not just the per-parameter average.

IV
Examples

In this Section, we illustrate how the QFI bounds apply to an array of tasks in quantum noise metrology. Across all settings, the Markovian assumption enforces SQL scaling in the total sensing time
T
, while nontrivial enhancements arise from (i) how many dissipative channels are available
and
identifiable, and (ii) how densely the parameter dependence and probe correlations are spread across those channels. We focus on four representative use-cases: Sec.
IV.1
analyzes Heisenberg-in-
N
scaling in networked quantum sensors; Sec.
IV.2
analyzes super-Heisenberg scaling when collective
K
-body dissipation channels are present; Sec.
IV.3
analyzes learning Pauli noise rates as a high-dimensional multiparameter sensing problem; and Sec.
IV.4
analyzes subdiffraction incoherent imaging as a joint eigenrate/eigenframe estimation problem.

IV.1
Networked Quantum Noise Metrology

A quantum sensor network consists of
N
spatially distributed sensors, each coupled locally to a “sample” (e.g., condensed matter system) or classical field
93
;
139
;
32
;
43
;
133
. For stochastic signals, the local jump operators
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{R}
represent the
single-sensor
(single-body) operators and
\Gamma(\bm{\theta})
encodes spatial correlations of the stochastic signal across the sensor array. In this case, the number of dissipation channels (determined by the rank of
\Gamma
) scales as
R=q_{1}N
, where
q_{1}
is the number of distinct single-body operators per sensor (see Sec.
IV.2
); we take
q_{1}=1
below for clarity. Theorem
2
then suggests that
N^{2}
scaling is permissible, in principle, provided the dissipation kernel and quantum correlator matrix of the network are sufficiently dense.

Corollary 1

(Quantum Sensor Network Scaling)
.

Consider a quantum sensor network of
N
sensors with single-body jump operators
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
(so
R=N
). Assume that, for the parameter family of interest, the dissipation kernel is dense across channels (row-connectivity
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}=\Theta(N)
) and that the probe state realizes dense jump correlations (row-connectivity
r_{\mathcal{C}}=\Theta(N)
). Then for total sensing time
T=\nu t
, there exists a constant
c
independent of
N
and
T
, such that the average precision (per parameter) satisfies (cf.
9
)

\bar{\tau}\leq c\,TN^{2}.

(55)

Proof sketch.

For single-body operators,
R=N
. Under connectivity assumptions, Theorem
2
indicates that
\bar{\tau}\leq c\,TR^{2}=c\,TN^{2}
with a constant
c
absorbing operator-norm and kernel prefactors.
∎

The
N^{2}
scaling originates from the joint effect of (i) spatial correlations in the stochastic signal, encoded in
\Gamma(\bm{\theta})
, and (ii) quantum correlations between sensors, encoded in the correlator
\mathcal{C}
, as emphasized in Ref.
9
. Networked quantum noise metrology arguably provides one of the most experimentally relevant settings for quantum enhancements, e.g., via superdecoherence
80
;
108
, when interrogating many-body noise correlations
101
;
138
;
126
, and distributed sensing of stochastic forces and fields (including waveform estimation

;
81
;

) with bosonic degrees of freedom
7
;
6
;
130
;
51
;
44
.

Corollary
1
provides an upper bound but does not, by itself, guarantee achievability. In the important class of eigenrate-estimation problems, however, Theorem
3
provides a sufficient route to saturation whenever the canonical jump operators admit a probe with a distinguishable jump basis. We illustrate this with a simple example involving collective spin dissipation.

Example: Collective spin dissipation.

Consider collective spin dissipation along each direction
(x,y,z)
,

\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}(\rho)=\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\gamma_{\alpha}\left(S_{\alpha}\rho S_{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{S_\alpha^2}{\rho}\right),

(56)

with canonical jump operators

S_{\alpha}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{(\alpha)},\qquad\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}.

(57)

We now argue that we can estimate the rates
\bm{\theta}=(\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y},\gamma_{z})
in parallel, with precision scaling
\Theta(N^{2})
for each using (isotropic, quasi-classical) 3D quantum compass solutions
123
.

The (isotropic) 3D quantum compass
\ket{\psi_\circledast}
has uniform collective-spin fluctuations and vanishing means,
\expectationvalue{S_\alpha}{\psi_\circledast}=0

123
. Consequently, the off-diagonal symmetrized correlators vanish,
\expectationvalue{\{S_\alpha,S_\beta\}}{\psi_\circledast}=0
for
\alpha\neq\beta
, which together with
\commutator{S_\alpha}{S_\beta}=2i\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}S_{\gamma}
implies

\expectationvalue{S_\alpha S_\beta}{\psi_\circledast}=0\quad(\alpha\neq\beta).

(58)

Thus the states
\{\ket{\psi_\circledast},S_{x}\ket{\psi_\circledast},S_{y}\ket{\psi_\circledast},S_{z}\ket{\psi_\circledast}\}
are mutually orthogonal, i.e.,
\ket{\psi_\circledast}
admits a distinguishable jump basis in the sense of Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
). Moreover, the quantum compass has collective spin fluctuations

\expectationvalue{S_\alpha^2}{\psi_\circledast}=\Theta(N^{2}),

(59)

so an RPM protocol with a 3D quantum compass achieves
\Theta(TN^{2})
precision scaling for each
\gamma_{\alpha}
.

A GHZ probe is not optimal for this multiparameter task, since it is aligned along a preferred axis and therefore suppresses sensitivity in the longitudinal direction; in that case multiple probe settings may be required, with the GHZ aligned along a different axis in each setting. By contrast, separable probes can estimate the rates simultaneously but only with scaling
\Theta(TN)
.

IV.2
Super-Heisenberg Scaling

Suppose the jump operators consist of many-body terms, e.g.,
k
-local operators, so that the channel dimension
R
grows superlinearly with the system size
N
. Moreover, suppose the canonical jump operators are genuinely collective, i.e., sums of
k
-local operators. In this setting, the Heisenberg-in-
R
scaling from Theorem
2
translates into super-Heisenberg scaling in
N
, analogous in spirit to unitary metrology with nonlinear collective generators
5
;
103
.

Corollary 2

(Super-Heisenberg Scaling)
.

Consider a probe of
N
subsystems. Suppose the Lindbladian includes jump operators that act
nontrivially on at most
K
subsystems, and that for each support size
k\leq K
there are

q_{k}
independent operators per support. Then the number of dissipation channels obeys

R\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\binom{N}{k}=\Theta\!\left(N^{K}\right)\quad(N\gg 1,\;K\;\text{fixed}).

(60)

Moreover, if the dissipation kernel and correlator are dense across the active channels (
r_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}},\,r_{\mathcal{C}}=\Theta(R)
), then there exists a
constant
c
such that

\bar{\tau}\leq c\,TR^{2}=\Theta\!\left(TN^{2K}\right).

(61)

Proof.

The bound (
60
) follows by counting supports and the number of independent operators per support. Under the connectivity assumptions, Theorem
2
indicates that
\bar{\tau}\leq c\,T\,R^{2}
, and substituting (
60
) yields the claim.
∎

For finite-dimensional subsystems of local dimension
d_{\rm loc}
, the space of traceless operators on
k
subsystems has dimension
d_{\rm loc}^{2k}-1
. Hence, for fixed
k
, a Lindblad dissipator can involve at most
d_{\rm loc}^{2k}-1
linearly independent traceless jump operators, so
q_{k}\leq d_{\rm loc}^{2k}-1
(up to physical restrictions on the allowed jump algebra).

For bosonic modes, the local operator space is infinite-dimensional; a finite
q_{k}
arises by restricting the operator class. For instance, if each
k
-mode jump operator is a linear combination of normal-ordered monomials in
\{a_{j},a_{j}^{\dagger}\}
of bounded total degree
\leq r
, then
q_{k}
is finite and scales as
q_{k}=\order{r^{2k}}
. Here
k
counts the number of modes on which the operator acts, while
r
bounds the polynomial degree. For instance, single-photon loss has
(k,r)=(1,1)
, single-mode two-photon loss has
(k,r)=(1,2)
, Kerr-type processes have
r=2
, and higher-order nonlinear dissipation has
r>2
. Combining this with (
60
) indicates super-Heisenberg bounds with exponent set by the locality
K
, up to local factors
q_{k}
. Finally, we remark that if
K
grows with
N
, then
R
can, in principle, scale exponentially with
N

103
;
5
.

Regarding achievability of Corollary
2
, it is useful to consider eigenrate-estimation problems in which collective channels are resolved individually. In this setting, Lemma
2
and Theorem
3
imply that, when the canonical jump operators admit a probe with a distinguishable jump basis, an RPM protocol attains the corresponding eigenrate QCRB. For collective
k
-body channels whose jump amplitudes scale extensively with system size, this yields super-Heisenberg scaling
\Theta(TN^{2k})
, as we now demonstrate with an example.

Example: Collective multipole dissipation.

We extend the collective spin dissipation example to collective multipole dissipation, which showcases a concrete realization of super-Heisenberg scaling. This provides a family of collective channels for which we can, in principle, estimate
(N+1)^{2}
dissipation rates in parallel, with rank-
k
multipoles admitting super-Heisenberg scaling
\Theta(N^{2k})
.

Consider
N
spins with collective operators
S_{\alpha}
[Eq. (
57
)] and
S_{\pm}\triangleq S_{x}\pm iS_{y}
. The fully symmetric subspace carries spin quantum number
s=N/2
, and the collective irreducible spherical tensors,
T_{q}^{(k)}
, on this sector have multipole order
k=0,1,\dots,N
and components
q=-k,\dots,k
; see Section 15.3 of Shankar
109
. These operators correspond to degree-
k
polynomials in the collective spin operators
S_{z}
and
S_{\pm}
. There are
\sum_{k=0}^{N}(2k+1)=(N+1)^{2}
tensor operators in total. For example, up to normalization conventions,

T_{0}^{(0)}=S^{2},\quad T_{0}^{(1)}=S_{z},\quad T_{\pm 1}^{(1)}=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}S_{\pm},

(62)

and

\displaystyle T_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\left(3S_{z}^{2}-S^{2}\right),

(63)

\displaystyle T_{\pm 1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mp\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{z}S_{\pm}+S_{\pm}S_{z}\right),

(64)

\displaystyle T_{\pm 2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}S_{\pm}^{2},

(65)

where
S^{2}=S_{x}^{2}+S_{y}^{2}+S_{z}^{2}
.

For the moment, we take the non-scalar tensors (
k\neq 0
) as the canonical jump operators of the parametrized dissipator; we include the scalar tensor
T_{0}^{(0)}
thereafter. Physically, this model describes an ensemble of identical spins subject to genuinely collective, rotationally structured dissipation
108
. It should therefore be understood as an effective description of dissipative dynamics on the symmetric
s=N/2
manifold, which may arise from coupling to a common reservoir, cavity-mediated decay, or engineered reservoir interactions.

The tensors furnish a symmetry-adapted operator basis for collective multipole relaxation, which spans the operator space on the fully symmetric (
s=N/2
) subspace
83
. We thus assume the dissipator diagonalizes in this basis,

\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}(\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{q=-k}^{k}\gamma_{q}^{(k)}\left(T_{q}^{(k)}\rho T_{q}^{(k)\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{T_{q}^{(k)\dagger}T_{q}^{(k)}}{\rho}\right),

(66)

where
\gamma_{q}^{(k)}=\gamma_{q}^{(k)}(\bm{\theta})
denotes the parametrized multipole dissipation rates. For concreteness, we take the estimands to be

\bm{\theta}=(\gamma_{q}^{(k)}),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N,\ \ -k\leq q\leq k,

(67)

so that there are
(N+1)^{2}-1
parameters to estimate not including
\gamma_{0}^{(0)}
, which we eventually circle back to.

We now construct a method to simultaneously estimate the multipole rates. Consider the symmetric Dicke states
\{\ket{D_n^N}\}_{n=0}^{N}

113
, which satisfy

S_{z}\ket{D_n^N}=(N-2n)\ket{D_n^N},

(68)

where
n
counts the number of spins in the
\ket{\downarrow}
state. Dicke states form a basis of the fully symmetric subspace, with normalized projector

P_{\rm sym}\triangleq\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\outerproduct{D_n^N}{D_n^N}.

(69)

On this sector, the tensor operators are orthogonal,

\Tr\!\left(P_{\rm sym}\,T_{q}^{(k)\,\dagger}T_{q^{\prime}}^{(k^{\prime})}\right)=C^{(k)}(N)\,\delta_{qq^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}},

(70)

with
C^{(k)}(N)=\Theta(N^{2k})
for
k\neq 0
, while for the scalar operator
T_{0}^{(0)}=S^{2}
, we have
C^{(0)}=\Theta(N^{4})
. Moreover,

\Tr\!\left(P_{\rm sym}\,T_{q}^{(k)}\right)=0\qquad(k\neq 0).

(71)

We make use of these properties to construct an orthogonal jump basis in the sense of Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
).

To do so, we introduce an ancillary system of dimension
N+1
; for instance, if the ancilla consists of
L
spins, then we require only
L=\lceil\log_{2}(N+1)\rceil
ancillary spins. Define the Dicke-Choi state (cf.
9
),

\ket{\psi_{\rm sym}}\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\ket{n}_{\rm anc}\ket{D_n^N}_{\rm sys},

(72)

so that the reduced system state is the normalized projector onto the symmetric subspace,

\Tr_{\rm anc}(\outerproduct{\psi_{\rm sym}}{\psi_{\rm sym}})=P_{\rm sym}.

(73)

From Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
), it follows that
\ket{\psi}_{\rm sym}
and the jump vectors

\ket*{\mathfrak{j}_q^{(k)}}\triangleq(C^{(k)}(N))^{-1/2}\,(I_{\rm anc}\otimes T_{q}^{(k)})\ket{\psi_{\rm sym}},

(74)

form a distinguishable jump basis. Therefore, by Lemma
2
, an RPM protocol with the Dicke-Choi probe can estimate the
(N+1)^{2}-1
rates
\gamma_{q}^{(k)}
(
k\neq 0
) in parallel. Furthermore, since
\expectationvalue*{T_q^{(k)\dagger} T_q^{(k)}}{\psi_{\rm sym}}=C^{(k)}(N)=\Theta(N^{2k})
, the achievable precision for each parameter
\gamma_{q}^{(k)}
scales as

\tau(\gamma_{q}^{(k)})=\Theta(TN^{2k})\qquad(k\neq 0).

(75)

The prefactor depends on the
\order{1}
constants in
C^{(k)}(N)
together with a factor of
1/\gamma_{q}^{(k)}
per Eq. (
47
).

We now include the scalar relaxation rate
\gamma_{0}^{(0)}
generated by
T_{0}^{(0)}=S^{2}
. To make this channel identifiable, the probe must have support on more than one total-spin sector, equivalently
\operatorname{Var}(S^{2})>0
. Take
N
even for simplicity, and let
\ket{\mathfrak{s}}_{\rm sys}
denote a spin singlet, so that

T_{q}^{(k)}\ket{\mathfrak{s}}_{\rm sys}=0\qquad\forall\ k.

(76)

Introduce an extra ancillary state
\ket{\chi}_{\rm anc}
orthogonal to
\{\ket{n}_{\rm anc}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, and let

\ket*{\widetilde{\psi}_{\rm sym}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\psi_{\rm sym}}+\ket{\chi}_{\rm anc}\ket{\mathfrak{s}}_{\rm sys}\right).

(77)

For
k\neq 0
, the non-scalar jump states remain unchanged. We handle the scalar component by introducing the centered jump vector

\ket*{\mathfrak{j}_0^{(0)}}\triangleq\frac{I_{\rm anc}\otimes\Delta T_{0}^{(0)}\ket*{\widetilde{\psi}_{\rm sym}}}{\sqrt{\expectationvalue*{(I_{\rm anc}\otimes\Delta T_0^{(0)})^2}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{\rm sym}}}},

(78)

where
\Delta T_{0}^{(0)}\triangleq T_{0}^{(0)}-\expectationvalue*{T_0^{(0)}}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{\rm sym}}
. Since
T_{0}^{(0)}=S^{2}=N(N+2)
on the symmetric sector and
S^{2}=0
on the singlet, we have

\expectationvalue*{(I_{\rm anc}\otimes\Delta T_0^{(0)})^2}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{\rm sym}}=\Theta(N^{4}).

(79)

Thus the rate
\gamma_{0}^{(0)}
is also estimable with precision

\tau(\gamma_{0}^{(0)})=\Theta(TN^{4}).

(80)

This centered construction is consistent with the Hermitian-jump refinement of the QFI matrix, in which the disconnected correlator is replaced by the connected one (i.e., the variance); see the discussion following Theorem
1
and Proposition
B.1
of Appendix
B.2
.

Altogether, this protocol enables estimation of the
(N+1)^{2}
collective dissipation rates in parallel, with precision scaling
\Theta(TN^{2k})
for the
k\neq 0
multipoles and
\Theta(TN^{4})
for the scalar
k=0
channel.

It is plausible to estimate all multipole dissipation rates in parallel with separable probes. However, due to the independence of the local probes, the estimation precision of
\gamma_{q}^{(k)}
scales at best as
\order{TN^k}
for each
k
, indicating substantially reduced precision relative to the Dicke-Choi construction.

IV.3
Learning Pauli Noise Rates

In this Section, we consider estimation of Pauli noise rates for the Pauli noise channel
36
;
35
, a fundamental task in characterization and benchmarking of quantum devices
53
;
54
;
121
;
55
;
94
. This example is somewhat atypical from the viewpoint of quantum metrology: there is no Heisenberg-type scaling enhancement because the noise acts independently across Pauli channels (Theorem
2
). Nevertheless, there is an
exponential
separation in learning complexity once we constrain quantum resources—viz., learning with quantum memory versus without
20
;
19
;
18
;
14
;
64
.

Estimating (or learning) Pauli noise rates is a canonical instance of eigenrate estimation (cf. Proposition
1
): The channel structure is known (diagonal in the Pauli basis), while the Pauli error rates are unknown. Moreover, the RPM protocol achieves the QCRB for this task (Lemma
2
and Theorem
3
) provided the Pauli channels are distinguishable, as we show below.

Before proceeding, we clarify two points of terminology. Throughout this section, we use the term
learning
to emphasize reconstruction of an unstructured, high-dimensional noise model, although we formulate our analysis in metrological terms through quantum estimation theory rather than statistical learning theory
18
. In the asymptotic small-error regime relevant here, these viewpoints largely align, since the inverse Fisher information matrix controls local learning complexity
68
. We also fix the resource terminology: Specifically, we refer to a
quantum memory
here as noiseless ancillary qubits that may be initially entangled with the system qubits and measured jointly with them, but do not themselves undergo the parametrized dissipative dynamics. By contrast,
without quantum memory
means that no entangled ancillae are available, although classical registers and adaptive protocols are allowed.

Consider an
N
-qubit probe state
\psi\triangleq\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
subject over a short time
\delta t
to the Pauli channel with parameters
\bm{r}=\{r_{a}\}
,

\mathcal{E}_{\bm{r}}(\psi)=\left(1-\sum_{a=1}^{R}r_{a}\right)\psi+\sum_{a=1}^{R}r_{a}\,P_{a}\psi P_{a},

(81)

where
\{P_{a}\}_{a=1}^{R}
are the non-identity Pauli strings and
R=4^{N}-1
.
In the Markovian regime, this channel arises from a Lindblad generator with jump operators
J_{a}=P_{a}
and rates
\gamma_{a}
, so that
r_{a}=\gamma_{a}\,\delta t+\order{\delta t^2}
. Hence we focus on weak noise estimation (cf. the seminal work of
35
), though the main conclusions do not rely on this assumption
19
;
18
. We take the estimands as
\bm{\theta}=(\gamma_{a})_{a=1}^{R}
.

Complexity heuristics.

We provide a heuristic complexity analysis based on Projection-Valued Measures (PVMs), which makes the underlying dimensional obstruction for learning without quantum memory transparent. This PVM-based argument serves as an intuitive proxy for the full problem. However, the exponential complexity barrier persists under more refined analysis and general measurements: Appendix
D
proves that the same conclusion holds for arbitrary no-memory protocols in the short-time regime, including arbitrary POVMs supplemented by classical memory
18
; cf.
118
;
68
for related Fisher-information based analysis.

We quantify learning complexity through the average absolute precision,
\bar{\tau}
(see Definition
1
). For a protocol with total Fisher information matrix
F_{\rm tot}
accumulated over total sensing time
T
, the multiparameter Cramér-Rao bound implies

\bar{\tau}\leq\frac{1}{R}\Tr(F_{\rm tot}).

(82)

Indeed, given
\tau_{a}=1/\bm{\Sigma}_{aa}
and using
\bm{\Sigma}\geq F_{\rm tot}^{-1}
together with
(F_{\rm tot}^{-1})_{aa}\geq 1/(F_{\rm tot})_{aa}
, it follows that
\tau_{a}\leq(F_{\rm tot})_{aa}
and hence
\bar{\tau}\leq\Tr(F_{\rm tot})/R
.

To upper bound
\Tr(F_{\rm tot})
, decompose the protocol into
\nu
experimental configurations indexed by
j
, each run for time
t_{j}
with
T=\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}t_{j}
. Let
F^{(j)}
denote the Fisher matrix of configuration
j
, so that
F_{\rm tot}=\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}F^{(j)}
and
\Tr(F_{\rm tot})=\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\Tr(F^{(j)})
.

Estimating Pauli error rates is an eigenrate estimation problem and Proposition
1
implies a uniform spectral bound on the QFI rate. Since
F^{(j)}\leq F^{(j)}_{Q}
, we have

\lambda_{\max}\left(F^{(j)}\right)\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(F^{(j)}_{Q}\right)\leq t_{j}\,\gamma_{\min}^{-1},

(83)

where
\gamma_{\min}\triangleq\min_{a}\gamma_{a}
and
\|J_{a}\|=\|P_{a}\|=1
for Pauli jumps. Therefore,

\Tr\,(F^{(j)})\leq\lambda_{\max}(F^{(j)})\,\rank(F^{(j)})\leq t_{j}\gamma_{\min}^{-1}\,\rank(F^{(j)}).

(84)

Suppose that each experiment ends with a
projective
measurement on the available quantum Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
of dimension
D\triangleq\absolutevalue{\mathscr{H}}
. Then each configuration has at most
D
outcomes, so
\rank(F^{(j)})\leq D-1
. Combine these bounds to obtain

\Tr(F_{\rm tot})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}t_{j}\gamma_{\min}^{-1}(D-1)=T\gamma_{\min}^{-1}(D-1),

(85)

and substitute into Eq. (
82
) to reckon
2
2

2

This bounds the absolute precision; a bound on the (dimensionless) average relative precision reads
\bar{\tau}_{\rm rel}\leq T\gamma_{\rm max}(D-1)/R
.

\bar{\tau}\leq T\gamma_{\min}^{-1}\,\frac{D-1}{R}.

(86)

Learning without quantum memory.

Without quantum memory and assuming projective measurements, the available Hilbert space is
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{S}
with
D_{S}=2^{N}
, while
R=4^{N}-1
. Thus Eq. (
86
) yields

\bar{\tau}\leq T\gamma_{\min}^{-1}\,\frac{2^{N}-1}{4^{N}-1}=\Theta\!\left(T\gamma_{\min}^{-1}\,2^{-N}\right).

(87)

Equivalently, achieving a target
\bar{\tau}
without a quantum memory requires an exponential sensing time,

T_{\text{no-mem}}\geq\Omega\!\left(\bar{\tau}\gamma_{\min}2^{N}\right),

(88)

consistent with tight complexity bounds
18
;
64
.

Allowing a large POVM
\{E_{x}\}_{x=1}^{m}
with
m\gg D
can, in principle, render
F_{\rm tot}
full rank using fewer measurement settings. However, when only system degrees of freedom and classical registers are available, these measurements do not circumvent the exponential learning barrier; see Appendix
D
for proof. Intuitively, although a large POVM increases the number of outcomes, the resulting information is spread across exponentially many Pauli directions, so that
F_{\rm tot}
cannot provide uniformly strong information about all parameters. Equivalently, the root-score vectors
\{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{p_{x}}\}_{x}
[Eq. (
3
)] cannot resolve all directions in parameter space with comparable strength
118
;
68
.

This reflects a fundamental tradeoff when estimating an exponentially large number of parameters without quantum memory: either (i) we use distinguishable measurements (PVMs), which provide well-conditioned Fisher information on a low-dimensional subspace per measurement setting and therefore require exponentially many settings to identify the full model, or (ii) we use a large POVM to make
F_{\rm tot}
full rank with fewer settings, in which case the information per direction becomes diluted across the exponentially large parameter space, again resulting in an exponential sample complexity
18
;
68
.

Learning with quantum memory.

To circumvent this complexity barrier, we introduce a quantum memory
20
;
19
. Specifically, consider an
N
-qubit noiseless quantum memory
\mathscr{H}_{\rm mem}
, which evolves trivially, and prepare Bell pairs between system and memory,
\ket{\Phi_N}\triangleq\ket{\Phi}^{\otimes N}
with
\ket{\Phi}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}
. This enlarges the available Hilbert space to
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{S}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\rm mem}
with
D_{\rm mem}=4^{N}
, and indeed removes the exponential sampling overhead
19
;
18
;
14
;
64
, as we now show.

Using
\expectationvalue{A\otimes B}{\Phi_N}=\Tr(AB^\top)/2^{N}
, the Pauli basis becomes perfectly distinguishable (up to the transpose convention),

\expectationvalue{(P_a \otimes P_b^\top)}{\Phi_N}=\delta_{ab}.

(89)

In other words, the family
\{(P_{a}\otimes I)\ket{\Phi_N}\}_{a=1}^{R}
is orthonormal and orthogonal to the no-jump state
\ket{\Phi_N}
, so
\ket{\Phi_N}
admits a distinguishable jump basis spanning all
R=4^{N}-1
Pauli channels. Therefore, the jump POVM
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
of Eq. (
43
) can be implemented on
\mathscr{H}_{S}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\rm mem}
.

As a result, Lemma
2
and Theorem
3
apply directly: RPM with quantum memory achieves the optimal eigenrate QCRB, and yields a full-rank Fisher matrix for learning the complete Pauli model with a
single
experimental configuration. Hence all Pauli rates are simultaneously identifiable and achieving a target average precision
\bar{\tau}
requires time

T_{\rm mem}\geq\Omega\!\left(\bar{\tau}\gamma_{\min}\right),

(90)

indicating an exponential complexity advantage over learning without memory
19
;
18
;
64

Subdiffraction Quantum Imaging

In this Section, we close with a short coda from subdiffraction incoherent imaging to highlight joint
eigenrate
and
eigenframe
estimation, and to illustrate how our framework applies in the seemingly disparate setting of quantum-optical imaging

;

We consider the paradigmatic problem of imaging two weak, identical thermal emitters with centroid
\bar{x}
and separation
d
imaged through a diffraction-limited system with Gaussian point-spread function of width
\sigma

115
;
74
. As
d/\sigma\to 0
(subdiffraction limit), the two intensity profiles merge on the image plane and the image becomes nearly indistinguishable from that of a single emitter, so direct imaging (i.e., local intensity detection) suffers from “Rayleigh’s curse”

. Quantum mechanically, however, the separation information remains accessible: the QFI for
d
stays finite, but the optimal measurement basis depends on the unknown centroid
\bar{x}

74
;
115
. Estimating the separation

is an eigenrate estimation problem, while estimating the centroid
\bar{x}
is an eigenframe estimation problem (cf. Theorem
1
).

Following the weak-thermal model of Ref.
115
, the weak thermal field is approximated as a single-photon state characterized by a mutual coherence
\Gamma(\bar{x},d)
, defined as the two-point correlation matrix of the thermal field on the image plane, with brightness
\Tr(\Gamma)=\varepsilon\ll 1

73
. In our framework, we regard
\Gamma(\bar{x},d)
as the dissipation matrix of a weak Markovian excitation process with jump operators
L_{u}=\hat{a}_{u}^{\dagger}
, indexed by image-plane coordinate
u
, acting on the optical vacuum. In the weak-signal regime (Scenario 3, Eq. (
40
)), the per-measurement QFI reads
F_{Q}(\delta t)\approx\delta t\,\dot{F}_{Q}(0^{+})
and is controlled by the initial QFI flow
\dot{F}_{Q}(0^{+})
, so Theorem
1
applies directly with vacuum correlator
\mathcal{C}(0^{+})=I
. For
\nu
identical bins and measurements,
F_{Q}^{\rm tot}=\nu F_{Q}(\delta t)
.

For identical emitters and
d/\sigma\ll 1
, translation symmetry suggests an eigen-decomposition,

\Gamma(\bar{x},d)=V(\bar{x})\,\Lambda(d)\,V^{\dagger}(\bar{x}),

(91)

where
V(\bar{x})
encodes the centroid as a coordinate shift, while the nonzero eigenvalues
\Lambda(d)=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{+}(d),\lambda_{-}(d))
depend only on the separation
115
;
74
. Thus
\bar{x}
appears purely as an eigenframe parameter (generated by
K_{\bar{x}}=(\partial_{\bar{x}}V)V^{\dagger}

), whereas

appears purely through the eigenrates. Applying Theorem

and assuming a Gaussian point-spread function yields (see Appendix

\lim_{d/\sigma\to 0}F_{Q}^{\rm tot}=\frac{\nu\varepsilon}{\sigma^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\[2.0pt]

(92)

which is finite as

and reproduces the known separation QFI

;

In the eigenbasis of
\Gamma(\bar{x},d)

\bar{x}
is known, a spatial-mode demultiplexing (SPADE) measurement aligned to the centroid projects onto this eigenbasis and realizes an eigenrate-estimation protocol for
\lambda_{-}
that saturates the separation QFI

;

, precisely as in the RPM protocol. However, because
\bar{x}
enters through the eigenframe

\bar{x}

\bar{x}
is unknown
a priori
, a fixed SPADE measurement is generally not optimal. One needs adaptive centroid alignment or a more general joint measurement strategy to recover the sub-Rayleigh separation advantage

Similar considerations apply to active quantum imaging, where a sample undergoing incoherent emission and/or absorption is probed with a structured quantum-optical field

\mathcal{C}(0^{+})
acquires a photon-number enhancement that depends on the probe state (e.g., Fock or two-mode squeezed), and the probe is initialized in the eigenmode basis of

Appendix A
Details on the Collisional Purification

In this Appendix, we provide a self-contained derivation of the collisional purification (or purification) used to realize Markovian open-system dynamics [see Sec.
II.2
], by showing (i) how a vacuum, memoryless bosonic bath produces a generic Lindblad dissipator, and (ii) how the same physics arises as the continuous-time limit of a discrete repeated-interaction (collision) model. Throughout, the dilation provides a
purification
of the reduced dynamics in the sense that if the probe is initialized in a pure state
\rho(0)=\psi
, with
\psi=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, and each bath component is initialized in the vacuum, then the joint system-environment state remains pure under the global unitary evolution, and the reduced system’s time evolution exactly agrees with the dynamics predicted by the Lindblad master equation.

Target dynamics.

We take the reduced probe state
\rho(t)
to satisfy a parametrized Lindblad master equation,

\partial_{t}\rho(t)=-i\commutator{H_c(t)}{\rho(t)}+\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}(\rho(t)),

(A1)

where
H_{c}(t)
is a (parameter-independent) control Hamiltonian, and the parametrized dissipator is written in the canonical form as

\mathscr{D}_{\bm{\theta}}(\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})\left(J_{k}(\bm{\theta})\,\rho\,J_{k}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{J_k^\dagger(\bm{\theta})J_k(\bm{\theta})}{\rho}\right).

(A2)

We realize (
A1
) by a unitary dilation on
\mathscr{H}_{S}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\rm env}
generated by

H_{\rm tot}(\bm{\theta};t)=H_{c}(t)+H(\bm{\theta};t),

(A3)

where the system-bath interaction reads

H(\bm{\theta};t)=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})}\,J_{k}(\bm{\theta})\otimes e_{k}^{\dagger}(t)+{\rm h.c.},

(A4)

The bath operators
\{e_{k}(t)\}
are bosonic annihilation operators satisfying

\commutator{e_k(t)}{e_{k'}^\dagger(t')}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\qquad\Tr\!\big(\varphi_{\rm env}e_{k}(t)\big)=0,\qquad\Tr\!\big(\varphi_{\rm env}e_{k}(t)e_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\big)=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(A5)

with all other second moments vanishing in the vacuum. The state of the system is obtained from the global state

\Psi(t)\triangleq U(t)\,[\psi\otimes\varphi_{\rm env}]\,U^{\dagger}(t)
by
\rho(t)=\Tr_{\rm env}\Psi(t)
with
\rho(0)=\psi
. We define this construction as the canonical collisional purification.

A.1
Collisional Model Implies Markovian Evolution

We sketch how the memoryless bath specified in (
A4
)–(
A5
) yields the Lindblad form (
A1
), emphasizing how Gaussianity and Markovianity reduce the Kubo cumulant expansion to second order
66
.

Interaction picture.

First, define
U_{c}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\big(-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\,H_{c}(s)\big)
and the reduced state

\tilde{\rho}(t)=U_{c}^{\dagger}(t)\rho(t)U_{c}(t)
in the system’s interaction picture governed by quantum control.
The interaction-picture propagator on
\mathscr{H}_{S}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\rm env}
is

U_{I}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\big(-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\,\tilde{H}(s)\big)
, where

\tilde{H}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{R}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}(\bm{\theta})}\,\tilde{J}_{k}(\bm{\theta};t)\otimes e_{k}^{\dagger}(t)+{\rm h.c.},\qquad\tilde{J}_{k}(\bm{\theta};t)\triangleq U_{c}^{\dagger}(t)J_{k}(\bm{\theta})U_{c}(t).

(A6)

The reduced interaction-picture dynamics are

\tilde{\rho}(t)=\Tr_{\rm env}\!\left[U_{I}(t)\,\big(\psi\otimes\varphi_{\rm env}\big)\,U_{I}^{\dagger}(t)\right],

(A7)

with
\tilde{\rho}(0)=\psi
.

Cumulant expansion and Gaussian truncation.

The Kubo cumulant expansion
66
expresses the reduced map generated by
\tilde{H}(t)
in terms of cumulant superoperators. Schematically,

\tilde{\rho}(t)=\exp\!\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}(t)\right]\psi,

(A8)

the first two cumulants are

\displaystyle K_{1}(t)\rho

\displaystyle=-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\,\Tr_{\rm env}\!\big[\tilde{H}(s),\rho\otimes\varphi_{\rm env}\big],

(A9)

\displaystyle K_{2}(t)\rho

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\int_{0}^{s}\differential{s}^{\prime}\,\Tr_{\rm env}\!\big[\tilde{H}(s),[\tilde{H}(s^{\prime}),\rho\otimes\varphi_{\rm env}]\big].

(A10)

Since
\Tr(\varphi_{\rm env}e_k(t))=0
, one has
K_{1}(t)=0
. Moreover, the bath is Gaussian and Markovian, so all connected cumulants beyond second order vanish,
K_{n>2}(t)=0
. Thus the reduced dynamics are entirely captured by
K_{2}(t)
.

Time-local evolution via white noise.

Finally, the Markov assumption (
A5
) collapses the double integral in
K_{2}(t)
to a single time integral: the bath trace produces
\delta(s-s^{\prime})
, so the contribution from
s^{\prime}\approx s
dominates and the evolution becomes time-local. Concretely,

Remark.

A.2
Discrete-Time Model

In this Appendix, we construct an explicit discrete-time dilation that converges to the Lindblad evolution (

Let the environment be a chain of independent, identical bath elements

(A15)

For
R
jump channels, take each

to consist of
R

Discretize time as

with system-bath interaction Hamiltonian

(A18)

The

change in the

Define

Expanding

(A21)

A short calculation then gives the increment

(A22)

where

(A23)

Thus

coincides with the Lindblad generator in (
A1
)–(
A2

Iterating
n=t/\Delta t
steps gives the global unitary

(A24)

and the joint state

\Psi_{1:n}(\bm{\theta})=U_{1:n}(\bm{\theta})\big(\psi\otimes\varphi_{\rm env}\big)U_{1:n}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})
,
which is pure whenever
\psi
is pure.
The reduced state is
\rho(t)=\Tr_{\rm env}\Psi_{1:n}(\bm{\theta})
.
Taking

with

\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\rho(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{t}\differential{s}\,\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\theta},s}\right)(\psi),

(A25)

which is the continuous-time Markovian evolution generated by (
A1
).

Appendix C
Uhlmann Extremality

In this Appendix, we analyze the gauge freedom in purifications of the reduced state
\rho(\bm{\theta};t)
and characterize the Uhlmann-extremal (tight) purification. In the main text we primarily work with the canonical collisional purification (Appendix
A
), which is convenient and useful for obtaining upper bounds, but is not generically tight for any given
\psi

on mixed states and the Fubini–Study metric

on purifications, where the Bures metric is directly related to the QFI via

g_{uv}^{\rm Bures}\big[\rho(\bm{\theta};t)\big]=\tfrac{1}{4}(F_{Q})_{uv}\big[\rho(\bm{\theta};t)\big].

(C1)

Fix a time
t
and a parameter value
\bm{\theta}
. The canonical collisional purification reads

\ket{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)}=U(\bm{\theta};t)\,\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\varphi_{\rm in}},

(C2)

where
\ket{\psi}
is a pure system probe state and the environment is initialized in the bosonic vacuum

\ket{\varphi_{\rm in}}=\ket{0}
. The corresponding reduced output states are

\rho(\bm{\theta};t)=\Tr_{\rm env}\outerproduct{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)}{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)},\qquad\varphi_{\rm out}(\bm{\theta};t)=\Tr_{\rm sys}\outerproduct{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)}{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)}.

(C3)

Any other purification of
\rho(\bm{\theta};t)
(on the same environment Hilbert space) is obtained by a
\bm{\theta}
-dependent environment unitary,

\ket{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)}\longmapsto\ket{\Psi_{U_E}(\bm{\theta};t)}\triangleq\big(I\otimes U_{E}(\bm{\theta})\big)\ket{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)},

(C4)

which leaves
\rho(\bm{\theta};t)
invariant. By Uhlmann’s theorem
119
, a purification variational
Ansatz
admits the Bures metric. That is, for any tangent vectors

at
\bm{\theta}

g^{\rm Bures}_{uv}\big[\rho(\bm{\theta};t)\big]=\min_{U_{E}}\,g^{\rm FS}_{uv}\big[\Psi_{U_{E}}(\bm{\theta};t)\big],

(C5)

where the minimum ranges over
\bm{\theta}
-dependent environment unitaries
U_{E}(\bm{\theta})
. Let
U_{E}^{\star}(\bm{\theta})
denote an extremal gauge and define the corresponding extremal purification

\ket{\Psi^\star(\bm{\theta};t)}\;\triangleq\;\big(I\otimes U_{E}^{\star}(\bm{\theta})\big)\ket{\Psi(\bm{\theta};t)}.

(C6)

Then the extremal Fubini–Study metric coincides with the Bures metric of the reduced state,

g^{\star}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\theta};t)\;\triangleq\;g^{\rm FS}_{\alpha\beta}\big[\Psi^{\star}(\bm{\theta};t)\big]\;=\;g^{\rm Bures}_{\alpha\beta}\big[\rho(\bm{\theta};t)\big].

(C7)

Below we characterize Uhlmann extremality through independent Lyapunov equations satisfied by the extremal environment generators
h^{\star}_{\alpha}\triangleq i{U_{E}^{\star}}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\alpha}U_{E}^{\star})

U_{E}^{\star}(\bm{\theta})=I_{E}

\alpha

Throughout we use the shorthand
\Psi\triangleq\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}
to denote the density matrix of the global pure state.

C.1
Extremality Condition

Let
\partial_{\alpha}
denote differentiation with respect to
\theta_{\alpha}\in\bm{\theta}
, and define a real direction
u
with
\partial_{u}\triangleq\sum_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}
. Introduce the Hermitian generators for the collisional unitary and the environment gauge,

5

5

Here,

relates to

of Appendix

(C10)

Since the Fubini-Study metric is invariant under
(I\otimes U_{E})

Details on Learning Pauli Noise

In this Appendix, we derive a precision bound for the RPM protocol that indicates exponential complexity overhead for learning Pauli noise rates without quantum memory. The arguments rely on general system-only POVMs, as opposed to the simplification using projective measurements (PVMs) in the main text (Sec.
IV.3
). This analysis applies to the short-time, or equivalently weak-noise, Pauli noise rate problem; cf.
35
for a complementary approach in the weak-noise setting and Refs.
118
;
68
for related Fisher-information analyses and more broadly Refs.
20
;
19
;
18
;
14
;
64
.

Consider an
N
-qubit pure probe state
\ket{\psi}
evolving for a short time bin
\delta t
under the Pauli-noise channel:

\rho(\bm{\theta};\delta t)=\left(1-\delta t\sum_{a=1}^{R}\gamma_{a}\right)\psi+\delta t\sum_{a=1}^{R}\gamma_{a}\,P_{a}\psi P_{a}+\order{\delta t^2},

(D1)

where
\{P_{a}\}_{a=1}^{R}
are the non-identity Pauli strings,
R=4^{N}-1
,
\bm{\theta}=(\gamma_{a})_{a=1}^{R}
are the estimands with
\gamma_{a}\geq 0
the Pauli noise rates. Let
D\triangleq 2^{N}
denote the system dimension, and define
\gamma_{\min}\triangleq\min_{a}\gamma_{a}
.

We consider an arbitrary system-only POVM
\{E_{x}\}_{x}
on the system Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{S}
, with outcome probabilities

p_{x}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)=\Tr(E_x\,\rho(\bm{\theta};\delta t)).

(D2)

Let
F(\delta t)
denote the associated classical Fisher information matrix with respect to the parameters
\bm{\theta}
, and consider the short-time Fisher rate
\dot{F}(0^{+})=\lim_{\delta t\to 0}F(\delta t)/\delta t
[see Eq. (
40
)]. We now establish the following complexity bound on the average precision for any RPM protocol.

Proposition D.1

(RPM Complexity: Learning Pauli Noise Without Quantum Memory)
.

Consider any short-time RPM protocol of total sensing time
T
, built from an arbitrary sequence of rapid prepare-and-measure rounds on the system alone (no quantum memory); a classical register and adaptivity are allowed. Then the total Fisher information satisfies

\Tr(F_{\rm tot})\leq T\,\frac{D-1}{\gamma_{\min}},

(D3)

at leading order in
\delta t
. Consequently, the average absolute precision (per parameter) obeys

\bar{\tau}\leq\frac{T}{\gamma_{\min}(D+1)}=\Theta\!\left(T\gamma_{\min}^{-1}2^{-N}\right).

(D4)

Proof.

Consider a single short-time round with probe state
\psi
, POVM
\{E_{x}\}_{x}
, and duration
\delta t
. For each measurement outcome
x
, define

e_{x}\triangleq\Tr(E_x\psi),\qquad q_{x,a}\triangleq\Tr(E_x P_a \psi P_a).

(D5)

Expanding the outcome probabilities (
D2
) to first order in
\delta t
gives

p_{x}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)=e_{x}+\delta t\sum_{a=1}^{R}\gamma_{a}\big(q_{x,a}-e_{x}\big)+\order{\delta t^2}.

(D6)

Hence

\partial_{\gamma_{b}}p_{x}=\delta t\big(q_{x,b}-e_{x}\big)+\order{\delta t^2}.

(D7)

Now split the outcomes into two classes:

If
e_{x}>0
, then
p_{x}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)=e_{x}+\order{\delta t}
is order unity, while
\partial_{\gamma_{b}}p_{x}=\order{\delta t}
. Therefore the contribution of these outcomes to
F(\delta t)/\delta t
vanishes as
\delta t\to 0
.

If
e_{x}=0
, then positivity implies
E_{x}\ket{\psi}=0
, and

p_{x}(\bm{\theta};\delta t)=\delta t\sum_{a=1}^{R}\gamma_{a}q_{x,a}+\order{\delta t^2},\qquad\partial_{\gamma_{b}}p_{x}=\delta t\,q_{x,b}+\order{\delta t^2}.

(D8)

Thus only these outcomes contribute to the short-time Fisher rate, and

\dot{F}_{bc}(0^{+})=\sum_{x:\,e_{x}=0}\frac{q_{x,b}q_{x,c}}{\sum_{a=1}^{R}\gamma_{a}q_{x,a}}.

(D9)

Taking the trace,

\Tr(\dot F(0^+))=\sum_{x:\,e_{x}=0}\frac{\sum_{b=1}^{R}q_{x,b}^{2}}{\sum_{a=1}^{R}\gamma_{a}q_{x,a}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\sum_{x:\,e_{x}=0}\frac{\sum_{b=1}^{R}q_{x,b}^{2}}{\sum_{a=1}^{R}q_{x,a}}.

(D10)

For each
x
and
a
,

0\leq q_{x,a}\leq\Tr(E_x),

(D11)

since
P_{a}\psi P_{a}
is a rank-
1
projector. Therefore

\sum_{b=1}^{R}q_{x,b}^{2}\leq\Tr(E_x)\sum_{a=1}^{R}q_{x,a},

(D12)

and so

\Tr(\dot F(0^+))\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\sum_{x:\,e_{x}=0}\Tr(E_x).

(D13)

Define

E_{\perp}\triangleq\sum_{x:\,e_{x}=0}E_{x}.

(D14)

Because each
E_{x}\geq 0
and
e_{x}=\expectationvalue{E_x}{\psi}=0
, we have
E_{x}\ket{\psi}=0
for every such
x
, hence

E_{\perp}\ket{\psi}=0.

(D15)

Moreover,
0\leq E_{\perp}\leq\sum_{x}E_{x}=I
, so
E_{\perp}
is supported on the
(D-1)
-dimensional orthogonal complement of
\ket{\psi}
. Therefore

\sum_{x:\,e_{x}=0}\Tr(E_x)=\Tr(E_\perp)\leq D-1.

(D16)

Combining the above inequalities yields

\Tr(\dot F(0^+))\leq\frac{D-1}{\gamma_{\min}}.

(D17)

Now consider an arbitrary RPM protocol, consisting of many rounds and taking a total time
T=\sum_{j}\delta t_{j}
. In each round
j
, possibly conditioned on the entire previous classical record, the same one-round bound applies. Hence, within an interval
\delta t_{j}
,

\Tr(F^{(j)})=\delta t_{j}\Tr(\dot F(0^+))+\order{\delta t_j^2}\leq\delta t_{j}\,\frac{D-1}{\gamma_{\min}}+\order{\delta t_j^2}.

(D18)

Summing over all rounds gives

\Tr(F_{\rm tot})\leq T\,\frac{D-1}{\gamma_{\min}},

(D19)

at leading order. Finally, using
R=D^{2}-1
and the average absolute precision bound
\bar{\tau}\leq\Tr(F_{\rm tot})/R
, which follows from the Cramér-Rao bound, we obtain

\bar{\tau}\leq T\,\frac{D-1}{\gamma_{\min}(D^{2}-1)}=\frac{T}{\gamma_{\min}(D+1)}=\Theta\!\left(T\,\gamma_{\min}^{-1}2^{-N}\right),

(D20)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Thus, without quantum memory, the average precision in the RPM protocol is exponentially suppressed in
N
, even when allowing arbitrary system-only POVMs.

Remarks.

Spectrum of
\Gamma
.

The identical-emitter assumption implies global translations act unitarily on the mode space,

\Gamma(\bar{x},d)=T(\bar{x})\,\Gamma(0,d)\,T^{\dagger}(\bar{x}),

(E8)

where
(T(\bar{x})f)(u)=f(u-\bar{x})
.
At
\bar{x}=0
, define the overlap of the mode functions,

S(d)\triangleq\innerproduct{\phi_1}{\phi_2}=e^{-d^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})}.

(E9)

The rank-
2
operator
\Gamma(0,d)
then has eigenvalues
74

\lambda_{\pm}(d)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(1\pm S(d)\big),

(E10)

and normalized eigenvectors

\ket{\phi_\pm(d)}=\frac{\ket{\phi_1}\pm\ket{\phi_2}}{\sqrt{2(1\pm S(d))}}.

(E11)

Thus
\Gamma(0,d)=V_{0}(d)\Lambda(d)V_{0}^{\dagger}(d)
where

V_{0}(d)=(\ket{\phi_+(d)},\ket{\phi_-(d)})
and
\Lambda(d)=\mathrm{diag}\{\lambda_{+}(d),\lambda_{-}(d)\}
, and

V(\bar{x},d)=T(\bar{x})V_{0}(d),\qquad\Gamma(\bar{x},d)=V(\bar{x},d)\Lambda(d)V^{\dagger}(\bar{x},d).

(E12)

In the subdiffraction regime
d/\sigma\to 0
, the dependence of
V_{0}(d)
on
d
is subleading compared to the eigenvalue dependence of
\Lambda(d)
; in particular we may use the approximations

V(\bar{x},d)\approx V(\bar{x})=T(\bar{x})V_{0}(0),\qquad\lambda_{+}(d)\approx\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{d^{2}}{16\sigma^{2}}\right),\qquad\lambda_{-}(d)\approx\frac{\varepsilon d^{2}}{16\sigma^{2}}.

(E13)

Subdiffraction QFI matrix.

We now evaluate the QFI matrix using Theorem
1
. Since
\mathcal{C}(0^{+})=I
, the QFI is determined completely by the dissipation kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha\beta}
.

We first calculate the QFI for
\bar{x}
. From
V(\bar{x},d)=T(\bar{x})V_{0}(d)
, the centroid dependence enters through the translation unitary. Writing
T(\bar{x})=e^{-i\bar{x}\hat{p}}
with generator
\hat{p}=-i\partial_{u}
on mode functions, the eigenframe connection is

K_{\bar{x}}=(\partial_{\bar{x}}V)V^{\dagger}=(\partial_{\bar{x}}T)T^{\dagger}=-i\hat{p},\qquad K_{\bar{x}}^{\dagger}=-K_{\bar{x}}.

(E14)

Combining this with the rate equation [Eq. (
24
)], we have
\partial_{\bar{x}}\Lambda=0
and hence
\partial_{\bar{x}}\Gamma=[K_{\bar{x}},\Gamma]
. Therefore,

\partial_{\bar{x}}\Gamma+\{K_{\bar{x}},\Gamma\}=[K_{\bar{x}},\Gamma]+\{K_{\bar{x}},\Gamma\}=2K_{\bar{x}}\Gamma,

(E15)

and application of Theorem
1
yields

\mathcal{K}_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}=4K_{\bar{x}}\Gamma K_{\bar{x}}^{\dagger}.

(E16)

Contracting with
\mathcal{C}(0^{+})=I
gives

(F_{Q})_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}(\delta t)=4\Tr(K_{\bar{x}}\Gamma K_{\bar{x}}^\dagger)=4\Tr(\hat p^{\,2}\Gamma).

(E17)

In the limit
d/\sigma\to 0
, a direct calculation for the Gaussian PSF gives
\Tr(\hat p^{\,2}\Gamma)=\varepsilon/(4\sigma^{2})

73
, hence

\lim_{d/\sigma\to 0}(F_{Q})_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}(\delta t)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma^{2}}.

(E18)

We now calculate the QFI for
d
. In the subdiffraction regime
V(\bar{x},d)\approx V(\bar{x})
, we have
\partial_{d}\Gamma\approx V(\partial_{d}\Lambda)V^{\dagger}
. Thus

\mathcal{K}_{dd}\approx V\big((\partial_{d}\Lambda)\Lambda^{-1}(\partial_{d}\Lambda)\big)V^{\dagger},

(E19)

so with
\mathcal{C}(0^{+})=I
,

(F_{Q})_{dd}(\delta t)\approx\frac{(\partial_{d}\lambda_{+})^{2}}{\lambda_{+}}+\frac{(\partial_{d}\lambda_{-})^{2}}{\lambda_{-}}.

(E20)

Using
S^{\prime}(d)=-(d/(4\sigma^{2}))S(d)
and
\partial_{d}\lambda_{\pm}=\pm(\varepsilon/2)S^{\prime}(d)
, we obtain

\lim_{d/\sigma\to 0}(F_{Q})_{dd}(\delta t)=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\sigma^{2}}.

(E21)

We now calculate the cross terms of the QFI matrix. To leading order, the off-diagonal term of the dissipation kernel takes the form

\mathcal{K}_{\bar{x}d}\ \propto\ V\,(\partial_{d}\Lambda^{1/2})\Lambda^{1/2}\,V^{\dagger}\,K_{\bar{x}}^{\dagger},

(E22)

so that, in the
\Gamma
-eigenbasis,

\Tr\!\big(\mathcal{K}_{\bar{x}d}\big)\ \propto\ \Tr\!\big((\partial_{d}\Lambda^{1/2})\Lambda^{1/2}\,(K^{\prime}_{\bar{x}})^{\dagger}\big).

(E23)

Here
(\partial_{d}\Lambda^{1/2})\Lambda^{1/2}
is diagonal, while
K^{\prime}_{\bar{x}}
has vanishing diagonal entries in the real eigenbasis
\phi_{\pm}
. Hence

\Tr\!\big(\mathcal{K}_{\bar{x}d}\big)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad(F_{Q})_{\bar{x}d}(\delta t)=0.

(E24)

Collecting everything together yields the subdiffraction QFI matrix for
(\bar{x},d)
after
\nu
measurements:

\lim_{d/\sigma\to 0}F_{Q}^{\rm tot}=\frac{\nu\varepsilon}{\sigma^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\tfrac{1}{4}\end{pmatrix},

(E25)

where
\nu\varepsilon
is the total mean-number of photons detected. This agrees with the fundamental quantum limits for centroid and separation estimation in the
weak-thermal, Gaussian-PSF model
115
;
98
;
73
.
