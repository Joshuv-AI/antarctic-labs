Kinetic model-based prediction of fuel ignition quality using ignition delay times
Authors: Jiacheng Zhou, Gang Wu, Yong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Octane numbers (ON) and derived cetane numbers (DCN) are widely used as indicators to quantify the ignition qualities of gasoline- and diesel-type fuels, respectively. In this study, a chemical kinetics-based methodology is proposed for quantitatively relating ignition delay times (IDTs) to the ON and subsequently deriving correlations to predict DCN. The methodology is based on the ignition-delay-time equivalence principle, according to which fuels exhibiting ignition delay times identical to those of primary reference fuel (PRF) mixtures under well-defined RON-like thermodynamic conditions are assigned the same octane number. To assess the predictive capability of this methodology, ignition delay times of both test fuels and PRF mixtures were simulated under RON-like conditions using detailed chemical kinetic mechanisms for toluene primary reference fuels (TPRF) developed independently by various research groups. The predicted ON values were validated against experimentally measured ON data obtained using standard ASTM methods. For PRF, TRF, and TPRF blends under RON-like conditions over 30–80 bar at φ = 1.0, the best overall correlation was achieved at 40 bar, with an R2 of 0.988 and an RMSE of 1.322. The validation results confirm that the proposed kinetic modeling approach provides reliable and accurate predictions of ON, with improved accuracy correlating closely with the precision of simulated ignition delay times. Subsequently, a linear correlation between the predicted ON and DCN values, measured in accordance with ASTM D6890 standards, was established. The predicted DCN values exhibit satisfactory agreement with experimental data under the specified RON-like conditions. The developed kinetic-based methodology provides valuable theoretical insights for engine design and operation, as well as the development of future fuels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56712-1.

Introduction

Cetane number (CN) and octane number (ON) are used to quantify fuel ignition characteristics in compression ignition (CI) and spark ignition (SI) engines, respectively, which are the key fuel properties affecting engine combustion and emission performance.

By definition, the fuel’s ON was evaluated under highly constrained engine test conditions, in a particular type of cooperative fuels research (CFR) test engine1–3. The ON of a given test fuel was determined by associating the spontaneous combustion behavior with the binary mixture of n-heptane and iso-octane (PRF), where 0 ON corresponds to a PRF with 100% n-heptane and 100 ON corresponds to a PRF with 100% iso-octane4,5. Under the same conditions as the test fuel in the CFR engine, the ON of any fuel is equal to the percentage of iso-octane number in PRF. In general, fuels with higher octane numbers are more resistant to knock than those with lower values because, under the relevant high-temperature and high-pressure end-gas conditions, they require a longer induction time before the onset of rapid chain-branching-driven autoignition. The fuel mixtures were used as the test fuels, and the ON of the fuel mixtures component was similar to that of the single component fuel.

The CN is measured in a homologous way to the RON1,2, whose specifications are defined in a CFR single-cylinder engine according to ASTM standards6. The same IDT of the test fuel was obtained with the matched PRF mixture. Ryan et al.7 carried out the work of identifying IDTs with constant volume combustion chambers (CVCC), resulting from the cost and time-consuming testing. Since CN is not measured in the CFR engine, but by CVCC, the quantity measured is called derived cetane number (DCN), leading to the development of the commercial Ignition Quality Tester (IQT)8, which offers advantages over the CFR engine measurement of CN, including less fuel consumption, shorter test times, and a higher degree of reproducibility. Currently, these CVCCs are the most commonly used instruments for testing the quality of the ignition and have been widely used to determine the DCN8–12.

The operation expertise and expensive standard fuels are required in above experimental methods. Thus, in addition to experimental methods, the RON and DCN of actual fuels can be interpreted in a complementary way through more basic spontaneous combustion measures, such as modeling and simulation-based techniques. A non-linear blending rule for the mixture of toluene, iso-octane and n-heptane (TPRF) was proposed by Morgan et al.3. Kalghatgi et al.13 developed empirical correlations for TPRF surrogates and RON/MON. Griffiths et al.14 found a better correlation between ONs occurred in the minimum autoignition temperature, but were unable to find suitable correlations between IDT and ONs. Mehl et al.15 correlated the slope of IDT curves in Arrhenius plots with experimental octane sensitivity measurements for the fuel, as indicated a higher slope in NTC regions showed higher sensitivity of the fuel. Besides, for the study of predicting RON and MON by using IDT values directly, Badra et al.16 correlated IDT with RON and MON of PRF and TPRF mixtures at various constant volume conditions (835 K, 20 atm and 825 K, 25 atm and 850 K and 50 atm and 980 K, 45 atm). With the prediction of RON, simulated IDTs at 850 K and 50 atm showed the best results. The works of Westbrook et al.17 and Corrubia et al.18 used the detailed gasoline surrogate chemical kinetic model19 to simulate the IDT from pressure profile, pressure and volume profile-driven zero-dimensional homogeneous reactor module for fuels in CFR condition, respectively. The correlations between the simulated IDT with their RON and MON values are estimated. A methodology of estimating the RON and MON from homogeneous reactor IDT data was proposed by Naser et al.20 at pressure from 10 to 50 bar with an increment of 5 and the equivalence ratios of 1. From the data validation, the predicted RON/MON showed well predictions against measurements obtained by the standardized RON and MON tests and blending rule.

For CN research, numerous efforts were paid to correlate and to predict the cetane number of fuel mixtures. 22 equations for prediction the CN of diesel fuels were tested by Ladommatos and Goacher21. It was found that only 8 equations could predict CN with standard errors below 1.5. Ghost et al.22 presented a molecular composition-based model, where a simple empirical correlation equation was used to show the correlation between the model and the enhancement of CN, and the standard error of the prediction of CN enhancement was ± 0.8 CN. In addition, the value of CN was derived from the data provided by the experimental instrument. For instance, Williams et al.23 had established the correlation between the Near-IR Fourier-transform (FT) Raman data and fuel quality (CN and cetane index) with CN between 42 and 51. Most of the analyses perform multivariate linear regressions with a subset of these predictors. Additionally, many research has linked CN/DCN with either RON or MON19,24–27. Kalghatgi25 reported that the equations between the IDT from IQT with the RON and DCN. The IDT increases as RON increases or CN decreases. In describing the ignition quality of gasoline fuel, Naser et al.27 reported that DCN measurement could satisfactorily predict the RON of gasoline fuels at a standard IQT pressure of 21.3 bar.

According to the preceding literature, the RON and DCN prediction model conditions are commonly employed in the CFR and IQT test settings. Given that the operating conditions of modern spark ignition engines have shifted from the standard RON and MON tests. In this study, RON and DCN prediction models based on the chemical kinetics model were developed for the TPRF fuel mixture under conditions in shock tubes (ST) and rapid compression machines (RCM). Additionally, the suggested methodology was validated for RON prediction using various mechanisms created by different groups in order to confirm the methodology’s applicability to all situations. The proposed correlations of a non-linear model between RON and IDT and a linear model between DCN and RON allow an estimate of a TPRF fuel mixture’s RON and DCN using simulated IDT and anticipated RON under specific conditions, which is essential to design fuels for future engines.

According to the preceding literature, the RON and DCN prediction model conditions are commonly employed in the CFR and IQT test settings. However, the operating conditions of modern spark ignition engines have increasingly shifted away from those of the standard RON and MON tests. In response to this, RON and DCN prediction models based on chemical kinetic simulations are developed in this study under conditions representative of shock tubes (ST) and rapid compression machines (RCM). Specifically, a kinetics-based modeling approach is proposed to establish a quantitative relationship between IDT and two fundamental indicators of fuel ignition quality: RON and DCN. The method is demonstrated using toluene primary reference fuel (TPRF) mixtures as a controlled surrogate system, where a nonlinear dependence of RON on IDT and a linear correlation between RON and DCN are systematically identified. The predictive framework will be further validated using multiple detailed chemical kinetic mechanisms developed by different research groups to assess its robustness and general applicability.

By directly leveraging the underlying reaction chemistry represented in kinetic models, this methodology enables physically interpretable and transferable predictions without reliance on empirical fitting or data-driven algorithms. Although applied to TPRF in this study, the framework is inherently extensible to more complex fuel surrogates and a broad range of emerging low-carbon fuels, including alcohols, ethers, and bio-derived compounds. This work provides a scientifically rigorous and scalable tool for fuel ignition characterization, supporting the development of advanced combustion systems that require precise control over autoignition behavior.

Methodology and validation

Methodology

This study develops a chemical kinetics-based approach to estimate octane numbers by correlating ignition delay time (IDT) with fuel reactivity under representative thermodynamic conditions. The method consists of three main components: (1) defining RON- and MON-like simulation conditions, (2) constructing a correlation between IDT and octane number using primary reference fuels (PRFs) and (3) applying the correlation to predict the RON and MON of a given fuel.

To replicate the thermodynamic environment relevant to standard octane number evaluation, constant-volume homogeneous reactor simulations are employed under representative pressure–temperature conditions. These conditions are derived from the end-gas thermodynamic trajectories of the Cooperative Fuel Research (CFR) engine, as reported by Szybist and Splitter28. In this study, pressure–temperature trajectories characteristic of RON and MON testing are used to define the so-called RON-like and MON-like conditions. It should be noted that the present 0D simulations were not carried out under the full mechanical boundary conditions of the CFR engine, such as variable compression ratio or engine speed. Instead, they use representative end-gas pressure–temperature states extracted from CFR operation to evaluate the chemical-kinetic autoignition response under equivalent RON-like and MON-like conditions. Accordingly, the present 0D reactor calculations do not explicitly reproduce frictional losses, blow-by losses, wall heat losses, or in-cylinder turbulence and eddy structures in the CFR engine. These effects are not modeled individually in the reactor, but are only reflected indirectly through the adopted CFR-derived end-gas thermodynamic trajectories. These conditions are not limited to a single point, but instead reflect a physically meaningful approximation of the autoignition environment during standard octane testing. The resulting (P, T) pairs serve as the basis for all subsequent IDT simulations, ensuring that the calculated ignition delay times are relevant and comparable to experimentally measured octane numbers. It should also be noted that the present method does not reproduce the full moving-boundary compression history of the CFR engine, nor does it employ a knock-integral treatment based on continuously varying in-cylinder conditions. Instead, it evaluates the chemical-kinetic autoignition response in a constant-volume homogeneous reactor under representative CFR-derived end-gas pressure–temperature states. Thus, the present approach should be interpreted as a representative-thermodynamic-state method rather than a full CFR cycle simulation.

A set of physically realizable PRF mixtures spanning PRF20 to PRF100 is simulated using a detailed chemical kinetic mechanism. At each PRF composition, the IDT is computed under both RON-like and MON-like conditions defined previously. Figure 1a presents the IDT-temperature profiles for these PRFs, as well as for toluene, under a fixed pressure of 25 bar. Vertical lines indicate the selected reference temperatures. This procedure is based on the ignition delay time equivalence principle, which assumes that fuels with the same IDT under the same thermodynamic conditions exhibit equivalent octane ratings. A sixth-order polynomial function is fitted to the IDT-ON data between PRF20 and PRF100, and, when needed, extrapolated to ON-equivalent values up to 120. It should be noted that values above 100 refer to octane-number-equivalent extrapolation of the fitted IDT-ON relation rather than to physical iso-octane volume fractions greater than 100%. To evaluate the generality and robustness of the method under varying pressures, the approach is extended using RON-like temperature–pressure trajectories extracted from CFR engine studies. As shown in Fig. 1b, a temperature–pressure correlation is constructed using third-order polynomial fitting, allowing the determination of RON-like temperature at any specified pressure. Its role is to provide a representative RON-like temperature for a specified pressure within the investigated pressure range used in this study. For each selected pressure level, such as 20, 40, 60, and 80 bar, the corresponding RON-like temperature is calculated. The IDT of the test fuel is then simulated at that condition. In parallel, PRF mixtures are also simulated under the same pressure and temperature conditions. In addition to the specific application to TPRF mixtures, the proposed methodology is also applicable to general fuel systems. An integrated computational framework is developed to systematically correlate ignition delay time (IDT) with research octane number (RON), as illustrated in Fig. 2. The procedure consists of three main steps:Figure 2Schematic diagram of the integrated calculation scheme.

Schematic diagram of the integrated calculation scheme.

Step 1: Mechanism selection and thermodynamic condition definition. Detailed kinetic mechanisms for both the test fuel and the PRF surrogates are selected. Given specified initial conditions such as pressure and equivalence ratio (P, φ), the corresponding RON-like temperature is determined based on a fitted temperature–pressure correlation (Eq. 2). This step defines a unique RON-like thermodynamic condition (P, T, φ) for subsequent IDT simulations.

Step 2: IDT simulation using a zero-dimensional reactor. Using the CANTERA zero-dimensional reactor model37, ignition delay times are simulated for both the test fuel and a series of PRF mixtures ranging from PRF0 to PRF100 by volume. All simulations are performed under the RON-like thermodynamic condition determined in Step 1. This step produces the IDT profiles required for octane number determination. The 0D ignition calculations were carried out in an ideal-gas constant-volume reactor using ct.ReactorNet. The original script did not explicitly modify the solver tolerances, so the default ReactorNet settings were used. For Cantera, the default relative tolerance is 1.0 × 10–9 and the default absolute tolerance is 1.0 × 10–15. Under the default reactor-network implementation, ReactorNet uses the CVODE integrator with the BDF method and a dense linear solver. No additional user-defined maximum time-step limit was imposed in the present calculations. In this work, ignition delay time was defined as the time corresponding to the maximum OH net production rate in the reactor simulation. A tolerance-sensitivity study was also carried out for representative PRF cases under RON-like conditions. After tightening the solver tolerances, the maximum relative variation in IDT was only 0.00246%. This confirms the numerical convergence of the 0D ignition calculations.

Step 3: Octane number estimation via IDT equivalence. Based on the established hypothesis that fuels exhibiting identical ignition delay times under identical thermodynamic conditions possess the same octane number, the RON of the test fuel is determined. Specifically, the iso-octane volume fraction of the PRF whose IDT most closely matches that of the test fuel is assigned as the predicted RON. This integrated methodology ensures consistency, mechanistic interpretability, and extensibility to a broad range of surrogate and real fuels. It provides a physically grounded alternative to empirical or data-driven methods for estimating fuel ignition quality.

These results suggest that the compatibility of a kinetic mechanism with the present framework should be judged primarily by its ability to reproduce IDTs under the relevant RON-like conditions. More specifically, a suitable mechanism should provide accurate IDT prediction under the relevant RON-like conditions, preserve the correct temperature- and pressure-dependent trends including the main NTC behavior, and maintain acceptable accuracy within the intended pressure and equivalence-ratio range of application. It should also be noted that the present framework correlates octane number with the overall ignition-delay response under the specified conditions. Although this treatment gives good ON prediction in the present study, it does not explicitly establish separate low-temperature and high-temperature ignition-delay correlations within the NTC regime. Future work may therefore examine whether extending the present framework to staged ignition characterization in the NTC regime could further improve ON prediction for fuels with pronounced low-temperature reactivity.

Results and discussion

This section describes in detail the validation of RON and DCN, as well as the validation of the methodology using chemical kinetics models. Validation under typical engine operating conditions is also conducted.

The CFD simulation did not include the full gas-exchange process. Instead, it was initialized from the in-cylinder state at intake valve closing (IVC), and the subsequent compression, injection, ignition, and combustion processes were simulated. In the present engine case, the intake air pressure and intake air temperature were 2.5 bar and 330 K, respectively, while the pressure and temperature at IVC were 2.7 bar and 390 K, respectively. In the present closed-cycle CFD calculation, constant wall temperatures of 500 K, 550 K, and 450 K were prescribed for the cylinder head, piston, and liner, respectively.

Due to the axisymmetric piston geometry and the 8-hole injector, a 45°-sector mesh was used in the simulation to reduce computation costs, as shown in Fig. 17. A base mesh size of 2.0 mm was used, together with a fixed embedding refinement scale of 3.0 around the nozzle. In addition, an adaptive mesh refinement scale of 3.0 was adopted, resulting in a minimum mesh size of 0.25 mm35,36. The transient simulation was performed using a variable time-step algorithm. The initial, minimum, and maximum time steps were 1.0 × 10–7, 1.0 × 10–8, and 1.0 × 10–4 s, respectively. The time-step size was further constrained by convection, diffusion, Mach-number, and conduction limits. The governing transport equations were solved in CONVERGE within its finite-volume framework using the standard second-order spatial discretization scheme. Further mesh refinement produced only negligible changes in the predicted in-cylinder pressure and heat release rate, indicating that the selected mesh setup was sufficient for the present simulations.Figure 17Computational mesh.

Conclusions

Research Octane Number (RON) and Derived Cetane Number (DCN) are critical fuel properties influencing combustion characteristics and emissions performance in internal combustion engines. In this study, a chemical kinetics-based methodology has been developed to predict both RON and DCN by establishing correlations with ignition delay times (IDTs). The accuracy and applicability of the proposed method were systematically evaluated over a wide range of temperatures, pressures, and equivalence ratios. Additionally, the developed gasoline surrogate mechanism was validated using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations of spray combustion in practical engine conditions. The major conclusions drawn from this study are summarized as follows:The predicted RON values were validated against experimental measurements using three independently developed chemical kinetic mechanisms. The results demonstrate that the proposed method is applicable within the investigated RON-like thermodynamic window. It was also observed that improved accuracy in simulated ignition delay times directly improves the precision of the RON predictions, indicating that octane number can be estimated from simulated IDTs under appropriate conditions when a suitable kinetic mechanism is used.An optimized reduced chemical kinetic mechanism for toluene primary reference fuel (TPRF) mixtures was developed, demonstrating accurate predictions of ignition delay times under various thermodynamic conditions. Based on this optimized mechanism, robust non-linear correlations between ignition delay times and RON were established for primary reference fuels (PRFs), toluene reference fuels (TRFs), and TPRF mixtures. The predicted RON values exhibit satisfactory agreement with experimental data, validating the effectiveness of the correlation framework.Correlations between DCN and RON were further developed under diverse operating conditions. The results revealed a clear linear dependence of DCN on RON. Predicted DCN values derived from this linear correlation at specified RON-like conditions showed good agreement with experimental measurements, demonstrating the practicality and reliability of the proposed approach for DCN estimation.

Future work will further examine the extension of the present framework to methane number analysis through the establishment of an appropriate reference scale, representative thermodynamic conditions, and a dedicated validation database for gaseous fuels.
