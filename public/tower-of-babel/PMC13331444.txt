Impact of Balloon Guide Catheter on Thrombectomy Outcomes in Intracranial Carotid Artery Embolic Occlusion: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jinfeng Miao, Hanrong Dong, Yanmin Wu, Ding-lai Lin, Xiao-hui Lin, Zhinan Pan, Li-san Zeng, Wenhuo Chen, Tingyu Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The balloon guide catheter (BGC) is widely used in endovascular thrombectomy, but evidence of its effect in intracranial internal carotid artery embolic occlusion is limited, and recent randomized trials have reported contradictory outcomes. We aimed to explore the potential association between BGC use and outcomes of thrombectomy in intracranial internal carotid artery embolic occlusion.METHODS:Patients with acute intracranial internal carotid artery occlusion from a prospectively maintained 2-center stroke registry were classified according to BGC use. Propensity score matching and overlap weighting were used to balance baseline differences. The primary outcome was functional independence at 90 days (modified Rankin Scale score, 0–2). Secondary outcomes included first-pass complete reperfusion (expanded Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 3), distal emboli, and 90-day modified Rankin Scale score shift.RESULTS:Among 397 patients (median age, 72 years; 45.1% men), 90 (22.7%) underwent BGC-assisted endovascular thrombectomy. After 1:2 propensity score matching (84 BGC versus 168 non-BGC), the groups were well balanced. BGC use was associated with higher odds of 90-day functional independence (propensity score matching odds ratio [OR], 2.24 [95% CI, 1.07–4.72]; overlap weighting OR, 1.97 [95% CI, 1.07–3.62]), higher rates of first-pass expanded Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction 3 reperfusion (propensity score matching OR, 1.98 [95% CI, 1.09–3.60]; overlap weighting OR, 1.74 [95% CI, 1.05–2.89]), and fewer distal emboli (overlap weighting OR, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.25–0.97]). The 90-day modified Rankin Scale score distribution favored BGC use, but was not statistically significant (P=0.20).CONCLUSIONS:In acute intracranial internal carotid artery embolic occlusion, BGC use was associated with better functional outcomes, higher first-pass reperfusion rates, and fewer distal emboli. These findings suggest that BGC may be a beneficial adjunct in this population and warrant validation in prospective trials.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

These findings suggest that proximal flow arrest with a balloon guide catheter may be a beneficial adjunct in patients with intracranial internal carotid artery embolic occlusion, particularly in younger patients, those with severe deficits (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≥15), and those presenting within 6 hours of stroke onset, although confirmation in adequately powered randomized trials focused on this subgroup remains warranted.

Endovascular thrombectomy is the standard treatment for acute ischemic stroke caused by large-vessel occlusion.1–3 Balloon guide catheters (BGCs) are commonly used adjuncts during endovascular thrombectomy because proximal flow arrest may help limit embolic migration, stabilize the device system, and control hemorrhage.4 Several BGC-based techniques have shown promising results in addressing the unique anatomic challenges of intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusions. The Aspiration-Retriever Technique for Stroke was one of the earliest techniques to combine a large-bore aspiration catheter with stent retriever deployment under proximal flow arrest, and it was associated with improved recanalization rates.5 Building upon this concept, the Balloon Guide With Large Bore Distal Access Catheter With Dual Aspiration With Stent-Retriever as Standard Approach technique further refined the combined approach by incorporating dual aspiration with BGC support. Both the Aspiration-Retriever Technique for Stroke and Balloon Guide With Large Bore Distal Access Catheter With Dual Aspiration With Stent-Retriever as Standard Approach techniques have been reported to achieve fast, safe, and complete reperfusion, often on the first pass.6 Another BGC-based technique for ICA embolic occlusions is repeat thrombectomy with a retrieval stent performed under continuous proximal flow arrest with a BGC to manage large clot burdens. This approach has been reported not only to substantially reduce procedure time and achieve a high rate of first-pass effect but also to reduce the occurrence of distal emboli.7

However, 2 recent randomized controlled trials have challenged the benefit of using a BGC during thrombectomy.8,9 The PROTECT-MT trial (Proximal Temporary Occlusion Using Balloon Guide Catheter for Mechanical Thrombectomy) reported worse functional outcomes with BGC use, whereas the ProFATE trial (Effect of Proximal Flow Arrest During Thrombectomy) found no difference in final near-complete reperfusion rates between BGC and control groups. Both trials enrolled relatively few patients with intracranial ICA occlusions. In the PROTECT-MT trial, intracranial ICA occlusions comprised 29% (47/164) of cases in the BGC arm versus 25% (41/165) in the control arm, and in the ProFATE trial, 13.6% (9/66) versus 10.8% (7/65), respectively. Intracranial ICA occlusion is typically associated with more severe initial deficits, poorer outcomes, and larger clot burdens; therefore, it often necessitates specialized thrombectomy strategies.10

To date, no study has specifically evaluated the potential efficacy of BGCs in intracranial ICA embolic occlusion, a particularly devastating stroke subtype. We hypothesized that BGC use may improve thrombectomy outcomes in intracranial ICA embolic occlusions. This study aimed to test that hypothesis.

Endovascular Procedure

Endovascular therapy was performed by senior neurointerventionalists according to standard institutional protocols. In the BGC group, we used the Balloon Guide With Large Bore Distal Access Catheter With Dual Aspiration With Stent-Retriever as Standard Approach technique, which combines an 8F BGC (FlowGate2; Stryker, CA/SpaceGate; Ricoton, Hunan, China; inner diameter, 0.84/0.87 inch) to arrest antegrade flow, distal aspiration via a large-bore intermediate catheter, and stent retriever deployment. In all BGC cases, the balloon was inflated for every patient and during every thrombectomy pass to ensure proximal flow arrest. The duration of balloon inflation was determined at the operator’s discretion based on procedural circumstances. In the majority of cases, the balloon was maintained in a continuously inflated state throughout the retrieval maneuver. In a minority of cases in which the thrombus could not be successfully retrieved after multiple passes, intermittent balloon inflation was used; during the intervals between inflation, a stent was deployed to maintain vessel patency and prevent distal thrombus migration. In the non-BGC group, the SWIM technique—defined as the stent retriever with intracranial aspiration (a combined method integrating stent retriever and large-bore intermediate catheter aspiration)—was performed with an 8F guide catheter/neuron sheath (inner diameter, 0.88 inch). Specifically, a large-bore intermediate catheter was advanced to the clot face for distal aspiration concurrent with stent retriever deployment, analogous to the BGC group but without proximal flow arrest.12 Adjunctive angioplasty at the intracranial occlusion site was performed when necessary to address residual stenosis or refractory occlusion. Key procedural variables, including puncture-to-reperfusion time, number of passes, use of aspiration, and rescue techniques, were recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was functional independence, defined as mRS score of 0 to 2 at 90 days.13 Secondary outcomes included first-pass complete reperfusion,14 the incidence of distal emboli,15 and the distribution of 90-day mRS scores (analyzed as an ordinal shift outcome). Final reperfusion of the target vessel was quantified using the expanded Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (eTICI) scale. Each operator recorded the eTICI score in real time at the end of the thrombectomy. All angiographic runs were then reviewed in a blinded manner by 2 independent neurointerventionalists (study investigators) who were unaware of the original operator scores and clinical outcomes, and a consensus eTICI grade was assigned for each case. This dual-step assessment (initial operator grading followed by blinded adjudication) ensured objective and consistent reperfusion scoring. Angiographic success was defined as an eTICI grade ≥2b, and complete reperfusion was defined as eTICI grade 3.16 Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage and other procedure-related complications were also recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median (interquartile range) based on distribution and compared using the independent-samples t test or Mann-Whitney U test, respectively. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test.

To address baseline imbalances, we performed propensity score analysis. Propensity scores for BGC treatment were estimated using generalized boosted regression modeling,13 incorporating baseline covariates including age, sex, baseline NIHSS score, Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score, premorbid mRS score, onset-to-treatment time, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, prior stroke, and occlusion site characteristics. Patients treated BGC were matched to patients without BGC at a 1:2 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper of 0.15× the SD of the logit-transformed propensity score.14 Covariate balance was assessed using standardized differences. In addition, overlap propensity score weighting analysis was conducted15 to create a weighted population approximating a randomized trial, and offering favorable statistical properties for variance and bias reduction.16

Outcomes were analyzed using 3 regression models: (1) unadjusted; (2) core-adjusted for age and sex; and (3) fully adjusted for age, sex, and all covariates with residual imbalance after propensity score matching. Binary outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs reported. The ordinal 90-day mRS shift was analyzed using proportional odds models, with the proportional odds assumption evaluated using the Brant test.17 All models used robust SEs to account for heteroscedasticity and matching-induced clustering. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons given the exploratory nature of the secondary end points.18

Prespecified subgroup analyses examined treatment effects according to baseline NIHSS score (≤15 versus >15), age (<70 versus ≥70 years), sex, onset-to-puncture time (<6 versus ≥6 hours), and Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (≤7 versus 8–10). Interaction terms were tested using likelihood ratio tests.19 Given the limited sample size within individual subgroups, these analyses were considered hypothesis-generating and were likely underpowered to detect clinically meaningful treatment-effect modifications; therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons given the exploratory nature of the secondary end points.

All analyses were performed using Python 3.11.0. Missing data were present in <5% of observations for most variables and were assumed to be missing at random based on examination of missing data patterns. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with an iterative random forest algorithm, which was chosen for its capacity to handle nonlinear relationships and mixed variable types without parametric assumptions. Five imputed data sets were generated. All subsequent analyses were performed separately on each data set, and results were pooled using Rubin rules. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

Between January 2018 and December 2024, a total of 397 consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke due to occlusion of the intracranial ICA underwent endovascular thrombectomy (Figure S1). Of these, 90 (22.7%) patients were treated with BGC, and 307 (77.3%) underwent thrombectomy without BGC. Propensity score matching was then performed at a 1:2 ratio (caliper=0.15 SD), resulting in a matched cohort of 252 patients (84 with BGC and 168 without BGC), which corresponds to a 93.3% matching rate.

Baseline Characteristics

In the unmatched cohort, the BGC and non-BGC groups were similar in basic demographic characteristics, as shown in Table 1. However, there were significant differences in some baseline comorbidities. Specifically, hypertension was less common in the BGC group (42.2% versus 59.6% in the non-BGC group; P=0.005; standardized mean difference, 0.353), whereas prior stroke was more common in the BGC group (16.7% versus 6.8%; P=0.008; standardized mean difference, 0.309).

After propensity score matching, baseline covariate balance improved markedly. Nonetheless, some imbalance remained: prior stroke continued to be more frequent in the BGC group (17.9% versus 4.2%; P<0.001; standardized mean difference, 0.448), and a residual difference in hypertension persisted (45.2% versus 57.1%; P=0.099; standardized mean difference, 0.240).

Primary Outcome

In the original cohort, 37.8% of patients in the BGC group achieved functional independence at 90 days (mRS score, 0–2) compared with 29.3% in the non-BGC group (P=0.163). In the propensity score–matched cohort, the functional independence rate was 35.7% with BGC versus 23.8% without BGC, a difference that approached statistical significance (P=0.066; Table 2).

Multivariable analyses revealed progressively stronger associations between BGC use and favorable outcomes after adjustment for confounders. In the fully adjusted model (model 3), BGC use was independently associated with higher odds of 90-day functional independence in both the propensity score–matched cohort (adjusted OR, 2.24 [95% CI, 1.07–4.72]; P=0.033) and the overlap-weighted analysis (adjusted OR, 1.97 [95% CI, 1.07–3.62]; P=0.030). By contrast, the unadjusted (crude) analysis showed only a nonsignificant trend toward benefit with BGC (OR, 1.65 [95% CI, 0.91–2.98]; P=0.100), as shown in Table 3.

Secondary Outcomes

Rates of complete reperfusion (eTICI grade 3) were similar between the BGC and non-BGC groups. In the original cohort, complete reperfusion was achieved in 71.1% of patients with BGC versus 66.8% of patients without BGC (P=0.518), and in the matched cohort, the rates were 70.2% with BGC versus 61.9% without BGC (P=0.244). Fully adjusted analyses likewise showed only a modest, nonsignificant advantage for BGC in achieving complete reperfusion (propensity score–matched OR, 1.41 [95% CI, 0.78–2.52]; P=0.255; Table 3).

By contrast, first-pass complete reperfusion (achievement of eTICI grade 3 on the first thrombectomy pass) was significantly more frequent with BGC. In the matched cohort, 42.9% of patients with BGC achieved first-pass eTICI grade 3 compared with 27.4% of patients without BGC (P=0.020). This first-pass advantage persisted after adjustment in both the matched and overlap-weighted analyses. In the propensity score–matched model, BGC use had an adjusted OR of 1.98 (95% CI, 1.09–3.60; P=0.025), and in the overlap-weighted model, the adjusted OR was 1.74 (95% CI, 1.05–2.89; P=0.031), as presented in Table 3.

The incidence of distal embolization was also significantly lower with BGC. In the matched cohort, distal emboli occurred in 11.9% of patients with BGC versus 23.2% of patients without BGC (P=0.049). Adjusted analyses confirmed this protective effect; for example, the overlap-weighted analysis showed an OR of 0.49 (95% CI, 0.25–0.97; P=0.040) for distal emboli with BGC, indicating roughly half the odds compared with the non-BGC group (Table 3). Regarding other procedure-related complications potentially associated with BGC deployment, vasospasm occurred in 2 (2.4%) patients in the BGC group compared with 1 (0.6%) patient in the non-BGC group (P=0.258), and guide catheter–related arterial dissection was observed in 0 patients in the BGC group versus 3 (1.8%) patients in the non-BGC group (P=0.553). The 2 vasospasm cases in the BGC group were transient and were successfully managed with intra-arterial vasodilators without persistent sequelae. Overall, no significant differences were observed between groups in vasospasm, dissection, or other serious adverse events (Table 2).

In the overall unmatched cohort of 397 patients, the distribution of 90-day mRS scores showed a trend toward better functional outcomes in the BGC group than in the non-BGC group, but the difference was not statistically significant (adjusted common OR for worse outcome with BGC, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.51–1.16]; P=0.204). After propensity score matching (n=252), a similar pattern was observed: BGC use was associated with a shift toward better mRS outcomes (adjusted common OR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.46–1.17]; P=0.199), corresponding to lower odds of worse outcome with BGC. However, this favorable shift did not reach statistical significance (Figure S2).

Subgroup Analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses revealed significant heterogeneity in BGC treatment effects across patient subgroups. The association between BGC use and functional independence appeared most pronounced in patients with severe strokes (NIHSS score ≥15; OR, 3.47 [95% CI, 1.33–9.06]; P=0.011), male patients (OR, 3.22 [95% CI, 1.42–7.30]; P=0.005), younger patients (<70 years; OR, 5.45 [95% CI, 1.40–21.27]; P=0.015), and those presenting early (≤6 hours; OR, 2.89 [95% CI, 1.16–7.18]; P=0.022). Discussion

Our study suggests that using a BGC during thrombectomy for intracranial ICA embolic occlusions may be associated with improved clinical and angiographic outcomes, although these findings should not be interpreted as establishing BGC as a standard-of-care recommendation. After adjustment for confounders using propensity score methods, BGC use was independently associated with higher odds of functional independence at 90 days. Notably, the BGC group achieved a significantly higher rate of first-pass complete reperfusion, which has been linked to good outcomes.18 Mechanistically, proximal flow arrest with a BGC may help prevent antegrade thrombus dislodgement and facilitate the retrieval of large clots in a single pass. The NASA registry (North American Solitaire Acute Stroke) demonstrated that BGC use was associated with superior revascularization rates (53.7% versus 32.5%), shorter procedure times, and improved 3-month functional outcomes (51.6% versus 35.8%), with BGC use identified as an independent predictor of favorable outcome (OR 2.5).17 Consistent with this mechanism, an analysis of the STRATIS registry (Systematic Evaluation of Patients Treated With Neurothrombectomy Devices for Acute Ischemic Stroke) found that adding a BGC nearly doubled the first-pass effect rate (48% versus 26%) and yielded a higher rate of 3-month functional independence (61% versus 42%).18 Likewise, pooled analyses have indicated that BGC use is associated with higher odds of complete revascularization and favorable functional outcomes, along with fewer thrombectomy attempts and shorter procedure durations on average.19 Interestingly, in our cohort, we did not observe a significant decrease in overall procedure time or total passes with BGC use, likely reflecting selection bias inherent in the nonrandomized design. Operators may have preferentially selected BGC for patients with a larger clot burden or more complex anatomy, rather than avoiding it in simpler cases in which direct aspiration could achieve rapid revascularization. As observed in the PROTECT-MT and ProFATE trials, there were numerically more thrombectomy attempts in the BGC group before matched and longer procedure time—albeit without statistical significance. This observation implies 2 potential contributors to the procedure time difference beyond just device access: first, the restricted compatibility of larger-bore aspiration catheters and BGCs; and second, BGCs were frequently used for more challenging cases with a large clot burden, such as embolic tandem occlusion, whereby any procedural time savings could be offset by the inherently longer duration required to treat such difficult cases. Consequently, these factors could obscure any true time advantage of BGC use in our study.

However, recent randomized controlled trials have challenged the routine use of BGCs in endovascular thrombectomy. The PROTECT-MT trial in China was halted early due to safety concerns and it reported worse outcomes in the BGC arm.8 Patients in the BGC arm had significantly worse 90-day functional outcomes (common OR, 0.66), including a lower rate of favorable outcome and a nominally higher mortality. Procedural metrics from PROTECT-MT also hinted at drawbacks of BGC use, including longer procedure times and more frequent severe ICA vasospasm in the BGC group. Similarly, the ProFATE trial failed to demonstrate any overall efficacy benefit from BGC use.9 In the ProFATE trial, proximal flow arrest did not improve the rate of final near-complete reperfusion or 90-day outcomes, although it was associated with fewer emboli to new territory and a higher first-pass complete recanalization rate. Of note, only a minority of ICA occlusions were included in these trials, limiting the generalizability of their conclusions to ICA occlusions. Moreover, both trials may have been underpowered to assess BGC efficacy specifically in ICA occlusions with large clot burdens. Thus, their neutral or negative findings should be interpreted cautiously in the context of intracranial ICA embolic occlusion. Nonetheless, these randomized controlled trial findings do not preclude a potential benefit of BGC in select contexts, such as the intracranial ICA large-vessel occlusions examined in our study, and may be further evaluated in ongoing randomized controlled trials (IAT-BOOST [Balloon Guide Catheter to Optimize Outcomes in Stroke Thrombectomy of the Internal Carotid Artery]; ClinicalTrials.gov; identifier: NCT07192029) in the future.

Our findings and prior evidence are consistent with the hypothesis that the efficacy of BGC may vary by occlusion site and stroke cause.20,21 In distal anterior occlusions (eg, isolated M1 segment), large-bore aspiration alone may achieve rapid and effective revascularization, thereby reducing any additional benefit from proximal flow arrest.18 Conversely, in cases of intracranial atherosclerosis or arterial dissection, BGC use may impair collateral flow or increase the risk of vessel injury. However, in intracranial ICA embolic occlusions, BGC use may prevent thrombus fragmentation and facilitate clot retrieval in a single pass, potentially making its benefits more pronounced. This context-specific effect may help reconcile the neutral results of the randomized controlled trials with the positive findings in our cohort. In addition, BGC efficacy appears to be influenced by the first-line thrombectomy technique used. Recent analysis of the MR CLEAN registry (Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands) demonstrated that BGC combined with stent retriever thrombectomy yielded superior functional outcomes (adjusted common OR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.65–0.96]), whereas direct aspiration combined with non-BGC achieved higher first-attempt reperfusion rates.22,23 In our retrospective cohort, institutional practice patterns resulted in both groups receiving combined stent retriever and large-bore intermediate catheter aspiration techniques, differing primarily in the presence or absence of proximal flow arrest via BGC. This consistency in technical approach lends support to the hypothesis that our observed improvements in first-pass complete reperfusion and reduced distal emboli may be attributable to BGC-mediated flow arrest.6,24 Consistent with this, we observed that use of BGC was associated with improved first-pass complete reperfusion rates and reduced distal embolization in our cohort. Supporting evidence from the ESCAPE-NA1 trial (Safety and Efficacy of Nerinetide [NA-1] in Subjects Undergoing Endovascular Thrombectomy for Stroke) showed that the absence of a BGC doubled the risk of emboli to a new territory.25 Although small, these distal emboli can still infarct eloquent brain tissue and worsen outcomes. Moreover, a prospective diffusion-weighted imaging study demonstrated that BGC use significantly reduced the number and volume of new ischemic lesions, even among patients with near-complete angiographic reperfusion (eTICI 2b/2c).26 This finding suggests a potential role for BGC in minimizing subclinical microembolization—subtle effects that are not captured by eTICI grading alone.

Our subgroup analyses provide insight into which patients might derive the greatest benefit from BGC use. Notably, patients with more severe strokes (eg, NIHSS score ≥15) typically have a larger territory at risk and poorer collateral circulation, meaning they have less physiological reserve during occlusion.27 In such cases, transient flow arrest could further reduce already inadequate collateral perfusion, which may have a negative impact on these patients. However, among patients with NIHSS score ≥15, BGC use may facilitate faster and more secure revascularization by potentially reducing downstream emboli and reducing the need for multiple passes, thereby shortening the ischemic time for critical brain tissue.26,28,29 We also observed trends suggesting that younger patients and those treated earlier (within 6 hours) derived a more pronounced benefit from BGC. By contrast, this advantage was less evident in older patients and in those treated in extended time windows. It is plausible that younger patients have more to gain from optimal reperfusion, given their better neuroplasticity and fewer comorbidities. Likewise, early presenters may benefit more because every minute saved in procedure time can translate into additional salvaged penumbra.29,30 In addition, we observed a possible interaction with sex, with male patients seemingly deriving more benefit from BGC use than female patients. The reasons for this finding are unclear, and it may be spurious. However, biological differences in clot composition or collateral physiology between sexes have been postulated, warranting further investigation.31,32 However, it should be acknowledged that these subgroup analyses were underpowered due to the limited sample size within individual subgroups, and no corrections for multiple comparisons were applied. Accordingly, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and they require validation in larger, adequately powered prospective studies. Overall, these subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Nevertheless, they hint that careful patient selection may be important if the potential benefits of BGC observed in this study are to be realized in clinical practice, and that a one-size-fits-all approach to BGC deployment is unlikely to be optimal.

This study has several limitations. Because this was a dual-center observational cohort, it is susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding despite propensity score adjustments. Notably, operator preference and experience may have influenced BGC utilization decisions. Although BGC use was generally guided by institutional protocols, individual operators likely had varying thresholds for deployment based on their training and familiarity with the technique. We did not collect operator-level data such as years of experience or annual case volumes, which limits our ability to assess the impact of operator-related factors on outcomes. Although propensity score methods adjusted for patient and procedural characteristics, residual confounding from operator-level variation cannot be fully excluded. Although our sample of ICA embolic occlusions is among the largest to date, certain subgroup analyses were still underpowered. Specifically, the limited number of patients within individual subgroups precluded definitive conclusions regarding differential treatment effects, and the subgroup findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating only. Importantly, we focused exclusively on embolic ICA occlusions to reduce etiologic heterogeneity. Although this approach improves internal validity, it also limits generalizability to other stroke mechanisms, such as intracranial atherosclerotic disease or arterial dissection. In addition, treatment strategies were heterogeneous, with variations in devices and techniques left to operator discretion. Although we adjusted for key procedural variables, subtle technique-related effects might still have affected the results. Furthermore, precise balloon inflation times were not systematically recorded for each thrombectomy pass, precluding analysis of the relationship between inflation duration and procedural outcomes; future prospective studies incorporating standardized inflation-time documentation are warranted. Despite these limitations, our findings provide preliminary evidence suggesting, a potential role for BGC in a clinically important yet underexplored subgroup of large-vessel occlusions, which merits further investigation in adequately powered randomized trials.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that proximal flow arrest using a BGC may be associated with improved outcomes in patients with acute stroke with embolic intracranial ICA occlusions, potentially through increasing the first-pass reperfusion rates and reducing distal embolization. As thrombectomy technology advances, further large-scale randomized studies focusing on intracranial ICA embolic occlusions are warranted to determine whether the potential benefits of BGC use observed in this study can be confirmed and to optimize individualized thrombectomy strategies for improved patient outcomes.
