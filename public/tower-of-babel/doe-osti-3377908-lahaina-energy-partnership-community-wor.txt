  drones to measure the

Additional Technologies
• Waste-to-energy: Turning waste, such as
 “municipal solid waste” (or garbage), into energy
 historically took the form of incinerating as fuel
 and was associated with a high degree of air
 quality emissions. Modern waste-to-energy has
 many more options, such as capturing natural
 gas from landfills or wastewater treatment,
 biofuels production from food waste, or more
 modern ways of treating solid waste that have
 much lower emissions.
• Agrivoltaics: PV production can be combined
 with agricultural land uses in ways that can
 increase yields and revenue.
• Distributed wind: Wind power is dominated by
 large, multi-megawatt (MW) scale turbines but
 generation from much smaller turbines is
 possible with lower visibility and environmental
 impacts. A small-scale anaerobic digestion plant, a form of waste-to-energy.
 Photo by Anelia Milbrandt, NLR

Results: Energy Design Strategies
Water heating, appliances, lighting, and miscellaneous electric loads
represent other major categories of electricity use that drive utility bill
costs. Key energy design strategies/considerations include the following:

• Local energy code-required solar water heaters (SWHs) for efficient
 residential water heating, unless exemption (e.g., installing PV)

• If SWH exemption, heat pump water heaters (HPWHs) offer efficient
 electric water heating and secondary space cooling benefits.*

• ENERGY STAR Appliances and LED lighting: Efficient appliances and
 LED lighting can help reduce energy bill costs and have the secondary
 benefit of reducing cooling loads by reducing internal heat added to the
 living space.

Results: Energy Design Strategies
Residential PV/battery, backup power, and microgrid-ready energy design
strategies/considerations include the following:

• Designing homes to be ready for potential future solar PV* and battery systems.

• Optimizing on-site PV + battery sizes and battery dispatch strategy could help
 reduce energy bills and energy-related costs for residents.

• Potential for PV/battery to be designed for backup power during grid outage.

• Battery and fuel-fired generators have potential for backup ranging from device-
 level to whole-home backup; this strategy comes with important safety-related
 considerations.

• Selecting equipment/devices that have communication capabilities and can respond

Rooftop PV: Results
Our assessment finds in Lahaina in early 2023, if every rooftop
contained as much PV as technically possible, an estimated 84
GWh/year could be generated. This doesn’t consider whether this PV
would make financial sense for the building owner.

Lahaina’s energy needs at 77% of the gross electricity demand.
This suggests the excellent solar resource, low latitude, and nature of
Lahaina’s built environment lend themselves to high rooftop PV
potential. NLR’s national rooftop PV potential study in 2016 (Gagnon et
al. 2016) found very few urban areas would be able to meet even 60%
or more of their gross electricity needs from rooftop PV alone (although
PV panel improvements cause these estimates to be conservative for
modern rooftop PV systems).

detached homes.

 • “Single-family housing: A detached dwelling unit designed to be occupied by one

 located on individual lots” (CDBG-DR Construction Standards; County of Maui 2025).

 • This appendix does not focus on single-family attached, multifamily, or commercial

Design strategies presented in this appendix are for informational purposes only; refer to

Envelope Energy Design Strategies: Air Tightness
The degree of air tightness of a home affects its energy performance. In the Lahaina, Maui climate, air
tightness primarily prevents cool/conditioned air from leaving the home and warm/humid air from entering
the home when it is warmer outside, but it may actually help cool the home when it is cooler outside.

Approach: Reduce uncontrolled leaks (e.g., though cracks, holes, and gaps) and manage ventilation
through natural ventilation (e.g., operable windows) and, if needed, controllable whole-home mechanical
ventilation. Local energy code: Air-Leakage-installation: “Continuous air barrier; Breaks or joints are
sealed; Recessed lighting sealed; Fenestration air leakage” (applies to air-conditioned space) (Hawaii
State Energy Office and Hawaii Energy 2025).

ACH50: Air changes per hour when a home is pressured to 50 pascals pressure differential during a
blower door test.* Local energy code requires ACH50 <=5.**

For the prototype modeled home, reducing ACH50 below 5 is not estimated to save energy/bills.***

System sizing, area required, expected cost savings, and resilience benefits for microgrids comprising solar PV,

Simplifying Assumption: Aggregated Loads
For load scenarios that encompass multiple buildings/customers, the economics are simplified so we
assume:
• The loads of individual buildings in each load group are aggregated behind a single meter and assumed
 to be on the same utility rate, General Service Non-Demand (Schedule G). The effective cost of energy
 on this rate (~$0.39/kWh) is nearly the same as Hawaiian Electric’s residential (Schedule R) rate but
 does not contain energy “tiers” (making it more appropriate to use for aggregated loads).
• Export compensation is applied to the net export of the aggregate generation after serving the
 aggregate load. Because there would likely be more export if we modeled each building individually, this
 is likely slightly overestimating the value of solar because export is compensated less than the cost of
 electricity.
• These assumptions impact the cost trade-offs in REopt when determining the cost-optimal (least cost
 over 25 years) system sizing for solar, batteries, generators, and wind. However, true “cost-optimal”
 results are likely to be within the bounds of the scenarios analyzed.

(comparable to PSPS) but best strategies combine multiple approaches such as covered conductors, PSPS, forecasting, etc. Comparison

 practices like PSPS, policy and programs, and analytics — details in December 2025 workshop slides. Tradeoffs between
 covered conductors on wood, steel and composite poles addressed above.

 conductors both cost less than undergrounding. Can also take advantage of other construction disturbances such as road paving
