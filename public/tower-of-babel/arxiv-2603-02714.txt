Gaussian Width of Convex Sets via Integral Decompositions, Projections, and the Distribution of Intrinsic Volumes
Reese Pathak, Nikita Zhivotovskiy

Abstract
We revisit the problem of bounding the expected supremum of a canonical Gaussian process indexed by a convex set T ⊂ 𝐑 d T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d} . We develop two decompositions for the Gaussian width, based on the geometry of the index set. The first decomposition involves metric projections of Gaussians onto rescaled copies of T T . The second involves fixed points arising from a quadratically penalized variant of the local width. Neither decomposition directly invokes generic chaining constructions. Our results make use of recent work in geometric analysis and Gaussian processes. The work of Chatterjee [ Ann. Statist. , 2014] characterizes the behavior of the metric projection of a Gaussian random vector onto rescaled copies of T T with a variational problem involving localized Gaussian widths. We use these bounds to develop decompositions of the Gaussian width using the local metric structure of T T . Second, we leverage the work of Vitale [ Ann. Probab. , 1996] to form a connection between the Wills functional (and hence the intrinsic volumes of T T ) and the first terms that appear in our decompositions. Finally, invoking recent work by Mourtada [ J. Eur. Math. Soc. , 2025] on the logarithm of the Wills functional, we show that the width is controlled by a single, “peak index” of the intrinsic volumes. In the worst case, our bound recovers a local form of the classical Dudley integral.

Introduction

Bounding the Gaussian width of a set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
is an intensively studied problem with applications across probability, geometry, signal processing, statistics, and machine learning. Recall that the Gaussian width of
T
is defined as

w(T)=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{x\in T}\;\langle x,g\rangle,

(2)

where
g\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
is a standard Gaussian vector in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Talagrand’s majorizing measure theorem
[
53
, Theorem 2.10.1]
implies that

w(T)\asymp\gamma_{2}(T),

(3)

where
a\asymp b
means that there are absolute constants
c,C>0
such that
ca\leqslant b\leqslant Ca
, and

\gamma_{2}(T)=\inf_{(\mathcal{A}_{n})}\;\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\,\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr).

(4)

Here the infimum is taken over all admissible, nested sequences of partitions
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
of
T
in the sense of
[
53
, Definition 2.7.1]
. In particular,
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{T\}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
refines
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, and
|\mathcal{A}_{n}|\leqslant 2^{2^{n}}
. Moreover,
A_{n}(x)
denotes the unique element of the partition
\mathcal{A}_{n}
that contains
x
, and
\diam(A)=\sup_{u,v\in A}\|u-v\|_{2}
.
Despite providing a complete characterization of the Gaussian width, exhibiting an optimal admissible sequence of partitions for a general
T
can be extremely challenging. In fact, only a few such explicit constructions are known. As Talagrand notes in his monograph
[
53
]
, even for ellipsoids, constructing an optimal admissible sequence is “surprisingly non-trivial.” These observations motivate developing alternative characterizations of
w(T)
that avoid explicit generic chaining constructions.

This paper pursues such an alternative by developing connections with the Gaussian sequence model, as studied in statistics. For
\sigma>0
and
g\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
, we observe

Y=\theta^{\star}+\sigma g.

(5)

Above,
\theta^{\star}
is an unknown target vector belonging to a known parameter set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Three central objects of study within the Gaussian sequence model are the least squares estimator (LSE), its variance, and the optimal statistical rate of estimation. Geometrically, the LSE—defined in
eq.

6a
—is the metric projection of
Y
onto a closed, convex constraint set

:

\Pi_{T}(Y)=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\vartheta\in T}\|Y-\vartheta\|_{2}^{2}.

(6a)

When

is additionally centrally symmetric—
i
.
e

T=-T
—and
\theta^{\star}=0
, the projection is mean-zero and thus the variance of the LSE simplifies to

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\bigl\|\Pi_{T}(Y)-\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\Pi_{T}(Y)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\bigl\|\Pi_{T/\sigma}(g)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

(6b)

Notably, the latter quantity
\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\bigl\|\Pi_{T/\sigma}(g)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}

is a closed convex cone, is referred to as the

and was previously studied
[
3
,
11
,
25
]
. Finally, the optimal rate of estimation within the model (
5
), also commonly referred to as the

, is given by

\big(\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\big)^{2}=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

(6c)

The three objects in eqns. (
6
) will play a key role in our decompositions of the Gaussian width.

It is well-understood that the difficulty of estimating
\theta^{\star}
from
Y
is related to the geometry of
T
.
In particular, if
T
is closed and convex, then the risk and thus the variance of the least squares estimator (LSE), e.g., (
6b
), are tightly connected with fixed points of the localized Gaussian width, as shown by Chatterjee
[
19
]
. More generally, the optimal estimation rate (
6c
) in the model (
5
) is essentially determined by the metric structure of
T
, via an appropriately defined local packing number of
T
(see
Section

5
).

Gaussian width bounds are prevalent in statistical analysis of particular estimators. A main message of this paper, however, is that the “reverse direction” is also fruitful: statistical rates and information-theoretic bounds can produce new insights into the behavior of the Gaussian width itself.
Our approach consists in varying the noise level
\sigma
, and leveraging the profile of the risk of the LSE and the optimal statistical rate, to yield a decomposition of the width itself. Surprisingly, this approach leads to new identities for the width and, in particular, sharp characterizations that bypass generic chaining constructions.

Our analysis of
w(T)
leverages several deep results regarding canonical Gaussian processes:

•

Wills functional and the distribution of intrinsic volumes.

Connections between the Wills functional and suprema of Gaussian processes go back to Vitale
[
59
]
.
More recently, Mourtada
[
43
]
characterized the logarithm of the Wills functional via fixed point equations involving localized Gaussian widths and covering number quantities closely related to those that appear in this paper.
In our approach, the Wills functional enters as an intermediate geometric object for bounding the Gaussian width: combining our width decomposition with the observation from
[
43
]
that this functional is effectively governed by a single peak intrinsic volume index yields bounds on

in terms of the intrinsic volume profile of suitable rescalings of
T
.
We exploit these results to obtain a sharp characterization of

; see
Theorem

2.3
.

•

Chatterjee’s analysis of Gaussian projections.

Chatterjee
[
19
]
analyzes the least squares estimator in the Gaussian sequence model and shows, in particular, that fixed points of localized Gaussian width govern LSE performance.
We exploit two outcomes of this analysis: fixed points control the risk of the LSE across noise levels, moreover, the variance of the LSE can be bounded using only the local metric structure of
T
, bypassing direct appeals to
w(T)
.
In the worst case, the bounds developed by this technique include the Dudley integral upper bound on

, in a fashion that neither invokes
\gamma_{2}(T)
nor any related generic chaining constructions.

We note that in the finite-dimensional setting in which
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the
\gamma_{2}
functional can be simplified. Specifically, it can be split into two parts, indexed by any integer
p\geqslant 1
.
Indeed, for any admissible sequence of partitions
(\mathcal{A}_{n})
,

\gamma_{2}(T)\leqslant\underbrace{\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}2^{n/2}\,\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)}_{\text{``head'' part}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\,\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)}_{\text{``tail'' part}},

(7)

where
A_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
is the unique set containing
x

For bounded sets in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, and for

2^{p}\gtrsim d\log d
, the “tail” part can be controlled by standard volumetric and entropy estimates:

\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n\geqslant p}2^{n/2}\,\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)\lesssim\diam(T)\lesssim w(T).

(8)

One interpretation of our main results,

2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
, is that in many cases, it is possible to make earlier, geometry-driven splits, with

. The
“tail” part is then controlled by geometric or information-theoretic tools, such as the intrinsic volumes or the Wills functional structure. Note that this is precisely in the regime where covering numbers alone do not correctly capture the scale of the Gaussian width. The “head” part is then correspondingly handled by statistical risk bounds, for instance from the LSE or the minimax rate, which are never worse than the classical Dudley entropy integral estimates.
A comparison of our results with generic chaining is provided in
Section

5.4
.

As a byproduct of our analysis, we obtain several results that may be of independent interest:

•

Local and global Dudley and Sudakov bounds are equivalent up to constants.

A basic lesson from statistical estimation is that, due to the difference between “local” and “global” forms of the packing and covering entropies, optimal estimation generally depends sensitively on which form is being used to construct estimators or to establish lower bounds

•

Sudakov minoration via information-theoretic tools.

While for the dual version we additionally use a Gaussian shift-type result, namely Anderson’s lemma, for the proof of the primal version no Gaussian comparison inequalities are used; the Gaussian input enters only through bounding the Kullback-Leibler divergence for Gaussian measures. A statistical explanation of when Dudley is loose.

As with generic chaining, particular examples pose their own challenges; our methods are not always the simplest. Nonetheless, we illustrate some of our approaches in two basic examples: the standard crosspolytope (see

Decompositions of suprema of stochastic processes

Our first main result is a decomposition of the suprema of stochastic processes. For a random vector

\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
we define for a nonempty set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

w_{\xi}(T)=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\xi}\Big[\sup_{x\in T}\;\langle\xi,x\rangle\Big].

(10)

We will refer to this as the
\xi
-width of the set
T
. In the case
\xi\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
, this quantity is typically referred to as the Gaussian width (or complexity) of the set
T
. In this case we simply write
w(T)
.

We often make the assumption that
T
is closed, convex, and contains the origin, which is essentially no restriction.

Remark 2.1

(Convexity and containment of the origin)
.

The assumption that
T
is convex and contains the origin is essentially without loss of generality. Indeed, for nonempty
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, define
T_{0}=\mathrm{cl\,conv}(T-t_{0})
where
t_{0}\in T
is fixed. Then, we clearly have

w_{\xi}(T)=w_{\xi}(T_{0})+\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\xi}~\langle t_{0},\xi\rangle,

(11)

provided that
\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\,\langle t_{0},\xi\rangle>-\infty
. Hence, in this way, it is typically possible to reduce to the setting in which the process is indexed by a closed, convex set
T_{0}
containing the origin.

Our first decomposition involves an auxiliary variational problem, indexed by
\sigma>0
:

r(\sigma)=\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{r\geqslant 0}\Big\{\,w_{\xi}\big(T\cap rB^{d}_{2}\big)-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma}\,\Big\}.

(12)

It is not

is well-defined; hence, we will tacitly assume that this is the case in the remainder of the paper. The next result characterizes precisely when this occurs.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any random vector
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, and any convex set

(i)

the fixed point

for every

.

We use the shorthand
\omega(r)=w_{\xi}(T\cap rB^{d}_{2})
. Note that
\omega(r)\geqslant\omega(0)=0

for all
r\geqslant 0
.
Assume
(ii)
.
Then, for
r\geqslant r_{0}
,
we have from the inclusion

Gaussian width via the index of the largest intrinsic volume

Another consequence of

A closely related question, namely the distribution of the normalized intrinsic volumes, is studied in

]

We will only need the integer index of the largest intrinsic volume; namely, we set

i^{\star}_{\sigma}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{i\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}}\left\{V_{i}\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right\},

. We assume in what follows that

is the smallest maximizer if there is more than one maximizer.
Of course, finding the peak intrinsic index is a nontrivial problem in general. However, from (

) and unimodality we have the following limiting cases:

i^{\star}_{\sigma}=d\quad\text{iff}\quad\sigma<\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\operatorname{Vol}_{d}(T)}{\operatorname{Sf}_{d-1}(\partial T)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad i^{\star}_{\sigma}=0\quad\text{iff}\quad\sigma\geqslant w(T).

(26)

Thus, the interesting regime
i^{\star}_{\sigma}>0
corresponds to
\sigma\in(0,w(T))
. It is straightforward to argue that
\sigma\mapsto i^{\star}_{\sigma}
is a nonincreasing, integer-valued map that changes only at scales where two consecutive intrinsic volumes of
\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
are equal.

Before stating the main result, let us recall a basic comparison between the Gaussian width of a set and its diameter. For a bounded set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we have (see
[

, Proposition 7.5.2]
)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\diam(T)\leqslant w(T)\leqslant\sqrt{d}\,\diam(T).

(27)

Based on these bounds, it is natural to wonder if, up to the diameter,

[w(T)]^{2}
behaves as some form of an “effective dimension,” which takes values between a constant and
d
, depending on the geometry of the underlying set. Our next result shows
that the mode of the intrinsic volumes plays precisely this role.

Theorem 2.3
.

For a compact convex set
T
in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
containing the origin, and for any
\sigma\in(0,w(T))

w(T)\asymp\sigma i^{\star}_{\sigma}+\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T/\nu}(g)\|_{2}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\nu,\quad\text{and}\quad w(T)\asymp\sigma i^{\star}_{\sigma}+\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{r(\nu)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\mathrm{d}\nu,

(28)

where the peak intrinsic index
i^{\star}_{\sigma}
is given by (

). In particular, assuming
\diam(T)\neq 0

i^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\left\{V_{i}\left(\frac{T}{\diam(T)}\right)\right\}.

(29)

Then, it holds that

w(T)\asymp i^{\star}\diam(T).

(30)

The full proof of this result is deferred to

The upper bound in

Remark 2.2

The distribution of intrinsic volumes was studied in

We start with the following definitions.

Definition 3.1

For a convex set

for each
\sigma>0

The next definition is a shorthand for the first term which appeared in

2.1
.

Definition 3.2
.

For a convex set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
containing the origin, we define

\mathcal{T}(\sigma)=w\big(T\cap r(\sigma)B^{d}_{2}\big)-\frac{r(\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}=\sup_{r\geqslant 0}\left\{\,w\big(T\cap rB^{d}_{2}\big)-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma}\right\},

(54)

for each
\sigma>0
.

Note that although both
Definitions

3.1
and
3.2
depend on the underlying set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we suppress this from the notation as it will always be clear from context when we use these notions.
Versions of
r^{\star}(\sigma)
and
\mathcal{T}(\sigma)
play a prominent role in the analysis of least squares estimators in statistical literature (see
[
60
, Chapter 13]
for an account of related results). Note that the critical radius need not satisfy
r^{\star}(\sigma)\leqslant\rad(T)
, in contrast to the fixed point

. The next result quantifies some relations (and in particular

19
) between the quantities
r,r^{\star}
, and
\mathcal{T}
, more formally.

Proposition 3.1

(First terms of decompositions in the Gaussian case)
.

Let
T
be a bounded, nonempty, centrally symmetric, convex set in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Fix any
\sigma>0
. Then, it holds that

\mathcal{T}(\sigma)\leqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\sup\limits_{x\in T}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)\right]\leqslant 140\;\frac{(r^{\star}(\sigma))^{2}}{\sigma}\leqslant 280\;\mathcal{T}(\sigma).

(55)

Furthermore, it holds that

r(\sigma)\leqslant\min\left\{r^{\star}(2\sigma),\,\rad(T)\right\}\leqslant\min\left\{2\sqrt{\sigma\mathcal{T}(2\sigma)},\,\rad(T)\right\}.

(56)

The inequalities (
55
) show that, up to multiplicative constants, the first terms of
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
coincide. The second inequality, (
56
) should be viewed as a crude relation between the two fixed points

, which in general cannot be reversed. A significantly sharper form of these bounds will be developed in
Corollary

5.1
.
There, we show that the gap between
r(\sigma)
and
r^{\star}(\sigma)
is related to the difference between the Sudakov minoration and the local Gaussian width of
T
.

First, observe that

r^{\star}(\sigma)=\sigma\sup\{r>0:w\big(T/\sigma\cap rB^{d}_{2})\geqslant r^{2}\}.

(57)

We also have the following inequality

\mathcal{T}(\sigma)=\max_{r>0}\left\{w\big(T\cap rB^{d}_{2})-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma}\right\}\leqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\sup\limits_{x\in T}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)\right]=\sigma\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\bigg[\sup\limits_{x\in T/\sigma}\Big(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\Big)\bigg]

(58)

Now, Proposition 4.1 in
[
43
]
shows via the peeling argument combined with the Gaussian concentration inequality that for a convex set
T/\sigma
it holds that

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\bigg[\sup_{x\in T/\sigma}\;\Big(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\Big)\bigg]\leqslant 140\left(\sup\{r>0:w\big(T/\sigma\cap rB^{d}_{2})\geqslant r^{2}\}\right)^{2}.

(59)

Note that Proposition 4.1 is stated for a slightly different notion of
r^{\star}
, which does not require central symmetry. However, by Lemma 8.2 in
[
43
]
, in the case of centrally symmetric convex bodies the result holds as claimed above.
Using the above chain of inequalities and (

), we get

\mathcal{T}(\sigma)\leqslant\frac{140(r^{\star}(\sigma))^{2}}{\sigma}.
Finally, by the definition we have

\frac{(r^{\star}(\sigma))^{2}}{2\sigma}\leqslant w\big(T\cap r^{\star}(\sigma)B^{d}_{2}\big)-\frac{(r^{\star}(\sigma))^{2}}{2\sigma}\leqslant\sup\limits_{r>0}\left\{w\big(T\cap rB^{d}_{2})-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma}\right\}=\mathcal{T}(\sigma).

(60)

To show the second claim we note that
w\big(T\cap r(\sigma)B^{d}_{2}\big)-\frac{r(\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}\geqslant 0
, and hence by definition
r(\sigma)\leqslant r^{\star}(2\sigma)
. Finally, we use again
\frac{(r^{\star}(2\sigma))^{2}}{4\sigma}\leqslant\mathcal{T}(2\sigma)
and
r(\sigma)\leqslant\operatorname{rad}(T)
. The claim follows.

Remark 3.1

\theta^{\star}\in T
when observed through Gaussian noise. This is the minimax mean squared error (MSE), and is given by

\big(\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\big)^{2}=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

(105)

Above, the infimum is taken over estimators (
i
.
e
., measurable functions)
\widehat{\theta}\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Our main result shows that the radii and statistical rate are related via
r(\sigma)\lesssim\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that for a compact, centrally symmetric, convex set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
it holds that

\max\left\{r(\sigma)^{2},\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T}(\sigma g)\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\lesssim\big(\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\big)^{2},

(106)

for any
\sigma>0
.

The proof is presented below in
Section

5.6.1
. Although from
Theorem

5.1
, it appears as though the bound on the radius
r(\sigma)
could be difficult to compute, it turns out that the variational problem underlying
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)
can be simplified when
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
is convex, using metric properties of
T
. Recall that the packing entropy is given by

M(A,B)=\sup_{\text{finite}~A_{0}\subset A}\Big\{\,|A_{0}|:x-y\not\in B\quad\mbox{for all distinct}~x,y\in A_{0}\,\Big\}.

(107)

We define the
local
packing entropy of a convex set
T
at scale
\varepsilon>0
by

M^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)=\sup_{\theta\in T}M\big(T\cap(\theta+2\varepsilon B^{d}_{2}),\varepsilon B^{d}_{2}\big).

(108)

Then, the next result shows that
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)
is determined by a suitable fixed point of the local entropy.

Proposition 5.1

(Metric entropy characterization of statistical rate)
.

For any bounded convex set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, and any
\sigma>0
, the statistical rate satisfies

\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\asymp\sup\bigg\{\,\varepsilon>0:\log M^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\bigg\}.

(109)

This result was first claimed, to the best of our knowledge, in
[
45
, Theorem 2.11]
, building on
[
13
,
62
]
. Since technical issues with the proof of
[
45
, Theorem 2.11]
have been reported in
[
63
]
, we provide a simplified alternative argument in
Appendix

A
: we show that the upper bound in
eq.

109
is achieved by the LSE over an appropriately constructed global
\varepsilon
-net of
T
, in contrast to the more complicated iterative procedure in
[
45
]
.

As a first immediate corollary of
Theorem

5.1
and
Proposition

5.1
, we have the following improvement of
Proposition

3.1
.

Corollary 5.1
.

For a compact, centrally symmetric, convex set
T
in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, it holds that

r(\sigma)\lesssim\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\lesssim\min\{r^{\star}(2\sigma),\operatorname{rad}(T)\}.

(110)

The lower bound
r(\sigma)\lesssim\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)
follows from
Theorem

5.1
.
Next,
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\leqslant\rad(T)
since the constant estimator
\widehat{\theta}=0
has

\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta\|_{2}^{2}=\sup_{\theta\in T}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}=\rad(T)^{2}
. For the remaining bound, consider the projection
\widehat{\theta}=\Pi_{T}(\theta+\sigma g)
. By definition of the minimax risk,

\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}\leqslant\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T}(\theta+\sigma g)-\theta\|_{2}^{2}.

(111)

The standard localized Gaussian-complexity analysis of least squares over star-shaped classes
(see, e.g.,
[
60
, Ch. 13]
, in particular the proof based on the critical radius)
yields

\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T}(\theta+\sigma g)-\theta\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim r^{\star}(2\sigma)^{2},

(112)

up to universal constants.
Combining the displays proves
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\lesssim r^{\star}(2\sigma)
.
∎

We note a curious aspect of the gap between
r(\sigma)
and
r^{\star}(2\sigma)
highlighted by
Corollary

5.1
.
Both
r(\sigma)
and
r^{\star}(2\sigma)
depend explicitly on the Gaussian width of
T
at different scales and, in general, are not expected to be determined by metric entropy at a single scale.
In contrast, the intermediate quantity
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)
that controls their discrepancy is purely metric: it admits a characterization in terms of local packing numbers of
T
and does not depend on any additional structure.

A second immediate consequence of
Theorem

5.1
and
Proposition

5.1
is the following bound on the integrated terms which appeared in
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
.

Corollary 5.2
.

For any compact, centrally symmetric convex set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
and any
\sigma>0
, it holds that

\max\left\{\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2}(\nu)}{\nu^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu,\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T/\nu}(g)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}\lesssim\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\star}(\nu)}{\nu^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu.

(113)

Combining
Corollary

5.2
with the metric entropy characterization in
Proposition

5.1
yields inequalities solely in terms of local entropy of the underlying set
T
. To state the result, we recall the (truncated) Dudley entropy integrals,

\mathcal{J}^{\rm loc}_{\delta}(T)=\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log M^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon.

(114)

In the case that
\delta=0
, we simply write
\mathcal{J}^{\rm loc}(T)
. Our next result relates the right-hand side quantity in
Corollary

5.2
with the Dudley integral.

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists a constant
c>1
such that the following inequality holds for every
\sigma>0
and any bounded convex set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
:

\mathcal{J}^{\rm loc}_{c\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)}(T)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\star}(\sigma)}{\sigma}\lesssim\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{\star}(\nu)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\lesssim\mathcal{J}^{\rm loc}_{\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)/c}(T)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\star}(\sigma)}{\sigma}

(115)

The proof is presented in
Section

5.6.3
. Combining
Proposition

5.2
with
Theorem

2.1
,
Proposition

3.2
(i), and
Corollary

5.2
, and the fact that
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{d}
(see
Lemma

A.4
), we recover the classical Dudley entropy integral bound,

w(T)\lesssim\limsup_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\Big\{\sigma d+\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{\star}(\nu)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\mathrm{d}\nu\Big\}\asymp\mathcal{J}^{\rm loc}(T)\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon.

(116)

Remark 5.1

(Sharpness of Dudley’s entropy integral)
.

Combining
Corollary

5.1
with
Proposition

5.2
, and leveraging the equivalence between the local and global Dudley integrals (see
Proposition

5.5
), it can be seen that

w(T)\ll\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{if and only if}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T}(\sigma g)\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\ll\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(117)

In other words, the Dudley entropy integral bound is loose whenever the (integrated) statistical minimax rates are much larger than the (integrated) performance of the LSE at the origin, or equivalently, the (integrated) expected norm squared of the metric projection.

5.2
Sudakov minoration via information-theoretic tools

In this section, we revisit the classical Sudakov minoration
[
52
]
in the context of our results and the Gaussian sequence model viewpoint.
Our first step is a localized inequality that is, on its face, weaker than the standard global Sudakov bound.
Nevertheless, our key observation is that when combined with the comparison results developed below (
Proposition

5.6
), it is already sufficient to recover the usual Sudakov lower bound up to constants.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any closed convex bounded set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the Gaussian width satisfies

w(T)\gtrsim\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}.

(118)

Let
\Pi_{T}(Y)=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\vartheta\in T}\|Y-\vartheta\|_{2}^{2}
denote the Euclidean projection onto
T
.
By the projection inequality, for any
\theta^{\star}\in T
and any
Y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

\|\Pi_{T}(Y)-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\langle Y-\theta^{\star},\Pi_{T}(Y)-\theta^{\star}\rangle.

(119)

Taking
Y=\theta^{\star}+\sigma g
with
g\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
, taking expectations, and then taking
\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in T}
yields

\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}\leqslant\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\Pi_{T}(\theta^{\star}+\sigma g)-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\sigma w(T).

(120)

Combining this with the metric characterization of the statistical rate in
Proposition

5.1
, we obtain for a universal constant
c>0
,

w(T)\geqslant c\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma>0:\\
\varepsilon^{2}\leqslant\sigma^{2}\log M_{T}^{\rm loc}(\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sigma}\geqslant c\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M_{T}^{\rm loc}(\varepsilon)},

(121)

where in the last step we take
\sigma^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}/\log M_{T}^{\rm loc}(\varepsilon)
(when
\log M_{T}^{\rm loc}(\varepsilon)>0
).
∎

Remark 5.2

(Proof method for
Lemma

5.1
)
.

We emphasize that our argument is information-theoretic. The only Gaussian-specific input is the explicit form of the Kullback-Leibler divergence in the Gaussian shift model, as it enters Fano’s lemma, and the proof would go through under any comparable control of the divergence at the relevant scales. This contrasts with standard proofs of Sudakov minoration, which typically proceed either via Gaussian comparison inequalities such as Sudakov-Fernique, or via Gaussian shift arguments combined with the dual Sudakov inequality and entropy duality; see
[
58
,
30
,
57
,
53
]
.

Remark 5.3

(On the convexity assumption in
Lemma

5.1
)
.

The same approach yields the lower bound

\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)\gtrsim\sup\Big\{\varepsilon>0:\ \varepsilon\leqslant\sigma\sqrt{\log M_{T}^{\rm loc}(\varepsilon)}\Big\}

(122)

for any
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Convexity is only used to obtain the complementary upper bound
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}\leqslant\sigma w(T)
.
For a general closed set
T
, the projection estimator still satisfies

\|\Pi_{T}(Y)-\theta\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2\langle Y-\theta,\Pi_{T}(Y)-\theta\rangle,

and therefore
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}\leqslant 2\sigma w(T)
, leading to an analog of
Lemma

5.1
with slightly worse constants.

Now, using information-theoretic arguments based on Anderson’s lemma, we obtain a dual form of Sudakov minoration.
Here we show that the same comparison principle, combined with a Fano-type argument, yields the following dual Sudakov bound.
We note that the dual Sudakov inequality is classical and is often proved via Gaussian shift and volumetric arguments
(see
[
30
,
57
,
53
]
), whereas in our approach the Gaussian shift is combined with the information-theoretic method.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
be a centrally symmetric convex body.
Then the Gaussian width satisfies

w(T)\gtrsim\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(B_{2}^{d},\varepsilon T^{\circ})}.

(123)

Together with the duality of entropy
[
6
]
,
Proposition

5.3
implies the usual Sudakov minoration

w(T)\gtrsim\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B_{2}^{d})}.

(124)

For an elementary argument relating the primal and dual forms of Sudakov minoration, see
[
57
, Lemma 2.5]
.

Fix
\sigma>0
and consider the Gaussian location model
Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)
.
Since
T
is centrally symmetric,
h_{T}=\|\cdot\|_{T^{\circ}}
is a norm.
By the minimax identity for Gaussian shifts based on Anderson’s lemma
(see
[
46
, Theorem 28.7]
and
[
46
, Lemmas 28.9–28.10]
),

\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\theta}\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta\|_{T^{\circ}}=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\sigma g\|_{T^{\circ}}=\sigma\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|g\|_{T^{\circ}}=\sigma\,w(T),

(125)

where
g\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and set
N=M(B_{2}^{d},\varepsilon T^{\circ})
.
Choose corresponding
\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N}\}\subset B_{2}^{d}
such that

\|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\|_{T^{\circ}}>\varepsilon
for all
i\neq j
.
A standard application of Fano’s inequality (see e.g.,
[
60
, Proposition 15.12]
) yields

\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\theta_{i}}\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta_{i}\|_{T^{\circ}}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}\log N}-\frac{\log 2}{\log N}\Big).

(126)

If
N\geqslant 6
, take
\sigma^{2}=(\log N)^{-1}
in the previous display and combine with
(
125
) to obtain

w(T)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sqrt{\log N}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\log 2}{\log N}\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{20}\,\varepsilon\sqrt{\log N}.

(127)

If
2\leqslant N\leqslant 5
, then
\varepsilon\leqslant\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{T^{\circ}}\leqslant 2\rad(T)
, so
\rad(T)\geqslant\varepsilon/2
.
Let
t_{0}\in T
satisfy
\|t_{0}\|_{2}=\rad(T)
. Then

w(T)=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{t\in T}\,\langle g,t\rangle\geqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\max\{\langle g,t_{0}\rangle,0\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\|t_{0}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\rad(T)\geqslant\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{1}{20}\,\varepsilon\sqrt{\log N},

(128)

where we used
\sqrt{\log N}\leqslant\sqrt{\log 5}
when
N\leqslant 5
. Combining the two cases, for every
\varepsilon>0
we have

w(T)\gtrsim\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(B_{2}^{d},\varepsilon T^{\circ})}.

(129)

Taking the supremum over
\varepsilon>0
gives the claim.
∎

Similarly, one can use Fano’s inequality, combined with the properties of the Wills functional, to obtain the usual form of the Sudakov minoration.
Again, the Sudakov minoration is classical, but is typically proved via comparison arguments, and so we emphasize the interest is primarily in the combination of information-theoretic and geometric tools in our proof.

Proposition 5.4
.

For any convex body
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the Gaussian width satisfies

w(T)\gtrsim\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})}.

(130)

Arguing as in the proof of
Proposition

5.3
, by adjusting constants, it suffices to show that
w(T)\gtrsim\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})}
for
\varepsilon>0
such that
M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})\geqslant 16
.
Let us fix such
\varepsilon>0
and define

\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8w(T)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\theta}=\mathsf{N}\left(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right).

(131)

Fano’s inequality
[
60
, Ch. 15]
gives the
following lower bound on the statistical minimax rate,

\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim P_{\theta}}\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{\inf_{Q}\max_{j}\mathcal{KL}\left(P_{\theta_{j}}\big\|\,Q\right)+\log 2}{\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})}\right).

(132)

Above, the maximum ranges over
\theta_{j}
in a maximal packing set corresponding to the packing number
M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})
, and infimum ranges over all probability measures
Q
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Arguing as in
Lemma

5.1
, it holds that
\varepsilon_{\star}(\sigma)^{2}\leqslant\sigma w(T)\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}/8
. Combined with
eq.

132
, we obtain,

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})-\log 2\Big)\leqslant 8\sigma w(T)\,\inf_{Q}\max_{j}\mathcal{KL}\left(P_{\theta_{j}}\big\|\,Q\right).

(133)

Now, denoting by
\dist(y,T)=\inf_{x\in T}\|x-y\|_{2}
, we take
Q
with Lebesgue density

q(y)=\frac{\exp(-\dist(y,T)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))}{(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})^{d}Z_{T}(\sigma)},\quad\mbox{where}\quad Z_{T}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})^{d}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\exp(-\dist(x,T)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))\,\mathrm{d}x.

(134)

By construction
\sup_{y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{\theta_{i}}}{\mathrm{d}Q}(y)\leqslant Z_{T}(\sigma)
, and hence

\mathcal{KL}\left(P_{\theta_{j}}\big\|\,Q\right)=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim P_{\theta_{j}}}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\theta_{j}}}{\mathrm{d}Q}(Y)\leqslant\log Z_{T}(\sigma)=\log W\Big(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big)\leqslant\frac{w(T)}{\sigma}.

(135)

Above, the penultimate equality arises by Hadwiger’s formula for the Wills functional
[
24
]
, while the final inequality is due to McMullen
[
35
]
.
Combining
eqs.

133
and
135
, we obtain

w(T)\geqslant\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\,\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B^{d}_{2})},

(136)

as required.
∎

5.3
Equivalence of “local” and “global” parameters

In this section, we discuss the relationship between the “local” versions of the results obtained in the previous sections (for instance, the local entropy integral bound in
Proposition

5.2
and the local minoration lower bound in
Lemma

5.1
) and their more standard “global” counterparts, such as the usual Sudakov minoration and the usual Dudley entropy integral.
Although even for the crosspolytope local and global covering numbers are known to differ at some scales, we show that the Sudakov minoration and Dudley entropy integrals remain of the same order, for any set
T
, when the global entropy is replaced by the local entropy.
A related one-sided observation appears in
[
55
, Section 4.2]
, where a “local Dudley” inequality is proved in which the generic chaining functional is upper bounded by a Dudley-type sum built from entropy numbers of local neighborhoods.

Throughout this section, it will be convenient to introduce the following notation.
Given a bounded set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the local and global packing entropies of
T
at scale
\varepsilon>0
are defined, respectively, as

h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)=\sup_{\delta\geqslant\varepsilon}\sup_{x\in T}\log M\bigl(T\cap(x+2\delta B_{2}^{d}),\delta B_{2}^{d}\bigr),\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{T}(\varepsilon)=\log M(T,\varepsilon B_{2}^{d}),

(137)

for any
\varepsilon>0
.
When
T
is convex, the supremum over
\delta\geqslant\varepsilon
in the definition of
h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)
is attained at
\delta=\varepsilon
, so this definition coincides with (
108
).
The starting point of our analysis is the following basic observation, which essentially follows from Yang-Barron
[
62
]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
be a bounded set, and let
\Delta=\diam(T)
. Then, for any integer
k\geqslant 1
, it holds that

h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\Delta 2^{-k})\leqslant h_{T}(\Delta 2^{-k})\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{k}h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\Delta 2^{-j}).

(138)

The first inequality in (
138
) is immediate, so we focus on the second.
We recall the relationship between covering and packing numbers
[
60
, Ch. 5]
:

N(A,\varepsilon B_{2}^{d})\leqslant M(A,\varepsilon B_{2}^{d})\leqslant N(A,\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}B_{2}^{d}),\qquad\mbox{for any }A\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }\varepsilon>0.

(139)

Fix
\delta>0
, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a maximal
\delta
-packing of
T
with respect to
\ell_{2}^{d}
.
Let
\{\theta^{(i)}\}_{i\in[N]}\subset T
be a
2\delta
-net for
T
with respect to
\ell_{2}^{d}
of minimal cardinality, so that

N=N(T,2\delta B_{2}^{d})
.
Then

|\mathcal{M}|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|\mathcal{M}\cap(\theta^{(i)}+2\delta B_{2}^{d})\big|\leqslant N\max_{i\in[N]}\big|\mathcal{M}\cap(\theta^{(i)}+2\delta B_{2}^{d})\big|\leqslant N\sup_{\theta\in T}M\bigl(T\cap(\theta+2\delta B_{2}^{d}),\delta B_{2}^{d}\bigr),

(140)

where the last inequality uses that
\mathcal{M}\cap(\theta^{(i)}+2\delta B_{2}^{d})
is
\delta
-separated, and hence its cardinality is at most the corresponding local packing number.
Taking the supremum over all
\delta
-packings
\mathcal{M}
yields

M(T,\delta B_{2}^{d})\leqslant N(T,2\delta B_{2}^{d})\cdot\sup_{\theta\in T}M\bigl(T\cap(\theta+2\delta B_{2}^{d}),\delta B_{2}^{d}\bigr).

(141)

Since
N(T,2\delta B_{2}^{d})\leqslant M(T,2\delta B_{2}^{d})
by (
139
), we obtain

h_{T}(\delta)-h_{T}(2\delta)\leqslant h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\delta),\qquad\mbox{for any }\delta>0.

(142)

Applying (
142
) with
\delta=\Delta 2^{-j}
for
j\geqslant 1
and summing over
j\in[k]
gives

h_{T}(\Delta 2^{-k})-h_{T}(\Delta)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{k}h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\Delta 2^{-j}).

(143)

Finally, we have
M(T,\Delta B_{2}^{d})=1
and hence
h_{T}(\Delta)=0
, which completes the proof.
∎

We can now use
Lemma

5.2
to show that the “local” and “global” versions of the Dudley entropy integral and Sudakov minorations are equivalent up to universal constant factors. To state the first result, we recall the local and global Dudley entropy integrals, which are respectively given by

\mathcal{J}_{\delta}^{\rm loc}(T)=\int_{\delta}^{\diam(T)}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{J}_{\delta}(T)=\int_{\delta}^{\diam(T)}\sqrt{h_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon,

(144)

for
\delta\in[0,\diam(T)]
.

Proposition 5.5

(Equivalence of local and global Dudley integrals)
.

For any bounded set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\mathcal{J}_{\delta}^{\rm loc}(T)\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{\delta}(T)\leqslant 4\;\mathcal{J}_{\delta/4}^{\rm loc}(T),

(145)

for any
\delta\in[0,\diam(T)]
.

By rescaling, assume without loss of generality that
\diam(T)=1
.
The first inequality is immediate.
For the second, using that
h_{T}
is nonincreasing, fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and let
k=\left\lceil\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)\right\rceil
.
Then
Lemma

5.2
and subadditivity of the square root give

\sqrt{h_{T}(\varepsilon)}\leqslant\sqrt{h_{T}(2^{-k})}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\left[\,\varepsilon\leqslant 2^{1-j}\,\right]\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}.

(146)

Integrating (
146
) over
\varepsilon\in[\delta,1]
yields

\mathcal{J}_{\delta}(T)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(2^{1-j}-\delta)_{+}\,\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}.

(147)

For each
j\geqslant 1
, monotonicity of
h^{\rm loc}_{T}
implies

2^{-(j+1)}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}\leqslant\int_{2^{-(j+1)}}^{2^{-j}}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon,

(148)

and hence
(2^{1-j}-\delta)_{+}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}\leqslant 4\int_{2^{-(j+1)}}^{2^{-j}}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon
.
Summing over
j
for which
2^{1-j}>\delta
gives

\mathcal{J}_{\delta}(T)\leqslant 4\int_{2^{-(N_{\delta}+1)}}^{1}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\leqslant 4\int_{\delta/4}^{1}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon=4\,\mathcal{J}_{\delta/4}^{\rm loc}(T),

(149)

where
N_{\delta}=\left\lfloor\log_{2}(2/\delta)\right\rfloor
.
∎

We now introduce two versions of the minoration,

\Psi^{\rm loc}(T)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\;\varepsilon\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(\varepsilon)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi(T)=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\;\varepsilon\sqrt{h_{T}(\varepsilon)}.

(150)

Proposition 5.6

(Equivalence of local and global Sudakov minoration)
.

For any bounded set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we have

\Psi^{\rm loc}(T)\leqslant\Psi(T)\leqslant 4\,\Psi^{\rm loc}(T).

(151)

In particular, this result combined with
Lemma

5.1
immediately implies the usual Sudakov minoration inequality for any bounded (not necessarily convex due to
Remark

5.3
) closed set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
:

w(T)\gtrsim\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\varepsilon\sqrt{\log M(T,\varepsilon B_{2}^{d})}.

(152)

By rescaling, assume without loss that
\diam(T)=1
.
The first inequality is immediate.
For the second, using (
146
), for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
k=\left\lceil\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)\right\rceil
,

\varepsilon\sqrt{h_{T}(\varepsilon)}\leqslant\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}\leqslant\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{k}2^{j}\cdot\underbrace{2^{-j}\sqrt{h^{\rm loc}_{T}(2^{-j})}}_{\leqslant\Psi^{\rm loc}(T)}\leqslant\varepsilon(2^{k+1}-1)\Psi^{\rm loc}(T)\leqslant 4\Psi^{\rm loc}(T).

(153)

Taking the supremum over
\varepsilon>0
yields the claim.
∎

5.4
Comparisons with generic chaining

One of the most natural questions is to relate Talagrand’s
\gamma_{2}
functional to the decompositions implied by
Theorem

2.1
. Recall that by the Fernique-Talagrand majorizing measure theorem
[
53
, Theorem 2.10.1]
we have
w(T)\asymp\gamma_{2}(T)
,
where we use the formulation in terms of admissible partitions.
Concretely, an admissible sequence of partitions of
T
is a nested family

\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
such that
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{T\}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
refines
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for every
n
, and
|\mathcal{A}_{n}|\leqslant 2^{2^{n}}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.
For
x\in T
, let
A_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
denote the unique cell containing
x
.
Then (
[
53
, Definition 2.7.3]
),

\gamma_{2}(T)=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\;\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\,\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr),

(154)

where the infimum is over all admissible sequences of partitions
\mathcal{A}
of
T
. We now introduce truncated generic-chaining quantities in the language of admissible partitions.
Fix an admissible sequence of partitions
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and an integer
p\geqslant 1
, and define the corresponding
first
p
levels
and
tail
contributions by

\gamma_{2,<p}(T;\mathcal{A})=\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr),\quad\textrm{and}\quad\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T;\mathcal{A})=\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr).

(155)

We set
\gamma_{2,<0}(T;\mathcal{A})=0
. Finally, we define the corresponding optimized truncated functionals

\gamma_{2,<p}(T)=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\gamma_{2,<p}(T;\mathcal{A}),\qquad\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T)=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T;\mathcal{A}),

(156)

where the infima are over admissible sequences of partitions of
T
. We remark that corresponding truncated functionals appear naturally in the literature involving the analysis of generic chaining functionals
[
20
,
38
,
43
]
. It appears that the format of our result and corresponding derivations simplify significantly if we focus on a decomposition of
Corollary

4.1
, namely

w(T)\asymp\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)+\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{r(\nu)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\mathrm{d}\nu.

(157)

Since in the context of generic chaining we can only capture the Gaussian width up to multiplicative constant factors, the bound (
157
) is meaningful, even though its decomposition is not exact as in
Theorem

2.1
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Given a compact convex set
T\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
containing the origin and
\sigma>0
, set

p_{\sigma}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{p\geqslant 0}\left\{\sigma(2^{p}-1)+\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T)\right\}.

(158)

Then, it holds that

w(T)\asymp\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)+\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T),

(159)

and

\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)\asymp\gamma_{2,\geqslant p_{\sigma}}(T)+\sigma(2^{p_{\sigma}}-1).

(160)

Remark 5.4
.

The proposition does not identify the integral term
\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}\frac{r(\nu)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\mathrm{d}\nu
with
\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)
. Rather, it shows that, up to universal constants, the Wills term captures the large-scale (tail) contribution of generic chaining, while
\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)
can be viewed as a small-scale complement in the sense of (
157
).

First, by Corollary 4.1 in
[
43
]
with the only observation that
\sigma\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T/\sigma)=\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T)
we have

\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)\asymp\gamma_{2,\geqslant p_{\sigma}}(T)+\sigma(2^{p_{\sigma}}-1).

(161)

Next, by the majorizing measure theorem we have for some absolute constant
c>0
,

w(T)\leqslant c\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr).

(162)

Now we argue that

w(T)\leqslant c\bigl(\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)+\gamma_{2,\geqslant p_{\sigma}}(T)\bigr).

(163)

This step is almost obvious, but requires some technicalities to merge together two admissible sequences corresponding to
\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)
and
\gamma_{2,\geqslant p_{\sigma}}(T)
respectively.
Set
p=p_{\sigma}
. If
p=0
, this is immediate from (
3
). Let
\mathcal{A}^{-}=(\mathcal{A}^{-}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and
\mathcal{A}^{+}=(\mathcal{A}^{+}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be arbitrary admissible sequences of partitions of
T
.
Define a new sequence
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
by

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}^{-}_{n},&0\leqslant n\leqslant p-1,\\
\mathcal{A}^{-}_{p-1},&n=p\\
\{A\cap B:A\in\mathcal{A}^{-}_{p-1},B\in\mathcal{A}^{+}_{n-1}\},&n\geqslant p+1.\end{cases}

(164)

Then,
\mathcal{A}
is admissible. Indeed, nesting is clear, and for
n\geqslant p+1
,

|\mathcal{A}_{n}|\leqslant|\mathcal{A}^{-}_{p-1}||\mathcal{A}^{+}_{n-1}|\leqslant 2^{2^{p-1}}2^{2^{n-1}}\leqslant 2^{2^{n}}
.
Moreover, for
n\geqslant p+1
we have
\diam(A_{n}(x))\leqslant\diam(A^{+}_{n-1}(x))
.
Therefore, for every
x\in T
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A^{-}_{n}(x)\bigr)+2^{p/2}\diam\bigl(A^{-}_{p-1}(x)\bigr)+\sum_{n=p+1}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)

(165)

\displaystyle\leqslant\gamma_{2,<p}(T;\mathcal{A}^{-})+\sqrt{2}\cdot 2^{(p-1)/2}\diam\bigl(A^{-}_{p-1}(x)\bigr)+\sqrt{2}\sum_{m=p}^{\infty}2^{m/2}\diam\bigl(A^{+}_{m}(x)\bigr)

(166)

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\sqrt{2})\gamma_{2,<p}(T;\mathcal{A}^{-})+\sqrt{2}\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T;\mathcal{A}^{+}).

(167)

Passing to the supremum over
x\in T
, we obtain

\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)\leqslant(1+\sqrt{2})\gamma_{2,<p}(T;\mathcal{A}^{-})+\sqrt{2}\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T;\mathcal{A}^{+}).

(168)

We emphasize that
\mathcal{A}
is obtained by gluing together the two generic admissible sequences
\mathcal{A}^{-}
and
\mathcal{A}^{+}
. Plugging this
\mathcal{A}
into the infimum over admissible sequences and then taking the infimum over
\mathcal{A}^{-}
and
\mathcal{A}^{+}
(separately) gives

\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\sup_{x\in T}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{n/2}\diam\bigl(A_{n}(x)\bigr)\leqslant(1+\sqrt{2})\gamma_{2,<p}(T)+\sqrt{2}\gamma_{2,\geqslant p}(T),

(169)

and hence
w(T)\leqslant c\bigl(\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)+\gamma_{2,\geqslant p_{\sigma}}(T)\bigr)
after adjusting absolute constants. By (
161
) this simplifies for some
c_{1}>0
,

w(T)\leqslant c_{1}\left(\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)+\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)\right).

(170)

For the lower bound, from McMullen’s inequality (
82
), we have
\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)\leqslant c_{2}\,w(T)
. The lower bound in (
3
) yields
\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)\leqslant\gamma_{2}(T)\leqslant c_{3}\,w(T)
. Together, these give

\sigma\log\left(W\left(\frac{T}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\right)+\gamma_{2,<p_{\sigma}}(T)\leqslant c_{4}\,w(T),

(171)

and the proof is complete.
∎

5.5
Upper bounds based on the Donsker-Varadhan variational identity

One of the notable techniques for bounding the first term in
eq.

17a
in the Gaussian case relies on variational methods based on the Donsker–Varadhan duality formula.
This identity appears in universal coding and sequential prediction under logarithmic loss, where it underlies normalized maximum-likelihood constructions and minimax regret
[
50
,
46
]
; see also the regret viewpoint in
[
43
]
.
In statistics, this approach has been developed extensively by Catoni and co-authors, mainly in the context of robust estimation
[
18
,
7
,
17
]
.
Recently, it was shown in
[
65
]
that this method yields a sharp bound on the Gaussian width of ellipsoids, a case where the Dudley entropy integral is known to be suboptimal.

What remained unclear was whether the same variational method could be extended to other convex bodies to bound the Gaussian width.
We demonstrate that, at least for canonical examples such as ellipsoids and crosspolytopes, it does provide not only the optimal bounds on the Gaussian width but a sharp bound on the smaller first term in our decompositions.
In what follows, we present a self-contained exposition of this approach.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\mu
be any distribution on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and let
g\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
. Then

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\sup_{\rho\ll\mu}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\rho}\left[\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right]-\mathcal{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\mu)\right)\right]\leqslant 0.

(172)

Using Jensen’s inequality, the Donsker–Varadhan variational formula
[
14
, Corollary 4.14]
, and Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{\rho\ll\mu}\Big(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\rho}[\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2]-\mathcal{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\mu)\Big)

(173)

\displaystyle\leqslant\log\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{\rho\ll\mu}\exp\Big(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\rho}[\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2]-\mathcal{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\mu)\Big)\right)

(174)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\mu}\exp(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2)\right)

(175)

\displaystyle=\log\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\mu}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\exp(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2)=0.

(176)

The claim follows.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The result of
Proposition

5.8
can also be established through connections with sequential probability assignment in the Gaussian setting.
A key observation is that
\log W(\cdot)
admits an interpretation in terms of minimax regret via Shtarkov’s integral representation
[
50
]
; see
[
43
]
for a detailed account in the present geometric setting.
Importantly,
Theorem

5.2
shows that in our context the bound of
Proposition

5.8
can be systematically improved.

The idea of our approach is the following: once we choose the distribution
\mu
(depending on
T
), the Donsker–Varadhan formula implies that

\sup_{\rho\ll\mu}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\rho}\left[\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right]-\mathcal{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\mu)\right)

(177)

is attained at the Gibbs posterior
\widetilde{\rho}
given by

\widetilde{\rho}(\mathrm{d}x)\ \propto\ \exp(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

(178)

Our aim will be to choose
\mu
so that

\sup_{x\in T}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right)\leqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x\sim\widetilde{\rho}}\big[\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\big]+\Delta,

(179)

where
\Delta
is a nonnegative complexity term. This yields the desired bound on the first term in both decompositions. To motivate our next result, we first discuss the application of this method to ellipsoids in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

\mathcal{E}_{a}=\Big\{\,x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}:\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}^{2}}\leqslant 1\,\Big\}.

(180)

We note that a technically more interesting computation for the crosspolytope is given in
Section

6.1
.

Corollary 5.3

(Logarithm of the Wills functional of the ellipsoid via the variational approach)
.

Fix
\lambda>0
.
For the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{a}\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, and with the choice

\mu=\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\lambda}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}\right),
it holds that

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{a}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right)\right]\leqslant\log\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\exp\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{a}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right)\right]\right)\leqslant\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(1+\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\lambda}\right).

(181)

Note that
Corollary

5.3
bounds a larger quantity: via (
), it yields an upper bound on
\log W(\mathcal{E}_{a}/\sqrt{2\pi})
(up to the factor
1/\sqrt{2\pi}
), rather than directly bounding the first term in
eq.

17a
. Moreover, by the lower bound in
[
43
, Proposition 7.1]
, this estimate is optimal up to multiplicative constants for the logarithm of the Wills functional of the ellipsoid.

Remark 5.6

(Width of the ellipsoid)
.

Observe that by a rescaling argument, the bound in
Corollary

5.3
is already enough to recover the Gaussian width of the ellipsoid. Indeed, in the context of
Theorem

2.1
we consider

w\big(\mathcal{E}_{a}\cap r(\sigma)B^{d}_{2}\big)-\frac{r(\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}=\sigma\left(w\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{a}}{\sigma}\cap\frac{r(\sigma)}{\sigma}B^{d}_{2}\right)-\frac{r(\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\leqslant\sigma\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{a/\sigma}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right).

(182)

Applying

Corollary

5.3
to
\mathcal{E}_{a/\sigma}
then yields

\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\sigma\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{a/\sigma}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)\leqslant\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\inf_{\lambda>0}\left(\frac{\lambda\sigma}{2}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(1+\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\lambda\sigma^{2}}\right)\right)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2}},

(183)

which is a sharp upper bound on the Gaussian width of the ellipsoid.

Although sufficient to bound the Gaussian width of the ellipsoid, the estimate in
Proposition

5.8
is suboptimal. We now introduce another tool that at least for the ellipsoid yields a sharp bound on the first term in
eq.

17a
.
Rather than directly invoking chaining arguments, we again employ the variational approach, but now with localized priors, introduced in the statistical framework by Catoni
[
18
]
(see also
[
42
]
).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and let
g\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
. Define the localized (
g
-dependent) distribution

\mu_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{\exp(-\|x-g\|^{2}/8)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int\exp(-\|x^{\prime}-g\|^{2}/8)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})}.

(184)

Then

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\Bigl[\sup_{\rho\ll\mu}\Bigl(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x^{\prime}\sim\rho}\bigl[\langle x^{\prime},g\rangle-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}/2\bigr]-2\,\mathcal{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\mu_{\operatorname{loc}})\Bigr)\Bigr]\leqslant 0.

(185)

Unlike
Proposition

5.8
, whose proof only uses the moment generating function of
g
and extends to sub-Gaussian
g
, the proof of
Theorem

5.2
, deferred to
Section

5.6.4
, critically exploits Gaussianity through an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup argument.
We now apply
Theorem

5.2
in a style resembling the proof of
Corollary

5.3
.

Corollary 5.4

(Local width of the ellipsoid via the variational approach)
.

Fix
\lambda>0
. For the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{a}
, and with the choice of measure

\mu=\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{\lambda}\,e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}\right),

it holds that

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{a}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right)\right]\leqslant 2\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{4a_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}^{2}+4\lambda}.

(186)

Again, optimizing over
\lambda>0
in
Corollary

5.4
yields an optimal bound directly for the quantity

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\,\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{a}}(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2)

]

Proof of

(Worst-case variance of the LSE)

For any nonempty, closed convex set

\sup_{\theta\in T}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\big\|\Pi_{T}(Y)-\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[\Pi_{T}(Y)]\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\lesssim\varepsilon_{\star}^{2}(\sigma),

;

6
Example: Gaussian width of the crosspolytope in
\mathbf{R}^{d}

In this section we focus on a single illustrative example: the Gaussian width of the crosspolytope

B_{1}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}:\ \|x\|_{1}\leqslant 1\}.

(221)

Of course, one can bound
w(B_{1}^{d})
by the elementary identity

w(B_{1}^{d})=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup_{x\in B_{1}^{d}}\,\langle x,g\rangle=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\max_{i\in[d]}|g_{i}|\leqslant\sqrt{2\log(2d)}.

(222)

This example is instructive because it highlights the limitations of entropy-based chaining bounds: Dudley’s integral incurs its largest possible gap for sets in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and yields an upper bound of order
\log^{3/2}(d)
for
B_{1}^{d}
.
Sharp bounds can be obtained by constructing an optimal admissible sequence (see, e.g.,
[
44
, Section 4.4]
), and related work develops more explicit geometric relaxations of generic chaining for convex bodies
[
56
]
recovering the correct bound on
w(B_{1}^{d})
.

Below we present three different methods to bound
w(B_{1}^{d})
for the crosspolytope that avoid both the coordinatewise maximum argument and explicit generic chaining constructions (and their relaxations). The purpose is not to compete with the shortcut (
222
), but to illustrate how our decomposition viewpoint leads to sharp bounds through several complementary mechanisms.

6.1
A bound based on the variational approach

Since our goal is to avoid the explicit admissible sequence constructions and their relaxations, we first use
Proposition

5.8
with a specifically chosen prior, almost identical to one introduced recently in the information theory literature
[
41
]
in the context of proving asymptotic regret bounds with respect to logarithmic loss, namely

\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\propto\exp(-\lambda\|x\|_{1})\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\left(\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}x_{i})+\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x_{i}|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\mathrm{d}x_{i}\right),

(223)

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac measure at zero. As will be clear from our computations below, the logic of this prior lies in mimicking the typical solution of the
\ell_{1}
-regularized minimization problem: the Dirac mass is responsible for possible sparse solutions, namely a coordinate being equal to zero. A straightforward computation shows that the normalization constant
Z_{0}
for this distribution is equal to

Z_{0}=\left(1+\frac{2}{\lambda^{3}}\exp(-\lambda^{2}/2)\right)^{d}.

(224)

We are ready to state the result of this section.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
. Then, if in
Proposition

5.8
we choose the prior
\mu
according to (
223
) with
\lambda=\sqrt{2\log(d)}
, we obtain

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\sup\limits_{x\in B_{1}^{d}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\|x\|^{2}/2\right)\right]\leqslant\sqrt{2\log(d)}+\frac{3\log(2\log(d))}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\log(d)}}+\frac{c}{{\log^{3/2}(d)}},

(225)

where
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}
.

Note that the leading term
\sqrt{2\log(d)}
is sharp for the Gaussian width of
B_{1}^{d}
. Moreover, our
Theorem

2.1
with
\sigma=1
immediately implies the correct bound on
w(B_{1}^{d})
with the correct leading term
\sqrt{2\log(d)}
. Indeed, in the second term of the decomposition we may use that for the crosspolytope
r(\sigma)\leqslant 1
and therefore, for the same
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}
,

w(T)\leqslant\sqrt{2\log(d)}+\frac{3\log(2\log(d))}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\log(d)}}+1+\frac{c}{{\log^{3/2}(d)}}.

(226)

First, by
Proposition

5.8
and the Donsker–Varadhan variational formula we have

0\geqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup\limits_{\rho\ll\mu}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x^{\prime}\sim\rho}\left[\langle x^{\prime},g\rangle-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}/2\right]-\mathcal{KL}(\rho\,\|\,\mu)\right)=\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\log\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x^{\prime}\sim\mu}\left[\exp(\langle x^{\prime},g\rangle-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}/2)\right]\right).

(227)

We can write
\log\left(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{x^{\prime}\sim\mu}\left[\exp(\langle x^{\prime},g\rangle-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}/2)\right]\right)
as

\displaystyle-\log(Z_{0})+\log\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\exp\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}-\lambda\|x\|_{1}\right)\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\left(\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}x_{i})+\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x_{i}|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\log(Z_{0})+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda|x|\right)\left(\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)+\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\right).

(229)

Observe that

\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbf{R}}(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda|x|)=\begin{cases}0,&|g_{i}|\leqslant\lambda,\\[6.0pt]

First, consider the case where

\displaystyle\log\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda|x|\right)\left(\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)+\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\right)

(232)

Otherwise, if

\displaystyle\log\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda|x|\right)\left(\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)+\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\right)

(233)

\displaystyle\geqslant\log\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda|x|\right)\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

(234)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\lambda}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2+\lambda x\right)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\lambda}^{+\infty}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda x\right)\mathrm{d}x\right)\right)

(235)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\left(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Bigl[\exp(\tfrac{(g_{i}+\lambda)^{2}}{2})\,\Phi(-g_{i}-2\lambda)+\exp(\tfrac{(g_{i}-\lambda)^{2}}{2})\,\bigl(1-\Phi(2\lambda-g_{i})\bigr)\Bigr]\right)\right)

(236)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|g_{i}|-\lambda\bigr)^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}-2\log\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+\log(1-\Phi(\lambda))

(238)

where in the last line we used
\log(1-\Phi(\lambda))\geqslant-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}-\log(\lambda)-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}
. Therefore, combining the above inequalities we have

\displaystyle-\log(Z_{0})+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}}\exp\left(xg_{i}-x^{2}/2-\lambda|x|\right)\left(\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)+\frac{\exp(\lambda^{2}/2)}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left[\,|x|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\right)

(239)

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{x\in B_{1}^{d}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}-\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup\limits_{x\in B_{1}^{d}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}-\lambda\|x\|_{1}\right)

(240)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}-\lambda\|x\|_{1}\right).

(241)

Combining this with (

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup\limits_{x\in B_{1}^{d}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)\leqslant\lambda+\log(Z_{0})+d\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{P}}\nolimits\{|g_{1}|\geqslant\lambda\}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}+3\log(\lambda)\Bigr).

(242)

We choose
\lambda=\sqrt{2\log(d)}
(note that
\lambda\geqslant 1
for
d\geqslant 2

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\sup\limits_{x\in B_{1}^{d}}\left(\langle x,g\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)\leqslant\sqrt{2\log(d)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\;{\log^{3/2}(d)}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\log^{3/2}(d)}+\frac{3\log(2\log(d))}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\log(d)}}.

(243)

The claim follows.

6.2
A bound based on the profile of intrinsic volumes

Recall from
Theorem

2.3
that, since
\diam(B_{1}^{d})=2
, we have

w(B_{1}^{d})\asymp i^{\star}\,\diam(B_{1}^{d}),

(244)

where

i^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\left\{V_{i}\left(B_{1}^{d}/2\right)\right\}.

(245)

Thus, to upper bound
w(B_{1}^{d})
it suffices to control the location of
i^{\star}
. By unimodality of the intrinsic volume sequence, this reduces to understanding the ratios

\frac{V_{i}(B_{1}^{d}/2)}{V_{i+1}(B_{1}^{d}/2)}=2\,\frac{V_{i}(B_{1}^{d})}{V_{i+1}(B_{1}^{d})}.

(246)

The next two lemmas provide lower bounds on these ratios that are sharp enough to yield the optimal order for
w(B_{1}^{d})
.
We remark that our argument uses explicit formulas for intrinsic volume ratios of
B_{1}^{d}
for
i\geqslant 1
. While this is a strong input, once the zeroth intrinsic volume is excluded it is still scale-free, and it is not immediate from the ratios alone how to recover the Gaussian width without the structural connection provided by
Theorem

2.3
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-2
set
m=d-i-1
and denote

Z_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-(i+1)x^{2})\big(\operatorname{erf}(x)\big)^{m}\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{0}}\exp(-(i+1)x^{2})\big(\operatorname{erf}(x)\big)^{m}\mathrm{d}x,

where
\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}\exp(-y^{2})\mathrm{d}y
.
Let
Y
be a non-negative random variable distributed according to
\nu
. Then

\frac{V_{i}(B_{1}^{d})}{V_{i+1}(B_{1}^{d})}=\frac{i+1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[Y]}.

(248)

First, the identity in
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
gives for
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-2

\frac{V_{i}(B_{1}^{d})}{V_{i+1}(B_{1}^{d})}=\frac{(i+1)^{2}}{2m}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-(i+1)x^{2})(\operatorname{erf}(x))^{m}\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-(i+2)x^{2})(\operatorname{erf}(x))^{m-1}\mathrm{d}x}.

(249)

This is equivalent to

\frac{V_{i}(B_{1}^{d})}{V_{i+1}(B_{1}^{d})}=\frac{(i+1)^{2}}{2m}\frac{1}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[\exp(-Y^{2})/\operatorname{erf}(Y)\big]}.

(250)

Set

g(x)=\exp(-(i+1)x^{2})\big(\operatorname{erf}(x)\big)^{m}.

Since
m\geqslant 1
, we have
g(0)=0
and
g(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. Observe that

g^{\prime}(x)=-2(i+1)x\exp(-(i+1)x^{2})\big(\operatorname{erf}(x)\big)^{m}+\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp(-(i+2)x^{2})\big(\operatorname{erf}(x)\big)^{m-1}.

(251)

We divide the identity
\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0
by
Z_{0}
and obtain

-2(i+1)\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[Y]+\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\left[\frac{\exp(-Y^{2})}{\operatorname{erf}(Y)}\right]=0.

(252)

Plugging this into (
250
) completes the proof.
∎

To complete the derivation of the upper bound on
w(B_{1}^{d})
, note that if for some
j
we have

\frac{j+1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[Y]}>1
, then by unimodality we may use
j
as an upper bound on the maximizer
i^{\star}
above.
Thus it suffices to lower bound
\frac{j+1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[Y]}
, which reduces to upper bounding
\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[Y]
.
The following lemma provides the required estimate.

Lemma 6.2
.

For the random variable
Y
defined in
Lemma

6.1
we have

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits Y\leqslant\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{d}{i+1}\right)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(i+1)}}.

(253)

As above, let
g(x)=\exp(-(i+1)x^{2})\big(\operatorname{erf}(x)\big)^{m}
, so that
\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\frac{g(x)}{Z_{0}}\mathrm{d}x
.
Let
t
be a maximizer of
g
. Using
g^{\prime}(t)=0
, we have

-2(i+1)t+\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\exp(-t^{2})}{\operatorname{erf}(t)}=0,\quad\textrm{which implies}\quad\exp(t^{2})\,\operatorname{erf}(t)=\frac{m}{\sqrt{\pi}(i+1)\,t}.

(254)

Using the bound
1-\operatorname{erf}(x)\leqslant\dfrac{\exp(-x^{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\,x}
for
x>0
,
we have

\exp(t^{2})\,\operatorname{erf}(t)\geqslant\exp(t^{2})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,t}.

Combining this with (
254
), we obtain

t\exp(t^{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{i+1}\Big).

If
t\geqslant 1
, then
\exp(t^{2})\leqslant t\exp(t^{2})
and hence
t^{2}\leqslant\log\bigl(1+\frac{m}{i+1}\bigr)
.
If
t<1
and
\log\bigl(1+\frac{m}{i+1}\bigr)<1
, then
\frac{m}{i+1}\leqslant e-1
and (
254
) together with

\operatorname{erf}(t)\geqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}t\,\exp(-t^{2})
yields
t^{2}\leqslant\frac{m}{2(i+1)}\leqslant\log\bigl(1+\frac{m}{i+1}\bigr)
.
Thus,

t\leqslant\sqrt{\log\left(1+\frac{m}{i+1}\right)}.

(255)

Given this bound on
t
it remains to bound
\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits(Y-t)_{+}
, where
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. Let
h(x)=\log g(x)=-(i+1)x^{2}+m\log(\operatorname{erf}x)
. Since for any
x>0
,

h^{\prime\prime}(x)=-2(i+1)+m\big(\log(\operatorname{erf}x)\big)^{\prime\prime}\leqslant-2(i+1),

(256)

and
h^{\prime}(t)=0
, we have for all
x\geqslant t
,

h(x)\leqslant h(t)-(i+1)(x-t)^{2},\quad\textrm{which implies}\quad\frac{g(x)}{g(t)}\leqslant\exp\big(-(i+1)(x-t)^{2}\big).

(257)

Finally, using this inequality we have

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[(Y-t)_{+}\big]=\frac{\int_{t}^{\infty}(x-t)\,g(x)\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)\mathrm{d}x}\leqslant\frac{\int_{t}^{\infty}(x-t)\,g(x)\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{t}^{\infty}g(x)\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}u\,g(t+u)\mathrm{d}u}{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t+u)\mathrm{d}u}.

(258)

We now bound the last ratio. We introduce the random variable
U
, with density proportional to
\exp(-(i+1)u^{2})
on
[0,\infty)
, so that

\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}u\,g(t+u)\mathrm{d}u}{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t+u)\mathrm{d}u}=\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[U\,r(U)\big]}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[r(U)\big]},

(259)

with
r(u)=\frac{g(t+u)}{\exp(-(i+1)u^{2})}
. Note that the function
r
is non-increasing. Indeed, recalling that

h^{\prime}(x)=-2(i+1)x+\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\exp(-x^{2})}{\operatorname{erf}(x)}
and that
t
maximizes
g
, we have
h^{\prime}(t)=0
, that is,

\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\exp(-t^{2})}{\operatorname{erf}(t)}=2(i+1)t.

(260)

Define
\varphi(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\exp(-x^{2})}{\operatorname{erf}(x)}
for
x>0
.
A direct derivative calculation shows that
\varphi
is strictly decreasing. Now,

\log(r(u))=h(t+u)+(i+1)u^{2}
, and hence

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\log(r(u))=h^{\prime}(t+u)+2(i+1)u=-2(i+1)t+m\,\varphi(t+u).

(261)

Because
\varphi
is decreasing and
t+u\geqslant t
, we have
\varphi(t+u)\leqslant\varphi(t)
, and thus, by (
260
),

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\log(r(u))\leqslant-2(i+1)t+m\,\varphi(t)=0,

(262)

so
r
is non-increasing. Therefore, by Chebyshev’s association inequality
[
14
, Theorem 2.14]
we have

\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[U\,r(U)\big]}{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[r(U)\big]}\leqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits U=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}u\exp({-(i+1)u^{2}})\mathrm{d}u}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp({-(i+1)u^{2}})\mathrm{d}u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(i+1)}}.

(263)

This combined with (
255
) proves the claim.
∎

We are ready to provide the desired upper bound on
w(B_{1}^{d})
. By
Lemma

6.1
and
Lemma

6.2
, for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-2
,

\frac{V_{i}(B_{1}^{d}/2)}{V_{i+1}(B_{1}^{d}/2)}=\frac{i+1}{\sqrt{\pi}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[Y]}\geqslant\frac{i+1}{\sqrt{\pi}\left(\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{d}{i+1}\right)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(i+1)}}\right)}.

(264)

Since
\log\left(\frac{d}{i+1}\right)\leqslant\log d
, the right-hand side is at least one as soon as
i\gtrsim\sqrt{\log d}
.
By unimodality this implies

, and therefore
w(B_{1}^{d})\lesssim\sqrt{\log d}
, as desired.
