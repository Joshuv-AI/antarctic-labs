Abstract
. The anthyphairetic process leads from a pair ( a , b ) (a,b) of coprime positive integers to the pair ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) by successive subtractions of the smaller number from the bigger one. This process, which is a slow version of Euclid’s algorithm applied to the pair ( a , b ) (a,b) , corresponds naturally to the process of successive blowups leading to the minimal embedded resolution of the plane curve defined by y a − x b = 0 y^{a}-x^{b}=0 . This blowup process may be represented graphically by a special two-dimensional simplicial complex called a lotus . This allows to localize the various numbers appearing either during the anthyphairetic process or during the Euclidean algorithm at precise positions inside the lotus. In this introductory article, I recall first the construction of this lotus starting from the sequence of quotients generated by the Euclidean algorithm. I present then an alternative way of constructing it directly from the sequence of pairs of coprime integers generated by the anthyphairetic process, using what I call anthyphairetic rectangles . I conclude by explaining how to reconstruct from a lotus the corresponding sequence of pairs of coprime integers. This is a simple illustration of the way lotuses may serve as computational architectures .

1.
Introduction

Anthyphairesis
is a term classically used in the research on ancient Greek theories of
ratio and proportion, related to what is called the
Euclidean algorithm

(see
[
18
]
and
[
10
]
). Here we
will use the term in a sense close to David Fowler’s
[
7
, Chapter 2]
or
[
6
, Section 6]
. Let me quote from the introduction of
[
7
, Chapter 2]
:

I shall be arguing that substantial portions of the Elements, Plato’s mathematical references, and other fourth- and third-century testimonies can be interpreted very plausibly in anthyphairetic terms. The sequence of repetition numbers that arises from anthyphairesis represents the result of repeated subtractions, not divisions, and this interpretation is corroborated by the
Greek name, derived from anti-hypo-hairesis, ‘reciprocal sub-traction’.

Let us say that an
anthyphairetic process

starts from two quantities
(A,B)
and replaces them iteratively by new pairs of quantities obtained by subtracting each time the smaller one from the greater one. If this
anthyphairetic process
leads to a pair
(D,D)
of equal quantities – or, equivalently, in modern terms which accept the existence of a zero quantity, if they lead to a pair
(D,0)
in which one of the quantities is zero – then
A
and
B
are called
commensurable
; otherwise, they are called
incommensurable
. In the commensurable case, the quantity
D
is the
greatest common divisor
of
A
and
B
. Then
(A,B)
are proportional to a unique pair
(a,b)
of coprime positive integers, related to
A
and
B
by the equalities
A=aD
and
B=bD
.

Nowadays, the previous process of computation of the greatest common divisor is usually not presented as a finite process of iterative
subtractions
, but of iterative
divisions
with remainders, starting from the division of
B
by
A
(assuming that
B>A
). One speaks then of the
Euclidean algorithm
. If
q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{m}
are the positive integers appearing as quotients of the divisions performed during the algorithm, then in the commensurable case
one gets a finite continued fraction expression of the ratio
B/A=b/a
:

\frac{b}{a}=q_{1}+\cfrac{1}{q_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\cdots+\cfrac{1}{q_{m}}}}.

There is a strong relation between the anthyphairetic process and the theory of singularities
of plane curves. Let us explain its simplest manifestation. Associate to each pair
(a,b)

of coprime positive integers the
binomial curve

C_{a,b}
defined by the equation

y^{a}-x^{b}=0
. If
a,b>1
, then this curve has a unique singular point, which lies
at the origin of coordinates. By blowing up this point, one transforms
C_{a,b}

either into the curve
C_{a,b-a}
if
a<b
, or into the curve
C_{a-b,b}
if
a>b

(see Proposition
2.5
). If one
keeps blowing up the singular point of each new curve, one reaches therefore the curve

C_{1,1}
through a sequence of curves determined by the pairs of positive integers appearing
during the anthyphairetic process applied to the pair
(a,b)
. After one more blowup,
one gets the so-called
minimal embedded resolution
of the starting curve
C_{a,b}
.

Therefore, the process of minimal embedded resolution of
C_{a,b}
by blowups of points
mimicks the anthyphairetic process applied to
(a,b)
. This fact explains
the appearance of the Euclidean algorithm and of continued fraction expansions of positive
rational numbers in the study of plane curve singularities. Note that a second kind of
continued fraction expansions, the
Hirzebruch-Jung expansions
,
appears in the study of surface singularities (see
[
13
]
).

Max Noether showed in
[
12
]
how Euclidean algorithms appear naturally when one tries to
analyse the structure of a curve at one of its singular points by a sequence of blowups.
His description was extended by Federigo
Enriques and Oscar Chisini in
[
5
, Libro IV, Capitolo I]
to the case of germs of
plane curves admitting various branches. They introduced
in this context the first graphical representation
of such a blowup process, using what came to be known as
Enriques diagrams

(see
[
2
, Section 3.9]
or
[
14
, Section 3]
), which are special types of
rooted trees.

Later on, a second type of graphical representation of such a process was introduced,
namely the
dual graph of the
final exceptional divisor
(see Definition
4.4
). One may
consult
[
1
, Section 8.4]
,
[
11
, Appendix]
,

[
4
, Sections 5.3–5.4]
or
[
17
, Section 3.6]

for a description of their structure for plane curve singularities and
[
15
]

for a historical discussion of the use of dual graphs in the study of surface singularities.
Both the Enriques diagram and the dual graph of a blowup process are finite trees
endowed with supplementary structures: straight/curved edges and broken vertices for
the Enriques diagram, negative vertex weights for the dual graph.
In general, they are not isomorphic even as abstract trees.

In
[
14
]
I introduced a new graphical object allowing us to understand the relation between
the two trees: a special kind of simplicial complex of dimension two, which I called a
kite
.
Later, in
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
, Evelia Rosa García Barroso,
Pedro Daniel González Pérez and I studied many more invariants of plane
curve singularities using a simpler kind of

.
All lotuses may be obtained by gluing simpler lotuses, which are associated to positive
rational numbers. As explained in
[

and
[

associated to the rational number

In this paper I present an alternative way of constructing

run on the coprime pair

(see Proposition

Conversely, given a lotus, I explain how to get the pairs of positive integers
of the corresponding anthyphairetic process (see Proposition

Both constructions are based on a new way to represent graphically any

and an associated

2.
The blowup of an irreducible binomial curve

In this section I explain the standard representation of the blowup morphism of the origin
of an affine plane by two monomial coordinate changes and how the curves defined
by the binomial equations
y^{a}-x^{b}=0
are transformed by this blowup.

Let us fix a field
{\mathbb{K}}
of characteristic zero (one may think for instance about
{\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
).
We will work inside smooth algebraic surfaces
S_{i}

obtained from the affine plane
\boxed{{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}}
with coordinates
(x,y)

by a finite sequence of blowups of points with coordinates in
{\mathbb{K}}
(see Definition
2.1
).
We will explain below that such surfaces
S_{i}
may be constructed by gluing finitely many copies
of the affine plane using particular types of maps. We will call those copies
charts
,
because they endow
S_{i}
with a structure of
atlas
, in the sense of differential geometry.

We will look at irreducible and reduced curves contained in such a surface
S_{i}
.
Such curves are subsets of
S_{i}
whose intersections with each chart
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{u_{j},v_{j}}

is the vanishing set
Z(f_{j})
of a non-zero irreducible polynomial
f_{j}\in{\mathbb{K}}[u_{j},v_{j}]
.
More generally, by a
curve
contained in such a smooth surface, which is then called the
ambient surface
of the curve, we will mean a non-zero effective divisor, that is, a finite non-empty
formal sum of irreducible and reduced curves with non-negative integer coefficients.

If
f
is a
rational function
defined on the smooth surface
S_{j}

(that is, a function which is a quotient of polynomials in each chart),
we denote by
\boxed{Z(f)}
the curve defined by it, which is in this case
a principal divisor
.
For instance, if
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then on the affine plane
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
one has the relation:

Z(x^{m}y^{n})=mZ(x)+nZ(y),

because the vanishing and the polar loci of the rational monomial

x^{m}y^{n}\in{\mathbb{K}}[x,y]
is included in the union
of the coordinate axes
Z(x)
and
Z(y)
, and that the corresponding vanishing or polar
orders of
x^{m}y^{n}
are
m
and
n
respectively. Namely, if
m\geq 0
, then one interprets
it as the vanishing order of
x^{m}y^{n}
along
Z(x)
and if
m<0
, then one interprets it as the
polar order of
x^{m}y^{n}
along
Z(x)
.

Once one blows up
at least one point of
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
, not all curves on the resulting surface are principal divisors.
For instance, the exceptional divisor of a blowup is not principal
(see point (
3
) of Remark
3.2
).

The following computational description of
blowups
is crucial in order to establish
the parallel between the anthyphairetic processes and the resolution of binomial curves
by blowups:

Definition 2.1
.

The surface
\boxed{{\mathbb{B}}_{0}{\mathbb{K}}^{2}}
obtained by blowing up the origin of
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}
and
the associated
blowup morphism

\boxed{\pi}:{\mathbb{B}}_{0}{\mathbb{K}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{K}}^{2}

are obtained by gluing two copies of the affine plane
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}
,
with coordinates
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
, in such a way
that the morphism
\pi
is described by the following changes of variables in those two charts:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x=\bar{x}\\
y=\bar{x}\bar{y}\end{array}\right.,\ \ \ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x=\tilde{x}\tilde{y}\\
y=\tilde{y}\end{array}\right..

The gluing of the two affine planes
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}
and
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}
is
therefore described by the equations
\bar{x}=\tilde{x}\tilde{y}
and
\bar{x}\bar{y}=\tilde{y}
.
Those equations allow to express
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
as a rational function
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and conversely. The corresponding rational maps

(\bar{x},\bar{y})\mapsto(\bar{y}^{-1},\bar{x}\bar{y})\mbox{ and }(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\mapsto(\tilde{x}\tilde{y},\tilde{x}^{-1})

restrict to isomorphisms of their
determinacy loci
(the subsets of the corresponding
affine planes on which the two maps take well-defined values in
{\mathbb{K}}
),
which are
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}\setminus Z(\bar{y})

and
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}\setminus Z(\tilde{x})
. They are mutually inverse
birational maps

{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}},\ {\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}},

that is, maps defined by rational fractions which establish an isomorphism between the
corresponding fields
{\mathbb{K}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
{\mathbb{K}}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
of rational fractions.
Similarly, the blowup morphism
\pi
is also birational. For more details about

In order to explain the relation between successive blowups and the anthyphairetic processes,
we need to emphasize the restrictions of
\pi
to both charts
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}

and
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}
of the surface
{\mathbb{B}}_{0}{\mathbb{K}}^{2}
:

Definition 2.2
.

The morphism
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}\to{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
described by the left-hand
system of (
2.1
) is
called the
x
-blowup morphism
and the chart
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}
is called
the
x

. One defines analogously about the

y

and the
y
-chart of the blowup
.

We will use the blowup morphism
\pi
in order to
transform
curves
C
passing
through the origin of
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
. More generally, we will transform such curves by
compositions of blow ups. Such compositions are birational morphisms

\varphi:S\to{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
which restrict to isomorphisms between

S\setminus\varphi^{-1}(0)
and
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}\setminus\{0\}
.
There are two notions of
transform a curve
C\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
by
\varphi
:

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\varphi:S\to{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
be a birational morphism from a smooth surface
S

to
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
, which restricts to an isomorphism between
S\setminus\varphi^{-1}(0)

and
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}\setminus\{0\}
. The
exceptional divisor
of
\varphi

is the preimage
\varphi^{-1}(0)
of the origin. Let

{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
.
The
total transform
\boxed{\varphi^{*}(C)}
of

by

is the divisor

Z(\varphi^{*}(f))
on

defined by the

\boxed{\varphi^{*}(f)}:=f\circ\varphi

of a defining polynomial

of the curve
C
. The

strict transform
\boxed{\varphi^{-1}_{s}(C)}
of
C
by
\varphi

is the part of the total transform which consists of curves not contained in the exceptional divisor.

Remark 2.4
.

The curve
C_{a,b}
is always
reduced
, because we assumed that the field
{\mathbb{K}}

is of characteristic zero (if it were of positive characteristic
p>0
, then for instance

y^{p}-x^{p}=(y-x)^{p}
would define a non-reduced curve). Moreover, it is
irreducible

if and only if

are coprime (if

and

,

In the sequel we will assume that

Let us express the pullback
\pi^{*}(B_{a,b})
of the binomial

by the morphism
\pi

{\mathbb{B}}_{0}{\mathbb{K}}^{2}
.

Assuming that

, we have:

\displaystyle B_{a,b}(\bar{x},\bar{x}\bar{y})=(\bar{x}\bar{y})^{a}-\bar{x}^{b}=\bar{x}^{a}(\bar{y}^{a}-\bar{x}^{b-a}),

\displaystyle B_{a,b}(\tilde{x}\tilde{y},\tilde{y})=\tilde{y}^{a}-(\tilde{x}\tilde{y})^{b}=\tilde{y}^{a}(1-\tilde{x}^{b}\tilde{y}^{b-a}).

One gets analogous formulae if
a>b
.

We see that in both cases the total transform of
C_{a,b}
decomposes as follows:

(2.3)

\pi^{*}(C_{a,b})=\mathfrak{m}(C_{a,b})E_{0}+\pi^{-1}_{s}(C_{a,b}),

where
\boxed{\mathfrak{m}(C_{a,b})}
denotes the

at the origin of the binomial curve

\min\{a,b\}

. Relation (
2.3
) holds in fact
for every curve

contained in
{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}
.

Note that
the strict transform
\pi^{-1}_{s}(C_{a,b})

is completely contained in exactly one of the two charts
of
{\mathbb{B}}_{0}{\mathbb{K}}^{2}

We get as strict transform of

\pi^{-1}_{s}(C_{a,b})=Z(\bar{y}^{a}-\bar{x}^{b-a}).

•

If

We get as strict transform of

:

\pi^{-1}_{s}(C_{a,b})=Z(\tilde{y}^{a-b}-\tilde{x}^{b}).

.

(1)

and is contained in the

(2)

Assume that the coprime positive integers

verify the inequality

.
Then the strict transform of the binomial curve

by the blowup of the origin
is isomorphic to the binomial curve
C_{a-b,b}
and is contained in the
y
-chart.

3.
The minimal embedded resolution of an irreducible binomial curve

In this section I explain the relation between the anthyphairetic process applied to a
pair
(a,b)
of coprime positive integers and the process of minimal embedded resolution
of the binomial curve
C_{a,b}
by blowups of points.

Proposition
2.5
shows that at the level of pairs of exponents which determine an irreducible binomial curve, a blowup corresponds to an elementary subtraction in the
anthyphairetic process
analyzed by Fowler in
[
7
, Chapter 2]
. A finite process of blowups leads therefore to the binomial curve
C_{1,1}
.

In order to illustrate this fact, let us consider the binomial curve

\boxed{C_{4,11}:=Z(y^{4}-x^{11})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x,y}}.

C_{1,1}
. Instead of using the notations
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})

2.1
), we will use indexed notations
(x_{i},y_{i})
.

Here
\boxed{{\mathbb{K}}^{2}_{x_{i},y_{i}}}
denotes the unique chart of the
i

\boxed{S_{i}}

which contains the whole strict transform of
C_{4,11}
. We indicate each change of coordinates, as well as the binomial defining polynomial of the strict transform in the new coordinate system:

Unlike the starting curve

in the ambient surfaces. These numbers may be computed
recursively, starting from the fact that at the moment of its creation, the self-intersection
of an exceptional divisor is

1

Remark 3.2
.

(1)
