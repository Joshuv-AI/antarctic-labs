Participatory development of patient-targeted guideline material in palliative care: A qualitative evaluation
Authors: Hannah Demleitner, Saskia Kauzner, Ronny Schneider, Manuela Schneider, Christoph Ostgathe, Carsten Klein, Maria Heckel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient and public involvement (PPI) improves patient-targeted guideline material and is recognised internationally as essential in guideline development. Whilst current research has focussed on PPI both in guideline development and palliative care, evidence regarding its implementation in developing patient-targeted guideline material is limited.AimTo explore participants’ experiences and identify supportive and challenging factors in the participatory development of patient-targeted guideline material in palliative care.DesignQualitative evaluation of the participatory development process (04/2024 - 09/2024) of a patient guide to a German best practise recommendation on intentional sedation using questionnaires with open-ended questions followed by semi-structured interviews. Data were analysed using structuring qualitative content analysis according to Kuckartz.Setting/ParticipantsFour PPI members and four healthcare professionals randomly selected from the editorial panel tasked with developing the patient guide.ResultsThe participatory approach with experienced PPI-members as experts for lay-comprehensibility fostered an equal and respectful environment, allowing all participants to contribute. The participants did not require additional methodological training. Working in subgroups proved effective and offered emotional support but carried the risk of creating inequalities.ConclusionPPI can be supported by recruiting experienced PPI advisory board members, providing flexibility through hybrid sessions and managing emotional distress through creating a save atmosphere with peer support in subgroup sessions. Communication barriers in hybrid meetings, rejection of digital co-working formats, inattention to PPI in subgroup sessions and time management issues must be considered when planning a project.

Background

Patient and public involvement (PPI), i.e. the active involvement of patients, patient representatives or members of the public in research, 1 is internationally recognised as essential for developing (patient) guidelines.1,2 It enhances patient-centredness, promotes trustworthiness and informed decision-making 1 and supports implementability. 3 In practice, however, PPI is still rarely implemented and often insufficiently reported and evaluated, particularly regarding the participatory development of patient-targeted guideline materials.2,4 Patient-targeted guideline materials, e.g. patient versions of guidelines or best practice recommendations, make guideline recommendations and their rational understandable to patients and the public. Different from patient decision aids, which support the individual patient´s shared decision-making process, they focus on communicating recommendations in plain language, thereby promoting health literacy and empowering informed decision-making. 5

Recent research on PPI in guideline development identified barriers such as difficulties with recruitment and representativeness,1,3,6 unfamiliarity with medical terminology and methodology, necessitating training and support for PPI members, unclear role expectations and limited resources.3,4 Supportive strategies include pre-training, diverse recruitment, small panels, skilled moderation and feedback. 3 Particularly in palliative care, attention should be paid to support systems to mitigate emotional distress, flexibility, early involvement, clarity of roles and recruitment of suitable participants.7–9 While these findings focus on guideline development, there is little evidence on how they apply to the participatory development of patient-targeted guideline materials, specifically in palliative care.

We considered this shortage of evidence while developing a patient guide to the German best practise recommendation on the ‘Use of sedative drugs and intentional sedation in specialist palliative care’.10,11 This recommendation, systematically developed in cooperation with the German association for Palliative Medicine (DGP), supports healthcare professionals in dealing with intentional sedation, the “result or process of sedating a patient as a means of achieving a previously defined treatment goal”;11,12 PPI was included in its development. In accordance with the revised EAPC framework on palliative sedation that also emphasises patient autonomy,13,14 we aimed to promote communication and informed decision-making by explaining recommendations targeted at healthcare professionals in lay language to patients and their proxies.15,16 We assembled an editorial panel of healthcare professionals, researchers and patient representatives, which – as is often the case in palliative care research – included relatives of patients and interested members of the public. To properly incorporate PPI, patient representatives attended all meetings and were involved in all decisions.

To further strengthen participation, we incorporated experiences from guideline development. To analyse how well this participatory collaboration worked out and which factors help or hinder PPI during development, we carried out an accompanying qualitative evaluation. This study aims to explore the experiences of the panel members and to identify supportive and challenging factors regarding the participatory development of patient-targeted guideline material.

Methods

Design

We chose an exploratory design to reveal the experiences of panel members as a basis for identifying supportive and challenging factors for developing patient-targeted guideline material.

Our participatory project is situated in social constructivism with PPI shaping the development process, patient guideline and evaluation through sharing participants experiences, viewpoints and interpretations unique to their social contexts. 17 For the evaluation, we analysed their subjective perspectives on the participatory development using a pragmatic approach. An existing evaluation questionnaire 18 was adapted to our study design and research question. 19 To explore initial responses in more depth, we then conducted qualitative interviews. Questionnaire and interview data were analysed jointly using structuring qualitative content analysis according to Kuckartz.20–22

Setting – Organisation of the editorial panel

This evaluation (July 2024 – August 2024) follows the development of a patient guide (April 2024 – September 2024) to the best practise recommendation on the ‘Use of sedative drugs and intentional sedation in specialist palliative care’.10,11 The patient guide is aimed at palliative care patients and their proxies facing acute or future decisions about intentional sedation as well as the interested public.

Following a methodological report for developing patient guidelines, 15 HD, SK, MS and MH organised an editorial panel consisting of patient representatives, healthcare professionals and researchers. In accordance with the methodological report, we focused primarily on recruiting participants who were involved in the development and/or implementation of the above-mentioned best practice recommendation and/or who had experience with intentional sedation. Information about the project and an invitation to participate were therefore sent to members of the respective project groups, as well as to palliative and hospice care healthcare specialist and PPI advisory boards, who were familiar with the best practice recommendation. In addition, the public was informed about the project on the website of the Department of Palliative Medicine at Erlangen University Hospital and invited to participate. Inclusion criteria included prior knowledge or, ideally, experiences of intentional sedation, an interest in the topic, good communication skills, and a willingness to engage in panel discussions. Palliative care patients were also part of advisory boards who received information about the study and an invitation to take part; however, they were not actively recruited due to the long duration of the project and the considerable time commitment it entailed.

Fifteen participants were recruited for the project; however, two patient representatives withdrew before the project began for personal reasons. The final editorial panel comprised four patient representatives and nine healthcare professionals/researchers (four from specialised outpatient palliative care, three from in-patient palliative care and two from hospice care). They received reimbursement for time and travel expenses. Panel members worked directly on the original text of the best practice recommendation in mixed subgroups (PPI and professionals, supported by an organiser). Additionally, participants commented remotely and asynchronously on drafts using digital co-working formats (onlyoffice via Nextcloud implementation “FAUBox”). Their tasks included rephrasing recommendations into lay language and identifying needs for clarifications and examples. In hybrid panel meetings (in-person and online via videoconference), drafts were discussed and approved unanimously, with each member having one vote. A subsequent public consultation gathered feedback on accuracy and comprehensibility from specialists and the public. Materials

A questionnaire gathered demographic data and participants’ experiences using ten scales and 14 open-ended questions (Supplemental Material 1). It is based on a tool about experiences of patient representatives recommended by the methodological report.15,18

We also developed an interview guide focused on motivation and experience, achievements, working environment, organization, burdens, participation and outcome (Supplemental Material 2). These categories emerged from a literature review and are based on evaluation frameworks,24–26 a methodological guideline 27 and a policy paper. 28 The guide was piloted with two members of the research department, focussing on duration and comprehensibility, leading to the exclusion of two sub-questions. It was furthermore slightly tailored to each participant according to their questionnaire responses to elicit more detailed explanations.

Data collection

Participants received the questionnaire during the fourth panel meeting and completed it within one week.

The interviews were conducted afterwards in person at the Palliative Care Research Department or online via videoconference by RS (M. Sc.), a research assistant in medical process management and PhD student in the Department of Palliative Medicine. To minimise bias, RS conducted the interviews as an external researcher after receiving information about development and having completed interview training; he knew only one participant beforehand. Participants were informed about RS’s qualifications and his role as a neutral external researcher, separate from the organising team, to reduce socially desirable responses and encourage open criticism. During the interview, there was no one else present besides the participant and RS. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, anonymized and uploaded to MAXQDA 24, 29 along with the questionnaires and field notes.

Data analysis

The interviews lasted between 15 and 38 minutes (average duration: 27 minutes). PPI members (n=4) varied in age (mid-40s to mid-70s) and lived experiences (family members, hospice volunteers, PPI advisory board members); they all participated in research projects before, but not as panel members. Healthcare professionals (n=4, aged early 30s to late 60) included doctors and nurses working in outpatient and inpatient specialist palliative care and researchers; their experiences with participatory projects varied (first-time panel member vs. previous panel member/organiser).

We conducted a structuring qualitative content analysis according to Kuckartz:20,21 Initially, HD and RS created case comparisons and developed deductive categories based on the questionnaire, interview guide and case comparisons. Three interviews were co-coded until intercoder agreement of 80% was achieved (measured via MAXQDA). HD then coded the remaining material, including the interviews and free-text responses from the questionnaires. Inductive and deductive subcodes were added and the coding process (including co-coding) was repeated (final code system see Supplemental Material 3). Theme saturation was assessed through iterative coding of the entire data set until no new codes emerged and both coders agreed on theme adequacy. RS´s role as an external researcher was raised repeatedly during the coding process and was taken into account in the discussion of unclear passages. After a consistency check by MH (senior palliative care researcher), HD compiled thematic summaries using cases-by-categories matrices. Results

In the following presentation of the findings, which include both questionnaire and interview data, patient representatives are anonymised as P1-4, healthcare specialists as M1-4. If no affiliation (PPI or healthcare professional) is specified, the findings refer to the entire panel. The original German answers are included as Supplemental Material 5; they were translated for this report using DeepL Translator (DeepL SE, version 27.7.0) and proofread by HD, who ensured consistency of content. Where there was any ambiguity, back translation was used. The results of the questionnaire scales have been factored into the analysis and are listed under the relevant subsections.

Participants cited interest in intentional sedation and in panel participation as their motivation for joining this project. Healthcare professionals aimed to expand informational material for patients and relatives, having observed its necessity in their professional experience. PPI members were generally interested in palliative research, had positive experiences in previous projects and valued accessible informational material. One participant viewed the compact schedule as an incentive. Discussion

Main findings

Our panel included experienced PPI advisory board members and healthcare professionals who contributed equally and constructively to the outcome. Participants were satisfied with organisation and communication but criticised limited time for discussions and technical challenges. Whilst subgroup sessions proved effective and emotionally supportive, there was concern that specialist discussion could overshadow PPI. As burdens were considered appropriate and benefits clear, participants praised the participatory approach and expressed satisfaction with the outcome.

Supportive and challenging factors

The literature on the participatory development of guidelines has identified factors that support or hinder the involvement of PPI. It is not yet clear, however, how these factors can be applied to the participatory development of patient-targeted guideline material. By comparing known supportive and hindering factors with the results of our qualitative evaluation, we aim to identify commonalities that should be taken into account when implementing participatory projects, as well as to highlight differences between the development of guidelines and the development of patient guidelines. We have categorised the supportive and hindering factors into four main themes: recruitment, set up, group environment and emotional support.

Recruitment

Recruiting participants with relevant experiences is essential. 9 However, in a palliative care setting – especially concerning rare treatments like intentional sedation - it proved difficult to recruit multiple PPI members with lived experience. Thus, including individuals with general palliative care experience and theoretical knowledge of intentional sedation was a pragmatic and effective compromise. However, this strategy raised concerns about role definition, as some participants felt uncomfortable identifying as patient representatives due to lack of personal experiences. This insight is in line with Brouwers et al.´s findings that potential participants might feel inappropriate to assume the role of patient representatives if they lack experience with the specific (sub-)topic. 6 To mitigate misunderstandings regarding role expectations 7 and minimise recruitment difficulties, using the term PPI instead of patient representative - as preferred by our panel – may be beneficial. While mainly recruiting experienced PPI members limits representativeness, we followed Johnson et al.´s recommendation to establish broader networks through hybrid sessions connecting participants from different regions. 9 In accordance with Armstrong et al., the public consultation phase additionally addressed this homogeneity by combining participatory and consultative approaches. 1 With regard to our results, it should be noted that the consultation ensures the inclusion of a broader range of experiences and views into the developed patient guide; the evaluation, however, is based solely on the experienced members of the editorial panel. Our results might therefore not adequately reflect the perspectives and needs of inexperienced and first-time PPI members.

Set-up

Hybrid sessions are important for flexible participation in palliative care research, especially for caregivers or participants living with chronic illnesses. 9 However, digital and hybrid formats are not without challenges: In-person participants felt less connected to online participants, consistent with findings of reduced social interaction in digital PPI sessions. 25 Decreased non-verbal communication and a disruption to the natural flow of conversation through the use of microphones and technical difficulties can hinder discussions, but they also hold the potential for a more focused and engaged discussion by encouraging participants to speak in turn. These benefits have become more apparent as digital PPI has gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic: Molinari-Ulate et al. build on previous publications which mainly highlighted challenges in digital communication. In their framework for digital co-production, they pointed out that the aforementioned challenges also encourage reflection on communication methods. Additionally, they highlight that smaller groups – or subgroups in our case – can improve digital communication. 25 Digital co-working tools increase flexibility, 9 but some participants appeared less eager to contribute remotely and asynchronously, citing difficulties with the programme and viewing it as too labour-intensive. Consequently, discussions required more time during meetings. While some suggested extending or adding meetings, not all participants may agree to a greater time commitment. Overall, a discursive approach is time-consuming, highlighting the need for research into more efficient and well-accepted formats.

Group environment

Effective participation relies on building relationships. 8 While our participants describe working together as equals, power imbalances during participatory guideline development are described in literature: Providing additional support to PPI members, like simplified explanations, can unintentionally highlight differences from academic team members. Balancing support and inclusion with the need for technical discussions complicates true cooperation on equal terms. 8 When developing patient-targeted guideline material, these challenges were less prevalent, as PPI members were more clearly recognised as experts in lay comprehension and patient perspective, counterbalancing the professional expertise of healthcare providers. The joint translation of technical content into plain language made separate training in specialist terminology unnecessary – reducing the need for resource-intensive training, which is often reported as insufficient –3,31 and supports a more equal working atmosphere. Nonetheless, subgroup sessions can create power imbalances, particularly when healthcare professionals outnumber PPI members and discussions lack moderation, leading to exclusionary technical dialogue. Where possible, subgroups should include multiple PPI members to maintain balance, 32 and moderators may need to steer conversation towards lay comprehensibility.

Emotional support

Emotional support – particularly important in palliative care - 7 enables sharing personal and emotionally sensitive experiences. 9 In a safe environment based on acceptance and equality, emotional distress can be alleviated through debriefings and peer support – both facilitated through subgroup sessions. 9 Smaller groups were themselves perceived as support, as they encouraged more reserved members to voice their opinions. 32 Working with prior acquaintances was also important for some participants to feel secure. This contradicts findings from a study involving guideline-naive participants who reported feeling (more) comfortable sharing their opinions with strangers. Further research might be required to refine panel composition accordingly.

When these supportive structures are in place, sharing emotionally distressing experiences can transform from a burden into an opportunity for mutual support and deepened expertise among participants.

Strengths and limitations

Our evaluation demonstrates that participatory development of patient-targeted guideline material can be realised effectively to the satisfaction of all participants. It also demonstrates two practicable evaluation methods; follow-up interviews enhanced the precision, depth and nuance of our results beyond what questionnaires alone could provide. The questionnaire was adapted from a working aid developed by the German National Disease Management Guideline Programme; while its use was pragmatic and efficient, it lacks formal scientific validation, which could limit the significance of the results. On the other hand, triangulation of methods - questionnaires, interviews, and joint analysis - increases the study´s validity.

Our small sample, however, limits the scope and generalisability of our findings, which are based on a single project and may not adequately reflect broader populations. Furthermore, the involvement of experienced PPI contributors from established research advisory boards, most of whom were already acquainted with at least some organising team members, likely facilitated cooperation and influenced the project structure and our results. Projects involving less experienced participants and seldom-heard groups may require additional resources and training. The influence of panel compositions requires further investigation.

The dual role of researchers as organisers and evaluators may have influenced participants’ willingness to express criticism. Social desirability and selection biases, as well as retrospective positivity in the evaluation of the development process following completion of the first draft, may have resulted in an overly positive portrayal. To reduce bias, an external researcher conducted interviews and contributed to co-coding. In addition, audio and video recordings of panel meetings will undergo linguistic analysis, offering a more objective view of participatory collaboration. Comparing these future findings with our study may yield further insights.

Conclusion

Successful PPI in the development of patient-targeted guideline material can be supported by recruiting experienced PPI advisory board members, combining participation with consultation strategies, offering flexible hybrid formats and ensuring a safe, equal group atmosphere with space for emotional support – particularly in subgroup sessions. Challenges like communication difficulties in hybrid formats, limited engagement with digital co-working tools, reduced attention to PPI in subgroup discussions, and time constraints should be addressed during planning. Building on previous findings regarding guideline development, we have found that known barriers – such as the need for additional training and power imbalances due to differences in expertise - may be less pronounced when developing patient-targeted guideline material. Furthermore, our study highlights peer support in subgroup as a practical solution to reduce emotional distress. By challenging the need for resource- and time-intensive training and specialised support for PPI members, we hope to alleviate some reservations and concerns regarding participatory development.

In demonstrating that participatory development is feasible even outside large organisations specialised in guideline development, this study offers a transferable model for small teams, clinics and research institutes worldwide. The qualitative evaluation using questionnaires and interview guides - which can pragmatically be adapted from preexisting materials and tailored to specific needs and circumstances - ensures the continuous improvement of participatory projects. This enhances PPI both at local level and internationally by expanding knowledge of how best to implement it. Our findings thus provide a foundation for future research on how to optimise PPI when developing patient guidelines in palliative care, contributing to more patient-targeted material and ultimately supporting shared decision-making.
