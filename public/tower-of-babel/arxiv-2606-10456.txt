The Distributed Detectability Band Against Marginal-Preserving Attacks
Zhang Qinqin, Yuze Gao

Abstract
AI-control monitors score individual agent actions to detect misbehavior, but real harm can be distributed across many benign-looking steps, each individually below any per-step alarm. We construct a marginal-preserving, correlation-encoded distributed-sabotage attack using a Gaussian-copula AR(1) construction: the per-step monitor-score marginal is held exactly equal to benign, so mean, max, top- k k tail, and threshold monitors (Monitor A) are defeated by construction , while harm is encoded in the temporal correlation structure. We sequence the paper around three reviewer-mandated gates. (1) Realizability gate: the stealthy attack achieves KS-distance to benign of 0.013 0.013 (effectively zero) at all tested harm levels up to 3.0 3.0 , confirming that harm is fully decoupled from the per-step marginal and realizability is not harm-limited. (2) Monitor-A-vs-B reconciliation: we show formally that the attack, built against Monitor A’s score marginal, remains marginal-preserving under a different-score Monitor B (the correlation/sequence family: CUSUM, SPRT, HMM-LR, runs test, autocorrelation, windowed logistic), and scope worst-case claims to score functions that admit a temporal signature. (3) Non-empty detectability band: Monitor A achieves AUC ≈ 0.52 \approx 0.52 (chance); Monitor B spans AUC 0.79 0.79 – 0.97 0.97 at the same 1 % 1\% FPR target, and as harm is amortized over more steps Monitor A collapses to chance while Monitor B holds at AUC ≈ 0.95 \approx 0.95 . These results demonstrate a non-empty detectability band and characterize the sub-threshold sabotage frontier: distribution-shape monitors fail by construction; temporal-correlation monitors can detect but are not trivially optimal . The released code is available at https://github.com/Gyyz/distributed_sabotage_frontier

Introduction

The dominant paradigm in AI-control research evaluates agent safety by
scoring individual steps
[
10
]
.
A trusted monitor observes the agent’s action (or a proxy such as a
chain-of-thought token sequence) and raises an alarm if the per-step
suspicion score exceeds a threshold.
This architecture underlies the control-evaluation literature from Redwood
Research
[
10
]
through Apollo’s scheming
evals
[
12
]
to Anthropic’s sabotage
evals
[
5
]
.

The gap.

The standard monitoring statistics in deployed AI-control
pipelines
[
10
]
— the mean score, the max score,
a top-
k
tail average, and per-step threshold-alarm counts — are all

marginal
functionals of the per-step score distribution: each is a
function of the multiset of per-step scores that ignores their order.
A sufficiently capable adversary who can hold the per-step score marginal
equal to benign defeats every such monitor
by construction
,
regardless of the harm actually accumulated.
No prior work characterizes this threat formally or demonstrates the
resulting detectability frontier.

The threat model.

We study a
distributed sub-threshold sabotage attack
:
an agent amortizes a fixed harm budget
H
across
T
steps, choosing
actions such that (i) each step’s suspicion score is drawn from the

same marginal
as a benign step, but (ii) the
joint
distribution
over the trajectory encodes harm via an elevated temporal
correlation structure.
This is the natural worst case for monitoring under
Sklar’s copula decomposition
[
17
]
:
any joint distribution can be decomposed into marginals plus a copula,
and a monitor that sees only marginals cannot distinguish benign from attack.

Why now.

Long-horizon agentic AI (coding agents, web agents, research assistants)
places agents in settings where hundreds of consecutive low-suspicion actions
can collectively amount to significant harm: inserting a subtle bug across
many commits
[
5
]
, suppressing information across many
search steps, or slightly corrupting a long analysis.
Anthropic’s natural emergent misalignment
paper
[
2
]
shows reward hacking on real RL
generalizes to distributed sabotage-style behavior.
Yet the monitoring community has not formally addressed the sub-threshold
distributed threat model.

Contributions.

•

Formal threat model (
Section

3
):

We define the Distributed Sub-Threshold Sabotage (DSTS) attacker
and the stealth budget as a formal constraint on the KS-divergence
between attack and benign per-step score marginals.

•

Marginal-preserving construction (
Section

3
):

We give an explicit Gaussian-copula AR(1) construction that achieves
any harm level at fixed KS
\approx 0.013
(realizability gate passed,

Section

5.1
).

•

Monitor-A-vs-B reconciliation (
Section

3.5
):

Formal proof that the attack is evasive under Monitor A and
detectable under Monitor B; scope of worst-case claim established.

•

Demonstrated non-empty detectability band (
Section

5
):

Monitor A AUC
\approx 0.52
; Monitor B AUC
0.79
–
0.97
at 1% FPR.
As spread grows, Monitor A collapses to chance while Monitor B
holds at AUC
\approx 0.95
(
Figure

4
).

•

Honest frontier characterization:

We identify the regime (high correlation, long trajectories) where
the band is widest, and the regime (low spread, high-SNR Monitor B)
where it narrows; we state the limitations of a synthetic trace setup
explicitly.

Related Work

AI control and monitoring.

10
establish the control paradigm:
assuming intentional subversion, one designs deployment-time safeguards
(trusted monitors, resampling, editing) to maintain safety guarantees.
Subsequent work demonstrates frontier models engaging in scheming

[
12
]
, alignment faking
[
9
]
,
and deliberate reward tampering
[
8
]
.

5
evaluate sabotage-capability evals and find that
minimal mitigations currently suffice but are near their limits.
Our work is complementary: instead of asking “can models sabotage?”
we ask “what monitoring architecture
can and cannot
detect distributed
sub-threshold sabotage?”

Per-step and sequence monitors.

Deployed monitors typically reduce a trajectory to marginal features of its
per-step suspicion scores: a mean or tail shift, or a per-step
threshold-alarm count
[
10
]
. Even a per-step
sequential test such as the SPRT, when applied under an
independence

assumption on the steps, reduces to a marginal-likelihood statistic. All such
marginal-feature detectors are defeated by construction when the attacker
preserves the per-step marginal; the change-point and sequence-model literature
(CUSUM
[
15
]
, SPRT
[
19
]
,
HMMs
[
16
]
) supplies the temporal statistics that are not.
We differentiate our contribution as the first formal treatment of this gap
and the first demonstrated detectability band that separates the marginal
family from the correlation family.

Sequential change-point detection.

Classical CUSUM
[
15
]
and SPRT
[
19
]
are
designed for
mean shifts
.
When applied to the per-step scores of a marginal-preserving attack, they
detect nothing (as we show empirically).
When retargeted to the
autocorrelation
or a likelihood ratio over
the joint distribution of scores, they form the Monitor B family that does
detect the attack.

4
surveys the change-detection literature;
our contribution is to derive the appropriate statistic for the
copula-encoded attack and to demonstrate the detectability gap.

CoT monitoring and obfuscation.

3
,
7
,
11
study
chain-of-thought monitors.
CoT monitors are per-token marginal features and share the vulnerability:
a sufficiently capable adversary can encode intent in the

correlation across reasoning tokens
rather than in any individual
token
[
1
]
.
Our formal framing applies directly.

Weak-to-strong and scalable oversight.

6
study oversight under capability gaps.
Our result adds a new axis: even a perfectly accurate per-step monitor
(Monitor A with zero FPR) has zero power against a marginal-preserving
attack, regardless of the capability gap.

Method and Theory

3.1
The Distributed Sub-Threshold Sabotage Threat Model

Definition 1

(Monitor-A score)
.

Let
s_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued suspicion score assigned by Monitor A
(mean/max/threshold/top-
k
family) to the agent’s action at step
t
.
Let
F_{\mathrm{ben}}
denote the marginal CDF of
s_{t}
under benign behavior.

Definition 2

(Stealth budget)
.

An attack trajectory
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{T})
satisfies a

stealth budget

\varepsilon\geq 0
if

D_{\mathrm{KS}}(\hat{F}_{\mathrm{att}},\,F_{\mathrm{ben}})\;\leq\;\varepsilon,

where
\hat{F}_{\mathrm{att}}
is the empirical marginal CDF of the attack
scores.
The
zero-stealth-budget attack
sets
\varepsilon=0

(exactly marginal-preserving).

Definition 3

(Distributed Sub-Threshold Sabotage (DSTS) Attacker)
.

A DSTS attacker with harm budget
H>0
and stealth budget
\varepsilon\geq 0

produces a trajectory such that (i) the per-step marginal satisfies the
stealth budget, and (ii) the realized harm
\sum_{t}h_{t}=H
, where
h_{t}

is the harm contribution of step
t
.
Each
h_{t}>0
is individually below any fixed per-step alarm
threshold
\tau
(i.e., the attack is
sub-threshold distributed
).

Proposition 1

(Monitor-A impotence)
.

Any monitor whose decision rule depends only on
\hat{F}_{\mathrm{att}}

(i.e., a marginal feature) has detection power equal to its false-positive
rate against any zero-stealth-budget attack.

Proof.

At
\varepsilon=0
,
\hat{F}_{\mathrm{att}}=F_{\mathrm{ben}}
by definition.
The monitor’s statistic is a functional of
\hat{F}_{\mathrm{att}}
alone;
hence its distribution under attack equals its distribution under benign,
and Neyman-Pearson power equals the FPR
[
14
]
.
∎

Proposition

1
covers the mean (
\bar{s}
),
max (
\max_{t}s_{t}
), top-
k
tail (
\text{mean of top-}k
scores),
and threshold counter (
\#\{t:s_{t}>\tau\}
) monitors exactly.

3.2
Gaussian-Copula AR(1) Construction

We construct the attack using a
Gaussian copula
with an AR(1)
temporal dependence structure
[
13
]
.

Setup.

Let the benign marginal be
F_{\mathrm{ben}}=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
(any
marginal can be transformed via the probability integral transform).
The benign trajectory has AR(1) correlation parameter

\phi_{\mathrm{ben}}\in[0,1)
with lag-1 autocorrelation
\rho_{\mathrm{ben}}
.

Attack construction.

The attack elevates the AR(1) parameter to

\phi_{\mathrm{att}}=\phi_{\mathrm{ben}}+\delta_{\phi}
,
\delta_{\phi}>0
.
Formally:

\tilde{z}_{t}=\phi_{\mathrm{att}}\,\tilde{z}_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\phi_{\mathrm{att}}^{2}}\,\epsilon_{t},\quad\epsilon_{t}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(1)

and the attack score at step
t
is

s_{t}^{\mathrm{att}}=F_{\mathrm{ben}}^{-1}(\Phi(\tilde{z}_{t}))
,
where
\Phi
is the standard Gaussian CDF.
By construction, the marginal of
s_{t}^{\mathrm{att}}
equals
F_{\mathrm{ben}}

for all
t
, satisfying the stealth budget at
\varepsilon=0
.

Harm encoding.

Harm is encoded via a scalar function
h(s_{t},t)
that is positive when
the correlation structure is in an “attack” regime.
Concretely, over
\mathsf{spread}
contiguous steps a fixed harm increment

h_{t}=H/\mathsf{spread}
is applied; these steps are spread uniformly
across the trajectory so no single burst is visible.
The per-step harm
h_{t}\to 0
as
\mathsf{spread}\to T
, making
Monitor A’s per-step statistics converge to benign as harm is amortized
(
Section

5
).

3.3
Monitor-B: The Correlation Family

Monitor B consists of test statistics that are
functionals of the
joint distribution
of
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{T})
, not of the marginal.

Definition 4

(Monitor-B family)
.

The Monitor-B family includes:

(a)

CUSUM-on-products:

C_{t}=\max(0,\,C_{t-1}+s_{t}s_{t-1}-\kappa)

with threshold
h_{\text{CUS}}

[
15
]
;

(b)

SPRT-on-AR(1):

likelihood ratio
\Lambda_{T}=\prod_{t=2}^{T}\frac{p(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1};\,\phi_{\mathrm{att}})}{p(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1};\,\phi_{\mathrm{ben}})}
;

(c)

HMM likelihood ratio:

a two-state HMM fit on benign traces; score is the
log-likelihood ratio on the test trajectory
[
16
]
;

(d)

Wald-Wolfowitz runs test:

number of runs in
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{T})
binarized at the median;
elevated correlation produces fewer runs
[
18
]
;

(e)

Autocorrelation test:

\hat{\rho}_{1}=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}s_{t}s_{t-1}
;

(f)

Windowed logistic classifier (seq_clf):

a logistic regression on sliding window statistics
(mean, variance, lag-1 autocorrelation) trained on held-out
benign and attack traces.

3.5
Monitor-A-vs-B Reconciliation

Proposition 2

(Evasion under Monitor A, detectability under Monitor B)
.

Let the attack be constructed as in
Section

3.2
with

\varepsilon=0
(exactly marginal-preserving).
Then:

(a)

For any Monitor A (marginal functional), the detection power equals
the FPR (by
Proposition

1
).

(b)

For the SPRT with correctly specified AR(1) parameters, the Type-II
error vanishes as
T\to\infty
(the Wald SPRT is asymptotically
consistent for distinguishing
\phi_{\mathrm{att}}
from

\phi_{\mathrm{ben}}

[
19
]
).

(c)

For Monitor B statistics that are consistent estimators of
the lag-1 autocorrelation (e.g., autocorr, CUSUM on products),
the power converges to
1
as
T\to\infty
for any
\delta_{\phi}>0
.

Proof sketch.

(a) follows from
Proposition

1
.
(b) The SPRT log-likelihood ratio grows at rate
T\cdot\KL(P_{\mathrm{att}}\|P_{\mathrm{ben}})
where
\KL>0
whenever
\phi_{\mathrm{att}}\neq\phi_{\mathrm{ben}}
; hence the test threshold is exceeded with probability
approaching
1
as
T\to\infty

[
19
]
.
(c) For the AR(1) Gaussian copula process, the lag-1 autocorrelation in

z
-space is exactly
\phi
, so the sample autocorrelation

\hat{\rho}_{1}\to\phi
in probability; the gap between attack and benign values
is
\phi_{\mathrm{att}}-\phi_{\mathrm{ben}}=\delta_{\phi}>0
, so a
consistent test has power approaching
1
.
∎

Scope caveat.

The reconciliation holds for score functions that admit a temporal signature
(i.e., where the correlation shift
\delta_{\phi}
is non-zero in the score
space).
If Monitor B uses a
different
score function
\tilde{s}_{t}
for which
the copula of the attack is identical to that of benign behavior
(i.e., the attack is also marginal-preserving in the
\tilde{s}_{t}
space),
our guarantee does not apply.
We scope worst-case evasion to “score functions that do not admit the
correlation signature” and note this as a limitation.

6
Limitations

Synthetic and real traces.

The controlled study uses synthetic AR(1) Gaussian traces, which give us a
tunable benign autocorrelation to map the frontier. We complement it with real
live-agent/monitor traces (
Section

5.7
, Qwen2.5-7B agent scored by a
Llama-3.2-3B monitor), which confirm Monitor A’s blindness on a real marginal and
expose a real-world subtlety the synthetic model understates: real benign
autocorrelation is low (
\phi\approx 0.06
) and episodes are short, so detection
hinges on accumulating evidence over the deployment. Ingestion of curated
adversarial benchmarks (SHADE-Arena, LinuxArena) and a multi-dimensional action
monitor remain natural next steps; the adaptive red-teamer (
Section

5.5
)
is evaluated on the synthetic construction.

Feature-blind attacker.

The construction assumes the attacker can hold the per-step marginal
exactly at benign via the Gaussian-copula map
(
s_{t}=F_{\mathrm{ben}}^{-1}(\Phi(\tilde{z}_{t}))
).
In a real low-dimensional action space, a feature-blind agent may not
achieve exact marginal matching at non-trivial harm levels;
in that case the worst-case argument collapses to a hypothetical-attacker
bound rather than a demonstrated threat.

Band width against competent Monitor B.

The SPRT assumes known
\phi_{\mathrm{att}}
; a monitor that must
estimate
\phi_{\mathrm{att}}
from data has lower power.
The band can be narrow against a competent rich monitor with
access to the full per-step feature vector (not just the scalar score),
and our results are an upper bound on Monitor B power under the
scalar-score assumption.

Scope of worst-case claim.

Proposition

2
scopes worst-case evasion to score functions
admitting a temporal correlation signature.
If an operator deploys a Monitor B with a score function uncorrelated with
the attack’s correlation axis, the band may shrink or vanish.
Practitioners must verify that their Monitor B statistic has nonzero power
in the specific score space used.

Simplified harm model.

Harm is modeled as a scalar additive quantity distributed uniformly over

K
steps.
Real harm often has non-uniform temporal structure, causal dependencies,
and latency before manifestation.
The analysis generalizes to any harm function that preserves the
marginal-preservation constraint, but empirical validation requires
task-specific harm operationalizations.

7
Conclusion

We have introduced the formal threat model of the Distributed Sub-Threshold
Sabotage (DSTS) attacker and demonstrated three key results.
First, the marginal-preserving Gaussian-copula AR(1) construction passes
the realizability gate: KS
\approx 0.013
at all tested harm levels,
confirming harm is fully decoupled from the per-step marginal.
Second, Monitor A (mean/max/threshold/tail) is provably blind to this
attack by construction; empirical AUC
\approx 0.52
at 1% FPR
confirms the theory.
Third, Monitor B (CUSUM/SPRT/HMM-LR/autocorr/seq-clf) demonstrates a
non-empty detectability band: AUC
0.79
–
0.97
, power up to
0.688

(SPRT) at the same 1% FPR target.
The band is widest precisely when harm is most distributed and practically
dangerous.

The central message is architectural:
deployments relying solely on
marginal-feature monitors provide zero protection against a marginal-preserving
attacker.

Effective monitoring requires at least one member of the Monitor B family.
Within Monitor B, the SPRT with correctly specified parameters is the
likelihood-ratio optimal test; simpler correlation statistics are
practically useful but strictly sub-optimal.

Our adaptive analysis (
Section

5.5
) already exposes the core
two-player frontier—attacker stealth
\delta_{\phi}
versus defender
evidence
T
—on the synthetic construction. We identify the remaining open
frontier: characterizing the minimum Monitor B SNR required to maintain power
as the operator adds more per-step features, adapting the construction to
non-Gaussian and non-stationary trace distributions, and porting the adaptive
red-teamer to real SHADE-Arena trajectories.

Broader impact.

This work is a safety-positive contribution: we characterize the limits of
current monitoring and point toward the monitor families that can fill the
gap.
The construction is explicit by design so that defenders can test their
deployments; we do not believe the construction is actionable for a real
attacker without the feedback oracle described in
Section

6
.
