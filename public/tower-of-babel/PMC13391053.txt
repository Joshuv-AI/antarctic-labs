Design‐Assisted Chemometric UV Spectrophotometric Determination of Candesartan Cilexetil, Chlorthalidone and Amlodipine Using Principal Component Regression, Partial Least Squares and Genetic Algorithm‐Partial Least Squares Models
Authors: Khanda F M Amin, Samar H Elagamy, Reem H Obaydo, Hayam M Lotfy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study presents a sustainable chemometric‐assisted UV spectrophotometric strategy for the simultaneous determination of candesartan cilexetil (CAN), chlorthalidone (CTL) and amlodipine (AML) in laboratory‐prepared mixtures and commercial pharmaceutical formulations. Owing to the extensive spectral overlap of the three drugs in the UV region, conventional spectrophotometric methods are inadequate for their direct simultaneous analysis. To address this challenge, multivariate calibration models based on principal component regression (PCR), partial least squares (PLS) and genetic algorithm‐optimized partial least squares (GA‐PLS) were developed, enabling accurate quantification without prior separation or complex sample preparation. A design of experiments (DoE) approach was employed to construct the calibration set, while an independent validation set was generated using orthogonal array‐based Latin hypercube sampling (OALHS) to ensure robust external validation across the concentration domain. The GA‐PLS model enhanced predictive performance through effective wavelength selection, reducing spectral redundancy and improving model robustness. Furthermore, variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis was employed to identify the most influential spectral variables and provide insight into the spectral regions contributing to analyte quantification. The developed models exhibited excellent analytical performance, characterized by low calibration errors (root mean square error of calibration [RMSEC] < 0.30), strong predictive ability and satisfactory external validation results (RMSEP = 0.2278–0.4419; RRMSEP = 0.1558–0.3978), with recoveries ranging from 99.0% to 100.0%. The sustainability of the proposed methodology was assessed using the multi‐colour assessment (MA) tool according to white analytical chemistry principles, yielding a high whiteness score and demonstrating superior environmental performance compared with conventional chromatographic methods. In addition, the graphical layout tool for analytical chemistry evaluation (GLANCE) visualization framework provided a comprehensive graphical assessment of analytical performance and sustainability metrics. The proposed chemometric strategy offers a rapid, reliable, cost‐effective and environmentally friendly alternative for routine quality control of multicomponent pharmaceutical formulations.Keywords: amlodipine, candesartan cilexetil, chemometric analysis, chlorthalidone, GLANCE, green analytical chemistry, multi‐component pharmaceutical analysis

Introduction

Hypertension remains a major global health challenge, ranking among the leading contributors to disease burden and premature mortality due to its strong association with renal and cardiovascular complications [1, 2]. Over the past two decades, combination therapies have emerged as an effective strategy to reduce cardiovascular risk while enhancing blood pressure control [3]. Among these, the combination of candesartan cilexetil (CAN), chlorthalidone (CTL) and amlodipine (AML) targets multiple complementary physiological pathways.

CAN (Figure 1) is a prodrug hydrolyzed to its active metabolite, a selective antagonist of the angiotensin II Type 1 (AT1) receptor that induces vasodilation and reduces aldosterone production [4, 5]. It is widely prescribed as monotherapy or combination therapy for hypertension and heart failure [6, 7].

Structure of candesartan cilexetil (CAN), chlorthalidone (CTL) and amlodipine (AML).

CTL (Figure 1) is a long‐acting thiazide‐like diuretic that lowers blood pressure by preventing sodium reabsorption in the renal tubules, thereby promoting the urinary elimination of sodium, chloride and water [8, 9, 10, 11].

AML (Figure 1) is a long‐acting dihydropyridine calcium channel inhibitor that induces arterial vasodilation and reduces systemic vascular resistance by blocking calcium influx into vascular smooth muscle cells [12, 13]. Its delayed onset minimizes the risk of reflex tachycardia.

While fixed‐dose binary combinations of CAN and CTL (e.g., Atacand; 16.0 mg CAN/12.5 mg CTL) are commercially available, this combination is typically co‐administered with AML (Norvasc; 5.0 mg) in clinical practice. This triple regimen works synergistically to improve therapeutic efficacy, enhance patient compliance and reduce cardiovascular complications compared to monotherapy [14]. To mitigate ‘pill burden’ and maximize adherence, a fixed‐dose single‐pill triple combination (CAN 16 mg/CTL 12.5 mg/AML 5.0 mg) is currently under clinical evaluation, notably in the Phase III OPTION TREAT trial (NCT05920005) [15]. Extensive pre‐formulation, stability testing and rigorous analytical quality control are required for the registration and market release of this emerging formulation [16].

Various analytical methods have been reported for quantifying these drugs individually or in binary combinations, predominantly utilizing chromatography [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], capillary electrophoresis [26], spectrophotometry [27, 28, 29] and chemometric approaches [30, 31, 32]. However, most previously reported methods for these analytes rely on chromatographic techniques that require hazardous organic solvents, extensive sample preparation, prolonged analysis times and costly instrumentation. These limitations conflict with the growing demand for sustainable, economical and environmentally responsible analytical approaches in the pharmaceutical field [33].

UV spectrophotometry is widely favoured as an alternative due to its simplicity, cost‐effectiveness and accessibility, particularly in resource‐limited regions [34]. Nevertheless, the simultaneous spectrophotometric determination of this specific ternary system remains analytically challenging because of the severe overlap of its UV absorption spectra. Resolving such highly overlapping spectral signals requires the implementation of advanced multivariate calibration strategies [35].

A typical drawback in standard chemometric investigations arises from the reliance on random data partitioning into calibration and validation sets, which may introduce selection bias and artificially overestimate model performance [14]. The structural validity of any chemometric calibration model relies heavily on the independence and spatial distribution of its external validation framework. A pervasive vulnerability in standard chemometric protocols is the reliance on random split‐sample configurations for validation. Random allocation frequently generates sample clustering, creating local redundancies or structural voids where analyte coordinates exhibit severe multi‐collinearity [36]. Consequently, the resulting validation set may either reside completely nested within tight calibration boundaries leading to over‐optimistic error metrics or demand extreme extrapolation, which unfairly penalizes model performance. To overcome these sampling constraints, this investigation incorporates orthogonal array (OA)‐based Latin hypercube sampling (OALHS) into the analytical design. By blending the non‐overlapping properties of a Latin hypercube with the low‐dimensional projection balancing of an OA, OALHS forces the correlation coefficients (r) between the concentration vectors of CAN, CTL and AML towards zero. This mathematical framework systematically eliminates selection bias, minimizes multi‐collinearity and ensures a uniform, space‐filling exploration of the experimental cube. The resulting model undergoes evaluation against an authentic representation of the ternary system, fulfilling the rigorous validation criteria demanded by contemporary white analytical chemistry (WAC) practices [37].

Furthermore, to optimize the partial least‐squares (PLS) models, a genetic algorithm (GA)‐assisted variable selection strategy is introduced to identify the most informative spectral wavelengths and reduce data redundancy. To enhance model interpretability, variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis is integrated into the workflow. VIP scores quantify the contribution of each spectral variable to the model's prediction, providing mechanistic insight into the specific spectral regions responsible for analyte discrimination [38].

In line with the ongoing evolution of sustainability assessment in analytical sciences, method evaluation has expanded beyond traditional validation metrics to encompass eco‐efficiency, practicality and innovation. An ideal analytical method should strictly align with the principles of green analytical chemistry (GAC) and WAC [39, 40].

To objectively assess these parameters, this work utilizes two complementary diagnostic frameworks—The multi‐colour assessment (MA) tool: A web‐based platform that integrates greenness, analytical capability, applicability and innovation into a unified evaluation system, generating both individual and overall whiteness scores to allow direct sustainability comparisons with reported high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) procedures [17, 21, 41]. The graphical layout tool for analytical chemistry evaluation (GLANCE) framework: A visual layout that systematically summarizes method performance across 12 structured attributes, including novelty, analytes, reagents, instrumentation, validation, matrix effects, recoveries and real‐sample applications [42]. Together, these tools establish a transparent approach for verifying both the analytical performance and the environmental profile of the proposed method.

The novelty of the present work lies not in the introduction of a new chemometric algorithm, but in the integration of multiple contemporary analytical concepts into a unified workflow for the analysis of a highly overlapping pharmaceutical ternary system. First, OALHS was employed to generate an independent and statistically representative validation set, thereby reducing the risk of selection bias associated with conventional random data partitioning. Second, a GA‐assisted variable selection strategy was combined with PLS modelling to identify the most informative spectral variables, reduce spectral redundancy and improve model robustness. Third, VIP analysis was utilized to provide mechanistic insight into the spectral regions contributing most significantly to analyte prediction and to support the interpretation of the selected wavelengths. Fourth, the analytical methodology was assessed using both the MA tool and the GLANCE framework, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of analytical performance, sustainability, practicality and innovation according to WAC principles.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has reported the simultaneous determination of CAN, CTL and AML using an integrated chemometric workflow incorporating OALHS validation design, GA‐driven wavelength selection, VIP‐based variable interpretation and comprehensive WAC assessment. Consequently, this study aims to develop a rapid, economical, environmentally friendly and reliable multivariate spectrophotometric method for the concurrent quantification of CAN, CTL and AML in laboratory‐prepared mixtures and combined pharmaceutical formulations without prior separation steps. The CAN/CTL/AML system serves as an ideal; it represents a clinically relevant emerging antihypertensive combination characterized by highly overlapping spectral profiles, making it a challenging model to demonstrate the capacity of sustainable chemometrics to deliver high‐performing, eco‐efficient pharmaceutical analysis.

Experimental

Software and Instrument

A data programme called Shimadzu UV Prop was used with a Shimadzu UV‐1800 PC‐type spectrophotometer from Kyoto, Japan. Using MATLAB R2021a (version 9.10.0.1669831) and the PLS Toolbox, chemometric analysis was performed on the collected data.

Materials and Reagents

Pure standards of CAN (CAS no. 145040‐37‐5), CTL (CAS no. 77‐36‐1) and AML (CAS no. 88150‐42‐9) were purchased from MedChemExpress (18 Wilkinson Way, Princeton, New Jersey, 08540, USA). Their purities were verified according to the British Pharmacopoeia [43] and found to be 98.9% ± 0.68% for CAN, 99.76% ± 0.44% for CTL and 99.87% ± 0.49% for AML. Analytical grade methanol was procured from Sigma–Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany. Commercial pharmaceutical formulations used in this study included Atacand 16.0 mg (CAN), CTL IP 12.5 mg and Norvasc 5.0 mg (AML besylate).

Standard Solutions

In a volumetric flask (25 mL), 25.0 mg of each prescription medication was dissolved separately in methanol to create stock solutions of 1000.0 µg/mL of CAN, CTL and AML. The stock solutions were diluted with the appropriate amount of solvent to create the working solutions; all prescribed drugs had a final concentration of 100.0 µg/mL. Standard solutions in the combination were prepared using three sets of 5.0 mL volumetric flasks. Each set concentrated on a particular range of drug concentrations: 2.0–40.0 µg/mL for CAN, 5.0–45.0 µg/mL for CTL and 4.0–40.0 µg/mL for AML. Methanol was used to adjust the volume.

Procedure

Spectral Features and Linearity

To characterize the spectral features, the individual UV absorption spectra of CAN, CTL and AML were recorded over 200–400 nm. Linearity was assessed using multicomponent mixtures across the full spectral range at 1 nm intervals when a standard solution representing common pharmaceutical ratios for CAN (10.0 µg/mL), CTL (20.0 µg/mL) and AML (15.0 µg/mL) were recorded, as illustrated in Figure 2. The following concentration ranges were used to create calibration curves: 2.0–40.0 µg/mL for CAN, 5.0–45.0 µg/mL for CTL and 4.0–40.0 µg/mL for AML. These ranges were selected to cover the instrument's linear response while representing expected concentrations in pharmaceutical formulations.

Zero‐order absorption spectra of CAN (10.0 µg/mL), CTL (20.0 µg/mL) and AML (15.0 µg/mL).

Algorithmic Formulation and Implementation of OALHS for Validation Set Designing

To secure an objective, unbiased evaluation of the predictive integrity of the developed multivariate calibration models, the external validation cohort was structured utilizing an OALHS routine. While standard Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) ensures uniform one‐dimensional projections, it is susceptible to structural voids within multidimensional subspaces [36]. The integration of an OA network overcomes this limitation by enforcing space‐filling equilibrium across all low‐dimensional projections simultaneously. The generation of the validation matrix followed a strict mathematical pipeline executed in a MATLAB environment [37].

Sample Preparation and Spectral Acquisition

Accurately measured aliquots from each primary stock solution were systematically combined and transferred into 5.0 mL volumetric flasks, with final dilutions carried out using HPLC‐grade methanol. Electronic absorption spectra were captured across the 200–400 nm region using standard 1 cm path‐length quartz cuvettes against a methanol solvent blank. Preliminary screening revealed substantial photometric noise below 205 nm and negligible analyte absorption above 340 nm. Consequently, the analytical wavelength window was trimmed to 205–340 nm, generating a digitized spectral matrix of 136 variables sampled at 1‐nm increments under optimized instrument configurations. Prior to executing the multivariate calibration routines, the raw spectra underwent mean‐centring to mitigate baseline drift and stabilize numerical computations. No additional digital smoothing or derivative algorithms were introduced, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the baseline spectral data.

Chemometric Model Construction

Simultaneous quantification of the ternary mixture was optimized by evaluating and comparing three multivariate calibration algorithms: principal component regression (PCR), PLS and GA‐PLS. The calibration set (n = 25) was designed using a multilevel orthogonal framework consisting of five concentration levels per analyte to ensure systematic coverage of the experimental space.

For unbiased model validation, an independent prediction set (n = 16) was generated utilizing an OALHS routine. The space‐filling properties of the OALHS algorithm bypass the discrete concentration constraints of the calibration design, thereby providing a rigorous evaluation of model generalization. To maintain strict experimental independence, validation mixtures were prepared via distinct, individual dilution workflows rather than split‐sample partitioning, validating the model parameters solely through external prediction. During the tuning phase, a leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOOCV) protocol was employed exclusively to establish the optimal number of principal components (PCs) or latent variables (LVs), mitigating the risk of overfitting in the standard PCR and PLS architectures.

To enhance feature selection, a GA was coupled with the PLS framework to identify highly informative spectral variables and exclude redundant data regions. The GA optimization cycle utilized the minimization of the root mean square error of cross‐validation (RMSECV) as the objective fitness function. To confirm the stability and reproducibility of the selected wavelength sub‐matrices, the algorithm was executed over several independent runs. Crucial GA hyperparameters including population size, mutation rate, crossover probability and maximum generations were systematically adjusted to balance predictive capacity with model parsimony (detailed in Table S1). This comparative framework enabled a direct performance assessment between full‐spectrum approaches and wavelength‐optimized chemometric models under identical baseline conditions.

Model Performance Evaluation Metrics

The predictive performance, precision, sensitivity, accuracy and robustness of the optimized chemometric models were evaluated using a comprehensive set of statistical metrics [45].

The performance of the optimized chemometric models was validated in compliance with standard analytical guidelines by evaluating accuracy, precision, robustness and sensitivity limits. Method accuracy was established by preparing and analysing three distinct concentration levels spanning the low, middle and upper regions of the linear working range for each component. Each concentration point was evaluated in triplicate. The specific target levels were selected to reflect typical pharmaceutical ratios for the ternary system (CAN: 2.0, 10 and 30 µg/mL; CTL: 5.0, 15.0 and 35.0 µg/mL and AML: 4.0, 10 and 30 µg/mL). Analytical accuracy was expressed through the calculated recovery percentages (%R) for each concentration point.

Method precision was partitioned into intra‐day repeatability and inter‐day intermediate precision. Repeatability was monitored by evaluating the three concentration levels in triplicate within a single operational day. Intermediate precision was documented by executing the identical triplicate protocol across three separate, consecutive days. Precision profiles for both parameters were calculated and expressed as percentage relative standard deviation (%RSD).

The resilience of the multivariate calibration methods against subtle operational deviations was tested by introducing minor, controlled adjustments to key instrumental variables during spectral acquisition. The parameters evaluated included spectral bandwidth by adjusting the optical slit width from 1.0 to 0.8 nm, digitization interval through modifying the wavelength collection increments from 1.0 to 0.9 nm and scan speed by altering the monochromator velocity between fast and medium operational modes. The effect of these minor perturbations on the predictive metrics was monitored to confirm method robustness. Assay of CAN, CTL and AML Besylate in Tablet Formulations

To ensure sample homogeneity, five tablets of each commercial formulation Norvasc (5 mg AML besylate), Atacand (16 mg CAN) and CTL IP (12.5 mg) were individually weighed and reduced to a fine powder. In the absence of a commercially available fixed‐dose combination containing all three active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), synthetic multicomponent matrices were prepared in the laboratory. Accurately weighed portions of the pulverized tablets, each equivalent to 10.0 mg of CAN, CTL and AML, were transferred into a single 100‐mL volumetric flask. The mixture was subjected to sonication for 15 min to facilitate API dissolution, followed by filtration to eliminate insoluble excipients. The resulting filtrates were diluted to a volume with methanol, yielding stock solutions with a uniform concentration of 100.0 µg/mL for each analyte.

Aliquots of these stock solutions were subsequently diluted to prepare working test solutions with final target concentrations of 16.0 µg/mL CAN, 12.5 µg/mL CTL and 5.0 µg/mL AML, all falling within the established linear range of the analytical method. For quantitative assessment, chemometric algorithms were applied to the UV absorption spectra recorded across the 200–400 nm wavelength range. Method accuracy was verified via standard addition recovery studies conducted at three distinct concentration levels in triplicate. Method performance was ultimately quantified through the calculation of percentage recovery (%R) and standard deviation (SD) for each respective analyte.

Results and Discussion

The simultaneous determination of CAN, CTL and AML presents a significant analytical challenge due to the extensive overlap of their UV absorption spectra, which prevents their direct quantification using conventional univariate spectrophotometric methods. The development of reliable analytical methodologies for this ternary system is of increasing pharmaceutical relevance, particularly in light of ongoing efforts towards fixed‐dose combination therapies designed to improve patient compliance and therapeutic outcomes. Chemometric techniques have become valuable tools for addressing complex analytical problems involving highly collinear and overlapping spectral datasets. By extracting latent information from multivariate data, these approaches enable the simultaneous quantification of multiple analytes without prior physical separation. In addition to enhancing analytical performance, chemometric‐assisted spectrophotometric methods support the principles of GAC and WAC by reducing solvent consumption, minimizing waste generation and simplifying analytical workflows [46, 47].

In the present study, a systematic experimental design strategy was adopted to construct representative calibration and validation datasets for the CAN/CTL/AML system. Laboratory‐prepared mixtures covering the concentration domain of the three analytes were generated using DoE principles, while OALHS was employed to create an independent validation set with uniform coverage of the multidimensional concentration space. This approach enhanced the statistical reliability of model evaluation while minimizing the number of required experiments. As illustrated in Figure 2, the UV spectra of CAN, CTL and AML exhibit severe overlap throughout the investigated spectral region, rendering direct spectrophotometric analysis impractical. To overcome this limitation, PCR, PLS and GA‐PLS models were developed and evaluated. Furthermore, VIP analysis was employed to identify the spectral variables contributing most significantly to model performance and to support the interpretation of the selected wavelengths. The developed chemometric models successfully extracted concentration‐related information from the overlapping spectral profiles and enabled accurate simultaneous quantification of the three analytes without chromatographic separation. Their analytical performance was assessed through calibration, cross‐validation, external validation, pharmaceutical application and sustainability evaluation. The obtained results demonstrated high predictive accuracy, satisfactory precision and robustness, effective wavelength optimization and strong alignment with GAC and WAC principles, confirming the suitability of the proposed methodology for sustainable pharmaceutical quality‐control applications.

Evaluation and Chemometric Properties of the Calibration Matrix

The application of a multifactorial, multilevel design allowed for an efficient and orthogonal exploration of the ternary concentration space [48]. By allocating the analyte concentrations across five symmetrically coded levels (−2, −1, 0, +1, +2), the intrinsic mathematical collinearity within the calibration matrix was heavily suppressed. This structural independence is critical for multivariate calibration; it ensures that the subsequent regression algorithms (PCR, PLS and GA‐PLS) map authentic spectral responses rather than chance correlations or inter‐analyte signal interactions. Furthermore, utilizing a 25‐sample matrix effectively balanced model parsimony with predictive capacity. It provided sufficient degrees of freedom to capture potential non‐linearities in the UV absorption profiles without requiring an excessively large sample size that could cause overfitting. From a green analytical perspective, this optimized sample throughput minimized the consumption of organic solvents and the generation of chemical waste during the calibration phase, demonstrating strong compliance with the core tenets of GAC.

Spatial Evaluation and Diagnostic Integrity of the Validation Matrix

In contrast to the rigid, discrete levels of the Brereton‐based calibration matrix, the OALHS validation framework successfully populated the multidimensional concentration space via a design‐independent, space‐filling geometry. This approach provides a more rigorous environment for verifying model generalizability than internal cross‐validation or simple random sampling. Because random sampling often yields accidental sample clustering or regional voids, the combination of OA balance with stratified LHS ensures that every sub‐region of the experimental universe is uniformly evaluated. The spatial distribution of the 16 validation mixtures is illustrated via the two‐dimensional projection plots in Figure 3. Visual and statistical inspection of these coordinates confirms an optimal, unclustered distribution across the multidimensional domain. By ensuring that exactly one sample entry resides within each of the 16 stratified concentration brackets per axis, inter‐analyte correlation was effectively eliminated. This structural independence is vital for testing chemometric predictors; because the validation mixtures were prepared via completely isolated physical dilutions rather than split‐sample partitioning, they present a true external challenge to the trained PCR, PLS and GA‐PLS engines. This spatial efficiency directly supports sustainable laboratory workflows. Maximizing the diagnostic power per sample injection enabled a comprehensive validation of model predictive limits using only 16 experimental runs. Consequently, this design protocol dramatically minimizes reagent consumption and solvent waste, seamlessly uniting statistical integrity with the principles of GAC [49].

Optimization, Feature Extraction and Comparative Modelling

To extract concentration‐dependent profiles from the highly collinear spectral data, three multivariate calibration engines, PCR, PLS and GA‐PLS, were systematically optimized and compared. Model parameterization relied heavily on internal LOOCV to balance predictive power with model parsimony, establishing an objective criteria for selecting the optimal number of LVs or PCs.

LV Optimization via PCR and PLS

For the unsupervised PCR framework, dimensionality reduction was executed by decomposing the spectral data into orthogonal PCs. Because PCR isolates directions of maximal spectral variance without considering analyte concentrations, the selection of relevant components requires rigorous monitoring. The cross‐validation profile indicated a progressive decrease in the RMSECV up to four PCs. Beyond this threshold, additional components yielded negligible error reduction, signifying the onset of noise over‐parameterization; the optimal PCR model was thus configured with four components, as shown in Figure 4.

RMSECV plot of the calibration set against the number of optimized latent variables/principal components in PCR, PLS and GA‐PLS.

Conversely, PLS directly maximized the covariance between the spectral response and concentration matrices. Due to the supervised nature of the PLS engine, the algorithm achieved equivalent predictive capacity using fewer LVs. The cross‐validated residual profiles reached an optimal global minimum at three LVs, which were chosen to maintain structural stability and minimize the propagation of instrumental baseline noise.

Targeted Wavelength Selection via GA‐PLS and VIP Diagnostic Profiles

To eliminate spectral redundancy and strip away uninformative data variables, a GA was integrated with the PLS engine. Operating on binary chromosome encoding where individual genes dictated the inclusion or exclusion of specific wavelength channels, the GA iteratively sought to minimize the RMSECV. This feature extraction workflow fundamentally compressed the spectral dimensionality.

As a result of discarding non‐informative data channels and improving the local signal‐to‐noise ratio, the required number of LVs dropped from three in the full‐spectrum PLS model to just two in the optimized GA‐PLS framework (Figure 4). This structural contraction confirms enhanced model compactness without any sacrifice in predictive capacity. To validate the statistical authenticity of the GA‐isolated wavelength sub‐matrices, a VIP analysis was executed as a secondary diagnostic. As illustrated by the alignment between the algorithms, the wavelengths selected by the GA corresponded predominantly to the absorbance maxima and critical slope‐transition zones of CAN, CTL and AML.

External Validation Assessment

The diagnostic superiority of targeted wavelength selection was ultimately confirmed through external validation using the design‐independent OALHS dataset. The GA‐PLS model consistently yielded lower RMSECV and RMSEP values relative to the conventional full‐spectrum PLS and PCR models. This performance advantage confirms that eliminating uninformative variables successfully minimizes collinear noise propagation while securely preserving concentration‐dependent variance.

Chemometric Modelling and Hyperparameter Optimization

PCR

PCR was evaluated as an initial unsupervised baseline for resolving the high‐dimensional, collinear spectral data. PC analysis (PCA) was first applied to the 136‐variable spectral matrix to extract orthogonal PCs that capture the variance while isolating baseline noise and instrumental artifacts [49]. To determine the optimal number of PCs and prevent noise over‐parameterization, LOOCV was implemented [50]. The RMSECV was monitored as a function of the number of components, and the ‘elbow method’ was applied to identify the leveling‐off threshold [48]. As illustrated in Figure 4, the RMSECV dropped sharply and stabilized at four PCs. These four PCs were retained for the final regression model, successfully balancing model simplicity with the retention of variance critical for the quantification of CAN, CTL and AML across the multidimensional domain.

PLS

In contrast to the unsupervised nature of PCR, PLS was utilized to construct LVs that directly maximize the covariance between the spectral predictors and the analyte concentration responses [49]. This supervised alignment systematically discounts spectral variations uncorrelated with the target responses, resulting in a more compact model architecture. The selection of LVs was similarly optimized using the minimum RMSECV criterion via LOOCV. The cross‐validation trajectories reached an optimal global minimum at three LVs (Figure 4), beyond which the inclusion of subsequent LVs offered no statistically significant improvement in predictive accuracy. By explicitly modelling the structure of the concentration‐spectra relationship, the full‐spectrum PLS framework demonstrated superior noise‐handling capability compared to the PCR baseline [48].

GA‐PLS Optimization, Feature Extraction and Spectroscopic Interpretation

To enhance predictive performance and improve model interpretability, the GA was employed as a targeted variable‐selection strategy to eliminate uninformative and redundant spectral variables. The GA optimization was applied to the full spectral dataset comprising 136 wavelength channels within the 205–340 nm region. Key GA parameters were systematically optimized to maximize convergence efficiency while preserving sufficient search diversity (Table S1). To ensure structural robustness, the optimization procedure was repeated over multiple independent runs, and only wavelength channels consistently selected across iterations were retained for final model construction. The optimization dynamics revealed that population size played a critical role in balancing global exploration and local convergence; consequently, an optimal population range of 45–52 individuals was adopted. A double‐point crossover strategy was implemented to improve the recombination of candidate solutions, whereas a mutation rate of 0.005 was maintained to preserve genetic diversity and minimize premature convergence [51].

Random cross‐validation was employed as the fitness criterion, with minimization of the root mean square error of cross‐validation (RMSECV) serving as the optimization objective. Following GA optimization, the original spectral matrix was substantially compressed to only 46, 38 and 35 variables for CAN, CTL and AML, respectively (Figures S1–S3). This corresponds to a reduction of approximately 65%–75% of the original 136‐channel dataset, resulting in analyte‐specific wavelength sub‐matrices enriched with highly informative spectral variables. The distribution of these selected wavelength channels is presented in Figure 5. Such targeted feature extraction markedly enhanced model parsimony, reducing the required number of LVs from three in the full‐spectrum PLS models to only two in the optimized GA‐PLS models (Figure 4), without compromising predictive performance. Furthermore, the GA‐PLS models achieved a consistent reduction in RMSECV of approximately 30%–40% relative to the corresponding full‐spectrum models (Table S2). The authenticity of this improvement was verified through external validation using the design‐independent OALHS dataset, confirming that the observed gains were not attributable to model overfitting.

Spatial distribution of the consistently selected wavelength vectors isolated by the genetic algorithm for CAN, CTL and AML.

To verify that the observed dimensionality reduction originated from chemically meaningful spectral information rather than stochastic algorithmic behaviour, the selected wavelength subsets were examined using VIP scores and PLS loading profiles. The retained variables were predominantly concentrated within the 210–230 nm and 250–300 nm regions, together with several analyte‐specific higher‐wavelength features. These regions corresponded to zones of pronounced absorbance variability and extensive spectral overlap among the investigated compounds. As shown in Figures S4A–S6A, the selected wavelengths generally coincided with regions exhibiting VIP values greater than 1.0, indicating a significant contribution to model variance and predictive capability. In contrast, broad spectral regions characterized by persistently low VIP values were systematically excluded during optimization. Further evidence for the chemical relevance of the selected variables was obtained from the loading profiles of the principal LVs (Figures S4B–S6B). This principal LV (LV1) primarily described the major concentration‐dependent absorbance variations of the analytes, isolating key structural bands such as the dominant positive peak between 270 and 285 nm. By systematically framing these high‐amplitude loading regions, the GA successfully captured the essential spectral fingerprints required to resolve the heavily overlapping signals of the ternary mixture.

The close agreement between the dominant loading features, VIP maxima and GA‐selected wavelength intervals confirms that the optimization process was guided by genuine spectroscopic information. Collectively, these complementary diagnostic tools demonstrate that the retained variables provide sufficient discriminatory and quantitative information for the accurate simultaneous determination of CAN, CTL and AML.

Analyte‐Specific Interpretation of GA‐Selected Variables

As shown in Figure S4, the GA‐selected wavelength intervals for CAN exhibited excellent agreement with the major VIP maxima distributed across the investigated spectral range. Several regions centred around approximately 218, 246, 292, 305 and 341 nm displayed VIP values exceeding the critical threshold of 1.0, highlighting their importance for model construction and prediction. The LV1 loading profile revealed a characteristic oscillatory pattern consisting of alternating positive and negative loading regions. Pronounced positive loading maxima were observed near 248 and 306 nm, whereas significant negative minima occurred around 257 and 276 nm. The simultaneous selection of positive and negative loading regions preserved complementary concentration‐dependent information and enhanced model robustness towards baseline variations and spectral overlap within the ternary mixture. Consequently, the GA‐PLS model retained only 46 informative variables while maintaining excellent predictive capability and model stability.

Figure S5A illustrates that the variable‐selection pattern obtained for CTL was more localized and reflected the distinctive spectral characteristics of this analyte. The most influential spectral feature was observed between 270 and 285 nm, where VIP values reached approximately 1.8 and coincided with a broad positive LV1 loading region exhibiting a maximum loading value approaching +0.7, as shown in Figure S5B. This strong correspondence indicates that this spectral interval constitutes the principal source of CTL‐related concentration variance. Within the 220–260 nm region, the VIP profile displayed rapid fluctuations between significant and non‐significant values. Rather than retaining the entire interval, the GA selectively preserved discrete wavelength regions around 222, 231, 242 and 255 nm. Examination of the loading profile showed that these selected variables coincided with sharp transitions between positive and negative loading features, providing additional discriminatory information for resolving overlapping contributions from CAN and AML. Through this targeted optimization strategy, the CTL model was reduced to only 38 informative variables without sacrificing predictive performance.

As shown in Figure S6A, AML exhibited the most concentrated and well‐defined variable‐selection profile among the investigated analytes. The VIP profile was dominated by a narrow spectral region between approximately 290 and 300 nm, where the highest VIP value observed in the study (≈1.9) was recorded. A second highly informative spectral feature was identified near 335 nm. Both regions coincided with the strongest positive loading contributions in the LV1 profile, reaching loading values of approximately +0.7, as shown in Figure S6B. The strong agreement between the selected GA windows, VIP maxima and loading peaks indicates that these wavelengths represent the most influential spectral features governing AML‐related concentration variance. Consequently, the majority of the quantitative information required for AML determination was concentrated within a relatively narrow near‐UV region. A smaller interval within the 210–220 nm region was also retained despite exhibiting lower VIP values, indicating the presence of additional localized variance useful for quantitative modelling. Conversely, spectral regions between 265 and 285 nm and beyond approximately 340 nm exhibited consistently low VIP scores and negligible loading contributions and were therefore excluded from the final model. The absence of significant variance structures within these regions suggests that they contributed little to analyte discrimination while potentially introducing unnecessary spectral noise. As a result, AML exhibited the greatest degree of dimensionality reduction among the investigated compounds, requiring only 35 informative variables for accurate quantification.

Comparative Evaluation of Variable‐Selection Patterns

Comparison of the optimized wavelength‐selection profiles revealed distinct analyte‐specific spectral behaviours. CAN exhibited the most distributed selection pattern, indicating that its quantitative information was dispersed across multiple spectral regions. CTL displayed an intermediate pattern characterized by a dominant variance block within the 270–285 nm region supplemented by several localized discriminative features. In contrast, AML showed the most concentrated spectral fingerprint, with the majority of its predictive information confined to a narrow near‐UV region between approximately 290 and 335 nm. These findings demonstrate the ability of the GA optimization procedure to selectively retain chemically relevant variables while eliminating redundant spectral information. The resulting parsimonious models retained only 46, 38 and 35 variables for CAN, CTL and AML, respectively, thereby enhancing model interpretability, reducing computational complexity and improving predictive performance relative to the corresponding full‐spectrum PLS models.

Comparative Evaluation of Chemometric Models

The predictive performance of the developed chemometric models was assessed using several complementary statistical parameters, including RMSEC, RMSECV, RMSEP, RRMSEP and BCMSEP (Tables 2 and 3). Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of calibration quality, internal predictive ability and external validation performance.

The PCR and full‐spectrum PLS models exhibited acceptable calibration performance, with RMSEC values ranging from 0.1229 to 0.2895 for the investigated analytes. However, the corresponding RMSECV values suggest that the predictive capability of these models was partially limited by the substantial spectral overlap and collinearity inherent to the CAN/CTL/AML system. Compared with PCR, the PLS model provided slightly improved predictive performance owing to its ability to maximize the covariance between spectral variables and analyte concentrations. Nevertheless, both approaches remained susceptible to the influence of redundant and non‐informative spectral variables present in the full‐spectrum dataset.

The application of GA‐based wavelength selection resulted in a marked improvement in model performance. Following variable optimization, the GA‐PLS models exhibited substantially lower calibration and prediction errors. More importantly, RMSECV values were reduced by approximately 30%–40% relative to the corresponding full‐spectrum PLS models (Table S2), indicating enhanced model stability and reduced susceptibility to overfitting. These improvements can be attributed to the ability of the GA to eliminate irrelevant variables, reduce multicollinearity and retain only the most informative spectral regions associated with analyte quantification.

The superiority of the GA‐PLS approach was further confirmed through external validation using an independent set of OALHS‐generated mixtures (n = 16). Significant reductions in RMSEP values were observed for all analytes, decreasing from 0.2991 to 0.2138 for CAN, from 0.2535 to 0.2105 for CTL and from 0.2647 to 0.2280 for AML. These improvements correspond to reductions in prediction error, demonstrating enhanced predictive accuracy and generalization capability. Similar trends were observed for RRMSEP values, confirming improved relative prediction accuracy across the investigated concentration ranges. Additional evidence of model improvement was provided by the BCMSEP values, which were consistently lower for GA‐PLS than for PCR and full‐spectrum PLS models. The reduction in prediction bias indicates that wavelength optimization enhanced not only numerical model fitting but also the reliability of predictions for previously unseen samples. Such behaviour is particularly important for routine pharmaceutical applications, where robust and unbiased quantification is essential.

The enhanced performance of the GA‐PLS model can be attributed to effective dimensionality reduction of the spectral dataset. By retaining only the most informative wavelengths, the GA reduced noise propagation, improved the signal‐to‐noise ratio, enhanced model interpretability and minimized the risk of over‐parameterization. Unlike full‐spectrum models that assign equal importance to all wavelengths, the optimized GA‐PLS model focused on spectral regions containing the most relevant analytical information, resulting in a more parsimonious and chemically meaningful calibration model.

The superior performance of the GA‐PLS model was further supported by VIP analysis, which identified the spectral variables contributing most significantly to model prediction. The wavelengths retained by the GA largely coincided with regions exhibiting elevated VIP scores, providing independent confirmation that the selected variables contained the most relevant analytical information. This agreement between GA selection and VIP interpretation enhances confidence in the robustness and chemical relevance of the optimized model.

Overall, although PCR and PLS provided satisfactory baseline performance, the GA‐PLS approach consistently demonstrated superior calibration accuracy, predictive ability and robustness. The integration of wavelength selection with multivariate calibration produced lower prediction errors, reduced model complexity and improved generalization performance. These findings highlight the effectiveness of GA‐assisted chemometric modelling for resolving highly overlapping spectral systems and support its application for the reliable simultaneous determination of CAN, CTL and AML in pharmaceutical preparations.

By combining these pillars, the MA tool provides a transparent, quantitative and visual approach for assessing analytical methods beyond classical validation metrics, ensuring that they are not only accurate and reliable but also eco‐efficient, applicable and forward‐looking.

Limitations and Future Perspectives

Although the proposed chemometric‐assisted UV spectrophotometric approach demonstrated satisfactory analytical performance, robustness and sustainability, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the developed models were evaluated using laboratory‐prepared mixtures and commercial single‐component formulations because a marketed fixed‐dose CAN/CTL/AML product is not yet commercially available. Consequently, additional studies involving future commercial ternary formulations and larger batches of real pharmaceutical samples will be necessary to further confirm method applicability under routine manufacturing and quality‐control conditions.

Second, the developed calibration models were optimized for a specific concentration domain and spectral range. Although excellent predictive performance was achieved within these experimental boundaries, extrapolation beyond the investigated concentration levels may require model recalibration or updating. Broader validation across different formulation strengths, excipient compositions and manufacturing sources would further strengthen model generalizability.

Another limitation arises from the inherent characteristics of UV–Vis spectrophotometry. Despite the successful resolution of overlapping spectra through chemometric modelling, UV spectroscopy provides limited structural information compared with more selective techniques such as chromatographic methods coupled with mass spectrometry or nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. Consequently, the proposed methodology is primarily intended for quantitative quality‐control applications rather than structural characterization or impurity profiling. Furthermore, highly complex matrices may introduce additional spectral interferences, necessitating appropriate sample preparation strategies or complementary analytical techniques.

The present study focused on a ternary pharmaceutical system consisting of CAN, CTL and AML. Future investigations may extend the proposed workflow to more complex multicomponent systems containing a larger number of APIs, impurities or degradation products. In particular, the integration of advanced experimental design strategies, OALHS‐based validation, GA‐driven wavelength selection and VIP analysis could be explored for higher‐dimensional pharmaceutical systems. Such developments would further evaluate the scalability and practical utility of sustainable chemometric methodologies for industrial applications. Despite these limitations, the proposed approach offers significant advantages in terms of simplicity, rapidity, cost‐effectiveness and environmental sustainability. The successful integration of chemometric modelling, wavelength optimization and WAC assessment demonstrates the potential of sustainable spectroscopic methodologies as viable alternatives to conventional chromatographic techniques. Future studies incorporating larger datasets, more diverse pharmaceutical matrices and emerging machine‐learning approaches may further expand the applicability and predictive capabilities of such analytical platforms.

Conclusion

This study successfully established sustainable, chemometric‐assisted UV spectrophotometric methods for the concurrent determination of CAN, CTL and AML in multi‐component pharmaceutical formulations. By implementing PCR, PLS and GA‐PLS calibration engines, the severe spectral overlap of the target analytes was resolved within the 205–340 nm region without requiring resource‐intensive chromatographic separations. Among the evaluated frameworks, the GA‐PLS model exhibited superior diagnostic performance; its targeted wavelength selection achieved a 65%–75% reduction in spectral dimensionality, which mitigated noise propagation and yielded significantly lower prediction errors alongside enhanced model parsimony. A core contribution of this work lies in combining rigorous experimental design with advanced chemometric validation protocols. The application of an OALHS routine ensured a highly representative, space‐filling distribution of the external validation cohort. This design‐driven configuration provided an unbiased evaluation of model generalization while optimizing sample throughput. Importantly, VIP analysis and LV loading profiles successfully validated the chemical authenticity of the GA‐selected wavelengths. The tight alignment between VIP scores greater than 1.0 and the specific absorption bands of the analytes confirmed that feature selection was dictated by genuine electronic transitions rather than stochastic algorithm artifacts. Furthermore, the ecological and operational sustainability of the proposed methodology was verified via WAC profiling. The resulting high whiteness score confirms a favourable equilibrium between analytical precision, minimized environmental impact and cost‐effective throughput. Conclusively, this chemometric‐assisted UV protocol offers a rapid, robust and environmentally responsible alternative to conventional assays, making it highly suitable for routine pharmaceutical quality control applications.
