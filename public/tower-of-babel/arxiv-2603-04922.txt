Abstract
The density matrix is a positive semidefinite operator of trace 1 1 characterizing the state of a quantum system. We consider the inverse problem to reconstruct such density matrices from indirect measurements, also known as quantum state tomography. To solve such inverse problems in high or infinite dimensional settings, we study variational regularization using the quantum relative entropy as penalty functional. Quantum relative entropy is an analog of the well-known maximum entropy functional with compositions of functions replaced by the spectral functional calculus. The main aim of this paper is to establish the regularizing property of this scheme. As a crucial intermediate step, we establish lower semi-compactness of the penalty functional with respect to the weak- ∗ * -topology. Moreover, we compute the subgradient, proximal operator, and conjugate functional of the quantum relative entropy on finite dimensional spaces. This enables us to apply iterative algorithms from convex optimization to solve the regularized problems numerically. To show the validity and practical value of our results, we apply our theory to the examples of Photon-Induced Near-field Electron Microscopy (PINEM) and to optical homodyne tomography. Introduction

Quantum state tomography aims at determining the state of a quantum system by reconstructing its density matrix through a sequence of different measurements. It is a well established technique in quantum optics where heterodyne or homodyne measurements can be used to obtain the density matrix using different reconstruction techniques
[
19
,
10
]
.
More recently, quantum state tomography for electrons has been established using photon induced near field electron microscopy (PINEM)
[
27
]
.

Density matrices are positive semidefinite operators
\rho:\cal{H}\to\cal{H}
of trace
1
on an
underlying complex, separable Hilbert space
\cal{H}
. We will denote the set
of positive semidefinite operators
\rho:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
which are trace class by
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}
.
Only quantities of the form
(e_{l},\rho e_{l})
can be measured directly.
Here
e_{l}\in\cal{H}
is an eigenvector of a (self-adjoint)
operator associated to the observable. These quantities can be seen
as diagonal elements of a matrix representation of
\rho
corresponding to the eigen-basis.
Off-diagonal elements can only be observed indirectly by measuring an evolution of
\rho
.
The time evolution of a density matrix is described by the von Neumann-Liouville equations, and
solutions of this equation with initial value
\rho
are of the form
U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*}
for some unitary operator
U_{\theta}:\cal{H}\to\cal{H}
.
We assume that
U_{\theta}
is parameterized by some interaction parameter
\theta
in a bounded
subset
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. This leads to forward operators of the form

\displaystyle T:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}\rightarrow L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})),\qquad(T\rho)(\theta,l)=\langle e_{l},U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*}e_{l}\rangle

(1)

where it can sometimes be useful to use operators
U_{\theta}
of slightly different form.
The inverse problem of reconstructing a density matrix
\rho
from the measured data
T\rho
is
typically ill-posed
[
35
,
1
]
.
It poses the additional challenge that estimators should be
physical density matrices, i.e., positive semi-definite with trace equal to
1
.
Whereas it is easy to compute the metric projection of a given estimator onto
this set, such a procedure may not fully exploit the strongly stabilizing effect that
the positive semidefiniteness and the trace constraints often exhibit.
For many problem instances, the regularizing effect of these constraints is already
sufficient, and iterative schemes that ensure these constraints without
employing any additional regularization, except possibly for early stopping,
are often successfully used in practice
[
23
]
.
The SQUIRRELS algorithm proposed in
[
27
]
uses quadratic regularization
by a Hilbert-Schmidt (or Frobenius) norm together with positive semidefinite programming.

Instead of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm that was mainly chosen for algorithmic convenience,
we propose to use the quantum relative entropy as a physically more meaningful penalty functional. Viewing density matrices as a non-commutative version of probability distributions, the quantum relative entropy can be understood as an equivalent of the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
For operators
\rho,\rho_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
it is defined by

\displaystyle\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0}):=\begin{cases}\infty\quad\text{if }\rho\notin\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}\text{ or }\rho_{0}\notin\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}\text{ or }\ker\rho_{0}\nsubseteq\ker\rho\\
\tr(\rho_{0}-\rho+\rho\ln\rho-\rho\ln\rho_{0})\quad\text{else},\end{cases}

(2)

see
[
21
]
.
Here
\rho\ln\rho-\rho\ln\rho_{0}
is defined to be zero on the kernel of
\rho
for
\ker\rho_{0}\subseteq\ker\rho
.
Some authors omit the linear terms in this definition.
For further details we refer to
section

2.1
.

In this paper we will consider generalized Tikhonov regularization of the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}\in\underset{\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}}{\argmin}\,\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(T\rho)+\alpha\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})

(3)

with regularization parameter
\alpha>0
and some noisy data
g^{\text{obs}}\approx T\rho^{\dagger}
.
As a data fidelity functional
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}
we consider either the
L^{2}
-norm or the Kullback-Leibler divergence because the data are usually probability densities.

In
[
25
]
the quantum relative entropy was already used in a more practical setting to incorporate prior information if the measurement data does not allow for a unique reconstruction.
In a finite dimensional statistical setting such estimators were analyzed in
[
16
]
,
and error estimates have been derived.

We will show, under mild assumptions, that the minimization problem (
1
) has a unique
solution
\rho_{\alpha}
, and that, for suitable choice of the regularization parameters,
the method is regularizing
in the sense that
\rho_{\alpha}\to\rho^{\dagger}
in the trace norm and with respect to
\QKL

as the noise level vanishes.

After repeating the conditions for the regularizing property we summarize many important properties of the quantum relative entropy and show that it is weak-
*
lower semicompact. We also characterize the corresponding Bregman divergence, which is again the quantum relative entropy. After showing that
T
is weak-
*
to weak-
*
continuous, we are then able to conclude the regularizing property and show that the sequence of solutions for decreasing noise levels which appropriately chosen regularization parameters converge weak-
*
and even in the trace norm. In chapter
3
we move to the finite dimensional setting and derive subgradient, conjugate functional and proximal operators for the quantum relative entropy. With them the applications of iterative solution algorithm such as FISTA and the primal-dual hybrid gradient algorithm are possible. This section is mostly self-contained and provides the final solution algorithms such that they can easily be re-implemented and used in practice. To demonstrate the validity of our theoretical results, we apply them in the setting of homodyne tomography and PINEM and add corresponding numerical examples.

2
Regularizing property of quantum relative entropy

The purpose of this section is to show the use of quantum relative entropy as a penalty functional
in generalized Tikhonov regularization defines indeed a valid regularization method in the sense
that the estimation error tends to
0
with the noise level.
We begin by recalling classical broadly applicable sufficient conditions for this property.
In the case of data fidelity terms of norm-power type, the corresponding result dates back to
[
34
]
.
More general data fidelity terms were investigated in the PhD thesis
[
26
]

from which the following theorem is adapted.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
T:\dom T\subset\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be an operator between Banach spaces and let
\tau_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}
be topologies on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. Consider an exact solution

\rho^{\dagger}\in\dom T
with corresponding exact data
g^{\dagger}:=T\rho^{\dagger}
,
a sequence of observed data
(g^{\text{obs}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with corresponding data fidelity functionals

\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
and a penalty functional

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(C.1)

(\mathcal{X},\tau_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\tau_{\mathcal{Y}})
are real locally convex vector spaces.

(C.2)

\mathcal{R}
is sequentially lower semicontinuous.

(C.3)

\mathcal{R}
is sequentially lower semicompact, i.e.,
\{\rho\in\mathcal{X}:\mathcal{R}(\rho)\leq C\}

is compact for all
C\in\mathbb{R}
.

(C.4)

\dom T\cap\dom\mathcal{R}\neq\emptyset
and
T
is sequentially continuous on
\dom T\cap\dom\mathcal{R}
.

(C.5)

\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}
is sequentially lower semicontinuous for all data
g^{\text{obs}}_{k}
,
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}(g)=0\Leftrightarrow g=g^{\text{obs}}_{k}
, and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}(g^{\dagger})=0
.

(C.6)

For the exact data
g^{\dagger}
there exists a unique
\mathcal{R}
-minimizing solution
\rho^{\dagger}\in\argmin_{T\rho=g^{\dagger}}\mathcal{R}(\rho)
.

(C.7)

\partial\mathcal{R}(\rho^{\dagger})\neq\emptyset
.

Under assumptions
(C.1)
-
(C.5)
there exists a solution

\rho_{k}\in\argmin_{\rho}\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}(T\rho)+\alpha_{k}\mathcal{R}(\rho)\quad\text{for all}k\in\mathbb{N}

for any regularization parameter
\alpha_{k}>0
. If additionally
(C.6)
is satisfied, and
the regularization parameters are chosen such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{k}=0\text{ and }\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}(g^{\dagger})}{\alpha_{k}}=0,

then the sequence
\rho_{k}
converges to
\rho^{\dagger}
in the
\tau_{\mathcal{X}}
topology. Furthermore,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{S}_{g^{\dagger}}(T\rho_{k})=0\text{ and }\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}(\rho_{k})=\mathcal{R}(\rho^{\dagger}).

Finally, if also
(C.7)
holds true, then

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}^{\sigma}(\rho_{k},\rho^{\dagger})=0\quad\text{for }\sigma\in\partial\mathcal{R}(\rho^{\dagger})

where
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}^{\sigma}
denotes the Bregman divergence.

2.1
Properties of the quantum relative entropy

Due to the different definitions and settings used for the quantum relative entropy in the mathematical physics literature, we first summarize the main properties we need. Our setting includes linear terms and does not assume the operators to act on finite dimensional spaces or be of trace
1
as it is often done in the physics literature. Most results still transfer to this setting, but some care has to be taken.

Let us first introduce our notation. As in the introduction,
\mathcal{H}
denotes a complex, separable Hilbert space. Let
\{f_{l}\}
denote an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Following
[
28
, Chapter VI]
we define the spaces of operators:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

(bounded operators)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})

(compact operators)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle:\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{K}:\sum\nolimits_{l}\|\rho f_{l}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty\right\}

(Hilbert-Schmidt operators)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{\rho\in\mathcal{B}_{2}:\tr(|\rho|)<\infty\}

\displaystyle\text{(trace-class operators)}.

Here
\mathcal{B}_{2}
can be shown to be independent of the choice of the basis.
We use
\tilde{\mathcal{B}},\tilde{\mathcal{K}},\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{2}}
to denote the corresponding subspaces of hermitian (equivalently, self-adjoint) operators. By the spectral theorem, any
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{K}}

has an orthonormal basis
\{e_{l}\}
of eigenvectors with corresponding eigenvalues
\rho_{l}
.
For a real-valued function
\varphi
on the spectrum of
\rho
, the functional calculus

\varphi(\rho)
is defined by
\varphi(\rho)f:=\sum_{l}\varphi(\rho_{l})(f,e_{l})e_{l}

for all
f
for which the series converges, and
\varphi(\rho)\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
if
f
is bounded
[
28
, Theorem VII.2]
.
This is used both in the definition (
2
) of the quantum relative entropy
and in the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, where
|\rho|:=\sqrt{\rho^{*}\rho}
and

\tr(|\rho|):=\sum_{l}(f_{l},|\rho|f_{l})
is again independent of the choice of
\{f_{l}\}
.

We will choose
\tau_{X}
as weak-
*
-topology on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
in Theorem
2.1
.
To properly define this, recall that
\mathcal{B}_{1}
can be identified with the dual space of
\mathcal{K}
,

\mathcal{B}_{1}\cong\mathcal{K}^{*}
via the bilinear dual pairing
\langle\rho,\sigma\rangle:=\tr(\rho\sigma)

for
\rho\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
, see, e.g.,
[
28
]
.
In other words,
\mathcal{B}_{1}
has a predual

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\#}:=\mathcal{K},

and the weak topology on
\mathcal{B}_{1}
induced by
\mathcal{K}
will be called weak-
*
-topology.

In Theorem
2.1
we need real vector spaces, so we cannot use
\mathcal{B}_{1}
directly. However, we can treat
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
as a real vector space and characterize the corresponding weak-
*
-topology.

Lemma 2.2
.

Treating
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
as real vector spaces we have
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
, and the weak topology on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
with respect to
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
(called
weak-
*
-topology on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
in the following)
coincides with the relative topolog on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}
induced by the
weak-
*
-topology on
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

Proof.

One can check with a few simple calculations that for every
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
the functional
f_{\rho}:\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\sigma\mapsto\tr(\sigma\rho)
is bounded,
\mathbb{R}
-linear and well-defined. For the other direction, assume that
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
, then
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

\tilde{f}(\sigma)=f\left(\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+\sigma^{*})\right)-if\left(\frac{i}{2}(\sigma-\sigma^{*})\right)

is well-defined,
\mathbb{C}
-linear and bounded. Then
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
and there exists
\rho\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
such that
\tilde{f}(\sigma)=\tr(\sigma\rho)
. Then for all
\sigma\in\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
we have

\tr(\sigma\rho)=\tilde{f}(\sigma)=f\left(\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+\sigma^{*})\right)-if\left(\frac{i}{2}(\sigma-\sigma^{*})\right)=f(\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}.

Now for every unit vector
v\in\mathcal{H}
there exists a projection
P_{v}\in\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
onto the subspace spanned by
v
. Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Im}(\tr(P_{v}\rho))=\tr(P_{v}\rho)-\overline{\tr(P_{v}\rho)}=\tr(P_{v}(\rho-\rho^{*}))=\langle v,(\rho-\rho^{*})v\rangle

which implies
\rho-\rho^{*}=0
and therefore
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
.

To see that the topologies coincide, we use a net
(\rho_{n})_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
. If it converges with respect to the weak-
*
-topology of
\mathcal{K}
, it also converges with respect to the weak-
*
-topology of
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
. For the opposite direction, assume that

\lim_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\tr(\sigma\rho_{n})=\tr(\sigma\rho)\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in\tilde{\mathcal{K}}.

Then for
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\tr(\sigma\rho_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\tr\left(\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+\sigma^{*})\rho_{n}\right)+\tr\left(\frac{1}{2}(\sigma-\sigma^{*})\rho_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+\sigma^{*})\rho\right)+\lim_{n\in\mathcal{N}}(-i)\tr\left(\frac{i}{2}(\sigma-\sigma^{*})\rho_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+\sigma^{*})\rho\right)-i\tr\left(\frac{i}{2}(\sigma-\sigma^{*})\rho\right)=\tr(\sigma\rho).

So it also converges with respect to the weak-
*
-topology coming from
\mathcal{K}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

The quantum relative entropy
\QKL
defined on trace class operators on a separable Hilbert space has the the following properties.

1.

\QKL(\rho,\sigma)\geq 0
and
\QKL(\rho,\sigma)=0\Leftrightarrow\rho=\sigma

2.

\QKL
is jointly convex.

3.

\QKL
is monotonous, meaning

\QKL(\Phi(\rho),\Phi(\sigma))\leq\QKL(\rho,\sigma)
for
\Phi
a trace preserving expectation (see
[

, Thm. 1]
for further details). Especially for a projection

\Phi(\rho)=P\rho P+(I-P)\rho(I-P)

\QKL(P\rho P+(I-P)\rho(I-P),P\sigma P+(I-P)\sigma(I-P))\leq\QKL(\rho,\sigma)

The quantum relative entropy also satisfies an inequality which relates it to the trace norm in the same way as shown for example in
[
11
]
for the Kullback-Leibler divergence and the
\ell^{1}
-norm.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\rho,\sigma\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{2}{3}\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}+\frac{4}{3}\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\right)\QKL(\rho,\sigma).

Proof.

A proof for operators with trace
1
can be found in
[
13
]
where it was shown that

\QKL(\rho,\sigma)\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}^{2}

for
\tr\rho=\tr\sigma=1
. We will follow this proof and modify the relevant parts to get the more general form. For convenience we define the corresponding functionals
f_{\rho}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},a\mapsto\tr(a\rho)
and
f_{\sigma}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},a\mapsto\tr(a\sigma)
.
As in
[
13
]
we can find an orthogonal projection
P=1_{[0,\infty)}(\sigma-\rho)
on
\cal{H}
such that

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}=(f_{\sigma}-f_{\rho})(P)-(f_{\sigma}-f_{\rho})(I-P)\leq|(f_{\sigma}-f_{\rho})(P)|+|(f_{\sigma}-f_{\rho})(I-P)|.

It was also shown there that by monotonicity

\displaystyle\QKL(\rho,\sigma)\geq\left[f_{\sigma}(P)-f_{\rho}(P)+f_{\rho}(P)\ln\frac{f_{\rho}(P)}{f_{\sigma}(P)}\right]+\left[f_{\sigma}(I-P)-f_{\rho}(I-P)+f_{\rho}(I-P)\ln\frac{f_{\rho}(I-P)}{f_{\sigma}(I-P)}\right].

From
[
4
]
we have the inequality

|x-y|\leq\sqrt{\left(\frac{2}{3}x+\frac{4}{3}y\right)\left(y-x+x\ln{\frac{x}{y}}\right)}\quad\text{for all }x\geq 0,y>0.

Together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality this implies

\displaystyle(|x-y|+|a-b|)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{2}{3}(x+a)+\frac{4}{3}(y+b)\right)\left(y-x+x\ln{\frac{x}{y}}+b-a+a\ln{\frac{a}{b}}\right)\quad\text{for all }x,a\geq 0,y,b>0.

Finally setting
x=f_{\rho}(P),y=f_{\sigma}(P),a=f_{\rho}(I-P)
and
b=f_{\sigma}(I-P)
gives us the desired inequality.
∎

We can also bound the trace by
\QKL
as follows:

Lemma 2.6
.

With Euler’s number
\mathrm{e}
, for all
\rho,\rho_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
the following inequality holds true:

\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})\geq\tr(\rho)+(1-\mathrm{e})\tr(\rho_{0}).

Proof.

If
\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})
is infinite, the inequality is trivial. In particular, we can assume
that
\rho,\rho_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}
.
We can further assume that the discrete spectrum of
\rho_{0}
does not contain zero by switching to a subspace if necessary.
Then the spectral decomposition of
\rho_{0}
has the form
\rho_{0}=\sum\nolimits_{j}d_{j}P_{j}
with
eigenvalues
d_{j}>0
and corresponding eigen-projections
P_{j}
satisfying
\sum\nolimits_{j}P_{j}=I
. By Jensen’s trace inequality (see
[
5
]
) for the convex function
\eta,[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},t\mapsto t\ln t
we get

\displaystyle\tr\left(\eta\left(\sum\nolimits_{j}P_{j}\rho P_{j}\right)\right)\leq\tr\left(\sum\nolimits_{j}P_{j}\eta(\rho)P_{j}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{j}\tr(P_{j}\eta(\rho)P_{j})=\sum\nolimits_{j}\tr\left(P_{j}\eta(\rho))=\tr(\eta(\rho)\right).

Assuming now that the eigenvalues
d_{j}
occur with their multiplicities, i.e.,
\operatorname{rank}P_{j}=1

for all
j
, we have
P_{j}\rho P_{j}=\rho_{jj}P_{j}
for some “diagonal elements”
\rho_{jj}\geq 0
of

\rho
. This yields

\sum\nolimits_{j}\eta(\rho_{jj})\leq\tr(\eta(\rho)).

With this we now get

\displaystyle\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})

\displaystyle=\tr(\rho_{0})-\tr(\rho)+\tr(\eta(\rho))-\tr(\rho\ln\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\sum\nolimits_{j}\left(d_{j}-\rho_{jj}+\eta(\rho_{jj})\right)-\tr\left(\rho\sum\nolimits_{j}\ln(d_{j})P_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{j}\left(d_{j}-\rho_{jj}+\rho_{jj}\ln\rho_{jj}-\rho_{jj}\ln d_{j}\right).

The function
x\mapsto x\ln x-x\ln y-x
is convex on
[0,\infty)
and is therefore larger than its linear approximation at
\mathrm{e}y
. For
x\in[0,\infty)
and
y\in(0,\infty)
this implies the inequality

\displaystyle x\ln x-x\ln y-x

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}y\ln(\mathrm{e}y)-(\mathrm{e}y)\ln y-\mathrm{e}y+(\ln(\mathrm{e}y)-\ln y)(x-\mathrm{e}y)

\displaystyle=0+1\cdot(x-\mathrm{e}y)=x-\mathrm{e}y\,.

Hence,
d_{j}-\rho_{jj}+\rho_{jj}\ln\rho_{jj}-\rho_{jj}\ln d_{j}\geq d_{j}+\rho_{jj}-\mathrm{e}d_{j}
,
and

\displaystyle\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\sum\nolimits_{j}\left(d_{j}+\rho_{jj}-\mathrm{e}d_{j}\right)=\tr(\rho)+(1-\mathrm{e})\tr(\rho_{0}).\qed

Often the quantum relative entropy is just considered for matrices with trace one, so this inequality is not very useful. However in our case, it allows us to show lower semicompactness without using additional trace constraints.

Theorem 2.7

(
(C.3)
)
.

The functional
\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0}):\mathcal{B}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

is sequentially weak-
*

Proof.

Let
C>0
and let

\rho\in S_{C}:=\{\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0})\leq C\}
. Because
\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})
is finite we have
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}
and

. From

C\geq\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})\geq\tr(\rho)+(1-\mathrm{e})\tr(\rho_{0})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\leq C+(\mathrm{e}-1)\tr(\rho_{0}).

With this we have shown that

is contained in a norm bounded set.
As

is the norm closure of the space of finite rank operators it is separable

, Theroem VI.13]
. Then, the sequential version of Banach-Alaoglu’s theorem

is sequentially weak-

is sequentially weak-

∎

Lemma 2.8

For
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}

\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is well-defined, then

\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}\in\partial(\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0}))(\rho).

Proof.

We take
\sigma\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
and calculate

\displaystyle\QKL(\sigma,\rho_{0})

\displaystyle-\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})

\displaystyle-\tr((\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0})(\sigma-\rho))=\tr(\rho_{0}-\sigma+\sigma\ln\sigma-\sigma\ln\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\phantom{-\tr((\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0})(\sigma-\rho))=}-\tr(\rho_{0}-\rho+\rho\ln\rho-\rho\ln\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\phantom{-\tr((\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0})(\sigma-\rho))=}-\tr(\sigma\ln\rho-\rho\ln\rho-\sigma\ln\rho_{0}+\rho\ln\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\phantom{-\tr((\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0})(\sigma-\rho))}=\tr(\rho-\sigma+\sigma\ln\sigma-\sigma\ln\rho)=\QKL(\sigma,\rho)\geq 0.

The last inequality follows from
Lemma

2.4
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

In this context by well-defined we mean that we treat
\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}
as single operator and not as the difference of two operators, that have to be well-defined. This entails that we set
\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}
to be
0
on the intersection of the kernels of
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
. Furthermore, for
v
in a common eigenspace of
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
corresponding to
\lambda
and
\lambda_{0}
respectively, we have
(\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0})v=(\ln\lambda-\ln\lambda_{0})v
and then accordingly define
\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}
on the union of the common eigenspaces via linear extension. This allows us to still work with operator logarithms even though the spectra of
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
always have
0
as an accumulation point so
\ln\rho
and
\ln\rho_{0}
separately are not well-defined bounded operators.

Remark 2.10

(
(C.7)
)
.

To fulfill condition
(C.7)
the subdifferential has to be considered as a subset of the dual space of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
with respect to the weak-
*
topology. This is not the same as the usual dual space
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
and by the properties of the weak-
*
topology
[
33
, Section 3.14]
coincides with its predual
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
instead. Therefore, the stronger assumption
\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
is necessary for
(C.7)
.

With this subdifferential we can calculate the corresponding Bregman divergence.

Corollary 2.11
.

For
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}
if
\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
is well-defined, then the Bregman divergence with respect to
\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}\in\partial(\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0}))(\rho)
is given by

\Delta_{\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0})}^{\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0}}(\sigma,\rho)=\QKL(\sigma,\rho).

Proof.

In the proof of the previous theorem we already calculated

\displaystyle\QKL(\sigma,\rho_{0})

\displaystyle-\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})-\tr((\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{0})(\sigma-\rho))=\QKL(\sigma,\rho)

and the left hand side is exactly the Bregman divergence.
∎

2.2
Weak-
*
-continuity properties of operator and data fidelity functional

We write the real sequence spaces as
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})
and indicate the complex sequence spaces by
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C})
. The codomain of the operator
T
also has a predual, giving rise to a weak-
*
-topology,
which is given by

\displaystyle L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}))^{\#}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}L^{2}(I,c_{0}(\mathbb{Z})),&p=1\\
L^{2}(I,\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z})),&p\in(1,\infty)\quad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1.\end{cases}

The facts that
L^{2}(I,c_{0}(\mathbb{Z}))^{*}=L^{2}(I,\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}))
and

L^{2}(I,\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))^{*}=L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}))

can be derived from
[
14
, Chapter 1.3]
using the separability of
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
.

We first show that the operator
T
is weak-
*
to weak-
*
sequentially continuous.

Lemma 2.12

(
(C.4)
)
.

An operator
T
of the form (
1
) is weak-
*
to weak-
*
sequentially continuous
if
\{U_{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}:\theta\in I\}
is uniformly bounded (in particular, if
all operators
U_{\theta}
are unitary).

Proof.

We start with a sequence
\rho_{n}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{+}
that converges to
\rho
in the
weak-
*
topology, i.e.,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tr(\sigma\rho_{n})=\tr(\sigma\rho)\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.

We first show point-wise convergence where we fix
\theta
. For
(a_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\in(\ell^{p^{\prime}})
we have

\displaystyle\langle(T\rho)_{\theta},a\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}(T\rho)(\theta,l)a_{l}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle a_{l}e_{l},U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*}e_{l}\rangle=\tr(D_{a}U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*})

\displaystyle\text{ with }D_{a}v

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l}a_{l}(e_{l},v)e_{l}.

Then
D_{a}
is compact because
a\in(\ell^{p^{\prime}})\subset c_{0}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tr(D_{a}U_{\theta}\rho_{n}U_{\theta}^{*})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tr(\rho_{n}U_{\theta}^{*}D_{a}U_{\theta})=\tr(\rho U_{\theta}^{*}D_{a}U_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\tr(D_{a}U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*})=\langle(T\rho)_{\theta},a\rangle.

Because every weak-
*
-convergent sequence is norm bounded we can assume that
\rho_{n}
is bounded in norm by some constant
C
. We now take
a(\theta)\in L^{2}(I,(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z})))
and get

\displaystyle|\langle(T\rho_{n})_{\theta},a(\theta)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|a(\theta)\|_{(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))}\|(T\rho_{n})_{\theta}\|_{\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\leq\|a(\theta)\|_{(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))}\|(T\rho_{n})_{\theta}\|_{\ell_{1}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle=\|a(\theta)\|_{(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))}\tr(U_{\theta}\rho_{n}U_{\theta}^{*})\leq\|a(\theta)\|_{(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))}\|U_{\theta}^{*}U_{\theta}\|\tr(\rho_{n})

\displaystyle=\|a(\theta)\|_{(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))}\|U_{\theta}\|^{2}\|\rho_{n}\|_{1}\leq\|a(\theta)\|_{(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z}))}\|U_{\theta}\|^{2}C.

So
|\langle(T\rho_{n})_{\theta},a(\theta)\rangle|
is point-wisely uniformly bounded by a function in
L^{2}(I)
and by the dominated convergence theorem
\langle(T\rho_{n})_{\theta},a(\theta)\rangle
converges to
\langle(T\rho)_{\theta},a(\theta)\rangle
in
L^{2}
which finishes the proof.
∎

To analyze both
L^{2}
and Kullback-Leibler data fidelity terms simultaneously and additionally allow the use of different mixed
L^{p}
-norms, we define
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}:L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}))\rightarrow[0,\infty]
by

\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(f):=\int_{I}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}s(g^{\text{obs}}(\theta,l),f(\theta,l))\mathrm{d}\theta

where
s:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. We get the usual
L^{2}
-norm with
p=2
and

s_{2}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}.

Furthermore by setting

\displaystyle s_{KL}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\infty\quad&\text{if }x<0\text{ or }y<0\text{ or }(x>0\text{ and }y=0)\\
y\quad&\text{if }x=0\text{ and }y\geq 0\\
y-x+x\ln\frac{x}{y}\quad&\text{else}\end{cases}

and
p=1
we get the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
As the codomain is a mixed
L^{p}
-space, weak-
*
lower semicontinuity of the data fidelity functional is not immediately obvious. We will see, that in our case we can reduce the problem to investigating the lower semicontinuity on
L^{2}(I)
.

Lemma 2.13
.

If
\mathcal{F}_{s,l}:L^{2}(I)\rightarrow[0,\infty],f\mapsto\int_{I}s(g^{\text{obs}}(\theta,l),f(\theta))\mathrm{d}\theta
is convex and lower semicontinuous for all
l\in\mathbb{Z}
with respect to the norm topology, then
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}
is weak-
*
lower semicontinuous. Furthermore, if
s(x,y)=0\Leftrightarrow x=y
then
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(g)=0\Leftrightarrow g=g^{\text{obs}}
.

Proof.

We will use that the supremum of lower semicontinuous functionals is lower semi-continuous as well. We then for
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

\mathcal{F}_{n}(f):=\int_{I}\sum_{|l|<n}s(g^{\text{obs}}(\theta,l),f(\theta,l))\mathrm{d}\theta

and because of the non-negativity of the summands we get
\mathcal{F}(f)=\sup_{n}\mathcal{F}_{n}(f)
.
Now consider
(f_{k})_{k}\subset L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}))
which converges in the weak-
*
-topology to
f
. This means

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\langle f_{k},a\rangle=\langle f,a\rangle\quad\text{for all }a\in L^{2}(I,(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z})))

Now let
P_{n}:L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}))\rightarrow L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2n-1})
be the point-wise restriction of
f_{k}
such that

(P_{n}f)(\theta,l)=f(\theta,l)\quad\text{for all }|l|<n

Furthermore, define the embedding
\iota_{n}:L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2n-1})\rightarrow L^{2}(I,(\ell^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Z})))
by

(\iota_{n}a)_{j}(\theta)=\begin{cases}a(\theta,l)\quad|l|<n\\
0\quad\text{ else}\end{cases}

We get for all
a^{(n)}\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2n-1})

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\langle P_{n}f_{k},a^{(n)}\rangle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\langle f_{k},\iota_{n}a^{(n)}\rangle=\langle f,\iota_{n}a^{(n)}\rangle=\langle P_{n}f,a^{(n)}\rangle

This implies that
(P_{n}f_{k})_{k}
converges weakly in
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2n-1})
. For convex functionals weak lower semicontinuity is equivalent to strong lower semicontinuity. Since
\mathcal{F}_{s,l}
is lower semicontinuous on
L^{2}(I)
this transfers to the restriction of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
on
L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2n-1})
, which by definition coincides with
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is weakly lower semicontinuous and we get

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}_{n}(f_{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{F}_{n}(P_{n}f_{k})=\mathcal{F}_{n}(P_{n}f)=\mathcal{F}_{n}(f).

This shows that
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is weak-
*
lower semicontinuous and by taking the supremum then also
\mathcal{F}
is weak-
*
lower semicontinuous.

For the second part of the statement we use that if
g=g^{\text{obs}}
then
s(g^{\text{obs}}(\theta,l),g(\theta,l))=0
which implies
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(g)=0
. For the opposite direction, if
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(g)=0
we know from the positivity of
s
that
s(g^{\text{obs}}(\theta,l),g(\theta,l))=0
almost everywhere. This implies
g=g^{\text{obs}}
almost everywhere.
∎

As a corollary we get the desired properties for
\KL
and
\|\,\|_{2}
.

Corollary 2.14

(
(C.5)
)
.

The functionals
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot-g^{\text{obs}}\|_{L^{2}(I,\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}))}^{2}
on
L^{2}(I,\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
and
\KL(g^{\text{obs}},\cdot)
on
L^{2}(I,\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}))
are weak-* lower semicontinuous and fulfill
(C.5)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.13
we just need that
\|\cdot-f\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}
is lower semicontinuous for all
f\in L^{2}(I)
which is obvious and for
\KL
we need that
\KL
is lower semicontinuous on
L^{2}(I)
which follows from the lower semicontinuity of
\KL
on
L^{1}(I)
that was shown in
[
11
]
.
∎

If one is willing to impose further a-priori assumptions on the unknown density matrix
\rho
, then
weak-
*
-continuity of
T
can often be established by the following simple lemma:

Lemma 2.15
.

Suppose that a linear operator
T:\dom T\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}
mapping into some
Banach space
\mathcal{Y}
is defnied on a domain
\dom T
that is weakly sequentially compact in some
Banach space
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is continuously embedded in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
with respect to the norm topologies. Further assume that
T
is continuous with respect to the norm topologies of
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
and

\mathcal{Y}
. Then
T
is weak-
*
to weak seqeuntially continuous from
\mathcal{B}_{1}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

Consider a sequence
(\rho^{(k)})
in
\dom T
converging to
\rho
with respect to the weak-
*
-topology
of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
induced by
\mathcal{K}
. By weak compactness of
\dom T
,

(\rho^{(k)})
has a subsequence converging weakly to some
\tilde{\rho}
in
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
.
Since the embedding
\hat{\mathcal{B}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{1}
is also weakly continuous, the subsequence also
converges weakly and weak-
*
to
\tilde{\rho}
in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
. Hence
\tilde{\rho}=\rho
, and
since every subsequence has a subsequence converging weakly to
\rho
in
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
, the whole
sequence converges weakly to
\rho
in
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
. As strong continuity of
T
implies weak continuity
from
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
to
\mathcal{Y}
, it follows that
T\rho^{(k)}\rightharpoonup T\rho
in
\mathcal{Y}
.
∎

Note that in this case it is possible to choose
\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}
as the weak topology, rather than
a weak-
*
-topology, which facilitates the verification of sequential lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}
.

2.3
Main result

After these preparations we are able to prove the regularizing property for quantum relative entropy.

Theorem 2.16
.

Assume that
T
fulfills the assumption of
Lemma

2.12
and that
\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}}
is either the
2
-norm or the Kullback-Leibler divergence. Furthermore, assume that
(C.6)
is satisfied and that we have sequences
(g^{\text{obs}}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of observed data and
(\alpha_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of parameters that fulfill the same assumptions as in
Theorem

2.1
.
Then the solutions to the regularized problem

\rho_{k}:=\underset{\rho}{\argmin}\;\mathcal{S}_{g^{\text{obs}}_{k}}(T\rho)+\alpha_{k}\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})

exist, converge to
\rho^{\dagger}
in the weak-
*
-topology and

\displaystyle\QKL(\rho_{k},\rho_{0})\overset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}\QKL(\rho^{\dagger},\rho_{0})\text{ and }\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{S}_{g^{\dagger}}(T\rho_{k})=0.

If additionally
\ln\rho^{\dagger}-\ln\rho_{0}
is well defined and compact, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\QKL(\rho_{k},\rho^{\dagger})=0\text{ and }\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\rho_{k}-\rho^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}=0.

Proof.

The assumption in this theorem together with the previous results in this chapter guarantee that
(C.1)
-
(C.6)
are satisfied and the first three convergence results then follow directly from
Theorem

2.1
. The assumption that
\ln\rho^{\dagger}-\ln\rho_{0}
is well-defined and compact additionally gives us
(C.7)
by
Lemma

2.8
. This property gives us convergence in Bregman divergence which we have established to be
\QKL
again in
Corollary

2.11
. Finally, for the norm convergence we use that
\rho_{k}
is weak-
*
convergent and therefore norm bounded. By
Lemma

2.5
we then have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\rho_{k}-\rho^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}^{2}\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{2}{3}\|\rho_{k}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}+\frac{4}{3}\|\rho^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\right)\QKL(\rho_{k},\rho^{\dagger})\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}C\QKL(\rho_{k},\rho^{\dagger})=0.\qed

Remark 2.17
.

The only condition we have not discussed so far is
(C.6)
. It is satisfied for example if
T
is injective. Even without it we still at least get all the convergence results for a subsequence of
\rho_{k}
to some
\mathcal{R}
-minimizing solution
[
26
]
.

3
Algorithmic approaches

In this section we investigate methods to solve the regularized problem.
To this end, we first define the corresponding discrete problem and then characterize the subdifferential, conjugate functional and proximal operators for the quantum relative entropy.
Then the problem can then be solved by standard algorithms from convex optimization.

3.1
Description of discrete setting

We discretize the space
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
by choosing a suitable finite orthonormal basis
V:=\{v_{n}\in\mathcal{H}:1\leq n\leq N\}
with corresponding orthonormal projection
P_{V}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\spn V\cong\mathbb{C}^{N}
. The discrete version of
\rho\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
is then given by
\hat{\rho}:=P_{V}\rho P_{V}^{*}

and can be identified with a Hermitian matrix
\hat{\rho}\in\text{Herm}(N)
. The actual choice of the basis
V
depends on the experimental setup and should be done in a way that the discretization error
\|\rho^{\dagger}-P_{V}^{*}P_{V}\rho^{\dagger}P_{V}^{*}P_{V}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
is small for the solution. We furthermore assume that
\hat{\rho}_{0}
has full rank because
\QKL(\rho,\rho_{0})
is infinite for operators with a nullspace that is not included in the nullspace of
\rho_{0}
. This means that restricting ourselves to a subspace where
\hat{\rho}_{0}
has full rank does not reduce our search space. To apply methods of convex analysis to
\text{Herm}(N)
it is advantageous to treat it as a real vector space with inner product given by
\langle\hat{\sigma},\hat{\rho}\rangle_{\text{Herm}(N)}:=\tr(\hat{\sigma}\hat{\rho})\in\mathbb{R}
(cf.
Lemma

2.2
).

The discretization of the codomain is usually determined by the physical setup which results in an operator

Q:L^{2}(I,\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M_{\theta}\cdot M_{l}}
defined by local averaging
around a finite set of values for
\theta
and
l
.
The discrete forward operator is now given by
\hat{T}:\text{Herm}(N)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M_{\theta}\cdot M_{l}}

\hat{T}(\hat{\rho}):=QT(P_{V}^{*}\hat{\rho}P_{V}).

As discussed later for specific applications, it is sometimes useful to use a slightly modified discrete operator instead which mimics properties of the continuous operator in the discrete case.
In this section we only assume that we have some discrete version of our operator for which we can also compute its adjoint
\hat{T}^{*}
. Finally, as in the continuous case, we can define
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{g^{\text{obs}}}:\mathbb{R}^{M_{\theta}\cdot M_{l}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(f):=\sum_{l,\theta}s(g^{\text{obs}}(\theta,l),f(\theta,l))

with
s
defined as in
Section

1
.
This leads us to the discrete problem

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}=\underset{\hat{\rho}\in\text{Herm}(N)}{\argmin}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(\hat{T}\hat{\rho})+\alpha\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{0}).

(4)

As for the other quantities we use
\widehat{\QKL}
to indicate that we work in a finite dimensional space.

3.2
Convex analysis for the quantum relative entropy on matrix spaces

We now investigate further properties of the quantum relative entropy in the finite dimensional setting. Note that all results for the infinite version are also still valid but now we can additionally apply convex analysis on hermitian matrices as developed in
[
20
]
to exactly compute the subdifferential and the conjugate functional.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
\hat{\rho}
in the essential domain of
\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})
the subdifferential is given by

\displaystyle\partial\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)(\hat{\rho})=\begin{cases}\{\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\}\quad\text{if }\ker\hat{\rho}=\{0\}\\
\emptyset\quad\text{else}\end{cases}

Proof.

We first assume that
\hat{\rho}
has eigenvalues contained in
(0,\infty)
so we can compute
\ln\hat{\rho}
and
\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}
.
Furthermore, the function
f:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},t\mapsto t\ln t-t
is convex, and the function
\tilde{f}:(0,\infty)^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},(t_{1},...,t_{N})\mapsto\sum_{j}f(t_{j})
is convex and does not depend on the order of its arguments. This means that
[
20
, Theorem 3.2]
applies, and the corresponding spectral function
f_{\mathrm{spec}}:\text{Herm}(N)\rightarrow[0,\infty),\hat{\rho}\mapsto\tr(\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho})
has the subdifferential

\partial f_{\mathrm{spec}}(\hat{\rho})=\{f^{\prime}(\hat{\rho})\}=\{\ln\hat{\rho}\}.

To get the subdifferential of
\widehat{\QKL}
we use that
-\tr(\cdot\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})
is linear and
\tr(\hat{\rho}_{0})
is constant. We can then add the corresponding subdifferentials to get

\partial\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)(\hat{\rho})=\partial f_{\mathrm{spec}}(\hat{\rho})+\{-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\}+\{0\}=\{\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\}.

Now we just have to show that the subdifferential is empty if the kernel of
\hat{\rho}
is nontrivial. Let
P
be a projection onto a one dimensional subspace of the nullspace of
\hat{\rho}
. In contradiction to the statement of the lemma, we now assume that there exists
\hat{\sigma}
such that
-I+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}+\hat{\sigma}\in\partial(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0}))(\hat{\rho})
. Then by the definition of the subdifferential we have for all
t>0
that

\displaystyle\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho}+tP,\hat{\rho}_{0})-\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\tr((-I+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}+\hat{\sigma})(\hat{\rho}+tP-\hat{\rho}))

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\tr(-tP+(\hat{\rho}+tP)\ln(\hat{\rho}+tP)-\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}+tP\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\tr((-I+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}+\hat{\sigma})tP)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\tr((\hat{\rho}+tP)\ln(\hat{\rho}+tP)-\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle\geq t\tr(\hat{\sigma}P)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ln t

\displaystyle\geq\tr(\hat{\sigma}P).

As the last inequality holds for all
t>0
and the left hand side tends to
-\infty
as

t\searrow 0
, we have arrived at a contradiction.
∎

We can now derive the conjugate functional using Young’s equality.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a hermitian matrix
\sigma
, the conjugate functional of
\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})
is given by

\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)^{*}(\hat{\sigma})=\tr(\exp(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})-\hat{\rho}_{0})

Proof.

Take
\hat{\sigma}
hermitian and define
\hat{\nu}:=\exp(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})
.
Then
\hat{\nu}
has full rank, and

\displaystyle\partial\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)(\hat{\nu})=\{\ln\hat{\nu}-\ln\hat{\rho_{0}}\}=\{\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}-\ln\hat{\rho_{0}}\}=\{\hat{\sigma}\}.

Young’s equality now yields

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)^{*}(\hat{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\hat{\sigma}\hat{\nu}\right)-\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\nu},\hat{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\hat{\sigma}\hat{\nu}\right)-\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{0}-\hat{\nu}+\hat{\nu}(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\hat{\nu}-\hat{\rho}_{0}\right)=\tr\left(\exp(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})-\hat{\rho}_{0}\right).\qed

With the subdifferential we can also compute the proximal mapping

\prox_{\tau\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0})}(\hat{\sigma}):=\argmin_{\hat{\rho}}\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{0})+\frac{1}{2\tau}\|\hat{\rho}-\hat{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}.

It is essentially the same as for the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and its computation requires the inverse of the bijective function
g:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},t\mapsto\ln t+t
which can be expressed using the Lambert
W
function as
g^{-1}(t)=W\left(e^{t}\right)
. Using this expression is numerically unstable for large
t
, so Newton’s method should be used instead.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\hat{\sigma}
hermitian

\prox_{\tau\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0})}(\hat{\sigma})=\tau g^{-1}\left(\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\tau}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}-\ln\tau\right)=\tau W\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\exp\left(\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\tau}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\right)\right).

Proof.

The proximal mapping can alternatively be characterized by

\prox_{\tau\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0})}(\hat{\sigma})=\hat{\rho}\Leftrightarrow\hat{\sigma}-\hat{\rho}\in\tau\partial\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0})\right)(\hat{\rho}).

Because the image of
g^{-1}
is positive
\tau g^{-1}(\tau^{-1}\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}-\ln\tau)=:\tau g^{-1}(\hat{\omega})
has full rank and we have

\displaystyle\tau\partial\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0})\right)(\tau g^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))

\displaystyle=\tau(\ln(\tau g^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tau(\ln\tau+\ln g^{-1}(\hat{\omega})+g^{-1}(\hat{\omega})-g^{-1}(\hat{\omega})-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tau(\ln\tau+\hat{\omega}-g^{-1}(\hat{\omega})-\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tau(\tau^{-1}\hat{\sigma}-g^{-1}(\hat{\omega}))=\hat{\sigma}-\tau g^{-1}(\hat{\omega}).\qed

With the same approach we can also compute the gradient for the conjugate functional, and we can use Moreau’s identity to get an expression for the proximal operator of the conjugate functional.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\hat{\sigma}
hermitian

\partial\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)^{*}(\hat{\sigma})=\{\exp(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\}

and

\prox_{\tau(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0}))^{*}}(\hat{\sigma})=\hat{\sigma}-W\left(\tau\exp(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)=\hat{\sigma}-g^{-1}\left(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}+\ln\tau\right).

Proof.

The conjugate functional is just a shifted version of
\tr(\exp(\cdot))
which by the previously mentioned theory has the subdifferential
\{\exp(\cdot)\}
. Inserting the shift, we get the claimed formula. For the proximal operator of the conjugate functional we can use Moreau’s identity and get

\displaystyle\prox_{\tau(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0}))^{*}}(\hat{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}-\tau\prox_{\tau^{-1}\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\rho_{0})}(\tau^{-1}\hat{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}-\tau\tau^{-1}W\left(\tau\exp\left(\tau\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\tau}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}-W\left(\tau\exp(\hat{\sigma}+\ln\hat{\rho}_{0})\right).\qed

Now that we have computed theses quantities for the quantum relative entropy, we can use standard algorithms from convex optimization to solve the regularized problem. The main downside of this approach is that many calculations require the computation of an eigenvalue decomposition to get the matrix logarithm or apply the function
g^{-1}
on the spectrum.
Fortunately, the desired density matrices are often not very large, so these calculations are still feasible.

Many iterative algorithms can be accelerated significantly if one of the functionals that is minimized is strictly convex with some known convexity parameter
\mu
. While
\widehat{\QKL}
itself is not strictly convex it is strictly convex if the eigenvalues of the matrix-valued argument are bounded from above. As we usually try to find density matrices, the eigenvalues of which are bounded by
1
, the following lemma can be useful:

Lemma 3.5
.

\hat{QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})
is strictly convex with parameter
\mu
on the set

M_{\mu}:=\{\hat{\rho}\in\text{Herm}(N):\text{eigenvalues of }\hat{\rho}\text{ not bigger than }\mu^{-1}\}.

Proof.

We have to show that
\hat{\rho}\mapsto\hat{QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{0})-\frac{\mu}{2}\tr(\hat{\rho}^{2})
is convex on the claimed set. For this we can ignore the terms that are constant or linear in
\hat{\rho}
. Hence, it suffices to show that
\hat{\rho}\mapsto\tr(\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}-\frac{\mu}{2}\hat{\rho}^{2})
is convex on
M_{\mu}
.
As this function only depends on the eigenvalues of
\hat{\rho}
and is independent of their order, this is the case if and only if the corresponding real-valued function
x\mapsto x\ln x-\frac{\mu}{2}x^{2}
is convex (see
[
8
]
). Computing the second derivative shows that this is the case for
x\leq\frac{1}{\mu}
.
∎

3.3
Iterative solution algorithms

We will now present some convex optimization algorithms that are able to efficiently and reliably compute the solution of the discrete regularized problem (
4
).
We begin with the simpler case where the squared
L^{2}
-norm is used as a data fidelity term.
In this case we can use a version of FISTA proposed in
[
2
]
, which had already been proposed for this problem without regularization in
[
3
]
.
As usual for forward backward splitting algorithms, we alternate between a gradient step for the data fidelity functional and an application of the proximal operator. During the iteration the step size is changed to accelerate the convergence. As we usually reconstruct density matrices which have eigenvalues not bigger than
1
, we can in practice assume
\widehat{\QKL}
to be strictly convex with parameter
\mu
and use the faster versions of the corresponding algorithm which converge exponentially
[
7
]
. In the settings we considered, choosing
\mu=0.5
, which by
Lemma

3.5
corresponds to eigenvalues smaller than
2
, works well.

Algorithm 1

Generalized accelerated FISTA

\mu>0;\,\tau\in(0,\|T^{*}T\|^{-1});\,\rho^{(0)};

t_{0}:=0;\,\rho^{(-1)}:=\rho^{(0)};\,q:=\frac{\tau\mu}{1+\tau\mu};

for

l=0,1,2,...

do

t_{l+1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-qt_{l}^{2}+\sqrt{(1-qt_{l})^{2}+4t_{l}^{2}}\right)

\beta_{l}=\frac{t_{l}-1}{t_{l+1}}(1+(1-t_{l+1})\tau\mu)

\tilde{\rho}^{(l)}=\rho^{(l)}+\beta_{l}(\rho^{(l)}-\rho^{(l-1)})

\sigma_{l}=\tilde{\rho}^{(l)}-\tau T^{*}(T\tilde{\rho}^{(l)}-g^{\text{obs}})
\triangleright
Gradient step

\rho^{(l+1)}=\prox_{\alpha\tau\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0})}(\sigma_{l})
\triangleright
Proximal step

end

for

If we use the Kullback-Leibler divergence, forward backward splitting algorithms often become numerically unstable because taking a gradient step for the data fidelity functional can be impossible if the iterate is not in the essential domain or the gradient can get extremely large for values close to zero.
As an alternative, one can use primal dual hybrid gradient methods, like the Chambolle-Pock algorithm
[
6
]
. Here we alternate between a proximal step for the conjugate of our data fidelity functional and a proximal step of our penalty functional. By assuming strict convexity, we can again optimize our parameter choices and significantly accelerate the convergence such that it is quadratic
[
7
]
.

Algorithm 2

Accelerated Chambolle-Pock algorithm

\mu>0;\,\alpha>0;\,\tau_{0}>0;\,\nu_{0}>0;\,\tau_{0}\nu_{0}\|T^{*}T\|\leq 1;\,\rho^{(0)};\,g^{(0)};

\tilde{\rho}^{(0)}=\rho^{(0)}

for

l=0,1,2,...

do

\tilde{g}^{(l)}=g^{(l)}+\nu_{l}T\tilde{\rho}^{(l)}

g^{(l+1)}=\frac{1+\tilde{g}^{(l)}}{2}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{1+\tilde{g}^{(l)}}{2}\right)^{2}-\tilde{g}^{(l)}+\nu_{l}g^{\text{obs}}}
\triangleright
Prox of conjugate data fidelity functional

\rho^{(l+1)}=\prox_{\tau_{l}\alpha\QKL(\cdot,\rho_{0})}(\rho^{(l)}-\tau_{l}T^{*}g^{l+1})
\triangleright
Proximal step for penalty functional

\beta_{l}=(1+2\mu\tau_{l})^{-\frac{1}{2}};\,\tau_{l+1}=\beta_{l}\tau_{l};\,\nu_{l+1}=\frac{\nu_{l}}{\beta_{l}};

\tilde{\rho}^{(l+1)}=\rho^{(l+1)}+\beta_{l}(\rho^{(l+1)}-\rho^{(l)})

end

for

So far we have not mentioned when to stop the algorithms.
One of the simplest of multiple different possibilities is to check the change between consecutive iterates in some norm and to stop when this change becomes smaller than a predefined threshold.
A more sophisticated approach that additionally provides an estimate of the distance between the current iterate to the exact minimizer, relies on the duality gap.

Theorem 3.6

(Duality gap)
.

Let
\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}
be the solution to problem (
4
) with full rank, then for all
\hat{\rho}\in\dom\hat{T}
and
\hat{g}\in\ran\hat{T}
we have

\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{\alpha})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(\hat{T}\hat{\rho})+\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{0})+\frac{1}{\alpha}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{g^{\text{obs}}}^{*}(-\alpha\hat{g})+\left(\widehat{\QKL}(\cdot,\hat{\rho}_{0})\right)^{*}(\hat{T}^{*}\hat{g}).

A proof of this statement, which is based on Rockafellar-Fenchel duality
[
32
,
40
]
, is included in the appendix. The rank assumption is needed to guarantee the existence of subdifferentials in line with
Lemma

3.1
.

The right hand side of this inequality, the duality gap, can be evaluated without knowing the solution
\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}
and can therefore be used to obtain an upper bound on the distance to the current iterate
\hat{\rho}
. To make this bound as tight as possible, we choose
\hat{g}\in-\frac{1}{\alpha}\partial\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{g^{\text{obs}}}(T\hat{\rho})
. We then stop the iterations if the duality gap and hence the error

\widehat{\QKL}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{\alpha})

is smaller than a predetermined threshold. The possibility to control the distance to the solution is a unique advantage of this approach compared to other iterative solution methods used in quantum tomography so far.

While both presented algorithms are numerically relatively stable, due to inaccuracies during the eigenvalue decomposition it can still happen that eigenvalues are slightly smaller than zero which leads to problems during the computation of the duality gap. For this reason we add a small offset of about
10^{-10}
to the eigenvalues before doing further computations.

Electron tomography by PINEM

In

For the reconstruction of the density matrix from the data a new method named SQUIRRELS (Spectral Quantum Interference for the Regularized Reconstruction of free ELectron States) has been proposed, which relies on Hilbert-Schmidt norm regularization and also incorporates positive semi definiteness- and trace constraints. [

]
that
T
is injective.
This means that
Theorem

2.16
applies directly and yiedls the regularizing property of quantum relative entropy regularization for this example.

For the numerical experiments we use the same general setup as described in the supplementary information of
[

4.2
Homodyne tomography

Homodyne tomography is a well established technique to reconstruct the quantum state of light by using a balanced homodyne detector. It was developed by Vogel and Risken in
[
39
]
and later demonstrated experimentally in
[
36
]
. The measured data can be viewed as the Radon transform of the Wigner function, which is related to the density matrix via the Fourier transform.
Therefore, originally methods from classical tomography for the inversion of the Radon transform, like filtered back-projection, have been used to reconstruct quantum states from experimental data
[
19
]
.
Another approach for the inversion has been the derivation of pattern functions which can be multiplied with the data and then integrated to directly obtain any element of the density matrix
[
31
]
. To guarantee physically meaningful results, it is also possible to use a fixed point algorithm based on maximum likelihood estimation
[
29
]
. Furthermore, methods from convex optimization have recently been used to incorporate the trace and positive semi-definiteness constraints
[
38
]
.

We will consider homodyne tomography with perfect detection efficiency for which the forward operator
T
can be defined by

\displaystyle(T\rho)(\theta,x)=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{m,n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n-m)\theta}u_{m}(x)u_{n}(x)\qquad\text{with }u_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sqrt{\pi}m!2^{m}}}H_{m}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}.

(5)

Here
H_{m}
denotes the
m
-th Hermite polynomial
[
31
]
. Note that the functions
u_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
and we can define
U_{\theta}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
,
e_{n}\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}u_{n}
with the standard basis

\{e_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C})
.

Using a Gelfand triple and the Dirac delta function one could then formally write

\displaystyle(T\rho)(\theta,x)=\langle\delta_{x},U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*}\delta_{x}\rangle

to get to a similar structure as we have assumed until now.
Unfortunately, the forward operator does not have the required continuity properties as the
following lemma shows:

Lemma 4.1
.

The operator
T
in (
5
) is not weak-
*
to weak-
*
sequentially continuous from

\mathcal{B}_{1}
to
L^{2}
.

Proof.

We just show this for a single fixed
\theta
, but the argument can easily be extended to an interval
of
\theta
’s. Take a bounded real-valued function

\tilde{a}\in(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\})
.
Then the multiplication operator
M_{\tilde{a}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded and self-adjoint, but not compact as it is not identically
0
. Then the operator
U_{\theta}^{*}M_{\tilde{a}}U_{\theta}
is also not compact.

For a linear functional
F
on
\mathcal{B}_{1}
weak-
*
continuity is equivalent to both weak-
*
continuity on the unit ball and the existence of an element
K\in\mathcal{K}
such that
F=\tr(K\cdot)

[
12
, Corollary 4.46]
. Therefore, the linear functional
\rho\mapsto\tr(U_{\theta}^{*}M_{\tilde{a}}U_{\theta}\rho)
cannot be weak-
*
continuous and it also cannot be weak-
*
continuous on the unit ball. As
\mathcal{K}
is separable, the unit ball of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
with the weak-
*
topology is metrizable and the functional is also not sequentially weak-
*
continuous
[
12
, Proposition 3.24]
. This implies that there exists a weak-
*
convergent sequence
(\rho^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}
converging to
\rho
such that
\tr(U_{\theta}^{*}M_{\tilde{a}}U_{\theta}\rho^{(k)})
does not converge to
\tr(U_{\theta}^{*}M_{\tilde{a}}U_{\theta}\rho)
. Now we observe

\displaystyle\tr(U_{\theta}^{*}M_{\tilde{a}}U_{\theta}\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\langle e_{m},U_{\theta}^{*}M_{\tilde{a}}U_{\theta}e_{n}\rangle\langle e_{m},\rho e_{n}\rangle=\sum_{m,n}\langle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}u_{m},M_{\tilde{a}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}u_{n}\rangle\rho_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{a}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\theta}u_{m}(x)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}u_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x\rho_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{a}(x)\sum_{m,n}\rho_{m,n}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(n-m)\theta}u_{m}(x)u_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\langle\tilde{a},(T\rho)(\theta)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

So
(T\rho^{(k)})(\theta)
does not converge weak-
*
to
(T\rho)(\theta)
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

This problem also cannot be fixed by considering a semi-discrete version and switching to a sequence space by defining
T_{\mathrm{semi}}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\rightarrow L^{2}([0,\pi),\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}))

\displaystyle(T_{\mathrm{semi}}\rho)(\theta,l):=\int_{lh}^{(l+1)h}(T\rho)(\theta,x)\mathrm{d}x

for some
h>0
.
The proof for this is essentially the same, but now instead of
\tilde{a}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
one considers for a sequence
a\in c_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
the function
\tilde{a}(x):=\sum_{l}a_{l}1_{[lh,(l+1)h)}(x)
, which also does not generate a compact multiplication operator and satisfies

\langle\tilde{a},(T\rho)(\theta)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=h\langle a,(T_{\mathrm{semi}}\rho)(\theta)\rangle_{\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})}
.

Remark 4.3
.

To the best of our knowledge, even the question whether the operator is continuous with respect to the norm topologies appears to be still open. Its relationship to the Radon transform
[
39
]
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
suggests, that on
\mathcal{B}_{2}
it is injective
[
22
]
but unbounded
with respect to
L^{2}
as codomain
[
37
]
.

One way to still apply our theory, is to use decay conditions on the matrix elements
\rho_{m,n}

and apply
Lemma

2.15
.
E.g., similar to
[
1
]
, we could assume that
\rho
belongs to a weighted
\mathcal{B}_{2}
space
with norm
\|\rho\|_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}:=\|(\rho_{m,n}\exp(B(m+n)^{r/2}))_{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}
for some
B>0
and
r\in(0,2)

and define
\dom T
as a closed ball in
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
. As
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is a separable Hilbert space,

\dom T
is weakly sequentially compact,
and from
\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}=\sup\{|\tr(\sigma\rho)|:\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 1,\,\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\}\leq\sum_{m,n}|\rho_{m,n}|
it follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is continuously embedded in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
To prove continuity of
T
in (
5
), we can proceed as in
[
1
]
and
use the fact that
T\rho
is the Radon transform of the Wigner function
W_{\rho}
which
satisfies
\|W_{\rho}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}=\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}^{2}/(2\pi)

[
1
, eq.(5)]
and
exhibits an exponential decay controlled by
\sup_{m,n}||\rho_{m,n}|\exp(B^{\prime}(m+n)^{r/2})

[
1
, Prop. 1]
.
For
B^{\prime}<B
the latter norm can be bounded by
\|\rho\|_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

In principle, a uniform hard constraint on
\|\rho\|_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}
for a sequence of data with vanishing noise level would have to be implemented numerically to guarantee convergence of the corresponding estimators, which
is numerically inconvenient.

A different approach to obtaining a well-defined operator within our framework avoiding strong a-priori assumptions
on
\rho
, is to replace the Dirac delta functions by any orthonormal basis
\{v_{l},l\in\mathbb{Z}\}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
and get
\tilde{T}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\rightarrow L^{2}([0,\pi),\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}))
by

(\tilde{T}\rho)(\theta,l)=\langle v_{l},U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*}v_{l}\rangle.

Note that this involves integration over squares of the Schwartz kernel of
U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{*}
rather than integration only over the diagonal.
To still fit the measurement data, the choice of
v_{l}
should be well localized. One could for example use an appropriately scaled Haar wavelet basis and in the end arrive at an operator which is close to
T_{\mathrm{semi}}
, particularly when passing to the fully discrete setting with sufficiently small bin size.

After these modification we can easily see, that
\tilde{T}
is weak-
*
to weak-
*
continuous by
Lemma

2.12
. The injectivity of
\tilde{T}
depends on the choice of the actual basis
v_{l}
but as we stated before also in the non-injective case we still get the results from
Theorem

2.16
at least for a subsequence.

As an example we consider the reconstruction of a Schrödinger’s cat state with
a=3
which is the superposition of the two coherent states
|a\rangle+|-a\rangle
. It is a standard example for a state that exhibits non-classical behavior
[
19
]
.
We consider a density matrix of size
21\times 21
and use
60
different phases and choose
v_{l}
to be indicator functions of the intervals
[-5+\frac{l}{12},-5+\frac{l+1}{12}]
for
0\leq l<120
such that we cover the interval
[-5,5]
which contains the relevant part of the data.
We generate Poisson distributed synthetic data using the operator
T_{\mathrm{semi}}
. The stopping rule,
\delta
and
\alpha
are then chosen in the same way as in the PINEM example. The restriction on the number of iterations is again only relevant for the last three data points with the Kullback-Leibler divergence as a data fidelity functional.
We perform the reconstruction with different data fidelity functionals and we use either
T_{\mathrm{semi}}
or
\tilde{T}
and compare the results. In

5
Conclusions

As a main theoretical result we have demonstrated the regularizing property of the quantum relative entropy functional as a penalty term in variational regularization methods.
In contrast to some competing methods, it does not depend on the choice of a basis.
We have further established all properties of the quantum relative entropy
in finite dimensional settings required for the application of algorithms from convex optimization.
All assumptions of our setting have been verified for a forward operator describing PINEM experiments,
and stability and convergence of the corresponding density matrix estimators as the noise level tends to
0
was demonstrated numerically.
For homodyne tomography, the natural exact forward operator does not fit into our framework but a suitable semi-discrete approximation still leads to partial results. In practice using the exact forward operator works well and quantum relative entropy regularization reliably reconstructs the correct solutions.

The present work suggests a number of new and potentially fruitful lines of research:
A natural question concerns statistical consistency for Poisson data as an analog to the regularizing property
for deterministic noise models. This seems to require more restrictive assumptions on the forward operator
and/or the set of possible solutions. A further potential line of research concerns rates of convergence
of quantum relative entropy penalized estimators under decay conditions on the density matrix elements as
shown for a projection estimator for homodyne tomography in
[
1
]
and for the SQUIRRELS estimator for PINEM in
[
35
]
. Furthermore, a proper functional analytic setting for homodyne tomography without
artificial constraints on the set of density matrices would be interest as well as
applications to further tomographic settings such as homodyne tomography with imperfect detection efficiency (see
[
19
, Section 5.3]
) or heterodyne tomography (see
[
30
]
).

Finally, we emphasize that positive semidefinite matrices and operators arise in many contexts beyond quantum state reconstruction, for instance as covariance operators.
The results of this paper provide a general new regularization framework for inverse problems in which the unknown is a positive semidefinite operator.

Theorem A.1

(General duality gap)
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces,
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
and let
\mathcal{R}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\mathcal{S}:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be convex functionals. Furthermore, for
\alpha>0
let

f^{\dagger}\in\underset{f}{\argmin}\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}(Tf)+\mathcal{R}(f)

such that
\partial\mathcal{R}(f^{\dagger})
and
\partial\mathcal{S}(Tf^{\dagger})
are non-empty. Then for all
f\in X
,
p\in Y^{*}
and
g\in\partial\mathcal{R}(f^{\dagger})
the inequality

\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}^{g}(f,f^{\dagger})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}(Tf)+\mathcal{R}(f)+\mathcal{R}^{*}(T^{*}p)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}^{*}(-\alpha p)

holds.

Proof.

Define the primal and dual objective functionals by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}(Tf)+\mathcal{R}(f),\qquad\mathcal{J}_{*}(p)=\mathcal{R}^{*}(T^{*}p)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}^{*}(-\alpha p)

and recall the
\mathcal{J}(f)\geq-\mathcal{J}_{*}(p)
for all
f\in X
and
p\in Y^{*}
by weak Rockafellar-Fenchel
duality, see
[
32
]
. Then for all
f,p
it holds by Young’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(f)+\mathcal{J}_{*}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}(Tf)+\mathcal{R}(f)+\mathcal{R}^{*}(T^{*}p)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}^{*}(-\alpha p)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\langle Tf,-\alpha p\rangle+\langle f,T^{*}p\rangle=0

Now assume that
f^{\dagger}
is a solution to the optimization problem. Then

0\in\partial J(f^{\dagger})=\partial\mathcal{R}(f^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{\alpha}T^{*}\partial\mathcal{S}(Tf^{\dagger})

Then by out assumptions there exists
g\in\partial\mathcal{R}(f^{\dagger})
and
q\in\partial\mathcal{S}(Tf^{\dagger})
such that
-\frac{1}{\alpha}T^{*}q=g

This implies

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}^{g}(f,f^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{q}(Tf,Tf^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f^{\dagger})-\langle g,f-f^{\dagger}\rangle+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{S}(Tf)-\mathcal{S}(Tf^{\dagger})-\langle q,Tf-Tf^{\dagger}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f^{\dagger})-\langle g,f-f^{\dagger}\rangle+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{S}(Tf)-\mathcal{S}(Tf^{\dagger})\right)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\langle T^{*}q,f-f^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(f)-\mathcal{J}(f^{\dagger}).

Now as the Bregman divergence is non-negative, we get that for all
f,p

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}^{g}(f,f^{\dagger})\leq\Delta_{\mathcal{R}}^{g}(f,f^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{q}(Tf,Tf^{\dagger})\leq\mathcal{J}(f)-\mathcal{J}(f^{\dagger})\leq\mathcal{J}(f)+\mathcal{J}_{*}(p)

using weak duality in the last inequality.
∎
