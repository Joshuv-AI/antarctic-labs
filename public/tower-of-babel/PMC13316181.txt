Subchronic low dose dioxin exposure disrupts hepatic and metabolic homeostasis in a sex- and diet-dependent manner
Authors: Oluwanifemi E Bolatimi, Yuan Hua, Gavin J Phillips, Abigail Ekuban, Paxton Brizendine-Ames, Walter H Watson, Mayukh Banerjee, Frederick A Ekuban, Banrida Wahlang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as “dioxins” have been associated with liver disease development. Population studies have highlighted sex-specific susceptibility to POP-induced liver damage, yet toxicological models looking at sex-specific toxic responses and the combined effects of dioxin mixtures remain insufficiently characterized. This study addresses a critical knowledge gap by investigating sex-dependent effects from exposure to a mixture of dioxin and dioxin-like compounds in the context of sex differences and dietary patterns. Male and female C57BL/6 J mice were fed either a low- (LFD) or high-fat diet (HFD) and administered a weekly dose of 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD-10 ng/kg), 3,4,7,8-pentachlorodibenzofuran (PeCDF-80 ng/kg) and polychlorinated biphenyl 126 (PCB126-140 ng/kg) or vehicle control. After 12 weeks, the study was terminated and samples collected for downstream analyses. LFD-fed females exposed to the dioxin mixture displayed reduced liver weight but elevated hepatic triglycerides alongside elevated circulating alanine aminotransferase levels and upregulation of the hepatic fatty acid transport gene Cd36. This observation was absent in diet-matched males. Instead, exposed LFD-fed males showed suppressed de novo lipogenic gene expression coupled with impaired glucose tolerance. Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) activation (↑Cyp1a1, Cyp1a2) was observed with exposure regardless of sex or diet, although HFD diminished AHR signaling, especially in males. Exposure also resulted in constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) activation (↑Cyp2b10, Cyp2c29), specifically in LFD-fed males. Notably, the dioxin mixture did not exacerbate HFD effects. Sex-specific toxicant responses were observed including dioxin-mediated steatotic liver phenotype in females and glucose perturbation in males, highlighting the negative impact of these chemicals on liver and metabolic outcomes even at low doses.Keywords: Dioxins, Mixture, Biological sex, Liver, Metabolic

Introduction

Environmental pollutants are increasingly recognized for their role in the development and pathophysiology of metabolic dysfunction associated-steatotic liver disease (MASLD). One category of notorious pollutants are dioxins and dioxin-like (DL) persistent organic pollutants (dioxins, furans and DL-biphenyls), which consist of various classes of polyhalogenated aromatic hydrocarbons. While production have long since been banned, these man-made chemicals still remain a pressing public health concern due to their stability, bioaccumulation, and long biological half-lives (7–11 years) (Eskenazi et al., 2018; Milbrath et al., 2009). Toxicant associated-steatotic liver disease (TASLD) is the term used to describe the development of steatotic liver disease (SLD) arising from exposure to chemical toxicants/pollutants from environmental and/or occupational sources (Wahlang et al., 2013). TASLD, similar in classification to MASLD, is one of several types of SLD which while sharing the same phenotypic progression of liver pathologies, differ by etiologies. SLD initially presents with lipid accumulation in the liver followed by infiltration of inflammatory cells and Kupffer cell activation, a hallmark of steatohepatitis (Schuster et al., 2018; Tantu et al., 2025; Wahlang et al., 2026). In the absence of early diagnosis and treatment, disease advancement to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma is inevitable.

Development and progression of liver disease associated with environmental and occupational pollutant exposures have been well-documented and shown to impact endocrine and immune functions, in addition to energy metabolism (An et al., 2025; Hammoudeh et al., 2025; Le Magueresse-Battistoni et al., 2018; Suzuki et al., 2020). Epidemiological studies have correlated dioxin exposure with insulin resistance, elevated liver enzymes, and dyslipidemia (Kim et al., 2019; Pelclová et al., 2002; Pham et al., 2022), all of which are associated with SLD progression. Exposure to environmental pollutants is known to activate hepatic transcriptional factors such as xenobiotic and nuclear receptors (Rakateli et al., 2023; Wahlang, 2023; Wang et al., 2025). These xenobiotic-sensing receptors and their downstream targets not only govern hepatic responses to environmental, dietary, and pharmacological factors but are themselves subjected to modulation by endocrine signaling (Wahlang, 2023). The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) is well characterized as being directly activated by dioxins, especially in mouse models (Becker et al., 2015; Birnbaum, 1994; Schecter et al., 2006; Sorg, 2014). AHR activation has been repeatedly reported to disrupt hepatic lipid balance as well as metabolic and endocrine signaling, thereby establishing dioxins as endocrine- and metabolic- disrupting chemicals. However, current knowledge of AHR-dependent and independent pathways, including alternative nuclear receptor mechanisms driving dioxin hepatotoxicity, remains incomplete.

Physiological differences in sexes also elicit differential hepatic responses that may either confer protection or promote SLD development or progression to advanced disease stages. It is well known that male and postmenopausal females have more readily observable signs of MASLD and worse prognosis in comparison to pre-menopausal women (Cherubini et al., 2024; Lonardo et al., 2019). Importantly, mounting evidence indicates that toxicity to dioxins and DLCs is not uniform across sexes (Eti et al., 2022; Gang et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2015; Nault et al., 2017). This variation may be attributed to cross talk between the AHR and sex hormone receptors, which may modify susceptibility and interfere with AHR-mediated xenobiotic responses. It is therefore critical to examine how sex-dependent programming of the hepatic environment shapes the liver's response to xenobiotics and underlying molecular mechanisms governing SLD outcomes.

We recently reported that acute exposure to low doses of an environmentally relevant mixture of dioxins detected in simulations of POPs emitted from domestic, military, and industrial waste incineration, specifically, TCDD, PeCDF, and PCB 126 was sufficient to disrupt hepatic energy metabolism in a sex-specific manner (Bolatimi et al., 2024). Male mice exhibited elevated gonadal adiposity and indications of dysregulation in plasma glucose homeostasis and hepatic lipid transport, while females demonstrated disruption in lipid metabolic signaling and accumulation, as well as impaired liver function, suggesting differential propensity to steatotic outcomes. These findings highlighted the sensitivity of the liver to low dose dioxin mixtures but also raised critical questions regarding the course of these early perturbations over time, their interaction with dietary factors, and their long-term pathophysiological relevance.

To address these gaps, the present study investigates the effects of 12-week subchronic exposure to the same dioxin mixture under both low-fat diet (LFD) and high-fat diet (HFD) conditions. Given that HFD consumption is an established driver of steatosis and metabolic dysfunction (Softic et al., 2016), we hypothesized that prolonged exposure within this dietary context would exacerbate SLD endpoints in a sex-dependent manner. By comparing outcomes across diet and exposure duration, we aim to define how early dioxin induced molecular disruptions progress to overt physiological and structural changes and obtain new insights into the time-dependent and sex-specific dynamics of dioxin-driven TASLD pathogenesis.

Methods

Chemicals

2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD; Catalog # D-404 N) and polychlorinated biphenyl 126 (PCB126; Catalog # C-126 N) were purchased from Accustandard (New Haven CT, USA). 3,4,7,8-pentachlorodibenzofuran (PeCDF; Catalog #38626) was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor MI, USA). Chemicals were dissolved in acetone (Catalog # AX0120, St Louis, MO, USA) prior to solubilization in corn oil (Catalog # C8267, MilliporeSigma, Milwaukee, WI, USA).

Animals, conditions and chemical exposure

The animal studies were approved by the University of Louisville Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and performed in accordance with the National Research Council Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines. Male and female C57BL/6 J mice (6–8-week-old; n = 96) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and housed in a temperature- and light- controlled room (12 h light; 12 h dark) with food and water ad libitum. Four mice were housed per cage. All mice were fed a 10.2% low-fat diet (LFD), (TD.06416, Inotiv, Lafayette, IN, USA) or a 42% high-fat diet (TD.88137) designated as HFD. Notably, this diet resembles the western diet consumption pattern. Diet composition is provided in Supplemental Table S1. At week 0, mice were randomly assigned into eight groups (n = 12 per group) based on sex, diet and exposure utilizing a 2x2x2 design. Male and female mice were administered a weekly dose of the mixture components (10 ng/kg TCDD, 140 ng/kg PCB 126 and 80 ng/kg PeCDF) in corn oil or vehicle (corn oil only) via oral gavage over the 12-week study period (Fig. 1A). The rationale for the doses employed is as previously described (Bolatimi et al., 2024). Body weight and food consumption were recorded weekly. Estrus cycle was monitored daily in female mice by vaginal cytology. Prior to euthanasia, body composition analysis was performed using the EchoMRI-500 Body Composition Analyzer (Echo Medical Systems, Houston, TX, USA). Mice were fasted for 6 h prior to euthanasia. At study termination, mice were euthanized using ketamine/xylazine (100/20 mg/kg, i.p.) and subsequent exsanguination via inferior vena cave for blood collection. Plasma and organ/tissue samples including liver were collected for downstream analysis.

Glucose tolerance test

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (IPGTT) were conducted at week 7 (mid-study) and week 12 (study termination). Mice were fasted 6 h before IPGTT was conducted, after which, baseline blood glucose was measured. Mice were then challenged with glucose (1 mg glucose/g body weight, dissolved in sterile saline) via intraperitoneal injection. Blood samples were obtained from the tail vein and blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 15-, 30-, 60-, 90-, and 120-min post glucose challenge using a hand-held glucometer (Contour Next EZ, Parsippany, NJ, USA). Blood glucose values were plotted over time and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the trapezoidal rule (Allison et al., 1995).

Tissue histology

Liver sections were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 48 h before transfer into 75% alcohol, and subsequently processed through a graded ethanol dehydration series, citrisolv clearance, and paraffin embedding. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were and sectioned at 5 μm using a Leica Biosystem's Histocore Autocut Automated Rotary Microtome (Leica Biosystem; Deer Park, IL, USA) for routine histological examination. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was conducted to assess hepatic morphology and degree of steatosis using the Epredia™ Gemini™ AS Automated Slide Stainer (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Photomicrographic images at 10×, 20× and 40× magnification were captured on the Olympus DP74 digital camera fitted on Olympus BX43 microscope (Olympus America, Breinigsville, PA, USA) via the cellSens Standard XV image processing software.

Plasma analyte measurements and homeostasis model assessments

Plasma alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), triglycerides, cholesterol, glucose, high-, low- and very low-density lipoproteins (HDL, LDL, VLDL) levels were measured using Lipid Panel Plus disks (catalog:400-0030; Abaxis Inc.; Union City, CA, USA) on the Piccolo Xpress Chemistry Analyzer (Abbott; Abbott Park, IL, USA). Plasma cytokines and chemokines were measured using the Meso Scale Discovery Proinflammatory and cytokine panels 1 (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD) per the manufacturer's instructions. Plasma insulin and leptin values were also measured using the Meso Scale Discovery Mouse metabolic panel. Using IPGTT fasting blood glucose values and insulin values, insulin resistance and beta cell function were assessed. Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) and Homeostasis Model Assessment of Beta Cell Function (HOMA-β) were calculated as previously described (Matthews et al., 1985). The quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) was also calculated using fasting blood glucose and plasma insulin concentrations (Katz et al., 2000).

Hepatic lipid analysis

Frozen liver tissues (70–100 mg) were homogenized in 50 mM NaCl solution. Hepatic lipids were extracted using chloroform and methanol in a 2:1 ratio according to the Bligh and Dyer method (Bligh and Dyer, 1959). The lower chloroform layers were collected, and an appropriate amount of extraction was dried. Triglyceride and cholesterol content were measured by colorimetric assays using commercial kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reagents used in the assays were: Infinity Triglycerides Reagent (TR22421, ThermoFisher Scientific, Middletown, VA, USA), Infinity Cholesterol Reagent (TR13421, ThermoFisher Scientific, Middletown, VA, USA), and Triglyceride and Cholesterol Standards (catalog: T7531-STD, C7509-STD; Point Scientific; Canton, MI). Extracted lipids were quantified using a microplate absorbance reader (BioTek Gen 5; Winooski, VT, USA).

Real-time RT-PCR

Liver tissues were suspended in RNA-STAT 60TM (catalog: CS502; Tel-test Inc.; Friendswood, TX) and homogenized with 0.5 mm glass silica beads using a Mini-Beadbeater 16 bead mill homogenizer (Biospec Products, Bartlesville, OK, USA) for 30s. Total RNA was extracted, precipitated, and washed following the RNA-STAT 60 reagent protocol for the isolation of total RNA, DNA, and protein by AMSBIO. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using Nanodrop OneC (Thermo Scientific, catalog: 701-058112; Madison, MI, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Quantabio; Catalog: 95048-500, Beverly, MA, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. RT-PCR was performed on a CFX384 TM Real-Time System (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA) using iTaq Universal Probes Supermix and Taqman probes (Supplemental Table 1) purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Several reference genes (GAPDH, ACTB, 18 s, PPIA) were tested and their ΔCt were run through the GeNorm software algorithm to identify the most stable housekeeping gene for our tissue of interest. Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was determined as the most stable and the levels of mRNA were normalized relative to the levels of GAPDH mRNA (catalog:4352339E, ThermoFisher Scientific; Madison, MI, USA) and mean expression levels in unexposed, male mice were set at ∼1.

Western blotting

Liver tissue was homogenized in commercial RIPA buffer, containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Madison, MI). The homogenates, which contain both fat-soluble and membrane-bound proteins, were then centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the total protein fraction. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23225). Protein samples (30 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE using Any kD™ Criterion™ TGX Stain-Free™ precast gels (Bio-Rad, Cat# 5678125) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Sigma, Cat# IPVH00010). Membranes were probed with primary antibodies against CYP1A1 (1:2000, ab79819, Abcam), CYP1A2 (1:2000, 14719S, Cell Signaling), CYP2B10 (1:2000, AB9916, Sigma), CD36 (1:2000, ab133625, Abcam), FOXA1 (1:2000, ab170933, Abcam) and AHR (1:1000, MA1-514, Invitrogen), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated Goat anti-rabbit (1:8000, Jackson ImmunoResearch) or Goat anti-mouse (1:8000, 31430, Invitrogen) secondary antibody. Signals were visualized using Clarity Max™ Western ECL substrate, and images were captured with the ChemiDoc™ imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software, version 6.0.1 (Bio-Rad) and normalized to β-ACTIN (1:10000, A5441, Sigma).

RNA-seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from frozen liver tissue samples (n = 6 through random selection), using the RNeasy plus mini kit (catalog: 74134; Qiagen; Germantown, MD) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quantity and integrity were measured using the NanoDropTM 2000 spectrophotometer (catalog: 701-058112); Qubit (ThermoFisher) and Agilent TapeStation (Agilent Technologies). Samples with RIN ≥ 8 were used for poly(A) enrichment. mRNA was enriched by poly(A) selection and premade libraries were used (Azenta Life Sciences, Burlington, MA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 flow cell using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration generating ∼20 million read pairs per sample. Raw FASTQ files were generated and raw data quality evaluated with FastQC. Sequence reads were trimmed to remove possible adapter sequences and nucleotides with poor quality using Trimmomatic v.0.36. The trimmed reads (Supplemental Table S2) were mapped to the Mus musculus GRCm38 reference genome available on ENSEMBL using the STAR aligner v.2.5.2b. Unique gene counts were calculated by using featureCounts from the Subread package v.1.5.2. Differential gene expression analysis was performed in DESeq2 (R Bioconductor package) using the Wald test to calculate log2 fold changes and p-values. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with cut-offs set at p ≤ 0.05, q ≤ 0.05 and fold change in either direction. These cut-off values were selected to retain sensitivity to dioxin-associated transcriptional changes in the context of a large background of DEGs driven by basal sex differences and HFD feeding. Furthermore, because the focus of the study in on sex- and diet-dependent dioxin effects, this approach prioritizes detection of biologically relevant dioxin-induced transcriptional responses. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of significant genes was conducted using GeneSCF v1.1-p2 with the MGI GO annotation database to identify overrepresented biological processes. Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) was also performed to identify canonical pathways representative of the DEGs observed for the male vs. female comparisons. Activated pathways were defined as −log(p-value) >1.3 and z-score > 1, while inhibited pathways were defined as those with −log(p-value) >1.3 and z-score < −1. The RNA-seq data from the current study have been deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE327003 and will be made publicly available at manuscript acceptance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted and graphs plotted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1) for Windows (GraphPad Software Inc.; La Jolla, CA, USA). Two-, Three-way ANOVA or Kruskal Wallis statistical tests were performed to determine factor effects of sex, diet, exposure and interaction effects (p < 0.05). Multiple comparison tests were also conducted with Holm Sidak correction for subgroup comparisons within each sex (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). All data are expressed as mean ± SD, and p-values provided in the respective Figure/Table with significant p-values highlighted by red font and bolded.

Results

Body composition

Periodic tracking of animal body weight (Fig. 1B&C) and body composition during the study revealed distinct sex and diet driven effects (Supplemental Fig. S1). Male mice showed notable HFD-induced body weight gain relative to females. As expected, HFD feeding increased total body fat and gonadal white adipose tissue (WAT) weights while reducing lean tissue mass in both sexes (Supplemental Fig. S1). By the end of the study, LFD-fed dioxin exposed female mice showed significantly increased fat mass compared to their controls (Fig. 1D). In addition to WAT, brown adipose and other additional organ weights were also measured (Supplemental Fig. S2). While no exposure effects were observed for these organ weights, HFD feeding selectively decreased spleen mass in males, an effect that was absent in females. Overall, effects observed in terms of body weight and composition parameters were primarily diet-driven in both sexes with exposure effects noted only with regards to increased fat mass in LFD-fed females.

Liver steatosis, function and injury markers

To determine the degree of steatosis resulting from diet and/or exposure to the dioxin mixture, histological analysis was conducted on liver sections (H&E staining) which demonstrated sex-specific patterns and degrees of hepatic steatosis. As expected, HFD-feeding resulted in steatosis (Fig. 2A). HFD-fed males especially developed markedly exacerbated micro- and macro-vesicular steatosis compared to HFD-fed females. These histological findings were corroborated by quantitative measurements of hepatic triglycerides which were significantly elevated in HFD-males, confirming a sex-diet interaction (Fig. 2B). Intriguingly, LFD-fed exposed females exhibited a significant increase in hepatic triglyceride levels, highlighting a female-specific susceptibility under basal dietary conditions, and this effect was notably absent in males as well as HFD-fed females (Fig. 2B). Plasma triglyceride measurements also underscored sex-diet effects as HFD elevated plasma triglycerides in females but not males (Table 1). Additionally, females consistently displayed higher hepatic (Supplemental Fig. S3A), but lower circulating cholesterol levels (Table 1) compared to males, regardless of diet. As expected, HFD increased both plasma and hepatic cholesterol in both sexes. Assessment of circulating levels of lipoproteins as an indication of dyslipidemia showed females having significantly lower HDL, LDL and vLDL levels compared to males (Table 1). Quantitative analysis of liver-to-body ratios showed sex differences under HFD feeding. A sex-diet interaction was observed as HFD-fed males exhibited increased liver-to-body weight ratios compared to their sex- and diet-matched controls (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, both sex-exp and diet-exp interactions were evident in the LFD-fed female group, wherein exposure to the dioxin mixture unexpectedly decreased liver-to-body weight ratio in LFD-fed females, an effect that was absent in their sex- and diet-matched counterparts.

Circulating liver enzymes levels, namely ALT and AST, were also measured. Systemic levels of ALT and AST also showed clear sex bias with males exhibiting higher levels compared to females, while both enzymes were increased with HFD feeding in both sexes (Fig. 2C&D). Importantly, LFD-fed, dioxin-exposed females displayed increased ALT levels compared to their sex- and diet- matched controls (Fig. 2C). Further, liver gene expression of hepatic function markers namely Alb, Hnf4α, and Foxa1 were assessed (Supplemental Fig. S3B—D). Dioxin exposure had no direct effect on transcription of these hepatic function markers. Hepatic expression and plasma concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines were also examined. Females exhibited higher mRNA levels of interleukin-6 (Il-6) than their sex-matched counterparts (Supplemental Fig. S3E). Contrary to hepatic Il-6 gene expression, circulating levels of IL-6 were higher in males than females, and were elevated with HFD feeding in both sexes (Table 2). Similarly, hepatic expression of Tnfα was largely unchanged, but plasma TNFα was significantly elevated in HFD-fed mice of both sexes, with higher absolute levels in males (Supplemental Fig. S3F). Circulating levels of interferon gamma (INF-γ) were also generally higher in males vs. females and was decreased by HFD feeding in the male group (Table 2). LFD-fed exposed males notably exhibited increased plasma concentrations of INF-γ which was not present in their sex- and diet matched counterparts (sex-diet and diet-exposure interactions). Albeit sex and diet effects, no dioxin effects were noted for other additional inflammatory markers measured in the study.

Sex-and diet-dependent dioxin effects on glucose metabolism

Glucose tolerance tests were performed at the mid-study period (week 7) and prior to study termination (week 12). Blood glucose levels over the two-hour time course were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) calculated and normalized to lean tissue mass. At week 7, a diet-exposure interaction was observed wherein LFD-fed male mice showed dioxin-mediated increase in AUC compared to their unexposed counterparts (Fig. 3A-C). By the end of study however, HFD-driven impairment of glucose uptake was observed in both male and female groups (Fig. 3D-F). A sex-diet-exposure effect was also noted in which LFD-fed, exposed males displayed resistance to glucose uptake (increased AUC) while their female counterparts showed the opposite (Fig. 3F). Conversely, HFD-fed, exposed females displayed heightened resistance to glucose uptake while their male counterparts showed relatively better glucose handling (decreased AUC). Sex differences were noted for baseline glucose levels at the end of study period but not at the mid-study time point (Supplemental Fig. S4A&B). HFD feeding increased the baseline glucose in males but not in females at the mid-study time point (sex-diet interaction), while HFD effects were present in both males and females by the end of the study. Plasma glucose levels at euthanasia closely reflected baseline glucose level trends similar to the end of study GTT levels (Supplemental Fig. S4C). Homeostasis model assessments, namely HOMA-IR and HOMA-β, were calculated using fasted glucose and insulin values to determine insulin resistance and beta cell secretory function. As previously observed (Bolatimi et al., 2024), male mice generally displayed higher levels of HOMA-IR in comparison to females (Fig. 3G). Further increase was driven by HFD feeding. Similarly, HOMA-β scores were also elevated in HFD-fed male and female mice (Fig. 3H). QUICKI-derived estimates of insulin sensitivity were higher in females vs. males but were decreased by HFD feeding with a more pronounced reduction in the male group (Fig. 3I). In addition, plasma insulin and leptin levels were elevated only with HFD feeding, predominantly in males (sex-diet interaction, Supplemental Fig. S4D&E). At the molecular level, Gck, the gene encoding glucokinase, a sensor for high glucose levels, was increased by HFD feeding only in females (Supplemental Fig. S4F). Expression of G6pc, a gene encoding glucose-6-phosphatase, the enzyme involved in the final step of gluconeogenesis was unchanged by sex or diet but exhibited a diet-exposure effect suggesting dioxin-mediated upregulation of this target in the LFD groups (Supplemental Fig. S4G). Likewise, expression of the insulin receptor substrate 1 gene (Irs1), which encodes a cytoplasmic adaptor protein that is critical to the glucose uptake and metabolism, exhibited a diet-exposure interaction, indicating the likelihood of altered insulin signaling particularly in exposed LFD-fed groups (Supplemental Fig. S4H).

Alterations on lipid homeostasis at the molecular level

To investigate molecular perturbations driving diet- and exposure- induced changes to hepatic lipid levels, hepatic expression of lipid metabolism genes was assessed. Genes involved in de novo lipogenesis, specifically, acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (Acaca), fatty acid synthase (Fasn) and stearoyl-coenzyme A desaturase 1 (Scd1) all showed interaction effects including increased expression of Acaca in LFD-fed females (Fig. 4A) but suppression with HFD feeding (sex-diet). Notably, a sex-diet-exp interaction was also observed and driven by downregulated Acaca expression in LFD-fed, exposed males but increased Acaca expression in LFD-fed, exposed females. Likewise, interaction effects were observed for hepatic expression of Fasn and Scd1, which were both selectively downregulated with dioxin exposure in the LFD-fed males but not females (Fig. 4B&C). Hepatic expression of genes encoding proteins involved in triglyceride synthesis (Mogat1, Dgat2), lipolysis (Pnpla2), and transcriptional regulation (Srebf1, Chrebp), were also analyzed (Supplemental Fig. S5). HFD-fed males displayed higher expression of Mogat1 vs. females, while Dgat2 expression was decreased with HFD feeding in males but paradoxically increased in HFD-fed females (sex-diet). Hepatic Pnpla2 expression was increased with HFD feeding in both sexes while hepatic Srebf1 expression was unchanged across all the groups. Further, HFD-driven increase in Chrebp expression was observed in males only (sex-diet). Regarding lipid binding and uptake, HFD-feeding upregulated Cd36 in both sexes but selectively decreased Fabp1 expression in males (Fig. 4D&E). Females showed higher hepatic expression of Cpt1a, the gene encoding the rate-limiting enzyme in fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 4F). Notably, dioxin exposure downregulated Cpt1a expression levels in LFD-fed males (diet-exp). Importantly, while Cd36 expression was generally higher in females vs. males, it was further upregulated by dioxin exposure in LFD-fed females. The same sex- and diet- effects were also reflected at the protein level as demonstrated by western blot analysis of CD36 alongside a non-significant trend for increased CD36 in LFD-fed, exposed females (Fig. 4G-H).

Dioxin effects on xenobiotic and sex hormone receptors

As anticipated, dioxin exposure strongly activated canonical xenobiotic receptor signaling. Gene expression for Ahr, which encodes the AHR, the xenobiotic receptor most directly associated with dioxins and dioxin-like compounds, was higher in females and further increased with HFD feeding in females vs. males (Fig. 5A). AHR activation was confirmed by induction of canonical AHR target genes namely Cyp1a1 and Cyp1a2 which were upregulated by exposure to the dioxin mixture regardless of sex and diet (Fig. 5B&C). HFD feeding appeared to dampen Cyp1a1 induction as indicated by a diet-exposure interaction (Fig. 5B), while Cyp1a2 exhibited opposing sex-specific response with HFD-driven suppression in males but upregulation in females (Fig. 5C). Hepatic expression of another AHR target, namely Fmo3, showed a sex-specific exposure effect (sex-exposure interaction) as evident by increased Fmo3 expression in exposed females (Supplemental Fig. S6A). Hepatic expression of Cyp2b10, a classic CAR target gene, was generally lower in males vs. females but was increased with HFD feeding only in males (Fig. 5D). Surprisingly, LFD-fed, exposed males showed liver Cyp2b10 induction vs. their control group. This exposure-driven induction was also mirrored by another CAR target gene, namely Cyp2c29 (Fig. 5E). Curiously, dioxin effects were more prominent in females at the protein level, with seemingly decreased expression in LFD fed females, but an opposing increase in HFD- fed exposed females. Further, females also exhibited higher hepatic Cyp3a11 expression than males, while HFD feeding appeared to increase Cyp3a11 mRNA levels in both sexes (Fig. 5F). Consistent with the mRNA data, western blot analysis demonstrated significantly higher protein expression levels of CYP1A1 in all exposed groups (Fig. 5G&H). However. western blot analyses of CYP2B10 did not show significant induction at the protein level (Fig. 5I&J). Finally, given the role of dioxins as endocrine disruptors, sex hormone receptor expression was examined. As expected, Esr1, which encodes estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), was highly expressed in females vs. males and was further increased by HFD (Supplemental Fig. S6B).

Liver RNA sequencing

DESeq2 was used to identify the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the liver between the male and female groups on either LFD or HFD, as well as between unexposed (control) vs. dioxin-exposed groups. Both sex and diet had massive effects on the hepatic transcriptome (Table 3) with 3153 genes differentially expressed in the LFD-fed female control group and 2050 genes differentially expressed in the HFD-fed female control group vs. their respective male counterparts. Likewise, in each sex, HFD feeding led to pronounced changes in hepatic gene expression vs. LFD feeding (4083 for males and 4225 for females). Dioxin exposure resulted in 2696 DEGs in the male vs. female LFD-fed groups and 2749 DEGs between HFD-fed males vs. female HFD-fed groups. Examination by exposure revealed that dioxins altered the hepatic transcriptome to a greater extent in females when on LFD feeding (40 DEGs identified in the female LFD-fed control vs. exposed group and 12 DEGs identified in the male LFD-fed control vs. exposed group). In contrast, dioxins appeared to have a greater impact on the hepatic transcriptome in males when on HFD feeding as seen by 87 DEGs identified in HFD-fed male control vs. exposed while only 4 DEGs were identified in the female comparison group (Fig. 6A&B and Supplemental Table S3-S6).

To further identify biological processes significantly affected by dioxin exposure in livers of male and female LFD- and HFD-fed mice, GO enrichment analyses were performed on significant DEGs. Expectedly, dioxin exposure mostly affected genes involved in several processes critical to xenobiotic and drug metabolism; particularly pathways in dibenzo-p-dioxin, heterocycle, toxin and steroid metabolic processes, as well as hydrogen peroxide biosynthetic and oxidative de-ethylation processes (Fig. 6F). Select processes were altered in LFD-fed control vs. exposed female groups, such as regulation of protein SUMOylating, while processes involved in regulation of T-cell cytokine production and pigment cell differentiation and maturation were observed in HFD-fed control vs. exposed males (Supplemental Fig. S7 & S8). Furthermore, IPA analysis was performed to identify how the altered hepatic transcriptome impact key pathways and their directionality. While sex differences were apparent for numerous canonical pathways identified including “xenobiotic metabolism”, HFD feeding also interacted with sex to impact several pathways including “HIF1a signaling” and “hepatic fibrosis signaling” (Fig. 7A&B and Supplemental Excel Table 1). Intriguingly, AHR signaling caused by dioxin exposure was both sex- and diet-specific with males exhibiting higher AHR signaling on LFD while females displayed higher AHR signaling on HFD. Similarly, diet and sex impacted exposure responses to cytokine signaling with IL-6 and IL-8 signaling pathways upregulated in exposed females in the LFD group but downregulated in the exposed female group with HFD feeding.

Discussion

The current study was designed to extensively characterize translationally applicable interactions between sex, diet, and environmentally relevant low-dose dioxin mixture exposure on liver health and metabolic dysfunction in a subchronic model. The present 12-week study builds upon our prior acute exposure model, in which male and female mice were exposed to the same environmentally relevant low-dose dioxin mixture for 2 weeks (Bolatimi et al., 2024). Our present goal was to investigate how early phenotypic and biochemical hepatic and metabolic dysregulation induced by dioxin exposure were intensified, unchanged or resolved with physiological adaptation afforded by prolonged exposure and nutritional status. Given that dioxins have and continue to affect multiple communities in the US, the study is significant and relevant and contributes to the body of evidence on dioxin toxicity while attempting to fill knowledge gaps on sex-specific dioxin effects with the consideration of a western diet lifestyle.

In this study, we demonstrated that prolonged low-dose exposure to a defined dioxin mixture interacts with sex and diet to differentially influence hepatic and systemic outcomes relevant to MASLD progression including steatosis, dyslipidemia, glucose metabolism, and inflammation (Fig. 7C). HFD feeding itself was the primary driver of weight gain, adiposity, and steatosis, and these effects were consistently more pronounced in male mice than females. The observed diet effects based on biological sex are consistent with our prior reports pertinent to HFD-driven MASLD progression in males (Wahlang et al., 2020; Wahlang et al., 2026), while females maintain a degree of protection that is lost upon attenuation of estrogen signaling (Lonardo et al., 2019; Palmisano et al., 2018; Shaheen et al., 2021; Wahlang, 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). This sexual dimorphism is widely attributed to protective actions of ERα signaling, which enhances fatty acid oxidation and suppresses de novo lipogenesis (Della Torre et al., 2016; Palmisano et al., 2017). Our data are consistent with this paradigm, as females expressed higher Cpt1a (β-oxidation) and showed greater resilience to HFD-induced lipid accumulation. However, the striking finding that LFD-fed, dioxin-exposed females accumulated hepatic triglycerides, exhibited increased WAT weights, and had elevated ALT levels, compared to their unexposed counterparts, indicates that low-dose dioxin exposure alone is sufficient to impair lipid handling and instigate metabolic dysfunction in females under basal nutritional conditions. Mechanistically, this was accompanied by altered expression of Cd36, whose expression is naturally low in hepatocytes but significantly elevated with HFD feeding (Liu et al., 2018; Wilson et al., 2016). Yet female mice already exhibited higher CD36 gene and protein expression levels, suggesting that females are already naturally primed for increased hepatic fatty acid uptake. Dioxin exposure in LFD-fed females increased Cd36 abundance levels, consistent with reports that AHR activation promotes hepatic lipid uptake (Angrish et al., 2012; He et al., 2011; Lee et al., 2010). In contrast, males exhibited decreased de novo lipogenic gene expression (Fasn, Scd1) and increased fatty acid breakdown (Cpt1a). Together, these results implicate that females, despite lower HFD-driven steatosis, are uniquely sensitive to environmental AHR ligands under non-obesogenic dietary conditions with regards to steatosis and liver injury. This indicates that perhaps these low doses of exposures can still pose harm and impact adverse outcome pathways independent of HFD.

AHR activation is well-established to promote liver hypertrophy through several processes (Hall et al., 2012; Liu, 2024; Vondráček and Machala, 2016), such as, robust induction of xenobiotic metabolizing enzymes which leads to expansion of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, thus increasing hepatocyte cytoplasmic volume. Additionally, AHR-driven reprogramming of lipid metabolism can consequentially result in lipid accumulation and steatosis, which further increases hepatocyte size and liver mass. These mechanisms partially explain why AHR activation classically yields hepatomegaly and increased liver-to-body weight ratio in both rodent and human studies (Kopec et al., 2013; Mitchell et al., 2010). Curiously, LFD-fed exposed females showed a reduction in liver-to-body weight ratio despite increases in hepatic triglycerides and ALT, suggesting a deviation from the canonical hepatomegaly response (Vacca et al., 2024). This paradox suggests that perhaps, in females under non-obesogenic conditions, dioxin exposure induces more subtle hepatocellular remodeling characterized by metabolic dysfunction without gross enlargement.

HFD feeding increased circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, with males exhibiting higher systemic levels despite females showing higher hepatic mRNA expression. RNA-seq analysis also demonstrated reduced activation of inflammatory signaling pathways in HFD-fed females compared with males, consistent with our prior phenotypic findings that females subjected to 12 weeks of HFD feeding exhibit a milder MASLD phenotype than males. This contrast between hepatic transcription and circulating cytokines may reflect sex differences in hepatic cytokine production, retention and export (Dunn et al., 2024). The misalignment between hepatic cytokine gene expression and circulating inflammatory markers also suggest that in addition to plausible liver-centric transcription or post translational modifications, perhaps the inflammatory signaling induced by HFD is not liver-driven but could originate elsewhere and points to other potential target organs. Dioxin exposure specifically increased plasma IFN-γ only in LFD-fed males. Given the AHR's established role in shaping immune cell differentiation and cytokine production (Esser and Rannug, 2015), these findings suggest that low-dose dioxin mixtures only subtly skew systemic immune responses.

The modulation of glucose tolerance by diet, sex, and exposure imply another axis of metabolic disruption. HFD predictably impaired glucose handling in both sexes, but dioxin exposure introduced nuanced effects. At the mid-study period, LFD-fed exposed male mice had increased AUC, indicating impaired glucose tolerance in this sex with dioxin exposure, and this effect was retained till the end of the study period. With females however, dioxin-mediated glucose intolerance was only seen at the end of the study period with HFD feeding. This divergence suggests that dioxins may modulate insulin signaling pathways differently in males and females, in part, through interactions with diet. Upregulation of G6pc and altered Irs1 liver expression in dioxin-exposed groups also support dysregulation of gluconeogenesis and insulin signaling pathways, consistent with previous studies linking dioxin exposure to insulin resistance and diabetes risk (Hectors et al., 2011; Novelli et al., 2005). Together, these effects paralleled epidemiological findings associating exposure to dioxins and dioxin-like compounds with increased type 2 diabetes prevalence (De Tata, 2014; Gang et al., 2021).

The consistent induction of canonical AHR targets (Cyp1a1, Cyp1a2) across sexes and dietary groups, as demonstrated by both RT-PCR and RNA-seq analyses, confirms bioactivity of the dioxin mixture and supports the established role of AHR-mediated dioxin toxicity. Sex-specific AHR signaling was observed with IPA analysis with males showing a more pronounced signaling than females on LFD feeding, while HFD modified this pattern. It is worth noting that, independent of dietary confounders, sex differences in AHR target gene expression were also previously reported with TCDD exposure and attributed partly to microRNA modulation (Hammoudeh et al., 2023). Intriguingly, in the current study, HFD dampened Cyp1a1 induction, highlighting nutrient-toxicant interactions wherein dietary lipid load may compromise AHR-driven transcription. In contrast, Cyp1a2 showed sex-specific modulation by HFD, suggesting the possibility of differential AHR co-regulator availability in males versus females among other mechanisms. Nonetheless, these observations suggest that lipid-induced ER stress and inflammatory signaling may repress xenobiotic receptor activity, in concordance with prior reports (Sadler et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019). Cross-activation of CAR (Cyp2b10, Cyp2c29) and PXR (Cyp3a11) further underscores the complexity of receptor crosstalk. Our findings that only LFD-fed exposed males showed strong Cyp2b10 induction is novel, suggesting that under basal nutritional states, CAR may compensate for or synergize with AHR activation to influence phenotypic outcomes and in part, contribute to the sex-specific outcomes. Importantly, sex-dependent changes in Cyp3a11 also implicate sex hormones as potential modifiers of xenobiotic receptor crosstalk. Together, these results highlight that perhaps alleviated AHR signaling in the HFD groups corresponded to the lack of exacerbation of HFD-driven metabolic and hepatic changes observed, while also pinpointing that other non-AHR signaling networks may be at play driving dioxin-mediated liver and metabolic endpoints in females under LFD feeding.

Contrary to previous reports that AHR activation worsens HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction, we observed minimal metabolic changes in dioxin-exposed male and female HFD-fed mice vs. HFD-fed mice only. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, our study employed substantially lower exposure levels (ng/kg) than prior studies. For example, one study reported increased body weight and adiposity following 1 μg/kg TCDD exposure over 32 weeks (Brulport et al., 2017), a dose and duration far exceeding those used here. Differences in exposure timing may also contribute, as many studies initiate TCDD or PCB126 exposure during prenatal or early postnatal development (Hoyeck et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2022), thereby altering metabolic programming before HFD exposure and predisposing animals to worsened obesity and glucose dysregulation later in life. In contrast, our study examined exposure beginning in early adulthood. Additionally, we utilized a mixture of potent AHR agonists, which may exhibit toxicodynamic properties distinct from those of single high-affinity compounds. It is also possible that the HFD alone induced near-maximal metabolic and hepatic dysfunction, masking any subtle additive effects of low-dose dioxin exposure. Finally, many studies demonstrating protection from HFD-induced obesity rely on AHR deficiency or antagonism models (Kerley-Hamilton et al., 2012; Moyer et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2015), indicating that endogenous AHR signaling contributes to diet-induced metabolic dysfunction. In this context, exposure to a stable exogenous dioxin mixture may not further exacerbate an already saturated HFD-driven phenotype.

Beyond inducing chronic metabolic stress, the HFD may have attenuated canonical AHR signaling, as evidenced by reduced Cyp1a1 induction. AHR activity is regulated by both exogenous ligands, such as dioxins, and endogenous ligands derived from diet and gut microbiota metabolites (Coumoul et al., 2026; Han et al., 2021). The purified obesogenic diet used in this study enriched in saturated fat and simple carbohydrates, lacks detectable phytochemicals, indoles, and flavonoids, that may serve as precursors for endogenous AHR ligands. Importantly, the diet also lacks known AHR antagonists, making ligand competition an unlikely explanation for the limited dioxin-induced effects observed in HFD-fed mice. Instead, the data suggest that HFD-driven metabolic dysfunction may impair hepatic AHR signaling by reducing receptor expression or limiting the availability of downstream cofactors and transcriptional partners. For instance, chronic nutrient excess activates multiple metabolic nuclear receptors, including PPARs, LXR, and FXR, which may compete for shared co-activators (Torre-Villalvazo et al., 2026). Nonetheless, the current HFD findings are novel and longer-term studies looking at these diet-chemical interactions are warranted.

A major strength of this study is its integration of sex, diet, and environmentally relevant dioxin mixture exposure in a subchronic model, addressing a critical gap in the literature where most studies have examined single compounds, acute exposures, or male-only cohorts. By demonstrating that low dose exposure can unmask sex-specific hepatic susceptibility under LFD conditions, this work provides novel insight into how background environmental exposures may contribute to metabolic disease risk. Limitations include the focus on gene expression endpoints without direct measurement of protein activity, lack of fibrosis assessment, and the absence of gut microbiome analysis, which is increasingly recognized as a mediator of diet-toxicant interactions. Future studies should extend the timeline to capture fibrosis progression, evaluate mitochondrial and oxidative stress pathways, and include proteomics and lipidomics platforms to further dissect sex-specific mechanisms. Finally, translational studies in human cohorts with well-characterized dietary and exposure histories, and utilization of humanized AHR models are needed to validate the sex- and diet-dependent risk patterns identified here. Such efforts would advance precision environmental health approaches, identify vulnerable populations and inform on intervention strategies to reduce environmentally-driven liver disease risk.

Overall, the present 12-week study builds upon our prior acute exposure work, in which male and female mice were exposed to the same environmentally relevant dioxin mixture for 2 weeks (Bolatimi et al., 2024). In the acute study, we observed early dysregulation in hepatic energy metabolism characterized by induction of xenobiotic metabolism and lipid regulatory genes, accompanied by initial signs of steatosis with females showing stronger AHR signaling. By contrast, in the current 12-week subchronic study, both sex and diet played key contributory role towards differences observed in metabolic and steatotic outcomes. This was also mirrored by the array of hepatic gene expression changes in sex and diet comparisons observed with the RNA-seq analysis. Surprisingly, dioxin-mediated steatotic and metabolic effects, although subdued compared to sex and diet effects, were observed largely in the LFD groups. Dioxins did not exacerbate HFD-driven adiposity and steatosis, suggesting that unlike other EDCs, dioxin mixtures are not obesogens. Sex-specific dioxin effects were also echoed by the higher number of DEGs in dioxin-exposed females in the LFD group, while in the HFD group, exposed males exhibited greater alterations in hepatic gene expression. These findings support the evidence that both biological sex and diet modulate dioxin effects on the liver transcriptomic landscape. In closing, the data from this study highlights how a mixture of dioxin and dioxin-like compounds produced selective and sex-specific effects with LFD-fed females showcasing a steatotic phenotype and males a more diabetic-prone phenotype. Furthermore, exposure to the mixture modulated hepatic expression of lipogenic and β-oxidation genes, and circulating cytokine and chemokine profiles in a sex- and diet-dependent manner, pointing to complex interactions between diet, sex, and environmental toxicant exposure. Finally, these metabolic perturbations were evident even at low levels of these dioxin and dioxin-like compounds, and sex-specific susceptibility to AHR activation shifted with duration of exposure and was dependent on diet. This work advances the field by confirming the continued harmful effects of these chemicals at current exposure levels and highlighting the importance of considering exposure duration and dietary status.
