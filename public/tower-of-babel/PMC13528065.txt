ELF-3 and TSC-1 as potential markers of recurrence in low-risk non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Enes Altın, Çağrı Doğan, Ayşegül İsal Arslan, Erdem Can Topkaç, Cenk Murat Yazıcı, Rıdvan Özcan, Hacı Murat Akgül, Mehmet Fatih Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTumor recurrence is a major clinical challenge in low-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). Molecular studies have identified two main subtypes with distinct genetic pathways: papillary, low-grade tumors, often with FGFR3 mutations and favorable outcomes, and nonpapillary, high-grade tumors, which are linked to higher rates of progression and muscle invasion. This study evaluates the prognostic value of immunohistochemical (IHC) markers— cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44), cytokeratin 5/6 (CK5/6), cytokeratin 20 (CK20), GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (Cerb-B2), E74-like factor 3 (ELF-3), and tuberous sclerosis complex 1 (TSC-1)—for recurrence in patients with low-risk NMIBC.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study evaluated 40 patients with NMIBC selected from 320 individuals who underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumors (TUR-BT) between 2011 and 2019. Patients were categorized into two groups: recurrence (n = 20), defined as tumor recurrence following TUR-BT, and nonrecurrence (n = 20), those with no recurrence during follow-up. Tissues were reevaluated and stained for the following markers using IHC methods: CK5/6, CK20, CD44, GATA3, Cerb-B2, ELF-3, and TSC-1. Statistical analysis was performed to assess associations between clinicopathological variables and recurrence.ResultsRecurrence was significantly correlated with tumor number (p = 0.036), whereas age, sex, smoking status, and tumor size showed no significant association (p > 0.05). CD44 positivity was predominantly observed in nonrecurrent cases (p = 0.022), whereas TSC-1 positivity was strongly associated with recurrence (p = 0.003). ELF-3 expression was uniformly positive in all cases and did not predict recurrence. Multivariate regression revealed that CD44 positivity reduced the recurrence risk (OR = 0.142, 95% CI: [0.028–0.714]), while TSC-1 positivity increased it more than 21-fold (OR = 21.871, 95% CI: [2.125–115.15]). Tumors located at the ureteral orifice were associated with increased recurrence risk (OR = 10.231, 95% CI: [1.121–93.341]).ConclusionThis study suggests that low CD44 expression and high TSC-1 expression in patients with low-risk NMIBC may serve as prognostic markers for tumor recurrence. Assessing these markers can facilitate earlier identification of at-risk patients, enabling strict surveillance and timely management of treatment strategies.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Non-muscle invasive, ELF-3, CD44, TSC-1, NMIBC

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) accounts for approximately 5% of all newly diagnosed cancers in the United States and represents the seventh most common malignancy [1]. Based on the molecular pathways of tumorigenesis, two distinct categories of BC have been described. The first category includes localized, papillary, and low-grade tumors arising from urothelial hyperplasia. This histological type generally exhibits a more favorable clinical course and is frequently associated with mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) gene [2]. In contrast, the second category includes nonpapillary, high-grade tumors that typically develop in the setting of severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ (CIS). These cancers are characterized by higher rates of progression and muscle invasion, resulting in poor long-term survival outcomes [3].

Although immunohistochemical (IHC) markers, such as CK5/6, CK20 (cytokeratin 5/6 and 20), TP53 (tumor protein 53), and CD44 (cluster of differentiation 44), are commonly used to distinguish between basal and luminal molecular pathways, no universally accepted gold standard marker has been established [4, 5]. Building upon this, the primary objective of our study was to evaluate the potential of ELF-3 (E74-like transcription factor 3) and TSC-1 (tuberous sclerosis complex subunit 1), along with other IHC markers, as prognostic markers of recurrence and progression in patients with low-risk non-muscle invasive BC (NMIBC). Specifically, ELF-3 is associated with urothelial differentiation and may have tumor-suppressive effects; however, its role in BC remains poorly defined. In addition, TSC-1, a regulator of the mTOR signaling system, is linked to molecular subtypes of BC and may affect tumor formation and progression. Given these biological functions and the sparse data on their predictive relevance in low-risk NMIBC, these markers were selected for further analysis. Finally, a secondary objective was to explore whether risk stratification using these markers could help tailor patient-specific surveillance strategies, even though cystoscopy remains the gold standard for follow-up in bladder cancer [6].

Materials and methods

This study received approval from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Tekirdağ Namık Kemal University (No: 2021.227.09.13) and was supported by the Scientific Research Projects (BAP) Coordination Unit of Tekirdağ Namık Kemal University as a medical specialty thesis project (Project Code: NKUBAP.02.TU.22.361). Accordingly, we retrospectively reviewed data from 320 patients who underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumors (TUR-BT) between 2011 and 2019 in our clinic. The patient selection process, including detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, is illustrated in Fig. 1. Of these, 40 patients aged 18 years or older with a pathological diagnosis of NMIBC and regular cystoscopic follow-up met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. Low-risk NMIBC was delineated according to existing guideline criteria, principally encompassing Ta stage and low-grade tumors without carcinoma in situ. However, due to the study’s retrospective design, the cohort exhibited specific clinicopathological variations, including tumor multiplicity and size heterogeneity. To avoid potential bias, patients who had received Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy before recurrence were excluded. Moreover, patients were retrospectively selected based on established criteria and subsequently assigned to the recurrence or non-recurrence group based on their clinical outcomes. To ensure balanced comparison, each group included an equal number of patients; as a result, the study cohort is not a sequential series. Consequently, this study should be regarded as an exploratory, non-consecutive, group-balanced retrospective analysis rather than a conclusive cohort study.

Immunohistochemical examination

For IHC analysis, 4-µm sections were prepared from all paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. First, sections were incubated in a 60 °C oven for 1 h. They were then deparaffinized with xylene for 15 min and rehydrated through graded alcohols. Next, antigen retrieval and staining were performed using an automated staining system (BenchMark XT, Ventana Medical Systems, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. All samples were stained with these antibodies: CK5/6 (Cytokeratin 5/6), CK20, CD44 (anti-CD44 SP37 rabbit monoclonal primary antibody), GATA3 (L50-823 mouse monoclonal primary antibody), Cerb-B2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2), ELF-3, and TSC-1. Slides were coverslipped using a liquid-based mounting medium. Antibodies were applied according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Positive and negative controls were included in each staining run.

Microscopic evaluation was performed using an Olympus CX41 microscope (Munich, Germany). All histological assessments were conducted on TUR specimens by an experienced pathologist (AİA) who was blind to clinical information and recurrence status. In each instance, a minimum of 100 tumor cells were assessed at ×100 magnification. Staining was evaluated semi-quantitatively according to the proportion of positive tumor cells. Staining intensity was categorized into five groups: score 0 (0–10% staining), score 1 (10–25%), score 2 (25–50%), score 3 (50–75%), and score 4 (> 75%).

The cutoff values for positivity were set at 50% for GATA3 and CD44 and at 25% for CK5/6 and CK20. Accordingly, scores 0 and 1 were classified as negative, while scores 2–4 were considered positive for CK5/6 and CK20 (Fig. 2). For GATA3 and CD44, scores 0–2 were considered negative, whereas scores 3 and 4 were considered positive (Fig. 3). For Cerb-B2, ELF-3, and TSC-1, the cutoff value for positivity was set at 75%. Complete membranous staining in neoplastic cells was required for positivity; score 4 was considered positive, score 3 equivocal, and scores 0–2 negative (Fig. 4). These cutoff values were determined based on the existing literature and established IHC assessment protocols, acknowledging that marker-specific thresholds are typically set according to staining features and biological significance. Prior research on urothelial cancer has likewise employed marker-dependent scoring systems and diverse thresholds for markers such as CD44, CK20, and CK5/6 [6, 7].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. Frequency tables and descriptive statistics were used to summarize the findings. Parametric tests were applied to continuous variables with normal distributions, whereas non-parametric tests were used for variables that did not meet the assumption of normality. For comparisons between the two independent groups, the independent samples t-test was employed for normally distributed variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson’s χ² test. Factors influencing recurrence were further analyzed using binary logistic regression with the backward likelihood ratio (backward LR) method. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all logistic regression analyses. Given the exploratory nature and numerous variables examined, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was made. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 40 patients with NMIBC were included in the study. Of these, 20 had recurrence during follow-up, and 20 were recurrence-free. No statistically significant associations were found between recurrence status and gender, occupation, ongoing smoking, tumor localization, or early postoperative Epirubicin administration. In contrast, a significant difference was observed in the initial tumor count (p = 0.022). Sixteen patients (80.0%) in the nonrecurrence group presented with a single tumor, whereas nine patients (45.0%) in the recurrence group had 2–4 tumors. The mean tumor number was 1.50 ± 1.15 in the nonrecurrence group and 2.45 ± 2.09 in the recurrence group. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified 1.5 as the optimal cutoff for tumor number to predict recurrence, with 60.0% sensitivity and 80.0% specificity (AUC = 0.686; p < 0.05). The AUC score indicates moderate discrimination and warrants careful interpretation.

When postoperative Epirubicin was assessed, it was given to 8 of 20 patients (40%) without recurrence and 9 of 20 patients (45%) with recurrence. Epirubicin use did not significantly affect recurrence development, time to recurrence (months), or recurrence size (cm). A statistically significant difference was observed between recurrence status and CD44 expression (χ² = 5.227; p = 0.022). CD44 positivity was detected in 11 patients (55.0%) without recurrence, whereas 16 patients (80.0%) in the recurrence group were CD44 negative. Thus, CD44 positivity was predominantly associated with the absence of recurrence, while CD44 negativity was associated with recurrence. Similarly, a significant relationship was found between recurrence status and TSC-1 expression (p = 0.003). Nine patients (45.0%) in the nonrecurrence group were TSC-1 negative, compared with 19 patients (95.0%) in the recurrence group who were TSC-1 positive. Accordingly, TSC-1 positivity was primarily associated with recurrence, whereas TSC-1 negativity was associated with nonrecurrence. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the multiple comparisons performed.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis, incorporating all parameters that showed significance in univariate analyses —including tumor number, CD44 expression, TSC-1 expression, and ureteral orifice localization confirmed these findings. In the final model, CD44 remained an independent factor influencing recurrence (p < 0.05) (Table 3), with CD44 positivity reducing recurrence risk by approximately 85.8% compared with CD44 negativity (OR = 0.142, 95% CI: [0.028–0.714]). Conversely, TSC-1 positivity was associated with a 21.8-fold increased risk of recurrence (OR = 21.871, 95% CI: [2.125–115.15], p < 0.05). In addition, ureteral orifice involvement was a significant factor (p < 0.05), with tumors located at the ureteral orifice demonstrated significantly higher odds of recurrence compared with tumors without ureteral orifice involvement (OR = 10.231, 95% CI: [1.121–93.341], p = 0.039).

The median follow-up period for the study cohort was 36 months (IQR: 24–52 months). The median duration of recurrence-free survival was 28 months. All recurrences were histologically confirmed after cystoscopic identification, ensuring diagnostic accuracy and consistency in the outcome evaluation.

Discussion

Immunohistochemical (IHC) markers have been progressively used as effective substitutes for molecular subtyping in BC. Markers, including CK5/6, CK20, GATA3, and CD44, are frequently employed to differentiate between basal and luminal phenotypes, which correlate with specific biological activities and clinical outcomes. Nonetheless, growing evidence indicates that the predictive significance of these indicators is variable and context-dependent, especially in non–muscle-invasive disease. Recent studies have underscored that IHC-based classification offers clinically relevant insights. However, it may not fully capture the complexity of molecular subtypes and tumor biology, thereby necessitating integration with genomic profiling methodologies [8].

CD44 is a cell adhesion molecule that has been implicated in promoting tumor progression and dissemination [9–11]. However, its prognostic value in urothelial carcinoma remains controversial [12, 13]. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated a statistically significant difference between CD44 expression and advanced T stage, lymph node metastasis, and adverse oncological outcomes, suggesting that CD44 expression may be linked to tumor progression, metastasis, and treatment failure in bladder cancer [13, 14]. Conversely, Raspollini et al. reported in their study that CD44 expression was not associated with poor prognostic factors on T1 high-grade urothelial carcinoma [15]. In our cohort, CD44 positivity was noted in 11 of 20 patients (55%) without recurrence, in contrast to 4 of 20 patients (20%) in the recurrence group, indicating a statistically significant difference (p = 0.022). These results are consistent with those reported by Raspollini et al. This apparent discrepancy in the literature may be explained by the specific characteristics of our study population, which included only patients with NMIBC. Although CD44 has been linked to cancer stemness, tumor aggressiveness, and poor prognosis in many malignancies, its role in BC appears to be context-dependent. In low-risk NMIBC, CD44 expression may indicate a more differentiated urothelial phenotype rather than stem-like features, which could explain its lower recurrence risk in our cohort. These data show that CD44 biology is heterogeneous and may differ by tumor grade, stage, and molecular subtype, with implications for prognosis.

Cytokeratins (CKs) are structural proteins that form part of the cytoskeleton of squamous (stratified) epithelial cells. Extensive IHC research has been performed on CKs in a variety of cancers, as they are regulators of proliferation in epithelial-derived squamous cells [16, 17]. Jung et al. found that within the NMIBC group, tumors expressing CK5/6 were more likely to be of a higher grade [18]. While some studies in the literature suggest that CK5/6 expression may serve as a prognostic marker, others have concluded that it lacks prognostic significance. In our study, no statistically significant difference was observed between patients with and without recurrence in terms of CK5/6 staining (p > 0.05).

GATA3, also known as GATA binding protein 3, is a T cell–specific gene that functions as a transcription factor located in the T cell receptor (TCR) α region [19]. GATA3 has been investigated in a variety of tumors other than bladder cancer, and it has been suggested to serve as an IHC marker with potential prognostic relevance [20, 21]. In studies conducted by Kamel et al., GATA3 expression in patients with NMIBC was reported to be associated with favorable prognostic factors [22]. However, most published studies have been conducted in either MIBC or general NMIBC populations, and data specific to low-grade NMIBC remain limited. In the present study, GATA3 staining was observed in all patients (100%) without recurrence and in 17 of 20 patients (85%) with recurrence. This difference did not show statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Previous studies have demonstrated that low CK20 expression is associated with poor recurrence-free survival (RFS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) [23, 24]. In contrast, Buchumensky et al. reported no association between tumor grade and CK20 expression [25]. While the literature supports CK20 as a reliable marker for identifying the luminal subgroup, its prognostic value for predicting recurrence, tumor grade, and aggressiveness remains inconsistent. Our results indicate that no statistically significant differences in CK20 expression were observed between the recurrence and nonrecurrence groups (p > 0.05). Studies conducted in populations similar to ours have yielded comparable results, thus supporting the results of our study.

Cerb-B2, also known as HER2/neu, is a protein encoded by the ERBB2 gene. Enache et al. concluded that Cerb-B2 expression is important for identifying poorly differentiated, advanced-stage tumors [26], whereas Alexa et al. found no relationship between Cerb-B2 overexpression and tumor stage [27]. We also evaluated Cerb-B2 expression, and no significant differences were observed between the recurrence and nonrecurrence groups.

ELF-3 is a member of the ETS (erythrocyte transformation-specific) transcription factor family [28]. Na et al. demonstrated that increased ELF-3 signaling exerts a tumor-suppressive role in bladder cancer [29]. However, its role in the molecular subtyping of bladder cancer remains uncertain, and research in this area is still in its early stages. In our cohort, ELF-3 expression was uniformly positive (100%) in both groups, with no ability to differentiate between patients with and without recurrence. Thus, ELF-3 was not found to be an effective marker for predicting recurrence. Despite ELF-3 being initially considered a possible marker due to its reported role in urothelial differentiation, our results demonstrated consistent expression across all specimens, suggesting that it lacks predictive value in low-risk NMIBC. Consequently, the study’s primary focus transitioned to CD44 and TSC-1, which exhibited strong and independent correlations with recurrence risk. The uniform expression of ELF-3 may indicate biological homogeneity within low-risk NMIBC; however, it may also be influenced by technical factors, such as staining features or chosen cutoff levels, which could limit its discriminatory ability in this cohort. Therefore, ELF-3 should be considered a negative finding in this study despite its initial rationale for inclusion.

TSC-1, also known as hamartin, is a protein encoded by the TSC-1 gene and functions as a co-chaperone that inhibits the ATPase activity of HSP90, thereby regulating the chaperone cycle. Dadhania et al. identified TSC-1 as a marker of the luminal subtype in bladder cancer [6]. Although the precise role of TSC-1 in bladder cancer biology remains unclear, it has been implicated in tumor development through its interaction with the mTOR pathway. This has prompted researchers to consider mTOR inhibitors as targeted therapeutic agents for patients with TSC-1 mutations. For example, Iyer et al. reported that TSC-1 mutations are associated with increased sensitivity to everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor [30]. According to the results of our study, TSC-1 expression was significantly associated with recurrence (p = 0.003). Patients positive for TSC-1 were predominantly found in the recurrence group, while TSC-1 negativity was associated with nonrecurrence. According to our findings, TSC-1 may serve as a prognostic marker for recurrence in patients with low-grade NMIBC. The importance of this observation lies in the fact that, to our knowledge, TSC-1 has not previously been studied in the context of recurrence prediction in this specific patient group. Given the many comparisons, these correlations should be evaluated cautiously since some may be chance findings and may not remain significant after multiple testing. TSC-1 expression in this study was measured by IHC and reflects protein overexpression, not gene mutation. Thus, the link with recurrence does not indicate TSC-1 genetic changes.

It is essential to differentiate between prognostic and predictive implications when interpreting biomarker data. Prognostic markers estimate the intrinsic risk of recurrence or progression independent of treatment, while prognostic markers signify the probability of benefitting from a particular treatment. In this study, CD44 and TSC-1 served as prognostic predictors of recurrence risk in low-risk NMIBC, rather than as predictive markers of treatment response. This interpretation is consistent with the 2024 EAU Guidelines, which underscore the persistent necessity for validated biomarkers to enhance risk classification in NMIBC, while acknowledging that none have been established for practical application [31]. Moreover, current genomic classifications, including the extensive molecular subtyping outlined by Robertson et al. [32], establish a framework that connects genetic abnormalities to clinical behavior. Our results indicate that TSC-1 may represent biological processes linked to these subtypes and could facilitate a more refined prognostic classification if validated in larger, prospective, genomically informed cohorts. Nevertheless, given the multiple comparisons conducted, these correlations must be evaluated with caution, as some may be coincidental and may lose significance after adjustment for multiple testing.

Alongside genetic markers, certain clinicopathological characteristics remain reliable predictors of recurrence and progression in NMIBC. Tumor stage, size, and multiplicity are critical factors incorporated into established risk stratification models, with multifocal tumors and larger ones consistently associated with higher recurrence risk, and higher T stage associated with poorer oncological outcomes. Additionally, adjuvant intravesical therapies, such as BCG and intravesical chemotherapy, are essential in reducing recurrence and progression after TURBT. A re-TURBT is advised for select patients, especially those with high-risk or T1 disease, as it enhances staging precision and may decrease recurrence rates. Current research underscores that effective management of NMIBC requires integrating existing clinical variables and treatment strategies with emerging molecular biomarkers to achieve precise risk stratification [33]. Although the study focused on patients classified as low-risk NMIBC, the presence of tumor multiplicity, variation in tumor size, inconsistent postoperative intravesical therapy administration, and the retrospective balanced-group design introduced clinical heterogeneity that may limit the homogeneity of the cohort and the generalizability of the findings. Ureteral orifice involvement was associated with increased recurrence risk in the multivariate model; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of cases and wide confidence interval.

Limitations

This study, due to its limited sample size and retrospective, group-balanced design, should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. It was retrospective and conducted at a single center with a limited sample size, which restricts its generalizability. No formal sample size calculation was done due to the retrospective design; the sample size reflected all eligible patients during the study period. The small sample size and the limited number of recurrence events (n = 20) led to a low events-per-variable ratio in the multivariate model, falling below recommended thresholds and increasing the risk of overfitting and instability in the regression results. Consequently, the outcomes of the multivariate analysis must be evaluated cautiously and regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Despite the post hoc power analysis indicating a power of 0.74 to detect an OR greater than 3.0 at α = 0.05, the limited cohort size inherently limits statistical precision. The high odds ratios, possibly accompanied by wide confidence intervals and a low events-per-variable ratio, suggest potential model instability and the possibility of overfitting. The notably high odds ratio observed for TSC-1 positivity (OR ≈ 21.8) warrants cautious interpretation, as it may reflect statistical instability rather than a genuine biological effect. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was implemented, and this limitation has been clearly acknowledged. Notwithstanding these challenges, concentrating only on low-risk NMIBC enhances internal validity and offers new insights into the predictive significance of TSC-1. We anticipate that TSC-1 and CD44 may guide future research and contribute to risk stratification in low-grade NMIBC. Another limitation of this study is the potential for selection bias, as the cohort was retrospectively assembled with equal group sizes instead of being sourced from a consecutive patient series. While the study focused on low-risk NMIBC, characteristics such as tumor multiplicity, variability in tumor size, and inconsistent application of postoperative intravesical therapy may introduce heterogeneity and partial overlap with intermediate-risk disease. Furthermore, the lack of assessment of interobserver variability may limit the reproducibility of the IHC evaluations. The comprehensive technical characteristics of IHC staining, encompassing specific antibody sources, antigen retrieval settings, and interobserver repeatability, were not entirely accessible owing to the retrospective design, potentially limiting reproducibility. Moreover, detailed stratification based on recognized prognostic markers, including tumor stage, size, multiplicity, adjuvant intravesical therapy, and re-TURBT, was limited, potentially affecting the analysis of recurrence outcomes. Specifically, patients with prior intravesical BCG therapy were eliminated to exclude the immunomodulatory influence of BCG on tumor biology and biomarker expression. This method decreased confounding but also limited the generalizability of our findings predominantly to BCG-naïve, low-risk NMIBC groups. Consequently, the findings may not be entirely applicable to individuals receiving adjuvant intravesical therapy or to those classified under higher-risk groups. This study contained multiple comparisons across various IHC markers and was exploratory; hence, the p-values were not adjusted for multiple testing. Also, the statistically significant relationships identified -particularly for CD44 and TSC-1- should be considered with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, given the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons. Assessing many IHC markers and clinicopathological factors without correcting for multiple comparisons increases the risk of type I error; hence, statistically significant results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Significantly, applying a strict Bonferroni correction would reduce some reported significances. The study did not analyze TSC-1 mutations at the molecular level; protein expression and genetic modifications could not be assessed. Furthermore, recurrence-free survival analysis with Kaplan–Meier curves was not conducted due to the limited sample size and event number, which may have led to unreliable estimates and restricted the evaluation of the time-dependent prognostic significance of the examined markers.

Conclusion

In our study, CK5/6, CK20, GATA3, and ELF-3 did not demonstrate statistical significance in predicting tumor recurrence in NMIBC. In contrast, CD44 and TSC-1 were identified as potential prognostic markers. Specifically, low CD44 expression and high TSC-1 expression were associated with an increased risk of recurrence in patients with low-risk NMIBC. These IHC markers may allow for the early identification of patients at higher risk, may represent potential prognostic markers; however, these findings are exploratory and require validation in larger, prospective studies before clinical application.
