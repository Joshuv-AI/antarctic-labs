ALDH1L2 suppresses ferroptosis-associated responses and reduces sunitinib sensitivity in renal cell carcinoma organoids
Authors: Xinming Hu, Jie Yang, Desheng Li, Meizhou Deng, Mei Xie, Jiangtao Zhan, Changmin Yang, Xianping Che, Kangli Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSunitinib is an important therapeutic option for renal cell carcinoma (RCC), but reduced response to sunitinib still limits its clinical benefit. This study explored the mechanism underlying reduced sunitinib sensitivity in RCC and aimed to identify potential strategies to improve sunitinib response.MethodsRCC organoids were established and characterized, and sunitinib-responsive genes were screened by transcriptomic analysis. Public datasets and clinical RCC samples were used to evaluate the clinical relevance of ALDH1L2. ALDH1L2 overexpression and knockdown were performed in RCC organoids to examine its effects on sunitinib sensitivity and ferroptosis-related changes. Organoid-derived xenograft models were used for in vivo validation. Molecular docking, cytotoxicity assays, CETSA, SPR, and functional experiments were used to screen and validate candidate compounds targeting ALDH1L2.ResultsSunitinib inhibited RCC organoid growth, with IC50 values of approximately 8 μM. Transcriptomic analysis showed that ALDH1L2 was upregulated after sunitinib treatment. Clinical analyses indicated that ALDH1L2 was highly expressed in RCC tissues and was associated with poorer survival and advanced stage. In RCC organoids, ALDH1L2 overexpression attenuated sunitinib-induced ROS accumulation and ferroptosis-related changes, as reflected by changes in GPX4, SLC7A11, GSH/GSSG, NADP+ /NADPH, Fe2 +, and MDA. In contrast, ALDH1L2 knockdown enhanced these changes and increased the sensitivity of RCC organoids to sunitinib. In organoid-derived xenograft models, ALDH1L2 overexpression reduced the antitumor effect of sunitinib and decreased tumor ROS accumulation. Erastin counteracted the protective effect of ALDH1L2 overexpression. Hederacolchiside A1 (HA-1) was identified as a candidate compound targeting ALDH1L2. HA-1 reduced ALDH1L2 protein abundance, altered its thermal stability, enhanced the inhibitory effect of sunitinib, and counteracted ALDH1L2-mediated sunitinib tolerance in RCC organoids.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that ALDH1L2 contributes to reduced sunitinib sensitivity in RCC organoids by attenuating ferroptosis-related responses. HA-1 may improve the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib by targeting ALDH1L2, supporting further evaluation of this combination strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00854-9.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors in the urinary system, with incidence and mortality rates increasing year by year [1]. The clinical treatment options for patients with advanced RCC are limited and the prognosis is poor [2]. Sunitinib, as a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has been widely used as an important therapeutic option for advanced RCC due to its dual inhibitory effects on tumor angiogenesis and cell proliferation [3, 4]. However, reduced response and acquired resistance to sunitinib in clinical practice have seriously weakened the drug’s efficacy, leading to tumor recurrence and progression, and remain important factors limiting the survival benefits of patients [5]. Although previous studies have attempted to explore the potential mechanisms of sunitinib resistance in RCC, including target mutations, abnormal activation of signaling pathways, and changes in the tumor microenvironment [6, 7], the specific regulatory networks have not yet been fully elucidated, and there is a lack of effective resistance reversal strategies and targeted drugs. Therefore, there is an urgent need to further explore the key regulatory factors related to reduced sunitinib sensitivity and the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Ferroptosis represents a newly discovered regulated pattern of cell death, which is initiated by the buildup of iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. This process is significantly different from traditional cell death pathways like apoptosis and autophagy [8]. Its core regulatory pathways include glutathione (GSH) metabolism, lipid peroxidation balance, and iron ion homeostasis [9, 10]. Recent studies have shown that the abnormal regulation of the ferroptosis pathway is closely associated with the occurrence, development, and drug resistance of tumors [11]. Many anti-tumor drugs can exert therapeutic effects by inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells [12], while tumor cells can also acquire resistance by inhibiting the ferroptosis pathway [13]. However, the specific role and regulatory mechanisms of the ferroptosis pathway in the response of RCC to sunitinib have not yet been clarified, and whether there are key genes that regulate sunitinib sensitivity in RCC through ferroptosis-related processes still needs further investigation.

Traditional tumor models (such as cell lines) struggle to simulate the heterogeneity and microenvironmental characteristics of tumor tissues, resulting in low efficiency in translating basic research findings to clinical applications [14]. Patient-derived organoids, as a novel 3D in vitro model, are capable of retaining the histological features, cellular heterogeneity, and biological behavior of the primary tumor, and have a high correlation with clinical tumors. They have become an ideal tool for studying tumor resistance mechanisms and screening for drugs [15, 16]. Meanwhile, organoid-derived xenograft models can provide an in vivo platform for evaluating tumor growth and drug response, thereby helping to verify the validity of in vitro research results [17, 18]. The combined application of organoids and organoid-derived xenograft models can offer a research system closer to clinical reality for in-depth dissection of sunitinib response-related mechanisms in RCC.

Therefore, based on the above background, this study aims to identify key sunitinib-responsive genes and elucidate new mechanisms associated with reduced sunitinib sensitivity in RCC by combining RCC organoids and organoid-derived xenograft models with transcriptomic analysis. Through transcriptomic analysis, ALDH1L2 was identified as a candidate gene upregulated after sunitinib treatment, and clinical analyses suggested that ALDH1L2 was associated with poor clinical features in RCC. Considering the role of ALDH1L2 in mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism and redox regulation, we hypothesized that ALDH1L2 may contribute to reduced sunitinib sensitivity by attenuating oxidative stress and ferroptosis-related responses. Focusing on this hypothesis, we explored the effects of ALDH1L2 overexpression and knockdown on sunitinib response, iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis-related markers. We further screened small-molecule compounds targeting ALDH1L2 from a library of traditional Chinese medicine natural products, aiming to identify a potential strategy to improve the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib.

Materials and methods

Collection of clinical samples

We collected fresh RCC tissues for organoid establishment and paired RCC/peritumoral tissues for clinical validation from patients diagnosed with RCC at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University through imaging, serological, or histopathological examinations. The fresh RCC tissues used for organoid establishment were obtained from patients with clinically documented poor response or resistance to sunitinib. Three RCC samples were used for organoid culture, and five paired RCC and peritumoral tissue samples were used for IHC and western blot validation of ALDH1L2 expression. No patients had undergone preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Patients with pathologically confirmed non-RCC tumors or incomplete clinical data were excluded. Informed consent was obtained from all patients providing samples, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University (approval number LW2021643). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Isolation, culture, and processing of RCC organoids

RCC tissue samples were obtained and promptly immersed in a tissue protection solution at 4 °C. A 35 mm dish with ice-cold PBS containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin was prepared. The RCC samples were then retrieved, and normal and necrotic tissues were dissected. The isolation of RCC organoids was conducted using a previously published protocol with minor modifications [19]. The growth of organoids was observed daily, and the culture medium was changed every 2–3 days. Organoid growth was recorded on day 1, day 3, day 5, and day 7. When the organoids reached a size of 50 μm, they were treated with different concentrations of sunitinib (0, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000 μM) for 48 h. After drug treatment, representative images were captured, and ATP activity was measured. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated based on the inhibition rate of organoid activity by different drug concentrations.

RCC organoids were infected with lentivirus-mediated ALDH1L2 overexpression and then treated with sunitinib. The efficiency of ALDH1L2 overexpression was verified by RT-qPCR and western blot before functional assays. The experimental setups included the negative control (NC) group (RCC organoids), the ALDH1L2-OE group (ALDH1L2 overexpressing organoids), the SU group (RCC organoids treated with 10 μM sunitinib), and the SU+ALDH1L2-OE group (ALDH1L2 overexpressing organoids treated with 10 μM sunitinib).

For loss-of-function experiments, RCC organoids were infected with lentivirus carrying shRNA targeting ALDH1L2 or the corresponding negative control shRNA. The knockdown efficiency of ALDH1L2 was confirmed by RT-qPCR and western blot. The groups included the shNC group, sh-ALDH1L2 group, SU group, and SU+sh-ALDH1L2 group. The SU and SU+sh-ALDH1L2 groups were treated with 10 μM sunitinib for 48 h.

Additional experiments with ferroptosis inducer intervention were set up: the SU+ALDH1L2-OE+Erastin group (ALDH1L2 overexpressing organoids treated with 10 μM sunitinib and Erastin). To evaluate the direct effect of HA-1 on the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib, organoids were divided into the Control group, HA-1 group, SU group, and SU+HA-1 group. The HA-1 group was treated with HA-1, the SU group was treated with 10 μM sunitinib, and the SU+HA-1 group was treated with both HA-1 and 10 μM sunitinib for 48 h. Traditional Chinese medicine monomer intervention experiments were also performed: the SU+ALDH1L2-OE+Hederacolchiside A1 (HA-1) group (ALDH1L2 overexpressing organoids treated with 10 μM sunitinib and HA-1.

Construction of the organoid-derived xenograft model

All animal protocols were approved by the committee of the The Ethical Committee of Guangzhou Forevergen Medical Laboratory Animal Center (approval number: IACUC-AEWC-F250801001). All animal experiments comply with the ARRIVE guidelines and be carried out in accordance with the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986 and associated guidelines, EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments. Twenty-four 6-week-old nude mice were acclimated to the laboratory environment. After light anesthesia with isoflurane or local disinfection, 1 mL syringes were used to draw up a mixture containing dissociated RCC organoids (adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/100 μL) in a solution of 50% PBS and 50% Matrigel (total volume 100 μL). The mixture was injected subcutaneously at an angle of 3–5 mm into the disinfected skin, forming a distinct subcutaneous bulge (ensuring no leakage). After injection, the puncture site was gently pressed with a sterile cotton swab for 5 s. The injection site was observed daily for redness, ulceration, and changes in the bulge to ensure successful subcutaneous tumor formation from the RCC organoids. The day of injection was marked as Day 1 (D1), and tumor formation could be observed by D7. The 24 nude mice were then randomly divided into four groups of six mice each: NC group (intratumoral injection of NC lentivirus (50 μL, titer 1 × 108 TU/mL) on D7, D14, and D21, and daily gavage with normal saline), ALDH1L2-OE group (intratumoral injection of ALDH1L2 overexpression lentivirus (50 μL, titer 1 × 108 TU/mL) on D7, D14, and D21, and daily gavage with normal saline), SU group (intratumoral injection of NC lentivirus (50 μL, titer 1 × 108 TU/mL) on D7, D14, and D21, and daily gavage with sunitinib (50 mg/kg)), and SU+ALDH1L2-OE group (intratumoral injection of ALDH1L2 overexpression lentivirus (50 μL, titer 1 × 108 TU/mL) on D7, D14, and D21, and daily gavage with sunitinib (50 mg/kg)). Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every 7 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula (V = L×W2 /2). Growth curves were plotted based on these measurements. After 4 weeks of intervention, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were removed, weighed, and photographed.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining

The morphology of organoids and organoid-derived xenograft tumor tissues was evaluated with HE staining kit (G1100, Solarbio). The samples were fixed in formaldehyde, decalcified, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin (BL957A, Biosharp). Tissue sections measuring 3–5 μm in thickness were created. These sections underwent dewaxing using a graded ethanol series (75–100%), followed by staining with hematoxylin for 1 min. They were then rinsed with distilled water and subjected to bluing in PBS. Eosin staining was applied for 0.5 min, after which sections were rinsed once more with distilled water. Dehydration was achieved either by immersing the sections in ethanol gradients (95–100%) for 5 min each or by air-drying them directly. Subsequently sections were placed in xylene for 10 min, repeated twice, and finally mounted with neutral resin (G8590, Solarbio) for observation under a microscope (BA410T, Motic).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was employed to measure the expression of Vimentin and Cytokeratin 7 (CK7) in organoids, ALDH1L2 in clinical RCC tissues, and Ki67 in organoid-derived xenograft tumor tissues. Paraffin sections were made from organoids and tumor tissues, which were then subjected to baking at 60 °C for a duration of 12 h. Following the dewaxing procedure to water, antigens were repaired by heat. Sections were treated with 1% periodic acid at room temperature for 10 min, then washed three times with PBS for 3 min each. Primary antibodies against Vimentin (10366–1-AP, Proteintech), Cytokeratin 7 (CK7, 17,513–1-AP, Proteintech), ALDH1L2 (21391–1-AP, Proteintech), and Ki67 (27309–1-AP, Proteintech) were applied and incubated at 4 °C overnight, followed by three 5-minute PBS washes. The secondary antibody was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. DAB (G1212, Servicebio) was used for color development for 1–5 min. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 5–10 min and then washed with distilled water. Dehydration was achieved by immersing the sections in graded alcohol, and they were mounted with neutral gum for microscopic examination.

Detection of ATP activity in organoid

Organoids were seeded into a 96-well plate and allowed to grow until the average diameter exceeded 50 μm, after which the medium was changed. After treatment, the 96-well plate to be tested was removed from the incubator and placed at room temperature for 30 min to equilibrate the temperature of the culture plate to room temperature. An equal volume of 100 μL of CellCounting-Lite 3D (DD1102, Vazyme), which had been equilibrated to room temperature, was added to the wells containing the organoids. The plate was then vigorously shaken for 5 min to ensure complete lysis of the organoids. The plate was left at room temperature for an additional 25 min to stabilize the luminescent signal before detection.

Transcriptome sequencing

Organoids treated with 10 μM sunitinib for 48 h were designated as the drug treatment group (Sunitinib group), while organoids without drug treatment served as the Control group, with three biological replicates per group. RNA was extracted, and its purity was measured using Nanodrop (A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0). The integrity of RNA was checked with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. RNA-seq was carried out by Illumina. cDNA libraries were built and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to get raw data. After filtering out low-quality reads in quality control, the data were mapped to the human reference genome (hg38). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software. Genes with |log₂(FoldChange)| >1 and padj < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed, and volcano plots were created based on these criteria.

Clinical correlation analysis

Transcriptomic data and clinical information from RCC patients were obtained from the TCGA-KIRC database. The expression differences of ALDH1L2 between RCC and normal tissues were analyzed using the GEPIA2 online tool. Survival analysis was employed using the Kaplan-Meier method to compare the overall survival rates of patients with high and low ALDH1L2 expression. The correlation between ALDH1L2 expression levels and RCC clinical stages was examined. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied to assess the ability of ALDH1L2 expression to distinguish RCC tissues from normal kidney tissues, and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) value was calculated. Because detailed sunitinib treatment-response information was not available in the TCGA-KIRC dataset, this analysis was not used to directly evaluate sunitinib resistance.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Initially, total RNA was isolated using TriQuick Reagent Total RNA Extraction Kit (R1100, Solarbio). Following this, the RNA was converted into cDNA via the 5×RT SuperMix for qPCR (K1074, APEXbio). The expression level of ALDH1L2 was measured using the 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (K1070, APEXbio) on the iQ5 Multicolor Real-Time PCR Detection System (582BR 005500, BIO-RAD). GAPDH served as the internal control, and relative expression levels were determined using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are as follows: ALDH1L2-F: GCTGAAGTTGGCACTAATTGGC, ALDH1L2-R: TGAACACCCCTACTACTCGGT; GAPDH-F: CATCATCCCTGCCTCTACTGG, GAPDH-R: GTGGGTGTCGCTGTTGAAGTC.

Western blot

Western blot was conducted to measure the expression of ALDH1L2 in clinical RCC tissues, ALDH1L2 overexpression and knockdown efficiency in RCC organoids, GPX4 and SLC7A11 in organoids, ALDH1L2, GPX4, and SLC7A11 in organoid-derived xenograft tumor tissues, and ALDH1L2 after treatment with different concentrations of HA-1 (0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 μM) and sunitinib. Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime). After quantification with a BCA kit (BL521A, Biosharp), the target proteins were detected using a 12% polyacrylamide gel with a loading amount of approximately 20 μg. The total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The PVDF membrane was then blocked in 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ALDH1L2 (21391–1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), GPX4 (67763–1-Ig, 1:1000, Proteintech), SLC7A11 (A13685, 1:1000, ABCLONAL), and GAPDH (60004–1-Ig, 1:50000, Proteintech). Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (BL003A/BL001A, 1:50000, Biosharp). Finally, the membrane was exposed to ECL substrate (K-12045-D50, Advansta) and the target proteins were visualized using the ChemiScope6100 (CLiNX).

Organoid live/dead cell staining

Staining was performed using Calcein-AM/PI Double Stain Kit (40747ES76, YEASEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Under a fluorescence microscope, a 490 ± 10 nm excitation filter was used to simultaneously observe the distribution of live cells (emitting yellow-green fluorescence) and dead cells (emitting red fluorescence).

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in organoids and tumor tissues

ROS levels in organoids were measured using the ROS Assay Kit (50101ES01, Yeasen). The probe was loaded in situ by diluting DCFH-DA 1:1000 in serum-free culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 10 μM and added to the cells to ensure full coverage. Cells were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed 1–2 times with serum-free medium to remove residual DCFH-DA. Fluorescence microscopy was used to capture images, and the average fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. ROS levels in organoid-derived xenograft tumor tissues were also detected using the same ROS Assay Kit. For tumor tissue ROS detection, freshly collected xenograft tumor tissues were processed according to the kit protocol. Fluorescence intensity was measured and normalized to the corresponding control group.

Biochemical assays

GSH and GSSG levels in organoid lysates were detected by GSH/GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the GSH/GSSG ratio was calculated. NADP+/NADPH ratio was determined with NADP+/NADPH Assay Kit (S0179, Beyotime). Absorbance values at 450 nm were measured using a microplate reader (ELx808, BioTek) to calculate the NADP+/NADPH ratio. Fe2 + levels in organoid lysates were measured using a ferrous ion assay kit (BC5415, Solarbio). MDA levels were detected using a lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (S0131S, Beyotime).

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of ALDH1L2 protein was downloaded from the PDB database (PDB ID: 6Z3V). Water molecules and ligands were removed using PyMOL software, followed by energy minimization processing. The structural files of 310 traditional Chinese medicine monomers were collected (downloaded from the TCMSP database) and subjected to virtual docking using the LibDock module of Discovery Studio 2019 software. The top 10 candidate monomers were selected based on the LibDockScore. Further precise docking was performed using AutoDock-Vina software to calculate the binding affinity, and monomers with a binding affinity of less than −6.9 kcal·mol− 1 were selected.

Cytotoxicity assay

RCC 786-O cells (CTCC-002–0016, Meisen) were cultured in RPMI-1640 Medium containing 10% FBS (16000–044, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (BL505A, Biosharp). After trypsinization, cells were counted and seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate. Cells were then cultured overnight in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO₂ to achieve 70–90% confluence the next day. Six candidate traditional Chinese medicine monomers were selected: Solasonine (T3901, TOPSCIENCE), Raddeanin A (T3878, TOPSCIENCE), Proanthocyanidins (T7883, TOPSCIENCE), Theaflavin_3,3’-digallate (T5429, TOPSCIENCE), HA-1 (T2P2806, TOPSCIENCE), and Tubuloside B (T8852, TOPSCIENCE). These monomers were used to treat RCC 786-O cells at concentrations of 0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 μM. After 48 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 kit (CK04, Dojindo), and the IC₅₀ values of each monomer were calculated.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

To assess whether HA-1 affected the thermal stability of ALDH1L2, CETSA was performed. Cells were first cultured to the logarithmic growth phase and then treated with DMSO (control group) and different concentrations of HA-1 for 24 h. After treatment, cells were collected, washed with PBS containing protease inhibitors, and resuspended to an appropriate density. Cells were subsequently divided into several PCR tubes and subjected to heat treatment in a thermal cycler for 3 min at a range of temperatures, including 40 °C, 43 °C, 46 °C, 49 °C, 52 °C, and 55 °C, in order to trigger protein denaturation at different thermal conditions. After heating, the cells were resuspended in lysis buffer, subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen to completely lyse cells, and then centrifuged at 4 °C, 20,000 g for 20 min to collect supernatant. Supernatant was subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and Western blot analysis to evaluate ALDH1L2 levels at different temperatures using specific antibodies. By comparing the thermal stability changes of ALDH1L2 in different treatment groups, changes in ALDH1L2 thermal stability after HA-1 treatment were assessed.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

The binding of HA-1 to ALDH1L2 was confirmed using SPR assay. A COOH chip was mounted following the standard procedure for the OpenSPRTM instrument. Initially, the system was operated at the maximum flow rate (150 µL/min) using PBST (pH 7.4) as the buffer. Once the baseline signal was stable, 200 µL of IPA was injected and run for 10 s to eliminate bubbles. After the signal stabilized, the sample loop was washed with buffer and purged with air. When the baseline signal was steady, the flow rate was reduced to 20 µL/min. The chip was activated by injecting an EDC/NHS (1:1) solution. A 200 μL solution of recombinant human ALDH1L2 protein, diluted in immobilization buffer, was injected and run for 4 min. The sample loop was washed with buffer and purged with air again. A 200 µL volume of Blocking solution was introduced, succeeded by a buffer rinse and an air flush. The system’s baseline was then observed for a duration of 5 min to confirm its stability. The analyte was diluted in analyte buffer and injected at a flow rate of 30 µL/min, with binding time set at 180 seconds and dissociation time at 300 s. Data analysis was conducted using TraceDrawer software (Ridgeview Instruments ab, Sweden) based on the One To One model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Measurement data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two or more groups were made using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Inhibitory effects of sunitinib on RCC organoids and transcriptomic analysis of resistance-related genes

To investigate the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib, this study initially cultured three RCC organoids and validated their consistency with key indicators of RCC through pathological detection. Representative bright-field images showed that RCC-O-1, RCC-O-2, and RCC-O-3 gradually formed and expanded during culture from day 1 to day 7 (Supplementary Figure 1A). It can be observed that as the culture time extended, the size of organoids gradually increased and their morphology became more complex, indicating that the organoids could successfully form and grow under in vitro culture conditions. HE staining revealed the structural organization of the organoids, and a tissue-like cell arrangement could be observed. In IHC staining, the expression of Vimentin, a mesenchymal cell marker [20], and CK7, an epithelial cell marker [21], indicated that the organoids retained RCC-related pathological features (Supplementary Figure 1B). Subsequently, RCC organoids were treated with different concentrations of sunitinib (0, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 1000 μM). After 48 h of treatment, the morphology of the organoids was clearly affected at higher concentrations of sunitinib, especially from 10 μM onward, showing reduced organoid number and impaired structural integrity (Fig. 1A). The ATP activity of organoids also decreased after sunitinib treatment, indicating that the drug inhibited the metabolic activity of RCC organoids (Fig. 1B). Based on the inhibitory effects of different concentrations of sunitinib on organoid growth, the IC50 values of sunitinib in the three RCC organoid lines were approximately 8 μM (Fig. 1C). Further transcriptomic sequencing was performed after treatment with 10 μM sunitinib. Based on the screening criteria of |log₂(FoldChange)| >1 and padj < 0.05, 236 differentially expressed genes were identified in the Control-vs-Sunitinib group, including 80 upregulated genes and 156 downregulated genes. The volcano plot displayed the upregulated and downregulated differentially expressed genes (Fig. 1D). Combining the sequencing results and literature review, ALDH1L2, with a padj value of 8.297E-34, was selected as the key candidate gene for further study. The read count of ALDH1L2 was increased in the Sunitinib group, and RT-qPCR further confirmed that ALDH1L2 expression was significantly upregulated after sunitinib treatment (Figs. 1E and 1F). Clinical relevance and validation of ALDH1L2 expression in RCC

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of ALDH1L2 in RCC, public RCC datasets and clinical samples were analyzed. Single-gene analysis based on RCC datasets revealed that ALDH1L2 was highly expressed in RCC tissues compared with normal kidney tissues (Fig. 2A). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that high ALDH1L2 expression was associated with lower overall survival (Fig. 2B). The expression level of ALDH1L2 also tended to increase with tumor stage, suggesting that ALDH1L2 may be related to RCC progression (Fig. 2C). ROC analysis showed that ALDH1L2 had a certain ability to distinguish RCC tissues from normal kidney tissues, with an AUC value of 0.695 (Fig. 2D). Since detailed sunitinib treatment-response information was not available in this dataset, this result was interpreted as supporting the clinical relevance of ALDH1L2 in RCC, rather than as direct evidence for predicting sunitinib resistance. IHC and Western blot were further performed to examine ALDH1L2 expression in clinical RCC samples. IHC staining showed stronger ALDH1L2 expression in tumor tissues than in peritumoral tissues, and the histological score was significantly increased in tumor tissues (Figs. 2E and 2F). Western blot analysis also confirmed that ALDH1L2 protein expression was higher in tumor tissues than in peritumoral tissues (Figs. 2G and 2H). ALDH1L2 overexpression attenuated sunitinib-induced ferroptosis-related changes in organoids

Subsequently, RCC organoids were infected with lentivirus-mediated ALDH1L2 overexpression and then exposed to sunitinib. ALDH1L2 overexpression was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels before functional analysis (Supplementary Figure 2). Compared with the NC group, the ALDH1L2-OE group exhibited more intact organoid structures, increased ATP activity, little cell death as shown by live/dead staining, and reduced ROS levels. For the morphological comparison, we mainly considered organoid number, contour continuity, structural collapse, and fragmented or degenerated organoids, as lumen formation and branching were not obvious features in this RCC organoid model. In the SU treatment group, organoid growth was significantly inhibited, ATP activity decreased, live/dead staining showed increased cell death, and ROS levels were significantly increased. Screening and validation of traditional Chinese medicine monomers targeting ALDH1L2 for RCC therapy

Subsequently, we conducted molecular docking experiments on the 310 traditional Chinese medicine monomers previously screened, aiming to identify potential candidate drugs that can target the core target ALDH1L2. We downloaded the PDB structure of the ALDH1L2 protein from the PDB database and performed docking of all the monomers with this protein. Based on the LibDockScore, we found that 10 monomers, including Theaflavin_3,3’-digallate, Solasonine, Plantamajoside, Ginsenoside Rf, Tubuloside B, Proanthocyanidins, Raddeanin A, HA-1, Punicalagin, and Cephalomannine, could form stable docking models with ALDH1L2, showing potential targeting effects. To further verify the binding ability of these candidate drugs with ALDH1L2, we conducted a more precise molecular docking analysis using AutoDock-Vina software. The results showed that, except for Punicalagin, the other nine monomer components could all form stable binding to the active site of ALDH1L2, and their binding affinities were all lower than −6.9 kcal·mol− 1 (Fig. 7A and B). Considering root-mean-square deviation, binding energy, and docking scores, we selected six monomers (Solasonine, Raddeanin A, Proanthocyanidins, Theaflavin_3,3’-digallate, HA-1, and Tubuloside B) for cytotoxicity experiments in RCC cells to evaluate their cytotoxic effects on RCC cells (Fig. 7C). Ultimately, we chose HA-1, which had the lowest IC₅₀ value, as the focus of subsequent research. Western blot analysis revealed that treatment with different concentrations of HA-1 (0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 μM) significantly reduced the protein expression levels of ALDH1L2 (Fig. 7D and E). Moreover, the CETSA further verified the effect of HA-1 on the stability of the ALDH1L2 protein. The experiment found that HA-1 could modulate the stability of ALDH1L2, suggesting that HA-1 affected the thermal stability of ALDH1L2 (Fig. 7F and G). Finally, SPR technology confirmed the direct binding between HA-1 and ALDH1L2. The concentration gradient binding curve showed good dose dependency, with an association rate constant Ka of 1.02E + 04 (1/(M·s)), a dissociation rate constant Kb of 0.07991 (1/s), and an affinity constant of 7.83E-06 (M) (Fig. 7H). Discussion

This study suggests that ALDH1L2 is involved in reduced sunitinib sensitivity in RCC organoids, at least partly by attenuating oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated responses. By combining RCC organoid models with organoid-derived xenograft models, we found that ALDH1L2 overexpression reduced the response to sunitinib, whereas ALDH1L2 knockdown produced the opposite effect. In addition, HA-1 was identified as a candidate compound targeting ALDH1L2 and partly counteracted ALDH1L2-associated sunitinib tolerance. Together, these findings point to a role of ALDH1L2-related redox regulation in the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib.

Sunitinib is an important therapeutic option for RCC, but reduced response and acquired resistance remain major clinical challenges [22]. Through transcriptomic analysis of RCC organoids treated with sunitinib, this study identified ALDH1L2 as a sunitinib-responsive candidate gene. ALDH1L2, a member of the aldehyde dehydrogenase superfamily, is a key enzyme in mitochondrial folate metabolism and is mainly involved in one-carbon metabolism and oxidative stress regulation [23, 24]. Its role in tumors has not been fully explored. Previous studies have suggested that ALDH1L2 may be associated with metabolic reprogramming and antioxidant regulation in tumor cells [25, 26], but its role in RCC and sunitinib response remains unclear. In this study, ALDH1L2 was upregulated after sunitinib treatment. Clinical correlation analysis also showed that ALDH1L2 was highly expressed in RCC tissues, and higher ALDH1L2 expression was associated with advanced tumor stage and poorer overall survival. However, because detailed sunitinib treatment-response information was not available in the TCGA-KIRC dataset, the ROC result should be interpreted as supporting the clinical relevance of ALDH1L2 in RCC, rather than as direct evidence for predicting sunitinib resistance.

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death involving GSH metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and iron homeostasis [27]. In recent years, ferroptosis has attracted increasing attention in tumor drug resistance, and previous studies have shown that tumor cells may acquire tolerance to chemotherapy and targeted therapy by limiting ferroptosis [28, 29]. To clarify the functional role of ALDH1L2, we performed both gain- and loss-of-function experiments in RCC organoids. Sunitinib treatment increased ROS levels, downregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11, decreased the GSH/GSSG ratio, and increased the NADP+/NADPH ratio. It also increased Fe2 + and MDA levels, indicating enhanced iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation-related changes. ALDH1L2 overexpression attenuated these changes, as shown by increased GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, improved redox balance, and reduced Fe2 + and MDA levels. Conversely, ALDH1L2 knockdown enhanced sunitinib-induced organoid injury, increased ROS, Fe2 +, and MDA levels, and further reduced GPX4/SLC7A11 expression and the GSH/GSSG ratio. These data support the involvement of ALDH1L2 in regulating sunitinib-induced ferroptosis-associated changes in RCC organoids.

GPX4 is a key inhibitor of ferroptosis and maintains cell survival by reducing lipid peroxides [30]. SLC7A11 participates in oxidative stress balance by regulating glutathione synthesis, and together they form an important defense system against ferroptosis [31]. In this study, ALDH1L2 overexpression increased GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, whereas ALDH1L2 knockdown showed the opposite tendency. Based on the known function of ALDH1L2 in one-carbon metabolism [32], ALDH1L2 may help maintain the NADPH/GSH-related redox state and thereby limit lipid peroxidation-related injury under sunitinib treatment. This interpretation is consistent with the metabolic features of ALDH1L2 and the redox-dependent nature of ferroptosis. In addition, the ferroptosis inducer Erastin weakened the protective effect of ALDH1L2 overexpression and restored ferroptosis-related responses in RCC organoids. These findings support the possibility that ferroptosis-related processes are involved in ALDH1L2-associated sunitinib tolerance, although additional ferroptosis-specific assays would further strengthen this conclusion.

Natural products and traditional Chinese medicine monomers remain useful sources for anti-tumor drug discovery because of their structural diversity and biological activities [33]. These compounds may enhance the efficacy of anti-tumor drugs through multiple mechanisms, such as inhibiting drug efflux pumps, restoring apoptotic balance, interfering with DNA repair pathways, and regulating the tumor immune microenvironment [34, 35]. In this study, molecular docking screening of 310 traditional Chinese medicine monomers, together with cytotoxicity assays and biophysical validation, identified HA-1 as a candidate compound targeting ALDH1L2. SPR confirmed the direct binding of HA-1 to ALDH1L2, and Western blot and CETSA further indicated that HA-1 reduced ALDH1L2 protein abundance and altered its thermal stability. These results suggest that HA-1 may affect ALDH1L2 protein stability, although the precise mechanism of ALDH1L2 reduction still requires further study.

Functional experiments showed that HA-1 counteracted the sunitinib tolerance associated with ALDH1L2 overexpression and restored ferroptosis-related responses, including impaired organoid growth, increased ROS levels, reduced GPX4/SLC7A11 expression, decreased GSH/GSSG ratio, and increased NADP+/NADPH, Fe2 +, and MDA levels. HA-1 alone showed a moderate inhibitory effect on RCC organoids, while the combination of HA-1 and sunitinib produced stronger growth inhibition and more evident ferroptosis-associated changes than sunitinib alone. HA-1 has been reported to possess anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory activities [36, 37]. Our findings add preliminary evidence that HA-1 may modulate the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib by targeting ALDH1L2, but its therapeutic value still needs further validation. Compared with traditional targeted drugs, traditional Chinese medicine monomers are widely sourced and structurally diverse [38]. The binding of HA-1 to ALDH1L2 and its effect on ferroptosis-related responses suggest that HA-1 may be a useful candidate for further preclinical evaluation.

We used RCC organoids together with organoid-derived xenograft models to examine the role of ALDH1L2 in vitro and in vivo. RCC organoids, as a 3D in vitro model, retain some histological features, cellular heterogeneity, and biological behavior of the primary tumor [39]. Their response to sunitinib provided an in vitro platform for studying drug response-related mechanisms. The organoid-derived xenograft model further supported the regulatory role of ALDH1L2 in vivo [40, 41]. In this model, ALDH1L2 overexpression reduced the antitumor effect of sunitinib, decreased tumor ROS accumulation, and partly restored GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression. The combined use of organoids and xenograft models helps compensate for some limitations of traditional cell line models and provides a useful experimental system for studying RCC drug response.

At the clinical level, the findings of this study have potential translational implications. ALDH1L2 may serve as a molecule associated with RCC progression and reduced sunitinib sensitivity in experimental models, but it should not yet be regarded as a validated clinical biomarker for selecting patients treated with sunitinib. HA-1, as a candidate compound targeting ALDH1L2, may be further evaluated in combination with sunitinib. In addition, modulation of ferroptosis-related responses may represent a potential strategy to improve sunitinib response in RCC models with high ALDH1L2 expression. Further studies using larger patient-derived organoid cohorts, clinically annotated treatment-response samples, and in vivo combination-treatment models are needed before clinical application can be considered.

This study has several limitations. As a metabolic enzyme, the specific molecular details by which ALDH1L2 regulates GPX4 and SLC7A11 remain to be explored, and whether this effect involves specific metabolites or signaling pathways requires further investigation. Although Fe2 + and MDA assays strengthened the evidence for iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, additional assays, such as lipid ROS detection or ferroptosis inhibitor rescue, would further define the contribution of ferroptosis. The TCGA-KIRC analysis lacks detailed sunitinib treatment-response information, and therefore the clinical predictive value of ALDH1L2 for sunitinib response remains uncertain. The number of RCC organoid lines was also limited, and larger patient-derived organoid cohorts are needed. Finally, the pharmacokinetic properties and potential toxicity of HA-1 still need to be comprehensively assessed in broader preclinical studies.

Conclusion

Our study supports the idea that ALDH1L2 contributes to reduced sunitinib sensitivity in RCC organoids, at least partly by limiting ferroptosis-associated oxidative and lipid peroxidation responses. HA-1 was identified as a candidate compound targeting ALDH1L2 and may improve the response of RCC organoids to sunitinib. These findings support further evaluation of the ALDH1L2-related ferroptosis response and HA-1-based combination strategies in RCC.
