Controlled polymerization of methyl acrylate in the presence of copper MOF (Basolite®C300): strategy and characterization
Authors: Edina Rusen, Cristina Stavarache, Aurel Diacon, Bogdan Vasile, Alexandra Mocanu, Oana Brincoveanu, Gabriela Toader, Cosmin Romanitan, Adina Boldeiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The controlled polymerization of methyl acrylate (MA) in the presence of Basolite copper C300 (MOF@Cu) is presented in the present study. MOF@Cu plays a single role, acting as a cocatalyst that is reduced by the copper wire or light (red LED) to produce Cu(I) ions necessary to activate the controlled polymerization. Thus, the polymerization in the presence of Cu(0) wire and MOF@Cu can follow two reaction pathways: SARA-ATRP or SET-LRP. Based on the kinetics investigation, the SET-LRP route is supported by GPC, UV-Vis, and 1H NMR analyses. The percentage of the living chain (26%) was determined using 1H NMR. Under red LED irradiation, photoinduced polymerization leads to the formation of copper nanoparticles, as characterized by BFTEM. Also, using the same initiation procedure, an emulsion was obtained directly via polymerization, and the particles were investigated by SEM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57663-3.

Introduction

Reversible-deactivation radical polymerization (RDRP) is one of the most modern polymerization techniques1,2. Using this method, polymers with complex architecture can be obtained3–5. From this class of polymerization, it is important to remember atom transfer polymerization (ATRP)6,7, nitroxide-mediated polymerization (NMP)8,9, and reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT)10,11. It can also consider the derivative methods of previously presented types of polymerizations, such as Activator Regenerated by Electron Transfer (ARGET)12–14, Supplemental Activation Reducing Agent (SARA)15,16, Single-Electron Transfer Living Radical Polymerization (SET-LRP)17–19, etc. Reducing agents such as ascorbic acid (AsA), tin(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (Sn(EH)2), hydrazine, glucose, sodium sulphite, iodide ions, phenol derivatives, etc., are used to obtain copper (I) from the copper (II) ions20,21. It is well known that copper(I) acts as an activator in ATRP polymerization. The requirement for such polymerization is that the reducing agent consistently interacts with Cu(II) ions and Pn-X (in SARA ATRP).

More recently, our group published studies22–24 regarding the polymerization of methyl methacrylate (MMA) in the presence of halogenated organic derivatives/polymers and copper wire. The mechanism can follow two distinct pathways: one is SARA-ATRP, as highlighted by Matyjaszewski[25,26,27, and the other is SET-LRP, sustained by Percec28,29. The percentage of both mechanisms has been determined.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are hybrid materials that combine inorganic and organic components, characterized by their porous, crystalline, and network-like structures30,31. These frameworks are constructed by coordinating metal ions or clusters with organic ligands. The specific coordination geometries of the metals and ligands influence their diverse periodic arrangements and porosity levels32. A variety of metals and ligands can be employed to design MOFs with tailored properties, making them suitable for applications such as industrial use (gas separation, storage, and adsorption), biomedical (drug delivery, bioimaging, phototherapy), and environmental applications (catalysis)33–35.

Using MOFs in RDRP is valuable because of their particle size of a few hundred nanometres; the MOF catalyst can be easily collected by centrifugation and reused for other polymerizations. Additionally, copper-based MOFs can play a dual role, serving as both catalysts and ligands.

As previously presented, the mechanism in the presence of a copper wire could be SARA-ATRP or SET-LRP. The main differences between the suggested mechanisms consist of the following36:

❖ in the case of SARA-ATRP, Cu (I) is the main activator, and Cu (0) is a supplemental and reducing agent, in contrast to SET-LRP, where Cu (0) is the activator of the alkyl halide;

❖ comproportionation is specific for SARA-ATRP, and disproportionation for SET-LRP;

❖ in the case of SARA-ATRP, there is limited termination, versus SET-LRP, where zero termination and end group functionality are 100%;

❖ activation step via Inner Sphere Electron Transfer (ISET) for SARA-ATRP, and Outer Sphere Electron Transfer (OSET) for SET-LRP;

Both mechanisms are characterized by primary deactivation by Cu(II) and negligible deactivation by Cu(I), as shown in Scheme 1.

Also, as a reducing agent for Cu (II), it could be an external stimulus, like light37. It is well known in the specific literature concerning the photo-induced ATRP reactions38–40.

Thus, the second objective of our study was to highlight the importance of the red LED (λ = 630 nm) (1 mW/cm2) on the polymerization of methyl acrylate (MA) in the presence of MOF@Cu. The polymerization in the presence of light is purer than in the presence of Cu(0), because it is not necessary to remove the copper wire from the reaction. The proposed mechanism for photoinduced ATRP is shown in Scheme 2. This mechanism is a hybrid of ICAR ATRP and ARGET ATRP. Because radiation induces the reduction of X-Cu(II)/L to Cu(I)/L, the mechanism resembles ARGET ATRP. However, a halogen radical is formed, which resembles the generation of radicals in ICAR ATRP37.

The present study aims to synthesize and characterize the MA polymerization in the presence of the MOF@Cu. The polymerizations were induced thermally in the presence of the copper wire or by using a red LED. Also, the kinetics of the polymerization were presented and followed a SET-LRP-type reaction, as determined from GPC, UV-Vis, and1 H NMR characterizations. The percentage of the living chain was determined using1 H NMR. Emulsion polymerization was conducted using a red LED, and the resulting particles were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Also, in the case of light-induced solution polymerization, Cu nanoparticles were generated and characterized by BFTEM. MOF@Cu plays a single role as a cocatalyst and is reduced by the copper wire to produce Cu(I) ions necessary to activate the polymerization.

Materials and methods

Materials

Methyl acrylate (MA) and methyl methacrylate (Sigma-Aldrich) were purified by vacuum distillation under reduced pressure in a stream of argon gas. Cu (0) wire with a diameter of 0.025 mm was obtained from Alfa Aesar. Tetrahydrofuran (THF) (Aldrich), dimethyl formamide (DMF) (Aldrich) were purified by distillation, 2,2’-bipyridine (BiPy) (Aldrich), N, N, N′, N′′, N′′-pentamethyldiethylenetriamine (PMDETA) (Aldrich), CuCl (Aldrich), methanol (Aldrich), diethyl ether (EtOEt) (Aldrich), ethyl α-bromoisobutyrate (EBiB) (Aldrich), Copper benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylate, Cu-BTC MOF, HKUST-1, Basolite C300 (MOF@Cu) (Aldrich), and sodium dodecyl sulphate (Merck) were used as received. In the case of Basolite C300, the terminology used in this paper was MOF@Cu.

Experiments

Polymerization of MA in the presence of different concentrations of MOF@Cu and copper wire

In a 10 mL glass vial, 4 mL (4.4 × 10− 2 mol) of MA was added, followed by 3 mL of DMF and different amounts of MOF@Cu, namely 0.001 g (1.65 × 10− 6 mol) (MOF 1@Cu), 0.002 g (3.3 × 10− 6 mol) (MOF 2@Cu), and 0.003 g (4.95 × 10− 6 mol) (MOF 3@Cu). A magnetic PTFE stirring bar, wrapped with 5 cm of copper wire, was subsequently added to the vial in the presence of 10 mmol PMDETA. The vial was bubbling with nitrogen for 10 min and then sealed with a silicon rubber septum. Afterward, 0.06 mL of EBiB (3 × 10− 4 mol) was injected into the vial, and the polymerization was allowed to proceed at 70 °C under stirring at 200 rpm. Samples were taken periodically under a nitrogen blanket for gravimetric and GPC analysis. To establish the conversion, the collected samples were precipitated in methanol, filtered, and dried under reduced pressure.

Polymerization of MA in the presence of MOF 2@Cu and red light

The reaction is presented as in 2.2.1, but using a red LED (40 W) instead of a copper wire. The processing of the samples has been identical to that described in Sect. 2.2.1.

Chain extension of the poly (MA) with MMA obtained at points 2.2.1, 2.2.2

The polymer synthesis was performed in DMF using the modified poly MA as the starting material, as described in Sect. 2.2.1 and 2.2.2. Thus, 0.2 g of poly MA was dissolved in 5 mL DMF, and 2 mL (1.88 × 10− 2 mol) MMA was added. The mixture was subjected to nitrogen purging, after which 0.0003 g CuCl (3 × 10− 6 mol) and 0.0005 g BiPy (3.2 × 10− 6 mol) were added. The reaction vial was sealed and heated at 80 °C under stirring for 2 h. Then, the reaction mixture was precipitated in methanol, filtered, and dried under vacuum until a constant mass was achieved.

Emulsion polymerization of MA in the presence of red LED

In a 25 mL glass vial containing 10 mL of water, 0.02 g (6.93 × 10− 5 mol) sodium dodecyl sulfate was dissolved. After that, 4 mL (4.4 × 10− 2 mol) of MA was added, followed by 0.003 g (4.95 × 10− 6 mol) of MOF@Cu and 0.17 mL (10 mmol) of PMDETA. The mixture was purged with nitrogen. After that, 0.06 mL (3 × 10− 4 mol) of EBiB was injected into the vial, and the polymerization was allowed to proceed under a red LED with stirring at 200 rpm for 24 h.

Characterization

The molecular weights of the obtained polymers were analyzed using a PL-GPC 50 Integrated GPC/SEC System (Agilent Technologies) with a 1 mL/min THF flow rate and a column oven temperature of 40 °C, employing polystyrene as a standard and a refractive index detector. The GPC equipment was calibrated using polystyrene standards (molecular weights ranging from 162 to 10 000 000 g/mol, in 18 points). Two types of columns have been used: MIXED-C: Range: 200 − 106 g/mol and Low Molecular Weight (100–104 g/mol).

The HNMR analyses were performed on a Bruker Advance III HD 600 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany), with a resonance frequency of 600.12 MHz for the 1 H nucleus, equipped with a 5 mm multinuclear direct detection z-gradient probe head (BBI). Samples were analyzed using CDCl3 as a solvent.

The UV-VIS spectra were recorded on a UV-VIS-NIR spectrophotometer (UV 3600, Shimadzu) equipped with a quartz cell having a 10 mm light path. The synthesis protocol used was the same as presented in 2.2.1. section (MOF 1@Cu).

Bright-field (BFTEM) Transmission Electron Microscopy images were acquired using an aberration-corrected 80–200 kV Titan Themis transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Sample preparation involved placing a small amount of the dried material onto a 400-mesh holey coated Ni grid. Ni grid was chosen due to the nature of the particles. EDS analysis was also performed in STEM (scanning transmission electron microscopy) mode on a single particle to see the composition.

The morphology of the polymer was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Nova NanoSEM 630 instrument (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV.The samples’ hydrodynamic diameter and surface charge were analyzed using a Beckman Coulter Delsa™Nano C instrument, employing dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering (ELS), respectively. A dual 30 mW laser diode was used to illuminate the particles, generating time-dependent fluctuations in light intensity. For size determination, scattered light was detected at an angle of 165°, while zeta potential measurements were conducted at 15°, using appropriately diluted samples. A highly sensitive detector captured the scattered light signals. Data were processed using Delsa™Nano software version 3.73. Before measurement, the sample was diluted with deionized water at a 1:5 volume ratio (polymer dispersion: deionized water) and subjected to ultrasonication for 10 min to ensure proper dispersion. All measurements were conducted at room temperature, with each sample analyzed in triplicate.

During the measurements of the powder sample, the scan was initiated in θ/2θ mode (i.e., the source and detector arms are coupled). In both configurations, the scan step was 0.01˚/min, while the speed was set as 8˚/min.

The linear relationship of ln (M0/M) versus time provides initial evidence of controlled polymerization in all cases.

To emphasize the controlled nature of the polymerization, Fig. 2 shows how molecular weight and polydispersity index depend on conversion. The polydispersity index values range from 1.2 to 1.48. Additionally, molecular weight increases as conversion progresses. Regarding the molecular weight dependence on conversion, two zones are observed: one with Mn values higher than the theoretical ones (slow initiation)43, and the second with smaller values (irreversible transfer)44. This different behavior could be explained by the different concentration of MOF@Cu used. The slow initiation is described by the failure to initiate all the monomer molecules. Irreversible transfer is possible due to the loss of equilibrium between copper species. Probably, Cu(0) concentration is much higher than that of Cu (II) provided by the MOF@Cu, maybe due to the high reaction rate. In this context, for the MOF 1@Cu system, control of polymerization is lacking. Also, Fig. 3 presents the GPC traces for the sample from all polymerizations.

To emphasize the SET-LRP polymerization, the next step includes characterizing the polymerization using ¹H NMR. SET-LRP involves heterogeneous outer-sphere electron transfer (OSET) by nascent Cu(0), leading to faster activation of alkyl halides, a higher concentration of active radicals, and, consequently, more radical coupling and termination (Fig. 6). The percentage of the chain end functionality (26%) and molecular weight (9 764 g/mol) were calculated according to49. Thus, in the case of SET-LRP, a higher concentration of active radicals leads to a significantly greater rate of radical coupling and bimolecular termination. The spectrum obtained from PMA (Fig. 6), prepared in DMF, matches the spectrum of PMA prepared in DMSO49. The resonance of methyl protons from the ester group of the repetitive units is assigned at 3.65 ppm, while the ones from the terminal bromo chain-end are at 3.77 ppm. The signal of the methyl protons of the OCH2CH3 group is at 1.24 ppm. The low intensity signal at 4.24 ppm corresponds to the -CH-Br of the active terminal bromo chain-end. To evaluate the chain‑end functionality, a ¹H NMR spectrum was recorded (Figure S2), yielding a value of 98%. When compared with the polymerization conducted in the presence of copper wire, the higher conversion attained and it is accompanied by an increased extent of bimolecular termination, both processes being further promoted by the elevated reaction temperature. Photo-induced reaction can often be conducted at or near ambient temperatures, which minimizes thermally driven side reactions, chain transfer, and irreversible bimolecular termination57.

Moreover, the polymerizations displayed temporal control behavior in response to light-on/off change cycles (Fig. 9). In the absence of the LED, the conversions are limited. These results demonstrate the efficient nature of the MOF 2@Cu catalyst in achieving well-controlled characteristics through temporal control of the polymerization.

As a direct application of red LED-induced polymerization, an emulsion was obtained with a conversion of 90%, as presented in Fig. 12. The mean diameters of the particles are around 230 nm. To confirm the controlled characteristics of polymerization, GPC analysis was also performed. The molecular weight obtained was 574 200 g/mol, with Đ = 1.4. Because SEM provides only localized surface information, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted to obtain a statistically representative assessment of the dispersion. As shown in Fig. 12d, the particles exhibit an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 250 nm. The emulsion displays good colloidal stability, as indicated by the zeta potential value of − 28.56 mV, which is sufficiently negative to ensure electrostatic repulsion between particles. Also, by 1H NMR (Figure S3), the chain end functionality was estimated to be around 67%. This value is consistent with the sequence of physicochemical processes occurring in the system—namely nucleation, monomer diffusion, and particle growth—which collectively govern the chain‑end fidelity60.

Conclusions

The present study, for the first time in the specific literature, shows the polymerization of MA in the presence of MOF@Cu, using copper wire or a red LED as a cocatalyst. In the case of copper wire, the kinetics exhibit controlled polymerization, as evidenced by the linear dependence of molecular weights on conversion, narrow polymerization indices, and the possibility of block copolymer synthesis. The characterizations were made using GPC, UV-Vis, 1H NMR. In the case of the red LED, polymerization proceeds more slowly than with copper wire, and LED-based temporal control of the reaction was demonstrated. In this case, copper particles with a diameter of around 250 nm were generated and characterized by BFTEM and UV-Vis. As a practical application of polymerization initiated by a red LED, an emulsion has been obtained with Mn = 574 200 g/mol and Đ = 1.4.
