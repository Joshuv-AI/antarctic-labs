1.0 INTRODUCTION
1.1 Scope and Objectives
Prior research has shown that radio frequency identification (RFID) technology offers potential
benefits for streamlining inventory management amidst rising production levels across the
NNSA/DOE complex, yet it also presents substantial risks. RFID presents challenges never faced
by LANL but also significant opportunities for accelerated inventory identification, real-time
locating of assets, and minimized radiation exposure. This risk assessment evaluates the
implementation of RFID technology at different Technical Area 55 (TA-55) locations, primarily
withing the RLUOB basement and PF-4.

1.2 Readers
The types of RFID readers to be employed include mobile ultra-high frequency readers (Figure 3),
fixed ultra-high frequency readers, and fixed ultrawide band readers. The specific brands and
models of the tags and readers are outside the scope of this assessment.

1.3 Purpose of the Risk Management Plan
A risk is an event that has a probability of occurring and an impact if it occurs, be it positive or
negative, as shown in Figure 4. The process of risk management is used to identify, access, and
respond to risks inherited from specific projects [4]. Risk is often misunderstood as solely
representing threats, whereas it encompasses both threats and opportunities. This risk
management plan aims to outline how threats and opportunities associated with this project will
be managed and monitored while taking into consideration international RFID standards,
TEMPEST guidelines, and outside resources.

4.1 Plan Risk Management
4.1.1 Context
Context refers to the environment in which the team operates, affecting its ability to achieve and
complete objectives. We identified two types of contexts: internal and external. Internal context
arises from within the organization, in our case, LANL, while external context originates from
forces outside or beyond LANL’s control.

For the internal context, several factors were identified: limited RFID capabilities (no Bluetooth,
Wi-Fi, or microphone configurations), limited IT support, a historical desire for proper RFID
implementation in the lab, the simultaneous implementation of different pilots, funding
constraints, unclear pathways to meet security and cybersecurity requirements, and the need for
compatibility with existing infrastructure at TA-55, specifically PF-4, along with the challenges of
integrating additional infrastructure into these facilities.

For our external context, we noted several factors: the constant evolving industry of RFID
technology creates the need for adaptation and the risk of obsolescence of established
technology.

Given the unique context of the project, a multitude of unprecedented threats and opportunities
have emerged that have not previously been addressed at LANL.

4.1.2 Risk Categories and Context
Several categories were chosen for the identification of threats and opportunities derived from
risks. These categories were chosen because they are ALDWP and RMSO standard categories that
fit our risk assessment criteria. The categories chosen for threats derived from risk are Schedule,
Safety, Budget, Programmatic, Security, Facilities, Longevity/Maintainability, and Conduct of
Operations (CONOPS).

The number of opportunities derived from risks tends to always be lower compared to threats;
thus, we chose to define fewer categories for opportunities. The categories chosen for
opportunities are Inventory/Traceability, Analytics, Work Quality, Scalability, Worker Interaction,
Worker Safety, and Worker Scope. The proceeding table gives a definition of each of the chosen
risk assessment categories:

4.2 Identify Risks
 RFID is an ever-evolving technology, as its hardware and software are continually updated. This
technology is new to secure areas at LANL such as those in TA-55, which is why it is crucial to
understand all the risks it may pose before its introduction. It is also important to understand
that emergent risks are always a possibility and that a small number of risks can emerge in later
stages.

The RFID technologies utilized in this project encompass two primary risk categories: RFID risks
and TA-55 risks. Each of these categories is further subdivided into two distinct areas: threats and
opportunities. Both RFID and TA-55-specific risks were identified separately but were combined
into a single register for the purposes of this analysis.

To identify risks, we employed a risk questionnaire, SWOT analysis, and risk register. All identified
risks were documented in a risk register. Additionally, we used a risk matrix to visualize both
threats and opportunities from the register of risks. This section will cover each of the risk
assessment methods in detail.

4.2.1 Risk Questionnaire
A risk questionnaire is normally used to review previously encountered risks on similar efforts.
Typically, this includes risk libraries, checklists from similar efforts, and risk checklists from
external resources. For this new project, we are aiming at utilizing a risk questionnaire created
by The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley.

From the risk questionnaire, four categories were focused on:

 • Ownership of the risk (who takes the final decision)
 • Organization of risk (Is there a main office or organization that manages risks across the
 organization)
 • Methodology (What risks methods/equations/technologies are used)
 • Key performance indicators (How is return on investment measured)

4.2.2 SWOT Analysis
A SWOT analysis serves as a strategic planning tool employed to evaluate the internal strengths,
weaknesses, opportunities, and threats inherent in a project or business venture. Its primary
objective is to systematically assess both internal capabilities and external factors that could
influence the achievement of its objectives.

By delineating areas of organizational strength, areas requiring improvement, potential avenues
for expansion, and risks needing mitigation, the SWOT analysis offers a structured framework for
informed decision-making and strategic planning.

Conducting a SWOT analysis tailored specifically to RFID implementation has provided us with a
thorough comprehension of the project's internal dynamics and external environmental factors.
This analysis has enabled us to identify and scrutinize potential risks within the RFID pilot
program, facilitating the development of a comprehensive risk register and risk profile matrix.

 Figure 5. SWOT Analysis Diagram

The SWOT analysis revealed that the project’s greatest strength lies in the improvement of
worker safety by removing direct line of sight and physical contact for taking inventory, as well
as approval of the Zebra M3330R handheld RFID reader for use within PF-4. However, a
significant weakness was identified: ensuring TA-55 workers are fully trained for correct use of

Page 10 of 38

RFID technology. On a positive note, a previous weakness was mitigated by tests showing
radiation resilience in current RFID tags. Additionally, the project’s most promising opportunity
is its potential to support LANL’s 30 pits per year mission.

4.2.4 Risk Register
To document risks as they were identified, our team created a threat log and an opportunity log
in Microsoft Excel, which can be found in Appendices 5 and 6. These logs were then used to
establish a comprehensive risk register regarding both RFID and PF-4 threats and opportunities,
which can be found in Appendix 4. Typically, the software used to create risk registers in the
ALDWP organization is ARM, but to simplify the process, the risk register will be in the format of
a Microsoft Word table.

Each of the risk registers includes the ID number for each threat and opportunity, which
corresponds with the risk assessment matrix. If the ID number begins with 'T', it indicates a threat;
if it starts with 'O', it signifies an opportunity; and if it commences with 'K', it denotes a key risk.
The register also includes a risk statement, which is a concise expression that describes the risk
event's key causes and, finally, its estimated key consequences. Along with this, the register has
a possible response or mitigation for each risk and, finally, its impact, probability, and risk score.

4.2.5 Risk Profile Matrix
The risk profile matrix is a summary of the identified risks organized on a 5x5 matrix, which is
further explained in Appendix 3. As previously stated, each plotted risk is assigned an ID value,
which can be referenced in the corresponding risk register found in Appendix 4 for further details.
By plotting each risk on a matrix heat map, we can visualize and prioritize them. The plot reveals
that while each risk has been assigned a score subject to interpretation, it also demonstrates that
regardless of score variability, these risks collectively occupy a significant area that does not
significantly alter their threat level when the impact or probability is adjusted.

4.3 Analyze and Evaluate
Not all risks are equal, as depicted in Figures 6 and 7. Risks classified in the red zone present the
most daunting challenges to implementation in TA-55 and thus require immediate attention.
Risks in the yellow zone should be closely monitored and addressed promptly after mitigating
those in the red zone. Risks in the green zone, while still requiring monitoring, are either unlikely
to occur or have a minimal impact on the planned implementation.

Similarly, the opportunities identified in this assessment are categorized using three
progressively lighter shades of blue instead of the traditional red, yellow, and green. The darker
blues indicate more promising and impactful opportunities.

The two matrix heat maps offer valuable insights into the threats and opportunities associated
with the planned implementation. The threat heat map also allows for a deeper understanding
of the performance of mitigations that have been implemented, as the matrix is constantly
evolving. Due to mitigations implemented by previous pilots, our threat matrix appears to have
very little high impact and probability risks. As mitigations continue, more of these said threats
will move into a more favorable range. In addition to the threats, this project reveals a significant
proportion of promising opportunities.

5.0 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

5.1 Conclusion
The second version of the RFID assessment has been completed, with some missing steps that
will be further developed as the project progresses. Within the planning itself, 40 threats were
identified, and possible mitigations were discussed. These mitigation measures will be further
developed by SMEs as the project progresses. In total, seven opportunities were also found, and
some steps to attempt to get the best out of these opportunities have been discussed. The risk
assessment mainly concentrated on planning, identifying, analyzing, and evaluating risks. Once
more approvals are acquired, further steps will be implemented, like monitoring and the proper
implementation of responses. The risk assessment process will continue to evolve as the project
progresses, with regular updates and adjustments as needed. The goal is to ensure that any
potential risks are managed effectively throughout the project lifecycle.

5.2 Future Work
This project remains in progress and will necessitate ongoing refinement and future
enhancements. Risk analysis is an iterative process that cannot be considered complete,
particularly given the continued development of the project which is expected to introduce new
and evolving risks. Furthermore, due to the presence of multiple facilities within TA-55, it is
essential to conduct risk monitoring on a per-building basis to ensure a comprehensive
understanding of risk across the site. As the project continues to demonstrate its value, it is
anticipated that additional divisions and operational areas will express interest in the
deployment of RFID technology, thereby significantly expanding the project’s overall scope.

We would like to thank NEN-1, and more specifically Brendon Parsons and Alessandro Cattaneo,
for providing academic documentation on RFID technology and for their guidance and expertise
on risks involving RFID systems.
