Schottky Barrier as Photodetector Based on Nanostructured Cd x Zn1–x S along with Switchable Photocurrent Generation
Authors: Priyanka Das, Satyajit Saha, Amit Kumar Bhunia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this report, Schottky barrier devices (SBDs) have been fabricated based on CdS nanorods (NRs), ZnS nanoparticles (NPs), and Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs + NRs) using a cost-effective spin coating technique with the use of metallic “Ag”. Photodetection properties based on fabricated Schottky barriers of CdS NRs/Ag, Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs+NRs)/Ag and ZnS NPs/Ag have been studied. Before device fabrication, a simple chemical precipitation method has been used for the growth of CdS NRs, Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs + NRs) and ZnS NPs. Formations of nanostructures have been observed through HRTEM and FESEM. The structural and optical analyses of the grown nanostructures have been computed from XRD, XPS, UV–vis spectra and Raman studies. The responsivity, detectivity,and response time of the three photodetectors based on SBDs have been measured and compared. The photodetectors exhibited a high rectification ratio up to 106 within ±1 V in the dark and a sharp photoresponse behavior under light illumination. Photoresponse analysis showed that a photodetector based on SBD of CdS NRs was highly sensitive to light illumination with detectivity ≈2.75 × 105 jones and responsivity ≈2.2 × 10–6 A/W. The above parameters were reduced for photodetector based on SBD of ZnS NPs. Also, switchable photocurrent generation have been observed for three photodetectors. The photoresponse rise/fall times of CdS NRs based SBD was ≈187/194 ms and showed fast response speed compared to other two photodetectors based on SBDs.

Introduction

A photodetector (PD) is a device that detects the light signals and converts them into electrical signals. PD technology based on nanostructured materials is gaining importance due to various applications, which include medical diagnostic equipment, communication, and environmental monitoring. , Cadmium chalcogenide materials have band gaps with 1.4 eV < E g < 2.4 eV and a reasonable overlap with the solar spectrum. , The optical, electrical, and structural studies are essential in characterizing the material for its applications in optoelectronic devices. Photovoltaic (PV) devices are appropriate choices for clean and renewable power sources. , One example is solar cells and converts solar energy into electrical energy with high conversion efficiency. The huge concern about energy sources has created an urge in the effort to explore solar cells. Metal/semiconductor (MS) connections are widely utilized in solar cells, light detectors, and integrated circuits, as is well-known. One of the most basic MS contact devices is the rectifying Schottky barrier diode. , Semiconducting thin films are very attractive for optical and optoelectronic applications. , Deposition of a metal onto a semiconductor substrate/thin film results in the creation of a Schottky junction, which affects charge carrier dynamics by forming an energy barrier. By influencing vital performance metrics including responsivity, detectivity, and response speed, this barrieralso referred to as the Schottky barrierplays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of photodetectors. − The presence of nanostructured semiconducting material layers between metal and semiconductor might alter the electrical characteristics of metal–semiconductor devices and affect the interface states. − Schottky barrier devices (SBDs) are very attractive for photodetection properties and hence fabrication of such photodetectors is important. Nanostructured semiconducting cadmium sulfide (CdS) is very attractive in optoelectronics because of its possible application in solar cells, photodetectors, lasers, LEDs, high density magnetic information storage and many others in semiconductor industries. − Cd x Zn1–x S NPs are active materials whose band gap is varied with the change of x, and simultaneously the band gap can be varied, keeping x fixed by varying the size of the nanocrystal suitable to be used in the visible region. , CdS is appropriate for photodetection up to the middle of the visible region. But Cd0.5Zn0.5S and ZnS cover from the middle of the visible region to the UV region. The growth technique of Cd x Zn1–x S is relatively cheap and hence cost-effective chemical reduction is followed to grow nanostructured Cd x Zn1–x S at room temperature. In the present case three values of x (x = 1, x = 0.5 and x = 0) for the nanostructured Cd x Zn1–x S are preferred. Hence three nanostructures, CdS NRs, Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs+NRs) and ZnS NPs, correspond to x = 1, x = 0.5 and x = 0 in Cd x Zn1–x S. Apart from CdS, this work is also extended to grow and characterize Cd0.5Zn0.5S as well as ZnS nanostructures. The characteristic absorption of light is mainly in the visible region for CdS NRs. The main aim is to grow the nanomaterials in a cost-effective way and fabricate the Schottky devices as photodetectors from the middle of the visible region to the ultraviolet region also in a cost-effective way. Though works have been done introducing Zn in Cd x Zn1–x S to fabricate photodetectors from the middle of the visible region to the infrared region, no such systematic study on the photodetection and switching ability of different compositions has been done from the middle of the visible region to the ultraviolet region. This work demonstrates how the photodetection properties change with a systematic change of composition in Cd x Zn1–x S, with a change of nanostructure and with a change of strain. As the band gap increases with a change of composition as well as quantum confinement, the photodetection properties have been systematically studied and compared from the visible region to the ultraviolet region. The present study demonstrates the photodetection from the middle of the visible region to the ultraviolet region by changing the composition from CdS to ZnS through Cd x Zn1–x S by fabricating Schottky devices in a cost-effective way. Thus, Cd x Zn1–x S is selected so that any composition between CdS and ZnS can be grown by varying x. The aim was to systematically study photodetectivity and switching activity by varying x = 1, x = 0.5, and x = 0 corresponding to the middle of the visible region to the ultraviolet region. The influence of nanostructure, strain, and defect states on the performance of these devices has been determined. It is expected that inexpensive SBD photodetectors will have great potential for application in high speed sensing devices.

In the present work, PDs are fabricated based on Schottky devices of CdS NRs/Ag and Cd0.5Zn0.5S NPs/Ag as well as ZnS NPs/Ag using a cost-effective spin-coating method. A comparative study of the photodetection of Schottky devices based on CdS NRs or Cd0.5Zn0.5S NPs and ZnS NPs is done. The parameters used for comparison are resistivity, detectivity, growth time, and decay time and hence determine which photodetector will be an effective one out of the grown devices. It is expected that inexpensive SBD photodetectors will have great potential for application in high-speed sensing devices.

Experimental Details, Device Fabrication and Instrumentations

Synthesis by Chemical Reduction Route

Here we used a simple and cost-effective Chemical Reduction Route to prepare Cd x Zn1–x S by varying the proportion of the starting materials, i.e CdCl2 and ZnCl2. The grown nanomaterials are observed to be CdS, Cd0.5Zn0.5S and ZnS. A stoichiometric amount of CdCl2 or ZnCl2 is added to a beaker containing THF as capping agent which opposes Ostwald ripening and prevents formation of larger crystallites. A white coloration appears in both cases on stirring with a magnetic stirrer. After stirring the solutions for few minutes, appropriate amounts of sulfur powder are placed in the beakers. NaBH4, which acts as reducing agent, is then added to each solution. The suspension turns light yellow with the use of CdCl2 while it turns white with the use of ZnCl2. Each separate white or yellow solution mixture is stirred for 16 h. The yellow precipitate CdS is formed from the mixture containing CdCl2, while the white precipitate ZnS is obtained from the mixture containing ZnCl2. The precipitates are collected after centrifugation and washed with DI water to remove any impurities. Finally, the samples are dried at room temperature for 24 h. The starting materials are CdCl2 as well as ZnCl2 in stoichiometric amount to grow Cd0.5Zn0.5S following the same steps as in the case of CdS or ZnS. The chemical reaction for the formation of CdS is 1 CdCl2↔Cd2++2Cl− 2 2NaBH4+2Cl−→2NaCl+B2H6+2H+2e− 3 2H+S→H2S 4 H2S+Cd2++2e−→CdS+H2

There is structural change as the samples are made from CdS to ZnS by varying x in Cd x Zn1–x S. Phase engineering is involved while varying the crystal structure of Cd x Zn1–x S. The material’s crystal phase change depends on the relative amounts of cadmium and zinc. CdS forms in a hexagonal wurtzite phase, but ZnS forms in a cubic zinc blende phase. By varying the x value in Cd x Zn1–x S there is a gradual transition between these phases.

Fabrication of the Schottky Barrier devices

The spin coating method is cheap and reliable for the fabrication of devices. The rotational speed is maintained constant for each sample. The thicknesses of films of different nanomaterials CdS, Cd x Zn1– xS and ZnS on ITO coated glass are maintained constant by varying the time of deposition. The grown nanomaterials with varying composition are applied for the fabrication of Schottky junction based photodetectors over a wide wavelength region. The nanostructures of different compositions of Cd x Zn1–x S are spin coated on ITO coated glass. Initially, a 1 × 1 cm2 dimension of ITO coated glass as a substrate was cleaned with acetone, then ethanol, and then DI water several times using ultrasonication. A small part of the ITO coated glass was covered with Teflon tape as shown in Figure . The nanoparticles grown (50 mg of Cd x Zn1–x S NPs) are dispersed in ethanol and ultrasonicated for 40 min. Then the dispersed solution has been spin coated on ITO coated glass (rest of the Teflon tape coated, i.e. free exposed part) at 3000 rpm for 35 s as shown in Figure . The Cd x Zn1–x S NPs coated ITO coated glass was dried for 35 min at 65 °C. The same process has been repeated for 3 samples, i.e. CdS NRs, Cd0.5Zn0.5S NPs and ZnS NPs, taking same amount of sample in ethanol for each case. Finally, the nanostructured Cd x Zn1–x S thin film was annealed at 60 °C for 40 min in an N2 atmosphere. The covered Teflon was removed and ITO of that part was exposed.

Schematic of the different steps for the fabrication of Schottky barrier devices and device structure with bias.

The film thickness has been measured through a surfometer, and the film thickness is on the order of 1000 Å, which is identified by the peak height at the step. The composition has been determined through EDAX and the composition of each nanostructured material is determined. The surface roughness is checked with a surfometer for each composition. The materials CdS, Cd x Zn1–x S and ZnS grown by the chemical reduction route are p type and are verified through hot probe measurement. Also, in the chemical reduction route the starting materials are cadmium chloride, sulfur and THF. THF as solvent acts as a medium. These materials used are not of high purity grade. Each ingredient contain trace impurities of sodium (Na). Sodium borohydride, which acts as a reducing agent, can contain impurities as traces of sodium (Na). Hence, during reaction there is a possibility of Na being present as dopant in grown CdS or Cd x Zn1–x S nanomaterials. Sodium (Na) takes sites of cadmium (Cd) in cadmium sulfide (CdS), and the material becomes a p-type semiconductor. Thus, Na in the lattice introduces a missing valence electron with respect to the site, creating a shallow acceptor defect. From EDX analysis (see section 3.1.1 and section S1 in the Supporting Information), this grown material has a natural tendency to be p-type. Hence, the proposed devices are explained after considering the p-type nature of the grown nanostructures. The up contact has been done with “Ag” by thermal evaporation (Figure ). The silver (Ag) metal contacts are of 1 mm diameter and the back contact was ITO of ITO coated glass. A schematic of the different steps for the fabrication of Schottky barrier devices along with device structure is shown in Figure . The current–voltage characteristics of grown Schottky barrier devices (SBDs) were determined in the dark and under illumination of light. Ag can be thermally evaporated easily by a vacuum evaporating unit on CdS or Cd x Zn1– xS or ZnS for fabrication of Schottky devices. Ag is selected because the work function of the metal (φm) is less than the work function of CdS, Cd x Zn1–x S and ZnS (p-semiconductors) (φs). Hence Ag acts as a Schottky (since φm < φs, p type) contact for all these semiconductors. Also Au is checked in place of Ag as contact for which φm > φs p type. Thus, I–V shows linear variation passing through the origin. Hence gold acts as an Ohmic contact in Au/CdS or Cd x Zn1–x S junctions. Thus, Ag/p-CdS or p-Cd x Zn1–x S junction acts as a Schottky barrier.

Interface engineering focuses on the physical and chemical characteristics at the junction where the material Cd x Zn1– xS meets a metal. Defects at the interface will cause charge carriers to recombine prematurely and thus reduce device efficiency. Lattice mismatch between successive layers will induce strain.

Instrumentations and Device characterization

Using a Rigaku mini flex-II X-ray diffractometer, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the collected powder samples were produced in the angular (2θ) range of 20° to 70° using Cu–Kα radiation and Rigaku software with Windmax/Apps system was used to collect the data. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) uses a field-emission cannon as an electron source to produce high-resolution images of nanostructures and surface morphology. With an operating voltage of 5 kV, FESEM pictures were captured using a JEOL JSM-5800 scanning microscope, OXFORDISIS-300, manufactured in England. The optical absorbance is measured by dispersing the produced nanomaterials in ethanol. The sample dispersed in ethanol is optically absorbed in the 200–900 nm wavelength range using a Cary 5000 UV–Vis–NIR Spectrophotometer. Following drop casting on a carbon-coated Cu-grid and vacuum drying, TEM/HRTEM in situ SAED images of the nanomaterials were obtained using a JEOL-JEM-200 microscope running at 200 kV. A MODELT64000 (Make JobinYvonHoriba, France) Raman spectrometer was used to acquire the Raman spectra, and the excitation source was an argon–krypton mixed ion gas laser. Following coating of a thick film of nanomaterials on a small square glass, XPS spectra were taken using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), model PHI 5000 VERSA PROBE III, ULVAC PHI, USA.

Using an electrical probe station and a Keithley Semiconductor parameter analyzer [4200 SCS] with solar simulator, several characterizations of the manufactured SBDs were performed. The SBDs’ current (I)–voltage (V) properties were noted under reverse bias both in the presence of light and in the absence of it. Response time at the −1 V bias condition was determined by studying the current (I)–time (t) variation using a magnified single pulse. A 500 W mercury bulb is used as the light source to illuminate light. A 500 W mercury bulb was kept at 70 cm away from the device. The incidence was normal and the spot area is 0.5 cm–2. The light falling on the sample was measured by a power meter and was found to be 100 mW cm–2. Responsivity was measured in the presence of light keeping the intensity the same for Schottky barriers based on CdS NRs, Cd0.5Zn0.5S NPs and ZnS NPs.

The compositions of the nanostructures were observed from the EDX spectrum (Figure s1 in section S1 of the Supporting Information). From EDX analysis it is observed that there is no excess of Cd or S in grown CdS. Therefore, the above mechanism is mainly caused by CdS to be p-type. But for ZnS there is a slight excess of zinc relative to sulfur. Commercial zinc chloride typically contains impurities like sodium which possibly takes the place of zinc in the lattice, also creating shallow acceptor defects. Thus, in ZnS though there is a slight stochiometric excess of Zn still it behaves as a p-type semiconductor. But other compositions like Cd0.5Zn0.5S show sulfur excess relative to the counterpart.

The parameters pH and temperature were constant during growth of the different nanomaterials. The capping agent THF plays a role in the formation of nanostructures. A Raman study indicates that lattice strain increases with an increase of Zn content. Therefore, the formation of nanorods in CdS is possible, but with an increase of lattice strain in Cd x Zn1–x S, the nanorods break and convert partly to nanoparticles. Hence, in ZnS, nanoparticles are the only nanostructure possible.

The Raman shift for each composition is determined through a Raman study (Figure g–i). The Raman shift is the difference between the peak energies and the excitation laser energy. A blue shift occurs for Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs+NRs) (311 cm–1), while a red shift occurs for CdS NRs (302 cm–1). Thus, the frequency of phonons interacting with the incident photon is increased. This means the lattice is more strained for Cd0.5Zn0.5S since blue shift occurs. The Raman spectrum of ZnS NPs within 200 to 400 cm–1 is shown in Figure i. Thus, for CdS-based SBPD, the change of photocurrent is large under illumination, and thus photodetection is good but not excellent. Though there is no defect state due to stoichiometric excess, which is confirmed by EDAX and XPS, the defect states at the interface of the metal/semiconductor junction trap the photogenerated carriers significantly. The deviation of binding energy is maximum in ZnS, which leads to a stoichiometric excess of Zn in ZnS. The excess zinc gives rise to defect states within the bandgap and acts as a recombination center. The photogenerated carriers will not be fully transported to the other side but will be trapped by the center. Thus, photocurrent significantly decreases due to the recombination center in ZnS nanostructures as well as defect states at the interface of the ZnS/Ag junction.

Device Analysis

Specific detectivity (detectivity) (D*) and responsivity (R) of the fabricated Schottky barrier are measured from the change of photocurrent under illumination. Responsitvity (R) is a measure of sensitivity of light for a photodetector. It is defined as ratio of photocurrent (Iph) to incident light power Popt and the responsivity (R) defined as , R=IphPopt(A/W) 5 where Iph is the photocurrent density and Popt is the optical power density.

The general expression for specific detectivity (D*) is derived from the noise-equivalent power (NEP). NEP is the incident optical power required to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 1. In term of NEP, the specific detectivity (D*) is defined as 6 D*=√AΔfNEP where NEP=inR and i n is the total noise current. If the noise is dominated by short noise of dark current, in this case 7 D*=R(2eJd)1/2 or 8 D*=Ad1/2R(2eId)1/2 where Jd is the dark current density, Id is the dark current, and Ad is the area of the device.

Current (I)-Time (t) Characteristics of Schottky Barrier Based Photodetectors: Switchable Photocurrent Generation

Mechanism of Schottky Barrier Based Photodetection and Switchable Photocurrent Generation

Figure shows a Schottky barrier on p-type semiconductor of Cd x Zn1–x S with Φ m < Φ s . The Fermi level comes to equilibrium as the Fermi level on both sides comes to the same position. The electrons will be transferred from the metal side to the semiconductor side in which the atoms near the depletion region will be ionized by capturing the incoming electrons.

Energy band diagram of Ag/nanostructured (p) Cd x Zn1–x S Schottky barrier junction (a) before contact, (b) after contact, and (c) after contact with light illumination condition under the reverse bias of the device.

The negative charge accommodated by a depletion region “w” in which ionized acceptors are left uncompensated by holes. The holes on the the p side will find a barrier as per the relation 10 q(Φs−Φm)=qV0 while the electrons on the metal side will find a barrier q (χ s -Φm).

The general band diagram of the fabricated Schottky barrier is shown in Figure . The band diagram shows before contact with Ag and after making contact of Ag with nanostructured Cd x Zn1–x S (Figure ). Negatively charged ionized acceptors create a depletion layer that is offset by a positive surface charge on the Ag metal.

The built-in voltage (Vbi) is given by the relation 11 qVbi=Eg+χs−Φm−ζ

The Schottky barrier height (ΦB) is represented by the relation 12 ΦB=q Vbi+ζ=Eg+χs−Φm

Hence, in dark condition under reverse bias voltage VR, the Schottky barrier height is increased due to an increase in built-in potential. Hence under dark condition the barrier potential energy ΦB is given by 13 ΦB|Dark=q Vbi+qVR+ζ

Under light illumination condition barrier height is reduced due to the reduction of built-in potential as per the relation 14 ΦBB|Light=q(Vbi−ΔVbi)+qVR+ζ

Thus, as light falls on the semiconductor side (Figure c), excess holes will be generated as the electron transfers from the valence band to the acceptor level as well as to the conduction band. The accumulated holes will reduce the barrier height by compensating for the reduced acceptor to some extent. This will lead to the reduction of built-in potential as well as the barrier height and enables the hole to go to the other side. Hence, current increases under illumination.This change of barrier height will be maximum for CdS NR-based Schottky since the optical absorption coefficient is large. This leads to higher sensitivity under illumination as compared to the other two devices (as shown in Figures and ). Also, the band gap in CdS NRs is less compared with other compositions. However, the band gap of ZnS NPs is high. Therefore, the change of the barrier height will be minimum for ZnS NPs. Hence, detectivity (D*) and responsivity (R) are maximum for CdS NRs. The detectivity and responsivity decrease significantly with a decrease of x in nanostructured Cd x Zn1–x S.

The bandgap of intrinsic CdS semiconductor is about 2.5 eV. Only photons with energy above the bandgap (corresponding to light wavelength around 500 nm) can excite electron–hole pairs, while lower energy photons are not absorbed and thus contribute nothing to the conductivity. That is to say, a considerable part of the visible light (500–800 nm) is beyond the response range of the CdS based photodetector. A possible solution to enlarge its response range is to reduce its bandgap. In fact, by varying the composition of the alloy, a sequential tuning of the bandgap is viable over a rather wide spectrum range from green to near-infrared. For example, a wide range tunable bandgap from visible (2.25 eV for CdS) to near-infrared (1.7 eV for CdSe) can be realized by substituting part of S with Se. Much work has been done during the past two decades to investigate the synthesis and properties of alloy semiconductor nanostructures, such as CdS x Se1–x and Zn x Cd1–x S y Se1–y , as well as their application in nanostructured lasers, waveguide, optical switches, and full color solar cells Only photons of energy greater than the band gap of CdS (2.49 eV) will be able to excite electron-hole pairs, but lower energy photons (500–800) nm do not contribute. Therefore, we selected Cd x Zn1–x S so that photodetection and switching activity can be extended to the UV region by increasing the bandgap. The photodetection activity is reduced due to defect states which arise due to the increase of Zn content in Cd x Zn1–x S. The response is enhanced by reducing the bandgap by part of S with Se.

The reduced performance of detection for CdS-based photodetectors is not due to a considerable part of the visible light (500–800 nm). The responsivity (ii) of the prepared n-ZnS Schottky diodes of different thicknesses of gold thin films (50, 80, and 100 nm) was measured. These detectors have a low response to wavelengths longer than 340 nm, which correspond to energies less than the band gap of the zinc sulfide.

Sulfide compounds within semiconductor materials, such as CdZnS, are considered promising photocatalysts and photodetectors due to their exceptional light-harvesting capabilities and suitable band gaps. However, the high electron-hole recombination rate in CdZnS severely affects its ability to produce hydrogen as a photocatalyst. The primary goals for improving photocatalytic and photodetection performance lie in enhancing the light absorption capacity.

The improvement mainly encompasses the construction of junctions. Hence, Schottky junctions have been fabricated (Ag/Cd x Zn1–x S/ITO) to improve the photodetection activity.

We have fabricated a Schottky barrier junction (Ag/Cd x Zn1–x S/ITO) to improve the photodetection activity. The photodetection activity is reduced due to increase of band gap and defect states which arise due to increase of Zn content in Cd x Zn1–x S. Also the photodetection efficiency gradually decreases due to the increase of band gap as Zn content increases in Cd x Zn1–x S, and the presence of interfacial states between metal/semiconductor junctions allows the premature recombination of photogenerated carriers and defect states arising from deviations of binding energy in CdS, Cd x Zn1–x S and ZnS as determined by XPS.

Conclusion

In summary, all the three fabricated devices of CdS NRs/Ag, ZnS NPs/Ag and Cd0.5Zn0.5S NPs/Ag exhibited SBPD characteristics. The characteristics clearly showed the photoresponse of a Schottky device is very much dependent on the composition of semiconducting nanostructured materials. CdS NRs based SBPDs were more efficient compared with the other two, i.e. Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs+NRs) and ZnS NPs based SBPDs. This is due to an increase of band gap and defect state with decrease of x in Cd x Zn1–x S leading to loss of photogenerated carriers by recombination. The rise and fall nature of the current under light illumination and dark condition of SBPDs showed good switching behavior due to the photodetection properties of the SBPDs. During switching properties the response time of CdS NRs based SBPDs showed fast response properties compared with the other two SBPDs (Cd0.5Zn0.5S NPs/Ag and ZnS NPs/Ag SBPDs). Surface states and recombination centers play important roles in the large growth time and large decay time in Cd0.5Zn0.5S (NPs+NRs) and ZnS NPs, respectively.

Future use of such high-performance SBPDs in optoelectronic devices is anticipated to be very promising. It is expected that inexpensive SBD photodetectors will have great potential for application in high-speed sensing devices since there is no minority carrier on the metal side. As a result, the sensitivity will be higher in Schottky devices than in p–n junctions.
