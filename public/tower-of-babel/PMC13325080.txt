Mechanism and Dominant Controlling Factors of Borehole Failure in Soft Coal Seams
Authors: Qianru Lei, Cunbao Deng, Tao Yuan, Yansheng Wang, Zihao Zhao, Qingsheng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Borehole failure can cause difficulties in borehole sealing and restrict the methane extraction efficiency, thereby directly affecting the development effect of coal-bed methane. For the phenomenon of borehole spallation failure in soft coal seams, considering the influences of three dominant controlling factors of stress, methane, and coal strength on the formation of spallation, the multifield coupling model of stress, methane flow, and strength field is established. The mechanical condition and mechanism of borehole spallation failure are proposed. Results show that borehole spallation failure is the combined effect of a series of physical processes, including stress transfer, methane diffusion-seepage response, stress-relieved methane flow, and coal strength evolution. Under the benchmark working condition of this study, the plastic damage within the stress concentration zone significantly enhanced methane seepage and diffusion capabilities, with an increase of more than 3 orders of magnitude. Formation of a high methane pressure gradient within 0.12 m of the borehole wall is induced by significant differences in the methane flow rate among various stress zones. The tensile strength of coal around the borehole is reduced by plastic damage by about 14.86%. Eventually, the differential of methane pressure around the borehole can overcome tensile strength, which leads to the spallation structure being formed by the tensile damage of coal. The stress dominates plastic damage during the borehole spallation failure. Methane, as the driving force for spallation, dominates the tensile damage. Coal strength can resist the formation of spallation. This study reveals the borehole failure mechanism in soft coal seams and provides theoretical support for quantitatively regulating the negative pressure of methane extraction to improve its efficiency.

Introduction

Coal-bed methane (CBM), as an important unconventional natural gas resource, plays a critical role in global energy transition and carbon emission reduction. Effective development of CBM can not only alleviate the shortage of traditional energy but also capture methane released from coal mining activities, thus greatly reducing greenhouse gas emissions. However, with increasing coal seam mining depth, the coupling effect of multiple factorsincluding high in situ stress, high methane pressure, and mining-induced disturbanceleads to frequent borehole failure during drilling and methane extraction processes. , Borehole failure can cause difficulties in borehole sealing and restrict the methane extraction efficiency, thereby directly affecting the development effect of CBM. To optimize the drilling processes, improve the methane extraction efficiency, and ultimately achieve the safe development of CBM, it is necessary to reveal the internal mechanism of borehole failure.

Extensive research has been conducted on borehole instability. Traditional studies, primarily based on elastic or elastoplastic mechanics, held that borehole failure is a mechanical response dominated by in situ stress. , The stress field is recognized as the primary driving force for the deformation of the surrounding rock, controlling the evolution of the plastic zone. For example, experimental and numerical studies have confirmed that stress concentration induces the plastic zone, and the anisotropy of in situ stress affects its shape and the coal’s transition from the elastic to plastic state. − However, these early studies often focused on the static influence of single mechanical factors, which proves insufficient to explain borehole failure behavior in deep complex geological conditions where multiple fields interact. −

In recent years, the research focus has shifted from single-factor analysis to the construction of multiphysical field coupling models. These models provide a theoretical basis for studying the mechanism of borehole failure from different aspects: ,− On the one hand, studies have confirmed that stress redistribution not only induces the plastic zone, but the unloading process itself significantly enhances the permeability of coal through damage, creating dominant channels for methane migration. − On the other hand, the understanding of methane’s role has evolved from static pressure to a fine characterization of its flow behavior, including the study of its nonlinear seepage laws in fracture networks and its diffusion behavior at engineering scales. , Simultaneously, coal strength is no longer regarded as a constant, and its damage evolution has become a core component of the coupling models. , Experimental observations, such as quantitative studies of microheterogeneity based on CT imaging under uniaxial loading, have established a direct link between macroscopic mechanical degradation and the evolution of microfractures. Research on multiphysical field impacts has further revealed the complexity of material damage under the coupled effects of temperature, fluid, and stress fields. The outcomes of these studies have been widely applied to guide engineering practices such as borehole layout, drilling parameter optimization, and borehole stability prediction. − These studies collectively conclude that borehole instability is the result of the nonlinear coupling and dynamic evolution of three factors: stress disturbance, methane flow, and coal damage.

Soft coal seams exhibit characteristics such as low strength, high methane content, and low permeability. , When borehole failure occurs in such low strength coal with eliminated anisotropy, laminated macroscopic fractures parallel to the coal surface often are formed in the coal around the borehole. Despite the consensus on multifield coupling, when focusing on the specific failure mode of borehole spallation (i.e., flake-like exfoliation parallel to the borehole wall) in soft coal, significant gaps remain in existing research:1.Lack of targeted mechanistic models. Existing coupling models predominantly address generalized borehole shrinkage or shear failure, lacking a dedicated theoretical framework to reveal the tensile-dominated failure mechanism of spallation.2.Inadequate description of the failure evolution process. Existing borehole stability coupling models typically emphasize two-way stress-seepage coupling. There is a lack of a quantitative, systematic description of the borehole failure evolution process.3.Unclear role of dominant factors. The specific roles played by stress, methane, and coal strength in the borehole spallation failure process as well as when each factor becomes dominant remain unclear. Their quantitative contributions and the conditions for their shifting dominance have not been elucidated.

In summary, this study focuses on the phenomenon of borehole spallation failure in soft coal seams, aiming to bridge these gaps in existing research through the following approaches: (1) Establishing a multifield coupling model that integrates the stress field, the methane flow field (considering multiple diffusion mechanisms), and the strength field (considering the damage evolution process); (2) proposing the mechanical conditions for the initiation of spallation and elucidating the complete interrelated evolution mechanism of “stress-flow-damage” through numerical simulation; and (3) quantitatively analyzing the distinct roles played by the three main factors (stress, methane, and coal strength) at different stages of the failure process. The findings reveal the mechanism of borehole spallation failure in soft coal seams, which provides a theoretical basis for the quantitative regulation of negative pressure during methane extraction to enhance methane recovery efficiency and prevent borehole instability.

Theoretical Model

Basic Assumptions and Scope of the Model

Borehole spallation failure is the result of the combined action of stress, methane, and coal strength, involving stress transfer, methane diffusion-seepage response, stress-relieved methane flow, and coal strength evolution behavior. To establish a clear theoretical framework for analyzing the intrinsic mechanism of this complex coupling process, this study adopts the following fundamental assumptions. These assumptions define the scope and applicable conditions of the model.1.Assumption of coal as an isotropic continuum medium. This model primarily targets tectonically deformed soft coal that has undergone intense geological tectonic action, with its original bedding and structure severely damaged. Due to the highly developed and randomly oriented internal fractures, the macroscopic mechanical behavior of such coal often exhibits approximately isotropic characteristics. Adopting an isotropic continuum medium model and a macroscopic stress-based failure criterion is an effective theoretical tool to capture the overall failure behavior of such materials and to focus on the core coupling mechanism of “stress-flow-damage” itself. , This model does not currently consider the anisotropy induced by primary bedding or dominant joint sets, which will be a valuable direction for future research.2.Assumption of a hydrostatic in situ stress boundary. A hydrostatic (isotropic) in situ stress boundary condition is applied in the model. This simplification aims to construct the clearest possible theoretical framework for the initial revelation of the borehole spallation failure mechanism in soft coals. The anisotropy of in situ stress, prevalent in actual coal seams, alters the deviatoric stress tensor and the morphology of the plastic zone around the borehole and may influence the specific location and mode of failure. Analyzing the effect of in situ stress anisotropy is an important direction for aligning with engineering reality but is regarded as a future extension of the current coupling framework.3.Assumption of failure plane orientation. The potential tensile failure plane for spallation is assumed to be approximately parallel to the borehole wall (i.e., perpendicular to the direction of the methane pressure gradient).

The aforementioned assumptions enable this study to achieve a reasonable trade-off between model complexity and mechanistic clarity, thereby allowing a focus on elucidating the core causal chain of borehole spallation failure in soft-coal seams. The multifield coupling theoretical framework established in this study lays a foundation for the subsequent introduction of more complex factors such as in situ stress anisotropy and primary structural planes.

Stress Field

As the borehole is drilled into the coal seam, the stress is redistributed. The stress unloading zone (III), the stress concentration zone (II), and the original stress zone (I) around the borehole are caused by stress transfer, as shown in Figure . Different mechanical behaviors of coal are exhibited across these zones. Stress distribution around the borehole.

In the analysis of stress, deformation, and failure in methane-bearing coal, the effective stress principle for porous media is followed. , The mechanical response and strength characteristics of the coal are governed by the effective stress rather than the total stress. In this study, to more accurately describe the mechanical response of the dual-porosity medium under methane pressure, the effective stress law for dual-porosity media is introduced. Consequently, the effective stress tensor σ ij e is defined as eq σije=σij−(βfpf+βmpm)δij 1 where σ ij is the total stress tensor (i, j = x, y, z), MPa; βf is the fracture effective stress coefficient, βf=1−KKm ; K is the bulk modulus of the coal, MPa; K m is the bulk modulus of the coal matrix, MPa; p f is the methane pressure in the fractures, MPa; βm is the pore effective stress coefficient, βm=KKm−KKs ; K s is the bulk modulus of the coal skeleton, MPa; p m is the methane pressure in the matrix pores, MPa; δ ij is the Kronecker delta; if i = j, then δ ij = 1.

The subsequent constitutive relationship, the yield criterion, and the failure criterion are all derived and applied based on the effective stress. In the following descriptions, unless otherwise specified, the term “stress” refers to effective stress.

Stress Concentration Zone

In the stress concentration zone, the radial stress within the coal is significantly reduced, whereas tangential stress is intensified (Figure ). The coal yield strength is markedly diminished by the mechanical evolution path with radial stress unloading and tangential stress loading. When the Mohr circle of the coal is tangent to the coal strength line, plastic damage of coal is induced.

The yield damage criterion is formulated by integrating Drucker-Prager and Mohr-Coulomb criteria (eq ) F=J2+αDPI1−kDP 6 where I 1 is the first stress invariant, MPa; J 2 is the second deviator stress invariant, MPa2; αDP and k DP are constants related to cohesion c and internal friction angle φ, αDp=2sinφ/3(3−sinφ) , kDP=6ccosφ/3(3−sinφ) .

This study adopts a reasonable simplification of the full stress–strain behavior of the coal. In view of the brittle-plastic characteristics of soft coal, the model assumes that its yield point coincides with its peak strength point, thereby neglecting the postyield ideal plastic plateau stage. Before reaching the peak strength, the coal primarily undergoes recoverable elastic compressive deformation, with the tangential stress increasing linearly with strain. When the stress reaches the yield strength, the coal undergoes plastic failure and immediately exhibits significant strain-softening behavior, i.e., its load-bearing capacity continuously decreases with the development of plastic deformation. The aforementioned elastoplastic damage evolution process can be elucidated by an idealized uniaxial compression stress–strain curve for coal, as shown in Figure . The core internal variable describing this process is the volumetric plastic strain εb p. The curve in Figure clearly illustrates three typical stages: (1) Elastic stage (origin to point A). In this stage, εb p = 0, the stress–strain relationship is linear and the deformation is recoverable, corresponding to the preyield state described in the text. (2) Strain-softening stage (point A to point B). Starting from point A (the yield point), the coal enters a plastic state, while its strength (characterized by cohesion and the internal friction angle) continuously declines (i.e., softens) with the development of plastic deformation. Therefore, plastic flow and strength softening occur simultaneously and are inseparable in this stage. In this stage, 0 < εb p < εbc p, where εbc p is the volumetric plastic strain threshold for the onset of the residual stage. (3) Residual stage (beyond point B). Upon reaching point B, the internal structural reorganization of the coal is complete, forming a new stable damaged structure. Its strength no longer decreases but maintains a stable residual strength (indicated by the dashed platform in the Figure ). At this point, εb p ≥ εbc p.

Stress Unloading Zone

Within the stress unloading zone near the borehole wall, both the tangential and radial stresses decrease significantly with the radial stress approaching zero at the borehole wall (Figure ). Following plastic damage, the permeability of the coal in this zone is enhanced, leading to an acceleration of the methane flow near the borehole. The methane pressure rises rapidly from the pressure at the borehole wall to the initial value within the coal, resulting in a high methane pressure difference. Consequently, the governing mechanical condition for failure in this zone shifts from being controlled by shear yield to being primarily influenced by tensile action. Tensile damage is mainly driven by this high-pressure methane difference.

The tensile failure of coal is evaluated using the classical maximum tensile stress criterion, , expressed in the following form. This criterion states that fracture occurs when the maximum principal effective tensile stress at a given location reaches the material’s tensile strength. In the specific context of borehole spallation in soft coal, this general criterion can be concretized into a more direct and practical form (eq )­ KI≥KIC 7 where K I is the intensity factor of the type I fracture, KI=σ2πl , MPa·m0.5; σ is the distant stress, MPa; l is the extension radius of the fracture, the value of pure type I fracture damage is a constant, m; K IC is the fracture toughness of the type I fracture, MPa·m0.5.

Considering that the potential failure plane is approximately parallel to the borehole wall, the normal stress acting on this plane is crucial. Within the unloading zone near the borehole, the radial effective stress is very low due to stress relief. Under such conditions of low confinement, the net normal force causing tensile failure perpendicular to this plane can be attributed to the pressure difference between the methane pressure in the fractures p i and the methane pressure in the borehole p a. Therefore, the methane pressure difference p i – p a can be regarded as the equivalent effective tensile stress driving the spallation phenomenon. Consequently, the specialized mechanical condition proposed for the initiation of borehole spallation failure is eq pi−pa≥σt 8 where p i is the methane pressure at a point within the coal behind the potential failure plane, MPa; p a is the methane pressure in the borehole, MPa; σt is the tensile strength of the coal at that point, MPa. This criterion indicates that the spallation phenomenon occurs when the methane pressure difference exceeds the tensile strength of the coal.

While adhering to the assumptions outlined in Section and the preceding text, this criterion also relies on the following key assumptions:1.Low radial confinement. The derivation assumes that the radial effective stress within the near-field unloading zone is sufficiently small such that its contribution to the normal stress on the potential failure plane is negligible compared to the methane pressure difference.2.Material response. Tectonically deformed soft coal is considered to possess a relatively low tensile strength, making it susceptible to failure driven by a methane pressure difference.

This tensile criterion, based on the pressure difference value, effectively captures the main driving force (methane pressure difference) behind the spallation phenomenon in soft coal. It represents a simplified yet physically reasonable formulation of the maximum tensile stress criterion for this specific scenario. It avoids the complexity of precisely defining the local effective stress tensor within a severely damaged and porous medium. The effectiveness of this approach in analyzing methane-driven tensile spallation in coal seams is supported by its successful application in the study of outburst-induced coal fragmentation. It should be particularly noted that due to the aforementioned assumptions, the valid application scope of the criterion shown in eq is limited to the borehole wall and its immediate, extremely thin vicinity. For the internal regions of the coal seam away from the borehole surface, which are in a triaxial stress state, the radial stress is not negligible. Therefore, the initiation criterion for spallation established in this paper is not applicable to assessing overall failure within the interior of the coal seam.

Strength Field

Since the radial stress is reduced to zero at the borehole wall, the influence of radial stress on coal strength near the borehole is negligible. The joint influence of plastic damage and methane flow is the determining factor in the alteration of the coal strength. ,

Alteration of Coal Strength by Plastic Damage

Irreversible alterations in certain mechanical parameters of coal are induced by plastic damage, and consequently the overall strength is diminished. Cohesion and internal friction angle are important mechanical parameters of coal. Although the tensile strength σt is the ultimate criterion for judging tensile damage, it has no direct conversion relationship with the core mechanical parameters describing plastic damage (cohesion and internal friction angle) as they correspond to different damage mechanisms. However, these two damage processes are not isolated. The plastic damage that first occurs in the coal under stress disturbance induces significant material damage. This damage is macroscopically manifested as the weakening of the overall mechanical properties of the coal, reflected in the reduction of both compressive strength and tensile strength. Fracture toughness is an important parameter characterizing the tensile damage of coal. To establish an indirect link between the compressive strength and tensile strength, the study introduces fracture mechanics as a bridge. Interaction between Multiphysical Processes

Based on the theoretical model established above, the interaction relationships between the multiphysical processes of borehole spallation failure are illustrated in Figure .

Interaction between multiphysical processes.

The stress within the coal is rapidly redistributed as the borehole drills into the coal seam. The stress unloading zone, the stress concentration zone, and the original stress zone are formed in sequence around the borehole. Both the methane flow process and the coal strength evolution are further affected by the mechanical state of coal within different stress zones.

Within the stress concentration zone, the coal undergoes plastic yielding under a mechanical path of radial unloading and tangential loading, leading to irreversible damage evolution. This process promotes the initiation and propagation of microfractures, which manifests macroscopically as an intensification of damage degree and a significant enhancement in the connectivity of the pore-fracture system, subsequently resulting in a reduction in the characteristic scale of the coal matrix (e.g., diffusion length). These alterations in the coal structure cause a decrease in the sorption time τ and an increase in the permeability k, significantly accelerating the diffusion-seepage response of methane. Consequently, methane within the plastic zone migrates rapidly and its pressure drops swiftly, thereby establishing a pronounced methane pressure gradient between the different stress zones surrounding the borehole.

Meanwhile, plastic damage also leads to the deterioration of the macroscopic mechanical parameters of the coal, reducing its compressive and tensile strengths through the strain-softening model. On the other hand, the rapid desorption and migration of methane play a role to some extent in strengthening the coal. When the methane pressure difference between a point and the borehole (i.e., the equivalent effective tensile stress acting on the potential failure plane) exceeds the tensile strength of the coal at that location, the maximum tensile stress criterion based on the effective stress is satisfied. The coal will undergo tensile failure, forming an approximately spheroidal-shell-like spallation structure.

Numerical Simulation

A multifield coupling model of stress field, methane flow field, and strength field for borehole spallation failure is composed of partial differential equations. These equations can be calculated by COMSOL Multiphysics.

Geometrical Model and Boundary Conditions

In numerical simulation, the coal seam is represented by a two-dimensional planar model. Figure indicates the model dimensions, boundary conditions, and monitoring line AB. The hydraulic flushing technology utilizes high-pressure water jets to expel a large amount of coal mass, thereby enhancing the permeability. The large-diameter boreholes created by this technology are more prone to failure. To facilitate the analysis of changes in physical parameters around the borehole, this paper takes a borehole with a diameter of 1 m formed by hydraulic flushing as its research object.

Dimensions and boundary conditions of the model.

The initial conditions are described as follows: before the simulation commences (i.e., prior to drilling initiation), the entire computational domain is in an equilibrium state under the original in situ stress. The stress field is uniformly distributed, characterized by an initial hydrostatic stress of 18 MPa, and the methane pressure is also uniformly maintained at an initial value of 2.6 MPa. The drilling process is simulated by instantaneously activating a circular boundary at the model center and converting it to a free-stress boundary with a specified methane pressure. This represents stress release and initiation of methane flow after borehole excavation.

The boundary conditions are described as follows: (1) Solid module: The left and upper boundaries of the model are subjected to a constant pressure of 18 MPa. Right and bottom boundaries are designated as rolling boundaries. After drilling, the center borehole wall is defined as the free boundary. (2) PDE module: The four sides of the model are set to zero flow boundary. Initially, the borehole wall is set to a zero flow boundary. After drilling, a constant pressure of 0.085 MPa on the borehole wall is sustained. The initial methane pressure within the coal seam was 2.6 MPa.

Although the model geometry, the load configuration, and the material properties are symmetric with respect to both the x- and y-axes, the model used in this study is a full model rather than a quarter model. This choice was made primarily to intuitively and comprehensively visualize the distribution of physical fields around the entire borehole. The computational cost of the current model scale remains within an acceptable range. Employing symmetric boundary conditions to enhance efficiency is a standard practice, and this approach will also be considered in the future for parametric investigations or for constructing more complex models.

Numerical Simulation Methods and Parameters

To enhance the computational efficiency, physical fields are sequentially calculated with the solution of each subsequent field being dependent on the results obtained from the preceding field. Throughout the computational procedure, the methane flow field is affected by the stress field through its effects on the permeability and adsorption time. The stress field is influenced by the effective stress and adsorption-induced expansion, which are affected by the methane flow field.

The procedure for the numerical solution is illustrated in Figure , which is outlined as follows. (1) The initial solid module is utilized to obtain the initial mechanical properties of coal; thereby, the stress–strain value of the coal is obtained. (2) The stress–strain value is used as the initial condition of the second solid module, and the mechanical behavior of coal after drilling is analyzed. (3) The governing equation for methane flow is computed using two PDE modules. (4) Coal tensile strength is calculated with the information gathered from the stress and methane flow fields. Model Verification

Verification against Field Methane Extraction Data

Directly and comprehensively recording the evolution of physical parameters during the borehole spallation process poses a significant challenge, both in field and laboratory environments. Therefore, a stepwise verification strategy is adopted. First, the model will be validated by comparing it with a macroscopic field observation processmethane extraction. This process shares the same core physical mechanisms characterized by the model: the coupled dynamic processes of stress redistribution, methane diffusion, and permeability evolution.

The field monitoring data for methane extraction are sourced from a well-documented study on soft coal seams (i.e., ref ). The field conditions involved coal seam characteristics that were consistent with typical features of soft coal. For model verification, the model setup parameters were aligned with those of the initial field data. The methane extraction borehole was constructed by using hydraulic flushing technology, and the methane flow rate was monitored over an extended period.

Figure compares the methane extraction flow rate predicted by the current model with the field monitoring data. The results show that the model predictions align with the broad trend of the field data. This consistency in trends indicates that the model can reliably capture the key coupled dynamics of the “stress field-seepage field,” particularly the permeability enhancement driven by stress release and damage. This is the prerequisite and core element for predicting spallation failure. However, inherent uncertainties exist in both the field data and model predictions due to factors such as geological heterogeneity, measurement errors, and model simplifications. The primary value of this comparison lies in confirming the model’s capability to describe the critical physical processes governing methane flow, thereby providing a reliable basis for using the model to analyze the otherwise unobservable failure mechanisms.

Fitting result between the calculated data of the model and the field monitoring data from ref .

Indirect Evidence and Physical Consistency

To validate the spallation geometry predicted by the model, the following indirect evidence and rationale are proposed based on physical consistency. Field studies and engineering reports consistently indicate that instability and failure in soft coal boreholes are typically concentrated in a region very close to the borehole wall. , The predicted failure occurring within a very shallow range (a few centimeters to decimeters) from the borehole wall is consistent with this macroscopic field observation regarding the spatial scale of failure. More importantly, the predicted spallation mechanism (tensile damage triggered by a high methane pressure gradient) and the resulting failure morphology (a shell-like structure parallel to the free surface) are consistent with the physical principles underlying the study of outburst-induced coal spallation by Tu et al. The consistency in the fundamental driving factors and failure modes between these two phenomena provides strong indirect validation for the model’s predictions.

Direct quantitative verification of the precise geometrical dimensions of the spallation requires future targeted measurements using advanced technologies, such as borehole optical imaging, underground probing radar, or well-designed physical model experiments. The theoretical model and numerical tools developed in this study provide a predictive basis and analytical framework for planning and interpreting future experimental and field investigations.

Mechanism of Borehole Spallation Failure

The isotropic characteristics and low-strength mechanical properties of the soft coal seam are the basis of borehole spallation failure. The mechanism of borehole spallation failure caused by methane, stress, and coal strength is studied by theoretical modeling and numerical simulation in this work. As the borehole drills into the coal seam, stress transfer occurs within the coal, triggering a series of physical behaviors, stress-relieved methane flow, and coal strength evolution. Figure shows the mechanism of borehole spallation failure in soft coal seams.

Mechanism of borehole spallation failure in soft coal seams.

The stress field in coal around the borehole is divided into stress unloading zone, stress concentration zone, and original stress zone in sequence. In theb stress concentration zone, the Mohr’s circle of coal being tangential to coal strength envelope is caused by the mechanical path with tangential stress loading and radial stress unloading. This consequently induces plastic damage in the coal, characterized by the accumulation of volumetric plastic strain. Plastic damage leads to the deterioration of the coal’s structural integrity and the development of microfractures. This evolution of the microstructure manifests macroscopically as a reduction in the coal matrix size and a significant enhancement in the connectivity of the pore-fracture system. Therefore, under the aforementioned baseline working conditions (parameters taken from the initial conditions in Section and Table ), the permeability increases to 1313.44 times its initial value, and the sorption time is significantly reduced. The capacities of methane seepage and diffusion are increased by more than 3 orders of magnitude. Due to the rapid methane flow, methane pressure within the stress unloading zone is markedly reduced. Formation of high methane pressure gradient within 0.12 m near borehole wall is caused by significant differences in methane flow rate among various stress zones around the borehole. Concurrently, some mechanical parameters of coal are irreversibly changed by plastic damage, which markedly diminishes the tensile strength by about 14.86%. Conversely, the coal strength near borehole wall is marginally increased by the swift desorption of adsorbed methane. With further stress unloading near the borehole, when the differential of methane pressure p i – p a is sufficient to overcome the tensile strength σ t , the tensile damage of coal is triggered. Macrofractures propagate and connect along the direction perpendicular to the maximum methane pressure gradient, forming a spherical shell-shaped spallation structure.

Methane, stress, and coal strength are the dominant controlling factors of borehole spallation failure, and they play different roles in various stages of the failure process. During the plastic damage of the coal stage, free methane affects the effective stress of coal, and the nonmechanical effect of adsorbed methane has a cracking effect on the coal strength. However, compared to stress, the role of methane pressure can be ignored. Therefore, plastic damage of coal at this stage is only promoted by methane but is dominated by stress. Under the aforementioned baseline working conditions, with the increase in stress, the plastic area and the maximum plastic strain of coal are increased by nearly 3.57 times (from 0.092 to 0.328 m2) and 2.71 times (from 0.136 to 0.369), respectively, resulting in the expansion of the spallation failure range. In the tensile damage of the coal stage, with the unloading of radial stress, the methane pressure gradually becomes the dominant factor in spallation. With the increase of methane pressure, the distance of the spallation point from the borehole wall decreases by 50.50% (from 0.101 to 0.050 m). Methane is the driving force of borehole spallation failure. Coal strength has the ability to resist the borehole spallation failure. Especially in the tensile damage stage, coal strength is an important parameter for resisting the differential methane pressure. As the fracture toughness decreases, the distance between the spallation point and the borehole wall decreases by 48.54% (from 0.103 to 0.053 m). By determining parameters such as the methane pressure of the coal seam and the coal strength and according to the mechanical condition of borehole spallation failure, the threshold value for negative pressure in borehole extraction can be calculated. It is possible to prevent borehole spallation failure by keeping the methane pressure difference lower than the coal strength.

Formation of a high methane pressure gradient within 0.12 m of the borehole wall is induced by significant differences in the methane flow rate among various stress zones around the borehole. The tensile strength of coal is reduced by plastic damage by about 14.86%. When the mechanical condition of borehole spallation failure is satisfied, the spallation structure is formed. This mechanical condition can be used to guide the adjustment of the negative pressure of methane extraction, so as to reduce the methane pressure gradient and prevent borehole spallation failure.

3.Methane, stress, and coal strength are the dominant controlling factors of borehole spallation failure, and they play different roles in various stages of the failure process. Methane is the driving force of borehole spallation failure, which plays a major role in the tensile damage of coal. As the methane pressure increases, the distance from the spallation point to the borehole wall is reduced by 50.5% (from 0.101 to 0.050 m). Stress dominates the plastic damage to coal. The plastic area and the maximum plastic strain within the coal are, respectively, enhanced by approximately 3.57 times (from 0.092 m2 to 0.328 m2) and 2.71 times (from 0.136 to 0.369) with increasing stress. Thus, the range of spallation failure is expanded. Tensile strength is a key factor in resisting the spallation failure. The distance from the spallation point to the borehole wall is decreased by 48.54% (from 0.103 to 0.053 m) with decreasing fracture toughness.
