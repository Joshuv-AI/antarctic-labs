 Task 2 Results:

 The following discussion summarizes the net metering and billing, energy efficiency, demand
 response programs and other deployment models that may allow the export of excess power from
 FarmAfield’s technology, such as net metering and billing and COGEN/SPP Tariffs for Federal
 Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Qualifying Facilities.

 Nebraska Public Power District (NPPD)

 Net Metering and Billing

 The Rider NM rate is applicable to Class 0 systems that are less than 25 kilowatts (kW) in size.
 Under Rider NM, the system must also be located behind the customer’s utility meter and
 operate in parallel with the local distribution system. Net Energy Generation (NEG) credit rates
 are published for exported solar, with Rider NM’s NEG credit rate including a summer rate of
 $0.0874 per kilowatt-hour (kWh) from June through September and a winter rate of $0.052 per
 kWh from October through May. There is no banking or carrying forward of credits allowed.
 Whether Rider NM export compensation terms provide additional project value can only be
 determined through site-specific modeling.

 COGEN/Small Power Producer (SPP)

 The Simultaneous Buy Sell Rider (SI Rider) appears to be the NPPD version of a federal
 “Cogenerating and Small Power Producing Facilities” tariff for Qualifying Facilities as required
 under the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC). SI Rider is applicable to Class 1
 systems greater than 25 kW in size and up to 100 kW for solar plus battery energy storage
 systems (BESS), wind, or methane-fueled baseload projects that operate in parallel with the local
 distribution system from behind the utility meter for customers who do not qualify for or take

 3

 service under the Net Metering (NM Rider), do not take service under the Energy Curtailment
 Service Rider (EC Rider), or do not register in the Southwest Power Pool (SPP) with an entity
 other than NPPD. The summer and winter compensation rates under SI Rider for excess
 generation not used to offset customer load and exported to the grid are similar to the rates under
 Rider NM; however, these credits can be banked and carried forward to the subsequent billing
 period. For FarmAfield’s target customers and on-site generation technology, potential project
 value from Rider NM export compensation can be assessed only through site-specific modeling.

 SunWiseSM Community Solar

 SunWiseSM Community Solar eligibility requires customers to be in good standing with NPPD,
 pay an NPPD electric bill, and reside in communities covered by NPPD’s Community Solar
 Energy Rider Rate Schedule. NPPD publishes a list of eligible communities. Subscriptions must
 be at least one month and may last up to the full term of the Power Purchase Agreement (PPA)
 between the developer and NPPD for the selected program.

 Energy Efficiency

 There are no NPPD incentives for the electrification of propane heating systems. However,
 NPPD does offer dedicated agricultural equipment energy efficiency incentives for specific
 measures, including center pivot or linear outlet component replacements for all-electric
 irrigation, irrigation moisture sensors incorporated with irrigation management programs,
 irrigation corner pivot variable frequency drives (VFDs), irrigation pump efficiency testing,
 automated grain aeration, and hog heat mats.

 NPPD offers project-based agricultural energy efficiency incentives of up to $0.0100 per kWh
 for verified energy savings. Eligible improvements include irrigation pump upgrades, low-
 pressure pivot or subsurface drip irrigation, improved ventilation and lighting in livestock
 facilities, dairy heat reclaimers, upgrades to electrically heated livestock watering systems, and
 insulation of electrically space-conditioned surfaces.

 Demand Response

 • NPPD has three demand response rider programs:

 o Pilot Program Demand Response Resource Rider (DRR Rider)
 o Demand Waiver Program Rider (DW Rider)
 o Energy Curtailment Service Rider (EC Rider)

 4

 Pilot Program Demand Response Resource (DRR) Rider

 • The Pilot Program DRR Rider is available to a retail customer at a single location that:

 o Has a DRR Service Agreement with NPPD, and
 o Is providing a DRR with an output of at least 500 kW

 • A DRR is defined as a customer’s controllable load that is:

 o Registered in the SPP market, and
 o is capable of being reduced as directed by SPP through the District and/or its
 designated SPP market participant

 Based on NLR’s understanding of FarmAfield’s target customers and on-site generation
 technology focus, it is unlikely the DRR Rider offers an opportunity for additional project value.

 Demand Waiver Program Rider

 • The Demand Waiver (DW) Program Rider is available to customers on

 o Rate Codes 17 and 18 (General Service Demand), and
 o Rate Codes 19 and 20 (High Tension Service)

 • To qualify for service under DW Rider, a customer must:

 o Reduce their load by 100 kW or more during on-peak hours that have not been
 waived by the District

 Based on NLR’s understanding of FarmAfield’s target customers and on-site generation
 technology focus, it is unlikely the DW Rider offers an opportunity for additional project value.

 Energy Curtailment Service Rider (EC Rider)

 The EC Rider is available to customers who are served under the General Service Demand, High
 Tension Service, and Large Industrial Service rate schedules. The EC Rider compensates
 customers who can voluntarily reduce electricity demand by a minimum of 50 kW during an
 Energy Curtailment Period (ECP). Based on NLR’s understanding of FarmAfield’s target
 customers and on-site generation technology focus, it is unlikely that the EC Rider offers an
 opportunity for additional project value.

 5

 Omaha Public Power District (OPPD)

 Net Metering and Billing

 Rider 483 is applicable to systems up to 100 kW in size. Under Rider 483, the system must also
 be located behind the customer’s utility meter and operated in parallel to the local distribution
 system. Net excess generation credit rates are published for exported solar, with the current rate
 from June to September being $0.0400 per kWh and the current rate from October to May being
 $0.0352 per kWh. BESS systems are not allowed under Rider 483, and there is no banking as is
 available under some net metering rates. Based on NLR’s understanding of FarmAfield’s target
 customers and on-site generation technology focus, it is unlikely that the Rider 483 terms for
 export compensation offers an opportunity for additional project value.

 COGEN/SPP

 Rider 355 – Electric Energy Purchased from Cogenerating and Small Power Producing Facilities
 – allows customer solar plus BESS systems that operate in parallel to the local distribution
 system from behind the customer’s utility meter to export power to OPPD. For systems of less
 than 1,000 kW, the summer and winter off-peak and peak compensation rates for excess
 generation range from $0.0273 per kWh for off-peak to $0.0540 per kWh for summer peak. For
 systems greater than 1,000 kW, the compensation rate for excess generation is set at OPPD’s
 avoided cost. Based on NLR’s understanding of FarmAfield’s target customers and on-site
 generation technology focus, it is unlikely that the Rider 355 terms for export compensation
 offers an opportunity for additional project value.

 Community Solar

 Rider 500 – Community Solar – is applicable to all customers throughout OPPD's service area
 taking service under any retail rate schedule. There is a refundable enrollment deposit that can be
 refunded for enrollments of five, ten, or twenty years depending on the customer’s rate schedule.
 The subscription quantity of a community solar share is a minimum of 100 kWh per month. The
 subscription credit reflects the market-based value of solar. It is calculated by taking the
 interconnection cost of the community solar power purchase agreement (PPA), then subtracting:
 (1) the value of the prior year’s actual hourly community solar production, priced at the
 corresponding Southwest Power Pool (SPP) day-ahead hourly rates, and (2) the value of the
 accredited capacity that SPP assigns to the community solar facilities, priced at OPPD’s annual
 levelized marginal generation capacity cost. Further analysis is needed for FarmAfield to
 determine if their target customers could recognize savings under Rider 500 outside of on-site
 generation technology deployments under applicable customer rate schedules.

 Energy Efficiency

 OPPD does not appear to have equipment rebates (prescriptive) or project-based (custom)
 incentives for energy efficiency.

 Solar Leases and PPAs in Nebraska

 OPPD’s Customer-owned Generation Frequently Asked Questions has the following exchange:

 • Question: Can I lease a solar system from a third party?
 • Answer: Solar leases are not allowed in Nebraska as only publicly-owned electric utilities
 are permitted to sell electricity to end users.

 Further, the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency® maintained by the NC
 Clean Energy Technology Center maintains a resource titled, Third-Party Solar Power Purchase
 Agreement Policies. This resource indicated that Nebraska was listed as a state where 3rd party
 PPAs were “apparently disallowed by state or otherwise restricted by legal barriers”.

 Task 3 Results:

 REopt® Background

 To examine how FarmAfield could deploy behind-the-meter technology projects that reduce
 customer utility bills, NLR used REopt® to evaluate solar PV for cost savings for a sample
 project at University of Nebraska meters. REopt® is a techno-economic modeling tool used to
 evaluate the feasibility of distributed energy resources and optimizes their operation to minimize
 energy costs.

 Modeling Inputs and Assumptions

 Scenarios: Because federal incentives changed after passage of the One Big Beautiful Bill
 Act (OBBBA), the analysis compared two federal tax credit structures:

 1. Pre-OBBBA:
 a. 30% Investment Tax Credit (ITC) for PV

 b. 5-year Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS) for PV

 c. 20% bonus depreciation for PV

 d. Note: To qualify for the ITC, construction must start or meet the 5% safe harbor rule
               by July 4, 2026.

 2. Post-OBBBA:

 a. 0% ITC for PV

 b. 5-year MACRS for PV

 c. 100% bonus depreciation for PV

 Both scenarios applied REopt® default modeling assumptions to evaluate PV cost savings.

 Sensitivity Analysisis: Additional analyses tested how variations in PV capital cost and
 electricity escalation rate (EER) affected net present value (NPV). PV system sizes of 10 kW, 30
 kW, and 50 kW were analyzed to match typical loads at FarmAfield’s target customers.

 Location: University of Nebraska facility, Mead, Nebraska

 BFNOR. A representative load profile was scaled to meet the monthly kWh usage and monthly

 Based on the assumptions listed above, REopt® determined the cost-optimal PV system size for
 the Pre-OBBBA scenario to be 33 kW, which is estimated to produce an equivalent amount of
 electricity to the annual kWh consumption of meter BFNOR.

 Building a system larger than this was not identified as cost-effective because the compensation
 rate decreased when exporting power beyond the site’s annual load. If a different electric meter
 was chosen with higher annual electricity consumption, REopt® would likely identify a larger
 system as cost effective and size the PV system such that the annual PV production was
 equivalent to the meter’s annual kWh consumption. If the site was unable to use the Net
 Metering Rider 483, the identified PV system sizing would have been much smaller.

 PV was not found to be cost-effective for the Post-OBBBA scenario based on the assumptions
 listed in the previous section.

 Sensitivity Analysis: Net Present Value (NPV) vs. PV Capital Costs vs. Electricity Escalation
 Rate (EER)

 Sensitivity results comparing NPV versus solar PV capital costs and EER were assessed for PV
 systems of 10 kW, 30 kW, and 50 kW sizes. These PV system sizes were chosen because they
 were expected to span a similar range to the peak loads at most of the University of Nebraska
 meters.

 The sensitivity results were intended to aid FarmAfield in their decision-making process,
 allowing them to understand how estimated project economics changed based on solar PV
 capital costs, EER, and PV system size.

 13

 The sensitivity analysis charts, displayed in Figures 3–8, illustrate a color scale for the NPV of
 the project indicating variations based on the modeled PV capital cost (x-axis) and EER (y-axis).
 Green regions of the chart are areas that REopt® identified a positive NPV over the lifecycle of
 the project for the associated PV capital cost and EER. Red regions of the chart indicate areas
 where REopt® identified negative NPV values.

 For example, Figure 3 illustrates the sensitivity results for a 10 kW PV system with Pre-OBBBA
 assumptions, if it was assumed that the electricity escalation rate over the next 25 years would be
 3%, to break even (i.e., NPV = $0), the PV capital cost was estimated to need to be ~$2,700/kW-
 dc. Alternatively, if a 3% electricity escalation rate was assumed for a 30 kW system with Post-
 OBBBA assumptions and the PV capital cost was estimated to be $4,000 per kW, then the
 resulting NPV was estimated at roughly -$50,000, as shown in Figure 6.

 The breakeven cost curve ($0 NPV) for the 10 kW and 30 kW system followed the same path for

 REopt® Modeling Conclusions

 Assuming a PV capital cost of $2,232 per kW and an electricity escalation rate of 1.66%,
 REopt® determined that it was cost-effective to build a 33 kW PV system sized such that that
 annual generation from the PV system would be equivalent to meter BFNOR’s load. Net
 Metering Rider 483 could add significant value to PV projects, up to and including the meter’s
 load. The solar ITC could significantly boost the project’s cost-effectiveness. To take advantage
 of the ITC for PV, the project must either begin construction by July 4th, 2026, or meet the 5%
 safe harbor rule by July 4th, 2026.
