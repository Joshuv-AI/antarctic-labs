The Role of Cognitive Function in Complex Gait Patterns
Authors: Seong-Beom Koh
Published: 2026

Falls and gait disturbances are among the most disabling complications of Parkinson’s disease (PD), particularly in individuals with mild cognitive impairment (PD-MCI) [1]. However, despite the growing recognition of the interaction between cognition and locomotion [2,3]. the temporal dynamics of cortical activation during prolonged obstacle walking have remained largely unexplored.

In this issue of the Journal of Movement Disorders, Wong et al. [4] investigated the effect of cognitive dysfunction on gait performance and addressed an important and clinically relevant issue in PD: how cognitive impairment influences neural adaptation and gait regulation during complex walking conditions. Their study provides mechanistic insight into this important area.

A major strength of their work lies in the integration of functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) with continuous gait analysis during ecologically relevant obstacle walking. Unlike conventional paradigms that focus on isolated or single-obstacle crossing, their protocol involved sustained obstacle negotiation over repeated walking cycles [5]. This design more closely reflects real-world ambulation, where patients must continuously adapt their gait to environmental challenges while maintaining cognitive and motor control. By examining prolonged obstacle walking, their study captures adaptive neural processes that are difficult to observe in short-duration tasks.

Another important strength is the temporal analysis of cortical activation. Rather than treating obstacle walking as a static condition, the authors divided the walking task into early and late phases, allowing investigation of dynamic cortical adaptation over time. This temporal approach revealed that both the PD-MCI and PD-nonMCI groups demonstrated greater activation during the early phase, suggesting greater neural engagement during task initiation. Importantly, however, individuals with PD-MCI exhibited a steeper decline in supplementary motor area and premotor cortex (PMC) activation across phases. These findings suggest altered neural adaptation strategies and reduced neural efficiency in cognitively impaired PD patients [3].

Their findings also provide important insights into the relationship between cortical activation and gait performance. PD-MCI participants demonstrated poorer gait and obstacle-crossing performance despite greater cortical recruitment, supporting the concept of compensatory but inefficient neural activation. Furthermore, the phase-specific associations between gait performance and cortical activity observed only in the PD-MCI group suggest that cognitive impairment alters the balance between motor-related and cognitive control mechanisms during walking. Specifically, the transition from early PMC-related associations to later prefrontal cortex-related associations may reflect a shift from motor planning toward increasingly effortful cognitive control as the walking task progresses.

They believe these findings are clinically meaningful because they advance the current understanding of cognitive–motor integration in PD. Their study highlights that gait dysfunction in PD-MCI is not merely a motor deficit but reflects altered cortical regulation and adaptive neural processing under cognitively demanding conditions. This perspective may have important implications for rehabilitation strategies aimed at improving gait automaticity, neural efficiency, and fall prevention in vulnerable PD patients [6].

Methodologically, their study also benefits from comprehensive gait assessment, the inclusion of multiple cortical regions relevant to locomotor control, and appropriate statistical modeling using generalized estimating equations to account for repeated measures. Additionally, the incorporation of neuropsychological assessments allowed the characterization of executive, attentional, and visuospatial dysfunctions that may contribute to impaired obstacle negotiation.

Moreover, we acknowledge several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the sample size was modest, which may have limited the statistical power for subgroup and correlation analyses. Second, the absence of a healthy control group restricted direct comparisons of disease-specific cortical adaptation patterns. Third, short-separation channels were not included in the fNIRS setup, which may have limited the ability to fully remove extracerebral physiological noise from cortical signals. Nevertheless, we believe that the present study provides preliminary evidence regarding temporal brain–gait coupling in PD-MCI patients.

We believe that the manuscript will be of interest to readers of the Journal of Movement Disorders because it connects movement disorders, cognitive neuroscience, and neurorehabilitation. These findings may also stimulate future investigations into targeted interventions designed to improve neural efficiency and adaptive gait control in PD patients with cognitive impairment.
