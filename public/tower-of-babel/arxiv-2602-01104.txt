Fast k k -means Seeding Under The Manifold Hypothesis
Poojan Shah, Shashwat Agrawal, Ragesh Jaiswal

Abstract
We study beyond worst case analysis for the k k -means problem where the goal is to model typical instances of k k -means arising in practice. Existing theoretical approaches provide guarantees under certain assumptions on the optimal solutions to k k -means, making them difficult to validate in practice. We propose the manifold hypothesis , where data obtained in ambient dimension D D concentrates around a low dimensional manifold of intrinsic dimension d ≪ D d\ll D , as a reasonable assumption to model real world clustering instances. We identify key geometric properties of datasets which have theoretically predictable scaling laws depending on the quantization exponent ε = 2 / d \varepsilon=2/d using techniques from optimum quantization theory. We show how to exploit these regularities to design a fast seeding method called Qkmeans \operatorname{Qkmeans} 1 1 1 The Q \operatorname{Q} stands for Quantization, as the analysis of our algorithm was motivated by results from optimum quantization theory. which provides O ⁡ ( ϱ − 2 ​ log ⁡ k ) O(\varrho^{-2}\log k) approximate solutions to the k k -means problem in time O ⁡ ( n ​ D ) + O ~ ​ ( ε 1 + ϱ ​ ϱ − 1 ​ k 1 + γ ) O(nD)+\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{1+\varrho}\varrho^{-1}k^{1+\gamma}) ; where the exponent γ = ε + ϱ \gamma=\varepsilon+\varrho for an input parameter ϱ < 1 \varrho<1 . This allows us to obtain new runtime - quality tradeoffs. We perform a large scale empirical study across various domains to validate our theoretical predictions and algorithm performance to bridge theory and practice for beyond worst case data clustering.

Introduction

Data clustering is a fundamental task in machine learning and data science. The goal is to partition a dataset into subsets so that points within the same subset (or cluster) are more similar to each other than to points in other clusters. Such partitions help uncover latent structure and compress data into a small number of interpretable groups. Among the many approaches to clustering, the
k
-means formulation is perhaps the most widely used. In this formulation, we are given a dataset
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and our task is to find a set of
k
centers
C=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
which minimize the total within-cluster variance:

\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{X},C)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\min_{c\in C}\|x-c\|^{2}.

These centers induce a partition
\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j}
, where
C_{j}
contains the points in the dataset
\mathcal{X}
closest to
c_{j}
. An optimal set
C_{k}^{*}
achieves cost
\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\operatorname{cost}(\mathcal{X},C_{k}^{*})
. Optimizing the
k
-means cost then serves as a proxy for uncovering some natural clustering of the dataset. This formulation has become a key component of algorithm design in several fields such as vector quantization and compression
(
55
)
, approximate nearest neighbor search
(
48
;
49
)
along with numerous others.

Given the immense importance of
k

This heuristic, also known as Lloyd’s iterations

The heuristic starts with an arbitrarily chosen set of

Beyond worst case analysis.
A large fraction of theoretical algorithms for
k
-means are designed to be used as
off the shelf

\mathcal{X}
can be any arbitrary set of points in

The pervasive issue is that algorithms designed to resist pathological instances, i.e. inputs that maximize resource usage-often yield complexity bounds far removed from their typical empirical performance. The realization that real datasets rarely resemble these theoretical worst-case examples provides the core motivation for moving beyond pessimistic, data-oblivious constraints

Indeed, it is a non trivial problem to figure out how to check if a given dataset instance follows these assumptions. Although these notions have been studied theoretically, not much work has been done in studying whether they hold (even approximately) true for real world datasets, in part due to the fact that these assumptions bestow constraints on the optimal cost, which are computationally hard to verify.

Motivating Questions

The above discussion naturally leads to the following questions:

1.

Can we identify structural properties of real-world datasets that are computationally verifiable, are well motivated in theory and empirically hold true for diverse modern datasets ?

2.

If so, can we exploit the existence of such structural properties to design better algorithms for
k
-means clustering?

Contributions.
In order to tackle these questions, we present the following contributions, which are discussed in detail in the next section :

1.

We propose the manifold hypothesis as a theoretically grounded, empirically tested and computationally verifiable assumption for real world clustering instances.

2.

Under this framework, we identify geometrical parameters which have predictable scaling law behaviour.

3.

We use this structure to design a fast seeding procedure through analyzing a
perturbed
version of
k
-means++ sampling

4.

We perform a large scale empirical study to validate our assumptions and the performance of our seeding algorithm.

1.1
Our Contributions

We propose the idea that the
manifold hypothesis
is a good candidate to derive such structural properties. The manifold hypothesis states that although real-world data often reside in high-dimensional ambient spaces, their probability mass tends to concentrate near low-dimensional, smooth submanifolds
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
with intrinsic dimension
d\ll D
. This hypothesis forms the conceptual bedrock for explaining the success of modern deep-learning systems
(
16
)
and has subsequently attracted thorough theoretical and experimental investigation
(
35
;
22
;
20
)
and hence is widely accepted and established. We formally state this:

Assumption 1
.

(Manifold hypothesis assumption)
The dataset
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
is generated by drawing
n
independent and identically distributed samples from a density function
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
whose support
\operatorname{supp}(f)
is a measurable subset of a smooth, compact
d
-dimensional submanifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

From the point of view of beyond–worst-case analysis, the manifold hypothesis offers a different type of structural regularity: instead of assuming properties about the
optimal solution
, it assumes geometric regularity of the
input distribution
. This makes it attractive for algorithm design, as the assumption is intrinsic to the data rather than to the algorithm’s output. Moreover, it is compatible with the increasing body of empirical evidence that high-dimensional machine learning data has low intrinsic dimension
(
66
;
44
)
. Finally, from a practical standpoint, the manifold hypothesis is more aligned with modern applications than separation- or stability-based assumptions. While the latter are tailored to idealized clustering scenarios, the manifold hypothesis is already the implicit structural model underlying essentially all deep learning architectures used in vision, NLP, physics, and generative tasks
(
16
;
28
)
What does this imply for the
k
-means problem ? A fundamental question is to study how the optimal

(

Empirical results.

We perform extensive empirical analysis to test whether our theoretical assumptions are reasonable for practical datasets. Our study captures cross-domain datasets covering image data, image and text embeddings, as well as tabular data captured from sensors and scientific experiments. Our assumptions can be easily checked by computing the cost for various values of
k
, then evaluating the geometric properties of the resulting clusters. We find that the scaling laws strongly manifest in these datasets. We also analyze the effect of intrinsic dimension on these laws using independent ID estimates. Finally, we observe that our seeding method gives about an order of magnitude speedup for
k
-means++ seeding on high dimensional datasets when compared to prior work.

D^{2}
distribution—which is expensive to update after every new center—we
sample from a much easier distribution and correct the bias using a rejection step.
The efficiency of this correction is controlled by a single quantity: the max
Rényi divergence between the two distributions.

Suppose
\mu
is a target distribution over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, and
\nu
is a proposal
distribution from which sampling is easy. Rejection sampling allows us to draw exact
samples from
\mu
using only samples from

, provided that
\mu
is not too
“peaked” relative to
\nu
.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability distributions supported on
\mathcal{X}
.
The max Rényi divergence of
\mu
from
\nu
is defined as

D_{\infty}(\mu\|\nu)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}.

Intuitively,
\exp(D_{\infty}(\mu\|\nu))
measures the worst-case mismatch between
\mu

and
\nu
, and directly determines how many proposal samples we need before one is
accepted.

RejectSample

Lemma 9

(Rejection sampling guarantee)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be distributions over a finite set
\mathcal{X}
with

D_{\infty}(\mu\|\nu)<\infty
, and set
M=\exp(D_{\infty}(\mu\|\nu))
.
Then:

1.

Conditioned on acceptance, Algorithm
4
outputs an exact

\mu
.

2.

Each iteration succeeds with probability at least
1/M
.

3.

Running the algorithm for

iterations succeeds with probability
at least

.

Proof.

In one iteration, the probability of accepting a point

is

\nu(x)\cdot\mu(x)/(M\nu(x))=\mu(x)/M
.
Thus, conditioned on acceptance, the output distribution is exactly
\mu
.
The total acceptance probability is
1/M
, and repeating for

∎

Conclusion, Limitations, Future Directions

In this work, we modelled typical
k
-means clustering instances from a manifold hypothesis perspective. Using techniques from optimum quantization theory, we identified key geometric properties of the dataset that follow predictable scaling laws. We also showed how to exploit these regularities to design a fast rejection sampling-based seeding algorithm with new tradeoffs. Our theoretical results were backed by extensive empirical evaluations. We feel that due to its broad impact on the machine learning community, studying algorithms under the manifold hypothesis is an appealing and exciting direction for future research.

Appendix A
Theoretical Analysis of Scaling Laws

In this section we provide theoretical proofs for the scaling laws presented in Theorem
4
. We mention that the techniques used here are well developed in optimum quantization theory
(
70
;
39
;
38
;
40
)
and we provide an elementary proof for the sake of being self-contained. Since we work under the manifold assumption, we require definitions and techniques from differential geometry for our proofs. We refer the reader to the book by
(
53
)
for obtaining familiarity with such notions. Recall that we are given a dataset
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
where the
x_{i}
’s are sampled i.i.d. from a probability density function
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
supported on a compact, connected and smooth subset of a manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The proof can be broken down into the following two steps :

•

Step 1
Showing that the optimum distortion
\Delta_{k}(f)=\inf_{|C|=k}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\min_{c\in C}\|x-c\|^{2}f(x)\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
follows the scaling law
\Delta_{k}(f)\in\Theta_{f}(k^{-2/d})
.

•

Step 2
Showing how to translate this in the finite sample case by studying how
\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\to\Delta_{k}(f)
as
n\to\infty
.

Preliminaries.

A function
\varphi:A\to B
between two metric spaces
A
and
B
is called a

homeomorphism
if
\varphi
is bijective and both
\varphi
and
\varphi^{-1}
are
continuous. A set
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
is called a smooth
d
-dimensional manifold embedded in

\mathbb{R}^{D}
if for each point
p\in\mathcal{M}
there exists an open neighborhood

W\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
of
p
and a smooth diffeomorphism

\varphi:W\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
such that
\varphi(\mathcal{M}\cap W)=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}^{D-d}.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{M}
admits a collection of local coordinate maps as follows.
A
chart
on
\mathcal{M}
is a pair
(U,\varphi)
, where
U\subset\mathcal{M}
is open and

\varphi:U\to V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a homeomorphism onto an open subset of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A collection of charts

\mathcal{A}=\{(U_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha\in I}

is called an
atlas
if
\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in I}U_{\alpha}
.
The atlas is said to be
smooth
if for every pair of overlapping charts

(U_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha})
and
(U_{\beta},\varphi_{\beta})
, the transition maps

\varphi_{\beta}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}:\varphi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta})\to\varphi_{\beta}(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta})

are smooth diffeomorphisms. Throughout, we assume that
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with the smooth atlas induced by its
embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. This atlas endows
\mathcal{M}
with the structure of a smooth
manifold and allows us to perform local analysis by identifying sufficiently
small neighborhoods of
\mathcal{M}
with open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 16

(Zador bounds for manifolds)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a compact,
d
-dimensional
C^{2}
submanifold and let

J\subset\mathcal{M}
be compact, measurable, and satisfy
\operatorname{vol}_{\mathcal{M}}(J)>0
. Let
f:J\to R_{+}
be a probability density function on
\mathcal{M}
such that
f_{min}<f(x)<f_{max}
and
\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=1
. Define

\Delta_{k}(f):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\int_{J}\min_{p\in S}\|x-p\|^{2}\,f(x)\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathcal{M}}(x).

Then there exist constants
0<c<C<\infty
depending only on
\mathcal{M}
,
J
and
f
such that

c\,k^{-2/d}\leq\Delta_{k}(f)\leq C\,k^{-2/d}\quad\text{for all }k\geq 1.

Proof.

Upper bound.

Since
J
is compact and
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth embedded submanifold, there exists a
finite atlas
\{(U_{\ell},\varphi_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{m}
such that

J=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m}J_{\ell},\qquad J_{\ell}\subset U_{\ell},

where each
\varphi_{\ell}:U_{\ell}\to V_{\ell}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a diffeomorphism.
By compactness and smoothness, shrinking charts if necessary, there exists
a constant
C_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
\ell
and all
x,y\in U_{\ell}
,

C_{0}^{-1}\|\varphi_{\ell}(x)-\varphi_{\ell}(y)\|\leq\|x-y\|\leq C_{0}\|\varphi_{\ell}(x)-\varphi_{\ell}(y)\|,

(1)

and the induced volume form satisfies

C_{0}^{-1}\,du\leq\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)\leq C_{0}\,du\quad\text{under }u=\varphi_{\ell}(x).

(2)

Fix
k\geq m
and set
k_{\ell}:=\lfloor k/m\rfloor
. The set

is compact, hence contained in a cube of side

. Partition this cube into

. Selecting one point from each subcube intersecting

\varphi_{\ell}(J_{\ell})
yields a set

\{u_{\ell,i}\}_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}}\subset\varphi_{\ell}(J_{\ell})
such that

\varphi_{\ell}(J_{\ell})\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}}B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u_{\ell,i},Ck^{-1/d}),

for a constant

depending only on
J
and

.

Define

and let

so that
|S|\leq k
. By (
1
), for every

,

\min_{p\in S}\|x-p\|\leq C^{\prime}k^{-1/d},

and hence

\min_{p\in S}\|x-p\|^{2}\leq C^{\prime\prime}k^{-2/d}.

Integrating over
J
and using the finiteness of

gives

\int_{J}\min_{p\in S}\|x-p\|^{2}\,f(x)\operatorname{dvol}_{\mathcal{M}}(x)\leq Ck^{-2/d},

which implies
\Delta_{k}(f)\leq Ck^{-2/d}
.

Lower bound.

Since
\operatorname{vol}_{\mathcal{M}}(J)>0
and
\mathcal{M}
is smooth, there exists a point
x_{0}\in J
and

\varrho>0
and
c_{0}>0
such that the geodesic ball

for all

\operatorname{vol}_{\mathcal{M}}(B_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{0},r))\geq c_{0}r^{d}.

(3)

Let

Therefore,

\operatorname{vol}_{\mathcal{M}}\!\left(\bigcup_{p\in S}B(p,r)\cap\mathcal{M}\right)\leq Ckr^{d}.

Choosing

Hence there exists a measurable subset

Lemma 17

(Hoeffding’s Inequality)
.

Let

be independent random variables such that

almost surely for each

\mathbf{Pr}[|S_{m}-\mathbf{E}[S_{m}]|\geq t]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(b_{j}-a_{j})^{2}}\right)

Lemma 18

(Covering Lemma)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded set. Then for any

For every

Using these, we can show the following :

Lemma 19

(Finite sample bound on

For any

\left|\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})-\Delta_{k}(f)\right|\in O_{f}\left(\sqrt{\frac{kD\log n+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

Proof.

Let us define the set of possible centers
\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\{(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}:\|c_{j}\|\leq 1,1\leq j\leq k\}
. For a fixed set of centers
C=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
, let us define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\ell(x|C)

\displaystyle:=\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\|x-c_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle L_{n}(C)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\leq i\leq n}\ell(x_{i}|C)

\displaystyle L(C)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{E}_{x\sim f}\ell(x|C)

It can be easily seen that the function

\ell(x|C)=\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\|x-c_{j}\|^{2}\leq\|x-c_{1}\|^{2}\leq 2(\|x\|^{2}+\|c_{1}\|^{2})\leq 4

Notice that by definition,
L_{n}(C)
is the average of
n
i.i.d. random variables
\ell(x_{i}|C)
each of which is bounded in
[0,4]
. Hence, applying Lemma
17
implies that for any
t>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\bigl(|L_{n}(C)-L(C)|\geq t\bigr)\leq 2\exp(-nt^{2}/8)

We next control the dependence of the loss on the centers. Let

C=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
and
C^{\prime}=(c_{1}^{\prime},\dots,c_{k}^{\prime})
be two elements of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
such
that

\max_{j\in[k]}\|c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}\|\leq\delta.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|\ell(x\mid C)-\ell(x\mid C^{\prime})\bigr|

\displaystyle=\left|\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\|x-c_{j}\|^{2}-\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\|x-c_{j}^{\prime}\|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\left|\|x-c_{j}\|^{2}-\|x-c_{j}^{\prime}\|^{2}\right|.

For a fixed
j
, we may expand

\displaystyle\left|\|x-c_{j}\|^{2}-\|x-c_{j}^{\prime}\|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\langle c_{j}^{\prime}-c_{j},\,2x-c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\|c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}\|\cdot\|2x-c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}\|.

Since
\|x\|\leq 1
and
\|c_{j}\|,\|c_{j}^{\prime}\|\leq 1
, it follows that

\|2x-c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}\|\leq 2\|x\|+\|c_{j}\|+\|c_{j}^{\prime}\|\leq 4.

Consequently,

\bigl|\ell(x\mid C)-\ell(x\mid C^{\prime})\bigr|\leq 4\max_{j\in[k]}\|c_{j}-c_{j}^{\prime}\|\leq 4\delta.

Averaging over the samples and taking expectation yields

|L_{n}(C)-L_{n}(C^{\prime})|\leq 4\delta,\qquad|L(C)-L(C^{\prime})|\leq 4\delta.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a parameter to be chosen later. By
Lemma
18
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-net

\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{C}_{k}
such that

|\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}|\leq\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}+1\right)^{kD}\leq\left(\frac{3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{kD}.

For any
C\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
, let
\Pi(C)\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
denote a nearest
net point. Then

|L_{n}(C)-L(C)|\leq|L_{n}(\Pi(C))-L(\Pi(C))|+8\varepsilon.

Applying Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound over

\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain for any
t>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\!\left(\sup_{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}|L_{n}(C^{\prime})-L(C^{\prime})|\geq t\right)\leq 2|\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}|\exp(-nt^{2}/8).

Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}|L_{n}(C^{\prime})-L(C^{\prime})|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{kD\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

Combining the above bounds, we conclude that with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}|L_{n}(C)-L(C)|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{kD\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\varepsilon.

Choosing
\varepsilon=1/n
gives
\log(1/\varepsilon)=\log n
, and hence

\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}|L_{n}(C)-L(C)|\in O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{kD\log n+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right).

Finally, observing that

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\inf_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}L_{n}(C),\qquad\Delta_{k}(f)=\inf_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}L(C),

we obtain

\left|\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})-\Delta_{k}(f)\right|\leq\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}|L_{n}(C)-L(C)|,

which completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to provide a proof for Theorem
4
.

Theorem 20

(Scaling laws)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
consists of i.i.d. samples
drawn from a distribution
f
supported on a compact, smooth
d
-dimensional
manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, with density bounded above and below on its support.
Let
\varepsilon:=2/d
. Then, with probability at least

1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
, the following hold:

\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\left(1+O_{f}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{kD\log n}{n}}\right)\right)k^{\varepsilon},\qquad\eta(\mathcal{X})\in O_{f}(1)\,n^{3\varepsilon/2}.

Proof.

We prove the two statements separately.

Analysis of
\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.

By definition,

\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{\operatorname{opt}_{1}(\mathcal{X})}{\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})},\qquad\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\min_{C:|C|=k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{c\in C}\|x_{i}-c\|^{2}.

Define the normalized empirical distortion

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X}).

By Lemma
19
, for any fixed
k
, with probability at least

1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
,

\left|\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})-\Delta_{k}(f)\right|\leq C_{f}\sqrt{\frac{kD\log n}{n}}.

In particular, the same bound holds for
k=1
with the factor
k
removed.

On the other hand, by Zador’s theorem on manifolds
(Lemma
16
and its matching lower bound),

\Delta_{1}(f)=\Theta_{f}(1),\qquad\Delta_{k}(f)=\Theta_{f}(k^{-2/d}).

Combining these estimates yields, with high probability,

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{1}(\mathcal{X})=\Theta_{f}(1)+O_{f}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{D\log n}{n}}\right),

and

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\Theta_{f}(k^{-2/d})\left(1+O_{f}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{kD\log n}{n}}\right)\right),

where the error term is of lower order provided

n\gg k^{1+2/d}\log n
.

Taking the ratio, we obtain

\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{\operatorname{opt}_{1}(\mathcal{X})}{\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{1}(\mathcal{X})}{\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{opt}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}=\left(1+O_{f}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{kD\log n}{n}}\right)\right)k^{2/d},

which proves the first claim.

Analysis of
\eta(\mathcal{X})
.

By definition,

\eta(\mathcal{X})=\frac{\max_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{\min_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}.

Since
f
is supported on a compact set, we have

\max_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{supp}f)=O_{f}(1)\quad\text{almost surely}.

It remains to lower bound the minimum interpoint distance.

By the volume growth property of a smooth
d
-dimensional manifold and the lower
bound on the density of
f
, there exists a constant
c_{f}>0
such that for all
sufficiently small
r
and all
x\in\mathcal{M}
,

\mathbf{Pr}(\|X-x\|\leq r)\leq c_{f}r^{d}.

Applying a union bound over all unordered pairs
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
, we obtain

\mathbf{Pr}\!\left(\min_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq r\right)\leq n^{2}c_{f}r^{d}.

Choosing
r=n^{-3/d}
yields

\mathbf{Pr}\!\left(\min_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq r\right)\leq c_{f}n^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{3}}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)},

for all sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, with probability at least

1-\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
,

\min_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq c_{f}^{\prime}n^{-3/d}

for some constant
c_{f}^{\prime}>0
. Combining this with the diameter bound yields

\eta(\mathcal{X})=\frac{O_{f}(1)}{\Omega_{f}(n^{-3/d})}=O_{f}\!\left(n^{3/d}\right)=O_{f}\!\left(n^{3\varepsilon/2}\right).

∎

For part 1, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\delta)\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}+\delta\cdot\frac{1}{n}}{(1-\delta)\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}+\delta\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\delta)\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}+\delta\cdot\frac{1}{n}}{(1-\delta)\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}

For part 2, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\delta)\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}+\delta\cdot\frac{1}{n}}{(1-\delta)\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}+\delta\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}\left(\frac{1+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}{n\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}}{1+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}{n\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}\left(1+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}{n\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(y,C)}{\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{X},C)}{n\cdot\operatorname{cost}^{\mathscr{L}}(\mathcal{Y},C)}\right)

where in the last step we used
(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
for any
x\geq 0
.
∎
