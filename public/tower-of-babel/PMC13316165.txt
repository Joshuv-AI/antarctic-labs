Genotoxicity of Carbohydrate Derived Fulvic Acid
Authors: Stewart James Clark, Michael Graz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Naturally derived and extracted fulvic acids have been used for immune modulation, antioxidant, diuretic, and antihypertensive applications for centuries. However, fulvic acid materials from the environment vary substantially in composition and may contain impurities such as heavy metals, increasing the toxicological risk to consumers when using these products. To overcome compositional variability and avoid environmental contaminants, carbohydrate-derived fulvic acid (CHD-FA) was developed by wet oxidation of sucrose using only molecular oxygen and water, and is manufactured under GMP via a patented process. To determine the genotoxicity of CHD-FA, we evaluated its potential toxicity using the OECD bacterial reverse mutation (Ames) test, the in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test and the mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test. In the Ames test, CHD-FA did not induce reverse mutations in either S. typhimurium or E. coli, nor did it increase the number of revertant bacterial colonies due to base-pair substitution or frameshift mutations. Furthermore, CHD-FA did not affect the Micronucleated Polychromatic Erythrocyte (MNPCE) count or Polychromatic/Normochromatic (PCE/NCE) ratio in an in vivo micronucleus test. A weak genotoxic effect was observed in human lymphocytes. This may have been due to extrinsic factors, such as the pH of the test solution, rather than because of the CHD-FA. These data indicate that CHD-FA is not genotoxic in the experimental models used.

Introduction

Humic substances are ubiquitous organic constituents of soils, peat, lignite or aquatic environments, formed through the long-term degradation of plant and animal biomass [1]. Humic substances comprise complex associations of smaller organic molecules rather than a single definable chemical structure and are commonly described as heterogeneous “superstructures” that are stabilised by a series of low-affinity interactions. Humic substances are divided into humic acids and fulvic acids based on their solubility during extraction and pH manipulation. Fulvic acids are soluble across a broader pH range than are humic acids, and they display a comparatively higher abundance of functional acidic groups [2].

Considering that fulvic acids comprise mixtures of numerous organic acids and can interact with minerals and other organic compounds, they have been used in a range of consumer and industrial products, including nutritional or wellness products and agricultural applications [3], [4]. In human health, fulvic acids have been used for immune modulation, antioxidant, diuretic, and antihypertensive applications [5].

However, fulvic acid materials that are sourced from the environment can vary substantially in composition depending on origin, extraction method, and processing conditions [6]. In addition, environmental sources may contain impurities that must be controlled (e.g., trace metals and other co-extracted constituents), increasing the analytical and toxicological burden required to establish a robust safety profile for regulated applications [7].

To overcome compositional variability, reduce environmental contaminants, and ensure consistent quality suitable for regulated use, carbohydrate-derived fulvic acid (CHD-FA) was developed [8]. CHD-FA is manufactured by the wet oxidation of sucrose using only molecular oxygen and water according to a patented process [8] following GMP. This generates a reproducible mixture of predominantly aliphatic- and aromatic carboxylic acids and related compounds (unpublished data).

CHD-FA has been characterised using a strategy designed for complex mixtures, employing multiple orthogonal methods to (i) identify- representative volatile, semi-volatile, and non-volatile constituents, with various molecular weights (ii) assess relative similarity across batches, and (iii) support the establishment of verifiable specifications. CHD-FA comprises on the order of 50–60 components, consisting largely of- organic acids with smaller amounts of residual sugars and other low-molecular-weight oxidation products (unpublished data). The major components of CHD-FA identified by LC-MS, GC-MS and NMR include acetic acid, levulinic acid, and succinic acid (unpublished data). CHD-FA is registered in the CAS Registry under 308066–67–3.

To our knowledge, no studies have reported a comprehensive genotoxicity assessment of a chemically defined, carbohydrate-derived fulvic acid. This distinction is important, as previously reported genotoxic effects of fulvic substances have been attributed to environmental variability, extraction processes, and co-extracted contaminants rather than to the intrinsic properties of the fulvic acid fraction itself.

Given that the intended application of CHD-FA is in products where repeated exposure may occur, a comprehensive safety evaluation program was warranted. Genotoxicity is a foundational element of such programs, supporting early identification of mutagenic or clastogenic hazards which inform the overall risk assessment for continued development [9]. Genotoxic agents are compounds that interact with DNA and alter its structure or function [9]. If these alterations become permanent and heritable, they are called mutations. Mutations generate genetic variation within populations and are essential for the long-term survival of species [10].

The present manuscript is the first in a planned series describing the toxicological and environmental safety profile of CHD-FA. Here, we report the results of in vitro genotoxicity and mutagenicity testing of CHD-FA using established assay systems designed to detect gene mutations and chromosomal damage. Subsequent manuscripts will describe (i) systemic and organ toxicity findings following extended repeated exposure in vivo and (ii) aquatic and environmental safety testing in relevant ecotoxicology models, providing an integrated assessment of safety for both human and environmental exposure scenarios.

Materials and methods

Reagents

4-nitro-o-phenylenediamine, DMSO, sodium azide, Eagle’s minimal essential medium, L-glutamine, penicillin, streptomycin, amphotericin B, foetal calf serum, Cytochalasin B, and cyclophosphamide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). All microbiological media, cell culture media, plasticware as well as histidine, biotin, phytohaemagglutinin, methanol, acetic acid, Giemsa stain, and formaldehyde were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific UK (Paisley United Kingdom). All other reagents were of analytical grade.

Microbiological strains

Salmonella typhimurium strains TA1535, TA1537, TA98, TA100 were obtained from the University of California (Berkley, USA). Escherichia coli strain WP2uvrA was obtained from the UK Health Security Agency.

Carbohydrate-derived fulvic acid

Carbohydrate-Derived Fulvic Acid (CHD-FA) CAS 308066–67–3 was obtained from Fulvimed SA (Pty) Ltd (Stellenbosch, South Africa). The CHD-FA was supplied as an aqueous 47.1 mg/ml solution with a molecular weight of less than 5000 Da.

Genotoxicity assays

Ames test

CHD-FA was fully miscible in water at 50 mg/ml and therefore sterile distilled water was used as the test vehicle. Vehicle and positive controls were used in parallel with the test material [11].

Salmonella typhimurium strains TA1535, TA1537, TA98, TA100 are histidine-dependent mutants and are used to identify base-pair substitutions (TA1535, TA100) and frameshift mutations (TA1537, TA98) [11], with TA100 and TA98 featuring enhanced sensitivity due to the pKM101 plasmid. Escherichia coli WP2uvrA is a tryptophan-auxotrophic, DNA repair-deficient (uvrA) strain to detect base-pair substitutions.

Following the plate incorporation method of Maron and Ames [11], [12], different concentrations of CHD-FA (50, 150, 500, 1500 and 5000 μg/plate) were mixed with 0.1 ml of bacterial culture and 2 ml of top agar containing trace amounts of histidine and biotin. No test material precipitate was observed on the plates at any of the doses tested in either the presence or absence of the S9 microsomal fraction.

For assays with metabolic activation, 0.5 ml of a S9 microsomal fraction from Sprague-Dawley rat livers was added. Sterile distilled water was used as the negative control. The positive control for TA98 was 4-nitro-o-phenylenediamine in DMSO at 10 μg/plate. For TA100 without metabolic activation, sodium azide in distilled water was used at 1.25 μg/plate.

In assays with metabolic activation, 2-anthramine at 3 μg/plate served as the mutagen for TA1535, TA1537, TA98 and TA100. After gentle homogenization, the mixtures were poured onto minimal glucose agar plates, incubated for 48 h at 37◦C, and the number of revertant colonies per plate was counted; all assays were performed in triplicate.

The vehicle control returned revertant colony counts within the normal range, and all positive control chemicals induced significant increases in revertant colony frequency, both with and without metabolic activation. Thus, the sensitivity of the assay and the efficacy of the S9-microsomal fraction were validated.

In vitro micronucleus assay

The in vitro micronucleus assay was performed according to the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Guidelines for the Testing of Chemicals, Test Number 487 [13].

Cells

Peripheral whole blood was obtained from a 37-year-old female volunteer. The volunteer had not been exposed to high levels of radiation or hazardous chemicals and had not knowingly suffered from a viral infection recently. The cell-cycle time for the lymphocytes from the donors used in this study was determined using Bromodeoxyuridine incorporation to assess the numbers of first-, second-, and third-division metaphase cells, and thus calculate the average generation time, which was about 17 h under typical experimental conditions.

The work described was performed in compliance with UK GLP standards (Schedule 1, Good Laboratory Practice Regulations 1999 (SI 1999/3106 as amended by SI 2004/0994)).

Cell culture

Cells were grown in Eagle's minimal essential medium (MEM), supplemented with L-glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin, amphotericin B and 15% foetal calf serum, at 37°C with 5% CO2 in air. The lymphocytes of fresh heparinised whole blood were stimulated to divide by the addition of phytohaemagglutinin (PHA) at 90 μg/ml final concentration.

Assay

Lymphocytes were exposed to CHD-FA for 4 h without S9 microsomal fractions, followed by 28 h of culture in MEM supplemented with Cytochalasin B prior to cell harvest. A second experiment was performed in which lymphocytes were exposed to CHDFA for 4 h with S9 microsomal fractions, followed by 28 h of culture in MEM supplemented with Cytochalasin B prior to cell harvest. In both experiments, CHD-FA was dosed at 78.13–1250 μg/ml.

The cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in methanol/glacial acetic acid (5:1 v/v). A drop of the cell suspension was placed on a clean, dry slide to prepare a smear. Slides were air-dried at room temperature and stained with 5% Giemsa.

The micronucleus frequency was determined in 2000 binucleated cells per dose (1000 binucleated cells per culture, two cultures per concentration). Cells with 1, 2 or more micronuclei were recorded.

All vehicle controls showed frequencies of micronucleated cells within the range expected for normal human lymphocytes [13]. The positive control induced statistically significant increases in the frequency of cells with micronuclei, indicating the satisfactory performance of the test system and of metabolic activation.

In vivo micronucleus assay

The in vivo micronucleus assay was performed in accordance with the OECD Guidelines for the Testing of Chemicals, Test Number 474 [14].

Animals

Albino Hsd: ICR (CD−1®) strain mice were used for the assay. At the start of the test, the male mice weighed 24–30 g and were approximately five to eight weeks old. After a minimum acclimatisation period of five days, animals were randomly selected for testing.

The animals were housed in groups of up to seven, by sex, in solid-floor polypropylene cages with wood-flake bedding. Free access to mains drinking water and food was allowed throughout the study.

The temperature and relative humidity were set to achieve limits of 19–25ºC and 30–70%, respectively. Any occasional deviations from these targets were considered not to have affected the purpose or integrity of the study. The rate of air exchange was approximately 15 changes per hour, and the lighting was on a 12-hour light/dark cycle.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986.

Micronucleus test

The micronucleus test was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the OECD Test Guideline 474. Male mice were randomly allocated into groups of six animals each. Negative control groups received only the vehicle. Treatment groups were given oral CHD-FA solutions at doses of 100, 50 or 25 mg/kg body weight, in an average volume of 3 ml, by gavage once daily for two consecutive days. The positive control group received cyclophosphamide monohydrate at 20 mg/kg body weight via intraperitoneal injection; this agent is a well-established positive control known to induce micronuclei under the test conditions. An additional naïve group of rodents received no treatment and served as an untreated control. After the treatment period, the animals were euthanised by decapitation.

Femurs were removed, and the proximal epiphysis was opened to access the medullary canal. Bone marrow cells were flushed out with 3 ml of 150 mM NaCl using a 13 × 4.5 mm needle. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet resuspended in 4% formaldehyde (v/v). A drop of the cell suspension was placed on a clean, dry slide and air-dried at room temperature, fixed in absolute ethanol, and stained with May-Grünwald/Giemsa 24 h later for microscopic evaluation.

For micronucleus scoring, 2000 polychromatic erythrocytes (PCE) per animal were counted using an optical microscope. Additionally, 1000 erythrocytes were evaluated to determine the number of normochromatic erythrocytes (NCE), which were also assessed for micronuclei. The ratio of polychromatic to normochromatic erythrocytes was calculated, along with group mean values and standard deviations.

Statistics

For the human lymphocyte micronucleus test, the frequency of cells with micronuclei was assessed using Chi-squared Test (p < 0.05). For the mouse micronucleus tests the data was analysed using a two-tailed T-Test and any significant results (p < 0.05) were confirmed using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

At 5000 µg/plate, CHD-FA caused a visible reduction in the growth of the bacterial lawns of several experimental strains both in the presence and absence of the S9 microsomal fraction. However, the sensitivity of the strains to the toxicity of the test material varied between strain type and exposure to the S9 microsomal fraction. CHD-FA was, therefore, tested up to the maximum recommended dose of 5000 µg/plate.

No toxicologically significant increases in the frequency of revertant colonies were recorded for any of the bacterial strains, with any dose of the test material, either with or without metabolic activation.

Although a small response was observed for TA1537 at the higher dose levels, it was considered doubtful that the CHD-FA was causing a true mutagenic effect because there was a lack of a clear, dose-related, reproducible response and CHD-FA exhibited direct toxicity at 5000 µg/plate under certain circumstances and exposure conditions.

When compared to the negative control group, none of the tested concentrations of CHD-FA elicited a significant mutagenic response

There were no premature deaths seen in any of the groups dosed with CHD-FA. Clinical signs were observed in animals dosed with CHD-FA ≥ 50 mg/kg in both the 24 and 48-hour groups where appropriate for the experiments and included: hunched posture, ptosis and ataxia.

A statistically significant decrease in the PCE/NCE ratio was observed in the 48-hour 100 mg/kg dose group and, whilst not statistically significant, a small decrease was also observed in the 24-hour test 100 mg/kg dose group when compared to the vehicle control.

One mouse in the 48-hour 100 mg/kg test material dose group, evidenced a majority of NCE over PCE. It was considered that CHD-FA induced toxicity had halted the formation of PCE. Therefore, the decreases in PCE/NCE ratio, together with the high level of toxicity observed in this mouse and the observation of clinical signs at and above 50 mg/kg, were taken to indicate that systemic absorption of the CHD-FA had occurred and exposure to the target tissue had been achieved.

There was no evidence of a significant increase in the incidence of MNPCE in animals dosed with the test material when compared to the concurrent vehicle control groups.

The positive control showed a marked increase in the incidence of MNPCE, hence confirming the sensitivity of the system to the known mutagenic activity of cyclophosphamide under the conditions of the test.

Discussion

The results of the bacterial reverse mutation assay demonstrate that CHD-FA does not induce gene mutations in standard prokaryotic systems, consistent with previous reports on fulvic and humic substances that have not undergone oxidative modification or environmental contamination [15], [16]. However, the variability in genotoxic responses reported for environmentally sourced fulvic acids highlights the importance of the material's origin and composition, including any non-fulvic acid contaminants [17], [18], [19].

Unlike naturally derived fulvic acids, which are heterogeneous mixtures influenced by environmental conditions and may contain trace metals or nitrogen-containing contaminants, CHD-FA is produced by the wet oxidation of sucrose in a GMP-certified facility [8]. This results in a reproducible and chemically defined material, reducing the likelihood that observed biological effects are driven by extrinsic contaminants rather than intrinsic properties of the fulvic acid fraction.

In the present study, a weak but statistically significant increase in micronucleus formation was observed in human lymphocytes in vitro. Similar observations have been reported for fulvic and humic substances in mammalian cell systems [18], [19] under conditions associated with reduced cell proliferation and a measurable decrease in pH. It is well established that changes in pH or other culture conditions can induce cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and false-positive micronucleus formation [22]. As such, the observed response may be an artefact of assay conditions rather than a direct genotoxic effect of CHD-FA.

Previous in vivo studies using environmentally derived fulvic acids have reported chromosomal aberrations, potentially associated with either secondary chemical transformations of the fulvic acids, such as chlorination, or with the presence of co-extracted impurities [17], [20]. In contrast, the present study utilises a chemically defined material and demonstrates no genotoxic effect in vivo.

Importantly, no genotoxic effect was observed in the in vivo micronucleus assay, despite clear evidence of systemic exposure as indicated by changes in the polychromatic to normochromatic erythrocyte (PCE/NCE) ratio. This finding is critical, as in vivo assays account for absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion processes and are therefore considered more relevant for assessing human risk. The absence of in vivo micronucleus induction is also consistent with reports of rapid biotransformation and limited systemic persistence of fulvic acid components [15], [21].

Taken together, the absence of mutagenicity in the Ames test, the lack of genotoxicity in vivo, and the known susceptibility of in vitro micronucleus assays to pH-related artefacts support a weight-of-evidence conclusion that CHD-FA does not pose a genotoxic risk under physiological conditions.

To contextualise the results of this study, several factors are worth considering. Firstly, the pH of CHD-FA solutions could not be fully controlled in the in vitro micronucleus assay without compromising solubility, which may have contributed to the observed weak positive response [22]. Secondly, CHD-FA, like environmental fulvic acid extracts, is a complex mixture rather than a single defined compound, which may complicate mechanistic interpretation. Thirdly, no mechanistic assays were performed to investigate the origin of the weakly-positive in vitro signal. Finally, while the in vivo micronucleus assay provides important information on chromosomal damage, additional long-term studies may be required to fully characterise the genotoxic risk of CHD-FA under chronic exposure conditions.

This study, therefore, provides a comparison between environmentally derived fulvic acids and CHD-FA, helping to reconcile previously inconsistent reports on the genotoxicity of environmental fulvic acids by distinguishing intrinsic effects from artefacts arising from composition and assay conditions.

Conclusion

Similar to results previously reported for the toxicology of environmental fulvic acids [15], [17], in the Ames test, CHD-FA was not found to be genotoxic, based on reverse mutation tests using S. typhimurium and E. coli, and did not result in an increased number of revertant bacterial colonies by base-pair substitution or frameshift mutation. Furthermore, CHD-FA did not affect the MNPCE count or PCE/NCE ratio in an in vivo micronucleus test. Finally, the weak genotoxic effect observed in human lymphocytes may have been due to extrinsic factors, such as the pH of the test solution [22], supporting the conclusion that CHD-FA is unlikely to be genotoxic under the experimental conditions employed.
