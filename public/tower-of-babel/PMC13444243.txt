Spatial transcriptomics reveals coordinated ventricular patterning and maturation in the developing human heart
Authors: Zehao Yao, Lina Bai, Yifan Xie, Yixun Li, Feiyang Ma, Songrui Jia, Shijie Sun, Minjiong Zhao, Yanchun Wang, Cheng-Ran Xu, Hou-Zao Chen, Yu Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human heart development requires coordinated gene regulation across spatially organized tissues. However, the principles by which the heart establishes spatially organized structures and how regional programs integrate to orchestrate coordinated development remain unclear. Here, we generate a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of the developing human hearts, profiling 30 sections spanning 8–15 post-conception weeks. By treating each spatial spot as a multicellular unit, we resolve anatomically coherent compartments and identify specialized subpopulations, including the Papillary muscle and Atrioventricular Plane. Focusing on the ventricle, trajectory inference reveals a continuous endocardium-to-epicardium transcriptional gradient, accompanied by coordinated changes in gene expression, transcription factor activity, and signaling pathways. Integrative developmental analysis identifies shared maturation-associated transcriptional changes, with increasing contractile and metabolic programs and declining proliferative signatures, alongside region-specific specialization across spatial domains. Together, this study defines a spatial framework for ventricular patterning and maturation, providing a resource for investigating human cardiac development and disease.

Introduction

The human heart is a structurally intricate organ in which diverse cell types are coordinated under precise spatiotemporal regulation to establish the four-chambered architecture during development. Cardiogenesis begins with cardiac progenitors from the first heart field forming the primitive heart tube, followed by contributions from the second heart field, progressing through elongation, looping, and chamber specification1. Coordinated morphogenetic events, including trabecular compaction, endocardial cushion formation, septation, and valve development, together with maturation of the conduction system, culminate in a fully functional heart, whereas perturbations of these tightly coordinated processes result in a broad spectrum of congenital heart diseases. Compared with model organisms, human heart development exhibits prolonged temporal scaling and distinctive ventricular wall maturation, characterized by more complex trabecular architecture and extended compaction dynamics. These processes involve extensive tissue remodeling and dynamic spatial reorganization over developmental time. Thus, a comprehensive map of how anatomical structures emerge in the human embryonic heart is needed.

Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing has enabled systematic characterization of cell type diversity and lineage specification in the developing human heart2–4. Spatial transcriptomics further complements these datasets by anchoring transcriptional states to anatomical coordinates, thereby providing a framework to link molecular identity with tissue architecture3,5. However, most current studies focus on individual cells or discrete cell types, whereas organogenesis emerges from the coordinated behavior of multicellular communities embedded within spatially structured environments. Consequently, how these multicellular communities are spatially organized and how their coordinated gene programs shape human cardiac development remain unknown.

In this study, we constructed a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of the developing human heart across four embryonic/fetal stages (8, 9, 10, and 15 post-conception weeks) using the 10× Visium platform. Considering each sequencing spot as a functional unit (containing 5–10 spatially adjacent cells), we resolved the spatial heterogeneity of the developing heart. This approach identified ten major cardiac compartments and further delineated eight ventricular and eight atrial subpopulations. Notably, our analysis revealed spatial gene-expression gradients extending from endocardium to epicardium across the ventricular wall, indicating that the ventricular wall represents a continuum of transcriptional states. Moreover, we delineated the developmental trajectories of the ventricle, identifying both global programs shared across regions and region-specific patterns that confer local specialization. This atlas provides a rich resource for exploring the spatial principles of human heart development, highlighting how multicellular organization underpins morphogenesis and disease susceptibility.

Results

Spatial transcriptomic map of the developing human heart

To define how transcriptional programs are spatially organized during human heart development, we generated a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas using 10× Visium spatial platform on 30 serial sections from four fetal hearts spanning 8, 9, 10, and 15 post-conception weeks (PCW) (Fig. 1a). Non-cardiac spots inadvertently captured during tissue sampling were excluded, including trachea/lung-associated regions (marked by SCGB3A2), skeletal muscle regions (marked by MYBPH), and thymic tissue (marked by TBATA) (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). After quality control and filtering, a total of 60,157 spots (an average of 2005 spots per section and 3170 features per spot) were collected (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Pseudo-bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed global transcriptional distinctions across development stages (Supplementary Fig. 2a), highlighting dynamic molecular transitions during heart development. We concatenated the spatial transcriptomic data across all captured spots and utilized Harmony6 to eliminate batch effects (Supplementary Fig. 2b, 2c). After unsupervised clustering, the spots were categorized into 20 distinct clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e) and were subsequently consolidated into 10 compartments annotated based on their transcriptional and spatial patterns (Figs. 1b, c, and Supplementary Data 1). These compartments correspond to anatomical and functional units, including ventricular and atrial myocardium, valvular structures, coronary vasculature, epicardium, and surrounding pericardial tissues, etc (Fig. 1b, c, Supplementary Fig. 2f). Rather than representing isolated cell types, each compartment comprised spatially organized multicellular niches with coordinated transcriptional programs. To annotate the cellular identities underlying each spatial compartment, we integrated multiple complementary analyses using the published human fetal heart single-cell RNA-seq dataset from ref. 5. We first deconvoluted the cellular composition of each compartment using Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) analysis7. The spatial transcriptomic compartments show strong concordance with their expected cell type compositions, reinforcing the robustness of clustering based on spot-specific transcriptional profiles (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3). We further applied Seurat’s TransferData framework for spot-level reference mapping (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), quantified transcriptional similarity between spatial compartments and reference cell types using the Jaccard index of marker gene sets (Supplementary Fig. 4d), and assessed cell-type signature enrichment by AddModuleScore (Supplementary Fig 4e, f). These analyses consistently demonstrated strong concordance between the spatial compartments and their expected reference cell identities, thereby strengthening the robustness of our spatial annotation.

Differential gene expression analysis revealed distinct molecular signatures corresponding to each compartment (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 2). The ventricular compartment exhibited strong expression of MYH7, COX6A2, and MT3, consistent with their roles in contractile function and metabolic activity. The atrial compartment exhibited high expression of MYH6, COL2A1, and GJA5, delineating their distinct structural and electrophysiological properties8,9. The coronary-associated compartment, located at the coronary ostia or embedded within the ventricular wall (Fig. 1f), was distinguished by CLDN5, a critical endothelial tight junction component, and CLEC14A, a regulator of endothelial adhesion and angiogenic signaling10,11, indicating their endothelial identity and involvement in coronary vasculature development. The valvular compartment was characterized by the expression of PENK, HAPLN1, and FMOD, genes essential for extracellular matrix composition and biomechanical integrity during valvular development12–14. Furthermore, we identified the neural compartment surrounding the atria (Fig. 1f), characterized by high expression of neural genes SST, STMN2, and PRPH. The epicardial compartment, located at the outermost layer of the heart, was marked by the expression of ITLN1, ALDH1A2 and PRG4, associated with barrier function15, retinoic acid signaling16,17, and tissue lubrication18, respectively, highlighting its protective and paracrine signaling functions during heart development. Notably, the pericardial compartment (DLK, SPP1, LUM) encapsulates both the heart and the adjacent perivascular compartment (MYH11, FBLN5, ACTG2) (Fig. 1f), lying external to the epicardium and separated from it by a cavity-like space (pericardial cavity) (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Its spatial location (Supplementary Fig. 5b), cellular composition (Supplementary Fig. 5c), and enrichment of ECM and WNT-related genes19,20 (Supplementary Fig. 5d–f) support its identity as fibrous pericardium, which provides mechanical protection to the developing heart21.

These findings define spatially resolved cardiac compartments, emphasizing that the transcriptional signatures of each multicellular community (i.e., a spot), despite comprising a diverse array of cell types, serve as the cornerstone underpinning the maintenance of anatomical integrity during heart development.

Spatial transcriptional architecture of the developing ventricle

The ventricle, as the primary chamber responsible for cardiac pumping, represents a central locus of morphogenetic complexity during heart development. To dissect its spatial and molecular organization, we extracted the ventricular compartment for further investigation. Reanalysis of the ventricular compartment resulted in eight spatially coherent subpopulations, corresponding to anatomically defined territories, including left ventricular compact (LV-Compact), LV-Hybrid, LV-Trabecular, right ventricular compact (RV-Compact), RV-Trabecular, Papillary muscle, Atrioventricular Plane and Ventricular Endocardium subpopulation (Fig. 2a–c, and Supplementary Data 3), which represent spatially organized multicellular transcriptional domains. To characterize their functional properties, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on the top 100 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each subpopulation (Figs. 2d, e). The LV-Compact subpopulation exhibited high expression of HEY2, MT3, and RBP7, localizing to the outer left ventricular free wall while seamlessly extending into the muscular portion of the interventricular septum. The LV-Hybrid, characterized by high expression of PNCK and IGFBP3 and exhibiting features of both compact (HEY2, MT3, RBP7) and trabecular (MYOM2, IRX3, CRNDE) myocardium (Fig. 2d), represents a hybrid zone undergoing progressive trabecular compaction that ultimately contributes to enhanced contractile strength22,23, while also potentially reflecting spot-level mixing between adjacent compact and trabecular cardiomyocytes. Unlike LV-Compact extending into the interventricular septum, RV-Compact is restricted to the outer layer of the right ventricle and shows high expression of HSPB6, IGFBP4, and CKM. RV-Trabecular is enriched for MASP1, a serine protease primarily involved in the lectin pathway of the complement system, and PRRX1, a transcription factor involved in epithelial to mesenchymal transition24 and cardiac electrical conduction25. We also identified the Ventricular Endocardium subpopulation, marked by a high proportion of endocardial cells (Supplementary Fig. 8b) and elevated expression of CDH11, LEPR, and CTHRC1, with gene ontology enrichment in extracellular matrix organization and vasculature development (Fig. 2e). Interestingly, unsupervised clustering did not resolve the muscular interventricular septum (IVS) and left ventricular free wall (LVFW) as distinct subpopulations; instead, both regions mapped to the LV-compact subpopulation (Fig. 2c), suggesting their broadly shared transcriptional programs. To elucidate the potential regional difference, we manually annotated spots into the IVS and LVFW components and performed pseudobulk differential gene expression analysis at each developmental stage (Supplementary Fig. 7a, 7b). Consistent with their similar cellular composition (Supplementary Fig. 7c), only a small number of genes met the significance threshold (P < 0.01 and |log2FC | > 0.5) at each stage (Supplementary Fig. 7b), indicating limited transcriptional divergence. Across stages, no genes were consistently enriched in the IVS. In contrast, HAND1 was reproducibly enriched in the LVFW and expressed at lower levels in the IVS (Supplementary Fig. 7d), in agreement with prior lineage-tracing studies in mice using Hand1-eGFPCre models that demonstrated preferential HAND1 activity in the left ventricular free wall myocardium26.

Together, our findings unveil a hitherto unrecognized layer of cellular heterogeneity, suggesting a greater level of complexity than previously appreciated.

Functional specialization at the Papillary muscle and Atrioventricular Plane

In addition to compact and trabecular myocardium, we identified two anatomically and functionally specialized subpopulations shared across both ventricles: the Papillary muscle and the Atrioventricular Plane (Fig. 2a–c). The Papillary muscles, which anchor the atrioventricular valves via the chordae tendineae, function to prevent valve prolapse or regurgitation during systole. Regional enrichment of ACTA1 and elevated MYL2 expression suggest specialized contractile properties that enhance mechanical resilience, while high expression of NPPB indicates a hemodynamic load response, consistent with their adaptation to fluctuating intracardiac pressure (Figs. 2d, 3a). To further characterize spatial gene variation within the Papillary muscle, we applied SPATA2 to infer a continuous spatial trajectory across this structure. Along the papillary muscle axis, we observed genes showing progressive upregulation, including ACTA1 and MYL2, as well as genes exhibiting decreasing trends, such as NPPA and MYL7 (Figs. 3b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 8a), suggesting progressive transcriptional remodeling across the Papillary muscle. We further examined spatial communication flow within the Papillary muscle niche. The Atrioventricular Plane, enriched in ASPN, SPON1, and TAGLN, originates from the fusion of endocardial cushions, incorporates the membranous portion of the IVS, and plays a critical role in atrioventricular septation and valve anchorage27, corresponding to the cardiac fibrous skeleton. Compared to other subpopulations, it exhibited a higher proportion of fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Consistently, GO analysis reveals significant enrichment for biological processes associated with extracellular matrix organization, TGF-β signaling, valve development and cardiac septum development (Fig. 2e). The spatial communication flow analysis revealed that TGF-β and BMP signaling formed pronounced convergent patterns within the membranous IVS and valve-associated regions (Fig. 3e), underscoring the pivotal role of these signaling pathways in the Atrioventricular Plane subpopulation in orchestrating EndMT, septal development, and valvulogenesis. We applied genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) to integrate spatial transcriptomics with GWAS data and map disease associations in a spatially resolved manner28. Mitral valve prolapse (MVP), a valvular disorder characterized by leaflet displacement and regurgitation, showed the strongest spatial associations with the Papillary muscle and the Atrioventricular Plane (Figs. 3f, g), underscoring the critical roles of the papillary muscle in tethering the leaflets and the Atrioventricular Plane in supporting the valvular annulus. Notably, ALPK3, an MVP candidate gene29, had the highest gene specificity scores in these regions (Figs. 3h, i), highlighting these regions as key sites of genetic convergence.

Having resolved anatomically and transcriptionally distinct ventricular subpopulations, we next asked whether these spatial domains also harbor differential vulnerability to genes implicated in noncompaction-associated cardiomyopathies. We therefore analyzed pathogenic genes associated with LVNC, DCM, and HCM from ClinVar30. Many of these genes were preferentially expressed in the papillary muscle region (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Although each cardiomyopathy showed a distinct subset of genes, 24 pathogenic genes were shared across all three diseases, suggesting a common core module underlying convergent vulnerability during ventricular wall development and maturation (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Expression of these shared genes is summarized in the heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Functionally, they include sarcomeric and cytoskeletal genes (e.g. MYH7, MYBPC3, TTN, ACTN2, DES, and FLNC) as well as ion-handling and signaling genes (e.g., SCN5A, PLN, and CACNA1C). Their coordinated spatial enrichment suggests localized developmental vulnerability to perturbations in contractile, structural, and electrophysiological programs during ventricular wall remodeling.

Together, these findings indicate that the Papillary muscle and Atrioventricular Plane emerge as clinically relevant loci linking developmental architecture with the mechanical and genetic bases of heart disease.

Anatomically informed trajectories in the developing ventricle

To characterize connectivity among ventricular subpopulations, we performed Partition-based Graph Abstraction (PAGA) analysis. The Atrioventricular Plane and Papillary muscle exhibited relatively weak connectivity to other ventricular subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b) and were therefore excluded from trajectory inference at individual time points to ensure robust topology reconstruction. The inferred topologies consistently revealed a spatially guided trajectory connecting left (LV-Compact, LV-Hybrid, LV-Trabecular) and right (RV-Compact, RV-Trabecular) ventricular branches, with the Ventricular Endocardium subpopulation serving as a central hub across developmental time (8, 9, 10, 15 PCW) (Fig. 4a). To further explore the transition patterns among these subpopulations, we inferred pseudotime trajectory using Monocle3, designating the Ventricular Endocardium as the root. As expected, the analysis resolved two distinct branches, with pseudotime progression recapitulating an inward-to-outward spatial gradient across the ventricular wall (Figs. 4b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 9c). We defined a transmural graph depth to quantify each spot’s relative position across the ventricular wall (see “Methods”), ranging from 0 (endocardial surface) to 1 (epicardial boundary and IVS midline) (Figs. 4d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 9d). To explore the spatial transcriptional dynamics of the ventricles, we identified genes that varied significantly along the trajectory and grouped them into four modules (Left-end, Left-mid, Middle, Right) (Fig. 4g, and Supplementary Fig. 10). GO enrichment revealed a transmural reorganization of dominant biological programs across the left and right ventricular wall (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 9f and Fig. 10). The Left-end module was enriched for ATP metabolic processes, consistent with the high bio-energetic demand of compact myocardium. The Left-mid module was enriched for terms related to cardiac contraction and electrical conduction, aligning with functional specialization in excitation–contraction coupling. In the Middle module, enrichment shifted toward endothelial development, WNT signaling, wound healing, and ECM organization, suggesting tissue restructuring within the Ventricular Endocardium subpopulation. The Right module similarly showed enrichment for oxidative phosphorylation and ATP-generating metabolic processes, mirroring the metabolic profile of the left-end module. This parallel metabolic demand is consistent with their shared identity as highly contractile compact myocardium, which requires abundant ATP to sustain pumping activity. Notably, all modules shared GO terms related to heart morphogenesis and muscle tissue development, reflecting a common foundational program of ventricular structure.

To further assess signaling dynamics along this trajectory, we inferred pathway activities across pseudotime and ventricular subpopulations using decoupleR. This analysis revealed spatially coordinated signaling gradients along the endocardium-to-epicardium axis (Supplementary Fig. 11). In particular, TGF-β signaling was preferentially activated at the trajectory origin corresponding to the endocardium, whereas the LV-Hybrid region showed relatively elevated hypoxia pathway activity. These results suggest that ventricular wall patterning is accompanied by spatially organized shifts in signaling activity.

Given the distinct developmental origins of the left ventricle (LV) and right ventricle (RV), we next examined potential differences in their transcriptional progression using gene-level pseudotime alignment (Genes2Genes)31 (Fig. 4i, Supplementary Fig. 12a, 12d). Despite these differences in origin, LV and RV trajectories exhibited highly concordant transcriptional dynamics, with mean alignment similarity scores of 95.50%, 90.93%, and 91.35% at 8, 9, and 10 PCW, respectively (Fig. 4j, and Supplementary Fig. 12b, 12e). To identify chamber-specific divergence, we defined trajectory mismatches as genes with low alignment similarity (≤ 50%) and high optimal alignment cost (≥ 30 nits) (Supplementary Fig. 12c, 12f, 12g). Mismatched genes were identified independently at each time point, and the intersection across 8–10 PCW yielded six recurrent genes (HSPB6, CSMD1, MYL4, LINC02500, TNNC1, and LGALS3) (Fig. 4k, Supplementary Fig. 12h).

Together, these analyses reveal that formation of the ventricular wall is accompanied by a spatially coordinated continuum of transcriptional programs. This continuum integrates metabolic, contractile, and tissue-remodeling signals to establish regional functional identities, while remaining largely conserved between the LV and RV despite their distinct developmental origins.

Transcriptional regulators underlying ventricular patterning and spatial specification

To delineate the regulatory landscape underlying spatially organized ventricular gradients, we inferred transcription factor (TF) activity across all ventricular subpopulations using decoupleR32. Distinct TF activity patterns were observed, reflecting region-specific regulatory states (Fig. 5a). JARID2 and REST, two transcriptional repressors, exhibited preferential activation in the compact myocardium. Classic cardiac transcription factors such as TBX20, NKX2-5, and HAND1, were predominantly activated within the LV-Trabecular subpopulation. MYF6, a myogenic transcriptional activator, and KLF3, a Krüppel-like transcriptional repressor, showed higher activation in the RV-Compact subpopulation. Meanwhile, ZNF804A and RLF were active in the RV-Trabecular myocardium and also upregulated in the LV-Trabecular myocardium. Region-specific regulatory signatures further highlighted functionally specialized domains. In the Papillary muscle, TEAD4 was strongly activated (Fig. 5a–d), with regulon targets including structural and contractile genes such as ACTA1, consistent with marker gene of this region. As a key effector of YAP-mediated mechanotransduction35,36, TEAD4 may link local mechanical cues to regionally enriched transcriptional programs, potentially contributing to the mechanical demands associated with valvular anchoring and ventricular contraction. Gene ontology enrichment of the top 5 regulon-associated targets in Papillary muscle subpopulation revealed processes related to muscle development, differentiation, extracellular matrix organization, and oxygen response (Supplementary Fig. 13), which may reflect structural remodeling and mechanical adaptation characteristics of the Papillary muscle region. The Atrioventricular Plane exhibited activation of TBX3, the key transcription factor for conduction-system formation37,38, and SMAD1, mediating BMP signaling39 (Figs. 2e, 5a), underscoring its dual role in conduction system development and valve morphogenesis.

We next examined chamber-specific regulatory programs by comparing TF activity between the left and right ventricles. Notably, HAND1, TBX20, and HOPX exhibited a left-ventricle-dominant activation pattern, with peak activity observed in the trabecular regions (Figs. 5e, f). HAND1, a basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor essential for ventricular morphogenesis, with mutations known to cause left ventricular hypoplasia40, conduction system defects41, and ventricular septal defects2, was further predicted in silico to contribute to maintenance of the left ventricular transmural gradient. Specifically, in silico knockout of HAND1 disrupted the trabecular-to-compact depth gradient, particularly within the LV trabecular region, whereas overexpression accentuated this gradient, underscoring its importance in preserving LV spatial patterning (Fig. 5g). In contrast, MYF5, MYF6, and KLF3 displayed a distinct right-ventricular enrichment. This asymmetric distribution of TF activity underscores distinct transcriptional programs shaping the identity and function of the left and right ventricles (Figs. 5e, f), and further supports the existence of asymmetric regulatory programs underlying chamber-specific ventricular identity.

To further identify regulators associated with transmural patterning, we projected TF activity along the ventricular transmural graph depth and applied generalized additive models to capture depth-dependent dynamics. We prioritized TFs based on the magnitude of their activity changes across the transmural axis, model fit, and statistical significance, and selected the top candidates (Fig. 5h). JARID2, TBX3, and IRF6, identified in our screening, display a parallel increasing gradient in both left and right ventricle, reaching maximal activity in the compact myocardium (Fig. 5i, j). JARID2, a histone lysine demethylase, has been linked to ventricular septal defects and excessive trabeculation, underscoring its role in trabecular-to-compact myocardium transition42,43. High activity of TBX3 in the compact myocardium, essential for cardiac conduction system development38, is consistent with the presence of Purkinje conduction cells across compact region of both ventricles. IRF6, a key regulator of neural crest cell development44, has not previously been explored in the heart. In silico simulations predicted that IRF6 promoted the trabecular-to-compact progression in both left and right ventricles (Fig. 5k), particularly at the hybrid region, the key site of compaction22, whereas knockout had the opposite effect, suggesting a potential role for IRF6 in driving trabecular compaction. Consistent with this prediction, immunostaining of 10-PCW human hearts revealed that IRF6 protein is localized to cardiomyocytes adjacent to the epicardium, where it colocalizes with the cardiomyocyte marker TNNI3 and shows minimal overlap with the mesenchymal marker Vimentin, and also exhibits a descending gradient from epicardium to endocardium within the compact myocardium (Fig. 5l). To further evaluate the functional role of IRF6 in ventricular morphogenesis, we overexpressed IRF6 using adenovirus (Ad-IRF6) in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs). IRF6 overexpression induced a significant downregulation of trabecular markers (NPPA and IRX3) accompanied by a concurrent upregulation of compact myocardium markers (HEY2 and MT3) (Fig. 5m, n), supporting the predicted role of IRF6 in maintaining compact myocardial identity along the transmural gradient. Collectively, our integrative analysis nominates IRF6 as a previously unrecognized regulator of ventricular compaction, linking depth-dependent transcriptional gradients to trabecular-to-compact transition.

Together, these analyses reveal a spatially organized regulatory architecture in the developing ventricle, in which region-specific and chamber-biased transcription factor programs are hierarchically coordinated along the transmural axis. Notably, this regulatory landscape not only parallels anatomical patterning but also helps establish the ventricular transmural gradient through continuous variation in TF activity.

Temporal dynamics of human ventricular development

Although the ventricle exhibits marked transcriptional heterogeneity across spatial subpopulations, it functions as a unified contractile chamber, raising the question of whether its development proceeds in a coordinated or divergent manner. Consistent with a progressively coordinated process, the number of DEGs among ventricular subpopulations gradually decreases as development progresses, indicating a gradual homogenization of transcriptional states in mature ventricles (Supplementary Fig. 14a, 14b). Notably, the inverse relationship between developmental age and transcriptional complexity is conserved in many species45,46. To systematically characterize developmental dynamics, we aggregated spot-level counts into pseudobulk profiles for each subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 14c) and performed a likelihood ratio test to identify developmentally regulated differentially expressed genes (devDEGs) that vary continuously with time (see “Methods”) (Fig. 6a). Developmental gene expression was dominated by shared programs across ventricular subpopulations, while only a limited number of devDEGs displayed subpopulation-specific patterns (Fig. 6a), suggesting that global developmental trajectories are complemented by subcluster-specific transcriptional programs. To further delineate the shared and subpopulation-specific developmental pattern, we next organized all devDEGs across subpopulations into co-expression modules using two complementary approaches. We first adopted a K-means clustering strategy to group all devDEGs into 12 development-associated gene modules (K_Module) (Fig. 6b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 14e). In parallel, to further capture continuous temporal dynamics, we applied generalized additive model (GAM) to fit smoothed trajectories of devDEGs and clustered them into 16 dynamic gene modules (G_Module) (Fig. 6e, and Supplementary Fig. 14f). Despite the greater model flexibility, the key developmental trends identified by the K-means analysis were recapitulated using GAM, with strong overlap between corresponding modules as assessed by Jaccard similarity (Fig. 6g). Among those dynamic gene modules, G_Module-2 and G_Module-5 (corresponds to K_Module-11 and K_Module-10 respectively) exhibited progressive upregulation during development, with GO enrichment highlighting contractile processes such as actomyosin organization, cardiac muscle contraction, and myofibril assembly, along with pathways related to lipid metabolism (Fig. 6c–f). Within this G_Module-2, characterized by smooth upregulation over developmental time, we identified four genes consistently increasing across all ventricular subpopulations (Fig. 6h): CMYA5, a structural component of dyads essential for excitation-contraction coupling47,48, MB and LPL, which reflect oxygen handling and lipid-based energy supply49,50, and MYO15B, a less characterized member of the myosin family. The co-enrichment of sarcomere organization and lipid metabolic remodeling suggests a coordinated program in which the structural maturation of the contractile apparatus is coupled to bio-energetic specialization, thereby supporting the increasing mechanical and energetic demands of the developing ventricle.

In contrast, G_Module-8 (corresponds to K_Module-1) showed a gradual decline across developmental stages, enriched for processes related to the cell cycle, as well as post-transcriptional and translational regulation (Fig. 6c–f). Shared devDEGs in G_Module-8 included PTMA, encoding chromatin-associated protein previously implicated in cardiomyocyte proliferation51, PCLAF, a DNA replication cofactor; HNRNPA1, encoding RNA-binding protein involved in RNA splicing52, and PPIA, encoding chaperone regulating protein folding and trafficking53 (Fig. 6i). The opposing trajectories of these two modules highlight a developmental transition from proliferative and biosynthetically active cardiomyocytes towards a terminally differentiated state with enhanced contractile and metabolic specialization.

Superimposed on these shared trajectories, we identified subpopulation-specific developmental signatures, defined by devDEGs unique to individual subpopulations within each module. Notably, in G_module-2, the Atrioventricular Plane harbored a set of genes associated with conduction system maturation and septal morphogenesis, including IRX3 (a core transcription factor for conduction system specification), AHNAK (a modulator of Cav1.2 calcium channels)54,55, CITED2 (a transcriptional co-regulator whose mutations are linked to atrial and ventricular septal defects)56, and FZD1 (a Wnt receptor with mutation leads to septal and outflow tract malformations)57 (Fig. 6j). These features point to an Atrioventricular Plane-specific program integrating conduction system elements with the maturation of the membranous septum. The Ventricular Endocardium showed developmental upregulation of genes associated with endothelial integrity and ECM organization, including PIEZO1 (a mechanosensitive channel sensing shear stress in endocardium)58, SPARCL1 (stabilizing endothelial integrity)59, and ECM components like COL1A1 and DCN (Fig. 6k). These signatures highlight progressive acquisition of mechanosensory and structural ECM functions within the maturing endocardium.

Collectively, our integrative analysis delineates a coordinated developmental framework in which shared maturation-associated transcriptional changes operate across the human ventricle, complemented by localized subpopulation-specific programs that provide additional layers of specialization to support structural and functional development.

A central feature of the atrial compartment was the identification of the atrioventricular canal (AVC) as a multifunctional hub integrating structural morphogenesis and conduction system development. The AVC subpopulation is marked by RSPO3, MSX2, and BMP2 (Fig. 7c). Consistent with its established role, a subset of cardiac endothelial cells in the AVC undergoes EndMT, invades the cardiac jelly, and proliferates to form endocardial cushions, contributing to the closure of the primary atrial foramen and the development of the atrial and ventricular septa and heart valves63. GO enrichment of AVC markers highlighted cardiac septum morphogenesis and endocardial cushion formation, and gene set scoring revealed enrichment of EMT, consistent with the role of the AVC in endocardial cushions development27(Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 15j). Beyond this structural role, the AVC also contributed to the conduction system, including the atrioventricular node (AVN) and the His bundle64, as reflected by GO enrichment for conduction system development. (Fig. 7e). RSPO3, a secreted WNT signaling co-agonist65, was strongly expressed in the AVC and valve regions (Fig. 7f) and has also been reported as a characteristic gene of the AVN66, suggesting that it bridges cushion morphogenesis and conduction system specification. Spatial communication flow analysis further revealed WNT signaling activity concentrated within the AVC (Fig. 7g). Functionally, the AVC exhibited a transcriptional profile characteristic of nodal tissues, including preferential expression of slow-conduction genes (GJC1) and pacemaker ion channels (CACNA1G, CACNA1D, HCN1, HCN4), a profile shared with the SAN (Fig. 7d), consistent with nodal specialization in rhythmic excitation and conduction delay. High expression of BMP2 and TBX3 (Fig. 7h and Supplementary Fig. 15k), both of which are established regulators of pacemaker differentiation and cushion development67,68, together with signaling-level enrichment of BMP2 signaling (Fig. 7i), highlights the dual contribution of the AVC to the embryonic foundation of endocardial cushion morphogenesis and conduction system specification.

We further identified a distinct His bundle subpopulation (IRX1, IRX2, and C5orf38), which originates from AVC and retained expression of AVC-associated genes, such as VCAN, BMP2, and TBX3. Intriguingly, the His bundle region co-expresses genes associated with rapid conduction alongside features of slow conduction and pacemaker activity (Fig. 7d), suggesting a transitional state between the slow-conducting atrioventricular node and the mature fast-conducting His bundle. Finally, using human cardiac scRNA-seq data69, we validated the pacemaker and conductive cell proportions in atrial subpopulations aligned with our annotations (Fig. 7j).

Collectively, these results define the atrial compartment as a spatially organized system in which chamber-specific identity, structural morphogenesis, and conduction system specification are tightly integrated.

Discussion

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing have enabled unprecedented insights into organ development, with spatial transcriptomics offering a unique opportunity to couple molecular programs with anatomical context. While previous human heart atlases have emphasized organ-wide or single-cell-resolved views, spatial transcriptomic analysis has often been confined to scRNA-seq-based deconvoluting annotation, thereby overlooking the multicellular neighborhoods that constitute functional units of cardiac tissue. By conceptualizing each Visium spot as a multicellular community, our study expands the reference landscape of human heart development and reveals how transcriptional programs organize within, and cross, such communities to orchestrate cardiac development. Accordingly, all spatial clusters described in our study refer to spatially defined anatomical subdomains rather than discrete single-cell populations. Moreover, broadly similar spatial subpopulations were recovered with the BANKSY method70 (Supplementary Fig. 16), supporting the robustness of our spatial annotations beyond the Seurat-based workflow.

Within ventricular compartment, the left ventricular free wall (FHF-derived) and the interventricular septum (a mixed FHF/SHF derivative)71 exhibited similar transcriptional profiles, in our dataset, consistent with observations in adult hearts72,73. This convergence suggests that lineage-specific differences may become transcriptionally homogenized during development, potentially reflecting the functional requirement for coordinated ventricular contraction. At the same time, the resolution and sensitivity limitations of the current Visium platform may preclude the detection of finer-scale regional heterogeneity. Notably, the Papillary muscle emerged as a previously unrecognized community not captured by scRNA-seq datasets2,72, likely owing to the loss of anatomical context during dissociation. Papillary muscles, which tether atrioventricular valves via chordae tendineae, are essential for valvular competence, and their dysfunction has been implicated in mitral valve malformations74, while mutations in Papillary muscle-enriched genes such as ACTA1 have been associated with dilated cardiomyopathy75,76. The enrichment of ACTA1 and MYL2 expression, together with strong TEAD4 activity, a mechano-sensitive transcription factor in the YAP/TAZ-Hippo pathway, highlights the biomechanical specialization of this structure and underscores the integration of hemodynamic forces with transcriptional control during cardiac development. Adjacent to the Papillary muscle, the Atrioventricular Plane forms a specialized domain. Derived from endocardial cushions, it is enriched for extracellular matrix and TGF-β/BMP signaling, consistent with its role in valve anchorage and septal formation. Spatial disease mapping linked the Atrioventricular Plane and Papillary muscle to mitral valve prolapse, indicating their central roles in disease etiology.

A key theme emerging from our analysis is the existence of a spatially coordinated continuum of transcriptional programs across the ventricular wall. Ventricular myocardium has traditionally been divided into trabecular and compact regions based on anatomical morphology, the former forming sponge-like protrusions with greater blood contact, and the latter characterized by tightly packed, orderly layers77. Through trajectory inference, we found that both left and right ventricles exhibit a continuous transmural gradient across the ventricular wall. Along this transmural axis, gene expression changed gradually rather than abruptly. This pattern is consistent with previous single-cell studies reporting gradual transmural transcriptional variation in ventricular cardiomyocytes78. Nevertheless, given that Visium spots capture multicellular niches and pseudotime algorithms tend to impose smooth trajectories, we cannot exclude the possibility that sharper boundaries exist below the spot scale.

Our analysis further uncovers a layer of spatially structured regulatory control that underlies ventricular patterning. By inferring transcription factor activity across ventricular subpopulations and along the transmural axis, we identify coordinated, region- and depth-specific regulon deployment that parallels anatomical organization. For example, compact myocardium is characterized by preferential activation of transcriptional repressors such as JARID2 and REST, whereas trabecular regions exhibit enrichment of canonical cardiac regulators, including TBX20, NKX2-5, and HAND1. In addition, chamber-specific asymmetry is reflected by selective activation of myogenic regulators such as MYF5/6 and KLF3 in the right ventricle. Importantly, projecting TF activity along the transmural axis reveals a continuous regulatory gradient, with factors such as JARID2, TBX3, and IRF6 progressively increasing toward the compact myocardium. These findings indicate that the observed transcriptional continuum is not merely descriptive but is actively shaped by spatially coordinated regulatory programs. In this context, IRF6 emerges as a candidate regulator of ventricular compaction, supported by in silico perturbation, spatial expression patterns, and functional validation. These results support a model in which ventricular patterning is governed by a hierarchically organized regulatory architecture integrating spatial gradients of TF activity.

Another unresolved question concerns how maturation patterns are coordinated across ventricular regions during development: whether they proceed in a region-specific manner or through shared programs across subgroups. By computing devDEGs for each subgroup and fitting them with generalized additive models followed by integrative clustering, we were able to capture developmental trajectories shared across spatial domains. This analysis revealed strikingly similar maturation programs in all ventricular regions, characterized by sarcomere assembly, metabolic remodeling, and reduced proliferative activity, consistent with a transition from hyperplastic growth to hypertrophic growth. Superimposed upon these global trends, individual regions exhibited additional specialization, for instance, conduction system and septation-associated genes in the Atrioventricular Plane, and endothelial mechanosensing programs within the Ventricular Endocardium. Notably, a similar paradigm, shared cross-lineage programs supplemented by cell type-specific developmental signatures, has also been observed in neuronal systems, suggesting a conserved principle of development79.

Our study also has limitations. The spatial gradients we captured are restricted to a two-dimensional plane within individual sections, reflecting changes across the ventricular wall from endocardium to epicardium. Although inter-slice comparisons suggest an additional z-axis dimension, we cannot confidently distinguish true three-dimensional spatial differences from potential batch effects. Moreover, the resolution of Visium precludes mapping finer anatomical microdomains, limiting our ability to detect subtle transcriptional variations across spatial gradients. Future advances in high-resolution spatial platforms and three-dimensional integration strategies will be critical to refine these observations and dissect subtle organization. In addition, the developmental analysis was based on a limited number of time points, with each stage represented by a single individual, which may not fully capture the extent of biological variability across stages and could therefore introduce some degree of individual-specific confounding into the inferred temporal patterns.

In summary, our study expands the reference landscape of human heart development by integrating spatiotemporal transcriptomic profiling with community-level analysis. We systematically delineated multicellular spatial communities and uncovered the transcriptional patterning of the developing human ventricle. Our analyses revealed continuous spatial gradients of gene expression and regulon activity across the ventricular wall, coupled with global developmental trajectories linking sarcomere assembly and metabolic maturation, and further complemented by region-specific developmental programs. These findings may provide a framework for dissecting the organizational principles of cardiac development and establishing a foundation for mechanistic insights into congenital heart disease and potential avenues for regenerative strategies.

Methods

Ethics statement

All research involving human embryonic/fetal tissues was performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (PU-IRB; certificate No. IRB00001052-18083). Human embryonic/fetal cardiac tissues were collected following elective surgical termination of pregnancy at Haidian Maternal & Child Health Hospital in Beijing, China. All participants were 18 years of age or older, and no financial compensation was provided for participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating pregnant women prior to tissue collection. Clinical personnel, including midwives and gynecologists involved in patient counseling and the termination procedure, were not involved in the research study. Sex and gender were not considered in the study design and were not collected.

Human fetal heart tissue collection

Cardiac tissues were obtained from four human embryos/fetuses at developmental stages corresponding to 8, 9, 10, and 15 post-conception weeks (PCW), as determined by clinical ultrasound and anatomical landmarks indicative of developmental stage. Tissue samples were collected within one h after elective surgical termination of pregnancy. Cardiac tissues were dissected under sterile conditions in physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl). Regarding sample disposition, the remaining tissue samples are archived at the biobank of the National Center for Cardiovascular Disease, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, Beijing, China. Due to ethical restrictions associated with human tissue, these samples are not available for sharing with other researchers.

Spatial transcriptomics processing

The Visium Spatial Gene Expression Reagents Kits (10× Genomics) were employed to perform a spatial transcriptome analysis on heart sections from the 8, 9, 10, and 15 PCW timepoints. Cardiac tissues were removed from saline solution and immediately embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane (−45 °C) to preserve RNA integrity and morphological structure. OCT-embedded blocks were stored at −80 °C in airtight containers or processed directly for cryosectioning. Serial sections were prepared using a cryostat at −20 °C. For each PCW timepoint, consecutive 10 μm cryosections were collected systematically from the sternocostal surface to dorsal surface. RNA Integrity Number (RIN) was assessed for randomly selected sections using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Only samples with RIN ≥ 7.0 were retained for downstream spatial transcriptomics analysis. After confirming the RNA quality, the tissue sections were mounted on Visium Spatial Gene Expression Slides (10× Genomics) at 200-section intervals to ensure spatial coverage across the organ. The tissue sections on the slides were fixed in methanol and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin. High-resolution whole-slide images were acquired using the Olympus BX53 and Olympus DP74 system. Due to differences in tissue size, the permeabilization treatment times were determined as 21, 21, 21, and 30 min for the heart tissues at 8, 9, 10, and 15 PCW, respectively. Subsequently, the optimized parameters were applied to the tissue sections on the spatially barcoded arrays to generate Spatial Transcriptome libraries. Released mRNA was captured by spatially barcoded oligonucleotides on Visium arrays. cDNA synthesis was performed immediately using the Visium Library Construction Kit. cDNA libraries were amplified, fragmented, and indexed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Paired-end 150 bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform operated by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Spatial transcriptome data analysis

Spatial gene expression data were processed using SpaceRanger (v2.1.0, 10× Genomics). Raw FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCh38 reference transcriptome (GRCh38-2020-A) and quantified with the SpaceRanger count pipeline. Tissue images were provided as brightfield.jpg files together with corresponding slide IDs, and Loupe alignment files were used to register tissue positions. All downstream analyses were performed using Seurat (v5.0.3) in R (v4.2.3). Data from individual sections were concatenated using JoinLayers to construct a unified object. For quality control, outlier sections with extreme library size or feature counts were excluded a priori. Low-quality spots with fewer than 500 UMIs or fewer than 500 detected genes were removed, as were spots in which hemoglobin transcripts (HBB, HBD, HBG1, HBG2, HBE1, HBZ, HBM, HBA2, HBA1, HBQ1) exceeded 25% of the total library. In subsequent preprocessing, we also removed erythroid-associated genes, mitochondrial genes, XIST, and all Y-chromosome genes (see Supplementary Data 6). Spots outside tissue-covered regions were manually removed based on spatial plots. Normalization and variance stabilization were performed with SCTransform, which models gene counts with regularized negative binomial regression and stores Pearson residuals in the SCT assay80. Dimensionality reduction was performed with principal component analysis (RunPCA), and batch effects were corrected using Harmony6 on the top 50 PCs. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings were initialized from the Harmony space. The spots were then clustered on harmony embeddings using the Leiden algorithm on k-nearest neighbors graph constructed using gene expression data as implemented in FindNeighbors and FindClusters function using 50 dimensions. Clusters corresponding to low-quality spots or non-cardiac tissues (e.g., trachea/lung-associated, skeletal muscle, thymic regions), guided by canonical markers (SCGB3A2 for airway epithelium, MYBPH for skeletal muscle, TBATA for thymic tissue) were removed, after which the dataset was re-normalized and re-clustered. Differentially expressed genes were identified with FindAllMarkers (min.pct = 0.5). Representative markers were visualized with DotPlot. Each cluster was annotated based on its spatial distribution in combination with differentially expressed genes.

Subset analysis

For each subcluster analysis, spots associated with ventricles or atria were extracted and analyzed using the same workflow as described above, beginning with raw gene count data.

Robust cell type decomposition analysis

To deconvolute the cellular composition of spatial transcriptomic spots, we performed Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) analysis (v2.2.1)7. As reference for cell type deconvolutions, scRNA-seq data from developing human hearts (9–15 PCW) were used5. We first subset scRNA-seq data by developmental age and extracted raw gene counts, cell types and UMI totals. Reference objects were then constructed using the Reference function. Each spatial transcriptomic section was assigned to the corresponding reference object based on the closest developmental stage. In parallel, to assess the relative abundance of conduction system-related cells, we performed a second round of deconvolution using a publicly available adult human heart scRNA-seq dataset as ref. 69. For each spatial section, spot-level coordinates and raw count matrices were used to initialize a SpatialRNA object. RCTD objects were then created using the create.RCTD function, and the deconvolution was performed during the run.RCTD function using “full mode” (no restrictions on number of cell types) to account for potential mixed cell types within each spot. Following RCTD processing, the raw cell type weights were normalized on a per-spot basis such that the sum of weights across all cell types equaled one. We used cell type prediction weights for spatial transcriptomes as a proxy for cellular composition. The normalized weights were aggregated across all spatial sections to generate a unified cell type composition matrix, which was used for downstream visualization and compartment-level analysis.

Integration of single-cell reference and spatial transcriptomics data

To annotate cell types and evaluate the spatial enrichment of cell-type-specific gene signatures in our ST data, we used a reference scRNA-seq atlas of the developing human heart5, comprising multiple developmental time points. Cell type identities from the published scRNA-seq atlas of the developing human heart were mapped to our ST data. Briefly, 3000 cells were randomly sampled from each cell type from the reference scRNA-seq dataset, which was then normalized via SCTransform. Anchor features between the scRNA-seq reference and our ST data were identified using FindTransferAnchors, and cell type labels were transferred to each ST spot via the TransferData function. To ensure annotation robustness, a spot was assigned a cell type label only if the maximum prediction score exceeded 0.8. To quantify the spatial enrichment of cell type-specific transcriptional signatures, module scores were calculated for each spot using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat. This score quantifies the relative expression of predefined cell type marker gene sets within each spatial location. Finally, the spatial distribution of transferred cell type labels and module scores was visualized, enabling the direct mapping of cell type signature enrichment patterns onto the anatomical coordinates of the tissue section.

Interventricular septum (IVS) annotation

Within the LV-Compact subpopulation, the interventricular septum (IVS) region was manually delineated in the Loupe Browser based on spatial position and histological landmarks. Spots within the selected area were annotated as IVS, while the remaining LV-Compact spots were annotated as LVFW.

Pseudobulk principal component analysis

Pseudobulk gene-level count matrices were generated from spatial transcriptomic data by aggregating gene counts within each sample. A DESeqDataSet was constructed using the DESeq2 package (v1.38.3), and variance-stabilizing transformation (vst) was applied to normalize the data. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on the top 500 most variable genes to visualize global transcriptional differences across developmental stages.

Pseudobulk differential expression analysis of IVS and LV free wall

To compare the transcriptional differences between the IVS and LVFW, we performed pseudobulk analysis at each development stages. Raw spatial counts were aggregated at the sample-by-region level to generate pseudobulk count matrices. Pseudobulk profiles from sections without appropriate LVFW representation were excluded. Lowly expressed genes were removed by retaining genes with counts greater than 5 in at least three pseudobulk samples. Differential expression analysis was then performed separately at each developmental stage using DESeq2 (v1.38.3). Genes with adjusted P value < 0.01 and absolute log2 fold change > 0.5 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Trajectory inference

For trajectory inference, ventricle at each developmental time was analyzed separately, excluding Papillary muscle and Atrioventricular Plane. Ribosomal genes were removed prior to analysis, and Harmony integration was applied to mitigate batch effects. To infer connectivity and subcluster relationships, partition-based graph abstraction analysis (PAGA) was performed using Scanpy (v1.9.8)81,82. First, the Seurat objects (SCT assay and subcluster identities as defined above) were converted into format using the convertFormat function from the sceasy package in R. Within Scanpy, the neighborhood graph was constructed using pp.neighbors and PAGA was run with tl.paga to compute inter-cluster connectivity. The PAGA-inferred abstract connectivity graph was then projected onto the UMAP embedding to visualize developmental trajectories and relationships among ventricular subclusters. Processed Seurat objects were then converted into CellDataSet format using the as.cell_data_set command from the SeuratWrappers package, with cluster annotations and UMAP embeddings transferred to the CellDataSet object. Pseudotime analysis was then performed following the Monocle3 (v1.3.1) workflow83. The root node for pseudotime ordering was defined at the Ventricular Endocardium subpopulation, and pseudotime was inferred using the order_cells function. The pseudotime was then visualized on spatial maps using the SpatialFeaturePlot command in Seurat v5. To compare left- and right-sided branches within each week, we used the choose_graph_segment command to separately define the left and right ventricular lineages, and lineage labels were corrected to maintain consistency with ventricular subpopulation annotations before downstream ordering. Genes whose expression varied along pseudotime were identified using the graph_test function with neighbor_graph = “principal_graph”. The top 1000 genes ranked by Moran’s I were retained for visualization. Heatmaps were then generated using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.14.0). For heatmap visualization, expression values for these genes were extracted and ordered by pseudotime. Spline smoothing was applied across the ordered pseudotime for each gene using smooth.spline function (degrees of freedom = 15). Smoothed expression profiles were then z-score normalized. Genes were subsequently ordered by the pseudotime position of peak expression to reflect progression along the trajectory, and were then grouped into four expression modules based on their temporal patterns (“Left-end”, “Left-mid”, “Middle”, and “Right”). The top 3 genes ranked by Moran’s I within each module were annotated on the heatmap.

Developmental responsiveness analysis using Augur

To identify ventricular subpopulations exhibiting notable transcriptional changes during development, we applied Augur (v1.0.3), which uses cross-validated random forest classifiers to quantify the separability of single-cell transcriptomes across conditions84. The dataset was first split by developmental stage, and ribosomal genes were removed. Augur was then run with parameters var_quantile = 0.9, n_subsamples = 50, and subsample_size = 20 to compute area under the curve (AUC) scores for each subpopulation. The resulting AUC values, reflecting each subpopulation’s transcriptional responsiveness to developmental progression, were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.5.1).

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.2)85. Enrichment was assessed for Biological Process (BP), and terms with adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) were considered significant.

Spatial communication flow analysis

Spatial communication flows were inferred using COMMOT (v0.0.3)86. Expression matrices were normalized with Scanpy (v1.9.8), and Seurat annotation was used to define spatial communities. A human CellChatDB secreted-signaling database was filtered to retain ligands/receptors expressed at least 5% of spots and applied to compute spatial communication within a 500 μm distance threshold. Directional flow fields were visualized with ct.pl.plot_cell_communication.

gsMap analysis

We applied genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) (v1.73.0) to integrate spatial transcriptomic data with genome-wide association study (GWAS) data and to identify spatial regions associated with complex traits28. Briefly, gsMap operates through four major steps. First, in the gene specificity assessment step, gsMap employs a graph neural network (GNN) to identify expression- and location-homogeneous neighboring spots for each focal spot, and computes gene specificity scores by aggregating information from these homogeneous neighbors. Second, gene specificity scores are assigned to single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) based on their proximity (± 50 kb) to gene transcription start sites and SNP-to-gene epigenetic linkage maps. Third, stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (S-LDSC) is used to associate LD scores of individual spots with GWAS summary statistics, thereby estimating the contribution of each spatial position to disease traits. Finally, spot-level P values were combined within anatomically defined spatial regions using the Cauchy combination test, yielding regional trait-association scores. All analyses were performed following the authors’ recommended workflow. Genome-wide association summary statistics for mitral valve prolapse were obtained from a published dataset29 and are publicly available via the Cardiovascular Disease Knowledge Portal (https://cvd.hugeamp.org/). Summary statistics were first reformatted into the gsMap-compatible structure using the gsmap format_sumstats command. For each developmental stage, the gsmap create_slice_mean command was applied independently to calculate slice-level mean ranks for each gene. Subsequently, trait-association analyses were conducted with gsmap quick_mode command, and regional significance was calculated using the gsmap run_cauchy_combination command.

Transcriptional factor activity inference

We inferred transcription factor (TF) activities using decoupleR (v2.9.7)32. A curated TF–target regulatory network was retrieved from CollecTRI, a comprehensive resource compiling human TF–target interactions from 12 databases. TF enrichment scores were inferred using the Univariate Linear Model (ULM) method and stored as a new assay in the Seurat object. TF activities were scaled with ScaleData, and subcluster-specific TFs were identified by differential testing using FindAllMarkers on the TF activity assay. Significant TFs were defined as those with |avg_log2FC | > 1 and adjusted P < 0.01. For spatial analyses, representative analyses were performed on 10 PCW data restricted to ventricular subpopulations, using each spot’s graph depth score (see above) as a continuous covariate.

To compare left and right ventricular branches, subpopulations were grouped into left branches (LV-Compact, LV-Hybrid, LV-Trabecular) and right branches (RV-Compact, RV-Trabecular), excluding endocardial spots. Differential TF activities between branches were identified using FindAllMarkers (|avg_log2FC | > 1.5, adjusted P < 0.01), and the top three TFs most enriched in each branch were selected for representative visualization. Depth–activity relationships were summarized by violin plots and smoothed line plots colored by region. Within each branch, TF activity was modeled as a smooth function of graph depth using generalized additive models implemented in the mgcv package (v1.9.1), with the model specified as activity ~ s(depth, k = 3). For each TF, we recorded the smooth-term P value, partial R², and fitted amplitude (max–min) scaled by its standard deviation (Δ/SD). Dynamic TFs were defined as those with FDR < 0.001, partial R² > 0.1, and Δ/SD > 1. The top 15 TFs per branch ranked by amplitude, together with the five most strongly enriched TFs per side (total n = 23), were selected for heatmap visualization.

Developmentally regulated differentially expressed gene (devDEG) analysis

devDEG analysis was performed following previously described approaches with minor modifications79. Pseudobulk RNA-seq counts were generated by aggregating spatial transcriptomic data per ventricular subpopulation and section. Genes expressed at counts > 10 in at least 10 sections were retained for downstream analysis. Developmental differential expression within each subpopulation was tested using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) likelihood-ratio tests (LRT) with full model ~ Time and reduced model ~ 1. Genes were considered devDEGs for a given subpopulation if they satisfied adjusted P < 0.01 and baseMean > 10. The presence of devDEGs across subpopulations was summarized in a binary matrix and visualized using ComplexHeatmap. Genes were categorized by their degree of “sharedness” based on the number of subclusters in which they were detected: cell type-specific (detected in only one subpopulation), intermediate (shared by two to six subpopulations), and shared (present in seven or all subpopulations). To delineate the devDEG pattern, we organized all devDEGs across subpopulations into co-expression modules using two complementary approaches. First, gene expression patterns were clustered using K-means clustering. Gene clustering was then performed with the clusterData function in ClusterGVis R package using the K-means algorithm with the number of clusters set to 12. Cluster-specific expression patterns were visualized using the visCluster function in the form of line plots and heatmaps. Second, GAM was used to fit smoothed trajectories of devDEGs. Pseudobulk gene expression counts were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) and log-transformed as log₂(TPM + 1) to generate expression trajectories. To linearize developmental spacing, numeric ages (weeks post-conception) were transformed via an arcsinh warp as shown in Eq. In silico gene perturbation analysis

We used CellOracle (v0.20.0)87 to simulate the regulatory effects of perturbations in transcription factors HAND1 and IRF6 on the establishment of the ventricular spatial gradient. In brief, spatial transcriptomic data from the ventricular subpopulations of 10 PCW embryonic hearts were used as input. Highly variable genes were filtered by setting the parameters ‘flavor = seurat’ and ‘n_top_genes = 3,000’. Incorporating the software’s built - in transcription factor database, regulatory edges were filtered at P ≤ 0.001, and the top 2000 edges were retained for gene regulatory network (GRN) construction. For in silico perturbation experiments, knockout was simulated by setting transcription factor expression to 0, while overexpression simulations used specific thresholds: 0.25 for HAND1 and 0.06 for IRF6. Based on the simulated gene expression changes, CellOracle predicted the trajectory of cellular transitions. Perturbation scores were calculated to compare simulated transcription factor perturbation vector fields with natural developmental vector fields. Positive and negative perturbation scores indicated promoting and inhibitory effects, respectively, on the establishment of the ventricular spatial gradient.

Spatial trajectory analysis

For spatial trajectory analysis, a representative 10 PCW ventricular Visium section (10w-135-C1) was extracted from the integrated ventricular Seurat object and converted to a SPATA2 object using the SCT assay. Spatial trajectories were generated in SPATA2 (v2.0.4). To define spatial paths for downstream analysis, spatial trajectories were initialized within the tissue section using createSpatialTrajectories function. To identify genes showing significant spatial variation across the tissue section, we applied runSparkx function and genes with a P value < 0.01 were retained as candidate spatially variable genes for trajectory-based screening. These genes were subsequently screened along the selected spatial trajectory using spatialTrajectoryScreening function in SPATA2 with all implemented model patterns considered. Genes categorized into ascending or descending groups according to their fitted expression trends along the trajectory were used for visualization.

Genes2Genes analysis

We used Genes2Genes (v0.2.0) to compare pseudotime-resolved transcriptional dynamics between left and right ventricular trajectories at each developmental stage separately. Left and right ventricular branches were first extracted from the trajectories previously reconstructed using monocle3. Stage-specific data were loaded for the left ventricle (reference) and right ventricle (query), and pseudotime values were min–max normalized to 0–1 before alignment. For each stage, genes were selected from precomputed stage-specific gene lists and restricted to those detected in both datasets. Gene-wise trajectory alignment was performed using Main.RefQueryAligner function with 14 pseudotime bins, and alignment statistics were extracted for all genes. Genes with low alignment similarity and high alignment cost (alignment_similarity_percentage ≤ 50% and opt_alignment_cost ≥ 30 nts) were defined as mismatch genes. Mismatch gene sets were identified separately for each stage and further intersected across stages to identify recurrently discordant genes between left and right ventricular developmental trajectories

Immunofluorescence

Cryosections were first rewarmed at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by rehydration in PBS for 10 min. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; LEAGENE, DF0135) at room temperature for 10 min, the sections were washed three times with PBS (Solarbio, P1020). Subsequent immunofluorescence processing included permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocking with 10% normal serum (species-matched to secondary antibodies). Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBS, the slides were incubated with appropriate fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The following primary antibodies and reagents were used (Supplementary Table 1): IRF6 (MedChemExpress, YA2662; 1:300), TNNI3 (Abcam, ab56357, 1:300), Vimentin (Abcam, ab8978; 1:300) antibodies. Secondary antibodies included: donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-21206; 1:400), donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (Invitrogen, A-31570; 1:400), donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen, A-21447; 1:400). The slides and cells were mounted in Fluoroshield™ with DAPl (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) (Sigma-Aldrich, F6057) shielded from light. Fluorescence was observed under a Zeiss LSM800 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Inc., Jena, Germany).

Culture and adenovirus treatment of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes

The hiPSC-CMs were obtained commercially as cryopreserved cells (CardioEasy® Human Cardiomyocytes, CA2201106) from Cellapy Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Cells were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen until thawed and cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, coat Costar® 24 cell plates with Matrigel for 2 h at 37 °C, then the cryopreserved hiPSC-CMs were thawed and plated on Costar® 24 well (15 × 104 cells/well) and cultured with CardioEasy® hiPSC-CM maintenance medium (MEA, CA2013100). The adenovirus overexpressing IRF6 (Ad-IRF6) and the adenovirus overexpressing GFP (Ad-GFP, used as a control) were provided by Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), both under the control of the CMV promoter. hiPSC-CMs were infected with adenovirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 in CardioEasy® hiPSC-CM maintenance medium for 12 h. The medium was then replaced with virus-free maintenance medium. After an additional 36 h of culture, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026CN).

Analysis of gene expression using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) with reverse transcription

Total RNA extracted from hiPSC-CMs was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036A). qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4309155) on a Vii7 RT-PCR System (Applied Biosystems, California, USA). Oligonucleotide primers used for qRT-PCR were synthesized by Beijing Tianyihuiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Relative expression was quantified using the ∆∆Ct method with GAPDH as the housekeeping gene.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. Randomization was not applicable to the spatial transcriptomic data analysis. For bioinformatics statistics, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for cluster marker gene identification and spot-level differential expression analyses. Pseudobulk differential expression analysis using DESeq2 was based on a negative binomial generalized linear model, and statistical significance was evaluated using the Wald test. Hypergeometric test was used for enrichment analysis. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used for false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment. Statistical analyses used to generate all figures and supplementary data are described in detail within the corresponding spatial transcriptomic analysis sections of the Methods. Unsupervised clustering, pseudotime trajectory inference and graph depth analysis were performed across all 30 heart sections across 8, 9, 10 and 15 PCW, yielding highly consistent biological patterns across replicates. For visual clarity, only one representative section per developmental stage was exhibited in figures. Spatial communication flow analysis was performed on one 10 PCW section with optimal papillary muscle morphology. Transcription factor activity was analyzed on all 10 PCW samples (8 technical replicate sections), and a single representative section was selected for graphical presentation. For experimental validation, including hiPSC-CM assays and qRT-PCR, all experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance between groups was determined using unpaired two-sided Student’s t-tests as indicated in the figure legends. All hiPSC-CM experiments were independently repeated at least three times with consistent results to ensure reproducibility. The experiments were randomized to ensure an unbiased distribution of samples across experimental groups. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. For immunofluorescence validation, immunostaining was performed on a 10 PCW human fetal heart sample to qualitatively confirm the spatial expression and co-localization patterns of IRF6.
