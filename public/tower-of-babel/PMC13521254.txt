Metabolomic Profiles and Changes During the 20 Years Preceding Heart Failure
Authors: Jiangtao Li, Shusi Ding, Zhao Yang, Mingdan Wang, Xuan Deng, Xueting Sun, Wenting Lu, Pingping Jia, Yan Liu, Yuhan Zhang, Jun Cai, Lemin Zheng, Yue Qi, Jing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Longitudinal metabolomic studies can refine understanding of heart failure (HF) progression and enable precision prevention. This study aims to identify serum metabolites associated with HF risk via longitudinal metabolomic analysis, delineate their dynamic trajectories, and explore metabolite profiles in populations with different metabolic disorders.METHODS:This study analyzed longitudinal serum metabolomic data from 4774 serum samples from 1728 HF-free participants in the Chinese Multi-Provincial Cohort Study Metabolomics Project at 4 time points over a 20-year follow-up. Intensity models and Cox proportional-hazards models identified metabolites associated with HF risk. Latent variable mixed-effects models evaluated metabolite trajectories.RESULTS:Of the 784 detected metabolites, 23 were associated with HF risk at a false discovery rate–adjusted P<0.05, including 9 not previously reported in relation to HF. The HF risk–associated metabolites exhibited 4 distinct trajectory clusters and corresponding biological trends. Most metabolites that showed positive associations with HF risk remained relatively stable throughout the 20-year follow-up period, whereas metabolites that were negatively associated with HF risk generally exhibited a declining trend. The levels of these 23 metabolites in the group who developed HF began to diverge from the levels in the non-HF group >5 years before clinical HF diagnosis, with most changes initiating 15 to 20 years before clinical manifestation. Populations with different metabolic disorders exhibited distinct metabolite profiles related to HF. The HF-associated metabolites were primarily involved in energy metabolism and the vasodilatory response among individuals with hypertension, lipotoxic effects and oxidative stress among those with obesity, and inflammatory processes and glucotoxic mechanisms among individuals with dysglycemia.CONCLUSIONS:This longitudinal metabolomic study identifies HF-associated metabolite profiles, characterizes their changes during the 20 years preceding clinical diagnosis, and reveals heterogeneity across individuals with different metabolic disorders, thereby informing future biomarker and intervention research.

Long-term metabolic alterations preceding the clinical onset of heart failure remain poorly characterized. By integrating repeated metabolomic measurements with longitudinal intensity models, cumulative-exposure analyses, trajectory modeling, and external validation, this study provides a temporal landscape of metabolic changes during the 20 years preceding heart failure. We identify 23 heart failure–associated metabolites and demonstrate that these metabolites follow distinct long-term trajectories, with metabolic differences becoming evident years before clinical diagnosis. The findings further reveal heterogeneity in heart failure–associated metabolomic profiles among individuals with hypertension, overweight or obesity, and dysglycemia, suggesting that different metabolic processes may contribute to heart failure development across risk factor populations. Together, these findings expand our understanding of the metabolomic landscape of heart failure and provide a framework for investigating metabolically tailored strategies for heart failure prevention and intervention.

Heart failure (HF), a life-threatening syndrome and an end-stage manifestation of cardiovascular disease, is a significant global health concern. The prevalence of HF is rising rapidly with population aging and the increasing burden of metabolic risk factors.1,2 Despite considerable progress in guideline-directed therapy, the burden of HF morbidity and mortality remains substantial.3 The heterogeneity of HF, coupled with the incomplete understanding of its underlying pathophysiology, impedes the development of effective therapeutic strategies and results in variable treatment responses across diverse patient populations.

Human metabolomics is a well-established approach for systematically profiling small-molecule metabolites, and it is particularly relevant to HF, where metabolic remodeling plays a causal role in disease pathogenesis.4–6 Metabolomics provides a detailed biochemical snapshot, offering insights into HF progression and cause,7,8 and identifying potential therapeutic targets.9,10 Notably, changes in the circulating metabolite profile rather than changes in a single metabolite are more representative and are useful for characterizing HF. Although recent studies have used circulating metabolomics to identify potential biomarkers of HF,11–16 the use of single–time point blood assays presents a challenge in identifying metabolite profiles and their changes associated with HF risk, confounded by time-dependent covariates. Metabolic alterations occur sequentially rather than simultaneously,17 but limited data exist regarding the dynamic changes in metabolite profiles preceding HF onset, which prevents the optimal timing of intervention from being determined. Although both untargeted and targeted metabolomics approaches are widely used, their inherent limitations hinder comprehensive biomarker identification in longitudinal studies. Emerging pseudotargeted metabolomics, characterized by broad coverage and high reproducibility, shows promise for investigating the relationships among metabolites and their alterations within the context of disease.18 Furthermore, HF is a heterogeneous condition driven by metabolic risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and obesity.19 The current lack of evidence on the metabolite profiles that link these risk factors to HF constrains the development of personalized and targeted interventions. Therefore, we hypothesized that longitudinal metabolomic profiling would identify metabolites associated with the risk of incident HF and capture their long-term dynamic trajectories. As a secondary hypothesis, we postulated that HF risk–associated metabolite profiles would differ across populations with distinct metabolic disorders.

This study, which is based on the CMCS (Chinese Multi-Provincial Cohort Study) Metabolomics Project, a prospective cohort with 20-year follow-up data and multiple time point metabolomics measurements, aims to (1) identify serum metabolites associated with HF risk using advanced longitudinal statistical modeling to capture temporal metabolite dynamics, with external validation; (2) delineate longitudinal metabolite changes to pinpoint critical time windows of dysregulation; and (3) explore metabolite heterogeneity related to HF risk among populations with different metabolic disorders.

Methods

Data Availability

The data and code that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. All statistical packages utilized are reported in the Major Resources Table. For full details of methods, please see the Supplemental Material.

Study Population

The study population was recruited from the CMCS, a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort study.20,21 Briefly, 21 953 Chinese participants who had no prior history of cardiovascular disease from 8 of the 11 provinces in the original CMCS were included, of whom 16 811 participants were recruited in 1992 and 1993, 3129 participants in 1996 and 1999, and 2013 in 2004.

The Beida subcohort of CMCS initially enrolled 1982 participants aged 35 to 64 years who had no prior history of cardiovascular disease in 1992 (wave 1). The participants were invited to undergo reexaminations in 2002 (wave 2), 2007 (wave 3), 2012 (wave 4), and 2020 to 2023 (wave 5). Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, medical history, anthropometric data, and venous blood samples were collected through face-to-face interviews and standardized health examinations during each reexamination wave (waves 2–5). For the discovery cohort, participants were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) attended at least 1 follow-up wave (waves 2–5) with a blood sample collection, and (2) were free of HF at the time of their first blood sample collection used in this analysis. Overall, 4774 serum samples were available for metabolomic measurements, which were collected from 1728 participants who were free of HF at the time of the first blood collection (Figure S1).

Of the 3129 participants recruited in 1996 and 1999 (wave 1) from the original CMCS, 194 individuals free of HF from the Beijing community completed the reexamination and provided blood samples in 2012 (wave 2). These participants constituted the validation cohort for this study, used to verify the association between serum metabolite levels and HF risk (Figure S1; Figure 1A). The data collection methodology for this cohort was consistent with that used in wave 4 of the discovery cohort.

Metabolite Quantification and Data Processing

Serum metabolomic profiling was performed in 2024 using an ultraperformance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry platform in positive and negative ionization modes (MetWare, Wuhan, China). Samples from the discovery and validation cohorts were randomized and analyzed concurrently under identical conditions. Metabolites were identified using retention time, precursor-product ion pairs, tandem mass spectra, reference standards, and metabolite databases, and were quantified using multiple reaction monitoring. Metabolite features with ≥20% missing values were excluded. Missing values for retained metabolites were imputed using the k-nearest neighbor algorithm, and signal drift was corrected using support vector regression. After deduplication across ionization modes, 784 metabolites were retained for analysis. Detailed analytical procedures and quality-control methods are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Outcome Ascertainment and Follow-Up

The primary outcome was HF hospitalization, defined as the first occurrence of hospitalization with a discharge diagnosis of International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code I50. Follow-up by active interview, supplemented with linkage to local disease surveillance systems, was conducted every 1 to 2 years from the time point of the first serum metabolomic measurement to December 31, 2023, to determine the occurrence of HF, acute coronary heart disease events, stroke, and all-cause death. Acute coronary heart disease events included nonfatal acute myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, and all coronary deaths.

Statistical Analysis

Longitudinal associations between serum metabolites and HF risk were evaluated using Poisson intensity models with time-updated metabolite levels and covariates. Associations of cumulative metabolite exposure with subsequent HF risk were assessed using Cox proportional-hazards models. Metabolite trajectories were characterized using k-means clustering and latent variable mixed-effects models. Analyses were adjusted for prespecified demographic, lifestyle, and medication-related covariates. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure across all 784 metabolites. Detailed model specifications, cumulative-exposure calculations, sensitivity and subgroup analyses, model diagnostics, pathway analyses, and prediction methods are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Results

Characteristics and Serum Metabolomic Profiles of Participants in the Longitudinal Cohort

Among the 1728 HF-free participants (age, 61.1±8.4 years; 52.7% women) with 4774 serum samples across 4 time points (Figure 1A) included in the baseline analysis, 84.0% (1452/1728) underwent examinations in wave 2 (age, 60.3±8.0 years; 52.9% women) in the discovery cohort. Furthermore, 84.1% of the participants completed at least 2 examinations, whereas 30.1% completed all 4. The characteristics of the discovery and validation cohorts are summarized in Tables S1 and S2.

Serum metabolomic profiling identified and robustly quantified a total of 784 metabolites, including 503 in positive ion mode and 281 in negative ion mode. The 784 metabolites spanned 13 superclasses, with amino acids and their metabolites, glycerophospholipids, fatty acyls, organic acids and derivatives, and benzene derivatives being the 5 most abundant classes (Table S3; Figure 1B). These metabolite classes were represented in both the discovery and external validation cohorts.

To quantify the participant specificity of metabolite profiles, we calculated the proportion of variance explained by interparticipant differences for each metabolite superclass and subclass. At the superclass level (Figure 2A), the fatty acyl superclass showed the highest degree of participant-specific variance, with a median participant-explained variance exceeding 20%, in some cases >40%. Acylcarnitines, the most numerous subclass within this superclass, made the greatest contribution to participant-specific variance (Figure 2B). Although peptide compounds had the highest number of metabolites, they exhibited relatively low participant-specific variance, with a median value of 10% (Figure 2B). Furthermore, we analyzed 2084 samples derived from 521 participants from whom we collected samples at 4 time points in the discovery cohort. Metabolite levels showed dynamic changes over time, which were particularly pronounced in the glycerophospholipids (Figure 2C).

Serum Metabolites Associated With HF Risk

Of HF-free participants at baseline in the discovery cohort, 169 (499 cases per 100 000 person-years) developed HF over the cumulative follow-up of 33 874 person-years (Figure 3A). Using the intensity models to adjust for age, period, cohort, sex, smoking status, and medication use, associations with HF risk were observed for 112 metabolites at P<0.05 and for 9 metabolites at an FDR-adjusted P<0.05 (Figure 3B; Figures S2 and S3; Table S4). Among the 1008 participants who attended both waves 2 and 4 and had no history of HF at wave 4 (Figure 3A), 96 (878 cases per 100 000 person-years) developed HF. The cumulative exposure of 101 metabolites at P<0.05 and 18 metabolites at an FDR-adjusted P<0.05 were related to the risk of HF in the Cox proportional-hazards model (Figure 3C; Figures S4 and S5; Table S5). Model diagnostics and analyses excluding potentially influential observations are summarized in Table S6. The direction and magnitude of the effect of these metabolites on HF risk were generally consistent across the discovery and external validation cohorts, with a correlation coefficient of 0.63 (Figure S6; Table S7).

Of the resulting 173 metabolites associated with HF risk at P<0.05 from the 2 analysis sets, 57 exhibited a negative association, and 116 exhibited a positive association with HF risk. Of the identified metabolites, amino acids accounted for 26.0%, fatty acyl compounds for 17.9%, and glycerophospholipids for 13.3% at superclass level (Figure 3D). At the subclass level, these were predominantly represented by acylated amino acids, choline-containing glycerophospholipids, nucleotides, and their metabolites (Figures S2 through S5). The association of glycerophospholipids with HF risk is predominantly negative, whereas other superclasses exhibit primarily positive associations (Figure 3D; Figures S2 through S5). Of the 173 metabolites, 23 were associated with HF risk at an FDR-adjusted P<0.05 (Figure 3E). Of these, 3 were negatively associated, and 20 were positively associated with HF risk. These metabolites were primarily classified as nucleotides and amino acids. These 23 metabolites encompassed 14 that have previously been reported to be associated with HF or cardiovascular disease, such as carnitine C16:3, and 9 potential novel metabolites, such as N6-(2-hydroxyethyl)adenosine. These associations identified in the discovery cohort changed little in the sensitivity analyses and were minimally influenced by sex and age (Figures S7 through S10). Eighteen metabolites were identified in both the intensity and Cox models in the discovery cohort, and 6 of these—namely, N-acetyl-beta-alanine, 2-(dimethylamino)guanosine, N6-(2-hydroxyethyl)adenosine, 2-methylguanosine, carnitine C16:3, and 2-hydroxyhexanoic acid—showed directionally consistent associations with nominal P<0.05 in the external cohort (Table S8). Most of these 23 metabolites linked to HF risk were consistently associated with NT-proBNP (N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide) and cTnT (cardiac troponin T) levels (Figure 3E), as well as with known risk factors for HF (Figure S11). Estimated glomerular filtration rate generally showed the largest partial η2 values, followed by body mass index and lipid-related traits (Table S9).

Longitudinal Trajectories of Metabolomic Profiles Associated With HF Risk

To characterize dynamic changes in the metabolite profiles over time, the metabolite trajectories were evaluated during the 20-year follow-up. Four distinct trajectory clusters were identified by applying k-means clustering to classify 173 HF-associated metabolites, based on their Z-scored mean across the 4 time points. Cluster 1 (n=69 metabolites) remained relatively stable over time; cluster 2 (n=4 metabolites) exhibited a sharp increase after wave 4; cluster 3 (n=56 metabolites) showed a modest rise followed by a decline; and cluster 4 (n=44 metabolites) exhibited a progressive decline (Figure 4A). The trajectory patterns were largely consistent across the subgroups stratified by age at the time point of the first serum metabolomic measurement, sex, and participation in all 4 waves (Figure S12).

Amino acids and fatty acyls were mainly distributed in clusters 1 and 3, whereas glycerophospholipids predominantly followed the progressively declining trajectory in cluster 4 (Figure 4B). The 23 metabolites associated with HF risk at an FDR-adjusted P<0.05 were predominantly distributed in cluster 1 (34.8%, 8/23) and cluster 3 (56.5%, 13/23), with 2 metabolites identified in cluster 4 (Figure S13).

The distinct enriched pathways illustrate the underlying biological trends associated with each metabolite cluster (Figure 4C). The prominent enriched pathway in cluster 1 was arginine and proline metabolism, which is involved in multiple interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms, including dysregulation of the nitric oxide signaling pathway, aberrant polyamine metabolism, proline-mediated fibrotic process, amplified oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, collectively contributing to metabolic imbalance and driving the development of HF. Notably, 80% (55/69) of the metabolites within this cluster showed a positive association with HF risk (Figure 4D; Figure 4E). The top enriched pathway in cluster 3, which exhibited a transient rise followed by a decline, was taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, which is involved in calcium regulation, blood pressure regulation, and antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses. Moreover, uridine and uracil in cluster 4, which demonstrated a progressive decrease over the 20-year follow-up period, were enriched in pyrimidine metabolism—a pathway that has been implicated in the regulation of cardiomyocyte energy homeostasis and redox balance, thereby suggesting its potential relevance to HF risk.

Metabolite-based models showed discrimination comparable to the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities model and improved prediction when added to conventional risk factors (Figure S14). Figure 5A illustrates the modeled estimated trajectories of these 23 metabolites among participants who developed HF and those who did not in the nested case-control study. Compared with participants who did not develop HF, the levels of the 23 metabolites in those who developed HF began to diverge >5 years before clinical diagnosis, with most changes initiating 15 to 20 years before clinical manifestation. Furthermore, the timing of alterations varied across the metabolites (Figure 5B). This pattern of change was even observed across different compounds within the acylcarnitine subclass. For example, differences in carnitine C16:3 and C3:0 emerged 18 to 20 years before HF onset, whereas differences in carnitine C4:0 and C9:1 emerged 11 to 12 years before HF onset (Figure 5B). Moreover, visual inspection of the metabolite change curves showed that differences in some metabolites, such as l-Kynurenine and uracil, continued to widen over time, whereas the between-group differences in others narrowed or disappeared, such as l-homocitrulline and carnitine C3:0 (Figure 5A).

The diverse etiologies of HF onset reflect its complex pathophysiology and highlight the need to identify metabolic profiles specific to populations with different metabolic disorders. Figure 6A illustrates metabolite profiles associated with HF risk among healthy participants (without hypertension, diabetes, obesity, coronary heart disease, or chronic kidney disease at the time point of the first serum metabolomic measurement), as well as participants with hypertension, dysglycemia, and overweight/obesity. HF risk–associated metabolites exhibited distinct patterns across participants with different metabolic disorders (Figure 6B). The metabolites positively associated with HF risk were primarily fatty acyl compounds in the healthy population, while amino acids predominated in individuals with hypertension or overweight/obesity. No specific metabolic class predominance was observed among participants with dysglycemia for positively associated metabolites. The metabolites negatively associated with HF risk were predominantly glycerophospholipids in the healthy population, whereas the predominant metabolites in the populations with hypertension, overweight/obesity, and dysglycemia were fatty acyl compounds, amino acids, and hormones and hormone-related compounds, respectively. Among the 23 metabolites associated with HF risk at an FDR-adjusted P<0.05 in the total cohort, the majority retained significance among participants with hypertension, followed by participants with overweight/obesity, but only 4 metabolites maintained significance among those with dysglycemia (Figure 6C).

The metabolite trajectories associated with HF risk were similar across all subgroups (Figure S15). The number of metabolites associated with HF risk varied across distinct trajectory clusters and participant groups (Figure 6D). Each participant group exhibited a distinct set of HF-associated metabolites within each cluster. Furthermore, participants with overweight/obesity largely shared common HF-linked metabolites with those having hypertension or dysglycemia, respectively. However, overlap was limited between individuals with hypertension and dysglycemia. The metabolic pathway analysis revealed distinct enrichment patterns across the subgroups (Figure 6E). Participants with hypertension exhibited enrichment in cysteine and methionine metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and the one-carbon pool by folate. Notably, steroid hormone biosynthesis were enriched in both dysglycemia and individuals with overweight/obesity. Primary bile acid biosynthesis was specifically enriched in the participants with dysglycemia, whereas arginine and proline metabolism were significantly enriched in those with overweight/obesity.

Discussion

In this 20-year longitudinal metabolomic study, we identified 23 metabolites associated with incident HF, including 9 not previously reported in relation to HF. Six metabolites showed directionally consistent and nominally significant associations in the external cohort. The HF-associated metabolites followed 4 distinct longitudinal trajectories: metabolites positively associated with HF risk generally remained stable, whereas those negatively associated with risk tended to decline over time. Differences between participants who subsequently developed HF and those who did not were detectable 5 to 20 years before clinical diagnosis. Moreover, the metabolite profiles and enriched pathways differed among individuals with hypertension, dysglycemia, and overweight/obesity.

Unlike previous population-based studies based largely on single–time point metabolomic measurements, our longitudinal design captured time-varying metabolite levels and cumulative exposure, providing a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic alterations associated with HF risk. Of the 23 metabolites significantly associated with HF risk after FDR adjustment, we confirmed 10 previously linked to HF onset (uracil,13,22,23 l-kynurenine,24–26 kynurenic acid,25,26), prevalence (carnitine C3:0, carnitine C4:0, carnitine C9:1, and carnitine C16:3),27–30 or prognosis (l-homocitrulline,31 urea,32 and cortisone33). Uracil showed a protective effect—consistent with the Jackson Heart Study’s finding of an inverse association between incident HF and uridine (a nucleoside formed by uracil and β-d-ribose).13 Experimental evidence suggests uridine-derived ribose supports myocardial energy production under nutrient stress, implying depleted pyrimidine metabolism in HF pathogenesis.22,23 l-Kynurenine and kynurenic acid were confirmed as potential risk factors, aligning with case-control data from the Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea study and Framingham Offspring cohort findings on cardiac remodeling.24,25 Mendelian randomization further links them to reduced ejection fraction and adverse cardiovascular outcomes.26 We also extend the roles of l-homocitrulline and cortisol, showing their prognostic role in previous cohorts of HF patients.31,33 Beyond known HF metabolites, we identified 4 metabolites associated with cardiovascular diseases and target organ damage (2-hydroxyhexanoic acid,34 2-(dimethylamino)guanosine,26,35,36 2-methylguanosine,37 lysophosphatidylcholine [17:2/0:0]38–40). Circulating lysophosphatidylcholine consistently correlated inversely with cardiovascular disease risk across 3 large prospective cohorts.38–40 Conversely, 2-hydroxyhexanoic acid was linked to cardiovascular mortality in atherosclerosis,34 and 2-(dimethylamino)guanosine was associated with elevated cardiovascular risk in type 2 diabetes.35,36 Overall, we identified 9 HF-related metabolites that are novel in that they have not been previously reported to be associated with HF. Most of these metabolites were associated with established biomarkers of cardiac structure and function, including NT-proBNP and cTnT, thereby supporting the critical role of these metabolic alterations in cardiac dysfunction within HF pathophysiology. These metabolites have been implicated in processes such as DNA damage and repair, protein synthesis, and metabolic regulation,41 and they warrant further investigation within the context of HF pathophysiology. Although our study did not establish causal relationships between the identified metabolites and HF risk, the prospective design and replication in an external validation cohort may unveil potential therapeutic targets for HF.

In addition to identifying HF-associated metabolites, this study is the first to characterize their dynamic trajectories using longitudinal metabolomics data. Most serum metabolites remain stable over time, whereas others decline progressively. Metabolites inversely associated with HF risk (eg, specific glycerophospholipids in cluster 4) show such decline, reflecting depletion of youthful metabolic reserves, while risk-associated metabolites (eg, certain acylcarnitines and amino acids in clusters 1 and 2) remain stable or increase, mirroring signatures of accelerated biological aging.42 Deviations from an individual’s healthy metabolic profile may thus signal early cardiac metabolic alteration and impending HF onset. We also uncovered distinct temporal sequences of metabolic alterations preceding HF onset. For instance, l-Kynurenine and kynurenic acid levels diverged from those of participants who did not develop HF 17 to 19 years before HF diagnosis. Because dietary tryptophan is metabolized by gut microbiota into kynurenine, tryptamine, and indole,43 these early shifts suggest a gut-metabolite-cardiac axis in HF pathogenesis. Although the precise roles and mechanisms of l-kynurenine and kynurenic acid in HF remain incompletely understood, their decades-long preclinical elevation highlights the potential of early lifestyle modifications or pharmacological intervention.44 Like cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers preceding Alzheimer disease,45 the timing and order of metabolic divergence deepened our understanding of HF pathophysiology and pointed to a critical window for early diagnosis and precise treatment.

This longitudinal metabolomics study also revealed differences in the HF-associated metabolite profiles across participants with distinct metabolic disorders. Although sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors46 consistently benefit those with obesity or diabetes, most other drugs lack consistent efficacy across these groups, highlighting the need for personalized interventions grounded in specific metabolite profiles and underlying mechanisms. HF-related metabolites engaged different biological pathways in individuals with different metabolic disorders. In hypertension, amino acids and fatty acyl compounds (carnitines) predominated, disrupting myocardial energy metabolism and contributing to vasodilation under elevated cardiac afterload.47 In dysglycemia, similar metabolites appeared in the overweight population, but several were involved in primary bile acid biosynthesis, likely reflecting hyperglycemia-induced damage through advanced glycation end products modification, impaired renal bile acid excretion, and insulin deficiency–driven metabolic decompensation. These mechanisms operate independently of obesity-related pathology, such as visceral adiposity and chronic inflammation.48 The significant heterogeneity in the HF-associated metabolite profiles across populations with distinct risk factors supports developing tailored monitoring and intervention strategies, improving both clinical precision and resource efficiency.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the HF events were primarily defined based on discharge diagnoses. However, this method of identifying HF events is widely used in major cohort studies, ensuring comparability between the present study and prior research. Second, this study did not classify HF subtypes based on ejection fraction or exclude patients using baseline echocardiography. Ejection fraction data were available for a small subset of HF patients in this study, and the results showed that most of them were diagnosed with HF with preserved ejection fraction (data not shown). Third, some of the insights were generated or remained unverified due to the limited sample size. However, our longitudinal study design incorporating multiple repeated metabolomic measurements per participant accounts for intraindividual correlations, substantially increases the effective sample size and statistical efficiency compared with traditional single–time point analyses.49 Moreover, we applied rigorous multiple testing correction with FDR<0.05 across our analyses. The external validation cohort had a relatively small sample size. Despite this, we identified many metabolites that have been reported in previous studies, supporting the reliability of the external validation findings. Finally, we did not integrate genomics or proteomics data, limiting our ability to explore the underlying drivers of the observed metabolite changes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this 20-year longitudinal metabolomic study expands our understanding of the serum metabolite landscape in the context of HF and its implications for disease prevention and treatment. We identified specific metabolites associated with HF risk, characterized their temporal evolution preceding HF onset, and demonstrated considerable heterogeneity among individuals with different metabolic disorders. We expect these findings to be of great clinical relevance, offering novel insights into the metabolic basis of HF and informing tailored preventive and intervention strategies for HF.
