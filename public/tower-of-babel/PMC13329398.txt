Effectiveness of a home-based training program on hand function after carpal tunnel release: A prospective multicenter trial
Authors: A. Uerschels, Y. Habib, V. Braun, S. Unkel, C. Scholz, M. Hohenhaus, A. Lenkeit, C. Brenke, A.E. Carolus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a condition that affects the wrist and is characterized by compression of the median nerve. Symptoms include pain, numbness and tingling in the hand, weakness and muscle wasting. Many people undergo surgery to reduce pressure on the nerve. It is likely that regular training following surgery has a positive effect on outcome. However, the recommendations regarding physical activity and exercise are inconsistent. The study aims to determine whether a home-based hand training program improves hand function and patient satisfaction.MethodsThe study was conducted as a prospective, randomized, multicenter trial. Between August 2022 and May 2025 80 consecutive cases of surgically treated CTS were included. All clinical data was recorded uniformly and grip strength was measured. Patients were randomly allocated to training group (T) and control group (C). All patients had open median nerve decompression via mini incision. After two weeks wound healing period the training group received specific instructions with therapeutic modelling clay and a training diary. All test subjects were examined six weeks after the operation.ResultsSignificant hand strength improvement was seen after 4 weeks of home training compared to the standard therapy group. Patient satisfaction with the additional therapy was high.ConclusionCarrying out regular home training after carpal tunnel surgery can improve both hand function and patient satisfaction. This is particularly relevant for patients who do not have easy access to rehabilitative services.

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most prevalent and well-recognized neuropathic condition characterized by compression of the median nerve in the wrist. This can result in numbness, tingling and pain in the hand. In some advanced cases, weakness, muscle wasting and loss of fine motor skills also occur. Given the prevalence of CTS, its impact on the quality of life and its economic impact on healthcare systems, there is a need for treatment options that extend beyond surgical intervention. It is assumed that postoperative care and hand exercises influence the rehabilitation and return-to-work processes (Millrose et al., 2020; Sheerin et al., 2024). Special hand rehabilitation programs are designed to speed up recovery (Provinciali et al., 2000), improve existing symptoms and prevent the development of muscle hardening. However the systematic evidence is limited. There is a lack of high-quality studies evaluating the effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions (Peters et al., 2016).

This prospective randomized multicenter study investigates the impact of a self-performed home-based training program on postoperative recovery of hand function and patient satisfaction.

Materials and methods

The local ethics committee approved the study, which was conducted across three neurosurgical centers in Germany. All participants gave their written consent. Between August 2022 and May 2025, 80 patients (hands) with indication to carpal tunnel surgery were included. The diagnosis and indication for surgery were based on the German guideline updated in 2022 (AWMF, 2022) and included neurographic examination and ultrasound examination in addition to typical clinical symptoms. For postoperative treatment participants were randomly assigned to either receiving training (training group T) or standard care including an additional follow-up visit (control group C).

Patient examination form

All patients were examined using a self-designed, standardized examination form encompassing both anamnesis and clinical assessments. Personal medical history and known risk factors of CTS such as arthritis, diabetes, amyloidosis, renal insufficiency, or pregnancy were recorded. The presence of atrophy of the thenar muscle, the grade of sensory disturbance and a reduction in strength of the flexor pollicis longus, opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis, flexor digitorum profundus, and lumbricales muscles were investigated. Although, typical tests as the Phalen test and the bottle sign were performed.

Hand gross strength at dynamometer

The gross hand strength was measured using an electronic hand dynamometer, Fa GripX. Patients were in a sitting position. They were instructed to exert maximal force with the affected hand in a fist grip. Three consecutive measurements were taken, and the average was calculated. The unit of measurement was kilograms.

Patient Satisfaction Index (PSI)

Patients were asked whether they were satisfied with the result of the operation, and, if they belonged to the (T) group, whether they were satisfied with the training programme. The selection menue used here was based on the Patient Satisfaction Index (PSI) commonly applied in spinal surgery. The possible answers in both questions were: a) very satisfied (with result of surgery/with training program), b) satisfied and would do it again, c) would not do it again, d) condition had worsened.

Study course

The study course is pictured in Fig. 1. Preoperatively, hand grip strength was measured in all patients and the examination form was applied. All patients underwent an open release of the flexor retinaculum, mostly under local anesthesia. Following surgery, a postoperative recovery period over two weeks was implemented for both groups to ensure consistent early recovery conditions. During this period, finger movements were recommended; no stress or active training was allowed. At the time of suture removal the participants in the training group (T) were given a training manual with a total of sixteen different training instructions and a training diary (Fig. 2). This training manual was developed specifically for this study in collaboration with an occupational therapist specialising in hand therapy. The exercises are designed to improve both hand strength and the mobility of the fingers and prevent secondary hardening of the connective tissue of the palm. The individual exercise steps were illustrated in photographs, making them easy to follow. The patients were instructed to choose a minimum of three exercises, to practice them for 10 min at a time daily for a total of twenty-eight days, and to document their training time. The control group was instructed to increase slowly the everyday activities without doing special training. At the end of the 6 weeks period all patients had an examination and a grip strength measuring again. Statistical analysis

Computations in this paper were carried out by using the software R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025) (R Core Team, 2025). All computer code is available from the authors upon request. The differences of means of grip strength were compared between the training and the control group. One sample t-test and Wilcoxon rank sum test were performed. A p-value ≤0,05 was considered statistically significant. A regression model was used to assess center effects.

Discussion

Previous consensus studies show that medical recommendations regarding hand exercises after surgery are inconsistent. The same applies to recommendations regarding the resumption of work. There is agreement, however, that a certain degree of finger activity is beneficial (Newington et al., 2018). This study investigated the potential benefit of a structured postoperative training program in patients undergoing surgical decompression for carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) for the first time. The multicenter prospective and randomized design permits strong conclusions. It could be shown that intensive postoperative training results in more rapid improvement of hand function. Grip strength measured through fist closure was chosen here as an objective measurement parameter. It does not provide a differentiated representation of the individual functions of the muscles innervated by the median nerve (M. abductor pollicis, M. flexor pollicis, M. opponenes pollicis, Mm. lumbricales). Nevertheless, it represents a variable that shows the interplay of both motor and sensible hand nerve function and includes pain-freeness in the palm. It will not work properly if the median nerve is affected by chronic compression. Sufficient finger flexion into a fist is a crucial factors for the functional usability of the hand in everyday life. Some activities requiring a sufficient fist closure include grasping and firmly holding tools such as kitchen utensils, carrying shopping bages, opening jars and bottles, writing with a pen for prolonged periods. The modified PSI shows high patient satisfaction with the experience of a four-week training phase. This indicates that the training program was well accepted.

A home-based training offers several advantages: It enables patients to perform exercise independently and flexibly in their own environment. This approach may improve adherence and continuity of training, particularly in patients with limited access to supervised therapy. Especially among older individuals, practical barriers such as long travel distances, limited mobility and challenges in obtaining therapy prescriptions normally restrict participation in continued rehabilitation. The COVID-19 pandemic has also demonstrated how quickly guided physiotherapy and occupational therapy can be deprioritized. In such exceptional situations, home training can ensure a good rehabilitation process. This also gains new relevance in light of the fact that treatment of carpal tunnel syndrome is typically carried out in an outpatient setting and does not involve prolonged physician contact. Patients are expected to engage in self-management, particularly during the recovery phase. Seel et al. emphasise the importance of targeted online education (Seel et al., 2025). A training guide could be part of this.

The cost of the home exercise program is low. A tub of therapeutic modeling clay costs about 10 euros. In addition, there is a 10- to 20-min instruction session, which in our study took place during the follow-up visit and thus slightly increased the treatment time which is also billed. However, the instruction can also be provided by a non-medical staff member. In contrast, a single occupational therapy session costs between 50 and 90 euros; 10 sessions cost between 500 and 900 euros (this applies to Germany). Therefore, the home-based training is a costeffective solution.

However, it should be noted that a session of professionally guided occupational therapy addresses more than just hand muscle training; for example, it includes sensory-perceptual training, exercises to improve central motor control, and, if necessary, scar treatment. It is important that the program is well guided and that supervising respectively monitoring take place with the aim to provide a time frame. A diary documenting training time and session help to increase awareness of the hand function and to boost motivation (Chan and Can, 2010).

Limitations

There was a statistically significant difference in the prevalence of comorbidities between the groups which could potentially bias the good recovery effect observed in the training group. However, the preoperative symptom severity showed no significant difference across all aspects (sensation, motor function, tests), meaning that the group with comorbidities was not more severely affected.

We see that the scar tissue should not be underestimated. Even several weeks after surgery there often is discomfort or even pain in the area of palm hand which negatively influences grip closure. It is conceivable that improvements in grip strength may manifest more clearly beyond the six-week mark, suggesting the need for extended longitudinal observation.

It is to be emphasized that self-directed training cannot replace supervised training conducted by a qualified person. In more complex conditions such as injuries professionals play an irreplaceable role for rehabilitation.

In hindsight, it would have been interesting to record the return-to-work date for both patients, as this is an indicator of successful hand rehabilitation.

Conclusions

Regular home training after carpal tunnel surgery can improve both hand function and patient satisfaction. Training can be recommended and should be incorporated into the CTS treatment plan. Surgeons should ensure that patients are properly informed and understand that rehabilitation continues beyond the surgical procedures. A home training is a practicable and sufficient option.
