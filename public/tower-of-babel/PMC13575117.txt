Circulating CD20-positive extracellular vesicles impair rituximab efficacy and predict outcomes in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Bon Park, Kyung Ju Ryu, Min Kyu Choi, Sang Eun Yoon, Won Seog Kim, Seok Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, is a cornerstone of therapy for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), yet a substantial proportion of patients develop treatment failure. While loss of CD20 expression has been considered a major mechanism of resistance, additional mechanisms limiting effective antibody binding remain incompletely understood. We hypothesized that circulating CD20-positive large extracellular vesicles (EVlarge) act as antigen sinks that sequester rituximab and attenuate its therapeutic activity. Circulating CD20-positive EVlarge were quantified from archived serum samples of newly diagnosed DLBCL patients treated with R-CHOP. The prognostic impact of EVlarge levels was evaluated in a training cohort (n = 26) and an independent validation cohort (n = 90). Functional effects of tumor-derived EVlarge were assessed using OCI-Ly1 and U2932 lymphoma cell models and T cell cytotoxicity assays. Rituximab binding to tumor-derived EVlarge was visualized using light-field 4D microscopy. CD20 positivity was consistently higher in EVlarge than in EVsmall across lymphoma cell lines. Tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge enhanced lymphoma cell proliferation and attenuated rituximab-mediated growth inhibition. EVlarge also impaired rituximab-mediated cytotoxicity in T cell co-culture assays. Light-field 4D microscopy demonstrated binding of rituximab to EVlarge near the tumor cell surface with reduced antibody binding to lymphoma cells, supporting an antigen-decoy mechanism. Clinically, high pre-treatment levels of circulating CD20-positive EVlarge were associated with significantly inferior disease-specific and overall survival in both training and validation cohorts and remained independently prognostic after adjustment for International Prognostic Index risk. In conclusion, tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge represent a novel mechanism of rituximab resistance by reducing effective antibody exposure and impairing immune-mediated cytotoxicity. Circulating CD20-positive EVlarge provide a biologically informative biomarker that predicts clinical outcomes independently of established prognostic factors and may guide optimization of antibody-based therapy in DLBCL.Subject terms: Translational research, Risk factors, B-cell lymphoma

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), accounting for approximately 30–40% of cases worldwide. For nearly two decades, standard frontline therapy has consisted of rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP), based on randomized phase III trials demonstrating improved survival with the addition of rituximab [1, 2]. Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20, enhances B-cell depletion through complement-dependent cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and induction of apoptosis [3–5]. Despite these advances, approximately 40–50% of patients ultimately develop relapsed or refractory disease, particularly those with high-risk disease according to the International Prognostic Index (IPI) [6–8]. Outcomes remain poor after treatment failure, highlighting the need for improved therapeutic strategies [9, 10]. Resistance to rituximab has been attributed to several mechanisms, including loss or downregulation of CD20 expression, activation of anti-apoptotic pathways, and tumor microenvironment–mediated immune escape [11, 12]. However, additional mechanisms contributing to rituximab resistance remain incompletely understood.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer–enclosed particles released by virtually all cell types and play important roles in intercellular communication. The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles recommends the use of the term EV for particles released from cells and delimited by a lipid bilayer, rather than biogenesis-based terms such as exosomes, ectosomes, or microvesicles [13]. As EVs are commonly isolated using methods such as filtration or differential ultracentrifugation, which enrich for vesicle populations based on physical properties including size and density, the terms small EVs (EVsmall) and large EVs (EVlarge) are operationally defined and reflect method-dependent fractions rather than strictly size- or biogenesis-defined entities. Although “small” EVs are often considered to be <200 nm in diameter, there is no universally accepted size cutoff, and substantial overlap in size distribution exists across EV populations obtained by different isolation techniques.

Importantly, these categories do not strictly correspond to distinct biogenetic pathways; however, EVlarge are generally thought to arise from direct budding of the plasma membrane, analogous to ectosomes or microvesicles, whereas EVsmall are associated with the endosomal pathway and released via exocytosis, similar to exosomes [14, 15]. Because EVlarge are shed directly from the plasma membrane, tumor-derived EVlarge circulating in the bloodstream may more closely reflect tumor burden and contribute to treatment resistance through modulation of the tumor microenvironment [16, 17]. Although EVsmall have been extensively investigated as potential biomarkers in malignancies, their clinical application remains limited by the technical complexity of isolation.

Given that CD20 is abundantly expressed on DLBCL cells, tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge may interact with rituximab and reduce effective antibody exposure to malignant B cells. A recent study reported an association between plasma CD20-positive EVs and treatment outcomes in patients with AIDS-related non-Hodgkin lymphoma [18]. Based on this observation, we therefore hypothesized that circulating CD20-positive EVlarge function as antigen sinks that attenuate rituximab activity. Accordingly, we investigated the association between circulating CD20-positive EVlarge and treatment outcomes in newly diagnosed DLBCL patients treated with R-CHOP and evaluated their potential role as biomarkers of rituximab resistance.

Materials and methods

Study design

The aim of this study was to evaluate the prognostic impact of circulating CD20-positive EVs in newly diagnosed DLBCL patients treated with standard R-CHOP therapy in routine clinical practice. CD20-positive EVlarge and EVsmall were isolated from archived serum samples and from supernatants of cultured primary tumor cells derived from malignant ascites or pleural effusions using our established protocol (Fig. 1A; Supplemental methods). Patients were diagnosed according to the World Health Organization classification, and CD20 expression in tumor cells was confirmed by immunohistochemistry. Circulating CD20-positive EVlarge and EVsmall were quantified from archived serum samples obtained from patients enrolled in previous prospective cohort studies and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The training cohort consisted of 26 patients selected from our second prospective cohort study (2012–2017; NCT01877109), including 14 patients with sustained remission and 12 with relapse or progression. Patients were dichotomized into high and low groups according to the median levels of CD20-positive EVlarge and EVsmall. The prognostic value of circulating EVs was validated in an independent cohort with long-term clinical follow-up from our first prospective cohort study (2008–2011; NCT00822731). Associations between EV levels and clinical outcomes were evaluated using archived clinical data, including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, disease stage, serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, B symptoms, extranodal involvement, bone marrow involvement, IPI risk group, treatment response, relapse, and survival outcomes. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Flow cytometric analysis of CD20-positive EVlarge and EVsmall

To determine CD20 positivity of EVlarge and EVsmall, EVs isolated from CD20-positive B-cell lymphoma cell lines were incubated with aldehyde/sulfate latex beads (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) on a rotator at room temperature for 15 min and then overnight at 4 °C. Unreacted aldehyde groups were blocked with 1 M glycine for 30 min at room temperature. After washing, bead-bound EVs were stained with anti-CD20 antibody (BB515) for 1 h at room temperature, washed twice, and resuspended in PBS or 4% paraformaldehyde for analysis. Data acquisition was performed using a FACS Lyric flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and results were analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.0.6). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blotting were performed to confirm EVlarge and EVsmall isolation (supplemental methods).

Cell culture and treatment conditions

The OCI-Ly1 B-cell lymphoma cell line was kindly provided by W.S. Kim and cultured in Iscove Modified Dulbecco Medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotics. U2932 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotics. For treatment experiments, OCI-Ly1 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL in medium supplemented with 20% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum. EVlarge isolated from patient-derived pleural effusions or ascites were added at a final concentration of 25 µg/mL. Rituximab (100 nM) was administered either alone or in combination with EVlarge. Cell proliferation was assessed by cell counting at 24, 48, and 72 h after treatment.

Generation of GFP-expressing stable lymphoma cell lines

GFP-expressing OCI-Ly1 and U2932 cell lines were generated using a lentiviral vector encoding firefly luciferase, GFP, and a puromycin resistance cassette (Lenti-fLuc-GFP-PuroR; Lugen). Cells were transduced with lentiviral supernatant in the presence of a lentiviral concentration reagent (Lugen Sci Co. Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following transduction, cells were cultured for 2–3 days before initiation of puromycin selection at optimized concentrations for each cell line. Selection was continued until all non-transduced control cells were eliminated. The resulting puromycin-resistant cells showed stable GFP and luciferase expression, confirming successful establishment of reporter cell lines.

Visualization of rituximab binding to tumor-derived EVlarge

Rituximab binding to tumor-derived EVlarge was visualized using a light-field 4D microscopy system. GFP-expressing OCI-Ly1 cells were used as target tumor cells. EVlarge were isolated from conditioned supernatants of fluorescently labeled Raji cells cultured for 72 h in medium supplemented with exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum. Two experimental conditions were examined. In the first condition, OCI-Ly1 cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated anti-CD20 antibody (rituximab biosimilar, clone 297; BD Pharmingen) at a final concentration of 100 nM for 30 min. In the second condition, OCI-Ly1 cells were preincubated with purified Raji cell–derived EVlarge (25 µg/mL) for 3.5 h before antibody treatment, followed by an additional 30-min incubation. These experiments were designed to assess rituximab binding and its interaction with tumor-derived EVlarge.

Statistical analysis

In vitro data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments performed in triplicate. Statistical comparisons were performed using the Student t test with Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to lymphoma relapse or progression. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Survival analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Serum derived CD20-positive EVlarge and EVsmall in the training cohort

To determine CD20 positivity of EVlarge and EVsmall, EVs isolated from B-cell lymphoma cell lines (Raji, Daudi, SuDHL2, OCI-Ly1, U2932, and Toledo) were analyzed by flow cytometry. Among the six cell lines, Raji-derived EVs demonstrated the highest CD20 positivity (EVlarge, 99.3%; EVsmall, 97.6%) and CD20 positivity was consistently higher in EVlarge than in EVsmall across all cell lines (Fig. 1B). Accordingly, Raji cells were used as the positive control to establish gating thresholds for CD20-positive EVs, consistent with previous reports [19]. Using these predefined cutoffs, CD20-positive EVlarge and EVsmall were identified and isolated from patient-derived serum samples and primary tumor culture supernatants (Fig. 1C). EVlarge and EVsmall were isolated from serum samples of 26 patients in the training cohort. Baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Successful isolation of EV fractions was confirmed by detection of Arf6 for EVlarge and CD63 and CD81 for EVsmall, whereas calnexin was absent in both fractions, indicating minimal cellular contamination (Fig. 1D). TEM demonstrated the typical morphology of EVlarge, and immunogold labeling confirmed CD20 expression on serum-derived EVlarge (Fig. 1E). NTA showed a mean particle size of 148.5 nm, with EVlarge particles extending up to 400 nm, consistent with the reported size range of large extracellular vesicles (approximately 100–1000 nm) (Fig. 1F). Given the known overlap in size distribution between EV populations, the EVlarge fraction in this study reflects an operationally defined population based on the isolation method rather than a strict size-based classification. Using Raji cell–derived EVlarge to establish the gating strategy, a cutoff for CD20 positivity was defined. The median proportion of CD20-positive EVlarge in the training cohort was 3.66%, and this value was used to stratify patients into high and low groups. Treatment responses to R-CHOP included complete response in 20 patients, partial response in 3 patients, and progressive disease in 3 patients. Notably, those three patients who were primarily refractory to R-CHOP belonged to the high EVlarge group. Patients with high CD20-positive EVlarge levels had significantly shorter EFS and OS compared with those with low levels (Fig. 2A, B), despite similar baseline clinical characteristics (Table 1). In contrast, CD20-positive EVsmall levels were not associated with EFS or OS (Fig. 2C, D). Likewise, CD19-positive and PD-L1–positive EVlarge showed no significant association with EFS, supporting the target specificity of CD20-positive EVlarge (Fig. 2E, F).

Tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge attenuate rituximab activity

EVlarge were isolated from culture supernatants of primary tumor cells derived from malignant ascites or pleural effusions of four patients with DLBCL (Pt 1–4). Flow cytometry confirmed CD20 expression on tumor-derived EVlarge (Fig. 3A). Treatment of OCI-Ly1 cells with patient-derived EVlarge (25 µg/mL) resulted in significantly increased cell proliferation compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3B). To evaluate the impact of EVlarge on rituximab activity, OCI-Ly1 cells were treated with EVlarge, rituximab, or the combination of both. EVlarge alone promoted tumor cell proliferation, whereas co-treatment with EVlarge and rituximab resulted in higher cell proliferation than rituximab alone (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate that tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge promote lymphoma cell growth and attenuate rituximab-mediated growth inhibition, consistent with an antigen-decoy mechanism.

Tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge impair rituximab-mediated cytotoxicity

To evaluate the impact of CD20-positive EVlarge on rituximab-mediated cytotoxicity, GFP-expressing OCI-Ly1 and U2932 cells were co-cultured with activated human T cells at a 1:1 effector-to-target ratio in the presence or absence of EVlarge (25 µg/mL). Rituximab (100 nM) enhanced T cell–mediated cytotoxicity, whereas the addition of EVlarge significantly increased tumor cell survival compared with both untreated and rituximab-only conditions (Fig. 3D). To further assess the effect of CD20-positive EVlarge on antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, GFP-expressing OCI-Ly1 and U2932 cells were co-cultured with peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which include key effector populations such as NK cells and monocytes, at an effector-to-target ratio of 5:1. Co-cultures were treated with rituximab (Rtx; 100 nM) in the presence of increasing concentrations of patient-derived EVlarge (12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL). Consistently, EVlarge significantly impaired rituximab-mediated cytotoxicity, as evidenced by increased tumor cell survival compared with both untreated and rituximab-only conditions (Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge impairs rituximab-mediated cellular cytotoxicity. To directly visualize the decoy effect of EVlarge on rituximab binding, light-field 4D microscopy was performed using OCI-Ly1 cells in the presence or absence of Raji-derived EVlarge. Rituximab binding to OCI-Ly1 cells was readily observed in the absence of EVlarge, whereas EVlarge bound rituximab near the tumor cell surface and reduced antibody binding to tumor cells (Fig. 4A), consistent with an antigen-decoy mechanism.

Prognostic implications of CD20-positive EVlarge in validation cohort

The validation cohort included 90 patients, and baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Compared with the training cohort, patients in the validation cohort had more favorable clinical features, with a higher proportion of localized disease and low IPI risk. Patients were stratified into high and low CD20-positive EVlarge groups using the predefined cutoff (3.66%) derived from the training cohort. Treatment responses to R-CHOP included complete response in 71 patients, partial response in 8 patients, and progressive disease in 11 patients. Progressive disease occurred more frequently in the high EVlarge group than in the low group (18.6% vs 6.3%, P = 0.08). Survival analysis based on IPI risk demonstrated favorable EFS in the low and low-intermediate risk groups, confirming the consistency of this cohort with established prognostic models (Fig. 4B). Patients with high CD20-positive EVlarge levels had significantly inferior EFS and OS compared with those with low levels (Fig. 4C, D), despite similar baseline clinical characteristics (Table 2). Similar results were obtained when patients were stratified using the median EVlarge level of the validation cohort (3.52%), with high CD20-positive EVlarge associated with inferior EFS and OS (data not shown). In multivariable analysis including IPI risk, high CD20-positive EVlarge remained an independent predictor of EFS (Fig. 4E). A schematic model illustrating the proposed interaction between tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge and rituximab is shown in Fig. 4F.

Discussion

Rituximab efficacy depends on adequate antibody binding to CD20 and subsequent activation of immune effector mechanisms. Previous studies have highlighted substantial interindividual variability in rituximab pharmacokinetics, particularly accelerated clearance in elderly men, which has been associated with inferior clinical outcomes compared with women [20, 21]. Accordingly, optimization of rituximab exposure has been an important strategy to improve treatment outcomes. Dose-dense or prolonged rituximab administration in the DENSE-R-CHOP-14 and SMARTE-R-CHOP-14 trials resulted in higher serum rituximab levels and improved outcomes in elderly patients, whereas sex-adapted dosing in the SEXIE-R-CHOP-14 trial significantly improved outcomes in elderly male patients [22–24]. These observations suggest that effective target engagement of rituximab is a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy.

In the present study, we demonstrate that tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge represent a previously unrecognized mechanism that may limit effective rituximab binding. CD20 positivity was consistently higher in EVlarge than in EVsmall across lymphoma cell lines, and patient-derived CD20-positive EVlarge directly bound rituximab and attenuated its anti-lymphoma activity in vitro. Light-field 4D microscopy further demonstrated that EVlarge sequester rituximab near the tumor cell surface and reduce antibody binding to lymphoma cells, providing direct evidence for an antigen-decoy mechanism. Clinically, high pre-treatment levels of circulating CD20-positive EVlarge were associated with significantly inferior EFS and OS in both training and validation cohorts and remained independently prognostic after adjustment for IPI risk. Importantly, CD20-positive EVlarge levels were not associated with age, sex, stage, LDH level, or IPI risk, indicating that EVlarge represent a biologically distinct determinant of treatment outcome that is not captured by conventional prognostic models.

Although CD20-positive EVlarge levels would be expected to correlate with tumor burden, EV release may also be influenced by tumor cell activation, cellular stress, and microenvironmental factors, including inflammatory and immune signals, which can enhance vesicle shedding independent of tumor mass. In addition, interpatient heterogeneity in vesicle biogenesis may contribute to variability in circulating EV levels. Clinically, commonly used surrogates of tumor burden, such as Ann Arbor stage, may not fully capture disease extent. Although PET/CT-derived metabolic tumor volume (MTV) provides a more quantitative assessment, it is not always readily available and may be influenced by the timing of imaging relative to treatment initiation. Serum LDH is also nonspecific and may be affected by factors unrelated to tumor burden.

In this study, we were unable to directly evaluate the association between tumor burden and CD20-positive EVlarge levels, as pre-treatment MTV data were not uniformly available across the cohort. Accordingly, the contribution of tumor burden to circulating EV levels cannot be fully excluded. Future studies incorporating standardized PET/CT-based tumor burden measurements are warranted to better define this relationship. If validated, assessment of CD20-positive EVlarge levels may provide a clinically practical surrogate for tumor burden, particularly in settings where quantitative PET/CT-based metrics are not routinely feasible.

Taken together, these findings suggest that CD20-positive EVlarge may represent a tumor-intrinsic source of functional rituximab underexposure. While previous optimization strategies primarily addressed patient-intrinsic pharmacokinetic variability, circulating CD20-positive EVlarge may function as antigen sinks that sequester rituximab before it reaches malignant B cells. Although loss or downregulation of CD20 has traditionally been considered a major mechanism of resistance, our data support an additional mechanism whereby tumor-derived EVlarge reduce effective antibody exposure despite preserved CD20 expression on tumor cells.

In contrast, CD20-positive EVsmall levels were not associated with clinical outcomes. Although CD20-positive exosomes have been reported to bind rituximab, EVlarge may have greater antibody-sequestering capacity because they arise through direct plasma membrane budding and more closely preserve tumor-cell surface antigen composition. This is consistent with our observation that CD20 positivity was higher in EVlarge than in EVsmall and may explain the stronger clinical associations observed for EVlarge.

Beyond antibody sequestration, CD20-positive EVlarge also impaired immune-mediated cytotoxicity in co-culture assays using GFP-expressing OCI-Ly1 and U2932 cells. The addition of EVlarge significantly reduced rituximab-mediated tumor cell killing and increased tumor cell survival, indicating that EVlarge-mediated resistance extends beyond simple antibody neutralization. These findings suggest that EVlarge may contribute to immune evasion by interfering with immune effector function. Further studies are warranted to define the molecular components of EVlarge, including potential immunomodulatory cargo such as PD-L1, Fas ligand, or TGF-β, that may suppress immune activation and cytotoxicity [25, 26].

The clinical implications of these findings are substantial. Measurement of circulating CD20-positive EVlarge before treatment may provide a biologically informative biomarker that complements established prognostic indices such as the IPI. In contrast to CD20-positive EVlarge, neither CD20-positive EVsmall nor CD19- or PD-L1–positive EVlarge showed significant prognostic associations, supporting the biological specificity of CD20-positive EVlarge. Identification of patients with high EVlarge burden may allow risk-adapted therapeutic strategies, including antibody dose optimization or alternative antibody-based therapies.

Finally, the antigen-sink effect mediated by EVlarge is unlikely to be restricted to CD20. Similar mechanisms may influence the efficacy of other B-cell–targeted therapies, including CD19-directed monoclonal antibodies, antibody–drug conjugates targeting CD79B, and cellular therapies directed against surface antigens such as CD19. These observations suggest broader relevance of EVlarge profiling for personalized antibody-based therapy in B-cell malignancies.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the prognostic significance of CD20-positive EVlarge was validated in an independent cohort, the overall sample size remained modest, and all patients were treated at a single institution. Second, although our findings suggest that CD20-positive EVlarge may function as an antigen sink that attenuates rituximab-mediated cytotoxicity, we did not systematically evaluate the quantitative relationship between EVlarge abundance, rituximab concentration, and the degree of cytotoxicity inhibition. In addition, we did not perform pharmacokinetic modeling to estimate the proportion of rituximab bound to circulating CD20-positive EVlarge, as such analyses would require quantitative assessment of EV concentration, CD20 antigen density per vesicle, and in vivo binding kinetics. Future studies integrating quantitative EV profiling with pharmacokinetic modeling, as well as rituximab–EVlarge dose–response matrices, will be necessary to define the extent to which EV-associated CD20 contributes to rituximab disposition and therapeutic efficacy, and to establish thresholds at which CD20-positive EVlarge meaningfully neutralize rituximab activity. Finally, the molecular determinants underlying antibody sequestration by EVlarge were not fully characterized, and standardized clinical assays for EVlarge quantification are not yet established. Prospective studies are warranted to determine whether EVlarge-guided therapeutic strategies can improve clinical outcomes.

In summary, tumor-derived CD20-positive EVlarge represent a novel mechanism of rituximab resistance in DLBCL through antibody sequestration and impairment of immune-mediated cytotoxicity. Circulating CD20-positive EVlarge provide a biologically meaningful biomarker that predicts clinical outcomes independently of established prognostic factors. These findings support further investigation of EVlarge-guided therapeutic strategies to overcome a tumor-intrinsic antigen-sink–mediated resistance in antibody-based therapies.
