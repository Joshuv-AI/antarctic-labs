 Abstract
 The deployment of artificial intelligence systems in critical applications requires higher levels of
 assurance for safety, security, and interpretability. While neurosymbolic (NESY) approaches
 combining neural networks with symbolic reasoning offer potential advantages for assured AI,
 existing differentiable neurosymbolic frameworks face significant limitations including
 computational overhead and performance constraints. This report investigates the ISED (Infer-
 Sample-Estimate-Descend) framework as an alternative approach that enables neurosymbolic
 learning without requiring end-to-end differentiability. We evaluate ISED’s utility for
 geointelligence applications by comparing neurosymbolic models against standard neural
 networks on aircraft classification tasks using the RarePlanes and MTARSI imagery datasets.
 Our results demonstrate that while ISED-based models achieve slightly lower accuracy (89.7%
 vs 92.1% on RarePlanes; 91.1% vs 92.5% on MTARSI), they provide critical explainability
 capabilities that enable tracing incorrect predictions back to specific attribute misclassifications.
 We also present an automated pipeline that generates both attribute-class mappings and
 neurosymbolic model architectures from natural language descriptions, significantly reducing
 the manual effort required for NESY model deployment. These findings suggest that ISED offers
 a promising direction for developing assured AI systems where interpretability and reasoning
 transparency are prioritized alongside performance.

 1.0 Introduction
 The need to assure the performance and deployment of Artificial Intelligence (AI)enabled
 systems is hitting a peak as many demonstrations of the technology have shown applicability to
 a growing number of tasks in a plethora of domains. Modern methods, especially in Machine
 Learning (ML) model development, focus on advances in deep learning or neural networks.
 Such models create many opportunities but also obfuscate our ability to model behavior for
 testing assurance encompassing safety, security, cross user-performance, and interpretability.
 When deploying AI to critical applications like controlling industrial systems, autonomous
 operation, and other applications with risks for people and their environments, stakeholders are
 rightfully demanding higher levels of assurance. The past decade in particular has outlined
 many risks of lack of cross user-performance [4], lack of robustness to data drift [13], and
 security vulnerabilities [2] for systems using machine learning.

 The concept of assured-by-design methods attempts to help address this by rethinking the
 fundamental building blocks of our AI systems to have these factors core to initial design of
 methods. However, this comes at a time when the basic building blocks in the modern paradigm
 are being scaled and given more autonomy in interacting with the digital and physical world.
 Assured-by-design methods can target representational learning [1], guarantees or certification
 of performance under stress [3]; [9], model architecture changes [5], and systems-of-systems
 enclosing networks [14]. However, despite modern pure artificial neural network-based models
 being able to perform more complex tasks alone, many AI systems continue to have ML models
 as components that feed into logic and other systems-of-systems to assure interpretability,
 performance, and even generalization in such critical applications. Many of these systems
 would be considered neurosymbolic (NESY) AI, a mixture of neural network methods combined
 with classic logic processing and programming. Defining the barriers of when a neural model
 becomes neurosymbolic is a topic of discussion, with some arguing modern Large Language
 Models (LLMs) operating in the discrete token space for inputs and outputs are neurosymbolic
 [10].

 In our FY24 efforts we investigated the potential of neurosymbolic methods to provide greater
 system assurance. Our preprint “On the Promise for Assurance of Differentiable Neurosymbolic
 Reasoning Paradigms,” [8] explores how recent advances in differentiable reasoning allow for
 the incorporation of symbolic programs directly into end-to-end model training. We conducted
 an empirical analysis using the Scallopy neurosymbolic library [7] to determine if these
 differentiable neurosymbolic systems could offer “assurance-by-design.” Our findings were
 mixed. While these methods demonstrated stronger assurance metrics in narrowly-defined
 tasks involving logic operations like counting or arithmetic, they also revealed several critical
 limitations. For more complex tasks, such as categorical concept reasoning, the neurosymbolic
 models exhibited lower performance and assurance than their purely neural counterparts.
 Critically, we observed that some differentiable neurosymbolic models were prone to learning
 shortcuts, leading to an increase in adversarial vulnerability. From a practical standpoint, these
 differentiable models were computationally exhaustive and slow, and with unclear performance
 advantages, its difficult to argue for their adoption given the significant training overhead.

 These challenges spanning performance, assurance vulnerabilities, and practical
 implementation overhead motivated our shift in approach for the work detailed in this report. The
 requirement for program differentiability appeared to be a core constraint. This led us to explore
 nondifferentiable frameworks that could circumvent these issues by design. As the developers
 of Scallopy acknowlege, “differentiable approaches” require programs—the symbolic

 components of a neurosymbolic model—to be differentiable. As such, these programs must be
 specified in languages like Datalog or Prolog, which are less expressive or accessible than
 general-purpose languages like Python [12].

 To overcome this, the Scallopy developers proposed the Infer-Sample-Estimate-Descend
 framework (ISED) [12]. ISED is a neurosymbolic learning approach “based on forward
 evaluation, which is compatible with arbitrary black-box programs that can be written in general-
 purpose languages”. Instead of relying on differentiable logic, ISED uses a sampling-based
 method to estimate gradients and optimize the neural components of the system. This allows for
 the integration of complex, nondifferentiable symbolic modules, opening the door to more
 expressive and potentially more robust reasoning without the constraints and drawbacks we
 identified in our prior research. This report details our investigation using the ISED model to
 address these previously identified gaps.

 1.1 ISED Model Background
 The main contribution of ISED is that it enables competitive neurosymbolic models that do not
 require end-to-end differentiability [11, 12]. Many computational tasks cannot be solved by
 neural perception alone but can be naturally expressed as a composition of a neural component
 followed by a symbolic program. ISED addresses the challenge of learning such “neural
 programs” in an end-to-end manner, where the symbolic component can be either a black-box
 program (such as API calls to large language models) or classically coded logic (such as
 decision trees) written in traditional programming languages.

 Unlike approaches that rely on differentiable logic, ISED uses a reinforcement learning-based
 sampling approach called Infer-Sample-Estimate-Descend to approximate gradients. This
 makes it possible to train the neural component while incorporating complex, nondifferentiable
 symbolic modules that cannot provide explicit gradients.

 To bridge the gap between the neural and symbolic components, ISED introduces structural
 mappings that define how inputs and outputs are translated between neural representations and
 symbolic data structures. The neural model outputs probability distributions over the symbolic
 program’s inputs, from which ISED samples representative symbols. These samples are then
 processed by the symbolic program to produce corresponding outputs, creating symbol-output
 pairs that can be viewed as a symbolic summary of the program’s behavior.

 A concrete example illustrates this approach: in leaf classification, traditional neural methods
 predict plant species directly from images, often resulting in data-inefficient and less
 interpretable solutions [11]. Instead, ISED uses a neural component that predicts leaf
 characteristics (shape, margin, texture) from images, followed by a symbolic component
 implementing a decision tree that classifies species based on these features. The neural
 component learns to produce better feature predictions through probabilistic sampling and
 feedback from the decision tree’s classification performance, even though the decision tree itself
 is not differentiable.

 During training, this sampling-based approach allows ISED to estimate gradients across the
 symbolic summary and propagate loss back through the composite model. This design makes it
 possible to combine expressive symbolic reasoning with neural learning, expanding the
 applicability of neurosymbolic frameworks to arbitrary programs that cannot be differentiated
 directly.

 2.0 Application of ISED to GeoIntelligence Context
 2.1 Datasets
 The original ISED framework was validated on tasks such as handwritten formula evaluation,
 plant disease classification, and scene recognition. While effective, these benchmarks have
 limited relevance to national security applications [11, 12]. To explore ISED’s utility in mission-
 aligned contexts, we adapted the framework to satellite imagery tasks.

 Our experiments compared ISED-based models against conventional neural networks using two
 established datasets: RarePlanes and MTARSI. In both datasets, each image was cropped so
 that it contained a single aircraft instance for classification. These datasets reflect operationally
 realistic challenges in geointelligence, such as detecting and identifying aircraft from satellite
 imagery.

 2.2 NESY Models
 We evaluated two types of models: baseline neural networks and ISED-based neurosymbolic
 models. The baselines consisted of standard deep neural networks trained end-to-end on
 aircraft images to predict class labels, with no symbolic reasoning component.

 In contrast, the ISED approach combines neural and symbolic elements. For each provided
 conceptual attribute — such as wing shape, propulsion type, and engine count — we trained a
 dedicated ResNet18 model. The softmax outputs of these networks represent probability
 distributions over possible attribute values. These distributions were then passed into the
 symbolic component, a decision tree. We refer to this model as the NESY model for the rest of
 this report.

 Rather than using a single deterministic attribute prediction, the NESY model performs
 probabilistic sampling from the softmax outputs. For each training instance, the system draws k
 samples (where k is a hyperparameter), passes them through the decision tree, and obtains
 candidate classifications. To compute the predictions used in the loss function, NESY then
 aggregates the sampled outcomes into a final prediction estimate.

 Figure 1 below shows the architecture of the NESY model used on RarePlanes dataset. There
 are three separate Resnet18 model components– one for each symbolic attribute. The resulting
 outputs are then passed into the ISED symbolic reasoning component to compute final
 predictions. A similar achitecture was used for the MTARSI dataset, containing ten Resnet18
 attribute predictions models to account for 10 symbolic attribute categories.

 A central distinction, therefore, is that while baseline neural networks rely solely on end-to-end
 feature learning, the NESY models leverage both explicit attribute modeling and structured
 reasoning. RarePlanes and MTARSI serve as testbeds to evaluate whether this neurosymbolic
 integration yields advantages for geointelligence tasks requiring not just perceptual accuracy,
 but also reasoning over structured concepts.

 2.3 Automation of Attributes and NESY Models
 The NESY models rely on two components that traditionally require manual effort: (a) a table
 specifying which symbolic attributes correspond to each class in the image dataset (attribute x
 class table), and (b) the combined neural and symbolic model that uses these attributes to make
 predictions. Our ansr python package allows both components to be generated automatically
 from a CSV specification, reducing the need for manual annotation and symbolic model design.
 This automation substantially lowers the barrier for adopting NESY in new application domains.

 1. Automated attribute–class table generation. We developed a Streamlit interface that
 allows users to describe the dataset and its classes in natural language. This description is
 paired with a structured prompt engineering strategy and submitted to a large language model
 (Gemma12B), which generates a candidate attribute–class table. A downstream parser
 validates the output for formatting consistency, ensures full coverage of all dataset classes, and
 converts it into a usable CSV file. Alongside the CSV, the system also creates a configuration
 file that can be directly referenced to initiate model training, enabling seamless integration
 between dataset description and model setup.

 2. Automated neurosymbolic model construction. The second automation capability
 constructs both components of the NESY model directly from the attribute–class CSV. On the
 neural side, the system automatically instantiates a separate ResNet18 subnetwork for each
 attribute defined in the table. On the symbolic side, before the decision tree is constructed, the
 system computes the Gini gain of each attribute and reorders the CSV columns so that the most
 informative features appear first. Once reordered, the CSV is converted into a decision tree that
 serves as the function in the symbolic program. This reordering improves the efficiency and
 discriminative power of the symbolic component.

 This pipeline—automatically generating both the attribute table and the corresponding
 neurosymbolic model—streamlines the process of configuring and training NESY models. By
 coupling dataset description with automated model construction, the ansr package provides a
 foundation for experimentation with neurosymbolic learning in geointelligence tasks.

 2.4.1 Reasoning for Aircraft Classification

 We evaluated NESY models on two datasets, Rareplanes and MTARSI, for categorizing
 aircrafts. Moreover, we compared the performance of NESY models against their counterpart,
 i.e., traditional neural networks. Table 1 presents the accuracy of each model across both
 datasets. As seen from that table, NESY models slightly underperformed compared to baseline
 neural networks (referred to as Baseline); however, NESY models still achieve highly accurate
 performance.

 Despite being slightly less performative, the NESY models provide a critical capability in
 profiling failure scenarios. In other words, we can leverage defined rules inside symbolic
 components of a NESY model to visualize incorrect decision processes. For instance, in Figure
 2, we display the decision tree traversed in the symbolic component of NESY model for a given
 input test sample. The transport node highlighted in red is the true class of the aircraft. For that
 particular sample, the NESY model provided an inaccurate prediction of fighter class, which can
 be traced back to incorrectly identifying the wing shape attribute as variable swept. Therefore,
 with NESY, we are able to explicitly determine how the model arrived at an incorrect prediction,
 providing a level of assurance and explainability lacking in the traditional neural network
 counterparts.

 2.5 Discussion and Conclusion
 This investigation of the ISED framework for geointelligence applications reveals both promising
 capabilities and important tradeoffs for assured AI system development. Our results
 demonstrate that ISED successfully addresses key limitations of differentiable NESY
 approaches while introducing new opportunities for explainable reasoning in critical applications.

Application of ISED to GeoIntelligence Context 7

 The core contribution of this work lies in demonstrating ISED’s practical utility for mission-
 aligned tasks. While NESY models showed modest performance decreases compared to
 traditional neural networks (2-3% accuracy reduction), they provided substantial gains in
 interpretability and reasoning transparency. The ability to trace incorrect predictions back to
 specific attribute misclassifications represents a significant advancement for assured AI
 systems, where understanding failure modes is often more critical than marginal performance
 improvements.

 Our robustness evaluation yielded mixed results across different perturbation types and
 datasets. Relative to standard neural networks, NESY models demonstrated improved
 resilience to adversarial attacks on both RarePlanes and MTARSI, suggesting that explicit
 attribute modeling may provide natural defenses against certain types of attacks. However,
 performance varied for corruption and patch attacks, indicating that robustness benefits are not
 guaranteed across all scenarios. An interesting relationship emerged between model complexity
 and robustness patterns. The transition from 3 symbolic attributes in RarePlanes to 10 attributes
 in MTARSI coincided with reduced robustness in the more complex model, suggesting that
 increased symbolic complexity may introduce new vulnerabilities, though further
 experimentation will be needed to establish this relationship definitively.

 The automated pipeline for generating neurosymbolic models represents an important practical
 contribution. By reducing the manual effort required to design attribute-class mappings and
 construct NESY architectures, our approach significantly lowers the barrier to adoption for
 neurosymbolic methods in operational contexts.

 Looking forward, several areas warrant further investigation. Future work should prioritize
 prompt optimization and systematic evaluation to verify that automatically generated symbolic
 attributes are domain-appropriate and accurate. Bootstrapping this system to a dedicated
 retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) database with mission-specific knowledge would
 substantially improve both trust and accuracy of automated attributes and resulting systems.
 Additionally, the relationship between symbolic program complexity and robustness benefits
 requires deeper analysis, with particular attention to maintaining robustness as models scale to
 include more symbolic attributes. Finally, extending ISED to more complex reasoning scenarios
 beyond classification could further demonstrate its potential for assured AI applications.

 Overall, this work establishes ISED as a viable framework for developing interpretable,
 reasoning-capable AI systems in geointelligence contexts. While performance tradeoffs exist,
 the combination of explainability, selective robustness improvements, and the promise of
 automated deployment capabilities positions ISED as a valuable tool for applications where
 assurance requirements outweigh pure performance optimization.
