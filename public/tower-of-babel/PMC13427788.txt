How I do it: Implant-free transposition technique for microvascular decompression in hemifacial spasm
Authors: Melanie Kristt, Raymond F Sekula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is most often caused by arterial compression of the facial nerve (CNVII). Microvascular decompression (MVD) of CNVII has excellent outcomes, achieving spasm freedom in approximately 90% of patients, and can be accomplished by inter positional or transpositional techniques.MethodWe present a step-by-step description of an experienced surgeon’s technique for MVD in HFS using vessel transposition with TachoSil®, supported by an illustrative video.ConclusionIn our experience, transposition using TachoSil® during MVD for HFS, in appropriately selected patients, is a safe and effective approach offering potential advantages over traditional materials and techniques.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06942-z.Keywords: Hemifacial spasm, Microvascular decompression, Outcomes, TachoSil®, Transposition

Introduction and anatomy

Hemifacial spasm (HFS), involuntary unilateral facial twitching, is usually caused by arterial compression along the centrally myelinated portion of the facial nerve (CNVII) [7, 10]. Microvascular decompression (MVD) is a highly effective HFS treatment, with > 90% success rate [4]. Decompression can be achieved through interposition, placing inert material between the nerve and vessel, or transposition, mobilizing and securing the vessel away from the nerve [7]. Since its inception, MVD has undergone refinements in visualization tools, decompression materials, and closure techniques. Notably, a recent study found that 96.5% of MVDs used nonabsorbable materials [3], underscoring the prevalence of permanent implants. In contrast, we describe one variation of an experienced surgeon’s implant-free transposition technique for MVD for HFS, using a fully bioabsorbable material.

Operative technique

Patient selection

All patients undergo high-resolution MRI to confirm arterial compression of CNVII, usually by the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA), anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA), or vertebral artery (VA) (Fig. 1).

Materials

Small pieces cut from a standard sheet of TachoSil® Fibrin Sealant Patch (Corza Medical), a collagen matrix coated with human fibrinogen and thrombin, are used in all procedures.

Patient position

After induction and intubation, the patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position, with all pressure points padded. The vertex of the head is gently depressed 10° for optimal visualization, and the head is stabilized on a soft rest. The patient is secured with straps and/or tape to prevent sliding while the table position is changed.

Incision

The transverse sinus course is identified by the line connecting the zygoma root and the inion. The posterior margin of the sigmoid sinus is identified by the digastric point [1]. The incision site, about 4 cm in length, is marked inferior to the transverse sinus and medial to the sigmoid sinus (Fig. 2). The hair is clipped, and the area is cleaned with betadine before draping in a sterile manner. An incision is made with a 15-blade scalpel down to the subcutaneous fat, utilizing monopolar cautery to achieve hemostasis. A Weitlaner retractor is introduced, and the fascia and muscles are opened down to the bone.

Patient position and skin incision for microvascular decompression. The patient, with left-sided hemifacial spasm, is placed in the right lateral decubitus position, and the head is flexed and secured. The retromastoid incision is delineated

Craniectomy

A 1.5 × 2 cm burr hole craniectomy is made below the asterion using a high-speed electric drill with a 5 mm coarse diamond drill bit and Kerrison rongeurs to carefully expose the dura. Bone debris is irrigated and removed. An effort is made to avoid the mastoid air cells; if exposed, the air cells are occluded with bone wax. Brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEPs) are continuously monitored. The dura is opened with a 15-blade scalpel and reflected with suture. A cotton patty is placed over the cerebellar surface, and the endoscope, exoscope, or microscope is introduced into the field.

Cranial nerve exposure, decompression, and closure

The arachnoid of the cerebellomedullary cistern is exposed, sectioned, and a copious amount of cerebrospinal fluid is drained to allow for brain relaxation. The arachnoid is opened around the 7th-11th cranial nerves. The offending vessel is identified and separated from CNVII, disturbing CNVII as minimally as possible. If the patient’s vascular anatomy allows, the vessel can be transposed away from CNVII and can be adhered to the petrous dura or another surface, such as the VA, with TachoSil® (Fig. 3). The integrity of the nerve is confirmed by 0.05 mA stimulation of the attached segment of CNVII. Irrigation confirms that the transposed vessel remains secure. A Valsalva maneuver is performed to ensure that there is no occult bleeding. The dura is closed with 4–0 Surgilon™ sutures in a watertight manner, a calcium phosphate cement cranioplasty is achieved [2], and the wound is closed in layers.

Indications

Patients with HFS confirmed by high-resolution MRI may benefit from MVD. If the offending vessel is mobile and anatomy permits adherence to the petrous dura without excessive traction, transposition using TachoSil® can be safe and effective. Additionally, TachoSil® may offer advantages over traditional materials. Unlike polytetrafluoroethylene(PTFE), TachoSil® is bioabsorbable, which can be advantageous in cases of spasm recurrence, as the absence of permanent foreign material may make it easier to assess for recurrent neurovascular conflict on postoperative imaging. PTFE may also be associated with increased scarring around CNVII. Additionally, TachoSil® was originally developed for vascular repair, supporting its safety profile for vessel adherence. These properties position TachoSil® as a practical, versatile material for transposition in MVD.

Limitations

This technique is not universally applicable. Transposition may be unsafe when the offending vessel is constrained by short brainstem perforators, as mobilization risks perforator avulsion. In these cases, interposition may be the more prudent option. As with all procedures, these decisions must be individualized, considering both the surgeon’s familiarity with the technique and the patient’s specific neurovascular anatomy. Additionally, TachoSil® is approximately 10 × more expensive than PTFE, increasing operative costs.

Specific information for the patient

Patients should be counseled that MVD is a well-established operation for HFS and provides a high likelihood (> 90%) of spasm relief [4, 6, 10]. In this variation, the compressing vessel is mobilized away from the facial nerve using a bioabsorbable material rather than a permanent implant. The final decompression strategy is determined intraoperatively based on the patient’s vascular anatomy, and not all patients are suitable candidates for transposition. Risks of the operation, while uncommon, include hearing loss, facial weakness, dizziness, CSF leak, infection, bleeding, lower cranial nerve dysfunction, persistent spasm, or recurrence [7, 10]. Improvement may be immediate, but in some cases relief is delayed. The absence of permanent implant material may also be relevant if re-exploration becomes necessary, as permanent prostheses can contribute to scarring and may complicate assessment of recurrent neurovascular conflict on MRI [5].
