Synthesis, biological evaluation and detailed computational studies of N-sulfonyl indole-based dihydrothiazoles against colorectal carcinoma
Authors: Zahra Batool, Iqra Naseer, Şeyma Ateşoğlu, Furkan Çakır, Fahri Akbaş, Norah A. Albekairi, Halil Şenol, Asif Rasool, Zahid Shafiq, Abdulrahman Alshammari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel series of N-sulfonyl indole–based dihydrothiazole hybrids (5a–5u) was synthesized and comprehensively evaluated as potential anticancer agents against colorectal carcinoma. All compounds were structurally confirmed by NMR and HRMS and screened for cytotoxicity against HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells, with selectivity evaluated using CCD-1076Sk normal fibroblasts. Several derivatives displayed significantly higher potency than sorafenib, with compound 5d emerging as the lead molecule (IC50 = 4.10 µM, SI = 12.0).To elucidate the molecular basis of activity, induced-fit docking and MM-GBSA calculations were performed against four key colorectal cancer-related targets (TNF-α, PI3K p110α, AKT1, and mTOR). Compound 5d exhibited the most favorable multi-target binding profile, supported by highly stable ligand–protein interactions. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the dynamic stability of 5d, particularly with TNF-α and mTOR. In addition, DFT, ESP, and GCR analyses revealed that 5d combines optimal electronic reactivity with stability, while ADME predictions indicated a favorable pharmacokinetic profile. Overall, 5d is identified as a promising multi-target lead for colorectal cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56442-4.

Introduction

As is well known, cancer is a group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells, which can invade surrounding tissues and metastasize to distant organs, leading to significant morbidity and mortality worldwide1–4. According to the most recent estimates, approximately 20 million new cancer cases were diagnosed worldwide in 2024–2025, with cancer-related deaths exceeding 10 million annually5–7. Among all malignancies, colorectal cancer remains one of the most prevalent and lethal types, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer globally and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, accounting for approximately 1.9 million new cases and over 900,000 deaths per year8,9.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) was selected as the target disease in this study due to its high global incidence, significant mortality rate, and increasing prevalence despite advances in prevention and treatment strategies. CRC remains one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies worldwide and is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths, particularly due to late-stage diagnosis, therapeutic resistance, and tumor heterogeneity. In addition, the limited efficacy and adverse side effects associated with currently available chemotherapeutic agents highlight the urgent need for novel anticancer compounds with improved potency and safety profiles. The molecular complexity of CRC and the involvement of multiple signaling pathways make it an appropriate and clinically relevant model for evaluating new small-molecule anticancer agents.

Protein kinases are critical regulators of cellular signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, migration, and survival. Dysregulation of kinase activity through mutation, overexpression, or abnormal activation has been closely associated with the initiation and progression of various cancers. Therefore, kinases have emerged as important molecular targets in anticancer drug discovery, and numerous kinase inhibitors have been successfully developed for cancer therapy. In particular, targeting aberrant kinase-mediated signaling pathways has provided promising therapeutic strategies for overcoming uncontrolled tumor growth and metastasis10.

CRC is a molecularly heterogeneous disease driven by dysregulation of multiple signaling pathways controlling cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, inflammation, and apoptosis. Major pathways implicated in CRC progression include Wnt/β-catenin, RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, VEGFR-mediated angiogenic signaling, TGF-β signaling, and inflammatory networks involving tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). Crosstalk among these pathways promotes tumor growth, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance, underscoring the need for multi-targeted treatment strategies11–17.

Among these pathways, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis is one of the most frequently dysregulated in CRC. Activating mutations in PI3K, encoding the catalytic subunit p110α, lead to constitutive PI3K signaling with enhanced AKT phosphorylation and mTOR activation, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and metabolic reprogramming while inhibiting apoptosis18. Importantly, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) can further amplify PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling through crosstalk with NF-κB–dependent pathways, contributing to tumor progression, therapeutic resistance, and poor clinical outcomes in CRC19–22. Inhibition of PI3K (p110α)/AKT/mTOR signaling suppresses CRC cell growth and survival, while TNF-α inhibition reduces inflammation-driven tumor progression. Combined targeting of these pathways disrupts key oncogenic and microenvironmental mechanisms, offering an effective multi-target therapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer23,24.

Indole is a privileged heterocyclic scaffold in medicinal chemistry, exhibiting diverse biological activities, including anticancer and kinase inhibitory effects25–30. Indole-based compounds are known to modulate key oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and NF-κB, and have shown significant cytotoxic activity against colorectal cancer cells through regulation of cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and inflammation31–36. Sulfonyl and sulfonamide groups are widely used in drug design due to their metabolic stability and strong hydrogen-bonding capacity. N-Sulfonyl indole derivatives enhance kinase binding, improve pharmacokinetic properties, and have been reported to inhibit angiogenesis, suppress TNF-α–mediated inflammation, and interfere with tumor growth and survival pathways31,37–39.

Thiosemicarbazones represent another important class of bioactive molecules with well-documented anticancer properties40–42. Thiosemicarbazone derivatives are also known to inhibit multiple molecular targets, including kinases and transcription factors, and several members of this class have advanced into clinical evaluation as anticancer agents. Importantly, the thiosemicarbazone moiety provides a versatile synthetic handle for further structural diversification and heterocycle formation43–50. Cyclization of thiosemicarbazones into dihydrothiazole rings enhances molecular rigidity, three-dimensional architecture, and target selectivity. Dihydrothiazole-containing compounds exhibit notable anticancer and kinase inhibitory activities, with sulfur and nitrogen heteroatoms enabling strong interactions with cancer-related targets and improved metabolic stability51–59. Based on the molecular hybridization strategy, the rational design of the present study combines multiple biologically privileged motifs, such as indole, sulfonyl, thiosemicarbazone-derived dihydrothiazoles, into a single molecular framework64. This hybrid approach is intended to simultaneously target cancer cell proliferation, survival signaling, and inflammation-associated pathways involved in colorectal carcinoma65. Starting from indole-3-carbaldehyde, N-sulfonylation with pyridin-3-ylsulfonyl chloride introduces a pharmacologically favorable sulfonyl group, followed by condensation with various thiosemicarbazides to generate thiosemicarbazones66.

In particular, pyridine-containing hybrid molecules have attracted considerable attention in anticancer drug discovery due to their favorable electronic properties, hydrogen-bonding capacity, and ability to enhance interactions with biological targets. Moreover, incorporation of the pyridine moiety may improve the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characteristics of anticancer agents, contributing to enhanced biological activity66. Subsequent cyclization with α-bromoketones affords the final N-sulfonyl-indole-based dihydrothiazole derivatives.

This study aimed to design, synthesize, and comprehensively evaluate a novel series of N-sulfonyl-indole based dihydrothiazole derivatives as potential anticancer agents against colorectal carcinoma. The rational design of the target compounds was based on a molecular hybridization strategy integrating biologically privileged scaffolds, including indole, sulfonyl/sulfonamide, and dihydrothiazole moieties, to achieve enhanced anticancer activity. The synthesized compounds were structurally characterized, and their in vitro anticancer potential was evaluated against the human colorectal cancer cell line HCT116, while cytotoxicity and selectivity were further assessed using CCD-1076Sk human dermal fibroblast cells. To elucidate their possible molecular mechanisms of action, molecular docking studies were performed against key CRC-related targets, including PI3K p110α, AKT1, mTOR, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), followed by molecular dynamics simulations to examine the stability of the ligand–target complexes. In addition, in silico ADME predictions were carried out to assess drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties, while density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to explore the electronic properties and structure–reactivity relationships of the synthesized compounds. Overall, this integrated experimental and computational approach aimed to identify promising lead molecules with potential therapeutic relevance for colorectal cancer treatment.

Results and discussions

Synthesis and characterization

In this study, a total of twenty-one N-sulfonyl indole–based dihydrothiazole derivatives (5a–5u) were synthesized through a two-step reaction sequence involving (i) thiosemicarbazone formation and (ii) cyclization with α-bromoketones. The synthetic route is outlined in Scheme 1. The synthesis started from N-(pyridin-3-ylsulfonyl)indole-3-carbaldehyde (1), which contains an aldehyde functionality suitable for Schiff base formation. In the first step, compound 1 was condensed with various thiosemicarbazide derivatives (2a–e) in ethanol under reflux to afford the corresponding thiosemicarbazones (3a–e). This reaction proceeded via nucleophilic attack of the terminal –NH2 group of the thiosemicarbazide on the aldehyde carbonyl group, followed by dehydration to form the characteristic C=N (imine) bond, while retaining the biologically relevant thiocarbonyl (C=S) moiety.Scheme 1Synthesis of indole‐based dihydrothiazoles 5a–u.

Synthesis of indole‐based dihydrothiazoles 5a–u.

In the second step, the thiosemicarbazones (3a–e) were reacted with a series of α-bromoketones (4a–e) in refluxing ethanol to generate the final dihydrothiazole derivatives (5a–5u) through an intramolecular cyclization process. Initially, the sulfur atom of the thiocarbonyl group performs a nucleophilic substitution on the electrophilic carbon of the α-bromoketone, forming an S-alkylated intermediate. Subsequently, intramolecular nucleophilic attack of the adjacent imine nitrogen on the carbonyl carbon leads to ring closure and formation of the five-membered dihydrothiazole ring, accompanied by elimination of bromide. This cyclization step is the key transformation that converts the flexible thiosemicarbazone scaffold into a conformationally constrained and pharmacologically favorable heterocyclic system.

Through variation of the R1 substituent on the thiosemicarbazide and the R2 substituent on the α-bromoketone, a diverse library of N-sulfonyl indole-dihydrothiazole hybrids was obtained. This structural diversity was designed to explore structure–activity relationships and optimize interactions with colorectal cancer–related molecular targets. The structures of all synthesized final compounds (5a–5u) were confirmed by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS analyses. The appearance of characteristic signals corresponding to the dihydrothiazole ring, imine (C=N), sulfonyl (SO2), and aromatic moieties verified the successful formation of the target molecules.

The successful formation of the N-sulfonyl indole–based dihydrothiazole derivatives (5a–5u) was confirmed by detailed analysis of their 1H and 13C NMR spectra, which showed characteristic features consistent with the proposed structures. In the thiosemicarbazone intermediates, the functional motif –NH–C(=S)–NH– gives rise to characteristic signals associated with a thiocarbonyl group43,46,47,67,68. After cyclization with α-bromoketones, this moiety is converted into the heterocyclic dihydrothiazole ring, leading to a pronounced change in both proton and carbon chemical shifts. In all final compounds, the thiocarbonyl carbon, which typically resonates above ~ 175 ppm in open-chain thiosemicarbazones, is shifted to the δ 165–172 ppm region, reflecting its incorporation into the imine-thioamide system of the dihydrothiazole ring. Likewise, the multiple NH signals present in thiosemicarbazones disappear upon ring closure, and a single dihydrothiazole NH proton appears as a sharp singlet in the range of δ 9.5–10.0 ppm, which is diagnostic for the cyclized heterocycle. The C=N proton of the dihydrothiazole ring consistently resonates as a singlet around δ 6.3–6.7 ppm, shifted relative to the azomethine proton of the thiosemicarbazone, further confirming ring formation52,69,70.

All compounds exhibit a complex aromatic pattern between δ 7.0 and 8.9 ppm, corresponding to the indole, pyridine, and phenyl (or benzyl) rings. For benzyl-substituted derivatives, a characteristic –CH2– singlet is observed at δ 4.8–5.2 ppm, while methyl-substituted derivatives display singlets at approximately δ 2.1–2.5 ppm. In addition, the OMe signal in methoxylated derivatives showed at 3.30–3.40 ppm and 53–55 ppm in 1H and 13C NMR, respectively.

In the fluorinated derivatives (5k–5n), the presence and regiochemistry of the fluorine substituent on the aromatic ring were unambiguously confirmed by characteristic 13C–19F coupling patterns observed in the 13C NMR spectra. The carbon directly bonded to fluorine (ipso carbon) appeared as a doublet at approximately δ 161–163 ppm with a large one-bond coupling constant (1JC–F = 240–245 Hz), which is diagnostic of a C–F bond49. The ortho carbons resonated near δ 115–116 ppm and exhibited two-bond couplings (2JC–F = 20–22 Hz), while the meta carbons were observed around δ 128–130 ppm with three-bond couplings (3JC–F = 8–9 Hz). In addition, the para carbon gave a weakly split signal at approximately δ 133–134 ppm with a small four-bond coupling constant (4JC–F = 2–3 Hz)11,71,72. This well-resolved coupling pattern is fully consistent with a fluorine atom attached to an aromatic benzyl or phenyl ring and provides strong spectroscopic evidence for the correct substitution pattern in all four fluorinated derivatives (5k–5n).

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was used to confirm the molecular formulas of the synthesized N-sulfonyl indole–dihydrothiazole derivatives. In all cases, the experimentally observed molecular ion peaks showed excellent agreement with the calculated exact masses, with deviations within 1–3 ppm, confirming the high purity and correct elemental composition of the compounds. For compound 5d (C29H20ClN5O3S2, exact mass = 585.06961), the calculated protonated molecular ion [M + H]+ was 586.07743, while the observed value was 586.07667, corresponding to a deviation of approximately 1.3 ppm. The fragmentation pattern of compound 5d was evaluated using HRMS analysis and included in the revised manuscript. The prominent molecular ion peak observed at m/z 586.0729 corresponds to the protonated molecular ion [M + H]⁺ of compound 5d, which is consistent with its calculated molecular weight (586.0810). In addition, the isotopic ion peaks detected at m/z 587.0666 and 588.0700 are attributable to the natural isotopic distribution arising mainly from chlorine and sulfur atoms present in the structure. The observed fragmentation behavior supports the proposed molecular structure of compound 5d. A proposed scheme of fragmentation is given in Scheme SI-1. Similarly, for compound 5n (C31H24FN5O2S2, exact mass = 581.13555), the calculated [M + H]⁺ ion was 582.14337, and the experimental value was 582.14190, giving a mass deviation of approximately 2.5 ppm. These very small deviations between calculated and observed masses provide strong confirmation of the proposed molecular structures, and similar accuracy was observed for all compounds in the 5a–5u series. Cytotoxicity studies and SAR analysis

The anticancer potential of the newly synthesized N-sulfonyl indole–based dihydrothiazole derivatives (5a–u) was evaluated by in vitro cytotoxicity studies against the human colorectal carcinoma cell line HCT-116. This cell line is widely used as a clinically relevant model of colorectal cancer due to its well-characterized genetic background and dysregulated oncogenic signaling pathways. To assess the selectivity and safety profile of the compounds, parallel cytotoxicity experiments were performed on CCD-1076Sk human dermal fibroblast cells, which serve as a representative non-cancerous cell model. The comparative evaluation of cytotoxic effects on malignant and normal cells allowed the determination of the therapeutic window and selectivity of the synthesized compounds, providing critical insight into their potential as safe and effective anticancer agents. The synthesized N-sulfonyl indole–based dihydrothiazole derivatives exhibited a broad range of cytotoxic activities against the HCT-116 colorectal cancer cell line, with IC50 values ranging from 4.10 to 38.32 µM, demonstrating that structural variations on the R1 and R2 substituents significantly influenced anticancer potency. Several compounds displayed markedly higher activity than the reference drug sorafenib (IC50 = 31.22 µM), highlighting the effectiveness of the designed scaffold.

Among all derivatives, 5d (3-Cl-phenyl, OH) and 5i (4-Cl-phenyl, OH) emerged as the most potent compounds, exhibiting IC50 values of 4.10 µM and 4.55 µM, respectively. Notably, these compounds also showed excellent selectivity toward cancer cells, with SI values of 12.0 and 9.2, which are substantially higher than that of sorafenib (SI = 1.8). This indicates that hydroxyl substitution on the phenyl ring dramatically enhances both anticancer potency and tumor selectivity, likely through improved hydrogen-bonding interactions with intracellular targets.

Compounds bearing a methyl group (5e, 5j, 5s) also demonstrated moderate to good activity (IC50 ≈ 11–15 µM) with acceptable selectivity (SI = 2.6–4.2), whereas derivatives containing nitro substituents (5b, 5g, 5l, 5t, 5u) were generally less active and poorly selective, suggesting that strong electron-withdrawing groups at this position may negatively impact cellular activity.

Interestingly, benzyl-substituted fluorinated derivatives (5k–5n) exhibited weak cytotoxicity (IC50 ≈ 24–38 µM) and low selectivity (SI ≈ 1.0–1.5), indicating that increased molecular flexibility and steric bulk in this region are detrimental to anticancer performance. Similarly, 4-methoxyphenyl derivatives showed moderate activity, with 5r (OH) standing out (IC50 = 8.14 µM, SI = 5.9), further supporting the beneficial role of hydrogen-bond donor substituents on the aromatic ring.

As a result, the cytotoxicity data clearly demonstrate that chloro-phenyl derivatives bearing hydroxyl groups provide the most favorable balance between potency and selectivity. These findings strongly support the rational design strategy and identify 5d as lead compounds for further mechanistic and computational investigations.

Molecular docking studies

To elucidate the molecular basis of the anticancer activity of the synthesized N-sulfonyl indole–dihydrothiazole derivatives, induced-fit docking (IFD) and MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations were performed against four key colorectal cancer–related targets: tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, PDB ID: 7KP9), PI3K p110α (4L23), AKT1 (4EJN), and mTOR (4JT5). IFD was employed to account for protein flexibility and to identify the most favorable ligand–receptor binding conformations, while MM-GBSA calculations were used to quantify the thermodynamic stability of the resulting complexes. Conclusion

In this study, a rationally designed series of N-sulfonyl indole–based dihydrothiazole hybrids (5a–5u) was successfully synthesized and fully characterized, providing a structurally diverse library built on three biologically privileged scaffolds (indole, sulfonyl, and dihydrothiazole). This hybridization strategy proved highly effective, yielding compounds with pronounced and tunable anticancer activity against colorectal cancer.

In vitro cytotoxicity studies against HCT-116 cells demonstrated that several derivatives were significantly more potent than sorafenib, with 5d, 5i, and 5r emerging as the most active and selective compounds. In particular, compound 5d (IC50 = 4.10 µM, SI = 12.0) showed outstanding potency and tumor selectivity, clearly validating the beneficial role of hydroxyl-substituted chloro-phenyl groups in enhancing anticancer performance.

Induced-fit docking and MM-GBSA calculations revealed that 5d acts as a true multi-target inhibitor of TNF-α, PI3K p110α, AKT1, and mTOR, displaying consistently superior binding affinities compared with sorafenib. These results were mechanistically supported by energy decomposition analysis, which showed that the binding of 5d is driven primarily by strong van der Waals and lipophilic interactions with relatively low desolvation penalties, explaining its high thermodynamic stability and broad target coverage. The docking poses and 3D interaction analyses further demonstrated that 5d establishes an optimized interaction network across all four targets, involving persistent hydrogen bonds via its phenolic OH and sulfonyl groups, together with π–π stacking of the indole and phenyl rings.

Molecular dynamics simulations over 250 ns confirmed the high dynamic stability of 5d protein complexes, especially with TNF-α and mTOR, where ligand RMSD values remained low and key hydrogen bonds showed occupancies above 80–95%. This provides strong dynamic evidence that 5d is not only a potent binder but also forms long-lived and stable complexes in a physiological-like environment.

DFT-based electronic structure analysis showed that although 5r is the most electronically reactive, 5d and 5i exhibit a more favorable balance between reactivity and stability. The HOMO–LUMO, ESP, and GCR descriptors consistently indicated that 5d has the most optimized electronic profile, combining high electrophilicity with controlled softness, which supports strong yet selective protein binding. Finally, in silico ADME predictions demonstrated that the series has generally acceptable pharmacokinetic profiles. Despite its relatively high molecular weight and lipophilicity, 5d shows good predicted oral absorption, acceptable membrane permeability, and limited BBB penetration, making it suitable for peripheral colorectal cancer targets and comparable to established kinase inhibitors.

As a result, the integrated experimental and computational results converge on compound 5d as the hit and lead molecule of this study. Its exceptional in vitro potency and selectivity, robust multi-target binding to key CRC signaling proteins, high dynamic stability, favorable electronic properties, and viable ADME profile strongly support 5d as a promising candidate for further preclinical development as a multi-target anticancer agent for colorectal cancer.

Experimental Section

General

All the raw materials were bought from Sigma Aldrich for the preparation of N-sulfonyl-indole based dihydrothiazoles. The reaction’s progress and completion were tracked using silica gel plates. 1H and 13CNMR spectra in DMSO-d6 at 25 °C were obtained using a Bruker Ascend 500 MHz NMR spectrometer (500 MHz for 1H and 125 MHz for 13C). NMR spectra were displayed as chemical shifts (ppm) and coupling constants (J) were represented in Hertz (Hz) to describe signal multiplicity. Using the electrospray ionization (ESI) method on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q ExactiveTM Hybrid Quadrupole-OrbitrapTM device, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) spectra were obtained. In addition, HPLC analyses were performed using a Waters preparative HPLC system equipped with a PDA detector.

Synthesis of target compounds

General Procedure for the synthesis of thiosemicarbazones (3a-e)

To the solution of 1-(pyridin-3-ylsulfonyl)-1H-indole-3-carbaldehyde (0.1 g, 0.35 mmol) in 10 mL ethanol, equimolar quantities of respective thiosemicarbazides (2a-e) (0.35 mmol) are added with a few drops of acetic acid (1–2 mL) as a catalyst. The reaction mixture is subjected to reflux for 4 h. The reaction progress was monitored by TLC using 1:5 petroleum ether/ ethyl acetate as the eluent. Upon completion of the reaction, the precipitates were washed with ethanol and dried to get the targeted thiosemicarbazones (3a-e) in excellent to good yields.

General Procedure for the synthesis of N-sulfonyl-indole based dihydrothiazoles (5a-u)

An equimolar solution of α-bromoketones and respective thiosemicarbazones (3a–e) was made in 10 mL of ethanol. A few drops of acetic acid were added as a catalyst. The reaction mixture was stirred overnight under reflux conditions. Progress and completion of the reaction were tracked using TLC. Upon precipitation, the reaction mixture was cooled, and the precipitate was collected by filtration, washed with ethanol, and air-dried to obtain the final product (5a-u).

Cytotoxicity studies

Cell culture

HCT-116 (ATCC® CCL-247™) and CCD1076Sk (ATCC® CRL-2091™) cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 and DMEM media, respectively, both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (P/S). Cells were maintained in 25 cm2 or 75 cm2 culture flasks at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination78–80.

MTT assay

HCT-116 (ATCC® CCL-247™) and CCD1076sk (ATCC® CRL-2091™) cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells/well in 200 μL of complete medium in 96-well plates. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with 100 μL fresh medium and 100 μL of test compounds (200–3.125 μM). Cells were incubated for 48 h, followed by addition of 10 μL MTT (5 mg/mL) and 4 h incubation at 37 °C. Formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL DMSO, and absorbance was read at 570 nm. Experiments were performed in triplicate in three independent sets. Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to untreated controls, and dose–response curves were plotted; results are presented as mean ± SD of at least three replicates81,82.

Computational studies

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using Schrödinger Molecular Modeling Software (2024–1) with the Maestro interface (v13.9) and Desmond (D. E. Shaw Research). Protein and ligand preparations were carried out as described in our previously published studies.

Preparation of ligands and proteins

The 3D crystallographic structures of AKT1 (PDB ID: 4EJN), mTOR kinase domain (PDB ID: 4JT5), PI3Kα catalytic subunit (PDB ID: 4L23), and TNF-α (PDB ID: 7KP9) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and prepared using Schrödinger’s Protein Preparation Wizard to ensure correct protonation states, bond orders, and structural integrity. Receptor grids were generated for docking with an inner box size of 10 Å and an outer box size of 25 Å, centered on the binding site defined by the co-crystallized ligands. Ligands were initially drawn in ChemDraw, converted to 3D structures, and imported into Schrödinger for geometry optimization and energy minimization using the OPLS4e force field83.

Glide docking, induced fit docking and MMGBSA calculations

Docking studies were performed using Glide XP and Induced Fit Docking (IFD) to account for both ligand and receptor flexibility. In the IFD workflow, binding site residues were allowed limited side-chain movements to accommodate ligand-induced conformational changes. For each ligand, 20 distinct poses were generated and ranked based on IFD docking scores, which consider Glide scoring functions and protein–ligand strain energy. The pose with the highest score, representing the most favorable binding orientation, was selected for further analysis. Protein–ligand interactions and binding affinities were further evaluated using Prime MM-GBSA with the VSGB solvation model and OPLS4e force field83.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using Desmond (Schrödinger Release 2024–3) for 250 ns to assess the stability of the protein–ligand complexes. Each complex was placed in an orthorhombic simulation box with a 10 Å buffer and solvated using the TIP4P water model, with counter ions added for neutralization and 0.15 M NaCl to mimic physiological conditions. The OPLS4 force field was applied to all interactions. Following energy minimization, the system was equilibrated under stepwise NVT and NPT ensembles. Production runs were performed under NPT conditions at 300 K and 1.01325 bar using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat and Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat. Structural stability and flexibility of the complexes were monitored throughout the simulation using RMSD and RMSF analyses12.

DFT calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the electronic structure and reactivity of selected compounds using the Jaguar module of Schrödinger Maestro (v14.3). Geometry optimizations and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analyses were carried out with the B3LYP functional and 6-31G basis set without symmetry constraints. Electronic and molecular descriptors, including electrostatic potential (ESP), atomic electrostatic potential (AEP), non-covalent interactions (NCI), electron and spin densities, and molecular orbital parameters, were evaluated. All optimized structures were confirmed as true minima by the absence of imaginary frequencies. The obtained electronic parameters were used to assess the compounds’ reactivity and interaction potential84.

ADME predictions

The pharmacokinetic properties of the compounds were predicted using QikProp (Schrödinger, 2025–1) to estimate key ADME parameters and evaluate their drug-likeness and bioavailability. Compounds satisfying at least 95% of QikProp’s recommended drug-likeness criteria were considered favorable for further pharmacological evaluation71.

Limitations

Although the synthesized compounds demonstrated promising in vitro anticancer activity and favorable computational results, the present study is limited by the absence of in vivo, mechanistic, and experimental pharmacokinetic investigations. Therefore, further preclinical studies are required to fully validate the therapeutic potential and safety profile of the lead compound 5d.
