Double disproportionality in industrial siting in Canada
Authors: Rivkah Gardner-Frolick, Shuoqi Ren, David R. Boyd, Amanda Giang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOur study identifies industrial subsectors in Canada where a few facilities are responsible for the majority of PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursor emissions and investigates whether these facilities are disproportionately sited near structurally marginalized socio-demographic groups, indicating the existence of “double disproportionalities.” We find that disproportionality in emissions exists across pollutants and subsectors. Patterns of disproportionate facility siting differ between urban and rural settings. Facility siting from “Oil and Gas Extraction” disproportionately affects more marginalized groups in urban areas, while “mining and quarrying” affects more groups in rural areas. Urban Indigenous populations are highly affected by disproportionate siting patterns for both all emitters and high emitters. Different patterns emerge when considering clusters of industrial facilities: Low-income and Black populations are more likely to reside in proximity to multiple emitting facilities. Our results support the existence of double disproportionalities for some sectors and highlight the need for research on cumulative environmental hazards.

Introduction

Decades of research on environmental justice (EJ) have shown that industrial facilities are often disproportionately located near marginalized communities with high proportions of racialized, Indigenous, low-income, and immigrant groups.1,2,3 In fact, the United States (US) EJ movement has its roots in studying historical disproportionate siting patterns of industrial and waste facilities near Black communities.4 Living near industrial facilities can pose many risks to human health and well being.5 While the most thoroughly studied risks of living near industry involve air, water, and soil pollution, the environmental, health, and policy communities are paying greater attention to other hazards and their cumulative impacts, such as noise, odor, and heavy-duty traffic.6 In Canada, research has highlighted the impacts of extractive industries on Indigenous cultural heritage and subsistence food sources.7 Industry can also benefit proximate communities, usually through potential economic benefits like jobs, infrastructure improvements, and investment.8,9

Although industry can benefit or harm neighboring communities in many ways, air pollution is still a paramount issue because of the magnitude of health risks. Industrial facilities often emit criteria air pollutants, such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and sulfur oxides (SOx), or their precursors like volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that may form ozone (O3). Criteria pollutants and their precursors have well-documented health effects such as asthma, cardiovascular disease, and pre-term births.10,11,12 Facilities also frequently emit other hazardous air pollutants, including benzene and toluene, which have equally serious health effects, such as an increased risk of cancer. Many toxic air pollutants have not been studied extensively, and effects due to exposure may be unknown.10 Importantly, most facilities emit a complex mixture of pollutants, but there is little research on the health effects of pollutant mixtures.10 Patterns of proximity of marginalized communities in the US to industrial areas with one or more facilities continue to be documented today.13,14 Indeed, the disproportionate exposure of marginalized groups to industrial hazards is a growing field of EJ research across the world.15,16

Previous research into air-polluting sectors in the US has shown that within a given sector, a small number of polluting facilities or vehicles emit disproportionately large quantities of air pollutants, relative to others with similar characteristics. This disproportionality in emissions has been observed across many non-industrial categories, including both passenger and heavy-duty vehicles fleets.17,18 The same pattern has been noted in industrial emissions of greenhouse gases including carbon dioxide and methane,19,20,21,22 as well as for health-harming air pollutants.23,24,25

The worst industrial polluters are often disproportionately located near marginalized communities, which is due to many converging social forces, contributing to environmental injustice.26,27,28,29,30 This intersection has been coined “double disproportionality.”27 Double disproportionalities are especially harmful when considering social determinants of health, healthy housing, access to healthy food, and that marginalized communities often lack access to adequate health care.28,31,32 In the worst cases, marginalized communities near heavy industrial activity may be deemed “sacrifice zones,” areas where highly polluting facilities are clustered near structurally vulnerable communities, a phenomenon that exists around the world.33,34

Historical patterns of facility siting and population settlement are often complex, and it can be difficult to disentangle the driving forces that lead to marginalized communities living in proximity to industries.35,36 In many cases, we lack information on whether populations moved to be near industrial areas, potentially due to factors such as jobs or housing costs, or whether an industrial facility was deliberately located near an area with high proportions of marginalized residents, possibly due to power differences or expectations of limited community opposition.35,37,38

Many countries outside of the US, including Canada, do not have as extensive a body of research on EJ. In Canada, research to connect patterns of industrialization to patterns of socioeconomic vulnerability is ongoing.39,40,41 Previous studies on air pollution and EJ in Canada have indicated that Canadian EJ concerns may have different dimensions compared with American EJ, where race has been shown to be the major predictor of relative air pollution exposure.42,43,44 In Canada, there is not yet enough research to draw broad conclusions at the national scale, though studies have investigated patterns in different cities and regions. Income, Indigeneity, and immigration status may be additional important factors.45,46,47 Because air quality is a significant contributor to Canada’s burden of disease and because industrial pollution may severely impact marginalized communities, more research into industrial air pollution and EJ is necessary.39,41,48,49,50

Additionally, past Canadian EJ, public health, and environmental sociology research has suggested that urban-rural stratification is a critical consideration due to the country’s large land area with concentrated population centers (∼80% urban population). This research has pointed to urban-rural differences in exposure to levels and types of environmental hazards, health outcomes, demographic patterns, and migratory flows, as well as environmental attitudes.44,51,52,53,54,55

Although, at the national scale, rural areas may have lower levels of some air pollutants, such as fine particulate matter,56 those of some others (e.g., certain pesticides) have been found to be higher in rural regions.57 Rural populations in Canada also generally experience higher morbidity and mortality rates, which may reflect a combination of environmental and non-environmental determinants (e.g., occupational activity).58 These exposure and health outcome differences intersect with distinct demographic profiles: urban Canada has substantially higher proportions of racialized minorities and recent immigrants, while Indigenous peoples are more evenly distributed between urban and rural/remote contexts, which also has implications for governance. In sum, past research suggests that in the Canadian context, stratifying analysis along the urban and rural lines may be important for interpreting distributions of environmental risk and informing action.

In this paper, we aim to answer three key questions:1.Does disproportionality exist in the distribution of industrial emissions of PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursors in Canada?2.Are disproportionate emitters of these pollutants located in or near marginalized Canadian communities, and do the results differ between urban and rural areas?3.Are clusters of industrial facilities disproportionately sited near marginalized groups?

Results

Emission disproportionality within subsectors

We expected different subsectors to have different levels of emissions of PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursors, as well as different emissions per number of facilities and emissions per dollars of contribution to GDP because of the different industrial processes that are included within subsectors. Through an additive ranking system, we identified a total of 9 subsectors as both emitting a large amount of PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursors, as well as having highly disproportionate emissions of one or more pollutants relative to the number of facilities and contribution to GDP—“primary metal manufacturing,” “non-metallic mineral product manufacturing,” “chemical manufacturing,” “petroleum and coal product manufacturing,” “paper manufacturing,” “wood product manufacturing,” “utilities,” “mining and quarrying,” and “Oil and Gas Extraction” (see Section S4 for descriptions of industries). Although we used a sectoral-based approach, there is strong overlap between the highest-emitting industrial facilities, regardless of sector, and those targeted through this method: for the top 10 highest-emitting facilities for each of PM2.5, NOx, SO2, ammonia (NH3), and VOCs regardless of subsector, all but 2 facilities fell within one of our selected subsectors. These two facilities both ranked in the top 10 of VOC emissions for 2019 in the subsectors “beverage and tobacco product manufacturing” and “warehousing and storage.” Further, while these subsectors were chosen for their high levels of emissions of PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursors and disproportionality within those emissions, these subsectors may present different additional risks. For example, the accumulation of persistent contaminants in soil from smelters within the “primary metal manufacturing” subsector, which occurred in the community of Trail, British Columbia, due to a local smelter.59 Other concerns may include noise pollution for communities near facilities from the “mining and quarrying” subsector.60

All subsectors display disproportionality in facility-level emissions. Gini coefficients for all pollutants of each subsector selected are higher than 0.6, one indication that emissions are disproportionately distributed (Table 1). The highest Gini coefficients, such as 0.99 for NH3 emissions from “wood product manufacturing,” are usually due to very few facilities emitting that pollutant. For example, only 4 out of 254 facilities in “wood product manufacturing” emit NH3. However, there are some high Gini values, such as 0.98 for SO2 from “Oil and Gas Extraction,” where significantly more facilities emit that pollutant (701 out of 2889 facilities for “Oil and Gas Extraction”). SO2, found in crude oil and natural gas, is emitted by facilities in the “Oil and Gas Extraction” subsector through incomplete flaring or leaks, and a high Gini coefficient may indicate significant differences in facility size or process efficiency. Within these subsectors, we found that while high emitters (see method details) consist of less than 25% of facilities (the percentage varies between subsectors), they consistently emit more than 50% of subsector emissions by weight. This ratio varies from 17% of facilities representing 54% of subsector emissions (“wood product manufacturing”) to 12% of facilities representing 92% of subsector emissions (“utilities”). The observed disproportionality in PM2.5 and precursor emissions within subsectors indicates that high emitters contribute the majority of subsector emissions, which can be especially significant for high-emitting subsectors.

When separating facilities into urban and rural areas, we see that the unequal distribution of emissions within a given subsector persists across the urban-rural divide, suggesting that disproportionate emissions can occur anywhere; however, we also see some differences in spatial distribution across subsectors. Geographically, both high-emitting and low-emitting facilities from the “Oil and Gas Extraction” subsector are overwhelmingly located in Alberta, indicating that for some subsectors, distributions are uneven both across facilities and across space. Across subsectors, with the exception of both “mining and quarrying” and “utilities,” facilities tend to be located in the southern portion of Canada where the majority of people live. All selected subsectors except for “Oil and Gas Extraction” and “mining and quarrying” show clusters of facility locations in the Toronto-Montreal corridor, including both low and high emitters.

Subsector siting patterns

For simplicity and in recognition of the cumulative effects of the studied pollutants, as well as other probable air emissions from these industries, we consider disproportionate siting from the subsectors for all subsector facilities (i.e., instead of only facilities that emit VOCs within a subsector, we consider facilities that emit any amount of PM2.5 or PM2.5 precursors). In Sections S5 and S6, we discuss sensitivity analysis with alternative buffer sizes and using the buffer touching method instead of the buffer containment method for population; alternative approaches yielded similar patterns overall, with some differences for specific sector-population links. Tabulated results from this section can be found in Section S7.

Urban vs. rural

The differences in disproportionate proximity to industrial facilities between rural and urban areas across sociodemographic groups highlight the importance of stratifying analyses in EJ studies. This is due not only to the differences in population patterns between urban and rural areas but also the emissions patterns. Among the subsectors and pollutants that we consider, 29% of the total mass of emissions occurs in urban areas, while 71% of emissions occur in rural areas. Out of the 15 disaggregated marginalized sociodemographic groups we focus on (i.e., disaggregated visible minority groups in addition to recent immigrant, Indigenous, and low-income groups), 5 groups have a higher number of disproportionate siting links (i.e., an instance where the subsector’s siting disproportionately affects that group) for all emitters in rural areas than in urban areas (Figures 1 and 2). The aggregate “visible minority” category had slightly more disproportionate siting links in rural areas (7 in rural and 6 in urban). Disproportionate siting links for White populations were predominantly in rural areas (8 in rural and 3 in urban); many rural areas of Canada predominantly have White populations. Conversely, 7 groups have a higher number of disproportionate siting links in urban areas. Latin American people experience the greatest difference in disproportionate siting links between urban areas and rural areas (0 in rural and 6 in urban), likely due to the much higher number of Latin American people in urban areas (98%). For high emitters, we similarly see differences in the number of disproportionate siting links in urban vs. rural settings. Most marginalized groups (9 out of 15) had a higher number of disproportionate siting links in rural areas. However, one group that has a significantly higher number of links in urban areas is Indigenous peoples, who have 8 links in urban areas and 0 in rural areas. As this analysis is focused on patterns at the national level, the aggregate metrics may mask localized impacts for Indigenous communities in specific rural communities that have been well documented. From an industrial subsector perspective, urban-rural stratification is less useful, though there are still some differences across the two categories. All subsectors are disproportionately sited near at least three marginalized demographic groups when considering all emitters and all pollutants in both urban and rural areas. For all emitters, facilities from “petroleum and coal product manufacturing” are disproportionately sited near the highest number of marginalized groups in both urban (12 marginalized groups) and rural areas (10 marginalized groups), suggesting that this subsector is a good target for further investigation for both air pollution and broader influences on community wellbeing. Nonetheless, a few sectors have significantly different disproportionate siting patterns between urban and rural settings. All emitters for “mining and quarrying” are disproportionately sited near 3 marginalized groups in urban areas yet are disproportionately sited near 9 in rural areas. The converse is true for all emitters in “utilities,” with 10 disproportionate siting links in urban areas and only 4 in rural areas. For high emitters, “paper manufacturing” disproportionately exposes 4 more groups in rural areas than in urban areas.

The overall similarities (with a few key differences) are likely a result of the relatively broad industrial areas within many of the selected subsectors. Some facilities and their processes may necessitate siting in rural areas across Canada regardless of proximity to population centers, notably many in “mining and quarrying,” where we find that siting practices disproportionately affect more marginalized groups. Siting facilities in urban areas are often done to provide easy access to infrastructure and markets, and this seems to be the case in which subsectors most affect urban populations. For example, ready-mix concrete facilities in the “non-metallic mineral product manufacturing” subsector are most usefully located near the end user of the concrete, and, indeed, we see that the majority of facilities in this subsector are located in Southern Canada near population centers. It is especially critical to evaluate facility siting and emissions from facilities with processes or products that necessitate urban locations, as they add environmental hazards to the already complex urban environments that pose risks to human health and well being.

All-emitters vs. high emitters

In urban areas, disproportionate siting of the highest-emitting facilities tends to affect fewer groups than all-emitting facilities (Figure 1). There are, however, exceptions. For instance, urban Indigenous populations disproportionately reside in proximity to high emitters for “mining and quarrying,” “paper manufacturing,” “chemical manufacturing,” and “primary metal manufacturing,” even though this pattern is not found when considering all facilities in the subsector. While industrial activities such as urban mining and quarrying may seem counterintuitive, the disproportionate siting patterns we see in urban areas is, in part, due to the low-density requirements to be defined as an urban area, as mining and quarrying activity does occur near small towns. Other urban groups that have fewer disproportionate siting links for high emitters than for all-emitters still disproportionately reside in proximity to high emitters from multiple subsectors (i.e., many still reside in areas that are close to high emitters from 2 or more subsectors). “Mining and quarrying” is a notable subsector, as with only two high-emitting facilities, they are disproportionately located near the two urban marginalized groups (Indigenous and Filipino) that are also heavily affected by high emitters from other subsectors. High emitters from “non-metallic mineral product manufacturing” are disproportionately located near 7 structurally marginalized groups, more than any other subsector.

We find different patterns when considering the difference between disproportionate siting for all rural facilities vs. high-emitting rural facilities (Figure 3). In rural areas, each sociodemographic group had a lower total number of disproportionate siting links for high emitters, except for White residents. White populations in rural areas disproportionately reside in proximity to emitters from 8 of the 9 selected subsectors for all emitters, a pattern that persists for high emitters of those subsectors. White populations also disproportionately reside in proximity to high emitters from the “utilities” sector. These superficially surprising findings are likely due to the large White population in rural Canada. Conversely, disproportionate siting links for all marginalized groups decrease for high emitters compared with all-emitters. Still, all marginalized groups disproportionately reside in proximity to rural high-emitting facilities from one or more subsectors, except Indigenous and Latin American populations. For all emitters, “petroleum and coal product manufacturing” and “primary metal manufacturing” are disproportionately sited near the most groups (10 out of the 15 marginalized groups), while for high emitters, those from “paper manufacturing” are disproportionately sited near the most marginalized groups (6 out of 15 groups). “Petroleum and coal product manufacturing” also shows the largest difference in disproportionate siting near marginalized groups between all rural emitters (10 out of 15 marginalized groups) and rural high emitters (0 groups). Clustered siting

In our analysis of clusters of industrial facilities, we focus on urban areas due to our buffer size and the geographic size of census dissemination areas (DAs). However, clusters of facilities can also have important air quality impacts in rural areas due to pollutant transport (Figure S12, map of area of clusters of rural facilities). We focus on a cluster analysis with all identified subsectors because siting of industrial facilities near each other is not necessarily sector specific, and industrial pollution impacts are cumulative across all industrial facilities. From our cluster analysis, we find that the majority of people (25.6 million out of 29.6 million, or 86% of urban residents) live outside buffer zones and have a National Pollutant Release Inventory (NPRI) influence score of zero, as most Canadians do not live within 1.5 km of an industrial facility (Table 2). Clusters tend to include multiple selected subsectors. For example, a cluster in Calgary, AB, has 9 facilities from “primary metal manufacturing,” “chemical manufacturing,” “non-metallic mineral product manufacturing,” and “petroleum and coal product manufacturing” within a ∼2 km radius. Two examples of clusters that have areas of 8 overlapping buffers are shown in Figures S10 and S11. Our two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicates that the distributions of NPRI influence scores are statistically different from the distribution of the total population for most marginalized groups, with p values well below 0.05 (Figure 1). The only exceptions are Korean (p = 0.21) and Japanese (p = 0.69) residents. When we compare the portions of marginalized groups who live in areas of higher NPRI influence scores and the mean NPRI influence scores across groups, similar marginalized groups stand out. Low-income, Black, and “visible minorities not included elsewhere” (NIE) groups all have high proportions of people living in areas of high NPRI influence scores. They also have high mean NPRI influence scores (0.192, 0.200, and 0.205 respectively) when compared with the mean NPRI influence score for the total population (0.155) and for White people We find clear differences between who is affected by clusters of industrial facilities, with urban White populations being relatively less likely to live in high-NPRI influence score areas, while many visible minority groups are relatively more likely to live in such areas. These findings, in combination with our findings that urban White populations are affected by disproportionate siting of high emitters for all but two selected subsectors, indicate that they may be significantly affected by living near a high-emitting facility but not necessarily near clusters of facilities. The high proportion of low-income people living in high-NPRI influence score areas indicates that industrial clustering is not just an issue for visible minorities. Metrics for socio-economic class may be important in an urban Canadian context. For example, we estimate that 1,016 low-income people live in areas that have an NPRI influence score of 6, while the total number of non-White people who live in the same areas is 797, indicating that at least 20% of those low-income people are White. These results are also in line with those of other research on industrial facility clustering in the US.62

The cluster analysis highlights the need to consider the siting of multiple facilities across multiple industries in small geographic areas, especially when those areas may be near communities. In Canada, air pollution regulation seldom takes a cumulative effects approach, which would consider the impacts of multiple facilities on air quality in processes like policy-making, environmental impact assessment, and site-level facility permitting.63 However, industrial clustering is a well-known phenomenon that occurs in Canada for a variety of reasons. Information-based industries have been found to cluster in larger urban areas of Canada, while resource and manufacturing-based industries tend to cluster in smaller urban centers, with industries showing higher rates of growth in clusters than in a non-clustered setting.64 Moreover, the spatial dynamics of industrial facilities has been shown to be relatively consistent in the latter part of the 20th century, with industrial movement largely occurring within spatial classes (i.e., peripheral urban areas), not between spatial classes.65 Consideration of industrial clustering is especially important in urban areas because industrial contributions to air pollution can be compounded by other significant sources such as vehicle emissions. This gap has led to ambient air quality that does not achieve standards, even though individual facilities appear to be in compliance with regulations and permits.66

Demographics relating to facility siting patterns

For both all-emitters and high emitters, we find potentially counterintuitive results for rural Indigenous and urban and rural White groups. In rural areas in this national-scale analysis, Indigenous populations do not disproportionately reside near all facilities or the highest-emitting facilities for most subsectors. This aggregate finding does not negate the many studies that have shown Indigenous populations are negatively affected by industry in specific rural areas of Canada.7,67 We believe this may be the result of the spatial unit used in this study, as we consider people within a 1.5 km buffer to be “affected” and that the influence of industrial facilities can stretch beyond this distance. For example, proximity to oil sands is not captured in this study, as in situ extraction facilities and central processing facilities owned by the same company are considered contiguous for the purposes of NPRI reporting. The NPRI also exempts some industrial activities that have been shown to affect rural Indigenous communities, such as oil and gas exploration; so, these facilities are not included in this analysis.67,68 For urban and rural White populations disproportionately located near industrial emitters, our results suggest that socio-economic class (in this study assessed using the low-income metric) is likely an intersecting factor.

There are also many other elements of marginalization and vulnerability that were outside the scope of this study, such as education level and home ownership, that may overlap significantly with rural White populations. Additionally, the patterns we find in rural areas may be partially attributable to the overwhelming White population in those areas (87% according to our rural classification vs. 70% in urban areas). When comparing urban and rural White populations with aggregate urban and rural “visible minority” populations, we see the most significant differences in proximity to high emitters, rather than all-emitters. The visible minority groups that are most affected by disproportionate siting patterns for all-emitters and high emitters in both urban and rural areas differ greatly when the “visible minority” category is disaggregated. While some results are unexpected, we believe they can be contextualized within the reporting guidelines of the NPRI, population patterns, and the scope of this study.

Canada’s unique population patterns have implications for EJ assessment, as recent immigration is a significant driver of population patterns for many visible minority groups. Between the late 1980s and the late 2000s, low-income populations in Canada’s largest cities have become more concentrated in pockets distributed throughout the cities, changing spatial patterns within cities, while the greatest increase in total poverty levels has occurred in suburban areas.69,70 Canada has experienced a few distinct waves of immigration, with one of the most recent beginning in the 1950s, with an influx of European immigrants that slowly shifted to consist of more Asian and African immigrants.71 In Toronto, the city that received the most immigrants in 2006, two distinct socioeconomic classes of immigrants settled, with low-income immigrants and refugees mainly settling into suburban areas due to job availability and higher-income immigrants settling into the gentrifying urban core.71 Patterns of visible minority settlement, which includes many immigrants as well as communities that have been in Canada for generations, have shown greater integration over time, and many communities are mostly in the urban core rather than the urban fringe of three of Canada’s largest cities (Vancouver, Toronto, and Montreal), though spatial segregation varies greatly between the cities.72 Increasing populations of low-income, visible minority, and recent immigrants in suburban areas of Canada, which are classified as urban instead of rural areas in Canada, contribute to their exposure to industrial activity as both the patterns of demographics and industrial siting in smaller cities outside the major urban cores overlap.

Discussion

EJ is historically understudied in Canada, and there have been no previous nationwide studies on disproportionality in industrial air pollution and EJ. As a first cut, we examined disproportionate emissions within selected industrial subsectors and connected that to the disproportionate siting near marginalized sociodemographic groups. We further investigated the clustering of industrial facilities in urban areas and the location of multiple facilities near marginalized groups. From our analyses, we found that patterns of disproportionality in emissions exist across industrial subsectors and pollutants, as expected from previous US research on disproportionality in many emissions categories. Dividing our analysis of disproportionate siting into urban and rural settings showed clear differences in which groups are most affected by both all-emitters and high emitters. For example, Latin Americans disproportionately reside near facilities from 6 of the subsectors in urban areas, while they disproportionately reside near 0 in rural areas. The urban-rural separation was less important from an industrial perspective, as differences in the number of groups affected tend to be less dramatic and because high emitters persist across the divide. Among industrial subsectors, “coal and petroleum product manufacturing” stands out as being disproportionately located near marginalized communities in both urban and rural areas. We found that high emitters are disproportionately sited near marginalized groups, with the most significant effect in urban areas in terms of the diversity of groups affected by disproportionate siting. Urban Indigenous populations are particularly affected by disproportionate siting of high emitters, as they are affected by high emitters from 8 out of the 9 selected subsectors. In rural areas, the predominantly White populations disproportionately reside in proximity to high emitters from all selected subsectors. We find that clusters of facilities in urban areas affect low-income, Black, and “visible minority NIE” people the most, with a larger share of those populations living in areas with high NPRI influence scores. Our results support the existence of double disproportionality from industrial air pollution in Canada—disproportionality that exists in both emissions and in who is most affected by high emitters.

Our findings highlight industrial sectors that should be more closely monitored for potential policy and regulatory actions to curtail high emitters or inequitable siting practices, as well as providing more evidence for which marginalized groups to focus on in future studies. Policies that target unpermitted releases (e.g., focused enforcement activities) or facilities that emit more than others with similar characteristics (e.g., technology or performance standards in permitting), whether as a result of inefficiency or outdated technology, may be especially effective at reducing inequity in air pollution exposure in areas where double disproportionalities exist.73 As these approaches are targeted at specific, high-impact facilities, rather than entire sectors, they may be an economically efficient way to reduce the worst environmental inequities. We acknowledge that while this study was conducted at the national scale, provinces and municipalities have jurisdiction over most facility-level releases in Canada, which highlights the multi-level governance challenges of regulating industrial pollution.74 In Canada, these challenges could be addressed by the Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment through the Air Quality Management System, which is a joint initiative of the federal, provincial, and territorial governments. Policy and regulatory frameworks need to be strengthened at provincial and municipal levels to support the previously identified actions. For instance, cumulative effects frameworks for air approvals (e.g., in the province of Ontario) could enable more responsiveness to existing industrial pollution burdens.63,75 However, federal leadership, using national-scale evidence, could identify good practices and provide guidance for approaches that explicitly take into account the marginalized and vulnerable communities and cumulative burdens through the process of implementing recent federal legislation around the right to a healthy environment and EJ.76 Finally, we want to emphasize that our national-scale results do not contradict specific place-based findings, which include a fuller local context. However, the methods illustrated here may also be applied at a provincial or regional scale to support more context-specific action on local environmental inequities.

While this work uses proximity for our definition of who is affected by industry, the inequities we find in siting are also related to a wide range of detrimental effects that stem from living near industrial facilities. Similar types of proximity analyses can be repeated for other industrial air emissions, whereas it is more difficult to quickly and accurately model concentrations and exposure, such as for air toxics. Proximity analysis is a useful tool for policy-makers to screen for communities that need more attention and resources. Additionally, industrial siting patterns are more fixed than other sources of pollution due to industrial zoning and large capital investments in facilities. As previously mentioned, though industrial hazards are wide ranging, we focus on industrial sectors and facilities that emit particulate matter and particulate matter precursors. A similar evaluation process can easily be applied to more pollutants in future studies, for example, an investigation into industrial emissions of air toxics. Identifying patterns of disproportionality in emissions and siting can help to inform targeted policy interventions to reduce disparities in exposure to both air pollution and wider-ranging detrimental impacts of living near an industrial facility. Future research on industrial emissions of PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursors, as well as other air pollutants, should investigate policy options to reduce double disproportionalities. This can be an effective and efficient way to reduce inequities in exposure to air pollution and achieve EJ. Some industrial “super emitters” in the US have been shown to emit significant amounts of pollution due to abnormal processes,20 which also may be the case for some Canadian industrial high emitters and could be a particularly advantageous area to target. The Canadian EJ field, especially related to air quality, would still benefit from additional research into which groups are most affected by air pollution, where those emissions come from, and most importantly, which policy interventions would be most effective in reducing the inequities that we see across Canada.

Limitations of the study

This study focuses on the presence of industrial facilities as a proxy for environmental risks and community exposure. While a model that includes chemistry and transport with high spatial resolution can give a more accurate estimate of exposure to air pollution from multiple industrial sources, the computational intensity and necessary expertise of that approach may be less accessible to many communities and practitioners. Additionally, industrial facilities are associated with other risks, in addition to air pollution, which may be captured by a buffer analysis approach. Other environmental pollution considerations include discharge of pollution into water and soil, particularly from industries such as “paper manufacturing” that rely on water-based processes. However, these additional considerations make buffer size for assessing facility impacts challenging. Even for air pollution, stack height varies, and, thus, the impact radius can also vary, especially when also considering chemistry and transport. However, previous studies on housing prices, a potential indicator for overall degradation of environmental quality, have found that industrial facilities severely negatively affect housing prices up to about 1 km away.77,78 An additional limitation is the narrow temporal focus (i.e., only using emissions from 2019), as there are changes in emissions year to year. Information on trends in emissions for the selected subsectors and pollutants over time can be found in Section S1.

Method details

We assess disproportionality in both PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursor emissions (SOx, NOx, ammonia (NH3), VOCs) from Canadian industry, as well as disproportionality in the impacts of Canadian industry on different sociodemographic groups. The impacts of industrial emissions on Canadian populations can be understood in multiple ways. In this paper, we focus on a proximity analysis to industrial facilities. This is a common type of EJ analysis, especially as a first look at overlapping spatial patterns of industry and socioeconomic vulnerability.41,79 A spatial analysis of who lives near industrial facilities can capture a range of detrimental effects, including noise, odor, associated rail/truck pollution, access to green spaces, property values, and access to food and services.80 From this analysis, we can understand if industrial facilities are disproportionately located near marginalized Canadian communities and if the communities may be disproportionately impacted by Canadian industrial activity. All data and code used for this analysis can be found in the associated data repository.81

Emissions and sociodemographic data

For industrial emissions data, we used the Canadian National Pollutant Release Inventory (NPRI), which is a database of industrial releases based on self-reported data supplied to the Government of Canada.82 Criteria air pollutants were added to the NPRI in 2002. The NPRI covers about 7000 total facilities. We used 2019 for this analysis, as facility locations remain relatively consistent over time. We used data on Gross Domestic Product (GDP) from Statistics Canada for 2019 as a surrogate for production levels to help assess disproportionality in industrial emissions intensity across subsectors.

We gathered sociodemographic data from the Canadian Census for 2016, which is the closest year to the NPRI data when this analysis began and the last census before the COVID-19 pandemic. We used Census data at the dissemination area (DA) level, which is the smallest publicly available level for complete data and is similar to the US census block level. Based on previous research, we focus on groups related to race/ethnicity, income, and immigration. For race and ethnicity groups, we include them as reported in the Canadian Census: Total Visible Minority, Visible Minority Not Included Elsewhere (NIE), Multiple Visible Minorities, South Asian, Chinese, Black, Filipino, Latin American, Arab, Southeast Asian, West Asian, Korean, Japanese, and Indigenous. We use White as a comparison group within our race/ethnicity analyses, as race/ethnicity has many groups. In other categories we apply binary definitions (i.e., Low-Income vs. not Low-Income). The White category was estimated by the number of people who reported not being a visible minority and not Indigenous. We used the census variable Low-Income Measure After Tax (LIM-AT) as our low-income indicator. We also included recent immigrant status, which is defined as immigrants who arrived within the past 5 years. More details on census data processing are available in the next section. We used information from the Population of Canada, a dataset on urban and rural areas of Canada created by Statistics Canada, to determine which DAs are urban or rural, with urban areas defined as those with settlements with populations over 1000 and population densities over 400 per km−2. The dataset is available on a 10 km grid from 2016, and we defined a DA as urban if 50% or more of the population in that DA is classified as urban.

Census data weighting

About 25% of households in Canada receive the long-form census with many more questions than the short-form census, which surveys 100% of the population. The low-income variable we use, the Low-Income Measure After Tax (LIM-AT), is from the short-form census. The other variables on immigration status, indigeneity, and visible minorities come from the long-form census. Due to the differences in sample size, the estimation of total population is different for each section of variables (i.e., the total population counted as either a visible minority or not a visible minority is different from the total population reported from the short-form census). We find the fraction that each group represents of the total population counted from that section – for example, we calculate the fraction of Arabs against the total population counted as either a visible minority or not a visible minority. We then multiply this fraction by the short-form census total population to get a consistent estimate of each groups’ population.

Disproportionality in industrial emissions

We first assessed the magnitude and relative emissions of primary PM2.5 and PM2.5 precursors from Canadian industry at the subsector level (Figure 3). Canadian industry is classified according to the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes, and we chose the subsector level to balance public understanding of what the categories are (i.e., Oil and Gas Extraction, Utilities), with sufficient specificity to capture significant differences in facility operations among different categories. Subsector definitions are described by Statistics Canada.83

For each subsector, we calculated 3 different metrics for each subsector for each pollutant (PM2.5, NOx, SO2, NH3, VOCs); emissions, emissions per facility, and emissions per dollars of contribution to GDP. Using these metrics, we created a ranking system to select subsectors for further study which is described in detail in SI Section 3. Briefly, for each metric and pollutant, we ordered the subsectors from highest to lowest, creating a “score.” Across pollutants, the sum of the scores for each metric would vary for each subsector. However, the highest scoring subsectors were relatively consistent across pollutants apart from the scores for VOCs. We then added the scores for each subsector and rank those, marking the highest-ranking subsectors for further investigation. For each pollutant, we calculated the Gini coefficient for facility level emissions using all facilities within each selected subsector. The Gini coefficient is a measure of inequality that is often used in economic analysis of inequalities among groups and in the existing literature on industrial emission disproportionality.19,27 The values of the Gini coefficient range from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality). Within selected subsectors, we designate a subset of facilities as “high emitters.” We consider high emitters to be the facilities that are in the top 10th percentile of emitters for each pollutant and subsector, as all Gini coefficients for each pollutant and subsector combination were over 0.6, a definition of inequality used in past studies of air pollution disproportionality.84,85 Because different facilities may be high emitters for different pollutants, our overall high-emitter category includes more than 10% of facilities for most subsectors.

Double disproportionality relating to emissions and siting

We performed a buffer analysis for the selected subsectors to assess the siting of industrial facilities near different sociodemographic groups. Buffer analyses are a type of proximity analysis which have historically been used for EJ studies on industrial emissions and can highlight areas for further analysis.31,86 We drew a 1.5 km buffer around each NPRI facility and define the population within the circle as affected and the population outside as not affected. A buffer size of 1.5 km is consistent with literature that uses buffer analysis for air pollution from US Toxic Release Inventory facilities.31 Many other studies on air pollution sources, including industrial facilities, use a similar distance for buffer analyses.1,87,88 This distance captures potential air pollution effects, as well as other disadvantages that may come from living near an industrial facility, such as traffic and noise, as well as housing values and health risks.77,89,90,91 In the SI, we also provide a more detailed review of past research on buffer size and impact zones that informed our choice. We used the buffer containment method, where the population affected is determined as the fraction of the DA that is contained within the circular buffer, due to its popularity and preservation of the original spatial unit around the industrial facility.86 We conducted sensitivity analysis with alternative buffer sizes ranging from 1 km to 2 km, which showed similar results and are available in the next section.

We calculated a variation of the Comparative Environmental Risk Index (CERI) (Equation 1), which was developed to measure whether disadvantaged populations are more likely to be exposed to environmental risks than other populations.92 The result identifies “disproportionate siting links” between selected subsectors and selected sociodemographic groups when the CERI value is greater than one. We repeated the analysis for high emitters to determine if facilities that emit a disproportionate amount compared to others with similar characteristics are disproportionately sited near marginalized groups.84,85Equation 1CERI=∑iGi/Gtot∑iPi/PtotWhere Gi is the population of the group within an NPRI buffer in dissemination area i, Gtot is the total population of the group, Pi is the total population in dissemination area i, and Ptot is the total population. CERI is calculated in rural and urban areas separately so for the urban calculation as an example, Gi is the population of the group within an urban NPRI buffer in dissemination area i and Gtot is the total population of the group in urban areas.

Discussion of buffer size

While a model that includes chemistry and transport with high spatial resolution can give a more accurate estimate of exposure to air pollution and combined effects of air pollution from multiple industrial sources, that is a much more intensive analysis and is less accessible to policymakers and some practitioners. Additionally, a model can only capture the air pollution concentrations for the modeled chemical species. Other environmental pollution considerations include discharge of pollution into water and soil, particularly from industries such as paper manufacturing that rely on water-based processes. All these considerations make buffer size for assessing facility impacts challenging. Even for air pollution, stack height varies and thus the impact radius can also vary, especially when also considering chemistry and transport. However, previous studies on housing prices, a potential indicator for overall degradation of environmental quality, found that industrial facilities severely negatively affect housing prices up to about 1 km away.77,78

Clustering of facilities

To better understand who lives in proximity to clusters of industrial facilities, we first found where facility buffers overlap and assign the intersection of buffers an “NPRI Influence Score,” which is the number of facilities’ buffers that overlap in that intersection. We then found the number of people from each sociodemographic group that are within overlapping buffers and created a frequency distribution of the number of people and their NPRI Influence Score. We compared the distributions of NPRI Influence Scores for different sociodemographic groups with the total population using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample test.
