A TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV Lifetime Management
Authors: Emiliano Bianchini, Mairead Linnane, Sara Sgreva, William Gibson, Angela McInerney, Darren Mylotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnowledge of prior valve design and failure mechanisms, as well as accurate multislice computed tomography (MSCT)–based planning are critical to optimize reintervention strategies in lifetime management of aortic stenosis.Case SummaryA 79-year-old man with prior surgical aortic valve (SAV) replacement in 2008 (27-mm Epic, Abbott) and transcatheter aortic valve (TAV)-in-SAV in 2019 (25-mm Lotus Edge, Boston Scientific) presented with acute heart failure. Echocardiography reported a severely stenotic Lotus valve, with evident asymmetrical underexpansion on MSCT. Optimization of Lotus expansion and Epic fracture was achieved with a 25-mm noncompliant balloon, followed by implantation of a 29-mm Evolut FX+, and then postdilatation with a 24-mm noncompliant balloon. Postprocedure MSCT demonstrated 25% increase in Lotus expansion at the midframe level, and symmetrical leaflet expansion of the Evolut, resulting in an echocardiographic gradient of 6 mm Hg after discharge.DiscussionSurgical valve fracture via an underexpanded Lotus is feasible and affords symmetrical expansion of complex TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV combinations with optimal hemodynamics.

Case Presentation

History of presentation

A 79-year-old man presented to our attention with acute heart failure requiring intravenous diuretics. Eighteen years before (2008), he had undergone a surgical aortic valve (SAV) replacement with a stented 27-mm Epic bioprosthesis (Abbott). Eleven years later (2019), he presented with chronic heart failure symptoms, and a transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (20%) and severe high-gradient SAV stenosis. He underwent successful transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve (TAV)-in-SAV implantation with a 25-mm Lotus Edge valve (Boston Scientific). At that time, the Lotus valve was postdilated with a 22-mm noncompliant (NC) balloon, and fracture of the SAV was deemed not necessary, given the acceptable invasive hemodynamic (peak-to-peak gradient: 4 mm Hg) and the absence of trans- or perivalvular leak. The patient was discharged on heart failure therapies with a postprocedure mean echocardiographic gradient of 12 mm Hg. Significant improvement in left ventricle function was evident at 1 year follow-up (ejection fraction: 40%), and the patient was discharged to the referring cardiology service.Take-Home Messages•Fracture of the Epic SAV is feasible through an underexpanded Lotus valve, and performing it upfront may reduce the risk of damaging TAV leaflets.•A combination of SAV fracture and supra-annular TAV can achieve optimal hemodynamics and symmetrical expansion in challenging TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV procedures.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated bioprosthetic failure, with an effective orifice area of 0.4 cm2 and a mean gradient of 50 mm Hg. Severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (15%) was evident again. Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) revealed a calcified leaflet degeneration and an asymmetrical underexpansion of the 25-mm Lotus valve, with inflow, midframe, and outflow area–derived diameters of 21.5 mm, 16.5 mm, and 22.2 mm, respectively (Figure 1). The internal perimeter averaged across the inflow, midframe, and outflow level was 70 mm. Management

Redo SAV replacement was considered prohibitive by the heart team (Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality: 17.5%), and hence a TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV procedure was considered. Transfemoral access was deemed uncomplicated, and optimization of Lotus expansion with Epic fracture was planned with subsequent implantation of a supra-annular 29-mm Evolut FX+ device (Medtronic), in accordance to the sizing chart for redo transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in a medium-sized Lotus.2

Single transfemoral access was performed using ultrasound guidance, and the stenotic Lotus valve was crossed and a Safari wire (Boston Scientific) placed in the left ventricle. A 25-mm NC balloon (True Balloon, Becton Dickinson) was advanced and inflated under rapid pacing until Lotus expansion and Epic fracture was observed (Video 1). The 29-mm Evolut FX+ was deployed under controlled rapid pacing aiming for an inflow-to-inflow position to optimize expansion of the supra-annular segment (Figure 3A, Video 1). Postdilatation was performed using a 24-mm NC balloon (True Balloon) (Figure 3A, Video 1). Final aortography demonstrated no trans- or paravalvular leak (Figure 3B, Video 2). Selective coronary angiography through the intercommissural opening window of the Evolut FX+ was performed with a Judkins Left 4 diagnostic catheter (Video 2). Outcome and follow-up

Discharge echocardiography reported a mean transvalvular gradient of 5 mm Hg, with an effective orifice area of 2.2 mm2 (Video 2). Postprocedure MSCT demonstrated optimal commissural alignment, symmetrical leaflet expansion inside the Lotus valve at node 6 of the Evolut (maximal leaflet angle: 131°), with area-derived internal diameters of 20.3 mm, 20.1 mm, 20.8 mm at nodes 1, 3, and 6, respectively. The Lotus valve showed a maximal 25% gain in the area-derived diameter at the midframe level (from 19.3 to 24.3 mm diameter) (Figure 1), with a 9.4% increase in the area-derived external diameter of the Epic valve (from 26.8 to 29.6 mm). The patient was discharged on post-TAVR day 3. At 3 months, he was at NYHA functional class I status, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels had significantly reduced, as had the need for diuretic therapy, and left ventricular ejection fraction improved to 30%, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 6 mm Hg.

Discussion

This case illustrates the contemporary management of aortic stenosis across 3 generations of bioprosthesis over an 18-year period. Preprocedural MSCT identified an asymmetrical underexpansion of the Lotus valve, as low as 77% at the midframe level ([19.3 mm/25 mm] × 100 = 77%). Such geometric distortion could have contributed to early structural valve failure. Achieving optimal valve expansion during early TAV interventions—such as in this 72-year-old patient at the time of Lotus-in-Epic implantation—is critical for the lifetime management strategy. In vitro studies suggest that TAV underexpansion might contribute to structural valve failure.3 Of note, at the time of Lotus implantation, underexpansion was not evident under fluoroscopy, highlighting the difficulties in recognizing geometrical distortions without a postprocedure MSCT.

In our case, an upfront surgical ring fracture represented a critical step to mitigate the risk of leaflet damage to the Evolut valve. Recent bench studies have shown that NC balloon diameters exceeding 24 mm in the 29-mm Evolut platform may result in leaflet tears, potentially compromising long-term durability.4 We required, at least, a 25-mm balloon for fracture and hence opted to perform this upfront. Of note, little is known about aggressive dilatation of the mechanically expanded Lotus valve, which incorporates a unique belt-and-buckle locking mechanism. We demonstrate that SAV fracture can be successfully performed through the Lotus frame with high-pressure balloon dilatation. Indeed, postprocedure MSCT confirmed resolution of the severe asymmetrical expansion of the Lotus frame, and a symmetric housing of the functional portion of the Evolut, an important procedure goal given the risks of valve thrombosis and associated durability concerns with leaflet asymmetrical expansion.5,6 In our case, the achieved valve hemodynamic reflected optimization of the Epic-Lotus-Evolut configuration.

Conclusions

Unrecognized TAV underexpansion may compromise long-term durability and complicate lifetime patient management. In complex valve-in-valve-in-valve configurations, upfront valve fracture may enable favorable valve geometry and hemodynamic results while avoiding damage to the leaflets of the newly implanted TAV.
