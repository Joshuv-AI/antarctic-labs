Benchmarking GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 4, and other LLMs on pediatric dentistry questions from a dental specialization exam
Authors: Sukriye Turkoglu Kayaci, Hamza Osman Ilhan, Melek Tassoker, Taha Cap, Helin Demir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), is an increasingly prominent tool in medical and dental education. Trained with deep learning and NLP techniques, these models interpret meaning, generate text, and manage complex information. They hold potential for practical educational applications, such as supporting exam preparation and personalized learning. Moreover, their performance in clinical case recognition suggests an emerging potential for use in diagnostic decision-support systems. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) on pediatric dentistry questions from the Dentistry Specialization Examination (DUS) in Türkiye, a high-stakes national exam for postgraduate training. A total of 119 pediatric dentistry questions from the past ten years of the DUS were compiled and presented to 11 recently developed LLMs (17 with reasoning mode activations), including GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok-4, and DeepSeek R1. Each model’s accuracy (%) and average response generation time (seconds) were calculated and compared. The Gemini 2.5 Pro (92.44%) demonstrated significantly higher mean scores compared to all other models except GPT-4 (78.15%), GPT-5 (90.76%), GPTOSS (78.99, 75.63%), and Grok-4 (88.24%). Similar patterns were also observed for GPT-5 and Grok-4. In contrast, Qwen-3 (49.58 - Reasoning, 54.62 - No Reasoning), and MedGemma (58.82%) exhibited notably lower accuracy rates across most comparisons. Overall, these findings highlight Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-5 as achieving the highest accuracy levels among the models, whereas Qwen-3, Qwen-3 (R), and MedGemma demonstrated the weakest performance. While models such as Gemma, LLaMA, and Mistral demonstrated faster response times (<1 s), they exhibited relatively low accuracy. In contrast, reasoning-intensive modes (e.g., DeepSeek R1) improved accuracy but required excessively long generation times (up to 68 s). LLM performance on pediatric dentistry questions was highly variable. Top models, notably Gemini 2.5 Pro (92.44%) and GPT-5 (90.76%), approached expert-level accuracy, while others (e.g., Qwen-3) performed poorly. A critical speed-accuracy trade-off was evident: reasoning modes improved scores but were impractically slow, whereas faster models had low accuracy. This variability necessitates careful validation before LLMs are used in high-stakes dental education or assessment.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has increasingly become a prominent tool in medical and dental education, with large language models (LLMs) attracting particular attention1–3. These models, trained on vast corpora using deep learning and natural language processing (NLP) techniques, are designed to interpret meaning, generate coherent text, and assist in managing complex information4,5. Beyond their technical capabilities, LLMs hold potential for practical applications such as supporting exam preparation, providing formative feedback, and facilitating personalized learning experiences1,6,7. Moreover, their demonstrated performance in recognizing clinical cases and engaging in diagnostic reasoning suggests an emerging potential for their use as diagnostic decision-support systems8–10.

A notable implementation of LLMs is through conversational agents, or chatbots, which enable text- and voice-based dialogue and are reshaping how individuals interact with technology. These developments have prompted evaluation of LLMs not only as general-purpose communication tools but also within professional education and assessment contexts11,12. Previous studies have examined LLM performance on dental licensing and admissions exams12–14. A recent meta-analysis of eleven studies evaluated dental licensing examinations from eight countries, reporting integrated accuracy rates of 54% for GPT-3.5, 72% for GPT-4, and 56% for Bard. GPT-4 outperformed GPT-3.5 and Bard, passing more than half of the evaluated exams13.

Recent advencements have introduced new generations of LLMs. In August 2025, OpenAI released GPT-5, its most advanced multimodal system to date15,16. Around the same period, Google DeepMind introduced Gemini 2.5 Pro, featuring enhanced reasoning and problem-solving capabilities15. In July 2025, xAI launched Grok-4, which integrates real-time information retrieval and advanced analytical functions17. Older LLM versions have already been tested in multiple dental domains, including endodontics18, restorative dentistry19, oral radiology20, anatomy21, oral surgery22, oral pathology6, prosthodontics8, and orthodontics9.

The Dentistry Specialization Education Entrance Examination (DUS) represents the competitive national examination for entry into postgraduate specialty training in dentistry. Administered by the Assessment, Selection and Placement Center (OSYM), the DUS assesses candidates’ proficiency across biomedical sciences and clinical dental knowledge, with outcomes determining access to accredited postgraduate programs23. Given its comprehensive scope and high-stakes nature, the DUS serves as an important benchmark for evaluating whether LLMs can perform reliably in a national, discipline-specific context, combining knowledge-based items with clinical case scenarios that require diagnostic reasoning24. Crucially, the DUS is not limited to simple fact-recall. A significant portion of the examination is designed to assess higher-order cognitive skills, presenting candidates with complex clinical case scenarios. These questions require test-takers to engage in sophisticated diagnostic reasoning, etiologic identification, and clinical decision-making, effectively mirroring the diagnostic challenges faced in real-world pediatric dentistry practice. Therefore, evaluating LLM performance on this exam provides a direct proxy for their potential in diagnostic support.

Previous investigations have evaluated LLM performance on various dental licensing and admissions examinations. Studies examining the Integrated National Board Dental Examination (INBDE), Advanced Dental Admission Test (ADAT), and related assessments have shown promising but variable results, with models generally excelling on fact-recall questions while struggling on items requiring multi-step reasoning or numerical computation. Similar analyses in medical licensing contexts, such as the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE)12,25, also report strong performance in some domains but inconsistent results when questions are translated or reflect local curricular emphases13,14. Meta-analytic work reinforces these observations, highlighting both the rapid progress of newer model generations and their fragility across settings and question formats.

Despite these rapid technological advances, no comprehensive study has yet assessed the knowledge and performance of these new LLM generations specifically in pediatric dentistry. In Türkiye, the Dentistry Specialization Examination (DUS) serves as the national gateway to postgraduate specialty training in dentistry. The current study aims to evaluate the performance of 11 contemporary LLMs–including the most well-known state-of-the-art models such as GPT-5, Grok-4, DeepSeek R1, and Gemini 2.5 Pro–on pediatric dentistry questions drawn from the past ten years of DUS examinations.

Materials and methods

Dataset information

The evaluation dataset consisted of 119 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) drawn from the Dentistry Specialization Examination (DUS). One canceled question was excluded. Questions were obtained from the official exam booklets published by OSYM23. All questions were translated from Turkish into English prior to submission to the AI models. Each item had five answer options (A–E), with a single correct response validated by the national examination authority (OSYM). Questions were transcribed from their original Microsoft Word 2019 version into a structured Excel 2021 file, including the stem, options, and official answer key. Image-based items were excluded to focus exclusively on text-based reasoning13,14. Questions were sampled from exams administered between 2015 and 2025, reflecting the standardized examination framework.

Responses from the chatbots were categorized as either “correct” or “incorrect” based on the official answer key provided by OSYM. The accuracy rate for each chatbot was calculated as the percentage of correct answers out of the total number of questions attempted6,12.

The questions were categorized into 11 pediatric dentistry topics as; Tooth eruption, growth and development, developmental anomalies, caries prevention and management, restorative procedures and materials, pulp therapies, dental trauma, behaviour management and sedation, space maintainers/interceptive orthodontics, systemic diseases, infections and pharm, periodontal diseases, local anesthesia1,10,26,27.

The distribution of the total number of questions across these 11 topics is shown in Fig. 1. To validate the dataset’s relevance for diagnostic benchmarking, the questions were analyzed for content. This analysis confirmed that the items frequently move beyond simple knowledge retrieval, focusing instead on clinical decision-making and etiological interpretation. A representative example is provided in Fig. 2, which presents a real-world dental trauma scenario requiring the selection of an appropriate treatment based on specific diagnostic findings (e.g., vital pulp, exposure size, time elapsed).

Models

In this study, a total of 17 experimental conditions were evaluated across 11 distinct large language model families (Mistral, Llama Maverick, Gemma, MedGemma, Qwen, DeepSeek, OpenAI GPT-OSS, GPT-4o, GPT5, Gemini 2.5 Pro and Grok), representing a diverse range of proprietary and open-weight systems4,15–17. A standardized evaluation protocol was applied to maximize comparability. Specifically, the temperature parameter was set to 0.0 for all models where the API permitted, ensuring deterministic outputs. However, it should be noted that GPT-5 was evaluated at its fixed default temperature of 1.0 due to provider-imposed API restrictions active during the testing window; thus, its results reflect a probabilistic variance inherent to its default setting. Detailed model specifications and parameters.

Mistral small

Mistral Small 3 is a 24-billion-parameter instruction-tuned, text-only model36. It is an open-weight release under the Apache 2.0 license and provides both base and instruction-tuned checkpoints. Peer-reviewed, exam-style evaluations in medicine have increasingly included Mistral family models, with reports showing competitive performance on expert-level multiple-choice critical-care questions and broader clinical QA, albeit typically below top proprietary systems40.

Llama 4 Maverick

Llama 4 Maverick is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with 17 billion active parameters across 128 experts, instruction-tuned and multimodal. Tested under both default and reasoning-enabled modes across 119 dental multiple-choice questions. Llama 3.1 lineage demonstrated strong performance on clinical MCQs and diagnostic tasks, offering proximate evidence of the family’s exam-like capabilities35.

Gemma 3

A 27-billion-parameter instruction-tuned model supporting text and image inputs with a 128K token context window. Evaluated under default and reasoning-enabled conditions across 119 dental multiple-choice questions . It supports multimodal inputs (text and images), an extended 128K token context window, and provides strong baseline performance on reasoning, summarization, and multilingual tasks–achieved through distillation and post-training that leverages reinforcement learning (RL) from human, machine, and execution feedback37,41.

MedGemma

Derived from Gemma-3, MedGemma is a 27B text-only model designed to enhance medical text comprehension. According to its 2025 technical report, MedGemma demonstrates advanced medical understanding and reasoning while maintaining the general capabilities of the Gemma 3 base models37,38.

Qwen3

The flagship MoE model of the Qwen3 family (235-billion total parameters; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{2}$$\end{document}2-billion activated per token), integrating thinking and non-thinking modes with a user-adjustable reasoning budget. Post-training explicitly targets long chain-of-thought (CoT) via a four-stage regimen–long-CoT cold start, reasoning RL, thinking-mode fusion (with think vs. no think flags in the chat template), and general RL–after which increasing the thinking budget generally improves performance on reasoning centric benchmarks39,42.

DeepSeek R1

A reinforcement-learning-based reasoning model trained to support chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. Beyond medical reasoning tasks such as USMLE-style items and clinical case analyses, recent peer reviewed research has also assessed DeepSeek in a dental context, showing competitive accuracy on multiple-choice endodontics questions derived from the Dentistry Specialization Entrance Exam (DUS)34.

OpenAI GPT-OSS

An open-weight MoE reasoning model with 120-billion total parameters, released under the Apache 2.0 license32. The model was designed as a publicly accessible counterpart to OpenAI’s proprietary reasoning models, with the aim of supporting reproducible academic research and fostering broader community development of reasoning-oriented LLMs. Independent evaluations on medical QA datasets indicate that GPT-OSS performs competitively with leading closed-weight systems, though peer reviewed studies have not yet investigated its application in dental education or specialty examinations, leaving its reliability in this domain untested43.

GPT-5

Provider-hosted model with text-only and multimodal capabilities30. Under a unified evaluation protocol, GPT-5 demonstrated state-of-the-art performance surpassing GPT-4o and even licensed human experts across multiple medical QA datasets such as MedQA, MMLU-Medical, USMLE self-assessment, and visual tasks like MedXpertQA and VQA-RAD15,16.

Gemini 2.5 Pro

Positioned as Google’s most advanced reasoning-capable model, Gemini 2.5 Pro leverages a sparse MoE architecture with native multimodal support and a context window of up to one million tokens28,29. It delivers state-of-the-art performance on benchmarks assessing coding, math, reasoning, and long context comprehension, such as AIME 2025, GPQA and various factual grounding tasks. In the medical domain, Gemini 2.5 Pro was evaluated using MRCGP-style primary care MCQs, achieving an impressive 95% accuracy–far exceeding the average clinician peer score of 73%44. At the time of data collection (March 2025), Gemini 2.5 Pro was accessed as an experimental preview release, and performance characteristics may evolve in the General Availability (GA) version.

Grok 4

Developed as a provider-hosted system with tool-integrated reasoning, Grok 4 includes native tool use, real-time search, and structured output capabilities; xAI has not publicly disclosed its parameter count31,45.

GPT-4o

GPT-4o is the most recent multimodal model in the GPT-4 family, released by OpenAI in 2024. It is accessible only as a provider-hosted system, and specific details such as parameter count or internal architecture have not been publicly disclosed33,46.

Experimental protocol

All models were evaluated under a standardized zero-shot setting47. Each question was presented in plain text with five options and accompanied by an identical system prompt:

“You are a board-certified dental expert. Answer the following multiple-choice question by returning ONLY the letter of the best option (A, B, C, D, or E). Do not output any explanation.”

The system prompt explicitly instructed models to return ’ONLY the letter’. Despite this, post-processing (as described above) was applied to handle verbose outputs. No in-context examples, fine-tuning, or retrieval augmentation were applied. Each model produced exactly one output per question. To maximize reproducibility, temperature was set to 0.0 for all models where the API permitted. However, due to provider-imposed constraints, GPT-5 was evaluated at its fixed default temperature of 1.0. Consequently, while inference for most models was strictly deterministic, GPT-5 results reflect its default probabilistic behavior. Reasoning features were enabled for models that natively support them, creating two distinct evaluation tiers: standard and reasoning-enhanced.

Data parsing and scoring logic

To ensure consistent scoring, model outputs were processed using a standardized parsing script. A regular expression was employed to extract the first valid option letter (A-E) from the response text. Formats such as ’(A)’, ’Option A’, or ’A)’ were normalized to ’A’. Any response that did not contain a clear selection, including empty strings, formatting errors, or policy-based refusals (e.g., ’I cannot answer medical questions’), was recorded as ’Incorrect’. No points were awarded for partial answers.

Evaluation metrics

Accuracy was calculated as the proportion of correct answers relative to the 119-item dataset. This measure directly reflected a model’s ability to align with the official key provided by OSYM. Average response time was recorded as wall-clock API latency recorded during inference, reported in seconds. Both accuracy and timing were reported separately for default and reasoning-enabled conditions to assess potential trade-offs between reasoning effort and computational overhead.

Computational environment

All evaluations were conducted using official API access. Inference latency was automatically captured within the request loop. No external optimizations, caching, or prompt engineering were applied. Fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, or third-party frameworks were not employed. API requests were initiated from Istanbul, Türkiye, using a standard commercial fiber connection. Requests were processed sequentially (no concurrency) to avoid rate-limit penalties. Streaming was disabled to capture the full response generation time. Consequently, reported times reflect the total ’wall-clock’ latency observed in this specific environment.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum) were reported along with skewness and kurtosis values to examine the normality of the distributions48. Differences between models were assessed using the Friedman test, and when significant results were obtained, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction49. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

Comparison of accuracy rates of LLMs.

Post-hoc analyses using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction revealed that certain models clearly outperformed others. The Gemini 2.5 Pro model demonstrated significantly higher mean scores compared to all other models except GPT-4, GPT-5, GPTOSS, and Grok-4. Similar patterns were also observed for GPT-5 and Grok-4.

In contrast, Qwen-3 (R), Qwen-3, and MedGemma exhibited notably lower accuracy rates across most comparisons. Overall, these findings highlight Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-5 as achieving the highest accuracy levels among the models, whereas Qwen-3, Qwen-3 (R), and MedGemma demonstrated the weakest performance. Discussion

In this study, 11 recently state of the art developed LLMs (17 models with reasoning mode activations) were evaluated on pediatric dentistry questions from the DUS, and their accuracy rates and average response generation times were compared. The findings revealed considerable variation in performance among the models. The highest accuracy rates were achieved by Gemini 2.5 Pro (92.44%), GPT-5 (90.76%), and Grok-4 (88.24%). The performance of these three models approached expert-level accuracy. Moderate accuracy levels were observed in DeepSeek R1 (Standard and High Reasoning modes, 72.26 - 74.78%), OpenAI GPT-OSS (Standard 78.99%, High Reasoning 75,63%), and GPT-4o (78.15%). While these models achieved reliable performance within the 74–79% range, they fell short compared to the top-tier models. In contrast, lower-performing models included Qwen3 (Standard 54.62%, High Reasoning 49.57%), MedGemma (58.82%), Gemma 3 (61.34%), Mistral Small (65.54%), and LLaMA 4 Maverick (69.74–70.58%). Their accuracy rates, ranging between 52% and 71%, were not clinically adequate. Gemini 2.5 Pro’s superior performance may be explained by its training on larger, multimodal corpora and fine-grained instruction tuning, which enhance its ability to manage domain-specific terminology and multi-step reasoning. Its ‘thinking model’ design also reduces hallucination and improves consistency, providing an advantage over GPT-5 and Grok-412,22,25.

Beyond overall accuracy rates, topic-based analyses provided further insights into the domain-specific strengths and weaknesses of the models. Subgroup analyses by topic revealed that the performance of LLMs was not homogeneous. In restorative procedures, periodontal diseases, and local anesthesia, several models achieved 100% accuracy, suggesting that AI models tend to perform better in areas dominated by knowledge-based and standardized approaches. On the other hand, models such as Qwen3, Mistral, and MedGemma demonstrated weaker performance with lower accuracy rates, particularly in caries management, trauma, and pulp therapies, which require more complex clinical reasoning. These findings indicate that LLMs have topic-specific strengths and weaknesses, and model selection for clinical and educational applications should take these differences into account.

Restorative Procedures & Materials, Periodontal Diseases, and Behavior Management models demonstrated perfect accuracy rates, while they also showed promising results in critical diagnostic areas such as case recognition and clinical decision-making. A key implication of this study is the strong potential for state-of-the-art LLMs to function as diagnostic decision-support systems in dentistry. This is evidenced by their high performance not just on knowledge-based questions, but specifically in areas requiring case recognition and diagnostic reasoning. For instance, top models achieved perfect (100%) accuracy in ’Restorative Procedures & Materials’ and ’Behavior Management’ , and over 90% in ’Caries Prevention & Management’ and ’Pulp Therapies’. The ability to accurately navigate these complex, vignette-based questions–which form the core of clinical practice–suggests a robust capability for diagnostic support. However, the weaker performance of models like Qwen3 and MedGemma in these same areas indicates that not all LLMs possess this diagnostic acumen, making model-specific validation essential.

In terms of response times, Gemma 3 (0.34 s), LLaMA 4 (0.57 s), and Mistral Small (0.49 s) were the fastest models but exhibited relatively low accuracy. GPT-5 (1.18 s), GPT-4o (1.2 s), and Gemini 2.5 Pro (9 s) fell within the medium speed category while maintaining high accuracy, thus demonstrating a balanced performance. Models such as Qwen3 High Reasoning (68 s) and DeepSeek R1 High (36 s) showed improved accuracy but at the cost of excessively long response times, which limits their practical utility. These findings highlight a clear trade-off between accuracy and speed. High reasoning modes increased accuracy but substantially prolonged response times, reducing usability in real-time educational or clinical contexts14,50. Therefore, GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro emerged as the most balanced models, while Grok-4 also achieved high accuracy but with relatively longer response times. Although Gemma and LLaMA models offered speed advantages (<1 s), they were inadequate in terms of accuracy, and reasoning-focused models like DeepSeek did not achieve acceptable response times for practical applications.

Recent evidence has demonstrated that Gemini 2.5 Pro achieves remarkable performance in radiology-related tasks. For instance, in MRI interpretation, ChatGPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro both reached 100% accuracy in identifying the imaging plane and 98.46% in determining contrast-enhancement status51. Moreover, MRI sequence classification accuracy was reported as 93.1% for Gemini 2.5 Pro, compared with 97.7% for ChatGPT-4o and only 73.1% for Claude 4 Opus. These results highlight Gemini 2.5 Pro’s strong capability in handling complex domain-specific diagnostic tasks, further supporting its high accuracy in pediatric dentistry questions observed in our study.

Recent studies have shown that GPT-5 consistently outperforms baseline models, including GPT-4o, across text-based and multimodal medical benchmarks, in line with our findings16. Notably, GPT-5 achieved significant gains in reasoning and comprehension, surpassing GPT-4o and even exceeding human expert performance, underscoring a major advancement in LLM capabilities16,51. Recent work in ophthalmology has shown that GPT-5 models achieved near-perfect accuracy and superior rationale quality on board-style multiple-choice questions, clearly surpassing GPT-4o and earlier reasoning models, which further supports our findings in pediatric dentistry52. Contrarily, in brain tumor MRI tasks53, models GPT-5 family were reported to achieve only moderate accuracy, with GPT-5 reaching 43.71% and GPT-5-mini performing similarly at 44.19%, indicating that their performance remains insufficient for clinical application54. In mammography tasks, although GPT-5 demonstrated improved performance compared to earlier GPT versions, it still lagged behind human experts and domain-specific fine-tuned models55. Consequently, it has been reported as unsuitable for direct use in high-risk clinical applications, with sensitivity and specificity values of 63.5% and 52.3%, respectively, compared to 86.9% and 88.9% achieved by human specialists56–58.

Introduced on July 9, 2025, by Elon Musk under xAI, Grok-4 was presented as a next-generation artificial intelligence system, claimed to possess problem-solving abilities surpassing the doctoral level across all academic domains45. Research conducted with Grok-4 in the literature is extremely limited. Supporting our observations, a recent study in cardiology highlighted Grok’s superiority in management-related tasks, outperforming other LLMs in areas such as congenital heart disease and heart failure, while GPT-4 showed slightly better overall performance and Gemini performed best in electrophysiology, though without statistical significance13. Overall, these studies show encouraging yet mixed results across different LLMs and highlight the need for more systematic research, particularly on Grok-4 where evidence is still very limited.

Future work and limitations

This study is limited by its reliance on a single dataset of pediatric dentistry questions from the DUS over a ten-year period, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other examinations. Moreover, as large language models are rapidly evolving, the results reflect only the performance of the most recent versions available at the time of data collection and may not be representative of future iterations. Another potential limitation is data contamination, as some exam-related content may have been included in the training data of the models, potentially influencing their performance6,22. Additionally, the lack of uniformity in temperature settings poses a limitation to direct comparability. Specifically, GPT-5 could not be adjusted to a temperature of 0.0 due to API restrictions, meaning its high performance must be interpreted with the caveat that it operated with higher variance (temperature=1.0) compared to the strictly deterministic settings of other models. Furthermore, latency measurements were derived from a single-pass evaluation. Due to the lack of repeated trials (n=3-5), we could not calculate stable measures of dispersion such as Interquartile Range (IQR). Thus, the reported times should be interpreted as indicative of the user experience under specific conditions rather than a robust benchmark of model compute speed. Finally, response time measurements reported in this study represent wall-clock API latency. These values are subject to external variables such as network conditions, server load, and rate limiting, and thus should be interpreted as a measure of real-world user wait time rather than intrinsic model generation speed.

Conclusions

This benchmarking study showed that large language models display variable performance on pediatric dentistry questions from a dental specialization exam. Gemini 2.5 Pro (92.44%), GPT-5 (90.76%), and Grok-4 (88.24%) achieved the highest accuracy, clearly outperforming GPT-4o and smaller models. While reasoning-focused settings improved accuracy for some models, they required substantially longer generation times, indicating a trade-off between speed and reliability12,22,25. Overall, the newest LLMs approach expert-level performance, but variability across models and slower response in reasoning-intensive modes highlight the need for further refinement before widespread use in high-stakes assessment or education6,14.
