Numerical characterization of electrochemical transport in three-dimensional rhombic zero-depth pores
Authors: Mohammad Matin Behzadi, Nika Sadat Moussavi Zarandi, Philippe Renaud, Mojtaba Taghipoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) rhombic zero-depth pores present a promising solution to the challenges associated with the requirement for ultrathin membranes in DNA sequencing. This study provides a comprehensive numerical analysis of 3D rhombic zero-depth pores formed at the intersection of triangular microchannels using finite element modeling. The model was validated with a mean absolute error of 2.75% between numerical and experimental results. We identified a critical channel length, beyond which pore conductance varies linearly with the diameter, vertex angle, and electrolyte concentration. We derived a mathematical correlation that provides a predictive framework for non-destructive pore size estimation without microscopy. As the vertex angle increases and the pore geometry approaches that of a 2D pore, its conductance converges toward the electrolyte conductivity. We also analyzed the electric field distribution, which influences signal amplitude and particle dwell time. Results showed maximum field intensity at the mid-plane origin along the Y-axis, with higher values at larger vertex angles and smaller pore diameters. Notably, the strongest electric fields occurred at the mid-points of the mid-plane sides, which we defined as critical points. We also derived equations that can determine the maximum electric field and effective length of the pore based on the applied voltage and the pore’s geometrical properties. Introduction

Resistive pulse sensing (RPS) was first developed by Wallace H. Coulter in 19531. The technique was initially used to study blood cells2, but its applications have since expanded to include the investigation of heavy metal ions3, microplastics4, porous hydrogel particles5, aptamers6–8, phages9, bacteria10–12, viruses13–15, extracellular vesicles16–18, liposomes and nanomedicines19–21, nano-toxic particles22, biopolymers23,24 and particularly proteins25,26, and DNA molecules sensing and sequencing27–29.

Micro- and nanopores act as the gate between electrolyte containers, serving as the key component of an RPS. Depending on their material, they can be classified as either biological or solid-state. In 1996, Kasianowicz et al30. employed α-hemolysin to form a single biological nanopore in a lipid bilayer, enabling the detection of individual polynucleotide strands. Biological nanopores are highly sensitive31 and have commercial applications in the sequencing of proteins and DNA molecules32,33. However, they have certain limitations, primarily due to their supporting membranes, which are lipid bilayers. Lipid bilayers are sensitive to temperature, voltage, mechanical stress, and pH, resulting in short lifetimes34. In addition, biological nanopores face challenges related to their limited pore size34. The better mechanical, chemical, and thermal stability of synthetic membranes has driven researchers towards developing solid-state nanopores for the detection of micro- and nano-particles. Solid-state nanopores can be fabricated in various shapes and sizes, and from different materials. They can also be made tunable, allowing their size to be adjusted in real-time during experiments35,36.

For DNA sequencing, nanopores alone are insufficient—ultra-thin (also referred to as zero-length) membranes are also required. Without such membranes, multiple nucleotides could occupy the pore simultaneously, making it impossible to determine which nucleotide corresponds to the measured signal37.

For fabricating low-aspect-ratio solid-state nanopores, different methods, including dry etching38, laser-assisted photothermal etching38, ion39 and electron beam irradiation40, transmission electron microscopy41, and electric breakdown42–44, have been employed. Such nanopores are typically fabricated in membranes pre-thinned by wet etching45 or in ultrathin films formed by atomic layer deposition (ALD) of materials like TiO246, SiO247, or Al2O348 on a sacrificial layer. Other research has investigated the use of two-dimensional (2D) materials49 such as graphene50, BN51, h-BN52, MoS₂53, and WS254. These techniques are costly, complex, and require advanced microfabrication facilities.

The concept of zero-depth nanopores was first proposed in the 2000 s by Fleming55 and Ling56. The concept is based on the intersection of two nanoscale channels, each only a few nanometers wide, forming a nanopore with effectively zero thickness. In 2013, Luan et al57. used COMSOL simulations to show that zero-depth pores (termed “slit-pores”) exhibit higher and more localized ionic currents than conventional cylindrical or square nanopores. They also showed that in slit-pores, the current maintains an approximately linear relationship with pore width and remains unaffected even when membrane thickness increases by two orders of magnitude. This study demonstrated the potential of thickness-independent zero-depth nanopores as a solution to the challenges of ultrathin solid-state nanopore fabrication for DNA sequencing. Arjmandi experimentally validated this concept in 201858by fabricating interfacial nanopores through the dissolution of two crossing metallic nanorods embedded in polymer slabs. The resulting structure had mathematically zero thickness and was successfully used to detect λ-DNA molecules. Further numerical investigations by Zhang et al59. in 2024 on the interfacial nanopores introduced by Arjmandi58 demonstrated that increasing the membrane thickness had minimal impact on the ionic current passing through the pore. Despite the ingenuity of Arjmandi’s method, its complexity presents practical challenges58.

In 2024, Ahmadi and Ardebili60 proposed a novel, simple, and cost-effective mechanical method for fabricating low-aspect-ratio 3D zero-depth pores by molding polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) at the intersection of two commercial sharp blades. When the two blades intersect, they form a 3D rhombic pore that appears square in the top view, and the diagonal of this square is defined as the pore diameter. This technique enabled the fabrication of pores over a wide size range, from 350 nm to more than 30 μm, and was successfully used for the detection of polystyrene beads. By overcoming several limitations of conventional solid-state pore fabrication methods, this approach established the foundation for a new generation of solid-state nanopores60. In 2025, Behzadi et al61. further extended this concept by fabricating sub-50 nm 3D rhombic zero-depth nanopores using synthetic silicon micro-blades with a tip angle of 135°, resulting in pores with 2.4 times higher conductance compared to the Reference60, which primarily discussed the fabrication strategy and demonstrated a linear relationship between pore conductance and pore diameter. In contrast, Reference61 examined a different channel geometry, featuring a distinct blade angle and pore configuration. This led to the creation of nanoscale pores, resulting in different conductance equations and transport behaviors.

Despite these important advances, the electrochemical physics governing 3D rhombic zero-depth pores has not been comprehensively studied. Key questions such as the origin of the linear conductance–diameter relationship, the role of channel geometry and electrolyte concentration, the conditions under which membrane thickness becomes negligible, and the spatial distribution of the electric field inside the pore region remain insufficiently understood. In the present work, we focus on the original geometry introduced in Reference60 and provide a comprehensive numerical characterization of three-dimensional triangular cross-section zero-depth (TCZD) pores. Using finite element simulations, we systematically investigate the effects of pore diameter, channel vertex angle, electrolyte concentration, and channel length on conductance, electric field distribution, and effective pore length. We introduce the concept of critical channel length to explain membrane-thickness independence and derive predictive correlations for pore conductance, maximum electric field, and effective length. In addition, we explain the physical mechanisms responsible for conductance linearity and electric field concentration at critical points. This study establishes the fundamental electrochemical physics of 3D TCZD pores, which could lead to their application in nanoparticle sensing and DNA sequencing in the future.

Luan et al. demonstrated that, unlike conventional nanopores, zero-depth nanopores maintain a linear relationship between pore conductance and pore diameter, even at the nanoscale57. Ahmadi and Ardebili further showed that the experimental sizing results of micro-scale 3D zero-depth pores, obtained through optical microscopy, can be generalized to pores of different sizes60. However, the challenges associated with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization of PDMS TCZD nanopores led us to develop an optical microscopy-based validation method that utilizes precisely measurable microscale pores (Fig. S 5). We measured the conductance of the micro-scale pores, whose sizes were determined through visual sizing using a microscope, with a potentiostat manufactured by Kianshar Co. (Tehran, Iran). During the experimental investigation, an AC voltage of 0.25 V was applied to the Ag/AgCl electrodes, with a variable frequency ranging from 1 to 100 Hz.

This experimental validation framework allows meaningful comparison between numerical simulations and empirical observations. Details of the experiment and the procedure for estimating experimental uncertainty are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Sect. 2.1, 2.2, 3.1, and 3.2).

Results and discussion

Impact of pore geometry on conductance

The total resistance of a pore consists of two components (Eq. 7). The first component, the pore resistance , is inversely proportional to the square of the pore diameter and directly proportional to the pore thickness. The second component, the access resistance , is inversely proportional to the pore diameter65.

The pore resistance can be ignored for pores with low aspect ratios, where the thickness is negligible compared to the pore diameter. In such cases, the electrical conductance of the pore is linearly proportional to the pore diameter. As shown in Fig. 3, TCZD pores maintain a linear conductance-diameter relationship even when the membrane thickness significantly exceeds the pore diameter. This confirms that the pore resistance is negligible, which is characteristic of zero-depth pores.

We also studied the vertex angle of the channels because of its significant impact on pore conductance66. Figure 4 shows a linear correlation between the normalized conductance at constant electrolyte concentration and the channel vertex angle .

Correlation in pore conductance

Determining the diameter of PDMS 3D TCZD nanopores with an optical microscope is not feasible. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) presents significant challenges for PDMS nanopore characterization, as the high-energy electron beam may induce pore deformation or irreversible structural modifications60,61.

To address the limitations of microscopy-based characterization, we developed a curve-fitted expression (Eq. 8) derived from finite element simulation results (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). This expression shows excellent agreement with the numerical data, yielding a coefficient of determination of 0.99.

Equation 8 enables the determination of pore diameter when the vertex angle and electrolyte concentration are known. By measuring ionic conductance using a potentiostat, the pore size can be estimated without requiring microscopy-based characterization. The linear dependence of conductance on pore diameter originates from the dominance of access resistance over pore resistance in TCZD pores. Equation 8 suggests that, at a constant electrolyte concentration, for smaller pores, the vertex angle has a reduced impact on pore conductance (Fig. S 6). The underlying reason can be explained by considering the two main parameters governing ionic transport at constant electrolyte concentration: the pore diameter and the channel width (Fig. 2). The pore diameter directly affects the flow of ions through the pore, while the channel width controls the amount of ionic current that can enter the channel and is strongly influenced by the channel vertex angle. For small diameters, the resistance of the pore is mainly affected by the size of the pore. However, for larger pores, the width of the channel and, consequently, the vertex angle become more significant.

Critical length of channels

Our results demonstrate that not all interfacial or slit pores formed at channels’ collision points exhibit zero-depth characteristics. This is an important distinction that has been overlooked in previous studies55–60. While Eq. 8 describes a linear conductance-diameter (G-D) relationship for specific vertex angles and electrolyte concentrations, Fig. 6 reveals that if the channel length is below a specific threshold, this relationship becomes nonlinear (Fig. 6E-G). To resolve this paradox, it is essential to examine the underlying physics of these types of pores closely.

In conventional cylindrical or conical pores, when a particle reaches the entrance, it blocks the pore from entry to exit57. This obstruction stops the ionic current, enabling detection of an electrical signal. However, the behavior differs significantly in TCZD pores with channel lengths substantially larger than the particle size. When a particle enters a channel, it may block the channel width-wise while maintaining length-wise openness (Fig. S 7). This unique geometry allows the electrolyte to bypass the particle, resulting in no substantial change in current. Only when the particle reaches the channels’ intersection does it fully obstruct electrolyte flow, creating the conditions for signal detection. Since zero-depth pores produce detectable signals only when particles reach the intersection point, where the pore has no geometric thickness, the membrane thickness and channel height become negligible factors in particle detection. The important question addressed in this study is how long the channels must be relative to the pore diameter to achieve this effect.

For any given channel vertex angle and pore diameter, there exists a critical length (). This critical length serves as a threshold; when the channel length is shorter than , the pore’s conductance is highly influenced by the length of the channel. Conversely, for channels longer than the critical length, the pore’s conductance becomes independent of channel length (Fig. 6A-B).

Figure 7D indicates that the maximum electric field happens at four points on the sides of the mid-plane that both blades have touched during the curing of PDMS (Fig. 7C). We refer to these as critical points.

The maximum electric field occurs at these points due to extreme changes in electrical potential. When a particle is at the origin of the mid-plane, it occupies both upper and lower channels simultaneously. However, at the critical points, when the particle passes through, it leaves the upper channel and enters the lower channel (Fig. 11), which means a change in the electrical potential over a shorter distance.

As Jodeyri has shown64, the off-axis passage of particles through a conventional cylindrical pore significantly affects both the pulse amplitude and dwell time. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies investigate the differences between particles that pass through the pore along the Y-axis and those that do so off-axis.

Conclusion

Three-dimensional (3D) zero-depth pores represent a promising advancement in cost-effective solid-state pore fabrication, achieved through simple mechanical methods. These pores function similarly to zero-length pores but are not influenced by the thickness of the membrane. In this study, we present a comprehensive numerical investigation of the electrical characteristics of a 3D rhombic zero-depth pore, formed at the intersection of two triangular cross-section channels. We derived a mathematical correlation that estimates the pore conductance as a function of pore diameter, the vertex angle of the channels, and electrolyte concentration. This equation offers a predictive framework for estimating pore size without microscopy. Our findings reveal that the resistance of the pore is negligible compared to the access resistance, and its conductance increases linearly with the diameter at a constant electrolyte concentration. In addition, as the channel’s vertex angle increases, the pore behavior approaches that of a 2D pore. In this study, we introduce the concept of a channel’s critical length to elucidate the underlying physics of zero-depth pore behavior and establish the conditions for membrane thickness independence. We formulated an equation demonstrating that the critical length is influenced by both the channel’s vertex angle and the pore size. Reduction in the total resistance of the pore, consequently decreasing the ratio of the critical length to pore diameter. We further investigated the electric field intensity within the pore region along the coordinate axes. This is a crucial parameter that affects pore sensitivity and particle dwell time. Our results indicated that the maximum electric field is located at the mid-plane of the pore—a 2D square plane at the middle of the pore region. Notably, reducing the pore size or increasing the vertex angle results in a more rapid change in electrical potential over shorter distances, thereby enhancing the electric field intensity. Using derived equations aligned with our numerical findings, we can predict the maximum electric field and effective length as functions of applied electrical potential, pore diameter, and the vertex angle of the channels. However, further investigations reveal that the electric field intensity at the mid-points of the mid-plane’s sides surpasses that at the origin. This observation suggests that particle translocation along the Y-axis or off-axis may yield significantly different signal ratios, warranting further study. Future research should delve into particle translocation dynamics through 3D TCZD pores using multiphysics modeling and assess the feasibility of molecular dynamics-guided DNA sequencing using these innovative nanopores.
