Introduction

The study of fine properties of functions in Sobolev and Besov spaces has played a central role in real and functional analysis, particularly in understanding the behavior of functions at small scales and in non-smooth settings. One of the foundational results in this direction is the classical Lebesgue differentiation theorem, which asserts that almost every point of a locally integrable function is a Lebesgue point. This result connects the integral properties of a function to its pointwise behavior and serves as a cornerstone in modern analysis. One can find proof of Lebesgue differentiation theorem in
[
4
,
5
]
.

In the context of function spaces that encode smoothness, such as Sobolev,
BV
, or Besov spaces, whose elements are locally integrable and therefore have a set of non-Lebesgue points of measure zero with respect to the underlying measure (Lebesgue measure in Euclidean space), it is natural to ask whether the set of non-Lebesgue points is in fact smaller than merely having measure zero, due to the additional regularity. If so, one may ask how small this set can be. In other words, if a function is not only locally integrable but also possesses some degree of regularity, does it follow that the set of its non-Lebesgue points becomes smaller?

One importance of such knowledge arises in the study of traces of measurable functions, for example in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Suppose we have a measurable function on a Euclidean domain, such as a solution to a partial differential equation, which we need to restrict (trace) to the boundary of the domain or to a surface of dimension less than
N
. In general, this restriction is meaningless unless we know how small the set of non-Lebesgue points is, for example in terms of capacity, Hausdorff measure, or Hausdorff dimension.

For instance, it is known that Sobolev functions
u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
have Lebesgue points outside a set of
\cp_{p}
-measure zero
[

,

,

,

; a similar result holds for functions in Bessel potential spaces

[

; for

-rectifiable set is a Lebesgue point

,

Metric measure spaces equipped with a Borel measure satisfying certain regularity assumptions, such as Ahlfors regularity, provide a general framework to extend many classical results of analysis beyond the Euclidean space. In such settings, questions about the local behavior of functions become more subtle due to the lack of linear structure. Nevertheless, a considerable amount of work in the past decades has aimed to extend Sobolev-type theories, including differentiation and capacity estimates, to such spaces, see for example

In this paper, we study the fine properties of functions

By

Our main focus is the characterization of the set of Lebesgue points of such functions (

\phi_{10\delta}(A)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\zeta(B_{5\rho_{i}}(x_{i}))\leq\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\zeta(B_{\rho_{i}}(x_{i}))\leq\frac{\lambda}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu\big(B_{\rho_{i}}(x_{i})\big)=\frac{\lambda}{t}\mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}B_{\rho_{i}}(x_{i})\right)\leq\frac{\lambda}{t}\mu(\Omega).

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a metric space. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{n}
be a Borel measure on

X
. Let
t\in[0,\infty)
, and let
r_{n}\downarrow 0
be a sequence
of positive numbers. Let

A\subset\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<r_{n}}\frac{\mu_{n}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)\geq t}\right\}.

(3.4)

Then, for every open set
\Omega
containing
A
we get

\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)\right)\geq t\psi(A).

(3.5)

Proof.

We can assume that
t>0
, otherwise (
3.5
) trivially holds. Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

A_{n,\beta}:=\left\{{x\in A}\,\middle|\,{\sup_{0<\rho<r_{n}}\frac{\mu_{n}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}>t\beta}\right\}.

(3.6)

By assumption (
3.4
), we have

A=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}.

(3.7)

Indee, by definition, we have
A\supset\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}
.
For the reverse inclusion, let
x\in A
and assume, by contradiction, that

x\notin\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}
.
Then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

x\notin\bigcap_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}
.
Hence, there exists a sequence of indices
\{j_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
with
j_{l}\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
such that

x\notin A_{j_{l},\beta}
, for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore, we obtain

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<r_{n}}\frac{\mu_{n}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)\leq\liminf_{l\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<r_{j_{l}}}\frac{\mu_{j_{l}}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)\leq t\beta<t.

(3.8)

This contradicts (
3.4
). It proves (
3.7
). Since
\psi
is a Borel regular measure and therefore has the monotonicity property (even when the sets are not
\psi
-measurable), we obtain from (
3.7
)

\psi(A)=\psi\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}\right)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\psi\left(\bigcap_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}\right).

(3.9)

Applying Lemma
3.2
(with
\Omega=X
), for every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m\geq n
, we obtain

\phi_{10r_{m}}\left(\bigcap_{j=n}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}\right)\leq\phi_{10r_{m}}(A_{m,\beta})\\
\leq\phi_{10r_{m}}\left(\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\sup_{0<\rho<r_{m}}\frac{\mu_{m}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}>t\beta}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda}{t\beta}\mu_{m}(X).

(3.10)

Taking the lower limit as
m\to\infty
in (
3.10
) and using that
\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\phi_{\delta}=\psi
, we get

\psi\left(\bigcap_{j=n}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda}{t\beta}\left(\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mu_{m}(X)\right).

(3.11)

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
in (
3.11
) and using (
3.9
), we get

\psi(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi\left(\bigcap_{j=n}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda}{t\beta}\left(\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mu_{m}(X)\right).

(3.12)

Since
\beta\in(0,1)
is arbitrary, taking the limit as
\beta\to 1^{-}
in (
3.12
), we get

\psi(A)\leq\frac{\lambda}{t}\left(\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mu_{m}(X)\right).

(3.13)

If
\Omega\subset X
is an open set containing
A
, we may replace the measures
\mu_{m}
by the measures
\mu_{m}^{\Omega}
defined by
\mu_{m}^{\Omega}(E):=\mu_{m}(\Omega\cap E)
. Note that, since
\Omega
is open and
A\subset\Omega
, the condition (
3.4
) still holds for these modified measures. So we get for every open set
\Omega
with
A\subset\Omega\subset X

t\psi(A)\leq\lambda\left(\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mu^{\Omega}_{m}(X)\right)=\lambda\left(\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mu_{m}(\Omega)\right).

∎

Remark 3.1
.

Recall that for a measure
\mu
on a metric space
X
, and for
s\in(0,\infty)
, the upper and lower
s
-dimensional densities of
\mu
at
x\in X
are respectively defined, up to multiplication by a positive constant, by

\Theta^{*}_{s}(\mu,x):=\limsup_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left(B_{\rho}(x)\right)}{\rho^{s}},\quad\Theta_{*s}(\mu,x):=\liminf_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left(B_{\rho}(x)\right)}{\rho^{s}}.

(3.14)

If
\Theta^{*}_{s}(\mu,x)=\Theta_{*s}(\mu,x)
, then their common value is denoted by
\Theta_{s}(\mu,x)
.

We refer to the inequality in (
3.4
) as a
density-type condition
, as it generalizes the usual upper density in the case where all measures in the sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are equal to the same measure and
\zeta(E):=\left(\diam(E)/2\right)^{s}
.

The following lemma is a generalization of Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{n}
be a Borel measure on
X
. Let
\{r_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive numbers with
\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=0
. Assume the existence of an open set
\Omega\subset X
, a set
\Theta\subset X
with
\psi(\Theta)=0
, and
\psi
-measurable function
g:\Omega\to[0,\infty]
, such that

\Omega\setminus\Theta\subset\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<r_{n}}\frac{\mu_{n}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)\geq g(x)}\right\}.

(3.15)

Then,

\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)\right)\geq\int_{\Omega}g(x)\,d\psi(x).

(3.16)

Proof.

The idea is to first prove inequality (
3.16
) for simple functions
h
that are bounded above by
g
, and then use the definition of the Lebesgue integral to derive inequality (
3.16
). Let
h=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\chi_{B_{i}}
be a function where
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
\beta_{i}
are positive numbers, the
B_{i}\subset\Omega
are pairwise disjoint
\psi
-measurable sets, and
\chi_{B_{i}}
denotes the characteristic function of
B_{i}
. Assume that
g(x)\geq h(x)
for
\psi
-almost every
x\in\Omega
. In particular, for each
i=1,\dots,k
, we have
g(x)\geq\beta_{i}
for
\psi
-almost every
x\in B_{i}
.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)<\infty
, otherwise the inequality (
3.16
) trivially holds. Therefore, by Lemma
3.3
, we obtain

\beta_{i}\psi(B_{i})\leq\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)\right)<\infty.

Since
\psi
is a Borel regular measure,
B_{i}
is a
\psi
-measurable set and
\psi(B_{i})<\infty
, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a closed set
C_{i}\subset B_{i}
such that
\psi(B_{i}\setminus C_{i})<\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{i}k}
. Since
C_{i}\subset B_{i}
,
C_{i}
is
\psi
-measurable and
\psi(C_{i})<\infty
, we obtain

\psi(B_{i})-\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{i}k}<\psi(C_{i}).

(3.17)

Note that since the sets
B_{i}
are pairwise disjoint and
C_{i}\subset B_{i}
, the closed sets
C_{i}
are also pairwise disjoint. As
X
is a metric space, it is normal; hence, there exist pairwise disjoint open sets
U_{i}
such that
C_{i}\subset U_{i}\subset\Omega
. Applying Lemma
3.3
again, we get

\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(U_{i})\right)\geq\beta_{i}\psi(C_{i}).

(3.18)

Therefore, since
\mu_{n}
is a Borel measure, we get from (
3.18
) and (
3.17
)

\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)\right)\geq\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}\right)\right)=\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{n}(U_{i})\right)\\
\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(U_{i})\right)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\psi(C_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\left(\psi(B_{i})-\frac{\epsilon}{\beta_{i}k}\right)\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\psi(B_{i})-\epsilon=\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,d\psi(x)-\epsilon.

(3.19)

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\lambda\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)\right)\geq\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,d\psi(x).

(3.20)

Therefore, by the definition of the Lebesgue integral (approximating
g
from below by simple functions), we obtain (
3.16
) for the
\psi
–measurable function
g
.
∎

4
Fine properties of general functions with respect to the Carathéodory resulting measure
\psi

The following lemma is a consequence of Lemma
3.2
and is independent of the results proved subsequently. We will use it in the proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be a metric space. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{n}
be a Borel measure on

X
. Let

A\subset\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<\delta}\frac{\mu_{n}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)>0}\right\}.

(4.1)

Assume the existence of an open set
\Omega
containing
A
such that

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}(\Omega)<\infty.

(4.2)

Then we have

\psi(A)=0.

Proof.

For
\beta>0
,
\delta>0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

A_{n,\beta,\delta}:=\left\{{x\in A}\,\middle|\,{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\rho<\delta\\
B_{\rho}(x)\subset\Omega\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{n}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}>\beta}\right\}.

(4.3)

Define

O:=\bigcup_{\beta>0}\bigcap_{\delta>0}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\delta}.

(4.4)

We will show that the property (
4.2
) gives
\psi\left(O\right)=0
, and the property (
4.1
) gives
A=O
. Therefore,
\psi(A)=\psi(O)=0
.

We prove
\psi(O)=0
. By Lemma
3.2

\phi_{10\delta}(A_{n,\beta,\delta})\leq\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\mu_{n}(\Omega).

(4.5)

Therefore, by
\sigma
-subaditivity of
\phi_{10\delta}
, estimate (
4.5
), and assumption (
4.2
), we obtain for
k\in\mathbb{N}

\phi_{10\delta}\left(\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\delta}\right)\leq\sum\limits_{j=k}^{\infty}\phi_{10\delta}\left(A_{j,\beta,\delta}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\sum\limits_{j=k}^{\infty}\mu_{j}(\Omega)<\infty.

(4.6)

Thus, by (
4.6
), we get

\phi_{10\delta}\left(\bigcap_{\xi>0}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\xi}\right)\leq\phi_{10\delta}\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\delta}\right)\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\sum\limits_{j=k}^{\infty}\mu_{j}(\Omega)=0.

(4.7)

Then, letting
\delta\to 0^{+}
and using the definition
\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\phi_{\delta}=\psi
, we obtain

\psi\left(\bigcap_{\xi>0}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\xi}\right)=0.

(4.8)

Therefore, since
A_{j,\beta^{\prime},\xi}\subset A_{j,\beta,\xi}
for
0<\beta\leq\beta^{\prime}<\infty
, and fixed
j,\xi
, we get

\psi(O)=\psi\left(\bigcup_{\beta>0}\bigcap_{\xi>0}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\xi}\right)=\psi\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{\xi>0}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,1/i,\xi}\right)=0.

(4.9)

We prove
A=O
. By the definition of
O
, we get
O\subset A
. For the opposite inclusion, let
x\in A
. Assume by contradiction that
x\notin O
. Then
x\notin\bigcap_{\xi>0}\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\xi}
for every
\beta>0
. Then, there exist
\xi,k
such that
x\notin\bigcup_{j=k}^{\infty}A_{j,\beta,\xi}
. Therefore, for every natural number
j\geq k

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\rho<\xi\\
B_{\rho}(x)\subset\Omega\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{j}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\leq\beta.

(4.10)

Therefore,

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<\delta}\frac{\mu_{j}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\rho<\delta\\
B_{\rho}(x)\subset\Omega\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{j}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)\\
\leq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\rho<\xi\\
B_{\rho}(x)\subset\Omega\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{j}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)\leq\beta.

(4.11)

Since
\beta>0
is arbitrarily small, we get that

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left(\sup_{0<\rho<\delta}\frac{\mu_{j}\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}{\zeta\big(B_{\rho}(x)\big)}\right)=0.

(4.12)

This contradicts (
4.1
).
This completes the proof.
∎

The following set-theoretic lemma will help us easily recognize the Borel measurability of functions that appear in the sequel.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
be a metric space. Let
\Sigma
denote the family of all closed balls in
X
, and let
F:\Sigma\to[0,\infty)
be a set function satisfying the following two properties: for
A,B\in\Sigma
, if
A\subset B
, then
F(A)\leq F(B)
; and for every
x\in X
and
r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
,

\lim_{r\downarrow r_{0}}F(B_{r}(x))=F(B_{r_{0}}(x)).

(4.13)

Fix
r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
and define

h(x):=F(B_{r_{0}}(x)).

(4.14)

Then
h
is upper semicontinuous and therefore Borel measurable.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
and
x_{0}\in X
such that
x_{j}\to x_{0}
as
j\to\infty
. Fix
\delta>0
. Then there exists
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

B_{r_{0}}(x_{j})\subset B_{r_{0}+\delta}(x_{0})\quad\text{for every }j\geq j_{0}.

Hence, by the definition of
h
and the monotonicity of
F
, we obtain

\limsup_{j\to\infty}h(x_{j})=\limsup_{j\to\infty}F(B_{r_{0}}(x_{j}))\leq F(B_{r_{0}+\delta}(x_{0})).

(4.15)

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
and using the assumed right-continuity of
r\mapsto F(B_{r}(x_{0}))
, we conclude that

\limsup_{j\to\infty}h(x_{j})\leq h(x_{0}).

Thus,
h
is upper semicontinuous. Any upper semicontinuous function
f
from topological space
Z
to
\mathbb{R}
is Borel measurable, since upper semicontinuity implies that the upper level sets

\left\{{z\in Z}\,\middle|\,{f(z)\geq a}\right\}=f^{-1}\left([a,\infty)\right)

are closed for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and hence Borel. Therefore,
f
is a Borel function.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
X
be a metric space. Let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
X
such that
0<\mu(B)<\infty
for every closed ball
B=B_{\sigma}(x)
,
x\in X
,
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
. Let
0\leq W\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X\times X,\mu\times\mu)
. Define

G(x):=\fint_{B_{\sigma}(x)}\fint_{B_{\sigma}(x)}W(z,y)\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(y).

(4.16)

Then
G
is Borel measurable.

Proof.

Define

F_{1}(B_{\sigma}(x)):=\int_{B_{\sigma}(x)}\int_{B_{\sigma}(x)}W(z,y)\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(y),\qquad F_{2}(B_{\sigma}(x)):=\mu(B_{\sigma}(x)).

Both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
satisfy the two assumptions of Lemma
4.2
; the property (
4.13
) holds since the balls
B_{\sigma}(x)
are closed,
\mu
is finite on balls and
W
is locally integrable. More precisely,

\lim_{\sigma\downarrow\sigma_{0}}F_{1}(B_{\sigma}(x))=\int_{\bigcap\limits_{\sigma>\sigma_{0}}B_{\sigma}(x)\times B_{\sigma}(x)}W(z,y)\,d\left(\mu\times\mu\right)(z,y)=F_{1}(B_{\sigma_{0}}(x));

(4.17)

\lim_{\sigma\downarrow\sigma_{0}}F_{2}(B_{\sigma}(x))=\mu\left(\bigcap\limits_{\sigma>\sigma_{0}}B_{\sigma}(x)\right)=F_{2}(B_{\sigma_{0}}(x)).

(4.18)

Hence, the functions

x\mapsto F_{1}(B_{\sigma}(x))\quad\text{and}\quad x\mapsto F_{2}(B_{\sigma}(x))

are upper semicontinuous and therefore Borel measurable. As
\mu(B_{\sigma}(x))>0
for every
x\in X
, we conclude that

G(x)=\frac{F_{1}(B_{\sigma}(x))}{F_{2}(B_{\sigma}(x))^{2}}

is Borel measurable.
∎

The following theorem provides an abstract framework for establishing certain fine properties of a general function
W
with respect to any measure
\psi
obtained via Carathéodory’s construction. In the sequel, we will choose
W(z,y):=\rho(u(z),u(y))^{q}

to be the Hausdorff measure in the case of Besov functions
u\in B^{r}_{q,\infty}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
\Omega\subset X
be an open subset.
Let
\mu
be an Ahlfors
s
-regular measure on
\Omega
for some
s\in(0,\infty)
. Consider any sequences
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\varepsilon_{n}>0
,
\eta_{n}>0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
, and let

W\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega\times\Omega,\mu\times\mu)
with
W\geq 0
. Finally,
assume that
\zeta
satisfies, in addition to (
2.3
) and (
2.2
) the following:

1.

There exist constants
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in(0,\infty]

\zeta(B_{R}(x))\leq\lambda^{\prime}\,\zeta(B_{R}(y))\quad\forall y\in B_{R/2}(x).

(4.19)

2.

For every

f_{R}(x):=\zeta(B_{R}(x))\quad\text{is $\mu$-measurable in $X$}.

(4.20)

Then:

1. There exists a constant

C(\zeta,\mu)
, depending on
\zeta,\mu
only, such that

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\eta_{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{s}_{n}}\zeta\big(B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)\big)\Bigg\{\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\chi_{\Omega}(z)W(z,x)d\mu(z)\Bigg\}d\mu(x)\right)\\
\geq C(\zeta,\mu)\int_{\Omega}\Bigg\{\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\eta_{n}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}W(z,y)d\mu(z)d\mu(y)\Bigg\}d\psi(x)\,.

(4.21)

2. If the left hand side of (

) is finite, then the set
D\subset\Omega

D=\left\{{x\in\Omega}\,\middle|\,{\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\eta_{n}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}W(z,y)d\mu(z)d\mu(y)\right)>0}\right\}

is
\sigma

\psi

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\eta_{n}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}W(z,y)d\mu(z)d\mu(y)\right)=0.

(4.23)

3. If we have

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\eta_{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{s}_{n}}\zeta\big(B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)\big)\Bigg\{\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\chi_{\Omega}(z)W(z,x)d\mu(z)\Bigg\}d\mu(x)\right)<\infty\,,

(4.24)

then the set
A\subset\Omega
defined by

A:=\left\{{x\in\Omega}\,\middle|\,{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\eta_{n}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}W(z,y)d\mu(z)d\mu(y)\right)>0}\right\}

(4.25)

is negligible with respect to
\psi
, i.e.
\psi(A)=0
. In particular, for
\psi
–almost every
x\in\Omega
, we have

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\eta_{n}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x)}W(z,y)d\mu(z)d\mu(y)\right)=0.

(4.26)

Proof.

Proof of part 1:

We prove inequality (
4.21
). Since
\mu
is Ahlfors
s
-regular, there exist constants
c,C
such that for every
x\in\Omega
and for every
0<r<\infty
with

cr^{s}\leq\mu(B_{r}(x))\leq Cr^{s}.

(4.27)

Note that for every
x,y\in\Omega
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we get from condition (

)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{s}}=\frac{1}{2^{s}(\varepsilon_{n}/2)^{s}}\geq\frac{c}{2^{s}\mu(B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x))},\qquad\frac{1}{\mu(B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(y))}\geq\frac{1}{C\varepsilon_{n}^{s}}\geq\frac{c}{C\,2^{s}\mu(B_{\varepsilon_{n}/2}(x))}.

(4.28)

Observe that for every

A particularly important case of Carathéodory’s construction is the Hausdorff measure, which we define separately below:

Definition 5.1

(Definition of the Hausdorff measure)
.

Let

The following lemma will help us determine the Borel measurability of a function that will appear in Theorem

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

be a metric space. Let

\sup_{0<\sigma<\sigma_{0}}\frac{\varphi\big(B_{\sigma}(x)\big)}{h(\sigma)}=\sup_{0<\sigma<\sigma_{0},\ \sigma\in\mathbb{Q}}\frac{\varphi\big(B_{\sigma}(x)\big)}{h(\sigma)}.

(6.1)

In particular, if
\varphi
is a Borel measure and finite on bounded sets, then the function

Q(x):=\sup_{0<\sigma<\sigma_{0}}\frac{\varphi\big(B_{\sigma}(x)\big)}{h(\sigma)}

is Borel measurable.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in(0,\sigma_{0})

\sigma_{n}\in(0,\sigma_{0})\cap\mathbb{Q}

(6.3)

Since (
6.3
) holds for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
h
is continuous at
\sigma

\frac{\varphi\big(B_{\sigma}(x)\big)}{h(\sigma)}\leq\sup_{0<q<\sigma_{0},\ q\in\mathbb{Q}}\frac{\varphi\big(B_{q}(x)\big)}{h(q)}.

(6.4)

Since
\sigma\in(0,\sigma_{0})
is arbitrary, we obtain

\sup_{0<\sigma<\sigma_{0}}\frac{\varphi\big(B_{\sigma}(x)\big)}{h(\sigma)}\leq\sup_{0<q<\sigma_{0},\ q\in\mathbb{Q}}\frac{\varphi\big(B_{q}(x)\big)}{h(q)}.

7
The logarithmic Hausdorff measure

In this section, we introduce an auxiliary measure, which we call the logarithmic Hausdorff measure. We will use it to prove Lemma
8.3
, which is the main ingredient in establishing an upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of non-Lebesgue points of Besov functions, as stated in Theorem
8.5
.

The following definition is a special case of Carathéodory’s construction. It gives rise to a measure that we call the
logarithmic Hausdorff measure
, which we denote by the Greek letter
\Lambda
. More precisely,

Definition 7.1

(Definition of the logarithmic Hausdorff measure)
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
s\in[0,\infty)
,
\delta\in(0,\infty]
,
\beta\in(0,\infty)
. The
s
-dimensional approximating logarithmic Hausdorff measure of size
\delta
and decreasing factor
\beta
denoted as
\Lambda^{s,\beta}_{\delta}
is defined for every
E\subset X
by

\Lambda^{s,\beta}_{\delta}(E):=\inf\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}g_{s,\beta}(\diam E_{i})}\,\middle|\,{E\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i},\,\diam(E_{i})\leq\delta}\right\},

where

g_{s,\beta}:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty],\quad g_{s,\beta}(r):=\begin{cases}\frac{r^{s}}{|\ln r|^{\beta}}\quad&if\quad r\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}\\
0\quad&if\quad r=0\\
\infty\quad&if\quad r\in\{1,\infty\}\end{cases}.

(7.2)

The
s
-dimensional logarithmic Hausdorff measure with decreasing factor
\beta
is defined by

\Lambda^{s,\beta}(E)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\Lambda^{s,\beta}_{\delta}(E).

We refer to
\beta
as a
decreasing factor
, since, for every small enough
\delta
, the larger
\beta
is, the smaller the function
g_{s,\beta}
becomes, and hence the smaller the measure
\Lambda^{s,\beta}_{\delta}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
X
be a metric space,

, and
\beta\in(0,\infty)
. Let

be the logarithmic measure on

. Then the measure
\Lambda^{s,\beta}

, where

is defined in (

\zeta
satisfies the four conditions
(
2.2
), (
2.3
), (
4.19
), and (
4.20
).

Proof.

Notice that, by definition,
\Lambda^{s,\beta}
is indeed obtained from Carathéodory’s construction associated with

\zeta
is defined on all subsets of

. For condition (

=\frac{(10\sigma)^{s}}{|\ln\sigma+\ln 10|^{\beta}}\frac{|\ln\sigma+\ln 2|^{\beta}}{(2\sigma)^{s}}=5^{s}\left(\frac{\left|-1+\frac{\ln 2}{|\ln\sigma|}\right|}{\left|-1+\frac{\ln 10}{|\ln\sigma|}\right|}\right)^{\beta}\leq 5^{s}2^{\beta}.

for every set
E
satisfying

0<\diam(E)\leq\delta
. Therefore,

(\diam(E))^{s}|\ln(\diam(E))|^{-\beta}\leq(\diam(E))^{s}.

Thus,
g_{s,\beta}(\diam(E))\leq(\diam(E))^{s}
for every set
E
with

\diam(E)\leq\delta
(including the case
\diam(E)=0
). Using this inequality, for every
\delta
–cover
\{E_{i}\}
of a set
A
, we obtain

\sum_{i}(\diam(E_{i}))^{s}|\ln(\diam(E_{i}))|^{-\beta}\leq\sum_{i}(\diam(E_{i}))^{s}.

Taking the infimum over all such covers yields (
7.6
). Passing to the limit as
\delta\to 0^{+}
in (
7.6
), we obtain (
7.7
).
∎

Definition 7.4

(Hausdorff dimension and logarithmic Hausdorff dimension)
.

Let
X
be a metric space.
For a set
E\subset X
and
\beta\in(0,\infty)
, define the Hausdorff dimension of
E
and the logarithmic Hausdorff dimension of
E
, respectively, by

\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}_{\dim}E:=\inf\left\{{s\geq 0}\,\middle|\,{\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=0}\right\}=\sup\left\{{s\geq 0}\,\middle|\,{\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\infty}\right\},\\
\Lambda_{\dim}E:=\inf\left\{{s\geq 0}\,\middle|\,{\Lambda^{s,\beta}(E)=0}\right\}=\sup\left\{{s\geq 0}\,\middle|\,{\Lambda^{s,\beta}(E)=\infty}\right\}.\end{cases}

(7.8)

Note that we omit
\beta
in the notation
\Lambda_{\dim}E
. The justification is given in the following proposition, which states that the logarithmic Hausdorff measure and the usual Hausdorff measure determine the same dimension:

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
X
be a metric space, let
\beta\in(0,\infty)
,
s\in[0,\infty)
, and let
E\subset X
.
Then the Hausdorff dimension defined via
\mathcal{H}^{s}
coincides with the dimension defined via the logarithmic Hausdorff measure
\Lambda^{s,\beta}
:

\mathcal{H}_{\dim}E=\Lambda_{\dim}E.

(7.9)

In particular, the logarithmic dimension does not depend on
\beta
.

Proof.

Fix
\beta\in(0,\infty)
. Let
s>0
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. There exists
r_{0}=r_{0}(\varepsilon,\beta)
such that
for all
0<r<r_{0}
,

r^{s+\varepsilon}\;\leq\;\frac{r^{s}}{|\ln r|^{\beta}}\;\leq\;r^{s-\varepsilon}.

(7.10)

Inequalities (
7.10
) follow from
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}r^{\varepsilon}|\ln r|^{\beta}=0,\,\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}r^{-\varepsilon}|\ln r|^{\beta}=\infty.

Let
\delta\in(0,r_{0})
and let
\{E_{i}\}
be a
\delta
–cover of
E
.
Using (
7.10
), we obtain

\sum_{i}\diam(E_{i})^{s+\varepsilon}\;\leq\;\sum_{i}g_{s,\beta}(\diam(E_{i}))\;\leq\;\sum_{i}\diam(E_{i})^{s-\varepsilon}.

Taking the infimum over all such covers and then letting
\delta\to 0^{+}
yields

\mathcal{H}^{s+\varepsilon}(E)\;\leq\;\Lambda^{s,\beta}(E)\;\leq\;\mathcal{H}^{s-\varepsilon}(E).

(7.11)

Let
d:=\mathcal{H}_{\dim}E
. If
s>d
, choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
s-\varepsilon>d
. Then
\mathcal{H}^{s-\varepsilon}(E)=0
, and by (
7.11
) we obtain
\Lambda^{s,\beta}(E)=0
. Hence
\Lambda_{\dim}E\leq s
. Since
s
is an arbitrary number greater than
d
, we get
\Lambda_{\dim}E\leq d
. If
s<d
, choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
s+\varepsilon<d
. Then
\mathcal{H}^{s+\varepsilon}(E)=\infty
, and again by (
7.11
) we have
\Lambda^{s,\beta}(E)=\infty
. Hence
\Lambda_{\dim}E\geq s
. Since
s
is an arbitrary number less than
d
, we get
\Lambda_{\dim}E\geq d
. Combining the two inequalities yields (
7.9
).
∎

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=0
. Let
\{\ell_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
be any sequence such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell_{n}=\infty
. Then,

\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{\ell_{n}}=0.

(8.1)

The point of the lemma is that
A_{\ell_{n}}
is not necessarily a subsequence of
A_{n}
, since we do not assume that
\ell_{n}<\ell_{n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
N_{0}
such that
|A_{n}|<\varepsilon
for all
n>N_{0}
. Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell_{n}=\infty
, there exists
L
such that
\ell_{n}>N_{0}
for all
n>L
. Thus, for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
L
such that for every
n>L
we have
|A_{\ell_{n}}|<\varepsilon
. Therefore,
\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{\ell_{n}}=0
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
X
be a metric space. Let
\Sigma
be the collection of all closed balls in
X
. Let
F:\Sigma\to[0,\infty]
be a monotone increasing set function, meaning that
A\subset B
,
A,B\in\Sigma
, implies
F(A)\leq F(B)
. Fix
\xi\in(0,\infty)
and
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
. Define

D_{seq}:=\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{n^{\alpha}}{\left(e^{-n}\right)^{\xi}}F\left(B_{e^{-n}}(x)\right)\right)>0}\right\},

(8.2)

and

D:=\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\limsup_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\left(\frac{|\ln\sigma|^{\alpha}}{\sigma^{\xi}}F\left(B_{\sigma}(x)\right)\right)>0}\right\}.

(8.3)

Then,

D=D_{seq}.

(8.4)

Proof.

Note that
n^{\alpha}=|\ln e^{-n}|^{\alpha}
. Therefore, by the definition of the upper limit, we have
D_{seq}\subset D
. We now show that
X\setminus D_{seq}\subset X\setminus D
, and therefore
D\subset D_{seq}
. Let
x\in X\setminus D_{seq}
. We need to prove that for every sequence
\sigma_{n}
of positive numbers converging to zero, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{|\ln\sigma_{n}|^{\alpha}}{\sigma_{n}^{\xi}}F\left(B_{\sigma_{n}}(x)\right)\right)=0.

(8.5)

Without loss of generality assume that
\sigma_{n}\leq e^{-1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
choose
\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

e^{-(\ell_{n}+1)}<\sigma_{n}\leq e^{-\ell_{n}}.

(8.6)

Thus, using (
8.6
) and the monotonicity of
F
, we obtain

\left|\ln\sigma_{n}\right|^{\alpha}\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{\xi}}F\left(B_{\sigma_{n}}(x)\right)\leq|\ell_{n}+1|^{\alpha}\left(e^{(\ell_{n}+1)}\right)^{\xi}F\left(B_{e^{-\ell_{n}}}(x)\right)\\
=e^{\xi}\left|1+\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\right|^{\alpha}\left(\frac{\ell_{n}^{\alpha}}{\left(e^{-\ell_{n}}\right)^{\xi}}F\left(B_{e^{-\ell_{n}}}(x)\right)\right).

(8.7)

Note that by (
8.6
) and since
\sigma_{n}
tends to zero when
n\to\infty
, we get
\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell_{n}=\infty
, and therefore, by the assumption that
x\notin D_{seq}
and Lemma
8.1
, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{\ell_{n}^{\alpha}}{\left(e^{-\ell_{n}}\right)^{\xi}}F\left(B_{e^{-\ell_{n}}}(x)\right)\right)=0.

(8.8)

Therefore, taking the limit as
n\to\infty
in (
8.7
) and using (
8.8
) and that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell_{n}=\infty
, we obtain (
8.5
). We conclude that
x\notin D
.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
X
be a metric space, and let
\mu
be an Ahlfors
s
-regular measure on
X
for some
s\in(0,\infty)
. Let

,

be such that

(Y,\rho)
be a metric space, and let

D_{\alpha}:=\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left(|\ln\varepsilon|^{\alpha}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u(y)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(y)\right)>0}\right\}.

(8.9)

Then,

D_{\alpha}=\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{\alpha}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u(y)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(y)>0}\right\},\quad\varepsilon_{n}:=e^{-n},

and if

In particular, the Hausdorff dimension of

Proof.

We intend to use the third part of Theorem

.
Let us set

W(z,y):=\rho\big(u(z),u(y)\big)^{q},\quad\eta_{n}:=n^{\alpha},\quad\varepsilon_{n}:=e^{-n},\quad\zeta(A):=g_{s-rq,\beta}\!\left(\diam(A)\right),

(8.12)

where

is defined in (

, our choice of

), (

), (

\frac{\eta_{n}}{(2\varepsilon_{n})^{s}}\zeta\big(B_{(2\varepsilon_{n})}(x)\big)=\frac{n^{\alpha}}{(2\varepsilon_{n})^{s}}g_{s-rq,\beta}(4\varepsilon_{n})=\frac{n^{\alpha}}{(2\varepsilon_{n})^{s}}\frac{(4\varepsilon_{n})^{s-rq}}{|\ln(4\varepsilon_{n})|^{\beta}}\\

Thus we can apply the

Since

C^{-2}(\varepsilon_{n})^{-2s}\leq\mu(B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x))^{-2}\leq c^{-2}(\varepsilon_{n})^{-2s}.

Thus, (

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
(Y,\rho)
be a metric space. Let
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\rho(v_{n},v_{n+1})<\infty.

(8.21)

Then
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that

\sum_{n=M}^{\infty}\rho(v_{n},v_{n+1})<\varepsilon.

Therefore, for every
j\geq M
and every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, by the triangle inequality we obtain

\rho(v_{j},v_{j+m})\leq\sum_{n=j}^{j+m-1}\rho(v_{n},v_{n+1})\leq\sum_{n=M}^{\infty}\rho(v_{n},v_{n+1})<\varepsilon.

(8.22)

Thus,
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy.

The following theorem shows that for
\beta>q+1
,
\Lambda^{s-rq,\beta}
–almost every point is a Lebesgue point of a Besov function in the class
B^{r}_{q,\infty}
. In other words, the exceptional set of non–-Lebesgue points has
\Lambda^{s-rq,\beta}
–measure zero. Consequently, its logarithmic Hausdorff dimension is at most
s-rq
, and therefore its (usual) Hausdorff dimension is also at most
s-rq
, that proves Theorem
1.7
. More precisely:

Theorem 8.5

(Lebesgue points of Besov functions
B^{r}_{q,\infty}
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space, and let
\mu
be an Ahlfors
s
-regular measure on
X
for some
s\in(0,\infty)
. Let
0\leq r\leq 1
,
0<q<\infty
be such that
rq\leq s
. Let
(Y,\rho)
be a complete metric space, and let
u\in B^{r}_{q,\infty}(X,\mu;Y)
. Let us denote

\mathcal{N}:=\left\{{x\in X}\,\middle|\,{\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),y\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)>0\quad\forall y\in Y}\right\}.

(8.23)

If
\beta>q+1
, then

\Lambda^{s-rq,\beta}(\mathcal{N})=0.

(8.24)

In particular, for every
x\in X\setminus\mathcal{N}
, there exists
u^{*}(x)\in Y
, such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left(\fint_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u^{*}(x)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)=0.

(8.25)

Therefore, almost every point is a Lebesgue point of
u
with respect to the measure
\Lambda^{s-rq,\beta}
. In particular, the exceptional set
\mathcal{N}
of points that are not Lebesgue points has Hausdorff dimension at most
s-rq
.

Proof.

Assume that
\beta>q+1
. Then, there exists
\alpha>q
with
\beta>\alpha+1
. Let
D_{\alpha}
be as in Lemma
8.3
. We prove first that for every
x\in X\setminus D_{\alpha}
, there exists
u^{*}(x)\in Y
such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left(\fint_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u^{*}(x)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)=0.

(8.26)

Let
x\in X\setminus D_{\alpha}
. Then, by (
8.10
) of
Lemma
8.3
, and raising to the power
1/q
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\alpha/q}\left(\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u(w)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(w)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=0,\quad\varepsilon_{n}:=e^{-n}.

(8.27)

In particular, using that
\alpha>q
, we get from (

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u(w)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(w)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty.

(8.28)

Obtaining the finiteness of the series in (

n^{\alpha/q}
, with
\alpha>q
, which arises from the expression of

in (

This finiteness will enable us to construct, for a given point

, a Cauchy sequence
\{v_{n}\}
in

By monotonicity of integral

\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),u(w)\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(w)\geq\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\left(\inf\limits_{v\in Y}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),v\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)d\mu(w)\\
=\inf\limits_{v\in Y}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),v\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z).

(8.29)

Therefore, by (
8.28
) and (
8.29
) we have

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\inf\limits_{v\in Y}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),v\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty.

By the definition of the infimum, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
v_{n}\in Y

\left(\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),v_{n}\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\left(\inf\limits_{v\in Y}\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),v\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}+2^{-n},

(8.31)

and therefore, from (

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\fint_{B_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)}\rho\left(u(z),v_{n}\right)^{q}\,d\mu(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty.

By the triangle inequality we have for
\mu

Therefore, from (

Therefore, by (

8.40
) give

X\setminus D_{\alpha}\subset X\setminus\mathcal{N}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{N}\subset D_{\alpha}.

(8.41)

Therefore, by Lemma
8.3
, since
\beta>\alpha+1
, we get

\Lambda^{s-rq,\beta}(\mathcal{N})\leq\Lambda^{s-rq,\beta}(D_{\alpha})=0,

(8.42)

and
\mathcal{H}_{\dim}D_{\alpha}\leq s-rq
.
By (
8.41

\mathcal{H}_{\dim}\mathcal{N}\leq s-rq
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8.1
.

The result of Theorem
8.5
holds immediately for functions in the class
W^{r,q}(X,\mu;Y)
, where
0<r<1
and
0<q<\infty
, because
W^{r,q}(X,\mu;Y)\subset B^{r}_{q,\infty}(X,\mu;Y)
. This inclusion follows from the Ahlfors regularity of
\mu
:

\int_{X}\fint_{B_{t}(x)}\frac{\rho(u(x),u(z))^{q}}{t^{rq}}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(x)\leq\frac{1}{c}\int_{X}\int_{B_{t}(x)}\frac{\rho(u(x),u(z))^{q}}{t^{rq+s}}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(x)\\
\leq\frac{1}{c}\int_{X}\int_{B_{t}(x)}\frac{\rho(u(x),u(z))^{q}}{d(x,z)^{rq+s}}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(x)\leq\frac{1}{c}\int_{X}\int_{X}\frac{\rho(u(x),u(z))^{q}}{d(x,z)^{rq+s}}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(x).

(8.43)

Since the right-hand side of (
8.43
) does not depend on
t
, we obtain
W^{r,q}(X,\mu;Y)\subset B^{r}_{q,\infty}(X,\mu;Y)
.
