Combined application of nano-magnesium oxide and humic acid improves potato drought tolerance and soil health
Authors: Beini Zhang, Zhudong Yuan, Yuming Liu, Jiangqing Wang, Muzammal Rehman, Aie Chen, Gang Deng, Hongyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought is a major threat to crop production. Although different biostimulants and nano-fertilizers have shown potential to mitigate drought stress, their combined effects remain poorly understood.MethodsThis study investigated the effects of soil-applied humic acid (HA) and foliar nano-magnesium oxide (Nano-MgO) to alleviate drought stress in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.). A pot experiment was conducted under two irrigation regimes, well-watered conditions maintained at 80% field capacity (FC) and drought stress conditions maintained at 40% FC; two HA levels (0 and 2000 mg kg⁻¹ soil); and four foliar Nano-MgO concentrations (0, 20, 40, and 60 mg L⁻¹).ResultsThe results demonstrated that drought stress significantly reduced plant height, stem diameter, biomass, chlorophyll content, and tuber starch and protein content while increasing oxidative stress markers like malondialdehyde (MDA). The combined application of HA and Nano-MgO (60 mg L⁻¹) significantly counteracted these detrimental effects. This treatment enhanced chlorophyll retention, increased antioxidant enzyme activities, reduced oxidative damage, and improved osmotic regulation. As a result, tuber yield, starch, and soluble sugar content under drought were significantly improved compared to untreated drought-stressed plants, although values remained below well-watered plants. Soil enzyme activities were also significantly improved, indicating enhanced soil health. The improved plant performance is attributed to HA amendment and efficient foliar Mg delivery by Nano-MgO, which together strengthen photosynthesis and antioxidant defense.ConclusionsThe combined use of humic acid and Nano-MgO is a sustainable and highly effective approach to enhance drought resilience in potato.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09350-8.Keywords: Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), Drought Stress, Humic Acid, Nano-Magnesium Oxide, Tuber biochemical attributes

Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) ranks as the fourth most important food crop worldwide after maize, wheat, and rice and plays a critical role in global food and nutritional security due to its high yield potential and rich content of carbohydrates, proteins, vitamins, and minerals [1, 2]. However, its productivity is highly vulnerable to abiotic stresses, with drought being a primary constraint, particularly in the context of escalating climate variability and freshwater scarcity [3, 4]. Agricultural drought adversities seriously threaten sustainable development [5]. Potato is particularly sensitive to water deficit because of its shallow root system, high canopy transpiration, and strong dependence on uninterrupted carbon assimilation during tuber initiation and bulking stages [6, 7]. Even short periods of drought can significantly reduce leaf expansion, impair photosynthetic capacity, disrupt source–sink relationships, and ultimately lead to substantial yield and quality losses [8]. At the physiological level, drought stress induces stomatal closure to minimize water loss, but this response restricts CO₂ diffusion and suppresses photosynthetic carbon fixation, resulting in excess excitation energy within chloroplasts and enhanced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide [9–11]. Excessive ROS accumulation causes oxidative damage to membrane lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, with lipid peroxidation, reflected by elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, being a prominent indicator of drought-induced cellular injury [12, 13].

Traditional drought mitigation strategies such as breeding and irrigation management have limitations in speed, resource availability, or scalability, which increased interest in new agronomic methods, especially biostimulants and nano-enabled technologies that prime plant stress responses and improve resource use efficiency [14, 15]. Humic acid could be a potent plant biostimulant with a remarkable capacity to enhance crop resilience under a wide spectrum of abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and heavy metal toxicity [16]. In the rhizosphere, HA acts as a soil conditioner by improving soil structure, increasing cation exchange capacity, and enhancing water retention, thereby mitigating the physical constraints of stress [17, 18]. In plants, HA functions as a biochemical signal that directly influences plant metabolism. It enhances root architecture and proliferation, leading to greater exploration of soil for water and nutrients [19, 20]. Humic acid promotes growth by increasing cytokinins and auxins and reducing ABA under stress [19, 21]. Despite these advantages, the effectiveness of humic acid alone may be constrained under severe drought, particularly when nutrient mobility and leaf nutrient supply are compromised.

Magnesium (Mg) is an essential macronutrient [22]. Mg deficiency in plants can lead to chlorosis, reduced photosynthesis, and inefficient assimilate transport [23, 24], which are detrimental to potato tuber yield and starch accumulation. Under stress conditions, the effectiveness of conventional soil-applied fertilizers is often limited due to reduced nutrient mobility and restricted root activity [25]. Foliar application of conventional fertilizers is limited due to their lower uptake efficiency [26], whereas nano-sized metal oxide fertilizers show higher efficiency due to their larger surface area, reactivity, and better foliar penetration [27, 28]. Nano-MgO has recently gained attention as an efficient Mg source capable of rapid foliar absorption and targeted delivery to photosynthetic tissues. Owing to their nanoscale dimensions, Nano-MgO particles can penetrate the cuticle and stomatal openings more effectively, facilitating Mg translocation into mesophyll cells and chloroplasts. This enhanced plant’s performance under adverse environmental conditions [23, 29]. Additionally, Nano-MgO has been reported to stimulate antioxidant enzyme activity, stabilize cellular redox balance, and reduce oxidative damage under abiotic stress [23]. Nevertheless, the standalone application of nanomaterials may not fully address drought-induced constraints at the rhizosphere level, highlighting the importance for integrated approaches.

Combining soil-applied humic acid with foliar Nano-MgO represents a novel and promising strategy to enhance drought tolerance through complementary root and shoot level mechanisms. HA could optimize the rhizosphere for better water and nutrient capture, while foliar Nano-MgO could directly rectify the photosynthetic and biochemical constraints in the canopy. Preliminary evidence suggests such synergies in other crops. For instance, the combination of organic amendments and nano-nutrients improved stress tolerance in barley [30]. However, a comprehensive investigation into their coordinated effects on potato under drought stress is lacking. We hypothesized that synergistic use of HA with Nano-MgO would mitigate drought-induced reductions in growth, physiological performance, and tuber biochemical attributes in potato. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the synergistic role of HA with Nano-MgO in alleviating drought-induced constraints on potato plant growth, physiological traits, tuber biochemical composition (starch, soluble sugars, and protein content), and soil health. By bridging the gap between rhizosphere management and foliar precision nutrition, this research will provide a novel, sustainable, and effective strategy to enhance drought resilience in one of the world’s most vital food crops.

Materials and methods

Plant material, soil preparation, and growth conditions

Certified seed tubers of potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) cultivar ‘Lishu No. 6’ were obtained commercially and were surface-disinfected by immersion in 3% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 3 min, followed by three rinses with sterile distilled water, and then air-dried under laminar flow conditions. To prepare soil, natural soil was collected from the campus research field of Yunnan Normal University, Kunming, Yunnan, China; air-dried under shade, passed through a 2-mm sieve to remove debris, and homogenized before use. The physico-chemical properties of the soil were pH 6.23, soil EC 9.68 mS m−1, organic matter 28.68 g kg−1, total N 1330.04 mg kg−1, total P 6.31 mg kg−1, total K 23.75 mg g−1, and available K 305.51 mg kg−1. Nano-MgO was procured from Dongzhong New Material Technology Co., Ltd., Nangong City, China, and supplied as Nano-MgO (ultrafine grade). According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the particles have an average size of 20 nm, a purity of ≥ 99.9%, and appear as a white powder. Furthermore, the humic acid used in this study was a commercially available product in China with 90% purity. Plants were grown in plastic pots (32 cm diameter, 30 cm height), each filled with 10.50 kg of prepared soil. The experiment was conducted in a glasshouse under natural photoperiod conditions, with photosynthetically active radiation ranging from 800 to 1200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at midday. Day/night temperatures were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C and 18 ± 2 °C, respectively, with relative humidity between 60% and 75%. A single pre-sprouted seed tuber containing at least two viable eyes was planted at a depth of 10 cm in each pot. After emergence, seedlings were thinned at the 5–6 leaf stage to retain one uniform and healthy plant per pot.

Experimental design and treatment application

The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized factorial design with three treatment factors: drought stress, humic acid (HA) soil amendment, and foliar application of Nano-MgO. Drought stress treatments consisted of a well-watered control (D0) maintained at 80% field capacity (FC) and a drought treatment (D1) maintained at 40% FC. Field capacity was determined before treatment initiation using the gravimetric method. For the D1 treatment, drought stress was imposed at the tuber initiation stage by withholding irrigation for 15 days, from early tuber initiation to peak flowering, until soil moisture reached approximately 40% FC. Soil moisture content was monitored at 2–3 day intervals using a soil moisture meter to ensure that the target moisture levels were maintained throughout the stress period. In contrast, pots assigned to the well-watered treatment were maintained at approximately 80% FC throughout the experiment. Following the drought period, normal irrigation was resumed according to the experimental schedule. The soil amendment factor comprised an unamended control (B0) and a HA treatment (B1), where humic acid was mixed uniformly into the soil at 2000 mg kg⁻¹ dry soil before potting. The Nano-MgO was applied as a foliar spray at four concentrations: 0 (T0), 20 (T1), 40 (T2), and 60 (T3) mg L⁻¹. The Nano-MgO suspensions were prepared by dispersing a precisely weighed mass of Nano-MgO in deionized water followed by sonication and were applied to runoff for complete foliage coverage at two phenological stages: tuber initiation (budding) and full bloom stages. The factorial combination of these factors (2 × 2 × 4) produced 16 treatments, each replicated three times, resulting in a total of 48 experimental units. The primary drought-HA treatment combinations are presented below, with each combination subsequently receiving all four Nano-MgO concentrations: D0B0: Well-watered, without humic acid (baseline control); D0B1: Well-watered, with humic acid amendment; D1B0: Drought-stressed, without humic acid; and D1B1: Drought-stressed, with humic acid amendment.

Agronomic trait measurement, harvesting, and sample collection

Agronomic traits were measured at plant maturity prior to harvest. Plant height was determined as the distance from the soil surface to the apical growing point using a measuring tape. Stem diameter was measured at the midpoint of the main stem using a vernier caliper. The number of main stems per plant and the number of branches per plant were recorded by manual counting. After measurement of agronomic traits, plants were harvested and separated into leaves, stems, and tubers for fresh and dry biomass assessment. Leaves and stems were immediately weighed to determine fresh biomass, blanched at 105 °C for 15 min, and subsequently dried at 60 °C to constant weight for dry biomass determination. Tubers were carefully excavated, washed to remove adhering soil, air-dried to eliminate surface moisture, weighed for fresh biomass, and then oven-dried at 65 °C to constant weight to obtain dry biomass.

For physiological and biochemical analysis, leaf samples were collected at the budding and full bloom stages. For each plant, fully expanded functional leaves were harvested from the second to third leaf pairs from the top, with main veins removed. Samples were immediately placed in labeled plastic bags, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, transported to the laboratory, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Rhizosphere samples were collected from the root zone. Collected soil was air dried and sieved according to assay requirements prior to enzyme activity determination. Tuber samples were harvested, cleaned, and prepared for analysis by cutting them into small pieces, rapidly freezing in liquid nitrogen, and storing at −80 °C until measurement.

Chlorophyll content measurement

The concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and total carotenoids were determined spectrophotometrically from fresh leaf tissue using the 80% acetone extraction method as detailed by [31]. Briefly, approximately 100 mg of fresh leaf tissue was ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen, and pigments were extracted in 10 mL of 80% (v/v) acetone. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 470 nm, 645 nm, and 663 nm against an 80% acetone blank. Pigment concentrations (µg mL⁻¹) were calculated using the Arnon equations [32], and the results were then expressed on a fresh weight basis (mg g− 1 FW).

Oxidative stress indicator and antioxidant enzyme activity measurement

Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) from the 2nd–3rd fully expanded leaves was homogenized in liquid nitrogen and extracted with ice-cold 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 1 mM EDTA and 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used as the crude extract for superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) assays. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined using a parallel extract prepared in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was quantified using the extraction reagent supplied with the assay kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The SOD, POD, and CAT activities and MDA content were determined using commercial assay kits provided by Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China.

Osmoregulators and amino acid content measurement

Proline content was quantified spectrophotometrically using the acid-ninhydrin method, where proline forms a red chromophore measured at 520 nm. Total soluble sugars were assayed via the anthrone-sulfuric acid method, with absorbance read at 620 nm. Total free amino acids were estimated using the ninhydrin method, with absorbance measured at 570 nm. For all the above assays the kits were provided by Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. Briefly, about 0.1 g of fresh leaf powder (ground in liquid nitrogen) was extracted with appropriate buffers as specified in the respective kits. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were used for the determinations. All procedures followed the manufacturer’s protocols.

Tuber quality measurement

Tuber quality parameters were assessed using the same commercial assay kits as used for leaf. However, extraction protocols were adapted to account for the high starch and secondary metabolite content of tuber tissue. Soluble sugars were extracted in hot 80% ethanol, and the extracts were clarified via ion-exchange chromatography prior to the anthrone assay. Starch was determined enzymatically following a defatting step and optimized hydrolysis time. Total soluble protein was quantified using the Bradford assay following protein precipitation with trichloroacetic acid (TCA)-acetone to eliminate interference from phenolics.

Assay of soil enzyme activities

Soil enzyme activities involved in key biogeochemical cycling processes were determined using commercial assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). All measurements were performed with three replications using air-dried soil samples sieved through a 2 mm mesh. Soil sucrase, acid phosphatase, polyphenol oxidase, urease, and catalase activities were quantified following the manufacturer’s instructions. Enzyme activities are expressed as units per gram of dry soil (U g⁻¹).

Statistics analysis

The collected data were subjected to statistical analysis using a three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the main effects and interaction effects of drought stress, humic acid amendment, and foliar Nano-MgO concentration on all measured parameters. Prior to ANOVA, data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variances. Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test at a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 (Table S1). All statistical procedures were performed using R software version 4.0.0 (R Core Team, 2020). Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE) of three independent replicates.

Results

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on agronomic traits of potato under drought stress

In this study, results showed that drought stress significantly reduced the agronomic growth attributes of potato (Fig. 1). It was recorded that plant height was decreased by 10.7% (from 75 cm to 67 cm), number of main stem branches was decreased by 7%, main stem diameter was reduced by 23% (0.78 cm to 0.60 cm) and total number of branches was slightly decreased by 2% (4.6 to 4.7) under drought conditions without any treatment. However, application of humic acid exhibited significant positive effects in alleviating the drought stress and recorded an increase of 22.7% in plant height, 48.8% in number of main stem branches, 25.6% increase in stem diameter, and 52.2% increase in total number of branches under drought conditions. It was further noticed that that addition of Nano-MgO at different concentrations significantly enhanced the growth parameters of potato under drought and no-drought conditions. However, among all the concentrations, Nano-MgO 60 mg L− 1 exhibited more promising results and showed highest increase in growth attributes of potato. Under no drought conditions, Nano-MgO 60 mg L− 1 increased the plant height by 56%, number of main stem branches by 93%, stem diameter by 37%, and total number of branches by 39.1% without humic acid. However, under drought conditions, Nano-MgO 60 mg L− 1 with humic acid increased the plant height by 54%, number of main stems by 1.45-fold, stem diameter by 96%, and total number of branches by 51%.

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on biomass accumulation of potato under drought stress

Results exhibited that drought stress showed a significant decline in biomass accumulation of potato (Fig. 2). Leaf fresh weight was reduced by 33% (15 g to 10 g), leaf dry weight by 15% (6.5 g to 5.5 g), stem fresh weight by 32% (95 g to 65 g), stem dry weight by 32% (11 g to 7.5 g), tuber fresh weight by 17% (48 g to 40 g), and tuber dry weight by 25% (8 g to 6 g) in untreated plants under drought stress. However, use of humic acid alleviated the drought stress and showed increased biomass of potato under both drought and no drought conditions. Leaf fresh weight was increased by 25% and stem fresh weight by 18% in drought stress with humic acid. Application of varying levels of Nano-MgO enhanced the biomass attributes of potato under no drought and drought conditions. Among all the levels, 60 mg L− 1 Nano-MgO showed an increase of 1.7-fold in leaf fresh weight in drought conditions without humic acid. This increase in leaf fresh weight was more pronounced and reached up to 2.2-fold in drought conditions with humic acid. A similar trend was recorded for other parameters. For example, stem fresh weight was increased by 1.5-fold with humic acid under no drought stress at 60 mg L− 1 of Nano-MgO. Tuber fresh weight also showed a similar trend and maximum increase in tuber fresh weight (2.75-fold) was recorded at 60 mg L− 1 Nano-MgO in drought conditions with humic acid as compared to the respective control.

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on chlorophyll contents of potato under drought stress

Table 1 represents the interactive effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on chlorophyll content of potato at budding and full bloom stage under drought stress. Under no drought stress, at the budding stage, total chlorophyll contents were at 1.16 ± 0.02 mg g− 1 in untreated plants and decreased up to 0.86 ± 0.02 mg g− 1 (34% decrease) in drought conditions without humic acid treatment. A similar trend was recorded at the full bloom stage, and drought stress reduced total chlorophyll content from 1.03 ± 0.18 mg g− 1 to 0.74 ± 0.11 mg g− 1 (39% decrease) in plants without humic acid treatment. However, humic acid treatment positively influenced the chlorophyll contents in potato under no drought and drought conditions. Total chlorophyll contents were increased by 23% (from 0.86 ± 0.02 mg g− 1 to 1.12 ± 0.03 mg g− 1) at the budding stage and by 16% (from 0.74 ± 0.11 mg g− 1 to 0.89 ± 0.20 mg g− 1) at full bloom stage with humic acid treatment in drought conditions. It was further noticed that application of Nano-MgO exhibited a strong dose-dependent improvement in chlorophyll content of potato under both moisture regimes and growth stages. At the budding stage under no drought with humic acid, total chlorophyll increased from 0.87 ± 0.05 mg g⁻¹ (no Nano-MgO) to 1.13 ± 0.05 mg g⁻¹ at 20 mg L⁻¹ Nano-MgO (23% increase). Under drought stress with humic acid treatment at the budding stage, Nano-MgO at 20 mg L⁻¹ raised total chlorophyll from 1.12 ± 0.03 mg g⁻¹ to 1.24 ± 0.06 mg g⁻¹ (10% increase). At the full bloom stage, the highest total chlorophyll was recorded at 40 mg L⁻¹ Nano-MgO combined with humic acid and reached 1.10 ± 0.11 mg g⁻¹ under no drought and 1.72 ± 0.32 mg g⁻¹ under drought conditions.

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on lipid peroxidation, carotenoids, amino acids and soluble sugars and protein contents in potato under drought stress

Table 2 represents the interactive effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO application on oxidative damage and osmolyte accumulation in potato at budding and full bloom stages under no drought and drought stress. Under no drought stress, malondialdehyde (MDA) content at the budding stage was 89.2 nmol g⁻¹ and increased to 104.2 nmol g⁻¹ at the full bloom stage in untreated plants. Drought stress significantly elevated MDA to 116.4 nmol g⁻¹ at the budding and 141.4 nmol g⁻¹ at the full bloom stage, representing increase of 30% and 36%, respectively, as compared to no drought without humic acid treatment. Concurrently, drought increased carotenoid content by 25–40%, while sharply increasing amino acids up to 4.4-fold, soluble sugars up to 3-fold, and protein content up to 2.1- fold in no drought without humic acid treatment. Humic acid application significantly alleviated drought-induced oxidative stress. Under drought stress, humic acid reduced MDA by 25–50% (decreasing MDA from 116.4 to 83.2 nmol g⁻¹ at budding and from 141.4 to 119.5 nmol g⁻¹ at full bloom). Carotenoid content increased by 30–60%, while amino acid, soluble sugar, and protein contents declined by 5–45%. Nano-MgO application exerted a strong dose-dependent effect. Increasing Nano-MgO concentration progressively reduced MDA content under drought, reaching minimum values at 60 mg L⁻¹ (from 116.37 ± 1.76 to 60.00 ± 0.20 nmol g⁻¹ at budding and from 141.42 ± 4.68 to 87.32 ± 8.71 nmol g⁻¹ at full bloom (45–55% reduction) with and without humic acid treatment in drought stress. Nano-MgO at 60 mg L⁻¹ showed more promising results in combination with humic acid and enhanced carotenoid accumulation (70% increase) while suppressing stress-induced amino acid, soluble sugar, and protein accumulation by 40–65% in combination with humic acid.

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on antioxidant enzyme activities and proline content in potato under drought stress

Table 3 represents the interactive effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO application on antioxidant enzymatic activities in potato at budding and full bloom stages under no drought and drought stress. Under no-drought conditions, antioxidant enzyme activities were consistently lowered than under drought stress. At the budding stage without drought, humic acid and Nano-MgO, CAT activity was 336.89 ± 6.57 U g⁻¹, superoxide dismutase (SOD) 873.35 ± 24.17, and peroxidase (POD) 16137.33 ± 98.17. Drought stress increased these activities by 30–60% at both growth stages; e.g., CAT increased up to 906.95 ± 14.16 U g⁻¹ and SOD up to 8438.83 ± 53.25 U g⁻¹ under drought stress (no humic acid, no Nano-MgO, budding stage). Humic acid application partially mitigated drought-induced stress by improving enzyme activities up to 10–40% as compared to untreated drought plants. At budding stage, drought stress with humic acid treatment decreased CAT activity from 906.95 ± 14.16 to 353.45 ± 46.18 U g⁻¹ and SOD from 8438.83 ± 53.25 to 3985.70 ± 9.12 U g⁻¹. Nano-MgO application also showed a dose-dependent effect on antioxidant enzymatic activities. At the budding stage, CAT and SOD activities decreased up to 160.14 ± 49.79 U g⁻¹ and 931.53 ± 20.08 U g⁻¹ respectively, with 60 mg L⁻¹ Nano-MgO plus humic acid treatment under drought stress. A similar trend was observed at the full bloom stage. Proline content, a key osmoprotectant, increased significantly under drought stress; however, humic acid and Nano-MgO application significantly reduced proline accumulation. The combined application of humic acid and Nano-MgO at 60 mg L⁻¹ decreased proline under drought up to 78.13 ± 1.38 µg g⁻¹ and 1415.98 ± 6.05 µg g⁻¹ at the budding and full bloom stages, respectively.

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on potato tuber quality under drought stress

Figure 3 illustrates the interactive effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on starch, soluble sugar, and protein contents of potato tuber under no drought and drought conditions. Results showed that drought stress significantly influenced the carbohydrate and protein metabolism in potato. The lowest starch contents (116.55 mg g− 1) were recorded in drought conditions without any treatment. Under no drought, starch contents were ranged from 138.72 ± 7.17 mg g− 1 in untreated plants to 192.74 ± 6.2 mg g− 1 with humic acid treatment. Nano-MgO at 60 mg L− 1 increased the starch content up to 321.65 ± 5.3 mg g⁻¹. In contrast, soluble sugar contents declined from 37.27 ± 0.71 mg g⁻¹ to 29.22 ± 1.43 mg g⁻¹ with Nano-MgO alone (22% decrease) and to 13.94 ± 2.3 mg g⁻¹ when combined with humic acid (60% decrease). Under no drought, protein contents were decreased up to 15.48 ± 0.36 mg g⁻¹ with Nano-MgO and up to 9.61 ± 0.5 mg g⁻¹ with combined application of humic acid and Nano-MgO. Drought stress markedly reduced starch accumulation and reached at 138.72 ± 7.17 mg g⁻¹ (no-drought) to 116.55 ± 3.65 mg g⁻¹, while substantially increased the soluble sugar content from 37.27 ± 0.71 mg g⁻¹ to 61.40 ± 0.059 mg g⁻¹ (65% increase) and protein content from 19.44 ± 0.86 mg g⁻¹ to 21.01 ± 0.66 mg g⁻¹ (8% increase) in untreated plants. Humic acid application enhanced starch accumulation up to 184.04 ± 5.89, soluble sugars up to 24.77 ± 1.09 mg g⁻¹, and protein content up to 15.37 ± 0.47 mg g⁻¹ under drought conditions. Nano-MgO application exerted a strong, dose-dependent effect under both no drought and drought stress. Nano-MgO at 60 mg L− 1 showed more promising results and raised starch content up to 293.89 ± 7.37 mg g⁻¹ under no-drought and 273.69 ± 4.99 mg g⁻¹ in drought conditions. Conversely, Nano-MgO reduced soluble sugar content by 20–35% under no drought and by 70% in drought conditions, while protein contents declined by 20–30%, particularly when Nano-MgO was combined with humic acid.

Effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on soil enzyme activities under drought stress

Figure 4 illustrates the interactive effects of humic acid and Nano-MgO on soil enzymatic activities under no drought and drought conditions. Under no drought, soil sucrase activity was reached at 15.7 ± 0.4 U g− 1, soil acid phosphatase at 35566.28 U g− 1, soil polyphenol oxidase activity at 63.79 U g− 1, soil urease activity at 500.26 U g− 1 and soil catalase activity at 10.49 U g− 1 in soil without humic acid treatment. It was observed that drought stress significantly influenced the soil enzymatic activities and increased soil sucrase activity up to 15.9 ± 0.6 U g− 1, soil acid phosphatase activity up to 39125.56 U g− 1, soil polyphenol oxidase activity up to 80.54 ± 0.38 U g− 1. Conversely, drought stress decreased soil urease activity up to 416.2 ± 4.8 U g− 1 and S-CAT activity up to 9.9 ± 0.8 U g− 1 under no humic acid treatment in drought conditions. However, application of humic acid showed significant positive effects on soil enzymatic activities. Soil sucrase activity was increased up to 16.8 ± 0.3 U g− 1, soil acid phosphatase activity was decreased up to 32156.89 U g− 1, soil polyphenol oxidase activity was decreased up to 47.08 U g− 1, soil urease activity was increased up to 502.26 ± 6.2 U g− 1 and S-CAT activity was increased up to 11.6 ± 0.4 U g− 1 in drought conditions. Nano-MgO application also exhibited a strong, dose-dependent effect under both no drought and drought stress. Among all the varying levels, Nano-MgO at 60 mg L− 1 showed more promising results and raised soil sucrase activity up to 20.44 U g− 1, soil acid phosphatase activity was decreased up to 19032.40 U g− 1, soil polyphenol oxidase activity was decreased up to 23.04 U g− 1, soil urease activity up to 719.0 ± 6.7 U g− 1, S-CAT activity up to 25.9 ± 0.8 U g− 1 in combined treatment with humic acid under drought conditions respectively.

Discussion

The combined application of Nano-MgO and humic acid alleviated drought-induced physiological and biochemical stress in potato, improving performance comparative to untreated stressed plants, though values remained below well-watered controls. This significant effect could be due to a series of interconnected mechanisms initiated by the applied treatments. Humic acid, a complex organic polymer, is known to enhance soil structure, improve water-holding capacity, and stimulate root growth and architecture [17]. The application of humic acid to soil creates a more favourable root environment which improves plant access to soil moisture and nutrients [33]. At the same time, foliar-applied Nano-MgO serves as a highly bioavailable Mg source for leaves. Nanoparticles penetrate leaf surfaces and enter cells more efficiently than conventional Mg salts. This pathway allows rapid Mg delivery to photosynthetic tissues and avoids soil-related constraints under drought conditions [34]. Notably, Nano-MgO alone often showed weaker or inconsistent responses compared with the combined treatment, highlighting the importance of its interaction with humic acid.

The improved nutritional status, particularly Mg availability, triggered a series of positive effects on photosynthesis, which is highly sensitive to drought stress. Magnesium is a central atom of chlorophyll and also activates RuBisCO and key Calvin cycle enzymes [35, 36]. Evidence from controlled studies indicates that foliar application of Mg, particularly in combination with boron, significantly improves key physiological traits and yield components under drought stress, with yield improvements of up to 25–36% depending on the growth stage of stress imposition [37]. Present results have shown that drought stress caused significant losses in chlorophyll content at both budding and full bloom stages, consistent with drought responses observed in maize and other cereals [33]. In contrast, the combined application of humic acid and Nano-MgO, especially at 60 mg L⁻¹, prevented pigment loss and increased chlorophyll levels under drought conditions. Although the treated plants showed improved chlorophyll levels under drought stress, the values remained lower than those of the well-watered control plants. The preservation and enhancement of photosynthetic pigments are critical for sustained light harvesting capacity. This effect extends beyond Mg supply alone. Drought stress caused severe reductions in leaf, stem, and tuber fresh and dry weights, whereas the combined humic acid and Nano-MgO treatment significantly alleviated these losses. Enhanced photosynthetic activity increased the production of photoassimilates, which provided the necessary carbon skeleton for vegetative growth and, most importantly, for translocation to the sink organ (tubers). The improved source-sink relationship could be evident from the significantly higher tuber fresh and dry weights under drought in treated plants, demonstrating a direct link between improved photosynthesis and yield stability under water deficit. The improvement in tuber quality under drought stress can also be attributed to more efficient carbon assimilation and allocation supported by the combined treatments. Drought usually disturbs primary metabolism and reduces starch, protein, and soluble sugar contents in storage organs [38]. The present results reveal that the combined amendments, particularly humic acid and Nano-MgO at 60 mg L⁻1 effectively reduced these losses. The increase in tuber starch content is of high agronomic value. Starch accumulation is a complex sink-driven process that depends on the efficient transport of sucrose from leaves and its subsequent conversion within the tuber. Mg plays two key roles in this process. First, it activates ATP synthase and enzymes involved in sucrose metabolism and starch biosynthesis. Second, it also supports photosynthetic sucrose production by sustaining efficient photosynthetic activity [39]. Humic acid may further promote this process by improving phloem loading and assimilate transport. Together, the amendments maintain carbon fixation and partitioning under water deficit, which allows sustained starch deposition in tubers and may help to improve its yield and quality. Similarly, the observed maintenance of tuber protein content also indicates stable nitrogen metabolism and amino acid synthesis. This response may relate to the ability of humic acid to stimulate nitrate reductase activity [40] and the role of Mg as a cofactor for many biosynthetic enzymes [39].

Osmotic adjustment represents a key component of drought tolerance. Plants accumulate compatible solutes such as soluble sugars and proline to lower cellular osmotic potential, maintain turgor, and protect cellular structures [41]. Our results showed a differentiated response to the applied amendments. Soluble sugar content in tubers, which decreased under drought stress, could be partially restored by the amendments. These increased likely functions both in osmotic regulation and as an accessible carbon reserve. In this study, proline responses showed a more complex pattern. Drought stress induced a strong accumulation of proline, which reflects a typical stress-driven response. In contrast, plants treated with humic acid and Nano-MgO accumulated lower proline levels under drought yet displayed improved growth and reduced oxidative damage. This pattern indicates a stress-mitigation strategy rather than a stress-endurance response. Humic acid amendment in soil improved plant water status through enhanced root development and soil moisture retention. They also reduced cellular oxidative pressure [20]. As a result, the primary signals that induce excessive proline synthesis were weakened. The plants therefore experienced lower cellular stress and required less extreme osmotic adjustment through proline accumulation. This response agrees with the concept that stress-tolerant plants show a more regulated and metabolically efficient response under stress [42].

Oxidative stress reduction emerged as an important mechanism in this study, as drought-induced stomatal closure disrupts the photosynthetic electron transport chain and promotes electron leakage to oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide. These ROS cause oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, with MDA being a key marker of lipid peroxidation [11]. Our results showed an increase in MDA content under drought stress. The combined application of humic acid and Nano-MgO significantly reduced MDA levels. The most effective treatment, humic acid, with Nano-MgO at 60 mg L-¹, lowered MDA content to values partially comparable to non-stressed plants, indicating strong protection of cellular membranes. The mechanism for this protection could be associated with the enhanced activity of the antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, P OD, and CAT. The SOD converts superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen while CAT and POD subsequently detoxify hydrogen peroxide [43]. Drought stress can induce these enzymes as a defense response. Furthermore, humic acid and Nano-MgO, particularly in combination, maintained or enhanced enzyme activities under stress. For instance, at the full bloom stage under drought, SOD and POD activities were significantly higher in plants treated with humic acid and 60 mg L⁻¹ Nano-MgO compared to the drought-stressed control. Humic substances function as abiotic elicitors [44, 45]. Mg on the other hand, acts as a cofactor for antioxidant enzymes such as SOD but is also essential for the synthesis of the key antioxidant glutathione and for maintaining the redox balance of the cell [46]. The enhanced scavenging system efficiently neutralizes ROS, reduces lipid peroxidation, and preserves membrane integrity. This protection further stabilizes the photosynthetic apparatus, supports sustained carbon fixation under drought stress, and reduces the need for excessive energy-dissipating processes. Drought stress reduces microbial activity and the release of extracellular enzymes essential for nutrient cycling. Humic acid provides a carbon and energy source for beneficial soil microbes, promoting their growth and activity [44]. Increased activities of dehydrogenase, urease, and phosphatase indicate a more active and healthy soil microbiome, which enhance the mineralization and availability of nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. The healthier soil microbiome fostered by humic acid may also support plant health through mechanisms such as induced systemic resistance, further enhancing overall plant resilience. Although the present study provided controlled conditions to assess the effects of Nano-MgO and HA, the results may not fully reflect field environments with greater soil and climatic variability. Therefore, field-scale studies are required to confirm the effectiveness of these amendments under practical agricultural conditions.

Conclusions

The combined use of soil-applied humic acid and foliar Nano-MgO provides a coordinated defense against drought stress in potatoes. The most effective treatment was humic acid with Nano-MgO at 60 mg L⁻¹. These treatments preserved chlorophyll, enhanced photosynthesis, and increased tuber biomass and starch content under drought. At the same time, the treatment strengthened the antioxidant system, reduced oxidative damage, and supported efficient osmotic adjustment. By simultaneously improving water relations, photosynthetic performance, and oxidative resilience, the present approach maintained plant productivity under water deficit conditions. The results offer a practical and sustainable strategy for potato cultivation in arid regions. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the combined effects of Nano-MgO and humic acid on drought tolerance remain to be elucidated. Therefore, future studies should test this combination under field conditions and investigate the underlying genetic mechanisms.
