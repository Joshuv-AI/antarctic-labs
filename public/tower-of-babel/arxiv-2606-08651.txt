The Four-Point Picard Theorem for Quaternionic Slice Regular Functions Thanks: Corresponding author: Xin Zhao.
Guangbin Ren, Xin Zhao

Abstract
. An entire slice regular function f : ℍ → ℍ f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H} can omit four prescribed quaternionic values only in the affine-dependent case. More precisely, four affinely independent omitted values force f f to be constant, while the converse follows from the plane-omission theorem of Bisi–Winkelmann. The proof passes to the real-symmetric stem function. For each omitted value a quadratic zero-divisor criterion gives a zero-free entire function Q j Q_{j} , and the component normal to the affine span is governed by a square-discriminant identity. Finite-order data are excluded by Hadamard factorization and a rigidity argument on the real axis. In the general case, logarithmic Bloch–Ochiai places the Q Q -curve in a translated algebraic torus. The Laurent-square case reduces to the finite-order contradiction, and the nonsquare case is excluded by an even-ramification argument together with the level-one truncated Second Main Theorem of Noguchi–Winkelmann–Yamanoi.

1.
Introduction

Quaternionic slice regular Picard phenomena are governed by the real affine geometry of omitted sets, rather than by isolated values alone. The Bisi–Winkelmann results needed here are the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Bisi–Winkelmann)
.

The following hold.

(i)

For every real affine
2
-plane
P\subset\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
, there exists a nonconstant entire slice regular function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
such that

f(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{H}\setminus P.

(ii)

If
q_{1},\ldots,q_{5}\in\mathbb{H}
are not contained in any real affine
3
-space, then every entire slice regular function omitting
q_{1},\ldots,q_{5}
is constant.

The plane-omission assertion follows from
[
1
, Proposition 5.1, Proposition 6.1 and the subsequent remark]
; the five-point assertion is
[
1
, Theorem 3.5]
.

Part (i) of Theorem
1.1
gives the sharpness direction in Corollary
1.3
. The unresolved finite configuration is a quadruple spanning a real affine
3
-space; the theorem below proves its rigidity.

The proof is written in the stem-function formalism for slice regular functions, following Cullen, Gentili–Struppa, and subsequent work
[
7
,
12
,
13
,
10
,
11
,
4
,
5
,
14
]
. Jensen-type formulae and zero-counting results for slice regular functions provide related value-distribution background; see
[
23
,
2
]
. The main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Four-point Picard theorem)
.

Let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{H}
be affinely independent. If

f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is an entire slice regular function omitting
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
, then
f
is constant.

Together with the Bisi–Winkelmann plane-omission construction, this gives the exact four-value criterion.

Corollary 1.3

(Exact four-value omission criterion)
.

Let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{H}
. There exists a nonconstant entire slice regular function omitting all four values if and only if the four points are affinely dependent.

Theorem
1.2
is not a formal consequence of the five-point theorem, since four affinely independent points always lie in a real affine
3
-space. The obstruction is encoded by a single normal coordinate after complexifying this affine span. Let

F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}

be the real-symmetric stem function associated with
f
. For
c\in\mathbb{H}
set

Q_{c}(z)=\langle F(z)-c,F(z)-c\rangle,

where the form is the complex bilinear extension of the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{H}
. The Bisi–Winkelmann zero-divisor criterion identifies omission of
c
with zero-freeness of
Q_{c}
. Thus the four omitted values give four zero-free entire functions of one complex variable.

After translating the values, put
p_{0}=0
and
p_{j}=a_{j}-a_{0}
for
1\leq j\leq 3
. Let

V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\},\qquad\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}=V_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\mathbb{C}n,

with
\|n\|=1
, and write
F=W+\tau n
. For

Q_{j}(z)=\langle F(z)-p_{j},F(z)-p_{j}\rangle\qquad(0\leq j\leq 3)

one has

\langle W,p_{j}\rangle=\frac{Q_{0}+\|p_{j}\|^{2}-Q_{j}}{2}=:b_{j}(Q).

If
G=(\langle p_{i},p_{j}\rangle)_{1\leq i,j\leq 3}
is the Gram matrix, then

W(Q)=\sum_{i,k=1}^{3}(G^{-1})_{ik}b_{k}(Q)p_{i},\qquad\langle W,W\rangle=b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q).

Consequently

\tau^{2}=Q_{0}-b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q)=:\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}).

Section
3
proves the converse: four-value omission is equivalent to the existence of real-symmetric zero-free functions
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{3}
for which
\Delta_{A}(Q)
has a real-symmetric entire square root. Proposition
3.9
records the corresponding normal-square coordinate form.

Affine independence is used through the invertibility of the Gram matrix: it makes the tangential component
W
a linear function of the four quadratic data and leaves the normal square as the remaining constraint. If the four points are affinely dependent, this reconstruction degenerates and the normal coordinate no longer yields a four-point obstruction; the plane-omission part of Theorem
1.1
then supplies nonconstant examples. The five-point theorem of Bisi–Winkelmann rests on an algebraic constraint among five quadratic functions. By contrast, Proposition
3.9
shows that no such algebraic constraint exists for four affinely independent points; the residual condition is the square-discriminant relation.

Finite-order
Q
-data are handled by one-variable methods. In that case
Q_{j}=e^{P_{j}}
with
P_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[z]
, by the zero-free case of the Hadamard factorization theorem
[
3
,
16
, Ch. I, §§ 8–9]
. Since

Q_{j}(x)=\|F(x)-p_{j}\|^{2}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}),

a real-axis distance estimate leaves only two asymptotic possibilities for the polynomials
P_{j}
. Each alternative makes the square-discriminant identity force either
e^{P}-C
or
1-Ce^{P}
to be an entire square, with
P
nonconstant and
C\neq 0
, which is impossible. The finite-order statement used later for auxiliary
Q
-data contains the polynomial and finite-order cases stated as Theorems
4.2
and
4.10
.

For unrestricted growth, let

Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}):\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4}.

Logarithmic Bloch–Ochiai gives the Zariski closure of
Q(\mathbb{C})
as a translate of an algebraic subtorus; the form used here is Noguchi’s semi-abelian theorem
[
17
, Main Theorem (i)]
, together with the formulations in
[
20
,
8
]
. Positivity of
Q_{j}(x)
on the real axis allows the translate to be chosen positive real. In torus coordinates,

Q_{j}=\lambda_{j}E^{m_{j}},\qquad\lambda_{j}>0,

with
E:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
Zariski dense. The discriminant becomes a Laurent polynomial

D_{M}(u)=\Delta_{A}(\lambda_{0}u^{m_{0}},\lambda_{1}u^{m_{1}},\lambda_{2}u^{m_{2}},\lambda_{3}u^{m_{3}}),

and
D_{M}(E)=\tau^{2}
. The Laurent-square case is reduced to the finite-order contradiction by pulling back along finite-order dense exponential curves in the torus.

For the nonsquare Laurent case, let
B_{D}
denote the squarefree branch part of a Laurent polynomial
D
. From
D(E)=\tau^{2}
every zero of
B_{D}(E)
has even order. Quotienting by the connected stabilizer of the branch divisor gives a Zariski-dense curve in a torus and a reduced divisor with trivial stabilizer whose pullback has multiplicity at least two everywhere. The level-one truncated Second Main Theorem of Noguchi–Winkelmann–Yamanoi gives

T_{G}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))\leq N_{1}(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D})+\varepsilon T_{G}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))

for reduced divisors on semi-abelian varieties, in the notation of Section
6
, by
[
22
, Main Theorem (iii)]
. The ramification hypothesis gives
N_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}N
, and the First Main Theorem gives
N\leq T_{G}+O(1)
. For
\varepsilon<1/2
the characteristic is bounded. Trivial connected stabilizer makes the relevant Newton polytope full-dimensional, so the divisor closure is big on a smooth toric compactification; bounded characteristic with respect to a big line bundle contradicts Zariski density. Positive toric rank is therefore impossible. This proves Theorem
1.2
. For background on Nevanlinna theory for semi-abelian varieties, see
[
15
,
21
,
19
,
18
]
.

2.
Stem functions and quadratic zero divisors

2.1.
Complexified quaternions

Let

\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}+\mathbb{R}\mathbf{i}+\mathbb{R}\mathbf{j}+\mathbb{R}\mathbf{k}

be the quaternion algebra, and let

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}:=\mathbb{H}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}

be its complexification. The scalar imaginary unit of
\mathbb{C}
is denoted by
\mathrm{i}
, to distinguish it from the quaternionic unit
\mathbf{i}
.

Every element
Z\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
can be written uniquely as

Z=z_{0}+z_{1}\mathbf{i}+z_{2}\mathbf{j}+z_{3}\mathbf{k},\qquad z_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}.

We extend the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
complex bilinearly:

\langle Z,W\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}z_{\ell}w_{\ell}.

This form is complex bilinear, not Hermitian. Its associated quadratic form is

\langle Z,Z\rangle=z_{0}^{2}+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}.

Let

\sigma:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}

denote the anti-linear involution induced by complex conjugation on the scalar factor and fixing
\mathbb{H}
. Thus

\sigma\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}z_{\ell}e_{\ell}\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}\overline{z_{\ell}}e_{\ell},

where
e_{0}=1
,
e_{1}=\mathbf{i}
,
e_{2}=\mathbf{j}
,
e_{3}=\mathbf{k}
.

We write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
for the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{H}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for its complex-bilinear extension to
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Quaternionic conjugation is denoted by
q\mapsto\overline{q}
for
q\in\mathbb{H}
, while complex conjugation of the variable is denoted by
z\mapsto\overline{z}
; the involution
\sigma
is the induced conjugation on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

2.2.
Stem functions

Let

F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}

be an entire holomorphic map. The map
F
is called real-symmetric if

F(\overline{z})=\sigma(F(z)).

Equivalently, writing

F(z)=F_{1}(z)+\mathrm{i}F_{2}(z),\qquad F_{1}(z),F_{2}(z)\in\mathbb{H},

one has

F_{1}(\overline{z})=F_{1}(z),\qquad F_{2}(\overline{z})=-F_{2}(z).

Let

\mathbb{S}=\{J\in\mathbb{H}:J^{2}=-1\}

be the sphere of imaginary units. A real-symmetric entire stem function
F=F_{1}+\mathrm{i}F_{2}
induces an entire slice regular function

f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

by

f(x+yJ)=F_{1}(x+\mathrm{i}y)+JF_{2}(x+\mathrm{i}y),\qquad J\in\mathbb{S}.

Conversely, every entire slice regular function arises in this way from a unique real-symmetric entire stem function. This stem-function viewpoint will be used throughout; see
[
14
,
11
]
for broader formulations.

2.3.
Quadratic omission functions

Let
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a real-symmetric entire stem function, and let
c\in\mathbb{H}
. Define

Q_{c}(z):=\langle F(z)-c,F(z)-c\rangle.

Then
Q_{c}
is entire and real-symmetric:

Q_{c}(\overline{z})=\overline{Q_{c}(z)}.

The Bisi–Winkelmann zero-divisor criterion, cf.
[
1
, Proposition 2.2]
, is used in the following form. In the standard slice-regular notation,
Q_{c}
is the stem of the normal function
N(f-c)=(f-c)*(f-c)^{c}
; see
[
10
,
11
]
for the zero structure of normal functions.

Lemma 2.1

(Omitted values and quadratic zero divisors)
.

Let
F=F_{1}+\mathrm{i}F_{2}
be a real-symmetric entire stem function, and let
f
be the induced entire slice regular function. For
c\in\mathbb{H}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

f
omits
c
;

(ii)

Q_{c}(z)=\langle F(z)-c,F(z)-c\rangle
has no zero in
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Fix
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
and write

F(z)=F_{1}(z)+\mathrm{i}F_{2}(z),\qquad F_{1}(z),F_{2}(z)\in\mathbb{H}.

The corresponding slice values over the sphere associated with
z
are

f(x+yJ)=F_{1}(z)+JF_{2}(z),\qquad J\in\mathbb{S}.

The parametrization
(z,J)\leftrightarrow(\overline{z},-J)
gives the same quaternionic point, so the criterion may be checked on these spherical fibers. When
z=x\in\mathbb{R}
, real-symmetry gives
F_{2}(x)=0
, and the criterion reduces to
Q_{c}(x)=\|F_{1}(x)-c\|^{2}=0
if and only if
f(x)=c
. On a nondegenerate fiber,
f
takes the value
c
precisely when there exists
J\in\mathbb{S}
such that

F_{1}(z)+JF_{2}(z)=c.

Set

a:=F_{1}(z)-c,\qquad b:=F_{2}(z).

The question is whether there is
J\in\mathbb{S}
such that

a+Jb=0.

On the other hand,

Q_{c}(z)=\langle a+\mathrm{i}b,a+\mathrm{i}b\rangle=\|a\|^{2}-\|b\|^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\langle a,b\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

Therefore
Q_{c}(z)=0
if and only if

\|a\|=\|b\|,\qquad\langle a,b\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=0.

Assume first that
Q_{c}(z)=0
. If
b=0
, then
a=0
, so
F_{1}(z)=c
, and
f
takes the value
c
. If
b\neq 0
, define

J:=-ab^{-1}.

Then

|J|=\frac{|a|}{|b|}=1,

and

\operatorname{Re}J=-\operatorname{Re}(ab^{-1})=-\|b\|^{-2}\operatorname{Re}(a\overline{b})=-\|b\|^{-2}\langle a,b\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=0.

Hence
J\in\mathbb{S}
, and
Jb=-a
. Thus
F_{1}(z)+JF_{2}(z)=c
.

Conversely, suppose
F_{1}(z)+JF_{2}(z)=c
for some
J\in\mathbb{S}
. Then
a=-Jb
. Left multiplication by
J
is an orthogonal transformation of
\mathbb{H}
, so
\|a\|=\|b\|
. Moreover,

\langle a,b\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=-\langle Jb,b\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=0.

Thus
Q_{c}(z)=0
.

Q_{c}
has a zero if and only if
f
takes the value
c
, as claimed.
∎

3.
The four-point discriminant

Let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{H}
be affinely independent. Translating the target by
-a_{0}
, we may assume that

p_{0}=0,\qquad p_{j}=a_{j}-a_{0}\quad(j=1,2,3).

Thus
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are real-linearly independent in
\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Let

V:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}.

Then
V
is a three-dimensional real subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. Choose a unit vector

n\in V^{\perp},\qquad\|n\|=1.

Let
V_{\mathbb{C}}:=V\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. Then

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}=V_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\mathbb{C}n.

Let
G=(G_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq 3}
be the Gram matrix

G_{ij}:=\langle p_{i},p_{j}\rangle.

Since
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are real-linearly independent,
G
is positive definite and invertible.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a real-symmetric entire stem function. With respect to the decomposition above, write

F=W+\tau n,

where

W:\mathbb{C}\to V_{\mathbb{C}},\qquad\tau:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}

are entire and real-symmetric. For
j=0,1,2,3
, define

Q_{j}(z):=\langle F(z)-p_{j},F(z)-p_{j}\rangle.

Definition 3.1

(Four-point omission datum)
.

A four-point omission datum is a pair

(F,(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}))

where
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a real-symmetric entire stem function, the points
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{H}
are affinely independent and normalized by
p_{0}=0
, and each function

Q_{j}=\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle,\qquad j=0,1,2,3,

is zero-free on
\mathbb{C}
. The datum is called nonconstant if
F
is nonconstant.

Remark 3.2
.

For a four-point omission datum, the associated
Q
-data satisfy the square-discriminant identity of Proposition
3.5
; this is a consequence of the decomposition
F=W+\tau n
, not an additional hypothesis.

Lemma 3.3

(Gram reconstruction)
.

For
j=1,2,3
, define

b_{j}(Q):=\frac{Q_{0}+\|p_{j}\|^{2}-Q_{j}}{2}

and let
b(Q)=(b_{1}(Q),b_{2}(Q),b_{3}(Q))^{T}
. Then

\langle W,p_{j}\rangle=b_{j}(Q),\qquad j=1,2,3.

Consequently,

W=\sum_{i,k=1}^{3}(G^{-1})_{ik}b_{k}(Q)p_{i},

and

\langle W,W\rangle=b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q).

Proof.

Since
p_{j}\in V
and
n\perp V
,

\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle=\langle W-p_{j},W-p_{j}\rangle+\tau^{2}.

For
j=0
, this gives

Q_{0}=\langle W,W\rangle+\tau^{2}.

For
j=1,2,3
, expanding gives

Q_{j}=\langle W,W\rangle-2\langle W,p_{j}\rangle+\|p_{j}\|^{2}+\tau^{2}.

Subtracting the formula for
Q_{0}
gives

\langle W,p_{j}\rangle=\frac{Q_{0}+\|p_{j}\|^{2}-Q_{j}}{2}=b_{j}(Q).

Now write
W=\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}p_{i}
. Then

b_{j}(Q)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{i}G_{ij}.

In matrix form,
b=Gu
, since
G
is symmetric. Thus
u=G^{-1}b
, and hence

W=\sum_{i,k=1}^{3}(G^{-1})_{ik}b_{k}(Q)p_{i}.

Finally,

\langle W,W\rangle=u^{T}Gu=b^{T}G^{-1}b.

∎

Definition 3.4

(Four-point discriminant)
.

The four-point discriminant associated with the ordered quadruple
A=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
is

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}):=Q_{0}-b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q).

Proposition 3.5

(Square-discriminant identity)
.

Let
F=W+\tau n
be a real-symmetric entire stem function and let

Q_{j}=\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle,\qquad j=0,1,2,3.

Then

(3.1)

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})=\tau^{2}.

Proof.

Since
F=W+\tau n
and
W\perp n
,

Q_{0}=\langle F,F\rangle=\langle W,W\rangle+\tau^{2}.

By Lemma
3.3
,
\langle W,W\rangle=b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q)
. Hence

\displaystyle\tau^{2}

\displaystyle=Q_{0}-b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}).

∎

Equation (
3.1
) will be called the square-discriminant identity.

Here and below
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})^{*}
denotes the group of units of
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
, equivalently the zero-free entire functions.

Proposition 3.6

(Reconstruction from square-discriminant data)
.

Let

Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})^{*}

be real-symmetric zero-free entire functions. Suppose that there exists a real-symmetric entire function
\tau\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})=\tau^{2}.

Define

W(Q):=\sum_{i,k=1}^{3}(G^{-1})_{ik}b_{k}(Q)p_{i}

and

F:=W(Q)+\tau n.

Then
F
is a real-symmetric entire stem function and satisfies

\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle=Q_{j},\qquad j=0,1,2,3.

Consequently, the slice regular function induced by
F
omits
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
.

Proof.

The functions
b_{j}(Q)
are real-symmetric because the
Q_{j}
are real-symmetric and all coefficients entering the definition are real. Hence
W(Q)
is a real-symmetric entire map into
V_{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
\tau
is real-symmetric and
n\in\mathbb{H}
,
F=W(Q)+\tau n
is a real-symmetric entire stem function.

First,

\langle F,F\rangle=\langle W(Q),W(Q)\rangle+\tau^{2}=b(Q)^{T}G^{-1}b(Q)+\tau^{2}=Q_{0}

by the square-discriminant assumption. Thus
\langle F-p_{0},F-p_{0}\rangle=Q_{0}
.

For
j=1,2,3
,

\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle=\langle F,F\rangle-2\langle W(Q),p_{j}\rangle+\|p_{j}\|^{2}.

By construction
\langle W(Q),p_{j}\rangle=b_{j}(Q)
, so

\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle=Q_{0}-2b_{j}(Q)+\|p_{j}\|^{2}=Q_{j}.

Thus all four identities hold. Since each
Q_{j}
is zero-free, Lemma
2.1
implies that the induced slice regular function omits
p_{j}
for all
j
.
∎

Theorem 3.7

(Four-point discriminant reduction)
.

Let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{H}
be affinely independent. After translating by
-a_{0}
, set
p_{0}=0
and
p_{j}=a_{j}-a_{0}
for
j=1,2,3
. Define
V,n,G,b(Q)
, and
\Delta_{A}
as above.

There exists a nonconstant entire slice regular function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
omitting
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
if and only if there exist real-symmetric zero-free entire functions

Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})^{*}

and a real-symmetric entire function
\tau\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})=\tau^{2},

and the stem function

F=W(Q)+\tau n

is nonconstant.

Proof.

Assume first that such a nonconstant slice regular function exists. Translate by
-a_{0}
and let
F
be the real-symmetric entire stem function inducing
f-a_{0}
. Decompose
F=W+\tau n
and define
Q_{j}=\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle
. By Lemma
2.1
, each
Q_{j}
is zero-free. Since
F
is real-symmetric and
p_{j}\in\mathbb{H}
, each
Q_{j}
is real-symmetric. By Proposition
3.5
,
\Delta_{A}(Q)=\tau^{2}
. Since
f
is nonconstant,
F
is nonconstant.

Conversely, suppose such
Q_{j}
and
\tau
are given. Define
F=W(Q)+\tau n
. By Proposition
3.6
,
F
is a real-symmetric entire stem function and
\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle=Q_{j}
for all
j
. Since the
Q_{j}
are zero-free, Lemma
2.1
implies that the induced slice regular function omits
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
. Adding
a_{0}
gives a function omitting
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
. It is nonconstant precisely when
F
is nonconstant.
∎

Remark 3.8

(Real structure of the square root)
.

The square root in Theorem
3.7
is part of the real stem data. The assertion that
\Delta_{A}(Q)
is a square in
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
alone is weaker: a square root need not be real-symmetric, and then
W(Q)+\tau n
need not define a real-symmetric stem function. Thus the condition is formulated with a real-symmetric root
\tau
.

3.1.
Normal form

The next coordinate form identifies the discriminant with the global normal-square coordinate in the four quadratic variables.

Define

R_{j}(W):=\langle W-p_{j},W-p_{j}\rangle,\qquad W\in V_{\mathbb{C}},\quad j=0,1,2,3.

Consider the polynomial map

\Theta:V_{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}

given by

\Theta(W,S)=\bigl(R_{0}(W)+S,R_{1}(W)+S,R_{2}(W)+S,R_{3}(W)+S\bigr).

Proposition 3.9

(Normal form for the discriminant)
.

The map
\Theta
is a polynomial isomorphism. Its inverse is

Q\longmapsto(W(Q),\Delta_{A}(Q)).

In particular,

\Delta_{A}(\Theta(W,S))=S.

Proof.

Let
Q=\Theta(W,S)
. Then

Q_{0}=\langle W,W\rangle+S.

For
j=1,2,3
,

R_{j}(W)=R_{0}(W)-2\langle W,p_{j}\rangle+\|p_{j}\|^{2},

so

Q_{j}=Q_{0}-2\langle W,p_{j}\rangle+\|p_{j}\|^{2}.

Therefore

b_{j}(Q)=\frac{Q_{0}+\|p_{j}\|^{2}-Q_{j}}{2}=\langle W,p_{j}\rangle.

The Gram reconstruction recovers the original
W
, and

\Delta_{A}(Q)=Q_{0}-\langle W,W\rangle=S.

Conversely, for arbitrary
Q
, setting
W=W(Q)
and
S=\Delta_{A}(Q)
gives
Q_{j}=R_{j}(W)+S
. Hence
\Theta
is a polynomial isomorphism with the claimed inverse.
∎

Remark 3.10

(Orthogonal model)
.

If
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are an orthonormal basis of
V
, then
G=I_{3}
and

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})=Q_{0}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{3}(Q_{0}+1-Q_{j})^{2}.

Example 3.11

(The standard tetrahedral frame)
.

Take

A=(0,1,\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}).

Then
V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{1,\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\}
and one may take
n=\mathbf{k}
. Since
1,\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}
are orthonormal in
\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}
, the preceding formula gives

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})=Q_{0}-\frac{1}{4}\bigl((Q_{0}+1-Q_{1})^{2}+(Q_{0}+1-Q_{2})^{2}+(Q_{0}+1-Q_{3})^{2}\bigr).

Writing

F=w_{0}+w_{1}\mathbf{i}+w_{2}\mathbf{j}+\tau\mathbf{k},

with
w_{0},w_{1},w_{2},\tau\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
, the corresponding quadratic functions are

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=w_{0}^{2}+w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}+\tau^{2},

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=(w_{0}-1)^{2}+w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}+\tau^{2},

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=w_{0}^{2}+(w_{1}-1)^{2}+w_{2}^{2}+\tau^{2},

\displaystyle Q_{3}

\displaystyle=w_{0}^{2}+w_{1}^{2}+(w_{2}-1)^{2}+\tau^{2}.

The discriminant recovers precisely the normal square
\tau^{2}
.

4.
Polynomial and finite-order cases

Throughout this section the four points are affinely independent and normalized as in Section
3
. Let
F=W+\tau n
be the stem function and

Q_{j}=\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle.

The polynomial case isolates the role of zero-free quadratic data; the finite-order result is the form needed in the toroidal argument.

Lemma 4.1

(Constant
Q_{j}
-data)
.

If
Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
are all constant entire functions, then
F=W+\tau n
is constant.

Proof.

If the
Q_{j}
are constant, then all
b_{j}(Q)
are constant. By the Gram reconstruction formula,
W
is constant. Since

Q_{0}=\langle W,W\rangle+\tau^{2},

it follows that
\tau^{2}
is constant. Hence
\tau
is constant: if the constant is nonzero,
\tau
takes values in the two-point set of its square roots, and if it is zero then
\tau\equiv 0
. Therefore
F
is constant.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(Polynomial case)
.

Let
f(q)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}q^{m}\alpha_{m}
be a slice regular polynomial. If
f
omits four affinely independent values, then
f
is constant.

Proof.

After translating the target, let
F
be the stem polynomial associated with
f-a_{0}
. Then each

Q_{j}(z)=\langle F(z)-p_{j},F(z)-p_{j}\rangle

is a complex polynomial. Since
f
omits
a_{j}
, Lemma
2.1
gives that
Q_{j}
is zero-free. A zero-free complex polynomial is constant. Hence all
Q_{j}
are constant. Lemma
4.1
implies that
F
is constant, and therefore
f
is constant.
∎

4.1.
Finite-order zero-free functions

For a complex entire function
h
write

\rho(h)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M(r,h)}{\log r},\qquad M(r,h)=\max_{|z|=r}|h(z)|,

with the convention
\rho(h)=0
for constant functions. If
F=\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}F_{\ell}e_{\ell}
is the stem function of an entire slice regular function, we say that
F
, and hence the induced slice regular function, has finite order when

\max_{0\leq\ell\leq 3}\rho(F_{\ell})<\infty.

By the representation formula, the same finiteness condition is equivalent to the corresponding maximum-modulus convention on quaternionic balls, up to the usual comparison of suprema; see
[
11
]
. The only property used below is the direct implication that finite order of the stem components gives finite order of the quadratic functions
Q_{j}
.

Lemma 4.3

(Finite order passes to the quadratic data)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a finite-order entire stem function. Then for every
p\in\mathbb{H}
,

Q_{p}(z)=\langle F(z)-p,F(z)-p\rangle

is a finite-order entire function.

Proof.

Write
F=\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}F_{\ell}e_{\ell}
. Let
\rho
be larger than the orders of all components
F_{\ell}
. For every
\varepsilon>0
and all sufficiently large
r
,

\max_{|z|\leq r}|F_{\ell}(z)|\leq\exp(r^{\rho+\varepsilon})

for all
\ell
. Since
Q_{p}
is a finite sum of products of the
F_{\ell}
and constants,

\max_{|z|\leq r}|Q_{p}(z)|\leq C\exp(2r^{\rho+\varepsilon})

for large
r
. Hence
Q_{p}
has finite order.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Zero-free finite-order entire functions)
.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})^{*}
be zero-free and of finite order. Then there exists a complex polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}[z]
such that
Q=e^{P}
. If
Q(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
P
may be chosen with real coefficients.

Proof.

The first assertion is the zero-free case of the Hadamard factorization theorem for finite-order entire functions; see, for instance,
[
3
, Ch. I, §§ 8–9]
and
[
16
, Ch. I, §§ 1–3]
. Since
\mathbb{C}
is simply connected and
Q
is zero-free,
Q=e^{g}
for some entire function
g
. If
Q
has order at most
\rho
, then the Borel–Carathéodory inequality applied to
g-g(0)
gives polynomial growth for
g
. Cauchy’s estimates at the origin then give
g^{(m)}(0)=0
for all sufficiently large
m
, and hence
g
is a polynomial.

Write
P=g
. If
Q(x)>0
for real
x
, then
e^{P(x)}=Q(x)>0
, so
\operatorname{Im}P(x)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. By continuity this integer is constant on
\mathbb{R}
. Subtracting the corresponding constant
2\pi\mathrm{i}m
from
P
, which does not change
e^{P}
, we may assume
P(x)\in\mathbb{R}
for all real
x
. A polynomial real-valued on the real axis has real coefficients.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(Simple-zero obstruction)
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{C}[z]
be nonconstant and let
C\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then neither
e^{P}-C
nor
1-Ce^{P}
is an entire square.

Proof.

Choose a logarithm
\log C
. The equation
e^{P(z)}=C
is equivalent to

P(z)=\log C+2\pi\mathrm{i}m

for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. The set of critical values

\operatorname{CritVal}(P)=P(\{P^{\prime}=0\})
is finite, while the set

\{\log C+2\pi\mathrm{i}m:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is infinite. Choose
m
such that

c_{m}=\log C+2\pi\mathrm{i}m

is not a critical value. Since
P
is surjective, there is
z_{0}
with
P(z_{0})=c_{m}
, and then
P^{\prime}(z_{0})\neq 0
. Thus

(e^{P}-C)^{\prime}(z_{0})=e^{P(z_{0})}P^{\prime}(z_{0})=CP^{\prime}(z_{0})\neq 0.

So
e^{P}-C
has a simple zero and cannot be an entire square. Since
1-Ce^{P}=-C(e^{P}-1/C)
, the second assertion follows.
∎

4.2.
Distance estimates

For real
x
, real-symmetry gives
F(x)\in\mathbb{H}
. Therefore

Q_{j}(x)=\|F(x)-p_{j}\|^{2}.

If
Q_{j}
is zero-free, then
Q_{j}(x)>0
.

Lemma 4.6

(Distance comparison)
.

For distinct indices
i\neq k
, set

d_{ik}:=\|p_{i}-p_{k}\|^{2}>0.

Then for every real
x
,

\left|Q_{i}(x)-Q_{k}(x)-d_{ik}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{d_{ik}}\sqrt{Q_{k}(x)}.

Proof.

Put
u(x)=F(x)-p_{k}\in\mathbb{H}
and
v_{ik}=p_{i}-p_{k}
. Then
Q_{k}(x)=\|u(x)\|^{2}
and

Q_{i}(x)=\|u(x)-v_{ik}\|^{2}=\|u(x)\|^{2}-2\langle u(x),v_{ik}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}+\|v_{ik}\|^{2}.

Therefore

Q_{i}(x)-Q_{k}(x)-d_{ik}=-2\langle u(x),v_{ik}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

Cauchy–Schwarz gives the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.7

(Finite-order rigidity alternatives)
.

Assume that the four functions
Q_{j}
are zero-free, finite-order, and arise from an omission datum. Write

Q_{j}=e^{P_{j}},\qquad P_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[z].

Suppose
P_{k}
is nonconstant. Along one of the two real directions
x\to+\infty
or
x\to-\infty
, one of the following alternatives occurs:

(A)

P_{k}(x)\to+\infty
, and then
P_{i}=P_{k}
for every
i\neq k
.

(B)

P_{k}(x)\to-\infty
, and then
P_{i}\equiv\log d_{ik}
for every
i\neq k
.

Proof.

Since
P_{k}
is a nonconstant real polynomial, along at least one real direction it tends either to
+\infty
or to
-\infty
.

If
P_{k}(x)\to+\infty
, then
Q_{k}(x)\to+\infty
. Lemma
4.6
gives

Q_{i}(x)=Q_{k}(x)+d_{ik}+O(\sqrt{Q_{k}(x)}).

Thus
Q_{i}(x)/Q_{k}(x)\to 1
, so

e^{P_{i}(x)-P_{k}(x)}\to 1.

Since
P_{i}-P_{k}
is a real polynomial, this implies
P_{i}-P_{k}\to 0
along an infinite real direction, hence
P_{i}=P_{k}
.

If
P_{k}(x)\to-\infty
, then
Q_{k}(x)\to 0
. Lemma
4.6
gives
Q_{i}(x)\to d_{ik}>0
. Hence
P_{i}(x)=\log Q_{i}(x)\to\log d_{ik}
. A real polynomial tending to a finite limit along an infinite real direction is constant. Therefore
P_{i}\equiv\log d_{ik}
.
∎

Lemma 4.8

(Basepoint invariance of the normal square)
.

Let
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
be affinely independent and let
V_{\mathrm{aff}}
be the direction space of their real affine span. Choose a unit vector
n\in V_{\mathrm{aff}}^{\perp}
and write, after the normalization
p_{0}=0
,

F=W+\tau n,\qquad W\in(V_{\mathrm{aff}})_{\mathbb{C}}.

If
F-p_{k}=W_{k}+\tau_{k}n_{k}
is the decomposition obtained after using
p_{k}
as basepoint, where
n_{k}
is a unit normal to
V_{\mathrm{aff}}
, then
n_{k}=\pm n
and
\tau_{k}^{2}=\tau^{2}
. Consequently the square in the discriminant identity is independent of the chosen basepoint.

Proof.

For the normalization
p_{0}=0
, the direction space is
V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
. If
p_{k}
is used as basepoint, the new direction space is

\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{p_{j}-p_{k}:j\neq k\}=V,

because it is the same affine-span direction. Hence its normal line is the same line
\mathbb{C}n
, so
n_{k}=\pm n
. Writing
F=W+\tau n
with
W\in V_{\mathbb{C}}
and using
p_{k}\in V
, one has

F-p_{k}=(W-p_{k})+\tau n.

Thus the normal coordinate changes at most by sign, and the square is unchanged.
∎

Corollary 4.9

(Finite-order
Q
-data)
.

Let
(F,(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}))
be a four-point omission datum in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Suppose that each

Q_{j}(z)=\langle F(z)-p_{j},F(z)-p_{j}\rangle,\qquad j=0,1,2,3,

is of finite order. Then
F
is constant.

Proof.

For real
x
, real-symmetry gives
F(x)\in\mathbb{H}
, and hence

Q_{j}(x)=\|F(x)-p_{j}\|^{2}>0.

By Lemma
4.4
,

Q_{j}=e^{P_{j}},\qquad P_{j}\in\mathbb{R}[z].

If all
P_{j}
are constant, then all
Q_{j}
are constant, and Lemma
4.1
implies that
F
is constant.

Assume that some
P_{k}
is nonconstant. By Lemma
4.7
, either Alternative A or Alternative B holds. The rebasing at
p_{k}
used below preserves the normal square by Lemma
4.8
.

In Alternative A, all
Q_{j}
are equal to
U=e^{P_{k}}
. Use
p_{k}
as the basepoint. Write

\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\}=\{0,1,2,3\}\setminus\{k\},\qquad r_{\alpha}=p_{i_{\alpha}}-p_{k}\quad(1\leq\alpha\leq 3),

and let
G_{k}
be the Gram matrix of
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
. Then all four
Q
-functions are
U
, so

b_{\alpha}=\frac{U+\|r_{\alpha}\|^{2}-U}{2}=\frac{\|r_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{2}.

With
d_{k}=(\|r_{1}\|^{2},\|r_{2}\|^{2},\|r_{3}\|^{2})^{T}
, the discriminant identity gives

\tau^{2}=U-\frac{1}{4}d_{k}^{T}G_{k}^{-1}d_{k}.

The constant
C_{k}=\frac{1}{4}d_{k}^{T}G_{k}^{-1}d_{k}
is positive, since
G_{k}^{-1}
is positive definite and
d_{k}\neq 0
. Hence

\tau^{2}=e^{P_{k}}-C_{k},

contradicting Lemma
4.5
.

In Alternative B, the other three functions are constants:

Q_{i}\equiv d_{ik}=\|p_{i}-p_{k}\|^{2}\qquad(i\neq k).

Again using
p_{k}
as basepoint, put
U=Q_{k}=e^{P_{k}}
. Then

b=\frac{U}{2}(1,1,1)^{T}.

Thus

\tau^{2}=U-\frac{U^{2}}{4}(1,1,1)G_{k}^{-1}(1,1,1)^{T}.

Let

D_{k}=\frac{1}{4}(1,1,1)G_{k}^{-1}(1,1,1)^{T}>0.

Then

\tau^{2}=U(1-D_{k}U)=e^{P_{k}}(1-D_{k}e^{P_{k}}).

Since
e^{P_{k}}
has the entire square root
e^{P_{k}/2}
, the function

S:=\tau e^{-P_{k}/2}

is entire and satisfies

S^{2}=1-D_{k}e^{P_{k}},

again contradicting Lemma
4.5
.

Both alternatives are impossible. Hence all
P_{j}
are constant, and
F
is constant.
∎

Theorem 4.10

(Finite-order case)
.

Let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{H}
be affinely independent. Let
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be an entire slice regular function of finite order. If
f
omits
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
, then
f
is constant.

Proof.

Translate by
-a_{0}
and let
F
be the stem function of
f-a_{0}
. Define
p_{0}=0
and
p_{j}=a_{j}-a_{0}
for
1\leq j\leq 3
. By Lemma
2.1
, the functions

Q_{j}=\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle

are zero-free, so
(F,(p_{j})_{j=0}^{3})
is a four-point omission datum. By Lemma
4.3
, each
Q_{j}
has finite order. Corollary
4.9
gives that
F
is constant, and hence
f
is constant.
∎

5.
Toroidal reduction

No growth condition is imposed on the original slice regular function in the rest of the proof. Finite-order curves appear only as auxiliary exponential curves in the torus, through Corollary
4.9
.

Let
(F,(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}))
be a four-point omission datum. Define

Q_{j}(z)=\langle F(z)-p_{j},F(z)-p_{j}\rangle,

so each
Q_{j}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})^{*}
. Thus

Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}):\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4}

is an entire curve.

Define its toric Zariski closure by

M_{Q}:=\overline{Q(\mathbb{C})}^{\mathrm{Zar}}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4},

and its toric rank by

r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q):=\dim M_{Q}.

Lemma 5.1

(Positivity on the real axis)
.

For every real
x
and every
j
,

Q_{j}(x)>0.

Proof.

Since
F
is real-symmetric,
F(x)\in\mathbb{H}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence

Q_{j}(x)=\|F(x)-p_{j}\|^{2}.

The function
Q_{j}
is zero-free, so it is strictly positive on the real axis.
∎

Proposition 5.2

(Positive real torus parametrization)
.

Let
Q:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4}
come from a real-symmetric four-point omission datum. Then
M_{Q}
is a positive real translate of an algebraic subtorus: if
d=\dim M_{Q}
, there exist
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and a Zariski-dense real-symmetric entire curve
E:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
such that

Q_{j}=\lambda_{j}E^{m_{j}},\qquad j=0,1,2,3,

the exponent vectors
m_{0},\ldots,m_{3}
generate the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and

E(x)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

By the logarithmic Bloch–Ochiai theorem for semi-abelian varieties, due to Noguchi
[
17
, Main Theorem (i)]
and used here together with the formulations in
[
20
,
8
]
,
M_{Q}
is a translate of an algebraic subtorus of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4}
. Choose
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
5.1
,
\lambda:=Q(x_{0})
belongs to
(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{4}
. Since
\lambda\in M_{Q}
, the translate may be written canonically as

M_{Q}=\lambda H,\qquad H:=\lambda^{-1}M_{Q},

where
H
is an algebraic subtorus.

Choose an algebraic group isomorphism

\iota:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}\to H

given by monomials

\iota(u)=(u^{m_{0}},u^{m_{1}},u^{m_{2}},u^{m_{3}}),\qquad m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Since
\iota
is an isomorphism onto
H
, the coordinate characters
u^{m_{0}},\ldots,u^{m_{3}}
generate the character lattice of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
; in the chosen basis,
m_{0},\ldots,m_{3}
generate
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Define

E(z)=\iota^{-1}(\lambda^{-1}Q(z)).

Then
Q_{j}=\lambda_{j}E^{m_{j}}
, and
E
is Zariski dense because
Q
is Zariski dense in
M_{Q}
. For real
x
,
\lambda^{-1}Q(x)\in H\cap(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{4}
. Lemma
A.1
identifies this positive real locus with
\iota((\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d})
; equivalently, the monomial inverse preserves positivity because the exponent vectors generate the full character lattice. Hence
E(x)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
. The same lemma gives the real-symmetry of
E
.
∎

Assume
d=r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)>0
and write

Q_{j}=\lambda_{j}E^{m_{j}},\qquad\lambda_{j}>0,\quad m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Define the restricted discriminant on the torus
M_{Q}
by

D_{M}(u):=\Delta_{A}(\lambda_{0}u^{m_{0}},\lambda_{1}u^{m_{1}},\lambda_{2}u^{m_{2}},\lambda_{3}u^{m_{3}}).

Then

D_{M}\in\mathbb{R}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}],

and the square-discriminant equation becomes

D_{M}(E(z))=\tau(z)^{2}.

Lemma 5.3

(Rank zero)
.

If
r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)=0
, then
F
is constant.

Proof.

If
r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)=0
, then all
Q_{j}
are constant. Lemma
4.1
implies that
F
is constant.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Finite-order dense exponential curves)
.

Let
d\geq 1
, and choose real numbers
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}
linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. Define

\gamma_{\alpha}(z)=(e^{\alpha_{1}z},\dots,e^{\alpha_{d}z}).

Then
\gamma_{\alpha}
is Zariski dense in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
, and each coordinate is real-symmetric and positive on the real axis. Moreover, if
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is nonzero, then the character
\gamma_{\alpha}^{m}
is nonconstant.

Proof.

If a Laurent polynomial
P(u)=\sum_{\nu\in S}c_{\nu}u^{\nu}
vanishes on
\gamma_{\alpha}
, then

0=P(\gamma_{\alpha}(z))=\sum_{\nu\in S}c_{\nu}e^{\langle\nu,\alpha\rangle z}.

The numbers
\langle\nu,\alpha\rangle
are distinct for distinct
\nu
, by
\mathbb{Q}
-linear independence. The corresponding exponentials are linearly independent, so all
c_{\nu}
vanish. The final assertion follows because
\langle m,\alpha\rangle\neq 0
for every nonzero
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Assume a nonconstant four-point omission datum exists with positive toric rank
d=r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)>0
, and let
D_{M}
be the restricted discriminant on its toric Zariski closure. Then

D_{M}\not\equiv 0.

Proof.

Suppose
D_{M}\equiv 0
. Choose a finite-order Zariski-dense exponential curve
\gamma_{\alpha}
as in Lemma
5.4
. Define

\widetilde{Q}_{j}(z)=\lambda_{j}\gamma_{\alpha}(z)^{m_{j}}=\lambda_{j}\exp(\langle m_{j},\alpha\rangle z).

Then each
\widetilde{Q}_{j}
is real-symmetric, zero-free, and finite-order, and

\Delta_{A}(\widetilde{Q}_{0},\widetilde{Q}_{1},\widetilde{Q}_{2},\widetilde{Q}_{3})=0.

Taking
\widetilde{\tau}\equiv 0
, Proposition
3.6
produces an entire stem function with zero-free finite-order quadratic data. Since
d>0
, not all exponent vectors
m_{j}
are zero; otherwise the torus parametrization would be constant. Choose an index
j
with
m_{j}\neq 0
. For the
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent vector
\alpha
of Lemma
5.4
, one has
\langle m_{j},\alpha\rangle\neq 0
, so
\widetilde{Q}_{j}
is nonconstant. If the reconstructed stem function were constant, all four functions
\langle F-p_{j},F-p_{j}\rangle
would be constant, contrary to the prescribed nonconstant
\widetilde{Q}_{j}
. Thus the reconstructed stem function is nonconstant, contradicting Corollary
4.9
.
∎

Lemma 5.6

(Real descent of Laurent squares)
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{R}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]
be nonzero. Suppose that over the complex Laurent ring

D=c\,u^{\ell}S(u)^{2},

where
c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
S\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]
. Then there exist
a\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
,
\ell_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
S_{0}\in\mathbb{R}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]
such that

D=a\,u^{\ell_{0}}S_{0}(u)^{2}.

Proof.

Put

R=\mathbb{R}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}],\qquad R_{\mathbb{C}}=R\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}.

Both rings are UFDs; the units of
R
are
au^{m}
with
a\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Write the factorization of
D
in
R
as

D=au^{m}\prod_{\beta}f_{\beta}^{e_{\beta}},

where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and the
f_{\beta}
are pairwise nonassociated real irreducible Laurent polynomials which are not units. Fix one factor
f=f_{\beta}
.

Over
R_{\mathbb{C}}
there are two possibilities. Either
f
remains irreducible, in which case the multiplicity of this complex irreducible factor in
D
is
e_{\beta}
. Or
f
is reducible over
\mathbb{C}
. Galois descent in the UFD
R_{\mathbb{C}}
gives, after multiplication by a Laurent unit,

f=b\,h\,\overline{h}

with
h
complex irreducible. The factors
h
and
\overline{h}
are nonassociated. Indeed, if
\overline{h}=c\,u^{k}h
with
c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then conjugating again gives
h=|c|^{2}u^{2k}h
, whence
k=0
and
|c|=1
. Writing
c=e^{2\mathrm{i}\theta}
, the Laurent polynomial
g=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}h
satisfies
\overline{g}=g
, so
g\in R
, and
g
is a proper nonunit factor of
f
in
R
, contradicting the irreducibility of
f
over
R
. Since
h
cannot divide the complex factorization of a different nonassociated real irreducible factor, the multiplicities of
h
and
\overline{h}
in
D
are both
e_{\beta}
. As
D
is a square up to a Laurent unit in
R_{\mathbb{C}}
, every complex irreducible factor appearing in
D
has even multiplicity. Hence
e_{\beta}
is even in either case.

Thus all non-unit real irreducible factors of
D
have even exponent. Therefore

D=au^{m}S_{0}^{2}

for some
S_{0}\in R
. Taking
\ell_{0}=m
gives the claimed form.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Assume a nonconstant four-point omission datum exists with positive toric rank
d=r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)>0
. Then the restricted discriminant
D_{M}
cannot be written as

D_{M}=c\,u^{\ell}S(u)^{2}

with
c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
S\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]
.

Proof.

A nonzero constant Laurent polynomial is a Laurent unit times a square, so the assertion also excludes the constant nonzero case. Assume that
D_{M}
is such a Laurent unit times a square. Since
D_{M}
has real coefficients, Lemma
5.6
gives

D_{M}=a\,u^{\ell_{0}}S_{0}(u)^{2}

with
a\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
,
\ell_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
S_{0}
real Laurent. Evaluate on the actual curve
E
. For real
x
,
E(x)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
, so
E(x)^{\ell_{0}}>0
and, because
S_{0}
has real coefficients,
S_{0}(E(x))^{2}\geq 0
. The normal coordinate is real on the real axis, and hence

D_{M}(E(x))=\tau(x)^{2}\geq 0.

If
a<0
, the identity

\tau(x)^{2}=aE(x)^{\ell_{0}}S_{0}(E(x))^{2}

forces both sides to vanish for every real
x
. Thus
S_{0}(E(x))=0
for all real
x
, hence
S_{0}(E)\equiv 0
. By Zariski density,
S_{0}\equiv 0
, contradicting Proposition
5.5
. Therefore
a>0
.

Choose a finite-order Zariski-dense exponential curve
\gamma_{\alpha}
as in Lemma
5.4
. Define

\widetilde{Q}_{j}(z)=\lambda_{j}\exp(\langle m_{j},\alpha\rangle z)

and

\widetilde{\tau}(z)=\sqrt{a}\,\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle\ell_{0},\alpha\rangle z\right)S_{0}(e^{\alpha_{1}z},\dots,e^{\alpha_{d}z}).

Then

\Delta_{A}(\widetilde{Q}_{0},\widetilde{Q}_{1},\widetilde{Q}_{2},\widetilde{Q}_{3})=\widetilde{\tau}^{2}.

The functions
\widetilde{Q}_{j}
are real-symmetric because
\lambda_{j}>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; the function
\widetilde{\tau}
is real-symmetric because
a>0
and
S_{0}
has real Laurent coefficients. Since the exponent vectors
m_{0},\ldots,m_{3}
generate
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
d>0
, at least one
m_{j}
is nonzero. By Lemma
5.4
, the corresponding
\widetilde{Q}_{j}
is nonconstant. Proposition
3.6
gives a stem function whose four quadratic functions are precisely the
\widetilde{Q}_{j}
; since one of them is nonconstant, the stem function itself is nonconstant. Its four quadratic functions are zero-free and of finite order, contradicting Corollary
4.9
.
∎

6.
Laurent branch divisors and the NWY ramification obstruction

The form of the theorem of Noguchi–Winkelmann–Yamanoi needed here is the following divisor case. Here
T_{g}(r;L)
denotes the Nevanlinna characteristic with respect to a line bundle
L
, and
N_{1}
denotes the counting function truncated at level one.

Notation 6.1
.

For fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the notation

A(r)\leq B(r)\quad\|_{\varepsilon}

means that the estimate holds for all
r
outside an exceptional subset of
(0,\infty)
of finite Lebesgue measure, depending on
\varepsilon
; this is the convention used in the theorem of Noguchi–Winkelmann–Yamanoi.

Theorem 6.2

(Noguchi–Winkelmann–Yamanoi, divisor case)
.

Let
A
be a semi-abelian variety, let
g:\mathbb{C}\to A
be a Zariski-dense entire curve, and let
\mathcal{D}\subset A
be an effective reduced divisor. Then there is a smooth equivariant compactification

\overline{A}=\overline{A}(A,\mathcal{D})

of
A
such that, for the closure
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

(6.1)

T_{g}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))\leq N_{1}(r;g^{*}\mathcal{D})+\varepsilon T_{g}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))\quad\|_{\varepsilon}.

This is the divisor case of
[
22
, Main Theorem (iii)]
. In the applications
A
is an algebraic torus; smooth equivariant compactifications are then smooth toric compactifications, and any two are dominated by a common smooth projective fan refinement.

Remark 6.3

(Equivariant refinements)
.

If
\mu:X^{\prime}\to\overline{A}
is a smooth equivariant refinement and
g^{\prime}:\mathbb{C}\to X^{\prime}
is the lift of
g
through the common open torus, then the open divisor pullback is unchanged:

(g^{\prime})^{*}(\mu^{*}\overline{\mathcal{D}}|_{A})=g^{*}\mathcal{D}.

Consequently the truncated and untruncated counting functions for the open divisor are the same before and after refinement; boundary components do not meet the open-torus image of the curve and contribute no counting term. Functoriality of characteristics gives

T_{g^{\prime}}(r;\mu^{*}L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))=T_{g}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))+O(1).

Finally, if
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
is the strict closure of the open divisor on
X^{\prime}
, then

\mu^{*}\overline{\mathcal{D}}=\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}+R

as Cartier divisors for some effective divisor
R
supported in the boundary and the exceptional locus. Hence bigness of
L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime})
implies bigness of
\mu^{*}L(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
.

Let

D\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]

be a nonzero Laurent polynomial. Since this Laurent ring is a unique factorization domain, write

D(u)=c\,u^{\ell}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N}R_{\alpha}(u)^{e_{\alpha}},

where
c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and the
R_{\alpha}
are pairwise nonassociated irreducible Laurent polynomials. Define the squarefree branch part of
D
by

B_{D}(u):=\prod_{e_{\alpha}\text{ odd}}R_{\alpha}(u).

Thus

D=c\,u^{\ell}S(u)^{2}B_{D}(u)

for some Laurent polynomial
S
. The branch part
B_{D}
is well-defined only up to Laurent units. It is a Laurent unit if and only if
D
is a Laurent unit times a square.

Lemma 6.4

(Even branch pullback)
.

Let
E:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
be an entire curve. Assume

D(E)\not\equiv 0

and

D(E)=\tau^{2}

for some entire function
\tau
. Then every zero of
B_{D}(E)
has even multiplicity.

Proof.

Pull back the factorization

D=c\,u^{\ell}S^{2}B_{D}.

Let
z_{0}
be a zero of
B_{D}(E)
. Since
D(E)\not\equiv 0
, neither
S(E)
nor
B_{D}(E)
is identically zero, and all local vanishing orders at
z_{0}
are finite. The factor
E^{\ell}
is zero-free, hence

\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}D(E)=2\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}S(E)+\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}B_{D}(E).

The left side is even because
D(E)=\tau^{2}
, and the first term on the right is even. Therefore
\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}B_{D}(E)
is even.
∎

6.1.
Stabilizers of Laurent divisors

Let
A=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
. For an effective divisor
\mathcal{D}\subset A
, define its connected stabilizer by

\operatorname{St}(\mathcal{D})=\{a\in A:a\cdot\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}\}^{0}.

The full stabilizer is a closed algebraic subgroup of
A
, so its identity component is well-defined. The following quotient step is the torus form of the stabilizer reduction used in
[
22
, Propositions 3.10–3.11]
.

Lemma 6.5

(Stabilizer quotient)
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]
be a reduced Laurent polynomial which is not a Laurent unit, and let
\mathcal{D}_{B}=\{B=0\}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
. Let
K=\operatorname{St}(\mathcal{D}_{B})
. Then
K\neq(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
. In particular, there exist a quotient torus

\pi:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r},\qquad r\geq 1,

and a reduced Laurent divisor
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r}
with trivial connected stabilizer such that

\mathcal{D}_{B}=\pi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}})

as reduced divisors. Moreover, if
E:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
is Zariski dense, then

G=\pi\circ E
is Zariski dense in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r}
.

Proof.

Since
B
is not a Laurent unit,
\mathcal{D}_{B}
is a nonempty proper divisor in the torus. If
K=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
, then
\mathcal{D}_{B}
would be invariant under all translations of the torus; the only such reduced closed subsets are
\varnothing
and the whole torus. This is impossible for a nonempty proper divisor. Hence
K\neq(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
, and the quotient has dimension
r=d-\dim K\geq 1
.

Since
K
is connected and
\mathcal{D}_{B}
has only finitely many irreducible components,
K
fixes each component. Thus
\mathcal{D}_{B}
is saturated with respect to the quotient map

\pi:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}/K.

It therefore descends to an effective divisor on the quotient torus. Taking the reduced structure downstairs gives a reduced divisor
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
satisfying

\mathcal{D}_{B}=\pi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}})

as reduced divisors; the equality is reduced because
\pi
is a smooth morphism of tori.

If
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
had a positive-dimensional connected stabilizer
L
in the quotient, then
\pi^{-1}(L)^{0}
would strictly contain
K
and would stabilize
\mathcal{D}_{B}
. This contradicts the maximality of
K=\operatorname{St}(\mathcal{D}_{B})
. Hence
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
has trivial connected stabilizer.

Finally, Zariski density is preserved under dominant quotient maps. If
\pi\circ E
were algebraically degenerate, then
E
would lie in the inverse image of a proper algebraic subset of the quotient.
∎

The corresponding local multiplicity comparison under the quotient map is the torus form of the same reduction in
[
22
, Propositions 3.10–3.11]
.

Lemma 6.6

(Multiplicity under the stabilizer quotient)
.

Let
\pi:A\to A^{\prime}
be a quotient of algebraic tori, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\subset A^{\prime}
be a reduced divisor, and suppose that

\mathcal{D}=\pi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}})

as reduced divisors. If
E:\mathbb{C}\to A
is an entire curve,
G=\pi\circ E
, and every point of
E^{*}\mathcal{D}
has multiplicity at least
2
, then every point of
G^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
has multiplicity at least
2
.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
with
G(z_{0})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
. Choose a local defining equation
h
for
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
near
G(z_{0})
. Since
\pi
is a smooth morphism of tori and
\mathcal{D}=\pi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}})
as reduced divisors,
h\circ\pi
is a local defining equation for
\mathcal{D}
near
E(z_{0})
, up to multiplication by a unit. Hence

\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}G^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}=\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}(h\circ G)=\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}(h\circ\pi\circ E)=\operatorname{ord}_{z_{0}}E^{*}\mathcal{D}.

The last order is at least
2
by assumption.
∎

6.2.
The toric ramification obstruction

The bigness statement used below is the toric specialization of
[
22
, Proposition 3.9(ii)]
; the proof here uses the Newton polytope.

Lemma 6.7

(Toric bigness for trivial stabilizer)
.

Let
A=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r}
and let
\mathcal{D}=\{B=0\}\subset A
be a reduced effective divisor, where

B=\sum_{m\in S}c_{m}u^{m}\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{r}^{\pm 1}]

is a nonzero Laurent polynomial. If
\operatorname{St}(\mathcal{D})=\{1\}
, then the Newton polytope
\operatorname{Newt}(B)\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
is full-dimensional. Moreover, if
X_{\Sigma}
is any smooth projective toric compactification of
A
and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
is the strict closure of
\mathcal{D}
in
X_{\Sigma}
, then
L(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
is big. In particular this holds on every smooth projective refinement of the normal fan of
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
were contained in a proper affine hyperplane, then there would be a nonzero integral cocharacter
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and an exponent
m_{0}\in S
such that

\langle m-m_{0},v\rangle=0\qquad(m\in S).

For the corresponding one-parameter subgroup
\lambda_{v}:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to A
, this gives

B(\lambda_{v}(t)u)=t^{\langle m_{0},v\rangle}B(u).

Hence the divisor
\{B=0\}
is invariant under the positive-dimensional subtorus
\lambda_{v}(\mathbb{C}^{*})
, contradicting the triviality of the connected stabilizer. Thus
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
is full-dimensional.

Let
X_{\Sigma}
be a smooth projective toric compactification with rays
\rho
and primitive ray generators
v_{\rho}
. As a rational function on
X_{\Sigma}
,
B
has boundary order

\min_{m\in S}\langle m,v_{\rho}\rangle

along the boundary divisor
D_{\rho}
. Hence the strict closure of
\mathcal{D}
is linearly equivalent to the torus-invariant divisor

D_{B}=-\sum_{\rho}\min_{m\in S}\langle m,v_{\rho}\rangle D_{\rho}.

The associated polytope contains
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
, because every
m\in\operatorname{Newt}(B)
satisfies

\langle m,v_{\rho}\rangle\geq\min_{m^{\prime}\in S}\langle m^{\prime},v_{\rho}\rangle

for all
\rho
. Since
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
is full-dimensional, the polytope of
D_{B}
is full-dimensional. The toric divisor–polytope correspondence gives

h^{0}(X_{\Sigma},\mathcal{O}(kD_{B}))=\#(kP_{D_{B}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r})

for
k\gg 0
; in particular this grows at least like the number of lattice points in
k\operatorname{Newt}(B)
, hence like
ck^{r}
for some
c>0
. Thus
D_{B}
, and hence
L(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
, is big. On a refinement of the normal fan of
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
, this is the usual toric line bundle attached to
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
. This is the standard correspondence between torus-invariant divisors, support functions and polytopes; see
[
9
, § 3.4]
and
[
6
, Proposition 4.3.3 and Ch. 9]
.
∎

The counting function
N_{1}
is truncated at level one, whereas
N
counts multiplicities. The exceptional-set notation is that of Notation
6.1
. All characteristic functions below are computed with respect to the chosen equivariant compactification and the indicated line bundle.

The ramification obstruction also uses the bounded-characteristic criterion for big line bundles, stated as Lemma
A.2
.

Theorem 6.8

(Toric ramification obstruction)
.

Let
A=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r}
and let
\mathcal{D}\subset A
be an effective reduced divisor with trivial connected stabilizer:

\operatorname{St}(\mathcal{D})=\{1\}.

If
G:\mathbb{C}\to A
is Zariski dense, then
G^{*}\mathcal{D}
contains a point of multiplicity one. Equivalently,
G
meets
\mathcal{D}
and does so somewhere with multiplicity one.

Proof.

Assume on the contrary that
G^{*}\mathcal{D}
is either empty or has all multiplicities at least
2
. Put the NWY compactification and the toric compactification used for bigness on a common model, using the convention of Remark
6.3
. Apply Theorem
6.2
and let
\overline{A}_{N}
be the smooth equivariant compactification it supplies, with
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
the closure of
\mathcal{D}
. Choose a reduced Laurent equation
B
for
\mathcal{D}
. Let
X
be a smooth projective toric compactification which dominates
\overline{A}_{N}
and also refines the normal fan of
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
; this is obtained from a common smooth projective fan refinement. Write

\mu:X\to\overline{A}_{N}

for the induced toric morphism and let
G^{\prime}:\mathbb{C}\to X
be the lift of
G
through the open torus.

Set

L^{\prime}:=\mu^{*}L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N}).

Pulling back the NWY inequality (
6.1
) to
X
gives the same open counting functions. Indeed,
G^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\subset A
, and the restriction of
\mu^{*}\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
to
A
is exactly
\mathcal{D}
; hence boundary and exceptional components of
X\setminus A
contribute no zeros to the open pullback. Therefore

N_{1}(r;G^{\prime*}(\mu^{*}\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N})|_{A})=N_{1}(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D}),\qquad N(r;G^{\prime*}(\mu^{*}\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N})|_{A})=N(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D}),

and functoriality of characteristic functions gives

T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})=T_{G}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N}))+O(1).

Consequently, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})\leq N_{1}(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D})+\varepsilon T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})+O(1)\quad\|_{\varepsilon}.

The same line bundle
L^{\prime}
is big. Let
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
be the strict closure of
\mathcal{D}
in
X
. Since
\mu
is an isomorphism over the open torus,

\mu^{*}\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{N}=\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{X}+R

as Cartier divisors, where
R
is effective and supported on boundary or exceptional divisors. By Lemma
6.7
,
L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{X})
is big: the connected stabilizer of
\mathcal{D}
is trivial, so
\operatorname{Newt}(B)
is full-dimensional. Adding the effective divisor
R
preserves bigness. Hence
L^{\prime}
is a big line bundle on
X
.

Now use the ramification hypothesis. Since all multiplicities of
G^{*}\mathcal{D}
are at least
2
,

N_{1}(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D})\leq\frac{1}{2}N(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D}).

The First Main Theorem for the divisor corresponding to
L^{\prime}
gives

N(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D})\leq T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})+O(1).

Substitution in the pulled-back NWY inequality yields

T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})+O(1)\quad\|_{\varepsilon}.

Choose
0<\varepsilon<1/2
. Then

T_{G^{\prime}}(r;L^{\prime})=O(1)\quad\|_{\varepsilon}.

The curve
G^{\prime}
is Zariski dense in
X
, because its image in the open torus is the Zariski-dense curve
G
. This contradicts Lemma
A.2
, which says that a Zariski-dense curve cannot have bounded characteristic with respect to a big line bundle outside finite-measure exceptional sets. The contradiction proves the theorem.
∎

Lemma 6.9

(Zariski density prevents identically vanishing branch pullback)
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]
be a nonzero Laurent polynomial and let

E:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}

be Zariski dense. Then
B(E)\not\equiv 0
.

Proof.

If
B(E)\equiv 0
, then
E(\mathbb{C})
is contained in the proper algebraic hypersurface
\{B=0\}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
, contradicting Zariski density.
∎

Theorem 6.10

(Laurent even-pullback obstruction)
.

Let

D\in\mathbb{C}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,u_{d}^{\pm 1}]

be a nonzero Laurent polynomial. Assume that
D
is not a Laurent unit times a square. Then there do not exist a Zariski-dense entire curve
E:\mathbb{C}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
and an entire function
\tau
such that

D(E)=\tau^{2}.

Proof.

Let
B_{D}
be the squarefree branch part of
D
. Since
D
is not a Laurent unit times a square,
B_{D}
is not a Laurent unit. Assume
D(E)=\tau^{2}
for a Zariski-dense
E
. Since both
D
and
B_{D}
are nonzero Laurent polynomials and
E
is Zariski dense, Lemma
6.9
gives

D(E)\not\equiv 0,\qquad B_{D}(E)\not\equiv 0.

Lemma
6.4
then implies that every zero of
B_{D}(E)
has even multiplicity.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{B_{D}}=\{B_{D}=0\}
. Quotient by the connected stabilizer as in Lemma
6.5
. We get a quotient torus
\pi:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r}
and a reduced divisor
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
with trivial connected stabilizer such that
\mathcal{D}_{B_{D}}=\pi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}})
. Put
G=\pi\circ E
. Then
G
is Zariski dense. Lemma
6.6
carries the even multiplicity of
E^{*}\mathcal{D}_{B_{D}}
to
G^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
, so every point of
G^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
has multiplicity at least
2
. This contradicts Theorem
6.8
.
∎

Remark 6.11
.

The Zariski-density hypothesis in Theorem
6.10
cannot be dropped: a curve contained in a proper subvariety of the torus may meet a locus on which the branch divisor becomes a square or disappears. In the four-point problem, density is part of the toric reduction; after passing to torus coordinates,
E
is dense in the torus on which
D_{M}
is defined.

Proposition 6.12

(Toroidal square-discriminant obstruction)
.

There is no nonconstant four-point omission datum whose
Q
-curve has positive toric rank.

Proof.

Assume
r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)>0
. By Proposition
5.2
, write
Q_{j}=\lambda_{j}E^{m_{j}}
with
E
Zariski dense. Define
D_{M}
as in Section
5
. Then
D_{M}(E)=\tau^{2}
. Proposition
5.5
gives
D_{M}\not\equiv 0
, and Proposition
5.7
says that
D_{M}
is not a Laurent unit times a square. This contradicts Theorem
6.10
.
∎

7.
Proof of the four-point Picard theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Assume for contradiction that a nonconstant entire slice regular function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
omits four affinely independent values
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
.

Translate by
-a_{0}
and let
F
be the real-symmetric entire stem function inducing
f-a_{0}
. Define

Q_{j}(z)=\langle F(z)-p_{j},F(z)-p_{j}\rangle,\qquad j=0,1,2,3.

By Lemma
2.1
, each
Q_{j}
is zero-free, and hence
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})
defines a curve in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4}
. By Theorem
3.7
,

\Delta_{A}(Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3})=\tau^{2}.

Let
M_{Q}=\overline{Q(\mathbb{C})}^{\mathrm{Zar}}
and
r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)=\dim M_{Q}
.

If
r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)=0
, then Lemma
5.3
gives that
F
is constant, so
f
is constant, contradiction.

If
r_{\mathrm{tor}}(Q)>0
, then Proposition
6.12
gives a contradiction.

Both alternatives lead to contradictions for a nonconstant
f
. Hence every entire slice regular function omitting four affinely independent quaternionic values is constant.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

If the four points are affinely dependent, they lie in a real affine subspace of dimension at most
2
, hence in a real affine
2
-plane. The Bisi–Winkelmann construction gives nonconstant entire slice regular functions omitting any prescribed real affine
2
-plane
[
1
]
. Thus the four points can be omitted by a nonconstant entire slice regular function.

If the four points are affinely independent, Theorem
1.2
says that any entire slice regular function omitting them is constant.
∎

Appendix A
Toric and Nevanlinna lemmas

This appendix contains the toric and Nevanlinna facts used in Sections
5
and
6
.

Lemma A.1

(Positive real parametrization of subtori)
.

Let
H\subset(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{N}
be an algebraic subtorus of dimension
d
. There is a monomial isomorphism

\iota:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}\longrightarrow H,\qquad\iota(u)=(u^{m_{1}},\dots,u^{m_{N}}),

with
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and this isomorphism identifies the positive real locus
(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
with

H\cap(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{N}.

Consequently, if
M=\lambda H
with
\lambda\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{N}
and
Q:\mathbb{C}\to M
is an entire curve such that
Q(x)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{N}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then the lifted curve

E:=\iota^{-1}(\lambda^{-1}Q)

satisfies
E(x)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and is real-symmetric.

Proof.

The character lattice of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{N}
is
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. Choosing a basis of the character lattice of
H
identifies
H
with
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
, and the restrictions of the ambient coordinate characters become monomials
u^{m_{j}}
. This gives the displayed isomorphism. Since
\iota
is an isomorphism, not merely an isogeny, the exponent vectors
m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}
generate the full lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The inclusion
\iota((\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d})\subset H\cap(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{N}
is immediate. Conversely, if
u\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d}
and
\iota(u)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{N}
, then
u^{m_{j}}>0
for all
j
. Since the
m_{j}
generate
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, each coordinate character
u_{a}
is an integral product of the characters
u^{m_{j}}
, and hence
u_{a}>0
. Thus
u\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
, proving that
\iota
identifies the positive real loci.

Thus
E(x)\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
for real
x
. For each component
E_{a}
, the holomorphic functions
E_{a}(z)
and
\overline{E_{a}(\overline{z})}
agree on the real axis; by the identity theorem they agree on all of
\mathbb{C}
. Hence
E
is real-symmetric.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Big characteristic detects Zariski density)
.

Let
X
be an irreducible normal projective variety, let
L
be a big line bundle on
X
, and let

g:\mathbb{C}\to X
be a holomorphic curve. If

T_{g}(r;L)=O(1)

outside an exceptional set of finite Lebesgue measure, then
g
is algebraically degenerate. Equivalently, for a Zariski-dense curve, the characteristic with respect to a big line bundle cannot be bounded outside such exceptional sets.

Proof.

Assume that the image of
g
is Zariski dense. If
\dim X=0
, then
g
is constant, so we may assume
\dim X\geq 1
. Since
L
is big, Kodaira’s lemma gives an integer
m>0
, an ample line bundle
H
, and an effective divisor
E_{0}
on
X
such that

mL\cong H\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(E_{0}).

The curve is Zariski dense, hence it is not contained in
\operatorname{Supp}E_{0}
. The First Main Theorem for the effective divisor
E_{0}
gives

T_{g}(r;\mathcal{O}_{X}(E_{0}))=N(r;g^{*}E_{0})+m_{g}(r;E_{0})+O(1)\geq-C_{1},

for a constant
C_{1}
, because the counting function is nonnegative and the proximity function of an effective divisor is bounded from below. Therefore

T_{g}(r;H)=mT_{g}(r;L)-T_{g}(r;\mathcal{O}_{X}(E_{0}))+O(1)\leq mT_{g}(r;L)+C_{2}.

By hypothesis, there are a constant
C_{0}
and an exceptional set
S\subset(0,\infty)
of finite Lebesgue measure such that
T_{g}(r;L)\leq C_{0}
for
r\notin S
. Fix a smooth metric on
H
with positive curvature form. Then
r\mapsto T_{g}(r;H)
is nondecreasing up to a bounded term. For arbitrary
r>0
, choose

r^{\prime}\in[r,r+|S|+1]\setminus S,

which is possible since the interval length is larger than the measure of
S
. Then

T_{g}(r;H)\leq T_{g}(r^{\prime};H)+O(1)\leq mC_{0}+C_{3}

for all
r>0
. Thus
T_{g}(r;H)
is bounded.

Choose
k>0
such that
H^{\otimes k}
is very ample, and let
\Psi:X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}
be the associated embedding. Functoriality gives

T_{\Psi\circ g}(r;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}(1))=kT_{g}(r;H)+O(1),

so the projective characteristic of
\Psi\circ g
is bounded. The classical bounded-characteristic criterion in projective space then makes
\Psi\circ g
constant
[
15
,
19
]
. Since
\Psi
is an embedding,
g
is constant, contradicting Zariski density in the positive-dimensional variety
X
.
∎

Appendix B
External results

For reference we record the results from the literature used above. The first two come from quaternionic slice regular function theory; the others come from toric geometry and semi-abelian value distribution theory.

(E1)

Bisi–Winkelmann zero-divisor criterion.

For a real-symmetric stem function
F
and a quaternion
c\in\mathbb{H}
, the induced slice regular function omits
c
if and only if

Q_{c}(z)=\langle F(z)-c,F(z)-c\rangle

is zero-free on
\mathbb{C}
. This is the consequence of
[
1
, Proposition 2.2]
used in Lemma
2.1
. The interpretation of
Q_{c}
as the stem of the normal function
N(f-c)
belongs to the standard zero theory of slice regular functions
[
10
,
11
]
.

(E2)

Bisi–Winkelmann plane-omission and five-point theorem.

Bisi–Winkelmann construct entire slice regular functions whose images are complements of real affine
2
-planes; in the notation of their paper this follows from
[
1
, Proposition 5.1, Proposition 6.1 and the subsequent remark]
. Their five-point theorem,
[
1
, Theorem 3.5]
, states that five quaternionic values not contained in any real affine
3
-space cannot be omitted by a nonconstant entire slice regular function. These two statements are recorded in Theorem
1.1
.

(E3)

Logarithmic Bloch–Ochiai for semi-abelian varieties.

The required statement is that the Zariski closure of an entire curve in a semi-abelian variety is a translate of a semi-abelian subvariety. This is the logarithmic Bloch–Ochiai theorem of Noguchi
[
17
, Main Theorem (i)]
; see also
[
20
,
8
]
for related formulations. For
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{4}
, this identifies
M_{Q}=\overline{Q(\mathbb{C})}^{\mathrm{Zar}}
as a translated algebraic subtorus, as used in Proposition
5.2
.

(E4)

NWY level-one truncated Second Main Theorem.

The divisor case of
[
22
, Main Theorem (iii)]
is used in the form

T_{G}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))\leq N_{1}(r;G^{*}\mathcal{D})+\varepsilon T_{G}(r;L(\overline{\mathcal{D}}))\quad\|_{\varepsilon},

for a Zariski-dense entire curve
G
into a semi-abelian variety and an effective reduced divisor
\mathcal{D}
. Here the ambient semi-abelian variety is always a torus. When a common smooth equivariant refinement is used, the curve still lies in the open torus; the open divisor pullback and the truncated counting function are unchanged, boundary components add no counting term, and functoriality gives the characteristic comparison up to
O(1)
. The proof of Theorem
6.8
keeps track of the same line bundle after refinement: the pulled-back NWY line bundle equals the strict Laurent closure plus an effective boundary-exceptional divisor.

(E5)

Toric bigness of Laurent divisors.

The bigness needed in Theorem
6.8
is supplied by Lemma
6.7
, the toric specialization of
[
22
, Proposition 3.9(ii)]
. For a reduced Laurent divisor in a torus, trivial connected stabilizer forces the Newton polytope to be full-dimensional, and the strict closure on a smooth projective toric compactification has big associated line bundle. Adding the effective boundary-exceptional divisor coming from the NWY compactification preserves bigness. The toric background is the divisor–polytope correspondence and the bigness criterion for toric divisors; see
[
9
, § 3.4]
and
[
6
, Proposition 4.3.3 and Ch. 9]
.

(E6)

Big characteristic and algebraic degeneracy.

Lemma
A.2
gives the implication from bounded characteristic for a big line bundle to algebraic degeneracy. It is used at the end of Theorem
6.8
; the projective-space input is the classical bounded-characteristic criterion from Nevanlinna theory for projective varieties
[
15
,
19
]
.
