Brief re-reading boosts sleep-related prose memory consolidation
Authors: Serena Malloggi, Francesca Conte, Oreste De Rosa, Letizia Nikehasani, Andrea Farolfi, Stefania Righi, Mattia Varriale, Maria Pia Viggiano, Gianluca Ficca, Fiorenza Giganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sleep plays a crucial role in memory consolidation. In everyday educational settings, students often rapidly review studied material just before sleep, but the effect of this strategy on subsequent memory has never been investigated. Here we assessed whether briefly re-reading a studied text immediately before a retention interval spent asleep or awake affects its delayed recall.In a mixed design, 34 university students were assigned to a Sleep or a Wake group. Eight hours after studying and immediately recalling a brief text, participants either quickly re-read it (for 1 min) or not, before an approximately 8-h retention interval spent asleep or awake.Main effects of Group and Condition were found (better performance in Sleep vs. Wake and in Re-reading vs. No Re-reading conditions, respectively), as well as an interaction Group x Condition. Crucially, only participants who re-read the text before sleeping showed a net improvement in recall relative to their own initial performance, whereas performance slightly deteriorated in all other conditions.These findings extend previous work on sleep-related prose memory consolidation by showing that a brief pre-sleep review, ecologically similar to common student study habits, can boost subsequent memory beyond mere protection from forgetting. They suggest that quick reviews of studied material at bedtime may represent an effective and practical strategy for memory enhancement in real-life learning contexts.

Introduction

Prose memory represents a form of long-term declarative memory, referring to our ability to remember the content of textual materials, regardless of their length or complexity. Unlike memory for isolated words or unrelated items, i.e., the most common stimuli used in laboratory memory research, prose memory involves the encoding and retention of semantically coherent, hierarchically organized information, in which meaning, narrative structure, and prior knowledge all play a role in determining what is remembered and how [1]. Central to prose comprehension is the construction of a coherent mental representation that integrates textual information with relevant background knowledge, a process that goes well beyond the simple storage of individual facts and involves active inference-making and meaning construction [2]. As a consequence, recall of a text is always a reconstruction rather than a reproduction, shaped by the reader's prior knowledge, inferential skills, and the coherence of the text itself. We frequently challenge prose memory abilities in different daily contexts, especially in educational ones, where students are constantly engaged in acquiring and retaining new information from various texts. For these reasons, prose memory represents a particularly ecologically valid domain for investigating memory consolidation processes, as it more closely mirrors the type of learning that occurs in real-life settings compared to standard laboratory tasks such as word list learning.

Although sleep is widely recognized for its crucial role in memory consolidation [3,4], its effect on prose memory consolidation has been surprisingly neglected. Indeed, in the sleep-memory domain as in memory research more broadly, declarative memory has been studied almost exclusively through word list learning paradigms, which, despite their experimental tractability, are incapable of capturing the complexity of the textual materials encountered in everyday life [5]. A few older studies showed that memory for short texts was impaired after REM sleep selective deprivation [6,7] but not after SWS deprivation [7]. Also, Verschoor and Holdstock [8] observed that several aspects of REM sleep (e.g., REM sleep proportion, number of eye movement bursts) were increased after learning of prose texts. Similarly, a more recent study by our group showed that learning monologues at bedtime increased sleep continuity and stability [9]. Other recent studies [10,11] found significant benefits of a retention period spent asleep vs. awake on recall of prose texts. An analogue sleep benefit was shown using a similar task (factual knowledge presented through bullet points) by Cousins et al. [12]. The same authors also observed that multi-night sleep restriction impairs long term retention of this kind of knowledge in adolescence [13].

In everyday educational settings, students frequently encounter a common learning scenario: encoding new textual information, often in the afternoon, after morning courses, with the expectation of being tested on it in the following days. Research on study habits highlighted that re-reading studied material is one of the techniques that students frequently adopt to enhance their learning (e.g., Refs. [14,15]). It appears to reinforce conceptual organization and processing of main ideas in a very effective way among students (e.g., Ref. [16]).

Studies from psychology of memory also underline the robust effect of testing the studied material on its long-term recall (the so called “testing effect”). Importantly, testing appears to be more beneficial than mere re-study of the same material [16,17]. However, this effect has been questioned when the retention interval is spent asleep. Specifically, Bäuml and colleagues [10] showed that, when participants sleep during the retention interval, effects of testing and restudying are reversed. In other words, post-sleep recall is better for restudied than for tested material.

Of note, in everyday settings with relevant time constraints, a common, intuitive strategy employed by many students is to engage in a very quick review (or “skimming”) of studied material just before sleep. However, to our knowledge, no study has directly investigated the effect of a rapid pre-sleep review of studied material on its subsequent consolidation, despite the ubiquity of this strategy among students and its potential practical relevance for optimizing learning outcomes in educational contexts.

Here we simulate an ecological learning scenario in which, several hours after a text has been studied, the same text is quickly re-read at bedtime. Specifically, in a mixed design, we assess delayed memory of the text after it has been quickly re-read or not (within-groups conditions) immediately prior to a retention interval spent asleep or awake (between-groups conditions). Through this procedure, we aim to address the following research questions: does a rapid re-reading aid in consolidating memory of a text? In case this effect was observed, would this benefit be specific to bedtime re-reading, or would it also be found after a retention period spent awake?

Our main hypothesis is that this rapid re-exposure, performed before sleep, could reactivate the memory trace acquired earlier in the day, thus promoting its consolidation during sleep. In line with classical literature on the sleep effect on memory [3,4], we expected this consolidation to be more pronounced when the retention interval between encoding and retrieval was spent in sleep rather than in wake.

Methods

Participants

Participants were selected from a sample of volunteer students recruited via online advertisements. Potential participants were previously asked to fill out a web-based set of questionnaires through the Google Form platform: the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; [18]), the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; [19]), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; [19]), the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire reduced version (rMEQ; [20]) and a set of ad hoc questions assessing general medical conditions and health habits.

Inclusion criteria were: absence of any relevant somatic or psychiatric disorder, absence of clinically significant depression and anxiety symptoms (BDI-II score ≤29; BAI score ≤25), no history of drug or alcohol abuse, reporting good sleep quality (PSQI <5), being classified as an Intermediate chronotype (rMEQ score between 12 and 17), having a regular sleep-wake pattern (e.g., individuals with irregular study or working habits such as shift-working were excluded; participants had to report usual bedtime around midnight), no use of psychoactive medication or alcohol at bedtime.

Thirty-eight students volunteered to participate. Of them, 4 potential participants had to be excluded because of poor sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score >5). Therefore, the final sample was made up of 34 university students (10M, 24F; mean age = 23.1 ± 2.73 years; age range = 20 - 32), who were randomly assigned to a Sleep group (N = 17; 9M, 8F; age = 23.6 ± 3.44) or a Wake group (N = 17; 12M, 5F; age = 22.5 ± 1.87). All participants signed a consent form before enrolling in the study.

We ran an a priori power analysis (through G∗Power) with 2 groups, 2 measurements, alpha = .05, MANOVA (repeated measures, within-between interaction). The expected effect size was set at f(V) = .50 (Pillai V = .20), targeted as a large effect size according to Cohen's criteria [21] for practically meaningful cognitive interactions, with a desired power of 80%. The analysis indicated that a total sample of 34 subjects was required.

The study design was submitted to the Ethical Committee of the University of Florence, which approved the research (code 363/2024; date 11/12/2024) and certified that the involvement of human participants was performed according to acceptable standards.

Procedure

In a mixed design, participants learned a prose text and, after ∼8 h, either re-read it or not (within groups conditions: Re-reading, No Re-reading) before an ∼8 h retention interval spent either asleep or awake (between groups conditions: Sleep and Wake). The order of conditions was balanced between participants. Two different texts were used for the within-groups conditions: their assignment to the Re-reading and No Re-reading condition was also balanced between participants. Specifically, participants were divided into four subgroups based on the order of within-subjects conditions and text assignment: (a) Re-reading with Text 1 first, then No Re-reading with Text 2; (b) Re-reading with Text 2 first, then No Re-reading with Text 1; (c) No Re-reading with Text 1 first, then Re-reading with Text 2; (d) No Re-reading with Text 2 first, then Re-reading with Text 1.

Participants of both groups, in both conditions, were administered the learning phase at the Sleep Lab. In the Sleep group, this phase was carried out in the afternoon (around 16:00), whereas, in the Wake group, it occurred in the morning (around 08:00).

About 8 h after the study phase (around 00:00 in the Sleep group and 16:00 in the Wake group), participants either were instructed to re-read the text (Re-reading condition) or not (No Re-reading condition). In the Re-reading condition, participants received the studied text through a Whatsapp ephemeral photo with the instruction to re-read it for 1 min (the text was visible for 1 min). After 1 min, the experimenter sent a message to verify whether the participant had read the text and had had sufficient time to do so. In the No Re-reading condition, participants did not receive the text and were not asked to perform any specific activity at the corresponding time point.

This specific schedule was planned in order to have re-reading occur near the participants' habitual bedtime (around midnight), ensuring the ecological validity of the pre-sleep re-reading manipulation.

The delayed test was administered (in both groups and both conditions) about 16 h after the study phase (around 8:00 in the Sleep group and 00:00 in the Wake group) through a video-call with an experimenter, who instructed the participants to recall the text in written form in as much detail as possible.

As for the Sleep group, the delayed test was administered ∼15 min after morning awakening. Furthermore, during the experimental night, as well as during the three preceding days, sleep was monitored through an actigraph (CamTeck, UK) and a sleep diary (to collect baseline sleep data and ensure that sleep on experimental nights adhered to habitual sleep schedules).

In both groups and both conditions, participants were requested to refrain from engaging in cognitively demanding activities (including studying, reading, or playing board games) during the retention interval (limited to the 8 h between the learning phase and bedtime for the Sleep group). Compliance was monitored through a brief oral check conducted via telephone call by the experimenter at the end of the retention interval. No participant reported having violated the study instructions.

Furthermore, in both groups, in both conditions, at the beginning of the learning phase and of the delayed testing phase, participants rated their subjective sleepiness through the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale [22].

Prose memory task

As in our previous study [11], the task consisted in studying one of two texts (text 1: “The Sun”; text 2: “The Sea Otters”, one for each within-groups condition), selected from the reading comprehension section of a test-preparation book for the Test of English as a Foreign Language test manual (TOEFL; [23]) and already used in Refs. [17] and [10]. The texts were translated into Italian by two experts. The length of the texts was 247 and 308 words, respectively.

Since both texts are currently used to assess language skills in young adult high school students, we considered them to be easy to understand and to learn for the university study sample, attending higher level courses of study.

During the learning phase, participants were administered in written form one of the two texts and instructed to study it for 5 min. Specifically, they were asked not to memorize the texts word for word, but to report as many text details as possible (in order for performance to reflect gist recall rather than verbatim reproduction, thus maintaining ecological validity with respect to real-life learning contexts). Then, the subjects were asked to complete a 2 min distractor task (i.e., an arithmetic task). Finally, a free recall test (immediate test) was administered, in which the participants had 10 min to write down on a blank piece of paper anything they remembered from the previously learned text. The same free recall test was performed as a re-test (delayed test) after the retention interval.

The validity of the two texts as comparable stimuli was assessed in our previous study [11], conducted on a similar sample of university students. In that study, participants rated their interest in the text, its perceived difficulty and their prior knowledge of the content of the text on 7-point scales at the end of the learning phase. No significant differences between the two texts were found for interest nor difficulty. A difference in prior knowledge was observed, but the Text factor did not reach significance in any statistical analysis in that study, nor in the present one (see Results), indicating that prior knowledge did not differentially influence memory performance.

As in Refs. [10,11,17], scoring was performed by dividing each text into 30 units. Text units consist of 30 brief sentences extrapolated from the prose passage, including content that was relevant for the comprehension of the text [17]. The experimenter assigned one point to each correctly recalled unit. Units containing synonyms or with reversal of word order were also considered correct recalls.

The complete texts and the list of text units for each are accessible as Supplementary materials.

Sleep scoring was performed automatically using the MotionWare software's validated algorithm with a low sensitivity threshold (activity count threshold = 20). Data analysis

To quantify memory changes across the retention intervals, we computed the number of text units correctly recalled at the delayed test minus the number of text units correctly recalled at the immediate test (Δunits) as the main dependent variable. This measure was chosen because it directly quantifies memory change across the retention interval at the individual level, controlling for inter-individual variability in initial encoding efficacy. Its use as a consolidation index is consistent with previous sleep-memory research (e.g., Refs. [12,24,25]). The absence of significant group or condition differences at the immediate test (see Results) further supports the interpretation of Δunits as reflecting consolidation-related processes rather than encoding differences. Note that, while positive values are to be interpreted as enhanced memory performance (more units recalled at the delayed compared to the immediate test), negative values indicate forgetting (less units recalled at the delayed compared to the immediate test).

To exclude differences in participants' memory encoding efficacy, a Linear Mixed Model (LMM) was employed with Group (Sleep; Wake), Condition (Re-reading; No Re-reading) and Text (Text 1; Text 2) as fixed factors, participant ID as random effect, and the number of text units correctly recalled at the immediate test as the dependent variable.

To evaluate the effect of re-reading on delayed performance across groups, an LMM was performed with Group (Sleep; Wake), Condition (Re-reading; No Re-reading), and Text (Text 1; Text 2) as fixed factors, participant ID as random effect, sleepiness levels at encoding as covariate, and Δ units as the dependent variable. Bonferroni test was adopted for post hoc analysis. The Group × Condition interaction was considered the primary test of our main hypothesis, as it directly assesses whether the effect of re-reading on delayed memory performance differs as a function of the retention interval spent asleep or awake.

A priori power analyses were conducted through the G∗Power software. Analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.6; The Jamovi Project, 2024); the significance level was set at p ≤ .05.

Memory performance

Mixed models showed no significant effect of Group (F1,66 = .34, p = .55), Condition (F1,66 = 3.06, p = .09), or Text (F1,64 = .61, p = .43), as well as no interaction Group x Condition (F1,66 = .03, p = .85), Group x Text (F1,64 = .02, p = .89), Condition x Text (F1,66 = .01, p = .90) or Group x Condition x Text (F1,64 = .22, p = .64) on performance (number of correctly recalled text units) at the immediate test. These results allow us to exclude that differences in delayed performance were due to differences in acquisition between groups or conditions.

As for the delayed test, analyses revealed significant main effects of Group (F1,66 = 16.31, p < .001) and Condition (F1,66 = 6.35, p = .01), as well as an interaction Group x Condition (F1,66 = 4.03, p = .04) (model R2 conditional = .27). Specifically, Δunits were less negative in the Sleep compared to the Wake group (Sleep: −.12 ± 2.72; Wake: −2.79 ± 2.88) and in the Re-reading (mean = −.67 ± 3.03) compared to the No Re-reading condition (−2.26 ± 2.99), indicating that delayed performance was better in the Sleep (vs. Wake) group and in the Re-reading (vs. No Re-reading) condition. As for the interaction effect, post hoc analyses revealed that Δunits in the Sleep Re-reading condition (mean = 1.44 ± 1.71) significantly differed from all other conditions: Sleep No Re-reading (mean = −1.59 ± 2.72; t = 3.29, p = .01), Wake Re-reading (mean = −2.65 ± 2.64; t = 4.39, p < .001), and Wake No Re-reading (mean = −2.94 ± 3.17; t = 4.47, p < .001) (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study investigated the effect of quickly re-reading a brief text before a retention interval spent asleep vs. awake on its delayed retrieval. Overall, our findings highlight that pre-sleep re-reading benefits the consolidation of the previously learned text, an effect not observed when participants spent the retention interval awake.

First, we observed a benefit of sleep relative to wake on delayed memory performance, regardless of the occurrence of re-reading. Indeed, negative Δunits at the delayed test (indexing worse performance relative to the immediate test) were fewer in the Sleep than the Wake group (significant main effect of the factor Group), i.e., participants who slept forgot significantly less text units than those who remained awake. This finding is consistent with the few previous studies specifically addressing sleep-related consolidation of prose memory [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], as well as with the broad extant literature highlighting sleep's pivotal role in memory consolidation: sleep is known to facilitate the transfer of labile memories from temporary hippocampal storage to more stable neocortical sites, strengthening and reorganizing neural connections formed during wakefulness [3,4]. The fact that average Δunit scores in the Sleep group are close to zero (−.12) is not surprising, as prior research on sleep-related declarative memory consolidation indicates that sleep primarily stabilizes rather than enhances declarative memory performance [26,27].

As for the efficacy of re-reading, our results showed that, overall, it had a positive effect on delayed performance (significant main effect of the factor Condition). Indeed, in both the Sleep and the Wake group, delayed performance was better when the text had been re-read than when it had not, supporting the idea that even a quick re-reading of studied material is beneficial to its long-term retention. However, whereas re-reading had only a protective effect on retrieval in the Wake group (Δunit scores were negative, indicating forgetting of text units), it had a boosting effect in the Sleep group. In fact, in the Sleep Re-reading condition: a) delayed performance was significantly better than in all other conditions and b) Δunit scores were positive (i.e., participants recalled more units at delayed compared to immediate testing), whereas they were negative in all other conditions. In other words, only subjects who quickly re-read the text and then went to sleep were able to recall more text details at the delayed compared to the immediate test.

Of note, our results cannot be attributed to between-groups or between-conditions differences in initial acquisition efficacy: statistical analyses confirmed the absence of significant differences in the number of text units recalled at the immediate test across all conditions, as well as in function of the specific text (text 1 and 2) and sleepiness levels.

Our findings align with those from Bäuml and collegues [10], who, in a series of five experiments, found that sleep preferentially benefits recall of material (both word lists and texts) re-studied before sleep, compared to material that is actively retrieved through testing (i.e., free recall task) before sleep. The authors explained this finding with the hypothesis that the free recall test, requiring subjects to actively retrieve items or text details, strengthens memory traces to a higher degree than restudy does. Therefore, the already robust memory traces would obtain scarce additional benefit from subsequent sleep. Conversely, weaker re-studied traces would benefit more significantly from sleep-related consolidation processes. This assumption is coherent with theoretical accounts suggesting that sleep is more effective in strengthening weaker rather than stronger memory traces (e.g., Ref. [28]) and it is all the more suited to explain our data. Indeed, rather than a thorough re-study of the material (as in studies on the testing effect [10,17]), our participants were allowed just a very quick re-reading (1 min, controlled by using an ephemeral WhatsApp message), which probably triggered a brief reactivation of the gist and main concepts of the text: plausibly, these then underwent boosted consolidation during the following sleep episode. Of note, at variance with our study design, Bäuml et al. [10] did not administer an immediate test after initial acquisition, so that they were unable to determine whether delayed performance was: a) influenced by initial acquisition levels, b) to be interpreted rather as a higher/lower decay of the acquired material or its higher/lower enhancement. Instead, in our study pre-sleep re-reading produced not only reduced forgetting but also an actual memory enhancement compared to initial acquisition levels. This implies that, in the student population, a rapid review of keywords, summaries, mind maps or diagrams before sleep could serve as an effective strategy to enhance the overall consolidation of the previously studied material.

Our findings also relate to those of Cousins et al. [12], who showed that sleep vs. wake significantly consolidates factual knowledge acquired through an ecologically valid learning task, but found no evidence that sleep enhances the capacity to relearn the same material in a subsequent full study session administered after the retention interval. Our study addresses a complementary question: rather than examining what happens after sleep when the material is re-encountered, we investigated whether a minimal re-exposure to the studied material immediately before sleep is sufficient to modulate sleep-dependent consolidation itself. Our results suggest that this is indeed the case: a 1-min re-reading, by briefly reactivating the memory trace just before the sleep episode, appears sufficient to trigger enhanced consolidation during the subsequent sleep period. Taken together, the two studies suggest that the timing and nature of re-exposure to studied material are critical determinants of sleep-dependent memory consolidation: whereas extensive relearning after sleep does not appear to benefit from the preceding sleep episode, a minimal reactivation before sleep may be sufficient to potentiate it.

A limitation of the present study concerns the possible time-of-day confound inherent to sleep-wake designs: encoding and delayed testing occurred at different times of day across groups (Sleep group: afternoon encoding, morning test; Wake group: morning encoding, evening test). While the absence of group differences at the immediate test suggests that time-of-day did not affect encoding, circadian effects on delayed retrieval cannot be fully excluded. Specifically, the Sleep group was tested in the morning, whereas the Wake group was tested in the late evening, a time at which fatigue and reduced alertness may have played a role. However, if circadian effects on memory retrieval had substantially influenced our results, we would have expected both Sleep conditions (regardless of the re-reading manipulation) to outperform both Wake conditions at the delayed test, simply by virtue of being tested in the morning. Instead, post-hoc analyses revealed that only the Sleep Re-reading condition significantly differed from all other conditions, while the Sleep No Re-reading condition did not differ significantly from either Wake condition. This pattern is inconsistent with a simple time-of-day advantage for the Sleep group and strongly suggests that the observed effects reflect the specific interaction between pre-sleep re-reading and sleep-dependent consolidation, rather than circadian differences in retrieval. Finally, it can be speculated that circadian effects could have also differentially influenced the re-reading manipulation (i.e., differential alertness between groups could have influenced the act of re-reading rather than initial encoding or retrieval). However, here again, if this were the case, we would have expected a performance benefit in the Wake rather than the Sleep Re-reading condition, as re-reading in the Wake group occurred close to the daily peak of vigilance, whereas, in the Sleep Group, it occurred in the late evening. The observed pattern (with benefits exclusive to the Sleep Re-reading condition) appears therefore inconsistent with a simple circadian advantage for that group.

An additional observation deserves consideration: as in previous research using the same learning material and procedures [10,17], here memory was assessed through written free recall, which may involve additional processing compared to spoken recall, with a potential enhancing effect on performance. Although this feature of the design cannot account for the differential effects observed here between groups and conditions (since the written format was identical across all conditions), the possible differential effects of spoken and written recall of texts represent an interesting research question for future studies, considering that, in everyday contexts, students engage in both forms of recall while preparing for exams.

Conclusion

This study aimed to assess the effect of a quick re-reading before sleep on prose memory, the form of long-term declarative memory that is most frequently used in everyday contexts, especially educational ones. Our findings, first of all, confirm the sleep effect on prose memory, by showing that a retention period spent in sleep counteracts forgetting rates of a text content compared to an equivalent wake period. We also observed that a very quick re-reading of a studied material is helpful to strengthen its long-term retention independent of the behavioral state in which the retention interval is spent. More critically, we observed that, when quick re-reading occurs just before sleep, it does not only reduce forgetting but also actively boosts recall beyond initial acquisition levels. These findings offer relevant applicative implications, suggesting that a quick review of studied material at bedtime may be a powerful strategy for memory enhancement. Therefore, they support the practical adoption of quick review prior to sleep as an effective strategy for promoting long-term retention of declarative knowledge.
