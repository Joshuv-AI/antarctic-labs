Costotransverse screws for the treatment of dystrophic thoracic rotatory dislocation: illustrative case
Authors: Nabil Alassaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDystrophic deformities in neurofibromatosis type 1 progress relentlessly. If left untreated, they may lead to neurological impairment. Routine stabilization methods are often not feasible at the dystrophic levels. Circumferential arthrodesis has historically been recommended to reduce the risk of pseudarthrosis.OBSERVATIONSA 14-year-old female presented with progressive kyphoscoliosis resulting in T2–3 rotatory dislocation without neurological deficits. She underwent posterior-only cervicothoracic arthrodesis with bone grafting. Thoracic fixation was achieved exclusively using costotransverse screws. Solid fusion was confirmed by a CT study 8 months postoperatively; therefore, no additional procedures were performed to augment the fusion. The anterior approach was avoided to prevent the associated morbidity. At the 21-month follow-up visit, the patient was asymptomatic, with no change in her neurological status and no progression of the deformity. At 8 years postoperatively, she did not require further surgical intervention and reported mild intermittent pain.LESSONSIn selected patients, costotransverse screws may provide safe and effective anchor points during posterior arthrodesis of dystrophic rotatory dislocations. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26213

Neurofibromatosis type 1 is an autosomal dominant, heterogeneous disease. Fifty percent of cases are sporadic, and scoliosis is reported in up to 70% of patients.1 The revision rate is as high as 60%.2 Dystrophic kyphoscoliosis distorts anatomy, which may hinder the safe placement of conventional instrumentation. To date, only 1 in vivo case of costotransverse screw instrumentation has been reported by De Iure et al., in which the technique was used for dystrophic kyphoscoliosis following anterior arthrodesis.3 The purpose of this report is to present, for the first time, posterior-only arthrodesis using costotransverse screws in a patient with dystrophic thoracic pedicles.

Illustrative Case

A 14-year-old girl presented with upper back pain and progressive deformity 10 months postmenarche. No neurological or constitutional symptoms were noted. Her family history was notable for neurofibromatosis type 1; her father was affected, and her older sister had undergone multiple spinal deformity surgeries and revisions at several institutions. Clinical examination revealed cutaneous stigmata of neurofibromatosis and a prominent upper thoracic deformity. Neurological assessment demonstrated normal motor and sensory functions with intact myotatic and superficial reflexes. Radiographic, CT, and MR imaging (Fig. 1) revealed a dystrophic thoracic kyphoscoliosis with T2–3 rotatory dislocation, without adjacent neurofibromas or dislocated ribs. Progression of the deformity was noted based on radiographs obtained 3 months earlier at another hospital. Only marginal correction was observed on the traction and side-bending radiographs. Bone mineral density assessment showed a Z-score (pediatric age group) of –1. Given the severely dystrophic pedicles, a costotransverse screw fixation was planned. FIG. 1.Preoperative images. Three months after her initial presentation and after comprehensive preoperative counseling, the patient underwent posterior spinal instrumented arthrodesis from C5 to T6. Preoperative or intraoperative traction was not performed. The patient was positioned prone on a specialized table with a head holder. A standard posterior approach was used to preserve the supraspinous ligament. Lateral mass screws were placed in the cervical spine and costotransverse screws were used at the thoracic level (Fig. 2). The screw diameter was 4.0 mm, with lengths ranging from 14 to 18 mm. Instrumentation on the left side of C7 and T1 was not feasible. CT-guided navigation (O-arm) was used to ensure safe screw placement, confirming the entry points and screw trajectories. All instrumented thoracic pedicles were dystrophic. Entry points located in the middle of the transverse processes were opened with a high-speed burr, followed by incremental drilling, length measurement, partial tapping, and screw insertion.3 Each screw was engaged in three to four cortices. Protecting the parietal pleura during cannulation and avoiding the inferior rib border prevent pneumothorax and neurovascular bundle injuries, respectively. Gentle deformity correction was performed during the placement of 3.5-mm titanium rods. After decortication, 8 cm3 of recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein–2, 20 cm3 of demineralized bone matrix, and 30 cm3 of allograft cancellous chips were applied. Estimated blood loss was 0.5 L. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring remained at baseline throughout the procedure. No surgical drains or chest tubes were used, and the patient was placed in a semirigid cervical collar for 3 months postoperatively. The patient’s hospital course was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 5. Postoperative imaging demonstrated a preserved chest cavity and well-balanced spine. Complete fusion of the instrumented segments was documented on CT imaging 8 months postoperatively and confirmed by a radiology consultant (Fig. 3). Had fusion not been achieved on follow-up CT imaging, the plan was to augment the posterior arthrodesis with repeat bone grafting. At the 21-month follow-up clinic visit, the patient was asymptomatic, with no progression of the deformity on radiography (Fig. 4). A halo sign was noted and was thought to be secondary to motion at the costotransverse and costovertebral joints, a finding considered clinically irrelevant once solid fusion was attained (Fig. 4C). The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up; however, she was contacted during the preparation of this report 8 years postoperatively. Only mild intermittent pain was reported. She did not seek additional imaging and, importantly, confirmed the lack of any additional surgical intervention.

Discussion

Observations

There are few alternatives to pedicle screws for dystrophic spine. Juxtapedicular (in-out-in) screw placement is challenging in the presence of severely distorted osseous morphology (Fig. 3B) but remains a viable option. The extreme rotation and dysplasia of the vertebral bodies make them an unpredictable and unsafe target. An outside-in trajectory in this distorted anatomy significantly increases the risk of a medial breach or injury to anterior vascular structures. The chosen technique provided secure fixation while mitigating these risks. Sublaminar wires, cables, and clamps are associated with an increased risk of bleeding and incidental durotomy in the presence of dural ectasia. Hooks rely on appropriate tensioning of the vertebrae, which may not be achievable when the anterior column is compromised. Thoracic intralaminar screws may represent a viable alternative, as they allow for greater screw length; however, they typically permit only single-cortex purchase.4

In an anatomical study, Xu et al. proposed costotransverse screws as a safe alternative to the existing fixation methods.5 Their investigation, conducted in 9 adult cadavers, demonstrated screw lengths ranging from 14 to 20 mm, with trajectories varying by vertebral level. Importantly, the exit point was consistently cephalad to the intercostal neurovascular bundle, and the parietal pleura was identified as the primary structure at risk.5 Little et al. later demonstrated that costotransverse screws placed perpendicular to the transverse process at the T3 and T4 levels achieved approximately 38% of the pullout strength of pedicle screws, underscoring the fact that costotransverse screws are a salvage option.6

The only reported use of costotransverse screws in spinal surgery was by De lure et al.3 Following anterior release and stabilization, the authors used costotransverse screws with posterior bone grafting in an 18-year-old female with cervicothoracic deformity secondary to neurofibromatosis type 1, which resulted in solid union without complications.3

There is no consensus on whether anterior arthrodesis is necessary even in the presence of rotatory subluxation.1 Tsirikos et al. reviewed the literature on dystrophic scoliosis with vertebral displacement and found that posterior-only arthrodesis was used in 8 of the 15 cases (53%).7 In the index case described in their report, the initial posterior arthrodesis used unilateral instrumentation; 7 months later, posterior fusion was augmented, followed by an anterior sternotomy approach. The anterior stage was associated with 4.5 L of blood loss and resulted in pericardial and pleural effusions requiring drainage.7 Therefore, in the present case, the plan was to proceed with anterior reconstruction only if progressive osseous erosion compromised spinal stability.

Lessons

Although the patient is doing well 8 years postoperatively and is not currently seeking medical care, close periodic follow-up is warranted as additional surgical intervention may be required in the future.1 Timely stabilization of dystrophic neurofibromatosis curves is warranted to avoid progression and neurological sequelae. This may be achieved in selected patients with posterior arthrodesis using a costotransverse screw-based construct.
