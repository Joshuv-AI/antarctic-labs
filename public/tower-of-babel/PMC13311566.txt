Clinical spectrum, familial patterns, and surgical interventions in patients with cleft lip and/or palate: experience from a specialized cleft care center in Pakistan
Authors: Areesha Rashid, Sidra Sajid, Muhammad Asghar Khan, Saima Naz, Tanveer Ahmad, Sajid Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) is a common congenital anomaly with multifactorial etiology. Data describing the phenotypic spectrum of CL/P in Pakistan is limited. This study aimed to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics, familial pattern, surgical intervention profile, and maternal risk factors associated with CL/P in a Pakistani cohort.MethodsPatients with CL/P were recruited from a tertiary care hospital in Faisalabad, Pakistan, into a cross-sectional study design during September 2023 and March 2024. Descriptive statistics were collected, and bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsA total of 503 independent patients with CL/P were enrolled (53% male). CL/P was categorized into cleft lip (CL, 26%), cleft palate (CP, 36%), and cleft lip with palate (CLP, 38%). Male sex was associated with higher odds of CL, while female sex was associated with higher odds of CP. Rural residence was a significant predictor for CLP (adjusted odds ratio (aOR)=1.80, p<0.05), whereas age 1–5 years was strongly associated with CP (aOR=2.36, p<0.001). Parental consanguinity was notably high (77%), and 18% had a positive family history of CL/P. Only 38% of mothers reported folic acid supplementation. Pesticide exposure was reported in 32% of mothers. Surgical correction had been performed in only 27% of patients.ConclusionCleft types demonstrate distinct demographic and maternal risk factor profiles. High rates of consanguinity, low folic acid uptake, and significant surgical gaps highlight critical areas for public health intervention in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Congenital Abnormalities; Epidemiology; Genetics; Pediatrics; Surgery, Plastic

Introduction

Orofacial clefts (OFCs) are the most common congenital craniofacial anomaly in humans and present as cleft lip (CL), cleft palate (CP), or cleft lip with palate (CLP).1 2 These malformations result from disruption of normal embryonic development of the cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) and can occur as isolated defects, in combination, or as part of recognized syndromes.3

Globally, the birth prevalence of CL/P is estimated at approximately 1 in 700 live births.4 However, high variation exists across geographic regions and ethnicities. For instance, prevalence was reported to be 1 in 500 live births in many developing countries, compared with around 1 in 700 in the UK and the USA.5 A meta-analysis estimated the global prevalence of CL/P to be 1.42 per 1000 live births, with CL alone accounting for 15%, isolated CP for 40%, and CLP for 45% of cases.1 It is often sporadic and multifactorial.6 7

Pakistan is ranked fourth globally for the incidence of CL/P after China, India, and Indonesia.8 In a hospital-based study on congenital anomalies carried out in Rawalpindi, Pakistan, CL/P cases comprised 4% of the total cohort.9 In addition to genetic and environmental factors implicated in the etiology of CL/P, high parental consanguinity has also been reported in certain populations.1 9 A genetic predisposition has been suggested from familial aggregation studies that demonstrated an increased recurrence risk among relatives of individuals affected by CL/P.1 6 10

OFCs exert a profound impact on structure, oral function, dental development, esthetics and psychosocial well-being.11 Infants with CL/P have feeding difficulties, which may lead to poor growth and impaired speech development. CL/P is also associated with otitis media and conductive hearing loss.12 The financial burden further compounds these challenges, and families affected by CL/P often face significant economic hardship, delays in receiving care, and geographic barriers to accessing tertiary care centers, particularly in resource-limited settings like Pakistan.4 11

This study aimed to elucidate the clinical and demographic profile, familial and environmental risk factors, and surgical care status of children with CL/P in a Pakistani hospital-based cohort, highlighting gaps in access to timely cleft care in a resource-limited setting.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a facility-based, cross-sectional observational study conducted at the Cleft Care Center in Faisalabad, Pakistan, between September 2023 and March 2024. The center is a tertiary referral facility dedicated to comprehensive cleft care and operates in collaboration with Smile Train, an international non-governmental organization. Although located in Faisalabad, the center serves a wide catchment area, receiving referrals from across the Punjab province and adjoining regions, thereby providing access to a geographically diverse patient population. The reporting of this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Study population and sampling strategy

The source population consisted of all patients presenting with CL, CP, or CLP at the Cleft Care Center during the study period. A consecutive sampling strategy was employed, whereby patients of any age presenting between September 2023 and March 2024 with any form of OFC (CL, CP, or CLP) by the attending plastic surgeon were eligible for inclusion. Patients with incomplete medical records or those whose guardians declined to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

Diagnosis and phenotypic classification and characterization regarding completeness and laterality were based on the Veau classification system13 and were established by the team of attending plastic surgeons based on standard clinical assessment. For each case, phenotypic and biodemographic information was collected from the mothers or guardians with the help of trained medical staff. All data were collected in face-to-face interviews. The definitions of demographic variables were adopted from the Pakistan Demographic Health Survey.14 15 Information was also collected on parental consanguinity, maternal medication use, complications during pregnancy, maternal risk factors, associated anomalies, and surgical interventions. Family history was recorded and pedigrees were constructed up to three generations to document familial occurrence and inheritance patterns.

Statistical analysis

As the primary objective was to describe the phenotypic spectrum of CL/P, a precision-based sample size was calculated. Expected phenotypic proportions were derived from prior literature, and a priori sample size of 85 was deemed sufficient to estimate common phenotypic features with acceptable precision. To maximize accuracy, enable subgroup analyses, and minimize selection bias, a consecutive sampling strategy was adopted, resulting in the enrollment of all eligible patients presenting during the 6-month study period.

For statistical analysis, only the index case (the recruited patient) was included to avoid familial clustering; however, family history data were used to classify cases as familial or sporadic. Descriptive statistics were applied, and the distribution of patient attributes across categorical variables was assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests.

To evaluate associations between CL/P types and demographic variables, bivariable logistic regression was performed by entering each independent variable individually. For subtype comparison, multivariable regression was carried out and all variables were entered into the model in a stepwise manner to identify independent predictors of disease category after adjusting for potential confounders. Logistic regression analyses were repeated separately for maternal and obstetric risk factors against CL/P types. Continuous variables (i.e., age) were converted into discrete variables.

Model diagnostics were conducted to assess the adequacy and robustness of the logistic regression. Goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and pseudo-R² statistics. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors, with values <5 indicating acceptable levels. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (V.5.00) and STATA (V.11).

Results

Sample characteristics

This study included 518 patients, of which 15 cases were excluded due to incomplete information or lack of guardian consent. Hence, 503 index cases were recruited (male 268; 53%). The majority of patients were aged 1–5 years (52%). In addition, 55% of cases originated from rural areas, 80% belonged to Punjabi-speaking families, and 60% were of low socioeconomic status.

Demographic predictors of cleft types

CL, CP, and CLP were present in 26%, 36%, and 38% of cases, respectively. To examine potential associations with demographic variables, bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted separately for each cleft type (table 1).

Cleft lip

Male patients had a higher likelihood of presenting with CL than female patients, although the difference was not statistically significant (table 1). Age emerged as the strongest predictor in the model. Compared with the reference group (infants under 1 year), children aged 1–5 years had significantly lower odds of presenting with CL (75% lower). For children aged 6 years and above, odds were also lower (adjusted OR (aOR) = 0.65), but the association was not statistically significant. Regarding district of residence, Jhang (aOR=0.57) and Toba Tek Singh (aOR=0.75) showed lower odds compared with the reference district (Faisalabad), although these differences were not statistically significant. Rural residence was associated with lower odds in the crude analysis (OR=0.71) but became statistically significant after adjustment (aOR=0.58).

Cleft palate

Male sex was significantly associated with lower odds of CP. In both crude (OR=0.60) and adjusted (aOR=0.59) models, males had approximately 40% lower odds of presenting with CP compared with females (table 1). Age also showed a strong association: compared with infants under 1 year, children aged 1–5 years had significantly higher odds of CP (crude OR=2.24; aOR=2.36), with odds more than doubling and the association remaining highly significant after adjustment. For patients aged 6 years and above, odds were also elevated (aOR=1.48), but this association was not statistically significant. All other variables were not statistically significant.

Cleft lip and palate

Rural residence was significantly associated with higher odds of CLP. In the crude analysis, rural residence was associated with 68% higher odds (OR=1.68); after adjustment, the association remained significant and strengthened (aOR=1.80). A positive family history also emerged as a significant factor associated with higher odds of CLP, with significance in both crude (OR=1.65) and adjusted (aOR=1.68) models.

Maternal and obstetric risk factors in CL/P cases

Among the mothers of patients, 38% reported folic acid and vitamin intake during pregnancy. Pesticide exposure during pregnancy was reported in 32% of mothers, and 7% had a history of previous miscarriage. The majority of births occurred in hospitals (99%), and cesarean section was a common mode of delivery (58%). Mean paternal age was 32±7 years, and mean maternal age was 28±6 years across all cleft types, with no significant variation between groups. To assess the associations of maternal and obstetric factors with cleft types, bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed (table 2).

Cleft lip

Pesticide exposure was significantly associated with lower odds of CL. In both crude (OR=0.55) and adjusted (aOR=0.58) models, pesticide exposure during pregnancy was associated with approximately 42% lower odds of CL (table 2). In addition, higher parity was significantly associated with lower odds of CL. For fourth parity compared with first pregnancy, the crude model showed significantly lower odds (OR=0.36). After adjustment, the association remained substantial and statistically significant (aOR=0.34), indicating approximately 66% lower odds. There were no statistically significant associations with CL in either the crude or adjusted models for any other variable.

Cleft palate

In the crude model, cesarean delivery was associated with 31% lower odds of CP compared with normal vaginal delivery (OR=0.69) (table 2). However, after adjustment for other maternal and obstetric factors, the association attenuated and was no longer statistically significant (aOR=0.69). There were no statistically significant associations with CP in either the crude or adjusted models for any other variable.

Cleft lip and palate

In the crude model, pesticide exposure was associated with 73% higher odds of CLP (OR=1.73) (table 2). After adjustment, the association remained significant, with exposed individuals having 66% higher odds (aOR=1.66). Furthermore, higher parity, specifically fifth parity or above, was significantly associated with higher odds of CLP. For fifth parity or above compared with first pregnancy, the crude model showed significantly higher odds (OR=2.07). After adjustment, this association strengthened and remained highly significant (aOR=2.44), indicating approximately 144% higher odds.

Clinical characteristics, surgical interventions for cleft types and total affected in all families

Heterogeneity was evident in the clinical characteristics and familial attributes of the cleft types. Unilateral clefts accounted for 73% of cases, while 27% were bilateral, and differences in the distribution in cleft types were statistically significant (p=0.0005) (table 3). Overall, 63% of patients presented with complete clefts, while 37% had incomplete clefts. Complete clefts were frequent in CLP (92%), whereas incomplete clefts predominated in isolated CP (66%) (p<0.0001).

A total of 27% of patients had undergone surgical correction (male: 29%; female: 25%). An estimated 24% had one surgical process, whereas 3% had two or more surgical interventions. Among children <1 year of age, the majority (85%) had not yet undergone surgery. In contrast, both the patients in 1–5 years and ≥6 years age categories showed high proportions who had undergone surgical intervention (i.e., 33% each). Repeated surgeries were more prevalent in patients aged ≥6 years (8%) (p<0.0001).

Across cleft types, patients with CLP had a higher likelihood of undergoing surgical corrections (47%) compared with CL (15%) and CP (14%) (p<0.0001) (table 3). The majority of the patients requiring repeated surgical corrections had CLP (7%).

In all families, a total of 618 total affected individuals were identified; 338 (55%) were male and 280 (45%) were female (p=0.0118).

Discussion

In this cohort, CLP was the most frequent phenotype (38%), followed by CP (36%) and CL (26%). This distribution aligns with international epidemiological data.1 16 Male patients had a higher likelihood of presenting with CL than females, whereas female sex was significantly associated with higher odds of CP. This pattern has been reported by others and is attributed to sex-specific differences in embryological timing and palatal shelf fusion.5 6

Regression analyses including demographic variables revealed that cleft lip types possess distinct risk factor profiles. For instance, within this cohort, CP had a higher proportion of females compared with other cleft subtypes and a later age (1–5 years) at presentation, whereas rural residence was more common among CLP cases than among other cleft subtypes in this cohort, and positive family history was more frequently reported in CLP compared with other subtypes. In contrast, CL was characterized by lower representation in older age groups and rural residence. The age differentials observed may be explained by delayed intervention for isolated CP in infancy. In subsequent years, the condition often manifests with feeding difficulties, speech delay, or recurrent infections, necessitating surgical treatment.17,19 It should be acknowledged that in the absence of a non-affected control group, logistic regression models were limited to comparing cleft subtypes against one another to identify demographic and clinical characteristics that differ between subtypes. These analyses are exploratory in nature and intended to describe the cohort’s internal heterogeneity rather than to infer population-level risk factors.

A positive family history was reported in 18% of cases in this cohort. Previous studies from Pakistan have reported comparable findings: a study conducted in Karachi identified a positive family history in 11% of CL/P cases, while studies from southern Punjab and Peshawar reported rates of 17% and 21%, respectively.2 The remarkably high rate of parental consanguinity (77%) observed in this study is striking compared with the background consanguinity rate of 42%.20 This finding is consistent with previous Pakistani reports, in which consanguinity rates among families with CL/P ranged from 50% to 70%.211 21,23 However, cleft types did not differ significantly with consanguinity rates, and consanguinity remained a non-significant variable in regression analyses.

High phenotypic heterogeneity was evident across cleft types with regard to laterality and completeness. The higher occurrence of unilateral (73%) and complete clefts (63%), particularly among CLP cases (92%), indicates a substantial burden of severe phenotypes. This pattern aligns with other studies suggesting a potential role of environmental risk factors, such as pesticide exposure, and poor maternal nutrition, which are more prevalent in rural settings.1 6 24 25

In the present study, only 38% of patients’ mothers reported folic acid or vitamin supplementation during pregnancy, which is lower than the national average and highlights a significant public health gap.19 Nisar et al. identified failure to take folic acid during pregnancy as one of the most influential risk factors for CL/P.19

Maternal pesticide exposure was reported in one-third of cases and was significantly associated with cleft type. This finding is consistent with previous epidemiological studies linking prenatal exposure to agricultural chemicals with an increased risk of orofacial clefts.1 18 25 Curiously, pesticide exposure was significantly associated with lower odds of CL. This unexpected inverse association should be considered hypothesis-generating only. Retrospective self-reported exposure data in the absence of a control group are subject to recall bias, which may be differential across cleft subtypes. It is quite likely that mothers of children with more severe phenotypes (e.g., CLP) might search their memory more thoroughly for potential causes, leading to overreporting of pesticide exposure, while mothers of children with isolated CL may underreport. Therefore, this finding does not imply a protective effect and requires confirmation in prospective case-control studies.

This study further revealed that only 27% of patients had undergone surgical correction, with repeated surgeries being rare (3%). Surgical interventions were most common among children aged 1–5 years and among those with CLP, likely reflecting prioritization of more severe phenotypes and delay of surgical intervention beyond 1 year of age. Nevertheless, these figures indicate a substantial unmet surgical need, particularly given that timely primary repair within the first year of life is recommended for optimal functional and psychosocial outcomes.1 2 4 Similar gaps in surgical coverage have been reported in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where financial constraints, limited availability of specialist centers, and delayed referral contribute to prolonged disability.4 11

Internationally, presurgical infant orthopedics (PSIO) has been extensively evaluated for its effects on nasolabial esthetics and maxillary arch development in patients with unilateral cleft lip and palate.26 2728 Studies comparing different PSIO modalities, including modified Grayson’s technique, DynaCleft nasal elevator, OrthoAligner NAM, and the Rhinoplasty Appliance System, have shown comparable short-term outcomes in alveolar molding and nasal symmetry. 27 These findings from high-resource settings highlight the need for similar prospective studies in LMICs, where access to PSIO and timely surgical care remains limited, as observed in our cohort.

This study has several limitations. Being hospital-based, it may be subject to selection bias, with possible overrepresentation of more severe cases presenting to the center for surgical intervention or management. This may limit generalizability, and the results may differ from the broader national population in terms of sociodemographic status, severity of illness, and access to care. In addition, the predominance of cases aged 1–5 years and the smaller proportion of older children may have influenced the surgical intervention rates we observed. Assessment of maternal risk factors relied on retrospective self-reported data, which is susceptible to recall bias. Furthermore, the authors acknowledge limitations of retrospective self-reported data which has recall bias specifically, the inverse association observed between pesticide exposure and cleft lip may be explained by differential recall bias across cleft subtypes rather than a true biological effect, and lack of a control population. The findings regarding pesticide exposure, consanguinity, and age at presentation are valuable as exploratory observations but require confirmation in case–control or prospective cohort studies before any causal interpretation can be justified.

In conclusion, we observed distinct demographic and clinical characteristics across cleft types in this hospital-based cohort of Pakistani children with CL/P. CLP was the most common and severe phenotype in this cohort and was associated with rural residence and positive family history. CP showed female predominance and later age at presentation, while CL was less frequent among older children and in rural settings. Parental consanguinity was strikingly high (77%) compared with the general population, which is consistent with genetic susceptibility within a multifactorial framework but, in the absence of a control group, does not establish causality. The inverse association between pesticide exposure and cleft lip observed in this study is likely influenced by recall bias and should not be interpreted as causal. Low periconceptional folic acid supplementation (38%) represents a public health gap, although recall bias and the lack of non-affected controls preclude causal attribution. The low surgical correction rate (27%) reflects a substantial unmet need for timely cleft care in this resource-limited setting.
