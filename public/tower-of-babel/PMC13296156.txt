Complexed rifampicin with iron mitigates doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity
Authors: Omnia A. Basal, Entsar A. Saad, Rana R. El Sadda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKidney disease represents a significant global health challenge that can lead to serious health complications. The chemotherapeutic agent ‘doxorubicin’ has nephrotoxic effects as an adverse effect. Ongoing research is focused on finding protective agents that can alleviate doxorubicin’s adverse effects and enhance treatment results. Recently, an iron-rifampicin complex has been developed, showing promising anticancer activity with improved safety compared to a conventional drug. This study aimed to evaluate the potential protective effects of this iron-rifampicin complex, which has not been previously explored, against doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity in mice.MethodsSix groups of sixty male albino mice were used: Control, Nephrotoxicity (Neph), Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex (0.071 mg iron-rifampicin complex/kg), Neph+high dose of iron-rifampicin complex (0.107 mg iron-rifampicin complex/kg), Complex control in high dose (0.107 mg iron-rifampicin complex/kg), and Vehicle control. The following parameters were evaluated: body weight, kidney weight, kidney index, creatinine, urea, uric acid, sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, malondialdehyde, nitric oxide, reduced glutathione, catalase, superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), along with kidney histopathology.ResultsDoxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity led to a significant reduction (p < 0.001) in body weight, kidney weight, kidney index, antioxidants, and VEGF, while increasing (p < 0.001) minerals, malondialdehyde, nitric oxide, creatinine, urea, and uric acid, alongside worsened kidney architecture. The iron-rifampicin complex improved all these parameters (p < 0.05), moving them closer to normal levels, particularly at the lower dosage, mainly by reducing oxidative stress and preserving VEGF.ConclusionIn conclusion, we propose the iron-rifampicin complex as a potential candidate for nephroprotection against doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity, subject to further validation.

Introduction

The kidney is a vital organ that excretes waste, balances water and electrolytes, and maintains body homeostasis through its functional units, the nephrons. Composed of nephrons (glomeruli, tubules, and sacs), blood vessels, nerves, and other tissues, the kidney filters blood to produce urine. Kidney disease damages these structures and functions, leading to symptoms such as proteinuria, hematuria, and high blood pressure. Kidney function can eventually impaired and impair other organs, leading to severe health consequences [1].

Nephrotoxicity, or kidney damage, can be caused by chronic diseases like diabetes, obesity, or cancer, and by drug side effects or exposure to toxins. This can lead to acute kidney injury (AKI), a sudden drop in kidney function, which is a serious global health issue. Inflammation and oxidative stress also contribute to the development of kidney damage. AKI is a significant global health problem, especially in developing countries, where 5–30% of hospital patients may develop it. In low- and middle-income countries, AKI affects about 11.3 million people and causes 1.4 million annual deaths [2]. These statistics were linked to the International Society of Nephrology’s “0 by 25” initiative launched in 2013 and published in 2015 [3].

Doxorubicin is an anthracycline antibiotic produced by the bacterium Streptomyces peucetius var. caesius that is used to treat various cancers by damaging cancer cell DNA. It is often used in combination with other drugs, surgery, or radiation to treat various cancers [4, 5]. However, the clinical use of doxorubicin is limited by its severe side effects, including cardiomyopathy, nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and neurotoxicity [5, 6], which can significantly impact a patient’s health and the ability to continue treatment. Doxorubicin causes nephrotoxicity by accumulating in the glomerulus, leading to kidney damage. This toxicity is a result of the drug generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammation in both normal and cancer cells and cause damage like glomerular and tubular atrophy, podocyte injury, and increased capillary permeability [7–9]. Research is ongoing to find ways to mitigate these side effects and improve treatment outcomes, such as developing new drug delivery systems or protective agents.

Rifamycins are a class of antibiotics produced by Amycolatopsis rifamycinica bacteria or synthesized artificially. Rifampicin (also called rifampin) is a semisynthetic rifamycin used to treat bacterial infections like tuberculosis and leprosy. It works by inhibiting bacterial transcription and also helps treat itching from certain liver diseases. In addition to its antibacterial and antipruritic effects, rifampicin has shown potential as an anticancer agent [4, 6, 10] and potential to protect against doxorubicin-induced heart damage through its antioxidant properties [11]. Transition metal complexes are being explored for medical applications, especially cancer therapy, due to their unique properties like redox activity and coordination versatility, which allow for the design of targeted drugs. Iron, as an essential and biocompatible trace element, is a promising candidate for new therapeutics because it can be tailored into complexes with broad reactivity and potentially lower side effects compared to traditional drugs. The iron-rifampicin complex was more effective than rifampicin alone at inhibiting tumor growth in animal models, as seen by reduced tumor mass, extended lifespan, and improved organ function and antioxidant status. Its mechanism is thought to involve redox activity and/or inducing mitochondrial apoptosis, and it showed a non-toxic effect in healthy mice. These findings suggest the iron-rifampicin complex could be a promising future cancer therapeutic pending further validation [6].

There are several potential strategies to reduce the toxic effects of doxorubicin without compromising its anticancer efficacy. One significant area of research focuses on developing protective agents. This study aims to assess the potential protective effects of the iron-rifampicin complex against doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity in mice.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All solvents were used as received, and other chemicals were of the highest available analytical grade. Specifically, the iron-rifampicin complex, previously synthesized and characterized by Prof. Hassanien and his colleagues [6], was kindly provided by Prof. Hassanien MM (Professor of Analytical Chemistry, Technology and Education College, Beni-Suef University), doxorubicin was from Hikma, rifampicin (Rifactine) was from MUP (Cairo, Egypt), diagnostic kits for creatinine, uric acid, and urea were from BIOMED DIAGNOSTICS (Germany), glutathione peroxidase, calcium, phosphorus, sodium, and potassium were from BIODIAGNOSTICS (Giza, Egypt), while a mouse VEGF immunoassay kit (catalogue no. E-EL-M1292) was from ELLABSCIENCE BIOTECHNOLOGY, USA.

Animals

Sixty male albino mice, weighing 20–25 g, were sourced from the Theodor Bilharz Research Institute in Giza, Egypt. They were housed in polypropylene cages at our institution for one week before the experiment began, under controlled conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 12:12 light/dark cycle, and controlled humidity within a range of 30% to 70%). They were fed a standard diet and water ad libitum, in accordance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” [12].

Experimental design

Group 1 (Control)

Mice received daily normal saline.

Group 2 (Neph; Nephrotoxicity group)

Doxorubicin (5 mg/kg) administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection in three equal doses over 14 days (with 4-day intervals), for a total cumulative dose of 15 mg/kg [13].

Group 3 (Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex)

Doxorubicin (as in Neph group) plus a daily i.p. injection of the freshly prepared iron-rifampicin complex in absolute ethanol at 0.071 mg/kg as described by Saad et al. [6] for 14 days.

Group 4 (Neph+high dose of iron-rifampicin complex)

Doxorubicin (as in Neph group) plus a daily i.p. injection of the iron-rifampicin complex at 0.107 mg/kg [6] for 14 days.

Group 5 (Complex control in high dose)

A daily i.p. injection of the iron-rifampicin complex (0.107 mg/kg in absolute ethanol) for 14 days, without doxorubicin.

Group 6 (Vehicle control)

A daily i.p. injection of the solvent (absolute ethanol) in the same absolute ethanol volume as that of Complex control in high dose group for 14 days, without the complex or doxorubicin.

Body weight was recorded every 7 days throughout the experimental period to calculate the body weight difference. At the end of the experiment, the mice were fasted for eight hours and then anesthetized using ketamine (75 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg). Under anesthesia, blood samples were collected from the hearts, after which the anesthetized mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The collected blood samples were allowed to clot. Sera were separated by cooling centrifugation (4 °C) at 3000 rpm for 15 min and stored at -20 °C until used for estimations. The two kidneys were removed, washed with normal saline, blotted dry on filter paper, and weighed. The percentage of the two kidneys’ total weight to the total body weight of mice has been considered as the kidney index (KI).

Estimations

Creatinine, urea, and uric acid levels were measured in blood using commercial kits. The Jaffè reaction for creatinine forms a yellow-orange complex with picric acid in an alkaline solution, with the color intensity proportional to creatinine levels. Urea was detected via an enzymatic reaction that releases ammonia, which then reacts with salicylate and nitroprusside to produce a green dye. Uric acid was determined by an oxidation reaction catalyzed by uricase, producing hydrogen peroxide, which then reacts with DHBS and 4-aminoantipyrine to form a red dye.

For sodium, excess uranyl acetate reacts with potassium ferrocyanide to produce a colored complex whose intensity is inversely proportional to sodium concentration. Potassium is analyzed by reacting it with sodium tetraphenyl boron to create a colloidal suspension. Calcium is measured by the blue-colored complex it forms with methyl thymol blue, and hydroxy 8-quinoline is used to remove magnesium interference. Phosphorus is determined by a phosphomolybdate complex that turns blue when reduced by stannous chloride.

Sandwich-enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay for quantifying vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) involves incubating samples with a capture antibody, adding a biotinylated detection antibody, followed by an Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate, and finally a substrate and stop solution to measure absorbance at 450 nm.

The concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured using the Ohkawa et al. method [14], while nitric oxide (NO) levels were estimated via the Montgomery and Dymock method [15]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using the DeChatelet et al. method [16]. The activity of catalase was assessed by measuring the rate of hydrogen peroxide degradation [17], glutathione peroxidase (GPX) was measured using the commercial kit (BIODIAGNOSTICS, Giza, Egypt), and the reduced glutathione (GSH) level was estimated by monitoring the breakdown of 5,5’- dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (Moron et al.,1979) [18].

Histopathological assay

Kidney tissue was prepared for subsequent microscopic evaluation by fixing in 10% formalin, dehydrating with alcohol, clearing with xylene, embedding in paraffin wax, and cutting into thin Sects. (5–6 μm) for staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). A pathologist then examined these kidney sections under a light microscope at 200× magnification, while being blinded to the treatment the animals received.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey-Kramer test was used for Gaussian data, while the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunnett’s test was used for non-Gaussian data. All tests were performed using Instat software, version 3.10, with results presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effect on kidney function markers

As revealed in Fig. 1, serum creatinine, urea, and uric acid levels were significantly elevated in the Nephrotoxicity group compared with the Control group (p < 0.001). In contrast, the two control groups (Vehicle control and Complex control in high dose) did not show any significant changes in these levels. However, there was a significant increase (p < 0.05) in uric acid levels in healthy mice after they were administered the iron-rifampicin complex, compared with the Control group. When treating nephrotoxicity with the iron-rifampicin complex at either low or high doses (Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex and Neph+high dose), the levels of creatinine, urea, and uric acid were significantly reduced (p < 0.001) compared to the Neph group. Significantly, the low dose of the iron-rifampicin complex had a more favorable effect on the levels of creatinine than the high dose (p < 0.01).

Effect on minerals

Sodium, calcium, phosphorus, and potassium levels, as shown in Table 3, were extremely significantly elevated in the Nephrotoxicity group compared to the Control group (p < 0.001). Iron-rifampicin complex treatment at a low dose significantly lowered all these levels compared to the Nephrotoxicity group (p < 0.01 for P, while p < 0.001 for Na, K, and Ca). Regarding treatment at the high dose, it significantly lowered the levels of Na, K (p < 0.001 for both), and Ca (p < 0.05), but non-significantly lowered the level of P. Although there were no significant differences found between the Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex and Neph+high dose of iron-rifampicin complex groups, mineral levels in the Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex group were closer to those in the Control group.

Effect on oxidative stress and VEGF

The levels of MDA and NO in the Nephrotoxicity group showed extreme elevations compared to the Control group (p < 0.001). In contrast, there was an extreme shortage in VEGF levels compared to the Control group (p < 0.001). When treated with the iron-rifampicin complex, significant improvements were displayed (p < 0.001 versus Nephrotoxicity group), and the improvements in NO and VEGF levels were significantly better (p < 0.001) by the low dose, as appeared in the Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex group compared to those exhibited by the high dose (Table 4).

Effect on kidney histology

The renal tissues of the Control group displayed normal morphology and structure, featuring a healthy renal cortex containing glomeruli surrounded by two types of tubules: proximal convoluted tubules and distal tubules (Fig. 3A). In contrast, doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity caused significant damage to the renal cortex, resulting in necrosis and congested renal glomeruli. Most proximal convoluted tubules and distal tubules exhibited signs of degeneration, with their epithelial lining showing vacuolated cytoplasm and evidence of local inflammation, characterized by intervening congested blood vessels (hemorrhage) (Fig. 3B). Histological sections of both the Complex control in high dose and the Vehicle control groups revealed normal renal cortices, with images similar to those of the Control group (Fig. 3C and D, respectively).

In the treated nephrotoxicity groups receiving the iron-rifampicin complex, improvements and normalization of renal tissues were more pronounced in mice treated with the lower dose of the complex. The Neph+low dose of iron-rifampicin complex group exhibited mild damage, with the renal cortex displaying less pronounced congested renal glomeruli and reduced vacuolation (Fig. 3E). In contrast, moderate damage was observed in the Neph+high dose of iron-rifampicin complex group, where the renal cortex showed congested and necrotic glomeruli, as well as vacuolated tubules, indicating signs of cellular stress (Fig. 3F).

Discussion

Drug-induced nephrotoxicity is a major health concern, often causing acute kidney injury that can progress to chronic or end-stage renal disease, and mortality [7, 19, 20]. Doxorubicin is a widely used anthracycline; however, its renal adverse effects may limit its use [21]. Moreover, it acts as an inducer of kidney damage in rodents [7, 22]. In the current experiment, body weight loss after doxorubicin administration is parallel with many studies [9, 23]. These studies elucidated that most anticancer drugs result in a reduction in weight, loss of appetite, anorexia, constipation, dysphagia, dyspepsia, and gastrointestinal disturbances. In addition, numerous studies explained loss of weight as toxicity of doxorubicin on the digestive system that led to food intake reduction [11, 24, 25]. Others suggested that doxorubicin causes loss of appetite by indirect toxicity on the gastrointestinal tract through decreasing secretion of internal hormones [26, 27]. Normal body weight gain in the Vehicle control and Complex control in high dose groups is due to the normal internal environment of metabolism, in agreement with [6], and [28].

According to [9, 29–32], doxorubicin stimulates alteration in kidney functions and construction as increased renal vascular resistance, histological damage to tubular cells represented in an extremely reduction in Kwt and KI due to doxorubicin-induced free radicals accumulation and glomerular toxicity that are not yet fully understood. Thus, the toxicity of doxorubicin includes inflammatory changes in kidney tissues, and doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity causes increased capillary porosity and glomerular shrinking, as proved in kidney histopathology after doxorubicin administration.

Sole rifampicin causes defect in renal tubules, due to acute kidney injury and allergic interstitial nephritis of rifampicin or one of its metabolites [33–35], but complexing it with iron may offer protection [6]. The present study supports this suggestion by the normal construction and features of renal sections, and showed normal KI after iron-rifampicin complex administration to healthy mice. Moreover, co-treatment of the iron-rifampicin complex stimulated weight gain and improvement of appetite. Further, the ability of iron-rifampicin complex treatment to ameliorate mice kidney of the doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity group was confirmed by the detection of different degrees of normalized architecture of the renal tissues of the kidney, suggesting a protective mechanism. The lower dose of the complex (0.071 mg/kg) was more effective at restoring renal tissue than the higher dose (0.107 mg/kg), showing fewer signs of stress on renal cells along with the positive impact on KI value. This suggests the iron-rifampicin complex has a protective effect, potentially by preventing or reducing the drug’s nephrotoxic effects.

Creatinine, a waste product from muscle metabolism, is produced at a constant rate and cleared by the kidneys. In fact, serum creatinine is elevated when there is a significant (roughly 50%) loss of kidney function or when urine elimination is obstructed. Thus, serum creatinine is a late marker of acute kidney injury [36]. Urea rises earlier than other nitrogenous wastes in kidney malfunction [37]. The traditional markers of nephrotoxicity, including creatinine and urea nitrogen, are specific with low sensitivity in the detection of early kidney damage [7, 38]. Moreover, the increase in urea production might induce modest deteriorations in glomerular filtration and a reduction in intra-glomerular pressure [29]. As well, Srivastava et al. [39] noted that reduced glomerular filtration in chronic kidney disease causes elevated serum uric acid, a risk factor for metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension. Furthermore, uric acid contributes to kidney damage through tubule obstruction and progression of chronic kidney disease via inflammation, oxidative stress, and renin- renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation.

In parallel with many researchers [21, 23, 32], our current results confirm that doxorubicin administration causes alterations in kidney functions, represented in an extremely significant increase in kidney function markers creatinine, urea, and uric acid due to doxorubicin-induced generation of free radicals. Treating doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity with the complex caused decreases in serum creatinine, urea, and creatinine levels, supporting the protective effect of the complex on the kidneys.

The present results also demonstrated that doxorubicin causes hypernatremia, hyperkalemia, hypercalcemia, and hyperphosphatemia. As shown in the studies [25, 27, 40, 41], there is a relation between hypernatremia and developing heart and kidney diseases. These reports attributed hypernatremia to decreased sodium reabsorption due to renal tubular disorders, leading to reduced urine sodium excretion. Studies of Habib et al. [42] and Wang et al. [27] reported doxorubicin administration causes hypokalemia while studies of Abou Seif [43] and Basal et al. [11] revealed elevated levels of potassium after doxorubicin administration due to the central role of the kidney in the maintenance of potassium homeostasis even in the setting of the chronic renal failure. According to Rakisheva et al. [44], hyperkalemia is linked to neurohormonal issues in kidney disease and can increase the risk of cardiovascular problems. In cases of doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity, renal dysfunction and damage can lead to an over-activation of the sodium/potassium pumps, resulting in extreme elevations in both sodium and potassium levels. Our study’s findings of hypercalcemia after doxorubicin injection are supported by research showing that doxorubicin may cause calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum, a process linked to free radical formation and oxidative stress [11, 25, 45–47]. On the other side, Mitry and Edwards [48] and Suleimani et al. [8] actually concluded that doxorubicin causes a loss of calcium homeostasis by affecting calcium release channels in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) and causing SR calcium underload (or impaired uptake). Also, in harmony with [8, 47, 49], the indirect toxicity of doxorubicin to the kidney led to hyperphosphatemia. Kidney deterioration due to intoxication reduces the elimination rate of renal phosphate, and as a result, serum phosphate levels elevate [37]. Concomitant injection of the complex with doxorubicin displayed improved levels of Na, K, P, and Ca, reflecting the complex’s positive influence on mineral imbalance.

Many studies have explained the role of oxidative imbalance in doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity [21, 29, 50]. Doxorubicin triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS overproduction, and a rapid decline in renal function because of renal tissue deformation [21, 31]. The current study findings: elevations in NO and MDA levels along with decreases in antioxidants (SOD, catalase, GSH, and GPX), support many previous studies that have reported that doxorubicin increases lipid peroxidation and decreases enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants [21, 29]. Doxorubicin-induced overproduction of reactive species interrupts antioxidant defense mechanisms and causes the consumption of cellular antioxidant contents. By injecting doxorubicin concomitant with iron-rifampicin complex, this combination prevented the generation of reactive species by doxorubicin, allowing the cell’s natural antioxidants to be preserved and protecting the kidneys from doxorubicin-induced damage (see Fig. 4). Ayla et al. [51] demonstrated that doxorubicin elevates iNOS and eNOS and may induce NO production, leading to oxygen-radical-mediated cellular damage. Besides, many other studies [8, 11, 23, 25, 32], reported that doxorubicin induces an elevation in MDA level as a result of overproduction of reactive oxygen species responsible for oxidation of macromolecules. Thus, doxorubicin causes kidney damage (nephrotoxicity) due to its toxicity and the free radicals it generates.

Proposed mechanism of action

VEGF is vital for forming embryonic blood vessels, supporting injury healing, aiding post-exercise muscle development, and promoting collateral circulation to bypass blockages [52]. VEGF, primarily produced by renal tubular cells and podocytes, acts as a crucial signaling molecule for glomerular and peritubular endothelial cells to maintain renal microvasculature and high blood flow. It facilitates vital cross-talk within the kidney, ensuring vascular growth and structural integrity. Its function also extends to maintaining the kidney’s metabolic needs and whole-body homeostasis, including blood pressure, fluid balance, pH, and electrolyte balance [53–55]. VEGF fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting T cell adhesion through downregulated adhesion molecules (vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 and intercellular adhesion molecule 1). It further inhibits dendritic cell differentiation and T cell activation by increasing the number of regulatory T cells [56].

VEGF is essential for maintaining renal structure and function. Inhibiting the VEGF pathway can cause severe renal side effects, particularly renovascular diseases that may manifest as proteinuria, hypertension, reduced glomerular filtration rate, and thrombotic microangiopathy [54, 55]. Anti-angiogenic anticancer drugs that block VEGF can cause kidney damage [57]. According to Hayman et al. [58], anti-angiogenic therapy that leads to low free VEGF levels may result in endothelial dysfunction and dysregulation of podocytes. This can lead to two significant clinical adverse effects: hypertension and proteinuria.

In this investigation, following doxorubicin administration VEGF levels dramatically decreased, supporting the fact that doxorubicin possesses anti-angiogenic properties, which contribute to its effectiveness as an antitumor drug. By blocking VEGF, doxorubicin can also negatively affect the kidney’s ability to function, leading to damage. The administration of the complex alongside doxorubicin resulted in an increase in VEGF levels. Mechanistically, this improvement may be attributed to the nephroprotective effects of the complex, which seem to mitigate the toxic impact of doxorubicin on the kidneys as illustrated in Fig. 4. Interestingly, the most effective dosage was the lowest one administered.

Based on our daily observations during the study, physically, the healthy mice administered the iron-rifampicin complex at the high dose appeared similar to the normal mice in the control group. The mice treated with the complex showed no pathological alterations in any of the physical, renal architectural, or biochemical parameters we investigated, indicating the complex’s safety. Regarding higher kidney weight, it is considered normal relative to their body weight because those mice were initially heavier. These findings align with [6], which noted that while rifampicin is used to treat tuberculosis, it is associated with serious adverse effects. However, after complexation with iron, the cytotoxicity was significantly reduced.

Limitations of our work include: due to economic constraints, we could not investigate the impact of a more extended period of iron-rifampicin complex on renal recovery, assess whether the complex has a protective effect on other vital organs, such as the liver and the heart, or evaluate its influence on inflammation and apoptosis markers.

Conclusion

Based on our findings, we conclude that the iron-rifampicin complex is a safe protector against doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity, and the lower dosage is the desired option. Ultimately, we recommend the iron-rifampicin complex as a promising candidate for nephroprotection, pending further validation.
