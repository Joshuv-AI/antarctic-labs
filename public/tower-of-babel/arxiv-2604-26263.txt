qSHIFT: An Adaptive Sampling Protocol for Higher-Order Quantum Simulation
Sangjin Lee, Sangkook Choi

Abstract
Quantum simulation is a cornerstone application for quantum computing, yet standard methods face a trade-off between circuit depth and accuracy: Trotterization depth scales with the number of Hamiltonian terms L L , while sampling-based qDRIFT is restricted to O ⁡ ( t 2 ) O(t^{2}) error scaling. Here, We introduce qSHIFT, an adaptive sampling protocol that overcomes these limitations. By adaptively updating sampling distributions, qSHIFT maintains L L -independent gate complexity while achieving an improved error scaling of O ⁡ ( t 1 + r ) O(t^{1+r}) for an adjustable parameter r r . This performance is enabled by a classical subroutine solving L r L^{r} linear equations per sampling round. Numerical demonstrations confirm the O ⁡ ( t 1 + r ) O(t^{1+r}) scaling, showcasing qSHIFT as a resource-efficient framework for high-precision quantum simulation. Furthermore, the protocol’s reduced circuit depth enhances its compatibility with physical error mitigation, making it a promising candidate for implementation on near-term quantum devices. In addition to its role as a standalone algorithm, qSHIFT can provide a high-precision foundation for modular quantum frameworks such as qSWIFT or Krylov quantum diagonalization.

II
Background

We consider the quantum simulation of a system governed by a Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}h_{i}H_{i}
, where
h_{i}>0
. For systems with geometrically local interactions, we can efficiently group commuting terms within
\{H_{i}\}
. This compression ensures the total number of grouped terms
L
does not grow with the system size.
This form is enough to encompass a wide range of physically relevant models.

Given an initial state
|\psi\rangle
, the time-evolution of the physical observable
\mathcal{Q}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{U}\equiv\langle\psi|U^{\dagger}(t)\mathcal{Q}U(t)|\psi\rangle,

(1)

where
U=e^{-iHt}
.
Since the exact implementation of
U(t)
is generally demanding computationally, practical simulation algorithms aim to approximate
\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{U}
with a manageable quantum circuit. In this section, we briefly review two such approaches: the product formula approach and the qDRIFT protocol.

II.1
Product formula approach: Trotterization

The product formula approach approximates
U(t)
by a designed product of unitary operators. For example, the first- and second-order Trotter formulas are given by

\displaystyle V_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\left(e^{-ih_{i}H_{i}t}\right),

\displaystyle V_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\left(e^{-ih_{i}H_{i}t}\right)\prod_{i=L}^{1}\left(e^{-ih_{i}H_{i}t}\right),

where the associated errors satisfy

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{V_{s}}-\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{U}=\mathcal{O}(t^{s}),\quad\text{($s$=2,3)}.

More generally, other designed sequences of unitary operators with higher(
s\geq 3
) precision are well-known
40
;
19
;
10
;
24
;
12
;
7
;
15
;
38
;
16
;
26
.
In addition, for a given product formula, the folding of the product formula can be introduced as

\displaystyle V_{N,\alpha}(t)\equiv\left(V_{\alpha}\left(\frac{t}{N}\right)\right)^{N},

which further improves the approximation accuracy since the induced algorithmic error is suppressed by an integer power of
1/N
.

In the product formula approach, the number of gates to achieve the target precision
\varepsilon
can be estimated analytically. For example, the gate complexities for the first- and the second-order formula scale as
\mathcal{O}\left(L^{3}\left(\Lambda t\right)^{2}/\varepsilon)\right)
, and
\mathcal{O}\left(L^{5/2}\left(\Lambda t\right)^{3/2}/\varepsilon^{1/2})\right)

respectively with
\Lambda=\text{max}_{i}\;h_{i}

10
;
6
.

While product formulas provide deterministic precision without sampling overhead, they typically necessitate long gate sequences that scale with the complexity of the system.
The origin of this complexity lies in the requirement to implement every term in the Hamiltonian within each of the
N
Trotter time steps. In systems such as complex molecules or long-range condensed matter models, the number of Hamiltonian terms can be quite large, often scaling polynomially with the number of orbitals or sites. Because the total gate count is roughly the product of
L
and the number of steps
N
required for precision, the resulting deep circuits become highly vulnerable to physical error accumulation. This
L
-dependent bottleneck in deterministic simulation provides the primary motivation for the sampling-based approaches introduced in the following subsection, which decouple circuit depth from the number of Hamiltonian terms.

II.2
Sampling-based approach: qDRIFT

A sampling-based approach leverages an ensemble of random circuits. A representative example of such methods is qDRIFT
6
. In the
N
-qDRIFT protocol,
N(>L)
unitary gates are sequentially sampled from an operator pool

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\{V_{i}(t)=e^{-i\frac{t\lambda}{N}H_{i}}|i=1,\cdots,L\},

with fixed probability
p_{i}=h_{i}/\lambda
for sampling the unitary operator
V_{i}(t)
with
\lambda=\sum_{i}h_{i}
.

After the sampling process is completed, the ensemble average of the measurement outcomes of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to evolved states is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{qDRIFT}}=\sum_{\vec{s}}p_{\vec{s}}\langle\psi|V^{\dagger}_{\vec{s}}(t)\mathcal{Q}V_{\vec{s}}(t)|\psi\rangle,

(2)

where
\sum_{\vec{s}}
is sum over all possible sequences with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} p_{\vec{s}}&=p_{s_{1}}\cdots p_{s_{N}},\\
V_{\vec{s}}(t)&=V_{s_{1}}(t)\cdots V_{s_{N}}(t),\end{aligned}

(3)

and
s_{i=1\cdots N}\in\{1,\cdots,L\}
.
From the direct calculation, (see Ref .
6
or Appendix
A
), one can show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{U}-\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{qDRIFT}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{N}\right).

(4)

Since qDRIFT relies on a sampling process, we also compute the sampling complexity for a target precision
\varepsilon_{\text{qDRIFT}}
, given by

\displaystyle N_{\text{qDRIFT,sampling}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(t\lambda/N)^{2}}{\varepsilon_{\text{qDRIFT}}^{2}}\right),

with the details of the calculation provided in Appendix
A.2
.

We remark that in the qDRIFT protocol, the number of gates required to achieve a target precision
\varepsilon
is generally independent of
L
(except in cases where
\lambda
scales with
L
). The
O(t^{2})
error scaling in qDRIFT arises because it acts as a first-order approximation of the time-evolution operator. By sampling individual terms
H_{j}
according to their weights
h_{j}/\lambda
, the protocol ensures the ensemble average of the random circuits matches the ideal evolution only up to the first order of the Taylor expansion. Consequently, the leading-order error term scales quadratically with the evolution time
t
, effectively restricting qDRIFT to short-time simulations or necessitating a prohibitive number of samples to achieve high precision over longer durations.

III
qSHIFT

In this section, we propose the qSHIFT protocol, a sampling-based quantum simulation protocol that achieves improved algorithmic error scaling while maintaining a gate count independent of
L
.
The key idea is that, rather than sampling unitary gates with a fixed probability distribution as in qDRIFT, qSHIFT adaptively updates the probability distribution at each sampling step so that the ensemble average matches the target time-evolution to a higher order in
t
. To keep the main discussion concise, we present only the essential framework here; full derivations and examples are provided in Appendix
C
.

The qSHIFT protocol proceeds as follows. Given input parameters
(N,r)
, where we assume
N
is a multiple of
r
for brevity, the protocol samples
r
operators during each of the
N/r
distinct rounds. In each sampling round
p\in\{1,\dots,N/r\}
, we determine the coefficients
p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}
for a sequence of
r
operators, where the superscript denotes the current round.
To obtain these coefficients, we require that the ensemble average of the circuit in the
p
-th round matches the target time-evolution by comparing:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}V^{\dagger}_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}(t)\left(V^{\dagger}_{S}\mathcal{Q}V_{S}\right)V_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}(t),\\
=&\sum_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}\sum_{n_{1},\cdots n_{r}=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}\left(\frac{it\lambda}{N}\right)^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}N[H,\vec{n},\vec{s}^{(p)}],\end{aligned}

(5)

to

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{pt}{N}H}\mathcal{Q}e^{-i\frac{pt}{N}H},

(6)

order by order up to
\mathcal{O}(t^{r})
. Here,
V_{S}
is the cumulative unitary operators from previously sampled rounds

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V_{S}=V_{\vec{s}^{(1)}}V_{\vec{s}^{(2)}}\cdots V_{\vec{s}^{(n-1)}}\end{aligned}

(7)

with
V_{s^{(1)}}=\mathbb{1}
. The nested commutator,
N[H,\vec{n},\vec{s}^{(p)}]
, is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} N[H,\vec{n},\vec{s}^{(p)}]=[H_{s^{(p)}_{r}},\cdots,[H_{s^{(p)}_{2}},[H_{s^{(p)}_{1}},V^{\dagger}_{S}\mathcal{Q}V_{S}]_{n_{1}}\cdots]_{n_{r}}.\end{aligned}

(8)

We observe that many nested commutators (e.g.,
[H_{i},\mathcal{Q}]_{n}
) appear repeatedly throughout the Taylor expansion. After eliminating these redundancies, the protocol yields exactly
L^{r}
independent linear equations, which determines the
\{p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}\}
. The full counting argument is detailed in Appendix
B.1
.

As an illustration, consider the
(N,r=2)
-qSHIFT. The linear systems to be solved classically at each round
p
are:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(p_{ij}+p_{ji}\right)=\frac{h_{i}p}{\lambda}-\#_{V_{i}},

(9)

\displaystyle p_{ij(\neq i)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{p}{\lambda}\right)^{2}h_{i}h_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{p}{\lambda}\right)h_{i}\#_{V_{j}},

where
\#_{V_{i}}
is the number of times
V_{i}
appeared in the cumulative sequence
V_{S}
. For example, if one sampled
e^{-\frac{it\lambda}{N}H_{1}}e^{-\frac{it\lambda}{N}H_{2}}
in the first round (
p=1
), then
\#_{V_{1}}=\#_{V_{2}}=1
in the equations for the next round (
p=2
).

We evaluate the linear combination in Eq. (
5
) stochastically through sampling. However, because the coefficients
p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}
can take negative values, they cannot be interpreted directly as standard probabilities. To address this, we adopt a quasi-probability sampling scheme to evaluate the expectation value as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}(\bullet)&=\sum_{\vec{s}}\left(p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}\;\bullet\right)\\
&=\sum_{\vec{s},p_{\vec{s}>0}}\Big(|p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}|\bullet\Big)-\sum_{\vec{s},p_{\vec{s}<0}}\Big(|p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}|\bullet\Big)\\
&=\sum_{\vec{s}}\Big(q_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}\;Z(p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}})\;\text{sign}(p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}})\bullet\Big).\end{aligned}

(10)

In the third line, we redefine
q_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}=|p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}|/Z(p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}})
with the normalization factor
Z(p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}})=\sum_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}|p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}|
, ensuring
q_{\vec{s}}
forms a valid probability distribution. We then sample a sequence of
r
operators according to
q_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}
in each round. Crucially, each
p_{\vec{s}^{(p)}}
is conditioned on the sampled history
V_{S}
, making the process adaptive. This procedure is repeated until
N
operators have been drawn across
N/r
sampling rounds.

After all
N
unitary operators have been sampled, we measure the observable
\mathcal{Q}
on the resulting state. The ensemble average of the measurement outcome is then

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{s}^{(N/r)}}p_{\vec{s}^{(N/r)}}\langle\psi|V^{\dagger}_{\vec{s}^{(N/r)}}\left(V^{\dagger}_{S}\mathcal{Q}V_{S}\right)V_{\vec{s}^{(N/r)}}|\psi\rangle.

(11)

By construction, the adaptive updating of the probability distribution at each round ensures that the ensemble average matches the target time-evolution to order
\mathcal{O}(t^{r})
at each step. The remaining algorithmic error of qSHIFT over the full simulation is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{U}-\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{qSHIFT}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lambda^{1+r}t^{1+r}}{N^{1+r}}\right).

The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
. Examples illustrating the computation of these probability distributions are provided in Appendix
C
.
As a final remark, the protocol can be easily generalized by replacing the fixed drawing number
r
with arbitrary integers
\{s_{i}\}
satisfying
\sum_{i}s_{i}=N
.

(N,r)
-qSHIFT protocol to 1D transverse field Ising model,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=h_{1}H_{1}+h_{2}H_{2},

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{5}Z_{i}Z_{i+1},

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{6}X_{i},

with
(h_{1}=J,h_{2}=h)=(1,0.1)
on a system of six qubits.
This model serves as a standard benchmark for quantum simulation algorithms.
For a randomly chosen initial state, we estimate the time-evolution of a physical observable
\mathcal{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{6}Z_{i}
.

We perform simulation using
(N=2,r=2)
-qSHIFT and
(N=3,r=3)
-qSHIFT. For the
(N=2,r=2)
-qSHIFT, the probability distribution obtained from our protocol is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} p_{11}&=\frac{h_{1}}{\lambda}\frac{h_{1}-h_{2}}{\lambda},\\
p_{12}&=p_{21}=\frac{h_{1}}{\lambda}\frac{h_{2}}{\lambda},\\
p_{22}&=\frac{h_{2}}{\lambda}\frac{h_{2}-h_{1}}{\lambda}.\end{aligned}

(12)

Note that since
h_{1}>h_{2}
, we have
p_{22}<0

(N,r)=(3,3)
-qSHIFT, the corresponding probabilities are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} p_{iii}&=\frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{3h_{i}}{\lambda}\right)^{3}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3h_{i}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{3h_{i}}{\lambda}\right),\\

p_{iji}&=\frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{3h_{i}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{3h_{j}}{\lambda}\right),\end{aligned}

(13)

with
i,j\in\{1,2\}
and
i\neq j
.
Detailed calculations for these probability distributions are provided in Appendix
C
.

V
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we proposed qSHIFT, a sampling-based quantum simulation protocol that adaptively updates the probability distributions for circuit sampling at each round. By sampling
r
operators in each step, qSHIFT achieves an algorithmic error scaling of
\mathcal{O}(t^{1+r})
while maintaining an
L
-independent circuit depth. This improvement is obtained at the cost of solving a system of
L^{r}
linear equations in a classical subroutine for each of the
N/r
sampling rounds. Since these adaptive updates can be computed on a classical computer, the protocol allows for the flexible pre-configuration of quantum circuits before execution without introducing additional quantum overhead. Consequently, qSHIFT provides a practical balance between accuracy and implementability on near-term quantum devices.

We estimated the gate complexity required to achieve a target precision
\varepsilon
per measurement of an observable. The resulting complexities are summarized and compared with existing methods in Table
1
. As shown,
(N,r)
-qSHIFT achieves improved scaling in both
t
and
\varepsilon
compared to qDRIFT, while remaining generally independent of
L
. The results for qDRIFT and standard Trotter formulas are adapted from Ref.
6
, while the
(N,r)
-qSHIFT results are derived in this work.

We also analyzed the sampling complexity of the qSHIFT protocol for a target precision
\varepsilon_{\text{qSHIFT}}
. Analogous to qDRIFT, the complexity
N_{\text{qSHIFT,sampling}}
is given by:

\displaystyle N_{\text{qSHIFT,sampling}}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Z(p_{\vec{s}})^{2}-1}{\varepsilon_{\text{qSHIFT}}^{2}}\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle^{2}\right),&\text{for }Z(p_{\vec{s}})>1,\\
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\|H\|^{2}t^{2}}{\varepsilon_{\text{qSHIFT}}^{2}}\right),&\text{for }Z(p_{\vec{s}})=1.\end{cases}

When all
p_{\vec{s}}>0
,
Z(p_{\vec{s}})=1
and the sampling complexity depends only on
\|H\|
and
t
, matching qDRIFT. In contrast, when
Z(p_{\vec{s}})>1
, the sampling overhead is proportional to
\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle^{2}
. Detailed calculations are provided in Appendix
B.2
. Furthermore, the sampling complexity of qSHIFT scales polynomially with the system size
N_{\text{sys}}
: in the
Z(p_{\vec{s}})=1
case, it is governed by
\|H\|^{2}\sim\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{sys}}^{2})
, while in the
Z(p_{\vec{s}})>1
case, it is proportional to
\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle^{2}
, which is also typically
\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{sys}}^{2})
for local observables.

Compared with conventional algorithms such as Trotterization and quantum signal processing, qSHIFT requires fewer quantum gates, enhancing its robustness to physical noise. This makes qSHIFT particularly compatible with error mitigation techniques like zero-noise extrapolation and probabilistic error cancellation
41
;
42
;
27
;
23
. Since the overhead of these methods grows rapidly with circuit depth, the reduced depth of qSHIFT can significantly lower the total mitigation cost. Combining qSHIFT with such strategies represents a promising avenue for improving practical quantum simulation performance.

Notably, in the
N=r\to\infty
limit, qSHIFT approaches the optimal complexity
\mathcal{O}(t)
implied by the no fast-forwarding theorem
8
;
3
, indicating that the adaptive sampling strategy is asymptotically optimal. These features establish qSHIFT as a robust framework for accurate and resource-efficient quantum simulation.

Several directions remain for future investigation. First, since qSHIFT can be integrated as a subroutine into broader algorithms like qSWIFT
33
and Krylov-based diagonalization
36
, exploring the performance gains in these contexts is a natural next step. Second, developing more efficient classical algorithms for generating the adaptive distributions would further improve practicality. Third, incorporating symmetry constraints into the sampling process could preserve selection rules and reduce sampling complexity. Finally, benchmarking qSHIFT on real quantum hardware will be essential to demonstrate its practical utility in the NISQ era and beyond.

Details of qDRIFT calculations

A.1
Mechanism of the qDRIFT

In this section, we review the original qDRIFT algorithms.
We consider a Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i=1}^{L}h_{i}H_{i},\penalty\ (\forall_{i}h_{i}>0),

and a physical observable
\mathcal{Q}
that we want to simulate with respect to an initial state
|\psi\rangle
.
The purpose of quantum simulation is to estimate

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{ideal}}=\langle\psi|e^{itH}\mathcal{Q}e^{-itH}|\psi\rangle,

as precisely as possible using quantum circuits.

To esmitate
\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{ideal}}
with qDIRFT, consider a set of operators,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\big\{V_{i=1,\cdots,L}\big|V_{i}=e^{-i\frac{\lambda t}{N}H_{i}}\big\},

where
\lambda=\sum_{i}h_{i}
and
N(>L)
is an integer parameter of qDRIFT.
The qDRIFT uses a probability distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\Big\{p_{i=1,\cdots,L}\big|p_{i}=\frac{h_{i}}{\lambda}\Big\},

of drawing an operator
V_{i}
in
\mathcal{V}
. The qDRIFT simulate the quantum system with the generated quantum circuit with drawn
N
unitary operators.

After drawing of
N
operators from
\mathcal{V}
following the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
, the expectation value of
\mathcal{Q}
is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{qDRIFT}}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{N}\langle V^{\dagger N}_{1}\mathcal{Q}V^{N}_{1}+\cdots+p_{L}^{N}\langle V^{\dagger N}_{L}\mathcal{Q}V^{N}_{L}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\cdots,s_{N}=1}^{L}p_{s_{1}}p_{s_{2}}\cdots p_{s_{N}}\langle V^{\dagger}_{s_{1}}V^{\dagger}_{s_{2}}\cdots V^{\dagger}_{s_{N}}\mathcal{Q}V_{s_{1}}V_{s_{2}}\cdots V_{s_{n}}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{s}}p_{\vec{s}}\langle V^{\dagger}_{\vec{s}}\mathcal{Q}V_{\vec{s}}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\cdots,n_{N}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{s}}p_{\vec{s}}\left(\frac{it\lambda}{N}\right)^{n_{1}+\cdots n_{N}}\langle[H_{s_{1}},[H_{s_{2}},\cdots,[H_{s_{N}},\mathcal{Q}]_{n_{N}}]_{n_{N-1}}\cdots]_{n_{2}}]_{n_{1}}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\vec{s}}p_{\vec{s}}\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle\right)+\left(\frac{it\lambda}{N}\right)\sum_{\vec{s}}p_{\vec{s}}\langle\left([H_{s_{1}},\mathcal{Q}]+\cdots[H_{s_{N}},\mathcal{Q}]\right)\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{t\lambda}{N}\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle+\left(\frac{it\lambda}{N}\right)N\sum_{i=1}^{L}p_{s_{i}}\langle[H_{s_{i}},\mathcal{Q}]\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{t\lambda}{N}\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle+\left(it\lambda\right)\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{h_{s_{i}}}{\lambda}\langle[H_{s_{i}},\mathcal{Q}]\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{t\lambda}{N}\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle+it\langle[H,\mathcal{Q}]\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{t\lambda}{N}\right)^{2}\right).

In the second line, we defined

\displaystyle p_{\vec{s}}=p_{s_{1}}\cdots p_{s_{N}},\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ V_{\vec{s}}=V_{s_{1}}\cdots V_{s_{N}},

for brevity of notations, and also introduced summation

\displaystyle\sum_{s_{1},\cdots s_{N}=1}^{L}\equiv\sum_{\vec{s}},

meaning that the sum over all possible
s_{i=1\cdots N}
.
In the third line, we used

\displaystyle e^{A}Be^{-A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}[A,B]_{n},

where

\displaystyle[A,B]_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv[\overbrace{A,[A,\cdots[A}^{\text{$n$ times}},B]]],\quad[A,B]_{0}\equiv B.

Since the ideally time evolved expectation value is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{Q}(t)\rangle_{\text{ideal}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}\left(it\right)^{n}\langle[H,\mathcal{Q}]_{n}\rangle,

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{Q}\rangle+\left(it\right)\langle[H,\mathcal{Q}]\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left(it\right)^{2}\langle[H,[H,\mathcal{Q}]]\rangle+\cdots,

and thus an algorithmic error of the qDRIFT protocol is
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
